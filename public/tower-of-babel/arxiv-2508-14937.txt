Hajrudin Fejzić

Abstract
We investigate a variation of Nicomachus’s identity in which one term in the cubic sum is replaced by a different cube. Specifically, we study the Diophantine identity ∑ j = 1 n j 3 + x 3 − k 3 = ( ∑ j = 1 n j + x − k ) 2 \sum_{j=1}^{n}j^{3}+x^{3}-k^{3}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}j+x-k\right)^{2} and classify all integer solutions ( k , x , n ) (k,x,n) . A full parametric family of nontrivial solutions was introduced in a 2005 paper, along with a conjectural condition for when such solutions exist. We provide a complete proof of this characterization and show it is equivalent to a structural condition on the prime factorization of n 2 + n + 1 n^{2}+n+1 . Our argument connects this identity to classical results in the theory of binary quadratic forms. In particular, we analyze the equation a 2 + a ​ b + b 2 = n 2 + n + 1 a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=n^{2}+n+1 , interpreting it as a norm in the ring of Eisenstein integers ℤ ⁡ [ ω ] \mathbb{Z}[\omega] , where ω = 1 + − 3 2 \omega=\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2} . This yields a surprising connection between a modified combinatorial identity and the arithmetic of algebraic number fields.

Introduction

In a 2005 paper
[
1
]
, the authors explored a variation of the classical identity

\sum_{j=1}^{n}j^{3}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}j\right)^{2},

often referred to as Nicomachus’s identity. The variant considered what happens when one of the terms in the cubic sum is replaced with a different cube. Specifically, the authors studied integer solutions to the identity

\sum_{j=1}^{n}j^{3}+x^{3}-k^{3}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}j+x-k\right)^{2},

and gave a full parametric classification of all solutions
(k,x,n)
. Trivial solutions such as
(x,x,n)
and
(n-1,2,n)
exist for all
n
, but the nature of nontrivial solutions turned out to be far richer.

In that same paper, the authors also announced a deeper result: that a nontrivial solution exists if and only if
N=n^{2}+n+1
is not equal to a prime or three times a prime.
1
1

1

The proof of this characterization was proposed to the author by Mladen Despić during their correspondence about earlier work on the problem.

While the main parametric result appeared in print, the full proof of this characterization—though elementary in nature—was omitted due to space and stylistic constraints.

This paper provides a complete proof of that result and also goes further. We establish that nontrivial solutions exist if and only if
N=n^{2}+n+1
has, counting multiplicity, **more than one** prime factor congruent to 1 modulo 3. In other words, we present two equivalent but structurally different characterizations: one phrased in terms of excluded cases (when
N
is a prime or three times a prime), and one phrased in terms of the number and type of prime divisors.

Combining these two characterizations yields a surprising consequence: if
N
has **only one** prime factor
p\equiv 1\mod 3
, then necessarily
N=p
or
N=3p
.

2
The Modified Cubic Identity and Quadratic Norms

We impose natural constraints on
x
and
k
in the identity (
1
), requiring that
x\neq k
,
x\neq 2
, and
k\neq n-1
, so that the identity does not reduce to one of the trivial cases. Suppose
(k,x,n)
is a solution to

\sum_{j=1}^{n}j^{3}+x^{3}-k^{3}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}j+x-k\right)^{2}.

(1)

By substituting and simplifying, one obtains the identity:

x^{2}+kx+k^{2}=n(n+1)+(x-k).

Introducing new variables
k=a-1
,
x=b+1
, this equation becomes:

a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=n^{2}+n+1=:N,

subject to the constraints
a\neq b+2
,
b\neq 1
, and
a\neq n
, which correspond to the conditions on
x
and
k
above.

In addition, we are interested only in
positive
solutions, meaning those for which both
a>0
and
b>0
.

The connection between the quadratic form
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
and the norm function in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, where
\omega=\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}
, is classical and well known to those familiar with algebraic number theory. The following section reviews basic properties of this ring and its norms, primarily for readers who may not be as familiar with this background. Readers comfortable with the arithmetic of
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
may wish to skip ahead to Section
4
, where the main argument resumes.

3
Arithmetic in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]

The following theorem is a classical result from the theory of binary quadratic forms, which plays a key role in counting the number of nontrivial solutions to our generalized cubic identity (
1
).

Theorem 1

(Representation by Norm)
.

Let
n=3^{j}p_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots p_{k}^{n_{k}}q_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots q_{d}^{m_{d}}
, where each
p_{i}\equiv 1\mod 3
and each
q_{i}\equiv 2\mod 3
is a rational prime. Then
n
can be expressed as
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
for some integers
a,b
if and only if all exponents
m_{i}
are even. In that case, the number of such representations is

6(n_{1}+1)(n_{2}+1)\cdots(n_{k}+1).

This result goes back to Euler and Gauss and was later generalized using the language of algebraic number theory. We now provide a detailed proof based on the arithmetic of the ring
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, where
\omega=\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}
.

Norms and Factorization in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]

The ring
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]=\{a+b\omega:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is a Euclidean domain with respect to the norm function:

N(a+b\omega)=a^{2}+ab+b^{2}.

This norm is multiplicative, i.e.,
N(zw)=N(z)N(w)
, and plays a role analogous to the absolute value in
\mathbb{Z}
. The conjugate of
z=a+b\omega
is defined as:

\bar{z}=a+b\bar{\omega}=a-b\omega^{2}=a+b-b\omega,

which also belongs to
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
.

The units in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
are the six elements:

\{\pm 1,\pm\omega,\pm\omega^{2}\},

all of which have norm 1. As a Euclidean domain,
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
is also a unique factorization domain (UFD), so every nonzero non-unit element can be written uniquely (up to associates and ordering) as a product of irreducible primes.

Classification of Primes

The behavior of rational primes in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
falls into three distinct cases:

•

If
p\equiv 1\mod 3
, then
p
splits as
p=\rho\bar{\rho}
, where
\rho,\bar{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
are distinct non-associate primes, and
N(\rho)=p
.

•

If
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, then
p
remains inert in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, i.e., it is a prime in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
and
N(p)=p^{2}
.

•

The prime
3
ramifies:
3=-\omega^{2}(1+\omega)^{2}
. Hence,
1+\omega
is a prime of norm 3, and all primes
\rho\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
with norm 3 are associates of
1+\omega
.

Conversely, any prime
\rho\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
falls into one of these cases:

•

N(\rho)=p
where
p\equiv 1\mod 3
, so
\rho
divides a rational prime that splits,

•

\rho=p\in\mathbb{Z}
where
p\equiv 2\mod 3
, so
\rho
is a rational prime,

•

N(\rho)=3
, in which case
\rho\sim 1+\omega
up to unit multiple.

Factorization of Integer Norms

Let
z=a+b\omega\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
, and define its norm as

n=N(z)=z\bar{z}=a^{2}+ab+b^{2}.

We now describe how the prime factorization of
z\in\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
determines the prime factorization of
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

By the multiplicativity of the norm, the norm
n=N(z)
factors as the product of the norms of the prime elements in the factorization of
z
. This yields the prime factorization of the integer
n
in
\mathbb{Z}
. Specifically:

•

Any rational prime
q\equiv 2\mod 3
that divides
n
must appear with an even exponent, since it corresponds to an inert prime in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
with norm
q^{2}
.

•

Any rational prime
p_{i}\equiv 1\mod 3
that appears in the prime factorization of
n
with exponent
n_{i}
, must have arisen from splitting:
p_{i}=\rho_{i}\bar{\rho}_{i}
. Then
z
must contain
\rho_{i}^{a_{i}}\bar{\rho}_{i}^{b_{i}}
such that
a_{i}+b_{i}=n_{i}
, and the number of such exponent combinations is
n_{i}+1
.

•

The prime 3 ramifies: since
3=-\omega^{2}(1+\omega)^{2}
, any power of 3 in
n
corresponds to an even power of the prime element
1+\omega
(up to unit multiple).

The six units in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
contribute to counting distinct associate elements
z
, but all these yield the same integer norm
n
. Therefore, each distinct factorization of
z
corresponds to six distinct ordered pairs
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
N(a+b\omega)=a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=n
.

Hence, the total number of ordered integer solutions
(a,b)
to
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=n
is

6(n_{1}+1)(n_{2}+1)\cdots(n_{k}+1).

This completes the proof of the representation theorem.

4
Characterization of Nontrivial Solutions

We now apply this number-theoretic structure to the problem of identifying when the modified cubic identity (
1
) has nontrivial solutions.

Theorem 2
.

Let
N=n^{2}+n+1
. Then the identity

\sum_{j=1}^{n}j^{3}+x^{3}-k^{3}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}j+x-k\right)^{2}

has a nontrivial solution
(k,x,n)
if and only if
N
has at least two prime factors (counting multiplicity) congruent to
1\mod 3
.

Recall that expressed in terms of quadratic forms, Theorem
2
is equivalent to showing that the quadratic form has positive solutions with
a\neq b+2
and
(a,b)\neq(n,1)
if and only if
N
has at least two prime factors (counting multiplicity) congruent to
1\mod 3
.

Lemma 1
.

If
N
is not a perfect square, then the orbit of any solution of
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N
contains exactly one positive solution.

Proof.

Suppose that
z=a+b\omega
is a solution of
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N
. Since
N
is not a perfect square, we must have
ab\neq 0
and
a+b\neq 0
. Consider the orbit

\{z=a+b\omega,\ -z=-a-b\omega,\ \omega z=-b+(a+b)\omega,\ -\omega z=b-(a+b)\omega,

\ \omega^{2}z=-(a+b)+a\omega,\ -\omega^{2}z=(a+b)-a\omega\}.

We examine which element of the orbit has both real and imaginary coefficients (in the basis
\{1,\omega\}
) positive.

If
z
is a positive solution, then it is the only one in its orbit with
a,b>0
. If
a<0
and
b<0
, then
-z
is positive. If
a>0,b<0
, then among
\omega z
,
\omega^{2}z
, one is positive. If
a<0,b>0
, then among
-\omega z
,
-\omega^{2}z
, one is positive.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

For every
n>1
, two distinct representations of
N=n^{2}+n+1
as
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
are given by
(a,b)=(n,1)
and
(a,b)=(1,n)
. We discard the latter, since it corresponds to
k=0
, which violates our constraints.

To determine the number of positive representations of
N=n^{2}+n+1
as
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
, we begin by applying Theorem
1
. This theorem gives the total number of such representations as

6(n_{1}+1)(n_{2}+1)\cdots(n_{k}+1),

where the
p_{i}\equiv 1\mod 3
are the prime divisors of
N
, and the
n_{i}
are their respective exponents. The factor of 6 arises from the fact that
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
has six units; that is, all solutions
(a+b\omega)
to the norm equation are grouped into orbits under multiplication by units, and each such orbit consists of exactly six distinct representations of
N
.

Since
N=n^{2}+n+1
lies strictly between
n^{2}
and
(n+1)^{2}
, it is never a perfect square. This allows us to apply Lemma
1
, which states that each orbit contains exactly one solution with
a>0
and
b>0
. It follows that the number of positive representations of
N
is

m=(n_{1}+1)(n_{2}+1)\cdots(n_{k}+1).

When
m=2
, the only positive representations are
(n,1)
and
(1,n)
, both of which are trivial. If
m=3
, then there is one additional positive solution
(a,b)
. If this solution satisfies
a=b+2
, then it is again trivial — but then by symmetry the pair
(b,a)
would also be a solution, giving four total representations, contradicting
m=3
. So the third solution must be nontrivial. In all other cases, where
m\geq 4
, at least one nontrivial solution exists.
∎

5
When
n^{2}+n+1
Is Prime or Three Times a Prime

We have seen that nontrivial solutions to the cubic identity (
1
) are closely related to the study of binary quadratic forms of the type
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N
, where
N=n^{2}+n+1
.

Building on Theorem
2
, we now restate the criterion in purely quadratic terms:

Theorem 3
.

The quadratic form
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N=n^{2}+n+1
has a positive solution other than
(n,1)
and
(1,n)
if and only if
N
has at least two prime factors (counting multiplicity) congruent to
1\mod 3
.

Clearly,
(n,1)
and
(1,n)
are always positive solutions to the quadratic form. By Theorem
1
, all prime divisors
q\equiv 2\mod 3
must occur with even multiplicity in the prime factorization of
N
, and at least one prime
p\equiv 1\mod 3
must divide
N
.

It is also immediate that if
N=p
or
N=3p
, where
p
is a prime congruent to
1\mod 3
, then the only positive solutions to
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N
are
(n,1)
and
(1,n)
. This observation forms the
if
direction in the next theorem. The
only if
direction, however, is more surprising.

Theorem 4
.

The quadratic form
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N=n^{2}+n+1
has a positive solution other than
(n,1)
and
(1,n)
if and only if
N
is not equal to a prime or three times a prime.

Proof.

We only need to prove that if
N\neq p
nor
N\neq 3p
for any prime
p\equiv 1\mod 3
, then there exists a positive solution
(a,b)\notin\{(n,1),(1,n)\}
to the equation
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N=n^{2}+n+1
.

We begin with the identity:

(an-b)(bn-a)=ab(n^{2}+n+1)-n(a^{2}+ab+b^{2}).

(2)

Suppose
N=n^{2}+n+1
satisfies the hypotheses. Then there exist positive integers
s
and
t
, both relatively prime to 3, such that
s\mid N
and
t\mid N/s
.

Now consider all pairs
(c,d)
with
1\leq c,d\leq n+1
. For each such pair, define the pair:

(u,v)=(cn-d\mod s,\ dn-c\mod N/s).

There are at most
N=n^{2}+n+1
such
(u,v)
pairs, but more than
N
such
(c,d)
pairs, so by the pigeonhole principle, at least two distinct
(c,d)
pairs map to the same
(u,v)
. Let
(c^{\prime},d^{\prime})
and
(c^{\prime\prime},d^{\prime\prime})
be two such pairs with
c^{\prime}\geq c^{\prime\prime}
.

Let
a=c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\prime}
,
b=d^{\prime}-d^{\prime\prime}
. Then
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
and both
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. By construction,
s\mid(an-b)
and
N/s\mid(bn-a)
.

Using identity (
2
), we conclude that
N\mid a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
. Since
0\leq a\leq n
and
|b|\leq n
, we have:

0<a^{2}+ab+b^{2}<3n^{2}.

This value cannot equal
2N
, since that would imply
N
is even, which is impossible. Thus

a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=N.

We now verify that this solution is positive and nontrivial. If
a=0
, then
N
is a perfect square which is not. Similarly
b\neq 0.
From
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=n^{2}+n+1
we see that

a(a+b)=(n^{2}-b^{2})+n+1>0\quad\text{and}\quad b(a+b)=(n^{2}-a^{2})+n+1>0.

Hence
ab(a+b)^{2}>0,
and so
ab>0.
Thus both
a
and
b
are positive.

From
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}=n^{2}+n+1
we have that
a=1
if and only if
b=n
, and that
b=1
if and only if
a=n
. In the case
b=1,a=n
, we have that
s
divides
(an-b)=(n^{2}-1)=(n-1)(n+1)
. Since
\gcd(n+1,n^{2}+n+1)=1
, we would have that
n\equiv 1\mod s
, which implies that
n^{2}+n+1\equiv 3\mod s
, contradicting the assumption that
\gcd(s,3)=1
. Thus
b\geq 2
and
a\leq n-1
.

If
a=1
and
b=n
, then since
N/s
contains a factor
t>1
with
\gcd(t,3)=1
, we would have
t\mid(bn-a)=n^{2}-1
, leading to a similar contradiction. Hence
a\geq 2
and
b\leq n-1
.
∎

An Unexpected Constraint on Prime Divisors

We conclude with an observation that emerges naturally from our results but appears to have escaped prior attention.

As noted in the introduction, combining Theorems
3
and
4
yields a striking consequence: if
N=n^{2}+n+1
has only one prime divisor
p\equiv 1\mod{3}
, then necessarily
N=p
or
N=3p
. In other words, the only way a value of
N
can have a unique “good” prime factor is if that prime appears alone or is multiplied only by 3.

Furthermore, Theorem
3
shows that
N
has exactly two positive representations as
N=a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
if and only if
N
has a unique prime factor congruent to
1\mod{3}
. Combined with the result above, this implies that in such cases, the only other possible prime factor of
N
is 3.

Thus, when
N=n^{2}+n+1
has only two positive representations, it is free of any prime divisors congruent to
2\mod{3}
. But even more striking is the following computational observation: for all
n\leq 10^{7}
, the integer
N
has no prime divisors congruent to
2\mod{3}
, regardless of how many representations it has.

This suggests the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1
.

Every prime divisor of
n^{2}+n+1
is either equal to 3 or congruent to
1\mod{3}
.

Whether this phenomenon admits a theoretical explanation, or whether counterexamples eventually emerge, remains an intriguing open question.
