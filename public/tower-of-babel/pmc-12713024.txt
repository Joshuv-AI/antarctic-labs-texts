Predictive Value of Sleep Architecture and Arousal Index for Surgical Outcomes in Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Dong Yun Lee, Hyun Jung Kim, Jayoung Oh, Siyeon Jin, Dongyoung Kim, Doo Hee Han, Hyun Jik Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWhile palatal surgery is an established treatment for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), variability in outcomes has prompted investigation into factors predicting surgical success. This study aimed to evaluate whether preoperative polysomnographic (PSG) parameters, along with drug‐induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) findings, can predict surgical outcomes in patients with OSA.MethodsThis retrospective study included 56 adult patients with OSA who completed both pre‐ and postoperative PSG and DISE assessments. Patients were classified into a successful outcome group (SG; ≥ 20 events/h reduction in apnea–hypopnea index [AHI]) or a worse outcome group (WG; ≥ 20 events/h increase in AHI). Baseline PSG and DISE findings were compared between groups, and multivariate regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of surgical success.ResultsPatients in the SG exhibited significantly higher preoperative AHI and larger tonsil size compared to those in the WG. While DISE collapse patterns did not differ significantly between groups, key differences emerged in PSG metrics. SG patients had a significantly greater proportion of non‐REM stage N3 sleep within non‐REM sleep and a lower arousal index, both of which were independently associated with improved surgical outcomes. Additionally, respiratory events were more prominent during non‐rapid eye movement sleep in SG patients. Multivariate analysis confirmed that a greater proportion of non‐REM stage N3 sleep and lower arousal index were strong predictors of postoperative AHI reduction.ConclusionsThese findings may offer more reliable predictors of which OSA patients will benefit from sleep surgery and underscore the importance of incorporating detailed PSG analysis into preoperative evaluations.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common sleep‐related breathing disorder characterized by partial or complete cessation of airflow during sleep, despite ongoing respiratory effort. The pathogenesis of OSA involves repetitive narrowing and collapse of the upper airway, often driven by increased negative pressure, turbulent airflow, and elevated airway resistance [1]. These anatomical and functional changes result in hallmark symptoms such as loud snoring, apneic episodes, excessive daytime sleepiness, and systemic complications including fatigue, cognitive dysfunction, and cardiovascular morbidity. Notably, OSA has been associated with increased mortality, particularly in patients with coexisting cardiovascular diseases such as arrhythmias and congestive heart failure [2].

Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the first‐line therapy for moderate‐to‐severe OSA, offering significant improvements in symptoms related to upper airway narrowing and reductions in cardiovascular risk [3]. However, long‐term adherence to PAP therapy is frequently poor, with many patients discontinuing its use within 1 year of initiation [4]. Consequently, alternative therapeutic approaches are often required, particularly for patients with anatomical features contributing to upper airway obstruction.

Surgical intervention has emerged as a viable option for selected patients, especially those with structural narrowing at the level of the soft palate or oropharynx [5, 6]. Various procedures—including relocation pharyngoplasty, expansion sphincter pharyngoplasty, and uvulopalatopharyngoplasty—have demonstrated efficacy in reducing AHI and improving subjective and objective sleep parameters [7, 8, 9]. Despite these advances, sleep surgery carries a risk of failure, with some patients experiencing persistent or worsening OSA postoperatively. Anatomical factors such as circumferential oropharyngeal collapse and tongue base obstruction are particularly associated with poor surgical outcomes [10, 11, 12].

To enhance surgical success rates, preoperative assessment tools such as drug‐induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) and polysomnography (PSG) have been employed to characterize individual patterns of airway collapse and sleep architecture. While DISE allows direct visualization of dynamic airway obstruction, PSG provides comprehensive data on sleep stages, arousal patterns, and respiratory event distribution. This increased potential for predicting surgical outcomes is expected to contribute to clinical research in sleep surgery.

In this study, we compared preoperative PSG and DISE findings between OSA patients who showed significant postoperative improvement and those who exhibited deterioration in AHI. Our objective was to identify specific physiological and anatomical predictors of sleep surgery outcomes and to inform more targeted patient selection for surgical treatment.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Study Design

We reviewed the medical records of patients retrospectively with OSA who underwent palatal surgery at the Otorhinolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery Department of Seoul National University Hospital from May 2015 to January 2022. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2206‐081‐1332) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All personal information was kept confidential as required. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with OSA, (2) patients who underwent palatal surgery (relocation pharyngoplasty, expansion sphincter pharyngoplasty, soft palate webbing, or uvulopalatoplasty) with nasal surgery, (3) patients who completed both pre‐ and post‐operative PSG and DISE examinations, and (4) patients with available medical records including surgical outcomes.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients who underwent additional surgical procedures at other anatomical levels (e.g., tongue base surgery, epiglottis surgery), (2) patients with incomplete medical or PSG records, (3) patients with central sleep apnea or other significant sleep disorders, and (4) patients with a prior history of sleep surgery, tonsillectomy, or nasal surgeries.

The patients were divided into two groups based on their surgical outcomes: the “successful surgical outcome group” (SG) was defined as those with an improvement in a post‐operative AHI score of 20 or more compared with their pre‐operative score and the “worse surgical outcome group” (WG) was characterized by a worsening in post‐operative AHI score of 20 or more compared with their pre‐operative score. We then analyzed detailed sleep parameters, including PSG and DISE data between the two groups (Figure 1). In addition, among the patients who underwent PSG at our institution, a subgroup of 18 patients had complete datasets that allowed detailed PSG parameter analysis, including sleep architecture variables, which were further compared between SG and WG (Figure 1).

The primary outcome of this study was the change in AHI before and after surgery, specifically whether patients showed a ≥ 20 events/h improvement or worsening. Secondary outcomes included detailed PSG parameters and DISE findings, which were analyzed and compared between the two groups.

Polysomnography

All patients underwent level 1 full‐time PSG, and OSA was defined as an AHI score of 5 or higher. Sleep parameters such as AHI, respiratory distress index (RDI), apnea index (AI), hypopnea index (HI), and AHI in supine and non‐supine positions, all of which are measured in events per hour, were examined according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) scoring manual [13]. For patients who underwent PSG examinations at Seoul National University Hospital (Grael 4K, Compumedics, Victoria, Australia), detailed sleep parameters, including clinical profiles, PSG parameters, and cephalometric variables, were studied.

Drug‐Induced Sleep Endoscopy

DISE was performed as part of our standard assessment for OSA [14, 15]. Briefly, sleep was induced by intravenous administration of midazolam (an initial dose of 3 mg for adult patients over 50 kg; 0.06 mg/kg). Once the patient had achieved an adequate sleep status, a flexible fiberoptic endoscope was introduced. Obstruction was assessed during desaturation events (a decrease of more than 3% in basal saturation during sleep), and representative findings were recorded. If no desaturation events occurred, changes were analyzed during snoring. An additional bolus of 0.5 mg of midazolam was administered in the event of an awakening. The DISE findings were classified by level, type, and degree of obstruction [6]. The level of obstruction was categorized into the velum, oropharynx, tongue base, and epiglottis. The type of obstruction was classified as concentric, lateral, or anteroposterior. The degree of obstruction was assessed on a scale from 0 to 4: 0 for no obstruction (0%), 1 for mild obstruction (0%–25%), 2 for moderate obstruction (25%–50%), 3 for severe obstruction (50%–75%), and 4 for complete obstruction (75%–100%) [16]. After each assessment, the patient was tilted to the right, and upper‐airway obstruction was observed and recorded again.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For continuous variables with a normal distribution, independent t‐tests were used to compare the SG and WG groups, while Mann–Whitney U‐tests were applied when the distribution was non‐normal. For categorical variables, Pearson's chi‐square test was used when all expected cell counts were ≥ 5; otherwise, Fisher's exact test was applied. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To adjust for multiple testing in tables with more than five comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of post‐operative AHI, including detailed PSG parameters.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Sleep Parameters Depending on Surgical Outcome

The SG comprised 41 males and 6 females, while the WG comprised 8 males and 1 female. The mean age of SG patients was 43.8 ± 13.1 years and 50.8 ± 11.5 years in the WG. No significant statistical differences in age or sex were evident between the two groups. Based on endoscopic examinations, the grade of tonsil size was significantly higher in the SG (2.3 ± 1.1) compared with the WG (1.2 ± 0.7) (p < 0.05). However, there was no significant difference in palatal position and the degree of tongue‐base narrowing between the two groups (Table 1).

In a comparison of sleep parameters from PSGs, the pre‐operative AHI score was significantly higher in the SG (60.8 ± 21.4) compared with the WG (26.1 ± 20.5), whereas the post‐operative AHI scores were significantly lower in the SG (23.2 ± 19.6) than in the WG (58.8 ± 19.2) (p < 0.05). Other sleep parameters such as RDI, AI, and HI were higher in the SG based on pre‐operative PSGs and declined significantly in the SG after sleep surgeries (p < 0.05). PSG data revealed that mean AHI scores decreased by 37.6 and RDI scores decreased by 34.5 following sleep surgery. In addition, post‐operative AI scores decreased by 20.9 and HI by 12.9 in the SG. In contrast to the SG, we found that the mean AHI score increased by 32.7 and the RDI score increased by 32.7 in the WG. AI scores also increased by 16.8 and HI scores elevated by 15.9. The AHI scores in the supine position were higher in SG patients before sleep surgery and improved significantly following sleep surgery (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was seen in non‐supine AHI scores between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

DISE Findings in SG and WG

When comparing pre‐operative DISE results between the SG and WG in the supine position, the results showed that all patients with OSA in the SG experienced a collapse at the level of the velum (concentric collapse; 62%, lateral collapse; 21%, anteroposterior collapse; 17%), 53% experienced a collapse at the oropharynx (lateral pharyngeal wall collapse), 74% at the tongue base (anteroposterior collapse), and 30% at the epiglottis (anteroposterior collapse). DISE findings also showed substantial incidences of collapse at the level of the velum (concentric collapse; 56%, lateral collapse; 11%, anteroposterior collapse; 33%) in the WG patients with OSA, 56% at the oropharynx (lateral collapse; 100%), 78% at the tongue base (anteroposterior collapse; 100%), and 44% at the epiglottis (anteroposterior collapse; 100%). There was no statistically significant difference in the patterns of upper‐airway collapse between the two groups in the supine position (p > 0.05) (Table 2). DISE findings also showed that the mean grade of collapse at the velum was 2.57 ± 0.74, 1.02 ± 1.01 at the oropharynx, 1.09 ± 0.95 at the tongue base, and 0.55 ± 0.95 at the epiglottis in SG patients. The mean grade of collapse at the velum was 2.78 ± 1.09, 1.11 ± 1.17 at the oropharynx, 1.00 ± 0.71 at the tongue base, and 0.56 ± 0.73 at the epiglottis of OSA patients in WG (Table 3).

When comparing the pre‐operative DISE results for the lateral position between the SG and WG, 85% of patients with OSA in the SG experienced collapse at the level of the velum (concentric collapse; 53%, lateral collapse; 33%, anteroposterior collapse; 15%), 47% at the oropharynx (lateral collapse; 100%), 32% at the tongue base (anteroposterior collapse; 100%), and 13% at the epiglottis (anteroposterior collapse; 100%). In the WG, 67% of patients experienced collapse at the level of the velum (concentric collapse; 67%, lateral collapse; 17%, anteroposterior collapse; 17%), 44% at the oropharynx (lateral collapse; 100%), 44% at the tongue base (anteroposterior collapse; 100%), and 22% at the epiglottis (anteroposterior collapse; 100%). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference in the type of collapse between the two groups in the lateral position (p > 0.05) (Table 2). In the lateral position, the mean degree of collapse at the velum was 1.81 ± 1.04, 0.91 ± 1.02 at the oropharynx, 0.47 ± 0.80 at the tongue base, and 0.19 ± 0.58 at the epiglottis of patients in the SG. The mean grade of collapse at the velum was 1.78 ± 1.48, 1.11 ± 1.36 at the oropharynx, 0.67 ± 0.87 at the tongue base, and 0.33 ± 0.71 at the epiglottis in WG patients (Table 3). We did not identify any significant difference in collapse sites, patterns of collapse, and degree of collapsibility between SG and WG patients.

Comparison of Clinical Characteristics and Sleep Parameters in the SG and WG

As a next step, we evaluated the clinical characteristics and sleep parameters of SG and WG patients with OSA, focusing on the subgroup of 18 patients who underwent both pre‐ and post‐operative PSG at our institution and had complete datasets available for detailed analysis. No significant differences were evident between the two groups in body mass index, neck circumference, or sleep questionnaires such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.

The PSG data revealed that sleep parameters related to sleep stage and sleep time did not differ significantly between the two groups, but SG patients experienced longer non‐REM stage N3 sleep periods compared with WG patients (Table 4). PSG data on respiratory events showed that AHI, RDI, AI, and HI scores were significantly higher in SG patients before sleep surgery. In particular, AI and HI scores were significantly higher during non‐REM sleep, and average O2 saturation was significantly lower in SG patients compared with WG patients (Table 4). After applying false discovery rate (FDR) correction, only the significance of the N3 sleep proportion remained robust, whereas other variables lost statistical significance. To identify PSG factors influencing post‐operative AHI scores, multiple‐regression tests were conducted. The results revealed a low proportion of non‐REM stage N3 sleep (β = −2.47, p = 0.006) and a high arousal index (β = 6.13, p = 0.016) were significantly correlated with aggravation of post‐operative AHI scores following sleep surgery. The overall regression model was significant (adjusted R 2 = 0.41, F(2,16) = 7.274, p = 0.006).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated pre‐operative sleep parameters and DISE findings in patients with OSA who had positive surgical outcomes with those whose sleep parameters worsened after sleep surgery. Specifically, we focused on patients who exhibited significant changes in their AHI scores following sleep surgery and aimed to identify predictive factors for surgical outcomes in patients with OSA. Our clinical data revealed that patients with large tonsils and a high AHI score were significantly prominent in the group with the positive surgical outcome group, irrespective of the severity of OSA and the pattern of collapse of the upper airway. In addition, some PSG parameters related to sleep stage and respiratory events provided meaningful information to predict surgical outcomes following sleep surgery. High AI and HI scores in non‐REM sleep and low O2 saturation were significantly prominent in the group with the positive surgical outcome group. Furthermore, longer durations of non‐REM stage N3 sleep and a lower arousal index were significantly correlated with positive surgical outcomes among patients with OSA.

Although sleep surgery has a positive effect in treating sleep apnea, it can fail by producing no change or aggravating post‐operative AHI scores. Extensive research has been conducted into the causes of surgical failure and the clinical parameters that can predict surgical outcomes [17, 18, 19, 20]. A previous study of patients with OSA who experienced surgical failure after palatal or oropharyngeal surgery suggested multiple mechanisms, including residual palatal obstruction, hypopharyngeal narrowing, and mouth opening that narrows the pharyngeal lumen, could contribute to surgical failure [21]. The main reasons for failure included incomplete resolution of the obstruction seen on pre‐operative DISE or exacerbation of obstruction at different levels despite performing multilevel sleep surgeries to reduce the failure rate in patients with OSA [7].

Previous studies used endoscopic findings of the upper airway to emphasize the importance of tonsil size as a positive predictor of surgical outcomes following sleep surgery, and suggested that a lower Friedman palatal position could be a clinical factor in surgical failure [11, 12, 22]. In particular, other research has also demonstrated a significant association between tonsil volume and surgical success, showing that a tonsil volume larger than 6.5 cm3 was predictive of favorable outcomes in pharyngeal surgery for OSA patients [23]. Our data also revealed that SG patients exhibited a significantly larger tonsil size compared with WG patients but there was no significant difference in Friedman palatal position or other oropharyngeal structures between the two groups.

Although DISE may not fully represent the natural sleep airway, it enables direct visualization of the upper respiratory tract under sedation. Therefore, the importance of DISE findings in determining the sites of sleep surgery has already been established, and interest in developing tools to predict surgical outcomes through DISE analysis is increasing [19]. In addition, a multicenter cohort study focusing on patients without tonsillar hypertrophy found that oropharyngeal lateral wall obstruction and complete tongue‐related obstruction observed during DISE were associated with poor surgical outcomes, whereas collapse at the velum or epiglottis showed no significant association with surgical success [24]. Our previous study also found that AHI scores following sleep surgery in patients with OSA with retropalatal circumferential narrowing improved by smaller increments compared with those in patients who experienced antero‐posterior narrowing or only lateral pharyngeal wall collapse [25]. In the present study, we did not identify any significant difference between SG and WG patients in terms of the sites of collapse or degree of collapsibility according to pre‐operative DISE findings. However, DISE can provide useful information about collapsible sites in patients with OSA during sleep and can help to design the plans of sleep surgery.

We also analyzed sleep parameters between patients with OSA in the SG and WG and found characteristic differences between the two groups in sleep parameters rather than DISE findings. SG patients experienced more respiratory events before sleep surgeries, and their pre‐operative PSG data exhibited significantly higher AHI, RDI, AI, and HI scores, along with lower average O2 saturation compared with WG patients. Our data indicate that sleep surgery should be considered for patients with OSA who experience more respiratory events during sleep if the obstructive sites of the upper‐airway obstruction are clear. In particular, a higher proportion of non‐REM stage N3 sleep and a lower arousal index were characteristic of SG patients, and these sleep parameters were significantly associated with a decrease in AHI scores following sleep surgery based on multiple‐regression analysis. Previous studies reported that longer periods of deep sleep and fewer arousals may be associated with a higher arousal threshold [26, 27]. Our findings also suggest that patients with OSA with a higher arousal threshold may be more suitable candidates for sleep surgery, and that non‐surgical treatment should be recommended to patients with OSA with a relatively low arousal threshold. In addition, SG patients experienced more respiratory events during non‐REM sleep, while WG patients showed more respiratory events during REM sleep. This aligns with findings from other studies, indicating that more than 90% of patients with REM‐predominant OSA had a low respiratory arousal threshold [28]. Clinically, it may be necessary to determine whether abnormal respiratory events occur predominantly in non‐REM sleep or are more dominant in REM sleep, and such information should provide useful information to help predict surgical outcomes. Various studies have been conducted to identify factors that can predict the outcomes of sleep surgery, but a definitive consensus has not yet been established. Our study determined which parameters can predict therapeutic results following sleep surgery using two commonly used diagnostic tools for patients with OSA: DISE and PSG. DISE was not found to provide useful information for predicting surgical outcomes, while PSG results revealed that patients with a higher proportion of non‐REM stage N3 sleep and a lower arousal index tend to enjoy superior surgical outcomes.

This current study has some limitations. First, it was conducted retrospectively, making it susceptible to selection bias because not all patient groups underwent the same surgery. The study included patients who underwent palatal surgeries combined with nasal surgeries, and there are various surgical interventions within palatal surgery and nasal surgery. However, to ensure that the target group consisted of patients who underwent similar interventions, patients who received surgeries at different anatomical levels, such as tongue base surgery, were excluded. Only 18 patients who underwent pre‐ and post‐operative PSG at the same hospital were included to compare sleep parameters, including sleep stage, and determination of sleep parameters as predictive factors may be limited. Furthermore, the small sample size for detailed PSG parameters limited statistical power. After FDR correction, only stage N3 sleep proportion remained significant, indicating that these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger cohorts.

Conclusion

The present study shows that patients with OSA with large tonsils and more respiratory events during non‐REM sleep can expect better surgical outcomes, but DISE results do not appear to be a very good predictor of therapeutic outcome of sleep surgeries. In particular, patients with OSA with a higher proportion of non‐REM stage N3 sleep, a lower arousal index, and non‐REM‐predominant respiratory events were more suitable for sleep surgery. These clinical findings suggest the importance of detailed evaluation of PSG data and endoscopic examination before deciding on sleep surgeries and provide clinical evidence on which patients with OSA are likely to have a relatively higher successful outcome after surgery.
