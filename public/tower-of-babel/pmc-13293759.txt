A component-wise commissioning and validation framework for non-transit electronic portal imaging device patient-specific quality assurance
Authors: Leyla Moghaddasi, Mario Perez, Maegan Stewart, Natasha Gabay, Stephanie Roderick, Regina Bromley, Ryan Brown, Jeremy Booth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeThe American Association of Physicists in Medicine Task Group 307 (AAPM TG-307) recommends validation of electronic portal imaging device (EPID) patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA), including verification of measured and calculated components. This study established a TG-307–aligned commissioning and benchmarking framework for non-transit EPID pre-treatment PSQA.Material and methodsFraction Zero Absolute Dose (FZAD) was commissioned on two TrueBeam linear accelerators equipped with amorphous silicon (aS) aS1000 and aS1200 EPIDs across flattened and flattening filter-free (FFF) photon beams (6MV/10MV) and two multi-leaf-collimator models. EPID performance was characterised for dose-rate linearity. Independent validation of measured and predicted dose components was performed using ion chamber, phantom-based, and treatment planning system dose planes in geometry replicating EPID configuration. System performance was benchmarked against Portal Dosimetry (PDIP) and phantom-based PSQA across >800 clinical fields. Error-detection sensitivity was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of externally supplied audit plans.ResultsThe aS1200 panel was linear across dose-rate and source-to-imager distance (SID) conditions, whereas the aS1000 exhibited saturation for FFF beams at shorter SIDs, with up to 5% under-response, with linearity restored at ≥150 cm SID. Independent component validation achieved mean gamma pass rates ≥97% (3%/2 mm). Bland–Altman analysis showed concordance within 95% limits of agreement, supporting equivalence in clinical decision-making. Sensitivity testing demonstrated discrimination at 2%/2 mm (area under ROC curve of 0.886), with improved detection compared to phantom-based PSQA at high specificity.ConclusionsA TG-307–aligned framework enables component-level interpretability and actionable insight for tolerance definition and model optimisation of EPID PSQA.

Introduction

In recent years, the use of amorphous silicon (aS) electronic portal imaging devices (EPIDs) for pre-treatment patient-specific quality assurance (PSQA) has grown rapidly due to their ease of use and high sensitivity and spatial resolution [1], [2], [3]. This has been accompanied by a rise in commercially available EPID PSQA tools designed to compare predicted and measured dose distributions [1], [3].

American Association of Physicists in Medicine Task Group 307 (AAPM TG-307) [1] recommends a structured validation process including independent verification of system components before integrated benchmarking. A component-level approach enables discrimination between measurement response and modelling behaviour, improves interpretability of predicted-measured dose discrepancies, and provides targeted feedback for beam model optimisation.

Several publications of EPID PSQA have focused on comparisons between different EPID systems [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], benchmarking against phantom-based detector arrays and log-based reconstruction methods [7], and evaluation of induced delivery errors [5]. Additional studies have explored transit in vivo applications for setup and anatomy change detection [3], [8]. While these demonstrate clinical feasibility and sensitivity to gross errors, they largely assess performance at the system level. Multi-centre evaluations show substantial variability in sensitivity and specificity across systems, driven more by clinical implementation than device characteristics, with clinically relevant delivery errors not consistently detected [9]. Consequently, the origin of suboptimal results - whether detector-related or modelling-related - remains unclear. Limitations in treatment planning system dose calculation accuracy, particularly in surface and low-dose regions, have been reported, supporting the need for independent measurement-based validation [10], [11]. These findings emphasise the importance of distinguishing measurement response from modelling behaviour, particularly where system-level agreement may mask underlying discrepancies. While recent guidance and clinical implementations [12], [13] emphasise retrospective system-level identification of error sources, prospective approaches remain scarce. To address this limitation, a component-level validation framework is required to decouple measurement response from dose modelling, enabling interpretable and actionable assessment of system performance.

This study aimed to establish and validate a component-based framework for a non-transit EPID pre-treatment PSQA system, including EPID characterisation across two panel models, independent verification of measured and predicted dose components, system-level performance evaluation, benchmarking against established quality assurance systems across a heterogeneous clinical cohort, and sensitivity analysis based on induced errors.

Materials and methods

Study systems and EPID characterisation

Two TrueBeam (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA) linear accelerators equipped with aS EPIDs were included in this study: one with an aS1000 panel and high-definition multileaf collimator (MLC) and the other with an aS1200 panel with Millennium MLC. Both platforms were evaluated across the flattened and flattening filter-free (FFF) photon beams at 6 MV and 10 MV. Statistical analysis was performed using in-house MATLAB (R2024b, MathWorks, Natick, USA) scripts. Gamma analysis was done using global gamma criteria of 2%/2 mm, 3%/2 mm and 3%/3 mm. A 10% low dose threshold was applied, with the exclusion of low-dose points bounded by above-threshold regions, to ensure all in-field points were evaluated.

The EPID PSQA system commissioned was Fraction Zero Absolute Dose (FZAD, Sun Nuclear Corp., Melbourne, USA). This is a calibration-based, non-transit EPID PSQA system corresponding to TG-307 [1] classification of forward, calibration-based EPID dosimetry. FZAD converts a measured EPID image to dose in a virtual water phantom using a look-up table calibration. Measured dose is compared to predicted based on a collapsed cone convolution/superposition dose algorithm (Sun Nuclear Dose Calculator (SDC)) [14].

The following acronyms are used throughout this work: EPID Phantom (50 × 50 × 8.4 cm3 solid water), DFZAD (SDC-predicted planar dose at depth 5 cm in EPID phantom), MFZAD (EPID-measured planar dose reconstructed in EPID phantom by FZAD), DTPS (TPS-Calculated (Eclipse AXB 15.6.06) planar dose at depth 5 cm in EPID phantom) and MMC3 (Measured planar dose in the MapCHECK3 phantom).

EPID characterisation was performed for both panels to confirm suitability for non-transit dosimetry [15], [16], [17], [18]. Standard checks, including dark-field/flood-field calibration, pixel sensitivity [19], linearity, and stability were completed. Characterisation followed TG-307 recommendations [1], with additional assessments consistent with the dosimetric framework later formalised in TG-330 [20].

Saturation behaviour formed the principal component of EPID characterisation, as it directly constrains the usable range of dose-rate (DR) and source-to-imager distance (SID) settings for measurements, particularly for FFF beams. Saturation testing was performed by delivering open fields across DRs of 600–2400 monitors unit (MU)/min, SIDs 100 cm, 150 cm, and 170 cm, and 6FFF and 10FFF energies. At 100 cm SID, detector DR linearity was assessed. Saturation onset was defined as the first deviation exceeding a 1% threshold from the expected profile.

Component-wise validation

Point-dose verification was performed using the EPID Phantom on the treatment couch (Fig. S1a). An ion chamber was positioned at 5 cm depth at an SSD of 145 cm to replicate DFZAD geometry. Square fields ranging from 2 cm to 20 cm were delivered and ion chamber-measured doses compared with MFZAD. DFZAD was compared with DTPS for the same fields. Evaluation was limited to 10FFF, representing the most challenging dosimetric scenario for the EPID.

Independent verification of planar dose was performed via comparisons between MFZAD and MMC3, and between DFZAD and DTPS (Fig. 1). For MapCHECK3 measurements, the detector was positioned at 5 cm water-equivalent depth within the EPID Phantom at 150 cm SID for 6FFF and 10FFF, and 100 cm SID for 6MV and 10MV (Fig. S1b). DTPS was generated by creating verification plans in the TPS using the EPID Phantom structure set, 2 mm calculation grid, dose-to-medium reporting (Material: Water), and gantry collapsed to 0° to reproduce FZAD delivery geometry.

Planar doses MFZAD and DFZAD were exported from SunCHECK and compared with the MMC3 and DTPS within SNC Patient (version 8.5.1.9). To eliminate differences in gamma analysis implementation between FZAD and SNC Patient, comparison between MFZAD and DFZAD was performed using SNC Patient for a representative subset of 10 fields per energy.

An empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) was used to characterise GPR distributions, representing the proportion of fields achieving a given threshold, and to identify underperforming energies or conditions for targeted investigation.

Clinical implementation and benchmarking

FZAD performance when applied to a range of treatment fields was analysed in accordance with TG-307 and general TPS commissioning principles described in MPPG-5b [1], [21]. Dynamic MLC fields and TG-119–style patterns were delivered to assess performance under increasing modulation complexity. Finally, a cohort of clinical IMRT, VMAT, SBRT, and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) patient plans spanning all four photon energies and diverse anatomical sites (Head and Neck (H&N), central nervous system, bone metastasis, breast, lung, abdomen, genitourinary) was evaluated.

FZAD results were benchmarked against the institution's established PSQA system (Eclipse Portal Dosimetry, PDIP v15.6.06, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, USA). PDIP is a non-transit EPID-based system that compares measured EPID image with a prediction based on an independent dose-calculation algorithm. For one linac, FZAD was also benchmarked against the MapCHECK3 phantom. For the phantom-based verification, methodology described in Section 2.2 was used. MapCHECK3 measurements were compared with DTPS in the corresponding geometry (Fig. S1) using gamma analysis. Agreement between systems was evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis, comparing paired measurements via bias and limits of agreement (Supplementary Material A).

Sensitivity testing

Sensitivity to delivery-related perturbations was assessed using datasets from the externally coordinated SEAFARER audit programme, comprising four reference fields and 44 predefined error fields. The audit provides a standardised 6MV VMAT H&N reference plan and a series of predefined plans modified to simulate realistic delivery errors (MLC shifts, gantry and collimator rotations, and MU scaling) [9], [22].

Reference and modified plans were assessed within our local QA workflow using FZAD, PDIP, and Delta4 (v1.00.018, ScandiDos AB, Uppsala, Sweden). For all systems, modified plans were compared to reference plan

Binary classification was based on the presence or absence of induced errors. Modified plans containing induced delivery perturbations were classified as error-positive (“should fail”), while unmodified reference plans were classified as error-negative (“should pass”). Gamma pass rate (GPR) threshold of 95% (PASS ≥95%, FAIL <95%) was used to assign classification outcomes. Sensitivity was defined as the proportion of error-positive cases correctly identified as FAIL. Given the limited number of reference plans (n = 4), specificity was not robustly estimable. This definition of ground truth isolates system sensitivity to known perturbations and avoids confounding from clinically derived thresholds in a limited dataset.

95% confidence intervals (CI) for binomial proportions were estimated using the Wilson score method, which is appropriate for small samples [23] (Supplementary Material A).

Discrimination performance was further evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with field-level GPRs treated as continuous classifiers. The area under the curve (AUC) quantified overall discrimination. The optimal GPR threshold was determined using the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity −1), corresponding to the cut-point maximising combined sensitivity and specificity [24], [25], [26]. ROC analysis assessed discrimination of controlled delivery perturbations, independent of clinical outcome interpretation.

Results

EPID panel characterisation

Dosimetric functionality including linearity, stability, uniformity, reproducibility, and positional reproducibility at multiple gantry angles are all within TG-307 recommended tolerances. For 10FFF beams at nominal dose rate (2400 MU/min), the aS1000 panel exhibitsexhibits signal saturation at 100 cm SID (under-response of up to 5%), with linearity restored only when the SID is increased to at least 150 cm. In contrast, the aS1200 panel remains linear across all SID conditions. Across all photon energies, the EPID response shows minimal dose-rate dependence within clinically relevant regions, remaining within 0.5%, with slightly greater variation observed for 10FFF (Fig. S2).

Component-based validation

Across all fields, ion chamber-measured doses demonstrate good agreement with MFZAD, exhibiting a mean difference of +0.4% (−0.4% to +1.2%). Comparison of DFZAD against DTPS also shows good agreement, with a mean difference of −0.3% (−1.2% to +1.2%). No systematic trend with field sizeare observed.

Independent verification of the measured and predicted planar dose components demonstrate high agreement across all energies. For MFZAD compared with MMC3, mean GPRs using 2%/2 mm global gamma criteria range from 93.9% ± 8.5% (10MV) to 97.0% ± 4.2% (6MV). For gamma criteria of 3%/2 mm, mean GPRs increase to between 96.8% and 98.7%, accompanied by a reduction in standard deviation for all energies. Using 3%/3 mm gamma criteria, mean GPRs exceed 97.3% for all energies, with further reduction in variability.

For DFZAD compared with DTPS, mean GPRs using 2%/2 mm criteria range from 90.2% ± 12.3% (6FFF) to 98.0% ± 2.8% (10×). Gamma criteria of 3%/2 mm results in mean GPRs exceeding 97.1% for all energies, with standard deviations below 3%. Using 3%/3 mm criteria, mean GPRs are ≥ 98.9% across all energies, with reduced variability. Summary of GPRs is provided in Supplementary Materials.

For simple fields and TG-119 test cases FZAD and PDIP demonstrates closely aligned GPRs. The mean difference between systems are −0.6%, which are within the observed inter-field variability (standard deviation 1.7–5.5%, depending on criterion). Given that GPRs are predominantly high for both systems, assessment of correlation are limited by clustering near the upper bound, rather than systematic disagreement.

Reproducibility across repeated deliveries shows low variability, with mean standard deviations of 0.5%, 0.4%, and 0.3% for 2%/2 mm, 3%/2 mm, and 3%/3 mm global gamma criteria, respectively.

Sensitivity testing

Wilson score analysis demonstrates highest sensitivity at 2%/2 mm for FZAD (0.89, 95% CI: 0.76–0.95), compared with 0.68 (0.53–0.80) for Portal Dosimetry and 0.70 (0.56–0.82) for Delta4. At 3%/2 mm, sensitivity decreases and converges across systems to approximately 0.50–0.52, with overlapping confidence intervals indicating reduced ability to discriminate delivery errors under this criterion.

ROC analysis at 2%/2 mm demonstrates strong discrimination for FZAD and PDIP (AUC = 0.886 for both), with lower discrimination for Delta4 (AUC = 0.804) (Fig. 5). At 3%/2 mm, discrimination performance converges (AUC range: 0.875–0.918), consistent with the reduced sensitivity observed under this criterion. Discussion

This study implemented and evaluated a TG-307–aligned commissioning framework for a non-transit EPID pre-treatment PSQA system, validating FZAD across two EPID generations and multiple photon energies. Independent verification of measured and predicted planar dose components provided quantitative insight for model tuning and measurement limitations. System-level benchmarking showed consistent trend-based agreement with established QA systems, and structured sensitivity testing demonstrated the capability of the framework to detect controlled delivery perturbations, providing a foundation for future evaluation of their clinical significance.

EPID characterisation showed saturation for FFF beams in the aS1000 panel, requiring extended SID, whereas the aS1200 remained linear, consistent with expected behaviour and confirming appropriate response within the framework [4], [27], [28].

In practice, the ECDF reveals underperforming conditions and directs investigation toward the contributing fields. Within this framework, this supports differentiation between modelling and measurement limitations. In contrast to transit PSQA approaches that rely on retrospective system-level analyses [12], [13], this framework allows prospective, component-level attribution.

Performance for flattened beams was high across both components. For 6FFF beams the predicted component showed a modest extension of the lower-tail, primarily associated with small fields. This indicates modelling sensitivity rather than detector limitation, likely related to MLC model parameterisation (e.g., DLG). For 10FFF, reduced agreement in the predicted component corresponded to low-dose valleys, suggesting limitations in MLC transmission modelling (overestimation of the field tails in the calculated model). In contrast, reduced agreement in some measured cases was attributable to the limited spatial resolution of the phantom-based system, resulting in volume-averaging effects in steep peak–valley regions.

FZAD performance across a heterogeneous clinical cohort showed high GPRs across energies and techniques, suggesting stable system behaviour. Higher mean GPRs and reduced variability for the aS1200 panel, particularly for FFF beams, reflect known hardware differences between EPID generations and are consistent with prior reports [27], [29]. Importantly, high GPRs alone do not define PSQA performance; rather, meaningful evaluation requires demonstrable sensitivity to relevant delivery perturbations within a structured validation framework.

Comparison with PDIP showed consistent trend-based behaviour between the systems. Although PDIP and FZAD employ fundamentally different methodologies, their role in pre-treatment PSQA is to flag suboptimal or failing deliveries rather than to achieve identical GPRs.

The small negative bias indicates systematically lower GPRs for FZAD; however, differences are bounded by the limits of agreement, suggesting no impact on pass/fail classification. Narrow limits of agreement support clinical decision equivalence. In high-performing cohorts, where GPRs cluster near the upper bound, Bland–Altman analysis is more informative for assessing decision equivalence than correlation metrics. Consistent with this, fields identified as suboptimal by FZAD were generally also identified by PDIP, indicating concordant clinical flagging between systems, in line with previous reports [4], despite differences in GPRs under strict gamma criteria.

This trend-based agreement was also observed when comparing EPID-based and phantom-based QA systems for fields with inferior GPRs. In these cases, EPID methods showed concordant reductions in GPRs, whereas phantom-based systems consistently passed these fields (Fig. 4), suggesting reduced sensitivity of lower-resolution systems rather than spurious EPID failures. This interpretation is supported by the lower position of the Delta4 ROC curve in the low false-positive region (Fig. 5), indicating reduced sensitivity under stringent specificity constraints, consistent with prior observations in highly modulated or small-field scenarios [27], [29], and large-scale clinical studies [3].

The convergence in sensitivity and ROC performance at 3%/2 mm, together with overlapping Wilson score confidence intervals, indicates that this criterion is insufficiently stringent in this setting, whereas 2%/2 mm provides improved discrimination of delivery errors.

This study was performed within a single-institution dataset, with sensitivity analysis limited by the number of reference deliveries and absence of clinically defined true-positive cases. Evaluation was restricted to non-transit workflows and did not include in vivo applications.

In summary, this work establishes a component-wise commissioning and benchmarking framework for non-transit EPID PSQA. The framework enables component-level interpretability and systematic localisation of discrepancies, supporting targeted model optimisation and informing the design of controlled, multi-site sensitivity studies for clinically relevant error detection.
