Defining metabolic heterogeneity in polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome based on the MTHFR C677T polymorphism: the potential pathway role of homocysteine
Authors: Lidong Zhao, Peipei Li, Jinhua Li, Hu Liu, Jinjuan Wang, Limei Qing, Jing Yang, Xiuzhen Wang, Lingxia Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) is characterized by diverse endocrine and metabolic abnormalities. Patients differ substantially in obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiometabolic risk. Homocysteine (HCY) is associated with insulin resistance and metabolic abnormalities in patients with PMOS, and the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) C677T polymorphism is an important genetic factor that influences HCY levels. However, whether MTHFR C677T genotype-associated HCY elevation is related to metabolic heterogeneity within PMOS remains unclear.MethodsA total of 213 women diagnosed with PMOS at the Reproductive Medicine Center, General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University, were retrospectively analyzed. Based on MTHFR C677T genotyping results, the participants were classified as the homozygous wild-type genotype C/C (CC, n = 48), the heterozygous genotype C/T (CT, n = 124), or the homozygous variant genotype T/T (TT, n = 41). Differences in clinical characteristics, glucose and lipid metabolism indices, inflammatory markers, and reproductive hormone levels were compared among the groups. Multivariable regression was used to evaluate the primary genotype-HCY association, whereas correlation, pathway, and prediction analyses were considered exploratory.ResultsHCY levels differed significantly among the genotypes. The TT group had the highest HCY levels, and this pattern remained consistent across BMI strata. The TT group also showed a tendency toward a more adverse metabolic profile, with relatively higher lipid-related and inflammatory indicators. Exploratory genotype-stratified analyses suggested that, within the TT group, higher HCY was associated with a less favorable metabolic pattern, particularly higher triglyceride levels. Pathway analysis further suggested a statistical relationship among MTHFR C677T genotype, HCY, and insulin resistance, although the cross-sectional design precluded causal interpretation. Prediction analyses also indicated that HCY contributed to the identification of the TT-associated high-HCY phenotype. These exploratory findings are presented in the Supplementary Material and require further validation.ConclusionThe MTHFR C677T TT genotype was closely associated with elevated HCY levels and a tendency toward a more adverse metabolic phenotype in patients with PMOS. The metabolic implications of this genotype-associated HCY elevation remain exploratory and require confirmation in prospective, multicenter studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13048-026-02259-8.Keywords: Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome, MTHFR C677T, Homocysteine, Insulin resistance, Metabolic heterogeneity

Introduction

Among women of reproductive age, Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS) remains a frequently encountered endocrine condition, affecting an estimated 11–13% globally [1]. In recent years, PMOS has been redefined as a complex syndrome involving both reproductive dysfunction and metabolic abnormalities, rather than a single disorder of ovulatory dysfunction [2, 3]. The latest international evidence-based guidelines further emphasize that patients with PMOS show marked individual differences in obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular risk, and that different patients present clearly distinct metabolic risk profiles [3, 4]. Recent studies have shown that PMOS can be classified into clinical subtypes with different metabolic risks and reproductive outcomes, indicating marked clinical heterogeneity within the disorder [1]. In addition, chronic low-grade inflammation, lipid metabolism disorders, and abnormal energy metabolism together form an important basis of the metabolic phenotype of PMOS and drive its clinical heterogeneity [5, 6]. Therefore, PMOS should be better understood as a disease with marked metabolic heterogeneity, and its precise stratification remains an important challenge in current research.

Homocysteine (HCY) is a key intermediate product in the methionine metabolic pathway. Elevated HCY has been widely shown to be associated with cardiovascular disease and metabolic abnormalities [7, 8]. Studies have shown that HCY may contribute to the development and progression of atherosclerosis and metabolic disorders by inducing oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory responses [8, 9]. Recent studies have reported that HCY levels are generally increased in patients with PMOS and are associated with insulin resistance and adverse metabolic phenotypes [8, 10]. However, studies based on genetic epidemiology and Mendelian randomization analyses have not supported a clear causal relationship between HCY and the development of PMOS [11, 12], suggesting that HCY may not be a direct causal factor for PMOS onset, but it may still reflect or participate in metabolic disturbance in patients with established PMOS.

At the genetic level, PMOS is considered a complex disease involving multiple genes, and its genetic basis has been confirmed by large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [13, 14]. However, existing studies have mainly focused on the association between genetic variants and the risk of PMOS, while less attention has been paid to how genetic factors affect the heterogeneity of metabolic phenotypes. The C677T polymorphism in the MTHFR gene, a key enzyme in the folate metabolic pathway, is one of the important genetic factors that affect HCY levels [15]. Previous studies have shown that the MTHFR C677T homozygous variant genotype T/T (TT genotype) is significantly associated with hyperhomocysteinemia and may be further associated with abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism and increased cardiovascular risk [15, 16]. However, few studies have systematically evaluated whether the genotype-defined high-HCY phenotype corresponds to a specific metabolic risk pattern within PMOS. Therefore, this polymorphism may be particularly useful for exploring genotype-associated metabolic differences among patients with established PMOS.

Because PMOS-related metabolic abnormalities involve overlapping pathways, including insulin resistance, lipid disorders, inflammation, oxidative stress, and one-carbon metabolism, integrating genetic and metabolic markers may help refine metabolic stratification [2, 17]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether MTHFR C677T genotype-associated HCY elevation is related to metabolic heterogeneity in women with PMOS. By integrating multiple indicators, we focused on evaluating the associations of this phenotype with multidimensional metabolic features, including lipid metabolism, inflammatory markers, and insulin resistance. We further explored its position within the metabolic network of PMOS, with the aim of providing new evidence for precise stratification of PMOS.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in strict accordance with medical ethical standards and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Information for all included patients was obtained from the hospital’s electronic health record system. Personal privacy information was anonymized during the study. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Review Committee of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (KYLL-2024-0205).

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was conducted at the Reproductive Medicine Center of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. The study population consisted of 213 women who were diagnosed with PMOS between 2020 and 2025 and had available clinical, biochemical, and MTHFR C677T genotyping data. PMOS was diagnosed in line with the 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline for PCOS [3]. Diagnosis required at least two of the following features after exclusion of related disorders: oligo/anovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and ultrasound-defined polycystic ovarian morphology. Clinical hyperandrogenism was defined as hirsutism based on the modified Ferriman–Gallwey score, and biochemical hyperandrogenism as total and/or free testosterone above the laboratory reference limits. Polycystic ovarian morphology was defined as ≥ 20 follicles in either ovary; for transabdominal ultrasound or incomplete follicle assessment, an ovarian volume of ≥ 10 mL or a follicle number per section of ≥ 10 in either ovary was used.Patients were excluded if they had thyroid dysfunction, systemic autoimmune disease, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing syndrome, androgen-secreting tumors, missing key clinical or biochemical data, or previous use of medications or supplements that may affect glucose-lipid metabolism, reproductive hormones, or HCY levels. Thyroid dysfunction was defined as a documented diagnosis of thyroid disease, current use of thyroid hormone or antithyroid medication, or TSH and/or free thyroxine values outside the laboratory reference ranges. Patients were divided into the homozygous wild-type genotype C/C group (CC group), the heterozygous genotype C/T group (CT group), and the TT genotype group. The TT genotype group was compared with the combined CC/CT genotype group in the subsequent exploratory analyses.

MTHFR C677T genotyping

MTHFR C677T genotyping was performed using EDTA-anticoagulated peripheral venous blood and the MTHFR C677T Genotyping Kit (PCR combined with GoldMag magnetic microparticle lateral-flow chromatography; Xi’an GoldMag Nanobiotechnology Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay uses allele-specific PCR to amplify the mutant and wild-type alleles, followed by GoldMag magnetic microparticle-based lateral-flow chromatography to determine the genotype. Uracil-N-glycosylase was included in the reaction system to reduce PCR carryover contamination. Genotypes were assigned according to the mutant- and wild-type detection-card patterns specified by the manufacturer. The manufacturer-specified control materials included mutant-positive recombinant DNA, wild-type-positive recombinant DNA, a negative control, and positive/negative control cards. All 213 participants included in the analytical dataset had reportable genotype results. However, because this retrospective study was based on finalized routine clinical reports, the research database did not contain the total number of specimens originally submitted for genotyping, indeterminate results, or repeated-sample quality-control records. Therefore, the overall laboratory call rate, missingness among all submitted specimens, and repeated-sample concordance could not be retrospectively determined.

Clinical data and laboratory index collection

All data were obtained from laboratory tests and physical examination results recorded during hospital visits. The data were collected from the hospital’s electronic health record system. General information included age, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Laboratory indices included: (1) blood routine-related indices: white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (Neu), lymphocyte count (Lym), monocyte count (Mon), eosinophil count (EOS), basophil count (BAS), and platelet count (PLT); (2) reproductive endocrine indices: basal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), basal luteinizing hormone (LH), LH/FSH ratio, basal estradiol (E2), basal testosterone (T), basal progesterone (Prog), basal prolactin (PRL), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH); (3) glucose metabolism indices: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 2-h postprandial glucose (120 min GLU), fasting insulin (FINS), and 2-h postprandial insulin (120 min INS); (4) lipid metabolism indices: triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), apolipoprotein A1 (apoA1), apolipoprotein B (apoB), and apoB/apoA1 ratio; and (5) other metabolism-related indices: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), uric acid (UA), 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH-D), HCY and TSH.

Definitions of related indices

BMI was calculated as follows: BMI = body weight (kg)/height² (m²). Insulin resistance was assessed using the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR): HOMA-IR = fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (mIU/L)/22.5. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) were derived by dividing the neutrophil and platelet counts, respectively, by the lymphocyte count. The apoB/apoA1 ratio was used to assess lipid metabolism-related risk. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was used to assess insulin resistance and glucose-lipid metabolic status. It was calculated using fasting TG and FPG collected in this study, as follows: TyG index = ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2]. If the original data were recorded in mmol/L, they were uniformly converted to mg/dL before analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted with SPSS version 27.0 and R software. The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test before further statistical analysis. Variables with a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance. Variables with a non-normal distribution were expressed as the median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) with between-group differences assessed by the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Deviation of the MTHFR C677T genotype distribution from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was evaluated using the χ² test.

Univariable and multivariable linear regression or logistic regression analyses were used to examine the associations between genotype and clinical metabolic indices, with adjustment for confounding factors such as age and BMI. Subgroup analyses were performed according to BMI strata (< 24, 24–27.9, and ≥ 28 kg/m²). A genotype × BMI interaction term was included to evaluate effect modification.

Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess correlations among metabolic indices. Correlation heatmaps and network plots were generated. An exploratory pathway analysis was performed to evaluate the statistical pathway linking MTHFR C677T genotype, HCY, and HOMA-IR. In this model, MTHFR C677T genotype was treated as the exposure, HCY as the intermediate variable, and HOMA-IR as the outcome. Age and BMI were included as covariates. Because HCY and HOMA-IR were measured simultaneously in this cross-sectional study, this analysis was interpreted as a descriptive and hypothesis-generating statistical pathway analysis rather than causal mediation.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to evaluate the discrimination, calibration, and clinical benefit of the exploratory classification model for distinguishing the TT genotype from the CC/CT genotypes.LASSO regression, random forest, and SHAP analysis were further used for feature selection and assessment of variable importance. Sensitivity analyses were performed by removing extreme values and repeating the analyses in the BMI < 28 kg/m² subgroup. For selected graphical comparisons of skewed metabolic and inflammatory variables, analyses after removal of extreme values were interpreted as supportive sensitivity analyses rather than as the sole basis for the main conclusions. All tests were two-sided. For the primary analysis of the association between MTHFR C677T genotype and HCY, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All other analyses were exploratory; their P values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should therefore be interpreted as nominal and hypothesis-generating.

Exploratory pathway analysis showed model-based associations among the MTHFR C677T genotype, HCY, and HOMA-IR after adjustment for age and BMI. Genotype was significantly associated with HCY levels (path a: β = 6.58, 95% CI: 4.77–8.39, P < 0.001), and HCY was positively associated with HOMA-IR in the fitted model (path b: β = 0.027, 95% CI: 0.015–0.040, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). However, because HCY and HOMA-IR were measured at the same clinical assessment, the temporal ordering required for causal mediation could not be established. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory statistical associations rather than evidence of causal mediation. The hypothesized framework illustrates potential relationships among the MTHFR C677T genotype, HCY elevation, obesity-related factors, and insulin resistance, but does not represent a causal mechanism demonstrated by the present study (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

This study evaluated the relationships among the MTHFR C677T genotype, HCY levels, and metabolic abnormalities in patients with PMOS. The main finding was that patients carrying the TT genotype had significantly higher HCY levels than those carrying the CC or CT genotypes, and this association remained significant after adjustment for age and BMI and was supported by sensitivity analyses. This finding supports that MTHFR C677T is strongly associated with HCY levels in this PMOS cohort.

In addition to the robust genotype-HCY association, exploratory graphical and sensitivity analyses suggested that the TT genotype was accompanied by a tendency toward less favorable lipid-related and CBC-derived inflammatory indices, including higher TG, LDL-C, apoB/apoA1 ratio, WBC, neutrophil count, and NLR. However, crude HOMA-IR levels did not differ significantly among genotype groups, and several metabolic comparisons were sensitive to distributional skewness and extreme values. These findings provide exploratory evidence of possible metabolic and inflammatory differences, but do not establish that the TT genotype defines a distinct subgroup with higher cardiometabolic risk (Fig. 3).

The correlations of HCY with HOMA-IR and TG differed by genotype. In the TT group, HCY showed a positive trend with HOMA-IR, whereas no positive correlation was observed in the CC/CT group. These exploratory correlations may differ by genotype, but the small subgroup sizes do not permit firm conclusions regarding effect modification (Supplementary Figure S3). Finally, exploratory pathway analysis showed model-based statistical associations among MTHFR genotype, HCY, and HOMA-IR. Because this was a retrospective cross-sectional study, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal biological pathway but rather as hypothesis-generating evidence supporting further longitudinal and mechanistic investigations. In addition, exploratory prediction analyses are presented in Supplementary Figure S5; their apparent performance should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of TT participants and the lack of external validation.

These findings suggest that the MTHFR C677T TT genotype is primarily associated with elevated HCY, while the accompanying glucose, lipid, and inflammatory patterns remain exploratory. Therefore, our focus was not whether the MTHFR C677T polymorphism determines the occurrence of PMOS, but whether it can help identify a subgroup of patients with PMOS who have higher metabolic risk. Although stratified management is an important direction in current PCOS research [1, 18], its clinical value requires prospective validation.

PMOS was previously defined as a reproductive endocrine disorder mainly characterized by ovulatory dysfunction and hyperandrogenemia [1, 3]. In recent years, evidence has shown that PMOS is also a syndrome with marked metabolic heterogeneity [1, 3]. Patients differ substantially in the degree of obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, and long-term cardiovascular risk [18, 19]. Therefore, identifying factors that may contribute to within-PMOS metabolic heterogeneity remains an important research question [3, 20–22].

The results of this study support the view that metabolic heterogeneity in PMOS may arise not only from obesity and insulin resistance themselves, but also from genetic background and one-carbon metabolic status [1, 20, 23, 24]. We observed that although the crude comparison in HOMA-IR among different genotypes was not significant, patients with the TT genotype had persistently elevated HCY levels, together with a tendency toward more adverse lipid metabolism and inflammatory indicators (Figs. 2 and 3). Exploratory pathway analysis further showed model-based statistical associations among MTHFR genotype, HCY, and HOMA-IR (Fig. 4) [8, 25, 26].However, this model should be interpreted as describing statistical pathway associations rather than causal biological mechanisms.

Notably, as a sensitivity analysis, BMI-stratified comparisons showed that HOMA-IR increased with increasing BMI in all genotype groups (Supplementary Figure S4), suggesting that obesity or fat accumulation may still be an important aggravating factor for insulin resistance in patients with PMOS [3, 27]. However, elevated HCY levels in the TT group remained relatively stable across different BMI strata (Supplementary Figure S1), In the interaction model using BMI as a continuous variable, the genotype-by-BMI interaction was not statistically significant. Given the small genotype-by-BMI subgroup sizes, these stratified findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not establish that the MTHFR-HCY association is independent of obesity. In other words, obesity-related metabolic stress and genetically related HCY elevation may jointly contribute to the development of metabolic abnormalities in patients with PMOS. Therefore, BMI alone cannot fully reflect metabolic risk in patients with PMOS. Some patients without markedly increased BMI may still be at higher risk because of abnormalities in other metabolic pathways [3, 8, 28].

Previous studies on HCY and PMOS have mainly focused on case-control comparisons. Most studies have suggested that HCY levels are generally increased in patients with PMOS and are associated with insulin resistance, obesity, and dyslipidemia [29, 30]. However, these studies mainly focused on whether HCY is elevated in patients with PMOS, with insufficient attention to which subgroups within PMOS are more likely to develop HCY-related metabolic abnormalities. Regarding the role of the MTHFR C677T polymorphism in PMOS, previous studies have more often focused on its association with disease susceptibility, but the findings have not been fully consistent [31]. Differences in population source, ethnic background, sample size, and diagnostic criteria across studies may lead to an unstable association between the TT genotype and the risk of PMOS. Therefore, it is not accurate to simply regard this locus as a pathogenic risk gene for PMOS [3, 32]. Based on the marked clinical and metabolic heterogeneity of PMOS [1, 3], this study did not focus on whether the TT genotype increases the risk of PMOS. Instead, it further explored whether this genotype corresponds to a specific state of HCY elevation and a metabolic abnormality phenotype in patients with confirmed PMOS (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). This may help explain how genetic background contributes to metabolic risk stratification in PMOS from the perspective of within-disease differences [8].

An important finding of this study was that the main reproductive endocrine indicators did not differ clearly among genotypes (Supplementary Figure S2), whereas the HCY difference was robust and lipid metabolism and inflammatory indicators showed exploratorygenotype-related patterns (Figs. 2 and 3). This suggests that the role of the MTHFR C677T polymorphism in PMOS may be more evident in the metabolic dimension rather than in the classical reproductive endocrine dimension [1, 20, 23]. In other words, the MTHFR-HCY axis may not determine whether patients present with hyperandrogenism or abnormal ovarian reserve, but may be more useful for identifying PMOS subgroups with a tendency toward metabolic abnormalities [1, 3, 8]. Therefore, the additional significance of this study is that it links the MTHFR C677T genotype and HCY levels with metabolic heterogeneity in PMOS, although its value for metabolic risk stratification requires prospective validation.

MTHFR is a key enzyme in the folate cycle and one-carbon metabolic pathway. It catalyzes the reduction of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate. The latter acts as a methyl donor and promotes the remethylation of HCY to methionine with the involvement of methionine synthase and vitamin B12 [7, 24, 33]. The C677T mutation can reduce MTHFR enzyme activity, leading to decreased HCY clearance and increased plasma HCY levels. This effect is most obvious in TT homozygotes [15, 26, 33]. In this study, HCY levels were significantly higher in the TT group than in the other genotype groups, and this difference remained stable in BMI-stratified and sensitivity analyses. This is consistent with the basic mechanism by which this locus affects HCY metabolism. Elevated HCY may contribute to metabolic abnormalities by inducing oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory responses [34, 35]. These inflammatory and stress pathways may also interfere with insulin signaling and promote insulin resistance [8, 36]. In addition, higher HCY levels are associated with increased TG and remnant cholesterol and decreased HDL-C/ApoA1, while an increased ApoB/ApoA1 ratio reflects a higher burden of atherogenic lipoproteins and an increased risk of metabolic syndrome [8, 29, 37].

In this study, the TT group had elevated HCY levels and showed a tendency toward higher TG, LDL-C, apoB/apoA1 ratio, and NLR in graphical and sensitivity analyses. In the TT group, HCY was positively correlated with TG, suggesting that HCY may be related to lipid metabolism abnormalities under this genetic background. Exploratory pathway analysis showed model-based statistical associations among the MTHFR C677T genotype, HCY, and HOMA-IR (Fig. 4A). However, because HCY and HOMA-IR were measured simultaneously and no significant genotype–HOMA-IR association was observed, the requirements for causal mediation were not met. Therefore, this analysis should be interpreted as an exploratory pathway analysis rather than evidence of mediation. Prospective and experimental studies are still needed for validation.

In current clinical practice, the assessment of metabolic risk in patients with PMOS mainly focuses on traditional indicators, including BMI/waist circumference, blood pressure, glucose metabolism status, lipid profile, and personal or family metabolic risk factors [3]. In other assessments, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are also commonly used to reflect the degree of insulin resistance. However, metabolic abnormalities in patients with PMOS are not always fully consistent with BMI [38]. Some non-obese patients may still have insulin resistance, abnormal glucose metabolism, abnormal lipid metabolism, or increased cardiometabolic risk [19, 22]. Therefore, identifying stratification markers that can supplement traditional indicators has practical value for recognizing subgroups with less favorable metabolic profiles within PMOS [39, 40].

This study showed that the BMI-only model had limited ability to identify the TT-associated high-HCY phenotype, whereas model performance improved markedly after HCY was included. However, this in-sample improvement does not establish that HCY provides incremental predictive value beyond BMI and routine laboratory markers. Although conventional metabolic assessments, including OGTT, fasting insulin, lipid profiles, blood pressure, and fatty liver-related indicators, remain important in patients with PMOS [3, 19, 41, 42], the present findings do not support HCY-guided changes in clinical management. The clinical utility of HCY and its effects on patient outcomes require prospective validation.

HCY concentrations are influenced by nutritional status and other clinical and lifestyle factors. Although folate and B-vitamin supplementation can reduce HCY levels in some settings [43–45], this study did not measure folate, vitamin B12, or vitamin B6 status and did not evaluate supplementation. Therefore, our findings do not support recommending folate or B-vitamin supplementation on the basis of MTHFR genotype or HCY level, and they do not establish that lowering HCY would improve metabolic outcomes. Studies involving direct measurement of nutritional status and prospective intervention trials are required.

This study has several strengths. First, this study integrated genotype, HCY, glucose and lipid metabolism, inflammatory indicators, and reproductive endocrine indicators, allowing a relatively comprehensive evaluation of the relationship between the MTHFR-HCY axis and different clinical phenotypes. Second, the adjusted analysis demonstrated a stable association between MTHFR C677T and elevated HCY, while BMI-stratified analyses were treated as sensitivity analyses and correlation, pathway, and prediction analyses were treated as exploratory. Finally, this study found that the main reproductive endocrine indicators did not differ clearly among genotypes, whereas genotype-related patterns were mainly observed in the metabolic and inflammatory dimensions. This suggests that this genotype is mainly associated with HCY elevation, whereas the accompanying metabolic findings require further validation [1, 3, 15, 23].

This study also has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size, especially the small number of patients with the TT genotype. Therefore, particularly for the BMI-stratified analyses, the small subgroup sizes may have resulted in insufficient statistical power, an increased risk of false-positive findings, and unstable estimates [46, 47]. Second, the cross-sectional design can only suggest associations among variables and cannot prove causal relationships among MTHFR C677T, HCY, and insulin resistance.In addition, because HCY and HOMA-IR were measured simultaneously, the temporal assumptions required for causal mediation analysis were not satisfied. Therefore, the exploratory pathway analysis presented in this study should be interpreted as describing statistical associations rather than establishing causal mechanisms. Reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded, and prospective longitudinal studies are required to validate the temporal relationships among genotype, HCY, and insulin resistance.Third, retrospective medical records could not fully capture long-term, undocumented, or unreported medication and supplement use such as information on folate intake, vitamin B12/B6 levels, diet, exercise, and long-term medication use, so interference from confounding factors cannot be excluded [7, 8, 26]. Additionally, the MTHFR C677T genotype distribution deviated from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Given the clinic-based PMOS cohort and unavailable call-rate and repeat-concordance data, selection effects, population structure, sampling variation, or genotyping error cannot be excluded. Therefore, the genetic findings require independent validation. Fourth, the prediction model included multiple variables despite only 41 TT cases, resulting in a low events-per-variable ratio (< 7) and a risk of overfitting. In addition, no independent validation cohort was available; therefore, the stability and generalizability of the model require further evaluation.

Conclusion

In patients with PMOS, the MTHFR C677T TT genotype was closely associated with significantly elevated HCY levels and was accompanied by a tendency toward more adverse inflammatory and glucose-lipid metabolic phenotypes. HCY may represent an important genotype-associated metabolic pathway related to insulin resistance and metabolic abnormalities. The prediction model incorporating HCY significantly improved the ability to identify TT-associated metabolic patterns. These findings provide hypothesis-generating evidence that the MTHFR-HCY axis may contribute to metabolic heterogeneity in PMOS ; however, its value for risk stratification and individualized management remains uncertain and requires prospective validation.
