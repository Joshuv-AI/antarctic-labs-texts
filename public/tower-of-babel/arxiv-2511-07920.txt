Abstract
A diffusion-based neural decoding framework optimized for real-time imagined speech classification in individuals with aphasia. The system integrates a lightweight conditional diffusion encoder and convolutional classifier trained using subject-specific EEG data acquired from a Korean-language paradigm. A dual-criterion early stopping strategy enabled rapid convergence under limited calibration data, while dropout regularization and grouped temporal convolutions ensured stable generalization. During online operation, continuous EEG streams were processed in two-second sliding windows to generate class probabilities that dynamically modulated visual and auditory feedback according to decoding confidence. Across twenty real-time trials, the framework achieved 65 % top-1 and 70 % top-2 accuracy, outperforming offline evaluation (50 % top-1). These results demonstrate the feasibility of deploying diffusion-based EEG decoding under practical clinical constraints, maintaining reliable performance despite environmental variability and minimal preprocessing. The proposed framework advances the translation of imagined speech brain–computer interfaces toward clinical communication support for individuals with severe expressive language impairment.

INTRODUCTION

Individuals with aphasia face profound challenges in establishing direct verbal communication, which significantly constrains their capacity to convey thoughts, needs, and emotional states. Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) offer a novel therapeutic modality to reinstate communicative function in such populations by facilitating direct decoding of neural signals for device control, thereby circumventing impaired motor and linguistic networks consequent to cerebral injury

Despite the considerable promise of BCIs, contemporary research predominantly relies on offline processing paradigms wherein neural signal acquisition, model training, and classification are executed sequentially in a post-hoc fashion. The transition to real-time, closed-loop BCI systems necessitates stringent constraints on both latency and computational complexity

In this study, we present a novel two-session online experimental framework specifically designed for individuals with aphasia. The framework incorporates culturally and linguistically appropriate Korean language stimuli to ensure relevance and accessibility. Cue presentation sequences are pseudo-randomized yet reproducible to balance experimental control and variability. Furthermore, the protocol integrates dynamically adjusted multimodal feedback mechanisms calibrated to model confidence levels, aiming to maintain participant engagement and sustained attention throughout the experiment. Furthermore, we introduce an optimized lightweight variant of the diffusion-based neural decoding architecture tailored for real-time inference capability through systematic architectural modifications, including dimensionality reduction, temporal kernel optimization, group normalization regularization techniques, and dual early-stopping criteria to minimize computational overhead while preserving decoding accuracy. The proposed framework successfully achieves classification performance exceeding 60 % accuracy in real-time imagined speech decoding from a post-stroke aphasia patient, demonstrating the clinical feasibility and practical applicability of computationally efficient BCI systems for communication restoration.

II
MATERIALS AND METHODS

II-A

Experiment Paradigm

The experimental protocol consisted of two consecutive phases
[
14
]
. The first was the data acquisition phase, followed by the real-time closed-loop feedback phase. Rest intervals were systematically incorporated to prevent participant fatigue and ensure sustained cognitive engagement. II-A
1
Data Acquisition Phase

During this phase (Fig.
1
a), the participant received simultaneous multimodal cues consisting of textual and auditory stimuli presented for a 2-second duration. Upon cue offset, a blank visual display appeared, during which the participant engaged in imagined speech production corresponding to the preceding cue for 2 seconds. This stimulus-imagery cycle was repeated consecutively. The trial structure including relaxation, cue presentation, and imagined speech is sequentially depicted in Fig.
1
a, emphasizing the fixed timing windows and alternating cognitive states. Following completion of 20 successive trials, an extended rest interval was provided prior to resumption of subsequent trial blocks; this rest protocol is also denoted in the figure
[
15

.

II-A
2
Real-Time Online Feedback Phase

This phase comprised a sequence of events visually summarized in Fig.
1
b. At the start of each trial, synchronized text and audio cues were presented to the participant for 2 seconds, immediately followed by a 2-second blank screen during which the participant performed imagined speech production. Subsequently, a fixation cross appeared for approximately 2 seconds, marking the interval in which the participant-specific decoder model executed real-time neural signal classification. Upon completion of decoding, multimodal feedback was delivered for 3 seconds
[
16
]
. This feedback comprised both visual and auditory presentations that indicated the predicted outcome. In the figure, the gradation of visual contrast and audio intensity represents dynamic scaling based on the decoder’s confidence score, illustrating how feedback salience was modulated to reflect prediction certainty. As indicated in the schematic, extended rest intervals are punctuated every 20 trials, mirroring the protocol used in the offline data acquisition phase
[
17
]
.

II-A
3
Baseline Recording

EEG during resting-state was recorded for 3-minute intervals preceding and following each experimental phase to capture baseline neural dynamics and assess potential shifts in spontaneous brain activity patterns
[
18
]
. These recordings provided reference measures for subsequent analysis, enabling evaluation of potential modulations induced by task engagement. Baseline data also served as internal controls for normalizing task-related spectral and temporal EEG features across participants.

II-B

Participant

A 54-year-old female with chronic anomic aphasia and mixed dysarthria following a left hemisphere stroke participated in this study. Her severe expressive language impairment made her an appropriate candidate for assessing brain–computer interface technology. She was the sole participant analyzed. The study was approved by the Korea University Institutional Review Board [KUIRB-2023-0429-01] and followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki.

II-C

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

EEG was acquired from 64 scalp electrode sites positioned according to the international 10-20 system using an actiCAP slim electrode cap, sampled at a rate of 500 Hz via the BrainVision Lab Streaming Layer recording interface. Raw EEG data were preprocessed using the Python-based MNE signal processing library
[
19
,
20
]
. A fifth-order Butterworth infinite impulse response bandpass filter with pass-band frequencies between 0 and 120 Hz was implemented to attenuate non-physiological frequency components and high-frequency noise artifacts
[
21
]
. A complementary notch filter centered at 60 Hz was applied to suppress power line contamination arising from alternating current electrical interference. Baseline normalization was performed by subtracting the mean voltage during the pre-stimulus interval (spanning -0.2 to 0 seconds relative to imagined speech onset) from the entire epoch. As a final preprocessing step, signals were re-referenced using the common average reference method. This process subtracted the global average voltage from each channel to reduce noise and enhance signal quality
[
22
]
.

Model Architecture

We implemented a lightweight diffusion-based framework optimized for rapid training and low-latency inference under limited session data. As illustrated in Fig.

. Timestep and class conditioning were applied through sinusoidal positional embeddings and linear projection layers. To improve generalization, dropout (
p
= 0.3) was inserted after each residual block and before the final classification layer (128
\rightarrow
4, no bias). Additional regularization included weight decay (
1\times 10^{-4}
), and label smoothing (0.05)
[
25
,
26
]
.

Fig.
2
further details the processing pipeline for both training and real-time inference. During training (left), the diffusion encoder and convolutional autoencoder are jointly optimized with dropout regularization. In real-time operation (right), the trained encoder processes the incoming EEG stream in inference mode, enabling fast and privacy-preserving classification. The modular design and regularization strategies facilitate robust performance under varied clinical constraints and ensure that the same decoding pipeline can be flexibly applied in both research and practical deployment scenarios.

II-D
2
Training Protocol

Training utilized the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-3}
. Dual-criterion early stopping was applied: training ceased when either the training accuracy exceeded 75 % or the validation accuracy surpassed 40 %
[
1
,
27
]
. This approach facilitated rapid convergence within clinical time constraints while minimizing overfitting risk. Additionally, per-class confusion matrices were generated to identify systematic misclassification patterns.

II-D
3
Real-Time Decoding

During online sessions, continuous EEG streams were processed in 2-second windows with 0.2-second baseline correction, matching the offline preprocessing pipeline
[
28
]
. The frozen model generated class probability distributions, which were converted to multimodal feedback. Classification confidence modulated both visual clarity and auditory intensity, providing graded feedback of prediction certainty
[
19
]
. All computations were executed locally to ensure data privacy.

Performance Comparison Between Offline and Real-Time Evaluation

Tab.

The online evaluation achieved a top-1 accuracy of 65 % and a top-2 accuracy of 70 %. In comparison, the offline model reached 50 % and 70 % for top-1 and top-2 accuracies, respectively. Notably, the real-time decoding yielded higher top-1 accuracy despite operating under stricter temporal constraints. The consistent top-2 accuracy in both modes indicates that the classifier maintains stable decision boundaries between training and operation. The 15-percentage-point gain in top-1 accuracy for real-time use may reflect increased participant attention and engagement during interactive feedback. From an implementation perspective, decoding was performed locally with a latency of less than 300 ms. Overall, these results support the practical viability of diffusion-based decoding for clinical BCI applications, balancing computational efficiency and decoding reliability.

III-B

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. The pilot cohort (
N=4
) restricts generalizability and reduces statistical power. Future research should include more participants to better assess the effects of lesion characteristics and aphasia profiles on decoding outcomes. Introducing transfer learning methods may help reduce the burden of model calibration. Adaptive fine-tuning strategies are also needed to sustain decoding accuracy over time. This initial proof-of-concept tested only a four-word vocabulary; subsequent studies should expand the vocabulary to better support daily communication needs.

IV
CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that diffusion-based neural decoding frameworks can be adapted for real-time use in clinical aphasia populations through systematic architectural optimization and task design. The proposed lightweight diffusion encoder showed stable, robust decoding performance despite limited computational resources and data. The two-phase experimental paradigm incorporated native-language stimuli, controlled randomization, and confidence-weighted feedback. Classifier performance remained resilient to contextual factors such as caregiver presence and background noise. These findings mark significant progress in translating imagined speech brain–computer interface technology from controlled research to practical clinical communication aids for individuals with severe expressive language impairments.
