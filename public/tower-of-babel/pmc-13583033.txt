Not All Children Are the Same: Differences in the Microbiome Assembly During Early Development of Seaweeds
Authors: Syukur Syukur, Jadranka Nappi, Marwan E. Majzoub, Torsten Thomas, Suhelen Egan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrobial symbionts play key roles in macroalgal development, yet the processes structuring early‐life microbiomes remain poorly understood. Using laboratory outgrowth experiments and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing we compared microbiome acquisition and assembly during the early development of three distinct macroalgae: Ulva australis (Chlorophyta), Hormosira banksii (Phaeophyceae) and Delisea pulchra (Rhodophyta). All species established distinct bacterial communities within the first week of outgrowth, with significant shifts in community composition and structure associated with major developmental stages. Stage‐enriched taxa included Phaeobacter, Roseobacter and Maribacter, which include members reported to influence algal growth or morphogenesis. Vertical inheritance contributed unevenly to the microbiome assembly across hosts. U. australis recruited low‐abundance environmental bacteria, possibly via strong host filtering. H. banksii selectively retained a small, consistent subset of adult‐derived bacteria, whereas D. pulchra retained fewer of its inherited bacteria. These results suggest that macroalgae can employ diverse transmission and recruitment strategies to assemble early microbiomes, combining selective inheritance with stage‐specific retention and/or environmental acquisition.

Introduction

Microbial symbionts play an important role in the life of their hosts, aiding their nutrition, influencing their behaviour and development as well as providing environmental resilience and disease protection (Rao et al. 2007; Rosenberg and Zilber‐Rosenberg 2011; McFall‐Ngai et al. 2013; Egan et al. 2013; Singh and Reddy 2014; Yokoya et al. 2014; Li et al. 2022). The temporal control of host‐microbiome relationships and the assembly of microbial communities during early developmental stages of an organism are likely important for host growth and performance. Indeed, extensive work, primarily in animal gut and rhizosphere systems, has demonstrated a strong influence of early colonising microorganisms on host development, with implications for long‐term function and health (Mendes et al. 2013; Stephens et al. 2016; Emmett et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2020; Sieler et al. 2023).

Direct transmission of symbionts from parent to offspring (vertical transmission) has long been assumed to be an important mechanism to ensure stable, functioning host‐microbiome relationships in early life stages and across generations. Yet studies suggest selective recruitment from the environment (horizontal transmission) or a mixed mode may be equally important by providing some degree of flexibility and adaptation potential through the acquisition of locally adapted microbial symbionts (Bright and Bulgheresi 2010; Foster et al. 2017; Bruijning et al. 2022). However, few studies have investigated how microbial communities develop during the very early life history stages in aquatic hosts, where early developmental stages are constantly exposed to a diverse range of environmental microorganisms (Damjanovic et al. 2020a; Han et al. 2021; Unzueta‐Martínez et al. 2022; Cai et al. 2023; Davis et al. 2023; Icaza et al. 2025).

Early microbiome development has been studied in the gastrointestinal tract of several species of farmed fish and crustaceans, with strong microbial signatures associated with feed or surrounding water at distinct developmental stages (Lokesh et al. 2019; Razak et al. 2019; Walburn et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020). In the broadcast spawning coral Acropora sp., the egg‐bundle stage was found to harbour bacterial communities that resemble the parent microbiome, while subsequent larval stages increase their bacterial diversity through local environmental recruitment (Zhou et al. 2017; Damjanovic et al. 2020a; Bernasconi et al. 2019). Even in systems with strong vertical inheritance, transmitted bacteria are not necessarily dominant across all ontogenetic stages. For example, in the sponge Amphimedon queenslandica, a rapid influx of environment‐derived bacteria during the motile larval stage was observed, and it was not until after settlement that the vertically inherited symbiont regained dominance (Fieth et al. 2016). Therefore, microbiome development in early life stages of aquatic organisms appears to be dynamic, with host organisms using a mixture of strategies to recruit microbial symbionts. Yet, how well these patterns can be applied to other marine hosts, including seaweeds, remains to be determined.

Seaweeds (macroalgae) play a key role as habitat formers and ecosystem engineers in temperate rocky shores and often rely on bacterial symbionts for development, growth and environmental resilience (Egan et al. 2013; Saha et al. 2024). In the green seaweed Ulva, bacterially derived algal growth and morphogenic factors (AGMFs) are required for normal development (Wichard 2023). For example, in axenic cultures of Ulva compressa , AGMFs production has been attributed to two key bacterial species, Roseovarius sp. MS2 and Maribacter sp. MS6, which provide cell division and cell differentiation factors, respectively (Spoerner et al. 2012). In addition, specific bacterial metabolites have been shown to induce spore release in red seaweeds (Weinberger et al. 2007; Singh, Bijo, et al. 2011; Singh et al. 2015) and influence spore settlement (Joint et al. 2007). Other seaweeds are known to benefit from bacterial symbionts that protect them from grazing herbivores (Zan et al. 2019) or the action of opportunistic pathogens (Li et al. 2022).

While there is an increasing appreciation of the diversity and function of bacterial symbionts in adult thalli (Egan et al. 2013; Hollants et al. 2012; Singh and Reddy 2014; Van der Loos et al. 2019), including across different macroscopic life history stages (Han et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2023; Davis et al. 2023; Icaza et al. 2025), we know little about the early microbiome development in seaweeds. Syukur et al. (2024) reported that seaweed species vary in their microbiome transfer to gametes, ranging from none (e.g., Ulva australis) to non‐selective (e.g., Delisea pulchra) to strongly‐selective (e.g., Hormosira banksii). Recently, Icaza et al. (2025) found that the microbiome of Macrocystis pyrifera at 72 h post settlement was significantly different from that of the newly released spores. However, the extent to which vertically transmitted symbionts persist, and the relative contribution of horizontally acquired bacteria to microbial community assembly during early development and seaweed outgrowth more broadly remains unknown.

Here, we address this knowledge gap by comparatively assessing the microbial communities associated with the early development of U. australis , D. pulchra and H. banksii. These species represent the three major seaweed groups; green (Chlorophyta), red (Rhodophyta) and brown (Phaeophyceae), respectively, and exhibit contrasting modes of symbiont transmission. We hypothesised that microbial communities would change over time according to host developmental stage, and that selectively transmitted bacterial symbionts (e.g., those associated with H. banksii) are likely important for the seaweed host and would therefore persist throughout early development. We found that seaweeds host distinct bacterial communities during very early developmental stages, with shifts in morphological development corresponding to changes in bacterial communities. We further found that the contribution of vertically transmitted bacteria to the microbial communities of developing thalli varies among species, highlighting the diversity of strategies used to assemble their early‐life microbiomes.

Experimental Procedures

Seaweed Cultivation and Sampling

The intertidal seaweeds U. australis and H. banksii were collected at low tide, and the subtidal D. pulchra by snorkelling at a depth of 5 m at Bare Island, New South Wales, Australia (33.9921° S, 151.2312° E). Fertile thalli were selected and gametes were isolated as described previously (Syukur et al. 2024). Five biological replicates per seaweed species were isolated and outgrown separately. Coverslips with D. pulchra tetraspores were transferred to 6‐well plates containing 5 mL of 11 μm filtered natural seawater (NSW). U. australis spores were counted under a Leica DMLB microscope, and approximately 400 spores were added to a 50 mL tissue culture flask containing 10 mL of NSW. The culture flasks were then placed in the dark overnight to allow for the spores to settle. For H. banksii, sperm and egg solutions were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The fertilised eggs were counted, and approximately 200 were added to 5 mL of NSW contained in each well of a 6‐well plate.

The spores and zygotes were then incubated in a HettCube 400R system (Hettich, Tuttlingen, Germany) at 20°C under cycled light (16 h dark and 8 h light; 2.916 μmol m−2 s−1) with NSW exchanges performed every 7 days. The morphological changes of seaweeds were regularly observed under a microscope. This specifically involved tracking development from initial attached spores or polarised zygotes into complex structures like filamentous tube (U. australis), anchored rhizoidal networks (H. banksii), or upright filaments emerging from basal crust ( D. pulchra ) (Figure 1 and Table S1). When these morphological changes were encountered representative images were captured. Seaweeds were then harvested and gently washed with autoclaved and filter‐sterilised natural seawater (FSW) three times to remove loosely associated microorganisms. After this, seaweeds were detached from the culture plates using a sterile loop or pipetted to be transferred into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube for storage at −80°C. Microbial communities in NSW used for U. australis cultivation were sampled by filtering onto a 0.2 μm filter (seawater I/II corresponding to stages I and II, and seawater III/IV for stage III and IV; see below).

Overview of developmental stages of U. australis (top): Stage I = attached spore with a primary rhizoid cell and a maximum of 2–3 blade cells (day 7); Stage II = spores with multiple divisions and forming a filamentous tube (day 52); Stage III and IV = elongation of filaments (day 84 and 114, respectively). H. banksii (middle): Stage I = polarised zygote with the formation of a rhizoidal tip (Day 1); Stage II = zygote with a thin single rhizoidal prolongation (Day 7); Stage III = zygote with attached multiple rhizoids (Day 16); Stage IV = young standing H. banksii supported with strong rhizoid attachment and filaments (Day 52); Stage V = young H. banksii with more rhizoids and an enlargement of the body and close to initiation of a first vesicle formation (Day 84). D. pulchra (bottom): Stage I = attached tetraspores after subsequent multiple small divisions (day 7); Stage II = crust with original tetraspore wall still visible near the centre (day 52); Stage III = sporeling with initial upright filament development from the crust (day 84).

DNA Extraction, 16S rRNA Gene Amplicon Sequencing and Data Analysis

Total DNA was extracted from growing seaweeds and seawater filters with the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen, Chadstone, VIC, Australia) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Blank extractions were also performed as negative controls. The V3‐V4 hypervariable regions of 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the NOCHL forward (tcgtcggcagcgtcagatgtgtataagagacagCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and reverse (gtctcgtgggtccggagatgtgtataagagacagCMGGGTATCTAATCCKGTT) primers containing Illumina overhang adaptor sequences (Thomas et al. 2020). The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) mixture of 50 μL contained 25 μL EconoTaqPLUS GREEN 2× Master Mix (Astral Scientific, Gymea, NSW, Australia), 15 μL of nuclease‐free water, 2.5 μL of each primer at a concentration of 10 μM and 5 μL of DNA template. The amplification was performed with an initial denaturation at 94°C (2 min), 35 cycles of denaturation at 94°C (30 s), annealing at 50°C (30 s), extension at 72°C (40 s), with a final elongation step at 72°C (7 min). Specific amplicons for samples and the absence of amplicons in negative controls were verified using agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples were then barcoded and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform with 2 × 300 base pair (bp) chemistry according to the MiSeq System User Guide (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the Ramaciotti Centre for Genomics, UNSW.

The raw sequences generated here for the growing seaweeds ( D. pulchra , H. banksii and U. australis ), seawater samples (referred to as dataset S26) and previously for adults and their gametes ( D. pulchra and H. banksii; referred to as dataset S24, Syukur et al. 2024) were processed as described previously by Wemheuer and Wemheuer (2017). Briefly, sequence reads were quality filtered using TRIMMOMATIC version 0.38, discarding reads with a quality score below 15 in sliding window of 4 bp, and reads shorter than 100 bp (Bolger et al. 2014). The paired‐end reads were then merged and quality filtered using USEARCH version 11.0.667 (Edgar 2010). The UNOISE3 algorithm in USEARCH was used to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Chimeric sequences were removed de novo and by a reference‐based comparison against the SILVA v138 database using UCHIME (Edgar et al. 2011). All non‐bacterial ASV sequences and singleton ASVs were removed. The remaining ASVs were taxonomically classified using BLCA (Gao et al. 2017) against a filtered version of the Genome Taxonomy Database r226 database (GTDB) (Parks et al. 2022), in which all sequences shorter than 1000 bp were removed.

All analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.2) using RStudio. For statistical analyses, only ASVs that occurred in more than two samples were retained. Following quality filtering, 74 samples were retained for analysis. D. pulchra was represented by 6 adult and tetraspore samples and 5 samples at each developmental stage, H. banksii by 3–5 samples per stage, and U. australis by 5 samples per stage. Rarefaction curves were generated to assess sequencing depth, with samples reaching saturation (vegan package in R). For alpha diversity analyses, ASV counts were standardised by random subsampling (rarefaction) within each analysis subset to account for differences in sequencing depth. Samples of U. australis and associated seawater were rarefied together to 34,210 reads per sample, while D. pulchra and H. banksii samples were rarefied separately to 39,347 and 40,294 reads per sample, respectively.

Observed richness and Shannon diversity were calculated from the rarefied ASV table using the vegan package in R. Differences in alpha diversity were assessed within each species separately across developmental stages using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc tests implemented in R (stats package). Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05. Significant differences among stages were visualised using compact letter displays.

Differences in community composition (presence/absence) and structure (abundance) were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, 999 permutations) implemented in the R package vegan. These analyses were performed on non‐rarefied ASV count data using both datasets (S24 and S26). Community differences between seaweeds were first tested using ‘seaweed’ as the only factor. Stage effects were then assessed separately within each seaweed species using ‘stage’ as the explanatory factor. Homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was evaluated using PERMDISP prior to interpreting PERMANOVA results. Because PERMANOVA can be sensitive to differences in multivariate dispersion among groups, we also conducted global model‐based tests using the mvabund package, which are less affected by heterogeneity of dispersion and provide a complementary assessment of treatment effects on assemblage composition. When significant effects were detected, pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons (Martinez Arbizu 2020) were performed with Benjamini‐Hochberg correction for multiple testing.

Community patterns were visualised using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Sørensen dissimilarity for community composition (presence/absence–based Bray–Curtis) and Aitchison distance for community structure (Euclidean distance on centred log‐ratio–transformed count data with a pseudocount of 1). The dataset was split by seaweed species, and a one‐factor design was used to fit a multivariate generalised linear model (MGLM) in the mvabund package (Wang et al. 2012) to identify bacterial ASVs that are significantly influenced by developmental stage. A separate generalised linear model (GLM) was used to treat each ASV as a variable, fitted with a negative binomial distribution and the setting ‘composition = true’ to account for the compositional nature of the data. Statistical significance was assessed using a p‐value threshold of < 0.05, adjusted with the Benjamini‐Hochberg correction.

Results

Host‐Specific Microbiomes Are Established During Early Developmental Stages

In this study, U. australis , D. pulchra , and H. banksii were cultivated from gametes and harvested at various developmental stages defined by specific morphological changes (Figure 1). Analysis of the bacterial communities associated with these early growth stages of each seaweed resulted in 8387 unique 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) from a total of 4,566,330 sequencing reads. Rarefaction curves plateaued across seaweed samples, indicating adequate sequencing depth (Figure S1).

Ordination of beta diversity revealed separation of bacterial community composition (Sørensen dissimilarity) and structure (Aitchison distance) by seaweed species (PERMANOVA: structure F seaweed = 8.790, R 2 = 0.346, p = 0.001, composition F seaweed = 11.023, R 2 = 0.399, p = 0.001; Figures S2A and S2B), with significant impact also on dispersion (PERMDISP: structure F seaweed = 32.644, p = 0.001, composition F seaweed = 5.536, p = 0.001). Significant differences in bacterial community composition and structure were also found in the pairwise comparisons (p < 0.001, Table S2), showing that each seaweed species harbours distinct microbiomes in early developmental stages.

Microbiome Changes During Early Stages of Development

We observed stage‐specific variation in microbial diversity across all three seaweeds (Figure 2 and Tables S6–S8). For U. australis , ASV richness did not differ significantly among developmental stages (Figure 2A and Table S7). In contrast, Shannon diversity varied significantly, with Stage I exhibiting lower diversity than all other stages (Tukey's HSD, p < 0.05; Figure 2A and Table S8). For H. banksii, ASV richness varied across development (Figure 2C and Table S7), with egg‐associated communities showing significantly lower richness than all subsequent developmental stages (Tukey's HSD, p < 0.05, Table S8), while later stages were more similar to each other. Shannon diversity showed a comparable pattern (Figure 2C), with adult‐ and egg‐associated communities exhibiting significantly lower diversity than other developing stages (Tukey's HSD, p < 0.05, Table S8). For D. pulchra , ASV richness differed among stages (Figure 2E and Table S7), with Stage I showing significantly lower richness than tetraspores (Tukey's HSD, p = 0.0229, Table S8). Similarly, Shannon diversity varied across stages (Figure 2E and Table S8), with Stage III exhibiting higher diversity than both adults (p = 0.0157) and tetraspores (p = 0.0291).

Bacterial community composition (Sørensen dissimilarity) and structure (Aitchison distance) associated with U. australis were also distinct across different developmental stages (PERMANOVA: structure F _stage = 3.343, R 2 = 0.385, p = 0.001; composition F _stage = 4.464, R 2 = 0.455, p = 0.001). Significant differences were also detected in all pairwise comparisons (Table S3), including comparisons with seawater samples (PERMANOVA pairwise: structure F _Ulva–Seawater = 8.022, R 2 = 0.222, p = 0.001; composition F _Ulva–Seawater = 7.915, R 2 = 0.220, p = 0.001; Figures 2B and S3A, Table S3). The bacterial community structure and composition across the development stages of D. pulchra were also distinct (PERMANOVA: structure F stage = 2.377, R 2 = 0.283, p = 0.001; composition F stage = 2.678, R 2 = 0.308, p = 0.001) and pairwise comparisons showed that all stages differed, except for the adults and tetraspores, and stages II and III for the bacterial community composition (Figures 2F and S3C, Table S5). Global mvabund analyses similarly identified significant differences in bacterial communities among seaweed species at each developmental stage (Tables S2–S5).

Enrichment of Specific Taxa During Early Developmental Stages in Individual Seaweeds

As distinct taxonomic profiles for the bacterial communities were observed for different stages of seaweed development (Figures S4–S6), we used MGLM analysis to evaluate which ASVs varied across stages. For U. australis , 18.8% of ASVs (765/4075 ASVs, LRT = 104266.9; p‐value = 0.001) significantly differed in relative read abundances across the development stages (Figure 3). Some ASVs were more abundant during early development (stage I), where the spores attached with a primary rhizoid cell and underwent the first few blade cell divisions. These ASVs included those assigned to the genera Pikelenuella (ASV69), Colwellia (ASV191), and Alteromonas (ASV1). Other ASVs showed higher relative abundance in later stages (stage II–IV) corresponding to multiple cell division and elongation of filamentous tube, and included, for example, those assigned to the taxa JADHLJ01 (ASV169), UBA4479 (ASV23), HXMU1428‐3 (ASV24), Phaeobacter_A (ASV11), and DT‐111 (ASV28), which together comprised 14.87% of the relative read abundance (Figure 3). These ASVs were also found in seawater but were generally in low relative read abundances or remained undetected. In addition, several ASVs abundant in seawater were absent or rare on U. australis including, for example, HIMB11 (ASV62) and Psychrilyobacter (ASV143) in seawater I/II, and Zhongshania (ASV36 and ASV102), Asprobacillus (ASV61 and ASV75), Oceanococcus (ASV50), and Amylibacter (ASV58) in seawater III/IV, illustrating a strong positive and negative selection for certain phylotypes by the algae.

For H. banksii, 31.2% (1643/5258 ASVs, LRT = 258378.6; p‐value = 0.034) of ASVs displayed significantly different abundances across the developmental stages (Figure 4). Twenty‐four hours post fertilisation (stage I), ASVs such as Pseudoalteromonas (ASV3), Marinomonas (ASV57 and ASV158) and Pelagimonas (ASV163) were enriched, and they were also observed in later stages (stage II and III), but in lower relative read abundances. Colwellia (ASV83, ASV191 and ASV54), Roseobacter (ASV3919), Paraglaciecola (ASV128), Maribacter (ASV40) and Tenacibaculum (ASV84) were enriched in stages II and III. At later stages (stage IV and V), the ASVs belonging to Alteromonas (ASV15) and Henriciella (ASV6 and ASV63) were among those that became enriched. Some other members continued to have higher abundances from stage II to V, including Marinobacter (ASV2), Roseobacter (ASV80), Qingshengfaniella (ASV44) and Maribacter_C (ASV133). The ASVs enriched during development were generally not detected or only sporadically and in low abundances within the egg‐associated microbiome, thus they are likely acquired from the surrounding seawater.

For D. pulchra , 22% (852/3870 ASVs, LRT = 54732.9; p‐value = 0.001) of the associated ASVs showed significant differences in relative read abundances across various developmental stages. ASVs, including Pseudoalteromonas (ASV3), Aquimarina (ASV280), Ruegeria (ASV16, ASV95 and ASV194), Vibrio (ASV4, ASV7, ASV8 and ASV37), Cobetia (ASV29 and ASV198), Alteromonas (ASV1), Paraglaciecola (ASV12) and Amphritea (ASV168) were enriched as tetraspores underwent initial cell division (stage I), and these were also significantly present in the microbiomes of adults and tetraspores prior to settlement. However, their relative read abundance significantly decreased in later stages (stage II–III) of development (Figure 5). When tetraspores became a crust (stage II), different ASVs were enriched, including Paraglaciecola (ASV13), Phaeobacter_A (ASV11), Minwuia (ASV18), Henriciella (ASV46 and ASV6) and Pikeienuella (ASV69). Interestingly, these ASVs remained enriched when upright filaments appeared in the newly developed sporelings (stage III).

Contribution of Vertically Transmitted Bacteria to Communities During Development

To determine the contribution of vertically transmitted bacteria to the microbiome of the developing seaweed, we assessed the number of shared ASVs between adults, gametes and the different development stages. UpSet plots show that only 259 out of 4517 ASVs (~6%) found on H. banksii (Figure 6A) and 318 out of 3576 ASVs (~9%) for D. pulchra (Figure 6B) are common between the adults, gametes and one or more algal developmental stage. In D. pulchra less than half (~32%) of the 985 ASVs shared between adults and tetraspores are retained in at least one later stage and few ASVs (~7%) are shared across all observed developmental stages (Figure 6B). In contrast, H. banksii adults transfer a smaller number of ASVs to their gametes (i.e., 303 ASVs), yet a higher proportion (~85%) of those ASVs are retained in later developmental stages, with more of these ASVs (~37%) shared across all developmental stages (Figure 6A).

Discussion

The importance of microbial communities to host health and development is now well established (Rosenberg and Zilber‐Rosenberg 2011; Egan et al. 2013; McFall‐Ngai et al. 2013). For seaweeds, studies have shown distinct bacterial communities correlated with morphological differences and/or changing environmental conditions over time (Lemay et al. 2018; Han et al. 2021; Cai et al. 2023; Davis et al. 2023; Osborne et al. 2023, 2024; Icaza et al. 2025). However, except for a recent study in giant kelp (Icaza et al. 2025), the extent to which these patterns are also seen during early (microscopic) developmental stages in different seaweed species and the influence of parental symbionts on microbiome establishment remains unknown.

Bacteria‐Seaweed Interactions During Early Development

Here we observed a correlation between the early stages of seaweed development and changes in the associated microbial communities. For example, in U. australis the transition of attached spores with few cell divisions of the blade (stage I) to a filamentous tube (stage II) was marked by an increase in ASV richness and changes in the bacterial community composition (presence/absence measured via Sørensen dissimilarity) and structure (abundance measured via Aitchison distances). Specifically, these changes were characterised by an enrichment of bacteria belonging to the genera Minwuia, Henriciella, and Phaeobacter_A, as well as the arrival of new colonisers, including members of the Thalassobaculum and Maricaulis. Similar microbiome transitions with host development were observed for H. banksii and D. pulchra , with a distinct shift in the microbiome correlating with the appearance of early rhizoid structures and/or upright filaments.

Morphological changes during early development may drive these microbial shifts through the creation of new niches that favour specific colonisers. Icaza et al. (2025) also proposed host factors were responsible for the distinct microbiome patterns associated with early settlement and outgrowth of Macrocystis pyrifera spores. Similarly, bacterial communities of adult Laminaria digitata are distinct between specific thallus regions, such as holdfasts and blades supporting the notion that host morphology can shape bacterial community structure (Ihua et al. 2020). In addition, morphologies, such as branched, thin blades and crusts, have been shown to influence the composition and structure of their microbial communities on artificial seaweeds (e.g., latex sheeting) and field specimens (Lemay et al. 2021).

The chemical composition of seaweeds at different developmental stages could also influence their microbiome composition. While the chemical composition of the early developmental stages of the species studied here is unknown, different anatomical regions of older and fully grown seaweeds have been shown to exhibit distinct chemical properties (Starko et al. 2018). For example, furanone concentrations in D. pulchra are highest at the growing tips (Dworjanyn et al. 1999) and inversely correlate with bacterial densities (Maximilian et al. 1998). Similarly, phlorotannin concentration can vary considerably across the thallus of different kelp species, correlating with age, tissue type and in some cases light exposure (Van Alstyne et al. 1999). Also, juvenile stages of the brown Fucus spiralis exclusively produce stearic acid and a variety of fatty acids, while the mature stages are more alkaline and have a higher sterol content (Seca et al. 2018). Importantly, furanones, phlorotannins and fatty acids can display antibacterial activities that could act as selective agents for microbial succession (Maximilian et al. 1998; Longford et al. 2019; Casillas‐Vargas et al. 2021). Seaweeds may also recruit beneficial bacteria through the release of bacterial chemoattractants. For example, the osmolyte dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP), released by Ulva, attracts Roseovarius spp. (Kessler et al. 2018), which subsequently produce morphogenic compounds that stimulate host cell division (Spoerner et al. 2012). Similarly, chemically mediated “gardening” of bacterial communities has also been proposed as a mechanism to attract beneficial symbionts while deterring detrimental ones (Saha and Weinberger 2019). Thus, the stage‐dependent pattern of bacterial communities found in this study might have been driven by a combination of both morphological and chemical changes that occur in the seaweed host during early development.

There is now substantial experimental evidence demonstrating the importance of bacterial derived AGMPFs for normal development of Ulva (Spoerner et al. 2012; Weiss et al. 2017; Alsufyani et al. 2020; Ulrich et al. 2022). Therefore, it is possible that certain bacteria or bacterial consortia are also responsible for triggering the morphological changes seen here. For example, in H. banksii, members of the genus Maribacter were enriched from rhizoidal prolongation (stage II) until body enlargement (stage V). Some strains of Maribacter can produce a phytohormone‐like factor called thallusin that induces cell wall and rhizoid formation (Ulrich et al. 2022). Similarly, the genus Marinomonas, which was enriched at the initial rhizoid (stage I and II) of H. banksii, consists of members that can induce normal morphogenesis of U. fasciata (Singh, Mantri, et al. 2011).

Interestingly, strains of Hyphomonas have been shown to attach to the rhizoid of Pyropia yezoensis, promoting seaweed growth and development (Fukui et al. 2014, 2023). The enrichment of related Hyphomonadaceae including Hyphomonas (e.g., ASV297) and Henriciella (e.g., ASV6, ASV20, ASV63, ASV46) during the later stages of development across all three seaweeds suggests that these stalked bacteria, predominantly studied for their role in aquatic biofilms (Abraham 2020), may also function as important seaweed symbionts. Phaeobacter is another notable genus, with members having potential to mitigate disease (Li et al. 2022) and enhance growth (Li et al. 2025; Ulrich et al. 2026) of specific seaweed hosts. Members of Phaeobacter have also been reported to promote the growth of the microalga Emiliania huxleyi via the production of the phytohormone indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) (Segev et al. 2016). IAA can also stimulate morphogenesis and growth in seaweeds, including inducing intercalary segments in Chondracanthus chamissoi (Yokoya et al. 2014), the upright growth in Gelidium floridanum (Batista et al. 2021), and apical‐basal patterning in Dictyota dichotoma , particularly in rhizoid pole formation (Bogaert et al. 2019). Here, Phaeobacter was enriched during filament elongation (stage II to IV) in U. australis and during crust formation and upright filament development (stage II and III) in D. pulchra , and thus future work could focus on determining the potential role of Phaeobacter‐derived IAA in these developmental changes.

Developmental stage is inherently linked to time in culture; therefore, the observed patterns likely reflect a combination of both morphological development and temporal microbial succession. It is also possible that selective retention of specific strains reflects their differential survival in culture with limited impact on host development. Thus, studies with finer temporal resolution will be required to disentangle the relative contributions of developmental progression and time in culture to microbial succession.

While our results suggest that host specificity of seaweed microbiota can emerge early in development and vary across developmental stage, phylogenetic differences do not necessarily reflect functional differences. In some seaweeds, bacterial community assembly appears to be driven by functional traits rather than taxonomic identity (Burke, Steinberg, et al. 2011; Ghaderiardakani et al. 2017). Moreover, the same bacterial taxa may perform different functions depending on the host and environmental context (e.g., Hmani et al. 2024; Seyedsayamdost et al. 2011). Future studies should combine functional genomic analyses with manipulative experiments to determine how specific microbial taxa influence host development under different environmental conditions.

The Importance of Vertically and Horizontally Transmitted Bacteria on Community Assembly

Given the potential for these early community members to have an impact on seaweed development and growth, direct (vertical) transmission of bacterial symbionts from parents could be important, yet modes of symbiont transmission in seaweeds have rarely been investigated. Davis et al. (2023) speculated that the strong host‐specificity of microbiomes for juveniles of the kelp S. latissima and A. marginata may be a result of vertical inheritance of their microbiome. However, strong host‐specificity in juveniles and adults can also occur without vertical transmission of symbionts, as seen in Ulva sp. (Burke, Thomas, et al. 2011; Syukur et al. 2024).

Instead, we found that the degree to which symbionts are selected during their transfer from the adults to gametes appears to correlate with how well they are retained during early developmental stages. Specifically, D. pulchra exhibits weak selection for vertically transmitted symbionts, with most ASVs present on adults also detected on gametes (Syukur et al. 2024 and Figure 5). Of those only a small fraction was shown here to be retained during the later stages of development (Figures 5 and 6B), suggesting that they are less important for seaweed outgrowth. In contrast, H. banksii strongly selects a subset of adult bacteria to be transferred to the gametes (Syukur et al. 2024), the majority of which were retained in at least one of the subsequent stages (Figures 4 and 6A). This suggests that some seaweed species have mechanisms that allow them to vertically transmit specifically those symbionts that could be important for early development.

While vertical transmission of individual bacteria is occurring and may be important during early development, it is likely that mature seaweed microbiomes are also influenced by early host selection of specific environment‐derived bacteria. Indeed, U. australis appears to have an early and strong selection for bacterial colonisation as it recruited bacteria with low relative read abundances in seawater, suggesting that bacterial abundance in seawater alone does not drive their recruitment. Similar trends occur in other seaweeds including U. rigida (Roth‐Schulze et al. 2018) and Nereocystis luetkeana (Chen and Parfrey 2018), but contrasts with recent findings for S. japonica , where seawater microbiomes appear to determine the composition of host bacterial communities (Cai et al. 2023). It is therefore likely that the relative influence of host filtering during microbiome establishment differs between seaweed groups or taxa.

Importantly, not all ASVs associated with later stages of H. banksii or D. pulchra development were detected in the corresponding adult or gamete samples (Figures 4 and 5), suggesting that these seaweeds acquire at least part of their microbiome from the surrounding seawater. Although direct characterisation of the seawater microbiome will be required to confirm the origin of these ASVs, the presence of both vertically transmitted and environmentally acquired symbionts is suggestive of a mixed mode of transmission.

Such a mixed strategy has been described in other marine organisms, including corals and oysters. For example, adults of the coral Pocillopora acuta transfer a subset of bacterial communities to the offspring (5%–26%) and recruit other bacteria from the surrounding environment (Damjanovic et al. 2020b). Likewise, the majority of microbial symbionts associated with the larvae of the rock oyster Saccostrea glomerata were acquired from the local environment, with only 14 parent‐derived ASVs persisting throughout all life‐cycle stages (Unzueta‐Martínez et al. 2022). This mixed mode can ensure the maintenance of key symbionts, especially those that are critical for very early development processes that cannot ‘wait’ for environmental recruitment, yet still allow for the establishment of microbiomes that are adapted to the local and potentially changing environment (Bruijning et al. 2022). Seaweeds are shown here to use a spectrum of transmission modes with differential retention of vertically transmitted symbionts that may support their specific ecological niches and successes.

Conclusion

Our observation that U. australis , D. pulchra , and H. banksii each construct their early microbiomes through distinct combinations of vertically transmitted symbionts, environmentally derived colonisers, and stage‐specific selection suggests that no single model of early holobiont formation applies across macroalgae. Moreover, by comparing different seaweed groups, this study provides a broader framework for understanding how these hosts establish their bacterial partnerships to support early development and growth. Expanding this comparative approach across additional macroalgal taxa and environmental conditions will further clarify how early microbiome assembly influences performance and environmental resilience of both natural and cultivated seaweeds. While the present work identifies candidate taxa and community patterns associated with early development, manipulative bioassays will be required to establish their causal developmental functions.
