Aptamers in Ocular Disease Therapy and Drug Delivery: Current Progress and Future Opportunities
Authors: Sandra Carolina Durán-Cristiano, Juan David Ospina-Villa, Marlies Gijs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSPurposeAptamers are single-stranded oligonucleotides with the ability to bind specific target molecules with high affinity and selectivity, positioning them as valuable tools for precision therapeutics and targeted drug delivery. In ophthalmology, aptamer-based platforms have emerged as effective solutions for managing retinal diseases, glaucoma, and ocular surface disorders. This review provides a comprehensive overview of aptamers that have received approval or are in experimental, preclinical, or clinical development stages for ocular diseases.MethodsA narrative literature search was conducted using PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science databases, concentrating on aptamers that have reached experimental, preclinical, clinical, or FDA-approved stages. Studies published up to March 2026 were screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, based on their reported efficacy, molecular targets, and relevance to ocular diseases.ResultsTwenty-four records were included, comprising 24 aptamer-based platforms. Across ocular diseases, aptamers were identified in three principal application domains: therapeutic intervention, diagnostic, and targeted drug delivery. In age-related macular degeneration, aptamers development was predominantly therapeutic, with agents targeting vascular endothelial growth factor, platelet-derived growth factor, fibroblast growth factor 2, complement components, nucleolin, CBF1, and CD44. In glaucoma, aptamers were mainly directed toward neuroprotection, antifibrotic modulation, aqueous humor outflow regulation, biomarker detection, and topical drug delivery. In ocular surface diseases, aptamers were primarily developed for antimicrobial and antiviral therapy, antiangiogenic treatment, anti-allergic modulation, corneal surface targeting, and controlled topical drug delivery. Overall, aptamers demonstrated broad translational versatility across both posterior and anterior segment diseases.ConclusionsThe available evidence demonstrates a diverse and rapidly expanding repertoire of aptamers developed for ocular diseases, underscoring their versatile potential as next-generation platforms for therapeutic, biomarker-guided diagnosis, and targeted drug delivery in ophthalmology.Key words: aptamers, biomarker, drug delivery, glaucoma, ocular diseases

Introduction

Aptamers are single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules (ssDNA or ssRNA) that adopt unique three-dimensional (3D) structures, enabling specific recognition of targeted molecules.1 Due to this capability, they are often referred to as “chemical antibodies.” Unlike traditional antibodies, aptamers are synthesized entirely in vitro, exhibiting minimal or no batch-to-batch variability. This enhances their reproducibility, scalability, and ethical sustainability by eliminating the need for biological models or cell lines.2 Consequently, aptamers can bind to a broader range of molecules, including toxic or nonimmunogenic compounds that cannot elicit an antibody response. Another key advantage is the precise control over their binding site, which can be rationally designed or optimized, in contrast to the immune-dependent variability of antibody epitopes.3

The analysis and application of aptamers in comparison to antibodies highlight several critical advantages of these synthetic molecules. Traditional antibody production requires immunization of laboratory animals with a purified immunogenic protein to stimulate B lymphocytes that generate specific antibodies.4 Although antibodies have broad clinical applications in diagnosis and therapy, their production is complex, time-consuming, and costly, involving multiple in vivo procedures.5 Additionally, antibodies are large molecules (∼160 kDa) that are temperature-sensitive and exhibit limited renal filtration capacity.6

In contrast, aptamers offer greater stability, faster generation, and improved pharmacokinetic properties, such as enhanced tissue penetration. Their development relies on the Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment (SELEX) process, first discovered in 1990 by Tuerk and Gold7 as well as Ellington and Szostak.8 SELEX involves iterative rounds of binding, partitioning, and amplification between a large random library of single-stranded oligonucleotides and a selected target. After each cycle, the bound sequences are enriched and amplified using PCR or RT-PCR, progressively isolating those with the highest specificity and affinity. Once the most selective aptamer sequences are identified, they are confirmed by sequencing (Figure 1).

A schematic diagram of SELEX process for the DNA or RNA library.

Over the years, SELEX has evolved alongside emerging technologies, leading to multiple variants that enhance selection speed, precision, and applicability to complex biological systems. Examples include Cell-SELEX (selecting whole cells as targets),9 tissue-SELEX (targeting isolated tissues),10 HTS-SELEX (integrating high-throughput sequencing),11,12 CE-SELEX (using Capillary Electrophoresis to separate aptamer-target complex), and BLI-SELEX (Bio-layer interferometry-based SELEX for real-time biomarker detection in biological samples).13 These advancements have considerably expanded the diversity of molecular targets and the applications of aptamer technology.

Aptamers are versatile molecular recognition tools with broad applications in biomedical and environmental sciences. Since their discovery, it’s have been engineered to bind a wide variety of targets, including small molecules, proteins, pathogens, cells, and even organs. For instance, therapeutic aptamers have been investigated for diseases, including cancer,14 obesity,15 rheumatoid arthritis,16 and COVID-19.17 Notably, some aptamers can traverse the blood-brain barrier, offering promise for neurological diagnostics.

In drug delivery, aptamers present several advantages that enhance their effectiveness in this field. Their high affinity and specificity allow for precise binding to target molecules, including cell surface receptors or disease-associated proteins. Moreover, aptamers can be chemically modified to increase stability in biological environments and conjugated with therapeutic agents,18 facilitating targeted drug release. This strategy enhances therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity, positioning aptamers as promising carriers in precision medicine.

Traditional ocular drug delivery systems, particularly eye drops for anterior and posterior eye diseases, encounter several limitations, including low drug bioavailability, rapid corneal clearance, and the risk of ocular toxicity.19 Integrating aptamers into drug delivery platforms offers a promising strategy to overcome these challenges by improving target specificity and prolonging retention time at the ocular surface.

The growing clinical relevance of aptamers in ophthalmology underscores the necessity for an updated and integrative assessment of the field. Recent advances (2024–2026), including progress in targeted biologics such as complement inhibitors for retinal diseases and the continued significance of aptamer-based therapies like Pegaptanib, reflect a broader shift toward precision medicine in ocular care. In addition, this review expands its scope to encompass a wide range of ocular conditions, including ocular surface diseases, which, despite being comparatively less explored in the literature, represent an important and emerging area for aptamer-based applications. Concurrently, an increasing number of aptamer-based platforms are advancing through clinical evaluation, as reported in ClinicalTrials.gov, highlighting their translational potential. Furthermore, recent regulatory and technological developments remain inadequately addressed.

While several reviews have examined aptamers in ophthalmology, most either concentrate solely on therapeutic applications or emphasize preclinical developments, resulting in limited integration of diagnostics, drug delivery systems, and recent translational advances. To address this gap and provide a clearer historical perspective, the key milestones in aptamer development, from the discovery of SELEX (1990) to the clinical approval of pegaptanib (2004) and the emergence of complement-targeting therapies (2023–2025), are summarized in a structured timeline (Figure 2). Importantly, these reviews often lack a critical appraisal of the factors influencing clinical translation, including issues related to aptamer stability, delivery efficiency, and the inconsistent outcomes observed in late-stage clinical trials. Moreover, existing reviews do not comprehensively incorporate developments from 2024 to 2026, such as emerging clinical trials and regulatory advancements. Thus, despite the growing body of literature, there is a clear unmet need for a comprehensive and critically integrative review that connects mechanistic insights with practical applicability and translational challenges.

Timeline illustrating key milestones in the development of aptamer-based strategies relevant in ophthalmology. From the discovery of SELEX in 1990 to the clinical approval of Pegaptanib in 2004, followed by advances in complement-targeted therapies (2023–2025) and the recent integration of nanocarrier-based delivery systems, these developments highlight the progression from fundamental discovery to clinical and translational applications.

Consequently, this review aims to integrate diagnostics, targeted therapy, and nanotechnology-based delivery systems to provide a comprehensive and updated perspective on aptamer applications in ocular diseases. Special emphasis is placed on their dual role in diagnostics and targeted therapy, along with their translational and regulatory implications. By concentrating on recent advancement and clinical applicability, this work aims to highlight the evolving role of aptamers in ophthalmology and identify key future directions in the field.

Methods

A comprehensive narrative literature search was conducted using the databases PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science. This review adopts a selective and interpretative approach to summarize current evidence on aptamers in ophthalmology. A qualitative synthesis of the identified studies was performed rather than employing a quantitative meta-analysis, due to heterogeneity in study designs, interventions, and reported outcomes; consequently, no formal statistical pooling or meta-analytic techniques were applied.

Additionally, we searched the following sources for ClinicalTrials.gov for ongoing studies. We use the following keywords and MeSH terms, combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR), to retrieve relevant studies (“Aptamers” OR “Aptamers-Nucleotide”) AND (“Ocular diseases” OR “Eye diseases” OR “Retinal diseases” OR “Macular degeneration” OR “Diabetic Retinopathy” OR “Glaucoma” OR “ocular surface” OR “dry eye”) AND (“Inflammation” OR “Therapy, Targeted” OR “Nanomedicine” OR “Drug-Delivery” OR “Oligonucleotides, Antisense”). Initial screening was conducted by reviewing titles and abstracts to evaluate relevance to the research topic, and we adapted the search terms to align with each database’s specific terminology (eg, utilizing controlled vocabularies like MeSH in PubMed).

We searched for studies published in peer-reviewed journals from January 2000 to March 2026, including only those studies published in English and Spanish. The reasons for exclusion were1 studies not related to ocular diseases,2 Articles lacking access to the full text, and3 studies with incomplete data or unclear methodologies. To enhance methodological rigor in line with the narrative design of this review, individual study outcomes were carefully described and clearly reported throughout the manuscript.

Aptamer targets and clinical progress in ophthalmic applications.

AMD

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of blindness and visual impairment worldwide, particularly among the elderly population.20 Its rising prevalence and socioeconomic impact have prompted extensive research into preventive and therapeutic strategies. The pathophysiology of AMD is multifactorial, involving oxidative stress, impaired autophagy, chronic inflammation, and pathological neovascularization, especially in the “wet” or neovascular form of the disease.21

Among the angiogenic factors studied, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) has emerged as a key regulator of choroidal neovascularization (CNV) associated with AMD.22 VEGF expression is primarily stimulated by hypoxia and is elevated in various retinal diseases characterized by ischemia, including diabetic retinopathy, retinal vein occlusion, and retinopathy of prematurity.23 This growth factor is produced by several ocular cells, including retinal pigment epithelium RPE cells, endothelial cells, pericytes, glial cells, Müller cells, and ganglion cells.24 Among its multiple isoforms, VEGF165 has been identified as the primary pathological driver of ocular neovascularization.25

Considering the evidence summarized in Table S1, aptamer applications in AMD are predominantly therapeutic and can be more coherently classified according to the molecular pathway targeted, including VEGF-dependent angiogenesis, alternative pro-angiogenic and fibrotic signaling, and complement-mediated degeneration.

Therapeutic aptamer targeting VEGF-dependent angiogenesis

Given its central role in neovascularization, VEGF became the first target for therapeutic intervention employing aptamer technology.26 One of the earliest successful aptamer-based strategies was the development of pegaptanib sodium (Macugen®), a 28-base RNA aptamer conjugated to polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains that selectively binds extracellular VEGF isoforms.26,27 Clinical trials demonstrated that pegaptanib stabilized or improved visual acuity in patients with neovascular AMD28 (Table S1). Importantly, pegaptanib’s isoform-specific inhibition facilitates targeted therapy, potentially minimizing off-target effects compared to pan-VEGF blockade.29

Pegaptanib is particularly relevant because it represents the first FDA-approved aptamer therapy in ophthalmology, establishing proof of concept for aptamer-based intravitreal therapy in retinal vascular disease.

Beyond pegaptanib, several next-generation aptamers have been developed to enhance angiogenesis control. For example, V7t1, an RNA aptamer targeting VEGF-165 and VEGF-121, has shown potent angiogenic activity in preclinical studies by reducing VEGF-165-induced endothelial proliferation by approximately 70% in an in vitro model.30 Similarly, Aptamer-3 (Apt-3) has been proposed as a multifunctional antiangiogenic aptamer with activity beyond simple ligand sequestration. In addition to interfering with retinal neovascular signaling, Apt-3 interacts with the Notch-CBF1 pathway, suggesting a broader modulatory role in endothelial proliferation and vascular remodeling.31 Together, these findings indicate that VEGF-directed aptamers have evolved from single-target antagonists toward more refined antiangiogenic molecules with broader pathway-level effects.

Therapeutic aptamers targeting alternative pro-angiogenic and fibrotic pathways

Because VEGF is not the sole driver of AMD progression, additional aptamers have been developed to target complementary anti-angiogenic and fibrotic mechanisms associated with treatment resistance and structural retinal damage.

One such example is E10030 (Fovista), an anti-platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)-BB aptamer that targets PDGF-BB, which is involved in pericyte recruitment and vessel stabilization—two processes implicated in resistance to anti-VEGF monotherapy.32 When combined with ranibizumab, E10030 achieved a 62% improvement in visual acuity compared to ranibizumab alone.33 This strategy is mechanistically relevant because PDGF blockade may destabilize mature neovessels and increase their susceptibility to anti-VEGF treatment, thereby enhancing therapeutic response.

Another promising molecule, RBM-007 (APT-F2P), is an RNA aptamer that neutralizes fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2), a key mediator of angiogenesis and photoreceptor stress. Preclinical data reveal that RBM-007 exerts dual anti-angiogenic and anti-fibrotic effects by blocking FGF2-induced epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in RPE cells and significantly reduces CNV and subretinal fibrosis in vivo.34,35 Taken together, E10030 and RBM-007 illustrate how aptamer-based therapies may address not only vascular leakage but also vessel maturation, fibrosis, and tissue remodeling. These features are increasingly recognized as major determinants of poor long-term visual outcomes in AMD.

Therapeutic aptamers targeting cellular stress and complement-mediated degeneration

In addition to angiogenesis, AMD progression is strongly influenced by oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and complement dysregulation.36,37 Accordingly, several aptamers have been developed to target these nonvascular components of disease pathogenesis.

The CD44 aptamer binds the CD44 receptor, which is upregulated in RPE-19 cells under oxidative stress, thereby influencing processes related to cell adhesion, migration, and inflammatory signaling implicated in neovascular and fibrotic remodeling.38,39 Although still in the preclinical stage, this aptamer broadens the therapeutic scope of AMD-targeted aptamers beyond angiogenesis alone.

In parallel, aptamers targeting components of the complement cascade have progressed to clinical development. The RNA aptamer Avacincaptad pegol (IZERVAY) specifically inhibits complement protein C5, preventing its cleavage into C5a and C5b and subsequent formation of membrane attack complex.40,41 By attenuating inflammation and retinal cell lysis, avacincaptad pegol has demonstrated robust efficacy in slowing geographic atrophy (GA) progression and received US FDA approval in 2023, with an expanded indication granted in 2025 following confirmation of long-term safety and sustained efficacy.42

This approval is particularly important because it confirms that aptamers are not limited to antiangiogenic applications but can also be successfully translated into therapies for the atrophic and inflammatory forms of AMD.

Complement inhibition has also been validated by pegcetacoplan (Syfovre), a C3 inhibitor approved in 2023 for GA secondary to AMD. Although not an aptamer, its success reinforces the therapeutic relevance of complement modulation. Notably, avacincaptad pegol acts downstream (C5 inhibition) and yields measurable effects within 6 months, whereas pegcetacoplan targets the upstream C3 and shows maximal efficacy between 12 and 24 months. This comparison highlights the mechanistic precision of aptamer-based complement inhibition and supports the clinical relevance of pathway-selective molecular design.

Emerging multifunctional therapeutic aptamers

Finally, the AS1411 G-rich DNA aptamer, which binds to nucleolin―a protein overexpressed on proliferating endothelial cells―exhibits potent antiproliferative and antiangiogenic activity by inhibiting nucleolin phosphorylation and downregulating pro-angiogenic microRNAs such as miR-22. Although primarily investigated in oncology, AS1411 shows translational potential for treating neovascular diseases.43

Its conclusion within the AMD therapeutic landscape further emphasizes the versatility of aptamers as multifunctional agents capable of modulating angiogenesis, endothelial proliferation, and microRNA-associated pathogenic pathways.

Glaucoma

Glaucoma represents another major cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, currently affecting over 76 million people, with numbers expected to rise significantly due to an aging population.44 As a chronic and progressive neurodegenerative disease, glaucoma is characterized by the degeneration of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) and the thinning of the retinal nerve fiber layer, ultimately leading to visual field loss.45 Because the early stages are often asymptomatic, delayed diagnosis remains a significant clinical challenge.

The pathophysiology of glaucoma is multifactorial, involving elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, cholinergic dysfunction, and impaired axonal transport within the optic nerve.46 Although IOP-lowering therapies remain the cornerstone of glaucoma treatment, disease progression can still occur even at normal IOP levels, underscoring the necessity for neuroprotective and regenerative strategies that extend beyond pressure control.47

Diagnostic applications of aptamers in glaucoma

Given the limitations in early detection, aptamer-based diagnostic platforms have emerged as highly sensitive tools for identifying glaucoma-related biomarkers.

A deficiency in neurotrophic factors, particularly Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), has been consistently reported in glaucoma,48 highlighting neurodegeneration as a critical pathogenic component.49 Reduced BDNF levels have been observed in RGCs in experimental models50 and in plasma, aqueous humor, and tear film samples from glaucoma patients,51 supporting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

To enhance early detection, Gao et al52 developed a biolayer interferometry (BLI) 3D aptasensor to quantify BDNF in serum from primary angle glaucoma (POAG) patients, while Wu et al49 designed a lateral flow aptamer-based assay to detect BDNF in tear film. Both platforms demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity.

Similarly, aptamer-based biosensors have been extended to other biomarkers such as growth differentiation factor-15 (GFD15). A BLI-ELASA aptamer platform successfully quantifies GDF15 in aqueous humor from human and animal samples, supporting its role as a potential early diagnostic indicator.52

Therapeutic applications: Neuroprotection and anti-fibrotic strategies

Beyond their diagnostic capabilities, aptamers have demonstrated promising therapeutic potential by specifically targeting molecular pathways involved in glaucoma.

Neurotrophic and neuroprotective strategies are particularly significant, as reduced levels of BDNF contribute to RGC degeneration. Furthermore, in vivo studies utilizing a nanoparticle platform co-loaded with a BDNF-encoding plasmid, administered intravitreally, have shown protective effects against RGC apoptosis.48

Moreover, targeting extracellular and fibrotic pathways has been explored.53 Elevated levels of soluble CD44 (sCD44) have been identified in the aqueous humor of POAG patients. sCD44 is internalized by trabecular meshwork cells, leading to cytotoxicity and contributing to increased outflow resistance. Blocking the internalization of sCD44 has demonstrated protective effects, suggesting that CD44-targeting aptamers could serve as novel therapeutic agents for IOP regulation.54

Additionally, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling plays a pivotal role in fibrosis, neuroinflammation, and apoptosis associated with glaucoma.55 Li et al56 demonstrated that the S58 aptamer, which targets the interaction between TGF-β receptor II (TβR II) and TGF-β, effectively reduced collagen deposition and activated the PI3K/Akt/Nrf2 antioxidant pathway in animal models, yielding in significant antifibrotic effects and improvements in glaucoma filtration surgery outcomes.

Aptamer-based drug delivery strategies

In addition to direct therapeutic effects, aptamers are increasingly being investigated as targeted drug delivery systems in glaucoma.

Integrating aptamer technology into nanoparticle systems may enhance the specificity, stability, and sustained release of neurotrophic agents targeting retinal cells.57 For instance, lipid-DNA aptamer conjugates have been investigated for the topical delivery of antiglaucoma medications such as brimonidine, improving drug delivery bioavailability and tissue targeting.58 Similarly, DNA aptamer-based systems have been designed to deliver conventional agents such as timolol, demonstrating potential to optimize pharmacokinetics and reduce off-target effects.59

Ocular Surface Disease

Ocular surface diseases encompass a heterogeneous group of infections, allergies, and neovascular conditions affecting the cornea and conjunctiva. Locally, these disorders often require frequent topical treatment and are limited by poor ocular bioavailability, rapid tear clearance, and local toxicity. Aptamer-based strategies have attracted increasing interest as both therapeutic agents and targeted drug delivery platforms. According to the current evidence, aptamer applications for ocular surface disease can be more coherently categorized into therapeutic and drug-delivery approaches.

Therapeutic applications of aptamers in ocular surface disease

Several aptamers have been developed as therapeutic agents that directly target pathogenic microorganisms, inflammatory mediators, or angiogenic pathways involved in ocular surface diseases.

Bacterial keratitis is a severe, potentially sight-threatening ocular infection that primarily impacts the corneal epithelium and stroma. It ranks among the most prevalent forms of infectious keratitis, especially in contact lenses wearers, where microbial colonization and epithelial microtrauma facilitate bacterial invasion.60 Although conventional antibiotic therapy remains the standard of care, it has increasingly been undermined by the global emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains, posing a critical challenge in clinical management.61

To address this therapeutic limitation, recent research has increasingly focused on innovative approaches, including nanotechnology-based and nucleic acid-driven therapies that enhance both the diagnosis and treatment of bacterial keratitis.62 A notable example is the DNA nanoflower-based eye drop developed by Ran et al63 This formulation encapsulates the β-lactam antibiotic ampicillin alongside zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles and is functionalized with an anti-MRSA aptamer. It exhibits potent bactericidal activity against resistant strains, disrupts biofilms, accelerates epithelial healing, and restores corneal transparency. Moreover, this aptamer-guided system modulates the host immune response by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression.63

Aptamers have also shown promise as direct antivirals in herpetic keratitis. In cases of herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) keratitis, DNA aptamers targeting viral glycoprotein D (gD) inhibit viral entry by 50% to 80% and significantly lower viral titers in infected epithelial cultures.64 By blocking early stages of viral attachment and fusion, these aptamers mitigate corneal cytopathology and offer a potential adjunct to current antiviral regimens.

Beyond infectious diseases, aptamers have demonstrated therapeutic potential in managing angiogenic and allergic ocular surface disorders. Corneal neovascularization, a chronic consequence of infection and inflammation, represents another targetable pathway. Although both VEGF-targeting and nucleolin-targeting aptamers have been explored, the nucleolin aptamer AS1411 is among the most thoroughly characterized in this context. AS1411 has been to suppress VEGF-induced angiogenesis in human limbal stromal cells. Topical administration of AS1411 downregulated miR-21 and miR-221 expression, inhibited endothelial proliferation and migration, and markedly reduced neovascular growth in experimental models.65,66 Similarly, VEGF aptamers have been evaluated in topical animal models for their inhibitory effects on corneal neovascularization,67 reinforcing the angiogenic potential of aptamer-based therapies for the ocular surface.

Aptamer-based therapies have also been investigated for allergic ocular surface disease. Allergic conjunctivitis is a primary reason for seeking eye care among both adults and pediatric populations.68 Numerous studies have established a strong link between allergic conjunctivitis in children and the development of keratoconus, underscoring the importance of implementing early intervention strategies to prevent complications.69

Over the past decade, multiple biomarkers for allergic conjunctivitis have been explored, particularly those associated with immunoglobulin E (IgE), including those linked to vernal keratoconjunctivitis, which features papillary reactions, Horner–Trantas nodules, and conjunctival chemosis.70 Research by Fukuda et al71 demonstrated that monoclonal antibodies outperformed conventional treatments in alleviating clinical signs. These antibodies specifically target IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, resulting in a significant reduction in critical clinical signs such as papilla size, corneal ulcers, and ocular symptoms.72,73 In this context, anti-IgE aptamers have emerged as promising candidates as they bind to the Cε3-Cε4 domains of IgE in in vitro sensitized cells, thereby inhibiting downstream allergic inflammatory signaling.74

Aptamer-based drug delivery for ocular surface disease

In parallel with their direct therapeutic role, aptamers have been increasingly employed as targeting ligands or drug-binding elements to improve ocular drug retention, local bioavailability, and controlled release. This is particularly relevant for topical ophthalmic therapy, where rapid tear turnover and corneal barriers limit treatment efficacy.

Recent innovations have integrated aptamers with pharmacological agents to improve delivery and minimize adverse effects. Schnichels et al58 developed a novel therapeutic approach that utilizes brimonidine-loaded aptamer nanoparticles, which maintain biocompatibility in primary porcine corneal epithelial cells and exhibited no toxicity in murine models. These conjugates enhanced neuroprotective efficacy and prolonged drug release while reducing ocular surface irritation associated with conventional eye drops.

More recently, cornea-targeting aptamers derived from tissue-SELEX have been further adapted into spherical nucleic acid platforms. In this context, Cornea-S5-functionalized gold nanoparticles demonstrated selective binding to porcine corneal tissue and human corneal epithelial cells, with a marked preference for staining of scarred corneal regions, thereby highlighting their value as ocular surface-targeting scaffolds with potential applications in both drug delivery and corneal imaging.75

Similarly, antibiotic-binding aptamers have been repurposed as enhancers for ocular surface delivery enhancers. Aptamers can be engineered to recognize antibiotics such as neomycin and kanamycin with nanomolar affinity, as demonstrated by the NEO5 aptamer, which improves their delivery, stability, and local retention on the ocular surface, maintaining therapeutic concentrations for up to 4 h without inducing cytotoxic effects in ex vivo and in vivo models.76,77 These findings indicate that aptamer-modified nanocarriers could optimize corneal pharmacokinetics, reduce dosing frequency, and minimize systemic exposure.

More recently, this concept has been expanded to tobramycin, a widely used aminoglycoside in ophthalmology. Zhang et al78 reported the development of high-affinity and selective DNA aptamers for tobramycin, demonstrating that aminoglycoside-binding aptamer platforms can be precisely engineered for high specificity and strong molecular recognition. Although primarily developed as a detection platform, this study is particularly relevant for ocular drug delivery because it reinforces the feasibility of using aptamers as tunable binding elements for antibiotic loading, controlled release, and targeted retention on the ocular surface.

Collectively, these findings suggest that aptamer-modified nanocarriers could enhance corneal pharmacokinetics, extend local bioavailability, reduce dosing frequency, and potentially improve the therapeutic index of topical antibiotics while minimizing systemic exposure and ocular surface toxicity.

Another notable example of aptamer-guided drug delivery pertains for dry eye disease and immune-mediated ocular surface inflammation. The limited ocular retention of cyclosporine A (CsA), a crucial immunomodulatory agent, has led to the development of targeted delivery systems.79 Wong et al80 engineered two DNA aptamers, Cornea-S1 and Cornea-S2, that selectively bind to the corneal surface, enhancing CsA retention and enabling sustained release. In vitro assays showed improved drug targeting and prolonged therapeutic exposure. Importantly, these aptamers were designed as corneal surface-targeting delivery vehicles rather than as direct therapeutic antagonists, exemplifying the drug delivery potential of aptamer platforms in managing ocular surface disease (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this review, we explore the principal aptamers developed for ocular applications, emphasizing their therapeutic and diagnostic potential for major conditions such as AMD, glaucoma, and ocular surface diseases. Current evidence supports the growing integration of aptamers as multifunctional platforms in ophthalmology, serving roles in both targeted therapy and precision diagnostic.

Ocular diseases present significant therapeutic challenges due to the eye’s complex anatomy and multilayered defense mechanisms. Drug efficacy is limited by both dynamic (tear turnover, blinking, and aqueous humor flow) and static barriers (corneal epithelium, blood-aqueous, and blood-retinal barriers).19,81 These constraints highlight the need for innovative molecular strategies to enhance intraocular bioavailability.

Aptamers, short single-stranded nucleic acid ligands, address these challenges by binding specific molecular targets with nanomolar to picomolar affinity. Their low immunogenicity, chemical versatility, and high biocompatibility,82 make them suitable candidates for long-term applications in chronic ocular diseases.

While this review provides a comprehensive overview of aptamer applications in major ocular diseases, including AMD, glaucoma, and keratitis, it is also important to acknowledge the historical development of aptamer technology, from the initial SELEX discovery in 1990 to the first clinical approvals. Understanding the historical trajectory highlights how aptamer design principles have evolved alongside advances in molecular biology, chemical modification, and delivery strategies. Future aptamer development will increasingly rely on insights from nucleic acid folding dynamics, target-binding thermodynamics, and chemical modifications that enhance stability, specificity, and bioavailability. Incorporating these considerations is critical for designing the next generation of aptamer with improved therapeutic and diagnostic performance.

The Kd is a crucial indicator of aptamer affinity and specificity toward their molecular targets.83 Low Kd values signify strong and selective interactions, facilitating effective target inhibition at low concentrations while minimizing adverse effects.84 However, therapeutic aptamers face challenges such as rapid renal clearance and nuclease degradation. Chemical modifications like PEGylation can enhance stability and circulation time,85 but may compromise affinity due to steric hindrance or structural interference.86 Optimizing aptamer design requires a careful balance between affinity, stability, and bioavailability, with postmodification Kd evaluation being essential to retain therapeutic efficacy.

From a clinical translation, pegaptanib sodium (Macugen) was the first clinically validated aptamer therapy. Specifically binding VEGF-A165, it demonstrated that isoform-specific inhibition could suppress pathological angiogenesis while preserving physiological VEGF functions, reducing systemic adverse effects, and informing the design of subsequent agents with improved pharmacokinetics and stability.

Building on this foundation, newer agents such as E10030 (Fovista) and avacincaptad pegol (Izervay) have targeted complementary pathways involved in angiogenesis and inflammation.33,41 Fovista, an anti-PDGF-B aptamer, disrupts pericyte recruitment and vascular stabilization, complementing anti-VEGF therapy in preclinical models. However, Phase III trials revealed limited additive benefit, illustrating the complexity of multifactorial diseases like AMD and the limitations of single-target approaches.87 These findings highlight the need for multi-pathway modulation, improved patient stratification, and realistic translational expectations.

Other aptamers, including S58, AS1411, and RBM-007, have demonstrated antiangiogenic and antifibrotic effects in preclinical models targeting VEGF, nucleolin, and FGF2.35,43,56,88 Lessons from Fovista and similar translational challenges have guided the development of these next-generation aptamers, emphasizing enhanced nuclease resistance, optimized tissue penetration, and improved intraocular bioavailability.

Recent regulatory and clinical updates further underscore the therapeutic relevance of aptamers. Most notably, Avacincaptad pegol (Izervay), a C5-targeting RNA aptamer for GA secondary to AMD,89 has now been supported by longer-term Phase 3 data from the GATHER2 trial.90 The recently published 2-year results demonstrated sustained efficacy in slowing GA lesion growth with an acceptable long-term safety profile, reinforcing the durability of complement inhibition as a therapeutic strategy in retinal degeneration. Early-phase clinical studies evaluating avacincaptad pegol in combination with ranibizumab for treatment-naïve neovascular AMD patients have demonstrated a favorable safety and tolerability profile,91 reinforcing the viability of combination strategies targeting complementary pathways.

These findings are particularly important because they support moving avacincaptad pegol beyond short-term lesion reduction and reinforce its continued use as a disease-modifying therapy for GA.90 In this context, the expanded FDA label in 2025, which removed the previous 12-month dosing limitation, reflects growing confidence in its long-term clinical benefits and translational relevance.

Furthermore, RBM-007, an anti-FGF2 aptamer, exemplifies the broader versatility of aptamer therapeutics.34,88 Originally developed for neovascular AMD and subretinal fibrosis in ophthalmology, RBM-007 has also shown promise in systemic disorders such as achondroplasia,35,92 highlighting the adaptability of aptamer-based platforms across different pathological contexts.

Future development of V7t1 and similar aptamers may benefit from engineering thermodynamic stability.93 The efficacy of V7t1 relies on stabilized dimeric G-quadruplex structures in sodium-rich extracellular environments.94 Strengthening these motifs could improve structural integrity and functional performance under physiological conditions, thereby enhancing therapeutic potency and longevity in vivo.30 Recent innovations, including the conjugation of V7t1 with the AS1411 aptamer to create hybrid Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras (PROTACs) that promote the degradation of VEGF165, underscore emerging strategies that extend beyond conventional inhibition toward targeted protein degradation.95

In glaucoma, S58 is particularly vulnerable to nuclease degradation and requires advanced delivery systems to achieve therapeutic efficacy. Encapsulation in vehicles, such as human embryonic kidney-derived exosomes or chitosan hydrogels, protects S58 from degradation and maintains its antifibrotic activity during glaucoma filtration surgery,96 demonstrating the future necessity of sophisticated nanocarriers for aptamer-based therapies.

Dysregulated levels of various factors, including GDF15, contribute to the pathophysiology of glaucoma.97 Elevated GDF15 in aqueous humor is associated with impaired outflow through the trabecular pathway, supporting its role as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.98 Aptamer-based biosensors, such as the BLI-ELASA system developed by Gao et al,52 offer high sensitivity and specificity, providing a cost-effective approach for risk screening, early diagnosis, and experimental evaluation.

Advances in omics technologies have identified additional potential biomarkers.99 However, specificity remains a challenge, as BDNF, TGF-β, CCN2, and GDF15 may be influenced by comorbidities including dry eye disease, cataract, and AMD, emphasizing the need for a multimarker panel and clinical validation.

Despite progress, clinical translation remains hindered by rapid ocular clearance, potential local toxicity, and the high production costs associated with chemical synthesis and stabilization.100 Achieving sustained intraocular concentration without inducing inflammation remains a significant technical challenge. Recent evidence

Safety considerations are critical, as complement inhibition may modulate immunity, PEGylation can provoke hypersensitivity,101 and long-term intravitreal administration poses risks of retinal toxicity and inflammation.102 Recent evidence (2026) underscores that intraocular inflammation (IOI) remains a significant and potentially vision-threatening complication of intravitreal therapies, influenced by various factors including drug properties, formulation, administration conditions, and patient-specific immune responses.103 Variability in clinical presentation, including severe cases such as retinal vasculitis, highlights the complexity of these events, which are often mediated by immune-related mechanisms. These findings stress the need for a deeper understanding and mitigation of inflammatory responses in ocular drug delivery. Combining stability optimization with thorough safety profiling is essential for the development of next-generation aptamer therapeutics. Despite their therapeutic potential, aptamers encounter challenges related to IOI and adverse effects, particularly in the posterior segment, where repeated intravitreal administration may constrain their clinical applicability.

Cornea-S1, currently experimental, has not received FDA approval. Discovered via of-SELEX, it is being evaluated in preclinical rabbit models to functionalize liposomes for CsA delivery.104 Alongside Cornea-S2, these DNA aptamers selectively bind to the corneal surface, improving drug retention and sustained release.10,80 From a clinical perspective, enhancing corneal drug retention may preserve visual quality and ocular surface integrity, illustrating the translational potential of these experimental strategies.

Although the ocular surface section is less extensively developed compared to other areas, it is clinically significant, as disorders like dry eye disease, keratitis, and allergic conjunctivitis can lead to substantial impairments in visual function and quality of life. Therefore, ongoing efforts to better understand the underlying biological pathways and to develop innovative delivery systems targeting the conjunctiva-tear film interface may enhance therapeutic responses and broaden the clinical relevance of aptamer-based approaches in ocular surface diseases.

Aptamers offer advantages over monoclonal antibodies and small molecules, including reversible denaturation, low batch variability, and ease of chemical modification.105 Their modularity enables dual-function applications—as both therapeutic inhibitors and diagnostic biosensors, especially when integrated into nanocarriers or hybrid delivery systems like liposomes and hydrogels.106 Emerging aptamer-nanoparticle conjugates and nanoaptasensors demonstrate improved tissue penetration and real-time biomarker detection,107, 108, 109 paving the way for noninvasive diagnostics and precision therapeutics.79,110,111

Ocular drug administration still relies largely on topical and intravitreal routes, with limitations in tissue penetration, barrier crossing, and toxicity.110,112 Innovative delivery systems, including aptamer-functionalized contact lenses, hydrogels, and molecular imprinting approaches79,113, offer sustained, targeted release and reduced toxicity for chronic conditions such as glaucoma, dry eye, and allergic conjunctivitis.114, 115, 116, 117

Despite advancements, translation barriers persist, including a lack of standardized formulations, limited scalability, insufficient long-term safety data, and variability across preclinical models. Addressing these challenges through standardized protocols, advanced delivery strategies, and rigorous safety evaluations is critical to realizing the clinical potential of aptamers.

In summary, aptamers represent a versatile molecular platform for therapeutic and diagnostic applications in ophthalmology (Figure 4). Future research must concentrate on enhancing in vivo stability, optimizing ocular delivery, broadening target validation beyond VEGF, and establishing a standardized regulatory framework to fully translate these innovative agents into clinical practice.

Overview of key technologies in ocular research and therapy. Current advances include (1) ocular drug loading strategies for improved retention and targeting, (2) the identification of biomarkers in tear and ocular samples for effective disease monitoring, (3) the fabrication of smart materials and contact lenses for therapeutic applications, (4) targeted drug delivery systems designed for both anterior and posterior eye segments, and (5) biosensing technologies that enable real-time monitoring of ocular health.

Future Directions

Building on the current understanding of aptamer applications in ophthalmology, several promising avenues can inform future research and clinical translation. One significant direction is multi-target aptamer design, given that diseases such as AMD, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy involve overlapping angiogenic and inflammatory pathways. Developing aptamers capable of simultaneously modulating multiple targets could enhance therapeutic efficacy and address limitations highlighted by the Phase III failure of Fovista. Moreover, artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches present opportunities to predict aptamer-target binding affinities, optimize sequence design, and improve stability, thereby accelerating discovery and reducing experimental costs.

Another promising strategy is the development of combination therapies, such as concurrently using aptamer conjugates with siRNA, miRNAs, or other biologics.18 This approach could effectively inhibit multiple disease pathways, increase therapeutic synergy, and potentially reduce dosing frequency. Additionally, personalized ocular therapy emerges as a critical consideration because patient-specific factors, including genetic background, disease stage, and ocular pharmacokinetics, can influence treatment outcomes. Tailoring aptamer-based therapies to individual patients could enhance both safety and efficacy, aligning with the principles of precision medicine.

In addition to therapeutic applications, aptamers hold significant promise in diagnostic and delivery technologies. Noninvasive approaches, such as tear-based biomarker analysis combined with aptamer-targeted nanocarriers and exosome-mediated delivery systems, present opportunities for early disease detection and site-specific drug release, thereby enhancing both therapeutic and diagnostic potential.118 Furthermore, aptamer-based biosensors represent highly sensitive and specific tools for real-time monitoring of ocular disease biomarkers, providing a platform for early diagnosis and personalized treatment monitoring. These approaches are particularly relevant for ocular surface disorders, as their accessibility to the tear film and conjunctival tissues can enhance drug delivery and therapeutic response in conditions such as dry eye diseases, keratitis, and allergic conjunctivitis.

Finally, successful translation to clinical practice necessitates addressing economic and regulatory considerations, including production costs, stability, scalability, and regulatory compliance. Early engagement with regulatory agencies and incorporation of cost-effective manufacturing strategies will be critical to facilitate adoption and accessibility.

Overall, these future directions emphasize a translational and integrative approach that combines innovative aptamer design, precision therapy, advanced delivery systems (including nanocarriers and exosomes), as well as biosensor-based diagnostics, along with practical implementation strategies to realize the full potential of aptamer-based ophthalmic interventions.

Conclusion

Ocular diseases such as glaucoma, dry eye, keratoconus, and AMD are significant contributors to visual impairment worldwide, highlighting the urgent need for more effective and targeted therapies. This review summarizes the primary therapeutic aptamers approved or under development for ocular disorders, underscoring their increasing importance as both diagnostic and therapeutic tools. Aptamers, which are synthetic nucleic acid ligands, offer high specificity, tunable binding affinity, and excellent biocompatibility, making them promising alternatives to antibodies for ocular drug targeting and sustained delivery. The successful approval of pegaptanib and the advancement of next-generation candidates such as avacincaptad and Cornea-S1 illustrate their expanding clinical potential. Furthermore, integrating aptamers with nanocarriers and hydrogels provides innovative solutions to overcome ocular barriers and achieve localized, long-lasting drug release.

Despite these advances, the clinical translation of aptamer-based therapeutics encounters significant hurdles, including limited in vivo stability, variable preclinical predictability, high production costs, and a lack of standardized delivery and regulatory frameworks. Lessons learned from previous clinical setbacks, exemplified by Fovista, emphasize the need for improved chemical stabilization, optimized formulations, and more predictive ocular models. Continued multidisciplinary efforts focused on addressing these challenges will be essential to bridge the gap between experimental success and clinical application, ultimately paving the way for a new generation of precise, safe, and patient-friendly aptamer-based therapies for vision preservation.
