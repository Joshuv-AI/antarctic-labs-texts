Reply to: “Actin-specific smooth muscle antibodies bridge adult and juvenile autoimmune hepatitis diagnosis”
Authors: Bastian Engel, Theresa Kirchner, Richard Taubert
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We thank Granito and colleagues for their supportive letter on our study1 and their suggestion to include further measures to quantify anti-smooth muscle antibodies (SMA). Due to limited sample volumes in retrospective studies using existing biorepositories, especially in children, we prioritized commonly used assays, as in the adult study that inspired our study.2

Luckily we were able to analyse left-over sera from our originally published cohort (n = 202)3 with an immunofluorescence assay including vascular smooth muscle (VSM) cells for the detection of F-actin-specific SMA (Leber-Mosaik 9; ID: FA 1300-1010-9, Euroimmun Medizinische Labordiagnostika AG, Germany). As we had little volume left after the original study, we were only able to perform the analysis at a dilution of 1:100 as recommended by the manufacturer.

F-actin-specific SMA had a sensitivity of 64.6% (53/82 patients with autoimmune liver disease [AILD; autoimmune hepatitis and autoimmune sclerosing cholangitis positive for F-actin on VSM cells]) (95% CI 53.3%-74.9%) and a specificity of 77.9% (34/120 of patients with non-AILD liver disease positive for F-actin on VSM cells) (95% CI 70.5%-84.2%) for the detection of AILD. The overall accuracy for the detection of AILD was 73.3% (95% CI 67.2%-78.8%). All eleven patients with staining of vessels, glomeruli and tubuli (VGT) on rodent tissue sections were positive for F-actin on the VSM cells. The AUC for F-actin antibody detection by VSM staining was 0.682 (95% CI 0.626-0.748). This was significantly lower than the AUCs for F-actin (p <0.001) and polyreactive immunoglobulin G (pIgG) (p = 0.002), both quantified using ELISAs with a patient serum dilution similar to that used for VSM cells (1:100).3 There was no difference in the AUC in comparison to those published by us in the original study of ELISAs for the detection of antinuclear antibodies (ANA), and immunofluorescence testing on rodent tissue sections (ANA, SMA) or HEp-2 cells (ANA).3 Due to the limited sample volume, we could not assess age-dependent titre cut-offs in VSM cells.

As we demonstrated for ANA and SMA, titres are important to enhance diagnostic procedures for either sensitivity or specificity. The titre of 1:100 may be regarded as a compromise to deploy the assay for screening purposes. In that regard, it aligns with detection of ANA and SMA by immunofluorescence in our cohort.3 The high correlation between VGT staining on rodent tissue sections and F-actin positivity on VSM cells supports the findings of Granito and colleagues and suggests that VSM cells specifically detect F-actin antibodies. In addition, the filamentous staining pattern on VSM cells is easier to recognize than VGT staining, which requires greater expertise and more highly trained personnel.4 Sensitivity was comparable in our cohort to that of Granito and colleagues while specificity was lower in our study.3,4 Whether this is related to differences between children and adults or to differences between products remains unclear. Additionally, the study by Granito and colleagues used single VSM cells at a different cut-off which is why results of their study and ours may not be fully comparable.4 We agree with Granito and colleagues that further efforts are needed to standardize autoimmune serological testing for antibody detection in AILD, including the use of different substrates and platforms. This is particularly important because current EU regulatory guidelines necessitate the use of commercial substrates for immunofluorescence rather than custom-made unfixed rat tissue sections, which may differ in performance from commercially available substrates. In addition, ELISAs are increasingly used instead of immunofluorescence, particularly in non-expert laboratories. As companies do not need to prove test performance in their target population for regulatory approval, they often lack sufficient control samples as well as sufficiently characterized samples of interest. Thus, it falls on academia to address these unresolved issues.

We demonstrated that an assay to quantify pIgG – IgG that binds a variety of proteins and peptides in patients with AIH, as well as drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis5 or vaccinia-induced liver injury,6 but only rarely in other non-AIH controls – elicits the best overall accuracy, surpassing immunofluorescence and being comparable to F-actin detection by ELISA.5,7,8 Furthermore, F-actin and pIgG ELISA did not necessarily detect the same patients but were complementary to each other, thereby reducing the diagnostic blind spot of non-invasive tests for the detection of AILD in the work-up of non-viral liver diseases.

We have started an investigator-initiated prospective multicentre observational trial which aims to address the issues of standardization and varying test performances across platforms, with the ultimate goal being to provide high-quality evidence on this open issue, with results expected in two to three years (NCT05810480). Finally, once the evidence is available, stakeholders in the field need to reconvene to potentially revise current recommendations.9
