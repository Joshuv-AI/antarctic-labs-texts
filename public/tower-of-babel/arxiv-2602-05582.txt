Geometric Observability Index: An Operator-Theoretic Framework for Per-Feature Sensitivity, Weak Observability, and Dynamic Effects in SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) Pose Estimation
Joe-Mei Feng, Sheng-Wei Yu

Abstract
We present a unified operator-theoretic framework for analyzing per-feature sensitivity in camera-pose estimation on the Lie group SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) . Classical sensitivity tools—conditioning analyses, Euclidean perturbation arguments, and Fisher-information bounds—do not explain how individual image features influence the pose estimate, nor why dynamic or inconsistent observations can disproportionately distort modern SLAM and structure-from-motion systems. To address this gap, we extend influence-function theory to matrix Lie groups and derive an intrinsic perturbation operator for left-trivialized M-estimators on SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) . The resulting Geometric Observability Index (GOI) quantifies the contribution of a single measurement through the curvature operator and the Lie-algebraic structure of the observable subspace. GOI admits a spectral decomposition along the principal directions of the observable curvature, revealing a direct correspondence between weak observability and amplified sensitivity. In the population regime, GOI coincides with the Fisher-information geometry on SE ⁡ ( 3 ) \mathrm{SE}(3) , yielding a single-measurement analogue of the Cramér–Rao bound. The same spectral mechanism explains classical degeneracies such as pure rotation and vanishing parallax, as well as dynamic-feature amplification along weak-curvature directions. Overall, GOI provides a geometrically consistent description of measurement influence that unifies conditioning analysis, Fisher-information geometry, influence-function theory, and dynamic-scene detectability through the spectral geometry of the curvature operator. Because these quantities arise directly within Gauss–Newton pipelines, the curvature spectrum and GOI also yield lightweight, training-free diagnostic signals for identifying dynamic features and detecting weak-observability configurations without modifying existing SLAM architectures.



Keywords:

Pose estimation, SLAM, Observability, Curvature spectrum,
Fisher information, Influence functions, Degeneracy detection,
Lie groups, Dynamic scenes, Pure rotation, M-estimation.

1
Introduction

Estimating camera pose from image measurements is a core problem in vision-based
robotics, structure-from-motion (SfM), and SLAM. Modern systems typically recover

g\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
by solving a nonlinear least-squares problem whose
Gauss–Newton updates rely on the residual Jacobians
J(z,g)
and weight
matrices
W
. Despite decades of progress, a fundamental theoretical question
remains insufficiently understood:

How does a single measurement
z
influence the final pose estimate?

Classical conditioning arguments quantify the stability of the linearized normal
equations but cannot attribute sensitivity to individual features. Fisher
information reveals asymptotic variance but does not describe the finite-sample,
per-feature effect on the estimator. Euclidean perturbation analyses, while
useful, do not fully account for the Lie-group geometry of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
and
therefore fail to capture the intrinsic coupling between rotational and
translational degrees of freedom in camera models.

These limitations obscure several widely observed phenomena:
(i) certain features disproportionately distort the pose estimate;
(ii) translational accuracy degrades under pure rotation or vanishing parallax;
(iii) dynamic or inconsistent measurements induce large biases even when few in number.

A unifying explanation of these effects requires understanding how the curvature
operator of the estimation problem interacts with individual measurements within
the observable subspace of
\mathfrak{se}(3)
. This motivates the need for an
intrinsic, per-feature measure of geometric sensitivity compatible with the
operator and spectral structure of Gauss–Newton estimation on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
.

1.1
Toward an Intrinsic Sensitivity Framework on
\mathrm{SE}(3)

We equip the parameter space with a symmetric positive definite metric
matrix
G\succ 0
, which defines the inner product

\langle a,b\rangle_{G}:=a^{\top}Gb,\qquad\|a\|_{G}^{2}:=\langle a,a\rangle_{G}.

All projections, eigenpairs, and spectral decompositions in this paper
are defined with respect to this
G
-metric.

Let
g^{*}
denote the true pose. The per-feature score is defined as

\psi_{j}(z):=J(z,g^{*})^{\top}W\,r(z,g^{*}),

and the global score is

\Psi(z):=\sum_{j}\psi_{j}(z).

Let
H
denote the population Gauss–Newton/Fisher curvature

H:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z,g^{*})\right].

Let
(v_{i},\lambda_{i})
be the
G
-eigenpairs of
H
.
We define the observable subspace
O
as the subspace onto which influence is measured,
and let
P_{O}
denote the
G
-orthogonal projection onto
O
.
Weak observability is characterized by small curvature eigenvalues within this subspace.

The projected per-feature score is

\psi_{O,j}(z):=P_{O}\psi_{j}(z),

and the projected global score is

\Psi_{O}(z):=\sum_{j}\psi_{O,j}(z).

Under mild regularity assumptions, the observable component of the influence
function induced by contaminating the data distribution by a point mass at
z

satisfies

P_{O}\,\mathrm{IF}(z)=-P_{O}H^{-1}\Psi(z).

Using the projector identity

P_{O}H^{-1}P_{O}=(H_{OO})^{-1},\qquad H_{OO}:=H|_{O}:O\to O,

this becomes, for a single feature
j
,

P_{O}\,\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z).

The magnitude of this projected influence under the
G
-metric defines the

Geometric Observability Index (GOI)

\mathrm{GOI}(z)=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\big\|_{G}.

This induces the operator-domain pipeline governing per-feature sensitivity:

z\;\mapsto\;r(z,g^{*})\;\mapsto\;J(z,g^{*})\;\mapsto\;\psi_{j}(z)\;\mapsto\;\psi_{O,j}(z)\;\mapsto\;H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\;\mapsto\;\mathrm{GOI}(z).

The spectral geometry of
H
within the observable subspace
O
is therefore
central to understanding how individual measurements affect
\mathrm{SE}(3)
pose estimation.

1.2
Geometric Principles Revealed by GOI

Weak observability is not defined by the subspace
O
itself, but by small
curvature eigenvalues within
O
.

Let
(v_{i},\lambda_{i})
be the
G
-eigenpairs of the observable curvature
operator
H_{OO}
, i.e.,

H_{OO}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i},\qquad i\in I_{O}.

The observable score admits the spectral expansion

\psi_{O}(z)=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)\,v_{i},\qquad\psi_{i}(z):=\frac{\langle\psi_{O}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G}}{\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}}.

The GOI therefore satisfies

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2},

revealing three key geometric principles.

Paper Organization

This paper develops a unified operator-theoretic framework for per-feature pose
sensitivity on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
, combining curvature geometry, Fisher information,

Section 2
reviews the most relevant directions in geometric vision,

Section 3
establishes the mathematical preliminaries used throughout the

\mathrm{SE}(3)
, the projection

Section 4

\mathrm{SE}(3)
under left-trivialization. The derivation yields a

-norm of the curvature-amplified projected score.

develops the core spectral theory governing per-feature

establishes the Fisher–Gauss–Newton equivalence, derives

and (ii) pure-rotation / low-parallax detection based on the smallest

Section 7
interprets the entire framework through the unified lens of

Overall, Sections 3–7 build a coherent operator-level theory in which per-feature
sensitivity, weak observability, stability, and degeneracy diagnostics all arise
from the spectral geometry of the curvature operator on
\mathrm{SE}(3)

Mathematical Preliminaries

This section establishes the notation, geometric conventions, and regularity
assumptions used throughout Sections 1–4. All operators are expressed in
left-trivialized coordinates on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
, and all norms

\|\cdot\|_{G}
are taken with respect to a fixed left-invariant Riemannian
metric
G
on
\mathfrak{se}(3)\cong\mathbb{R}^{6}
, unless otherwise stated.

3.1
Lie-Group Notation and Geometry

Let
\mathrm{SE}(3)
denote the special Euclidean group of rigid-body motions
with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{se}(3)\cong\mathbb{R}^{6}
. We represent elements

g\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
as homogeneous transforms
g=(R,t)
, where

R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)
and
t\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The group acts on points

X\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
via

g^{-1}X:=R^{\top}(X-t).

For
\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
, pose perturbations are written in
left
multiplicative
form as

g(\xi):=\exp(\xi)\,g,

where
\exp:\mathfrak{se}(3)\to\mathrm{SE}(3)
is the matrix exponential.

Left-trivialization identifies tangent vectors
T_{g}\mathrm{SE}(3)
with

\mathfrak{se}(3)
via left translation: for each
g\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
,

\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\xi_{g}^{\ell}:=(L_{g})_{*}\xi\in T_{g}\mathrm{SE}(3),

where
L_{g}:\mathrm{SE}(3)\to\mathrm{SE}(3)
is left multiplication and

(L_{g})_{*}
is its differential. Throughout, we use this identification and
freely represent tangent vectors in the Lie algebra.

A fixed left-invariant Riemannian metric
G\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times 6}
on

\mathfrak{se}(3)
induces the norm

\|\xi\|_{G}^{2}:=\xi^{\top}G\,\xi,\qquad\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3).

This metric is used to measure estimator perturbations and to define the
Geometric Observability Index (GOI) in later sections.

Observable subspace induced by curvature.

The observable subspace is defined intrinsically by the curvature operator
introduced later as

O:=\operatorname{range}(H)\subset\mathfrak{se}(3).

We denote by
H_{OO}
the restriction of
H
to this subspace.

We write

\lambda_{\min}:=\min\operatorname{spec}(H_{OO}),\qquad v_{1}:=\arg\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in O\\
\|v\|_{G}=1\end{subarray}}\langle v,H_{OO}v\rangle_{G},

for the smallest observable eigenvalue and its corresponding normalized
eigenvector.

3.2
Camera Projection Model

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote a world point and consider the calibrated
perspective projection
\pi
with domain

\pi:\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x_{3}\neq 0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
x_{3}
denotes the third (depth) coordinate. The observed image measurement is modeled as

z=\pi\big(g^{-1}X\big)+\eta,

where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is zero-mean noise with covariance

\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. We assume throughout that

\Sigma
is symmetric positive-definite, so that the associated weight
matrix

W:=\Sigma^{-1}

is well-defined.

Residual.

For a candidate pose
g
and measurement
z
, the (reprojection) residual is

r(z,g):=z-\pi(g^{-1}X)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

When a particular feature
X
is fixed and unambiguous from context, its
dependence is omitted from the notation.

At the true pose
g^{*}
, the residual reduces to the image noise:

r(Z,g^{*})=\eta,

which is zero-mean with covariance
\Sigma
and independent of the feature
geometry
X
. This fact will be used repeatedly in the derivation of the
curvature operator and the Fisher–Gauss–Newton equivalence.

3.3
Left-Trivialized Jacobian

The Jacobian of the residual with respect to a left-trivialized pose perturbation

\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
is

J(z,g):=\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\,r\bigl(z,\exp(\xi)g\bigr)\Big|_{\xi=0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 6}.

The Jacobian inherits smoothness from
\pi
and the smooth group action of

\mathrm{SE}(3)
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Because the derivative is taken with respect to a left-trivialized Lie-algebra
perturbation, the columns of
J(z,g)
correspond directly to directions in

\mathfrak{se}(3)
. Consequently, the score, curvature, and influence operators
defined later all act intrinsically on the same Lie-algebra coordinate space.

3.4
Score and Curvature Operators

The (left-trivialized) Gauss–Newton score associated with a single feature
z
is

\psi(z,g):=J(z,g)^{\top}W\,r(z,g)\in\mathfrak{se}(3)\cong\mathbb{R}^{6}.

We write
Z
for a generic random measurement drawn from the measurement
distribution, and distinguish between the random vector
\psi(Z,g)
and its
realizations
\psi(z,g)
.

The population curvature (Gauss–Newton / Fisher-information) operator at the
true pose
g^{*}
is defined as

H:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z,g^{*})\bigr]:\mathfrak{se}(3)\to\mathfrak{se}(3).

This operator will later arise naturally as the Fréchet derivative of the
population score when deriving the influence function in
Section
4
.

In left-trivialized coordinates,
H
is represented by a symmetric
6\times 6

matrix that is positive definite on the observable subspace
O
.

3.5
Regularity Assumptions

The following assumptions are used throughout Sections 1–4 and guarantee the
well-posedness of the influence function and of the GOI definition in
Section 3, as well as the stability of finite-sample curvature estimates.

A1 (Smoothness).

The projection
\pi
is
C^{2}
on its domain, and both
r(z,g)
and
J(z,g)

are
C^{1}
in a neighborhood of
g^{*}
with respect to the left-trivialized
coordinates on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
.

A2 (Bounded Jacobians).

There exist constants
C_{J},C_{J^{\prime}}>0
such that

\|J(z,g)\|_{2}\leq C_{J},\qquad\|D_{g}J(z,g)\|_{2}\leq C_{J^{\prime}},

for all
z
and all
g
in a neighborhood of
g^{*}
. Here
\|\cdot\|_{op}

denotes the operator norm induced by the Euclidean norm on

\mathbb{R}^{2\times 6}
, and
D_{g}J
is the derivative of
J
with respect
to the pose variable
g
(expressed in left-trivialized coordinates).

A3 (Identifiability on the observable subspace).

Let
O\subseteq\mathfrak{se}(3)
denote the observable subspace introduced in
Section 3. We assume that the curvature operator
H
is positive definite when
restricted to
O
, i.e., there exists a constant
\lambda_{\min}>0
such that

v^{\top}Hv\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}\,\|v\|_{G}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }v\in O.

Equivalently, the restriction
H_{OO}:O\to O
is invertible with spectrum
uniformly bounded away from zero. Any possible degeneracies (e.g., gauge
freedoms or unobservable directions) are confined to the orthogonal complement

O^{\perp}
.

A4 (Sub-Gaussian Observable Score).

The projected score random vector
P_{O}\psi(Z,g^{*})
is sub-Gaussian with parameter

\sigma^{2}
with respect to the metric norm
\|\cdot\|_{G}
, in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\exp\bigl(\langle u,\,P_{O}\psi(Z,g^{*})-\mathbb{E}[P_{O}\psi(Z,g^{*})]\rangle_{G}\bigr)\Bigr]\;\leq\;\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\|u\|_{G}^{2}\Bigr)\qquad\text{for all }u\in\mathfrak{se}(3).

In particular,
P_{O}\psi(Z,g^{*})
has finite second moments, and empirical
averages of
\psi
and
J^{\top}WJ
concentrate around their expectations at
sub-Gaussian rates, ensuring the stability of empirical curvature estimates

\widehat{H}
.

These assumptions are standard in Lie-group estimation and will be used to
justify the influence-function derivation and the GOI expression in
Section 3, as well as the perturbation bounds in Section 4.

3.6
Operator, Metric, and Spectral Notation (Unified)

All sensitivity quantities in this paper are defined through three objects:

•

the curvature operator
H:\mathfrak{se}(3)\to\mathfrak{se}(3)
,

•

a fixed left-invariant metric
G
on
\mathfrak{se}(3)
,

•

a chosen observable subspace
O\subset\mathfrak{se}(3)
onto which influence is measured.

G-inner product and norm.

For
u,v\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
,

\langle u,v\rangle_{G}:=u^{\top}Gv,\qquad\|u\|_{G}:=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle_{G}}.

Curvature operator.

At the true pose
g^{\ast}
,

H:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[J(Z,g^{\ast})^{\top}WJ(Z,g^{\ast})\right]

is a symmetric positive semidefinite operator. Because
J
is defined with
respect to left-trivialized Lie-algebra perturbations,
H
is self-adjoint
with respect to the
G
-inner product on
\mathfrak{se}(3)
.

Observable subspace.

The observable subspace
O
is specified as the subspace onto which
influence is measured. Weak observability corresponds to small curvature
eigenvalues within this subspace.

Restricted curvature operator.

We define

H_{OO}:=H|_{O}:O\to O.

G-orthogonal projection onto
O
.

The projection
P_{O}:\mathfrak{se}(3)\to O
denotes the
G
-orthogonal
projection onto
O
.

Spectral decomposition within
O
.

Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I_{O}}
be a
G
-orthogonal eigenbasis of
H_{OO}:O\to O

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}>0
. Then

H_{OO}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}.

Spectral coefficients of any vector.

For any
\psi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
, define

\psi_{i}(z):=\frac{\langle P_{O}\psi(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G}}{\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}}.

Then

P_{O}\psi=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}v_{i}.

Action of the inverse curvature on
O
.

On the observable subspace,

H_{OO}^{-1}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{-1}v_{i},\qquad i\in I_{O},

which yields the spectral identity

H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}}{\lambda_{i}}v_{i}.

All later GOI, influence-function, and degeneracy formulas follow from this operator identity.

Problem Formulation

This section formalizes the pose-estimation problem, introduces the population
score and curvature operators evaluated at the true pose
g^{*}
, derives the
intrinsic influence function on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
in left-trivialized
coordinates, and defines the Geometric Observability Index (GOI). All notation
and assumptions follow Section
3
.

4.1
Estimation Objective and Population Score

Let
Z
denote a generic random image measurement drawn from the data-generating
distribution, corresponding to a fixed 3D point
X\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as defined in
Section
3
. For a pose
g\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
, the residual
and Jacobian are exactly those introduced in Section
3
:

r(Z,g):=Z-\pi(g^{-1}X)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad J(Z,g):=J(z,g)\big|_{z=Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 6},

where
\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
denotes a left-trivialized pose perturbation.

The (left-trivialized) Gauss–Newton score for a single random feature is

\psi(Z,g):=J(Z,g)^{\top}W\,r(Z,g)\in\mathfrak{se}(3),

and the associated population score is

U(g):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Z,g)\bigr],

where the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution of
Z
.

We interpret
U(g)
as the population estimating equation of an underlying
M-estimation problem. The true pose
g^{*}
is assumed to satisfy the population
first-order condition

U(g^{*})=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Z,g^{*})\bigr]=0.

4.2
Curvature Operator

We now linearize the population score
U(g)
around the true pose
g^{*}
in
left-trivialized coordinates. For a small perturbation

\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
, define

g(\xi):=\exp(\xi)\,g^{*}.

Under Assumptions A1–A2 in Section
3
, the population
score admits the first-order expansion

U\bigl(g(\xi)\bigr)=U(g^{*})+U^{\prime}(g^{*})[\xi]+o\!\bigl(\|\xi\|_{G}\bigr).

Using the Gauss–Newton curvature approximation, the Fréchet derivative

U^{\prime}(g^{*})
is represented by the curvature operator

H:\mathfrak{se}(3)\to\mathfrak{se}(3)
defined in
Section
3
:

H:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z,g^{*})\bigr].

Thus,

U^{\prime}(g^{*})[\eta]\;\approx\;H\,\eta.

In left-trivialized coordinates,
H
is represented by a symmetric

6\times 6
matrix that is self-adjoint with respect to the

G
-inner product on
\mathfrak{se}(3)
.

Let
O\subseteq\mathfrak{se}(3)
denote the observable subspace introduced in
Section
3
. Assumption A3 implies that the restriction

H_{OO}:O\to O
is positive definite with spectrum bounded away from zero,
i.e., there exists
\lambda_{\min}>0
such that

\langle v,Hv\rangle_{G}\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}\,\|v\|_{G}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }v\in O.

Possible gauge freedoms and unobservable directions are confined to the
orthogonal complement
O^{\perp}
.

Thus the curvature operator
H
is not introduced by assumption,
but arises as the Fréchet derivative of the population score
that governs the influence-function derivation below.

4.3
Influence Function on
\mathrm{SE}(3)

For notational consistency with Section 3.6, we express all quantities using the
operator–metric–spectral framework.

Observable score component.

For any feature
z
, define

\psi_{O,j}(z):=P_{O}\psi_{j}(z),

where
P_{O}
is the
G
-orthogonal projection onto the observable subspace
O
.

Influence function.

Consider an
\varepsilon
-contamination of the data distribution

P_{\varepsilon}=(1-\varepsilon)P+\varepsilon\delta_{z},

and let
g_{\varepsilon}
be the corresponding population M-estimator satisfying

U_{\varepsilon}(g_{\varepsilon})=0.

Differentiating at
\varepsilon=0
yields the estimating-equation relation

U^{\prime}(g^{*})[\eta]+\psi_{j}(z)=0.

By Section 4.2, the Fréchet derivative satisfies

U^{\prime}(g^{*})[\eta]=H\,\eta,

so that

H\eta+\psi_{j}(z)=0.

Projecting onto the observable subspace gives

H_{OO}\eta_{O}+\psi_{O,j}(z)=0,\qquad\eta_{O}:=P_{O}\eta.

Solving for the observable component yields the influence function

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z):=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\in O.

Using the influence-function expression derived in
Section 4.3, this naturally induces the definition of the
Geometric Observability Index (GOI) as

\mathrm{GOI}(z):=\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\|_{G}=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\big\|_{G}.

Pose-only tracking special case.

In pose-only tracking with fixed landmarks, the entire Lie algebra

\mathfrak{se}(3)
is observable, so the projection becomes identity:

P_{O}=I.

All projected quantities therefore reduce to their full-space forms,

\psi_{O,j}(z)=\psi_{j}(z),\qquad H_{OO}=H.

4.4
Geometric Observability Index

The Geometric Observability Index (GOI) of a feature
z
is defined as the
magnitude of its observable influence on the estimator, measured in the
left-invariant metric
G
:

\mathrm{GOI}(z):=\big\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\big\|_{G}=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\big\|_{G}.

This makes GOI a coordinate-free quantity depending only on the intrinsic
curvature geometry within the observable subspace.

4.5
Spectral Form of the GOI

Using the spectral identities within the observable subspace from Section 3.6,

\psi_{O,j}(z)=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)v_{i},\qquad\psi_{i}(z)=\frac{\langle\psi_{O,j}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G}}{\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}},

and

H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)}{\lambda_{i}}v_{i},

we obtain the spectral representation

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}.

This identity governs all later sensitivity, weak observability, and
dynamic-amplification results.

5
Main Theoretical Framework

This section develops the spectral theory governing per-feature sensitivity of

\mathrm{SE}(3)
pose estimation. Building on the influence-function formulation
and the curvature operator
H
introduced in Section
4
, we
characterize weak observability, dynamic-point amplification, and classical
geometric degeneracies. All Assumptions A1–A4 of
Section
3
are in force.

5.1
Spectral Structure and Weak Observability

Recall from Section
4
that the curvature operator

H:\mathfrak{se}(3)\to\mathfrak{se}(3)
is symmetric and positive semidefinite
with respect to the
G
-inner product, and admits a
G
-orthonormal eigenbasis

\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{6}
with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{6}
:

Hv_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i},\qquad\lambda_{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\dots,6.

Let
O\subseteq\mathfrak{se}(3)
denote the observable subspace introduced in
Section 3. By Assumption A3, the restriction
H_{OO}:O\to O
is
positive-definite, and there exists
\lambda_{\min}>0
such that

v^{\top}Hv\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}\,\|v\|_{G}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }v\in O.

From Section
4
, the observable influence function of a
feature
z
is

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\in O,

and the Geometric Observability Index (GOI) is

\mathrm{GOI}(z):=\big\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\big\|_{G}=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\big\|_{G}.

Let
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I_{O}}
be a
G
-orthonormal eigenbasis of
H_{OO}
with
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in I_{O}}
. Expanding the projected score in this
basis,

\psi_{O,j}(z)=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)\,v_{i},\qquad\psi_{i}(z):=\langle\psi_{O,j}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G},

and using
H_{OO}^{-1}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{-1}v_{i}
, we obtain

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)}{\lambda_{i}}\,v_{i},

and hence the spectral representation

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}.

This reveals that sensitivity is governed by small curvature eigenvalues

within the observable subspace
and by the alignment of the score with
the corresponding eigenvectors.

5.2
Dynamic-Point Amplification

Let
z_{d}
be a feature whose residual can be decomposed as

r_{d}=r_{s}+b_{d},

where
r_{s}
is the residual under a perfectly static, well-modeled scene and

b_{d}
denotes the bias induced by motion or misassociation.

The corresponding observable score component satisfies

\psi_{O,j}(z_{d})=\psi_{O,s}(z_{d})+\psi_{O,b}(z_{d}),

where

\psi_{O,s}(z_{d}):=P_{O}\big(J^{\top}Wr_{s}\big),\qquad\psi_{O,b}(z_{d}):=P_{O}\big(J^{\top}Wb_{d}\big).

Expanding within the observable subspace in the eigenbasis

\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I_{O}}
of
H_{OO}
,

\psi_{O,j}(z_{d})=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}(\psi_{s,i}+\psi_{b,i})v_{i},

where

\psi_{s,i}:=\langle\psi_{O,s}(z_{d}),v_{i}\rangle_{G},\qquad\psi_{b,i}:=\langle\psi_{O,b}(z_{d}),v_{i}\rangle_{G}.

Theorem 1

(Dynamic-Point Sensitivity)
.

For any
i_{\star}\in I_{O}
with
\lambda_{i_{\star}}>0
,

\mathrm{GOI}(z_{d})^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{(\psi_{s,i}+\psi_{b,i})^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}},

and in particular

\mathrm{GOI}(z_{d})\geq\frac{|\psi_{s,i_{\star}}+\psi_{b,i_{\star}}|}{\lambda_{i_{\star}}}.

If
|\psi_{s,i_{\star}}|\ll|\psi_{b,i_{\star}}|
and
\lambda_{i_{\star}}
is small,
then

\mathrm{GOI}(z_{d})\approx\frac{|\psi_{b,i_{\star}}|}{\lambda_{i_{\star}}}.

Proof.

From Section 4,
\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)
.
Expanding
\psi_{O,j}(z_{d})
in the eigenbasis and taking the
G
-norm yields the
stated expression. The lower bound follows by isolating the term with index

i_{\star}
.
∎

5.3
Classical Geometric Degeneracies

The spectral framework above recovers well-known degeneracies of camera
geometry and clarifies how they appear as limiting cases of weak observability
within the observable subspace.

Pure rotation.

If the camera undergoes pure rotation, the translational component of the
Jacobian for each feature becomes arbitrarily small. Let

J(Z,g^{*})=[J_{t}(Z,g^{*})\;\;J_{r}(Z,g^{*})]
denote the translational and
rotational blocks of the Jacobian. In the pure-rotation limit,

\|J_{t}(Z,g^{*})\|_{2}\;\to\;0

for all features
Z
, causing the eigenvalues of
H
within the observable
subspace that are associated primarily with translation directions to collapse
toward zero:

\lambda_{i}\;\to\;0\qquad\text{for translation-dominated directions within }O.

Recall that the curvature operator is quadratic in the Jacobian:

\bar{H}:=\mathbb{E}[J^{\top}WJ].

Writing the Jacobian in translational and rotational blocks

J=[J_{t}\;\;J_{r}],

the translational curvature block takes the form

H_{tt}=\mathbb{E}[J_{t}^{\top}WJ_{t}].

Since the eigenvalues of a quadratic form scale with the squared magnitude of its generating vectors, the associated curvature eigenvalues satisfy

\lambda_{i}=O(\|J_{t}\|_{2}^{2})

for the translation-dominated directions.

Hence, when the translational component of the Jacobian becomes small under pure
rotation, the corresponding eigenvalues of
H
scale proportionally to

\|J_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
and collapse toward zero.

These configurations approach the boundary of the identifiability regime in
which Assumption A3 holds. By Theorem
3
, any
nonvanishing score component along eigenvectors within
O
corresponding to
small curvature eigenvalues is amplified by the factor
1/\lambda_{i}
, leading to
large GOI values and severe pose instability in the nearly-degenerate regime.
The collapse of translational curvature

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\to 0)
provides a direct criterion to detect
near-pure rotation or low-parallax configurations.

Vanishing parallax / planar scenes.

If all 3D points lie on (or near) a single plane, or more generally exhibit
vanishing parallax, the depth component of the Jacobian degrades, again driving
one or more eigenvalues of
H
toward zero. In particular, the smallest
nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
of
H_{OO}
tends to zero along such
sequences of configurations, implying by
Theorem
3
that GOI diverges for features whose score
aligns with the corresponding eigenvectors.

Interpretation.

In both cases, GOI quantifies precisely how individual features inherit the loss
of observability: weak curvature within the observable subspace, rather than the
magnitude of reprojection noise, is the root cause of instability. The
degeneracies manifest as directions in which the curvature eigenvalues collapse,
magnifying score components along those directions.

5.4
Unified Spectral Interpretation

For dynamic features, the spectral representation admits a natural decomposition
into intrinsic geometric and dynamic-bias contributions. For a dynamic feature

z_{d}
with score decomposition
\psi_{j}=\psi_{s,j}+\psi_{b,j}
, we have

\mathrm{GOI}(z_{d})=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}(\psi_{s,j}+\psi_{b,j})\big\|_{G}\;\leq\;\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi_{s,j}\big\|_{G}+\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi_{b,j}\big\|_{G},

by the triangle inequality. This motivates the definitions

\mathrm{GOI}_{\mathrm{geom}}(z_{d}):=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi_{s,j}\big\|_{G},\qquad\mathrm{GOI}_{\mathrm{bias}}(z_{d}):=\big\|H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi_{b,j}\big\|_{G},

so that

\mathrm{GOI}(z_{d})\;\leq\;\mathrm{GOI}_{\mathrm{geom}}(z_{d})+\mathrm{GOI}_{\mathrm{bias}}(z_{d}).

The term
\mathrm{GOI}_{\mathrm{geom}}
measures the intrinsic geometric
observability of the feature under a static, well-modeled scene, while

\mathrm{GOI}_{\mathrm{bias}}
captures the contribution of structured residual
inconsistencies (dynamic objects, misassociations, etc.). Both components are
amplified by the same spectral geometry of
H_{OO}
, particularly by small
curvature eigenvalues within the observable subspace.

Section 5 establishes the Fisher-information equivalence

H=I(g^{*})
and derives finite-sample stability guarantees using this
spectral framework.

Theorem 2

(Main Per-Feature Sensitivity Law on
SE(3)
)
.

Let
H_{OO}
denote the observable curvature operator with eigenpairs

H_{OO}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}
for
i\in I_{O}
. For any feature
z
with observable
score component
\psi_{O,j}(z)
, the Geometric Observability Index
admits the spectral representation

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}},\quad\psi_{i}(z):=\langle\psi_{O,j}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G}.

Consequently:

1.

Sensitivity is amplified along directions within the observable subspace corresponding to small curvature eigenvalues.

2.

Dynamic or biased measurements are amplified when aligned with such directions.

3.

Classical degeneracies (pure rotation, vanishing parallax) correspond to the collapse of specific
\lambda_{i}
.

Proof.

From Section 4.3, the observable influence function is

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z).

Expanding
\psi_{O,j}(z)
in the eigenbasis of
H_{OO}
,

\psi_{O,j}(z)=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)v_{i},\quad\psi_{i}(z)=\langle\psi_{O,j}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G},

and using
H_{OO}^{-1}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{-1}v_{i}
, we obtain

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)}{\lambda_{i}}v_{i}.

Taking the squared
G
-norm and using the
G
-orthogonality of
\{v_{i}\}
,

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\|_{G}^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}.

The three consequences follow directly:

(1) If
\lambda_{i}
is small, the corresponding term is amplified.

(2) For a dynamic feature with score decomposition

\psi_{j}=\psi_{s,j}+\psi_{b,j}
, alignment of
\psi_{b,j}
with small-
\lambda_{i}

directions yields large contributions to
\mathrm{GOI}
.

(3) In pure-rotation and vanishing-parallax limits, Sections 5.3 shows that
specific
\lambda_{i}\to 0
, producing divergence of the corresponding terms.
∎

6
Fisher Equivalence and Finite-Sample Stability

6.1
Fisher Information and Gauss–Newton Curvature

Consider the additive Gaussian noise model

Z=\pi(g^{-1}X)+\eta,\qquad\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

with
W=\Sigma^{-1}
and
\Sigma\succ 0
. The log-likelihood for a single
observation is

\ell(Z,g)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,r(Z,g)^{\top}W\,r(Z,g)+\text{const},

where
r(Z,g)=Z-\pi(g^{-1}X)
. Using the left-trivialized perturbation

g(\xi)=\exp(\xi)g
with
\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
, the left-trivialized
Gauss–Newton score is

s(Z,g):=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\right|_{\xi=0}\ell\bigl(Z,\exp(\xi)g\bigr)\in\mathfrak{se}(3).

By the definition of the Jacobian in Section
3
,

r^{\prime}(Z,g)[\xi]=J(Z,g)\,\xi,

so that

s(Z,g)=-\bigl(J(Z,g)^{\top}Wr(Z,g)\bigr)^{\top}\xi.

Therefore the score vector in left-trivialized coordinates is

s(Z,g)=-\psi(Z,g),

where
\psi(Z,g):=J(Z,g)^{\top}Wr(Z,g)
is the Gauss–Newton score defined in
Section
4
.

The (left-trivialized) Fisher information at the true pose
g^{*}
is

I(g^{*}):=\mathbb{E}\bigl[s(Z,g^{*})\,s(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Z,g^{*})\,\psi(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\bigr],

where the expectation is over the joint distribution of
X
and
\eta
.

Theorem 3

(Fisher–Gauss–Newton Equivalence)
.

Under the Gaussian model above,

I(g^{*})=H,\qquad H:=\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z,g^{*})\bigr].

This result is not an isolated identity.
The operator
H
has already appeared in Section 4 as the
Fréchet derivative of the population score governing the
influence-function relation.
The theorem therefore establishes that the same operator
simultaneously governs influence geometry and Fisher information.

Proof.

At the true pose
g^{*}
, the residual is
r(Z,g^{*})=\eta
, which is Gaussian
with mean zero and covariance
\Sigma
, and is independent of the feature
geometry
X
. The Jacobian
J(Z,g^{*})
depends only on
X
and
g^{*}
and is
therefore independent of
\eta
given
X
.

Conditioning on
X
and using the law of total expectation,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Z,g^{*})\,\psi(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}W\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[r(Z,g^{*})\,r(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\mid X\bigr]\,WJ(Z,g^{*})\Bigr].

Since
r(Z,g^{*})=\eta
and
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
is independent of

X
, we have

\mathbb{E}\bigl[r(Z,g^{*})\,r(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\mid X\bigr]=\Sigma.

Thus

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Z,g^{*})\,\psi(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}W\Sigma WJ(Z,g^{*})\bigr].

Using
W=\Sigma^{-1}
, we obtain

W\Sigma W=W,

so that

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\psi(Z,g^{*})\,\psi(Z,g^{*})^{\top}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[J(Z,g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z,g^{*})\bigr]=H.

Hence
I(g^{*})=H
in left-trivialized coordinates.
∎

Thus the curvature operator
H
coincides with the Fisher information matrix.
Consequently, the spectral quantities governing GOI (Section
5
)
admit a classical information-theoretic interpretation: the eigenvalues and
eigenvectors of
H
describe the per-direction information content about
g^{*}
,
while GOI measures how individual features interact with this curvature
geometry within the observable subspace through projection and curvature
amplification.

6.2
Finite-Sample Curvature Concentration

Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. measurements. The empirical curvature is

H_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J(Z_{i},g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z_{i},g^{*}).

Define the random matrices

X_{i}:=J(Z_{i},g^{*})^{\top}WJ(Z_{i},g^{*}),\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

By Assumption A2 (bounded Jacobians) and the boundedness of
W
, each
X_{i}
is
symmetric positive semidefinite and uniformly bounded in operator norm:

\|X_{i}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\;\leq\;\|J(Z_{i},g^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\,\|W\|_{\mathrm{op}}\;\leq\;C_{J}^{2}\,\|W\|_{\mathrm{op}}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=H.

Applying a standard matrix Bernstein inequality for sums of independent,
bounded, self-adjoint random matrices in fixed dimension (here
6
) yields the
following concentration result.

Theorem 4

(Curvature Concentration)
.

There exists a constant
C_{H}>0
, depending only on
C_{J}
,
\|W\|_{\mathrm{op}}

and the ambient dimension, such that for any
0<\delta<1
, with probability at
least
1-\delta
,

\|H_{n}-H\|_{\mathrm{op}}\;\leq\;C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}.

Restricting to the observable subspace
O
and writing

H_{OO}:=H|_{O}
and
H_{n,OO}:=H_{n}|_{O}
,
Weyl’s inequality implies that the nonzero eigenvalues of
H_{n,OO}

concentrate around those of
H_{OO}
at the rate

\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

6.3
Local Stability of Gauss–Newton Estimation

We now use the curvature concentration to obtain a finite-sample stability
guarantee for Gauss–Newton estimation on the observable subspace. Recall the
population score

U(g):=\mathbb{E}[\psi(Z,g)],

with
U(g^{*})=0
, and the empirical score

U_{n}(g):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(Z_{i},g).

For a left-trivialized perturbation
\xi\in\mathfrak{se}(3)
, write

g(\xi):=\exp(\xi)\,g^{*}.

The local behavior of
U_{n}(g(\xi))
around
\xi=0
can be expressed as

U_{n}\bigl(g(\xi)\bigr)=U_{n}(g^{*})+H_{n}\xi+R_{n}(\xi),

where
R_{n}(\xi)
satisfies

\|R_{n}(\xi)\|_{G}\leq C_{\mathrm{nl}}\,\|\xi\|_{G}^{2}.

Define the empirical bias

\varepsilon_{n}:=U_{n}(g^{*}).

By Assumption A4, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\|\varepsilon_{n}\|_{G}\leq C_{\psi}\,\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}}.

We study the solution of the empirical optimality equation restricted to the
observable subspace

P_{O}U_{n}(\widehat{g}_{n})=0.

Let
\widehat{g}_{n}=\exp(\xi_{n})g^{*}
and

\xi_{n,O}:=P_{O}\xi_{n}\in O
.

Assume there exists
r_{0}>0
such that for all
\xi\in O

within the observable subspace, with
\|\xi\|_{G}\leq r_{0}
,

U(g(\xi))=H_{OO}\xi+R(\xi),\qquad\|R(\xi)\|_{G}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}\|\xi\|_{G},

where
\lambda_{\min}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
H_{OO}
.

Theorem 5

(Local Stability Radius on the Observable Subspace)
.

If

n\geq C\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\log\frac{2}{\delta},

then with probability at least
1-\delta
:

1.

There exists a solution
\widehat{g}_{n}
of

P_{O}U_{n}(\widehat{g}_{n})=0
such that

\|\xi_{n,O}\|_{G}\leq r_{0}.

2.

The observable estimation error satisfies

\|\xi_{n,O}\|_{G}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\|\varepsilon_{n,O}\|_{G}}{\lambda_{\min}}\lesssim\frac{\sigma}{\lambda_{\min}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{n}},

where
\varepsilon_{n,O}:=P_{O}\varepsilon_{n}
.

Proof.

Projecting the empirical linearization onto
O
yields

P_{O}U_{n}(g(\xi))=\varepsilon_{n,O}+H_{n,OO}\xi+P_{O}R_{n}(\xi),

where
H_{n,OO}:=H_{n}|_{O}
.

On the event where

\|H_{n,OO}-H_{OO}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min},

we have

\|H_{n,OO}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}}.

Solving
P_{O}U_{n}(\widehat{g}_{n})=0
gives

\xi_{n,O}=-H_{n,OO}^{-1}\varepsilon_{n,O}-H_{n,OO}^{-1}P_{O}R_{n}(\xi_{n,O}).

Bounding terms and absorbing the quadratic remainder yields

\|\xi_{n,O}\|_{G}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{\|\varepsilon_{n,O}\|_{G}}{\lambda_{\min}}.

∎

This shows that on the observable subspace
O
, the Gauss–Newton estimator
has a finite-sample stability radius controlled by
\lambda_{\min}
, matching
the GOI-based sensitivity mechanism within the observable subspace.

6.4
Practical Detection of Dynamic or Inconsistent Features Using GOI

This subsection converts the GOI framework into a training-free diagnostic for
identifying dynamic or inconsistent features. All quantities are computed within
the observable subspace
O
defined in Section 3.

We do not assume that the eigenvectors
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I_{O}}
of
H_{OO}
are

G
-orthonormal; all spectral expressions therefore explicitly include

\|v_{i}\|_{G}
.

Observable-space influence function and GOI.

For any feature
z
, the observable score component is

\psi_{O,j}(z):=P_{O}\psi_{j}(z),

where
\{v_{i}\}_{i\in I_{O}}
is the eigenbasis of
H_{OO}
and

P_{O}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{v_{i}v_{i}^{\top}G}{\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}}.

The observable-space influence function and its
G
-norm are

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z),\qquad\mathrm{GOI}(z)=\big\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\big\|_{G}.

GOI threshold.

A feature is flagged as potentially dynamic whenever

\mathrm{GOI}(z)>\tau_{\mathrm{GOI}},

where
\tau_{\mathrm{GOI}}>0
is a dimensionless spectral threshold.

Alignment with the weakest-curvature direction.

Let
\lambda_{\min}
be the smallest eigenvalue of
H_{OO}
and let
v_{1}
be
the corresponding eigenvector. For each feature, define

\psi_{i}(z):=\frac{\langle\psi_{O,j}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G}}{\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}},\qquad i\in I_{O}.

The alignment ratio with the weakest-curvature direction is

\rho_{1}(z):=\frac{|\psi_{1}(z)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}}\in[0,1].

Proposition 6

(Dynamic-Feature Signature via GOI and Weak-Direction Alignment)
.

For any feature
z
,

\mathrm{GOI}(z)\;\geq\;\frac{|\psi_{1}(z)|}{\lambda_{\min}}\,\|v_{1}\|_{G}=\frac{\rho_{1}(z)}{\lambda_{\min}}\,\|v_{1}\|_{G}\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}.

Thus, for fixed score intensity

\sqrt{\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}
,

\mathrm{GOI}(z)
increases monotonically with
\rho_{1}(z)
.

Proof.

From the spectral expansion,

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\,\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}\geq\frac{\psi_{1}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}^{2}}\,\|v_{1}\|_{G}^{2}.

Taking square roots yields the result.
∎

Practical dynamic-feature detector.

At each time step:

1.

Compute the empirical curvature

H_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J(Z_{i},g^{*})^{\top}W\,J(Z_{i},g^{*}),

and restrict it to
O
via

H_{n,OO}:=P_{O}H_{n}P_{O}.

2.

Extract
\lambda_{\min}
and
v_{1}
from the spectrum of
H_{n,OO}
.

3.

For each feature
z
, compute
\psi_{O,j}(z)
and its coefficients

\psi_{i}(z)
.

4.

Evaluate

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\,\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2},\qquad\rho_{1}(z)=\frac{|\psi_{1}(z)|}{\sqrt{\sum\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}}.

5.

Flag
z
as dynamic or inconsistent if

This section interprets the theoretical results from

7.1
Relation to Conditioning Analysis

Classical conditioning analysis investigates how perturbations in the residual
map affect the estimator. Linearizing the population score in left-trivialized
coordinates yields, within the observable subspace,

U\!\left(g(\xi)\right)=H_{OO}\,\xi_{O}+R(\xi),\qquad g(\xi):=\exp(\xi)\,g^{*},

where
R(\xi)
is quadratic near
\xi=0
.

Ignoring
R(\xi)
leads to

H_{OO}\,\xi_{O}\approx-U(g^{*}),

whose conditioning is governed by the extremal eigenvalues of
H_{OO}
.
The global sensitivity is dictated by the condition number

\kappa(H_{OO})=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}},

where
\lambda_{\min}>0
is the smallest curvature eigenvalue within
O
.

This classical view is aggregate in nature. GOI refines it by decomposing the
effect of
H_{OO}^{-1}
at the feature level via

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z),\qquad\mathrm{GOI}(z)=\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\|_{G}.

Using the spectral identities of Section 3.6,

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\,\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2},\qquad\psi_{i}(z):=\frac{\langle\psi_{O,j}(z),v_{i}\rangle_{G}}{\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}},

which reveals which features align with directions in
O

corresponding to small curvature eigenvalues.

Thus, GOI preserves the global conditioning interpretation while providing a
direction-resolved, per-feature sensitivity measure.

7.2
Relation to Fisher Information

The Fisher–Gauss–Newton equivalence (Theorem
3
)
establishes that, under the Gaussian projection model,

H=I(g^{*}),

the left-trivialized Fisher information matrix at
g^{*}
.

Within the observable subspace
O
, the inverse
H_{OO}^{-1}
therefore equals
the Cramér–Rao covariance lower bound for any regular unbiased estimator.

Since the influence function satisfies

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z),

GOI takes the Fisher-natural form

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\|_{G}^{2}.

Consequently, GOI expresses how an infinitesimal contamination at
z
aligns
with the curvature eigenstructure of
H_{OO}
within
O
and how it contributes
to estimator variance. This yields a per-feature decomposition compatible with
Fisher-information geometry.

7.3
Relation to Influence Functions

Sections
4
–
5
derived the canonical
left-trivialized influence function on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
. Linearizing the
population score equation
U(g)=0
around
g^{*}
and projecting onto
O

P_{O}U^{\prime}(g^{*})[\eta]+\psi_{O,j}(z)=0.

Identifying
P_{O}U^{\prime}(g^{*})
with the observable curvature
H_{OO}
yields

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=\eta_{O}=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z).

Hence,

\mathrm{GOI}(z)=\|\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)\|_{G},

showing that GOI is the intrinsic norm of the standard influence function on a
Lie group, not an ad-hoc heuristic.

7.4
Unified Interpretation of Weak Observability and Dynamic Effects

Weak observability corresponds to small curvature eigenvalues within
O
, which
simultaneously:

(i) deteriorate conditioning,
(ii) inflate Fisher-information condition numbers, and
(iii) amplify influence functions.

For a dynamic feature
z_{d}
, we write

\psi(z_{d},g^{*})=\psi_{s}(z_{d},g^{*})+\psi_{b}(z_{d}),

with
\psi_{b}
representing motion or misassociation bias.

Expanding in the curvature eigenbasis (Section
5.1
) gives

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z_{d})=-\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{s,i}(z_{d})+\psi_{b,i}(z_{d})}{\lambda_{i}}\,v_{i}.

If
\psi_{b,i_{\star}}
is non-negligible for some index
i_{\star}\in I_{O}

for which
\lambda_{i_{\star}}
is small, then

\mathrm{GOI}(z_{d})\approx\frac{|\psi_{b,i_{\star}}(z_{d})|}{\lambda_{i_{\star}}},

explaining why even a few dynamic features can dominate pose drift.

7.5
Role of Empirical Illustration

The results in Sections 5–6 are operator-level consequences of the projection
model and the Lie-group structure of
\mathrm{SE}(3)
. These identities hold
independently of any particular dataset or implementation.

Nevertheless, empirical illustrations are valuable for demonstrating how GOI
distributions, curvature spectra, and degeneracy signatures manifest in practical
VO/SLAM systems. In this sense, experiments serve to visualize and interpret the
theoretical quantities derived in this work, rather than to validate the operator
identities themselves.

7.6
Operator-Theoretic Diagnostics

The spectral structure of
(H_{OO},\{v_{i}\},\{\lambda_{i}\})
induces

Dynamic-feature detection.

Using the observable influence function

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z),

GOI and the alignment with components corresponding to small curvature

(defined in Section
6.4
)
jointly indicate dynamic or inconsistent features. These quantities depend only
on residuals, Jacobians, and the curvature spectrum.

Pure-rotation / low-parallax detection.

The smallest curvature eigenvalue

within

A threshold on

7.7
Integration into Front-End and Back-End SLAM

In VO/SLAM systems, the same operator-theoretic quantities appear naturally in
both front-end and back-end modules.

In the front-end, GOI and alignment scores provide principled,
training-free criteria for feature filtering and prioritization during tracking.
In the back-end, the restriction of the curvature operator for each keyframe
onto the same observable subspace
O
yields a spectral measure of geometric
stability. The smallest eigenvalue

signals pure rotation
or low parallax, guiding fallback strategies such as freezing translation or

Thus, the same curvature spectrum governs both local feature robustness and
global geometric health.

7.8
Limitations and Future Work

Our analysis is intentionally restricted to the operator-theoretic level.
GOI, influence geometry, and degeneracy certificates describe sensitivity and
observability, but they do not prescribe full VO/SLAM designs. Integrating
GOI-based cues into large-scale systems requires additional engineering choices
(e.g., thresholds, scheduling, interaction with robust losses).

The analysis is also inherently
local
: influence functions rely on
Gauss–Newton linearization near
g^{*}
, and extreme outliers or
non-smooth residuals may require additional robustification.

Future research includes GOI extensions to visual–inertial systems,
multi-sensor fusion, and non-Euclidean shape spaces, and exploring combinations
of influence geometry with data-driven priors.

7.9
Summary

GOI provides a unified geometric perspective on conditioning, Fisher
information, and influence functions by expressing per-feature sensitivity
through the spectral geometry of the observable curvature operator

H_{OO}
. Small curvature eigenvalues within
O
determine both the stability
barrier
\lambda_{\min}^{-1}
and the amplification of dynamic or inconsistent
features.

The curvature spectrum also induces lightweight diagnostics: per-feature GOI
and alignment with small-curvature components identify dynamic points, and

\lambda_{\min}
signals pure-rotation and low-parallax degeneracy. These
mechanisms arise directly from operator structure and require no training or
heuristics.

Together with Fisher equivalence and finite-sample stability guarantees,
GOI offers a coherent operator-level characterization of observability and
robustness, applicable to a wide class of nonlinear inverse problems beyond
pose estimation on
\mathrm{SE}(3)
.

8
Conclusion

This work introduced a unified operator-theoretic framework for quantifying
feature-level geometric sensitivity in
\mathrm{SE}(3)
estimation. Starting
from the left-trivialized score
\psi(z,g)
and the curvature operator

H:=\mathbb{E}[J^{\top}WJ],

we derived the observable influence function

\mathrm{IF}_{j}(z)=-H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z),

and defined the Geometric Observability Index

\mathrm{GOI}(z)=\|H_{OO}^{-1}\psi_{O,j}(z)\|_{G}.

Throughout this paper, the curvature operator is never postulated.
It emerges naturally from the influence-function derivation of the
population estimating equation, and all spectral, Fisher-theoretic,
and degeneracy results follow from this operator identity.

GOI provides a directionally resolved measure of how each feature aligns with
components in
O
that are amplified by small curvature eigenvalues of
H_{OO}
.
Its spectral form

\mathrm{GOI}(z)^{2}=\sum_{i\in I_{O}}\frac{\psi_{i}(z)^{2}}{\lambda_{i}^{2}}\,\|v_{i}\|_{G}^{2}

reveals the explicit
1/\lambda_{\min}
amplification mechanism that explains
weak-observability phenomena such as pure rotation, planar scenes, and
low-parallax configurations. Dynamic points introduce additional score terms,
and their projections onto components associated with small curvature
eigenvalues account for their disproportionate influence on pose drift.

A second contribution is the connection between curvature and statistical
efficiency. We proved that
H
coincides with the Fisher information matrix for
the Gaussian projection model. Consequently, GOI quantifies per-feature
contributions to estimator variance and admits a direct semiparametric
interpretation. This eliminates the heuristic nature of sensitivity measures
and replaces them with intrinsic geometric quantities.

Finally, we established finite-sample stability guarantees for Gauss–Newton
estimation. Under standard regularity assumptions (A1–A4), the empirical
curvature satisfies a matrix Bernstein concentration bound, and the resulting
estimator obeys

\|\xi_{n,O}\|_{G}\;\lesssim\;\frac{\sigma}{\lambda_{\min}}\,\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

provided the error remains within the local quadratic basin of radius
r_{0}
.
This scaling matches the GOI prediction and confirms that weak curvature
fundamentally limits both per-feature and global estimation accuracy.

In summary, the proposed IF–GOI–curvature framework unifies geometric
conditioning, Fisher information, and influence-function analysis into a single
operator chain:

z\;\mapsto\;r\;\mapsto\;J\;\mapsto\;\psi\;\mapsto\;P_{O}\psi\;\mapsto\;H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi\;\mapsto\;\|H_{OO}^{-1}P_{O}\psi\|_{G}.

The resulting theory explains classical degeneracies, quantifies dynamic-feature
amplification, and provides sharp stability bounds for
\mathrm{SE}(3)

estimators. Future work will extend these ideas to general nonlinear inverse
problems and to settings involving learned priors, non-Gaussian noise models,
and data-driven approximations of geometric observability.

While the proposed diagnostics originate from an operator-theoretic analysis
rather than a system-level design, their observable quantities already arise in
any Gauss–Newton VO/SLAM pipeline. Thus, they can be incorporated as lightweight
front-end or back-end modules without modifying the underlying architecture,
even though developing a complete end-to-end SLAM system remains outside the
scope of this work.

Thus, the GOI spectrum provides practical diagnostic tools for identifying
dynamic features and detecting pure-rotation or low-parallax degeneracies,
linking the operator-theoretic analysis to real-world SLAM scenarios.

Proof of Theorem
3

Statement.
