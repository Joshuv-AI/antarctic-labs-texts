Abstract
For a set E ⊆ 𝔽 q d E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d} , the distance set is defined as Δ ( E ) := { ∥ 𝐱 − 𝐲 ∥ : 𝐱 , 𝐲 ∈ E } \Delta(E):=\{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|:\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in E\} , where ∥ ⋅ ∥ \|\cdot\| denotes the standard quadratic form. We investigate the Erdős–Falconer distance problem within the flexible class of ( u , s ) (u,s) –Salem sets introduced by Fraser, with emphasis on the even case u = 4 u=4 . By exploiting the exact identity between ‖ E ^ ‖ 4 \|\widehat{E}\|_{4} and the fourth additive energy Λ 4 ​ ( E ) \Lambda_{4}(E) , we prove that quantitative gains in Λ 4 ​ ( E ) \Lambda_{4}(E) force the existence of many distances. In particular, for a ( 4 , s ) (4,s) –Salem set E ⊂ 𝔽 q d E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d} with d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , we prove that if | E | ≫ q min ⁡ { d + 2 4 ​ s + 1 , d + 4 8 ​ s } , |E|\gg q^{\min\left\{\frac{d+2}{4s+1},\frac{d+4}{8s}\right\}}, then E E determines a positive proportion of all distances. This strictly improves Fraser’s threshold of d 4 ​ s \frac{d}{4s} and the Iosevich-Rudnev bound of q d + 1 2 q^{\frac{d+1}{2}} in certain parameter ranges. This provides a new approach to address this problem.

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field with
q
elements and write
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
for the
d
–dimensional vector space equipped with the quadratic form

\|\mathbf{x}\|:=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}.

For
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, the distance set of
E
is defined by

\Delta(E):=\{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\;:\;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in E\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}.

The following conjecture is known as the Erdős–Falconer distance conjecture in the finite field setting. It is worth mentioning that this conjecture represents the discrete version of the celebrated Falconer distance conjecture in geometric measure theory. Recent progress and applications concerning entropy inequalities and discretized sum-product estimates can be found in
[
10
,
11
,
16
,
27
]
.

Conjecture 1

(Erdős–Falconer distance conjecture)
.

Let
\alpha(d)
be the smallest exponent such that whenever
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
satisfies
|E|\geq Cq^{\alpha(d)}
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, one has
|\Delta(E)|\gg q
. If
d\geq 2
is even, then
\alpha(d)=\frac{d}{2}
.

Here, and throughout the paper,
A\ll B
(or equivalently
A=O(B)
) means there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that
A\leq CB
.

A\sim B
means
c_{1}B\leq A\leq c_{2}B
for some absolute constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
.

Iosevich and Rudnev
[
24
]
initiated the study of this problem via Fourier analysis and proved
\alpha(d)\leq\frac{d+1}{2}
. Hart, Iosevich, Koh, and Rudnev
[
19
]
later showed that this bound is sharp for odd
d\geq 3
. In the plane
d=2
, the threshold was improved through a sequence of works
[
2
,
6
,
18
]
to
\frac{4}{3}
, and more recently to
\frac{5}{4}
over prime fields by Murphy, Petridis, Pham, Rudnev, and Stevens in
[
36
]
. However, the conjectured
d/2
threshold for all even dimensions
d\geq 2
remains an open problem.

In a recent paper, Pham and Yoo
[
37
]
confirmed the rotational analog of the conjecture in the plane over prime fields by showing that for any
E,F\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}
with
q
an odd prime, if
|E|,|F|\geq q
, then for almost every rotation
\mathsf{g}
, one has
|\Delta(E,\mathsf{g}F)|\gg q
. Several applications of the distance problem on intersection patterns and expanding functions can also be found in
[
37
]
.

In
[
24
]
, Iosevich and Rudnev also verified the
\frac{d}{2}
threshold for Salem sets, i.e., sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
such that
\max_{\mathbf{m}\neq\mathbf{0}}|\widehat{E}(\mathbf{m})|\leq q^{-d}|E|^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Salem sets provide a bridge between Fourier decay and combinatorial expansion. Fraser
[
14
]
recently introduced a flexible notion of
(u,s)
–Salem sets. In particular, let
u\in[1,\infty]
and
s\in[0,1]
, we say

is a
(u,s)
–Salem set
if

\displaystyle\|\widehat{E}\|_{u}\ll q^{-d}|E|^{1-s},

(1)

where

\|\widehat{E}\|_{u}:=\begin{cases}\bigg(\frac{1}{q^{d}}\sum\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}_{q}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}}|\widehat{E}(\mathbf{x})|^{u}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{u}},~~~~~~~~~u\in[1,\infty),\\
\sup\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}_{q}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}}|\widehat{E}(\mathbf{x})|,~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~u=\infty.\end{cases}

(2)

This framework is quite general: for every
u\geq 2
, any set is
(u,\frac{1}{u})
–Salem. Fraser initiated a study of several problems in this setting, including the distance problem
[
14
]
and the projection problem
[
15
]
. In this paper, we investigate the distance set, its size and structure, within
(u,s)
–Salem sets for even
u
. The odd case is of independent interest and will be treated in a sequel.

1.1
Main question and conjectures

For
s\in[0,1]
and even
u=2k
with
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we begin with the even case since
(u,s)
–Salem sets possess robust additive structure. As can be seen from Corollary
19
in Section
2
, the Salem condition

\|\widehat{E}\|_{2k}\ \ll\ q^{-d}\,|E|^{1-s}

is (up to absolute constants) equivalent to the
2k
-fold additive energy bound

\Lambda_{2k}(E)\ \ll\ |E|^{2k(1-s)}\;+\;q^{-d}\,|E|^{2k}.

We focus on
u=4
; for even
u\geq 6
, the same approach yields analogous statements. In particular,
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is
(4,s)
–Salem if and only if

\Lambda_{4}(E)\ \ll\ q^{-d}|E|^{4}\;+\;|E|^{4-4s}.

(3)

The parameter restrictions for meaningful Salem sets emerge naturally from this framework. Since
\Lambda_{4}(E)\geq|E|^{2}
trivially, the upper bound

|E|^{4-4s}
is only non-trivial when
s\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Similarly, since
\Lambda_{4}(E)\leq|E|^{3}
, the bound
|E|^{4-4s}
provides meaningful control only when
s\geq\frac{1}{4}
. Thus, throughout our analysis, we focus on the parameter range
s\in\left[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}\right]
, which captures the most interesting and non-trivial behavior of
(4,s)
–Salem sets. Therefore, unless otherwise specified, we will always assume

\frac{1}{4}\leq s\leq\frac{1}{2}
throughout this paper.

Building upon the fundamental characterization established in equation (
3
), which provides a precise equivalence between the

(4,s)
–Salem condition and bounds on the fourth additive energy
\Lambda_{4}(E),
Fraser employed the Fourier analytic framework originally developed by Iosevich and Rudnev in
[
24
]
. Fraser was thus able to establish the following comprehensive result concerning the distance problem specifically designed for the class of
(4,s)
–Salem sets.

Theorem 2

(
[
14
]
, Theorem 9.3)
.

If
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is
(4,s)
–Salem with
|E|\geq Cq^{d/2}
with
C
sufficiently large, then

|\Delta(E)|\gg\min\{\,q,\,q^{1-d}|E|^{4s}\,\}.

This clearly implies that if
|E|\geq q^{\frac{d}{4s}}
, then
|\Delta(E)|\gg q
.

For the reader’s convenience, we now present a short and direct alternative proof of this Fraser’s result.
Suppose that
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
is
(4,s)
–Salem. Combining the inequality
\Lambda_{4}(E)\geq\frac{|E|^{4}}{|E-E|}
and the inequality (
3
), we have

|E-E|\ \gg\ \min\{\,q^{d},\,|E|^{4s}\,\}.

Since each sphere in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
has
O(q^{d-1})
points, it is clear that
|\Delta(E)|\gg|E-E|/q^{d-1}.

Hence, the first part of Theorem
2
is derived without the condition
|E|\geq Cq^{d/2},
and this implies that

|\Delta(E)|\ \gg\ \frac{|E-E|}{q^{d-1}}\ \gg\ \begin{cases}q,&\text{if }|E|\ \geq\ q^{\frac{d}{4s}},\\[5.69054pt]
|E|^{4s}\,q^{-(d-1)},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus, for any
(4,s)
–Salem set
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with
|E|\geq q^{\frac{d}{4s}}
, we obtain
|\Delta(E)|\gg q
, recovering Fraser’s threshold via the Iosevich–Rudnev argument
[
24
]
.

In this setting, the main question is:

Problem 3
.

Determine the smallest exponent
\alpha=\alpha(d,4,s)
such that for every
(4,s)
–Salem set
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with
|E|\geq Cq^{\alpha}
with
C
sufficiently large, one has
|\Delta(E)|\gg q
.

Because of this generality, the problem is harder than the classical Erdős–Falconer problem, and even formulating a conjecture is subtle: any threshold must be compatible with the known results for arbitrary sets and for those with controlled additive energy. Representative examples appear in Propositions
26
–
33
in Section
2
.

1.2
Main results

Our main contribution is a two-pronged improvement for
(4,s)
–Salem sets, with further refinements on spheres (with primitive radius) and applications to multiplicative subgroups and sets on an arbitrary variety. More precisely, the method derives two complementary thresholds via point–hyperplane incidences with multiplicities. For the exponent
\frac{d+4}{8s}
, we invoke an incidence inequality in which the point set must be
(4,s)
–Salem and the associated hyperplane multiset is controlled in
L^{2}
by
\Lambda_{4}(E)
. Thus, the Salem hypothesis is used twice.
For the exponent
\frac{d+2}{4s+1}
, we apply a companion incidence inequality that holds for arbitrary point sets, where the only structural input is the
L^{2}
control of the hyperplane multiset through
\Lambda_{4}(E)
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
s\in[\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}]
. Then

\alpha(d,4,s)\ \leq\ \min\Big\{\frac{d+2}{4s+1},\ \frac{d+4}{8s}\Big\}.

In particular,

(i)

if
s\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{d}
, then
\alpha(d,4,s)\leq\frac{d+2}{4s+1}
.

(ii)

if
s\geq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{d}
, then
\alpha(d,4,s)\leq\frac{d+4}{8s}
.

.

Theorem

\frac{d}{4s}

Corollary 9

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we fix notation, collect the basic definitions, and present examples of
(4,s)
–Salem sets. We begin by introducing the key concepts related to
(4,s)
–Salem sets and their connection to additive energy, followed by comprehensive examples that illustrate the rich geometric structures underlying these sets.

2.1
(4,s)
–Salem sets and additive energy

The notion of Salem sets provides a crucial bridge between Fourier analysis and combinatorial properties in finite field geometry. These sets are characterized by controlled Fourier decay, which translates directly into constraints on their additive energy. We start with the fundamental definition of the Fourier transform in our setting.

Definition 17
.

Let
E\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. Define its Fourier transform by

\widehat{E}(\mathbf{x})\;=\;q^{-d}\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in E}\chi(-\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d},

where
\chi(\cdot)
is a nontrivial additive character on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Here, and throughout the paper, we identify the set
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with the indicator function
1_{E}
.

The connection between the
L^{2k}
-norm of the Fourier transform and additive energy is made precise through the following exact identity, which forms the cornerstone of our approach.

Lemma 18

(Exact Identity)
.

For any positive integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the following fundamental relationship:

\|\widehat{E}\|_{2k}^{2k}=q^{-d(2k)}\Lambda_{2k}(E)-q^{-d(2k+1)}|E|^{2k}.

(6)

This identity reveals that the Salem condition on Fourier decay is essentially equivalent to controlling the additive energy of the set. The proof relies on orthogonality properties of additive characters and provides a direct computational tool for analyzing Salem sets.

Proof.

We write
S(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in E}\chi(-\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y})
so that
\widehat{E}(\mathbf{x})=q^{-d}S(\mathbf{x})
. By the orthogonality relations for additive characters, we obtain:

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}|S(\mathbf{x})|^{2k}\;=\;q^{d}\,\Lambda_{2k}(E).

Since
S(\mathbf{0})=|E|
, separating the zero-frequency term yields:

\sum_{\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}}|S(\mathbf{x})|^{2k}\;=\;q^{d}\,\Lambda_{2k}(E)-|E|^{2k}.

Substituting
S(\mathbf{x})=q^{d}\widehat{E}(\mathbf{x})
and dividing by
q^{d}
completes the proof.
∎

The immediate consequence of this identity is the equivalence between Salem conditions and additive energy bounds.

Corollary 19

(Equivalence with Salem Bound)
.

For any
s\in[0,1]
, the
(2k,s)
–Salem condition

\|\widehat{E}\|_{2k}\;\ll\;q^{-d}\,|E|^{1-s}

is equivalent to the additive‐energy bound

\Lambda_{2k}(E)\;\ll\;\;\frac{|E|^{2k}}{q^{d}}+|E|^{2k(1-s)}.

When specialized to the case
2k=4
, this equivalence takes the particularly useful form:

\Lambda_{4}(E)\ll\frac{|E|^{4}}{q^{d}}+|E|^{4-4s}.

(7)

This characterization allows us to work interchangeably between Fourier analytic conditions and combinatorial energy estimates, providing flexibility in our arguments.

Remark 20
.

If
|E|\ll q^{\frac{d}{4s}}
, then the condition (
7
) is equivalent to
\Lambda_{4}(E)\ll|E|^{4-4s}.

Hence,
E\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
with
|E|\ll q^{\frac{d}{4s}}
is a
(4,s)
–Salem set if and only if

\Lambda_{4}(E)\ll|E|^{4-4s}.

such that
\Lambda_{4}(E)=|E|^{2}

Proof.

\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\

\frac{q+1}{p+1}\sim p^{r-1}

In the case where

Lemma 35

If either
(d=4k

By Lemma
34

–Salem set

(18)

4.1
Proof of Theorem
6
–the exponent
\frac{d+4}{8s}

For
t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, let
\nu(t)
be the number of pairs
(x,y)\in E\times E
such that
||x-y||=t
.

Notice that Theorem
6
with the exponent

follows from the following proposition, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the assumption (
18

\Lambda_{4}(E)\ll|E|^{4-4s}

Proposition 40
.

We have the estimate

\sum_{t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\nu(t)^{2}\leq\frac{|E|^{4}}{q}+q^{\frac{d}{4}}|E|^{3-s}\Lambda_{4}(E)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Thus, it suffices to prove Proposition
40
. Before proving this, we establish the key incidence inequality for
(4,s)
–Salem sets.

Lemma 41
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
be a
(4,s)
–Salem set and let
P^{\prime}
be a set of points
(\mathbf{a},b)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
, and let
N(P,P^{\prime})
be the number of pairs
(\mathbf{x},(\mathbf{a},b))\in P\times P^{\prime}
such that
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b
. Then

N(P,P^{\prime})\leq\frac{|P||P^{\prime}|}{q}+|P^{\prime}|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d}{4}}|P|^{1-s}.

Proof.

For each pair
(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}
, define
N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}
as the number of points
\mathbf{x}\in P
such that
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b
.
We write

\displaystyle N(P,P^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}=\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}\bigg(N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}-\frac{|P|}{q}+\frac{|P|}{q}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{|P||P^{\prime}|}{q}+\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}\bigg(N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}-\frac{|P|}{q}\bigg).

By Hölder’s inequality, we have

N(P,P^{\prime})\leq\frac{|P||P^{\prime}|}{q}+|P^{\prime}|^{\frac{3}{4}}\left(\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}\bigg(N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}-\frac{|P|}{q}\bigg)^{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

(19)

In the rest of the proof, we focus on bounding the sum
\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}\bigg(N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}-\frac{|P|}{q}\bigg)^{4}
from above.

We first write

N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in P:\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b}1=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}P(x)\chi(-t(\textbf{a}\cdot x-b)).

Separating the sum over
t
into the cases
t=0
and
t\neq 0
, we obtain that

N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}-\frac{|P|}{q}=q^{d-1}\sum_{t\neq 0}\chi(tb)\widehat{P}(t\mathbf{a}).

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}\left(N_{(\mathbf{a},b)}-\frac{|P|}{q}\right)^{4}

\displaystyle=q^{4d-4}\sum_{(\mathbf{a},b)\in P^{\prime}}\left|\sum_{t\neq 0}\chi(tb)\widehat{P}(t\mathbf{a})\right|^{4}

\displaystyle\leq q^{4d-4}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d},b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},t_{4}\neq 0}\chi(b(t_{1}+t_{2}-t_{3}-t_{4}))\widehat{P}(t_{1}\mathbf{a})\widehat{P}(t_{2}\mathbf{a})\overline{\widehat{P}(t_{3}\mathbf{a})}\overline{\widehat{P}(t_{4}\mathbf{a})}

\displaystyle\leq q^{4d-3}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}}\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\neq 0,t_{1}+t_{2}-t_{3}\neq 0}\widehat{P}(t_{1}\mathbf{a})\widehat{P}(t_{2}\mathbf{a})\overline{\widehat{P}(t_{3}\mathbf{a})}\overline{\widehat{P}((t_{1}+t_{2}-t_{3})\mathbf{a})}

\displaystyle\leq q^{4d-3}\cdot q^{3}\cdot\left(\sum_{\mathbf{m}\neq 0}|\widehat{P}(\mathbf{m})|^{4}\right)\leq q^{d}|P|^{4-4s},

where we used the estimate that

\sum_{\mathbf{m}\neq 0}|\widehat{P}(\mathbf{m})|^{4}\leq q^{-3d}|E|^{4-4s}.

Plugging this bound into (
19
), we obtain the desired bound.
∎

||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}||=t
. Then, we have

\sum_{t\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\nu_{E,F}(t)^{2}\leq\frac{|F|^{2}|E|^{2}}{q}+q^{\frac{d}{2}}|F|^{3/2}\Lambda_{4}(E)^{1/2}.

As a consequence, we obtain

|\Delta(E,F)|\gg\min\left\{q,\frac{|E|^{2s}|F|^{1/2}}{q^{\frac{d}{2}}}\right\}.

So,
|\Delta(E,F)|\gg q
under
|E|^{2s}|F|^{\frac{1}{2}}\gg q^{\frac{d+2}{2}}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
16
.

5
Proof of Theorem
13
and Corollary
14

To prove Theorem
13
, we follow the same strategy as in the previous sections. Therefore, we omit the detailed proof. When a set
E
lies
on
a sphere, we can use extra structure: every line through the origin contains at most two (antipodal) points of
E
, and
\|\mathbf{x}\|
is constant for all
\mathbf{x}\in E
. These properties yield a better exponent. As in the previous sections, we consider

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\|\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbf{x}\cdot(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{z})=0,

using that
\|\mathbf{y}\|=\|\mathbf{z}\|
on the sphere. We then apply Lemma
44
with

P=\{\lambda\,\mathbf{a}:\ \mathbf{a}\in E,\ \lambda\in\mathbb{F}^{*}\},\qquad P^{\prime}=\{(\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b},\,0):\ \mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in E\}.

If
m(\mathbf{u},t)
denotes the multiplicity on
\overline{P^{\prime}}
, then

\sum_{(\mathbf{u},t)\in\overline{P^{\prime}}}m(\mathbf{u},t)^{2}\;=\;\Lambda_{4}(E).

Corollary
14
follows directly from Theorem
13
and Proposition
29
with
s=\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{2(d-\epsilon)}
.

6
A sharp point-hyperplane incidence bound for Salem sets

In this section, we establish a sharp point–hyperplane incidence bound for point sets that are
(4,s)
–Salem, which is a direct consequence of Lemma
41
. The result is of independent interest with potential applications in incidence geometry.

Theorem 45
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
be a
(4,s)
–Salem set and let
H
be a set of
hyperplanes in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
defined by
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b
with
b\neq 0
. Then

I(P,H)\leq\frac{|P||H|}{q}+|H|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d-1}{4}}|P|^{1-s}.

Remark 46
.

If
H
contains hyperplanes with
b=0
, then the proof of Theorem
45
implies a weaker result, namely,

I(P,H)\leq\frac{|P||H|}{q}+|H|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d}{4}}|P|^{1-s}.

Proof.

The proof makes use of Lemma
41
with the following crucial geometric observation:

\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b\quad\iff\quad(\lambda\cdot\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{x}=(\lambda\cdot b),

for all
\lambda\neq 0
. We identify each hyperplane defined by
\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}=b
with
(\mathbf{a},b)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d+1}
. Set
P^{\prime}=\{(\lambda\mathbf{a},\lambda b)\colon(\mathbf{a},b)\in H,\lambda\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\}
. A direct computation shows that

I(P,H)=\frac{1}{q-1}N(P,P^{\prime}).

Applying Lemma
41
, we obtain

I(P,H)\leq\frac{|P||H|}{q}+|H|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d-1}{4}}|P|^{1-s}.

This completes the proof.
∎

This incidence theorem is sharp in the sense that the term
|H|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d-1}{4}}|P|^{1-s}
cannot be improved to
|H|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d-1-\epsilon}{4}}|P|^{1-s}
for any
\epsilon>0
. To see this, let
H_{0}
be a hyperplane in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
, and
H=\{H_{0}\}
,
P=H_{0}
. So
|H|=1
, and

I(P,H)=|P|=q^{d-1}.

Note that
P
is
(4,\frac{1}{4})
–Salem. Theorem
45
tells us that

I(P,H)\leq\frac{|P||H|}{q}+|H|^{\frac{3}{4}}q^{\frac{d-1}{4}}|P|^{1-s},

which is at most

\frac{|P|}{q}+q^{\frac{d-1}{4}}|P|^{\frac{3}{4}}=q^{d-2}+q^{d-1}.

This matches the lower bound of
q^{d-1}
up to a negligible term confirming sharpness.

7
Open problems

Given the generality of the
(4,s)
–Salem framework, it would be interesting to study other topics in this setting. We propose the following directions for future research:

7.1
Odd Values of
u

Throughout this paper, we focused on even values of
u
(specifically
u=4
) because the Salem condition
\|\widehat{E}\|_{u}\ll q^{-d}|E|^{1-s}
translates cleanly into additive energy bounds. For odd values of
u
, this connection is less direct, and new techniques may be required.

Problem 47
.

Develop analogous distance set results for
(u,s)
–Salem sets with odd
u
, particularly
u=3
and
u=5
.

7.2
Solvability of Systems of Equations

The solvability of systems of polynomial equations over finite fields is a classical problem with connections to number theory and algebraic geometry. Some references include
[
17
,
38
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
.

Problem 48
.

Investigate the solvability of systems of bilinear and quadratic equations within
(4,s)
–Salem sets. Can the additional structure provided by the Salem condition yield improved bounds on the number of solutions?

7.3
Projection Problems

The study of orthogonal projections in finite fields has received considerable attention recently. Some references include
[
5
,
7
,
15
,
34
]
.

Problem 49
.

Determine the size threshold for
(4,s)
–Salem sets to guarantee that most orthogonal projections have nearly maximal cardinality. How does the threshold depend on
s
?

7.4
Expanding Functions

Expanding functions over finite fields play a crucial role in additive combinatorics and have applications to sum-product phenomena. Some references include
[
8
,
21
,
29
,
39
]
.

Problem 50
.

Study the expansion properties of polynomial functions restricted to
(4,s)
–Salem sets. For instance, under what conditions on
|E|
and
s
does a polynomial
f:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfy
|f(E)|\gg|E|
?

7.5
Intersection Patterns

The study of intersection patterns between geometric configurations in finite fields has connections to the distance problem and incidence geometry. Some references include
[
37
,
46
]
.

Problem 51
.

Investigate intersection patterns of spheres, paraboloids, and other quadratic surfaces when the point sets involved are
(4,s)
–Salem. Can the Salem structure lead to improved bounds on the size and distribution of intersections?

7.6
Packing Set Problems

Let
T
be a set of transformations from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{d}
. Define
T(E)=\{f(E)\colon f\in T\}
.
Packing set problems ask to determine the size of
T(E)
in terms of the size of
E
. Some references include
[
22
,
23
]
.

Problem 52
.

Study the packing set problems when
E
is a
(4,s)
–Salem set.

7.7
Sharpness of Energy Bounds

Throughout this paper, we relied on energy estimates from
[
26
,
25
,
30
]
. For primitive-radius spheres in odd dimensions, the sharpness of these bounds remains open.

Problem 53
.

Determine whether the energy bound (
9
) holds for primitive-radius spheres in odd dimensions. If true, this would yield the
\frac{d-1}{2}
threshold mentioned in Theorem
5
.

7.8
Higher-Dimensional Generalizations

Our results focused on distance sets defined by the quadratic form
\|x\|=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}
. It would be interesting to extend these results to other norms or distance functions.

Problem 54
.

Extend the
(4,s)
–Salem framework to distance sets defined by higher-degree forms or other algebraic invariants. For instance, study the set

\Delta_{k}(E):=\{\|x-y\|_{k}:x,y\in E\},

where
\|x\|_{k}=x_{1}^{k}+\cdots+x_{d}^{k}
for
k\geq 3
.
