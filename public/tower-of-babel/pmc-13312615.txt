Ethnobotanical study of wild edible plants used by the Yao people in Lingyun County, China
Authors: Xiangtao Cen, Wei Shen, Piyaporn Saensouk, Pathomthat Srisuk, Woranart Thammarong, Surapon Saensouk, Tammanoon Jitpromma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWild edible plants (WEPs) play a vital role in food security, nutrition, and cultural identity among ethnic minority communities in southern China. In the mountainous areas of Guangxi, traditional food systems remain closely connected to local ecosystems. However, quantitative and systematic documentation of WEP diversity and cultural importance among the Yao people is still limited. This study provides a comprehensive ethnobotanical assessment of WEPs used by the Yao community in Lingyun County, with an emphasis on their contributions to subsistence strategies, cultural practices, and biocultural resilience.MethodsEthnobotanical data were gathered using semi-structured interviews, key-informant discussions, and guided field walks. All recorded WEP species were taxonomically identified and classified according to growth habit, plant parts used, and food-use categories. The Cultural Importance Index (CI) was applied to quantify the cultural salience of each species across multiple uses. Floristic similarity between the study area and surrounding regions was assessed using the Jaccard Index and similarity measures.ResultsA total of 165 WEP species belonging to 61 families and 111 genera were documented. The most species-rich families were Rosaceae (16 species), followed by Moraceae (14 species), Asteraceae (12 species), and Zingiberaceae (9 species). Most WEPs were native species (90.91%) and were predominantly herbaceous (36.97%), followed by climbers (24.24%) and trees (21.21%), based on growth habit classification. Fruits and leaves were the most frequently utilized plant parts, highlighting the nutritional contribution of WEPs to local diets. The Cultural Importance Index (CI) ranged from 0.115 to 0.558, indicating the presence of a small number of culturally central, multifunctional species alongside many seasonal or supplementary resources. Wild vegetables represented the most diverse use category. Floristic similarity with neighboring regions was low to moderate (5.9–19.3%), based on comparisons with multiple ethnic groups and study areas across southern China and adjacent regions, rather than exclusively among Yao communities.ConclusionThe Yao people of Lingyun County maintain a diverse and culturally embedded WEP system that enhances food security, dietary diversity, and cultural continuity. Conservation and development strategies should integrate traditional knowledge and community-based management to sustain biocultural diversity in southern China and adjacent regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00905-5.

Introduction

Wild edible plants (WEPs) are a fundamental component of traditional food systems among indigenous and local communities, particularly in forest-dependent and mountainous regions. These plants contribute significantly to household food security by complementing cultivated crops and providing alternative food sources during periods of seasonal scarcity [1]. Long-term reliance on forests and surrounding natural ecosystems for WEPs reflects deep-rooted interactions between people and their environments, maintained through generations of accumulated ecological knowledge [2].

Beyond subsistence, WEPs are increasingly recognized for their nutritional value [3]. Many species provide essential vitamins, minerals, dietary fiber, and bioactive compounds that enhance dietary diversity and nutritional adequacy [4]. In rural and remote areas, where access to commercial food products may be limited, WEPs play a critical role in supporting health and well-being. Their continued use therefore represents both cultural continuity and adaptive strategies for nutritional resilience [5].

WEPs also contribute to household economies and local livelihoods. Surplus products may be exchanged, sold in local markets, or used to reduce food expenditures, thereby strengthening economic resilience [6]. However, rapid socio-economic transformation, changing land-use patterns, and reduced access to forest resources have altered traditional foraging practices in many regions, leading to declining dependence on wild resources and a potential loss of associated knowledge [7–9].

Ethnobotanical research has documented that knowledge of WEPs is closely associated with traditional knowledge systems, reliance on natural resources, and local food preferences [10]. Such knowledge is reflected in the selection, use, and cultural appreciation of plant species within everyday food practices. Despite growing academic attention to WEPs, existing studies often emphasize species inventories or general use patterns, while providing limited integration of nutritional significance, broader economic relevance, and conservation perspectives. Moreover, many studies remain spatially fragmented, site-specific, or restricted to single villages, making it difficult to understand broader patterns of plant use and knowledge distribution within a given cultural landscape [11–13].

China is one of the world’s centers of biological and cultural diversity, supporting numerous ethnic communities with long-standing ethnobotanical traditions [14–16]. Southern China, characterized by complex topography and diverse ecosystems, harbors a high diversity of WEPs that have historically supported local food systems. Although previous ethnobotanical studies have documented WEP use among several ethnic communities in this region, research coverage remains uneven [17]. However, existing studies are often spatially limited or site-specific, making it difficult to understand broader patterns of plant use and knowledge distribution within a given cultural landscape.

Yao people are widely distributed across several regions of southern China and possess rich and diverse ethnobotanical knowledge shaped by long-term interactions with local environments [18]. Ethnobotanical studies on the Yao people have been conducted in different parts of China; however, most existing research has predominantly focused on medicinal plants, ritual uses, and healing practices [19–21]. In contrast, systematic ethnobotanical documentation of WEPs used by the Yao people remains limited, particularly in southern China. Specifically, no comprehensive study has examined the diversity, use patterns, and cultural significance of WEPs among the Yao people in Lingyun County, highlighting a clear geographical and thematic research gap.

Lingyun County, located in Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, represents a biologically rich yet understudied area where traditional reliance on forest resources persists alongside increasing modernization. To date, there has been no comprehensive ethnobotanical study focusing specifically on WEPs used by the Yao people in this county that integrates species diversity, preparation and consumption practices, nutritional significance, and implications for biocultural conservation. This lack of systematic documentation constitutes a clear research gap, particularly at a time when both biological resources and traditional knowledge systems are facing increasing pressures.

The present study addresses existing knowledge gaps by investigating the diversity, ethnobotanical uses, and cultural significance of wild edible plants (WEPs) used by the Yao people in Lingyun County. Specifically, the study aims to:

(1) Document the species diversity and taxonomic composition of WEPs used by the Yao people in Lingyun County, China, based on field surveys and ethnobotanical interviews.

(2) Characterize the ethnobotanical uses of WEPs, including plant parts used, preparation methods, and modes of consumption, to understand their roles in local subsistence and food systems.

(3) Quantitatively evaluate the cultural importance of WEP species using the Cultural Importance Index (CI), in order to identify key multifunctional species embedded in traditional knowledge and daily practices.

(4) Compare patterns of WEP species composition between the Yao community in Lingyun County and other ethnobotanical regions using the Jaccard Similarity Index (JI), to assess cross-regional similarities in plant use.

We hypothesize that (i) WEP use among the Yao people is non-random and structured by both ecological availability and cultural preferences, resulting in the prominence of a limited number of culturally important species with high CI values, and (ii) cross-regional similarity in WEP composition will be relatively low overall, but higher among geographically or culturally related regions, reflecting shared traditional knowledge and environmental adaptation.

Materials and methods

Study area

Lingyun County is located in the northwestern part of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, southern China, and is administratively under Baise City (Fig. 1). Geographically, the county lies in a transitional zone between the Yungui Plateau and the hilly mountainous regions of Guangxi, extending between 106°23′–106°55′E and 24°06′–24°37′N. Lingyun County covers an area of approximately 2,048 km², with mountainous terrain accounting for about 93.32% of the total land area, and has a population of approximately 188,000 inhabitants [22].

The topography is characterized by higher elevations in the northwest and lower terrain in the southeast, with elevations ranging from approximately 191 m to over 2,000 m above sea level. The landscape consists mainly of clastic rock formations in the western areas and carbonate rock formations in the east, resulting in extensive karst landforms, steep slopes, and deep valleys. Several rivers belonging to the Youjiang River and Hongshui River systems flow through the county, supporting local hydrology and agricultural activities in valley areas [23].

Lingyun County has a humid subtropical monsoon climate (Köppen Cfa), with a mean annual temperature of approximately 20.7 °C and an average annual precipitation of about 1,729 mm, nearly 80% of which occurs during the monsoon season from May to September. The county maintains a forest coverage rate of approximately 71%, providing favorable conditions for high plant diversity and traditional plant utilization [24].

The county is ethnically diverse. According to the seventh national population census (2020), Lingyun County is home to 25 recognized ethnic groups. Han Chinese constitute the largest proportion of the population (44.08%), while ethnic minorities account for 55.92%, including the Zhuang people (31.23%) and the Yao people (24.69%) [25].

Ethnolinguistic and cultural background

Lingyun County is home to a culturally diverse population in which the Yao people, Zhuang people, and Han Chinese have coexisted for generations. This long-standing interaction has shaped a pluralistic cultural system in which multiple belief traditions overlap. Although Buddhism and Daoism are formally recognized, everyday life in rural communities continues to be strongly influenced by indigenous cosmologies, particularly the veneration of ancestral spirits and local deities associated with forests, mountains, and water sources. These beliefs remain closely linked to subsistence practices and the use of natural resources [26].

The Yao people, who are the focus of this study, maintain a rich body of traditional knowledge that is largely transmitted orally. Knowledge related to plant use, seasonal cycles, and landscape management is embedded in oral traditions, including ritual chants, narratives, and everyday practices. In many cases, the use of wild edible plants follows culturally defined norms; harvesting periods, processing methods, and consumption patterns are guided by customary rules. Certain species are also associated with ritual contexts or symbolic meanings, reflecting the close integration of subsistence activities with cultural belief systems [27].

Linguistically, the Yao people speak languages belonging to the Hmong–Mien family, while the Zhuang speak Tai-Kadai languages. Mandarin Chinese is widely used in formal contexts such as education and administration, whereas local languages remain dominant in households, markets, and community interactions. This multilingual environment facilitates the circulation and exchange of ethnobotanical knowledge across ethnic groups, allowing plant uses and practices to be shared and adapted over time [27].

Daily life in Lingyun County reflects a close interconnection between livelihoods, cultural practices, and the surrounding environment. The local economy, centered in Sicheng Town, is predominantly based on small-scale agriculture, with staple crops such as rice, maize, and soybean forming the foundation of subsistence production, while cash crops including sugarcane, tobacco, tea, and coffee provide important sources of household income. Among these, tea cultivation has become a key economic activity in the mountainous karst landscape, contributing substantially to rural livelihoods and poverty alleviation, with an annual output value exceeding 500 million yuan and benefiting tens of thousands of farmers. In addition to farming, households engage in forest product collection and seasonal foraging, particularly in areas where steep slopes and limited arable land constrain agricultural expansion [28].

Within this mixed subsistence–market economy, wild edible plants continue to play an important complementary role. They are gathered from surrounding forests and fallow lands, contributing to dietary diversity, especially during periods of seasonal scarcity. Beyond their nutritional value, these plants are embedded in local food culture and are commonly used in traditional dishes, communal meals, and seasonal practices. At the same time, the boundary between food and medicine remains fluid, as many species are consumed not only for sustenance but also for their perceived health-promoting properties, reflecting a long-standing integration of dietary and therapeutic knowledge within everyday life [29].

Despite increasing integration into market economies and broader socio-economic transformation, many Yao communities continue to rely on traditional ecological knowledge in their daily lives. However, processes such as formal education, migration, and changing livelihoods are influencing knowledge transmission, particularly among younger generations. Local markets, household practices, and intergenerational exchange remain important spaces for the maintenance and adaptation of ethnobotanical knowledge. These dynamics highlight both the resilience and vulnerability of traditional knowledge systems under conditions of rapid socio-economic change [30].

Field survey and data collection

Plant specimen collection and documentation were conducted in 2025 in Yao-inhabited areas of Lingyun County, located in the northwestern part of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, southern China. This study focused exclusively on WEPs traditionally collected from natural environments and consumed by the Yao people. Cultivated plants were deliberately excluded from the dataset. WEPs recorded in local markets were included only when informants confirmed that the materials originated from wild collection rather than cultivation.

Field investigations were carried out through repeated visits to multiple habitat types, including (1) natural forests, (2) fallow and abandoned lands, (3) areas adjacent to natural water sources such as streams and rivers, and (4) local traditional markets where wild-collected edible plants were traded seasonally. In natural habitats, community members acted as guides to facilitate plant identification and collection, whereas market surveys were conducted directly by the Chinese-speaking author without guides. This multi-habitat approach was designed to capture the diversity of wild plant resources across different ecological settings and utilization pathways while maintaining a clear focus on wild plant use.

Plant species were recorded through direct field collection in natural habitats and through market surveys when wild-collected plants were available. Field collection of plant specimens was conducted by Xiangtao Cen and Wei Shen. For each recorded species, ethnobotanical information was obtained through direct observation and semi-structured interviews with local informants, including elders, farmers, and household food preparers with experience in wild plant collection. Documented data included vernacular names, plant parts used, collection habitats, preparation and cooking methods, and modes of consumption (e.g., fresh, cooked, dried, or fermented).

Plant specimens were collected in situ from natural habitats when available and permissible. In cases where direct collection was not feasible, such as when only processed or dried materials were observed in markets, high-resolution photographs and detailed field notes describing diagnostic morphological characteristics were recorded to support taxonomic identification. All specimens and photographic records were documented with reference to their collection context (habitat or market source) to ensure traceability.

Representative plant materials were prepared as voucher specimens using standard herbarium techniques [31]. During the present study, these vouchers were curated and stored by the research team and have not yet been formally deposited in an external institutional herbarium, as a dedicated herbarium collection is planned for future establishment. All voucher specimens are currently accessible for examination and verification and are intended for future deposition at Baise University, Baise City, Guangxi, China.

Taxonomic identification was conducted by Wei Shen using authoritative floristic literature, including the Flora of China and relevant regional botanical references, with guidance from Surapon Saensouk and Tammanoon Jitpromma. Scientific names and current nomenclature were verified using world flora online (https://www.worldfloraonline.org/) [32]. Each voucher specimen was labeled with collection date, locality, habitat type or market source, vernacular name, plant parts used, and relevant ethnobotanical notes to ensure accurate botanical and cultural documentation.

Ethnobotanical data collection

Data were collected using standard qualitative ethnobotanical approaches, including semi-structured interviews, participant observation, and guided field walks with knowledgeable local informants to document traditional knowledge related to WEPs.

A total of 104 informants were interviewed from 13 villages, with eight informants selected from each village (four men and four women) (Table 1). Informants were chosen using a combination of purposive sampling techniques [33], prioritizing elderly villagers, traditional healers, experienced plant collectors, and individuals widely recognized within their communities for their ethnobotanical knowledge. Participants ranged in age from 20 to 71 years.

Interviews were conducted primarily in Mandarin Chinese, which is widely spoken by the local population; however, for a few elderly informants who were not fluent in Mandarin, communication was facilitated through translation by local assistants or guides fluent in both Mandarin and the Yao language. The interviews focused on local plant names, parts used, methods of preparation and consumption, collection habitats, and the nutritional or symbolic significance of each species. Guided field walks were carried out with selected knowledgeable local informants, particularly experienced plant collectors, elderly villagers, and traditional knowledge holders identified during the interview process, to locate and identify plant species in situ and to ensure accurate documentation and collection of voucher specimens.

The interviews were carried out by a native Chinese-speaking author, and Mandarin Chinese was used throughout the interview process to ensure effective communication and minimize potential misinterpretation of ethnobotanical information.

Prior to data collection, the aims and procedures of the study were clearly explained to all participants, and Prior Informed Consent (PIC) was obtained in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Code of Ethics [34] and the Nagoya Protocol on Access and Benefit-Sharing [35]. Participation was voluntary, and informants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences.

Although the research did not involve the collection of sensitive personal information and therefore did not require formal institutional ethical approval, all research activities strictly adhered to internationally accepted ethical standards for ethnobotanical research, ensuring respect, transparency, and reciprocity toward the participating communities.

Data analysis

Cultural Importance Index (CI)

The Cultural Importance Index (CI) was applied to evaluate the cultural significance of plant species used by local communities in Lingyun County. This quantitative ethnobotanical index integrates both the frequency of use and the diversity of utilization types, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of the cultural relevance of each species within the local sociocultural context.

Ethnobotanical data were obtained through interviews with local informants. All reported uses of each plant species were recorded and classified into seven predefined food-use categories: condiments and flavoring, food dyeing, fruit, herbal tea, staple food, sweet, dessert, or snack, and vegetable. Each mention of a species within a specific category was treated as an independent utilization report.

The CI was calculated following the method proposed by Tardío and Pardo-De-Santayana (2008) [36], using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rm{CI\:}{=\:}\sum\:_{\mathrm{u=}{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{1}}}^{{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{NC}}}\sum\:_{\mathrm{i=}{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{1}}}^{{\mathrm{i}}_{\mathrm{NC}}}\frac{\mathrm{U}{\mathrm{R}}_{\mathrm{ui}}}{\mathrm{N}}$$\end{document}

where CI is the Cultural Importance Index of a plant species, URui represents the utilization report of species i in use category u, NC is the total number of distinct use categories, and N is the total number of informants interviewed.

Higher CI values indicate plant species that are used frequently across multiple categories, reflecting multifunctional roles and strong cultural embeddedness. This index therefore highlights species that play particularly important roles in daily subsistence, traditional practices, and local knowledge systems within the study area.

Jaccard Similarity Index (JI)

To evaluate the degree of similarity in WEP use between the Yao community in Lingyun County and other ethnobotanical studies, a Jaccard similarity analysis was conducted. This index was used to compare species composition across different study areas and to assess shared and distinct patterns of wild plant utilization.

Comparative datasets were compiled from published ethnobotanical studies on WEPs conducted in geographically or culturally relevant regions. Species lists were standardized by scientific names, and synonyms were unified based on current accepted nomenclature prior to analysis. Only WEP species were included to ensure comparability among datasets.

The Jaccard similarity index (JI) was calculated using the following formula [37]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{JI=}\frac{\mathrm{c}}{\mathrm{a+b-c}}$$\end{document}

where a denotes the number of species recorded in study area A, b represents the number of species found in study area B, and c refers to the species shared by both areas. The Jaccard Index (JI) varies from 0, indicating no similarity, to 1, indicating complete similarity. When the JI is multiplied by 100, it is expressed as a similarity percentage (SM), which offers a clearer and more intuitive representation of the degree of species overlap between the study sites.

Result

Diversity of WEPs

A total of 165 WEP species, belonging to 61 families and 111 genera, were recorded in the study area (Table 2). Species richness was unevenly distributed among families. Rosaceae was the most species-rich family with 16 species, followed by Moraceae (14 species) and Asteraceae (12 species). Zingiberaceae also showed notable representation with 9 species, reflecting its importance in local food practices.

Several families were represented by a moderate number of species, including Actinidiaceae (6 species) and Rutaceae (5 species). Families with four species each comprised Cucurbitaceae, Dioscoreaceae, Rubiaceae, and Vitaceae. A group of families—such as Acanthaceae, Amaranthaceae, Anacardiaceae, Apiaceae, Campanulaceae, Fagaceae, Lauraceae, Phyllanthaceae, Primulaceae, Solanaceae, Theaceae, and Urticaceae—were each represented by three species.

In contrast, many families exhibited low species richness, with two or only a single species recorded. These less-represented families collectively contributed to the overall taxonomic diversity of WEPs, indicating a broad but uneven family composition within the local flora.

Origin, growth habit, and used parts of WEPs

Among the 165 WEP species recorded, 150 species (90.91%) were native to China, whereas 15 species (9.09%) were introduced (Fig. 2).

Based on growth habit classification (Fig. 2), herbaceous species constituted the largest proportion of WEPs, with 61 species (36.97%). This was followed by climbers, represented by 40 species (24.24%), and trees, accounting for 35 species (21.21%). Shrubs were less frequently recorded, comprising 28 species (16.97%) of the total documented flora, while grasses were rarely represented, with only one species (0.61%).

Regarding plant parts utilized (Fig. 2), fruit were the most frequently consumed, represented by 79 species (42.25%). Use of the whole plant accounted for 31 species (16.58%), followed closely by leaf with 29 species (15.51%). Shoot were recorded for 20 species (10.70%), while root were used in 11 species (5.88%). Less commonly utilized parts included inflorescence with 8 species (4.28%), tuber with 4 species (2.14%), seed with 3 species (1.60%), and rhizome with 2 species (1.06%).

Cultural Importance Index (CI) of WEPs

Across all utilization categories, the Cultural Importance Index (CI) values of WEPs used by the Yao people revealed a clear gradient of cultural salience, ranging from 0.115 to 0.558. Species with the highest CI values represent plants that are deeply embedded in daily subsistence, culinary practices, and cultural traditions, while those with lower values tend to be associated with seasonal, supplementary, or specialized uses.

The highest CI values (≥ 0.548) were recorded for a small group of multipurpose and widely used species, including Camellia oleifera, Musa balbisiana, Phyllanthus emblica, Buddleja officinalis, Alpinia japonica, and Morus alba. These taxa are notable for their broad utilization across different food categories (e.g., vegetable, fruit, oil, herbal tea, or food dyeing) and their frequent citation by informants, underscoring their central role in local livelihoods and food systems. Similarly, Illicium verum (CI = 0.538) and Dicliptera tinctoria, Oenanthe javanica, Codonopsis javanica, and Hypericum japonicum (each CI = 0.529) ranked among the most culturally significant plants.

Species with high CI values between 0.462 and 0.519, such as Castanea mollissima, Ficus simplicissima, Dioscorea polystachya, Eriobotrya japonica, and Solanum americanum, are mainly staple foods, vegetables, or commonly consumed fruits. Their prominence reflects both their nutritional importance and their regular presence in everyday meals. A large number of leafy vegetables and wild greens—including Youngia japonica, Pteridium revolutum, Portulaca oleracea, Amaranthus retroflexus, and Houttuynia cordata—also showed consistently high CI values, highlighting the strong reliance on wild vegetables in the Yao diet.

Moderate CI values (0.308–0.433) characterized a broad suite of species used as fruits, vegetables, condiments, and herbal teas. This group includes taxa such as Centella asiatica, Toona sinensis, Piper sarmentosum, Castanopsis chinensis, Pseudognaphalium affine, Lonicera hypoglauca, and Litsea cubeba. Although not as dominant as the highest-ranked species, these plants contribute substantially to dietary diversity and flavor enhancement and are regularly incorporated into local food practices.

Species with lower CI values (≤ 0.202), including Rubus parvifolius, Citrus maxima, Gaultheria crenulata, Imperata cylindrica, Aralia armata, Momordica subangulata, and Rubus corchorifolius, were cited less frequently. These taxa tend to be used seasonally, opportunistically, or in more specific contexts, suggesting a more limited but still meaningful role in the overall ethnobotanical knowledge system.

Utilization of WEPs

WEPs used as condiments and flavoring

A total of 26 WEP species were documented as being used as condiments and flavoring agents by the Yao people in Lingyun County (Table 2; Fig. 3). These species represent a taxonomically diverse assemblage and play a crucial role in enhancing aroma, taste, and overall palatability of traditional Yao cuisine. The Cultural Importance Index for condiments and flavoring (CICF) ranged from 0.173 to 0.558, indicating substantial variation in citation frequency and intensity of use among the recorded species (Table S1).

Camellia oleifera exhibited the highest CICF value (0.558), reflecting its prominent importance as a source of edible oil widely used in daily cooking. Other highly valued species included Alpinia japonica (0.548) and Illicium verum (0.538), both of which were commonly employed as culinary seasonings to enhance flavor and aroma. Ficus simplicissima (0.519) and Litsea pungens (0.442) were also frequently cited, particularly for their use in stewed meat dishes and as aromatic seasonings.

Aromatic taxa were especially well represented, notably members of the Lauraceae, such as Litsea pungens (0.442), L. cubeba (0.413), and L. mollis (0.260), which were consistently valued as natural flavoring agents. Species belonging to the Zingiberaceae, including Alpinia galanga, A. psilogyna, A. oblongifolia, A. stachyodes, Hedychium flavum, H. forrestii, and Wurfbainia villosa, were frequently cited for their aromatic rhizomes or fruits, underscoring the cultural preference for spicy and fragrant flavors in Yao food practices.

Several Ficus species, such as F. pandurata, F. bhotanica, F. formosana, and F. neriifolia, showed moderate CICF values (0.183–0.250) and were primarily used in meat- or rib-based soups to impart distinctive taste characteristics. In contrast, species with relatively lower CICF values, including Polygala fallax (0.423), Acorus gramineus (0.279), and Persicaria hydropiper (0.231), were cited less frequently and tended to be associated with more specific or occasional culinary applications.

WEPs used as food dyeing

Only three WEP species were recorded as being used for food dyeing by the Yao people in Lingyun County (Table 2; Fig. 4). All recorded species were associated with the coloring of glutinous rice, a staple food with strong cultural and ritual significance. The Cultural Importance Index for food dyeing (CIFD) ranged from 0.212 to 0.327, reflecting differences in both citation frequency and intensity of use among the species (Table S1).

Buddleja officinalis exhibited the highest CIFD value (0.548) and the greatest number of use citations (UR = 57), indicating its dominant role as a traditional dye plant. Its inflorescences were commonly employed to produce similar purple or red hues.

Dicliptera tinctoria ranked second, with a CIFD value of 0.529 and 55 citations, and it’s leaves and stems were widely used to impart purple or red coloration to glutinous rice.

In contrast, Liquidambar formosana showed a lower CIFD value (0.404) and fewer citations (UR = 42). Its leaves were specifically used to obtain black-colored glutinous rice, suggesting a more specialized but culturally well-recognized application.

WEPs used as fruit

A total of 66 WEP species were recorded as being used as fruits by the Yao people in Lingyun County, representing the most species-rich utilization category in the study (Table 2; Fig. 5). In most cases, fruits were consumed fresh when ripe, while a smaller number of species showed additional or specialized culinary roles. Cultural Importance Index values for fruit use (CIFT) ranged from 0.087 to 0.548, indicating substantial variation in citation frequency and cultural relevance among taxa (Table S1).

Phyllanthus emblica exhibited the highest CIFT value (0.548), highlighting its outstanding importance as a widely consumed and culturally valued fruit. Other highly important species included Eriobotrya japonica (0.500), Hovenia acerba (0.442), and Clausena lansium (0.423), all of which were frequently cited and commonly eaten when ripe. Although primarily categorized as a fruit, Rhus chinensis (CIFT = 0.356) and Rhus chinensis var. roxburghii (0.279) were notable for their distinctive use in adding a salty flavor to dishes, reflecting functional diversity beyond simple fruit consumption.

A large group of species showed moderately high CIFT values (0.308–0.346), including Saurauia tristyla, Rubus chroosepalus, Debregeasia orientalis, Saurauia thyrsiflora, Actinidia henryi, and Passiflora foetida. These fruits were widely available and regularly harvested, indicating their stable role in local diets. The genus Rubus was particularly prominent, with numerous species exhibiting CIFT values between 0.212 and 0.337, underscoring its importance as a major source of wild fruits.

Several species demonstrated multifunctional use patterns. For example, Choerospondias axillaris (0.269) was consumed fresh and also used in the production of distilled liquor, while Morus alba (0.183) provided both edible fruits and leaves used as vegetables. Species with lower CIFT values (≤ 0.163), such as Codonopsis javanica, Broussonetia papyrifera, Kadsura coccinea, and Pyrus calleryana, were cited less frequently and appeared to play more seasonal or supplementary roles.

WEPs used as herbal tea

A total of 15 WEP species were documented as being used for herbal tea preparation by the Yao people (Table 2; Fig. 6). These plants are mainly prepared as infusions or decoctions and are commonly consumed as daily beverages or for perceived health-promoting benefits. Cultural Importance Index values for herbal tea use (CIHT) ranged from 0.154 to 0.529, indicating marked differences in citation frequency and cultural prominence among species (Table S1).

Hypericum japonicum recorded the highest CIHT value (0.529), underscoring its dominant role in local herbal tea practices and its widespread recognition among informants. This was followed by Lonicera hypoglauca (0.413), which also showed strong cultural importance and frequent use. Camellia sinensis var. pubilimba (0.356) and Gynostemma pentaphyllum (0.337) were similarly prominent and were commonly prepared as regular beverages.

Several species exhibited moderately high CIHT values (0.298–0.308), including Lonicera macrantha, Camellia sinensis, and Mussaenda divaricata, reflecting their consistent but slightly less intensive use. Species such as Chrysanthemum indicum and Mussaenda pubescens (each 0.269) were also frequently cited, indicating their established place in everyday herbal tea consumption.

Plants with lower CIHT values, including Andrographis paniculata (0.240), Gardenia jasminoides (0.231), Nekemias grossedentata (0.231), Mussaenda kwangsiensis (0.212), Phyllanthus urinaria (0.163), and Lithocarpus litseifolius (0.154), were mentioned less often and appeared to be associated with more occasional or context-specific use.

WEPs used as staple food

A total of seven WEP species were documented as being used as staple foods by the Yao people (Table 2; Fig. 7). These species primarily provide substantial caloric intake and function as major or supplementary carbohydrate sources in daily diets. Cultural Importance Index values for staple food use (CISF) ranged from 0.080 to 0.510, reflecting clear differences in the level of reliance and frequency of use among taxa (Table S1).

Dioscorea polystachya showed the highest CISF value (0.510), highlighting its central role as a staple food. Its tubers are widely cooked and consumed, indicating strong cultural importance and frequent utilization. Carex baccans ranked second (CISF = 0.433), with its edible parts also commonly prepared and eaten as a staple, underscoring its significance as an alternative carbohydrate resource.

Moderate CISF values were recorded for Dioscorea glabra (0.317) and Ficus pumila (0.308). While D. glabra is consumed similarly to other yams as a cooked staple, F. pumila occupies a distinct role, as its seeds are processed into grass jelly, which serves as a filling food rather than a conventional staple. Dioscorea alata and D. pentaphylla (each CISF = 0.231) were also important, though cited less frequently, suggesting supplementary or seasonal use within household food systems.

The lowest CISF value was observed for Clausena emarginata (0.080), which was infrequently mentioned and appears to play only a minor role in staple food consumption, being more commonly associated with fruit use and jam preparation.

WEPs used as sweet, dessert, or snack

A total of six WEP species were recorded as being used as sweets, desserts, or snacks by the Yao people (Table 2; Fig. 8). This category mainly includes plants consumed outside regular meals and valued for their taste, texture, and seasonal availability rather than for providing staple nutrition. Cultural Importance Index values for this use category (CISW) ranged from 0.135 to 0.519, indicating varying degrees of cultural recognition and frequency of use (Table S1).

Castanea mollissima exhibited the highest CISW value (0.519), highlighting its prominent role as a widely consumed snack fruit. This was followed by Castanopsis chinensis (0.423), which is also commonly eaten as a snack and represents an important wild nut resource. Pseudognaphalium affine showed a relatively high CI_SW value (0.413); rather than being eaten directly, it is incorporated as an ingredient in the preparation of traditional glutinous rice cakes, reflecting its cultural importance in festive or specialty foods.

Species with lower CISW values included Gnetum montanum (0.212), whose fruits are consumed after roasting, and Carya cathayensis (0.154), which was mentioned less frequently as a snack. Rhododendron simsii recorded the lowest CISW value (0.135); its petals are occasionally eaten as a snack, suggesting a more limited, context-specific use.

WEPs used as vegetable

A total of 45 WEP species were documented as being used as vegetables by the Yao people, making this the most diverse utilization category in terms of species richness (Table 2; Fig. 9). These plants are mainly consumed as leafy vegetables, young shoots, stems, immature fruits, or inflorescences and are typically prepared by stir-frying or cooking in hot pot, with a few eaten fresh or as cold salads. Cultural Importance Index values for vegetable use (CIVG) ranged from 0.125 to 0.548, reflecting substantial variation in frequency of citation and intensity of use (Table S1).

Musa balbisiana recorded the highest CIVG value (0.548), underscoring its prominent role as a commonly consumed vegetable, particularly when stir-fried. This was followed by Oenanthe javanica (0.529) and Solanum americanum (0.500), both of which are widely used leafy vegetables and frequently prepared in hot pot or stir-fried dishes. Other highly valued species included Youngia japonica, Pteridium revolutum, and Portulaca oleracea (each 0.462), highlighting their importance in daily household meals.

A large group of species exhibited relatively high CIVG values (0.433–0.442), such as Houttuynia cordata, Centella asiatica, Emilia sonchifolia, Toona sinensis, Piper sarmentosum, Amaranthus retroflexus, and Sonchus wightianus. These taxa are versatile vegetables, regularly incorporated into various dishes, and represent core components of the local vegetable repertoire. Several species, including Amaranthus retroflexus, Amaranthus spinosus, and Amaranthus viridis, were notable for their multi-part use, with both aerial parts and roots consumed.

Species with moderate CIVG values (0.346–0.404), such as Dicliptera chinensis, Taraxacum mongolicum, Stellaria media, Bidens pilosa, and Capsella bursa-pastoris, were also commonly cited, reflecting their widespread availability and routine use. In contrast, taxa with lower CIVG values, including Oxalis corniculata, Imperata cylindrica, Aralia armata, Momordica subangulata, and Senna occidentalis (≤ 0.144), were mentioned less frequently and appear to play more seasonal or supplementary roles.

Comparative of WEPs from Lingyun and surrounding regions

A comparison of WEPs used by the Yao people in Lingyun County with those reported from surrounding regions reveals generally low to moderate floristic similarity (Table 3). The highest similarity was observed with the Zhuang ethnic group in Fangchenggang, Guangxi, where 53 species were shared, yielding a Jaccard Index (JI) of 0.193 and a similarity measure (SM) of 19.3%. A comparable level of overlap was found with the Baiku Yao communities in Guangxi and Guizhou (57 shared species; JI = 0.188; SM = 18.8%), suggesting a degree of shared ethnobotanical knowledge among geographically proximate and culturally related groups.

Lower similarity values were recorded when Lingyun was compared with more distant or ecologically distinct regions. For example, only 41 species were shared with the three trans-boundary ethnic groups in Jiangcheng, Yunnan (JI = 0.122; SM = 12.2%), while comparisons with the Dulong people in Yunnan and the Mulam people in Guangxi resulted in even lower similarity percentages (SM = 9.8% and 9.5%, respectively). The Yao people in Gongcheng County shared 39 species with Lingyun (SM = 9.0%), indicating notable intra-ethnic variation in WEP use across different localities.

The lowest similarity was observed with Shui communities in Guizhou, where only 15 species overlapped (JI = 0.059; SM = 5.9%). Overall, these results highlight that, despite some shared core species, WEP knowledge in Lingyun County is strongly shaped by local ecological conditions, resource availability, and culturally specific food traditions, resulting in a distinct ethnobotanical profile relative to surrounding regions.

Novel reports

Notably, this study represents the first comprehensive ethnobotanical documentation of WEPs in Lingyun County, as well as the first such study within Baise City. The dataset therefore provides a valuable baseline for understanding the diversity, utilization patterns, and cultural significance of WEPs in this previously under-documented region.

It is important to note that the present study focuses exclusively on edible plant resources, while medicinal plant uses were intentionally excluded due to the extensive volume of associated knowledge. These aspects will be addressed in a separate study to allow for more detailed analysis and interpretation.

Discussion

Contribution of WEP diversity to local food security

The high diversity of WEPs maintained by the Yao people in Lingyun County highlights the central role of wild plant resources in sustaining local food security. A broad taxonomic spectrum supports dietary diversity, buffers seasonal food shortages, and enhances nutritional resilience—functions that are particularly critical in mountainous landscapes where market integration remains limited [44]. Reliance on a wide array of locally available species reduces dependence on external food systems and strengthens household-level adaptive capacity [45].

The dominance of native species reflects a food system deeply embedded in local ecosystems and traditional ecological knowledge [2]. Such systems are inherently resilient, as they provide alternative food sources during periods of crop failure or economic stress [46]. Comparable reliance on indigenous WEPs has been documented among upland communities in northeastern Thailand [6], the Lao uplands of Laos [47], and montane regions of Vietnam [48], where wild foods function as safety-net resources during lean seasons and climatic uncertainty.

The predominance of fruits and leafy vegetables further underscores the nutritional importance of WEPs. These plant parts are rich in micronutrients and dietary fiber, contributing to balanced diets beyond caloric sufficiency. Their frequent incorporation into daily meals indicates that WEPs are not emergency foods but stable components of everyday food practices [3]. In this context, WEP diversity represents not only a buffer against food shortages but also a critical component of long-term food sovereignty, as it enables local communities to maintain control over their food sources, dietary choices, and traditional knowledge systems under conditions of increasing market integration and environmental change.

Cultural importance and functional redundancy in food systems

Variation in Cultural Importance Index (CI) values reflects differences in how deeply individual species are embedded in daily foodways. Highly valued taxa tend to be multifunctional, serving as vegetables, fruits, condiments, beverages, or supplementary staples. This multifunctionality creates functional redundancy within the food system, allowing households to substitute resources as availability fluctuates across seasons or years [49].

Wild yams and sedges illustrate this principle well, as they provide alternative carbohydrate sources that complement cultivated staples [50]. Similar strategies have been widely reported among upland communities in northeastern Thailand and India, where wild tubers reduce vulnerability to crop shortfalls [51, 52]. Beyond staple substitution, the cultural prominence of condiments and flavoring plants—particularly aromatic taxa—highlights that food security also encompasses taste, cultural satisfaction, and appetite stimulation [53]. Flavoring plants enhance palatability and encourage food intake, reinforcing their importance within subsistence-oriented food systems [54]. Beyond cultural preference, this dimension has important public health implications, as improved palatability and dietary diversity can contribute to better nutritional intake and help mitigate risks associated with simplified, market-dependent diets.

Regional comparison with other countries

The taxonomic composition and usage patterns observed in Lingyun County show both convergence and divergence when compared with ethnobotanical studies from southwestern China and mainland Southeast Asia. While families such as Rosaceae, Moraceae, Zingiberaceae, and Dioscoreaceae are recurrently dominant in China [55, 56]—reflecting shared floristic backgrounds and long-standing cross-border cultural exchange—this pattern contrasts with many studies from mainland Southeast Asia, where Fabaceae frequently emerges as the most prominent family. In regions such as northeastern Thailand [57], India [58], and Indonesia [59], Fabaceae species are widely used as vegetables, condiments, and food-medicine resources, highlighting differences in vegetation structure, subsistence strategies, and cultural preferences. These contrasts suggest that, despite a shared regional pool of WEPs, local ecology and historical land-use practices play a decisive role in shaping dominant taxonomic patterns of WEP utilization [60]. Such cross-regional differences also highlight the need for context-specific strategies in promoting sustainable diets and food system resilience, rather than applying uniform models of food security across diverse ecological and cultural settings.

At the same time, the overall species richness recorded in Lingyun County is high, indicating extensive use of WEPs within the local food system. This pattern points to strong retention of traditional knowledge among the Yao people, particularly regarding wild vegetables and herbal beverages. While similar food–medicine continua have been documented in Thailand and Vietnam, the persistence of certain culturally specific practices—such as the use of natural dye plants in glutinous rice preparation—indicates localized traditions that are increasingly rare elsewhere.

Localized knowledge and cross-regional similarity patterns

Low to moderate Jaccard similarity values between Lingyun and surrounding regions reveal that WEP knowledge is strongly place-based rather than uniform across ethnic boundaries. Higher similarity with geographically proximate communities reflects shared environments and long-term interaction, whereas lower overlap with ecologically distinct or distant regions underscores the decisive influence of habitat, altitude, and vegetation structure on WEP selection [61].

Notably, substantial differences were observed even among Yao communities in different locations, emphasizing that ethnobotanical knowledge is adaptive and locally negotiated [62, 63]. From a food security perspective, such localization enhances resilience by encouraging communities to prioritize reliable, locally abundant resources rather than relying on a standardized set of wild foods. This flexibility is especially advantageous under changing environmental and socio-economic conditions [64].

The pronounced local specificity of WEP systems also has important conservation implications. Loss of habitats or traditional practices in a single area may result in irreversible erosion of unique knowledge, even when the same ethnic group persists elsewhere [65]. Conservation strategies should therefore emphasize community-based, landscape-level approaches that recognize local distinctiveness rather than assuming regional homogeneity [66]. From a future-oriented perspective, preserving this localized knowledge is essential for maintaining adaptive capacity under climate change, as communities rely on site-specific species and practices that are finely tuned to local environmental conditions.

Implications for conservation and sustainable use

The continued reliance on native WEPs presents both conservation opportunities and challenges. Traditional harvesting of fruits, leaves, and shoots is generally compatible with in situ conservation and helps maintain heterogeneous landscapes such as forests, fallows, and secondary vegetation. These landscapes function as living reservoirs of food biodiversity [67].

However, species with high cultural importance may face increasing pressure if social change or market demand intensifies. Aromatic taxa and popular wild vegetables are particularly vulnerable under such scenarios [68]. Integrating ethnobotanical knowledge into local resource management—through community stewardship, seasonal harvesting norms, and small-scale domestication—offers a pathway toward sustainable use [69]. Such approaches are particularly relevant in the context of accelerating environmental change, where the integration of traditional knowledge with contemporary conservation strategies can support both biodiversity conservation and resilient local food systems.

Overall, the WEP system of the Yao people in Lingyun County represents a resilient, culturally embedded food strategy that supports dietary diversity, food security, and local identity. Safeguarding this knowledge is therefore essential not only for cultural preservation but also for sustaining agrobiodiversity and adaptive food systems in southern China and neighboring regions of mainland Southeast Asia. Importantly, this system also aligns with emerging global priorities related to sustainable food systems, food sovereignty, and preventative public health, particularly in rural and marginalized landscapes.

Limitation of the study

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The research was conducted within a single geographic area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions. In addition, the study focused exclusively on wild edible plants, while medicinal plant uses were not included due to their extensive scope and will be addressed separately. Furthermore, ethnobotanical data were based on informant knowledge and recall, which may introduce potential bias or variation in reported uses.

Another important limitation relates to the erosion of local ethnolinguistic knowledge. During fieldwork, it was observed that many younger members of the Yao people community were unable to identify plant names in the Yao language. In some cases, even elderly informants predominantly used Mandarin Chinese names rather than indigenous terminology. This shift suggests a substantial loss of traditional linguistic knowledge associated with plant use and may affect the accuracy and completeness of recorded vernacular names. It also reflects broader processes of cultural and linguistic change, which could lead to the gradual disappearance of biocultural knowledge embedded in local languages.

Conclusion

This study documents a rich and culturally embedded system of WEPs use among the Yao people in Lingyun County, Guangxi, demonstrating the continued importance of wild plant resources in sustaining local food systems. The broad taxonomic spectrum of WEPs, encompassing a wide range of growth forms and use categories, reflects a food strategy that is both biologically diverse and functionally resilient. Wild vegetables and fruits constitute the core of daily consumption, complemented by condiments, herbal teas, supplementary staples, and culturally specific food dyeing plants, together forming an integrated subsistence system rather than marginal or emergency food use.

The distribution of Cultural Importance Index (CI) values reveals a clear internal structure within the WEP repertoire. A limited number of multifunctional species with high CI values function as culturally defined core foods that are deeply embedded in everyday diets and culinary traditions, while a broader pool of moderately and low-ranked species provides seasonal flexibility and dietary diversity. This combination of cultural centrality and functional redundancy enhances food security by allowing households to adapt to seasonal variation, resource availability, and environmental uncertainty without relying solely on cultivated crops.

Comparative analysis with surrounding regions highlights the strongly localized nature of WEP knowledge in Lingyun County. Low to moderate floristic similarity with neighboring communities, including both culturally related and ecologically distinct groups, indicates that wild food systems are shaped primarily by local environments and subsistence practices rather than ethnic affiliation alone. Even within the Yao ethnic group, substantial intra-ethnic variation underscores the place-based and adaptive character of ethnobotanical knowledge. Such localization contributes to resilience at the community level but also implies that the loss of habitats or traditional practices in a single area may result in irreversible erosion of unique knowledge.

From a broader perspective, the findings emphasize that traditional WEP-based food systems contribute simultaneously to dietary diversity, cultural identity, and landscape-level biodiversity conservation. Continued reliance on native wild species, combined with largely non-destructive harvesting practices, supports the maintenance of heterogeneous agro-forest landscapes. However, species with high cultural importance may face increased pressure under changing socioeconomic conditions, highlighting the need for community-based management, sustainable harvesting, and the selective domestication of key taxa.

Overall, the WEP system of the Yao people in Lingyun County represents a dynamic and locally adapted food knowledge system that remains highly relevant to contemporary challenges of food security and biodiversity conservation. Safeguarding this knowledge is not only essential for preserving cultural heritage but also for informing sustainable food system strategies in southern China and comparable regions worldwide. In particular, the integration of WEPs into future food policies and local development strategies may contribute to enhancing nutritional security, strengthening food sovereignty, and supporting ecosystem-based adaptation in the face of ongoing environmental and socioeconomic change.
