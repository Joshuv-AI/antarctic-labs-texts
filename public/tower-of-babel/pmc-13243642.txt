Organochlorine pesticide residues monitoring and human health risk evaluation in agricultural crops from Ilam Province, Southwest of Iran
Authors: Ali Amarloei, Zahra Noorimotlagh, Neemat Jaafarzadeh, Seyyed Abbas Mirzaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe issue of food supply and security remains a major challenge and crisis for the world’s growing population. In this study, a comprehensive analysis of the residues of fourteen organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) was conducted and detected using gas chromatography with an electron capture detector (GC/ECD) in sixty-one samples of twelve crops produced in ten counties within Ilam Province, southwest Iran. The samples included lentils, chickpeas, wheat, tomatoes, corn, rice, cucumbers, watermelons, melons, cantaloupes, edible (raw) vegetables, and stewed vegetables. The levels of all detected OCP residues in the twelve crop types were below the maximum residue limits (MRLs) established by Iran and the Food and Agriculture Organization/World Health Organization (FAO/WHO) (50 µg/L). Lindane, Endrin, and Heptachlor were detected at the highest concentrations of OCPs across all crops, while Methoxychlor and Endosulfan were found at the lowest levels. No prior studies have focused specifically on detecting the presence of OCPs in certain products such as lentils and chickpeas, making this study unique in its findings. The target hazard quotient (THQ) and hazard index (HI) were used to estimate human health risk assessment (HRA) associated with OCP residues in the samples. The results indicated that the HI value was lower than 1 in both adults and children for all samples, suggesting no considerable risk for consumers. Overall, stringent control programs, integrated pest management, quality control, and periodic monitoring of crops before they are released to the market are essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43702-6.Keywords: Agricultural crops, Organochlorine pesticides, pesticides, Health risk assessmentSubject terms: Plant sciences, Environmental sciences, Health care

Introduction

The urbanization and population growth led to increment demand to food and agricultural crops and resulted to uncontrolled use of chemical fertilizers and pesticides to meet the increasing world food demand1,2. Today, food security and safety are a global concern that has become increasingly important for consumers of agricultural products. Food pollution in developing countries is 13% higher than in developed countries, with industrial factors and agricultural activities, such as the use of chemical fertilizers, animal manures, compost, and pesticides, being among the contributing factors2–4. Agriculture is a cornerstone of sustainable development and is vital for human survival, as it is the primary source of food and basic commodities. Pesticides are extensively applied in agricultural systems to manage pest populations and protect crop productivity. It is estimated that the use of pesticides preserves approximately 33% of global agricultural production by preventing yield losses and on the other hand, about 45% of global crop production is lost each year due to damage caused by insect pests and plant diseases, illustrating the ongoing challenges faced by agriculture in ensuring food security for a growing population4,5.

Over the past three decades, global pesticide use has risen by more than 1.5 times. It is reported that worldwide annual use of pesticide is about two million tonnes and it estimated that increased to 3.5 million tonnes by the year 2020. Asian countries account for the majority of this consumption, representing approximately 53% of worldwide pesticide use, followed by the Americas at 30% and Europe at 14%2,6. China is one of the world’s largest pesticide users and the major contributing country, with application rates rising from 0.76 × 109 kg in 1991 to 1.8 × 109 kg in 2011. Over 14 kg/ha are applied annually to cultivated land in China, far exceeding rates in the USA (2.2 kg/ha), France (2.9 kg/ha), and the Netherlands (8.8 kg/ha)7,8. Approximately 24.5 million square kilometers of agricultural land in the world (about 64%) is at the risk of different class of pesticide pollution because of more than one active chemical, and 31% of agricultural land is reported as at high risk9,10.

Several studies indicated that more than 80% of pesticide residues especially OCPs are chemical compounds with the highly hazard and serious risks to the human health such as poisoning, autism and behavioral disorders, diabetes, cancer, abortion, skin and neurological complications and cardiovascular diseases, and surrounded environment, due to the excessive use of them in the farm and agricultural products1,6,11–14. It is reported that annual use of pesticides that sprayed on the agricultural crops including vegetables and Fruits is 140,000 tons in the European Union (EU). Based on the EUs data in the Pesticide Action Network, some 350 various kinds of pesticides were identified in food produced in the EU and levels of pesticides in the agricultural crops were detected that higher than the EUs maximum permitted level (MPL) in the 5 percent of products. Vegetables and Fruits play an important role in the human diet due to being a suitable source of vitamins, antioxidants, fibers and minerals, the World Health Organization (WHO) has reported that they account for more than 30% of the diet consumed in Europe15.

Farmers all over the world, especially in modern agricultural systems, frequently use a wide range of chemical pesticides, including OCPs, to combat pests and plant pathogens. These pesticides, which are characterized by low biodegradability and prolonged persistence in the environment, can remain on the surface of agricultural products and even penetrate their tissue, including fruits, vegetables, and seeds. Cereals are also susceptible to contamination with these types of pesticides16–18.

Pesticides play a vital role in controlling crop pests and diseases, regulating plant growth, increasing productivity, and improving crop quality. Pesticides are a mixture of chemical compounds that is used to eliminate, prevent, control or reduce the damage caused by a pest in agriculture and households, and it can be a chemical substance (synthetic or natural), a biological agent (such as a virus or bacteria) or any kind of it is a means of destroying pests (insects, pathogenic plants, weeds and nematodes)6,9. The pesticides are capable to control the quality and the quantities of agricultural crops, products and food, and enable to limit the transmission of various human diseases via insect/rodent related vector. The application of different type of pesticide is essential in the modern agriculture from two aspects i.e. to increase yield of crops and to control of pest. However, despite of this positive role of them, pesticide is considered as the chemical compounds with high toxicity, mobile and environmentally stable compounds in the environment. Therefore, the inappropriate use and misuse of pesticides is one of the most important and serious threats to the stability of chemical pest control, the health of farmers and the contamination of the environment with toxic chemical compounds7,9,15,19,20.

Due to the different chemical structures, applications and mode of actions of the pesticides, they categorized in the various classification (more than 30 class) according to the following criteria including structure of chemical compound, stability in the environment, purpose of application; toxicity and mode of action against the target organism14,21. Generally, they can be divided into two different groups based on their chemical structure including organic and inorganic compounds. In this monitoring study, the organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) as organic compounds were evaluated.

Organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) are synthetic, highly persistent, lipophilic organic compounds that were introduced into the environment in the late 1940s. They were widely used in agriculture and public health programs due to their effectiveness in controlling pests and vector-borne diseases. However, their environmental persistence, bioaccumulation and biomagnify in food chains, and long-range transport led to widespread contamination of various environmental matrices, including soil, water, air, and biota10,12,13,22. OCPs are a diverse group of chemical compounds that due to their cost –effectiveness and highly efficient effects on the pest, they have been widely used in the few past decades. OCPs are one of the most important organic pesticides and form a large class of insecticides15,22. The use of OCPs for agricultural and public health purposes in the world has caused widespread environmental pollution. OCPs due to the high toxicity nature, considerable stability and transport in the environment (as long as 30 years), persistence in the environment and capability to transfer by water and air and over long distances, and bioaccumulation and bio-magnification in the food web are considered as the very important threats to human health and wildlife as well as the environment17,20,23. Several OCPs are classified by IARC and the U.S. EPA as possible or probable human carcinogens, including heptachlor and dieldrin (Group 2A) and lindane, Aldrin, Endrin, DDTs, and Endosulfan (Group 2B). Given that dietary exposure is chronic and cumulative, the carcinogenic potential of these compounds should be considered when interpreting health risks, even when non-cancer indicators remain below threshold levels24,25. Due to the above-mention characteristics of OCPs compounds such as dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDTs), lindane and Aldrin, the use of them was prohibited by considering the Stockholm Convention, which considered OCPs as one of the original 12 persistent organic pollutants (POPs)17,20,26. OCPs are still found in the environment and subsequently in the agricultural crops due to their environmental stability.

Different crop categories vary in their ability to absorb OCPs from soil and irrigation water, with leafy and root vegetables generally exhibiting higher accumulation potential. Considering these characteristics helps in selecting representative matrices for exposure assessment. Numerous studies have investigated the occurrence of OCPs in agricultural products across Iran, including assessments in soils, fruits, vegetables, and cereals. Previous reports have identified detectable OCP levels in regions such as Bushehr17, Hamadan19, and West Azerbaijan (the city of Urmia)27, with certain residues exceeding national or international permissible limits. Similar monitoring studies conducted in China10,16, India4, Pakistan28,29, and other Middle Eastern (Saudi Arabia) countries30 have documented persistence of DDTs, HCH isomers, Aldrin, Dieldrin, and Endosulfan in various crops. However, limited data exist regarding OCP contamination in agricultural products of Ilam Province except pervious study conducted by our team31, and No published studies were found evaluating OCP residues in lentils or chickpeas staple crops with high regional consumption either in Iran or globally. Furthermore, systematic dietary risk assessments focusing on OCP exposure in southwestern Iran remain scarce1,32.

Taking this in the mind, one of the most important and high priorities challenges for worldwide sustainable development is food safety/security and monitoring the agro-products and crops to detection of pesticides and evaluation of the health risk of them should be addressed. Given the widespread use, environmental persistence, and potential health impacts of OCPs, understanding their presence in agricultural products is essential for evaluating consumer exposure and ensuring food safety. The practical goal of this research is to provide reliable, region-specific baseline data on OCP residues in agricultural crops produced in Ilam Province. By evaluating 14 OCPs across 12 commonly consumed crops including lentils and chickpeas, for which no prior monitoring data exist, this study aims to support food safety authorities in establishing effective surveillance and regulatory programs. The findings are intended to guide policymakers, agricultural organizations, and farmers in improving pesticide management practices, strengthening integrated pest management (IPM), and reducing potential human exposure. Ultimately, this work contributes practical evidence needed for safer crop production, sustainable agriculture, and the protection of public health in the region.

Given these gaps in regional monitoring data, the present study aims to: (i) quantify the residual concentrations of fourteen commonly used OCPs in twelve major agricultural crops produced across ten counties of Ilam Province; (ii) compare detected residues with national maximum residue limits (MRLs); and (iii) assess the potential non-carcinogenic dietary exposure risk for adult and child consumers using established health risk assessment (HRS) model. This work provides the first comprehensive dataset on OCP contamination in several locally important crops, including lentils and chickpeas, and contributes essential evidence for regional food safety management and pesticide regulation.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

During the whole study, the water produced by Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA) was used throughout all experiments. The chemical and solvents used to instrumental analysis of the pesticides be HPLC grade and used as received including cyclohexane (> 99.8%), dichloromethane (99.9%), methanol (99.85%), acetone (99.5%), hexane (95%), sodium chloride (99.5%) and sodium sulfate (99.0–100%) (Sigma-Aldrich).

Study area description and agricultural crops sample collection

This study was conducted in Ilam province with a population of 580,158 people, which is located in the southwest of Iran. The area of province is 20,164.11 km2 and its elevation above sea level is varied between 136 to 1450 m. Ilam province consists of 10 counties including Ilam, Dehloran, Sirvan, Ivan, Badreh, Abdanan, Malekshahi, Mehran, Darehshahr, and Chardavol (Fig. 1). The average annual rainfall of the province is 446.8 mm and there are different climatic variations in summer (hot and dry) and winter (cold). With 340 ha of agricultural land, the province plays an important role in the production of agro-products and crops, so the main occupation of the people in this province is agriculture and husbandry. The agricultural crops and products are usually export to other regions.

Sample collection and preparation for analysis

In the present study, 61 samples of 12 types of agricultural products that have the highest production in Ilam counties were randomly collected from local farms and major production (Table 1). The agricultural products were then transferred to the laboratory and were kept in the refrigerator (4˚C) until extraction and further experiments. Table 1 shows the samples of agricultural crops produced in different counties of Ilam province (The proposed list of the Ilam Agricultural Organization). The selection of agricultural products was based primarily on their high consumption and production rates in Ilam Province, ensuring that the assessed crops reflect the main dietary exposure pathways for the local population. In addition, the potential of these crops to absorb and accumulate OCPs from soil and irrigation water was also considered. Leafy vegetables, root crops, and fruit vegetables are known to have higher uptake capacities for POPs, and therefore their inclusion supports a more realistic estimation of human exposure. The weight of the collected agricultural crops samples for experiment was 200–500 gr for cereals (grains), 500 gr for vegetables, 1 kg for summer vegetables (such as tomatoes) and one for watermelon, melon, and cantaloupe. A suitable amount (100 gr) of vegetables and herbs was chopped with an appropriate chopper (Fig. 2A). The mill was used to powder grains such as peas, lentils, etc. Then, 50 gr of crushed or powdered sample was introduced into a glass container and 105 ml of dichloromethane was added to the previous solution and homogenized with a magnetic stirrer for 2 min. After the above time, 80 gr of anhydrous sodium sulfate was added to the previous solution. The above mixture was kept at rest in the laboratory for 2 min. After that, according to Fig. 2B, in order to separate solid parts of the samples, such as vegetables, etc., from the liquid sample, the mixture was filtered using a filtration set (12-cm Buchner funnel). Then, the filtered sample was again passed through the sodium sulfate column containing the filter (at its bottom). The obtained solution was completely dried in oven at 35–40 °C and 5 ml of cyclohexane solution was added to the dried residue sample. Finally, the sample was prepared in a glass tube with a lid to be injected into the GC-ECD to measure the amount of pesticide33.

Analysis, extraction and detection of OCPs by GC-ECD

First, 90 ml of water was placed in a special glass container and 10 µL of internal standard PCB29 with a concentration of 10 ppm and some sodium chloride salts were added to it. After mixing, 1 ml of hexane was added to it again. Then it was mixed for 20 min on a magnetic stirrer at a suitable speed, so that the exchange between the two phases is done completely. In this case, the hexane phase appears as a conical droplet. After 20 min, while the glass container is still rotating, remove the lid and draw the hexane phase with a Pasteur pipette and pour it into the vial. The sample is ready to be injected into the GC-ECD. Analysis and detection of OCPs were carried out using a GC equipped an electron capture detection (ECD) on an HP 6890 Plus gas chromatograph with a Capillary, HP-5MS (Agilent Technologies, USA), (Length: 30m internal diameter, Diameter: 0.25mm, Film thickness: 0.25µm). Injection technique and temperature was splitless and 290ºC. The carrier gas was the helium gas with a flow rate of 1 mL/ min with the injection volume of 2µl. The injection temperature was set at 290 °C and the temperature program for oven was performed as follows: Increase the temperature to 110 °C for 5 min, then 5 °C/min to 280 °C for 10 min. A relative retention time for detection of OCPs was 0.005 min which was similar to detection time of the standard pesticide17,20,33. Method validation was performed following standard pesticide residue analytical guidelines. Calibration curves were prepared using standard solutions at 20, 50, 100, and 200 ppb, yielding excellent linearity (R2 > 0.99). Recovery studies were conducted by spiking blank crop matrices at three fortification levels (20, 50, and 100 µg/kg), representing low, medium, and high concentrations relative to the national MRL. Each spiking level was analyzed in triplicate (n = 3). Mean recoveries ranged between 70 and 120% with relative standard deviation (RSD) values below 20%, meeting the acceptance criteria for pesticide residue analysis. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were calculated based on signal-to-noise ratios of 3 and 10, respectively. The method validation was carried out with the parameters of limit of quantification (LOQ), the limit of detection (LOD), RSD and Recovery (%) which depicted in the Table 1S.

Health risk assessment (HRA)

The HRA process is associated to pollutants exposure assessment. In this regard, target hazard quotient (THQ) is one of the most important parameters of HRA regarding the ingestion of vegetables and fruits (agricultural crops). The acceptable daily intake (ADI) values for all OCPs were sourced from the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) peer-reviewed pesticide assessments and the Joint FAO/WHO Meeting on Pesticide Residues (JMPR) toxicological evaluations. The estimation of daily intake (EDI) followed the dietary exposure methodology recommended by JMPR, while the THQ and hazard index (HI) equations were adopted from the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) Risk Assessment Guidance25,34. Result and discussion

Although the detected OCP concentrations were generally low and mostly below the national MRLs, the representativeness of the dataset is limited. Several crops were represented by only one or two samples in certain counties, largely due to seasonal availability and difficulties accessing farms during the sampling period. As a result, the observed residue levels should be interpreted as preliminary indicators rather than comprehensive estimates of pesticide contamination across Ilam Province. In this study, the residual concentrations of fourteen OCPs were analyzed across 61 samples of 12 agricultural crops collected from 10 counties in Ilam Province. The findings and their comparison with existing literature are presented and discussed below.

In this study, a comprehensive analysis of fourteen OCPs residues was conducted using GC/MS techniques in 61 samples of 12 types of agricultural crops cultivated across various counties in Ilam province, located in the southwestern region of Iran. These products include lentils, chickpea, corn, wheat, rice, tomato, edible vegetable, stewed vegetable, melon, watermelon, cucumber and melon. The OCPs investigated in this study were alpha-lindane, lindane, delta-lindane, heptachlor, Aldrin, epoxy heptachlor, D.D.E, dieldrin, D.D.D, Endrin aldehyde, Endrin ketone, methoxychlor, Endrin, and Endosulfan. Notably, the detected residual concentrations of all OCPs in the all agro-products cultivated in the various counties of Ilam province were found to be below the permissible limit (MRLs) of 50 μg/kg as set by the Iran national standard. Tables 3S to 7S present the residual concentrations of OCPs detected in various agricultural crops produced in various counties of Ilam province, Iran.

Table 3S presents the residual concentration (μg/kg) of 14 OCPs in lentil and chickpea samples from various counties (Ilam, Dehloran, Mehran, Chardavol, Ivan, and Sirvan) of Ilam province. As shown in Table 3S, Endrin aldehyde, Endrin ketone, methoxychlor, and Endosulfan pesticides were not detected in Lentil and chickpea agro-products cultivated in the counties of the province. The highest concentrations of OCPs (μg/kg) in the lentil products were 0.179 μg/kg for Delta-lindane in Sirvan, followed by 0.166 μg/kg for Endrin in Sirvan, and 0.146 μg/kg for Heptachlor in Ilam. The highest concentration of OCPs (μg/kg) in the chickpea was 0.689 μg/kg for Endrin in Mehran city, followed by 0.567 μg/kg for Endrin in Chardavol and 0.197 μg/kg for Heptachlor in Mehran city. The concentrations of OCPs in 100% measured samples were below the national MRLs, indicating that lentil and chickpea products had low residual contamination of OCPs. Among the investigated OCPs, Endrin, Heptachlor, and Delta-lindane were the most important in terms of residual concentrations in this work. It appears that no prior studies have focused specifically on detecting the presence of OCPs in lentil and chickpea products, making this study unique in its findings. The absence of related research highlights the need for further investigation into the occurrence and potential health risks associated with pesticide residues in these crops.

In the related study, authors assess the risk of OCPs presence in rice straw and grains from Punjab Province, Pakistan. The concentrations of OCPs (ng/g) were measured in total of 40 samples (20 samples each of rice straw and grains)29. The findings indicated that the levels of OCPs (∑OCPs) in rice straw ranged from 3.63 to 39.40 ng/g, while in rice grains, the range was 2.72 to 49.89 ng/g. DDTs were found to be the most prevalent OCP isomers, followed by HCH and heptachlor29. This pattern of OCPs was different from the current study being discussed. The authors concluded that the total concentration of OCPs (∑OCPs) in rice straw samples exceeded the MRLs set by Australian and Japanese standards29. Another related study focused on determining the levels of OCPs in rice samples collected from Jiangsu Province, China. The OCPs analyzed included HCH and its isomers, DDT congeners, heptachlor, heptachlor epoxide, Aldrin, dieldrin, and Endrin10. The findings showed that the total concentrations of HCH (∑HCH) and DDT (∑DDT) in the rice samples ranged from 0 to 0.039 mg/kg and 0 to 0.053 mg/kg, respectively. Other OCPs, except for HCH and DDT, were not detected in the rice samples10. These results indicate that although some rice samples contained detectable levels of HCH and DDT, the concentrations were relatively low. The absence of the other five OCPs may suggest a decline in their usage or more effective control measures being implemented in the study area10. In a study conducted by28, the researchers aimed to evaluate the contamination levels of OCPs in cereal crops, specifically wheat and rice, collected from the Gujranwala division in Punjab Province, Pakistan. A total of 28 samples were analyzed. The results showed that the total concentrations of OCPs (∑OCPs) ranged between 0.55 and 15.2 ng/g for rice and wheat samples, respectively. Among the detected OCPs, DDTs were found to be the most prevalent28. In a similar study, the researchers analyzed the residual levels of organochlorine pesticides (OCPs) in wheat and rice samples cultivated across river Chenab, Pakistan. The results showed that the levels of OCPs detected in wheat ranged from 1.78 to 12.46 ng/g, while in rice, the range was 4.92 to 18.19 ng/g. Among the detected OCPs, DDD, DDE, and HCH were the most prevalent in both wheat and rice samples37. Therefore, continuous monitoring of OCP residue levels in food crops is crucial to protect consumer health and ensure food safety. Moreover, implementing sustainable agricultural practices and proper pesticide management strategies can help minimize the risk of OCP contamination in agricultural products.

Table 6S presents the residual concentrations (in µg/kg) of OCPs detected in edible and stewed vegetable samples collected from five counties of Ilam province: Ilam, Dehloran, Darehshahr, Mehran and Chardavol. The analyses revealed that six of the fourteen OCPs, namely D.D.D, D.D.E, Dieldrin, Endrin ketone, methoxychlor and Endosulfan, were not detected in the edible vegetable samples collected from the different counties of Ilam province. Similarly, stewed vegetable samples from all counties were found to be free of the OCPs methoxychlor, Endrin aldehyde, D.D.E, Dieldrin, and Endosulfan, as they were not detected during the analyses. Based on the examination conducted in the samples of edible vegetables, the highest residual concentrations of OCPs were detected for lindane (0.835 μg/kg) and heptachlor (0.815 μg/kg) in produce from Dehloran city. Additionally, Endrin pesticide residue in edible vegetables from Mehran city was detected at a concentration of 0.612 μg/kg. Table 5S illustrates that the residual concentrations of lindane, Endrin, and heptachlor pesticides in edible vegetable samples from the other counties ranged from 0.2 to 0.4 μg/kg, while the remaining OCPs were detected at concentrations of 0.0 to 0.2 μg/kg. Other pesticides were also detected at various concentrations. The findings of this study align with the results of16 which investigated pesticide residues in vegetables in Jilin Province and reported detectable OCP residues that were within the permissible standard limit. They concluded that Leafy vegetables were found to contain the highest concentration of pesticides, possibly due to their larger surface area for pesticide absorption16. In a study investigating pesticide residues in vegetables from Haryana, India, researchers found that about one-third of the samples had detectable pesticide residues. Alarmingly, banned or withdrawn pesticides, including DDT and Endosulfan, were also detected, indicating a need for stricter enforcement of agricultural regulations. Of the analyzed samples, around 40% had pesticide residues within the MRLs, while 16% exceeded the established MRLs, emphasizing the importance of ongoing monitoring to ensure food safety and consumer well-being4. The elevated pesticide concentrations detected in vegetables compared to other food products can be attributed to several factors. Vegetables are often directly exposed to pesticides during cultivation, including foliar sprays, which can result in higher residue accumulation on edible parts15,38. Additionally, vegetables generally have a higher surface area-to-mass ratio than fruits or grains, which may enhance pesticide retention3,16. The frequency and timing of pesticide applications, crop type, and local agricultural practices in the region may also contribute to the observed higher residue levels2,7. These findings are consistent with previous studies that report higher pesticide residues in leafy and surface-exposed vegetables due to their direct exposure and growth characteristics4.

Several studies on the occurrence of OCPs residues in African environment have revealed that certain OCPs tend to persist in various environmental matrices (freshwater systems, sediments, soil, vegetables and various food substrates). α-HCH, β-HCH, DDTs, and Endosulfan have been identified as the most persistent OCP residues in Africa18. In a study conducted in the agricultural lands of Dalaki and Shabankare areas in Bushehr province, southern Iran, researchers analyzed topsoil samples for the presence of various OCPs, specifically HCH, heptachlor, DDT and CHL. The results showed that the highest to lowest concentration of pesticides in both areas followed the trend of DDT > CHL > HCH17. In another study was conducted to measure the concentration levels of OCPs residues in soil samples collected from a lowland paddy field in Kelantan, Malaysia. Out of ten OCPs analyzed, six were detected in the soil samples, including hexachlorocyclohexane isomers (α, β, γ, δ), 4,4-dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (4,4-DDT), and Endosulfan sulfate. The concentration levels of these detected OCPs were quantified in µg/kg. The results showed varying concentrations of the detected OCP residues in the soil samples. Our findings are consistent with recent studies from Iran and other countries indicating that, although OCP residues are generally below regulatory limits, their environmental persistence continues to result in detectable levels in fruits, vegetables, and cereals. Given the findings of this studies and the fact that these OCPs have been banned, their continued presence in topsoil, albeit in trace concentrations, demonstrates the need for further investigation of their potential accumulation in agricultural products. This is essential to ensure food safety, protect human health, and maintain the overall quality of the environment. This information can help guide policies and practices aimed at promoting sustainable agriculture and ensuring food safety in Iran.

Human health risk evaluation

Human health risk assessment was conducted using the maximum detected concentrations of OCP residues measured in the various agricultural crops (lentil, chickpea, corn, wheat, rice, tomato, edible vegetables, stewed vegetables, cantaloupe, watermelon, cucumber, and melon) collected from different counties. Based on these concentrations, the EDI values were calculated, and the corresponding THQ values for both child and adult consumers are presented in Table 3. As shown in Table 3, variations in OCP concentrations among crop types resulted in THQ values lower than 1 for all evaluated crops in both population groups. The individual HQ values for all agricultural products included in the study are summarized in Table 3. Furthermore, the sum of all HQ values, calculated as the HI, remained below the critical threshold of 1 for both adults and children, indicating no significant non-carcinogenic health risk, as also illustrated in Fig. 3. The HI values for adult consumers were calculated for each crop and were as follows: lentil (7.33E−04), chickpea (1.35E−03), corn (6.50E−05), wheat (2.85E−02), rice (1.69E−02), tomato (7.21E−03), edible vegetables (3.06E−03), stewed vegetables (1.98E−03), cantaloupe (1.27E−03), watermelon (3.59E−03), cucumber (1.82E−03), and melon (1.19E−03). Similarly, the HI values for child consumers were 1.03E−03, 1.89E−03, 7.45E−05, 3.99E−02, 2.37E−02, 1.01E−02, 4.75E−03, 2.86E−03, 1.78E−03, 5.03E−03, 2.55E−03, and 1.75E−03, respectively. In a similar study, researchers estimated the daily intake (EDI) of total OCPs (∑OCPs) through rice and wheat consumption, reporting 39 ng/kg/day for rice and 40 ng/kg/day for wheat. Using the 95th percentile concentrations of OCPs, they calculated the corresponding health risks (HRs), which are used to evaluate the potential adverse effects associated with chronic exposure to these pesticide residues. Their findings showed that the HRs for most of the OCPs detected in rice and wheat exceeded the acceptable safety threshold. This suggests a potentially severe health risk for the population in that study area due to consumption of contaminated cereal crops28. Consistent with the findings of the present study41, reported that the median HI values for DDTs and HCHs among child, adolescent, adult, and senior male and female consumers in Nanjing were all below 1, indicating no significant non-carcinogenic health risk from exposure to these pesticides. In their HRA, the researchers also found no meaningful non-cancer risk for local residents; however, the estimated cancer risk ranged from 1.0E−06 to 1.0E−04. Although this range exceeded the acceptable cancer risk threshold, it remained below the priority risk level, suggesting the need for continued monitoring and risk management interventions41. In a similar study, the authors evaluated the health risks associated with OCPs detected in wheat and rice samples. Their findings showed that both HQ and HI values exceeded 1 for several OCPs, indicating a potential non-carcinogenic risk from exposure to these pesticides through consumption of contaminated wheat and rice. The researchers further estimated cancer risk using the USEPA’s acceptable carcinogenic risk range, in which a tolerable risk level is defined as less than 10⁻⁶ (one in a million). However, in their assessment, the cancer risk values calculated for DDD, DDE, HCH, CT, and HC were higher than this acceptable threshold. These results suggest a potential carcinogenic risk to consumers of the affected cereal crops. Based on these findings, the study emphasized the urgent need to establish comprehensive assessment, monitoring, and reporting programs to reduce pesticide contamination and human exposure in Pakistan37. The findings of this study indicate that pesticide contamination does not pose a major human health concern, as all detected residues were below the EU MRLs. It is also important to note that the samples were collected directly from farms, where residue levels are typically lower than those found in samples taken from packing houses due to potential degradation of pesticides during the interval between harvesting and distribution. Nevertheless, continuous monitoring remains essential to ensure ongoing food safety for consumers. In addition, post-harvest activities such as storage, washing, peeling, and cooking can substantially reduce pesticide residue levels in agricultural products, as previously demonstrated by Kaushik, 200938. Although the calculated HI values for all age groups (adult and children) were below 1 for all crops, indicating no significant non-cancer risk, it is important to note that several detected OCPs (including lindane, heptachlor, Aldrin, Endrin, and DDT derivatives) are classified as possible or probable carcinogens. For example, heptachlor and dieldrin (detected in several samples) are classified as Group 2A (probably carcinogenic), while Lindane, Aldrin, Endrin, and DDT derivatives fall under Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic)25. Therefore, long-term exposure, even at low concentrations, may carry carcinogenic relevance, especially for children and populations with high consumption of local agricultural crops. This emphasizes the need for continuous monitoring and efforts to minimize OCP contamination in food crops.

Limitation section

Despite its strengths, this study is not without limitations. A major limitation of this study is the restricted sample size for several crop categories, which reduces the overall representativeness of the dataset and limits the statistical confidence of the dietary exposure estimates. The sampling was limited to a single province and one harvesting season, which may not capture seasonal or geographical variations in pesticide use and residue accumulation. Although 61 samples from 12 agricultural crops were collected across 10 counties in Ilam Province, the number of samples for certain crops (such as cucumber, cantaloupe, watermelon, and melon) was limited to a single sample per county due to seasonal availability, production variability, and logistical constraints. In some cases, only one sample was obtained from specific counties, further limiting spatial coverage. Consequently, although the results offer important preliminary insights into OCP contamination patterns, they should be interpreted with caution, as the findings cannot be fully generalized to the broader regional production system. The study focused only on OCPs, while other classes of pesticides (such as organophosphates, carbamates, and neonicotinoids) were not analyzed, although they may also pose potential risks.

Future monitoring programs should therefore incorporate substantially larger and more representative sample sizes for each crop, expand sampling across additional geographic areas, and include multiple sampling periods throughout the growing season to enhance both representativeness and reliability. While the analytical method used in this study was rigorously validated through calibration, recovery, and precision assessments, future investigations would benefit from inter-laboratory validation to further strengthen methodological robustness and ensure consistency across different analytical settings. The assessment was limited to non-carcinogenic health risks; future studies should also consider carcinogenic and cumulative risks from multi-pesticide exposures.

Conclusion and suggestions

This study presents a comprehensive investigation on the residual concentrations of fourteen OCPs in twelve agricultural crops (lentils, chickpea, corn, wheat, rice, tomato, edible vegetable, stewed vegetable, melon, watermelon, cucumber and melon) produced in various counties of Ilam province, Iran. The practical goal of this research is to provide reliable baseline data on OCP residues in locally grown agricultural products to support food safety monitoring, inform regulatory decision-making, and promote safer pesticide management practices in the region. This work offers new insights into the current status of pesticide exposure in southwestern Iran. Notably, the detected residues of all OCPs in the examined crops were below the MRL of 50 μg/kg established by the Iranian national standard and FAO/WHO guidelines. This study is the first to report OCP residues in lentil and chickpea crops in Iran, thereby filling an important gap in the literature and offering essential baseline data for future food safety surveillance programs. The lack of previous research highlights the need for continued investigation into the presence and potential health risks of pesticide residues in these widely consumed crops. The results of the HRA showed that the HI values for both adult and child consumers were below 1 for all crop types, indicating that exposure to OCP residues does not pose a significant non-carcinogenic health risk. Although the HRA suggests that consumers are not exposed to high-risk levels, the detection of OCPs in agricultural crops underscores the importance of strict monitoring and appropriate management of pesticide application practices to prevent potential health hazards. While minor variations in OCP concentrations were observed among counties, these differences were not meaningful at the population level and were consistent with the uniformly low HI values (HI < 1). Despite the negligible non-carcinogenic risks, the presence of OCPs classified as possible or probable carcinogens highlights the necessity for continued monitoring and the implementation of effective control strategies to reduce potential long-term cancer risks. Although the current findings indicate that the measured OCP levels do not pose an immediate dietary health risk for adult or child consumers, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size and the uneven distribution of crop samples across the region. The results, showing that all HQ and HI values were below 1, further underscore the relatively low risk associated with the consumption of these crops and provide important baseline information for policymakers, regulatory agencies, and agricultural stakeholders. To ensure a more robust and regionally representative assessment of dietary exposure, future studies should adopt broader and more systematic sampling frameworks. Consequently, prioritizing continuous monitoring of pesticide residues in food products and evaluating their potential health impacts is essential to ensure effective consumer protection against the harmful effects of pesticide contamination.
