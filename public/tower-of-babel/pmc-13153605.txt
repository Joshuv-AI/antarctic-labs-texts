Cassava oral immunotherapy in cassava-latex allergy: A pilot study
Authors: Roberta Almeida Castro Araújo, Claudia Leiko Yonekura Anagusko, Paula Lazaretti Morato Castro, Anne Karoline Rocha Medrado Ventura, Fábio Fernandes Morato Castro, Jorge Elias Kalil Filho, Keity Souza Santos, Ariana Campos Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCassava allergy is rare; however, because of the cross-reactivity of cassava with latex, it is clinically significant, affecting patient safety and quality of life. Oral immunotherapy (OIT) has been studied for use in patients with allergy to foods such as milk, egg, and peanut, but no studies have evaluated its use in patients with allergy to cassava.ObjectiveOur aim was to conduct a pilot study evaluating the feasibility, safety, and immunologic effects of a cassava OIT protocol in patients with IgE-mediated cassava and latex allergy.MethodsWe conducted a prospective longitudinal study involving 5 patients. The OIT protocol included a hospital-based induction phase (weekly escalation until tolerance to 100 g of cooked cassava had been achieved) and a 6-month home-based maintenance phase. Skin prick test results and specific IgE/IgG4 levels in response to latex and its components were evaluated at baseline and after maintenance, as were the results of latex use tests after maintenance.ResultsAll 5 patients reached the 100-g target in induction (a 100% success rate). After 6 months, 3 patients (60%) maintained full desensitization, with the remaining 2 experiencing a reduction of tolerance to 10 g. Adverse reactions were mostly mild (oropharyngeal pruritus and cough) and managed with antihistamines; no epinephrine use was required. Skin prick test wheal sizes in response to cassava decreased after OIT. No significant changes in IgE and IgG4 levels in response to latex occurred. The results of latex use testing were negative in 3 of 4 patients tested.ConclusionThis pilot study suggests that cassava OIT is a feasible and safe strategy for inducing desensitization in patients with casava allergy. We observed a suggestive signal of improved clinical tolerance to latex in some patients following therapy; however, the lack of a baseline latex challenge precludes definitive conclusions regarding cross-desensitization. These preliminary findings support the need for larger, controlled trials to confirm efficacy and further investigate this potential cross-reactive benefit.

Introduction

Food allergy is a growing global health concern that affects quality of life and safety, requiring strict avoidance of trigger foods.1 Although a limited number of foods account for most severe reactions worldwide, regionally important staples may represent overlooked clinical risks. Cassava (Manihot esculenta [Man e]), a primary dietary source of carbohydrates for hundreds of millions of people globally (especially in tropical and subtropical regions of Africa, Asia, and Latin America) is one such food.2 Its widespread use in both the food and pharmaceutical industries makes strict avoidance challenging, underscoring the need for active therapeutic strategies such as desensitization.3

Cassava allergy is further complicated by cross-reactivity with natural rubber latex, known as the cassava-latex syndrome, which is analogous to latex-fruit syndrome.4,5 This phenomenon results from homologous panallergens—class I chitinases (Hevea brasiliensis [Hev b] 6.02 and Hev b 11) and a patatin-like protein (Hev b 5)—that cross-react with cassava proteins, including the identified allergen Man e 5.6,7 Consequently, patients face a dual risk: dietary reactions to cassava and latex-induced anaphylaxis during medical procedures.8

Current management remains limited to avoidance and emergency epinephrine use, which is a strategy fraught with difficulty, as cassava is a ubiquitous food and latex is a material still encountered in health care settings.9 A compelling and translational question is whether oral immunotherapy (OIT) for use with a cross-reactive food allergen can modulate clinical reactivity to the nonfood allergen. Evidence from other cross-reactive syndromes, such as pollen-food allergy, suggests that continuous exposure to one allergen may reduce symptoms from another, even without dramatic serologic changes.10,11

Despite the clinical relevance of this syndrome, the prevalence of cassava allergy remains unknown, and no active interventions have been studied. To address this gap, we conducted a prospective pilot study evaluating a novel cassava OIT protocol in patients with dual IgE-mediated allergy to cassava and latex. Our primary aims were to assess the safety and efficacy of cassava desensitization and monitor subsequent changes in immunologic parameters and clinical reactivity to latex via a standardized use test.

We conducted the first pilot study to evaluate the feasibility and safety of cassava OIT and explore its potential effects on latex reactivity in patients with cassava-latex syndrome.

We conducted a prospective open-label pilot study at the Allergy and Clinical Immunology Unit of the Hospital das Clínicas, University of São Paulo School of Medicine. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (approval no. 3.281.927) and registered (Certificado de Apresentação para Apreciação Ética [CAAE] no. 10420619.6.0000.0068). All participants provided written informed consent.

A total of 5 adult female patients with a confirmed history of immediate hypersensitivity to cassava (Man e) and clinically diagnosed latex allergy were enrolled. The mean age of the participants was 49.6 years (range 26-68 years), with a median of 52 years. For patients P1 and P2, IgE-mediated cassava allergy was confirmed by a positive oral food challenge conducted before enrollment. For the remaining 3 patients (patients P3, P4, and P5), the diagnosis of cassava allergy was rigorously established on the basis of a well-documented clinical history of consistent and reproducible immediate hypersensitivity reactions following cassava ingestion or contact (Table I). Specifically, formal oral food challenges were not performed in these 3 patients on account of the recency and severity of their reactions (occurring within 12 months before enrollment), thus rendering further provocation ethically unwarranted. A protein-rich cassava extract was prepared in-house from raw roots. Briefly, peeled cassava was lyophilized, ground into a powder, and heated to degrade cyanogenic compounds. The powder was suspended in filtered water at a 1:3 (g/mL) ratio. The solution was filtered, and the total protein concentration of the extract was quantified using the Bradford method and standardized to 1 mg/mL for use in all procedures.

SPTs were performed using this extract in reverse 10-fold dilutions (from 1 × 10-6 mg/mL to 1 mg/mL). A wheal diameter of 3 mm or more after subtraction of the negative control (saline) was considered positive. The starting dose for OIT was individualized for each patient as 0.1 mL of a concentration 1000-fold more diluted than the last positive SPT dilution.

An OIT protocol with weekly supervised dose escalation was implemented until a target maintenance dose of 100 g of cooked cassava (equivalent to approximately 1.3 g of protein) was reached. This protocol was developed through a careful adaptation of well-established OIT protocols for use with other food allergens associated with a high prevalence and risk of anaphylaxis, such as milk and wheat.12 This was followed by a 6-month home maintenance phase (Fig 1).Fig 1OIT protocol for cassava allergy. The primary efficacy end point was the proportion of patients achieving and maintaining the target dose at 6 months. Safety was assessed by monitoring adverse events. Immunologic outcomes included changes in SPT reactivity to cassava and serum levels of specific IgE and specific IgG4 to latex extract (k82) and its components (recombinant Hev b 1, 3, 5, 6.02, 8, and 11), as measured by ImmunoCAP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Uppsala, Sweden). Clinical reactivity to latex was assessed after 6 months by using a standardized glove use test. Because of the pilot nature of the study, the data are presented descriptively.

Results and discussion

The study population comprised 5 female patients with confirmed cassava and latex allergy. The participants’ mean age was 49.6 years (range 26-68 years), with a median of 52 years. Regarding atopic comorbidities, all 5 participants had allergic rhinitis; 1 patient (P1) also had asthma (Table I).

The median duration of OIT induction was 11 weeks (range 9-34 weeks) over a mean of 15 dose escalation sessions (range 10-26). All 5 patients (100%) reached the 100-g target dose. At the 6-month follow-up, 3 patients (60%) maintained full desensitization (100 g daily), whereas the remaining 2 (40%) maintained partial desensitization at a reduced dose of 10 g daily (Table I). Patient P1 discontinued cassava consumption during the maintenance phase. This interruption was due not to symptoms but rather to a mistaken perception that she had been "cured" and no longer needed daily consumption. After attempting to consume cassava again following the interruption, she developed lip and eyelid angioedema along with oropharyngeal pruritus. Her symptoms resolved after antihistamine use and no need for epinephrine. The patient continued cassava avoidance until an open food challenge with 10 g of cassava, which yielded a negative result. The patient was then advised to maintain daily intake of 10 g of cassava to preserve partial desensitization. Patient P4 developed abdominal pain, bloating, and nausea after ingesting cassava flour and starch, later extending to ingestion of cooked cassava and therefore leading to discontinuation of daily cassava intake. After stopping cassava consumption, the patient experienced eyelid angioedema, tearing, and dyspnea following contact with raw cassava peel. An open food challenge with 10 g of cooked cassava was subsequently performed, yielding a negative result. The patient was advised to resume daily intake of 10 g of cassava.

For patients P2, P3, and P5, in addition to consumption of the 100 g of cooked cassava, the consumption of cassava-derived foods (such as tapioca, cheese bread, and polvilho biscuits) was allowed without quantity restriction, as these products contain minimal amounts of cassava protein, thus not representing a significant increase in total cassava protein intake beyond the maintenance dose.

The safety profile was favorable: there were 25 mild adverse events (after 0.66% of 3764 administered doses), primarily oropharyngeal pruritus and cough. All were managed with oral antihistamines; no epinephrine was required. Although some symptoms may have involved multiple body systems, they were predominantly mild and resolved with antihistamine treatment, without progressing to meet the criteria for anaphylaxis necessitating epinephrine.

A marked and consistent decrease in SPT reactivity to cassava was observed for all patients except 1 (Table III). Analysis of latex-specific serologic markers revealed high interindividual variability. For the major allergen Hev b 5, paired data were available for all 5 patients. One patient (patient P1) exhibited a pronounced decrease in both Hev b 5–specific IgE (from 67.3 to 2.50 kUA/L) and total latex IgE level (from 76.60 to 1.73 kUA/L). The other 4 patients had baseline values that were either low/undetectable or high, and these levels for both Hev b 5–specific and total latex IgE level remained largely stable after OIT (Table II). At the study end point, 4 of the 5 patients underwent a latex glove use test (Table I). Of those 4 patients, 3 (75%) tolerated the procedure without any reaction, whereas 1 (patient P2) developed mild localized urticaria on abraded skin. The fifth patient (patient P1) did not undergo the test because she had not maintained continuous daily cassava consumption for 6 months, as required by the study protocol for this specific test.

This pilot study demonstrates that cassava OIT is a feasible, safe, and promising strategy for inducing clinical desensitization, as evidenced by the absence of severe reactions and the low per-dose reaction rate. Immunologic findings were heterogeneous, characterized by a pronounced reduction in Hev b 5–specific IgE in 1 patient but no consistent group-wide pattern in serologic markers. This dissociation between successful clinical desensitization and the variable serologic changes underscores the importance of cellular and functional mechanisms, such as mast cell desensitization, particularly in the early phase of OIT.13, 14, 15

A key finding is the tolerance to latex, which was observed in 3 of 4 patients who underwent the glove use test. This clinical outcome is biologically plausible given the reported molecular cross-reactivity between cassava and latex allergens,2,6 and it mirrors observations seen in pollen-food allergy syndrome.10,16 However, a major limitation of this analysis is the absence of a baseline latex use test before OIT. Consequently, we cannot definitively attribute the observed tolerance to the intervention itself, as we lack a direct within-patient, pre-OIT versus post-OIT comparison. Although patient-reported historical reactions and confirmed latex sensitization support the likelihood of baseline reactivity, this finding must be considered as preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

Furthermore, the clinical relevance of the achieved desensitization is underscored by our patients' pre-OIT histories, which indicated that reaction-provoking doses typically ranged from 10 to 20 g of cooked cassava. This context highlights the fact that maintaining even partial desensitization at 10 g daily can protect against these previously reactive doses and most common accidental exposures (eg, cross-contamination), whereas full desensitization to 100 g provides a substantial safety margin for larger unintentional ingestions.

Other study limitations include the extremely small sample size (N = 5), open-label design, and absence of a control group. This limited sample size, although acknowledged, is inherent to a pilot study investigating a rare condition with a novel intervention. The results, however, provide crucial preliminary data on feasibility, safety, and initial efficacy signals, serving as an indispensable foundation for the design and justification of larger, controlled trials.

For patients with cassava-latex syndrome, avoidance of cassava can be culturally and nutritionally burdensome, whereas latex avoidance is often impractical, especially for health care workers.8 Cassava OIT is a promising and safe treatment that mitigates the dietary risk. Our observation of concomitant latex tolerance in most patients suggests a potential dual benefit, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for this complex syndrome.
