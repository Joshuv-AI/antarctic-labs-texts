Monodromy Equivalence for Lamé-type Equations I: Finite-gap Structures and Cone Spherical Metrics.
Ting-Jung Kuo, Xuanpu Liang, Ping-Hsiang Wu

Abstract
. Motivated by the finite-gap structure of the classical Lamé equation ( 1.2 ) and its central role in mathematical physics, generalized Lamé-type equations ( 1.12 ) are investigated. For the fundamental case n = 1 n=1 , a monodromy equivalence between the classical Lamé equation ( 1.18 ) and the generalized Lamé-type equation ( 1.19 ) is established. Two main applications are obtained: (i) the finite-gap structure of ( 1.19 ) is derived, together with a complete classification of the spectral curves σ 1 \sigma_{1} and σ 2 \sigma_{2} for τ ∈ i ​ ℝ > 0 \tau\in i\mathbb{R}_{>0} ; and (ii) the monodromy equivalence is applied to the construction of cone spherical metrics with three large conical singularities, each with cone angle exceeding 2 ​ π 2\pi . A family of such metrics is shown to exhibits a blow-up configuration, which is described explicitly in terms of the monodromy data.

2.1.
Finite-Gap Theory

Let
T\in\mathbb{C}
. Consider a nonconstant elliptic function
h(z;T)
in the
variable
z
with periods
1
and
\tau
, subject to the following two assumptions:

(1)

For fixed
z
,
h(z;\cdot)
is meromorphic on
\mathbb{C}
;

(2)

The second-order differential equation

(2.1)

y^{\prime\prime}(z)=h(z;T)y(z)

is assumed to be an apparent Fuchsian equation, that is, all of its
singularities are regular and the local solutions are single-valued (i.e.,
free of logarithmic terms).

Fix
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
h(x+z_{0};T)
is smooth on both

\mathbb{R}
and
\tau\cdot\mathbb{R}
. Consider the following Hill’s
equation

(2.2)

y^{\prime\prime}(x)=h(x+z_{0};T)y(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Definition 2.1
.

Equation (
2.2
) is said to be

conditionally stable
if it admits a nontrivial bounded solution on

\mathbb{R}
(or
\tau\cdot\mathbb{R}
). The associated
spectral sets

are defined as

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{T\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\text{Equation }\eqref{hill equ}\text{
admits a nontrivial bounded solution on }\mathbb{R}.\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{T\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\text{Equation }\eqref{hill equ}\text{
admits a nontrivial bounded solution on }\tau\cdot\mathbb{R}.\},

that is, the sets of parameters
T
for which (
2.2
) is
conditionally stable on the respective domains.

Let
M_{j}(T)
denote the monodromy matrix of equation (
2.1
)
with respect to the shift
z\rightarrow z+\omega_{j}
. The
Hill
discriminant
of (
2.1
) along the
\omega_{j}
direction is
defined by

(2.3)

\Delta_{j}(T):=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\,M_{j}(T),\quad T\in\mathbb{C},\quad j=1,2.

Note that
\Delta_{j}(T)
is independent of the choice of fundamental system
of solutions. By the classical Floquet theory,
\sigma_{j}
can be
characterized as

(2.4)

\sigma_{j}=\{T\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,-1\leqslant\Delta_{j}(T)\leqslant 1\},\,j=1,2.

Definition 2.2
.

A point
T_{0}\in\sigma_{j}
is called an
endpoint if an odd number of semiarcs of
\sigma_{j}
meet at
T_{0}
.

For any
T\in\mathbb{C}
, set

(2.5)

d_{j}(T):=\mathrm{ord}_{T}\left(\Delta_{j}^{2}(\cdot)-1\right).

The following lemma shows that
d_{j}(T)
completely characterizes the finite
endpoints of
\sigma_{j}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

T_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
is an endpoint of
\sigma_{j}

if and only if
d_{j}(T_{0})
is odd.

Proof.

Suppose
T_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
is an endpoint of
\sigma_{j}
. Without the loss
of generality, assume
\Delta_{j}(T_{0})=1
. Then locally,

\Delta_{j}(T)-1=l(T-T_{0})^{k}(1+O(|T-T_{0}|)),\quad l\neq 0,\ k\text{ is an
odd integer}.

Hence by (
2.5
), we obtain

d_{j}(T)=k,

which is odd.

Conversely, assume
d_{j}(T_{0})=k
is odd. Then
\Delta_{j}(T_{0})=\pm 1,
and
near
T_{0}
,

(2.6)

\Delta_{j}^{2}(T)-1=l(T-T_{0})^{k}(1+O(T-T_{0})),\quad l\neq 0.

On the other hand,

\Delta_{j}(T)\mp 1=l^{\prime}(T-T_{0})^{k^{\prime}}(1+O(T-T_{0})),\quad l^{\prime}\neq 0,\ k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N},

which implies

(2.7)

\Delta_{j}^{2}(T)-1=2l^{\prime}(T-T_{0})^{k^{\prime}}(1+O(T-T_{0})).

Comparing (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), we conclude that
k^{\prime}=k

and
l^{\prime}=\frac{l}{2}
. Thus, exactly
k
semiarcs of
\sigma_{j}
meet
at
T_{0}
and hence
T_{0}
is an endpoint of
\sigma_{j}
.
∎

Lemma 4.1

The generalized Lamé-type equation

Proof.

The local exponents of (

and

~-\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{3}{2}&\text{(at $z=p$)};\\
~-\frac{1}{2},\,\frac{3}{2}&\text{(at $z=-p$)}.\end{cases}

Accordingly, the singularities at

5.2.
Non-completely Reducible Case

To prove Theorem
1.1
, it remains to treat the
non-completely reducible case of equation (
1.19
)
p
.

The proofs of the following Lemmas
5.9
and

5.10
for determining the monodromy data
\mathcal{D}
in the
non-completely reducible case are essentially the same for the classical
Lamé equation (
1.18
) and for the generalized Lamé-type
equation (
1.19
)
p
. We therefore present the proofs only for
(
1.19
)
p
.

Fix a base point
x_{0}\in E_{\tau}\setminus\{0,\pm p\}
, for any

T\in\mathbb{C}
and
z
sufficiently close to
x_{0}
, define

(5.33)

\chi_{j}(z;T):=\int_{z}^{z+\omega_{j}}\dfrac{1}{\,\Phi_{e}(\xi;T)\,}\,d\xi,\quad j=1,2,

These integrals are locally holomorphic. Since
\Phi_{e}(z;T)
is elliptic,

\chi_{j}(z;T)
is independent of
z
, for
j=1,2
. We denote them by

\chi_{j}(T)
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Suppose the generalized Lamé-type equation
(
1.19
)
{}_{p,\,T_{0}}
is non-completely reducible at parameter
T_{0}

with monodromy data
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then

(5.34)

\mathcal{D}=\dfrac{\,\chi_{2}(T_{0})\,}{\chi_{1}(T_{0})}.

Proof.

Since (
1.19
)
{}_{p,\,T_{0}}
is non-completely reducible,
Theorem
5.1
implies that the Baker-Akhiezer
function
\psi(P_{0};z)
satisfies

(5.35)

\psi(P_{0};z+\omega_{j})=\epsilon_{j}\,\psi(P_{0};z),\quad\epsilon_{j}\in\{\pm 1\}.

Hence
\psi(P_{0};z)^{2}
is elliptic and solves (
4.14
).
Consequently, up to a nonzero multiple,

(5.36)

\Phi_{e}(z;T_{0})=\psi^{2}(P_{0};z),

Let
\tilde{y}(z)
be another solution of (
1.19
)
{}_{p,\,T_{0}}
linearly
independent of
\psi(P_{0};z)
, which is not elliptic of the second kind.
Define

(5.37)

\chi(z):=\dfrac{\tilde{y}(z)}{\,\psi(P_{0};z)\,}.

Substituting
\tilde{y}(z)=\chi(z)\,\psi(P_{0};z)
into
(
1.19
)
{}_{p,\,T_{0}}
yields

(5.38)

\dfrac{\chi^{\prime\prime}(z)}{\chi^{\prime}(z)}=-2\,\dfrac{\psi^{\prime}(P_{0};z)}{\psi(P_{0};z)}.

Integrating (
5.38
) and using (
5.36
), we obtain

(5.39)

\chi^{\prime}(z)=\dfrac{\tilde{b}}{\psi^{2}(P_{0};z)}=\dfrac{b}{\Phi_{e}(z;T_{0})},

for some
\tilde{b},b\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus
\chi(z)
is
quasi-periodic. By (
5.39
) and (
5.33
), we have

(5.40)

\begin{cases}~\chi(z+\omega_{1})=\chi(z)+b\,\chi_{1}(T_{0}),\\
~\chi(z+\omega_{2})=\chi(z)+b\,\chi_{2}(T_{0}).\end{cases}

Note that
\chi_{1}(T_{0})
and
\chi_{2}(T_{0})
cannot vanish
simultaneously; otherwise
\tilde{y}(z)
would be elliptic of the second
kind.

Case 1.
If
\chi_{1}(T_{0})=0
, then
\chi_{2}(T_{0})\neq 0
. Therefore
\frac{\chi_{2}(T_{0})}{\,\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,}=\infty
.
In this case, the fundamental solution

Y(z)=\big(b\chi_{2}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z),\,\tilde{y}(z)\big)^{T}

has monodromy matrices

(5.41)

\begin{split}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{2}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z+\omega_{1})\\
\tilde{y}(z+\omega_{1})\end{array}\right)&=\epsilon_{1}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{2}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z)\\
\tilde{y}(z)\end{array}\right),\\
\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{2}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z+\omega_{2})\\
\tilde{y}(z+\omega_{2})\end{array}\right)&=\epsilon_{2}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1&0\\
1&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{2}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z)\\
\tilde{y}(z)\end{array}\right),\end{split}

where
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}
. It follows from (
5.41
)
that

\frac{\chi_{2}(T_{0})}{\,\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,}=\mathcal{D}=\infty.

Case 2.
If
\chi_{1}(T_{0})\neq 0
, then
\frac{\chi_{2}(T_{0})}{\,\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,}\in\mathbb{C}
, and

Y(z)=\big(\chi_{1}(T_{0})\psi(P_{0};z),\,\tilde{y}(z)\big)^{T}

forms a fundamental system. Its monodromy matrices satisfy

(5.42)

\begin{split}\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z+\omega_{1})\\
\tilde{y}(z+\omega_{1})\end{array}\right)&=\epsilon_{1}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1&0\\
1&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z)\\
\tilde{y}(z)\end{array}\right),\\
\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z+\omega_{2})\\
\tilde{y}(z+\omega_{2})\end{array}\right)&=\epsilon_{2}\left(\begin{array}[c]{cc}1&0\\
\frac{\chi_{2}(T_{0})}{\,\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,}&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[c]{c}b\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,\psi(P_{0};z)\\
\tilde{y}(z)\end{array}\right),\end{split}

where
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in\{\pm 1\}
. It follows from (
5.42
)
that

\frac{\chi_{2}(T_{0})}{\,\chi_{1}(T_{0})\,}=\mathcal{D}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose the generalized Lamé-type equation
(
1.19
)
{}_{p,\,T_{0}}
is non-completely reducible at parameter
T_{0}

with monodromy data
\mathcal{D}\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then

\mathcal{D}
can be determined by

(5.43)

\mathcal{D}=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}P=(T,C)\,\rightarrow\,P_{0}\\
C^{2}=Q(T)\neq 0\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\,-r(P_{0})+r(P)\,}{s(P_{0})-s(P)}.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.1
, we have

(r(P_{0}),s(P_{0}))\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The polynomial

Q(T)\in\mathbb{C}[T]
is nontrivial with
Q(T_{0})=0
. Choose any sequence

P_{k}=(T_{k},C_{k})\in\Gamma(\tau,p)
with
C_{k}^{2}=Q(T_{k})\neq 0
such
that
T_{k}\to T_{0}
. Then the Baker-Akhiezer functions
\psi(P_{k};z)

converge uniformly to
\psi(P_{0};z)
on compact subsets of
E_{\tau}\setminus\{0,\pm p\}
. Consequently,

(5.44)

\left(r(P_{k}),\,s(P_{k})\right)\to\left(r(P_{0}),\,s(P_{0})\right)\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad\text{as }\,k\to+\infty.

For each
k
, set

(5.45)

f_{k}(z):=\dfrac{\psi(P_{k};z)}{\,\psi(P_{k}^{*};z)\,},

where
P_{k}^{*}=(T_{k},-C_{k})\in\Gamma(\tau,p)
is the dual point of

P_{k}=(T_{k},C_{k})
. Using (
5.2
), one obtains

(5.46)

\begin{split}f_{k}(z+1)&=\dfrac{e^{-2\pi i\,s(P_{k})}\,\psi(P_{k};z)}{e^{-2\pi i\,s(P_{k}^{*})}\,\psi(P_{k}^{*};z)}=e^{-4\pi i\,s(P_{k})}\,f_{k}(z),\\
f_{k}(z+\tau)&=\dfrac{e^{2\pi i\,r(P_{k})}\,\psi(P_{k};z)}{e^{2\pi i\,r(P_{k}^{*})}\,\psi(P_{k}^{*};z)}=e^{4\pi i\,r(P_{k})}\,f_{k}(z).\end{split}

Moreover, by (
4.43
) and (
4.44
), we
have

(5.47)

\begin{split}\left(\log f_{k}(z)\right)^{\prime}&=\dfrac{\,\psi^{\prime}(P_{k};z)\,}{\psi(P_{k};z)}-\dfrac{\,\psi^{\prime}(P_{k}^{*};z)\,}{\psi(P_{k}^{*};z)}\\
&=\dfrac{\,W(\psi(P_{k};z),\,\psi(P_{k}^{*};z))\,}{\psi(P_{k};z)\,\psi(P_{k}^{*};z)}=\dfrac{2i\,C_{k}}{\,\Phi_{e}(z;T_{k})\,}.\end{split}

Integrating (
5.47
) along the cycles
\ell_{j}
, it follows from
(
5.46
) that

(5.48)

\begin{split}\chi_{1}(T_{k})&=\frac{\,-2\pi\,s(P_{k})+\pi\,m_{1}(T_{k})\,}{C_{k}},\\
\chi_{2}(T_{k})&=\frac{\,2\pi\,r(P_{k})+\pi\,m_{2}(T_{k})\,}{C_{k}},\end{split}

for some
m_{1}(T_{k}),m_{2}(T_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\chi_{j}(z;T)
is
locally holomorphic, it follows that

(5.49)

\begin{cases}~\chi_{1}(T_{k})\rightarrow\chi_{1}(T_{0})\in\mathbb{C},\\
~\chi_{2}(T_{k})\rightarrow\chi_{2}(T_{0})\in\mathbb{C},\end{cases}\qquad\text{as }\,k\rightarrow+\infty.

Since
Q(T_{k})\to Q(T_{0})=0
, we have
C_{k}\to 0
, and hence

(5.50)

\begin{cases}-2\pi\,s(P_{k})+\pi\,m_{1}(T_{k})\rightarrow 0,\\
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}-}2\pi\,r(P_{k})+\pi\,m_{2}(T_{k})\rightarrow 0,\end{cases}\qquad\text{as }\,k\rightarrow+\infty.

Combining (
5.44
) and (
5.50
) yields

(5.51)

m_{1}(T_{k})=2s(P_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad m_{2}(T_{k})=-2r(P_{0}),

for
k
sufficiently large. Finally, by Lemma
5.9
together with
(
5.51
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\dfrac{\,\chi_{2}(T_{k})\,}{\chi_{1}(T_{k})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\dfrac{\,2\pi\,r(P_{k})+\pi\,m_{2}(T_{k})\,}{-2\pi\,s(P_{k})+\pi\,m_{1}(T_{k})}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\dfrac{\,-r(P_{0})+r(P_{k})\,}{s(P_{0})-s(P_{k})}.

This establishes (
5.43
) and thereby
completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

(5.65)

\psi_{p}(z)=e^{c_{p}z}\dfrac{\sigma\left(z-a_{1}^{(p)}\right)\sigma\left(z-a_{2}^{(p)}\right)}{\,\sigma(z)\sqrt{\sigma(z+p)}\sqrt{\sigma(z-p)}\,},\quad\begin{array}[c]{l}\text{where \,}c_{p}\in\mathbb{C},\\[4.0pt]
a_{1}^{(p)},\,a_{2}^{(p)}\in E_{\tau}\setminus\{0\}.\end{array}

From (
5.9
) we have,
for all
p\in E_{\tau}\setminus\{\tfrac{\omega_{k}}{2}:k=0,1,2,3\}
,

(5.66)

\begin{cases}~r+s\tau=a_{1}^{(p)}+a_{2}^{(p)},\\
~r\eta_{1}+s\eta_{2}=c^{(p)}.\end{cases}

Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume
a_{j}^{(p)}\to a_{j}
as

p\to\tfrac{\omega_{k}}{2}
,
j=1,2
. Let
p\to\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}
, we have

(5.67)

\psi_{p}(z)\to\psi_{k}(z)=e^{cz}\dfrac{\sigma(z-a_{1})\,\sigma(z-a_{2})}{\,\sigma(z)\sqrt{\sigma\left(z+\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\right)\,}\sqrt{\sigma\left(z-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\right)\,}\,},

uniformly on compact subsets of
E_{\tau}\setminus\{0,\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\}
,
as
p\to\tfrac{\omega_{k}}{2}
, where

(5.68)

\begin{split}c&=r\eta_{1}+s\eta_{2},\\
a_{j}&=\lim_{p\to\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}}a_{j}^{(p)},\quad j=1,2.\end{split}

As
p\to\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}
, it follows from (
5.66
) and
(
5.68
) that

(5.69)

\begin{cases}~r+s\tau=a_{1}+a_{2},\\
~r\eta_{1}+s\eta_{2}=c.\end{cases}

If
k=0
, the limiting function

\psi_{0}(z)=e^{cz}\dfrac{\,\sigma(z-a_{1})\,\sigma(z-a_{2})\,}{\sigma^{2}(z)}

is precisely the Baker-Akhiezer function of (
1.18
) used to define the
monodromy data
(r(\tilde{P}),s(\tilde{P}))
for
\tilde{P}=(\tilde{B},\tilde{C})\in\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau)
, hence

(r(\tilde{P}),s(\tilde{P}))=(r,s).

For
k\in\{1,2,3\}
, the Baker-Akhiezer function for the limiting equation
(
1.22
)
{}_{\tilde{B}_{k}}
, used to define the monodromy data

(r(\tilde{B}_{k}),s(\tilde{B}_{k}))
, is given by

\hat{\psi}_{k}(z)=e^{c_{k}z}\dfrac{\,\sigma(z-a_{1})\,\sigma(z-a_{1})\,}{\sigma(z)\,\sigma\left(z-\frac{\omega_{k}}{2}\right)},

which satisfies

(5.70)

\begin{cases}~r(\tilde{B}_{k})+s(\tilde{B}_{k})=a_{1}+a_{2}-\dfrac{\omega_{k}}{2},\\[4.0pt]
~r(\tilde{B}_{k})\,\eta_{1}+s(\tilde{B}_{k})\,\eta_{2}=c_{k}.\end{cases}

Comparing
\psi_{k}(z)
with
\hat{\psi}_{k}(z)
and using the transformation
law of the Weierstrass
\sigma
-function
(
5.10
), we obtain

c_{k}=c-\dfrac{\eta_{k}}{2}.

Together with (
5.69
), this yields

\begin{cases}~r(\tilde{B}_{k})+s(\tilde{B}_{k})=r+s\tau-\dfrac{\omega_{k}}{2},\\[4.0pt]
~r(\tilde{B}_{k})\,\eta_{1}+s(\tilde{B}_{k})\,\eta_{2}=r\eta_{1}+s\eta_{2}-\dfrac{\eta_{k}}{2},\end{cases}

and hence

\left(r(\tilde{B}_{k}),\,s(\tilde{B}_{k})\right)=\begin{cases}~\left(r+\frac{1}{2},\,s\right)&\text{if }\,k=1,\\
~\left(r,\,s+\frac{1}{2}\right)&\text{if }\,k=2,\\
~\left(r+\frac{1}{2},\,s+\frac{1}{2}\right)&\text{if }\,k=3.\end{cases}

∎
