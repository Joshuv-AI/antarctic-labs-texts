From early response to post-response impact: a phenomenological study of counsellors’ experiences following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes
Authors: Ibrahim Can, Elif Cimsir, Bahtiyar Eraslan Çapan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The twin earthquakes that struck Türkiye on 6 February 2023 (magnitudes 7.7 and 7.6) were among the most devastating in the nation’s history, mobilising thousands of mental health professionals to address survivors’ acute psychological needs alongside medical and humanitarian teams.Objective: This phenomenological study aimed to explore the lived experiences of counsellors who provided psychosocial support during the early response phase (i.e. the first 40 days following the earthquakes).Method: Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with 18 counsellors (aged 30–49; 33.3% male) and analyzed using a phenomenologically informed thematic analysis.Results: Five overarching themes and 17 subthemes were identified: (1) Moral and Professional Imperatives, reflecting ethical–humanitarian responsibility and professional commitment; (2) Multilevel Challenges, including physical hardship, emotional burden, role ambiguity, communication barriers, and organisational and coordination challenges; (3) Sustaining Professional Functioning under Crisis Conditions, involving collective coping, emotional regulation, adaptive practice, and task-focused engagement; (4) Post-Response Impact: Growth, Distress, and Moral Tension, encompassing delayed stress reactions alongside personal and professional growth; and (5) Systemic Lessons for Disaster Preparedness, highlighting the need for improved interinstitutional coordination and organisational structure, enhanced professional preparedness through psychological readiness and training, and strengthened resource and intervention capacity.Conclusions: These findings contribute to the field of psychotraumatology by providing an in-depth understanding of how mental health professionals experience, make sense of, and cope with providing psychosocial support in large-scale disaster contexts. The study extends existing research by highlighting the coexistence of vulnerability and resilience, shaped by personal, professional, and systemic factors. The findings also have practical implications for early interventions, trauma-informed training, and coordinated disaster response systems.KEYWORDS: Counsellors, earthquake, phenomenology, disaster response, coping strategiesPALABRAS CLAVE: Consejeros, terremoto, respuesta a desastres, fenomenología, estrategias de afrontamiento

Introduction

The earthquakes that struck Türkiye on 6 February 2023 (magnitude 7.7 and 7.6), centred in Kahramanmaraş, represent the most devastating disaster in the country’s modern history, affecting 11 provinces across the region (Çitil Akyol, 2024; Hussain et al., 2023; Kurt et al., 2023). These catastrophic earthquakes claimed the lives of more than 50,000 people and caused physical injuries to hundreds of thousands (Hussain et al., 2023), affecting more than 15 million individuals and resulting in widespread displacement as well as severe physical, economic, social, and psychological consequences (Çitil Akyol, 2024).

Following the earthquakes, thousands of disaster response personnel mobilised to meet survivors’ urgent needs, including search and rescue, shelter, food distribution, health care, and psychosocial support services (Çitil Akyol, 2024; Hussain et al., 2023; Kurt et al., 2023). Alongside medical and emergency response teams, mental health professionals – including counsellors, psychologists, and psychiatrists – played a critical role in supporting survivors’ psychological well-being, promoting resilience, and facilitating adaptation to dramatically altered living circumstances (Çitil Akyol, 2024; Mutlu & Koşan, 2024).

Earthquake-related trauma and the role of psychosocial support

The psychological consequences of major earthquakes have been extensively documented in Türkiye and internationally. Research following the 1999 Marmara earthquake and other large-scale earthquakes worldwide has consistently reported elevated levels of posttraumatic stress, depression, prolonged distress, and other adverse mental health outcomes among survivors (Başoǧlu et al., 2004; Beaglehole et al., 2019; Kunii et al., 2022; Salcioglu et al., 2007; Tanaka et al., 2016). Meta-analytic evidence further suggests that nearly one in four earthquake survivors meets diagnostic criteria for posttraumatic stress disorder (Dai et al., 2016), underscoring the substantial psychological burden associated with such disasters.

Timely psychosocial support plays a critical role in mitigating these adverse outcomes and fostering recovery. Such support may include needs assessment, psychological first aid, psychoeducation, crisis intervention, and individual or group-based services aimed at reducing distress and strengthening coping resources (Brown et al., 2017; Schonfeld et al., 2015; Seto et al., 2019; Thordardottir et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2015). These interventions help survivors regulate overwhelming emotions, reduce acute stress reactions, and facilitate adjustment following disaster exposure (Despeaux et al., 2019; Farchi et al., 2024; Reifels et al., 2013; Thapa et al., 2021).

However, delivering psychosocial support in disaster settings is often challenging. Large-scale destruction, resource limitations, environmental disruptions, and barriers to accessing affected populations may complicate intervention efforts (Kunii et al., 2022; Troup et al., 2021). Furthermore, counsellors and other helping professionals working in disaster contexts may themselves experience secondary traumatic stress, compassion fatigue, emotional exhaustion, and burnout, although some also report personal and professional growth following these experiences (Bride et al., 2009; Jiang et al., 2023; Spokane et al., 2011; Tominaga et al., 2020). These findings are particularly relevant given evidence that individuals experiencing psychological distress do not always seek professional psychological support, even when such services are available (Bayrakçeken et al., 2023; Güney et al., 2024). Understanding these experiences is important not only for counsellor well-being but also for the effectiveness and sustainability of disaster-related psychosocial services.

Current study

Building on this literature, recent studies conducted following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes have begun to examine counsellors’ experiences in the field (e.g. Çitil Akyol, 2024; Mutlu & Koşan, 2024). For example, Çitil Akyol (2024) explored school counsellors’ perceptions of the psychosocial needs arising in earthquake-affected regions, whereas Mutlu and Koşan (2024) examined counsellors’ experiences, evaluations, and recommendations regarding psychosocial service delivery following the earthquakes. Collectively, these studies suggest that counsellors encounter substantial practical and emotional challenges in disaster settings; however, they provide limited insight into how counsellors make meaning of these experiences across the broader trajectory of disaster response and post-deployment adjustment.

Despite these contributions, little is known about how counsellors experience, interpret, and adapt to disaster response from initial deployment through post-field reintegration. Moreover, no study to date has provided an in-depth phenomenological examination of counsellors who travelled to the disaster zone immediately following the earthquakes to deliver psychosocial support. Accordingly, the present study aims to deepen understanding of counsellor resilience and inform the development of more effective psychosocial interventions in disaster contexts. By examining counsellors’ experiences from deployment through post-field adjustment, this study extends existing disaster mental health research beyond needs assessments and challenge-focused accounts to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the lived processes underlying disaster response work.

RQ. What are the lived experiences of counselors who provided psychosocial support in the early phase following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes?

Method

Participants

This phenomenological study included 18 practicing school counsellors who provided psychosocial support following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes. Participants were recruited using criterion purposive sampling through Ministry of National Education in-service training programmes, which provided convenient access to eligible counsellors. Eligibility criteria required participants to (a) have provided psychosocial support in earthquake-affected regions and (b) be willing to participate in an interview. Because no centralised registry of counsellors involved in the early response phase exists, the size of the target population could not be determined. Consistent with phenomenological research, sample size was guided by depth and richness of experience rather than statistical representativeness, and recruitment continued until sufficient depth and breadth of experiential data had been obtained, and no substantially new insights were emerging from subsequent interviews, indicating that the sample was adequate to address the study aims (see Bartholomew et al., 2021; Dodgson, 2023).

The sample comprised 12 women and 6 men aged 30–49 years with 7–25 years of professional experience. Most participants held a bachelor’s degree (n = 14), while four held master’s degrees. Counsellors primarily served in Malatya, Hatay, Kahramanmaraş, Gaziantep, and Osmaniye, with initial deployment occurring between Day 3 and Day 40 following the earthquakes. Procedure

Following ethical approval from the university’s institutional review board, data were collected between November and December 2023 by the third author, a counsellor educator at a public university in Central Anatolia, Türkiye. Semi-structured face-to-face interviews lasting 20–40 min were conducted over a two-week period at mutually agreed locations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. With participants’ permission, all interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

The interview protocol was developed in accordance with phenomenological interviewing principles, emphasising open-ended questions designed to elicit rich descriptions of participants’ lived experiences rather than responses constrained by predetermined categories (Bartholomew et al., 2021; Dodgson, 2023). The protocol was subsequently refined through expert review by two counsellor educators with expertise in trauma and qualitative research. The final interview form consisted of five primary open-ended questions and follow-up probes. Questions explored participants’ motivations for travelling to the earthquake region (e.g. ‘What motivated you to travel to the earthquake region?’), their experiences providing psychosocial support (e.g. ‘Can you describe your experiences while working in the earthquake zone?’), challenges encountered in the field (e.g. ‘What were the most difficult aspects of this experience?’), coping strategies used during and after deployment (e.g. ‘How did you cope with these challenges?’), and perceived personal and professional changes following return from the field (e.g. ‘How did this experience affect you personally and professionally after returning from the field?’). Participants were also asked to provide recommendations for improving psychosocial response efforts in future disasters.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using phenomenologically informed thematic analysis. All interview transcripts were imported into MaxQDA 2022 to facilitate data management, coding, and retrieval. Analysis followed an inductive and iterative process informed by Braun and Clarke (2006). First, all authors read and re-read the transcripts to become familiar with the data and identify meaningful aspects of participants’ lived experiences. The research team subsequently met twice to discuss emerging patterns, preliminary codes, and potential thematic groupings.

Following these discussions, the first and second authors continued the analysis by refining the coding structure, grouping related codes into broader categories, and developing candidate themes and subthemes through constant comparison across participants’ accounts. Differences in interpretation were discussed during research team meetings until consensus was reached regarding the coding structure and thematic organisation. The final thematic structure was reviewed against both the coded extracts and the complete dataset to ensure coherence and consistency. This process resulted in five overarching themes and seventeen subthemes capturing counsellors’ experiences following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes.

Results

The analysis yielded five overarching themes and 17 subthemes, capturing participants’ moral and professional imperatives, multilevel challenges encountered in the field, adaptive processes used to sustain functioning, post-response impacts, and systemic lessons for disaster preparedness. Moral imperatives and professional calling in disaster response

Ethical–humanitarian responsibility

Participants repeatedly described an inability to remain passive while others were suffering, often framing their decision to act as something they had to do rather than something they simply chose. For instance, P4 emphasised this internal pressure by stating, ‘Instead of sitting here doing nothing, I preferred to go there and touch something – whatever needed to be done.’ Similarly, P6 described a sense of collective duty, noting, ‘There was a need there … I think it’s important to stand in line and go where people need you.’ In a more immediate and emotionally driven account, P15 reflected, ‘I suddenly felt the urge to be there. I wanted to help – I felt that I had to do something. I couldn’t stay where I was. I didn’t really think it through in a rational way.’ These accounts suggest that participants’ decisions to deploy voluntarily were shaped by a strong sense of moral obligation and an urgent need to respond to suffering.

Professional responsibility and development

Many participants framed volunteer involvement in disaster response as an opportunity to apply prior training in a real-world crisis setting, test their competence, and move beyond theoretical knowledge. P13, for example, stated, ‘After all, I have techniques I learned through my profession. I have worked on trauma for a long time and received training in EMDR. I felt that I had to use these skills to help someone there.’ In parallel, P4 highlighted a broader desire to act professionally in response to urgent needs rather than a specific focus on trauma work: ‘I also wanted to do something related to my profession. It wasn’t specifically about working with trauma when we first went – it was the fourth day. But it could have been psychological first aid or whatever was needed.’ These accounts suggest that entering the disaster zone involved not only applying existing skills but also enacting and testing one’s professional identity in an extreme context.

Multilevel challenges in a chaotic disaster context

Physical and environmental hardships

Participants described the field conditions as such that basic needs, including shelter, food, and hygiene, were often unmet, requiring continuous adaptation. For instance, P2 illustrated the precarious living conditions by stating, ‘We slept by putting two desks together … one side of your body was warm, the other side was cold.’ Similarly, P12 noted: ‘It was extremely cold, especially at night, and hygiene was a serious problem.’ In some cases, the lack of resources reached critical levels. P9 recalled, ‘One day we couldn’t find anything to eat … I found a single baby biscuit, and we ate that and continued.’ These accounts suggest that counsellors operated not only as helpers but also as individuals navigating survival-like conditions.

Emotional burden and exposure to survivors’ trauma

Participants’ narratives revealed intense emotional exposure to survivors’ grief, loss, and trauma, described as deeply affecting yet requiring emotional control to maintain functioning. P12 described this:

… there was an 11-year-old child who had spent 12 h with the lifeless body of a loved one, together with their parents and siblings. I found it very difficult not to react emotionally … but I had to in order to support them.

Similarly, P7 recounted a powerful emotional interaction, stating, ‘When she asked where we came from and we said, “From İzmir,” she hugged us and started crying. She kept saying, “You came for us.” It was a completely different kind of embrace.’ These accounts suggest that what made participants’ experiences especially difficult was not only exposure to trauma narratives, but also the immediacy of direct, embodied contact.

Professional uncertainty and role ambiguity in the early response phase

Participants’ accounts indicated that the early phase of intervention was marked by a complex experience of professional uncertainty intertwined with role ambiguity. For some, this uncertainty emerged even before entering the field. As P16 expressed:

On the way there, I had question marks in my mind: ‘Will I be able to do this? Will I be able to intervene?’ We were going to work with people who had lived through it directly. Of course, I had prior experience, but I felt somewhat anxious about intervening with those who had lived through it firsthand.

Beyond these internal concerns, participants also described that the lack of structure and clarity in the early response environment contributed to a broader sense of disorientation. As P7 articulated: ‘At first, we could not carry out a structured intervention. The environment was chaotic, our minds were chaotic, and we did not yet have a plan in our heads.’

For some participants, these conditions further evolved into a confrontation with perceived professional insufficiency, particularly when they encountered complex or high-intensity cases that exceeded what they felt prepared to handle independently. As P8 noted:

There were professional challenges. At one point, I realized that I was insufficient. While we were able to do some things within the scope of psychological first aid, I saw that I was not adequate for individuals who needed more intensive counseling or were in severe trauma, as I did not have enough experience in that area. In those cases, I tried to help by referring them to someone else.

These accounts suggest that early uncertainty reflected not a lack of competence, but the challenge of translating training into practice in a rapidly changing, emotionally overwhelming, and organisationally ambiguous context.

Communication barriers

Language barriers, particularly in encounters with Syrian children and other migrant groups in the region, limited the participants’ ability to deliver planned interventions and sometimes forced them to improvise. P7 articulated this:

When we first brought the children into the activity, we realized they were Syrian. We had planned to do a storytelling activity, but we were suddenly at a loss. We experienced a moment of confusion. We had a translator, who knew some of the language through their mother, but it still wasn’t sufficient. So we shifted to breathing exercises instead. I found this very challenging, and honestly, it discouraged me a lot …

Similarly, P9 expressed: ‘We were in a tent settlement with migrants, and one of our biggest problems was language.’ These accounts suggest that communication barriers disrupted planned activities and limited effective psychosocial engagement, leading to frustration.

Organisational and coordination challenges

A recurrent source of distress was the absence of clear structure, role definition, and inter-institutional coordination. Participants often described entering the field without sufficient orientation, information, or organisational support. For example, P2 stated: ‘We set out with a severe lack of organization. Nobody knew each other. There were no name tags, no materials, nothing. We kept asking, “Where are we going? What kind of place are we going into?”’ Similarly, P4 noted, ‘There was a lot of confusion. Deciding where to begin and what to do was a problem on the first day. Organizational deficiencies really challenged us.’ This was echoed by P5: ‘There were serious coordination problems between institutions and ministries, and that was directly reflected in the field.’ Participants’ accounts suggest that rather than functioning within a clearly organised response system, many had to improvise structure while already under pressure.

Sustaining professional functioning under crisis conditions

Collective support and team solidarity

One of the most consistent coping resources was collective support among team members in the form of evening conversations, emotional sharing, practical consultation, and a strong sense of solidarity. P2 described these interactions as both emotional and functional: ‘In the evenings, there were moments when we sat together, cried together, and even laughed together; but during the day, we had to remain strong somehow. A strong sense of solidarity developed among us.’ Similarly, P18 referred to these moments as a form of group healing: ‘We supported each other deeply, cried together, shared our emotions, and embraced one another.’ P18 highlighted the supportive nature of these exchanges:

Let me say this to my younger colleagues or those who will be going there: they are not alone. There is a strong sense of professional solidarity on the ground, far beyond what they experience in their usual professional lives. It is a very different psychological environment. Everyone becomes closely bonded, supporting one another and stepping in to fill each other’s gaps – “You sit, I’ll take care of it.” The level of collaboration is remarkable. People constantly ask each other questions and share answers, and this extends across teams from different cities as well.

These accounts suggest that coping was not solely individual but deeply relational.

Emotional self-regulation and boundary setting

Participants’ narratives revealed that maintaining emotional balance in the field required ongoing, active self-regulation alongside clear boundary setting, rather than passive endurance. As P15 explained:

But each time, I reminded myself of this: I am not Superman. Yes, I came here to help, but my arms are not long enough to reach everyone – I can only do as much as I can. Others will come after me; this will not end with me. What I leave unfinished, another colleague will continue. In fact, when we arrived in the second week, we carried on what had been left incomplete during the first week.

At the same time, participants emphasised the importance of acknowledging and normalising their own emotional responses. As P13 noted: ‘We have to remember that we are human too. We can be affected, feel bad, and cry. All of that is normal.’ Overall, these accounts suggest that emotional self-regulation in disaster settings is not a process of detachment but rather a balancing of emotional presence with the recognition of personal limits.

Adapting professional skills to crisis conditions

Participants described recalibrating their practice to meet immediate and context-specific needs. For example, P16 described a shift in understanding what survivors needed:

“Once I met the children and visited the tents, I relaxed. I realized they mainly wanted to be understood, to know whether what they were feeling was normal, and to be listened to.” Similarly, P3 emphasized the simplicity and immediacy of psychological first aid as a guiding framework: “We first asked: what are people’s basic needs, how do you feel, what are you thinking right now? That was our starting point in psychological first aid.”

Participants also described using flexible, activity-based approaches to engage children and provide psychosocial support. As P17 explained:

I realized how important psychosocial support is through the activities we carried out with the children. For example, I played the saz with them, we played games, sang folk songs and other songs, and engaged in various activities and conversations.

These accounts illustrate that professional practice in disaster settings involved not the direct application of formal training and/or conventional psychotherapy but its adaptation and simplification, with interventions drawing on core psychosocial competencies – such as listening, normalising, structuring, containing, and fostering a sense of safety.

Task immersion as a coping strategy

Participants’ narratives also highlighted deep task immersion as a coping strategy, allowing them to overlook fatigue and remain intensely engaged in their work. This was explained by P2:‘A friend grabbed my arm on the fourth day and said, “Sit down for a moment.” I said, “Why?” They said, “Do you realize you haven’t sat down for four days?” I genuinely did not realize it.’

In some cases, this immersion extended to a near-complete disconnection from bodily awareness. As P6 explained:

Was it dopamine or adrenaline – I don’t know which hormone kicked in, but I collapsed there. It was around the fifth or sixth day after the earthquake. I was working at a fairground during the distribution of supplies. I hadn’t really noticed it at the time – for example, I had been hurt, but I wasn’t even aware of the pain.

These accounts indicate that by staying in motion and focusing on what needed to be done, participants temporarily muted bodily discomfort and emotional overload.

Post-deployment impact: growth, distress, and moral tension

Personal and professional growth

Participants’ narratives revealed that providing psychosocial support in the disaster context was experienced as both deeply meaningful and professionally transformative. Being present in the field and contributing to survivors’ well-being fostered a strong sense of personal value, purpose, and fulfilment. As P17 stated:

I felt valuable because I thought my impact area was larger. Being there gave me a sense of satisfaction. I felt happy as a member of this country for being able to contribute, even in a small way, during such a traumatic time.

For some participants, meaning was articulated through metaphors that reflected the modest yet significant nature of their contributions. P8 described: ‘Being there made me feel good. I always describe it as carrying water to a fire. We may not have had huge hoses, but if we poured even a little water, I count that as something meaningful.’

Alongside this personal meaning, participants also described substantial professional growth. Field experiences contributed to increased confidence in working with trauma, enhanced observational capacity, and the development of experiential knowledge that extended beyond formal training. As P10 noted: ‘I think our observational and experiential skills regarding psychological first aid, post-traumatic stress responses, and trauma-related conditions developed. We were inside the cases – we saw and lived them.’

Similarly, P17 stated:‘Professionally, I think it gave me an important experience. In a disaster of this scale, that was the most professional thing I could do.’ P18 further emphasised the developmental significance of this experience:‘I think I took an important professional step. I overcame my fear of working with trauma. Saying yes to this team was itself a very big step for me.’

Overall, participants described fieldwork as building a form of embodied professional knowledge – an experiential archive of how trauma is expressed, how individuals seek support, and how interventions can be adapted within acute disaster contexts.

Post-field stress reactions

Despite the positive connotations of deployment, many participants reported that the emotional cost became apparent only after returning in the form of irritability, crying, emotional collapse, and somatic reactions. For instance, P12 reflected that ‘ … the children I had worked with wanted to contact me and called me on video. When I saw them, something changed in me – I felt much worse. I started having trouble sleeping, and I even developed eczema.’ Similarly, P6 noted,

After I returned, I began having crying spells. I realized I had held in a lot while I was there, and it came out as a kind of emotional release. Physically, the strain also caught up with me – I ended up needing physical therapy. For about a month, my feet were affected, and the bruising above my right knee spread down to my ankle after I returned.

These accounts suggest that, for some participants, the accumulated psychological burden became more difficult to contain after leaving the field.

Guilt and moral distress

Participants also reported a morally charged discomfort about leaving the field while needs persisted. For example, P6 shared that ‘After returning, I kept feeling: why did I come back here? They still needed us there. I had to cope with that feeling.’ Similarly, P1 noted, ‘When I came back, I felt bad again. While you are there, you are doing something. But when you come back and just keep hearing the news, that passive state is much worse.’ This theme reflects participants’ struggles with the ethics of withdrawal and the burden of resuming everyday life.

Systemic lessons for disaster preparedness

Interinstitutional coordination and organisation

Participants strongly recommended better coordination among ministries, local administrations, and non-governmental actors. They emphasised the need for orientation, role clarity, cultural briefing, and a shared organisational structure.

For instance, P10 noted that ‘Even a two- or three-hour orientation would have helped. People came from all over the country. There could have been cultural briefings and a clear explanation of what was expected from us.’ Similarly, P16 highlighted systemic coordination problems, stating,

There needed to be organization across ministries. The same child was being taken by different institutions and NGOs on the same day. There was an inter-ministerial coordination problem. We tried to solve it through our own initiative, but that should not have been left to us.

Participants’ recommendations suggest that psychosocial work in disaster settings cannot rely solely on goodwill and improvisation; it requires coordinated systems, shared planning, and explicit operational leadership.

Professional preparedness: psychological readiness and training

Participants emphasised that effective disaster response requires both psychological readiness and adequate training. They stressed that those entering the field should be emotionally prepared, self-aware, and able to monitor their psychological state while working under extreme conditions. For example, P6 noted: ‘I think people who go there need high psychological resilience. They need to prepare themselves emotionally for what they are going to see.’ Similarly, P9 reflected on field experiences, stating: ‘One of our colleagues wanted to return on the third day, saying, “I can’t stay here.” That is why I think those who go need to have very strong psychological resilience.’

Participants also highlighted the importance of ongoing self-monitoring. As P13 explained: ‘They need to ask themselves again and again, “What state am I in right now?” because your emotional ups and downs affect others too.’

Alongside psychological readiness, participants consistently emphasised the necessity of prior training in trauma-informed approaches and psychological first aid. P9 underscored this, explaining that responders ‘need to know psychological first aid from A to Z’ and should not hesitate to ask, ‘What am I supposed to do now?’Similarly, P12 stressed that: ‘It is absolutely, absolutely essential that they have received training related to trauma.’

Overall, these accounts suggest that preparedness was understood as a combination of emotional resilience, self-awareness, and practical, action-oriented training, with participants emphasising the importance of immediately applicable skills over purely theoretical knowledge.

Resource and intervention preparedness

Findings indicated that participants emphasised the need for both material and procedural preparedness to enable timely and effective psychosocial support in disaster settings. They highlighted the importance of having readily available, pre-prepared materials and standardised resource kits that could be deployed immediately, rather than relying on supplies that would arrive later. For example, P2 stated:

When an organization is sending people out – at the very least, for example, if the Ministry of Education is deploying staff – there should be a prepared backpack with basic materials, even simple items. Instead of things arriving later – like paints or paper – these should be prepared before the disaster. Those going to the field should also have a clear understanding of the conditions and what they are likely to encounter.

Participants also underscored the need for structured, stepwise intervention frameworks to guide practice in the field. As P16 explained,

The teams going to the field really need a step-by-step structure. Starting with psychological first aid, followed by post-trauma interventions. I also think psychosocial activities should be more enriched. In our recently updated psychosocial program, there isn’t much dance, movement, or music, but children really need these.

Overall, these accounts suggest that preparedness was understood as both material readiness (e.g. pre-prepared kits and activity sets) and procedural clarity (e.g. structured guidelines and phased interventions). In the absence of such resources, participants often relied on improvisation, whereas standardised tools were seen as essential for consistent and effective psychosocial support.

Discussion

The present study provides a phenomenological account of counsellors’ experiences delivering psychosocial support following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes. Findings revealed a trajectory marked by professional commitment, multidimensional challenges, adaptive functioning, complex post-deployment outcomes, and lessons for disaster preparedness. By shifting attention from survivors to frontline mental health professionals, the study contributes to psychotraumatology by illuminating how professional functioning is both sustained and strained under conditions of extreme uncertainty.

Consistent with prior research on altruistic engagement in disaster response (e.g. Jiang et al., 2023), participants were strongly driven by ethical–humanitarian responsibility and professional commitment, framing their involvement as both a moral imperative and an opportunity to enact their professional identities in real-world trauma contexts. However, these imperatives quickly confronted complex field realities, including harsh environmental conditions, intense emotional exposure, communication barriers, and coordination challenges.

The early phase was characterised by professional uncertainty and role ambiguity in chaotic, poorly organised settings, with some participants reporting anticipatory self-doubt that intensified in the absence of structured plans and clear expectations. Consistent with prior research on logistical constraints, resource scarcity, and organisational ambiguity in disaster settings (e.g. Kunii et al., 2022; Reifels et al., 2013; Seto et al., 2019), these challenges may also reflect the region’s socioeconomic disadvantage and high in-migration, which likely compounded vulnerabilities and hindered coordination (Çitil Akyol, 2024; Hussain et al., 2023; Mutlu & Koşan, 2024). Although the present study was not designed to examine subgroup differences, experiences of uncertainty and challenge may have been shaped by contextual factors such as professional experience, deployment conditions, and the severity of disaster impact across locations, warranting further investigation.

Despite these challenges, counsellors employed adaptive processes that sustained professional functioning in highly demanding conditions. A central mechanism was collective coping – reflected in team solidarity, emotional sharing, and informal debriefing – supporting the view that coping in disaster contexts is fundamentally relational (Jiang et al., 2023; Troup et al., 2021). In parallel, participants engaged in emotional regulation and boundary setting, balancing emotional presence with functional capacity.

Task immersion emerged as another key strategy, characterised by intense focus and reduced awareness of fatigue, thereby enabling continued functioning but often delaying emotional processing. Counsellors also extended their roles beyond conventional therapy, providing psychological first aid, emotional containment, normalisation, and basic human connection. Although these responses aligned with principles of Psychological First Aid (e.g. flexibility and responsiveness to immediate needs; Farchi et al., 2024; Schonfeld et al., 2015; Spokane et al., 2011), they were largely improvised, reflecting efforts to translate training into action under uncertain and resource-limited conditions.

At the same time, participants experienced significant emotional exposure, positioning them as both providers of support and witnesses to trauma, consistent with literature on secondary traumatic stress and compassion fatigue (Bride et al., 2009; Spokane et al., 2011). Importantly, vulnerability did not undermine functioning; rather, effective practice involved acknowledging emotional responses while maintaining the capacity to support others, aligning with perspectives that view emotional engagement as integral to empathic care (Jiang et al., 2023; Reifels et al., 2013; Tominaga et al., 2020).

Although participants remained highly functional during deployment – often supported by task immersion and collective coping – some reported emotional distress (e.g. irritability, emotional collapse, and somatic symptoms) after returning from the field. This delayed impact aligns with trauma literature suggesting that sustained functional engagement may temporarily suppress emotional processing, with distress resurfacing after deployment (Tominaga et al., 2020). The findings extend research on secondary traumatic stress by illustrating how delayed distress may unfold among mental health professionals working in large-scale disaster settings (Bride et al., 2009; Seto et al., 2019; Spokane et al., 2011; Tominaga et al., 2020). The intensity and timing of post-deployment reactions may also vary according to factors such as professional experience, deployment duration, and exposure to severely affected areas, although these influences were beyond the scope of the present study.

At the same time, participants described meaningful personal and professional growth, including increased empathy, enhanced competence, and shifts in life priorities, consistent with post-traumatic growth literature (Jiang et al., 2023; Tominaga et al., 2020). However, this growth coexisted with moral distress and guilt – particularly related to leaving the disaster zone – highlighting the ethical tension between the desire to help and the recognition of one’s limited capacity.

Bridging practice and preparedness: implications for disaster response systems

The findings have several implications that directly reflect counsellors’ recommendations regarding disaster preparedness and response systems. First, participants emphasised the need for stronger interinstitutional coordination and organisational structure, noting role ambiguity, overlapping interventions, and a lack of clear planning, aligning with research showing that effective psychosocial response depends on coordinated systems, defined roles, and operational leadership (Brown et al., 2017; Kunii et al., 2022; Reifels et al., 2013; Seto et al., 2019; Thordardottir et al., 2018). Similar coordination challenges have been documented in the Turkish context (Çitil Akyol, 2024; Hussain et al., 2023; Kurt et al., 2023; Mutlu & Koşan, 2024), whereas coordinated systems have been shown to improve implementation and service effectiveness in other settings (e.g. Kunii et al., 2022; Reifels et al., 2013; Thordardottir et al., 2018). Participants also emphasised the need for readily available materials and practical guidelines to support effective psychosocial interventions, consistent with recent findings from the 2023 earthquake context (e.g. Çitil Akyol, 2024; Mutlu & Koşan, 2024).

Second, beyond organisational preparedness, the findings highlight the importance of professional readiness. Participants stressed the need for trauma-informed training, psychological first aid, emotional preparedness, and self-awareness to support effective practice in crisis settings, aligning with recent findings from the 2023 earthquake context (e.g. Çitil Akyol, 2024; Mutlu & Koşan, 2024) and international research (e.g. Kunii et al., 2022; Reifels et al., 2013). Given the delayed distress reported by some participants and the risk of secondary traumatic stress and burnout documented in prior research (Bride et al., 2009; Seto et al., 2019; Spokane et al., 2011; Tominaga et al., 2020), pre-deployment preparation, in-field support, and post-deployment follow-up appear critical for sustaining counsellor well-being. Such support may be particularly important given evidence that individuals experiencing psychological distress do not always seek professional help, even when services are available (e.g. Bayrakçeken et al., 2023; Güney et al., 2024).

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this phenomenological study was based on a relatively small, context-specific sample, and the findings are embedded within the cultural, institutional, and organisational context of Türkiye, including its disaster response structures and sociocultural norms. Second, although participants had experience across multiple earthquake-affected provinces, provincial representation was uneven, with a higher proportion of counsellors serving in larger and more severely affected provinces. Consequently, the findings should not be interpreted as representative of all counsellors working throughout the earthquake region, and their transferability should be considered in light of local contextual differences. Third, the study was not designed to examine differences associated with professional experience, deployment timing, or province-specific disaster conditions. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the interviews may have influenced participants’ accounts, particularly regarding emotional experiences. Future research may explore how these contextual factors shape counsellors’ experiences in disaster response settings.

Future research may benefit from longitudinal designs examining the long-term psychological trajectories of counsellors, as well as mixed-method approaches integrating quantitative measures of secondary traumatic stress and resilience. Additionally, comparative studies across different disaster contexts may further illuminate cultural and structural variations in counsellors’ experiences.

Conclusion

The present study shows that counsellors’ experiences in disaster settings reflect a dynamic, process-oriented trajectory shaped by moral and professional imperatives, multilevel challenges, adaptive functioning, and post-response impacts. The findings highlight the coexistence of vulnerability and resilience and underscore the need for trauma-informed training, coordinated systems, and comprehensive preparedness, as well as improved interinstitutional coordination and resource readiness, to support counsellors and enhance psychosocial interventions in future disasters.
