Implementation of virtual parental presence during induction of anesthesia: a single-site descriptive study at a pediatric academic center

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative anxiety in children can cause distress for patients, families, and care teams. Children aged 4–12 undergoing inhalational induction for elective surgery were enrolled along with their parents. Background

Preoperative anxiety is a common and significant concern in children, with up to 75% experiencing anxiety and distress before surgery [1]. Elevated anxiety in this setting has been linked to increased perioperative distress, greater need for pharmacological interventions, and a higher risk of negative postoperative behaviors such as emergence delirium, nightmares, and feeding or behavioral issues [2–4].

To mitigate anxiety, various approaches have been implemented across institutions, including pharmacologic anxiolysis, distraction techniques, and parental presence during induction [5, 6]. Parental presence has shown benefits in reducing anxiety at separation and leading to greater satisfaction, although nervous parental demeanor and poor preparation can limit effectiveness [7]. Additionally, families and caregivers can feel helpless, anxious, and worried about their child’s well-being. These reactions in caregivers can manifest as behaviors that inadvertently increase patient distress during induction, like providing excessive reassurance, empathic comments, explanations, and apologies in an attempt to ease their child’s anxiety [8]. Meanwhile, humor and non-procedural-related language have been shown to be beneficial in anxiety reduction and support of children during anesthesia induction [9, 10]. However, parents and caregivers are rarely coached on the best way to support their child during the induction of anesthesia, despite an expressed desire for comprehensive perioperative information, the provision of which has been shown to attenuate parental preoperative anxiety [7–9]. Further complicating preoperative anxiety mitigation, COVID-19 pandemic measures led to a reduction of the use of parental presence during induction across institutions, leading to an increased use of pharmacologic anxiolytics.

Virtual Parental Presence during the Induction of Anesthesia (VPPIA) offers a novel alternative that allows caregivers to remain virtually present in the operating room (OR) during patient induction, preserving emotional support while minimizing logistical disruptions. Prior pilot work has demonstrated that VPPIA is feasible and well-accepted by patients, parents, and staff [11]. Our study advances the literature by integrating a coaching component to prepare parents for VPPIA use and support their child during induction. Prior research has shown that structured preoperative education can reduce preoperative anxiety in caregivers and patients as well as improve postoperative outcomes [12]. Building on this evidence, we tested a brief, video-based coaching strategy to address a critical implementation gap identified in previous work by Bell et al., extending the VPPIA model to include caregiver preparation.

This single-site descriptive study evaluates the implementation of VPPIA while incorporating a brief, video-based parental coaching component, to offer actionable insights for institutions seeking to integrate VPPIA into routine pediatric perioperative care. Specifically, we assessed feasibility, perceived effectiveness, and user satisfaction with VPPIA. The coaching component was evaluated using parent-reported measures of preparedness to participate in VPPIA, confidence in supporting their child during induction, and satisfaction with the clarity and helpfulness of the instructional video.

Methods

This was a single-site, descriptive implementation study conducted at Nationwide Children’s Hospital (NCH), a large tertiary pediatric academic center, that recruited children (4–12 years of age) undergoing inhalational induction of anesthesia for elective surgeries. The study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, effectiveness, and parent/patient experience of VPPIA. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained at NCH (IRB STUDY0002057). Patients were recruited between 12/7/2021 and 12/8/2022 at NCH.

Participants, enrollment, and selection process

Informed parental consent and, if applicable, patient assent were obtained prior to enrollment. Eligibility criteria included: 4 to 12 years of age; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I, II, or III; scheduled to undergo inhalational induction for elective surgery; presenting from home; and English-speaking parent and child. Exclusion criteria included: developmental delay; psychological/emotional disorders; altered mental status; absence of a legal guardian; anticipated difficult intubation/airway; history of violent behaviors during induction of anesthesia; COVID-19 positive; scheduled to receive intravenous induction of anesthesia; or (receipt of any medical sedative(s) or anxiolytic medication(s) prior to surgery (e.g., midazolam).

Eligible patients were identified by research personnel in advance by reviewing the surgical schedule. Parents were contacted by telephone using a standardized recruitment script (Additional File 1) and were provided with detailed information about the study. If interested, families were emailed a consent form to sign and return electronically. Once signed consent was received, patients were enrolled. No formal limit was placed on the number of dyads approached per day, but enrollment was subject to the availability of research personnel and therefore reflects a convenience-based sampling approach.

Procedure

All parents received coaching on VPPIA within two weeks of the procedure. A 4-minute coaching video (Additional File 2) explained how parents could support their child during anesthesia induction (e.g., using humor and non-procedure-related language), what to expect during induction of anesthesia, and behaviors and physical signs to watch for in their child. On the day of surgery, FaceTime video calls (Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA) were initiated between the patient and parent(s) using iPads before separation in the preoperative holding area. Video calls continued throughout the transport to the OR and during the induction until the patient was anesthetized. No FaceTime sessions were recorded. Research staff accompanied patients to the OR and held the iPad throughout the induction to minimize interference with anesthesia team workflows. The anesthesia team provided care according to standard institutional practices and were not instructed to modify their usual approach, aside from accommodating the presence of VPPIA. This approach was intended to preserve the integrity of routine clinical care while evaluating the feasibility of incorporating virtual parental presence into existing workflows. After induction was complete, research staff accompanied the parent(s) to the waiting room.

Anesthetic technique

Inhalational induction was conducted using a mixture of sevoflurane, nitrous oxide, and oxygen delivered via facemask at the discretion of the anesthesia care team. All care provided to the patient was dictated by the anesthesia team, and the anesthetic technique was not modified for the study.

Data collection and outcomes

Data was collected and managed using REDCap [13, 14]. The study utilized Epic’s Standardized Anesthesia InductioN Tool (SAINT) that incorporated the documentation of several induction behaviors in a standardized manner [15].

Feasibility

Data were collected on whether the study caused any OR delays, whether any technical issues occurred during VPPIA, and whether the video session was stopped before the child fell asleep.

Effectiveness

Effectiveness of VPPIA was assessed using both objective and subjective measures, supplemented by behavioral context tools.

The modified Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (mYPAS) was used to objectively assess patients’ anxiety levels prior to and during induction of anesthesia. Research staff were trained to rate facial expression, activity, alertness and arousal, vocalization, and interaction with staff, generating an overall mYPAS score based on direct observation. This scale has been shown to have good validity and inter-observer reliability [16, 17].

The Child Induction Behavioral Assessment (CIBA) tool was used to assess the child’s level of cooperation and behavioral distress during anesthesia induction and was documented by anesthesia staff in EPIC as part of routine clinical charting [18]. Induction behaviors were grouped as (a) smooth (patients did not exhibit negative behaviors, fear, or anxiety and cooperated with induction); (b) moderate (patients cried, pulled away from the mask, or verbalized fear, refusal, and/or worry); and (c) difficult (patients pushed away from staff or equipment, covered their faces/limbs, cried and/or screamed hysterically, kicked/flailed their body, required restraint, or made their body rigid or limp). The Standardized Anesthesia InductioN Tool (SAINT) embedded within EPIC was used to capture mask acceptance and provider-rated VPPIA efficacy. Mask acceptance was scored as (a) unafraid, cooperative, accepts mask readily, (b) slight fear of mask, easily reassured, (c) moderate fear of mask, calmed with reassurance, or (d) terrified, crying, or combative behavior [15]. Anesthesia staff also subjectively rated whether they believed VPPIA was “Effective”, “Partially Effective”, “Not Effective”, or “Not Recorded” in reducing anxiety during induction. All items were abstracted into REDCap by research staff.

Parent and patient satisfaction

To measure parental satisfaction with induction, parents completed a satisfaction questionnaire, which consisted of 11 questions scaled on a 5-point Likert scale (from “Excellent” to “Poor”) and two free-response questions following their participation in the study (Additional File 3). Patient satisfaction was measured using a survey with the following responses: “Yes,” “I don’t know,” or “No” (Additional File 4). The survey was administered by research staff in the postoperative anesthesia care unit (PACU) following surgery or emailed to families for completion within 24 h. Patients who were able to read completed the surveys independently; research staff read questions aloud and recorded patients’ responses for children who could not read. If completing the survey in the PACU was too stressful for the patient or if there was not enough time due to prioritization of clinical needs, patients were emailed the satisfaction form to complete at home.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). The analysis was primarily descriptive. Continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) due to non-normal distribution as assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. No formal hypothesis testing or power calculation was performed, as the primary goal of this study was to evaluate feasibility and descriptive implementation outcomes.

Results

A total of 169 dyads were screened for eligibility, of whom 85 enrolled in the study and 84 were excluded. Reasons for exclusion included parents not wanting to participate (n = 31), patients receiving premedication or had an order for premedication (n = 20), parents not responding to the email (n = 12), patients not wanting to participate (n = 9), patients meeting other exclusion criteria (not including premedication) (n = 7), surgery starting earlier than scheduled (n = 3), and surgery being cancelled (n = 2).

A total of 85 patient-parent dyads were included in the final analysis. The median age of pediatric patients was 7.0 years (IQR: 6.0–9.0). The cohort included slightly more males (50.6%, n = 43) than females (49.4%, n = 42). Most patients were non-Hispanic White (75.3%, n = 64), followed by non-Hispanic Black (16.5%, n = 14), and individuals identified as Other or Unknown (7.0%, n = 6). Only one patient (1.2%) was identified as Hispanic. Regarding ASA physical status, most patients were classified as ASA I (90.6%, n = 77), with the remainder classified as ASA II (9.4%, n = 8) (Table 1). Feasibility

Feasibility of the VPPIA intervention was high across all measured domains. The study did not cause an operating room (OR) delay in 97.6% of cases (n = 83), with only 2.3% (n = 2) reporting any OR disruption. Technical difficulties were infrequent (7.1%, n = 6) and included minor issues such as difficulty connecting to FaceTime (n = 3), a child accidentally lowering the device volume (n = 1), network instability (n = 1), and a child navigating to unrelated content (n = 1). In 94.1% of cases (n = 80), the video connection was maintained until the child was fully anesthetized. In the four cases where the video was stopped early, this was either due to a technical issue (n = 2) or at the request of the anesthesiologist (n = 2). These results support the intervention’s strong feasibility for integration into perioperative workflows with minimal disruption. In addition to minimal OR delays and infrequent technical difficulties, the ability to use existing iPads and Wi-Fi infrastructure without altering standard anesthetic practice further supports the practicality of VPPIA implementation within routine perioperative workflows (Table 2).

Measures of feasibility

Effectiveness

Median mYPAS scores remained constant before (23.3, IQR: 23.3, 23.3) and during (23.2, IQR: 23.2, 33.3) induction. Most children (94.1%, n = 80) were rated as having a “Smooth” induction, indicating no negative behaviors or signs of distress. Only 4.7% (n = 4) exhibited “Moderate” behavior such as crying or pulling away from the mask. No patients were rated in the “Difficult” category. Most children (76.5%, n = 65) accepted the anesthesia mask readily and without fear. An additional 14.1% (n = 12) showed slight fear but were easily reassured, while 5.9% (n = 5) exhibited moderate fear that could be calmed. Only 3.5% (n = 3) of patients displayed terrified or combative behavior. Anesthesia providers rated VPPIA as “Effective” in 85.9% (n = 73) of cases, “Partially Effective” in 11.8% (n = 10), and “Not Effective” in just one case (1.2%. n = 1) (Table 3).

Patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction was high across all surveyed domains. Most children (74.1%, n = 63) reported that they understood what to do before talking with their parents, and 83.5% (n = 71) stated that they could see their parents the entire time. Nearly all patients (88.2%, n = 75) said that being able to see their parents helped them feel okay, and 70.6% (n = 60) felt like their parents were with them the entire time. Looking ahead, 76.5% (n = 65) said they would want to talk with their parents like this again, and 57.6% (n = 49) said they would tell their friends to do the same. Finally, 88.2% (n = 75) of children reported that they had a good time talking with their parents during the experience, further reinforcing the acceptability of VPPIA from the patient perspective (Table 4).

Parent satisfaction

Parent satisfaction with VPPIA was overwhelmingly positive. Most rated their overall experience as “Excellent” (80.0%, n = 68) or “Very Good” (14.1%, n = 12). Nearly all found the coaching video and handout helpful for supporting their child during induction, and 87.1% (n = 74) reported that the equipment and WiFi worked well. Over 90% agreed that VPPIA helped their child tolerate induction more easily and appear calmer. Notably, 95.3% (n = 81) felt more comfortable supporting their child, and 94.1% (n = 80) said they would use VPPIA again (Table 5).

Discussion

This single-site implementation of VPPIA with brief parental coaching at NCH demonstrates that virtual parental integration can be achieved using standard hospital devices (iPads, FaceTime, Wi-Fi) with minimal workflow adaptation and technical burden. There were few delays to operating room start times (2.3%) and limited technical issues (7.1%), reinforcing the feasibility of real-world integration. Most children (94.1%) had smooth inductions without behavioral escalation, and 76.5% accepted the anesthesia mask without fear. Providers rated VPPIA as “Effective” in 85.9% of cases, and satisfaction was high among both patients and parents, with 88.2% of children reporting a positive experience and 94.1% of parents indicating they would use VPPIA again. These outcomes suggest that VPPIA is a well-tolerated, low-resource intervention that can be seamlessly deployed in pediatric surgical settings without burdening perioperative workflows.

These results reinforce and extend previous pilot findings [11], and align with Matava et al.’s randomized pilot at SickKids, which found that virtual parental presence with coaching is feasible and acceptable even in the context of a controlled trial [18]. In this present study, most patients displayed smooth behavioral responses, and staff reported strong perceived efficacy, suggesting that VPPIA can be implemented reliably in real-world surgical settings. These findings are especially meaningful given the search for scalable, family-centered strategies to reduce perioperative anxiety in children.

Preoperative anxiety in children is associated with increased risk of emergence delirium, higher pain scores, greater need for pharmacologic anxiolysis, and long-term behavioral disturbances [1–4]. Institutions have historically employed a combination of parental presence, distraction techniques (e.g., video games), and medications such as midazolam to alleviate this distress [5, 6]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic prompted a reduction in parental presence policies across many hospitals, increasing the reliance on pharmacologic interventions and reducing opportunities for emotional support at the bedside. In this context, VPPIA emerges as a valuable alternative that preserves parental presence while minimizing staff burden and infection control concerns [11].

While parental presence at induction has been shown to reduce child anxiety, the impact is often modulated by the parent’s own preparedness [7–9]. Although all parents received coaching, we did not include a control group without coaching and therefore cannot determine the independent effect of coaching outcomes. In our study, the brief video-based coaching appeared to be sufficient for helping parents engage in effective VPPIA, with high satisfaction reported and minimal behavioral escalation observed among children. However, the high satisfaction rates and low incidence of negative behaviors suggest that the combination of coaching and VPPIA was well-tolerated and supported effective inductions.

Notably, while most children reported positive experiences with VPPIA, only 57.6% indicated they would recommend it to a friend. This discrepancy between subjective enjoyment and peer endorsement may reflect several factors, including children’s uncertainty about whether others would find the experience helpful, discomfort with the technology or setting, or limited recall due to the anesthetic context. These findings underscore the need for further qualitative research to better understand how pediatric patients interpret and express value judgments about perioperative interventions like VPPIA.

The logistics of implementation are also a key strength of VPPIA – bringing together anesthesia, perioperative staff, and research teams to deploy a low-resource intervention using existing iPads and Wi-Fi. These findings echo those from the Shriners Hospitals for Children-Canada mixed‑methods feasibility trial, which piloted continuous VR distraction from the waiting room through anesthesia induction [19]. While participants in that study found the approach acceptable and easy to use, the investigators reported frequent implementation barriers, including interruptions in over 90% of cases, surgical delays, and issues with mask seal or patient positioning. By contrast, our VPPIA model maintained uninterrupted connection, did not require any mask modification, and avoided common disruption points. Several factors likely contributed to this difference: VPPIA was implemented using hospital-owned iPads with built-in FaceTime software already familiar to clinical teams; research staff were present to facilitate each call and physically hold the device; and the intervention was integrated into routine workflows through close coordination with OR staff. Together, these design choices minimized the risk of technical or operational failure and supported seamless delivery of the intervention, offering a more robust and replicable model.

Despite being introduced into busy preoperative workflows at NCH, the VPPIA intervention was seamlessly integrated using existing hospital resources with minimal delay to OR start times or interference with clinical responsibilities, reinforcing its real-world practicality and low implementation burden. Although we did not formally assess implementation barriers using a structured framework, the feasibility data and staff-rated effectiveness offer useful insights into the conditions that support successful integration. This positions VPPIA as a practical adjunct to standard care, particularly in institutions facing staff shortages or seeking to limit in-room parent presence for operational or safety reasons. Unlike traditional parental presence, which can require additional PPE, physical space, and staff coordination, VPPIA can be conducted from any location with a mobile device and Wi-Fi access. This contrasts with more complex telehealth initiatives that require dedicated platforms, IT architecture, or regulatory changes.

This study demonstrated multiple strengths, including use of standardized behavioral assessments, integration within routine clinical workflows, and a low-resource implementation model that enhances scalability, ecological validity, and feasibility in real-world pediatric settings. By assessing multiple dimensions of feasibility, effectiveness, and satisfaction, this study provides a comprehensive view of VPPIA’s implementation potential. Another strength is that the intervention was implemented using existing mobile infrastructure, namely hospital-owned iPads and FaceTime software, without requiring dedicated telehealth platforms or complex IT support. This technical simplicity increases the potential scalability and accessibility of VPPIA, particularly in institutions with limited resources. The intervention was embedded within standard perioperative workflows at a large pediatric academic center, rather than an artificial research environment, further reinforcing its real-world applicability and enhancing its ecological validity. Moreover, the involvement of frontline anesthesia staff in rating outcomes further demonstrates that VPPIA can be adopted without substantial workflow modification.

Limitations include the lack of a control group, which precludes definitive conclusions about VPPIA’s efficacy relative to other interventions such as midazolam or physical parental presence. First, all participants received brief parental coaching to prepare parents for what to expect and how to support their child during induction. As a result, our study does not allow us to isolate the independent effects of coaching versus virtual presence alone since our primary aim was to evaluate the combined implementation of VPPIA with coaching as a pragmatic care model. Future studies should consider comparing this bundled approach to VPPIA without coaching to better understand the additive value of structured caregiver preparation. Second, the exclusion of children who received anxiolytics may have biased the sample toward children with lower baseline anxiety. Third, the sample was relatively homogenous in terms of clinical acuity, with over 90% of patients classified as ASA I, likely reflecting the exclusion of children who received anxiolytic premedication and the predominance of healthier patients undergoing routine procedures at NCH. This limits generalizability to more medically complex pediatric populations. Fourth, participation also required parental consent, and families who agreed to enroll may have been more comfortable with perioperative processes or less anxious at baseline, introducing potential self-selection bias that could further overestimate perceived effectiveness. Mask acceptance and child cooperation may also have been influenced by factors unrelated to VPPIA, such as individual temperament, procedure type, or staff communication style, which were not controlled for in this study. Furthermore, since this study focused solely on inhalational inductions, we did not evaluate VPPIA use in the context of total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA), which often involves IV placement using topical anesthetic agents. Future research should explore how VPPIA might be adapted to support children undergoing IV-based induction workflows. Finally, as a single-site study at a large pediatric academic center, the findings may not be generalizable to smaller community hospitals or settings with limited telecommunication infrastructure.

By piloting VPPIA within our institutional environment, we created a replicable model that other centers with basic mobile infrastructure and Wi-Fi capabilities can adopt, without requiring formal telehealth platforms. Our findings support the pragmatic integration of VPPIA into pediatric perioperative care, especially in contexts where in-person parental presence is restricted or impractical.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature by reinforcing prior findings on the feasibility and acceptability of VPPIA, while demonstrating that it can be implemented using standard hospital devices and workflows in a real-world perioperative setting. When paired with coaching, VPPIA appears to reduce patient distress and promote a smoother induction process without burdening perioperative workflows. These findings support the integration of VPPIA into routine pediatric anesthesia practice and highlight the value of coaching for parental engagement. Future studies should compare VPPIA to traditional parental presence and pharmacologic approaches in randomized designs and explore how VPPIA could be tailored for diverse surgical populations and settings.
