Data denoising with self consistency, variance maximization, and the Kantorovich dominance
Joshua Zoen-Git Hiew, Tongseok Lim, Brendan Pass, Marcelo Cruz de Souza

Abstract
We introduce a new framework for data denoising, partially inspired by martingale optimal transport. For a given noisy distribution (the data), our approach involves finding the closest distribution to it among all distributions which 1) have a particular prescribed structure (expressed by requiring they lie in a particular domain), and 2) are self-consistent with the data. We show that this amounts to maximizing the variance among measures in the domain which are dominated in convex order by the data. For particular choices of the domain, this problem and a relaxed version of it, in which the self-consistency condition is removed, are intimately related to various classical approaches to denoising. We prove that our general problem has certain desirable features: solutions exist under mild assumptions, have certain robustness properties, and, for very simple domains, coincide with solutions to the relaxed problem. We also introduce a novel relationship between distributions, termed Kantorovich dominance, which retains certain aspects of the convex order while being a weaker, more robust, and easier-to-verify condition. Building on this, we propose and analyze a new denoising problem by substituting the convex order in the previously described framework with Kantorovich dominance. We demonstrate that this revised problem shares some characteristics with the full convex order problem but offers enhanced stability, greater computational efficiency, and, in specific domains, more meaningful solutions. Finally, we present simple numerical examples illustrating solutions for both the full convex order problem and the Kantorovich dominance problem.

2
General problem formulation and basic properties

Let
{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
denote the set of all probability measures on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with finite second moments, and let
{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=\{\mu\in{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\mid\int x\,d\mu(x)=0\}
represent the set of centered probability measures in
{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. In this paper, all probability measures are assumed to be in
{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, unless stated otherwise.

For
\mu,\nu\in{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the Wasserstein distance
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\mu,\nu)
between
\mu
and

{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|x-y|^{2}\,d\pi(x,y),

(3)

where
{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)=\big({\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\mu,\nu)\big)^{2}
, and
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e.,

\Pi(\mu,\nu)=\{\pi={\cal L}(X,Y)\mid{\cal L}(X)=\mu,\,{\cal L}(Y)=\nu\},

where
{\cal L}(X)
denotes the law (distribution) of the random variable
X

[
22
]
,
[
24
]
. We denote
{\cal L}(X)=\mu
and
X\sim\mu
interchangeably.
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm.

In a typical application, we will assume
Y\sim\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is the given data distribution, satisfying the following model assumption:

Y=X+R,

(4)

where
X\sim\rho\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
R\sim\epsilon\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Our goal is to recover
\rho
from the observed data
\nu
perturbed by
\epsilon
. With prior knowledge or constraint about
\rho
, we assume it belongs to a known domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Given
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we thus arrive at the following problem (which is simply (
1
) rewritten in probabilistic notation):

\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}}\min_{\pi\in{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[|X-Y|^{2}],

(5)

where
{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)
represents the set of martingale couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
:

{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)=\{\pi={\cal L}(X,Y)\mid{\cal L}(X)=\mu,\,{\cal L}(Y)=\nu,\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[Y|X]=X\}.

(6)

The martingale condition is often referred to as
self-consistency
in the statistics literature. In the generic model (
4
), it says that conditional on
X
, the average noise vanishes,
{\mathbb{E}}[R|X]=0
.

We say that
\mu
and
\nu
in
{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
are in convex order, denoted by
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu
, if
\int f\,d\mu\leq\int f\,d\nu
for any convex function
f
. Strassen’s theorem states that
{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu
.

If
\pi\in{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)
is a martingale coupling of
X\sim\mu
and
Y\sim\nu
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[XY]={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[X{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[Y|X]]={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[|X|^{2}]
. Therefore, if
\mu,\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, it follows that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[|X-Y|^{2}]={\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}[|Y|^{2}]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}[|X|^{2}]=\Var(\nu)-\Var(\mu),

where
\Var(\mu)
denotes the variance of
\mu
, defined as
\Var(\mu)=\int|x-\int x\,d\mu(x)|^{2}d\mu(x)
.

Since
\nu
is given and fixed, this shows that problem (
5

\max_{\mu\in{\cal D},\,\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu}\Var(\mu).

(7)

Remark 1
.

Since
{\cal D}
typically consists of measures supported on low dimensional spaces (for instance, curves), this formulation appears to achieve a spectacular dimensional reduction, as it involves optimizing over
\mu\in{\cal D}
, rather than

with
\mu\in{\cal D}
and
\pi\in{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)

involves
\nu

Remark 2
.

We have
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu
if and only if

\int f\,d\mu\leq\int f\,d\nu
for every Lipschitz convex function
f
.
To see this, assume the inequality holds for all Lipschitz convex

be any convex lower semicontinuous function. Then there exists a sequence of Lipschitz convex functions (indeed, one may take piecewise affine convex functions)

which increases to

\int g_{n}\,d\mu\leq\int g_{n}\,d\nu\leq\int g\,d\nu

and applying the monotone convergence theorem yields

\int g\,d\mu\leq\int g\,d\nu
,
which is the convex order condition.

Note that, with the definition of Wasserstein distance in hand, we can rewrite the relaxed problem (

\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu).

\displaystyle{{\cal W}_{2}}(\mu^{*},\rho)

\displaystyle\leq{{\cal W}_{2}}(\mu^{*},\nu)+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}[|X-Y|^{2}]}+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho),\quad\text{for any}\,\pi\in{\cal M}(\mu^{*},\nu)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\Var(\nu)-\Var(\mu^{*})}+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\Var(\nu)-\Var(\rho)}+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho),

where the last inequality follows from the optimality of
\mu^{*}
in problem (
7
).
∎

3
The Kantorovich dominance relation

Despite its conceptual appeal, the learning problem with self consistency (
5
) (or equivalently (
7
)) has certain drawbacks: the convex order constraint is difficult to check, the problem may be unstable (recall Example
4
), and the intersection of the certain natural domains
{\cal D}
with the set
\{\mu\,|\,\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu\}
may be nearly empty, and thus not contain a reasonable solution (see, for example, the application in Section
4.4
).

These challenges prompt us to introduce a weaker relation, the Kantorovich dominance relation, which still captures some aspects of the martingale property, while avoiding some of the computational and theoretical difficulties associated with the convex order relation.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the inner product. For
\mu,\nu\in{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we say that
\mu
is less than
\nu
in the Kantorovich dominance relation (KDR), and write
\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
, if there is
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that

\int\langle x-b_{\nu},y-x\rangle d\pi(x,y)=0,\ \text{ where }\ b_{\nu}=\int y\,d\nu(y)\text{ is the barycenter of }\nu.

(17)

Let
T_{k}(x)=x+k
be a translation map. (
17
) says
\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
iff the translated measures
\mu^{\prime}={T_{-b_{\nu}}}_{\#}(\mu)
and
\nu^{\prime}={T_{-b_{\nu}}}_{\#}(\nu)
(thus
\nu^{\prime}
is centered) admits a coupling
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
such that

\int\langle x,y-x\rangle\,d\pi^{\prime}(x,y)=0.

By definition, the KDR relation is translation invariant: if
\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
, then for any common shift vector
a
, the translated measures
\mu_{a}:=(T_{a})_{\#}\mu
and
\nu_{a}:=(T_{a})_{\#}\nu
also satisfy
\mu_{a}\preceq_{\rm K}\nu_{a}
. Accordingly, throughout this paper we assume for simplicity that
\nu
is centered (i.e.,
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
), unless stated otherwise. Since
\nu
represents the given data distribution, this centering step is straightforward in practice.

Remark 5
.

If
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu
, then there exists a martingale coupling
\pi\in{\cal M}(\mu,\nu)
such that

\int\langle x,y-x\rangle d\pi(x,y)=\int\langle x,\Big(\int(y-x)d\pi_{x}(y)\Big)\rangle d\mu(x)=0.

Therefore, the KDR is a relaxation of the convex order relation, capturing a weaker form of the martingale property. Precisely,
v(x):=\int(y-x)d\pi_{x}(y)
for some coupling
\pi
, the KDR requires only that
v
is orthogonal to
\id
in
L^{2}(\mu)
.

A key part of the motivation for the Kantorovich dominance is that it arises as a sort of optimality condition for the relaxed problem (
8
) for a certain class of domains (see Theorem
4.9
below), analagously to the way that self consistency arises as an optimality condition for particularly simple domains (Proposition
2.2
).

Remark 6
.

If
\nu
is centered, for
\pi=\mu\otimes\nu
(the product measure), we have

\int\langle x,y-x\rangle d\pi(x,y)=\langle\int x\,d\mu(x),\int y\,d\nu(y)\rangle-\int|x|^{2}d\mu(x)=-\int|x|^{2}d\mu(x)\leq 0.

Since the image of the weakly continuous mapping
\pi\mapsto\int\langle x,y-x\rangle d\pi(x,y)
over the connected set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is itself connected, we conclude that (
17
) is equivalent to

D(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y-x\rangle d\pi(x,y)\geq 0.

(18)

(
18
) motivates the term
Kantorovich dominance
, as it shows that the "Kantorovich cost"
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi
dominates (i.e., is greater than or equal to) the second moment of
\mu
.

Since
\displaystyle{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)=\int|x|^{2}d\mu+\int|y|^{2}d\nu-2\sup_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi
, (
18
) is equivalent to

{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)\leq\int|y|^{2}d\nu-\int|x|^{2}d\mu.

(19)

It follows immediately from (
19
) that the set of probability measures dominated by
\nu

{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}:=\{\mu\in{\cal P}_{2}(\Omega)\mid\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu\}

is closed with respect to
{{\cal W}_{2}}
-metric for any compact
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. However, in general, the set

{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}:=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\mid\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu\}

is not closed with respect to the
{{\cal W}_{2}}
metric (see later discussion).

Let

\Omega=\text{con}(\mathop{\rm spt}(\nu))
. Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}(\Omega)
. Motivated by our earlier discussion, given a domain
{\cal D}\subset{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we now study the following variance maximization problem:

\max_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}}\Var(\mu).

(22)

Theorem 4.1
.

i)

If
{\cal D}\subset{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is closed in the
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-metric and
{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
non-empty, then problem (
22
) admits a solution.

ii)

Let
\mu^{*}
be any solution to (
22
). Then for any
\rho\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}\cap{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have:

{{\cal W}_{2}}(\mu^{*},\rho)\leq\sqrt{\int|y|^{2}d\nu-\int|z|^{2}d\rho}+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho).

Consequently, problem (
22
) exhibits a denoising property, meaning that as the noise in the measure
\nu
decreases, the optimal solution
\mu^{*}
tends to recover the original measure.

i) Since
{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
is compact in the
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-metric by Corollary
3.2
, and
{\cal D}
is
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-closed, it follows that
{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
is also
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-compact. The continuity of the variance functional with respect to the
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
-metric guarantees that problem (
22
) admits a solution.

ii) The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

\displaystyle{{\cal W}_{2}}(\mu^{*},\rho)

\displaystyle\leq{{\cal W}_{2}}(\mu^{*},\nu)+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\int|y|^{2}d\nu-\int|x|^{2}d\mu^{*}}+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho)\leq\sqrt{\int|y|^{2}d\nu-\int|z|^{2}d\rho}+{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu,\rho),

where the second inequality is by the KDR and the last inequality follows from the optimality of
\mu^{*}
, which gives
{\mathbb{E}}_{\rho}[|X|^{2}]=\Var(\rho)\leq\Var(\mu^{*})={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{*}}[|X|^{2}]-|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{*}}[X]|^{2}\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu^{*}}[|X|^{2}]
.
∎

4.1
Stability

Under a certain condition on
{\cal D}
, we now show problem (
22
) is stable, in contrast to (
5
), as shown in Example
4
. The hypothesis we will impose on
{\cal D}
in the following definitions is quite weak; in particular, it is satisfied by all domains we have considered.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. We say that
{\cal D}
is
approachable from the interior with respect to
\nu
if, for any
\mu\in{\cal D}
with
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
and
\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
, there exists a sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}\subset{\cal D}
such that
{{\cal W}_{2}}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
and
D(\mu_{n},\nu)>0
in (
18
) for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

The class of domains that are closed under contractions serves as a key example of domains that can be approached from the interior.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\lambda_{\#}\mu
denote the
dilation
of
\mu
by
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.,
\lambda X\sim\lambda_{\#}\mu
when
X\sim\mu
.
We say that
{\cal D}
is
closed under contractions
if
\lambda_{\#}\mu\in{\cal D}
for any
\mu\in{\cal D}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
{\cal D}
is closed under contractions, then for any
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2,0}(\Omega)
and
\delta_{0}\neq\mu\in{\cal D}
with
\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
, there is a sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}\subset{\cal D}
such that
W_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)\rightarrow 0
and
D(\mu_{n},\nu)>0
.

If
D(\mu,\nu)>0
there is nothing to prove. If not, there exists some
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
with
\int\langle x,y-x\rangle d\pi(x,y)=0
. Let
\lambda_{n}
be an increasing sequence of positive numbers converging to
1
and choose
\mu_{n}=\lambda_{n\#}\mu
and
\pi_{n}=\big((x,y)\mapsto(\lambda_{n}x,y)\big)_{\#}\pi
, so that
\int\langle x,y-x\rangle d\pi_{n}(x,y)=\lambda_{n}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)-\lambda_{n}^{2}\int|x|^{2}d\mu(x)>\lambda_{n}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)-\lambda_{n}\int|x|^{2}d\mu(x)=0
.
∎

The following result illustrates a stability property inherent to problem (
22
), where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is not required to be compact.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2,0}(\Omega)
and assume that
{\cal D}
is approachable from the interior w.r.t.
\nu
. Let
\{\nu_{n}\}\subset{\cal P}_{2}(\Omega)
such that
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\nu_{n},\nu)\to 0
. Let
\mu_{n}
be a solution to (
22
) with
\nu_{n}
. If
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
for some
\mu
, then
\mu
is a solution to (
22
) with
\nu
.

Consider first an arbitrary
\xi\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
satisfying
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(\xi,\nu)<\int|y|^{2}d\nu(y)-\int|x|^{2}d\xi(x)
. By the strict inequality, for all large
n
we have
\xi\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu_{n},\Omega}^{K}
. By optimality of
\mu_{n}
in (
22
) with
\nu_{n}
, we have
\Var(\mu_{n})\geq\Var(\xi)
; taking limits then yields
\Var(\mu)\geq\Var(\xi)
.

Now consider any
\xi\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
. Letting
\{\xi_{m}\}\subset{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
approximate
\xi
such that
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(\xi_{m},\nu)<\int|y|^{2}d\nu-\int|x|^{2}d\xi_{m}
, the above argument yields
\Var(\mu)\geq\Var(\xi_{m})
; taking limits implies
\Var(\mu)\geq\Var(\xi)
. As
\xi\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
is arbitrary, optimality of
\mu
follows.
∎

Remark 8
.

A natural question is whether a quantitative version of the approachable from the interior condition yields quantitative stability of (
22
). Namely, suppose we require that for every
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
in
{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu,\Omega}^{K}
the existence of an approximating sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}\subset{\cal D}
as in Definition
4.2
with the additional condition that
f({\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\mu_{n},\mu))<f(D(\mu_{n},\nu))
, for some suitable strictly increasing continuous function
f:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, with
f(0)=0
. Does this then imply that
g({\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\hat{\mu},\tilde{\mu}))<g({\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\hat{\nu},\tilde{\nu}))
, where
\hat{\mu}
and
\tilde{\mu}
are solutions in (
22
) for
\hat{\nu}
and
\tilde{\nu}
, respectively, for an appropriate, strictly increasing continuous functions
g:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, with
g(0)=0
?

Like related issues regarding the quantitative stability of optimal transport (see for instance
[
9
]
), this question seems very challenging. Our proof of Theorem
4.5
does not seem to provide a particular insight into how one might approach the quantitative stability question, and its resolution will likely require significant new ideas and techniques.

4.2
Conic domains and equivalence with the relaxed problem

We establish the equivalence between the problem (
22
) and the relaxed problem (
8
) under certain structural assumptions on
{\cal D}
. Notably, this result applies even when
\mathop{\rm spt}(\nu)=\Omega
is not compact, ensuring the existence and stability of solutions in such cases as well.

Definition 4.6
.

{\cal D}\subset{\cal P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is called a
cone
if
\lambda_{\#}\mu\in{\cal D}
for any
\mu\in{\cal D}
and
\lambda\geq 0
.
{\cal D}
is called
translation invariant
if
{T_{k}}_{\#}\mu\in{\cal D}
for any
\mu\in{\cal D}
and
k\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, where
T_{k}(x):=x+k
.

It is worth noting that problem (
22
) is stable on cone domains, as these domains are closed under contraction.

Many of the domains we are interested in are cones and translation invariant, including those in Examples
2
and
3
, as well as measures supported on lines and multivariate Gaussians as will be explored in Section
4.4
below. Note that the domain in Example
1
is not a cone.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in{\cal P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
\mu_{k}:={T_{k}}_{\#}\mu
denote the translation of
\mu
by
k
. Among all translations
\mu_{k}
, the one that minimizes the
{{\cal W}_{2}}
distance to
\nu
is the one for which

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\,d\mu_{k}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,d\nu(y).

(23)

The
{{\cal W}_{2}}
distance between
\mu_{k}
and
\nu
is given by

{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu_{k},\nu)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|(x+k)-y|^{2}d\pi(x,y),

where
\pi
is an optimal transport plan between
\mu
and
\nu
. Differentiating with respect to
k
and setting the derivative equal to zero gives
k=\int yd\nu(y)-\int xd\mu(x)
.
Thus, the translation
\mu_{k^{*}}
that minimizes the
{{\cal W}_{2}}
distance satisfies (
23
), as claimed.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
{\cal D}
be a cone. For any
\mu^{*}\in\argmax_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}}\Var(\mu)
and
\pi^{*}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{*},\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)
, the following equality holds:

\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi^{*}(x,y)=\int|x|^{2}d\mu^{*}(x).

(24)

If, in addition,
{\cal D}
is translation invariant, then
\mu^{*}
must be centered;
\int x\,d\mu^{*}(x)=0
.

By the KDR (
18
), for any
\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}
and for any corresponding optimal coupling
\pi^{*}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)
, we have the inequality:

\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi^{*}(x,y)\geq\int|x|^{2}d\mu(x).

(25)

Suppose this inequality is strict for some
\mu^{*}\in\argmax_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}}\Var(\mu)
. As
{\cal D}
is a cone,
\mu^{*}_{\lambda}=\lambda_{\#}\mu^{*}\in{\cal D}
for any
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
\pi^{*}_{\lambda}=\big((x,y)\mapsto(\lambda x,y)\big)_{\#}\pi^{*}
be the corresponding coupling of
\mu^{*}_{\lambda}
and
\nu
.
If the inequality (
25
) is strict, there exists a
\lambda>1
such that:

\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi^{*}_{\lambda}=\lambda\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi^{*}\geq\lambda^{2}\int|x|^{2}d\mu^{*}=\int|x|^{2}d\mu^{*}_{\lambda},

meaning that
\mu^{*}_{\lambda}\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
. However, the variance of
\mu^{*}_{\lambda}
exceeds that of
\mu^{*}
(unless
\mu^{*}=\delta_{0}
, in which case the result is trivial), contradicting the optimality of
\mu^{*}\in\argmax_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}}\Var(\mu)
. We conclude that the inequality (
25
) must hold with equality for any optimal
\mu^{*}
and
\pi^{*}
.

To see
\int xd\mu^{*}=0
if
{\cal D}
is translation invariant, let
k=-\int xd\mu^{*}
and
\mu^{*}_{k}={T_{k}}_{\#}\mu^{*}
be defined as in Lemma
4.7
. Setting a coupling
\pi^{*}_{k}=\big((x,y)\mapsto(x+k,y)\big)_{\#}\pi^{*}
of
\mu^{*}_{k}
and
\nu
and recalling that
\nu
is centered, the following calculation

\int|x|^{2}d\mu_{k}^{*}=\int|x+k|^{2}d\mu^{*}\leq\int|x|^{2}d\mu^{*}=\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi^{*}=\int\langle x+k,y\rangle d\pi^{*}=\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi^{*}_{k}

shows
\mu_{k}^{*}\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
. With this,
\Var(\mu_{k}^{*})=\Var(\mu^{*})
implies
\mu_{k}^{*}
is also optimal. Now
k\neq 0
yields strict inequality above, which contradicts (
24
). We conclude
k=0
.
∎

We can now extend the solution existence result for the problem (
22
) when
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and let
{\cal D}
be a cone. Suppose that
{\cal D}
is either translation invariant or
{\cal D}\subset{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Then, the problem (
22
) with
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is equivalent to the relaxed problem (
8
), meaning that both formulations have the same set of solutions.

For any
\displaystyle\mu^{*}\in\argmin_{\mu\in{\cal D}}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
and
\displaystyle\pi^{*}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu^{*},\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)
, we claim

\int\langle x,y-x\rangle\,d\pi^{*}(x,y)=0,

(26)

that is,
\mu^{*}\preceq_{\rm K}\nu
. To see this, for any
\mu\in{\cal D}\setminus\{\delta_{0}\}
, consider a rescaling factor
\lambda^{*}
that solves

\min_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}{\mathcal{W}}_{2}^{2}(\lambda_{\#}\mu,\nu)=\min_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\lambda^{2}\int|x|^{2}d\mu-2\lambda\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)+\int|y|^{2}d\nu\right).

The optimal scaling is attained at:

\lambda^{*}=\frac{\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)}{\int|x|^{2}d\mu}.

Since
\lambda^{*}=1
for the optimal
\mu^{*}
(no rescaling improves the objective function), (
26
) follows.

Next, by the assumption on
{\cal D}
and Lemma
4.7
,
\int xd\mu^{*}=\int yd\nu=0
. This and (
26
) imply

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}}{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}\cap{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}\cap{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\left(\int|y|^{2}d\nu-\int|x|^{2}d\mu\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Var(\nu)-\max_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu}^{K}}\Var(\mu),

where we have used Lemma
4.8
to remove
\bigcap{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
in (
27
). This shows any solution to the relaxed problem (
8
) is also a solution to (
22
) with
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Conversely, if
\mu^{*}
solves (
22
), the equality of values (
27
) and Lemma
4.8
imply that
\mu^{*}
solves (
8
), completing the proof.
∎

Remark 9
.

Observe that the proofs of Lemma
4.8
and Theorem
4.9
do not rely on full translation invariance of
\mathcal{D}
. Instead, the following weaker condition suffices (recalling that these results assume
\nu
is centered): for any
\mu\in\mathcal{D}
with
k=-\int xd\mu(x)
, the centered measure
\mu_{k}={T_{k}}_{\#}\mu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
also belongs to
\mathcal{D}
.

4.3
Weak optimizer closedness and equivalence to the problem with self-consistency

We have shown that for appropriate domains, the problem (
22
) is equivalent to the fully relaxed problem (
8
). We now consider when (
22
) is equivalent to the problem with the full self-consistency condition (
5
).
Recall the
\pi
-conditional barycentric map from Definition
2.1
.

Definition 4.10
.

Let
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}(\Omega)
and
{\cal D}\subset{\cal P}_{2}(\Omega)
.
We say that
{\cal D}
is closed under weak optimizers if,
for any
\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}^{K}_{\nu}
that solves (
22
), there exists
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that

\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)\geq\int|x|^{2}d\mu(x)\quad\text{and}\quad{c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu)\in{\cal D}.

(28)

For instance, the domains
{\cal D}^{m}
and
{\cal D}^{m}_{u}
in Example
3
are closed under weak optimizers.

Theorem 4.11
.

If
{\cal D}
is closed under weak optimizers, then every optimizer
\mu
for the problem (
22
) satisfies
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu
, and consequently,
\mu
solves the original problem (
7
).

Assume that
\mu
solves (
22
), so there exists
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
satisfying (
28
). Since
\gamma=\big((x,y)\mapsto(c_{\pi}(x),y)\big)_{\#}\pi\in{\cal M}({c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu),\nu)
is a martingale measure, we have

\int|x|^{2}d{c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu)-\int\langle x,y\rangle d\gamma=0\geq\int|x|^{2}d\mu-\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi.

Now, as the barycenter minimizes the expected squared distance, we have the inequality

\int\frac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}d\gamma(x,y)\leq\int\frac{1}{2}|x-y|^{2}d\pi(x,y),

with strict inequality if
{c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu)\neq\mu
. After canceling
\int|y|^{2}d\gamma=\int|y|^{2}d\pi=\int|y|^{2}d\nu
, we get

\int\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}+\langle x,y\rangle\right)d\gamma\geq\int\left(-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2}+\langle x,y\rangle\right)d\pi,

with strict inequality if
{c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu)\neq\mu
. Adding this to the previous inequality gives

\Var({c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu))=\int|x|^{2}d{c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu)\geq\int|x|^{2}d\mu\geq\Var(\mu).

The optimality of
\mu
implies equality. Hence
{c_{\pi}}_{\#}(\mu)=\mu
, which implies
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu
.
∎

4.4
Relationship with principal component analysis

In this section, we explore the relationship between our formulation (
22
) and principal component analysis (PCA). Let
V_{m}
denote the set of all
m
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Consider the following cone domain:

{\cal D}_{m}:=\{\mu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,|\,\mu(E)=1\text{ for some }E\in V_{m}\}.

Let
p_{E}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to E
denote the orthogonal projection map onto a subspace
E
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Any
\mu
solving the problem
\displaystyle\max_{\mu\in{\cal D}_{m},\,\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu}\Var(\mu)
, where
\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, is the orthogonal projection of
\nu
onto some
E\in V_{m}
.

Theorem
4.9
shows the problem
\max_{\mu\in{\cal D}_{m},\,\mu\preceq_{\rm K}\nu}\Var(\mu)
is equivalent to the relaxed problem
\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}_{m}}{\cal W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
. Noting
{\cal D}_{m}=\cup_{L\in V_{m}}{\cal D}_{m}^{E}
, where
{\cal D}_{m}^{E}:=\{\mu\in{\cal D}_{m}\,|\,\mu(E)=1\}
, we can decompose the relaxed problem as
\min_{E\in V_{m}}\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}_{m}^{E}}{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)
. The lemma follows by the fact that the projection
{p_{E}}_{\#}\nu
is the unique minimizer of
\min_{\mu\in{\cal D}_{m}^{E}}{{\cal W}_{2}^{2}}(\mu,\nu)
.
∎

Lemma
4.12
reveals that PCA can be viewed as a particular case of problem (
22
) with the domain
{\cal D}_{1}
. Specifically, the first principal component is defined as the direction that maximizes the variance of the projected data. Lemma
4.12
demonstrates that the projected data satisfies the variance maximization problem under the Kantorovich dominance constraint.

We now consider the following version of the PCA in
[
6
, Section 3.3]
. Consider the model

Y=L^{*}W+R,

(29)

where
W\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{m})
is an
m
-dimensional Gaussian vector of latent factors,
L^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times m}
is an unknown factor loading matrix of rank
m
, and
R\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
represents random noise that is independent of
W
and cannot be explained by the latent factor. Without loss of generality, assume that
L^{*}=U^{*}(\Lambda^{*})^{1/2}
, where the columns of
U^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times m}
form an orthonormal set, and
\Lambda^{*}={\rm diag}[\lambda_{1}^{*},\dots,\lambda_{m}^{*}]
is a diagonal matrix with
\lambda_{1}^{*}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{m}^{*}>0
.

Let
\nu={\cal L}(Y)
. We now focus on solving the problem (
22
) over the following cone domain

\displaystyle{\cal D}=\{\mu_{L}

\displaystyle={\cal L}(LW)\mid L=U\Lambda^{1/2},\text{ where the columns of }U\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times m}\text{ are orthonormal,}

(30)

\displaystyle\text{and }\Lambda={\rm diag}[\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}]\text{ is a diagonal matrix with }\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{m}\geq 0\}.

Note that the problem (
22
) remains equivalent to the relaxed problem (
8
), by Theorem
4.9
.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
\nu_{n},\nu\in{\cal P}_{2,0}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
{{\cal W}_{2}}(\nu_{n},\nu)\to 0
. Then for all
n
,
{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu_{n}}^{K}\neq\emptyset
with
{\cal D}
in (
30
), and for any
\displaystyle\mu_{L_{n}}\in\argmax_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu_{n}}^{K}}\Var(\mu)
with
L_{n}=U_{n}\Lambda_{n}^{1/2}
,

L_{n}L_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}-\sigma_{n}^{2}U_{n}U_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\to L^{*}{L^{*}}^{\mathsf{T}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{n}^{2}\to\sigma^{2}\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty,

(31)

where
\sigma_{n}^{2}:=\frac{1}{d-m}\int|y-U_{n}U_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}y|^{2}d\nu_{n}(y)
is an estimator of the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Remark 10
.

(
31
) indicates that the optimization problem
\max_{\mu\in{\cal D}\cap{\cal M}_{\nu_{n}}^{K}}\Var(\mu)
can recover
{\cal L}(L^{*}W)
as the empirical distribution
\nu_{n}
converges to the population distribution
\nu
.
This result cannot hold if the full convex order constraint is imposed. In many practical cases, such as when
\nu_{n}
is discrete (e.g.,
\nu_{n}
is an empirical measure sampled from
\nu
), the convex order condition
\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu_{n}
fails for any Gaussian
\mu\in{\cal D}
unless
\mu=\delta_{0}
. As a result, the domain
{\cal D}\cap\{\mu\preceq_{\rm C}\nu_{n}\}
reduces to
\{\delta_{0}\}
.

Lemma 4.14
.

For
{\cal D}
in (
30
), the set
{\cal D}\cap\{\mu\mid\Var(\mu)\leq\delta\}
is
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-compact for any
\delta>0
.

For any
\mu_{L}\in{\cal D}
with
L=U\Lambda^{1/2}
,
\Var(\mu_{L})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}
. Thus,
\max_{i}\lambda_{i}\leq\delta
.
∎

Lemma 4.15

To approximate a solution to (

. These examples use the constant weight on the points of
\mu

\mu=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta_{x_{i}}
.
\pi
represents a transport plan / coupling between the variable
\mu
and the data
\nu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{y_{j}}

\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}=\frac{1}{m}

\sum_{i}\pi_{ij}=\frac{1}{n}
for all

The optimization begins with an initial set
x^{0}
satisfying the constraints
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}x_{i}=0
and
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq 1
and the domain constraint (e.g., the set of PCA projections in both examples). At each iteration
t\geq 1
, we perform the following two steps, repeating until convergence. The final output is the pair
(x,\pi)
, where
x
is rescaled by the factor
\hat{\lambda}
given in (
36

Step 1.
Given
x
, find
\pi
that solves

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi(1/m,1/n)}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}\left\langle x_{i},y_{j}\right\rangle\quad\text{st.}\quad\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}x_{i}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq 1.

Step 2.
Given
\pi

and find

that solves

\displaystyle\max_{x\in{\cal D}}\sum_{i}\left\langle x_{i},\bar{y}_{i}\right\rangle\quad\text{st.}\quad\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}x_{i}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq 1.

In Step 1, note that the constraints on
\pi
are satisfied by construction, since the marginal condition imposes
\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}x_{i}=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{m}x_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\pi_{ij}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{1}{m}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq 1
. Thus, the problem falls into the class of traditional optimal transport with fixed marginals. To solve it, in both examples we apply the Sinkhorn method, as noted in Subsection
5.1
. Then to solve Step 2 in Subsection
5.2
, we explicitly state the domain constraint on

as follows:

\displaystyle\max_{x\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\langle x_{i},\bar{y}_{i}\right\rangle\quad\text{st.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}=0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq 1,\ \sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left\|x_{i+1}-x_{i}\right\|\leq B.

Since the second constraint is clearly binding, together with the third constraints it implies that any solution to the above satisfies the domain constraint
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}\left\|x_{i+1}-x_{i}\right\|}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}}\leq B
. We convert this problem into a second-order conic program, which can be solved efficiently by interior point methods – see

3
]
for an overview. To do that, we define the variable to be optimized as

(x_{1}^{1},\ldots,x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{m}^{1},\ldots,x_{m}^{d},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m-1})

where the superscript in

denotes the dimensional component and

is an auxiliary variable. The second-moment constraint on

is replaced by the equivalent

\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_{i}^{k})^{2}}\leq 1.

The constraints are equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle x_{1}^{k}+\ldots+x_{m}^{k}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle k=1,\ldots,d,

\displaystyle\left\|x_{1}^{1}\ldots x_{1}^{d}\ldots x_{m}^{1}\ldots x_{m}^{d}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq 1,

\displaystyle\left\|x_{i+1}-x_{i}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq a_{i}

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,m-1,

\displaystyle a_{1}+\ldots+a_{m-1}

\displaystyle=B.

We solve the problem in this format using Python and CVXOPT package.

In Step 2 of Subsection
5.3
, we replace the domain constraint
\phi\left(\mu\right)\leq B
by a linear penalization together with another, inner iteration loop, as follows. Call
a_{i}=x_{i+1}-x_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
, and
e_{i}=\frac{a_{i}}{\left|a_{i}\right|}-\frac{a_{i-1}}{\left|a_{i-1}\right|}
for
i=2,\ldots,m-1
. Notice that
\phi(\mu)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}\left\|e_{i}\right\|^{2}
. At iteration
t
, the problem is solved for

\phi^{t}
defined as

\displaystyle\phi^{0}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\phi^{t}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}\left\langle e_{i}^{t},\frac{x_{i+i}^{t-1}-x_{i}}{{\left|a_{i}\right|}^{t-1}}-\frac{x_{i}-x_{i-1}^{t-1}}{{\left|a_{i-1}\right|}^{t-1}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}\varphi_{i}^{t}x_{i}+\text{constant},

where
\varphi_{i}^{t}=-\frac{1}{4}e_{i}^{t}(\frac{1}{{\left|a_{i}\right|}^{t-1}}+\frac{1}{{\left|a_{i-1}\right|}^{t-1}})
. The penalized problem is written as

\displaystyle\max_{x\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{m}}\sum_{i}\left\langle x_{i},c_{i}\right\rangle\quad\text{st.}\quad\sum_{i}x_{i}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i}\left\|x_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq m,

where
c_{i}=\bar{y}_{i}-\lambda\varphi_{i}^{t}
for some penalization multiplier
\lambda
. We solve it through the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(x,\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{k}c_{i}^{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha^{k}x_{i}^{k}-\beta\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}^{k})^{2}-m\Big].

The first derivatives are

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{x_{i}^{k}}\left(x,\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=c_{i}^{k}-\alpha^{k}-2\beta x_{i}^{k},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\alpha^{k}}\left(x,\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}^{k},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\left(x,\alpha,\beta\right)

\displaystyle=m-\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}^{k})^{2}.

We can assume that the second-moment condition is binding and
\beta>0
,
since otherwise it would be possible to increase the objective function.
The first order conditions imply

\displaystyle\left(i\right)\ c_{i}^{k}-\alpha^{k}-2\beta x_{i}^{k}=0\implies\hat{x}_{i}^{k}=\frac{1}{2\beta}\Big(c_{i}^{k}-\alpha^{k}\Big)\quad\forall k,i,

\displaystyle\left(ii\right)\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{x}_{i}^{k}=\frac{1}{2\beta}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{k}-\alpha^{k}\Big)=0\implies\alpha^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{k}\quad\forall k,

\displaystyle\left(iii\right)\ \sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat{x}_{i}^{k})^{2}=\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(c_{i}^{k}-\alpha^{k}\Big)=m

\displaystyle\qquad\implies\beta=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(c_{i}^{k}-\alpha^{k}\Big)^{2}}.

Replacing
\alpha
and
\beta
gives
x

as a function of
c
. Finally, we update
x^{t}
partially at each iteration, as
x^{t}=\epsilon x_{\text{opt}}^{t}+\left(1-\epsilon\right)x^{t-1}
where
x_{\text{opt}}^{t}
solves the problem for
\phi^{t}
. The loop stops when the sequence
\left(x^{t}\right)
converges to a fixed point
x^{*}
,
and we get

\phi^{*}\left(\mu\right)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=2}^{m-1}\left\langle e_{i}^{*},\frac{x_{i+i}^{*}-x_{i}^{*}}{{\left|a_{i}\right|}^{*}}-\frac{x_{i}^{*}-x_{i-1}^{*}}{{\left|a_{i-1}\right|}^{*}}\right\rangle=\phi\left(\mu\right).
