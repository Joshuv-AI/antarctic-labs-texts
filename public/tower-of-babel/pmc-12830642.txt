Chromosome-level genome assembly of predatory ladybug Coccinella transversoguttata
Authors: Dong Xiang, Jinge Yuan, Zhen Wang, Mengmeng Zhao, Haozhuo Yang, Qiao Liu, Huai Liu, Hanqiu Chen, Wenfeng Wang, Yaying Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coccinella transversoguttata is a crucial beneficial insect within Coccinellidae, playing a significant role in biological pest control and maintaining ecological balance. Given its adaptability to agricultural ecosystems and natural regulation of pest populations, C. transversoguttata could serve as a valuable model for genetic research in the family Coccinellidae and contribute to the development of sustainable pest management strategies. Here, we report a chromosome-level genome assembly of C. transversoguttata with a genome size of 453.64 Mb and a contig N50 length of 22.99 Mb. The genome contained 16,464 protein-coding genes, of which 14,286 genes (86.77%) were functionally annotated. Additionally, we identified 309.93 Mb (68.32%) of the assembled sequences are repetitive sequences. Our study provides a reliable genome resource, and lays a solid foundation for understanding the evolution, adaptation, and genetic improvement of ladybird beetles within Coccinellidae.

Background & Summary

The family Coccinellidae, commonly known as ladybird beetles, encompasses numerous species that play a crucial ecological role as natural enemies of pests, especially aphids, scale insects, and mites1. They are widely distributed across the globe and are of great significance in maintaining ecological balance and promoting sustainable agriculture2. Coccinella transversoguttata is a notable member of this family. It is well-recognized for its distinctive appearance and active foraging behavior3. This species has a broad distribution range, inhabiting various ecosystems from agricultural fields to grasslands3. Its adaptability to different environments and high predation efficiency make it a key subject for ecological and biological control research4. Despite its importance, the lack of comprehensive genomic information on C. transversoguttata has limited our understanding of its genetic mechanisms underlying traits such as pest-hunting behavior, environmental adaptation, and population dynamics.

To address this gap, we present a chromosome-level genome assembly of C. transversoguttata. We generated high-quality genomic data using advanced sequencing technologies. The assembled genome has a size of 453.64 Mb, with a contig N50 length of 22.99 Mb (Table 1). Methods

Library construction and genome sequencing

The original population of C. transversoguttata was collected from Gandan Chuguozhen, Linzhou County, Lhasa City, Xizang Autonomous Region (N 29°53′44.794″, E 91°15′35.060″), and was reared on Peanut aphid (Aphis craccivora) in the incubators of the Xizang Autonomous Region Academy of Agricultural and Animal Husbandry Sciences, China. The rearing conditions are maintained at a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, a relative humidity of 70 ± 5%, and a photoperiod of 14 L:10D. A near-isogenic line population of C. transversoguttata was established using one virgin female and one male adult ladybug as parents. After raising for more than 10 generations, select healthy male adults, remove their intestines, and extract genomic DNA using a modified CTAB method5. Short-read sequencing libraries were fabricated and sequenced on the Illumina platform with a paired-end (PE) 150 bp strategy. The raw data was then filtered to obtain clean data. Reads containing adapters, those with over 10% unidentified nucleotides (N), and paired reads having more than 20% of bases with a quality score ≤ 5 were removed. For long-read sequencing, the PacBio SMRT sequencing technology was utilized6. Sub-reads were generated on the PacBio Sequel platform to yield high-quality long reads.

To perform Hi-C-assisted genome assembly, a Hi-C library was constructed (Fig. 1b). The process involved treating cells with paraformaldehyde to fix DNA conformation. Subsequently, cells were lysed, and the cross-linked DNA was digested with the MboI restriction enzyme to create sticky ends. The DNA ends were repaired and marked with biotin. DNA ligase was used to connect the fragments. Afterward, the cross-link between DNA and proteins was reversed through protease digestion, and the DNA was purified and randomly fragmented into 300–500 bp segments. Genome size estimation, genome assembly and quality assessments

Genome size estimation was carried out using a Kmer-based method with Illumina sequencing data in GCE (v1.0.2)6,7. A Kmer size of 17 was used to obtain a Kmer frequency distribution for C. transversoguttata, resulting in an estimated genome size of C. transversoguttata was determined to be 453,641,947 bp (Fig. 1a).

To determine the chromosome number in newly emerged male adults of C. transversoguttata, testes were dissected from the abdominal cavity and treated with hypotonic distilled water for 20–30 min. Samples were fixed in 3:1 methanol: glacial acetic acid (v/v) for 10–14 h with 2-3 solution changes, then transferred to 70% ethanol. After mincing in fresh fixative for 1-2 h, cells were spread on frozen slides, air-dried over flame, and aged for 1-2 days. Slides were hydrolyzed in 0.2 mol/L HCl (25 °C, 30 min), rinsed, incubated in 2 × SSC (60 °C, 24 h), and stained with 10: 1 Giemsa (pH 6.8–7.2) for 15–20 min. Meiotic metaphase cells, imaged using a BX53 microscope (Olympus), consistently revealed a diploid chromosome number of 2n = 20, confirming the species’ karyotype (Fig. 1d).

For genome assembly, a combination of long-read and short-read data was employed. Multiple assembly software tools were utilized, including hifiasm (0.15.1-r334)8, MECAT2 (v2.0.0)9, and NextDenovo (v2.4.0)10, with default parameters. The assembly process resulted in 77 contigs, with a contig N50 length of 22,986,307 bp. To evaluate the quality of the genome assembly, several approaches were adopted. First, the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO, v5.2.2) assessment was performed using the endopterygota_odb10 gene set11. The results showed that out of 2,124 total BUSCO groups searched, 2,069 (97.41%) were complete BUSCOs, 22 (1.04%) were fragmented BUSCOs, and 33 (1.55%) were missing BUSCOs (Table 1). Second, the BWA software (version 0.7.17-r1188)12 was used to map the Illumina short-read clean data back to the assembled genome. The mapping rate was 99.75%, the average sequencing depth was 28.9, the coverage was 99.95%, the 5X coverage was 99.59%, the 10X coverage was 97.42%, and the 20X coverage was 80.42% (Table 1). These metrics indicated a high-quality alignment between the reads and the assembly. Finally, Hi-C data was used to improve the assembly to the chromosome level. The Juicer software (v1.6)13 was used to map the Hi-C clean data to the reference genome and filter out noise reads. The filtered data was then processed by the 3D-DNA software14 for automatic clustering, ordering, and orientation of contigs, generating hic and assembly files. The JuiceBox (v2.13.7), was employed for visual inspection and error correction. Genome annotations

We employed a combination of homology-based and de novo prediction methods. Homology-based prediction utilized the RepBase library with software like RepeatMasker and RepeatProteinMask (open-4.0.9, http://www.repeatmasker.org/) to identify transposable elements. For de novo prediction, we used RepeatModeler (open-1.0.11, http://www.repeatmasker.org/RepeatModeler.html) and LTR-FINDER (v1.0.5)15 to create a library, and then applied RepeatMasker to annotate the genome. Additionally, the TRF (v4.09, http://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) was used to detect tandem repeats. The results showed that repetitive sequences accounted for 65.99% of the genome (Table 3). Multiple approaches were integrated for gene structure prediction. Homolog-based prediction involved using Exonerate with sequences from Coccinella septempunctata16, Harmonia axyridis17, Adalia decempunctata18, and Propylea japonica19. De novo prediction utilized software such as Augustus (v3.3)20, Genscan (v1.0)21, and GlimmerHMM (v3.0.4)22. RNA-seq data was processed through Gmap (2020-10-24) and Stringtie (v2.1.1)23 for alignment and transcript reconstruction, and then TransDecoder (https://github.com/TransDecoder) and PASA24 were used to predict coding frames. The MAKER (v3.00)25 or PASA software was applied to integrate and filter the gene sets from different methods. The final non-redundant gene set had an average gene length of 11,303.47 bp (as per PASA results), with an average CDS length of 1,371.13 bp, 4.81 average exons per gene, an average exon length of 351.87 bp, and an average intron length of 2,482.08 bp. The gene functions were annotated against multiple databases. Among the 16,464 genes, 86.77% were annotated at least in one of the databases including InterPro26, GO, KEGG27, SwissProt28, TrEMBL28, and NR. Specifically, 62.42% of the genes were annotated in the InterPro database, 39.24% in GO, and 83.45% in KEGG. The remaining 13.23% of the genes remained unannotated.

Different strategies were used for annotating various non-coding RNAs. tRNAscan-SE (v1.3.1, http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/) was utilized to identify tRNA sequences based on their structural features. For rRNA annotation, we selected rRNA sequences of related species as references and performed BLASTN (v2.7.1) alignment. miRNA and snRNA sequences were predicted using INFERNAL (http://infernal.janelia.org/) with the covariance models from the Rfam (v14.1). The annotation results showed the presence of 31 miRNAs, 692 tRNAs, 304 rRNAs (including different subtypes like 18S, 28S, and 5S), and 141 snRNAs in the genome.

Data Records

The genomic Illumina sequencing, PacBio sequencing, and RNA-Seq were uploaded to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the accession number SRR3514541229, SRR3514541130 and SRR3514541031, with the BioProject accession number PRJNA1268667, biosample number SAMN48760088. The chromosomal-level genome assembly data are available at GenBank with the accession number JBPBLL00000000032. The genome assembly and annotation files are available in the Figshare database33.

Technical Validation

Evaluating the quality of the genome assembly

For the genome of C. transversoguttata, we employed multiple methods for this assessment. First, we used the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) with the endopterygota_odb10 gene set11. The results indicated that 96.6% of the BUSCO genes were complete, with 95.8% being single-copy and 0.8% being duplicated. However, 0.8% were fragmented and 2.6% were missing. Second, we mapped the filtered short reads back to the assembled genome using BWA12. The mapping rate was 99.75%, and the coverage was high, suggesting a good alignment between the reads and the assembly. Additionally, we examined the gene structure prediction results. By comparing the gene length, exon number, and other characteristics with related species, we found that they showed similar patterns, which also indicated the reliability of the annotation. Overall, these comprehensive evaluations suggest that the genome assembly of C. transversoguttata is of relatively high quality, providing a solid foundation for further genomic research on this species.
