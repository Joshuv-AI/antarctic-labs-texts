m6A‐Mediated Stabilization of MRPS23 Drives NSCLC Progression Via HSPA8 and ERK Signaling Modulation
Authors: Sihong Le, Yitao Le, Xianshen Sha, Feng Qiu, Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, largely due to therapeutic resistance and tumor progression. Mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs), particularly MRPS23, have recently emerged as critical regulators of cancer progression in various malignancies, while N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) modification has been established as a key epigenetic mechanism driving tumorigenesis. However, whether MRPS23 is regulated by m6A modification and contributes to lung cancer pathogenesis remains completely unexplored. Here, we identified MRPS23 as a critical oncogenic driver in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). MRPS23 expression was significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues and cell lines, and high MRPS23 levels correlated with poor patient prognosis. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that WTAP‐mediated m6A methylation and subsequent IGF2BP3 recognition stabilized MRPS23 mRNA. Functionally, MRPS23 promoted lung cancer progression both in vitro and in vivo. Further mechanistic studies revealed that MRPS23 exerted its oncogenic effects through physical interaction with the molecular chaperone HSPA8, and this interaction was functionally associated with activation of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling cascade. However, the precise molecular steps linking the MRPS23‐HSPA8 complex to ERK phosphorylation remain to be fully defined. Collectively, our findings unveil a previously unrecognized m6A‐dependent MRPS23/HSPA8/ERK regulatory axis in NSCLC progression, highlighting MRPS23 and its associated components as promising prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, with non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all cases [1, 2, 3, 4]. Despite significant advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, the clinical outcomes for many patients remain unsatisfactory due to acquired drug resistance, tumor recurrence, and metastasis [5, 6, 7]. Consequently, the 5‐year overall survival rate for lung cancer patients remains frustratingly low [5, 8], underscoring an urgent need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying NSCLC progression and to identify novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs) have recently emerged as critical regulators of cancer progression beyond their canonical role in mitochondrial protein synthesis [9, 10, 11]. Among them, mitochondrial ribosomal protein S23 (MRPS23), a key component of the small mitochondrial ribosomal subunit, has been identified as a putative oncogene in multiple malignancies [12, 13, 14]. It promotes proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance in breast cancer, particularly triple‐negative breast cancer, and serves as a prognostic biomarker in hepatocellular carcinoma and glioma, where its elevated expression correlates with poor patient outcomes [15, 16, 17]. However, despite its established oncogenic role in these cancers, the expression pattern, biological function, and clinical significance of MRPS23 in lung cancer remain poorly defined.

N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) modification, the most prevalent internal modification in eukaryotic messenger RNAs, has emerged as a critical epigenetic mechanism driving tumorigenesis [18, 19, 20, 21]. M6A modification influences multiple aspects of RNA metabolism, including splicing, nuclear export, stability, and translation efficiency [22, 23, 24]. Given the well‐documented role of m6A modification in lung cancer and the emerging oncogenic function of MRPS23 in other malignancies [12, 25], we hypothesized that MRPS23 might be subject to m6A‐dependent regulation and contribute to lung cancer pathogenesis [26, 27, 28]. However, whether MRPS23 is modified by m6A in lung cancer, and if so, which methyltransferase is responsible, has never been investigated.

The RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling cascade governs cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival, and its aberrant activation is a hallmark of many cancers, including lung cancer [29, 30, 31]. Heat shock protein family A member 8 (HSPA8/HSC70), a constitutively expressed molecular chaperone critical for protein homeostasis [32, 33, 34], promotes cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance in various malignancies through diverse mechanisms [35, 36]. Interestingly, HSPA8 functions as a key molecular chaperone that can stabilize critical signal transducers, including components of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK pathway [37]. Meanwhile, MRPS23 has been established as an oncogene driving proliferation and metastasis in breast cancer [25], processes that are often orchestrated by ERK signaling. Notably, the potential functional link between MRPS23 and HSPA8 remains completely unexplored. Both MRPS23 and HSPA8 have been independently implicated in cancer cell proliferation and survival, and HSPA8 is known to interact with a diverse array of client proteins through its chaperone function [38, 39]. Given that HSPA8 facilitates protein complex formation and stabilization, and that MRPS23 is a mitochondrial ribosomal protein whose stability and interactome may be modulated by chaperone proteins, we hypothesized that MRPS23 might physically interact with HSPA8 to execute its oncogenic functions. Based on these observations, we further proposed a previously unexplored regulatory axis in which m6A‐modified MRPS23, through its interaction with HSPA8, may activate the ERK signaling pathway, thereby promoting NSCLC progression.

In this study, we systematically investigated this hypothesis through a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments. We demonstrated that MRPS23 is significantly upregulated in NSCLC tissues and cell lines, and that high MRPS23 expression correlates with poor patient prognosis. Mechanistically, we revealed that WTAP‐mediated m6A methylation and subsequent IGF2BP3 recognition stabilized MRPS23 mRNA. Functionally, MRPS23 promoted NSCLC progression both in vitro and in vivo. Further analysis revealed that MRPS23 exerted its oncogenic effects through physical interaction with HSPA8, which was functionally associated with activation of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK signaling cascade. Collectively, our findings unveil a previously unrecognized m6A‐dependent MRPS23/HSPA8/ERK regulatory axis in NSCLC progression, highlighting MRPS23 and its associated signaling components as promising prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Materials and Methods

Tissue Specimens and Patient Data

A fresh‐frozen tissue cohort, comprising lung carcinoma and matched adjacent normal mucosa, was collected from 8 patients undergoing radical lung resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, and snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen at −80°C. A larger formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) cohort was retrospectively assembled, including 99 lung cancer tissues and 27 adjacent normal tissues from patients who underwent pulmonary resection between June 2021 and December 2022. FFPE sections were prepared by the hospital's Pathology Center. Clinicopathological data and postoperative follow‐up information were collected. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All methods in this study were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional and national guidelines, regulations, and ethical standards.

Bioinformatics and Database Analysis

TCGA‐LUAD (535 tumors/59 normals) from https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov was normalized using DESeq2 in R (v4.2.1) and assessed by Wilcoxon/paired t‐test. Survival was evaluated via Kaplan–Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis, median‐stratified, Log‐rank test, ggplot2). Pearson correlations among MRPS23, WTAP, and IGF2BP3 were performed, and m6A sites in MRPS23 mRNA were predicted via SRAMP (https://www.cuilab.cn/sramp/).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Analysis

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) sections. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked and non‐specific binding sites were sealed. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti‐MRPS23 (1:500) and anti‐HSPA8 (1:300), followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody and DAB development. Sections were scored semi‐quantitatively by two independent pathologists, based on the product of staining intensity (0–3) and the proportion of positive cells (0–4). Detailed information for the antibodies used is provided in Table S1A.

Cell Culture

A panel of human lung cancer cell lines (A549, H1299, H1975, HCC827, and PC9), the human bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS‐2B, and the human embryonic kidney cell line 293T were maintained by the laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. All cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI‐1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Plasmids, siRNA, Lentivirus, and Transfection

FLAG‐MRPS23 and HA‐HSPA8 plasmids were purchased from Miaoling (Wuhan). siRNAs targeting MRPS23 and a control siRNA were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai). The siRNAs used are listed in Table S1B. A lentiviral vector carrying the MRPS23 sequence (5′‐CCCGGAAATCCGAACACTT‐3′) was sourced from GeneChem (Shanghai). Transfection was performed using TurboFect (Thermo Scientific).

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein lysates were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non‐fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies detailed in Table S1A. After incubation with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody, protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system.

RNA Isolation and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (GECELL, China). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript FAST RT Reagent Kit (RR092A, TaKaRa, Japan). qRT‐PCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, using GAPDH as an internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1C.

Actinomycin D Assay

A549 cells (5 × 104 per well in 24‐well plates) were treated with 5 mg/L Actinomycin D (TARGETMOL, USA). Total RNA was isolated at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h post‐treatment, and mRNA decay was analyzed by qRT‐PCR to determine half‐life.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Dual‐luciferase reporter assays were performed using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Cat# 11402, Yeasen). The wild‐type (MRPS23‐WT) and mutant (MRPS23‐MUT, in which all predicted m6A sites were mutated) reporter plasmids were constructed by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). 293 T cells were co‐transfected with these reporter plasmids, pRL‐TK Renilla, and siRNAs (si‐NC, si‐WTAP, or si‐IGF2BP3). After 48 h, Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured. Relative activity was calculated as the Firefly/Renilla ratio and normalized to si‐NC.

Methylated RNA Immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)‐qPCR

m6A modification on specific RNA transcripts was analyzed using the MeRIP m6A Kit (Catalog #5203, BersinBio, China). Total RNA was isolated and fragmented into ~100‐nucleotide fragments, then incubated with an anti‐m6A antibody or normal IgG. After washing, co‐precipitated RNA was eluted, ethanol‐precipitated, and quantified by qRT‐PCR, normalized to input.

RNA Pull‐Down Assay and Mass Spectrometry

Biotin‐labeled MRPS23 RNA probes, synthesized and transcribed in vitro by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), were incubated with whole‐cell protein extracts, followed by isolation with streptavidin‐coated magnetic beads. After washing, bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by LC–MS/MS at Puduozhonghe Biotech (Wuhan, China) to identify specific MRPS23 mRNA‐binding proteins.

RNA‐Sequencing (RNA‐Seq)

Total RNA from A549 cells with MRPS23 knockdown and controls was subjected to RNA‐seq (BGI Genomics, Shenzhen, China).

Colony Formation Assay

Treated cells were plated at 1000 cells per well in 6‐well plates and cultured for 2 weeks. Colonies were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and quantified.

5‐Ethynyl‐2′‐Deoxyuridine (EdU) Assay

Treated cells were seeded in 96‐well plates (1 × 104 cells/well), incubated with EdU for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized. Incorporated EdU was labeled with reaction solution, and nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. EdU‐positive cells were visualized by fluorescence microscopy and quantified.

Wound Healing Assay

Cells were seeded into 6‐well plates. At ~90% confluence, a uniform wound was created with a sterile pipette tip, and images were captured at 0, 24, and 48 h for wound closure analysis.

Transwell Migration and Invasion Assay

Transwell chambers were used to evaluate migration and invasion. Cells (1 × 104) suspended in serum‐free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, with Matrigel‐coated membranes for invasion. After 24 h, migrated/invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified.

Protein–Protein Docking

Protein–protein docking between MRPS23 and HSPA8 was performed using the GRAMM web server, with protein structures from UniProtKB.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)

Protein–protein interactions were examined using a Protein A/G Co‐Immunoprecipitation Kit (Beaver, China). Cell lysates were incubated with anti‐MRPS23 or anti‐HSPA8, followed by precipitation with Protein A/G agarose beads, with normal IgG as a negative control. Immunoprecipitated complexes were washed, eluted, and analyzed by Western blotting.

In Vivo Tumorigenesis Model

Approved by the institutional ethics committee, four‐week‐old female nude mice (Hangzhou Ziyuan) were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 106 stably transfected A549 cells in 100 μL PBS. One week later, mice received intragastric GDC0994 (10 mg/kg, TARGETMOL) or PBS twice daily for 5 weeks. Tumor volume was measured weekly (length × width2/2), and tumors were harvested and weighed at the endpoint.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. One‐way ANOVA or Student's t‐test was used for comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Result

MRPS23 Is Highly Expressed in Lung Cancer Tissues and Its Correlation With Clinicopathological Characteristics

Given the emerging role of MRPS23 in cancer progression, we first analyzed transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (598 lung cancer tissues and 59 normal tissues). MRPS23 expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1A), and paired analysis confirmed its elevation in cancer tissues (Figure 1B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis using an online plotter indicated that lung cancer patients with low MRPS23 expression had better overall survival (Figure 1C). To further investigate the role of MRPS23 in lung cancer, we examined both protein and mRNA levels in 8 pairs of lung cancer tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues using Western blot and qPCR. Results showed that MRPS23 protein levels were elevated in 7 out of 8 tumor samples, and mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in lung cancer tissues (Figure 1D,E). Additionally, IHC staining was performed on 99 lung cancer specimens and 27 adjacent normal tissues, showing significantly higher MRPS23 expression in tumor tissues (Figure 1F). Patients were divided into high‐ and low‐MRPS23 expression groups based on the median IHC score, and the high‐expression group had significantly lower 3‐year overall survival compared to the low‐expression group (Figure 1G). We further analyzed the association between MRPS23 expression and clinicopathological characteristics in the 99 patients. MRPS23 expression was significantly correlated with T stage, N stage, and clinical stage (Table 1). Univariate Cox analysis identified MRPS23 high expression, advanced T/N stage, and advanced clinical stage as significant risk factors for poor survival (Figure S1A). To avoid multicollinearity, two multivariate models were constructed: Model A (adjusted for age, sex, and clinical stage) and Model B (adjusted for age, sex, T stage, and N stage). In both models, high MRPS23 expression remained independently associated with poor overall survival (Model A: HR = 1.874, p = 0.0317; Model B: HR = 1.839, p = 0.0391), whereas staging variables did not (all p > 0.05; Figure S1B). These findings suggest complementary prognostic value of MRPS23 to TNM staging, warranting further validation. We then examined MRPS23 expression in a panel of lung cancer cell lines. qPCR analysis confirmed that MRPS23 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in these cell lines relative to normal Beas‐2B cells (Figure 1H). Consistent with this, Western blot analysis showed that MRPS23 protein levels were markedly higher in various lung cancer cell lines (including A549, H1299, HCC827, PC9, H1975, and H1755) compared to BEAS‐2B (Figure 1I). These findings collectively establish MRPS23 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and independent prognostic indicator in lung cancer.

MRPS23 Is Regulated by m6A Methylation in NSCLC

To investigate whether MRPS23 expression is regulated by m6A RNA methylation, we initially performed in silico prediction using the SRAMP database. This analysis identified five m6A modification sites on the MRPS23 mRNA with high confidence (Figure 2A). Based on these predictions, we designed three pairs of primers targeting the corresponding regions (sites 1–3) for subsequent experimental validation (Figure 2B). Subsequently, MeRIP‐qPCR was conducted in both the normal bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS‐2B and the NSCLC cell line A549. The results confirmed significant m6A enrichment on MRPS23 mRNA in both cell types, with a notably higher level in A549 cells compared to BEAS‐2B cells (Figure 2C). To identify m6A regulatory proteins interacting with MRPS23, we performed RNA pull‐down assays using MRPS23‐specific probes in A549 cells, followed by mass spectrometry analysis. This led to the identification of the methyltransferase WTAP and the m6A reader protein IGF2BP3 as interactors of MRPS23 (Figure 2D). Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) revealed that MRPS23 expression was significantly positively correlated with both WTAP and IGF2BP3 (Figure 2E). To investigate their functional relevance, we knocked down WTAP and IGF2BP3 individually. qRT‐PCR analysis demonstrated that knockdown of either gene significantly reduced MRPS23 mRNA levels (Figure 2F,G). Conversely, knockdown of FTO, a key m6A demethylase, significantly upregulated MRPS23 mRNA levels (Figure S1C), suggesting that WTAP and IGF2BP3 are involved in modulating the stability or expression of MRPS23 mRNA. To directly test this hypothesis, we examined MRPS23 mRNA stability using an actinomycin D (ActD) assay. The results showed that, compared to the control group, knockdown of WTAP or IGF2BP3 significantly accelerated the degradation rate of MRPS23 mRNA (Figure 2H). To further assess the functional contribution of the predicted m6A motifs to WTAP‐ and IGF2BP3‐mediated regulation of MRPS23, we generated a mutant reporter construct in which all five predicted m6A sites in the MRPS23 mRNA were substituted (MUT), alongside the wild‐type construct (WT). In the WT group, knockdown of WTAP or IGF2BP3 led to a significant decrease in luciferase activity. In contrast, in the MUT group, knockdown of WTAP or IGF2BP3 did not cause significant changes in luciferase activity (Figure 2I,J). Collectively, these findings are consistent with the functional involvement of the predicted m6A‐associated motifs in WTAP‐ and IGF2BP3‐dependent regulation of MRPS23 expression. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that mutating these sequences may affect RNA secondary structure or other regulatory elements. Therefore, additional approaches such as individual site‐directed mutagenesis are warranted to fully dissect the direct contribution of each specific m6A residue.

MRPS23 Knockdown Inhibits Proliferation, Migration and Invasion of NSCLC Cells

Having established the high expression of MRPS23 in NSCLC tissues, we next investigated its functional role in cancer progression using in vitro models. First, Western blot analysis confirmed variable MRPS23 protein levels across five NSCLC cell lines, with A549 and H1299 cells selected for subsequent knockdown experiments due to their relatively high endogenous expression. Transfection with two distinct siRNAs (si‐MRPS23‐1 and si‐MRPS23‐2) achieved significant MRPS23 depletion at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure 3A), validating the specificity of our knockdown approach. To assess the impact of MRPS23 on metastatic potential, we performed wound healing and Transwell assays. The wound healing assay revealed markedly reduced migratory capacity in MRPS23‐depleted A549 and H1299 cells compared to control groups (Figure 3B). Complementary Transwell migration and invasion assays further demonstrated that MRPS23 suppression significantly decreased both migratory and invasive cell numbers, suggesting a critical role in tumor cell motility (Figure 3C). We next evaluated MRPS23's contribution to cell proliferation using colony formation and EdU assays. MRPS23‐silenced cells exhibited a substantial reduction in colony‐forming ability relative to controls (Figure 3D), while EdU incorporation assays confirmed a parallel decline in DNA synthesis rates (Figure 3E). These results collectively indicate that MRPS23 promotes NSCLC cell proliferation and metastasis through multiple biological processes.

MRPS23 Activates ERK Signaling and Identifies HSPA8 as a Candidate Mediator

To explore the downstream signaling mechanisms by which MRPS23 promotes NSCLC progression, transcriptome sequencing was performed in A549 cells following MRPS23 knockdown. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes revealed significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway (Figure 4A,B), suggesting that MRPS23 may regulate ERK signaling. MRPS23 depletion markedly decreased p‐ERK1/2 levels in A549 cells (Figure 4C). Conversely, ectopic expression of MRPS23 robustly increased the phosphorylation level of ERK1/2 without altering total ERK expression (Figure 4D), confirming the regulatory role of MRPS23 in ERK pathway activation.

To identify potential proteins mediating MRPS23‐driven ERK activation, mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of MRPS23‐interacting proteins was performed. Interestingly, no direct components of the ERK cascade were identified among the MRPS23‐interacting proteins (Figure 4E), suggesting that MRPS23 may regulate ERK activity through an indirect mechanism involving intermediate adaptor proteins. To identify such potential mediators, we integrated two complementary datasets: (i) proteins functionally related to MRPS23 based on transcriptome sequencing, and (ii) proteins physically interacting with MRPS23 identified by MS (42 proteins with > 1.5‐fold enrichment). The intersection of these two datasets yielded four candidate proteins (Figure 4E). Among these, heat shock protein family A member 8 (HSPA8) exhibited the highest binding affinity score and was selected for further investigation. Representative MS peaks confirming the MRPS23‐HSPA8 interaction are shown in Figure S1D. Notably, HSPA8 has been reported to regulate MAPK/ERK signaling through its chaperone functions in other cellular contexts. Although the immediate mechanism by which the MRPS23–HSPA8 complex engages the ERK cascade in NSCLC remains to be determined, this prior evidence aligns with the potential role of HSPA8 as a functional link between MRPS23 and ERK signaling.

We next examined the clinical relevance of HSPA8 in lung cancer. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on the same cohort of 99 lung cancer tissues and 27 adjacent normal tissues revealed that HSPA8 expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues (Figure 4F). Based on the median IHC score, patients were categorized into high‐ and low‐HSPA8 expression groups; Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that the high‐expression group had significantly poorer overall survival (Figure 4G), consistent with MRPS23. Furthermore, analysis of paired IHC scores from the 99 NSCLC tissues demonstrated a strong positive correlation between MRPS23 and HSPA8 protein levels (Spearman's r = 0.8409, p < 0.0001; Figure 4H), reinforcing their coordinated upregulation in clinical tumors. These findings suggest that HSPA8 is not only a potential mediator of MRPS23‐driven ERK activation but also a clinically relevant oncogene in lung cancer.

MRPS23 Physically Interacts With HSPA8 and Regulates Its Protein Stability

To elucidate the molecular relationship between MRPS23 and HSPA8, we first evaluated their potential binding mode through protein–protein rigid docking simulations. The model revealed that MRPS23 forms hydrogen bonds with HSPA8 via residues such as GLN‐61 and TRP‐65, with a binding energy of −9.4 kcal/mol, indicating a stable binding potential (Figure 5A). To verify this prediction, Western blot and qPCR analyses showed that knockdown or overexpression of MRPS23 significantly altered HSPA8 protein levels without affecting its mRNA levels (Figure 5B,C and Figure S1E) suggesting that MRPS23 regulates HSPA8 stability at the post‐transcriptional level. Furthermore, exogenous and endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays confirmed that Flag‐MRPS23 effectively binds to HA‐HSPA8, and that endogenous MRPS23 interacts with HSPA8 in A549 cells (Figure 5D,E).

To gain initial insight into the interaction interface, we mutated the predicted contact residue GLN‐61 of MRPS23 to alanine (Q61A) based on the docking predictions. Co‐IP results revealed that the MRPS23‐Q61A mutant exhibited markedly reduced binding to HSPA8 compared with wild‐type MRPS23 (Figure 5F), suggesting that GLN‐61 contributes to the MRPS23‐HSPA8 interaction. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that this substitution affects protein folding, stability, or subcellular localization; complete mapping of the interaction domains will require systematic deletion mutagenesis of both MRPS23 and HSPA8 in future studies.

Given that MRPS23 knockdown did not affect HSPA8 mRNA levels, we further investigated the specific mechanisms underlying its regulation of HSPA8 protein stability. Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays revealed that MRPS23 knockdown significantly shortened the protein half‐life of HSPA8, accelerating its degradation (Figure 5G). Conversely, the addition of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 effectively rescued the decrease in HSPA8 protein levels caused by MRPS23 knockdown (Figure 5H). Subsequent ubiquitination assays demonstrated that MRPS23 knockdown markedly increased the ubiquitination levels of HSPA8 (Figure 5I), proving that MRPS23 stabilizes HSPA8 by suppressing the ubiquitin‐proteasome degradation pathway.

Finally, TCGA database analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the mRNA expression levels of MRPS23 and HSPA8 in LUAD tissues (Figure 5J). Collectively, MRPS23 maintains HSPA8 protein stability in NSCLC by directly binding to HSPA8 and suppressing its ubiquitination and proteasome‐mediated degradation.

HSPA8 Mediates MRPS23‐Driven Oncogenic Functions in NSCLC Cells

To investigate whether HSPA8 is functionally involved in MRPS23‐mediated oncogenic effects, we performed rescue experiments in A549 cells. Cells were transfected with si‐Control or si‐MRPS23, followed by transfection with an HA‐tagged HSPA8 plasmid. Western blot analysis confirmed that HSPA8 overexpression effectively restored HSPA8 protein levels that were reduced by MRPS23 knockdown (Figure 6A).

Colony formation and EdU assays demonstrated that HSPA8 overexpression partially rescued the reduced clonogenic ability and DNA synthesis caused by MRPS23 knockdown (Figure 6B,C). Wound healing assays and transwell were conducted to assess cell migration and invasion. The results revealed that MRPS23 depletion markedly impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of A549 cells, whereas HSPA8 overexpression substantially restored these capabilities (Figure 6D,E). These results indicate that HSPA8 acts as a critical downstream effector of MRPS23 in promoting NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

MRPS23 Promotes Tumor Growth via HSPA8‐Mediated ERK Activation in Vivo

To determine whether MRPS23 activates ERK signaling through HSPA8 in vivo, we performed a rescue experiment in A549 cells. Western blot analysis demonstrated that overexpression of HSPA8 effectively restored ERK phosphorylation that was suppressed by MRPS23 knockdown (Figure 7A), indicating that HSPA8 is required for MRPS23‐mediated ERK phosphorylation in this setting. However, the precise molecular intermediates through which HSPA8 transmits this signal remain to be identified.

MRPS23 promotes tumor growth in vivo in an HSPA8‐dependent manner associated with ERK signaling. (A) HSPA8 overexpression rescues ERK phosphorylation suppressed by MRPS23 knockdown in A549 cells. (B) Schematic of in vivo treatment regimen: A549 xenografts treated with GDC‐0994 or PBS. (C) Representative images of resected xenograft tumors from four groups. (D, E) Tumor volume (D) and tumor weight (E) across groups. (F) IHC staining of MRPS23, HSPA8, and p‐ERK in xenograft tissues (Scale bars, 50 μm). ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

We further assessed the functional role of the MRPS23‐HSPA8‐ERK axis in vivo using a xenograft tumor model. A549 cells were subjected to four treatments: PBS control, ERK inhibitor (GDC‐0994) alone, MRPS23 knockdown alone, and combined MRPS23 knockdown with GDC‐0994. Tumor growth was significantly inhibited in both the MRPS23 knockdown and ERK inhibitor groups, with the strongest suppression observed in the combination group (Figure 7B,C). Corresponding reductions in tumor weight and volume were also recorded (Figure 7D,E). Immunohistochemistry of xenograft tissues confirmed decreased HSPA8 and p‐ERK levels upon MRPS23 knockdown (Figure 7F).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that MRPS23 promotes tumor growth through stabilization of HSPA8 protein and functional engagement of ERK signaling, and this effect can be synergistically enhanced by ERK inhibitors. However, the precise molecular intermediates through which HSPA8 transmits this signal remain to be identified.

Discussion

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide, and the 5‐year overall survival rate has improved only marginally despite advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies [40]. A major barrier to durable clinical benefit is the frequent emergence of acquired drug resistance, tumor recurrence, and metastasis, underscoring the need to delineate the underlying molecular mechanisms [41]. In particular, the contribution of post‐transcriptional regulatory networks to lung cancer progression and therapeutic resistance is still incompletely understood [42]. N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification has been implicated in multiple oncogenic processes, including proliferation, invasion, and treatment resistance, yet the downstream effector molecules through which m6A drives lung cancer progression have not been systematically characterized [43, 44]. At the same time, mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (MRPs) are increasingly recognized as regulators of cancer metabolism and progression, but the role of MRPS23 in lung cancer is largely unexplored [45, 46]. Likewise, although the molecular chaperone HSPA8 is associated with cancer cell survival and proliferation [36, 47], its functional relationship with MRPs and the ERK signaling cascade in lung cancer has remained unclear, representing a critical gap in current knowledge.

In this context, our study identifies a previously unrecognized m6A‐MRPS23‐HSPA8‐ERK1/2 axis that drives lung cancer progression. We demonstrate that m6A modification directly enhances MRPS23 mRNA stability, resulting in elevated MRPS23 protein expression in lung cancer tissues and establishing MRPS23 as a novel downstream effector of m6A in this disease. Functional studies further reveal that upregulated MRPS23 promotes lung cancer progression by interacting with HSPA8, thereby activating the ERK1/2 signaling pathway and defining a new oncogenic regulatory cascade. These findings are supported by a combination of in vitro functional assays, RNA–protein interaction analyses, and in vivo xenograft experiments, which collectively confirm the physiological relevance of this axis. Clinically, MRPS23 and HSPA8 are significantly overexpressed in lung cancer tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues, and their high expression levels correlate with reduced overall survival, supporting their potential utility as prognostic biomarkers. Notably, while MRPS23 has previously been linked to breast cancer metastasis via arginine and lysine methylation [12], our work is the first to implicate MRPS23 in lung cancer through m6A modification, highlighting a context‐specific layer of post‐transcriptional regulation of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins in cancer.

The identification of the m6A‐MRPS23‐HSPA8‐ERK1/2 axis has important implications for lung cancer biology and therapy. Mechanistically, it uncovers a novel route by which RNA epigenetic modification can converge on canonical oncogenic signaling pathways, thereby expanding the current understanding of post‐transcriptional regulation in tumor progression. Therapeutically, MRPS23 emerges as a previously unrecognized and potentially druggable node within this axis, which may be exploited alone or in combination with existing ERK pathway inhibitors to overcome current treatment limitations. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrate that MRPS23 stabilizes HSPA8 and that both are required for ERK phosphorylation in NSCLC cells, the immediate molecular steps linking the MRPS23‐HSPA8 complex to ERK activation have not been fully resolved. While HSPA8 has been reported to interact with and stabilize components of the RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK cascade in other systems, whether MRPS23‐HSPA8 directly engages RAS, RAF, or other upstream regulators in NSCLC, and through what structural interface, requires further investigation. We therefore interpret the MRPS23‐HSPA8‐ERK relationship as a functional association rather than a fully defined linear pathway. Future studies employing RAS‐GTP pull‐down assays, targeted kinase activity profiling, and systematic mapping of HSPA8 client proteins in this context will be needed to dissect the precise signaling intermediates involved. Second, while our ubiquitination assays and proteasome inhibitor experiments have demonstrated that MRPS23 stabilizes HSPA8 by inhibiting its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, and our point‐mutation experiments identified GLN‐61 as a critical residue for the MRPS23‐HSPA8 interaction, the precise structural domains involved in this binding have not yet been fully mapped. Future studies using systematic deletion mutants (e.g., N‐/C‐terminal truncations) will be needed to fully characterize the interaction interface. Third, our in vivo validation was performed using a single A549 xenograft model; additional lung cancer cell lines and genetically engineered mouse models are needed to generalize the findings. To further strengthen the clinical relevance and generalizability, we plan to employ patient‐derived xenograft models and additional NSCLC cell lines in our future investigations. Fourth, the clinical cohort size and follow‐up duration should be expanded to refine the prognostic value of MRPS23 and HSPA8. In addition, covariates such as smoking status and histological subtype could not be included in the regression analysis due to extensive missing data in this retrospective cohort. Future large‐scale, multi‐center validation studies with comprehensive covariate collection are warranted to confirm the independent prognostic value of MRPS23. Finally, as noted above, the interpretation of the m6A reporter mutagenesis experiments is tempered by the possibility that sequence mutations may affect RNA structure or other regulatory elements beyond m6A modification itself. Despite these constraints, our study provides a substantive advance in elucidating the molecular mechanisms of lung cancer progression and lays a foundation for future translational efforts aimed at improving clinical outcomes through modulation of RNA epigenetic pathways.
