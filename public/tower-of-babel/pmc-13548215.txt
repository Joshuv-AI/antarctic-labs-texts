Staged, life-cycle approach to evidence generation for digital health interventions in low- and middle-income countries
Authors: Gareth Obery, Eva Weicken, Shubhanan Upadhyay, Max Rath, Bilal A Mateen, Saira Ghafur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital health interventions (DHIs) are rapidly expanding across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), driven by their potential to address persistent health system constraints, workforce shortages and health inequities. However, the evaluation of these interventions has not kept pace with their adoption. Traditional evidence-generation approaches, particularly those centred on rigid, resource-intensive study designs, are often poorly aligned with the iterative nature of digital technologies and the realities of resource-constrained settings. This misalignment can exacerbate existing system pressures, slow validation and contribute to an enduring evidence-implementation gap that limits sustainable adoption and scale.This narrative review synthesises peer-reviewed biomedical literature, grey literature, institutional reports and applied case studies published between 2015 and 2025 to examine how evidence for DHIs is generated in LMICs. The analysis is organised across three interrelated domains: contextual and stakeholder dynamics shaping evaluations, methodological innovations suited to LMIC realities and top-down policy and regulatory mechanisms influencing adoption and scale.Drawing on this synthesis, the review argues for a staged, life-cycle-based approach to evidence generation, in which evaluation methods and evidence strategies evolve as the intervention matures and the implementation context evolves. This approach emphasises pragmatic, context-sensitive strategies that balance scientific rigour with feasibility.This review aims to provide decision-oriented guidance for innovators, implementers, policy-makers and regulators seeking to generate appropriate, actionable and policy-relevant evidence to support the adoption, integration and scale of DHIs in LMIC health systems.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This narrative review introduces a staged, life-cycle framework that aligns evaluation methods with the maturity of a DHI and the readiness of the context in which it operates, treating context readiness as an explicit transition condition rather than a background assumption. By integrating bottom-up methodological innovations, including human-centred design, clinical simulation, context-appropriate study designs and real-world evidence, with top-down policy and regulatory levers, the review offers decision-oriented guidance for innovators, providers and regulators in place of a fixed hierarchy.

Introduction

The implementation of digital health interventions (DHIs) is expanding rapidly across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but evidence generation has lagged behind. A recent Cochrane review by Agarwal et al highlighted that despite the proliferation of mobile health tools for primary care, rigorous evidence linking these interventions to improved health outcomes remains limited.1 Evaluating DHIs is inherently complex, even in well-resourced settings. Conventional methodologies, such as randomised controlled trials (RCTs), are often incongruent with the agile, iterative development cycles of digital technologies, as they are costly and require labour-intensive effort over a protracted period of time. Tools may undergo multiple updates before trial results are published, leaving evidence outdated or irrelevant. This creates an evidence-implementation paradox: evidence is required for adoption, yet adoption is required to generate evidence.2

The problem is exacerbated by a lack of transparent reporting of negative results, meaning critical implementation barriers are repeatedly rediscovered by disparate research teams, hampering collective learning. This paper addresses this by arguing for a staged, context-sensitive evidence strategy that is rigorous enough for decisions, while realistic enough for LMIC constraints, and one in which evidence requirements evolve as technologies mature.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research. This study is a review that synthesises existing peer-reviewed literature, grey literature and institutional reports. Consequently, direct patient or public involvement was not sought in the preparation of this manuscript.

Conceptual background

Digital health refers to the systematic application of information and communications technologies, computer science and data to support informed decision-making,3 ranging from personal health trackers and digital therapeutics to artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models.4 The WHO classifies a DHI as a discrete digital capability used to achieve a health sector objective.3

This paper focuses on LMICs, defined by the World Bank’s income groups based on gross national income per capita, enabling comparisons of digital health ecosystems with distinct resource constraints, governance capacities and infrastructure maturity.5

The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health (2020–2027) positions digital health as a key enabler of universal health coverage, emphasising that technological innovation must be matched with institutional readiness and governance capabilities.6 The strategy calls for coordinated governance, strengthened evaluation and investment in digital infrastructure, while emphasising that benefits will only be realised if interventions are safe, effective, equitable and adapted to local contexts.6

Methods

Review type and rationale

This paper is structured as a narrative review, reported in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) criteria, allowing integration of academic and grey literature around a conceptual framework developed iteratively from the evidence.7

Search strategy

The search was conducted in two phases. First, broad exploratory reading across digital health, implementation science, health economics and health policy literatures relevant to LMICs. Sources included recent reviews, institutional reports and reference lists of key papers. The three-domain analytical framework (figure 1, table 1) was developed inductively, with framework development and targeted literature identification proceeding recursively rather than sequentially. Second, targeted searches in PubMed and Google Scholar deepened coverage within each domain. English-language publications dated January 2015 to December 2025 were eligible. Full search strings are provided in the online supplemental material. Additional records were identified through backward and forward citation tracking.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible sources focused on DHIs, health data systems, digital health policy or evaluation methodology transferable to DHIs in LMIC settings. Peer-reviewed articles, institutional reports, technical guidance, reporting standards, regulatory instruments and applied case studies were all considered. Editorials without substantive content, conference abstracts without full text, publications exclusively addressing high-income settings and non-English publications were excluded. Title and abstract screening was conducted by at least one author per domain, with ambiguous records resolved through discussion. No dual-blind screening, risk-of-bias appraisal or Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-compliant flow diagram was produced.

Grey literature and institutional reports were included as primary evidence. DHI implementation in LMICs outstrips the peer-review cycle, and programmatic lessons, regulatory developments and economic evaluations are often published first and sometimes only through WHO, World Bank, ITU, PATH, UNICEF and Africa CDC channels.

Case studies were selected to illustrate methodological approaches at each life-cycle stage, guided by: sufficient published evaluation detail, LMIC implementation setting, representation of distinct methodological approaches and variation in country and intervention type.

Limitations

Narrative reviews are subject to selection bias with inclusion and weighting reflecting author judgement rather than a prespecified protocol. The restriction to English-language sources and to two bibliographic databases likely under-represents regionally published work. Variability in grey literature quality is mitigated by prioritising established institutional publishers and triangulating claims across sources. Case studies are intended to be demonstrative rather than comprehensive.

Analytical framework

We have organised this review around a staged, life-cycle-based approach to evidence generation. The three stages of maturity (early, mid and late) form the central spine of the framework and are associated with a primary evaluation goal and a corresponding set of methodological approaches, developed in ‘Methodological innovations suited to LMIC realities’. ‘Contextual and stakeholder dynamics shaping evidence generation’ and ‘Top-down policy and regulatory mechanisms that enable sustainable adoption and scale’ act on every stage of the life-cycle, shaping both the feasibility of evaluation and the translation of evidence into adoption.

Maturity

Throughout this review, maturity describes the intervention’s development stage and its corresponding evidence needs. The three life-cycle stages are adapted from the WHO’s Monitoring and Evaluating DHIs: a Practical Guide.8 Critically, maturity is not determined by the technology alone. A DHI advances to the next stage when two conditions are met: the intervention itself is sufficiently refined for the next stage and the implementation context can support the next phase of evaluation. Treating context readiness as a transition condition, rather than a background assumption, is central to avoiding premature scale-up. In practice, this judgement is typically made jointly by the implementing team, the host health authority or facility leadership and the funder or programme steering body, drawing on the indicative markers set out in table 1 as a minimum evidentiary basis for transition rather than an exhaustive checklist.

Maturity is also not irreversible. A significant software update, algorithm change or shift in deployment context may necessitate regression to an earlier evaluation stage requiring re-validation. This recursive property distinguishes the framework from linear stage-gate models and reflects the iterative reality of digital health development.

Contextual and stakeholder dynamics shaping evidence generation

Systemic barriers

Many LMIC healthcare systems are resource-constrained, characterised by workforce shortages, high disease burden and complex governance.9 An immediate barrier is a lack of physical and digital infrastructure, including stable electricity supply, reliable internet connectivity, basic hardware and interoperable software. The Health Electrification and Telecommunication Alliance estimates that 100 000 public health facilities in sub-Saharan Africa lack reliable electricity and internet.10 Resource constraints drive reliance on unlicensed or legacy software, creating critical cybersecurity and privacy vulnerabilities.11 Evidence generation is further hampered by fragmented AI regulations, resulting in inconsistent oversight and guidance for innovators.12

Systemic challenges extend to human and institutional capacity. Limited familiarity with digital health among healthcare workers, policy-makers and administrators results in inconsistent data recording or tools that are misaligned with clinical workflows. Data integrity can also be influenced by the structure of systemic incentives. For example, performance-based financing that prioritises specific metrics, such as tuberculosis case finding, may unintentionally incentivise the overprioritisation of those outcomes at the expense of other essential but unmeasured tasks, such as long-term adherence.13 Low population digital literacy can undermine the quality of the evidence, as patients unfamiliar with digital platforms may provide incomplete or inaccurate self-reported data.1415

Equity considerations

These barriers are not evenly distributed. DHIs disproportionately reach urban populations, while rural, low-income and otherwise marginalised groups, who carry the greatest burden of disease, remain systematically underserved and under-represented in evaluations.16 Evidence generation processes themselves can perpetuate inequities. The framing of research questions, the selection of methods and the choice of outcomes can embed epistemic inequity within the evidence base.17 Addressing this requires equity-conscious study designs, several of which are discussed in ‘Methodological innovations suited to LMIC realities’.

Evidence generation is further complicated by donor-driven agendas that may impose technical priorities over local expertise, reinforcing hierarchies and limiting the agency of community-level actors.18 International partners often retain control over data, creating dependencies that limit national ownership and raise fundamental questions about data sovereignty, including where data are stored, who can access it and how it may be used. This results in overlapping data streams siloed across different sectors, leading to fragmented evidence and limited interoperability. Compounding this, substantial donor funding cuts are forecast to severely restrict healthcare expenditure, with projections of over 14 million additional deaths by 2030.19

Methodological innovations suited to LMIC realities

The proliferation of fragmented, payer-driven digital health pilots that fail to scale20—often referred to as ‘pilotitis’—has been driven in large part by short funding cycles that disproportionately incentivise novel, early-stage pilots, rather than providing the sustained, long-term investment required for mid-stage implementation research and late-stage scaling.21 Addressing this demands a deliberate shift away from traditional evaluation paradigms towards pragmatic, adaptive methodologies suited to resource-constrained systems.

Early-stage evaluations: de-risking through context-specific iteration

Early-stage evaluations focus on de-risking DHIs by ensuring they are technically viable and culturally appropriate for the specific infrastructure and workforce constraints of the local context, rapidly identifying those with the highest potential for long-term success.

Human-centred design and usability testing

Human-centred design (HCD) is a well-established methodology for rapid, iterative product development that keeps the user at the core of the design process, embedding usability, acceptability and sustainability from the outset. HCD is gaining traction in global health, particularly in LMICs (box 1). The approach emphasises empathic stakeholder engagement and iterative prototyping, often using low-fidelity tools such as personas, journey mapping and structured feedback.22 Living lab extends this further, embedding users in (semi)realistic contexts to combine co-creation and evaluation, bridging the gap between controlled usability studies and real-world implementation.23 Challenges remain in maintaining scientific rigour and consistent participant involvement,22 though frameworks such as UNICEF’s guide and the Vinyasa tool provide structured guidance.2425

Case study | Early-stage evaluation with usability-focused, Agile methodology in Malawi

Context: The study was conducted at Kamuzu Central Hospital, a tertiary referral centre in Lilongwe, Malawi. The 80-bed neonatal unit admits 5–10 sick or small neonates daily, in a country where neonatal mortality is 20 per 1000 live births.53

Intervention: NeoTree is a digital clinical decision-support application for neonatal healthcare professionals (HCPs), providing evidence-based guidelines, structured data collection and management prompts at the point of care. This study evaluated the beta-app across two minimum-viable-product iterations (MVP1 and MVP2).

Usability-focused methodology: A mixed-methods, agile user-centred design approach. Think-aloud interviews with six neonatal nurses were conducted before deployment to generate qualitative feedback on the app in theoretical conditions. Quantitative usability was measured using the System Usability Scale (SUS) before implementation (n=8) and 6 months postimplementation (n=8). A 6-month real-world pilot captured user feedback and usage data in routine care, with feedback subjected to thematic analysis within an Agile product backlog to prioritise iterative refinements.53

Findings and impact: 12 usability themes generated 57 app adjustments spanning data capture, integration with clinical workflow, locally coherent language and user-interface design. SUS scores were above average at both time points (88.1 at baseline; 89.4 at 6 months). During the pilot, 93 HCPs across five cadres recorded 1323 admissions and 1197 outcomes, achieving 100% digital coverage of sick neonates admitted. The evaluation delivered a prioritised set of product changes that de-risked implementation.

Clinical simulations

Author SG and colleagues have explored clinical simulation as a powerful tool for evaluating DHIs.2 This approach involves placing the end-user (eg, clinician or patient) in a high-fidelity scenario that requires them to perform a task closely resembling the real-world environment, often using synthetic data.26 Simulations can be deployed rapidly with minimal financial investment, a crucial advantage in resource-constrained settings. They also enable the inclusion of under-represented populations in conventional datasets, such as high-risk or marginalised groups, while preserving privacy.26 The controlled environment enables investigators to measure nuanced metrics, such as decision-making time and cognitive burden, which are challenging to evaluate in real-world settings.

Requiring specialised expertise to ensure realism and validity is a key limitation. While simulations can significantly reduce the risk of implementation, they cannot fully replace real-world evaluation. Its greatest utility lies in the broader evidence strategy, including safety, usability and potential efficacy.

Mid-stage evaluations: testing effectiveness in context

Mid-stage evaluations shift the focus towards validating clinical effectiveness and implementation within the complexities of real-world health systems. These studies aim to identify the operational drivers of success and ensure the intervention is robust enough for broader adoption.

Implementation science frameworks

Implementation science (IS) frameworks support the systemic uptake of evidence-based practice, with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR-2.0),27 Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (RE-AIM)28 and Proctor’s implementation outcomes,29 each examining how and why interventions succeed or fail in real-world settings.30 CFIR-2.0 is organised across five domains: innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals and process (applied to the Kenya Electronic Immunisation Registry case in box 2). RE-AIM assesses Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance (demonstrated in Nepal in box 3). Proctor’s taxonomy provides a structured vocabulary of eight implementation outcomes (acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration and sustainability) to specify what success means in a given context.

Case study | Late-stage evaluation with a qualitative, realist evaluation in Kenya

Context: The evaluation was conducted in routine immunisation clinics in Siaya County, western Kenya, where an Electronic Immunisation Registry (EIR) had been rolled out to all 161 immunisation facilities, but real-world workflow integration remained a challenge.54

Intervention: A tablet-based EIR application for healthcare workers (HCWs), intended to replace paper-based records for tracking child immunisations and growth monitoring. HCWs were required to perform dual data entry (EIR and traditional paper records).

Pragmatic methodology: Semistructured interviews with 19 HCWs and 58 direct workflow observations across 12 facilities were conducted to explore the interplay among technology, users and the clinical environment.

Findings and policy impact: The evaluation identified a gap in system-reality alignment. While the EIR was accepted by HCWs, its real-world use was hindered by workflow friction from dual entry, slow system performance and high patient load. Instead of a simple pass/fail verdict, the study produced actionable recommendations to guide the tool’s iterative refinement.

Application of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR-2.0): Applying CFIR-2.0, highlighted earlier in this case study, reveals implementation barriers across multiple domains. At the innovation level, the dual data entry undermined the EIR’s relative advantage over paper records. At the inner setting, high patient load, slow system performance and variable infrastructure created structural issues that the tool was not designed to accommodate. At the individual level, HCWs accepted the EIR in principle, suggesting the barrier was situational. The qualitative, realist methodology reflects strong implementation-process practices, and the resulting actionable recommendations support iterative adaptation rather than a binary adoption verdict.

Case study | Mid-stage evaluation with a hybrid design in Nepal

Context: The study was conducted in two rural, remote districts of Nepal (Achham and Dolakha), regions characterised by challenging terrain, poor infrastructure and among the nation’s poorest maternal health outcomes.

Intervention: The intervention deployed the CommCare mobile application to a cadre of salaried, supervised community health workers (CHWs). The platform provides decision support and data collection tools to guide the CHWs through a protocol of home-based maternal and child health counselling, health assessments and identification of high-risk pregnancies for referral.

Hybrid methodology: A type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation design with a mixed-methods approach allowed for the simultaneous evaluation of clinical effectiveness and key implementation outcomes. These were evaluated through institutional birth rate and the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance framework, respectively.55

Findings and impact: A statistically significant 30% increase in institutional births was found, confirming the intervention’s effectiveness. However, the hybrid design yielded more nuanced insights essential for policy and scale:

Implementation evidence: The study quantified programme reach among marginalised groups, tracked the fidelity of CHW home visits over time and documented the challenges and successes of adoption by local government bodies.

In LMICs, where infrastructure limitations, fragmented governance and equity concerns are prevalent, IS frameworks play a critical role in diagnosing system-level barriers and enablers before and during evaluations. However, their application in heterogeneous settings requires local adaptation and, where necessary, the active de-implementation of legacy practices or technologies that impede uptake.30 Yet, IS adoption remains patchy across LMIC digital health projects, with only a minority applying these strategies comprehensively,30 reflecting a broader fidelity-adaptation tension in which a departure from an intervention’s original design may be necessary for contextual fit but risks undermining the mechanisms responsible for its effectiveness.

One emerging translational approach is the silent trial, in which healthcare data are sent to a test environment to compare model performance against actual clinical outcomes. This leverages existing data infrastructure to generate early performance evidence with minimal resource burden, which is particularly valuable where prospective trials face logistical, financial and ethical constraints. When extended into translational trials, an additional sociotechnical layer assesses system-wide impact, such as workflow integration, ethical considerations and resource implications.31

Context-appropriate study design

Hybrid studies have emerged as a powerful methodology for evaluating the effectiveness of interventions and the contextual factors that shape their implementation.32 Three types of hybrid studies have been described, depending on whether the focus is primarily on effectiveness (type 1), equally on effectiveness and implementation (type 2) or primarily on implementation (type 3).32 A purpose-built framework for DHIs operationalises this across three phases: framing effectiveness and implementation questions, defining actors, activities and proximal outcomes and specifying design features that justify hybrid classification.33

There are several appropriate designs for effectiveness. Pragmatic RCTs evaluate interventions under routine care conditions, preserving internal validity while maximising external generalisability.34 Where phased or sequential rollout is operationally required, stepped-wedge cluster randomised trials offer an ethically and logistically efficient alternative, enabling all sites to eventually receive the intervention while generating robust comparative data over time.35 Adaptive platform trials extend this flexibility further, allowing investigators to modify, add or drop intervention arms in response to accumulating evidence.36

To guide design choices, investigators can use the PRagmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary (PRECIS-2),37 which evaluates a trial across nine domains to determine how closely the trial mirrors the usual care environment, ensuring that design choices are explicitly aligned with the implementation contexts in which DHIs must ultimately succeed.

Late-stage evaluations: scaling with real-world evidence

Late-stage evaluations are designed to generate the longitudinal real-world evidence (RWE) and economic data required to support sustainable national scaling and policy-level integration. These evaluations examine system-wide impact and affordability, providing the value proposition for long-term institutional investment.

Real-world studies

RWE refers to insights drawn from data collected outside controlled trials, such as routine clinical records, registries and programmatic evaluations. In LMICs, however, data are often fragmented across disparate physical and digital systems or within inoperable legacy infrastructure. This makes it challenging to build the standardised, longitudinal datasets typically required for RWE.

As DHIs expand, particularly mHealth platforms, telehealth services and electronic registries, they provide the opportunity to generate large volumes of real-world data. If standardised and structured, these data streams can provide the foundation for RWE that is not dependent on legacy and siloed infrastructure and systems.38 This enables digital tools to serve as both the subjects of evaluation and enablers of improved evaluation practices.

Health economics evidence

Economic evaluations of DHIs are crucial in LMIC settings, where funding constraints, overburdened health systems and competing priorities demand rigorous evidence of value and affordability. Increasingly, evaluations have shifted towards building disaggregated value propositions that detail components such as clinical impact, service delivery, efficiencies and system-level savings. These can be derived from routine data and non-trial evidence rather than relying solely on efficacy hierarchies.

Standard methods present distinct challenges in this context. Many interventions lack clear causal pathways to health outcomes, which in turn limits the use of conventional metrics such as cost per DALY averted. Benefits derived across various health areas are difficult to aggregate, yet essential; costing presents equally significant hurdles, as indirect infrastructure support remains challenging to capture.39 This is particularly difficult for digital health global goods, open-source platforms designed to be adapted and reused across multiple countries, which are often perceived to be free. In practice, however, they incur substantial costs, including localisation, integration, and equipment and licensing costs, none of which are captured in headline cost figures.40 A Total Cost of Ownership analysis of a cancer registry in Kenya, built on DHIS2 (a widely deployed open-source health information platform), found significant cost variation across facilities depending on size, service scope and ownership structure, underscoring that the implementation costs are neither fixed nor predictable.41 No standard method currently exists to quantify dependencies on human, physical and digital infrastructure.

The choice of evaluation method should be matched to the stage of the intervention lifecycle and the decision being made. Cost-consequence analysis is well-suited to early-stage digital health evaluations in LMICs, where causal pathways are often unclear and decision-makers need flexibility to weigh outcomes according to local priorities. Cost-effectiveness analysis and cost–utility analysis are most appropriate during and after efficacy trials, where causal pathways to health outcomes can be established and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios estimated against a comparator. Budget impact analysis becomes relevant at the adoption and scale-up point, where payers and health systems need to understand the aggregated financial consequences of implementation rather than relative efficiency.

Methodological implications for equity

The methodological approaches discussed in this section offer concrete mechanisms for addressing the equity failures identified in ‘Contextual and stakeholder dynamics shaping evidence generation’. HCD and living-lab approaches counter epistemic inequity by embedding local stakeholders directly in the framing of research questions and the selection of outcomes, rather than importing externally defined priorities. Clinical simulation and the Reach domain of RE-AIM, demonstrated in box 3, enable the inclusion and measurement of marginalised and high-risk populations, otherwise under-represented in conventional evaluations. The realist evaluation approach applied in box 2 resists donor-driven, one-size-fits-all effectiveness claims by generating context-specific evidence of what works, for whom and under what conditions. Data sovereignty concerns, meanwhile, are addressed through the national data governance mechanisms discussed in ‘Top-down policy and regulatory mechanisms that enable sustainable adoption and scale’.

Top-down policy and regulatory mechanisms that enable sustainable adoption and scale

In many LMICs, digital health frameworks and policies are siloed, fragmented or inconsistently applied, leaving innovators uncertain about what constitutes sufficient evidence for adoption and scale. A systematic review of eHealth policy frameworks across LMICs found that while many countries are investing in national digital health strategies, these efforts remain fragmented and their impact on health service delivery has not been adequately evaluated.9 Critically, only a minority of published frameworks were developed explicitly for LMIC contexts, with most drawing on European or high-income country experience.9

The regulatory heterogeneity observed at a global level plays out in different ways across LMIC regions. India demonstrates a framework in transition: the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation issued draft guidance for Software as a Medical Device (SaMD),42 and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act43 provides the statutory basis for health-data governance.43 Brazil has pursued a codified, risk-based route, with ANVISA Resolution RDC 657/2022 formalising dedicated SaMD pathways that align with international standards.44 Nigeria’s NAFDAC and South Africa’s SAHPRA have increasingly leveraged approvals from reference authorities to accelerate local registration, while simultaneously enforcing domestic data-privacy laws with Nigeria’s Data Protection Act (2023) and South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act, respectively.4546 Together, these show that LMIC regulatory development is not converging on a single model or mapping onto high-income templates.

Global frameworks can help bridge these gaps. Resources such as the WHO’s monitoring and evaluation guidance for digital health,8 the mHealth Reporting and Assessment checklist,47 the iCHECK-DH reporting standards48 and the International Telecommunication Union’s (ITU) Clinical Evaluation of AI for health49 provide practical templates for specifying outcomes and comparators. The challenge ahead lies in harmonising these global resources with national strategies, ensuring that evidence expectations are predictable for innovators while remaining sensitive to local realities.

While global frameworks exist,6850 the broad adoption of a rigorous risk-based classification system has been absent. This pattern is mirrored across the broader governance of AI: many LMICs formally endorse global AI and data governance principles, yet national adoption remains inconsistent and often lacks enforceable mechanisms.12 In these fragmented environments, unregulated digital marketplaces proliferate, undermining public trust due to concerns over data breaches, confidentiality and liability.5152 Addressing this fragmentation requires national data governance frameworks that are enforceable and explicitly designed to ensure that data generated in LMIC health systems remain under national control, with clearly defined terms for international access, data residency and secondary use. Without this, the evidence base generated through international partnerships risks being inaccessible to the very health systems it is intended to serve.

Conclusions

Translating this framework into practice requires two sequential conditions. First, national and global regulators must define explicit, stage-appropriate evidence requirements that recognise pragmatic, context-sensitive evaluation as sufficient for adoption decisions. Without this, the second condition cannot follow: that is, funders and implementers shift investments towards mid-stage evaluations to break the cycle of pilotitis. If evidence expectations remain opaque or implicitly anchored in pharmaceutical trial hierarchies, there is no rational incentive to fund this work. Together, these shifts make staged evidence generation not merely theoretically defensible but institutionally viable. Whether this causal chain holds in practice—specifically, whether regulatory clarity demonstrably shifts funding behaviour and evidence practices—remains an important empirical question and a priority for future research.
