“Truly having a conversation with each member about their needs”: accessibility considerations for the engagement of people with lived and living experience and caregivers in mental health and substance use health research
Authors: Lisa D Hawke, Wuraola Dada-Phillips, Charlotte Munro, Shoshana Hauer, Claudia Sendanyoye, Katie Upham, Mary Rose van Kesteren, Abigail Amartey, Yona Lunsky, Tanya Halsall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe engagement of people with lived/living experience and caregivers in the research process can have many benefits for research and all those involved. However, research engagement settings need to be accessible to ensure that people with lived/living experience and caregivers can contribute fully to the research process, including in mental health and substance use health research contexts. This qualitative descriptive study aimed to understand the perspectives of people with lived/living experience and caregivers with regards to accessibility needs in contexts of engagement in mental health and substance use health research.MethodA sample of 20 people with lived/living experience and caregivers with research engagement experience in the mental health and substance use health sector participated in one of five focus group discussions. Discussions were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using codebook thematic analysis. A Lived/Living Experience and Caregiver Working Group supported all stages of the project, from design to reporting.ResultsFour themes were generated from the data: (1) Ongoing and transparent accessibility processes. (2) Offering a range of supports. (3) Allowing flexible ways to contribute. (4) Providing fair compensation. Themes are supported by subthemes and described with illustrative quotes.ConclusionsEnsuring that engagement is accessible and that appropriate accessibility supports are provided is essential to ensuring an authentic, meaningful engagement experience. This is an ongoing process that should be considered throughout the lifespan of the research project. By planning for accessibility early and throughout the project, research teams can help ensure that people with lived/living experience and caregivers can contribute meaningfully to the research to the best of their ability and without undue hardship.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00912-6.Keywords: Lived experience, Patient engagement, Patient and public involvement, Mental health, Substance use health

Background

Health research paradigms are increasingly calling for the engagement of people with lived and living experience and caregivers as collaborators in the research process [1, 2]. Moving away from models in which researchers see ‘patients’ as ‘subjects’ to study, people with lived/living experience and caregivers are now recognized by many researchers as colleagues in the research endeavor, often in lived experience advisory or co-researcher capacities. Engagement in the research process has emerged as a science in itself, with a sizeable body of literature supporting it [3, 4]. Engagement is particularly important in the context of mental health and substance use health research, given factors such as past and present inequities in research and clinical care, widespread stigma, high rates of trauma, ongoing power imbalances in research and care settings, and the intersection of mental health and substance use health challenges with social and structural determinants of health [5–9].

In mental health and substance use health research, there are many benefits to engaging people with lived/living experience and caregivers (note: these groups are named separately in this paper to differentiate caregiver perspectives from personal past and current experience with mental health challenges/service use). Notably, lived/living experience and caregiver engagement can enhance the accessibility and relevance of the research for both the general public and lived/living experience populations [10, 11]. It can make the research more relevant, leading to care that is more responsive to the needs of those receiving it. People with lived/living experience and caregivers can help develop participant-facing materials and knowledge translation tools to make them more acceptable, accessible, and appealing, while helping establish research priorities that align with the needs and interests of lived/living experience groups [10, 11]. When people with lived/living experience and caregivers are engaged in mental health and substance use health research, there are positive impacts on the individuals engaged, on the researchers, on the research environment, and on the research itself [11].

However, researchers often encounter barriers in implementing engagement. Common challenges include inadequate training and knowledge of engagement practices, logistical barriers, problems with team dynamics, interpersonal challenges, and structural barriers within institutions, among others [11]. Recognizing and addressing these barriers is essential to advancing the science of research engagement and reducing administrative barriers to effective engagement, both to maximize its effectiveness and to proactively reduce potential challenges.

Ensuring engagement practices reflect and directly address the priorities articulated by people with lived/living experience and caregivers is crucial for impactful, non-tokenistic collaboration in research. Guidelines and frameworks are available to support authentic research engagement across all areas of health, as well as specifically within the mental health and substance use health sector [1, 2, 12]. These resources recommend many common concepts, including the notion that engagement must be accessible for the individuals engaged. They include some suggestions that accessibility might encompass aspects such as providing people with lived/living experience and caregivers with the time and opportunities to contribute meaningfully, choice and flexibility around how and what they contribute, and means of supporting their meaningful engagement when needed. Accessibility broadly refers to the design of environments, services, and resources for those who experience disabilities [13]. While there are publicly available guidelines from public and advocacy organizations to support the accessibility of physical spaces, print materials, and digital resources for people with physical disabilities [14, 15], little is known about accessibility support needs in research engagement settings, particularly in mental health and substance use health contexts where those engaged may have unique needs related to their mental health and substance use experiences and the nature of the research endeavor. Comprehensive accounts of how people with lived/living experience and caregivers experience accessibility and what specific supports they require in research settings remain largely absent from the literature.

Objective

This qualitative descriptive study aimed to understand the perspectives of people with lived/living experience and caregivers with regards to accessibility support needs in contexts of engagement in mental health and substance use health research.

Methods

Members of a Lived/Living Experience and Caregiver Working group, comprised of three people with lived/living experience and two caregivers, were engaged in all stages of the project, following the Patient Engagement in Research framework [1]. This study was guided by pragmatism as a paradigm, which is consistent with the engagement orientation of the project [16]. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guidelines (Appendix A) were used to describe the study as a whole [17]. We have described the research engagement in the project following a recent reporting framework specific to lived/living experience engagement in mental health and substance use health research [18], in Table 1. Description of the research engagement

Participants & recruitment

A sample of 20 people with lived/living experience and caregivers participated in one of five focus groups. Note, focus group participants were distinct from Working Group members. To be eligible, individuals had to be at least 16 years of age, living in Canada, and have experience with engagement as a person with lived or living experience in the mental health/substance use health research. Five focus groups is a sufficient sample size as recommended in the literature [20]. The research team judged that information redundancy had been achieved, as new themes were not generated from the data collected last.

We conducted the study at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH), a tertiary care hospital with a strong engagement infrastructure that encompasses people with lived/living experience and caregivers on staff, in advisory or working groups, and in other formats. Participant recruitment took place in a number of ways. We invited Canada-wide participants who had research engagement experience from across Canada (as collaborators, advisors, or co-researchers) and who had participated in one of our four previous projects on engagement (as participants) conducted out of the same research institute, who had provided consent for future research contact. In addition, we circulated the study flyer, which included contact information for the research team, to a range of engagement bodies and via social media (X). Participant recruitment began on Jan 6, 2025 and ended on Feb 13, 2025.

Procedure

Potential participants reached out to research staff by phone, email, or text, were screened, and provided virtual signed informed consent. As per our ethics board approval, parental consent was not required for youth under the age of 18. They then completed a demographic form, which was hosted on REDCap software on a secure server [21]. We scheduled them into the next available focus group discussion via email as numbers and schedules allowed. Focus groups took place on the WebEx system, which was hosted on a secure CAMH server. Focus group discussions took between 71 and 96 mins to complete and were attended by between two and six participants each. One research staff member and one research placement student co-facilitated the focus groups. The discussions were audio recorded, transcribed by the WebEx transcription service, and carefully cleaned by research staff members. To address accessibility, we assisted in troubleshooting connectivity issues, supported participants with WebEx functions, and enabled the use of the chat feature to facilitate participation, especially for participants who hesitated to speak aloud. Focus groups took place between January 23 and February 19, 2025. Compensation was provided to participants in the form of a $50 gift card. The study protocol was approved by the CAMH Research Ethics Board.

Data collection tools

The Lived/Living Experience and Caregiver Working Group helped refine a demographic form, which included basic personal information (i.e., age, gender, country of birth, and racial/ethnic background) and information about participants’ research engagement experience. The Working Group also co-developed an in-depth semi-structured interview guide. This interview guide addressed a range of aspects related to accessibility and inclusivity in engagement in mental health and substance use health research. It was pilot tested amongst research staff prior to use. The interview guide is provided in Appendix C.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using codebook thematic analysis [22], which combines deductive and inductive coding. After the completion of the five focus group discussions, the two co-facilitators, one Working Group member, and the research lead discussed the findings and summarized them in the form of a codebook to begin coding the data. These codes were entered into NVivo 12 [23], and the transcripts were coded by a staff member. Through semi-weekly discussions, we iteratively refined the codebook based on the data and coding experience. We brought the codebook, codes, and tentative themes to the Lived/Living Experience and Caregiver Working Group for discussion and refinement, and to help choose representative quotes to illustrate the findings, thereby enhancing rigor and trustworthiness.

The project was supported by a Lived/Living Experience Working Group who were full members of the research team engaged at all phases of the research. Research team reflexivity

The data analyst and lead facilitator (WDP) is a black woman with a Master’s in Public Health. She drew on her academic knowledge and lived/living experience to center lived/living experience and caregiver perspectives. She prioritizes fostering an inclusive, equitable research environment, with her reflective approach informing the data collection and analysis process. The scientific lead (LDH) is a white woman with a Ph.D. in psychology and a research focus on lived/living experience and caregiver engagement in research. She is committed to improving the accessibility of her engagement process and approached the current project as a form of pragmatic guidance for her ongoing work. The Working Group brings together experts with both lived and living expertise that is inclusive of mental health and substance use experience, as well as education, career histories, and life stories. The Working Group members have experience with research engagement and demonstrate commitment to improve the science and practice of engagement in research.

Accessibility needs in engagement in mental health and substance use health research: themes and sub-themes

Ongoing and transparent accessibility processes

Participants shared that accessibility needs can change over time. They stressed the importance of continuous check-ins to address emerging needs, documenting accessibility needs to inform future research, and maintaining transparency about what can and cannot be accommodated.

A key discussion from our focus groups was the importance of continuously checking in about accessibility needs (subtheme 1a). One participant expressed this by stating:

I also think the accessibility issue should not be a one-and-done thing, like where you decide at the start of a team that these are everyone’s needs, because it is something that constantly evolves and changes (Participant 01)

Participants added that the early involvement of people with lived/living experience and caregivers with diverse accessibility needs was essential, noting: “[I]f people were involved earlier, they would have designed things in a way they could participate” (Participant 02). Checking in regularly and authentically allows individuals to express new or changing needs as they arise, particularly for those who may not fully understand their accessibility requirements at the outset. One participant shared, “Truly having a conversation with each member about their needs […], I don’t know what I need. I only find out once I’m not able to participate fully” (Participant 03).

Moreover, participants stressed that these check-ins should go beyond generic options or assumptions, but should instead be a collaborative approach. As one participant pointed out, “Maybe ask me what my opinion might be […]. You gotta co-design everything so you’re doing the right thing from the start” (Participant 04).

Many participants discussed the importance of documenting accessibility needs as part of the ongoing accessibility process, emphasizing that this helps inform future research processes (subtheme 1b). Without records, future research teams cannot work as transparently and learn from past challenges, limiting opportunities for improvement.

I think it would also be helpful for research teams to identify what the limits and the accessibility accommodations were, because I think that can present for a more like a comprehensive research topic in the future where they may be able to include that, right? (Participant 05)

Participants highlighted that there is a need for a standardized process to systematically collect and document feedback from those engaged (subtheme 1c). They highlighted that a lack of proper documentation of feedback from the engaged results in “erasing that type of lived experience” (Participant 02) for those facing barriers. Additionally, one participant proposed that this could be in the form of “exit interviews” and these interviews be conducted “by other patient partners or somebody outside of the committee” (Participant 03) to enhance trust and transparency.

Some participants acknowledged that you cannot meet the needs of everyone, so providing options and communicating your desire to improve is essential. They appreciated when researchers were transparent about this and offered alternative solutions when certain accessibility supports are not feasible.

I think it’s an unfortunate case where we sometimes cannot meet the needs of everyone. Researchers should try their best, but it’s important to be up front in case recommendations cannot be met of certain individuals (Participant 06)

Offering a range of supports

Participants expressed that a range of accessibility supports are important. They highlighted the importance of providing language interpreters, American Sign Language (ASL), captioning, reliable internet connectivity, technology support, breaks during meetings, and ensuring accessible, non-stigmatizing communication for those with these types of accessibility needs.

Participants emphasized that language is a barrier to accessibility (subtheme 2a). Many agreed, noting that language differences can make it difficult for individuals to express their ideas in a way that feels comfortable and allows them to engage fully. One participant stated:

[A]nd there should be translators. Different people are from different communities and they have different languages, and it is very hard for them to express their perspectives and their views, their ideas (Participant 07)

The need for various types of translators or interpreters was also discussed, such as “ASL interpreters for people who are hard of hearing” (Participant 08). However, challenges related to the availability of interpreters were raised, with the participant adding that these supports can be “difficult with the budgets of research” (Participant 08).

The importance of captioning in supporting engagement in meetings was also discussed as an accessibility concern. Some participants suggested that captioning helps individuals stay informed and engaged, especially when they are unable to participate fully in real-time discussions. A participant shared, “And then the closed captioning – I’ve actually used it sometimes if I have something, like, if I have to take a call or something during a meeting” (Participant 09).

Discussions highlighted the need for internet connectivity and technology support for accessible engagement, particularly amongst those with limited internet access that may prevent access to engagement spaces (subtheme 2b). Participants shared concerns about limited internet access, explaining that they sometimes had to leave their home and rely on public spaces to connect. They explained that this issue is especially prevalent in remote areas, where disparities limit access. “I live in an area that if I just go, you know, X amount of kilometers down the street to a couple of blocks, folks don’t have fantastic internet” (Participant 10).

Participants explained that technology is always changing, acknowledging, “It’s a learning curve” (Participant 11) and that platforms like Zoom, Teams and Webex can be challenging to navigate. A participant suggested “5–10 minute […] tech checks” (Participant 04) before meetings could ensure smoother engagement, particularly for those less familiar with the platforms. Another participant suggested that a technology check-in could help participants familiarize themselves with systems to increase digital accessibility:

So, having a good sort of briefing/teaching period, that like allows people to get the hang of and have someone who’s already worked through the system sort of teach them all the tips and important things that allows them to jump fully into the research (Participant 1)

Additionally, participants highlighted the importance of taking breaks during meetings as an important accessibility consideration among individuals with considerations on the mental health or substance use spectrum (subtheme 2c). They discussed the challenges of maintaining focus during long sessions, particularly for those with conditions that affect attention span. One participant explained, “I find I get into the state of overwhelm […] sometimes, if I push my brain too hard” (Participant 08). Another participant also expressed the challenge of having to push back and defend their decision to take breaks.

I have had to advocate for myself and just say, ‘hey, this is like my retention. This is my period in which I can focus, and then I need a break. Then I can focus again.’ (Participant 12)

Many participants expressed that accessible communication styles are essential for improving accessibility (subtheme 2d). They mentioned that this involves using clear, plain language that is easy to understand. For example, one participant expressed, “The jargon, and it’s all the acronyms, and then ‘You know you what are you talking about? I have no idea what you’re talking about’” (Participant 04). Some participants highlighted the frequent lack of accessibility supports for people with lived/living experience and caregivers with cognitive disabilities and people who process information differently, recommending need for “visual aids” and avoiding “fast-paced discussions” (Participant 03).

Additionally, participants discussed the importance of non-stigmatizing communication as an accessibility consideration specific to mental health and substance use, noting that stigma and bias are present in research. They stressed the need for sensitivity in the language used and awareness of potential triggers, specific to the mental health and substance use health sector. For example, a participant mentioned that terms like “crazy” or “psychotic” (Participant 08) should be avoided. They called on researchers to create safe spaces where people with lived/living experience and caregivers feel comfortable expressing themselves without fear of judgment.

Allowing flexible ways to contribute

An essential aspect of accessibility is flexibility, allowing people with lived/living experience and caregivers to contribute in ways that work best for them. Participants highlighted that flexibility includes the option to join meetings in person or remotely, creating avenues to participate during meetings, adapting meeting times and deadlines, and providing different ways to review content.

Many participants explained that there is a need for flexibility regarding in-person and remote options to make engagement more accessible to people with a range of living situations and personal needs (subtheme 3a). They suggested offering options depending on team capacity, including a mixture of in-person and hybrid, or either option individually. They expressed that having in-person meetings is great for building relationships, but can exclude certain groups from an accessibility standpoint—particularly those who are “immunocompromised” (Participant 13) or have “geographic limitations” (Participant 09). A participant added:

I can count on my fingers how many in-person meetings [I] had, but the obvious difference is that, the physical accessibility because I’m also on a disability panel and we have people on wheelchairs and you never think about that when you’re in a Zoom environment (Participant 03)

For hybrid formats, there were varied opinions. Some mentioned this is a good way to reach more people with lived/living experience and caregivers, but if not done appropriately, it could lack coordination. A participant expressed that some teams may lack adequate technology or synchronization, leading to issues such as “loss in translation” and “chaos” (Participant 09) in communication.

Participants explained that it is great to provide multiple avenues for engagement when online, including chat and camera options to maximize digital accessibility (subtheme 3b). Many appreciated chat options during virtual meetings, especially for those who may not feel comfortable speaking up. One participant mentioned:

I think the chat option is great because a lot of people don’t like speaking up on Zoom. So, they’ll put their idea in chat and then the moderator may ask them if they want to talk about it or just read the comment (Participant 11)

Additionally, participants emphasized the importance of camera flexibility, noting that some people with lived/living experience and caregivers felt the need to keep their cameras off for personal reasons, such as privacy or fatigue.

So, I’m turning my camera off so I can actually eat my dinner, because I haven’t had any food all day long and I don’t want to be on camera doing it, but I can still be sitting here listening and contributing so very much (Participant 04)

Participants added that flexible meeting times and deadlines make engagement more accessible (subtheme 3c). Some explained that inconsistency in scheduling made it difficult to participate, as they never know exactly when meetings will take place.

And so the lack of consistent time kind of makes it challenging to make my way into the research world. So yeah, and then just the time aspect. Like, certain things run at 1:00 during the day and some of them can run up till 7:00 in the day. So, you just never know exactly what it will be (Participant 14)

Additionally, strict deadlines were considered to cause exclusion. A participant shared, “you know, deadlines aren’t really helpful” (Participant 12). Another participant expressed frustration about receiving a draft “two days before they needed feedback on it” (Participant 09), which they considered to be an inaccessibility factor that led them to question whether their feedback was genuinely valued. Many shared similar experiences, a participant expressed that this was simply a “tokenistic approach,” warning against “assuming capacity” and “cognitive mental skills” (Participant 03), urging researchers to check in on availability and workload and to not assume ability from an ableist perspective. Further discussions highlighted the interplay between deadlines and power dynamics, as they were often set within researchers’ preferences, disregarding the added burden on those engaged. As a participant pointed out, “I think it also speaks to power, right?” (Participant 09), emphasizing how these decisions can reflect power imbalances.

Participants felt that accessibility could be enhanced by offering flexible ways to review content, noting that Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools make this possible (subtheme 3d). Many suggested that this could be done by providing pre- and post-meeting materials supported by AI such as, recordings, summaries, agendas, action items and transcripts.

And so what I have really enjoyed is having my meetings recorded through an AI app, and so afterwards I will get a summary of what was discussed in the meeting. If I agreed to do something, it will actually give me a summary and say this person agreed to this and this person agreed to that (Participant 10)

Additionally, participants explained that reviewing materials can be overwhelming, suggesting that researchers provide a “format template or rubric” (Participant 15) to help guide them on what to focus on to make the review more accessible to them. Some also mentioned the benefits of collaborating in shared spaces using “online document repositories” (Participant 16) and engaging in “asynchronous time,” where they can “leave comments” (Participant 14).

Concerns about these options were also raised. One participant emphasized the need to accommodate “different levels of literacy,” noting that researchers expect people with lived/living experience and caregivers to “write stuff, which is not ideal at all” (Participant 12). Another participant highlighted ethical concerns regarding the use of recordings and transcripts, explaining that policies on their use may need to be updated or clarified within organizations.

Providing fair compensation

When discussing accessibility options that can be helpful, participants highlighted that people with lived/living experience and caregivers must be fairly compensated for their contributions to support the accessibility of research engagement. Two subthemes emerged: the importance of fair compensation whether online or in-person, recognizing that all forms of participation require time and resources, and the need for dependent care compensation.

Many participants discussed the financial burden of engaging in mental health and substance use health research (subtheme 4a). They shared that meeting in person requires additional costs such as transportation, which can make engagement inaccessible if not accommodated, particularly for those on a limited income. Many agreed, expressing that compensation for in-person meetings should reflect these additional costs, including “public transit fare” (Participant 1) or “Uber” (Participant 08). Remote participation was identified as an important alternative, but participants noted the need for “virtual options that are as close to the in-person as much as possible” (Participant 16).

Participants expressed that it is important to “accommodate people for their time” (Participant 17) and appreciated receiving food and drinks as part of compensation, insisting these benefits be available both in-person and remotely. One participant noted that the researchers should “send you like a gift card for your meals because they were providing meals at the event in person” (Participant 16). However, a major limitation was that many teams required those engaged to pay for their travel upfront, later reimbursing them. This delayed compensation, and it sometimes “took months for it to come” (Participant 15). Another participant expanded on this:

[S]ome of the teams require [that when] we travel, you have to pay everything up front and then they reimburse you. But other ones, they book everything for you and they pay it up front, so nothing comes out of your pocket. […] Do people have space in the bank account or on their credit card to put expenses? (Participant 16)

Participants expressed a need for dependent care compensation during engagement to increase accessibility (subtheme 4b). Childcare was identified as a significant accessibility consideration, and teams offering childcare services for in-person meetings were “always super appreciated” (Participant 17). However, they noted a lack of support for caregivers engaging in research. One participant explained that the inability of many research teams to provide funding or compensation for childcare affects who can engage in the research study, limiting accessibility and representativeness.

For example, like childcare stuff I know is tough because a lot of research teams don’t really have the capacity to provide funding for that or compensation, and that’s a big factor for who can participate in research (Participant 05)

Additionally, they shared concerns that research teams may not fully consider the varied caregiving responsibilities of people with lived/living experience and caregivers who rely on support animals. Taking caregiving into account was an important accessibility consideration.

I have a companion dog, one-step below service dog, and they would not provide allowance for my dog care when they want me to attend conferences (Participant 03)

Discussion

Our study explored lived/living experience and caregiver perspectives on accessibility needs in mental health and substance use health research. Four primary themes were generated: (1) ongoing and transparent accessibility processes, (2) offering a range of supports, (3) allowing flexible ways to contribute, and (4) providing fair compensation. Participants stressed that accessibility requires continuous check-ins, systematic documentation, and clear communication about evolving needs. Participants highlighted essential supports such as language interpreters, ASL, captioning, and technology assistance, as well as scheduled breaks, non-stigmatizing communication, and flexible ways to contribute. Additionally, fair compensation ultimately ensures equitable reimbursement for in-person and online contributions, including support for dependent care.

Accessibility goes beyond physical access, such as accessible travel, ramps, and spaces, to include access to information and the way spaces are run to enable full participation. Information access is defined in the literature as pertaining to the accessibility of presentations, discussions, and materials [24]. However, previous research has suggested that people with invisible disabilities, such as cognitive or developmental disabilities or differences, may struggle with tasks requiring intense concentration or long meetings with heavy agendas [25]. Research indicates that incorporating rest periods during mentally demanding tasks and providing material in advance and in various formats can help improve productivity [26]. This aligns with the current findings about addressing accessibility beyond physical access, to include greater accessibility for those with invisible disabilities. Researchers should challenge their unconscious bias or research preferences and work to create a space where people feel more comfortable and supported [25]. Findings highlighted the influence of power dynamics in relation to researcher expectations around deadlines for feedback, indicating accessibility supports should also account for potential relational or structural power imbalances within research settings. Furthermore, it is important to understand disability as a normal aspect of life and advocate for inclusive language that acknowledges individuals’ roles and identities beyond their disabilities [24]. In mental health and substance use health research contexts, accessibility includes facilitating meetings through a trauma-informed lens, as failure to do so may lead to re-traumatization [25]. Thinking holistically about accessibility, incorporating aspects specific to mental health and substance use health, may help people with lived/living experience and caregivers engage fully in the research process.

The current results highlight the essential role of technology in enhancing accessibility to mental health and substance use health research, while also exposing significant challenges. This dynamic was further amplified by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has been explained to have accelerated the shift of more lived/living experience and caregiver research engagement to online platforms [27]. Although this transition maintained participation, many have reported mixed or negative experiences due to technical difficulties, lack of internet access, and digital literacy challenges [27, 28]. To address these barriers, our findings and other research highlight that researchers should not presume that everyone has the necessary equipment, software, and expertise to engage in this work virtually [25]. Instead, they should consider strategies such as loaning necessary devices to people, encouraging the use of technology available through schools or public libraries, guiding those unfamiliar with digital tools, and offering resources on digital platforms [29]. Additionally, teams might consider offering alternative ways of engagement, for example by recording sessions for offline viewing, using accessible online tools, and archiving sessions online for future reference [28]. Recent research has further suggested that AI tools might provide additional options, for example generating summaries and actionable items from research interactions [30, 31]. However, the practicality of AI tools and other technology use must be balanced with privacy and confidentiality concerns given the sensitive nature of sharing lived experience, which was highlighted as especially important by our Lived/Living Experience Working Group members, adding lived experience voices to this concern. With proper implementation and upholding ethical standards, the use of technology can provide improved accessibility to enhance lived/living experience and caregiver engagement.

Participants pointed out the potential infeasibility of offering specific accessibility supports, sometimes from a budgetary perspective. While it is true that offering accessibility supports can sometimes come with a cost and can be challenging for some research teams, this can often be mitigated by careful project planning. If accessibility supports are planned for at the grant application stage, they can be written into budgets appropriately. Potentially important budget lines include equitable compensation [32], salaries for engagement support staff [33], ASL or other interpretation, dependent care expenses, travel expenses, and the cost for any software required to make print materials that are both engaging and accessible to people with disabilities. While research funding can be limited, researchers should also keep in mind that in the scope of a large health research grant, the cost of lived experience and caregiver engagement is modest, and some flexibility can be had here through appropriate forethought. Another barrier that may interfere with offering some forms of accessibility supports is the need for legal agreements for providers, like interpreters, who might have to go through a lengthy legal process to sign institutional confidentiality agreements to be involved. Future research might attend to the cost-benefit relationship of instituting strong lived/living experience and caregiver engagement practices, including recommended accessibility supports [34]; however, this should be done with due consideration that engagement is a democratizing practice and ethical imperative that should occur regardless of the results of any cost-benefit analysis.

The Lived/Living Experience Working Group raised a number of considerations for specific reflection and future consideration. Creating a safe space to discuss accessibility needs was highlighted as important, noting that acknowledging accessibility needs may involve a sense of vulnerability and requires trusting relationships. In addition, they considered compensation to be a very important consideration for accessibility, to enable people with lived/living experience and caregivers to engage in research without undue hardship. The Working Group highlighted the importance of compensating those engaged for all of their contributions, including meeting time, asynchronous work, debriefs, conflict resolution time, and conference or symposium attendance. It should be acknowledged that it might take different people different amounts of time to achieve a task, and timelines and budgets should therefore be flexible. This requires recognizing that individual payments should be equitable but may not always be equal, which could be a source of conflict in some teams. They emphasized that payment should be in cash or e-transfers whenever possible, not gift cards, to respect their financial autonomy. However, it was recognized that this might require accessibility supports, for example, in cases in which the individual does not have a bank account or a mailing address. They felt that at full-day events, harm reduction supplies should be provided for greater accessibility among people who use drugs, while peer support might be made available for both people who use drugs and people with mental health challenges.

Strengths and limitations

We engaged people with lived/living experience and caregivers in all stages of this study, from development to knowledge translation, increasing relevance. The sample includes substantial ethnic diversity, as well as diversity in age and experience in supporting research conducted across the lifespan. However, the sample size is not large enough to derive findings specific to particular subgroups of the population. Future research is required to understand the accessibility needs of specific populations, such as Indigenous, 2SLGBTQI+, and homeless or precariously housed participants, those outside of the Canadian context, and those with both physical and mental health challenges. While we recruited people with lived and living experience of mental health and substance use health challenges, which can be conceptualized as a disability, we did not ask participants if they personally identified as people with disabilities and we did not ask whether they had other overlapping disabilities, which may limit the findings. It should also be noted that we combined engagement in mental health and substance use health research, although there may be differences between these groups that were not detected. Future research could examine the differences between engagement and accessibility in mental health versus substance use contexts, as well as researchers’ experiences in generating accessible engagement settings, including the barriers encountered and mitigating strategies. Finally, lived/living experience of mental health and substance use challenges is a broad category encompassing different disorders at varying levels of severity. Future research should look to explore unique accessibility needs across specific disorder types.

Conclusions

Ensuring that research engagement is accessible and that appropriate accessibility supports are provided is essential to ensuring an authentic, meaningful engagement experience. This is an ongoing process that should be considered throughout the lifespan of the research project, through an ongoing and transparent process, in order to ensure that lived/living experience needs are met through a range of accessibility supports, with embedded flexibility and fair compensation. Researchers should consider offering supports for physical health needs, but also for invisible disabilities and cognitive needs, together with fair compensation for individuals in this sector. By planning for accessibility at the outset of the project, budgeting for it appropriately, and discussing needs regularly, researchers can ensure that they are equipped to support the people with lived/living experience and caregivers they engage, helping ensure that they can contribute meaningfully to the research to the best of their ability.
