Impact of Chronic Opioid Use in Recurrent Dislocations following Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty: An Iatrogenic Charcot-Like Arthropathy Case Report
Authors: Sang Yoon Kang, Hong Seok Kim, Sun-Hyung Lee, Hee Joong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The challenging complication of recurrent dislocation following total hip arthroplasty (THA) is often attributed to component malposition, soft tissue imbalance, or spinopelvic pathology. We report a case of intractable recurrent dislocation following revision THA for a periprosthetic femoral fracture in a 91-year-old female. The patient experienced multiple painless dislocations despite performing component revision to enhance stability and confirming acceptable combined anteversion by computed tomography. We performed a comprehensive review of the patient’s history and noted chronic, high-dose analgesic use, including the continuous use of a fentanyl patch for three years. Therefore, we hypothesized chronic opioid use contributed to the severe hyposensitivity to pain by eliminating the protective sensory feedback mechanisms essential to joint stability and mimicking the pathophysiology of Charcot arthropathy. Since potent analgesics can mask protective sensation and precipitate iatrogenic Charcot-like arthropathy, our case highlights the necessity to evaluate medication history in elderly patients with unexplained, painless recurrent instability.

Neuropathic arthropathy, first described by Dr. Jean-Martin Charcot1) in 1868, is characterized by rapid, progressive joint destruction due to loss of protective sensation and proprioception2). Although traditionally classified as a neurological disorder, the condition fundamentally arises from the absence of neuromuscular feedback mechanisms that protect the joint3,4).

In patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA), loss of protective sensation is a well-known risk factor for recurrent dislocation5,6). Although hyposensitivity is primarily due to intrinsic neurological deficits, a functionally similar state can be induced pharmacologically. We encountered a case of intractable, recurrent dislocation following stem revision, in which iatrogenic hyposensitivity by the administration of potent analgesics rather than an intrinsic neurological disorder was considered as the underlying cause. Our report aims to highlight the likelihood of analgesic-induced Charcot-like arthropathy and emphasizes the importance of medication history to manage recurrent instability.

CASE REPORT

A 91-year-old female presented with a periprosthetic femoral fracture (Vancouver type B2) around the femoral stem of the right hip, following primary THA performed eight years ago (Fig. 1A). The patient had a history of moderate lumbar spinal stenosis, anxiety disorder, and mild cognitive impairment. To address the unstable stem, we performed revision THA using a double-tapered wedge stem (Summit; DePuy-Synthes) combined with open reduction and internal fixation using a grip plate and cable system (Fig. 1B). In the absence of surgical complications and while on bed rest, the patient experienced a posterior dislocation on postoperative day (POD) 4 (Fig. 1C). Closed reduction under sedation was used to manage the dislocation. Subsequently, recurrent posterior dislocations occurred on POD 9, 23, and 25. Notably, sedation or anesthesia was not used to perform the reductions for the third and fourth events because the patient did not report any pain during the dislocation or reduction maneuvers. Due to intractable instability, we performed a second revision surgery to increase offset and soft-tissue tension using a large stem (Summit) (Fig. 1D). We performed both the first and second revision surgeries using a posterolateral approach.

The patient was discharged six weeks later without further events (Fig. 2A). However, we identified a posterior dislocation during a routine, postoperative three-month-follow-up (Fig. 2B). The patient had no specific complaints and the exact timing of the event was unclear. Closed reduction failed, and a computed tomography (CT) scan showed a femoral stem retroversion of 6° and an acetabular cup anteversion of 27° (Fig. 2C). Given the 10° elevated rim of the liner, the combined anteversion was considered acceptable. Open reduction without stem revision was performed through the previous posterolateral incision. Intraoperatively, we observed intact abductor muscles and high soft-tissue tension. Despite these observations, the patient experienced recurrent posterior dislocations following open reduction on POD 4 and POD 29. Since the patient did not complain of pain, these dislocations were detected solely because caregivers noted the abnormal limb alignment. A comprehensive review of the patient’s medication history revealed chronic use of multiple analgesics for back pain (Table 2). In particular, the patient had continuously used a fentanyl patch (25 µg/hr) for three years. We hypothesize that the cumulative effect of these medications may have contributed to severe iatrogenic hyposensitivity, which diminished protective sensory feedback.

The family of the patient declined additional surgery as they felt it would be impossible to wean the patient off opioids. There was also the high risk of re-dislocation due to excessive hip flexion (“curling up”) in the patient’s sleeping posture. The decision was further reinforced by the advanced age and severe postoperative delirium of the patient. Since there was no pain, the patient was discharged without reduction procedure. Ten months following the open reduction, the patient remained pain-free but wheelchair-bound (Fig. 2D). The patient provided consent after being informed that the data from the case would be submitted for publication.

DISCUSSION

Recurrent dislocation following revision THA has a typically multifactorial etiology6-9), including component malposition, spinopelvic relationship, or infection. Although conventional mechanical causes were evaluated in our present case, they could not fully explain the clinical course. The CT scan revealed combined anteversion within the functional “safe zone”10), when accounting for the elevated liner rim. In a spinopelvic relationship, patients with degenerative lumbar kyphosis typically exhibit posterior pelvic tilt that increases acetabular functional anteversion and predisposes to anterior dislocation11,12). However, posterior dislocations observed in our patient suggested that the spinopelvic relationship was not the primary driver. In addition, periprosthetic joint infection was considered unlikely on the basis of normal, serial inflammatory markers and intraoperative findings.

Since mechanical and infectious factors did not sufficiently account for these painless, recurrent dislocations, we directed our attention towards a probable neuropathic mechanism. We introduce the term ‘Charcot-like’ as a functional analogy related to sensory deprivation and instability, rather than classical Charcot neuroarthropathy that is characterized by progressive radiographic joint destruction.

There are no direct clinical studies that demonstrate a causal relationship between impaired pain sensation and recurrent hip dislocation. Nevertheless, pain perception plays a critical protective role by triggering avoidance behaviors and reflexive muscle guarding. When nociceptive feedback is diminished, it may reduce protective guarding and cause the hip to enter provocative positions without associated physiological warning signals.

In our case study, all the dislocations were painless and were detected only by abnormal lower-limb alignment. This supports our hypothesis that impaired sensory feedback might have contributed to the instability.

Chronic opioid exposure has been reported to affect central and peripheral somatosensory pathways13). In a large retrospective study using a national database, opioid use was reported to be associated with an increased risk of dislocation following THA after adjusting for confounders such as prior lumbar spine fusion. This suggests opioid exposure is associated with postoperative instability14). Furthermore, Lang-Illievich et al.15) studied patients receiving high-dose opioid agonist therapy and reported alterations in pain perception and modulation. They also observed a moderate dose-response relationship at a mean oral morphine equivalent dose of approximately 694 mg/day. The transdermal fentanyl patch of 25 µg/hr formulation used in this case is generally considered equivalent to approximately 60-134 mg/day of oral morphine16). Although the patient’s opioid dose was lower than those associated with marked somatosensory alterations in the literature, the altered pain perception and reduced protective feedback that potentially predisposes the patient to painless recurrent dislocation may have been due to the combination of long-term continuous fentanyl use, advanced age, and concomitant centrally acting medications.

In addition to opioids, destructive arthropathy associated with pharmacologic exposure has been described in the hip. Classic reports of steroid arthropathy of the hip have emphasized that corticosteroid therapy may be associated with rapidly progressive hip joint destruction, sometimes with relatively limited pain compared with the degree of structural damage2,17). The mechanism of iatrogenic arthropathy differs from that of classical neurogenic Charcot arthropathy because it supports the broad concept that medication induces altered symptom perception and joint-protective responses, which may contribute to atypical clinical presentations.

Previous studies demonstrate that THA in patients with Charcot arthropathy is associated with high complication rates, including dislocation and aseptic loosening2). Although recent reports suggest improved outcomes with modern surgical techniques or dual-mobility constructs7), the fundamental loss of protective sensation is a major risk factor. The present case highlights the likelihood of pharmacologically-mediated sensory impairment to mimic neuropathic arthropathy. We emphasize the importance of a comprehensive medication history taking and a cautious approach to prescribe analgesics to elderly arthroplasty patients with unexplained, painless recurrent dislocation.

There are limitations in our report. First, we did not perform the formal sensory testing and neurologic consultation to verify the hypothesis of analgesic-induced hyposensitivity. Second, the precise assessment of early stem rotational change was limited because CT evaluation was not available after the first revision. Third, the clinical presentation may have been influenced by concomitant cognitive impairment and multiple centrally-acting medications. Future studies incorporating objective sensory assessment and pharmacologic profiling are warranted to better clarify the relationship between chronic opioid therapy and postoperative instability.
