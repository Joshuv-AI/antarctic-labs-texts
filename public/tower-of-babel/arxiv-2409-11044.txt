Computation and Complexity of Preference Inference Based on Hierarchical Models Longer Version of IJCAI’15 publication
Nic Wilson, Anne-Marie George, Barry O’Sullivan

Abstract
Preference Inference involves inferring additional user preferences from elicited or observed preferences, based on assumptions regarding the form of the user’s preference relation. In this paper we consider a situation in which alternatives have an associated vector of costs, each component corresponding to a different criterion, and are compared using a kind of lexicographic order, similar to the way alternatives are compared in a Hierarchical Constraint Logic Programming model. It is assumed that the user has some (unknown) importance ordering on criteria, and that to compare two alternatives, firstly, the combined cost of each alternative with respect to the most important criteria are compared; only if these combined costs are equal, are the next most important criteria considered. The preference inference problem then consists of determining whether a preference statement can be inferred from a set of input preferences. We show that this problem is coNP -complete, even if one restricts the cardinality of the equal-importance sets to have at most two elements, and one only considers non-strict preferences. However, it is polynomial if it is assumed that the user’s ordering of criteria is a total ordering; it is also polynomial if the sets of equally important criteria are all equivalence classes of a given fixed equivalence relation. We give an efficient polynomial algorithm for these cases, which also throws light on the structure of the inference.

Introduction

There are increasing opportunities for decision making/support systems to take into account the preferences of individual users, with the user preferences being elicited or
observed
from the user’s behaviour.
However, users tend to have limited patience for preference elicitation,
so such a system will tend to have a very incomplete picture of the user preferences.

Preference Inference
involves inferring additional user preferences from elicited or observed preferences,
based on assumptions regarding the form of the user’s preference relation.
More specifically, given a set of input preferences
\Gamma
,
and a set of preference models
{\cal M}
(considered as candidates for the user’s preference model),
we infer a preference statement
\varphi
if
every model in
{\cal M}
that satisfies
\Gamma
also satisfies
\varphi
.
Preference Inference can take many forms, depending on the choice of
{\cal M}
,
and on the choices of language(s) for the input and inferred statements.
For instance, if we just assume that the user model is a total order (or total pre-order),
we can set
{\cal M}
as the set of total [pre-]orders over a set of alternatives.
This leads to a relatively cautious form of inference (based on transitive closure),
including, for instance, the dominance relation for CP-nets and some related systems, e.g.,

[
3
,
5
,
4
,
1
]
.

Often it can be valuable to obtain a much less cautious form of inference, for example, in order to have some help in deciding which options to show to the user next in a recommender system
[
7
,
13
]
.
This includes assuming that the user’s preference relation in a multi-objective context is based on a simple weighted sum of objectives
(as in a simple form of a Multi-Attribute Utility Theory model
[
9
]
);
this kind of preference inference is considered in

[
7
,
12
]
;
or alternatively, assuming different lexicographic forms for the preference models
as in
[
15
,
13
,
17
]
.
Note that all these systems involve reasoning about what holds in a set of preference models. This contrasts with work in preference learning

[
11
,
8
,
10
,
6
,
2
]

that typically learns a single model.

In this paper we consider a situation in which
alternatives have an associated vector of costs, each component corresponding to a different criterion,
and are compared using a kind of lexicographic order, similar to the way alternatives are compared in a Hierarchical Constraint Logic Programming (HCLP) model

[
14
]
.
It is assumed that the user has some (unknown) importance ordering on criteria, and that
to compare two alternatives, firstly, the combined cost of each alternative with respect to the most important criteria are compared;
only if these combined costs are equal, are the next most important criteria considered.

We consider the case where the
input preference statements are of a simple form that one alternative is preferred to another alternative,
where
we allow the expression of both strict and non-strict preferences
(in contrast to most related preference logics, such as
[
16
,
3
,
15
,
17
]
).
This form of preference is natural in many contexts, including for conversational recommender systems
[
7
]
.
The preference inference problem then consists of determining whether a preference statement can be inferred from a set of input preferences,
i.e., if every model (of the assumed form) satisfying the inputs also satisfies the query.
We show that this problem is
coNP
-complete, even if one restricts the cardinality of the equal-importance sets to have at most two elements, and one only considers non-strict preferences.
However, it is polynomial if it is assumed that the user’s ordering of criteria is a total ordering;
it is also polynomial if the sets of equally important criteria are all equivalence classes of a given fixed equivalence relation.
We give an efficient polynomial algorithm for these cases, which also throws light on the structure of the inference.

defines our simple preference logic based on hierarchical models,
along with some associated preference inference problems.
Section
3
shows that in general the preference inference problem is
coNP
-complete.
Section
4
considers the case where the importance ordering on criteria is a total order, and gives a polynomial algorithm for consistency.
Section
5
concludes.

A Preference Logic Based on Hierarchical Models

We consider preference models, based on an importance ordering of criteria, that is basically lexicographic, but involving a combination of criteria which are at the same level in the importance ordering.
We call these “HCLP models”, because
models of a similar kind appear in the HCLP system
[
14
]

(though we have abstracted away some details from the latter system).

HCLP structures:

Define an HCLP structure to be a tuple

{\cal S}=\langle{\cal A},\oplus,{\cal C}\rangle
,
where
{\cal A}
(the set of alternatives) is a finite set;

\oplus
is an
associative, commutative and monotonic operation
(
x\oplus y\leq z\oplus y
if
x\leq z
) on
the non-negative rational numbers
{\mathbb{Q}^{+}}
, with
identity element
0
;
and
{\cal C}
(known as the set of (
{\cal A}
-)evaluations)
is a set of functions from
{\cal A}
to
{\mathbb{Q}^{+}}
.
We also assume that
operation

\oplus
can be computed in linear time (which holds for natural definitions of
\oplus
, including addition and max).
The evaluations in
{\cal C}
may be considered as representing criteria or objectives under which the alternatives are evaluated.
For
c\in{\cal C}
and
\alpha\in{\cal A}
,
if
c(\alpha)=0
then
\alpha
fully satisfies the objective corresponding to
c
; more generally, the smaller the value of
c(\alpha)
, the better
\alpha
satisfies the
c
-objective.

With each subset
C
of
{\cal C}
we define ordering
\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}
on
{\cal A}
by

\alpha\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\beta
if and only if

\bigoplus_{c\in C}c(\alpha)\leq\bigoplus_{c\in C}c(\beta)
.
Relation
\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}
represents how well the alternatives satisfy the multi-set of evaluations
C
if the latter are considered equally important.

\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}
is a total pre-order (a weak order, i.e., a transitive and complete binary relation).
If
\alpha\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\beta
, we might also write

\beta\succcurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\alpha
.
We write
\equiv_{C}^{\oplus}
for the associated equivalence relation on
{\cal A}
,
given by
\alpha\equiv_{C}^{\oplus}\beta

\iff

\alpha\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\beta

and
\beta\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\alpha
.
We write
\prec_{C}^{\oplus}
for the associated strict weak ordering,
defined by
\alpha\prec_{C}^{\oplus}\beta

\iff

\alpha\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\beta
and
\beta\not\preccurlyeq_{C}^{\oplus}\alpha
.
Thus,

\alpha\equiv_{C}^{\oplus}\beta
if and only if

\bigoplus_{c\in C}c(\alpha)=\bigoplus_{c\in C}c(\beta)
;
and

\alpha\prec_{C}^{\oplus}\beta
if and only if

\bigoplus_{c\in C}c(\alpha)<\bigoplus_{c\in C}c(\beta)
.

HCLP models:

An HCLP model
H
based on
\langle{\cal A},\oplus,{\cal C}\rangle

is defined to be an ordered partition

(C_{1},\ldots,C_{k})
of a (possibly empty) subset
\sigma(H)

of
{\cal C}
.
The sets
C_{i}
are called the
levels of
H
,
which are thus non-empty, disjoint and have union
\sigma(H)
.
If
c\in C_{i}
and
c^{\prime}\in C_{j}
, and
i<j
,
then we say that
c
appears before
c^{\prime}

(and
c^{\prime}
appears after
c
) in
H
.
Associated with
H
is an ordering relation
\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}
on
{\cal A}
given by:

\alpha\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}\beta
if and only if either:

(I)

for all
i=1,\ldots,k
,

\alpha\equiv^{\oplus}_{C_{i}}\beta
; or

(II)

there exists some
i\in{\{1,\ldots,k\}}
such that
(i)
\alpha\prec^{\oplus}_{C_{i}}\beta
and (ii) for all
j
with
1\leq j<i
,
\alpha\equiv^{\oplus}_{C_{j}}\beta
.

Relation
\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}
is a kind of lexicographic order on
{\cal A}
,
where the multi-set
C_{i}
of evaluations at the same level are first combined into a single evaluation.

\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}

is a weak order on
{\cal A}
.
We write
\equiv^{\oplus}_{H}
for the associated equivalence relation
(corresponding with condition (I)),
and
\prec^{\oplus}_{H}
for the associated strict weak order
(corresponding with condition (II)),
so that
\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}
is the disjoint union of

\prec^{\oplus}_{H}
and
\equiv^{\oplus}_{H}
.
If
\sigma(H)=\emptyset
then
then the first condition for
\alpha\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}\beta
holds vacuously
(since
k=0
),
so we have
\alpha\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}\beta

for all
\alpha,\beta\in{\cal A}
,
and
\prec^{\oplus}_{H}
is the empty relation.

Preference Language Inputs:

Let
{\cal A}
be a set of alternatives.
We define
{\cal L}_{\leq}^{\cal A}
to be the set of statements of the form
\alpha\leq\beta
, for
\alpha,\beta\in{\cal A}

(the
non-strict
statements),
we write
{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}
for the set of statements
of the form
\alpha<\beta
, for
\alpha,\beta\in{\cal A}

(the
strict
statements),
and let
{\cal L}^{\cal A}={\cal L}_{\leq}^{\cal A}\cup{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}
.
If
\varphi
is the preference statement
\alpha\leq\beta

then
\neg\varphi
is defined to be the preference
statement
\beta<\alpha
.
If
\varphi
is the preference statement
\alpha<\beta

then
\neg\varphi
is defined to be the preference
statement
\beta\leq\alpha
.

Satisfaction of preference statements:

For HCLP model
H
over HCLP structure
\langle{\cal A},\oplus,{\cal C}\rangle
,
we say that
H
satisfies
\alpha\leq\beta

(written
H\models^{\oplus}\alpha\leq\beta
) if

\alpha\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}\beta
holds.
Similarly, we say that
H
satisfies
\alpha<\beta

(written
H\models^{\oplus}\alpha<\beta
)
if
\alpha\prec^{\oplus}_{H}\beta
.
For
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal L}^{\cal A}
,
we say that
H
satisfies
\Gamma

(written
H\models^{\oplus}\Gamma
)
if
H
satisfies
\varphi
for all
\varphi\in\Gamma
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
H
be a HCLP model over HCLP structure
{\cal S}
.
Then,

H
satisfies
\varphi

if and only if

H
does not satisfy
\neg\varphi
.

Proof:
Write
{\cal S}
as
\langle{\cal A},\oplus,{\cal C}\rangle
.
It is sufficient to show that, for any
\alpha,\beta\in{\cal A}

H
satisfies
\alpha\leq\beta

if and only if
H
does not satisfy
\beta<\alpha
.
We have that
H
satisfies
\alpha\leq\beta

if and only if

\alpha\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}\beta
,
which, since
\preccurlyeq^{\oplus}_{H}
is a weak order, is
if and only if

\beta\not\prec^{\oplus}_{H}\alpha
,
i.e.,
H
does not satisfy
\beta<\alpha
.
\Box

Preference Inference/Deduction relation:

We are interested in different restrictions on the set of models,
and the corresponding inference relations.
Let
{\cal M}
be a set of HCLP models over HCLP structure
\langle{\cal A},\oplus,{\cal C}\rangle
.
For
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal L}^{\cal A}
, and
\varphi\in{\cal L}^{\cal A}
,
we say that

\Gamma\models_{\cal M}^{\oplus}\varphi
,
if
H
satisfies
\varphi
for every
H\in{\cal M}
satisfying

\Gamma
.
Thus, if we elicit
some
preference statements
\Gamma

of a user, and we assume that their preference relation is an HCLP model in
{\cal M}
(based on the HCLP structure),
then
\Gamma\models_{\cal M}^{\oplus}\varphi
holds if and only if we can deduce
that the user’s HCLP model
H
satisfies
\varphi
.

Consistency:

For set of HCLP models
{\cal M}
over HCLP structure
\langle{\cal A},\oplus,{\cal C}\rangle
, and set of preference statements
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal L}^{\cal A}
,
we say that

\Gamma
is
({\cal M},\oplus)
-consistent if there exists
H\in{\cal M}

such that
H\models^{\oplus}\Gamma
;
otherwise, we say that
\Gamma
is
({\cal M},\oplus)
-inconsistent.
In the usual way, because of the existence of a negation operator,
deduction can be reduced to checking (in)consistency.

Proposition 1
.

\Gamma\models_{\cal M}^{\oplus}\varphi
if and only if

\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}
is
({\cal M},\oplus)
-inconsistent.

Proof:
Suppose that
\Gamma\models_{\cal M}^{\oplus}\varphi
.
By definition,
H
satisfies
\varphi
for every
H\in{\cal M}
satisfying (every element of)
\Gamma
.
Thus, there exists no
H\in{\cal M}
that satisfies
\Gamma
and
\neg\varphi
,
which implies that
\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}
is
({\cal M},\oplus)
-inconsistent.

Conversely, suppose
\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}
is
({\cal M},\oplus)
-inconsistent. By definition, there exists no
H\in{\cal M}
that satisfies
\Gamma\cup\neg\varphi
.
Thus, every
H\in{\cal M}
that satisfies
\Gamma
does not satisfy
\neg\varphi
,
and therefore satisfies
\varphi
. Hence,
\Gamma\models_{\cal M}^{\oplus}\varphi
.
\Box

t
be some
number in
{\{1,2,\ldots,|{\cal C}|\}}
.
We define

.
Thus,

.
Note that for

\oplus

(since there is no combination of evaluations involved),

.

Let

.
We define

.
It is easy to see that the relation

\models_{C^{\prime}(1)}

C^{\prime}
is in 1-1 correspondence with the set of

.
If

corresponding with

,

.

For

for some

,
or being

on

, we consider the following decision problem.

HCLP-Deduction for

:
Given
{\cal C}
,
\Gamma
and

is it the case that

?

In Section

,
we will show that this problem is

.
Thus it is polynomial also for

,

.
It is

,

.

Theorem 1
.

HCLP-Deduction for

,

,

Proving
coNP
-completeness of HCLP-Deduction for
\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}
for

Given an arbitrary 3-SAT instance we will show that we can construct a set
\Gamma
and a statement
\alpha\leq\beta

such that
the 3-SAT instance has a satisfying truth assignment if and only if

\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}\alpha\leq\beta

(see Proposition
2
).
This then implies that determining if
\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}\alpha\leq\beta

We have that
\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}\alpha\leq\beta

.

H\models^{\oplus}\Gamma_{1}^{i}

The construction of

:

For each

,

.

,

.

Lemma 3
.

If

in

\sigma(H)\ni q_{i}^{-}

The construction of

For each

.

.

If some element of

The following result implies that the HCLP deduction problem is

-hard (even if we restrict to the case when

).

Proposition 2
.

Using the notation defined above,
the 3-SAT instance is satisfiable
if and only if

\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}\alpha\leq\beta
.

Proof:
First let us assume that
\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}\alpha\leq\beta
.
Then by definition, there exists
an HCLP model

with

Since

\iff

,
we have
H\models^{\oplus}\Gamma\cup{\{\beta<\alpha\}}
.
Because
H\models^{\oplus}\beta<\alpha
,

\sigma(H)\ni c^{*}
.

Because also

,
either
\sigma(H)\ni q_{i}^{+}
or
\sigma(H)\ni q_{i}^{-}
,
by Lemma
3
.
Since

,
the set
\sigma(H)
does not contain both
q_{i}^{+}
and
q_{i}^{-}
,
by Lemma
2
.

Let us define a truth function

f(p_{i})=1

\iff

\sigma(H)\ni q_{i}^{+}
.
Since
\sigma(H)
contains exactly one of

q_{i}^{+}
and
q_{i}^{-}
,
we have

f(p_{i})=0

\iff

\sigma(H)\ni q_{i}^{-}
.
We extend

.

Since

,

\sigma(H)
contains at least one element of each

4
.
Thus for each

satisfies clause

.
We have shown that

satisfies each clause of the 3-SAT instance,

Conversely, suppose that the
3-SAT instance is satisfiable, so there exists a truth function

satisfying it.

We will construct an HCLP model
H\in{\cal C}(t)

H\models^{\oplus}\Gamma\cup{\{\beta<\alpha\}}
,

,

.

For

,
let
S_{i}={\{a_{i}^{1},\ldots,a_{i}^{t-1},q_{i}^{+}\}}

,
and otherwise, let

.
We then define
H
to be the sequence

.
Since

.

By Lemma

for all

.
By Lemma

.

Consider any

4
Sequence-of-Evaluations Models

In this section, we consider the case where we restrict to
HCLP models which consist of a sequence of singletons;
thus each model corresponds to a sequence of evaluations,
and generates a lexicographic order based on these.

Let
{\cal C}
be a set of evaluations on
{\cal A}
.
A
{\cal C}(1)
-model is a sequence of different elements of
{\cal C}
.
The operation
\oplus
plays no part,
so we can harmlessly abbreviate
ordering
\preccurlyeq_{H}^{\oplus}
to just
\preccurlyeq_{H}
,
for any
{\cal C}(1)
-model
H
,
and similarly for
\prec_{H}
and
\equiv_{H}
.

Some Basic Definitions and Results

We write
\varphi\in{\cal L}^{\cal A}

as
\alpha_{\varphi}<\beta_{\varphi}
, if
\varphi
is strict,
or as

\alpha_{\varphi}\leq\beta_{\varphi}
,
if
\varphi
is non-strict.
We consider a set
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal L}^{\cal A}
, and a set
{\cal C}
of evaluations on
{\cal A}
.
For
\varphi\in\Gamma
,
define
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
to be

{\{c\in{\cal C}\,:\,c(\alpha_{\varphi})<c(\beta_{\varphi})\}}
;
define

\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
to be

{\{c\in{\cal C}\,:\,c(\alpha_{\varphi})>c(\beta_{\varphi})\}}
; and
define

\textit{Ind}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
to be

{\{c\in{\cal C}\,:\,c(\alpha_{\varphi})=c(\beta_{\varphi})\}}
.
We may sometimes abbreviate

\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
to
\textit{Supp}^{\varphi}
,
and similarly for
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
and
\textit{Ind}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
.

\textit{Supp}^{\varphi}
are the evaluations that support
\varphi
;

\textit{Opp}^{\varphi}
are the evaluations that oppose
\varphi
.

\textit{Ind}^{\varphi}
are the other evaluations, that are indifferent regarding
\varphi
.
For a model
H
to satisfy
\varphi
it is necessary that
no evaluation that opposes
\varphi
appears before
all evaluations that support
\varphi
.

More precisely, we have the following:

Lemma 5
.

Let
H
be an element of

{\cal C}(1)
,
i.e., a sequence of different elements of
{\cal C}
.
For strict
\varphi
,

H\models\varphi
if and only if
an element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
before any
element in
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears
(and some element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
).
For non-strict
\varphi
,

H\models\varphi
if and only if
an element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
before any
element in
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears,
or no element of
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H

(i.e.,
\sigma(H)\cap\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}=\emptyset
).

Proof:
Let
H=(c_{1},\ldots c_{k})
be a
{\cal C}(1)
-model.
Suppose that
\varphi
is a strict statement.
Then
H\models\varphi
, i.e.
\alpha_{\varphi}\prec_{H}\beta_{\varphi}
,
if and only if there exists some
i\in{\{1,\ldots,k\}}
such that

\{c_{1},\dots c_{i-1}\}\subseteq\textit{Ind}^{\varphi}
and
c_{i}\in\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
,
which is if and only if
an element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
before any
element in
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears,
and some element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
.

Now suppose that
\varphi
is a non-strict statement.
Then
H\models\varphi
, i.e.
\alpha_{\varphi}\preccurlyeq_{H}\beta_{\varphi}
,
if and only if
either (i)
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
,
\alpha\equiv_{c_{i}}\beta
;
or (ii) there exists some
i\in{\{1,\ldots,k\}}
such that

\alpha\prec_{c_{i}}\beta
and for all
j
such that
1\leq j<i
,
\alpha\equiv_{c_{j}}\beta
.
(i) holds if and only if
\sigma(H)\subseteq\textit{Ind}^{\varphi}
,
i.e., no element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
or
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
.
(ii) holds if and only if
an element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
before any
element in
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears,
and some element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H
.
Thus,
H\models\varphi
holds if and only if
either no element in
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H

or some element of
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears in
H

and the first such element appears before any
element in
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
appears.
\Box

The following defines
inconsistency bases
, which are concerned with evaluations that cannot appear in any model
satisfying the set of preference statements
\Gamma

(see Proposition
3
below).

Definition 1
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal L}^{\cal A}
, and let
{\cal C}
be a set of
{\cal A}
-evaluations.
We say that
(\Gamma^{\prime},C^{\prime})
is an inconsistency base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})

if
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma
, and

C^{\prime}\subseteq{\cal C}
,
and

(i)

for all
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
,

\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\cup\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\subseteq C^{\prime}
(and thus
{\cal C}-C^{\prime}\subseteq\textit{Ind}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
); and

(ii)

for all
c\in C^{\prime}
, there exists
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}

such that
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\ni c
.

Thus, for all
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
,

C^{\prime}
contains all evaluations that are not indifferent regarding
\varphi
,
and for all
c\in C^{\prime}
there is some element of
\Gamma^{\prime}
that is opposed by
c
.
The following result motivates the definition:

Proposition 3
.

Let
H
be an element of
{\cal C}(1)
.
Suppose that
H\models\Gamma
,
and let
(\Gamma^{\prime},C^{\prime})
be an inconsistency base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
.
Then
C^{\prime}\cap\sigma(H)=\emptyset
.
Thus no
{\cal C}(1)
model of
\Gamma
can involve any element of
C^{\prime}
.
Also, we have for any
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
,

\alpha_{\varphi}\equiv_{H}\beta_{\varphi}
,
so
H\not\models\alpha_{\varphi}<\beta_{\varphi}
.

Proof:
Let
H=(c_{1},\dots c_{k})
be an element of
{\cal C}(1)
with
H\models\Gamma

and let
(\Gamma^{\prime},C^{\prime})
be an inconsistency base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
.
Suppose
H
contains some element in
C^{\prime}

and let
c_{i}
be the element in
C^{\prime}\cap\sigma(H)
with the smallest index.
By Definition
1
(ii),
there exists
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\ni c_{i}
.
Furthermore, since
c_{j}\notin C^{\prime}
for all
1\leq j<i
,
Definition
1
(i) implies
c_{j}\in\textit{Ind}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
.
But then, an evaluation that opposes
\varphi
appears before all evaluations that support
\varphi
.
By Lemma
5
, this is a contradiction to
H\models\Gamma
;
hence we must have
C^{\prime}\cap\sigma(H)=\emptyset
.
Also, for all
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
,
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\cup\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\subseteq C^{\prime}
and thus
\sigma(H)\subseteq{\cal C}-C^{\prime}\subseteq\textit{Ind}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
.
Hence, for any
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\prime}
,
\alpha_{\varphi}\equiv_{H}\beta_{\varphi}
.
\Box

Thus
H
does not strictly satisfy any element of
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

For inconsistency bases
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})
and
(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})

for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
,
define
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})\cup(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})

to be
(\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2},C_{1}\cup C_{2})
.
It is easy to show that if

(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})
and
(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})

are both inconsistency bases for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})

then

(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})\cup(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})
is one also.

Lemma 6
.

If
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})
and
(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})

are both inconsistency bases for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
,
then
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})\cup(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})

is an inconsistency base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
.

Proof:
Let
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})
and
(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})
be two inconsistency bases for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
.
Then by Definition
1
(i),
for all
\varphi\in\Gamma_{1}
,
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\cup\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\subseteq C_{1}
; and
for all
\varphi\in\Gamma_{2}
,
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\cup\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\subseteq C_{2}
.
Thus, for all
\varphi\in\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
,
\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\cup\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\subseteq C_{1}\cup C_{2}
.
Hence,
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})\cup(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})
satisfies condition (i) of the definition of inconsistency bases.
By Definition
1
(ii),
for all
c\in C_{1}
, there exists
\varphi\in\Gamma_{1}
such that
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\ni c
; and
for all
c\in C_{2}
, there exists
\varphi\in\Gamma_{2}
such that
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\ni c
.
Thus, for all
c\in C_{1}\cup C_{2}
, there exists
\varphi\in\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
such that
\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\ni c
.
And hence,
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})\cup(\Gamma_{2},C_{2})
satisfies condition (ii) of the definition of inconsistency bases.
\Box

Define

\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})
, the
Maximal Inconsistency Base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
, to be the union of all inconsistency bases
for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
,
i.e.,

\bigcup{\{(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})\in{\cal I}\}}
,
where
{\cal I}
is the set of inconsistency bases for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
.
The next result follows.

Proposition 4
.

\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})
always exists, and is an inconsistency base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
,
which is maximal in the following sense:
if
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})
is an inconsistency base for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})

then
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\Gamma^{\bot}
and
C_{1}\subseteq C^{\bot}
,
where
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})=(\Gamma^{\bot},C^{\bot})
.

Proof:
The trivial tuple
(\emptyset,\emptyset)
is always an inconsistency base.
Hence, since
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})
is defined to be the union of all inconsistency bases, it must always exist.
By Lemma
6
, the union of two inconsistency bases is an inconsistency base.
Consequently,
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})
is an inconsistency base.
Furthermore,
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})=(\Gamma^{\bot},C^{\bot})
is defined to be
(\bigcup_{(C^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\in{\cal I}}C^{\prime},\bigcup_{(C^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime})\in{\cal I}}\Gamma^{\prime})
,
where
{\cal I}
is the set of inconsistency bases for
(\Gamma,{\cal C})
.
Thus, if
(\Gamma_{1},C_{1})
is an inconsistency base for

A Polynomial Algorithm for Consistency and Deduction

Throughout this section we consider a
set
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal L}^{\cal A}
of input preference statements,
and a set
{\cal C}
of
{\cal A}
-evaluations.

Define

\textit{Opp}_{\Gamma}(c)
(abbreviated to
\textit{Opp}(c)
)
to be the set of elements opposed by
c
,
i.e.,
\varphi\in\Gamma
such that
c(\alpha_{\varphi})>c(\beta_{\varphi})
,
and define

\textit{Supp}_{\Gamma}(c)
(usually abbreviated to
\textit{Supp}(c)
) to be the set of elements
\varphi
of
\Gamma
supported by
c
,
(i.e.,

c(\alpha_{\varphi})<c(\beta_{\varphi})
).
Also, for sequence of evaluations
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
,
we define
\textit{Supp}(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
to be
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\textit{Supp}(c_{i})
.

The idea behind the algorithm is as follows: suppose that we have picked a sequence of elements of
{\cal C}
,

C^{\prime}
being the set picked so far.
We need to choose next an evaluation
c

such that,
if
c
opposes some
\varphi
in
\Gamma
,
then
\varphi
is supported by some evaluation in
C^{\prime}

(or else the generated sequence will not satisfy
\varphi
).

The Algorithm

H
is initialised as the empty list
()
of evaluations.

H\leftarrow H+c
means that evaluation
c
is added to the end of
H
.

Function
\textit{Cons-check}(\Gamma,{\cal C})

H\leftarrow()

for

k=1,\ldots,|{\cal C}|
do

if

\exists

c\in{\cal C}-\sigma(H)
such that
\textit{Opp}(c)\subseteq\textit{Supp}(H)

then
choose some such
c
;
H\leftarrow H+c

else

stop

end

for

return

H

The algorithm
involves often non-unique choices. However, if we wish, the choosing of
c
can be done based on an ordering

c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}
of
{\cal C}
, where, if there exists more than one
c\in{\cal C}-\sigma(H)
such that
\textit{Opp}(c)\subseteq\textit{Supp}(H)
,
we choose the element
c_{i}
with smallest index
i
.
The algorithm then becomes deterministic, with a unique result following from the given inputs.

A straight-forward implementation runs in
O(|\Gamma||{\cal C}|^{2})
time;
however, a more careful implementation runs in
O(|\Gamma||{\cal C}|)
time.

We describe this implementation in the following.
Let
H_{k}
be the HCLP model after the
k
-th iteration of the for-loop.
In every iteration of the for-loop,
we update sets
O_{k}^{\Delta}(c)=\textit{Opp}(c)-\textit{Supp}(H_{k})

and
S_{k}^{\Delta}(c)=\textit{Supp}(c)-\textit{Supp}(H_{k})

for all
c\in{\cal C}-\sigma(H_{k})
.
This costs us
O(|{\cal C}-\sigma(H_{k})|\times|\textit{Supp}(H_{k})\setminus\textit{Supp}(H_{k-1})|)=O(|{\cal C}-\sigma(H_{k})|\times|S_{k-1}^{\Delta}(c_{k})|)

more time for every iteration
k
in which we add evaluation
c_{k}
to
H_{k-1}
.
However, the choice of the next evaluation
c_{k}
be performed in constant time by marking evaluations
c
with
O_{k-1}^{\Delta}(c)=\emptyset
.
Suppose the algorithm stops after
1\leq l\leq|{\cal C}|
iterations.
Since all
S_{k-1}^{\Delta}(c_{k})
are disjoint,

\sum_{k=1}^{l}|S_{k-1}^{\Delta}(c_{k})|=|\textit{Supp}(H_{l})|\leq|\Gamma|
.
Altogether, the running time is
O(\sum_{k=1}^{l}|{\cal C}-\sigma(H_{k})|\times|S_{k-1}^{\Delta}(c_{k})|)\leq O(|{\cal C}|\times\sum_{k=1}^{l}|S_{k-1}^{\Delta}(c_{k})|)
,
and thus the running time is

O(|{\cal C}|\times|\Gamma|)
.

Properties of the Algorithm

Any
\varphi\in\Gamma
which is opposed by some evaluation
c
in
H

is supported by some earlier evaluation in
H
.
Consider any
\varphi
in
\textit{Supp}(H)
.
Let
c_{j}
be the earliest evaluation in
H
that supports
\varphi
.
None of the earlier evaluations than
c_{j}
oppose
\varphi
,
and thus
H
strictly satisfies
\varphi
.
A similar argument shows
that

H
satisfies
\Gamma^{(\leq)}
, defined to be

{\{\alpha_{\varphi}\leq\beta_{\varphi}\,:\,\varphi\in\Gamma\}}
,
i.e.,
\Gamma
where the strict statements are replaced
by corresponding non-strict statements.

The algorithm will always generate an HCLP model satisfying
\Gamma
if
\Gamma
is consistent.
It can also be used for computing the Maximal Inconsistency Base.
The following result sums up some properties related to the algorithm.

Theorem 2
.

Let
H
be a sequence returned by the algorithm
with inputs
\Gamma
and
{\cal C}
, and
write
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})
as
(\Gamma^{\bot},C^{\bot})
.
Then
C^{\bot}={\cal C}-\sigma(H)

(i.e., the evaluations that don’t appear in
H
),
and
\Gamma^{\bot}=\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
.
We have that
H\models\Gamma^{(\leq)}
.
Also,
\Gamma
is
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent if and only
if
\textit{Supp}(H)
contains all the strict elements of
\Gamma
,
which is if and only if
\Gamma^{\bot}\cap{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}=\emptyset
.
If
\Gamma
is
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent then

H\models\Gamma
.

Proof:
Because of the if-condition
\textit{Opp}(c)\subseteq\textit{Supp}(H)
, we never add an evaluation
c
to
H
throughout the algorithm that opposes some not yet supported preference statement.
In particular, because of Lemma
5
, this implies that
the returned sequence
H
satisfies every element of
\Gamma^{(\leq)}
.

In order to prove the remaining statements, first observe the following.
The algorithm terminates only if for all
c\in{\cal C}-\sigma(H)

\textit{Opp}(c)\nsubseteq\textit{Supp}(H)
, i.e.,
\textit{Opp}(c)-\textit{Supp}(H)\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, for all
c\in{\cal C}-\sigma(H)

\varphi\in\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
such that
c\in\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
.
Furthermore, since
H
satisfies
\Gamma^{(\leq)}
, we have that
for all
\varphi\in\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)

(and thus

).
Hence,
({\cal C}-\sigma(H),\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H))
satisfies (i) and (ii) of Definition
1
and thus is an inconsistency base.

By Proposition
4
,
C^{\bot}\supseteq{\cal C}-\sigma(H)
and
\Gamma^{\bot}\supseteq\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
.
We will show that
C^{\bot}\subseteq{\cal C}-\sigma(H)
and
\Gamma^{\bot}\subseteq\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
,
and thus
C^{\bot}={\cal C}-\sigma(H)
and
\Gamma^{\bot}=\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
.
We have that
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma^{(\leq)},{\cal C})=\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})=(\Gamma^{\bot},C^{\bot})
,
since the definition of
MIB
does not depend on whether statements are strict or not.
Proposition
3
implies that
C^{\bot}\cap\sigma(H)=\emptyset
,
and thus
C^{\bot}\subseteq{\cal C}-\sigma(H)
.
Next, we show
\Gamma^{\bot}\subseteq\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
and hence
\Gamma^{\bot}=\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
.
By Definition
1

\sigma(H)={\cal C}-C^{\bot}\subseteq\textit{Ind}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
for all
\varphi\in\Gamma^{\bot}
.
Thus,
\Gamma^{\bot}\cap\textit{Supp}(H)=\emptyset
, i.e.,
\Gamma^{\bot}\subseteq\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H)
.

Now,
\textit{Supp}(H)
contains all the strict elements of
\Gamma
if and only if
(\Gamma-\textit{Supp}(H))\cap{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}=\Gamma^{\bot}\cap{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}=\emptyset
.
Proposition
3
implies, if there exists
H\in{\cal C}(1)
with
H\models\Gamma
, then
\Gamma^{\bot}
can contain no strict statements, i.e.,
\Gamma^{\bot}\cap{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}=\emptyset
.
Now suppose,
\Gamma^{\bot}\cap{\cal L}_{<}^{\cal A}=\emptyset
.
Then all strict statements are contained in
\Gamma-\Gamma^{\bot}=\textit{Supp}(H)
.
Thus, all strict statements are satisfied by
H
and since
H
satisfies
\Gamma^{(\leq)}
,
\Gamma
is
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent.
Hence,
\Gamma
is
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent if and only if
\textit{Supp}(H)
contains all the strict elements of
\Gamma
.
Also, if
\Gamma
is
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent, then
H\models\Gamma
.
\Box

The algorithm therefore determines
{\cal C}(1)
-consistency,
and hence
{\cal C}(1)
-deduction (because of Proposition

), in polynomial time,
and also generates the Maximal Inconsistency Base.
For the case when
\Gamma
is not
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent,
the output

of
the algorithm is a model which, in a sense, comes closest to satisfying

\Gamma^{(\leq)}
and any element

\Gamma

\varphi

For, if

.
Proposition

implies that

\varphi
is strict.
Proposition

also implies that
\sigma(H^{\prime})\cap C^{\bot}=\emptyset
,
and thus
\sigma(H^{\prime})\subseteq\sigma(H)={\cal C}-C^{\bot}

.
We have proved the following result.

Lemma 7
.

Let
H
be a sequence returned by the algorithm
with inputs
\Gamma
and
{\cal C}
,
and suppose that

is such that

.

.

These properties suggest the following way of reasoning with inconsistent

Let us define
\Gamma^{\prime}
to be equal to

By Theorem

enabling easy computation of

.

We might then (re-)define the (non-monotonic) deductions from inconsistent

to be the deductions from
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Strong Consistency

In the set of models
{\cal C}(1)
, we allow models involving any subset of
{\cal C}
, the set of evaluations.
We could alternatively consider a semantics where we
only allow models
H
that involve all elements of
{\cal C}
,
i.e., with
\sigma(H)={\cal C}
.

Let
{\cal C}(1^{*})
be the set of elements
H
of
{\cal C}(1)

with
\sigma(H)={\cal C}
.

\Gamma
is defined to be
strongly
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent
if and only if
there exists a model
H\in{\cal C}(1^{*})
such that

H\models\Gamma
.
Let
\textit{MIB}(\Gamma,{\cal C})=(\Gamma^{\bot},C^{\bot})
.
Proposition
3

implies that, if
\Gamma
is strongly
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent
then
C^{\bot}
is empty, and

\Gamma^{\bot}
consists of all the elements of
\Gamma

that are indifferent to all of
{\cal C}
,
i.e., the set of
\varphi\in\Gamma
such that

c(\alpha_{\varphi})=c(\beta_{\varphi})
for all
c\in{\cal C}
.

We write
\Gamma\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}\varphi
if

H\models\varphi
holds
for every
H\in{\cal C}(1^{*})
such that

H\models\Gamma
.
The next result shows that the non-strict

\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}
inferences are the same
as the non-strict
\models_{{\cal C}(1)}
inferences,
and that (in contrast to the case of
\models_{{\cal C}(1)}
),
the strict
\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}
inferences
almost correspond with the non-strict ones.
The result also implies that the algorithm in Section
4
can be used to
efficiently determine the
\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}
inferences.

This form of deduction can be expressed in terms of strong consistency, as the following result shows.

Lemma 8
.

If
\Gamma
is strongly
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent,
then

\Gamma\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}\varphi
holds if and only if

\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}
is not strongly
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent.

Proof:
Assume that
\Gamma
is strongly
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent.
First suppose that

\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}
is strongly consistent.
Then there exists

H\in{\cal C}(1)
such that
H\models\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}

and
\sigma(H)={\cal C}
.
Thus
H\models\Gamma
and
H\not\models\varphi

(using Lemma
1
),
showing that
\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}\varphi
.

Now suppose that
\Gamma\not\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}\varphi
.
Then there exists

H\in{\cal C}(1)
such that

H\models\Gamma

\sigma(H)={\cal C}

and
H\not\models\varphi
.
Then
H\models\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}

(again using Lemma

),
so
\Gamma\cup{\{\neg\varphi\}}
is strongly
{\cal C}(1)
-consistent.
\Box

\Gamma\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}\alpha\leq\beta

\Gamma\models_{{\cal C}(1^{*})}\beta\leq\alpha

{\cal H}^{\prime}=\{H\in C^{\prime}(1)\;:\;H\models\Gamma\}

and
{\cal H}=\{H\in{\cal C}(1)\;:\;H\models\Gamma\}
.
Then
{\cal H}^{\prime}\subseteq{\cal H}
, because
C^{\prime}(1)\subseteq{\cal C}(1)
.
By Proposition
3
, for every
H\in{\cal H}
, we have
\sigma(H)\cap C^{\bot}=\emptyset
, and hence
H\in{\cal H}^{\prime}
.
Thus
{\cal H}^{\prime}={\cal H}
and
\Gamma\models_{{\cal C}(1)}\varphi
if and only if

\Gamma\models_{C^{\prime}(1)}\penalty\ \varphi
.
\Box

Orderings on evaluations

The preference logic defined here is closely related to a logic based on disjunctive ordering statements.
Given set of evaluations
{\cal C}
,
we consider the set of statements
{\cal O}_{\cal C}
of the form

C_{1}<C_{2}
,
and of
C_{1}\leq C_{2}
, where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are disjoint subsets
of
{\cal C}
.

We say that
H\models C_{1}<C_{2}
if
some evaluation in
C_{1}
appears in
H
before every element of
C_{2}
,
that is,
there exists some element of
C_{1}
in
H

(i.e.,
C_{1}\cap\sigma(H)\not=\emptyset
)
and the earliest element of
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
to appear in
H

is in
C_{1}
.

We say that
H\models C_{1}\leq C_{2}

if either
H\models C_{1}<C_{2}

or no element of
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
appears in
H
:

(C_{1}\cup C_{2})\cap\sigma(H)=\emptyset
.
Then we have that

H\models\alpha_{\varphi}<\beta_{\varphi}

\iff

H\models\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}<\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
,

and

H\models\alpha_{\varphi}\leq\beta_{\varphi}

\iff

H\models\textit{Supp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}\leq\textit{Opp}_{\cal C}^{\varphi}
.

This shows that the language
{\cal O}_{\cal C}
can express anything that can be expressed in
{\cal L}^{\cal A}
.
It can be shown, conversely, that for
any statement
\tau
in
{\cal O}_{\cal C}
, one can
define
\alpha_{\varphi}
and
\beta_{\varphi}
, and the values of elements of
{\cal C}
on these, such that
for all
H\in{\cal C}(1)
,

H\models\tau
if and only if
H\models\varphi
.
For instance, if
\tau
is the statement
C_{1}<C_{2}
,
we can define
c(\alpha_{\varphi})=1
for all
c\in C_{2}
,
and
c(\alpha_{\varphi})=0
for
c\in{\cal C}-C_{2}
;
and define
c(\beta_{\varphi})=1
for all
c\in C_{1}
,
and
c(\beta_{\varphi})=0
for
c\in{\cal C}-C_{1}
.

The algorithm adapts in the obvious way to the case where we have
\Gamma
consisting of (or including) elements in
{\cal O}_{\cal C}
. When viewed in this way, the algorithm can be seen as a simple extension of a topological sort algorithm;
the standard case corresponds to when the ordering statements
only consist of singleton sets.

5
Discussion and Conclusions

We defined a class of relatively simple preference logics based on hierarchical models.
These generate an adventurous form of inference,
which can be helpful if there is only relatively sparse input preference information.
We showed that the complexity of
preference deduction is
coNP
-complete in general,
and polynomial for the case where the criteria are assumed to be totally ordered
(the sequence-of-evaluations case, Section
4
).

The latter logic has strong connections with the preference inference formalism described in
[
17
]
.
To clarify the connection, for each evaluation
c\in{\cal C}
we can generate a variable
X_{c}
,
and let
V
be the set of these variables.
For each alternative
\alpha\in{\cal A}
we generate a complete assignment
\alpha^{*}

on the variables
V
(i.e., an outcome as defined in
[
17
]
)
by
\alpha^{*}(X_{c})=c(\alpha)
for each
X_{c}\in V
.
Note that values of
\alpha^{*}(X_{c})
are non-negative numbers,
and thus have a fixed ordering, with zero being the best value.
A preference statement
\alpha\leq\beta
in
{\cal L}_{\leq}^{\cal A}
then corresponds with
a basic preference formula
\alpha^{*}\geq\beta^{*}
in
[
17
]
.
Each model
H\in{\cal C}(1)

corresponds to a sequence of evaluations, and thus
has an associated sequence of variables; this sequence together with the fixed value orderings,
generates a lexicographic model as defined in
[
17
]
.

In contrast with the lexicographic inference system in
[
17
]
,
the logic developed in this paper allows strict (as well as non-strict) preference statements,
and allows more than one variable at the same level.
However, the lexicographic inference logic from
[
17
]
does not assume a fixed value ordering
(which, translated into the current formalism,
corresponds to not assuming that the values of the evaluation function are known);
it also allows a richer language of preference statements, where a statement can be a compact representation for a (possibly exponentially large) set of
basic preference statements of the form
\alpha\leq\beta
.
Many of the results of Section
4
immediately extend to richer preference languages (by replacing a preference statement by a corresponding set of basic preference statements).
In future work we will determine under what circumstances deduction remains polynomial when extending the language, and when removing the assumption that the evaluation functions are known.

The
coNP
-hardness result for the general case (and for the
\models_{{\cal C}(t)}^{\oplus}

systems with
t\geq 2
) is notable and perhaps surprising, since these preference logics are relatively simple ones.
The result obviously extends to more general systems.
The preference inference system described in
[
15
]
is based on much more complex forms of lexicographic models, allowing conditional dependencies, as well as having local orderings on sets of variables (with bounded cardinality).
Theorem
1
implies that
the (polynomial) deduction system in
[
15
]
is not more general than the system described here (assuming
{\sf P}\not={\sf NP}
).
It also implies that if one were to extend the system from
[
15
]
to allow a richer form of equivalence, generalising e.g., the
\models_{{\cal C}(2)}^{\oplus}
system,
then the preference inference will no longer be polynomial.
