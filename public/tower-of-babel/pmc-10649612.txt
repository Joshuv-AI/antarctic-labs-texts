Investigating the ability to adhere to cardiometabolic medications with different properties: a retrospective cohort study of >500 000 patients in the USA
Authors: Julie C Lauffenburger, Helen Tesfaye, Daniel H Solomon, Elliott M Antman, Robert J Glynn, Su Been Lee, Angela Tong, Niteesh K Choudhry
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePoor medication adherence remains highly prevalent and adversely affects health outcomes. Patients frequently describe properties of the pills themselves, like size and shape, as barriers, but this has not been evaluated objectively. We sought to determine the extent to which oral medication properties thought to be influential translate into lower objectively-measured adherence.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingUS nationwide commercial claims database, 2016–2019.ParticipantsAmong patients initiating first-line hypertension, diabetes or hyperlipidaemia treatment based on clinical guidelines, we measured pill size, shape, colour and flavouring, number of pills/day and fixed-dose combination status as properties.Outcome measuresOutcomes included discontinuation after the first fill (ie, never filling again over a minimum of 1-year follow-up) and long-term non-adherence (1-year proportion of days covered <0.80). We estimated associations between each property and outcomes, by therapeutic class (eg, statins), with multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAcross 604 323 patients, 14.6% discontinued after filling once (ie, were non-persistent), and 54.0% were non-adherent over 1-year follow-up. Large pill size was associated with non-adherence, except for thiazides (eg, metformin adjusted OR (aOR): 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.18). Greater pill burden was associated with a higher risk of non-adherence across all classes (eg, metformin aOR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.53 to 1.64 for two pills/day). Taking less than one pill/day was also associated with higher risk of non-adherence and non-persistence (eg, non-persistence statin aOR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.20 to 1.38). Pill shape, colour, flavouring and combination status were associated with mixed effects across classes.ConclusionsPill burden and pill size are key properties affecting adherence for almost all classes; others, like size and combination, could modestly affect medication adherence. Clinical interventions could screen patients for potential intolerance to medication and potentially implement more convenient dosing schedules.Keywords: clinical pharmacology, epidemiology, primary care, cardiology, diabetes & endocrinology

Introduction

Long-term adherence to evidence-based medications, especially in cardiometabolic diseases, remains suboptimal; on average, fewer than half of patients regularly take their prescribed medications.1–3 Poor adherence can lead to increased healthcare spending and adverse clinical outcomes.4 5

In spite of much effort to identify and intervene among patients with poor adherence, these low rates of adherence persist.6–10 Prior approaches may have been limited, in part, because other factors that affect the patient medication-taking experience, such as the properties of the medications themselves or their ‘adherability’, have often been overlooked.6 11 12 Pill burden, size, taste or shape, may worsen adherence by making medications more difficult to take. For example, more than 30% of patients report difficulty swallowing their prescribed medications, yet doctors are frequently unaware of these difficulties.12 Qualitative interviews of patients suggested that properties such as medication size could contribute to regimen deviations.13 Some metformin pills are also known to have a metallic flavour.14

Despite being potentially addressable, the possible influence of medication properties of cardiometabolic medications have been insufficiently examined, particularly when multiple therapeutic options may be available.15–17 Understanding medication properties that can lead to poor adherence is particularly important given that patients are now being prescribed multiple medications to reach clinical guideline targets.18–20

Accordingly, we evaluated the association between medication properties, such as size, shape, colour, flavour and number of pills per day, and adherence and persistence among patients initiating treatment for hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia. The objective was to determine the extent to which physical aspects of pills that are thought to affect the patient experience translate into lower objectively-measured adherence.

Methods

This study was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board and follows requirements of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.21 A waiver of informed consent was also approved as the data are secondary claims data. Because of the sensitive nature of the data used for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers are available on reasonable request and execution of appropriate data use agreements with the vendor.

Study design and patient population

In this retrospective cohort design, we identified patients who were newly-treated for cardiometabolic disease. To do so, we used de-identified claims data for commercially-insured beneficiaries from Optum’s de-identified Clinformatics Data Mart Database that covers patients in all 50 states, with data spanning 2016–2019. These data include complete processed and paid patient-level claims for inpatient and outpatient procedures, hospitalisations, emergency department and office visits and outpatient prescription fills linked with plan enrolment data (ie, the dates of their insurance coverage). The vast majority of medication fills for chronic medications in the USA are for 30 or 90 days supply22; prescriptions can be written for up to 12 months with 30–90 day refills before a new prescription is needed. Medications can also be written as ‘dispensed as written’, otherwise the generic medication available at the pharmacy will be dispensed.

We identified patients who newly-initiated a first-line cardiometabolic medication for hypertension, diabetes or hyperlipidaemia (ie, one of four therapeutic classes: statin, biguanide (metformin), ACE inhibitor/angiotensin receptor block (ACEI/ARB, considered as one class), thiazide diuretic) between 01 January 2017 and 12 December 2018, defined as their ‘index date’. These medications were considered ‘first-line’ because the relevant US clinical guidelines for that cardiometabolic condition have recommended them as the first treatment option for most patients.23–25 We excluded liquid and injectable preparations for comparability (<1% of fills); none of these classes have injectable preparations as their primary formulation. We defined initiation based on the absence of any medication for that disease state (eg, hypertension) at any time in their enrolment period before the index date (≥180 days). If patients initiated multiple eligible new medications on the same day, we randomly selected one for analysis (<5% of patients). Patients were also only eligible for their first initiated medication fill across disease states. We focused on these individuals as we hypothesised they may be particularly susceptible to medication properties; there is also variation in properties themselves, such as size or shape, within classes (online supplemental table 1).26–28 Other inclusion criteria included being ≥18 years of age on the index date and maintaining continuous healthcare plan enrolment for ≥180 days prior to and ≥365 days after the index date (figure 1), which helped ensure that healthcare usage and medication filling would be observable within these time periods.

Medication properties

Of properties identified in prior studies,13 we focused on factors thought to influence adherence that could hypothetically be within control when choosing or dispensing a medication by at least one actor in the health system (ie, clinician, pharmacist or managed care plan) and readily measurable in claims data. These included: pill size, shape, colour and flavouring (defined by whether there was a flavouring or not), number of pills per day (calculated as the ratio between quantity dispensed and days supplied for that medication), and whether that medication was dispensed as a combination. As a specification check in a sensitivity analysis, we also included copayment, widely-known to influence adherence but is more variable for non-clinical reasons.

We measured number of unique pills per day for that medication (based on quantity vs days’ supplied), combination status (ie, whether it was dispensed in a fixed-dose combination with another medication) and copayment (for sensitivity analysis) directly from the dispensed medication in pharmacy claims. Pill shape, colour and flavouring were extracted directly from the First DataBank MedKnowledge, a drug information database linked with National Drug Codes (NDCs) for the specific dispensed index medication, which provided information through 2019 in our data.29 Flavouring was classified directly from these data based on whether there was specific flavouring on the pill. Pill size (in mm diameter) was obtained from the US National Library of Medicine at the NDC-level (downloaded 3 January 2021).30 For the primary statistical analysis to ensure variation across dimensions of medication properties, we created the following categories for pill size, shape and colour: (1) pill size above and below the median for that therapeutic class, (2) shape as ‘round’ or ‘other’ for all classes except for statins (‘round’, ‘oval’ or ‘other’) and (3) colour as ‘white’ or ‘non-white’.

Medication adherence

To measure medication-taking outcomes, we first generated a supply diary for the initiated medication by stringing together consecutive fills based on dispensing date and days supplied from the index date until 365 days after.31 We allowed for switching within each of the therapeutic classes in the supply diary (with ACEI/ARBs also being considered interchangeable).32 We allowed supply from any overlap to accumulate up to 180 days.

Our primary outcomes included adherence and persistence measures based on these supply diaries. Both are important outcomes within the framework of adherence.33 34 We measured whether patients persisted with treatment after the index prescription fill (discontinuation defined as ‘non-persistent after first fill’, defined as ever filling again within the follow-up period, allowing for switching within therapeutic class). We chose this metric to assess whether the medication was potentially immediately intolerable. We also measured adherence to the initiated medications by the proportion of days covered (PDC) metric in claims data, commonly-used to measure adherence for research and quality metrics, over the 365-day follow-up period.9 35 36 For this metric, we allowed switching to any medication for that disease state (eg, hypertension) to be conservative.32 Using the supply diary, we classified adherence as a dichotomous measure of adherence (with PDC≥80% considered to be optimal).35 37 38 We also measured adherence as a continuous measure for sensitivity analyses.

Covariates

We measured several baseline characteristics previously found to have associations with adherence (shown in table 1).20 22 39 40 For demographics, we collected age, sex, race/ethnicity and geographical region of residence from enrolment files. We counted the number of outpatient visits (including laboratory studies), emergency department visits, number of days hospitalised and number of unique medications filled by the patient (excluding the index medication) to capture resource usage in the 180 days before and including initiation. Clinical comorbidities used for adjustment were assessed using International Classification of Disease 10th revision (ICD-10) codes, with codes drawn from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Condition Warehouse.41 We also measured characteristics of the index prescription as covariates including mail order status (ie, if the prescription was mailed directly to the patient) and number of days supplied (classified as ≤30 days or >30 days). We also measured potential indicators of intolerance swallowing or digesting medication, included as covariates. These characteristics included diagnoses for nausea, constipation, vomiting, diarrhoea, dry mouth and dysphagia and use of anti-nausea medication, measured in pharmacy claims or ICD-10 diagnoses in medical claims (shown in table 1).

Statistical analysis

First, we examined the prevalence of adherence and persistence outcomes, across the four therapeutic classes and in combination. We also described the patient characteristics and distribution of medication properties in the therapeutic classes as well as the number of patients within the different therapeutic classes who switched medication in the follow-up period.

Second, we used multivariable logistic regression analysis to evaluate the relationship between each of the medication properties (eg, pill size) and adherence, within the therapeutic classes (eg, statins, metformin, ACEI/ARB (as one class) and thiazides). These analyses were conducted separately for each adherence or persistence outcome, adjusting for measured baseline covariates. We conducted these analyses separately by therapeutic class to control for any differences that may exist and to explore factors that may influence adherence that are unrelated to the choice of class. In addition, not all characteristics (eg, each type of pill shape) existed for each class. In secondary analyses, we used multivariable linear regression to evaluate the relationship between each medication property and adherence measured as a continuous outcome, separately by class. We also conducted a subgroup analysis among patients with at least one indicator of potential swallowing or digestive intolerance.

Finally, we also conducted several secondary and sensitivity analyses, as follows: (1) repeating the analysis to include more categories for pill shape and colour and pill size as a continuous variable rather than collapsing, (2) including copayment for the medication in the multivariable regression, (3) including the proxies of intolerance swallowing or digesting medication as confounders, (4) restricting to individuals with ≥365 days of prior enrolment (ie, ≥365 days of no prior medication use), (5) restricting to those with a diagnosis of the condition of interest in the past 180 days, which is often undercoded in claims data and (6) restricting the adherence evaluation to those who filled their medication at least twice (including the index date) to fully disentangle adherence from persistence. As exploratory analyses, we also explored the two-way interactions between medication properties and their association with outcomes.

All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (Cary, North Carolina, USA). Dr Lauffenburger had full access to the data and takes responsibility for integrity and data analysis.

Results

In total, 3 287 923 adult patients filled ≥1 first-line cardiometabolic prescription within the specified classes, of which 1 085 282 (33.0%) met continuous enrolment criteria; specifically, 47.9% of patients out of the 3 287 923 were retained after applying the baseline enrolment criteria, and 72.9% of patients out of the 3 287 923 were retained after applying the post-eligibility enrolment criteria. Then, after identifying new users, 604 323 unique patients met our inclusion criteria. The cohort’s mean age was 60.9 (SD: 13.8), and 306 656 (50.7%) were women.

Across the classes, only 516 236 (85.4%) filled medication again after their first fill (‘persistence’). Rates of persistence varied across classes; they were slightly higher for ACEI/ARBs and statins and slightly lower for metformin and thiazide diuretics (figure 2). On average, long-term adherence was also only 46.0% and varied across classes. When descriptively examining switching after initiation in the follow-up period, 47 684 (16.2%) of patients initiating a statin switched to a different oral hyperlipidaemic (including a different statin), 28 952 (25.4%) of patients initiating metformin switched to a different oral hypoglycaemic, 59 444 (38.3%) of patients initiating ACEI/ARBs switched to a different oral antihypertensive (including a different ACEI/ARB), and 15 699 (31.5%) of patients initiating a thiazide switched to a different oral antihypertensive (including a different thiazide).

Table 1 provides patient baseline characteristics stratified by adherence status. Patients who were adherent and persistent were on average slightly older and less likely to be women. Clinical comorbidities and indicators of potential swallowing or digestion intolerance were similar between those who were adherent and non-adherent. Patients who were non-persistent generally had slightly higher rates of comorbidities and potential swallowing/digestion intolerance.

Medication properties are shown in table 2 by levels of persistence and adherence. Each therapeutic class varied in available pill shapes and sizes. Number of pills per day varied the most for metformin (biguanides); for other classes, the most common dosing was one pill/day (>90%). Taking more than or less than one pill per day (eg, half pill) was more common among non-adherent and non-persistent patients. Pill flavouring was also present in ~12% of metformin prescriptions and was common in patients who were non-adherent and non-persistent. The associations between medication properties and outcomes using multivariable logistic regression are shown in table 3. Larger pill size was consistently associated with a higher odds of non-adherence for all classes except for thiazides (eg, metformin adjusted OR (aOR): 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.18). Large pill size was not associated with non-persistence except for ACEI/ARB (aOR: 1.66, 95% CI: 1.58 to 1.75). Greater pill burden was associated with a higher risk of non-adherence across all classes (eg, metformin aOR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.53 to 1.64 for two pills/day vs one pill/day). Taking less than one pill/day was also associated with higher risk of non-adherence and non-persistence (eg, non-persistence statin aOR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.20 to 1.38) versus one pill/day.

In addition, pill shape had mixed associations with adherence across classes, but round shapes were associated with lower adherence and persistence for statins and ACEI/ARB (eg, aOR for oval vs round for statins: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.83 to 0.87) (table 3). No consistent pattern was observed for pill colours across classes. Patients initiating a medication with flavouring were more likely to become non-persistent for metformin but not for thiazides (eg, metformin non-persistence aOR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.11 to 1.23 vs no flavouring). Initiating a combination pill was inconsistently associated across classes with non-adherence and non-persistence (eg, statin non-adherence aOR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.06 to 1.47). Similar findings were observed when evaluating non-adherence as change in PDC rather than a binary outcome (PDC<0.80) (online supplemental table 3). For example, initiating a larger pill size for metformin was associated with lower PDC (-2.0%, 95% CI: −3.1% to −0.9%) in the 1-year follow-up than a smaller pill size.

Sensitivity analyses of medication properties where pill size was entered as a continuous variable and with more categories for pill shape and colour were similar (online supplemental table 4). In addition, two-way interaction p values from the exploratory analyses across medication properties are shown in online supplemental table 5, which suggest no consistent effect measure modification across therapeutic classes. While overall index copayments were on average <US$10 (online supplemental table 2), higher copayments were associated with significantly higher odds of non-adherence and non-persistence for all medication classes (online supplemental table 6).

The subgroup analysis among patients with potential swallowing or digestive intolerance were also similar to the main findings, except that some magnitudes were larger (eg, pill size and number of pills) (online supplemental table 6). In addition, including copayment in the analysis had no notable impact on the association between other medication properties and non-adherence and non-persistence. Other sensitivity analyses also did not materially change the findings (online supplemental table 6).

Discussion

In adults initiating medications for hypertension, diabetes and hyperlipidaemia, we found that adherence was poor and that almost 15% of patients never filled a medication for their condition again after filling the first time over a 1-year follow-up. Suboptimal adherence was more common for thiazide diuretics than for statins, metformin or ACEI/ARBs. Higher pill burden was consistently associated with suboptimal adherence; larger pill sizes was also an independent risk factor for non-adherence, except for thiazides. Taking less than one pill/day was also associated with a greater odds of non-adherence and non-persistence. We observed inconsistent associations of other properties on adherence and persistence across classes.

This study contains several observations confirming prior work but also provides new data. First, the relationship we observed between complex dosing and non-adherence has been reported previously13 42 43; remembering to take medication in the afternoon or evening is often more difficult.20 44 However, the association between lower doses (eg, every other day or a half-pill) and non-adherence for some medications is less established and might be due to issues cutting pills, reflective of cost-cutting measures correlating with non-adherence behaviours or difficulties forming habits when executing a behaviour like medication-taking on alternating days. Second, less well-known is the extent to which pill size impacts adherence, but self-reported adherence has been found to be lower for larger sizes.13 45 Our work confirms its potential impact on adherence. Third, prior work has found that fixed-dose combinations are, on average, associated with better long-term adherence than individually-dispensed medications, but our study suggests that this may not always be true, potentially for new initiators since combination drugs can be difficult to titrate.18 Further, the inconsistent effects we observed for pill shape, colour and flavour, which were hypothesised to potentially negatively affect the palate or individual emotions or perceptions about medication or how easily they would be remembered, do not suggest that these properties translate to impacts on adherence at least on the individual medication level. Our specification check confirms an association between copayment and non-adherence but that other properties are still associated.11 46 47

From an industry perspective, some aspects about the pharmaceutical formulation of medications (eg, pill size) or their pharmacokinetics or dynamics (eg, regimen) could affect their ‘adherability’ in the real-world. This research suggests that modifications to reduce complex dosing or pill size could improve patients’ medication-taking experience, if possible, within potency considerations for the medication. While patient-centred pharmaceutical design principles for manufacturer and regulatory purposes offer broad guidance, they may be insufficient to address individual needs.48 49

Our findings also hold implications for clinical care and adherence intervention design for individual patients. For instance, providers may not know what the medications they prescribe actually look like or what features are acceptable to or even preferred by their patients.50 51 Providers or their delegates could consider screening patients for potential intolerance and recommend easier dosing regimens. Future interventions could also incorporate a less reactive approach, for example, if clinical decision support or prescribing tools tailored medication choices to patients, especially those who may be in greater need. Individual patients may also have preferences related to properties of their other medications.52 Pharmacists and pharmacies may have an opportunity to take a greater role in establishing which medications may lead to greater adherence for individual patients, especially given the latitude for generic substitution by pharmacists.51 For instance, a therapeutically-equivalent medication from the same class that has more desirable features for that patient could be substituted.

When designing interventions, insurers could also identify patients potentially at risk for non-adherence using these same types of claims data to screen for suboptimal treatment regimens or tailor formulary options and identify whether the patient may benefit from an alternative regimen or pill size. Moreover, health plans and researchers may be able to identify whether patients have potential swallowing or digestive difficulty using routinely-collected data, which could help target potential adherence interventions further. Regardless, more research may be necessary to develop a greater understanding of challenges of medication properties, as more medications are becoming necessary to reach guideline targets.53

This study has limitations. First, as it is observational, there is potential for missing confounders and selection bias. Second, although claims data are often the gold standard for measuring adherence, adherence is measured indirectly (ie, it is not possible to ascertain that the patients actually took the medications within administrative data) and may miss prescriptions filled outside of insurance. The impact of properties could even be underestimated as primary non-adherence (ie, not filling after prescribing) would not be captured. Third, medication instructions (ie, signatura (SIG), or the medication instructions) are not available in claims, so it is not possible to distinguish the specific frequency of medication-taking within a given day for the number of pills; for example, for 60 pills dispensed for a 30-day supply, the medication could be taken two times per day (ie, 1 pill two times per day) or 2 pills one time per day (ie, 2 pills daily). Regardless, this calculation still captures daily pill burden and in these conditions, two pills per day would most likely be intended for dosing multiple times per day.23 54 Fourth, we chose to evaluate within class to control for confounding by indication but could have reduced our ability to observe a consistent effect. It is also not possible to distinguish among the reasons for non-adherence, as some may be due to clinicians changing medication, although we included any switches within the condition and would likely be non-differential. For 5% of patients who initiated multiple medications, we also randomly selected medications so that there was one observation per patient, which could affect generalisability. Finally, the data only included commercially-insured beneficiaries.

Conclusion

Several properties of medications, such as dosing regimen, are key for optimal patient medication-taking. However, less explored aspects, such as pill size, may have a modest impact for most classes. Interventions to improve adherence and select medications should consider the ability to adhere to certain medication properties. These factors could be even more important in patients with some potential difficulty taking medications.
