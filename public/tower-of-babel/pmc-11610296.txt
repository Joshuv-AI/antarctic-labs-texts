Long Term Evaluations of First Single-dose Rituximab in Children with Steroid-Dependent Minimal-Change Nephrotic Syndrome
Authors: Xiao-Ling Niu, Yun-Fan Gu, Dan Feng, Sheng Hao, Xin-Yu Kuang, Ping Wang, Wen-Yan Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the long-term efficacy and safety of the first single dose of rituximab in children with steroid-dependent minimal-change nephrotic syndrome (SD-MCNS) over a two-year period after infusion.MethodsA 2-year retrospective observational study was performed on children with SD-MCNS who received the first single dose of rituximab (375 mg/m2) from October 2011 to December 2018.ResultsSeventy-seven patients (median age 8.17 years) were included. The efficacy of the first single-dose rituximab in children with SD-MCNS was 90.91% (70/77). An overall relapse rate of 78.33% was achieved. Older age at rituximab treatment onset (>8.46 years), a lower steroid-dependent dosage (<18.76 mg/m2·d) and a higher CD4+ T-cell count before rituximab treatment (>31.22%) were positively related to treatment efficacy (p < 0.05). Male sex, younger age at rituximab treatment onset, a higher IgE level before rituximab treatment, and a higher white blood cell count and CD3+ T-cell count at the time of steroid withdrawal were associated with disease relapse (p < 0.05). A model for predicting relapse after rituximab treatment in SD-MCNS patients was established.ConclusionsThe first single-dose rituximab treatment for children with SD-MCNS was effective and safe. Greater efficacy was observed in patients who were older at rituximab treatment onset, had a lower steroid-dependent dosage, or had a higher CD4+ T-cell count before rituximab treatment. In contrast, younger male patients with a higher IgE level experienced an increased occurrence of relapse.Keywords: Rituximab, steroid-dependent minimal-change nephrotic syndrome, children, efficacy, relapse

Introduction

Primary nephrotic syndrome (PNS) is a common glomerular disease in children with a prevalence of 2–16.9/10 million in different races and areas [1]. PNS can be categorized as steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (SSNS) (80–90%) or steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) (10–20%) based on the response to steroids [2]. Minimal-change nephrotic syndrome (MCNS) is the most common type of PNS. Most MCNS patients are sensitive to steroids, but 50–60% of patients relapse when steroids are tapered. Various steroid-sparing agents (SSAs) are used to induce remission and reduce the negative effects of steroids [3].

Rituximab is a human–mouse chimeric monoclonal antibody for CD20 cells that was first used to treat B-cell lymphoma and was subsequently tested for its ability to treat PNS patients in 2004. Rituximab appears to be effective at maintaining remission in children with steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome/frequent-relapse nephrotic syndrome (SDNS/FRNS), reducing the number of relapses and enabling the tapering and withdrawal of steroids and SSAs [4–9]. However, 80% of children will relapse after rituximab treatment, with a median time of 12.5 months [10]. Several factors promote relapse but are still under debate, such as B-cell depletion and the dosage and frequency of rituximab [8,11,12]. No consensus of rituximab regimens for children with PNS has been established, and various doses of treatment are used worldwide at different frequencies. Multiple-dose rituximab treatment increases the risk of infection and thrombocytopenic disorders, as well as the economic burden [13]. We reported that a small sample of steroid-dependent minimal-change nephrotic syndrome (SD-MCNS) patients experienced persistent relapse at a short-term follow-up after single-dose rituximab treatment [6]. Thus, data were retrospectively compiled from SD-MCNS children receiving the first single-dose of rituximab over a seven-year period and the evaluation period after rituximab treatment was extended to at least two years to explore the efficacy and safety of single-dose rituximab in SD-MCNS patients, evaluate the risk factors for relapse, and establish a model to predict relapse.

Methods

Subjects

Inclusion criteria

PNS was defined by the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KIDGO) guidelines [14]. The following criteria were used for inclusion: (1) admitted to the inpatient ward of our department from October 2011 to December 2018 (2) diagnosed with SD-MCNS by renal biopsy and whose clinical category was SDNS (which was defined as children with PNS in whom two consecutive relapses occurred while still on weening off steroids or within 14 days of discontinuing steroid therapy) concomitant with steroids or other SSAs and (3) received the first single-dose of rituximab treatment.

Exclusion criteria

The following criteria were used for exclusion: (1) had a clinical category that did not present as steroid dependence (2) had received rituximab treatment for any reason before (3) had incomplete clinical records or (4) were followed up for less than 24 months.

Protocol for rituximab treatment

All patients received single-dose rituximab treatment as follows (1) Pretreatment tests were conducted to exclude contradictions before rituximab infusion. Promethazine (phenergan) was given intramuscularly, and methylprednisolone was given intravenously (2 mg.kg−1, 60 mg.kg−1 at the maximum) immediately before rituximab infusion. (2) A single-dose of rituximab varied from at least 375 mg.(m2)−1 up to 500 mg at the maximum. The infusion rate was 50 mg per hour at the beginning, increased by 25 mg per hour, and increased to 400 mg per hour. The patients were monitored for any acute side effects during rituximab infusion. (3) After rituximab infusion, prednisone was given orally at a dose of 1 mg/kg (≤60 mg.kg−1), or the minimum effective dose was given before rituximab treatment. All the SSAs were withdrawn. Prednisone was tapered by 5 to 10 mg every 2 weeks and stopped within three months after complete remission. All patients were followed up at 3 days, 1 month, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months after rituximab treatment.

Evaluation of treatment effects

The treatment effect was defined by the 2012 KIDGO guidelines as [14] complete remission (CR), partial remission (PR), or no remission. Relapse was defined as a uPCR ≥ 2 mg.mg−1 (≥200 mg.mmol−1) or ≥3+ protein on a urine dipstick for three consecutive days. We defined these patients as follows: (1) efficient: achieved CR/PR in SD-MCNS patients by the discontinuation of steroids for more than 2 weeks or reduced the use of steroids by ≥ 50% (2) inefficient: the patients did not reduce the use of steroids, reduced the use of steroids of less than 50%, or had no remission.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as medians (interquartile ranges). The factors associated with treatment efficacy were analyzed using logistic regression analysis. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was applied to the cutoff value for risk factors. Survival curves were generated using Kaplan–Meier analysis. A log-rank analysis was performed to determine the differences in the various influencing factors. Multifactor Cox regression analysis was applied to define the risk factors for relapse and construct the prediction model. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics 19.0 (IBM Corporation, USA).

Results

General

There were six hundred and forty-six children with PNS admitted to our hospital from October 2011 to December 2018, and 130 patients of them received the first single-dose rituximab. Among them, 82 patients were diagnosed with MCD by renal biopsy. Three patients were excluded for SRNS, and two patients were excluded because of incomplete clinical records. Therefore, a total of seventy-seven children with SD-MCNS were included in this study. The sex ratio was 1.96:1 (M/F). There were 12 patients with a history of steroids alone (15.58%), 28 patients with a history of using one SSA (36.36%), and 37 patients with a history of using two or more SSAs (48.05%). Before rituximab infusion, all the patients were taking steroids with or without SSAs (nine patients with tacrolimus, eight patients with cyclosporine, and five patients with mycophenolate mofetil). Twenty-one patients (27.27%) were in CR, 56 patients (72.73%) had proteinuria and 29 patients (37.66%) had severe proteinuria (Table 1).

Safety of the single-dose rituximab in children with SD-MCNS

Eight (10.39%) subjects had acute infusion reactions, including rash, dizziness, cough and asthma, vomiting and fever. The reactions were reversed by the use of dexamethasone and antihistamines. A full dose of rituximab was administered to all patients. During the follow-up period, eight (10.39%) patients had mild infections of the upper respiratory tract. One (1.30%) patient developed pneumocystis one month after rituximab and was cured after 2 weeks of oral antibiotics. No severe infections, hypogammaglobulinemia or neutropenia were observed.

Efficacy of the single-dose rituximab in children with SD-MCNS

Sixty (77.9%) of the 77 SD-MCNS patients discontinued steroids after the first single-dose of rituximab, 10 (13.00%) patients reduced their steroids use by≥ 50%, and 7 patients (9.10%) had no response. The total efficacy rate was 90.91%. Among the 60 patients who discontinued steroids, 13 (21.67%) patients experienced no relapse after achieving CR within 24 months of follow-up. Two (3.33%) patients relapsed within 3 months, 18 (30.00%) patients relapsed between 3 and 6 months, 19 (31.67%) patients relapsed between 6 and 12 months and 8 (13.33%) patients relapsed 12 months after rituximab treatment. The relapse rate was 78.33%. The Kaplan–Meier curve for relapse-free are shown in Figure 1. Most patients relapsed between 3 and 12 months after rituximab treatment. The median relapse-free duration was 9.5 (6.77, 12.23) months. The efficacy of the first single-dose rituximab in SD-MCNS patients was satisfactory, but the relapse rate was high.

Factors associated with treatment efficacy were analyzed using logistic regression analysis. Age at rituximab treatment (B = 0.390, p = 0.037), the steroid-dependent dosage (B = −0.091, p = 0.031), the platelet (PLT) (B = −0.008, p = 0.029), and the CD4+ T-cell count (B = 0.134, p = 0.043) before rituximab were significantly related to the efficacy of rituximab in children with SD-MCNS. However, the OR for the PLT (0.992) was close to 1. This might be the result of bias and have little impact on the efficacy of the treatment. However, a history of SSA use, other T cells (CD3+ and CD8+), B cells (CD19+ and CD20/CD45), NK cells (CD16+56+/CD3+), or immunoglobulins was not related to the efficacy of rituximab (p > 0.05). Interestingly, logistic regression analysis revealed that a history of mycophenolate mofetil before rituximab was related to the discontinuation of steroids in children with SD-MCNS after rituximab treatment (B = −2.155, p = 0.010).

To further confirm the above findings, a ROC curve was generated using the age at rituximab treatment and the steroid-dependent dosage and CD4+ T-cells before rituximab, and the areas under the curve (AUCs) were 0.773, 0.753, 0.698. The cutoff values for the three factors were 8.46 years, 18.76 mg/m2·d and 31.22%, respectively (Figure 2). While the steroid-dependent dosage had a negative relationship with the efficacy of rituximab, the other two factors had a positive relationship. These findings indicated that the steroid-dependent dosage was lower than 18.76 mg/m2·d in SD-MCNS children older than 8.46 years, or a CD4+ T-cell count > 31.22% before rituximab infusion had improved efficacy, and remission was more likely to occur with rituximab use.

Relapse associated with the first single-dose rituximab in children with SD-MCNS

To further explore the factors for relapse in children with SD-MCNS after the first single-dose rituximab treatment, multifactor Cox regression analysis revealed that sex (B = −1.485, p = 0.016), age at rituximab treatment (B = −0.373, p = 0.007), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) before rituximab (B = −0.074, p = 0.011), IgE(log) before rituximab (B = 1.278, p = 0.027), white blood cell (WBC) count and CD3+ T-cell count when steroids were discontinued (B = −0.417, 0.035, p = 0.015, 0.021) were associated with relapse after rituximab in children with SD-MCNS. However, the Exp (B) of ALT was 0.989, which was close to 1, possibly because of bias and had little impact on relapse. A history of using SSAs, other T cells (CD4+ and CD8+), B cells (CD19+ and CD20/CD45), NK cells (CD16+56+/CD3+), or immunoglobulins before rituximab treatment and the duration of steroid discontinuation were not related to relapse (p > 0.05). Therefore, younger boys with a higher IgE level experienced higher rates of relapse.

Discussion

Efficacy and safety of the single-dose rituximab in children with SD-MCNS

Rituximab is a new biologic agent used for treating PNS, especially refractory NS. Rituximab achieved a better therapeutic effect and a lower relapse rate than did cyclophosphamide [15] or tacrolimus [16] for treating PNS at the one-year follow-up after treatment onset. It was reported that SDNS/FRNS patients had a better response to rituximab than did SRNS patients [17], as did MCNS patients compared to FSGS patients [18]. Boumediene reported that the efficacy of rituximab in SD-MCNS children who received rituximab four times continuously (intravenously 2 times every other week) was 90% (9/10) [7]. Recently, a lower total rituximab dose has been advocated for the benefit of less economic burden and side effects [12]. We previously reported that single-dose rituximab therapy was efficient and safe in SD-MCNS patients and had good economic benefits [6]. Additionally, in larger samples, single-dose rituximab therapy was effective in 90.91% of patients for the first time. No severe infections, hypogammaglobulinemia or neutropenia after rituximab treatment, which has been reported previously [19,20], occurred in our study. This difference may be attributed to the lower dose of rituximab used. The efficacy of single-dose rituximab therapy in SD-MCNS patients was equivalent to that of high-dose or multiple-dose rituximab therapy, but with fewer side effects and economic burdens.

However, few reports or studies have investigated the factors influencing the efficacy of rituximab for treating PNS. Patient factors, rituximab dose and maintenance immunosuppression may influence the efficacy of rituximab [10]. Heavy proteinuria may shorten the half-life of rituximab and result in reduced efficacy [21]. Ravani et al. reported that children who were older than 9.4 years were more likely to be able to discontinue steroids and calcineurin inhibitors after rituximab treatment [22]. Patients with more severe disease are associated with a worse response to rituximab treatment [17]. Several studies have shown that SSAs influence the efficacy of rituximab for treating PNS [21–23]. We found that the patients with age at rituximab treatment >8.46 years, the steroid-dependent dosage before rituximab <18.76 mg.(kg·d)−1 and CD4+>31.22% before rituximab had better efficacy. These findings were in accordance with previous studies [7,22]. However, the 24-h urine protein concentration was not related to the efficacy of rituximab in our study. It is possible that more than half of the patients in our study achieved CR/PR before rituximab infusion.

Relapse in children with SD-MCNS after the first single-dose rituximab

Although the efficacy of single-dose rituximab therapy in SD-MCNS children is satisfactory, the relapse rate is still high. Kamei reported that the 5-year relapse rate of children with SDNS/FRNS who received four doses of rituximab was 94% (48/51) [24]. The relapse rate in our study was 78.3% at the end of the 24-month follow-up. Thus, identifying the risk factors for relapse in SD-MCNS patients after rituximab treatment is crucial. The use of mycophenolate mofetil following rituximab therapy is an independent risk factor for relapse [25,26]. More randomized controlled studies (RCT) exploring optimized treatments for SDNS/FRNS children are ongoing [27–29]. Fujinaga reported that patients with PNS had a short duration of B-cell depletion (<150 days) after rituximab therapy and that those with a younger age at rituximab treatment frequently relapsed [25]. The risk of relapse decreases by 5% as the age of onset increases every year [11]. This difference may be related to the shorter recovery time of younger patients because the B-cell number decreased [19]. Low-dose rituximab therapy (100 mg.(m2)−1) had a greater risk of relapse due to the shortened duration of B-cell depletion, while high-dose rituximab therapy (750 mg.(m2)−1) did not extend the duration of B-cell depletion [30].

The median relapse-free period in our study was 9.5 months, which was shorter than that reported in a previous study [10]. Most patients included in this study were difficult to treat. Multidrug-dependent patients tend to relapse and have shorter remission periods [18]. The duration of B-cell depletion was negatively associated with relapse, but sex (p = 0.016), age at rituximab therapy (p = 0.007), IgE level before rituximab therapy (p = 0.027), and WBC and CD3+ T-cell count when steroids were discontinued (p = 0.015, 0.021) were positively associated with relapse in SD-MCNS patients after the first single-dose of rituximab. One of the mechanisms of action of rituximab in treating PNS is B-cell reconstitution [31]. In addition to the shorter duration of B-cell depletion, children around the age of puberty are more likely to have balanced immune conditions after rituximab. Boys usually experience puberty 1 or 2 years after girls do. Therefore, younger boys are more likely to relapse. PNS and atopic diseases are the same kind of disease but with different presentations [32]. The IgE level was obviously increased in approximately 50–70% of PNSs and was positively associated with PNS relapse. A lower WBC count but a higher CD3+ T-cell count when steroids were discontinued resulted in a higher relapse rate in the SD-MCNS cohort, whereas the WBC count was normal for all the patients in our study. However, the underlying mechanism is still unclear and requires further research. These findings suggest that cell counts should be used to determine the optimal timing for discontinuing steroid treatments.

Cox analysis was used to establish a model to predict the risk of relapse in SD-MCNS patients after the first single-dose of rituximab. The sample set was sourced from the individuals in this study. Therefore, further studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings.

Although we included the largest population of SD-MCNS children receiving rituximab treatment, there are several limitations. This was not a prospective RCT, and there was bias for patient selection and data analysis. However, additional RCTs with larger populations of patients with different PNS conditions and different doses or frequencies rituximab treatment are needed.

Conclusion

In this study, the largest population of SD-MCNS children sampled to date received the first single-dose rituximab therapy. The first single-dose rituximab therapy in children with SD-MCNS was effective and safe, and it decreased economic burden. Patients who were older when receiving rituximab treatment, had a lower steroid-dependent dosage or had a higher CD4+ T-cell count before rituximab infusion experienced better efficacy. In addition, younger male patients with a higher IgE level relapsed more frequently. It is important to choose the appropriate time for discontinuing steroids. These findings can serve as a reference for clinicians when choosing rituximab treatment options for children with PNS.
