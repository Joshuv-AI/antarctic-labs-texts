Skeletal muscle-derived IL-33 mediates muscle-to-bone crosstalk and regulates bone metabolism via CD8+ T cell-secreted CCL5
Authors: Mingchong Liu, Yi Han, Jiaming Wang, Yimin Zhu, Yueqi Zhang, Qining Chu, Chensong Yang, Bingdi Chen, Guixin Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSkeletal muscle-to-bone crosstalk may play critical roles in the association between muscle atrophy and bone loss. Here, we report a myokine, IL-33, which may mediate muscle-to-bone crosstalk.MethodsA cohort study was conducted to elucidate the associations among circulating IL-33, osteoporosis, and sarcopenia in older adults. To delineate the role of IL-33 in muscle-to-bone crosstalk, we established rat models combining weighted-bearing exercise (WBE), botulinum neurotoxin A (BoNT/A)-induced muscle atrophy, and ovariectomy (OVX), and modulated muscle IL-33 levels both non-specifically and in a muscle-specific manner. Single-cell RNA sequencing was employed to identify the mechanisms by which IL-33 influences bone metabolism, and the downstream pathway was interrogated with anti-CD8β antibody, recombinant CCL5, and a selective CCR3 antagonist.FindingsIn humans, lower levels of IL-33 were linked to an increased risk of co-occurrence of sarcopenia and osteoporosis. In rats, strengthening muscle increased IL-33 levels and improved bone formation, while muscle atrophy had the opposite effect. These muscle-regulated effects can be manipulated by IL-33 supplementation or inhibition, or by skeletal muscle-specific IL-33 knockdown or overregulation. Single-cell sequencing and in vivo experiments confirm that IL-33 can regulate bone metabolism by promoting the release of CCL5 from CD8+ T cells via the ST2 receptor. The bone formation abilities improved by strengthening the muscle could be impaired by CD8+ T cell deletion and then rescued by CCL5 supplementation. CCL5 stimulated osteoclastogenesis through CCR3 and downstream ERK signalling, and blocking the CCR3 receptor can interrupt CCL5's osteogenic effect. Lastly, by improving the levels of IL-33 and CCL5, we may alleviate the bone loss in rats caused by OVX.InterpretationOur research has uncovered a muscle-bone communication mechanism primarily mediated by IL-33, linking muscle dysfunction to bone loss. This interaction could be a promising therapeutic target for musculoskeletal disorders and osteoporosis.FundingThis work was supported by the Leading Talents Training Program of Pudong New Area Health Commission (Grant ID: PWR 12020-06), Health Science and Technology Project of Shanghai Pudong New Area Municipal Health Commission (Grant ID: PW2024B-07), and 10.13039/501100015370Shanghai Key Laboratory of Peripheral Nerve and Microsurgery/10.13039/501100018684National Health Commission Key Laboratory of Hand Reconstruction (Fudan University) [Grant ID: 20DZ2270200].

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Previous work had established that sarcopenia and osteoporosis frequently co-exist in older adults. Low muscle mass and/or strength are associated with low bone mineral density and fracture risk, suggesting the existence of bidirectional muscle–bone crosstalk. Multiple muscle-derived factors (myokines) such as IL-6, irisin, IGF-1, and myostatin have been implicated in this dialogue. Among inflammatory mediators, IL-33 was known to be expressed in skeletal muscle and to regulate immune cells, yet its role as a genuine myokine and its relevance to bone homoeostasis in ageing remained controversial. In particular, the cellular and molecular pathways through which muscle-derived IL-33 might influence bone metabolism had not been defined, and prior studies had reported contradictory effects of IL-33 on osteoblast and osteoclast biology.

Added value of this study

In a cohort of older adults, we show that low IL-33 levels are specifically associated with the co-occurrence of sarcopenia and osteoporosis rather than with either disorder alone. Using muscle-specific knockdown and over-expression of Il33 in rats, we establish that skeletal muscle is the principal source of the IL-33 that mediates exercise-induced bone anabolism, and that muscle-derived IL-33 can rescue bone loss caused by BoNT/A atrophy. Single-cell RNA-sequencing, together with functional studies, reveals that IL-33 does not act directly on BMSCs, but rather signals through ST2 on CD8+ T cells to trigger CCL5 release. Depletion of CD8+ T cells or neutralisation of CCL5 abolishes the osteogenic benefit of muscle-derived IL-33; conversely, CCL5 reconstitution rescues bone formation when CD8+ T cells are depleted. Moreover, CCL5 promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs via CCR3-mediated ERK activation, and shows that selective CCR3 antagonism blocks the skeletal benefits of exercise. Finally, local supplementation with recombinant IL-33 or CCL5, or AAV-mediated muscle-specific Il33 expression, substantially mitigates ovariectomy-induced bone loss in aged rats.

Implications of all the available evidence

Collectively, the data delineate a previously unrecognised muscle/immune/bone signalling axis: muscle-derived IL-33/CD8+ T cell-secreted CCL5/CCR3 on BMSCs/enhanced osteogenesis. This pathway provides a unifying explanation for the clinical observation that poor muscle health predicts accelerated bone loss, and positions IL-33, CCL5, and CCR3 as tractable therapeutic targets for osteosarcopenia and post-menopausal osteoporosis. Future work should validate these findings in larger human cohorts, delineate the precise muscle cell subtype(s) responsible for IL-33 production, and explore whether muscle-directed IL-33 or CCL5 delivery can safely enhance bone formation in frail older adults.

Introduction

Sarcopenia and osteoporosis are two common chronic musculoskeletal disorders with a close relationship to ageing.1 As the ageing of society intensifies, the incidence of sarcopenia and osteoporosis is rising rapidly: in China, the prevalence of sarcopenia among the elderly reaches 20.7%,2 and that of osteoporosis is 29.49%.3 In recent years, the correlation between sarcopenia and osteoporosis has been widely reported: patients with sarcopenia have a higher risk of developing osteoporosis.4 Patients suffering from both osteoporosis and sarcopenia are diagnosed with osteosarcopenia.5 It has been reported that the prevalence of osteosarcopenia among the elderly worldwide is as high as 18.5%,6 and patients with osteosarcopenia may have a significantly increased risk of six-year mortality by 53%.7 Hence, comprehending the association between the pathogenesis of sarcopenia and osteoporosis can shed light on the underlying mechanisms, potentially leading to the development of novel therapeutic approaches targeting sarcopenia and osteoporosis.

Skeletal muscles and bones, as the main organs of the musculoskeletal system, communicate with each other, which may explain the positive link between sarcopenia and osteoporosis.8 Beyond its role in the motor system, muscle is a key secretory organ: skeletal muscle may release various factors, which can regulate processes throughout the body.9,10 Several muscle-secreted myokines have been identified to act as intermediaries in muscle's regulation of bone metabolism in recent years, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), irisin, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and myostatin.11, 12, 13, 14 Additionally, many other cytokines and small proteins are known to be secreted by muscles, but their potential effects on bone metabolism remain unclear.10,15

Considering that numerous myokines are inflammatory cytokines, this establishes a connection between muscle-bone communication, inflammaging, and the two age-related disorders of sarcopenia and osteoporosis.16,17 Inflammaging refers to the chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation associated with ageing.18 It is closely linked to sarcopenia and osteoporosis, as the inflammatory cytokines involved in inflammaging can negatively impact muscle and bone health.19,20 Thus, in the context of ageing and senescence, it is highly likely that the dysregulated secretion of inflammatory cytokines leads to the formation of a systemic chronic inflammatory cytokine network.21 This, in turn, disrupts muscle-bone communication, resulting in the consecutive occurrence of sarcopenia and osteoporosis in older adults.

In a cohort study conducted by our department, the inflammatory cytokine interleukin-33 (IL-33) caught our attention due to its significant difference in patients with osteosarcopenia. IL-33, a member of the interleukin-1 (IL-1) family, is an alarmin released from damaged cells and plays a crucial role in the immune response.22 IL-33 functions as both a transcription regulator and a pro-inflammatory cytokine: in its full-length form, IL-33 is primarily localised in the nucleus, where it regulates gene transcription.23 IL-33 can act on various cells, including T cells, neutrophils, and so on, to exert its biological effects and activate downstream signalling pathways like nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways.24, 25, 26, 27 This activation leads to the production of various cytokines, such as interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-5 (IL-5), interleukin-13 (IL-13), and C–C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5 or RANTES), and drives immune responses.28,29 IL-33 plays a crucial role in immune regulation and inflammation.

In the context of skeletal muscle and bone, the specific mechanisms of IL-33 in skeletal muscle-to-bone crosstalk, as well as the pathogenesis of osteosarcopenia, require further investigation. In this study, we aim to comprehensively explore the roles of IL-33 in bone-muscle crosstalk and bone metabolism. In human studies, we showed that the low levels of IL-33 are associated with a high risk of osteosarcopenia. In animal studies, we found that blocking IL-33 release or locally knocking down IL-33 expression in muscles could markedly suppress the weight-bearing exercise (WBE)-induced bone density increase. Conversely, exogenous IL-33 supplementation or muscle-specific upregulation of IL-33 expression in muscles could alleviate bone metabolic disorders caused by muscle atrophy due to botulinum toxin A (BoNT/A) injection. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) indicated that IL-33 might regulate bone formation by modulating CD8+ T cells to release CCL5. Animal experiments confirmed this and found that CCL5 regulates osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) through the C–C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3). Finally, we tried to target IL-33/CD8+T cell/CCL5/CCR3-related molecules and found that these treatments could potentially treat the bone loss of ovariectomy (OVX) rats. Overall, our findings reveal a non-traditional regulatory pathway involving IL-33, CD8+ T cells, CCL5, and CCR3 in bone metabolism, highlighting a new mechanism of skeletal muscle-to-bone crosstalk.

Methods

Ethical declarations

Human studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital (2020 research review No. 143) and were performed following the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The rat experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Uses Committee of Shanghai East Hospital (2022 animal research review No. 278).

Participants

The population study focussing on the relationship between sarcopenia and osteoporosis was a prospective cohort study performed at the Department of Traumatic Surgery, Shanghai East Hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China. Inclusion criteria were: a: age ≥65 years old; b: with low-energy hip fractures within 6 h; c: admitted to our department between September 2021 and March 2022. Exclusion criteria were set as follows: a: with systemic autoimmune diseases or severe infectious diseases; b: with pathological fracture; c: with musculoskeletal disorders (except for sarcopenia and osteoporosis).

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, height, weight, residence (urban vs. rural), smoking and alcoholism history, fracture type (femoral neck vs. intertrochanteric), electrocardiogram and chest radiograph results, and major comorbidities (Table S1). All the data of patients were retrieved from the electronic medical records of our hospital. Sex was self-reported by participants during the baseline visit and cross-checked against government-issued identification. The entire study cohort comprised Han Chinese individuals.

The measurement of HGS was conducted by using a grip dynamometer CAMRY EH101 (Senssun, China, Guangzhou). The ASM was measured by using bioimpedance analysis based on InBody BWA2.0 (InBody Co. Ltd.). This device employs bioimpedance analysis by conducting 40 impedance measurements across five segmental regions (right upper limb, left upper limb, trunk, right lower limb, and left lower limb) using eight distinct frequencies (1 kHz, 5 kHz, 50 kHz, 250 kHz, 500 kHz, 1 MHz, 2 MHz, and 3 MHz). For ASM quantification, the data from the four limbs were extracted and analysed via the integrated computational algorithm within the system and then adjusted for height. BMD was measured by using dual X-ray absorptiometry. Sarcopenia and osteoporosis were identified according to the AWGS 2019 consensus guidelines and diagnostic criteria authorised by the Chinese Society of Osteoporosis and Bone Mineral Research, respectively.30

For the human IL-33 analysis, detailed research project and biopsy collection were explained to all participants or their parents, or guardians before entering into the study. The patients who provided written consent for sample collection and met the sample collection requirements were included. Samples were mainly collected from discarded materials during hip joint replacement surgery, without any additional harm to the patients. Briefly, after osteotomy, the fractured femoral head and associated tendons were separated, and the bone matrix, bone marrow, and attached skeletal muscle were collected, respectively. Blood samples were collected during preoperative routine laboratory tests, and written consent was also obtained from the patients.

Mendelian randomisation

The GWAS data regarding IL-33 were derived from a meta-analysis encompassing 14,824 individuals of European ancestry across 11 cohorts.31 The protein levels were quantified utilising the Olink Target-96 Inflammation immunoassay panel. The summary GWAS for handgrip strength was conducted on 254,894 older adults from 22 cohorts, with poor handgrip strength classified as cases based on the EWGSOP criteria (males: <30 kg; females: <20 kg).32 The summary GWAS data for osteoporosis originated from the UK Biobank, involving 462,933 participants. Detailed information on all cohorts related to the traits included in our study is provided in Table S2.

To select IVs associated with exposures in this study, significance thresholds were established at p < 5 × 10−6 to ensure an adequate number of IVs.20 To satisfy the three fundamental assumptions of MR analysis and to mitigate biases from weak IVs and horizontal pleiotropy, we executed six stringent IV filtration steps following previously established protocols.33 Specifically, these steps included clumping to prevent linkage disequilibrium, excluding SNPs associated with multiple exposures, excluding SNPs linked to confounders, excluding palindromic SNPs, excluding SNPs associated with outcomes, and excluding SNPs identified by the MR-PRESSO outlier test to address potential horizontal pleiotropy. The random-effects inverse-variance weighted (RE IVW) analysis was selected as the primary method to investigate causal relationships due to its more conservative estimation.

Animal models and treatments

Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (female) were purchased from the SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Throughout the experiment, all rats were fed and watered as needed and housed in animal RT at 23 ± 2 °C with a continuous light/dark routine. To better mimic the ageing phenotype, rats began modelling at 18 weeks of age. The sample size for the animals used in each experiment was determined from our prior experience with comparable studies and is reported as n in the corresponding figure legends. Animals were randomised to cages and treatment order using a random-number table; treatment allocation was performed by an investigator not involved in assessment. Experimenter, functional assessors, and histopathology analysts were blinded to group assignment; drug codes were held by a third party and decoded only after statistical analysis.

Various models were used in this study. For the muscle-strengthening model, WBE models were applied.34 Specifically, male rats underwent treadmill exercise at a 5° slope, loaded with 35% of their body weight, and trained at 20 m/min for 30 min/day, 6 days/week for 90 days. For muscle atrophy, rats received intramuscular injections of BoNT-A (10 U/kg, #20200862, Hengli), equally distributed into the bilateral hindlimb, once every 4 weeks.35 For the OVX model, female rats underwent ovariectomy to simulate postmenopausal osteoporosis, following previously described surgical protocols. For the tranilast treatment, the rats were treated with tranilast (200 mg/kg, #T2690, TargetMol) every day via intraperitoneal injection after 4 weeks of WBE.36 For IL-33 treatment, rats received intramuscular injections of recombinant rat IL-33 (1 μg/kg, #P6317, Beyotime), equally distributed into the bilateral hindlimb once a day, starting from 4 weeks after BoNT/A injection or OVX. For the IgG and Anti-CD8 treatment, rats were treated with anti-rat CD8α depleting (0.5 mg, #BE0415, BioXcell) or IgG isotype control antibodies (2 mg/kg, #BE0088, BioXcell) intravenously after 4 weeks of WBE, followed by injections of 2 mg weekly to ensure the prolonged depletion.37 For CCL5 treatment, rats received subcutaneous injection of recombinant rat CCL5 (1.5 μg/kg, #SNCF-R040, Sunncell) twice a day, starting from 4 weeks after BoNT/A injection, Anti-CD8 treatment, or OVX. For BX471 treatment, rats were injected subcutaneously with BX471 (50 mg/kg, #SD3699, Beyotime) 3 times a day after 4 weeks of WBE.38 For SB3 treatment, rats were injected intraperitoneally with SB 328437 (13.75 mg/kg, #Y112727, Beyotime) every two days after 4 weeks of WBE.39 For Maraviroc treatment, rats were injected intraperitoneally with Maraviroc (10 mg/kg, #HY-13004, MCE) every day after 4 weeks of WBE.40 All injections were performed under brief isoflurane anaesthesia (3% induction, 1.5% maintenance) to minimise pain and stress. Animals were placed on a 37 °C pad until fully ambulatory. In addition to the experiments designed to verify the variation of IL33 levels at different times, other experiments were conducted 84 days after the rats underwent WBE, BoNT/A, and OVX.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

IL-33 and CCL5 protein levels were measured by using ELISA, according to the manufacturer's instructions. For sample preparation, bone marrow samples were flushed from the femur head and distal femurs of rats using 250 μl of PBS containing protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC) (#P1005, Beyotime). The samples were immediately centrifuged, and the supernatant containing intracellular fluid was collected. The bone matrix from human femur head and rat femur, as well as skeletal muscle from humans and rats, was chopped and homogenised, then immediately lysed in 250 μl of T-per buffer containing PIC. Serum samples from rats or humans were prepared by collecting whole blood into tubes without anticoagulants. After allowing the blood to clot at room temperature for 1 h, the tubes were centrifuged at approximately 1500 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the serum was harvested. ELISA plates were analysed by investigators blinded to sample identity. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader set at 450 nm with a wavelength correction of 540 nm. Consequently, the values obtained in ELISA tests were normalised based on the lysate volumes of the various samples. The following ELISA kits were used in our study: human IL-33 ELISA kit (#CB10366-Hu, Coibo), rat IL-33 ELISA kit (#CB10213-Ra, Coibo), and CCL5 ELISA kit (#CB10706-Ra, Coibo).

Micro CT analysis

The rats were sacrificed at 84 days after modelling. The femurs were scanned using the high-resolution micro-CT imaging device (Quantum GX, PerkinElmer) according to the guidelines of the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research.41 After scanning, the 3D image reconstruction was performed using NRecon software (Bruker), and the reconstructed images underwent quantitative analysis via CTAn software (Bruker). The region of interest was defined as commencing near the distal growth plate of the femur and extending approximately 2 mm in a proximal direction. The following trabecular and cortical bone parameters were quantified: bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), bone mineral density (BMD), and bone mineral content (BMC).

Biomechanical analysis

The femur underwent a 3-point bending test using an Instron 3366 Universal Tabletop Testing System (Instron Corp., Norwood, USA) to evaluate its biomechanical properties.42 The rat's femur was positioned on a support device with two loading points 2.0 cm apart to preserve its natural upward curvature. Mechanical loading was applied to the mid-shaft of the diaphysis at a constant displacement rate of 2 mm/min until fracture. Biomechanical parameters, such as maximum load, stiffness, and energy absorption, were determined by analysing the load–displacement curve from the test. Additionally, skeletal material properties, including ultimate stress, toughness, and elastic modulus, were assessed based on the same curve as previously described.43

Histological evaluation and immunofluorescent analysis

Femurs and quadriceps were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Additionally, the bone samples were decalcified in 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. All samples, including bones and skeletal muscles, were embedded in paraffin wax and sectioned into 5-μm-thick slides. Haematoxylin and eosin staining was performed. The CSA of muscle fibres was evaluated using a high-magnification field on a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L optical microscope, which was coupled with a Nikon DS-Fi2 digital camera for image acquisition. To identify osteoclasts, selected bone sections were subjected to tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining using a commercial kit (#BB-4421, Bestbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software version 1.53.

Immunofluorescence staining was performed using primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 30 min at 37 °C. Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, #C1002, Beyotime) for 10 min. The primary antibodies used were rabbit monoclonal anti-Collagen Type I (COL-L; 1:1000, #72026, CST), rabbit polyclonal anti-Osteocalcin (OCN; 1:200, #23418-1-AP, Proteintech), rabbit polyclonal anti-Laminin (Laminin; 1:200, #ab11575, Abcam), and rabbit monoclonal anti-IL-33 (IL-33: 1:1000, #ab187060, Abcam). Secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, #ab150078, Abcam), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, #ab150077, Abcam), and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, #ab150080, Abcam). Confocal imaging was conducted using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus). Wheat Germ Agglutinin, Alexa Fluor 488 Conjugate (W11261, Thermo Fisher) were used for wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining.

For immunohistochemistry analysis of CCL5, the primary antibodies used were CCL5 recombinant rabbit Superclonal™ antibodies (1:20, #710001, Thermo Fisher), and the secondary antibodies were goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (HRP, 1:1000, #ab6721, Abcam). The growth plate area was quantified using ImageJ software version 1.53.

Muscle measurement

Hind-limb muscle strength of rats was evaluated with a grip strength metre (LJ800-012, Nscing Es) according to a published protocol.44 In brief, the rat was first relaxed. Then, its two hind paws were placed on a grip rod, and the tail was pulled slowly to measure peak force. The fresh mass of the gastrocnemius (GA) and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles was measured, and the relative muscle mass was calculated by muscle mass/body weight × 100%. The measurements of CSA were calculated based on H&E staining by using ImageJ software version 1.53.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

In brief, RNA was extracted from tissue and cell samples utilising RNAeasy™ animal long RNA isolation kit with spin column (#R0026, Beyotime) following the protocol of the manufacturer. The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PrimeScript RT Master Mix (#RR036Q, TaKaRa, Japan) was employed for reverse transcription. Subsequently, the gene expression of Alp, Ocn, Col1α, Runx2 in BMSCs, Il33 in bone, muscle, heart, liver, as well as Ccl5 in bone and CD8+ T cells was measured by RT-qPCR analysis. This was done using BeyoFas SYBR Green qPCR Mix (#D7262, Beyotime) and the Light Cycler 480II (Roche). The experimental conditions involved 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15s and amplification at 60 °C for 1 min. Each assay was conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and results were analysed using the 2ΔΔCt method, with data normalised to β-actin expression. The primer sequences for each gene are listed in Table S3.

AAV delivery

AAV9s for in vivo knockdown of Il33 and the control AAV9s were generated and provided by PackGene Biotech. The knockdown sequence is as follows: shTfeb: 5′GCAAAGTGCGACAGCACATCA; shNC: 5′CGCGATAGCGCTAATAATTTC. The shRNA sequences were then ligated into the pAAV9-U6-EGFP plasmid. Similarly, the AAVs for in vivo expression of Il33 and EGFP were also generated and provided by PackGene Biotech. Briefly, the PCR products of related sequences were subcloned into an AAV9 shuttle vector pAAV9-CAG plasmid to generate the pAAV9-CAG-EGFP and pAAV9-CAG-Il33 plasmids. AAVs were then produced using the packaging plasmids Delta F6 helper vector (112867, Addgene) and RC2/9 vector (112865, Addgene) along with the generated pAAV9-CAG plasmids by PackGene Biotech. The titre of AAV9 vectors was determined by quantitative PCR, and 4 × 1012 vg/ml of AAV9 vectors were injected into the bilateral hindlimb skeletal muscles of rats every 4 weeks. Ten injections at different sites per hindlimb were administered, with each injection containing a dose of one-tenth.

In vitro study

BMSCs were isolated from 4-week-old female SD rats according to a prior protocol. The femur and tibia were dissected free of muscle and connective tissue. After excising both bone ends, bone marrow was flushed out and suspended in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (#SH30284.02, HyClone) with 10% FBS (#A5669701, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (#1514022, Gibco). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator with media changes every 2–3 days. Upon reaching 70–80% confluency, cells were passaged with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA. Cells from passages 4 were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 per well in a 24-well plate and cultured for 24 h in complete medium, and then the osteogenic differentiation medium (supplied with 10 mmol/L glycerophosphate, 0.1 mmol/L dantrolene, and 0.05 mmol/L ascorbic acid) was used to initiate osteogenic differentiation.45 The differentiation medium was changed every 3 days. BMSCs were transfected with 50 nM Ccr3-targeting siRNA (siCcr3, forward: GGUUGUGUUGAUCCUCAUAAA, reverse UAUGAGGAUCAACACAACCAU) or non-targeting control siRNA (siCtl) using Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000015, Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Rat CD8+ T cells were isolated according to the method described below (Flow cytometry and FACS sorting). The cells were maintained in complete medium consisting of Gibco RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS (#A5669701, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (#1514022, Gibco), under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Recombinant rat interleukin-2 (50 U/mL, #P6254, Beyotime) was added to sustain viability and proliferative capacity. We pre-incubated purified CD8+ T cells with an anti-ST2 neutralising antibody (1 μg/mL, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or IgG isotype control antibodies (1 μg/mL, #BE0088, BioXcell) for 30 min before stimulating them with recombinant IL-33 (10 ng/mL).

Subsequently, after 21 days of osteogenic induction, BMSCs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. For Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, cells were incubated with Alizarin Red S Staining Kit (#C0148, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining, cells were stained using an ALP Staining Kit (#C3206, Beyotime) following the supplier's protocol. After staining, samples were gently washed with PBS. Calcium deposition (ARS) and ALP activity were documented with a Canon camera mounted on an Olympus microscope.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

BMSCs were subjected to complete lysis in ice-cold RIPA buffer freshly supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and 1 × Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail A. Protein extracts were resolved by 10% SDS–PAGE and electro-transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 3% BSA: anti-ERK1/2 (1:10000, #ab184699, Abcam), anti-phospho-ERK1/2 (1:1,000, #ab201015, Abcam), and anti-Tubulin (1:10000, #ab7291, Abcam). Membranes were then washed three times with TBST and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the corresponding HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:3000, #SA00001-1, Proteintech) or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:3000, #SA00001-2, Proteintech). Immunoreactive bands were visualised using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and digitally captured with a ChemiDoc imaging system. Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software version 1.53.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Bone marrow samples were flushed from the distal femurs of rats with PBS. The samples were immediately dissociated by using the MobiSolution Tissue Dissociation Kit (MobiDrop), and a single-cell suspension was obtained. Single-cell nucleic acid capture and sequencing library construction are completed using the MobiNova-100 Library Construction System (MobiDrop) combined with the MobiCube Single-Cell Nucleic Acid Capture and Library Construction Kit (MobiDrop). Briefly, it leverages a microfluidic chip and microsphere molecules with millions of molecular tags to capture mRNA from tens of thousands of cells in a single run. Then the microfluidic chip facilitates single-cell separation. Within the chip, there are multiple microfluidic channels containing microsphere molecules with millions of molecular tags, cells, reagents, and oil. The microsphere molecules, cells, and reagents form the aqueous phase, which, when entering the oil, creates water-in-oil droplets. Based on the Poisson distribution principle, when the number of microspheres far exceeds the number of cells, it ensures that the vast majority of water-in-oil droplets contain only one cell and one microsphere molecule, thereby achieving single-cell separation. The next step entails sequencing the next-generation sequencing libraries constructed in step two.

Analysis of scRNA-seq data

The FASTQ files were processed and aligned to the rat reference genome using MobiVision software (version 3.0), with UMI counts tallied for each barcode. Subsequent analysis of the UMI count matrix was performed using the Seurat (version 5.1.0) R package. To eliminate low-quality cells and likely multiplet captures, filtering criteria were applied: cells were removed if they had1 fewer than 200 genes,2 fewer than 1000 UMIs,3 a log10GenesPerUMI value below 0.7,4 over 10% of UMIs mapped to mitochondrial genes, or5 over 5% of UMIs mapped to haemoglobin genes.

Subsequently, we applied the DoubletFinder package (version 2.0.3) to assess potential doublets and multiplets. To obtain the normalised gene expression data, library size normalisation was implemented by the NormalizeData function. We identified variable genes using the FindVariableFeatures function with the vst selection method. Data regression was conducted via the ScaleData function, adjusting for nUMI, percent mitochondrial counts, and sample origin as confounding factors. For dimensionality reduction, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied, and 40 principal components were chosen based on the elbow plot. The FindNeighbors function, utilising a shared nearest neighbour (SNN) modularity optimisation-based clustering algorithm, was then applied. Clusters were determined with a resolution of 1.4 by the FindClusters function, yielding 41 initial clusters. A further dimensionality reduction step was carried out, using the UMAP algorithm to visualise the data. Cell clusters were annotated by integrating results from the related reference and validated markers from the CellMarker database. Similarly, the T cell subtypes were further identified. To obtain the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), we used the FindAllMarkers function with default parameters. DEGs were considered statistically significant at a threshold of adjusted p-value set at 0.05. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for DEGs was performed. To further identify the communications and interactions between cells, CellChat (version 1.6.1) was used to visualise the relationships between cells and the CCL5-related communications.46

Flow cytometry and FACS sorting

Bone marrow samples were flushed from the distal femurs of rats with PBS, and then the lymphocytes were collected by using the Rat Lymphocyte Separation Kit (#C0027, Beyotime) following the protocol of the manufacturer. The samples were first stained with PE-conjugated CD3 (#201412, BioLegend) and APC-conjugated CD8 (#200610, BioLegend) membrane proteins, and kept protected from light for 30 min. Flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse) was then performed to determine the proportion of CD3+ CD8+ T cells. To identify the expression of CCL5 in CD8+ T cells, FACS sorting was performed on a FACS Aria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences) using FACSDiva software.

Statistics and reproducibility

GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) and R software (version 4.2.2) were used for statistical analysis. The sample size was calculated by using PASS (version 11.0.0), and the sample size in all experiments met the minimum sample size requirement. Sample sizes are stated in the Figure legends. Data collection in the population study was performed blindly. In the animal study, the animals were assigned to experimental groups using simple randomisation, and no data were excluded from the analyses. The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was performed to determine data distribution. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and Categorical variables were presented as count (percent). In the comparison between two groups, normally distributed data were analysed using Independent Student's T-tests, and for data that did not follow a normal distribution, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were utilised. In the comparison between more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used for normal distribution data, and Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn multiple comparisons test was performed for data without normal distribution. Categorical variables were assessed using Chi-squared tests or Fisher's exact test. Univariate and multivariate logistic models were established for each sarcopenia trait, and multivariate models were adjusted for the factors with significant differences in baseline characteristics (age, sex, and smoking). p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The detailed statistical method for scRNA-seq can be found in the corresponding section.

Results

Relationships between sarcopenia and osteoporosis in older adults with hip fractures

We initially recruited a prospective cohort to investigate the association between sarcopenia and osteoporosis, comprising patients with low-energy hip fractures aged ≥65 years who underwent systematic geriatric assessment through our department (September 2021–March 2022). The baseline features of participants were summarised (Table S1). Sarcopenia diagnoses adhered to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 consensus guidelines.30 Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) quantification employed bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), while hand grip strength (HGS) was assessed via a hydraulic hand dynamometer.30 Low ASM and low HGS were also identified according to AWGS 2019. Concurrent reductions in both parameters established a sarcopenia diagnosis. Osteoporosis was confirmed per diagnostic criteria authorised by the Chinese Society of Osteoporosis and Bone Mineral Research (https://csobmr.cma.org.cn/).

The final analytic cohort comprised 150 eligible participants (mean age 78.70 ± 8.70 years). We observed 119 cases of osteoporosis and 66 cases of sarcopenia. Among 31 individuals without osteoporosis, merely 4 (12.90%) exhibited sarcopenia. Conversely, 62 patients with osteoporosis (52.10%) satisfied sarcopenia diagnostic thresholds (p < 0.001, Chi-squared tests). Similarly, the individuals with osteoporosis may have significantly higher rates of low ASM and lower continuous HGS and ASM (Figure S1A). Subsequently, gender-stratified subgroup analyses yielded results consistent with our primary findings. Both males and females with osteoporosis demonstrated a significantly higher prevalence of sarcopenia (male: 78.26%; female: 45.84%) compared to non-osteoporotic counterparts (male: 15.00%; female: 9.10%), regardless of gender (all p < 0.001, Chi-squared tests, Figure S1B and C). Intriguingly, gender-stratified analyses revealed differential associations between low HGS, low ASM, continuous HGS/ASM parameters, and osteoporosis occurrence across sexes, with some associations lacking statistical significance. This pattern further validates the enhanced diagnostic specificity of combined ASM and HGS criteria in sarcopenia identification. As shown in the baseline chart, many baseline variables of patients between the normal and osteoporosis groups were significantly different (age: p = 0.001, sex: p < 0.001, smoking: p < 0.001, Chi-squared tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests). To avoid the bias caused by co-factors, we established univariate and multivariate logistic models adjusted by five sarcopenia traits (sarcopenia, low HGS, low ASM, continuous HGS, and continuous ASM), respectively. Univariate logistic regression models confirmed significant associations between sarcopenia, low ASM, continuous HGS, continuous ASM, and osteoporosis occurrence (Figure S1D). In the adjusted multivariate model incorporating sex, age, and smoking status, only sarcopenia, low ASM, and continuous ASM retained statistically significant associations with osteoporosis development (Figure S1E).

Roles of IL-33 in sarcopenia and osteoporosis

The relationship between IL-33 and osteoporosis and sarcopenia was captured in the cohort study. In this cohort, intraoperative biological samples from discarded femoral heads with attached skeletal muscle tissues of patients undergoing hip arthroplasty (collected under informed consent protocols) were systematically obtained for analysis. Concurrently, their peripheral blood samples were also collected. Using standardised Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) protocols, we quantified IL-33 concentrations across four anatomical compartments: skeletal muscle, serum, bone matrix, and proximal bone marrow. Compared to the healthy control group, patients with concurrent sarcopenia and osteoporosis exhibited significantly decreased IL-33 levels across all four tissues (Fig. 1A). Despite observing a downward trend in IL-33 levels in patients with isolated osteoporosis or sarcopenia alone, the differences were not statistically significant compared to healthy controls in serum, bone matrix, or proximal bone marrow. This observation prompted us to investigate the potential role of IL-33 in the co-development of sarcopenia and osteoporosis. Therefore, we performed Mendelian Randomisation (MR) analysis to estimate the roles of IL-33 in sarcopenia and osteoporosis. Mendelian randomisation is a method for assessing causal relationships between exposures and outcomes using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), leveraging the random allocation of alleles at conception to minimise confounding.47 We adopted a bidirectional MR approach by alternately setting IL-33, HGS, and sarcopenia as exposures and outcomes to infer the potential role of IL-33 in the co-occurrence of sarcopenia and osteoporosis. We included three meta-GWASs into MR (Table S2): the IL-33 cohorts (14,824 individuals from 12 cohorts),31 HGS cohorts (256,523 individuals from 22 cohorts),32 and osteoporosis cohorts (462,933 individuals from the UK Biobank, UK Biobank ID: ukb-b-12141). Following prior methodological standards,33 we rigorously screened instrumental variables to satisfy all three core assumptions of MR, and finally, 8 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) for IL-33 and 52 SNPs for HGS were selected as IVs (Table S4). The low HGS was estimated to causally relate to low IL-33 levels in MR analysis, and the high levels of IL-33 were proven to have a causality with the low incidence of osteoporosis (Fig. 1B). Our Mendelian randomisation results revealed a potential causal cascade wherein sarcopenia appears to significantly reduce circulating IL-33 levels, and this IL-33 depletion subsequently contributes to osteoporosis development.

Skeletal muscle regulates bone metabolism via IL-33

To prove the first estimation from MR that muscle causally relates to IL-33 levels, we established three kinds of rat models using WBE,34 BoNT/A,35 and OVX. WBE may significantly enhance muscle strength and mass, BoNT/A may induce marked muscle atrophy, and OVX creates an osteoporosis model. We analysed IL-33 levels in four tissue types from all three animal models and controls at multiple timepoints. Significant IL-33 changes first appeared in muscle at day 30 post-modelling (Fig. 1C), followed by serum at day 60 (Fig. 1D), with bone matrix (Fig. 1E) and epiphyseal marrow (Fig. 1F) alterations emerging by day 90. Specifically, at 90 days, WBE significantly elevated IL-33 across all four tissue types, while BoNT/A caused marked reductions. OVX showed no significant effects on IL-33 levels.

To understand the roles of IL-33 in muscle-bone crosstalk and bone metabolism, we further investigated the effects of WBE and tranilast, a pharmacological agent that inhibits the production of IL-33,48,49 on bone metabolism in rats (Fig. 2A). Consistent with the above results, WBE significantly increased IL-33 levels in four tissue types at day 90, and tranilast suppressed this effect (Figure S2A). No significant differences in body weight were observed among the three groups (control, WBE, WBE + tranilast). WBE significantly increased the weight of the gastrocnemius muscle (GA), GA percentage of total body weight, the Tibialis anterior muscle (TA) weight, TA percentage of total body weight, and hindlimb muscle grip strength (Figure S2B and C). However, tranilast appeared not to affect muscle phenotype. Moreover, H&E and WGA staining (Figure S2D) showed a similar pattern in muscle cross-sectional area (CSA). Consistent with previous studies, micro-CT (Fig. 2B and Figure S2E) revealed that WBE significantly increased bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and bone mineral density (BMD) and tranilast reduced the effect: compared with WBE group, the WBE + tranilast group had lower BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, BMD, and bone mineral content (BMC) and higher trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). Biomechanical analysis revealed similar results: WBE enhanced the bone strength parameters, including ultimate stress, maximum load, and elastic modulus, and tranilast impaired the improvement of WBE (Fig. 2C). Similarly, immunofluorescence staining revealed that the WBE rats displayed increased numbers of COL1+ and OCN+ cells, and tranilast counteracted the therapeutic effects (Fig. 2D and E). TRAP staining revealed no significant inter-group differences in either the activity or the number of osteoclasts (Figure S2F–H). To further verify the regulatory role of skeletal muscle in bone metabolism, we designed a contrasting experiment. We established a rat model of muscle atrophy using BoNT/A and treated it with exogenous recombinant protein IL-33, observing the effects (Fig. 2F). Similar to previous results, BoNT/A significantly reduced IL-33 levels in the four tissues, while IL-33 supplementation neutralised this reduction (Figure S3A). BoNT/A significantly decreased muscle strength, mass, and CSA, but IL-33 supplementation had no significant impact on the muscle (Figure S3B–D). Consistent with prior studies, micro-CT showed that BoNT/A significantly reduced bone mass (Fig. 2G and Figure S3E), impaired the biomechanical strength of the femur (Fig. 2H), and decreased osteogenesis-related markers (Fig. 2I and J). After IL-33 supplementation in BoNT/A-treated rats, these negative effects were significantly improved. Consistent with the aforementioned observations, although both BoNT/A and IL-33 modestly altered TRAP expression, the effect did not reach statistical significance (Figure S3F–H). These results indicate that muscle atrophy significantly reduced IL-33 and impaired bone metabolism, but IL-33 supplementation can improve the negative effects caused by muscle atrophy. These experiments together confirm the mediating roles of IL-33 in skeletal muscle-to-bone crosstalk.

Skeletal muscle-derived IL-33 mediates muscle-to-bone crosstalk

Given that exercise and BoNT/A injection may have systemic effects, we aimed to identify the specific tissue source of IL-33. Similar to the aforementioned experiments, we established control, WBE, BoNT/A, and OVX groups. We examined Il33 mRNA levels in several major organs at different time points (3, 7, 30, 60, and 90 days post-modelling), including skeletal muscle (Figure S4A), serum (Figure S4B), bone matrix (Figure S4C), epiphyseal bone marrow (Figure S4D), heart (Figure S4E), and liver (Figure S4F). From day 30, we observed a significant increase in Il33 gene transcription levels in skeletal muscle tissue of the WBE group, while the BoNT/A group showed a significant decrease. No significant differences were found in the other five tissues (Figure S5A). Furthermore, given the established role of adipose tissue in bone-metabolic crosstalk, we quantified IL-33 transcript levels in both brown (BAT) and white (WAT) adipose depots.8 Parallelling the preceding findings, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (Figure S5A). Moreover, OVX did not affect the transcriptional expression of Il33. This led us to hypothesise that IL-33 may be transcribed and released by skeletal muscle tissue, thereby regulating bone metabolism.

To test this hypothesis, we designed adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) vectors based on shRNA-Il33 to specifically knock down Il33 via local injection into skeletal muscle (Fig. 3A). Rats injected with a blank control AAV vector served as the control group (AAV-Sh-NC). In rats injected with the Il33 knockdown vector (AAV-Sh-Il33), IL-33 levels in skeletal muscle were significantly reduced (Fig. 3B and C). WBE + AAV-Sh-NC rats showed a significant increase in IL33 gene transcription levels, whereas the increase was inhibited in WBE + AAV-Sh-Il33 rats (Fig. 3C). The effects of the AAV vectors and WBE on Il33 transcription were not observed in other tissues (Fig. 3C and Figure S5B). Furthermore, analysis of IL-33 in various tissues revealed no differences in Il33 transcriptional expression in bone and serum, but significant changes in protein levels (Fig. 3C), supporting the conclusion that IL-33 originates from skeletal muscle. Immunofluorescence co-staining for laminin (skeletal-muscle outline) and IL-33 revealed that WBE markedly elevated IL-33 signal both intracellularly and extracellularly, whereas AAV-Sh-Il33 almost abolished intramuscular IL-33 expression (Fig. 3B). Analysis of muscle tissue indicated that while WBE improved muscle status, the AAV vector did not affect muscle mass (Figure S5C), muscle strength (Figure S5C), or CSA measured by H&E and WGA staining (Figure S5D). Analysis of bone tissue showed that AAV-Sh-Il33 significantly reduced bone mass (Fig. 3D and Figure S5E), bone strength (Fig. 3E), and osteogenesis indicators and increased osteoclastogenesis activity (Fig. 3F and G). The rats from the WBE groups had a significantly improved bone formation ability compared with the AAV-Sh-NC group and the AAV-Sh-Il33 group, while compared with the WBE + AAV-Sh-Il33 group, this improvement was blocked (Fig. 3D–G and Figure S5E and F). IL-33 promotes CCL5 secretion in CD8+ T cells

The above results confirm that IL-33 is secreted by skeletal muscle tissue, and changes in muscle status regulate the release of IL-33, which in turn regulates bone metabolism. However, the mechanism by which IL-33 regulates bone metabolism remains unclear. Based on an in vitro assay, we aim to explore whether IL-33 can regulate the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. BMSCs were treated with varying concentrations of IL-33. However, Alizarin Red S (ARS) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and RT-qPCR of osteogenic genes confirmed no significant impact of IL-33 on BMSCs (Figure S6F and G).

Given that IL-33 may have no direct effect on BMSCs, we hypothesise that IL-33 regulates bone formation by modulating the osteogenic microenvironment rather than directly regulating bone metabolism. To explore this, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on the epiphyseal bone marrow tissue of control rats and muscle-specific IL-33 knockdown rats to identify changes in their microenvironment (Fig. 5A). After quality control and cell filtration, 69,359 cells were finally collected, and 41 clusters of cells were identified (Figure S7A). According to the marker genes, 9 types of cells were annotated (Fig. 5B and C), including T cell (Cd3d, Cd3e, Cd3g), plasma cell (Mzb1, Jchain, Tnfrsf17, Slamf7), neutrophil (Pglyrp1, Lcn2, Cxcr2, Camp, S100a9, S100a8), monocyte (Cd14, S100a4, Vcan, Ccr2), macrophage (Cd163, Cd68, Cd86), erythrocyte (Gypa, Ahsp, Tfrc, Hba-a1), BMSC (Lepr, Cxcl12, Angpt1), basophil (Ms4a2, Prss34, Prss29, Fcer1a, Cma1, Dnase2b), and B cell (Cd19, Cd79a, Rag1, Cd79b, Ezh2). As the role of IL-33 in T cells has been broadly reported, we focused on T cells. We first explored the differentially expressed genes in T cells (Table S5) and conducted the gene enrichment analysis (Figure S7D). Among the top differentially expressed genes, Ccl5, a secretory protein and cytokine, emerged as the highest-ranked secreted cytokine, capturing our attention (Fig. 5D). We then checked Ccl5 expression in other cells and found that the expression of Ccl5 was poor in most cells except T cells (Fig. 5E). In T cells, Ccl5 expression was significantly lower in the AAV-Sh-Il33 group compared with the control group.

Subsequently, we further re-clustered T cells and 7 subtypes of T cells were identified (Fig. 5F and Figure S7E and F), including Cd8+ memory T cell (Tigit+, Prdm1+, Mbd2+, Cd8a+, Sell−, Lef1−), Cd8+ Mki67+ Tc1 cell (Klrb1a+, Mki67−, Stmn1−, Tbx21+, Eomes+, Cd8a+), Cd8+ Mki67+ Tc1 cell (Klrb1a+, Mki67+, Stmn1+, Tbx21+, Eomes+, Cd8a+), Cd8+ naïve T cell (Tbx21−, Eomes−, Cd8a+), NK cell (Cd3d−, Cd3e−, Cd3g−, Klrb1a+, Gzmb+), Tc9 cell (Il9r+, Irf4+), and Treg (Il2ra+, Ctla4+, Icos+). The rats treated with AAV-Sh-Il33 had a decreased proportion of CD8+ memory T cells (Figure S7G. We next analysed the Ccl5 expression in different T cell subtypes, and the Ccl5 expression in Cd8+ memory T cells and Cd8+ Mki67+ Tc1 cells was significantly different (Fig. 5G and Table S6).

Single-cell sequencing results showed that Ccl5 expression differences mainly originated from CD8+ T cells. Accordingly, we re-clustered the T-cell population into Cd8+ T, Cd4+ T, and NK cell subsets (Figure S8A–D). Within the CD8+ T-cell compartment, Ccl5 emerged as one of the most prominently expressed genes (Fig. 5H), whereas its expression levels in both CD4+ T cells and NK cells remained indistinguishable among groups (Fig. 5I and Table S7). These findings further substantiate the hypothesis that IL-33 may induce a differential alteration in Ccl5 expression specifically within CD8+ T cells. So, we verified this in animal experiments. We isolated CD8+ T cells from rat epiphyseal bone marrow and analysed Ccl5 transcription and protein expression in these cells. AAV-Sh-Il33 and BoBT/A significantly reduced Ccl5 expression in CD8+ T cells, while AAV-Il33 and WBE significantly increased it (Figure S8E and F). To further confirm our findings, we depleted CD8+ T cells in rats using a CD8 antibody.37 Results showed that AAV-Sh-Il33 and AAV-Il33 didn't change the proportion of CD8+ T cells, but the CD8 antibody significantly reduced their number (Fig. 5J and Figure S8G). AAV-Sh-Il33 significantly decreased Ccl5 gene transcription and protein levels in bone marrow tissue. Conversely, AAV-Il33 significantly increased Ccl5 expression in epiphyseal bone marrow, and the anti-CD8 antibody significantly neutralised this overexpression (Fig. 5K and Figure S8H).

Consistent with previous reports indicating that IL-33 modulates CD8+ T-cell function via the ST2 receptor,50 we conducted complementary in vitro experiments to verify this mechanism (Figure S8I). CD8+ T cells were treated with recombinant IL-33 either alone or in combination with a neutralising anti-ST2 antibody; an isotype-matched control IgG was included as a negative control. Quantitative RT-PCR and ELISA analyses revealed that IL-33 significantly up-regulated Ccl5 mRNA expression and increased secreted CCL5 protein levels. Co-incubation with anti-ST2 antibody, but not control IgG, abolished this IL-33-mediated induction. Collectively, these data demonstrate that IL-33 enhances CCL5 expression in CD8+ T cells through engagement of the ST2 receptor.

IL-33 regulates bone metabolism via CD8+ T cell-derived CCL5

Next, we sought to clarify whether IL-33 regulates bone formation via CCL5 release from CD8 T cells (Fig. 6A). We established WBE rat models, administered CD8 antibodies to deplete CD8+ T cells, and supplemented them with sufficient CCL5 protein to counteract the effects of CD8 depletion. CD8 antibodies significantly depleted CD8+ T cells, and extra CCL5 had no significant effect on CD8+ T cell numbers (Figure S9A). Similar to previous results, WBE significantly increased IL-33 levels, with no marked effects from CD8 antibodies or CCL5 (Figure S9B). WBE significantly upregulated Ccl5 expression, and this effect was significantly inhibited by CD8 antibodies (Fig. 6B and C). We then observed the effects of CD8+ T cells and CCL5 on bone metabolism (Fig. 6D–F and Figure S9C and D). As expected, WBE enhanced the ultimate stress, maximum load, stiffness, elastic modulus, energy absorption, BV/TV, Tb.Th, BMD and BMC of femurs and high-regulated osteogenic markers, including COL1 and OCN, but these effects were significantly suppressed after CD8+ T cell blockade, indicating CD8+ T cells are essential for muscle-derived IL-33 regulation of bone metabolism. However, simultaneously depleting CD8+ T cells and supplementing with CCL5 alleviated the inhibition of bone formation by CD8 antibodies, suggesting CCL5 may mediate CD8+ T cell regulation of bone formation downstream. TRAP staining revealed that, although WBE alone did not significantly affect TRAP + cell numbers, anti-CD8 markedly increased them, an effect partially reversed by CCL5 (Figure S9C). Given that the above results have proven that CCL5 mainly comes from CD8+ T cells, we went on to study the role of CCL5 in muscle atrophy-induced bone formation reduction (Fig. 6G). As expected, BoNT/A remarkably reduced Ccl5 transcription and protein levels (Figure S10A and B). Then, we supplemented exogenous CCL5 in BoNT/A rats. CCL5 stimulates bone formation through CCR3

The above studies confirmed that CCL5 from CD8+ T cells is a key factor in IL-33 from skeletal muscle regulating bone formation. As CCL5 acts via multiple receptors like C–C chemokine receptor type 1 (CCR1), C–C chemokine receptor type 3 (CCR3), and C–C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5), we conducted further experiments. We re-analysed scRNA-seq data and used the CellChat package to estimate communication pathways between CD8+ T cells and BMSCs, focussing on CCL5-related pathways (Fig. 7A). CellChat analysis showed that CCL5 interaction strength with CCR1, CCR3, and CCR5 decreased significantly in communications between Cd8+ memory T cells and BMSCs in the AAV-Sh-Il33 group, almost disappearing in the Cd8+ Mki67+ Tc1 cell and Tc9 cell communication with BMSCs. This led us to conduct animal experiments with three drugs: BX471 for CCR1 blockade,38 SB 328437 (SB3) for CCR3 blockade,39 and Maraviroc for CCR5 blockade,40 to test if they could block WBE-induced enhanced bone metabolism (Fig. 7B). As expected, WBE increased CCL5 and IL-33 expression, and the three blockers showed no significant effect on CCL5 and IL-33 (Figure S11A–C). We then analysed the rats' femurs (Figure S11D and E). Biomechanical analysis revealed similar results, with SB3 significantly lowering ultimate stress, maximum load, elastic modulus, and energy absorption in WBE rats (Fig. 7D). Immunofluorescence analysis of COL1 and ONC also showed similar trends (Fig. 7E). Muscle feature analysis indicated that WBE enhanced muscle strength and mass (Figure S11G and H). To extend these observations, we performed a series of in vitro experiments. Exogenous CCL5 markedly enhanced osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, as evidenced by increased osteogenic differentiation gene expression and ARS and ALP staining (Figure S12A–D). Pharmacologic blockade with SB3 or gene knockdown by siCcr3 significantly abolished this CCL5-mediated pro-osteogenic effect. Given that prior studies have linked CCR3 engagement to downstream ERK phosphorylation and that the ERK pathway is critical for osteogenic commitment,51 we hypothesised that CCL5–CCR3 signalling promotes osteogenesis through ERK activation. Western blot analysis confirmed that CCL5 treatment significantly elevated the p-ERK/ERK ratio (Fig. 7E and G), whereas SB3 co-incubation, as well as siCcr3 transfection, prevented this increase. These results indicate that CCL5 may drive BMSC osteogenic differentiation via CCR3-dependent ERK phosphorylation.

We induced postmenopausal osteoporosis in 18-month-old rats via OVX and treated them with exogenous CCL5, IL-33, and AAV-Il33 (Fig. 8A and Figure S8A). Discussion

Numerous studies have documented the communication between muscle and bone. As the two key organs of the musculoskeletal system, skeletal muscle and bone are anatomically adjacent and communicate via endocrine and paracrine pathways, which underpin the association between muscle and bone diseases. Muscle secretes myokines, such as IL-6 and irisin, to modulate muscle-bone crosstalk.8,13 The quantity or activity of myokines varies with muscle conditions. In this study, we observed that following WBE training, muscle strengthening was accompanied by increased IL-33 release and enhanced bone formation. Conversely, in the case of muscle atrophy induced by BONT/A, the release of IL-33 decreased, and bone formation was impaired. When we modulated IL-33 levels using tranilast and exogenous IL-33 protein, the bone metabolic changes caused by muscle condition were eliminated. To clarify the source of IL-33, we used an AAV9 vector to locally regulate IL-33 expression in skeletal muscle and found that altering IL-33 expression solely in skeletal muscle could affect bone metabolism. These findings confirm that IL-33 acts as a myokine, released from muscle in response to changes in muscle condition, such as strengthening or atrophy. It then reaches the bone via the circulatory or paracrine systems to regulate bone metabolism.

However, the role of IL-33 in bone metabolism remains controversial.52 On the one hand, many studies suggest that IL-33 may have adverse effects on bone metabolism. As a pro-inflammatory cytokine, IL-33 may induce inflammatory bone loss. In rheumatoid arthritis, IL-33 may promote inflammation during its early stages due to disordered cytokine release.53 Moreover, IL-33 may activate mast cells, and these activated mast cells may release IL-6 and RANKL, which may upregulate osteoclastogenesis.54 Additionally, studies have demonstrated that IL-33 can activate the CD14 monocyte ST2 receptor, leading to osteoclast differentiation and promoting the expression of osteoclast-related transcription factors.52 Although some studies have indicated that IL-33 promotes osteoclast formation, most of these findings are limited to in vitro experiments. Recent animal studies have increasingly supported the positive role of IL-33 in bone formation. For example, IL-33 knockout in mice has been shown to result in bone loss, which is consistent with the findings of our study.55 Further evidence comes from research demonstrating that IL-33 exerts a protective effect on bone formation. It has been reported that IL-33 can inhibit osteoclast formation through the IL-33-miR-34a-5p-Notch1 pathway.56 Additionally, studies have confirmed that IL-33 is capable of suppressing bone resorption induced by TNF-α.57 Some studies have also indicated that IL-33 may interact with vitamin D to jointly regulate bone metabolism. This crosstalk may also be associated with skin homoeostasis and PTH. Vitamin D, together with PTH, is essential for physiological bone turnover and also performs important immunoregulatory functions.58 For the roles of IL-33 in osteoblasts, a study based on ageing mice with bone loss suggested that IL-33 treatment combated d-galactose-induced ageing-related bone loss by inhibiting Th17 cell differentiation and encouraging the development of IL-10-producing T-regulatory cells.59

In our study, we found no evidence that IL-33 directly regulates BMSC osteogenic differentiation. Instead, we discovered a pathway involving CD8+ T cells by using scRNA-seq. IL-33 doesn't alter the number of CD8+ T cells, but it promotes the release of CCL5 from them. Subsequently, CCL5 acts to enhance osteogenic differentiation. In rat models of skeletal muscle-induced enhanced and suppressed bone formation, the transcription of Ccl5 in CD8+ T cells showed marked changes. When CD8+ T cells were depleted using a CD8 antibody, the exercise-induced upregulation of Ccl5 was significantly attenuated, and bone metabolism parallelled these changes in CCL5 levels. Many studies have reported the role of IL-33 in CD8+ T cells, including its involvement in asthma, tumour immunity, infections, and autoimmune diseases.60, 61, 62, 63 However, few studies have explored the role of IL-33 in CD8+ T cells regarding bone metabolism. This represents one of the innovations of our study.

In our study, we used drugs to block the three common receptors of CCL5: CCR1, CCR3, and CCR5, to determine which receptor is crucial for CCL5's function. Ultimately, we confirmed that CCL5 from CD8+ T cells regulates the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by binding to CCR3. The role of CCL5 in bone metabolism has been reported. Many in vitro studies indicate that CCL5 enhances osteoclast activity.64 However, previous animal experimental results seem to contradict this. Ccl5-deficient mouse models exhibited impaired bone formation, increased osteoclastogenesis, and a deficiency in bone macrophages.65 Some in vitro studies confirmed that CCL5 promotes osteogenic differentiation of human mesenchymal stem cells by binding to CCR1.66 Other research has shown that CCL5 can promote osteogenic differentiation by inducing M2 macrophage polarisation.67 Recently, it has also been found that CCL5 expression facilitates BMSC migration during bone regeneration.68 In our study, through detailed animal experiments, we further clarified the important knowledge of CCL5 in bone metabolism: the role of the CCR3 receptor in CCL5-regulated bone metabolism.

Limitation

Our investigation is constrained by several limitations. First, the sample size for examining the relationships between IL-33 levels and sarcopenia and osteoporosis was small due to sample acquisition difficulties, but it met the minimum requirement for achieving effective statistical power. Second, our AAV vector with a muscle-specific promoter failed to achieve muscle-specific IL-33 regulation due to AAV vector limitations, prompting us to adopt localised injection as an alternative. RT-qPCR validated that this approach effectively regulated specific IL-33 expression in skeletal muscle. Third, we only resolved CCL5-releasing T cells at the CD8 level, but the scRNA-seq suggested that IL-33's regulation of T cell subtypes may be more complex and warrants deeper exploration. Fourth, our study concentrated on CCL5's impact on osteogenic differentiation while largely overlooking its effects on osteoclastic differentiation, an area ripe for future investigation. Fifth, the cohort included in this study was almost exclusively Han Chinese; therefore, findings may not generalise to China's ethnic minorities, and future studies should adopt inclusive recruitment strategies. Sixth, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) was used in this study for the muscle mass measurement, and BIA yields wider limits of agreement than dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) for quantifying appendicular skeletal muscle mass. Moreover, although the SD rat model allows rigorous manipulation of genetic and environmental variables, interspecific differences may limit direct extrapolation of our findings to human physiology. Finally, the cellular origin of IL-33 remains uncertain. Future studies should focus on pinpointing the specific cellular sources of IL-33 to solidify our evidence chain.
