Value-oriented leadership among nurse managers in multicultural healthcare: a phenomenological study
Authors: Nojoud Abdullah Alrashidi, Maha Sanat Alreshidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study explores how internalized ethical and spiritual value systems shape clinical leadership, addressing a critical research gap in the management of multicultural healthcare settings.MethodsUtilizing Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), we conducted semi-structured interviews with 15 nurse managers between January and February 2026. The study focused on the lived experiences of leaders who integrate personal belief systems into their administrative roles.ResultsFive overarching themes emerged: Leadership as a Transpersonal Calling; Ethical Governance Rooted in Internalized Values (Integrity and Moral Consistency); Humanistic Dimensions of Care and Leadership (Proactive Empathy); Negotiating Professional and Personal Identity (Harmonizing Role Expectations with Personal Beliefs), and; Resilience through Mentorship (Seeking Reflective Guidance).ConclusionNurse managers perceive leadership as a transpersonal calling, emphasizing service-oriented morality as a core professional virtue. While integrating personal values with professional expectations poses unique challenges, participants demonstrated a high degree of balance through strategic reflection and peer support. This underscores the necessity for culturally sensitive leadership training, organizational support for diverse belief systems, and structured mentorship programs. Acknowledging the role of internalized values in leadership is crucial for fostering inclusive healthcare environments and developing globally relevant leadership curricula.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04820-8.Keywords: Leadership, Nursing, Religious beliefs, Phenomenological, Nurse managers

Introduction

Healthcare leaders today emphasize ethical intuition as a means for them to command the trust and respect of their subordinates and peers [1]. A leaders’ spirituality, emotional intelligence, and values positively impact clinical leadership and the administrative decision-making process [2]. This is especially true in ethno culturally diverse societies, as it reflects the integration of the workplace and the personal beliefs of the healthcare provider. While social responsibility [3], social empathy, and social altruism [4] are among the most dominant leading values, a values-centered paradigm is balanced in the sense that it cultivates and promotes responsible leadership and ethical caring. Evidence indicates that value-based leadership is responsible for the organization’s positive and cooperative citizenship behavior, which is positively correlated with the clinical staff’s job satisfaction and professional loyalty [5].

The practice of integrating cultural values within health management systems is an underexplored area, especially when considering the importance of cultural ethics within academia and the profession. There have been recent advancements in the academy that focus on aligning clinician-patient engagements based on the principles of culturally-congruent care [5]. Literature on nursing practice suggests that to provide spiritually caring interventions, health professionals need to comprehend the patients’ understanding of life, its purpose, and the values and beliefs—including religion—that are central to their spiritual well-being. A clinician’s moral (or ethical) sensitivity is said to make her/him more cognizant of the extra-role, holistic requirements of patients and their families [6]. There is a considerable shortfall in literature on the culturally-based spiritual aspects of interprofessional leadership and the management of diversity in international healthcare systems. While this research is focused on Saudi Muslim nurse managers in terms of a single cultural group, the workplace environments they manage employ bedside nursing staff from multiple nationalities and religious backgrounds, constituting a multicultural healthcare setting. Yet, it is this very understanding that builds the foundation of effective, inclusive leadership to serve the needs of a culturally diverse society.

One of the core aspects of value-oriented people management involves modeling ethics as a means of inspiring staff [7]. Modeling ethics is a means to inspire trust and integrity which is needed to increase thriving in healthcare organizations [8]. Although prior studies attempt to grapple with faith-oriented values in business ethics [9], such studies tend to be mono-religious in their focus [10]. There is little research looking at these internal value orientations in the multiculturally clinical contexts of varying belief systems. While the framework of leadership styles encompassing spiritual values is associated with increased commitment and job satisfaction [11], the communicative mechanisms are not clearly articulated. Additionally, while the literature identifies ethical leadership as important, a greater understanding is needed of the ways in which the cultural and spiritual dimensions of identity are woven into the management practices that enhance staff recruitment and retention [12, 13].

Drawing on the perspective of clinical managers who amalgamate universal and culturally-based spiritual principles in their management, this research aims to offer the healthcare management literature much needed contributions. The research aims to clarify ties between internalized faith as the dynamics of leadership and the lived realities of healthcare managers as phenomenon. This research, by looking at the lived experiences of the healthcare managers, aims to contribute to knowledge of the synergy between personal belief systems and professional practice and its impact on the organizational outcomes and social systems of healthcare delivery. Hence, this study seeks to investigate the impact of culturally-based spiritual values on healthcare leadership.

Methods

Design

The synthesis of the leaders’ behavior and decision-making, from values perspective, was explored using interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA). This study was designed and reported in accordance with the COREQ guidelines (see Supplementary File) for qualitative research. Key reporting elements are integrated throughout the methods and results sections to ensure transparency and rigor. This framework is meant to assist researchers in organizing and thoroughly covering all essential components of the research design, research instruments, data collection and analysis methods, and findings, thereby elevating the research quality.

Participants /sampling

There were 15 Nurse Managers at King Khalid Hospital and Hail General Hospital who participated in this study. The nurse managers consisted of Head Nurses, Directors of Nursing, Supervisors, and Charge Nurses. Purposive sampling was used. Participants were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: (a). They had to hold managerial or leadership positions—including Head Nurse, Director of Nursing, Supervisor, or Charge Nurse—within a department/unit of the hospital for at least six months; (b) They had to agree to participate in the study. The study was conducted in the Saudi public healthcare system, where nursing leadership positions are predominantly held by Saudi nationals, while bedside nursing staff represent diverse nationalities and religious backgrounds. This structural context shaped participants’ experiences of value-based leadership in multicultural settings. Participants were contacted via invitation letters sent once ethical review board approval and permission from participating hospital directors were obtained.

Setting

The study was conducted at King Khalid Hospital and Hail General Hospital in Hail City. Both of these hospitals are considered to be large hospitals in Hail City and have a bed capacity of over 250 beds. While the leadership positions are predominantly held by Saudi nationals, bedside nursing staff represent diverse nationalities and religious backgrounds. This structural context shaped participants’ experiences of value-based leadership in a multicultural work environment, despite the demographic homogeneity of the leadership sample itself.

Data collection

Following approval from the institutional review board and participating hospital directors, researchers distributed invitation letters to a purposive sample of potential participants, clearly outlining the study’s objectives. While 16 nurse managers initially expressed interest in the study, one individual subsequently declined, resulting in a final sample of 15 participants (N = 15). This sample size aligns with IPA methodological conventions, which recommend purposively selected samples of 6–15 participants to ensure sufficient idiographic depth for individual case analysis while enabling meaningful cross-case pattern identification (Smith, Flowers & Larkin, 2022).

Data collection involved semi-structured, individualized interviews conducted via Zoom teleconferencing software between January and February 2026. Each session lasted 50–60 min. To ensure privacy and participant comfort, interviews were scheduled during evening hours while participants were at home, with only the participant and interviewer present. All sessions were audio-recorded with written informed consent. Although interviews were conducted remotely, researchers maintained brief field notes immediately following each session to capture contextual observations, interviewer reflections, and non-verbal cues communicated through the video interface (e.g., emotional tone, pauses, visible gestures).

The researchers utilized a semi-structured approach including an interview guide that contained open-ended questions and probes. An example probe was, “How do you incorporate values into your practice of leadership? What is the influence of incorporating values into your practice of leadership on leadership efficacy and organization-wide outcomes?” Additional probing questions, such as “Can you expand upon your previous answer or can you give me an example?” were asked to promote additional responses. The interview guide was pilot-tested with 5 nurse managers that did not become part of the final study sample. Data collection concluded when the researcher(s) determined that saturation had been achieved, at which time interviews concluded. Repeat interviews were not conducted with individual participants.

Reflexivity

The researchers explicitly acknowledged their interpretive roles as a component of IPA double hermeneutics throughout all phases of the study. Both co-principal investigators (NAA and MSA) are Saudi female nursing educators who hold PhDs and serve as university administrators. Their extensive experience in academic leadership helped establish strong professional rapport with the participants and provided deep insight into the institutional context of nursing leadership.

To facilitate bracketing of preconceived notions and reduce potential interpretive bias, each researcher maintained a personal reflexive journal. In these journals, they documented assumptions, emotional responses, and analytical decision-making activities. This practice began during the initial interviews and continued until the final themes were consolidated. Furthermore, NAA and MSA held weekly peer debriefing meetings to critically examine emergent interpretations.

To introduce alternative analytical perspectives, two external research assistants without a clinical nursing background were incorporated into the project. Finally, to ensure transparency, participants were provided with detailed information regarding the researchers’ educational backgrounds and administrative experiences at the time of recruitment.

Ensuring research rigor

This research used member checking to improve the validity of this study. Member checking required participants to assess their own interview data for both accuracy and resonance. Additionally, participants were able to listen to and/or review their recorded interviews, provide additional context to their descriptive responses regarding the applicability of internally integrated values/principles to their leadership positions. The first stage of member check occurred immediately after transcription. All 15 participants received their verbatim transcript via secure e-mail within two weeks of conducting their interviews. Thirteen participants (87%) reviewed their transcript for accuracy and verified its accuracy. The other two participants (13%) made slight changes to the timestamp and one participant had some clarification regarding a single translated Arabic term. The second stage of member check occurred approximately four months later when the thematic structure developed from the data was sent to all participants along with specific quotes from the narrative. Twelve participants (80%) replied indicating that they felt the thematic structure captured their experience. Each of the twelve participants indicated that each theme was an accurate representation of their experience. Three participants added context (i.e. specified what month of Ramadan it was during fasting when reporting challenges related to fasting). These three participants’ comments were included in the narrative as part of the final version. None of the participants asked to have data removed from this study nor did any participant dispute the interpretation used to develop the study’s findings. A qualitative external audit was performed to establish dependability by examining whether the data appropriately supported the findings, analysis and conclusion. This provided sufficient credibility that the phenomenological results were based upon sufficient data. The researchers established comprehensive audit trails to illustrate, in a logical fashion, the reasoning behind the data collection, analysis, and interpretation.

Data collection and analysis

Data analysis followed the principles of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) to understand how clinical leaders made sense of their unique professional and personal experiences. Following verbatim transcript development, data analysis progressed systematically through four phases: (1) immersive reading and re-reading of transcripts, (2) exploratory note-taking and commenting, (3) identification of emergent themes, and (4) cross-case analysis to determine overarching patterns and categories.

To enhance trustworthiness and rigor, four coders (NAA, MSA, and two trained research assistants) independently reviewed all interview transcripts using this IPA protocol. Disagreement resolution proceeded in three stages. First, the coding team met biweekly to discuss emerging themes and resolve discrepancies through consensus dialogue, continuously returning to the original transcripts to ground decisions in the participants’ own words. Second, when consensus could not be reached, an independent external reviewer adjudicated the discrepancy. Third, the final thematic structure was validated through member checking with participants and an external qualitative audit by a senior researcher not involved in the primary data collection. NVivo software (QSR International Pty Ltd., Burlington, MA, USA) supported data management and coding efficiency, and all interpretative decisions were meticulously documented in an audit trail.

Ethical consideration

Study protocols were formally submitted and approved by Local Health Cluster of Hail, with Ethics Approval [2025-85]. All participants provided written informed consent prior to beginning the study, having been thoroughly briefed regarding study objectives, procedures, potential hazards, and their right to discontinue participation at any time. To maintain complete anonymity and confidentiality for all participants, researchers assured complete de-identification of data collected. Only after receiving formal consent did the researcher conduct interviews.

Theme 1: Leadership as a transpersonal calling

This theme delves into the participants’ value systems and how they intertwine them with their leadership identity. Rather than focusing on the management role, the aim is to assess how participants achieve overall personal and professional development. For these health care managers, leadership is not simply an exercise of authority, but, more importantly, an ethical and transformative role that advocates for a meaningful professional vocation.

Subtheme 1.1: Altruistic servanthood as a primary calling

Stressing their management roles as stewardship or professional calling indicates a responsibility to serve others beyond a job description. Managing within the clinic is also seen as a service and ethical commitment.

Participants 1 and 3 expressed strong views on professional accountability. They utilized the concept of “stewardship” (trusteeship), viewing leadership as a privilege to be honored rather than a set of uninspired job requirements. This suggests a deep-seated belief that serving others is a vocation where they act as guardians of the welfare of those within their clinical care and supervision.

I believe that internal values and professional dedication to subordinates inspire staff to do their best in all they do. (P1)

My calling in this profession reminds me that in my role as a leader, there are people around me whom I can help… and I should bear in mind that I must use these ethical beliefs at all times. (P3)

Several participants provided evidence that their personal belief systems redefined the very essence of their leadership. By focusing on “encouraging and directing others toward excellence,” they highlighted a leadership construct centered on the elevation of staff morale and the practice of professional morality. Participants 4 and 6 noted that their leadership was shaped by principles of equity, fairness, and a willingness to serve.

In my role as a manager, I relate my leadership style to my internal values. I was taught that these principles shape someone who is willing to serve others… someone who is equitable, fair, and capable of leading based on core ethics. (P4)

A leader is not someone who is just in a position of authority. A true leader inspires and brings people together toward a common goal. (P15)

Theme 2. Ethical governance rooted in internalized values

This theme reflects the belief that leading organizations entails much more than simply implementing organization-wide policies or procedures, and that these policies reflect the moral values embedded in the health care industry. As such, for many of these participants, ethics are not something external to their practice but rather part of what they do, and part of who they are when interacting with patients and other clinicians.

Subtheme 2.1. Integrity and moral consistency

This subtheme explores how healthcare managers identify and articulate basic universal morals such as honesty, fairness, among others which serve as the foundation of their professional practice. In particular, this subtheme identifies how these morals function as an ethical “compass” guiding other aspects of their behavior and interactions particularly those related to ethics.

P2 and P4 spoke on the value of utilizing an individual’s own internal ethical “map” to inform their actions and decisions in their role(s). These two participants further stated that in doing so they incorporate their personal convictions and values into their daily actions as a way to promote high levels of professionalism through their role(s).

Consistently attempt to act upon my core beliefs from an ethical standpoint in order to use them as a guide as a leader. (P15)

P2 discussed the importance of being truthful with oneself, as well as the need to maintain Integrity throughout the different components of a multi-disciplinary team. Some examples of behaviors that represented moral anchors in performing the duties of a leader included keeping one’s word and treating every person with respect.

…in addition to possibly being a bit more honest with myself, given that I believe based on my faith, I must make sure that I am completely fair and totally trustworthy in every aspect of my life. Examples would include respecting everyone present during meetings regardless of their nationality. (P2)

P7 was able to link personal Integrity to team motivation. From this perspective, ethical leadership goes far beyond developing positive relationships with others; it is also about fostering an environment that is just and welcoming. Through acting honestly and kindly, the leader creates an open climate that allows staff to perform to the best of their ability.

…the condition I have prior to making any judgments relative to ethics is that I must personally exhibit Integrity before I can ethically judge another. We must lead with kindness and Integrity if we want our team motivated enough to improve themselves professionally (P7)

Theme 3: Humanistic Dimensions of Care and Leadership

This theme analyzes what internal value systems and altruistic motivations say about the clinical managerial leadership style. It describes the role of professional conduct, and more so, the leading role of compassion and empathy, in explaining how these leaders, due to their inner convictions, embrace and focus on the entire human needs of patients and personnel.

Subtheme 3.1: Proactive support and empathetic engagement

This subtheme examines the concrete steps health care managers take to meet the needs of their teams and describe the shift from the more philosophical idea of empathy to the more operationalized empathetic leadership.

Most participants observed that building, and maintaining, a psychologically safe, and collaborative, environment is an important, if not critical, factor contributory to leader compassion, and indeed to all members of the group, regardless of role.

I try to ensure that the work atmosphere is friendly and supportive. (P4)

The reflection of P4 suggest that empathy is not just a professional requirement but a fundamental personal conviction. The emphasis on understanding the interests and feelings of team members demonstrates a commitment to human-centered workplace relations. By prioritizing the needs of others, these leaders align their management style with universal principles of altruism.

When faced with interpersonal friction within their teams, participants relied on empathetic listening and non-judgmental mediation. Participant 6 described the emotional weight of managing such conflicts, highlighting a preference for “listening more than talking” to reach a resolution rooted in understanding and fairness.

After dealing with conflicts on many occasions… I was forced to implement resolutions within my team. (P4)

…In the beginning, I felt very upset that there was a difference of opinion between these two colleagues. I wanted to, without judgment, hear their sides of the story, one at a time. (P6)

The phrase “I realized and felt” underscores the emotional labor involved in leadership. Rather than adopting a detached administrative stance, these managers experience the distress of their team members. Their response—characterized by patience and a search for mutual understanding—reflects an internal ethical framework that values harmony and the restoration of professional relationships over punitive measures.

Theme 4: Negotiating professional and personal identity

This theme recognizes the inherent tensions and challenges experienced by clinical leaders when integrating personal ethical and spiritual frameworks within a high-pressure, modern healthcare environment. It explores the reflective process of harmonizing individual belief systems with the rigorous professional expectations and fast-paced nature of clinical management.

Subtheme 4.1: Harmonizing role expectations with personal beliefs

This subtheme highlights the experiences of healthcare managers as they navigate the practicalities of observing personal rituals and commitments while meeting the intensive demands of their professional roles. It examines the impact of these commitments on time, cognitive resources, and physical stamina within the workplace.

Participants expressed that the effort to reconcile personal commitments with institutional role expectations can be a source of professional pressure. For instance, during periods of significant personal or religious observance, such as fasting, managers noted the potential for physical fatigue to impact their concentration levels.

…maybe part of the pressure, yes, it has been for me. Especially the part of having to reconcile personal commitments with role expectations. For example, during periods of fasting, it can be draining and impact one’s concentration. (P9)

The data captures a struggle to maintain a high level of professional focus while adhering to an internal value system. These accounts reflect the lived reality of how a leader’s belief system must sometimes be managed alongside the physical and mental requirements of a demanding career.

Participants highlighted the difficulty of maintaining spiritual or reflective routines within an “ever-changing and demanding” healthcare landscape. The narrative suggests that the traditional structures of personal reflection or prayer can be challenged by the “modern corporate working environment” and the constant presence of clinical conflict and administrative tasks.

It is true that the healthcare environment is constantly evolving. There is always conflict, and I have had to deal with that. (P10)

…at times, dealing with those demands… it seems that my personal routine is affected, especially when the unit is busy. (P8)

Theme 5: Resilience through mentorship and peer support systems

This theme explores how clinical managers integrate their personal belief systems with professional activities through strategic collaboration and mentorship. It highlights the synergy between internal ethical ideals and external administrative diplomacy, illustrating how healthcare leaders form a cohesive professional identity where personal values and organizational goals coexist. The integration of these five dimensions — transpersonal calling, internalized ethical governance, humanistic care, identity negotiation, and mentorship-based resilience — aligns with transformational leadership theory, wherein leaders inspire followers toward higher moral purpose and collective achievement.

Subtheme 5.1: Seeking mentorship and reflective guidance

This subtheme focuses on the role of professional and personal support networks in the successful integration of value-based leadership. It demonstrates how seeking advice and peer support assists managers in overcoming complex organizational tensions, ultimately fostering resilience and a clear professional vision.

Participants developed various strategies to reduce the perceived friction between personal commitments and job requirements. Proactive time management and fostering a culture of mutual understanding among co-workers were cited as essential methods for maintaining operational efficiency.

As a solution to these issues, I have structured my approach. For instance, mapping out my calendar beforehand and focusing on pressing issues… informing my colleagues about my planned personal leave or observances beforehand. (P3)

Experienced mentors and respected elders served as critical guides in the “self-improvement odyssey” of these leaders. Engaging with these support systems enabled participants to better understand their own strengths, limitations, and core values. This continuous development of skills and instincts over time translated into elevated self-awareness and a positive correlation between personal faith and leadership efficacy.

Participant 6 highlighted the importance of informal mentorship networks in social gatherings for discussing leadership challenges. These safe spaces for reflection allowed managers to gain insights that they could not always find within formal institutional structures.

I work on self-development alongside other managers. When facing these problems, I consult my mentors in social gatherings to gain perspective. (P6)

Being surrounded by a supportive community of peers and “elders” within the hospital empowered participants to take responsibility for their actions and speak with confidence. This collective wisdom was particularly valued during times of high stress, providing a sense of purpose and helping to refine decision-making processes.

Being surrounded by such supportive people made me fearless when it came to speaking out and taking responsibility for my actions; it gave me a purpose during challenging times. (P9)

I have been looking to my elders in the hospital and asking for ways to maintain composure, especially when I am stressed. I am grateful for their advice, which helps me control my decision-making. (P14)

Beyond thematic analysis, we conducted a narrative synthesis to examine how participants constructed professional identity through their leadership stories. This analysis revealed an existential core — termed the ‘narrative center’ — through which managers integrated personal spirituality with professional demands. The integration of these five dimensions — transpersonal calling, internalized ethical governance, humanistic care, identity negotiation, and mentorship-based resilience — aligns with transformational leadership theory, wherein leaders inspire followers toward higher moral purpose and collective achievement.

Discussion

Theoretical integration of value-oriented leadership

The five emergent themes from this IPA study align with established evidence-based leadership frameworks, suggesting that value-oriented leadership among Saudi nurse managers represents a culturally embedded synthesis of multiple complementary styles rather than a singular approach. Theme 1 (Leadership as a Transpersonal Calling) reflects the core tenets of servant leadership, wherein leaders view their role as stewardship and prioritize the growth and well-being of followers [14, 15]. Theme 2 (Ethical Governance Rooted in Internalized Values) corresponds to ethical leadership, characterized by moral personhood and moral management through integrity, fairness, and transparency [16]. Theme 3 (Humanistic Dimensions of Care and Leadership) embodies compassionate leadership, which operationalizes empathy into proactive support and conflict resolution to address staff well-being [17]. Theme 4 (Negotiating Professional and Personal Identity) resonates with authentic leadership, wherein leaders maintain consistency between internal values and external role demands despite contextual pressures [18]. Finally, Theme 5 (Resilience through Mentorship) integrates transformational leadership components, wherein leaders develop followers through inspirational motivation, individualized consideration, and intellectual stimulation [19]. Our findings further align with the concept of an adaptive values approach, which posits that personal values and contextual adaptation are dynamically integrated into leadership practices [20]. This perspective illuminates how participants in our study did not merely apply static ethical principles, but continuously negotiated between their internalized spiritual values and the demands of multicultural healthcare environments. This convergence demonstrates that internalized spiritual and ethical values serve as the integrative mechanism binding these leadership styles into a coherent, culturally situated practice.

In this study, we examined how an internalized framework for ethics shapes health care leadership. Leaders with a spiritual view of leadership believe that leading a team of diverse professionals requires both technical knowledge and a transformational process of the psyche. When a leader’s own internalized morality and authenticity are embedded within their leadership role, they have a greater opportunity to consistently apply core professional values. When leaders develop this type of alignment, it will create a positive work environment that supports and encourages the leader’s continued growth as a person and a professional. When a leader’s internalized moral perspective and authenticity are fully integrated into their role, the leader has the potential to be able to integrate (practice) his or her professional values on a regular basis. Additionally, through this integration the leader is able to continue developing holistically both personally and professionally [21]. These health care managers see their power not simply as a way to control others but as a chance to be morally refined through what may be referred to as “stewardship” [22].

The theme ‘Leadership as a Transpersonal Calling’ indicates that as leaders they are being called to a type of stewardship. This stewardship is characterized by professional integrity and moral obligation [22]. Viewing Leadership as a Transpersonal Calling implies that there is a call to stewardship characterized by moral obligation. Evidence from the Saudi public healthcare sector provides support for this, demonstrating ethical leadership — when enhanced by organizational support — has a significant positive effect on employee performance and also meets the diverse needs of clinical staff [23]. When health care managers view their leadership role as a call to something greater than just doing their job, they will use servant-leadership behaviors. They consistently place the interests of those they lead and the organization first [5]. Spiritual leadership creates a culture of spiritual values in the workplace where employees are able to develop their career callings and grow emotionally intelligent. This creates an environment for employers to reduce the amount of burnout that occurs among employees. Thus, reducing the amount of stress or psychological pressure employees experience as they intend to leave their jobs at a higher rate [24].

Ethical governance and the internalized compass

Internalized ethics (the manager’s own personal code) developed from a manager’s experiences in the health care environment are not just about compliance with rules and regulations. Instead, it is about the internalizing of those ethical principles which guide day-to-day leadership and decision making [25]. The managers interviewed in this research did not see values like fairness, compassion or accountability as abstracts; but instead, these were the very things that dictated how they treated their patients, employees and other professionals. These same values also helped create the types of roles in which advocating for equity, creating teams and promoting ethical behavior would be common characteristics.

“The Integrity as an Internal Compass” sub-theme defines how managers use their internally held values, such as integrity, honesty, and openness to develop their professional identities. The literature suggests that there are examples of ethical leaders in health care that extend beyond formal position designations to include many informal clinical leaders who demonstrate some level of adherence to certain moral codes [26]. Developing these competencies may require formal education/training programs designed to address specific ethical dilemmas. Managers who act ethically build employee motivation, organizational commitment, through building positive working relationships based on mutual respect [27].

The Humanistic Dimensions of Care and Leadership: compassion and empathy

The findings reveal that leadership styles are significantly affected by the virtues of compassion and empathy (that are core to the participants’ internally held beliefs). Recent empirical evidence indicates that a growing body of literature supports the contention that, as a result of the internal virtues of empathy and compassion, leadership has been increasingly influenced. Evidence supports the idea that the empathetically motivated compassionate leadership model will be the best way to address system-wide issues. This includes factors such as healthcare professional burn-out and patient safety through their synthesis of health-care professionals’ experiences [28]. These culturally anchored values will influence how we see the very base elements of our leadership as malleable, adaptable, and responsive. Personal belief combined with professionalism will lead to service provision characterized by being kind.

The sub-theme “Proactive Support and Empathetic Engagement” demonstrates that leaders will commit themselves to understanding other peoples’ emotions based upon their ability to demonstrate emotional intelligence. The commitment to emotional intelligence through empathy is essential for ethical leadership. It indicates therefore that a “commitment” to such can be made through “intentional efforts,” including perspective taking. Perspective taking will allow leaders to navigate the work environment. It will also provide opportunities for employees to feel as though they are being heard and understood; this ultimately fosters an atmosphere of trust among colleagues [29]. The practice of deliberate interpersonal training creates a workplace of empathetic relationships. For ethical leaders to provide ethically grounded care, this means to consider all aspects of a patients’ religious and cultural beliefs. This includes aspects such as the gender of their healthcare providers or if they require modesty. If a patient’s concerns are ignored, this may have negative impacts on their health status. It can also create additional disparities in care delivery for minority groups [30]. Empathy may be protective for informal caregivers as well as professionals.

Negotiating professional and personal identity

This theme reflects the struggle healthcare managers face in reconciling their internal values with the demands of a secularized healthcare workplace. Much of the literature indicates that managers report conflicting views as they attempt to align personal religious systems with institutional environments [31].

In this study, the “narrative center” of the participants—often defined by altruistic servanthood—acts as the primary mechanism for resolving this tension. By anchoring their professional identity in this spiritual core, managers are able to transform secular administrative tasks into acts of “transpersonal calling.” This existential synthesis allows them to navigate the professional demands of a multicultural workplace not as a compromise of their values, but as an expression of them [32]. When organizations encourage leaders to integrate these spiritual values, they enable a transformation that reduces managerial stress and stabilizes the “mixed environment” produced by globalization and migration [33].

Interconnectivity of values and peer support

In essence, healthcare managers occupy a “third space,” which means balancing the realities of their faith with the demands of their work [34]. The findings suggest that when a manager identifies their “narrative center,” they are better equipped to utilize peer support and reflective guidance as external resources for internal hardy resilience. Those who find ways to apply spiritual principles to their leadership practices gain a significant advantage; having both internal anchors (the “self”) and external resources (mentoring and peer support) aids in coping with the stresses associated with high-level management [35, 36].

To prepare emerging nurse leaders to respond positively to organizational problems, it is necessary for spirituality to be included within nursing education. Research has found a positive relationship between spirituality and servant leadership characteristics [37]. By fostering a “narrative center” during clinical training, educators can help nurses maintain their moral consistency as they transition into advanced practice roles. These findings highlight the need for a cross-cultural leadership model that acknowledges the diverse, holistic backgrounds of all healthcare professionals.

Implications for nursing practice and policies

As an important part of understanding the educational, practice and policy implications of the findings of these studies, it is necessary to identify them. The first implication for practice, training and policy in nursing is to revise the current curriculum for nursing students so that courses can be developed to emphasize the role of faith in nursing leadership. Examples of course content would include: (a) ethical decision-making frameworks grounded in Islamic nursing ethics, (b) cross-cultural spiritual care competencies addressing diverse religious traditions commonly encountered in Saudi healthcare settings, including Christian, Hindu, and Buddhist patient care protocols; and (c) competency planning for navigating value conflicts between personal faith commitments and secular institutional policies. A second area that requires urgent attention is for health care organizations to develop culturally sensitive programs that meet the religious and cultural needs of populations (the faith/religion of the nurse manager).

There are several possible ways that such programs could be developed including creating policies that promote employee fitness/sports, providing flexible scheduling to accommodate prayer time(s) and establishing minimal uniform/dress code expectations. The third implication for practice, training and policy in nursing is related to the fact that there appears to be an absence of adequate mentoring by experienced leaders who understand the internal conflict between maintaining one’s personal faith and adhering to professional responsibilities. This perceived lack of guidance may result in the loss of valuable insight and “professional street smarts” for nurse managers. Lastly, although numerous studies have identified a need for further research regarding the impact of combining faith and practice as a leader, very few researchers have investigated or attempted to study this phenomenon. As a result, practitioners generally continue to ignore some of the most critical concerns facing registered nurses, specifically their own nursing tradition and beliefs. The registered nurses working in the Western world are caught between adhering to both western nursing belief systems and attitudes, and their own nursing beliefs. Additionally, there is a remembrance that lived experiences of nurse managers remain unexplored. These factors collectively demonstrate a significant need for further qualitative in depth studies.

Study limitations

The current research offers an in-depth understanding of the lived experiences of healthcare leaders. However, there were many limitations to the research.

Firstly, because the researchers used purposive sampling based on a qualitative method with a very small group of 15 participants. Although this allows the researcher to provide the detailed descriptions necessary for phenomenology, this makes it difficult to generalize the statistical results of the study to the global population of healthcare managers. Secondly, the research was conducted in one specific region of a health care system in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, while some of the findings may reflect similar sociocultural influences found internationally, they will likely also include some unique cultural and geographic influences which would limit transferability of the results.

The third is relying solely on participant self-reporting using interview techniques can lead to social desirability bias. Also, a cross-sectional design does not allow for insight into how the value-based leadership style may evolve over time in response to evolving clinical demands. A longitudinal design combining observational data (triangulation) would provide more insight into this evolution.

Accounting for these limitations is important when considering the implications from the study. In future studies, it is recommended to increase the size and diversity of samples in multiple healthcare systems and to develop methods that combine longitudinal observation and quantifiable measures to both validate and extend these qualitative results.

Fourthly, the research did not account for organizational level factors. For example, what are the available resources at each institution? Are there differences in levels of support for culturally anchored leadership styles among various departments? Such organizational level factors need to be accounted for if management is going to adopt value systems internally.

Conclusion

This research explored how the work lives of healthcare administration managers in Saudi Arabia reflect their beliefs in and application of internally-held ethics and spirituality when they lead. Research participants viewed their leadership role as one of service. They believed that being an effective manager required adherence to a set of principles that included altruism, compassion, and doing what was morally right. Healthcare administrators with such a view, therefore, have a strong orientation toward helping others through acts of compassion and empathy; this guides both their interactions with other professionals and their clinical decisions.

Further, the researchers found that managers who adhere to the above mentioned values (a) manage to balance the pressures associated with practicing medicine today while maintaining an allegiance to their internal ethic and (b) do so by using pro-actively planned methods to support them. Examples of such pro-active methods include obtaining guidance/mentoring from established colleagues, regularly assessing themselves on performance issues, and developing groups of people around whom they can provide emotional support.

The findings suggest that value-integration processes resonate with transformational leadership theory, offering a potential framework for understanding how leaders’ internalized values shape professional communication. This connection between personal values and leadership practice may help inspire clinical staff toward higher levels of job satisfaction, organizational commitment, and compassionate patient care.
