A rare case of lesser omental hernia in a male patient following total colectomy
Authors: Yoshihito Abe, Yukiharu Hiyoshi, Mayuko Ouchi, Katsuhiro Ogawa, Yuji Miyamoto, Masaaki Iwatsuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:A lesser omental hernia is a rare internal hernia in which intestinal loops become entrapped through a defect in the lesser omentum, potentially causing bowel obstruction. Its diagnosis is challenging because of nonspecific symptoms that resemble those of other gastrointestinal diseases. This report presents a case of lesser omental hernia following total colectomy.Case presentation:A 65-year-old male with a history of laparoscopic total colectomy for ulcerative colitis 4 years prior presented with acute abdominal pain and distension. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a closed-loop small-bowel obstruction, leading to emergency surgery. Intraoperatively, no adhesions were found, but nearly the entire small intestine had herniated through a small foramen in the lesser omentum, passing dorsally behind the stomach. The intestine was repositioned, and the foramen was sutured closed. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 15, with no recurrence observed at the 6-month follow-up examination.Clinical discussion:A lesser omental hernia may occur after colectomy due to iatrogenic disruption of the gastrocolic ligament. This case represented a type II hernia, with the small intestine prolapsing from the retrogastric space through a defect in the lesser omentum. A review of eight post-colectomy cases showed that all were type II. Diagnosis is difficult, but CT findings such as anteriorly displaced, dilated bowel loops and gastric distortion are key. Careful CT evaluation and intraoperative identification are essential for appropriate diagnosis and treatment.Conclusion:A lesser omental hernia can occur after total colectomy. CT imaging is valuable for diagnosis, and surgical intervention is often required.

Introduction

A lesser omental hernia is an extremely rare type of internal hernia[1]. An internal hernia is a condition in which an organ protrudes through normal or abnormal openings or spaces within the abdominal cavity, potentially leading to severe complications, such as small bowel obstruction[2]. The lesser omentum is a double-layered peritoneal structure that normally serves to support and protect internal organs; however, in rare cases, intestinal loops may become entrapped through a defect in this structure. Diagnosing a lesser omental hernia is challenging because its symptoms closely resemble those of other gastrointestinal diseases, making early detection difficult[3]. Special attention should be paid postoperatively, as surgical adhesions and anatomical changes may contribute to the formation of a defect in the lesser omentum[4]. In this paper, we report a case of lesser omental hernia that occurred after total colectomy and compare its characteristics with those of previously reported cases. A literature search was performed using PubMed without restrictions on the publication period, using the keywords “lesser omental hernia” and “colectomy.” In addition, relevant articles were identified by reviewing the references of the retrieved reports. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[5].

Case presentation

The patient was a 65-year-old man who had undergone laparoscopic total colectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis for ulcerative colitis 4 years prior. The patient presented with sudden abdominal pain and distention. Upon presentation, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 180/105 mmHg; heart rate, 65 beats per minute; body temperature, 36.5°C; and oxygen saturation (SpO2), 95%/room air. Blood tests revealed only mild elevation of inflammatory markers (CRP, 0.2 mg/dL; white blood cell, 11 000 μL; Neut, 89.4%). Abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a closed loop in the small intestine (Fig. 1A), leading to a diagnosis of strangulated bowel obstruction for which emergency surgery was performed. When the abdomen was opened, no adhesions were observed within the peritoneal cavity, and a distended small intestine was observed immediately beneath the incision (Fig. 2A). After releasing the closed loop seen on the CT scan, further exploration revealed that the stomach was stretched caudally, and there was a small foramen in the lesser omentum. Furthermore, nearly the entire length of the small intestine passed dorsally behind the stomach (Fig. 3A), traversed the foramen of the lesser omentum, and reappeared anterior to the stomach, forming an internal hernia (Fig. 3B). The small intestine was repositioned to its anatomical position through the omental foramen. No ischemic or necrotic changes were observed in the small intestine. The omental foramen was closed with a running absorbable suture via laparotomy (Fig. 2B). Only simple closure was performed; no additional measures to prevent recurrence were taken. A retrospective review of preoperative CT images revealed gas within small-intestinal loops crossing the stomach and evidence of traction on the stomach (Fig. 1B, C). The patient’s recovery progressed well, and he was discharged without complications on the 15th postoperative day. Discussion

A lesser omental hernia is an extremely rare type of internal hernia, accounting for only 1.1% of all internal hernias[6]. The possible etiologies include congenital defects of the lesser omentum, trauma, inflammatory changes, and iatrogenic factors. In the present case, the patient had a history of total colectomy without any evidence of trauma or congenital abnormalities, suggesting an association with prior surgical history.

Kitagishi et al[7] classified lesser omental hernias into two types: type I, in which the intestinal tract prolapses directly into the lesser sac through the lesser omentum; and type II, in which the gastrocolic ligament is separated, and the intestine prolapses into the abdominal cavity from the retrogastric space through the lesser omentum. Based on intraoperative findings, the present case was classified as type II. Specifically, the small intestine herniated into the abdominal cavity through a defect in the lesser omentum that originated posterior to the stomach. In a total colectomy, the entire large intestine is removed, resulting in the absence of the gastrocolic ligament. A previous total colectomy likely contributed to this condition, thereby creating an anatomical environment conducive to herniation.

Diagnosing a lesser omental hernia is challenging because abdominal radiography alone often fails to provide useful information. CT plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of bowel obstruction. The following characteristic CT findings have been reported[4]: (1) dilated bowel loops located anterior to the stomach; (2) convergence of mesenteric vessels at the lesser curvature of the stomach; and (3) gastric distortion affecting the surrounding organs, such as the spleen and transverse colon. In the present case, a retrospective review of the preoperative CT images revealed the presence of findings 1 and 3.

A review of previously reported cases identified eight cases of lesser omental hernia occurring after colectomy, including the present case (Table 1)[3,4,8–12]. The mean patient age was 51 years (range, 21–73 years), with a male-to-female ratio of 3:5. Notably, all eight cases were classified as type II, and bowel resection was required in two cases. Closure of the lesser omental defect was performed in all of the cases. Given that all reported cases of lesser omental hernia following colectomy were classified as type II, it is crucial to consider the aforementioned CT findings when evaluating postoperative patients with suspected bowel obstruction. Moreover, since all cases were type II, surgical strategies to prevent lesser omental hernia should include intraoperative confirmation and closure or reinforcement of any defects in the lesser omentum. Closure of the lesser omental defect was performed in all of the cases, including the present case. However, if closure of the lesser omental foramen is challenging owing to a large defect or fragility of the lesser omentum, an alternative approach may be considered. These include (1) fixing the gastric wall to prevent internal herniation and (2) intentionally enlarging the defect in the lesser omentum to prevent bowel strangulation and ensure smooth passage in cases of internal herniation. However, these approaches warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

A lesser omental hernia is a rare condition; however, as seen in the present case, it is more likely to occur after colorectal surgery, and CT imaging is highly useful for its diagnosis.

In cases of bowel obstruction in the absence of the gastrocolic ligament, the condition observed in this case should be considered in the differential diagnosis. It is essential to identify the characteristic findings on CT and to consider surgical intervention if the diagnosis is confirmed.
