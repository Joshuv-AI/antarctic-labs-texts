Hematology in times of crisis: Lessons learned and strategic priorities from the EHA–ASH Joint Workshop on Ukraine
Authors: Oleksandr Istomin, Taisiya Yakimkova, Reggie Belkhedim, Natacha Bolaños, Konstanze Döhner, Arnold Ganser, Torsten Haferlach, André Ilbawi, Arman Kacharian, Roman Kizyma, Sergiy Klymenko, Sybiryna Korenkova, Frank Leebeek, Oleksandr Lysytsia, Bohdan Medvediev, Wojciech Mlynarski, Dietger Niederwieser, Yuliya Nogovitsyna, Damiano Rondelli, Iryna Selina, Ganna Usenko, Nazar Shokun, Mats Heyman, Charlotte Niemeyer, Alexandra Müller
Published: 2026

Armed conflict poses profound and sustained challenges to the delivery of hematological care, particularly for patients requiring complex diagnostics, multimodal therapies, and long‐term follow‐up. Children and adults with hematological malignancies and nonmalignant blood disorders are among the most vulnerable populations in humanitarian emergencies, as their care depends on uninterrupted access to specialized diagnostics, blood products, pharmacotherapy, cellular therapies, and highly trained multidisciplinary teams. Disruption at any point along this continuum can rapidly translate into increased morbidity and mortality.

Since Russia's full‐scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the Ukrainian healthcare system has faced sustained pressure from direct attacks on healthcare infrastructure, population displacement, workforce shortages, supply chain disruptions, failing physical infrastructure (water, heating, and electricity), and financial instability. 1 , 2 More than 2200 attacks on healthcare facilities have been reported till December 2025, alongside repeated interruptions to electricity, water supply, and the transport system. 3 Despite these challenges, the Ukrainian hematology community has demonstrated extraordinary resilience and adaptability, maintaining essential services while reorganizing care delivery under extreme constraints. With strong national commitment and sustained international solidarity, the strategic focus has evolved from crisis stabilization toward strengthening and rebuilding hematological services in the context of a protracted conflict.

To consolidate lessons learned and define priority actions for the future, the European Haematology Association (EHA) and the American Society of Hematology (ASH) convened a joint workshop entitled “Haematology in times of crisis: Lessons learned and strategies for the future in Ukraine” during the EHA 2025 Congress in Milan. This manuscript summarizes the structure and outcomes of the workshop, highlighting key insights from both the acute emergency response and the ongoing protracted crisis phase. Central themes included the role of national and international collaboration, the balance between emergency response and long‐term capacity building, the need for differentiated strategies across patient groups, and preparedness for compound risks, including nuclear catastrophes. Based on these discussions, we outline strategic priorities to strengthen hematological care in Ukraine, with relevance for conflict‐affected and fragile settings globally.

WORKSHOP DESIGN AND SCOPE

The EHA–ASH joint workshop took place on June 14, 2025, during the EHA Annual Congress in Milan, Italy. It brought together Ukrainian hematologists, international clinical experts, representatives from the World Health Organization (WHO), policymakers from the Ministry of Health of Ukraine, professional societies, non‐governmental organizations, and patient advocacy groups.

The program was co‐designed with Ukrainian experts and international partners over 6 months to address the full spectrum of crisis response and recovery, and was facilitated by 24 professionals sharing their real‐world experiences across three separate sessions. Initial sessions focused on emergency response strategies, including governmental and global approaches, mechanisms to maintain continuity of care close to the front lines, and international medical evacuation when local treatment could not be safely sustained. Subsequent session examined lessons learned from the protracted phase of the conflict, with particular attention to preserving quality of care, sustaining workforce capacity, and mitigating long‐term systems‐level attrition. Final sessions focused on strategic outlooks, addressing future priorities in capacity building, innovation, research, and preparedness planning. Throughout, interactive discussions emphasized co‐development of practical and implementable strategies.

Key lessons learned

Resilience and continuity of care

A central conclusion from the workshop was the exceptional resilience of the Ukrainian hematology community across both acute and protracted phases of the crisis. Despite ongoing safety threats and resource constraints, participants reported continued provision of vital and highly specialized care, including chemotherapy, transfusion support, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Care was delivered locally whenever feasible and complemented by cross‐border referral pathways when local provision was not possible.

Notably, HSCT capacity was not only sustained but also expanded from 8 to 13 active programs during the protracted phase of the war. 4 This reflects a deliberate strategic shift from emergency stabilization toward selective capacity building, enabled by strong national and international collaboration, targeted training initiatives, and coordinated logistical support. Increasing capacity and opening new programs resulted in doubling HCT activities from 48 and 200 in the year 2021 to 146 and 344 in 2025 for allogeneic and autologous HCT, respectively (Figure 1). More importantly, activities of allogeneic HCT in adults were almost not existent in 2021 increased from 6 in 2021 to 109 in 2025 with outcomes similar to the HCT of Ukrainian patients transplanted abroad. 5 Even more sophisticated allogeneic HCT were successfully performed and activities are expecting to growth even further by improving nontransplant hematology and infrastructure. 6

Role of national and international collaboration

National and international collaboration was identified as a critical determinant for sustaining hematological care throughout both acute and protracted crises. Partnerships among Ukrainian institutions, professional societies such as EHA and ASH, global agencies including the WHO, nongovernmental organizations, and academic centers facilitated rapid problem‐solving, mobilization of resources, and sustained knowledge exchange. Workshop discussions emphasized that long‐standing, clinician‐led partnerships, rather than isolated emergency interventions, were essential to preserve care quality, support workforce capacity, and enable system strengthening under prolonged instability.

The Help for Ukrainian Haematology Patients (HUP) initiative was highlighted as a key example of effective international professional collaboration supporting adult hematology and HSCT during wartime. HUP brought together Ukrainian hematologists, international experts, and professional societies in weekly remote meetings to understand the challenges and provide established transplantation programs with expert consultation, targeted training and mentoring, registry development, procurement of donated chemotherapy drugs, and facilitation of access to appropriate diagnostic laboratories in Germany. Importantly, HUP contributed to the expansion of autologous and allogeneic HCT capacity during the protracted phase of the crisis, demonstrating that meaningful capacity building is feasible even in an active conflict setting when international support is aligned with national clinical leadership. 5 , 6 , 7

Complementing these efforts, the Supporting Action for Emergency Response in Ukraine (SAFER Ukraine) initiative played a pivotal role during the acute phase, particularly for pediatric patients with cancer and blood disorders. 8 SAFER Ukraine established a centralized triage and referral system enabling timely clinical assessment, prioritization based on medical urgency, and coordinated allocation of patients to appropriate domestic or international treatment pathways. By embedding evacuation within a structured continuity‐of‐care framework, SAFER Ukraine reduced treatment delays, preserved therapeutic intensity, and mitigated inequities in access to care, while safeguarding local capacity when treatment could be safely administered in Ukraine through a standardized clinical consultation pathway. 9 , 10

The WBMT Nurses Group's collaboration with Ukrainian nurses has been both challenging and deeply rewarding, as it has provided them with a stronger understanding of HCT through a series of webinars, workshops, and specialized training sessions conducted in close partnership with the HUP team. A particular emphasis on Quality Management has played a pivotal role in supporting their efforts to enhance and strengthen systems.

Together, these collaborations illustrate how coordinated international support, integrating emergency response with capacity building, training, and research, can safeguard patient outcomes while laying the foundation for resilient hematological care systems in protracted conflict settings.

Need for differentiated care strategies

Workshop discussions emphasized that effective crisis response must be tailored to the specific needs of different patient populations. Adult and pediatric patients face distinct diagnostic, therapeutic, and psychosocial challenges, requiring differentiated yet coordinated strategies. Similarly, patients with benign hematological conditions often have chronic care needs that differ fundamentally from those with malignant diseases, particularly regarding continuity of treatment, transfusion support, and long‐term follow‐up. Failure to account for these distinctions risks oversimplifying response strategies and exacerbating inequities within already strained health systems.

Impact on the healthcare workforce

Participants consistently highlighted the profound impact of prolonged crisis on the healthcare workforce. Persistent security threats, high workloads, emotional strain, uncertainty, inability to plan, unpredictable and volatile context, undefined end point of the crisis, and insufficient recovery time have significantly affected staff well‐being. Workforce fatigue, moral distress, and attrition were identified as critical risks to long‐term system sustainability. Safeguarding workforce well‐being through psychosocial support, training opportunities, and career development was recognized as integral to resilient hematological care in crisis settings.

Preparedness for nuclear and compound risks

The risk of nuclear catastrophes was explicitly addressed, reflecting the Ukrainian context and broader geopolitical instability. Participants emphasized that hematological expertise is essential for preparedness planning, including management of acute radiation syndrome, long‐term surveillance of radiation‐associated hematologic malignancies, and integration of hematology into national and international emergency response frameworks. Strengthening preparedness for such compound risks was viewed as an urgent and underdeveloped priority.

Strategic priorities identified

Building on these lessons, the workshop identified strategic priorities chosen to improve hematological care in Ukraine during the ongoing protracted crisis.

1. Embedding hematological care in emergency preparedness planning Integrating hematological care, both for malignant and benign hematological disorders, into national emergency preparedness planning was identified as a critical gap, including planning for blood supply continuity, supply of essential medications including chemotherapeutic and immunological agents, management of therapy interruptions, maintenance of cellular therapy programs, and preparedness for radiation‐related emergencies; this priority aligns with the Lancet manifesto on improving cancer care in conflict‐impacted populations, which calls for preparedness, continuity of essential services, and investment in resilient health systems during armed conflict, as well as with subsequent WHO‐led recommendations to systematically integrate cancer care into humanitarian and crisis response frameworks. 11 , 12

4. Building workforce capacity and clinical networks Education and training of physicians, nurses, and laboratory staff remain essential pillars of resilience. Building workforce capacity and clinical networks

Education and training of physicians, nurses, and laboratory staff remain essential pillars of resilience. Accessible virtual case consultation platforms were highlighted as scalable tools to support clinical decision‐making, particularly during periods of restricted travel or insecurity, as well as for advanced and palliative cases.

5. Sustaining research and academic capacity Finally, sustaining and expanding local research capacity was viewed as a strategic investment. Inclusion of Ukrainian centers in international study groups and clinical trials enables continuity of scientific activity during a crisis, generates evidence on the long‐term health effects of war, and facilitates the translation of crisis experience into globally relevant knowledge. In parallel, strengthening national clinical registries represents a critical pillar of academic capacity‐building. High‐quality, patient‐level registries enable systematic outcome monitoring, benchmarking across centers, and hypothesis generation for future studies. They provide an essential foundation for participation in international research collaborations and ensure that data generated within Ukraine can inform both national policy decisions and the global evidence base

BROADER IMPLICATIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

Although grounded in the Ukrainian experience, the insights from this workshop have broad relevance. By 2030, an estimated 2.3 billion people worldwide will live in fragile and conflict‐affected settings, yet emergency preparedness policies for noncommunicable diseases, including hematological conditions, remain limited. 13 Only 8% of National Cancer Control Plans have a policy for emergency preparedness. 14 Ukraine's experience demonstrates that with coordinated, well‐aligned support, it is possible not only to sustain care during conflict but also to strengthen systems in ways that improve long‐term resilience.

The EHA–ASH joint workshop reaffirmed that effective hematological care in times of crisis requires a dual focus on immediate emergency response and deliberate long‐term capacity building. Resilience arises from committed healthcare professionals, strong national leadership, and sustained, well‐coordinated international collaboration. The strategic priorities outlined here provide a roadmap for strengthening hematological care in Ukraine during the ongoing protracted crisis and offer transferable lessons for other conflict‐affected settings.
