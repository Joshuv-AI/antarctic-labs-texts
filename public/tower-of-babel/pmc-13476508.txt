Associations between impaired intrinsic capacity and emergency room visits among community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Wen-Hsuan Hou, Nguyen Thanh Nhu, Jia-Ni Xie, Yann-Yuh Jou, Yuan-Fang Chung, Chung-Yi Li, Chiachi Bonnie Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•All six IC domain impairments were associated with higher ER visit risk.•Malnutrition showed the strongest association with ER visit risk.•ER visit risk increased with greater IC impairment severity.•The “impairments in all domains” class had the highest ER visit risk.

Introduction

The global population is aging rapidly, presenting significant challenges to healthcare delivery systems, especially acute care services [1]. Older adults constitute a rapidly growing segment of the population and account for a disproportionate share of emergency room (ER) visits and healthcare expenditures [2]. Evidence indicates that older adults utilize emergency medical services significantly more frequently than younger individuals, with utilization rates rising sharply among the oldest-old [3]. An ER visit in this demographic often signals decompensation, vulnerability, or the progression of underlying diseases, serving as a sentinel event for subsequent morbidity and mortality [1]. Furthermore, older adults discharged from the ER remain at high risk for recurrent visits, functional decline, hospital admissions, and nursing home placement, suggesting that the period surrounding an ER visit represents a critical window of vulnerability for community-dwelling older persons [4,5].

Despite the high volume of geriatric patients, traditional risk stratification models in emergency care have primarily relied on triage levels, vital signs, and distinct clinical diagnoses [6]. Moreover, these models, while accurate for the general adult population, are less effective when applied to older adults, partly because of the atypical clinical presentations often seen in older patients presenting the ER [7]. Previous research has consistently demonstrated that functional disability, particularly limitations in activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL), is strongly associated with increased acute care utilization and medical expenditures among older adults [[8], [9], [10]]. Consequently, proactive screening and early identification of functional deficits in community and primary care settings may facilitate the identification of older adults at increased risk of adverse health outcomes and future healthcare utilization.

To address the limitations of disease-centric models, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework, centered on the concept of intrinsic capacity (IC) [11,12]. Defined as the composite of all the physical and mental capacities of an individual, IC shifts the focus from treating specific illnesses to maintaining functional ability throughout the life course [13]. The IC construct encompasses five key domains: cognitive function, locomotor capacity, vitality, sensory capacity (vision and hearing, and psychological capacity [14]. Unlike traditional measures of disability, IC assessment aims to detect subtle declines in reserves earlier along the aging trajectory, offering a holistic strategy for prevention and health promotion [15,16].

Emerging evidence suggests that IC is a robust predictor of adverse health outcomes. Declines in IC have been independently linked to increased risks of frailty, disability, institutionalization, and mortality among community-dwelling older adults [15,17,18]. For instance, specific domains such as cognitive decline and limited mobility have been individually linked to higher risks of emergency department attendance [19]. Furthermore, longitudinal studies and reviews have highlighted a bidirectional relationship between IC and healthcare utilization [20,21]. However, current literature on IC often focuses on predicting hospital admission among patients presenting to the ED [22], or utilizes composite endpoints that bundle emergency visits with hospitalization [19,23], making it difficult to isolate factors specifically associated with emergency care utilization.

Understanding the relationship between IC impairments and ER utilization is critical for developing proactive community-based interventions that can mitigate avoidable acute care encounters. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations of individual IC impairments, overall impairment severity, and impairment patterns with ER visits among community-dwelling older adults in Taiwan. Leveraging the WHO ICOPE framework, we explored whether IC impairments were associated with acute care utilization, thereby informing strategies to optimize resource allocation and improve geriatric emergency care.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study used data from the nationwide Taiwan Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) Database (2022–2023), linked to National Health Insurance (NHI) claims data (2019–2023) and the Death Registry obtained through the Health and Welfare Data Science Center (HWDC), Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW), Taiwan. The NHI program covers over 99% of Taiwan's population and undergoes quarterly expert audits on randomly selected claims, ensuring data completeness and accuracy [24].

Since October 2020, Taiwan’s Health Promotion Administration (HPA), based on WHO ICOPE guidelines [25], has implemented nationwide functional assessments targeting adults aged ≥65 and indigenous adults aged 55–64 years [26]. These assessments cover six domains: cognition, mobility, vitality, vision, hearing, and psychological well-being. In Taiwan’s ICOPE implementation, sensory capacity is operationalized as separate vision and hearing domains. ICOPE programs were primarily conducted by trained healthcare professionals, including physicians, nurses, pharmacists, physical therapists, occupational therapists, and optometrists, as well as social workers who completed the national ICOPE training program, with additional qualified personnel approved under the national ICOPE program permitted to provide services in remote, offshore, Indigenous, and medically underserved areas [27]. Participants were recruited through community-based integrated health screening programs and healthcare services delivered in communities, clinics, public health centers, and hospitals. Hospitals in Taiwan provide extensive outpatient services and therefore also serve as accessible settings for community-based preventive health screening [28]. Between 2022 and 2023, a total of 294,917 older adults underwent ICOPE assessments across 883 healthcare institutions, including medical centers, regional hospitals, district hospitals, clinics, home nursing agencies, public health centers, physical therapy clinics, occupational therapy clinics, and optometry clinics, as well as 429 pharmacies and 16 social service institutions participating in the national ICOPE program [29].

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China Medical University and Hospital (Approval No. CMUH113-REC3-053). Informed consent was waived because only de-identified data were used. Data access was provided by the HWDC, where all data processing and statistical analyses were conducted on-site in accordance with data security regulations.

Study population

The nationwide Taiwan ICOPE database initially contained 320,317 assessment records collected between 2022 and 2023. Records were excluded if they had incomplete IC data (n = 20), were Indigenous adults aged 55–64 years (n = 5,098), involved individuals aged >112 years (n = 2), were obtained from anomalous institutions (n = 47,536), or represented subsequent assessments from individuals with multiple ICOPE screenings (n = 22,762). Only the first valid assessment was retained for analysis. An anomalous institution was defined as a facility selected for on-site inspection because of an unusually high service volume relative to staffing levels or outlier rates exceeding 25%. Institutions were classified as anomalous when audits identified deviations from standard operating procedures during the assessment process. After applying these exclusion criteria, the final study cohort comprised 244,899 older adults (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Follow-up

Participants were followed from their ICOPE screening date until death or December 31, 2023, whichever occurred first. Person-years were computed for each participant. All-cause ER visits were identified using the NHI outpatient and emergency claims databases. During 213,528 person-years of follow-up, 96,889 all-cause ER visits were recorded among 54,870 individuals, yielding an incidence rate of 453.75 per 1,000 person-years.

Measures and classifications of intrinsic capacity

The WHO ICOPE Screening Tool (WIST), adapted slightly for Taiwan, was used for initial screening [30]. According to the national ICOPE implementation protocol, participants with abnormal screening results underwent domain-specific confirmatory assessments for cognition, locomotion, vitality, psychological capacity, and vision. IC impairments were subsequently classified based on the results of these confirmatory assessments rather than the initial screening alone. In the WHO ICOPE framework, vitality is represented by nutritional status [25]. Accordingly, participants with abnormal vitality screening results underwent nutritional assessment using the Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form (MNA-SF), and those with an MNA-SF score ≤11 (at risk of malnutrition or malnourished) were classified as having vitality impairment [31]. Detailed measures and classification criteria for each IC domain are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

This study operationalized IC impairments using three complementary approaches. First, impairments in each of the six IC components were coded dichotomously as 0 (no impairment) or 1 (presence of impairment). IC impairment severity was categorized as low (0 impaired domains), moderate (1–2 impaired domains), and high (≥3 impaired domains) [32]. This classification was used to evaluate the dose-gradient relationship between IC impairment and ER visits. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was also employed to uncover distinct patterns of IC impairments. The five-class model was selected as the optimal solution for subsequent analysis. Classes were labeled based on conditional domain probabilities: (1) Low IC impairments, (2) Physio-cognitive decline (PCD) with sensory impairments, (3) Hearing and visual impairments, (4) Nutritional-visual-physical impairments, and (5) Impairments in all domains.

Covariates

The covariates analyzed in this study included sociodemographic characteristics and health status of older adults. Sociodemographic characteristics comprised sex (male or female), age group (65−74, 75−84, and ≥85 years), insured monthly salary, and residential area. Indigenous status was also included due to well-documented healthcare disparities in Taiwan's Indigenous populations [33]. Geographic socioeconomic disadvantage was quantified using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), derived through principal component analysis of five township-level variables: (1) median household income, (2) proportion of individuals aged >6 years who were either enrolled in school or employed, (3) proportion without senior high school education, (4) proportion of individuals employed in agriculture, forestry, fishery, and animal husbandry, and (5) proportion of individuals who were divorced, separated, or widowed [34]. Higher ADI scores indicated greater deprivation, and townships were grouped into least (lowest 25%), moderate (middle 50%), and most deprived (highest 25%). Because the NHI claims data often list the insured’s workplace rather than residence, a method validated in prior studies was used to infer the residential township [35].

Health status variables included comorbidity and the number of ER visits and hospitalizations during the three years preceding ICOPE screening. Comorbidity was assessed using the Deyo-Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [36,37], calculated from diagnoses identified in at least three outpatient visits or one hospitalization within a one-year period during the three years preceding ICOPE screening, and categorized into four groups (0, 1, 2, and ≥3).

Statistical analysis

Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was also employed to uncover distinct patterns of IC impairments. Model selection was based on multiple statistical indicators: the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), consistent AIC (CAIC), and sample-size-adjusted BIC (SABIC) [38,39]. Latent class models were estimated using maximum likelihood estimation via the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm with multiple random starting values. Model comparison further considered the Bayes Factor (BF), with higher values indicating stronger evidence in favor of a model with K classes over one with K + 1. Candidate latent class models (K = 1–6) were specified a priori, with the upper bound determined by interpretability, class size stability, and convergence considerations. The correct model probability (cmP) was also assessed, with preference given to models with the highest cmP [40]. Classification quality was evaluated using entropy, with values ≥0.80 indicating strong class separation [41]. Fit indices from one- to six-class models are presented in Supplementary Table S2. While AIC favored five- and six-class models, BIC, CAIC, SABIC, cmP, and entropy collectively supported the 5-class model.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for baseline characteristics using means (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables. The incidence rates of ER visits were calculated as events per 1,000 person-years. Associations between IC impairments and ER visits were evaluated using Poisson regression to estimate adjusted rate ratios (aRRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Models were separately constructed for: (1) individual IC impairments, (2) overall IC impairment severity categories, and (3) latent IC impairment patterns. Subgroup analyses were also performed by sex and age group to examine the potential effect modification. All models adjusted for age, sex, Indigenous status, comorbidities, and ADI. All tests were two-tailed, with significance set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Subgroup analyses by age and sex

Supplementary Table S4 and S5 show age- and sex-stratified incidence rates of ER visits according to IC impairment measures. Overall, ER visit incidence increased monotonically with age in both sexes, and was consistently higher in men than women (men: 397.84, 581.62, and 950.85; women: 348.09, 521.50, and 736.47 events per 1,000 person-years for ages 65−74, 75−84, and ≥85 years, respectively). Across all IC domains, the presence of impairment was associated with higher ER visit rates within each age stratum, with the largest absolute differences generally observed in the oldest group; for example, limited mobility was associated with substantially higher rates compared with those without mobility impairment (men ≥85 years: 1274.48 vs 830.50; women ≥85 years: 930.65 vs 636.21 per 1,000 person-years). When IC was categorized by the number of impaired domains, a clear dose-response pattern was observed, with progressively higher ER visit rates from low IC impairment (0 impairments) to moderate IC impairment (1–2 impairments) and high IC impairment (≥3 impairments) in both sexes (e.g., men ≥85 years: 752.21, 1016.00, and 1404.12 per 1,000 person-years; women ≥85 years: 612.58, 765.97, and 1005.16). Similarly, individuals with multi-domain impairment patterns, particularly impairments in all domains, showed the highest ER visit incidence.

The highest ER visit incidence was observed in the “impairments in all domains” pattern across all age and sex categories; notably, individuals aged 65–74 years with impairments in all domains (1,281.07 in men vs. 760.76 in women) had higher ER visit rates than adults aged ≥85 years with low IC impairment (802.07 in men vs. 653.60 in women). The nutritional-visual-physical impairment pattern constituted the second highest risk pattern, showing a pronounced age-related escalation, particularly among women aged ≥85 years, whose incidence rates approached those of the all-domain impairment group. The PCD with sensory impairments demonstrated intermediate risk, while the dual sensory (hearing and vision) impairment pattern exhibited the lowest ER visit incidence among the impairment patterns.

In age- and sex-stratified analyses, the associations between specific IC impairments and ER visits varied across domains (Fig. 3). Malnutrition demonstrated the most robust and consistent association with ER visits across all age and sex strata, with generally stronger associations observed among men than women. Mobility impairment was also consistently associated with higher ER visit risk in all subgroups, although the magnitude of association was generally modest after adjustment. The aRRs ranged from 1.07 to 1.24, with stronger associations observed in men aged 65–74 years (aRR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.18–1.30), men aged 75–84 years (aRR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.17–1.28), and women aged 65–74 years (aRR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.17–1.27). Cognitive impairment showed weaker and more heterogeneous associations. Significant positive associations were observed among men aged 75–84 years (aRR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.13–1.27), men aged ≥85 years (aRR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.27), women aged 65–74 years (aRR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.11–1.26), and women aged ≥85 years (aRR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.19), whereas the association was not statistically significant among men aged 65–74 years or women aged 75–84 years. Visual impairment was associated with only modest increases in ER visit risk, with significant associations observed in men aged 65–74 years, men aged 75–84 years, and women aged 65–74 years, but not among adults aged ≥85 years. Hearing impairment showed the weakest and least consistent associations; most estimates were close to the null, with a significant association observed only among men aged ≥85 years. Depressive symptoms were associated with increased ER visit risk among adults aged 65–84 years, particularly women aged 65–74 years (aRR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.27–1.45), but the association was not evident among those aged ≥85 years.Fig. 3Adjusted relative risks of emergency room visits according to intrinsic capacity impairments, stratified by age and sex.Fig. 3

Discussion

These findings contribute to the ICOPE and intrinsic capacity literature by demonstrating that IC impairments in community settings are associated with an increased risk of ER visits among older adults. Visual impairment was identified as the most common deficit, while malnutrition showed the strongest and most consistent association with ER visits. A dose-response relationship was observed for overall IC impairment severity, with ER visit risk increasing as the number of IC impairments increased. Latent class analysis revealed distinct patterns of co-occurring IC impairments, with the “impairments in all domains” and “nutritional-visual-physical impairments” classes exhibiting the highest risks. Subgroup analyses by age and sex demonstrated heterogeneity in the associations between IC domains and ER use, while the overall incidence of ER visits increased with age and remained consistently higher in men. Malnutrition was consistently associated with a higher risk of ER visits across age and sex groups, particularly among men, whereas depressive symptoms showed particularly strong associations among younger-old women, underscoring the need for age- and sex-specific intervention strategies to reduce acute healthcare utilization.

Our results are consistent with a meta-analysis of 37 studies showing that greater IC impairment was associated with adverse outcomes, including functional decline and mortality [42], which, by extension, may result in increased ER utilization. Our findings add population-level evidence that IC, as operationalized by the ICOPE framework, offers valuable risk stratification for subsequent acute healthcare utilization in community settings. Our findings highlight that IC is not merely a descriptive geriatric construct but a practical risk stratification tool that can be applied in community and primary care settings before acute health events occur and at a population scale. Our findings provide relevant evidence for clinicians to shift from event-based care toward the proactive identification of vulnerability among community-dwelling older adults, as well as for policymakers to implement targeted public health screening programs for early identification of high-risk older populations across various settings and to optimize the allocation of acute healthcare resources.

Among individual IC impairments, malnutrition showed the strongest association with subsequent ER visits in our overall model, which is consistent with an Irish study showing that over one-third of older adults admitted to the ED are either malnourished or at risk of malnutrition [43]. Nutritional vulnerability may reflect diminished physiological reserve and impaired stress response, increasing susceptibility to common precipitants of ER use such as dehydration, infection, medication intolerance, falls, and acute functional decompensation [44]. The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) has recognized that malnutrition frequently co-occurs with sarcopenia and frailty, forming a pathway in which reduced intake and muscle loss accelerate mobility decline and increase acute care needs [45]. These mechanisms are consistent with findings from a scoping review suggesting that nutritional signals may capture risk earlier than overt frailty and disability, offering a potentially high-yield target for prevention [46].

Our findings showed that mobility impairment was consistently associated with ER visits, supporting mobility limitation as a clinically actionable domain. Mobility impairment may increase ER use through multiple pathways: higher fall risk, reduced ability to manage symptoms at home, decreased access to outpatient care, and worsening cardiometabolic or respiratory reserve due to inactivity [47,48]. Notably, in stratified analyses, mobility impairment showed stronger relative associations with ER visits among younger-old adults than among the oldest-old, a pattern observed in both men and women. This pattern may reflect a ceiling effect in advanced age, where the baseline risk of ER visits is already high and multiple competing risks (e.g., mortality and institutionalization) may dilute relative differences. Survivor bias and heterogeneity in functional trajectories among the oldest-old may further contribute to this attenuation.

Psychological impairment was also associated with increased ER visit risk in our study, particularly among younger-old adults aged 65–84 years. Previous large cohort studies have reported that depressive symptoms among otherwise healthy older persons are associated with a 1.35-fold increase in the odds of ER utilization [49]. Furthermore, a strong dose-response relationship has been observed, with greater depression severity associated with a 2.5- to 3.0- fold increase in the odds of ER visits among both patients with chronic disease [50] and the general population [51]. In older adults, depression may overlap with other geriatric conditions, amplifying vulnerability to acute events and reinforcing recurrent ER use. Depression may increase ER utilization through interacting biological, behavioral, cognitive, and health-system mechanisms: inflammatory and neuroendocrine dysregulation contribute to somatic symptom burden and acute decompensation, while impaired self-management, heightened symptom perception, social isolation, and fragmented outpatient care further shift healthcare use toward emergency settings [52].

Our dose-response findings support the use of overall IC impairment severity as a pragmatic risk classification tool in primary care, which is similar to previous studies that reported dose-response effects and developed weighted IC impairment algorithm for predicting adverse health outcomes [9,19]. Our findings showed that older adults with a greater burden of IC impairments, or those belonging to high-risk latent classes, may benefit from prioritized community-based healthcare and preventive interventions. This is consistent with the WHO ICOPE 2nd edition and implementation framework, which emphasize scalable, person-centered care pathways that begin with simple screening and progress to targeted assessment and individualized care plans [53]. Importantly, in addition to impairments in all domains, other patterns characterized by broader multi-domain involvement, particularly the simultaneous presence of at least two IC impairments such as “physical-cognitive-visual-hearing impairments”, “visual and hearing impairments” or “nutritional-visual-physical impairments”, were also identified as high-risk phenotypes. Conceptually, these phenotypes resemble “multimorbidity-like” or “frailty-like” states in which deficits interact synergistically; for example, malnutrition may accelerate physiological decline and functional disability, contributing to frailty and immobility, limiting access to outpatient care, and ultimately increasing ER utilization [17,47]. Such clustering may therefore be useful for designing integrated intervention packages rather than single-domain interventions.

Our stratified analyses revealed that the relative importance of IC domains varied by age and sex, suggesting that a tailored interpretation of ICOPE results may be warranted. Malnutrition showed the most consistent association with ER visits across age and sex groups, with generally stronger associations observed among men. Given that vitality is a core domain of intrinsic capacity, these findings underscore the importance of routine nutritional screening and timely nutritional interventions within the ICOPE framework to prevent functional decline and reduce acute healthcare utilization. Mobility impairment was likewise associated with an increased risk of ER visits across all age and sex groups, highlighting the importance of preserving physical function as a cornerstone of healthy aging. In contrast, depressive symptoms demonstrated greater age- and sex-specific variability, with stronger associations observed among younger-old women and middle-old men, suggesting that psychological impairment may influence healthcare utilization differently across demographic subgroups. Cognitive and sensory impairments exhibited greater heterogeneity across age and sex strata, with less consistent associations observed for visual and hearing impairments. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these demographic differences. Collectively, these findings indicate that the clinical relevance of specific IC domains may differ across population groups, supporting the development of more targeted, person-centered prevention strategies.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, participation in ICOPE programs may introduce selection bias, potentially limiting generalizability to less engaged or more medically complex populations. Second, our observational study did not classify ER visits according to their underlying reasons or whether they were related to functional impairment; therefore, the observed associations between IC impairments and ER visits should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal relationship. Third, residual confounding is possible, as claims-linked and screening data may not fully capture health behaviors, social determinants, caregiver support, or detailed clinical severity. Finally, ER visits were identified from administrative data, which do not provide granular context regarding preventability, triage acuity, or immediate causes of presentation. In addition, LCA patterns were assigned based on posterior probabilities, and further longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to evaluate IC trajectories and the effectiveness of targeted interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, IC impairments, as defined by the WHO ICOPE framework, were associated with an increased risk of subsequent ER visits in community-dwelling older adults. A clear dose-response relationship was observed, with greater numbers of IC impairments and high-risk multi-domain impairment patterns associated with progressively higher ER visit risk. Notably, malnutrition was consistently associated with a higher risk of ER visits across age and sex groups, highlighting nutrition as a potentially modifiable target for reducing acute healthcare utilization in older adults. These findings support the use of the ICOPE framework for identifying older adults at increased risk of acute healthcare utilization and informing proactive, function-centered preventive care strategies.
