A Framework for Building an Inclusive Quality Program in a Pediatric Heart Center
Authors: Judson A. Moore, Jennifer Marietta, Adam L. Ware, David K. Bailly, Kevin Hummel, Aaron W. Eckhauser, Chuck G. Pribble, Melissa M. Winder, Tina Wadhwa, Emilee T. Glenn, Ashlee Gardner, Megan Wichser, Haley Francione, Carly Heyrend, Chelsea Buckalew, Bethany Marullo, Venu Amula, Adil Husain, Antonio G. Cabrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Pediatric heart centers have advanced quality improvement (QI) work through registries and collaboratives, improving benchmarking and shared learning. However, institutions must also build internal, sustainable QI programs tailored to local needs. We outlined our experience in developing an inclusive QI program that provides a structured framework to drive continuous improvement.Methods:We implemented a 5-step approach: (1) cultivating an environment ready for change, (2) optimizing streams to identify improvement opportunities, (3) building systems to support initiatives, (4) promoting QI work and successes, and (5) sustaining and scaling improvements. This included engaging diverse stakeholders, streamlining data, reducing participation barriers, and applying structured project management. Proposals underwent rigorous review to ensure equitable support.Results:Between 2021 and 2025, the program supported 17 subgroups across cardiology, surgery, anesthesia, and critical care, with 60% of projects led by nonphysicians. It facilitated 21 QI consults (71% progressing to formal projects), 13 publications, more than 30 national presentations, and additional impactful unpublished work, while strengthening leadership and team expertise through QI education. System-level outcomes improved, including surgical mortality and US News & World Report rankings. Performance rounds generated 170 improvement actions to guide systematic change.Conclusions:An inclusive QI program enhances outcomes, and our structure underscores the value of both multidisciplinary collaboration and strategic resource use. By investing in leadership, promoting diversity, and aligning with institutional goals, heart centers can achieve sustained improvement. This framework offers a potential model for institutions seeking robust QI infrastructure.

INTRODUCTION

During the past decade, quality improvement (QI) in pediatric cardiology and congenital heart surgery has increasingly emphasized external collaboration through registries and learning collaboratives.1–4 These efforts have driven advances in benchmarking, data quality, and integrated learning, fundamentally reshaping patient care. However, individual institutions must invest in internal QI programs tailored to their unique populations and operational structures.5 Pediatric heart centers are inherently complicated systems requiring coordinated efforts across teams. This complex structure causes operational and social silos that often impede progress. As expectations for transparency, efficiency, and outcomes grow, a structured multidisciplinary approach to improvement efforts is imperative.

In this article, we reflect on the process of formalizing our heart center’s QI program through a conceptual framework, such as the cultivation of flowers. Although many improvement efforts arise organically, like wildflowers, they often struggle without deliberate attention, structured support, and fertile conditions. Our approach emphasizes intentional design and consistent nurturing to help initiatives take root and endure. We propose 5 foundational program development steps: (1) cultivate an environment ready for change (prepare the soil), (2) optimize streams to identify improvement opportunities (gather the seeds), (3) build a system to support initiatives (grow the flowers), (4) promote QI work and successes (sell the flowers), and (5) maintain and scale the program (sustain and scale the garden). These steps, depicted in Figure 1, although not necessarily linear, can be adapted to the specific conditions of any heart center.

FRAMEWORK

Cultivate an Environment Ready for Change (Prepare the Soil)

To successfully navigate the complex landscape of a heart center, it is essential to first understand existing social networks. This includes the following: (1) build relationships with thought leaders who influence others, (2) recruit potential collaborators who can expand capacity for change, and (3) identify dissenters who can be late adopters and either derail or strengthen efforts depending on the approach. By establishing a diverse network and recognizing the significance of prior experience, a system capable of identifying areas in need of change and driving improvement can be created.

Our heart center’s QI program is within a large, free-standing, tertiary care children’s hospital as part of a broader health system with a longstanding commitment to QI. It relies on a well-structured, multidisciplinary Heart Center Quality and Value Committee with representatives from cardiovascular surgery, cardiology, critical care, and cardiac anesthesia. This committee includes nurses, nurse managers, physicians, advanced practice providers (APP), pharmacists, administrative leaders, and data specialists. Monthly meetings set strategic priorities, review initiatives, and ensure progress across the heart center.

Prompt and equitable access to data is another important element, and our process is outlined in Figure 3. Important components include: (1) quick turnaround for feasibility data, (2) structured methods for determining urgency, (3) representation from research and QI leadership if requests are processed jointly, and (4) discrete requests with clear deliverables. Dedicated personnel should manage data collection, processing, and dissemination to ensure transparency, accessibility, and actionability. An embedded consistent data specialist can foster understanding of program history, data sources, and integration into the QI culture. Dashboards also track key outcomes and performance indicators, enabling timely adjustments via platforms such as Microsoft Excel, Tableau, and others.

QI training opportunities build diverse leaders with unique perspectives and influence. Prioritizing education creates a pipeline capable of sustaining and expanding the program. Although quality leaders can be hired externally, many should be cultivated internally when possible. It is easier to teach QI methodology than to build trust and relationships among teams. Most hospitals have QI specialists who can be used to build expertise within the heart center. If this resource is unavailable, foundational online certifications, such as the Institute for Healthcare Improvement Open School, can be pursued, and small, low-risk projects can be undertaken to gain experience. Clinical training programs also foster future leaders, and clinical fellows should receive comprehensive QI training.6 This engages both trainees and faculty.

Optimize Streams to Identify Improvement Opportunities (Gather the Seeds)

Once a fertile environment for change is established, the next step is to actively cultivate lanes for identifying actionable change (Fig. 2). Opportunities such as performance rounding,7 morbidity and mortality reviews, voluntary event reporting (or trigger-identified events), subdivision updates, family advisory councils, parent listservs, and patient-reported outcome processes create structured interactions. Voluntary event reporting fails to capture many opportunities, which was a primary driver of our systematic review of individual patients (performance rounds). Our performance rounding also includes predetermined patient review triggers, such as unexpected deaths, unplanned reoperations, or specific hospital-acquired conditions and infections. Public forums, such as performance rounding, should include feedback loops to inform participants of actions taken to foster engagement.

Entry into QI involvement should be as low barrier as possible. To support this, we offer “QI consults” through our committee. These are informal brainstorming sessions for those desiring to improve. We also adopt yearly campaigns aligned with patient-centered domains of quality care, originally published by Brilli et al8 and further outlined in the Institute for Healthcare Improvement white paper summarizing the Safe, Timely, Effective, Efficient, Equitable, and Patient-centered model.9 In 2024, we focused on “Provide Me with the Right Care,” prompting projects on sedation reduction, diaper rash prevention, blood conservation, and intravenous potassium efficiency. Our 2025 theme is “Help Me Stay Well,” which remains inclusive and has prompted projects in internal jugular thrombus reduction, preoperative feeding practices, infant clinic access, and necrotizing enterocolitis prevention.

Diversity in improvement work and those participating should be intentional, spanning roles, disciplines, and environments. We proactively engage nonphysician groups, such as genetic counselors, nurses, feeding specialists, respiratory therapists, and pharmacists. Some require active recruitment, especially if they are strategically positioned yet lack QI experience.

QI should be embedded in core work, not reserved for those with academic support or done on personal time. Some have said, QI is not extra work; it is THE work. Contributions should be recognized in promotion, with impact measured beyond publication alone.

Build a System to Support Initiatives (Grow the Flowers)

Programmatic support for individual projects and a system’s capacity for change will be finite. Therefore, initiatives in our center complete a written proposal loosely guided by the Institute of Healthcare Improvement Model for Improvement. (See Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the QI project concept proposal document, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A749.)10 Proposals undergo rigorous review by our Heart Center Quality and Value Committee for feedback and identification of proper support. A structured review ensures equitable allocation of resources and appropriate prioritization. When a centralized review process is not incorporated, there is a risk of inefficiency and change fatigue.

Once projects have been approved, we use a structured project management process with an assigned coach to provide guidance from conception through maintenance (Fig. 4). The coaches are all quality committee members and provide updates and garner input from the group at large.

Promote QI Work and Successes (Sell the Flowers)

It is essential to share and promote QI work internally and externally. There should be venues within the heart center for teams to share their work, address barriers, collaborate, and be celebrated. Hospital and academic department priorities should be considered when designing initiatives. If work can impact hospital priorities, such as US News and World Report, Nursing Magnet, or the Joint Commission, it is more likely to garner support. The work can then gain broader recognition, and the program positions itself to secure additional resources.

Learning sessions are great opportunities to promote work. Our program accomplishes this through activities as simple as discussing QI publications in existing journal clubs, providing regular follow-ups during performance rounds, and offering cyclical program/subdivision updates. We have found tremendous impact from an annual Heart Center Quality Day. The day includes a kickoff morning, unit visits, and a poster session, providing valuable opportunities to showcase work and connect with colleagues and leadership. The poster session included 14 posters in 2023 and 16 in 2024. In 2024, 14 of 16 posters (87.5%) were presented by nonfaculty members (nurses, feeding specialists, APPs, genetic counselors, and clinical fellows).

In academic medicine, scholarly products are necessary for some to incentivize involvement. Therefore, a process is necessary to support conference presentations and publication. The priority for scholarly work should be tempered by what is best for the heart center. Some work is important yet difficult to publish, and some is publishable yet internally less valuable. This is where our Quality and Value Committee assists by setting priorities and reviewing proposed initiatives to strike this balance and steer resources.

Maintain Improvement and Grow the Program (Sustain and Scale the Garden)

A successful QI program must build a system to sustain over time and scale initiatives across the organization. We use a program management system maintained by quality leadership. It organizes each project’s proposal, supporting documents, and status. The sustainment process is outlined in a maintenance document. (See Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the project maintenance form, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A750.) We are continuing to further build out this portion of the program and fully integrate existing work.

Engaging with entities outside the heart center strengthens the program. We have forged partnerships with other divisions, specifically neonatology, critical care, palliative care medicine, neurology, radiology, behavioral health, and emergency medicine. Involvement with learning collaboratives, registries, and national organizations offers many opportunities: (1) multisite improvement work (often with supporting resources), (2) reliable outside data access, (3) social networks, (4) learning and speaking opportunities, and (5) national reputation and notoriety. Finally, program leaders should actively cultivate relationships with quality programs at other centers to foster collaboration in infrastructure development, overcome challenges, and explore opportunities for benchmarking and joint initiatives.

RE-AIM ANALYSIS

The RE-AIM framework evaluates interventions across 5 domains: Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance.11,12 It offers a comprehensive lens to assess programs across diverse populations and real-world settings.

Reach

“Reach” examines how well a program engages its intended populations. Our QI program emphasizes inclusivity, with participation from nurses, APPs, pharmacists, and 17 subgroups from 2021 to 2025. We held 21 “QI consults,” of which 15 (71%) became formal projects. Our involvement in learning collaboratives and registries has enabled recruitment of other centers into local efforts and participation in multicenter work. By lowering barriers to entry and emphasizing diversity, we have engaged a wide range of professionals. However, we continue to struggle to fully engage programs and subsections with limited time and/or QI interest. An isolated, project-focused approach has been unsuccessful and often leads to a lack of ownership. We are currently attempting a more widespread programmatic approach to better address underlying barriers and set clear standards across teams. Additionally, we intend to further engage senior hospital leadership to encourage further growth.

Effectiveness

This domain evaluates impact and unintended effects. Our structured project management and review systems have driven progress. Between 2021 and 2025, we wrote or contributed to 13 peer-reviewed publications (69% internal single-site work), with 2 more under review, and more than 30 national conference presentations.6,13–24 In addition, the QI program is supporting 15 impactful, ongoing, unpublished works, resulting in 12 national abstract presentations in 2021–2025, such as intravenous potassium replacement, line-associated internal jugular thrombus reduction, inpatient nursing burnout, and appropriate genetic consultation.

Across these studies, measurable improvements in outcomes, resource use, and care standardization were achieved. The interdigitating technique for repair of aortic arch obstruction reduced reintervention rates from 29% to 8% within 1 year.13 A shortened fat-modified diet protocol for postoperative chylothorax decreased median diet duration from 30 to 17 days.14 A nasotracheal intubation strategy reduced accidental extubation rates from 14.7 to 2.3 per 100 ventilator days,15 and a surgical wound infection prevention bundle decreased rates from 3.8% to 1.8%.16 Diagnostic testing in pediatric syncope was reduced by 31%, including a 79% decrease in electroencephalogram use.17 A multicenter chest tube initiative shortened median duration by 1 day and reduced length of stay by 2 days.18 Bedside benchmark dashboards helped sites reduce care variation by up to 50% in selected groups.19 A palliative care consultation QI initiative increased appropriate cardiac intensive care unit referral rates from 3.5% to 30%.23 A national survey revealed a 3-fold increase in cardiac intensive care unit QI publication rates during the prior decade.20 Specific longer term effectiveness will be judged by sustained outcomes, and more work is still required to fully capture actionable events.

There are composite quality metrics that are difficult to attribute to individual projects, but the capacity for accumulating opportunities, as well as a culture of improvement, are likely necessary contributions to addressing broader metrics. Our 4-year combined adjusted surgical mortality has been favorable in recent years, decreasing from 2.59% in 2016–2020 to 2.17% in 2021–2024 (observed-to-expected mortality decreased from 0.97 to 0.82). Additionally, our overall US News & World Report ranking improved from 44th to 12th with outcome rankings specifically improving from 56th to 12th. At least 3 discrete contributing efforts were in hospital length of stay, central line-associated bloodstream infections, and neurodevelopmental follow-up. Through performance rounding from 2022 to 2025, we accumulated 170 improvement ideas ranging from simple discrete learning opportunities (eg, arch branching in preoperative echocardiograms) to formal multidisciplinary improvement initiatives (eg, inpatient mental health support, identification of higher risk outpatients, and reducing necrotizing enterocolitis).

Adoption

Our program has been adopted broadly. Participating teams have included cardiac anesthesia, adult congenital cardiology, feeding specialists, nurse navigation, Fontan program, critical care teams, acute care teams, outpatient cardiology, cardiac surgery, genetic consultation, palliative care medicine, neurology, and neonatology. Low-barrier “QI consults” and annual themes simplify participation. Continued monitoring will help identify underengaged groups needing tailored support.

Implementation

Implementation has been systematic, with annual goals, monthly meetings, and structured coaching. Allocating protected time, especially for nursing-led efforts, ensures equity. Due to our robust project review process, we successfully procured nonclinical hours for nurse-led projects approved by our Quality and Value Committee. Nursing QI is crucial, yet nurses are often unfairly asked to complete this work during clinical duties or on their own time.

Cost is an important consideration. Our major cost areas include full-time equivalent (FTE) support, data/statistical assistance, training, learning collaborative/registry involvement, and travel funds. Our FTE allocation includes 0.1 for a quality director and 2.5 FTEs for quality specialists (with APP/nursing backgrounds). Funding is jointly covered by the cardiology division and the hospital system. Quality specialist time for project management, program development, and statistical support is offset by more tangible needs, such as registry/collaborative data abstraction, clinical care, and coordination of heart center activities. The heart center uses a 0.5 FTE data manager supported by the hospital. This person works on both traditional research and QI projects. Much of our statistical support comes from within our committee. The cost for QI training opportunities can vary widely. Providers are often partially removed from clinical work to participate in QI work. Involvement in learning collaboratives and registries is important for transparency, improved outcomes, and external benchmarking but can be quite costly and should be accounted for in budgeting. However, this cost further emphasizes the importance of an internal structure to leverage this resource. Finally, we regularly send team members to national conferences to present their work and lead sessions. Our service line administrative leader ensures we remain fiscally responsible and financially viable.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of well-described limitations of voluntary event reporting, which underestimate safety events compared with structured detection.25,26 The Global Assessment of Pediatric Patient Safety study, a multicenter analysis across 16 children’s hospitals, represents the most rigorous assessment of pediatric adverse events, identifying substantially higher rates than voluntary reporting.27 Subsequent Global Assessment of Pediatric Patient Safety analyses demonstrated that reliance on traditional reporting may obscure racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic disparities.28 Similar underdetection has been observed in adults, particularly vulnerable groups.29 Accordingly, our findings likely represent a conservative estimate of harm and underscore the importance of complementary, structured detection approaches when interpreting improvement opportunities and results.

Maintenance

To sustain progress, we rely on a project-tracking system, formal sustainment processes, and leadership development. Continued engagement with hospital departments and national collaboratives supports long-term momentum. However, our center still faces challenges in maintaining improvement and managing QI team member turnover. Some projects struggle to maintain success as interest wanes and formal coaching concludes. To address this, we are solidifying our maintenance structure and empowering teams more during the coaching period. Investing in QI education will always be a priority for our team. However, it has not consistently resulted in ongoing QI involvement or employee retention. To address this issue, we are drafting written expectations for those seeking QI training to improve accountability.

WHEN RESOURCES ARE LIMITED

Implementing an inclusive QI program in a pediatric heart center is challenging, and we recognize that resources allotted to our program are not universal. It is recommended to start with a realistic scope and be transparent with leadership on what is feasible. A quality program could start with a small group seeking very specific improvements, and with success, more enthusiasm can grow. Effective communication of positive results to those responsible for resources is how reinvestment and growth will ultimately occur. QI initiatives aligned with institutional goals (US News and World Report rankings, Nurse Magnet Status, Joint Commission requirements, etc.) are more likely to find resources and attention, and comparison to national benchmarks (especially programs that compete within your catchment area) can be impactful. Additional support personnel can be obtained by requesting partial FTEs combined with other tangible patient-directed tasks, such as having a QI specialist who is also a single ventricle program coordinator. Improvement efforts that begin on a small scale and hold significance for the team can often be achieved with limited resources, and demonstrating early success can lead to additional support.

CONCLUSIONS

Building a QI program requires thoughtful planning, clear processes, and resource stewardship. By creating a solid foundation, leveraging cross-disciplinary leadership, and promoting diverse participation, heart centers can enhance outcomes and innovation. Although multisite learning collaboratives and registries have offered more comprehensive data and shared resources for quality work, we must continue to build an infrastructure for improvement within our own institutions to meet unique needs and better contribute to broader outside efforts. By sharing insights from our experience, we offer a flexible framework that others can adapt and strengthen to improve care.
