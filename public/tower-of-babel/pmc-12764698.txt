The effects of adding decitabine to conditioning regimen for patients with acute myeloid leukemia or myelodysplastic syndrome undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chengxin Luo, Jianmin Zhang, Xiangtao Huang, Guixian Wu, Yarui Huang, Yaqun Ding, Zhen Huang, Qiuyue Song, Jieping Chen, Xi Li, Shuangnian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDecitabine shows favorable efficacy in induction and maintenance therapy for patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), but its role in pre-transplant conditioning still has not been established. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative studies investigating the effects of adding decitabine to conditioning regimens in AML/MDS patients undergoing allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Meta-analysis was conducted with Review Manager 5.4. For time-to-event outcomes, pooled hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using generic inverse-variance method. For dichotomous data, pooled risk ratios (RRs) were calculated using Mantel-Haenszel method. Fixed-effects model was adopted if there is no significant heterogeneity. Literature search and study selection identify 9 eligible studies, including 2 randomized controlled trials and 7 retrospective comparative studies. Meta-analysis show that addition of decitabine to conditioning regimen is associated with significantly better overall survival (HR 0.61; 95% CI: 0.49–0.76; P < 0.00001), reduced risk of relapse (HR 0.58; 95% CI: 0.46–0.73; P < 0.00001), and better disease-free survival (HR 0.67; 95% CI: 0.55–0.81; P < 0.0001) without increasing non-relapse mortality (HR 0.82; 95% CI: 0.56–1.18; P = 0.28) and grade II-IV acute graft-versus-host disease (RR 0.75; 95% CI: 0.54–1.04; P = 0.08).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06342-w.Keywords: Decitabine, Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Conditioning, Acute myeloid leukemia, Myelodysplastic syndrome

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) are two heterogeneous but closely associated myeloid neoplasms. AML is characterized by clonal expansion of abnormally differentiated blasts of myeloid lineage, resulting in accumulation of blasts and impairment of normal hemopoiesis [1]. MDS is characterized by myelodysplasia, ineffective hematopoiesis, cytopenia, and high risk of progression to AML [2]. Despite significant advances in chemotherapy and targeted therapy, relapse remains the leading cause of treatment failure and death in patients with AML or MDS [1]. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) provides a key consolidation strategy for patients with high-risk disease and the only potentially curative option for patients with relapsed and refractory disease [3–5]. For patients undergoing allo-HSCT, conditioning therapy before the infusion of allogeneic grafts is crucial to the success of transplantation. The aims of pre-transplant conditioning therapy include eradicating malignant cells and suppressing recipient’s immune response to ensure successful engraftment of donor cells [6]. The choice of conditioning regimen significantly affect incidence of post-transplant relapse and transplant-related mortality (TRM) [7, 8]. Optimizing conditioning regimen represents an important strategy to improve post-transplant outcomes of patients with AML/MDS undergoing allo-HSCT.

Epigenetic alterations, especially aberrant DNA methylation, play an important role in the pathogenesis of AML and MDS [2, 9]. Mutations in genes involved in DNA methylation such as genes encoding DNA methyltransferase 3 A (DNMT3A) and isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 (IDH1/2) were frequently detected in patients with AML or MDS, leading to changes in DNA methylation pattern [10]. Decitabine (DAC, 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine) is a hypomethylating agent with promising antileukemic activity and well-tolerated toxicities that has been approved for the treatment of AML, MDS and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia [11, 12]. Decitabine-based induction therapy achieved favorable clinical responses and robust mutation clearance in AML/MDS patients with unfavorable-risk cytogenetic profiles [12, 13]. As for patients undergoing allo-HSCT, decitabine is an attractive agent for pre-transplant conditioning therapy due to its synergistic effects with alkylating agents that commonly used in conditioning (such as busulfan and cyclophosphamide), its immunomodulatory properties and potentials of enhancing graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect [14–18]. Evidences from single-arm clinical studies suggested that incorporation of decitabine into conditioning regimens is a feasible strategy that may reduce post-transplant relapse risk in patients with AML/MDS [17–19]. Then a series of comparative studies were conducted to investigate the effects of decitabine-included conditioning regimens on post-transplant outcomes, but the role of decitabine in conditioning therapy still has not been established since inconsistent results were reported. In this study, we perform a systematic review and meta-analysis of all eligible comparative studies investigating the effects of adding decitabine to conditioning regimen in patients with AML/MDS undergoing allo-HSCT, aiming to establish that whether addition of decitabine can indeed decrease relapse risk and improve survival.

Methods

Literature search and study selection

This study was conducted and reported according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [20]. We performed literature search with databases including MEDLINE (by Ovid), Embase, Cochrane Library and China Biology Medicine (CBM) on June 30, 2022 with no date and language restriction. The search procedure was repeated on May 11, 2023. The main search terms included “acute myeloid leukemia”, “myelodysplastic syndrome”, “hematopoietic stem cell transplantation”, “conditioning”, and “decitabine”, combing MeSH terms and free-text words. References lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were manually checked for additional studies.

Two investigators (C.X.L and J.M.Z) independently screened retrieved citations and assessed the eligibility, any disagreements were resolved by discussing with a third investigator (S.N.X). Studies fulfilling the following inclusion criteria are included: (1) including patients with AML or MDS undergoing allo-HSCT; (2) study design is comparative studies, including both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and nonrandomized comparative studies (prospective or retrospective); (3) investigating the effects of adding decitabine to conditioning regimens on pre-transplant outcomes. Single-arm studies, case reports or case series were excluded. Studies including ≥ 10% patients with other diseases without providing subgroup data for AML/MDS patients were excluded to reduce heterogeneity.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators (C.X.L and J.M.Z) independently extracted data from eligible studies with predesigned forms. Data on study characteristics (first author, publication year, study design, number of participants), patients’ characteristics (age, gender, disease status), conditioning regimens (backbone regimens, dosage of decitabine), median follow-up and post-transplant outcomes were extracted. The primary outcome is overall survival (OS), secondary outcomes include cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR), disease-free survival (DFS), non-relapse mortality (NRM), incidences of acute or chronic graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) and other common adverse events. For time-to-event data, hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted. The ln(HR) and standard error (SE) were calculated directly with HR and 95% CI, or estimated from the log-rank P value and the number of events with the methods previously established by Tierney et al. [21]. For trials with multiple publications, data were extracted from the most comprehensive one and the report with longest follow-up. The extracted data were cross-checked to reach a consensus between the two investigators.

Methodological quality of included RCTs were assessed with the Cochrane risk of bias tool basing on random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, and selective outcome reporting [22]. Methodological quality of included non-randomized comparative studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) basing on selection of patients, comparability of cohorts, and outcome assessment [23]. The maximum total NOS score is 9, studies with scores lower than 5 are classified as low-quality, 6–7 as intermediate-quality, and 8–9 as high-quality.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with Review Manager version 5.3 (Revman; the Cochrane Collaboration; Oxford, England). For time-to-event data including OS, CIR, DFS and NRM, the pooled HRs and 95% CIs were calculated using the generic inverse-variance method. For dichotomous data such as incidences of acute and chronic GVHD, pooled risk ratios (RRs) and 95% CIs were obtained using the Mantel-Haenszel method. Heterogeneity was assessed with Chi-square test and I2 statistic. Fixed-effects model was adopted when there is no significant heterogeneity (I2 < 50%, P ≥ 0.1), otherwise random-effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome was performed via excluding studies with high risk of bias. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the influences of study design, underlying disease, dose of decitabine and backbone regimens. Publication bias was assessed and visualized with funnel plot. Two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of included trials

Databases searching retrieved 281 potentially relevant records. We removed 44 duplicates, screened title/abstract of the remaining citations and excluded 202 records accordingly. Full text of the remaining 35 citations were retrieved for further assessment. After excluding 4 single-arm trials, 12 retrospective non-comparative studies, 1 study including other diseases and 2 studies comparing decitabine-included regimens with regimens that intensified with other agents, we ultimately included 16 records (6 conferences abstract and 10 full-text article) for 9 eligible studies [24–33]. The detailed dosage of decitabine and backbone conditioning regimens in all included studies were summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The backbone regimens include myeloablative conditioning (MAC) regimens BuCy (busulfan plus cyclophosphamide), mBuCy (modified BuCy: 3-day busulfan plus cyclophosphamide and cytarabine, with or without semustine and hydroxycarbamide), mBuFlu (modified busulfan plus fludarabine), BuCyFlu, CyTBI (cyclophosphamide plus total body irradiation), and the reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) regimen Bu2Flu (2-day busulfan plus fludarabine). The total doses of decitabine range from 60 to 125 mg/m2, the most commonly used dose was 100 mg/m2 (20 mg/m2/day for 5 days in 5 studies). Two RCTs additionally incorporated granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) or N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) into decitabine-included regimen. For patients receiving transplants from unrelated donor or haploidentical donor, antithymocyte immunoglobulin (ATG) at a total dose of 6–10 mg/kg was administrated as a part of conditioning regimen and GVHD prophylaxis regimen. No included study reported the use of post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy).

Quality assessment results of the included RCTs are provided in Supplementary Table S2. One of the 2 RCTs is open-label (high risk of bias regarding blinding of participants and personnel), no other evidences of high risk of bias were identified. The results of quality assessment for the 7 non-randomized studies are provided in the Supplementary Table S3. Three studies reported differences in patient’s age, disease status or disease risk between two study groups. The NOS scores are 7–9, all included studies are medium- to high-quality.

Overall survival

The median follow-up time after transplantation range from 5 to 70 months. OS was reported in all the 9 included studies. The funnel plot was symmetrical, indicating low risk of publication bias (Supplementary Figure S1). Meta-analysis was conducted with fixed-effect model since there is no significant heterogeneity (I2 = 6%, P = 0.39), results show that adding decitabine to conditioning regimen is associated with significant improvement in OS (HR 0.61; 95% CI: 0.49–0.76; P < 0.00001) (Fig. 2A). The analysis was repeated in patients with high-risk disease, which included patients with high-risk cytogenetic profiles, relapsed or refractory disease, secondary AML, and high-risk MDS identified by international prognostic scoring system (IPSS) or revised IPSS (IPSS-R) [26, 27, 30]. Results show that addition of decitabine to conditioning regimen is associated with significantly improved OS (HR 0.54; 95% CI: 0.40–0.72; P < 0.0001) in patients with high-risk disease (Supplementary Figure S2A).

Forest plot of meta-analysis results for OS and CIR. (A) OS. (B) CIR. CIR, cumulative incidence of relapse. DAC, decitabine. OS, overall survival

Sensitivity analysis was performed via excluding the study with significant differences in patient characteristics and backbone regimens between two groups, and similar results were obtained (Supplementary Figure S2B). Subgroup analyses were conducted according to study design, underlying disease, total dose of decitabine and backbone regimens. Both analyses in RCTs and retrospective studies shows significantly improvements of OS in decitabine group (Supplementary Figure S3A). In addition, decitabine-included conditioning is associated with significantly better OS in both AML subgroup (HR 0.50; 95% CI: 0.35–0.71; P = 0.0001) and MDS or transformed AML (tAML) subgroup (HR 0.62; 95% CI: 0.44–0.87; P = 0.007) (Supplementary Figure S3B). As for total dose of decitabine, the improvement in OS is statistically significant in higher-dose (100–125 mg/m2) subgroup (HR 0.56; 95% CI: 0.43–0.72; P < 0.0001), but not in lower-dose (60–75 mg/m2) subgroup (HR 0.76; 95% CI: 0.51–1.13; P = 0.18), although the subgroup difference is not statistically significant (P = 0.20) (Supplementary Figure S4). Subgroup analyses according to backbone regimens suggest that addition of decitabine is associated with significantly better OS when combined with MAC regimen mBuCy (HR 0.47; 95% CI: 0.34–0.66; P < 0.0001) and BuCy (HR 0.61; 95% CI: 0.38–0.98; P = 0.04), but not when combined with BuCyFlu and the RIC regimen Bu2Flu (Supplementary Figure S5-S6, Supplementary Table S4).

Cumulative incidence of relapse and disease-free survival

Data for CIR were provided by the 9 included studies. Meta-analysis with fixed-effect model suggests that adding decitabine to conditioning regimen is associated with significantly reduced risk of relapse (HR 0.58; 95% CI: 0.46–0.73; P < 0.00001) (Fig. 2B). Repeated analysis in patients with high-risk disease obtained similar results (Supplementary Figure S7A). Results of subgroup analyses are shown in Supplementary Figure S8-12 and summarized in Supplementary Table S5. Subgroup analyses in RCTs and retrospective studies obtained similar results. As for underlying disease, addition of decitabine is associated with significantly reduced risk of relapse both in AML (HR 0.60; 95% CI: 0.44–0.83; P = 0.002) and MDS/tAML (HR 0.48; 95% CI: 0.29–0.78; P = 0.003). In addition, the decrease in CIR is statistically significant when used in higher dose (HR 0.53; 95% CI: 0.40–0.69; P < 0.00001), and when combined with mBuCy (HR 0.53; 95% CI: 0.39–0.73; P < 0.0001) or BuCy (HR 0.39; 95% CI: 0.19–0.79; P = 0.009), but not significant in lower-dose subgroup and when combined with BuCyFlu or RIC regimen Bu2Flu (Supplementary Table S5).

Data for DFS or leukemia-free survival (LFS) were reported in 7 studies. For the 2 other studies, HR for progression-free survival (PFS) was used as proxy in meta-analysis. Meta-analysis with fixed-effect model shows that adding decitabine to conditioning regimen is associated with significantly improved DFS (HR 0.67; 95% CI: 0.55–0.81; P < 0.0001). Repeated analysis in patients with high-risk disease obtained similar results (Supplementary Figure S7B). Subgroup analyses indicate that addition of decitabine is associated with significant improved DFS both in AML (HR 0.72; 95% CI: 0.53–0.97; P = 0.03) and MDS/tAML (HR 0.60; 95% CI: 0.43–0.83; P = 0.002) (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S13-14). Significant improvement of DFS was observed when used in higher dose (HR 0.62; 95% CI: 0.50–0.78; P < 0.0001), when combined with mBuCy (HR 0.59; 95% CI: 0.45–0.78; P = 0.0002) or BuCy (HR 0.58; 95% CI: 0.37–0.91; P = 0.02), but not in lower-dose subgroup and when combined with BuCyFlu or Bu2Flu (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S15-17).

Non-relapse mortality and common cause of death

Data for NRM are available from 7 included studies. Meta-analysis with fixed-effect model suggests that adding decitabine to conditioning regimen did not increase NRM (HR 0.82; 95% CI: 0.56–1.18; P = 0.28) (Fig. 3B). Subgroup analyses according to underlying disease, decitabine dose and backbone regimen show that adding decitabine to conditioning regimen did not increase the risk of NRM in any subgroups (Supplementary Table S7, Supplementary Figure S18-22). Meta-analyses results for common causes of death are shown in Supplementary Figure S23. The DAC group is associated with significantly lower relapse-related mortality (RR 0.52; 95% CI: 0.38–0.71; P < 0.0001). No significant differences in infection-, GVHD-, and graft rejection-related mortality were observed (P > 0.05).

Forest plot of meta-analysis results for DFS and NRM. (A) DFS. (B) NRM. DAC, decitabine. DFS, disease-free survival. NRM, non-relapse mortality

GVHD and other adverse events

Meta-analyses for incidences of grade II-IV and grade III-IV acute GVHD were conducted with random-effect models due to significant heterogeneity. No statistically significant difference in risk of grade II-IV acute GVHD (RR 0.75; 95% CI: 0.54–1.04; P = 0.08; Fig. 4A) or grade III-IV acute GVHD (RR 0.81; 95% CI: 0.51–1.29; P = 0.38; Supplementary Figure S24) was observed between the DAC and Non-DAC group. As for chronic GVHD, meta-analysis with fixed-effect model show no significant difference between the DAC and Non-DAC group (RR 1.01; 95% CI: 0.84–1.21; P = 0.92) (Fig. 4B). Meta-analyses results for other common adverse events that reported by ≥ 2 included studies were summarized in Supplementary Table S8. The DAC group is associated with higher risk of mucositis (RR 1.64; 95% CI: 1.10–2.24; P = 0.01), no significant difference regarding risk of fever, infection, respiratory infection, infection-related death, cytomegalovirus (CMV) viremia, hemorrhagic cystitis, nausea/vomiting, diarrhea, or hepatic and renal function impairment was observed.

Discussion

This study performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 eligible comparative studies investigating the effects of adding decitabine to conditioning regimens and demonstrated that addition of decitabine is associated with significantly improved OS and DFS, and reduced risk of relapse without increasing NRM and incidences of GVHD in AML/MDS patients undergoing allo-HSCT, even in patients with high-risk disease. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis integrating evidences from comparative studies to establish the benefits of decitabine in conditioning therapy before allo-HSCT. Post-transplant relapse is a major reason of treatment failure, intensified myeloablative conditioning can reduce the risk of post-transplant relapse, but at cost of increased non-relapse morbidity and mortality [34, 35]. Our results suggest that addition of decitabine represents a appealing strategy to decrease post-transplant relapse risk without increasing NRM. These results will provide important clues for clinicians and researchers regarding strategies of optimizing conditioning regimen and improve post-transplant outcomes.

The most common administration schedule of decitabine for conditioning therapy is 20 mg/m2/day for 5 days (total dose 100 mg/m2) before the initiation of backbone regimens, which is considered safe without inducing severe toxicity. Our subgroup analyses results suggested that decitabine in total dose that lower than 100 mg/m2 did not show benefits of improving post-transplant outcomes. The highest dose used in our included studies is 125 mg/m2 (75 mg/m2 on day − 9 and 50 mg/m2 on day − 8), which showed significant advantages over a lower-dose schedule of 60 mg/m2 (20 mg/m2 for 3 days) in a retrospective study, but it remains unclear that whether it is superior to the commonly used dose 100 mg/m2 due to the lack of direct comparison [25]. Recently, a retrospective study reported that 7-day decitabine (20 mg/m2/day) was associated with significantly accelerated engraftment and lower incidence of oral mucositis without increasing infectious complications and NRM in comparison with 5-day decitabine [36]. There are no evidences about extra benefits from further prolonged administration time or further increase in total dose. Besides, increase in risk of infection and infection-related death that are potentially associated with higher-dose decitabine raised concerns [37]. Potential increase of infection risk such as CMV reactivation is a major concern regarding incorporation of decitabine into pre-transplant conditioning regimens. Zhang et al. reported that addition of decitabine to modified BuCy was associated with slightly higher incidence of CMV reactivation (55.8% versus 43.9%) after allo-HSCT in patients with MDS [32]. Other studies did not report significant difference regarding incidence of CMV reactivation or other types of infection. Our meta-analyses show similar risk in CMV viremia, infection and infection-related death between the decitabine and non-decitabine group. Significant increase was only observed in mucositis incidence among the common adverse events reported by included studies (Supplementary Table S8). Higher risk of grade ≥ 3 infection (55%) were reported by a phase 2 trial, in which 10-day decitabine-based induction therapy was bridged to allo-HSCT and PTCy, using a MAC regimen consisting of BuFlu and 4-Gy TBI [37]. Therefore, further increase of total dose and extension of decitabine administration schedule should be in cautious.

As for the intensity of backbone conditioning regimen, the results of our subgroup analyses suggest that addition of decitabine is associated with significantly better OS, lower risk of relapse and better DFS when combined with MAC regimens, but no significant benefits are observed when combined with RIC regimens, although the subgroup differences are not statistically significant (P values for subgroup differences > 0.05, Supplementary Table S5-8). Most of the included studies used MAC regimens BuCy or modified BuCy as the backbone conditioning regimen, there only one eligible study investigating the efficacy of adding decitabine to RIC regimen (Bu2Flu) and the sample size is relatively small (the number of patients in decitabine group is 36). Therefore, more researches are required to investigate the influence of conditioning regimen intensity on the efficacy of decitabine and to determine the optimal backbone regimen.

The mechanism of decreasing post-transplant relapse risk by adding decitabine to conditioning regimens may mainly involve more efficient eradication of residual malignant cells before transplantation and enhanced GVL effects after allografting. Single-agent therapy with decitabine has been proved have favorable antileukemic activity even in high-risk AML/MDS patients [12, 13]. Combination of decitabine with alkylating agents that commonly used in conditioning therapy such as busulfan and cyclophosphamide showed synergistic cytotoxicity in vitro [15, 38]. In addition, decitabine can overcome drug resistance and enhance apoptosis in AML cells through upregulating epigenetically silenced proapoptotic genes [14, 39]. Results from clinical studies showed efficient measurable residual disease (MRD) eradication and mutation clearance after completion of decitabine-contained conditioning, and successful conversion of MRD to negative before transplantation predicts lower relapse risk and better OS after allo-HSCT [18, 40]. Above all, incorporation of decitabine into pre-transplant conditioning is supposed to reduce relapse risk by promoting MRD eradication before infusion of allografts.

Potentials of enhancing GVL effects by decitabine were proposed due to its ability of regulating T cell- and natural killer (NK) cell-mediated antitumor immunity. Loss of surface expression of human leukocyte antigens (HLAs) and dysregulation of costimulatory signals abrogate donor T cell-mediated allorecognition and drive immune escape of malignant cells, representing main mechanisms of post-transplant relapse in AML patients undergoing allo-HSCT [41, 42]. Administration of decitabine in vitro is capable of restoring HLAs and tumor associated antigens (TAAs) expression on cancer cells, upregulating costimulatory molecules, and eliciting tumor-specific T-cell response [43–48]. As for NK cell, low-dose decitabine can promote NK cells proliferation and activation in vitro, and enhance immune recognition and cytolytic functions of NK cells in murine AML model through upregulating activating receptors of NK cells and their ligands on leukemia cells [49, 50]. In high-risk AML patients underwent allo-HSCT, post-transplant administration of low-dose decitabine in combination with G-CSF significantly increased the number of NK cells in peripheral blood, accompanied by decrease of relapse risk [51]. However, excessive increase in frequencies of NK cells (≥ 70%) was associated with inferior DFS as reported by a retrospective study [19]. Future investigations are required to establish the benefits of decitabine in modulating T cell- and NK cell-mediated GVL effect and to optimize decitabine-based therapy strategies in patients undergoing allo-HSCT.

As for GVHD, results from our meta-analysis suggested that incorporation of decitabine into conditioning did not increase risk of acute or chronic GVHD and GVHD-related death. Administration of hypomethylating agents decitabine or azacitidine can upregulate FOXP3 expression, increase regulatory T (Treg) cells and mitigate GVHD without undermining GVL effects in murine allo-HSCT model [52, 53]. A retrospective matched-pair study reported that adding decitabine to modified BuCy was associated with significantly lower 100-day cumulative incidence of grade II-IV acute GVHD (15.2% versus 32.6%) and better GVHD-free relapse-free survival (GRFS) (2-year GRFS: 58.7% versus 40.9%) in AML patients [26]. Most of the other studies included in our meta-analysis reported comparable incidences of GVHD between the decitabine and non-decitabine group. Better understanding of mechanisms involved in decitabine-mediated modulation of T cell function and its impacts on GVHD are required to ensure optimal utilization of decitabine.

There are some limitations in this study. Firstly, the follow-up period are relatively short in some of the included studies. The long-term benefits of decitabine-included conditioning require to be established with data from future longer follow-up reports. Secondly, we included several retrospective studies in our analysis and the sample size of several included studies are relatively small. Although all of the included studies are medium- to high- quality, the retrospective nature of these studies may increase heterogeneity. There are only 2 RCTs investigating the effects of adding decitabine to conditioning regimen published when our manuscript is completed. Future prospective studies with large sample size and update of our analyses with data from these future studies will be helpful to further establish the efficacy and safety of decitabine-included conditioning. Lastly, the post-transplant maintenance therapy strategies were not reported in included studies. It remains unclear that whether the benefits of decitabine relied on continuous administration after transplantation. Future researches are required to optimize the decitabine-based treatment strategy in AML/MDS patients undergoing allo-HSCT.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 eligible comparative studies indicated that addition of decitabine to conditioning regimen is associated with significantly improved OS and reduced CIR without increasing NRM in patients with AML/MDS undergoing allo-HSCT.
