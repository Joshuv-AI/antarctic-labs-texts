Acute encephalitis syndrome and Japanese encephalitis in India: insights from a nationally representative laboratory surveillance network, 2014–2023
Authors: Rizwan Suliankatchi Abdulkader, Sabarinathan Ramasamy, Raman Swathy Vaman, Gajanan N Sapkal, Jitendra Narayan, Neetu Vijay, Vanessa Ravel, Sinthanai Selvan Palanisamy, Gururaj Rao Deshpande, Nivedita Gupta, Manoj V Murhekar, VRDLN

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute Encephalitis Syndrome (AES) is a critical public health issue, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality in India, particularly among children. Despite ongoing vaccination efforts, Japanese Encephalitis (JE) remains a major cause of AES. This study analyzes the epidemiology of AES and JE in India from 2014 to 2023, using data from the Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory Network (VRDLN).MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was conducted on AES cases reported by 159 VRDLN laboratories between 2014 and 2023. Standardized diagnostic tests such as polymerase chain reaction were used to identify causative pathogens, including JE virus. All the labs in the network followed BSL-2 standards. Descriptive analyses were performed on AES cases by year, etiology, and demographic factors. JE cases were further analyzed for geographic distribution, age, sex, and clinical characteristics.ResultsOver the study period, 124,890 AES cases were reported, with 16.3% testing positive for at least one pathogen, including 10% confirmed as JE. The highest JE positivity was observed in the northeastern states. An increasing trend in the number of cases tested and JE detections was noted, alongside a shift in the age distribution towards younger populations. The median (IQR) annual test positivity for JEV among tested was 16.2% (14.5%, 18.4%).ConclusionsThe VRDLN has substantially enhanced India’s capacity to diagnose and monitor AES and JE. Despite progress, JE transmission persists, particularly in endemic regions, posing significant public health challenges. Strengthened surveillance, improved diagnostic coverage, and integrated pathogen testing are essential for effective interventions. Background

Acute encephalitis syndrome (AES) is defined as “an illness characterized by the acute onset of fever, accompanied by altered consciousness, stupor, coma, and/or new-onset seizures, excluding simple febrile seizures” [1]. AES represents a significant public health challenge, causing substantial morbidity, mortality, and long-term neurological complications in both children and adults. Globally, AES accounted for an estimated 90,000 deaths and 4.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2019 [2].

The etiology of AES is diverse, involving several viral, bacterial, fungal, and parasitic pathogens with distinct epidemiological profiles and geographic distributions. Viral causes include pathogens such as Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), West Nile virus, enteroviruses, dengue virus, herpes simplex virus, varicella-zoster virus, Epstein-Barr virus, Nipah virus, and Chandipura virus. Bacterial pathogens include Rickettsia, Spirochaetes, Listeria, and Leptospira, while parasitic agents such as Plasmodium (malaria), Naegleria fowleri, and fungi like Cryptococcus and Histoplasma have also been implicated [3].

Among these, JEV is globally recognized as the most common cause of AES in children [4]. JEV, a vector-borne flavivirus, is associated with a case fatality rate of 5–30%, and 30–50% of survivors experience long-term neuropsychiatric sequelae [5]. Recent outbreaks of Nipah virus, Chandipura virus, and Naegleria fowleri in India have highlighted the urgent need for better understanding and control of AES. In India, approximately 11,000 AES cases and 1,200 deaths are reported annually, although these figures are likely underestimates, as captured by the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) [6]. Studies across various regions in India have reported pathogen detection rates ranging from 17% to 72% among AES patients [7–10]. Of the reported AES cases, 14–18% are attributed to JEV [11].

A large-scale study in India involving 10,107 AES cases identified JEV, Orientia tsutsugamushi (scrub typhus), and dengue virus as the most common causative agents [12]. JEV was first detected in India in 1952, followed by multiple outbreaks and widespread transmission across states and districts. In response, India initiated JEV vaccination campaigns between 2006 and 2011, integrating the JEV vaccine into the routine immunization schedule in endemic regions by 2013 [13]. Despite extensive vaccination efforts, the incidence of JEV-related AES has not significantly declined, and several outbreaks in recent years have raised concerns about ongoing transmission [14]. However, vaccination programs have demonstrated localized impacts, reducing incidence in endemic areas and shifting the age distribution of JEV cases in India [13, 15].

To enhance diagnostic capacity and strengthen public health responses, the Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory Network (VRDLN) was established under the Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. This network aims to ensure timely diagnosis of viral diseases of clinical and public health importance. This study analyzes the largest comprehensive data on AES and JE surveillance in India, spanning nearly a decade. Unlike previous surveillance studies, which were often region-specific or based on smaller sample size, our study leverages data from a nationwide laboratory network (VRDLN), allowing for a more robust assessment of temporal trends, geographic variations, and diagnostic patterns. In this study, we describe the epidemiological trends of AES and JE cases reported in India from 2014 to 2023 through the VRDLN.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study analyzed laboratory surveillance data from the Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratory Network (VRDLN) collected between 2014 and 2023.

Study population

The study population comprised all AES cases referred to the Virus Research and Diagnostic Laboratories (VRDLs) during the study period.

Data source

The VRDLs are district- and state-level laboratories operating under the VRDLN, established by the Department of Health Research, Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, Government of India. During the study period, 159 VRDLs were operational across India. These laboratories collect patient data using a standardized case report form that includes clinical and epidemiological details, sample collection information, laboratory tests performed, and their results. The VRDLs process clinical samples from both outpatients and inpatients in medical college hospitals, as well as samples from suspected disease outbreaks referred by state health authorities [16]. Data routinely collected by the VRDLN were utilized for this analysis.

Laboratory methods

The VRDLs follow a standardized testing algorithm and employ diagnostic methods such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for detecting viral pathogens. These laboratories are equipped to test for more than 70 pathogens using established protocols. Throughout the study period, in-house National Institute of Virology (NIV) JE IgM kit, commercial IgM ELISA kits complaint with ISO 13,485 were used, which had their specificity and sensitivity validated through clinical studies, as documented in their respective kit inserts [17, 18].

The NIV JE IgM Capture ELISA kit was used to detect IgM antibodies in serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [19]. The samples were diluted (serum − 1:100, CSF − 1:10) as per the protocol, while controls were used undiluted. Anti-human IgM-coated strips were washed, and 50 µL of samples and controls were added to the wells. Plates were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. Wells were then sequentially treated with JE antigen, biotin-labelled anti-JE monoclonal antibody, and avidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP), with appropriate incubations and washes. Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added, and the plate was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Reaction was stopped with 1 N H2SO4, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Quality assurance practices

The VRDLs follow protocol tested and approved by National Institute of Virology, Pune which complies with ISO 15189:2012 standard. Currently, labs are transitioning to ISO 15189:2022 standard. Strict biosafety protocols and workflow segregation were followed to maintain quality control. Protocols were followed during sample collection and transport, ensuring that samples were maintained at a temperature of 2–8 °C.Additionally, reagents were not brought to room temperature during testing to preserve their integrity. To ensure accurate and reliable results, the microplate reader was periodically calibrated in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.

The External Quality Assurance (EQA) program for Japanese Encephalitis (JE) virus assessed the diagnostic accuracy of VRDLs. A panel of six blinded serum samples (two positives, four negatives) was prepared, validated, and distributed on dry ice. Laboratories used JE IgM Capture ELISA kits from ICMR-NIV, Pune, following standardized protocols. Results were evaluated against reference values, assessing concordance, sensitivity, and interlaboratory variation. Labs with < 90% concordance underwent retesting and corrective measures. Continuous monitoring, training, and periodic EQA programs are conducted to maintain diagnostic quality.

Operational definition

AES was defined as the acute onset of fever accompanied by altered mental status (e.g., confusion, disorientation, coma, or inability to talk) and/or new-onset seizures in individuals of any age [1]. A case of Japanese encephalitis (JE) was defined as the detection of IgM antibodies against JEV in either serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of a patient with AES.

Data analysis

De-identified data were extracted from the VRDL database following a standardized process to ensure accuracy and consistency. The raw dataset was first screened for completeness and structural integrity, checking for missing values, duplicate entries, and inconsistencies in key variables such as age, district, date of sample collection, and test results. For missing data, we employed appropriate imputation methods to address gaps in age and district, as detailed in Fig. 1. Specifically, age was imputed using median values within similar demographic and epidemiological strata, while missing district information was inferred based on laboratory location. AES cases were categorized by year and etiology. A subset of AES cases positive for Japanese encephalitis (JE) was further analyzed based on year, geographic location (district, state, and laboratory zone), demographic characteristics (age group and sex), and clinical features.

Data cleaning workflow for the VRDLN lab network in India

To assess the spatial distribution of JE cases, the study period was divided into two intervals: 2014–2018 and 2019–2023. Percent positivity for JE was compared across these intervals, with 2018 selected as the median year to facilitate comparative analysis. Categorical variables were summarized as proportions. To account for the effects of seasonality and the expansion of the number of labs in the network, we modelled the tested counts, positive counts and test positivity using a time-series approach, in which seasonal-trend decomposition based on Loess was applied to the data after taking the ratio of the monthly counts to the number of reporting labs. This approach removed the effect of the seasonality and normalised the data for number of labs. We created time series plots to compare the adjusted and observed results. We performed statistical analyses using R (version 4.3.1). We used the fuzzyjoin package for imputing and cleaning district codes, ggplot2 for visualization and stats for time series modelling. We used QGIS (version 3.34.8). for generating choropleth maps using shapefiles downloaded from the Survey of India website [20].

Description of AES cases

The number of operational VRDLs in India increased significantly, from 20 in 2014 to 159 in 2023, covering 413 districts, including 60 JE-endemic districts. During this period, 1,24,890 AES cases were diagnosed across the network, with 20,376 (16.3%) cases testing positive for at least one pathogen. The number of AES cases tested annually increased from 4,607 in 2014 to 24,244 in 2019, while the test positivity rates ranged from 11.4% in 2014 to a peak of 20% in 2019 (Table 1).

Place distribution

Testing coverage and JE positivity improved during 2014–2023, with 21,931 (34%) samples originating from northern zone laboratories, followed by the northeast zone with 16,054 (25%). The highest positivity rates were observed in the northeast zone (21.2%) (Fig. 3).

District-level test positivity for JE, VRDLN, India, 2014–2023 (N = 65,066)

In the eastern zone, districts such as Khordha (Odisha), Giridih, Hazaribagh, Ranchi (Jharkhand), and Patna (Bihar) reported the highest percent positivity. In the northeast zone, districts including West Tripura, Sepahijala, Gomati (Tripura), Lakhimpur (Assam), and East Khasi Hills (Meghalaya) consistently showed high JE positivity.

In the western zone, districts such as Chandrapur, Nagpur, Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar, Hingoli, and Gadchiroli (Maharashtra) had the highest positivity rates. In the northern zone, districts including Jodhpur (Rajasthan), Karnal, Yamunanagar, Ambala (Haryana), and Shimla (Himachal Pradesh) reported elevated positivity rates.

The central zone saw the highest positivity in districts such as Lucknow, Sitapur, Bahraich, Gonda, and Hardoi (Uttar Pradesh). In the southern zone, Chennai, Villupuram, Cuddalore (Tamil Nadu), Chittoor (Andhra Pradesh), and Puducherry reported the highest percent positivity. These districts consistently reported high positivity rates throughout the study period.

Person distribution

Among AES cases tested for JE, 38,793 (60%) were male and 26,211 (40%) were female, with an equal JE positivity rate of 10% in both groups. The highest number of samples was from individuals aged 10–19 years (11,335; 17.4%). Of the 65,066 cases tested for JE, 6,540 (10.1%) were positive for JEV IgM by ELISA.

Fever was the most common clinical feature, present in 56,653 cases (87.2%), followed by seizures in 24,308 cases (37.4%) and altered sensorium in 18,616 cases (28.7%). Comparing 2014–2018 and 2019–2023, JE positivity increased by 3.1% among children under 10 years and decreased by 2% in the 51–60-year age group.(Fig. 4). The highest percent positivity was observed in the 80–90 years age group in the central zone (33.3%), the 30–40 years age group in the north zone (4.5%), and the 10–20 years age group in the east zone (13%), northeast zone (26.9%), south zone (11.3%), and west zone (27.8%).Among the JE cases, positivity was highest among children aged 6–14 years (13.8%), followed by those aged 45 years and above (11.7%). Both males (10.1%) and females (10.1%) had an identical positivity rate (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

We examined the epidemiology of AES and JE cases reported through the VRDL network in India from 2014 to 2023, making this the first study to analyze 10 years of AES and JE data from the VRDLN since its inception. The VRDLN has significantly enhanced India’s surveillance capacity, ensuring the timely identification and containment of outbreaks, particularly in the face of emerging and re-emerging infectious threats. Over the ten years, the network reported approximately 10 lakh AES cases, with over 50% tested for JE.

Before the establishment of VRDLs, India lacked sufficient capacity for testing viral pathogens and a centralized mechanism to aggregate and analyze etiological data. With the establishment of VRDLs, testing capabilities have expanded to include over 70 pathogens, accompanied by improved geographical coverage of endemic areas and the systematic collection of clinical and epidemiological data to inform public health policies and interventions. The significant increase in AES case testing—rising sixfold between 2014 and 2019—reflects this progress.

The study highlights a few important findings and implications. The positivity rate for AES cases in the VRDLs was 16.3%, lower than the 28.8% reported in a South Indian study [4]. This discrepancy may stem from VRDLs not routinely testing for bacterial, fungal, and non-infectious causes of AES. The ongoing transition of VRDLs to Infectious Diseases Research and Diagnostic Laboratories (IRDLs) offers a promising opportunity to expand testing capabilities to include bacterial, mycological, and parasitological etiologies [21]. Moreover, inconsistent use of case definitions, suboptimal sample collection and transportation, and deviations from testing algorithms might have further contributed to the lower positivity rates. The co-infections identified in our analysis were like previously reported from India [4, 22].

Variations in findings across studies may be attributed to several factors: (1) Our study focused exclusively on viral pathogens, whereas some other studies included a broader range of etiologies, including bacterial and parasitic infections; (2) Temporal shifts in epidemiology, which may influence disease burden and pathogen distribution over time; and (3) Differences in testing algorithms, case definitions, and surveillance methodologies, which can impact case detection rates and overall findings. These factors limit direct comparability between studies and highlight the importance of standardized surveillance approaches.

Among AES cases, positivity was highest for dengue virus, followed by chikungunya virus and JE virus. This finding contrasts with studies from other regions of India, where JE virus was the predominant etiological agent [4, 23, 24]. The increased reporting of JE cases facilitated by VRDLs indicates a previously undetected burden of the disease, emphasizing the need for enhanced surveillance efforts. Though JEV is the most common cause of AES all over India, emerging viral threats such as West Nile virus (WNV), Chandipura virus (CHPV), Kyasanur Forest Disease (KFD), Human enterovirus (EV), Nipah virus and other novel arboviruses pose an increasing risk, contributing to the evolving epidemiology of AES in India [25]. Enhanced surveillance, early diagnostic capabilities, and a One Health approach are crucial to identifying and mitigating the impact of these emerging pathogens.

JE positivity was equally distributed between males and females in this study, diverging from earlier research that reported higher positivity among males due to their greater exposure to vectors during outdoor activities [26, 27]. The large, nationally representative sample size in our study likely minimized the gender disparities observed in earlier institution-based studies.

Many studies conducted previously in India have identified co-infection of JE with other pathogens [7, 10, 22, 28]. The predominant co-circulation of flaviviruses (JEV, dengue, Chikungunya) in our study suggests overlapping transmission dynamics facilitated by shared mosquito vectors, reinforcing the necessity for integrated vector control strategies. The other coinfections of JE with varicella, measles, Scrub typhus, EBS etc. highlights the complexity of AES etiology and the need for multi-pathogen diagnostic approaches in endemic settings.

JE is primarily transmitted by Culex mosquitoes, which breed in water bodies with abundant vegetation, particularly in rice fields. The abundance of these vectors is closely linked to rice cultivation, shallow ditches, and standing water pools, creating ideal breeding sites. Ecological factors, such as changes in land use, expansion of rice paddies, and deforestation, may influence mosquito populations and, consequently, JE transmission dynamics [29, 30]. The regions consistently reporting high JE positivity in our study correspond to areas with a high density of such vector breeding habitats. In addition to ecological drivers, variations in JE vaccination coverage across endemic districts may contribute to differences in positivity rates. Some regions may have higher susceptibility due to lower vaccine uptake or gaps in immunization programs, which could result in higher case numbers. Furthermore, enhanced testing and reporting in these regions, particularly following the establishment of VRDLs, may lead to increased case detection, influencing observed JE positivity rates.

Currently, the inactivated SA 14-14-2 JE vaccine (JEEV®) and the inactivated Vero cell culture-derived Kolar strain (821564XY) JE vaccine (JENVAC®) are administered in a two-dose regimen at 9 months and between 16 and 24 months of age to children in endemic districts as part of UIP and adult vaccination using JENVAC vaccine in 42 districts of Assam, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal [31]. A recent study conducted in the endemic districts of Maharashtra and Telangana has reported a reduction in annual AES and JE incidence in these vaccinated districts [15]. However, in our study we report and increase in percent positivity among children under 10 years old from 2019 to 23. The observed shift in JE distribution toward younger age groups across the two time periods cannot be directly attributed to vaccine coverage for two key reasons: (a) our laboratory network does not collect data on vaccination status, and (b) our study period (2014–2023) falls entirely within the post-vaccination phase, with no pre-vaccination data available for comparison. Consequently, we were unable to assess the specific factors driving this shift. This may also be due to the fact that clinically diagnosed cases referred to the VRDLs are only getting tested for JE.

Strengths and limitations

One of the study’s key strengths is the large dataset, comprising approximately 10 lakh AES cases over a decade, which provides a robust basis for understanding the epidemiological trends of AES and JE in India. The VRDLs’ nationwide distribution, particularly in endemic districts, ensures comprehensive geographical representation, allowing the identification of regional patterns and hotspots. This study offers a decade-long, comprehensive evaluation into the etiology of Acute Encephalitis Syndrome (AES). By examining fluctuations in positivity rates over time, it provides valuable insights into the evolving epidemiology of AES & JE. The study also maps the geographical distribution of testing rates and JE positivity, highlighting regional patterns. A key breakthrough is the exploration of coinfections among AES patients—an aspect largely overlooked in previous surveillance studies. By uncovering the coexistence of multiple pathogens, this study enhances the understanding of AES/JE, laying the foundation for more effective disease control and prevention strategies. The study also demonstrates the utility of VRDLs in capturing the epidemiological landscape of AES and JE, highlighting their importance in India’s infectious disease surveillance framework.

However, our analysis has certain limitations. Since the data were derived from cases reported to VRDLs, it does not allow for estimating the population-level burden of AES and JE. Another limitation of this study is the absence of individual vaccination status data for JE cases. The VRDL network primarily functions as a laboratory surveillance system and does not routinely capture immunization histories of tested individuals. This restricts our ability to assess vaccine effectiveness, identify breakthrough infections, or determine the proportion of JE cases occurring in vaccinated versus unvaccinated individuals. Factors such as variations in vaccine uptake, delays in administration, and differences in immunization strategies across regions could influence JE trends but cannot be accounted for in our analysis. Although VRDLs adhere to SOPs and undergo regular training, variations in algorithmic testing patterns and testing kits may exist across some labs, potentially influencing the observed AES and JE positivity patterns in our study. However, these differences likely reflect real-world variations in testing practices, offering a pragmatic view of the epidemiology of these infections. Among these variations, the application of the AES case definition is likely the least affected, reinforcing our confidence in the reliability and validity of our findings. Furthermore, surveillance-based case detection inherently introduces biases, as severe or hospitalized cases are more likely to be captured, whereas milder or asymptomatic JE infections especially in vaccinated individuals may go undiagnosed. The unavailability of clinical outcome data also restricts the ability to measure pathogen virulence and the overall impact of the disease. Future studies incorporating vaccination history, seroprevalence surveys, and regional vaccine coverage data would be critical in further elucidating the impact of JE immunization programs on disease trends in India.

Conclusions and recommendations

The study underscores the importance of strengthening adherence to standardized case definitions and testing protocols at referring healthcare facilities. Such measures will improve diagnostic accuracy, enhance pathogen detection, and contribute to early warning and response systems. While JE vaccination and vector control programs remain central to disease prevention, the presence of other causative agents for AES highlights the need for a collaborative, multi-pathogen surveillance approach.

Finally, there is a pressing need to transition from JE-focused surveillance to a comprehensive system that captures the entire etiology matrix of AES. Such an approach would enable policymakers and healthcare professionals to plan and implement evidence-based public health strategies, addressing the broader spectrum of causes and ensuring better disease control and management.
