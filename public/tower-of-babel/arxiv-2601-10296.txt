Two dimensional covering systems and possible prime producing a m − b n a^{m}-b^{n}
Andrew Granville and Francesco Pappalardi

Abstract
. We exhibit a new application of two dimensional covering systems, examples of integer pairs a , b a,b for which a m − b n a^{m}-b^{n} has a prime divisor from some given finite set of primes, for every pair of integers m , n ⩾ 0 m,n\geqslant 0 . This leads us to conjecture what are the only possible obstructions to | a m − b n | |a^{m}-b^{n}| taking on infinitely many distinct prime values.

2.
Two-dimensional covering systems

A set of integer triples
\{(u_{i},v_{i},r_{i}):i=1,\dots,k\}
is a
two-dimensional covering system
if for every pair of integers
m,n
there exists
i
for which

mv_{i}\equiv nu_{i}\pmod{r_{i}}.

For example,
\{(1,0,2),(0,1,2),(1,1,2)\}
is a two-dimensional covering system.

In this section we will show that every triple of positive integers
\{a,b,Q\}
, where
Q
is squarefree and
(a,b)=1
for which

(a^{m}-b^{n},Q)>1
for all
m,n\geqslant 1
, can be obtained from a two-dimensional covering system. And, vice-versa, given a two-dimensional covering system we can find all of the corresponding triples
\{a,b,Q\}
.

2.1.
Two-dimensional congruences

For given integers
u,v,r
with
r\geqslant 1
we define

S(u,v,r)=\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:mv\equiv nu\pmod{r}\}.

We call
(u,v,r)
and
(U,V,R)

equivalent
if
S(u,v,r)=S(U,V,R)
.
We call
(u,v,r)

reduced
if
u
divides
r
,
(v,u)=1
and
1\leqslant v\leqslant r/u
.

Lemma 1
.

Every triple
(u,v,r)
with
r\geqslant 1
and
u
and
v
not both
0\pmod{r}
, is equivalent to a reduced triple.

Proof.

If
g=
gcd
(u,v,r)
and
u=gU,v=gV,r=gR
then
mv\equiv nu\pmod{r}
if and only if
mV\equiv nU\pmod{R}
and so
S(u,v,r)=S(U,V,R)
. Therefore we may assume that gcd
(u,v,r)=1
.

Suppose that
u
divides
r
and
1\leqslant v\leqslant r/u
but
(v,u)=g>1
.
Then
(g,r)=1
so we have
S(u,v,r)=S(U,V,r)
where
u=gU,v=gV
and we have
U
divides
u
which divides
r
, and

1\leqslant V=v/g\leqslant r/ug=r/Ug^{2}<r/U
.

Let
U=(u,r)
and write
u=Uh,r=UR
with
(h,R)=1
. Now
(U,v)=(u,v,r)=1
and so if
mv\equiv nu\pmod{r}
then
U
divides
mv
and so
U
divides
m
. Writing
m=MU
, we have
mv\equiv nu\pmod{r}
if and only if

Mv\equiv nh\pmod{R}
.
Let
k
be the inverse of
h\pmod{R}
and so we have
n\equiv MV\pmod{R}
where
V
is the least positive residue
\equiv vk\pmod{R}
so that
1\leqslant V\leqslant R=r/U
. Multiplying through by
U
this is equivalent to
nU\equiv mV\pmod{r}
. Thus
S(u,v,r)=S(U,V,r)
and we have
U=(u,r)
divides
r
and
1\leqslant V\leqslant R=r/U
. From the previous paragraph we deduce that
S(u,v,r)=S(u^{\prime},v^{\prime},r)
where
g=(u,v),u=gu^{\prime},v=gv^{\prime}
, which is reduced.
∎

The covering system
\{(1,2,2),(2,1,2),(1,1,2)\}
is made out of reduced triples and is equivalent to the one in the above example.

Next we see how two-dimensional congruences can be used to describe pairs
m,n\geq 0
for which
a^{m}-b^{n}
is divisible by a fixed prime
p
:

Proposition 1
.

Let
p
be prime and set
r=\text{ord}_{p}(a),s=\text{ord}_{p}(b)
and let
u_{p}=\frac{r}{(r,s)}
.
There exists an integer
v_{p}
for which
a^{u_{p}}\equiv b^{v_{p}}\pmod{p}
,
(u_{p},v_{p},[r,s])
is reduced and

\{(m,n):a^{m}\equiv b^{n}\pmod{p}\}=S(u_{p},v_{p},[r,s])\cap(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\ell=[r,s]
and select an arbitrary
h\pmod{p}
with
\text{ord}_{p}(h)=\ell
so that
a\equiv h^{i\ell/r}\pmod{p}

for some integer
i
coprime to
r
. Select
I\equiv i\pmod{r}
for which
(I,\ell)=1
and then let
g=h^{I}
so that

\text{ord}_{p}(g)=\ell
and
a\equiv g^{\ell/r}\pmod{p}
, also let
w_{p}=\ell/r=\frac{s}{(r,s)}
. Next
select
j
for which
b\equiv g^{j\ell/s}\equiv g^{ju_{p}}\pmod{p}
so that
(j,s)=1
.
Finally select
k
so that
kj\equiv 1\pmod{s}
and then let
v_{p}\equiv kw_{p}\bmod s
with
0<v_{p}\leq s
so that
a^{u_{p}}\equiv g^{u_{p}w_{p}}\equiv g^{ju_{p}\cdot kw_{p}}\equiv b^{v_{p}}\pmod{p}
.

Now
a^{m}\equiv b^{n}\pmod{p}
if and only if
m\cdot w_{p}=m\cdot\frac{\ell}{r}\equiv nj\cdot\frac{\ell}{s}=nj\cdot u_{p}\pmod{\ell}
. Multiplying through by
k
gives the claimed result by noting that

u_{p}
divides
[r,s]
,
0<v_{p}\leq[r,s]/u_{p}=s
and
(u_{p},v_{p})
divides
(r/(r,s),s/(r,s))=1
so that
(u_{p},v_{p},[r,s])
is reduced.
∎

Given the values
u_{p},v_{p},[r,s]
in Proposition
1
we can recover
s=[r,s]/u_{p}
and then
(v_{p},s)=\frac{s}{(r,s)}
(see the proof).

2.2.
Primes yielding a given reduced triple

For a given triple
(p,a,b)
where

(u_{p},v_{p},[\text{ord}_{p}(a),\text{ord}_{p}(b)])

be the reduced triple given by Proposition

Then we select integer

where

(2.1)

u_{p}=\frac{\text{ord}_{p}(a)}{(\text{ord}_{p}(a),\text{ord}_{p}(b))}\text{ and then }v_{p}=k\cdot\frac{\text{ord}_{p}(b)}{(\text{ord}_{p}(a),\text{ord}_{p}(b))}

where

.

For a given reduced triple
(u,v,\ell)

we define

\mathcal{P}(u,v,\ell):=\bigg\{(p,a,b):(u,v,\ell)=(u_{p},v_{p},[\text{ord}_{p}(a),\text{ord}_{p}(b)])\bigg\}.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
(u,v,\ell)
is reduced.
Let
s=\ell/u,r=\ell/(v,s)
and select
j
so that
j\cdot\frac{v}{(v,s)}\equiv 1\pmod{(r,s)}
with
(j,s)=1
.

Then

(p,a,b)\in\mathcal{P}(u,v,\ell)
if and only if
p\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
and there exists a residue
g\pmod{p}
of order
\ell

with
a\equiv g^{\ell/r}\pmod{p}
and
b\equiv g^{j\ell/s}\pmod{p}
.

In other words, if
(p,a,b)\in\mathcal{P}(u,v,\ell)
, then
a^{m}-b^{n}\equiv 0\pmod{p}
whenever

(m,n)\in S(u,v,\ell)
(in fact, if
mv\equiv nu\bmod(r,s)
,
a^{m}\equiv g^{\ell m/r}\equiv g^{mv/k}\equiv g^{jnu}\equiv b^{n}(\bmod p)
).

Proof.

If
(p,a,b)\in\mathcal{P}(u,v,\ell)
then it is clear that
\ell=[\text{ord}_{p}(a),\text{ord}_{p}(b)]
divides
p-1
and that
s=\text{ord}_{p}(b)
,
r=\text{ord}_{p}(a)
.

We can then select
g
as in the proof of Proposition
1
,
g=h^{I}
where
h
is an element of order
\ell
,
(I,\ell)=1
and
a\equiv h^{I\ell/r}\bmod p
. Hence
g
has order
\ell
modulo
p
and
a\equiv g^{\ell/r}\bmod p
and the definition of
v
in (
2.1
) immediately gives
b\equiv g^{ju}\bmod p
.

In the other direction, assume we are given prime
p\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}
, a residue
g
of order
\ell\pmod{p}
and
a
and
b
as in the hypothesis. Then
\text{ord}_{p}(a)=r
and
\text{ord}_{p}(b)=s
. Now
(u,v)=1
and so
(u,(v,s))=1
so that
(r,s)=(\ell/u,\ell/(v,s))=\frac{\ell}{u(v,s)}=\frac{r}{u}
and therefore
u=\frac{r}{(r,s)}=u_{p}
. Also
(r,s)=\frac{r}{u}=\frac{rs}{\ell}
, so that
\ell=\frac{rs}{(r,s)}=[r,s]
.

Now
a\equiv g^{\ell/r}\equiv g^{(v,s)}\pmod{p}
and
b\equiv g^{j\ell/s}\equiv g^{ju}\pmod{p}
, so that

b^{v}\equiv g^{juv}\equiv g^{j\cdot\frac{v}{(v,s)}\cdot\frac{r(v,s)}{(r,s)}}\equiv g^{\frac{r(v,s)}{(r,s)}}=g^{u(v,s)}\equiv a^{u}\pmod{p}

and therefore
v_{p}=v
by definition.
∎

2.3.
Two-dimensional covering systems

A set of integer triples
\{(u_{i},v_{i},r_{i}):i=1,\dots,k\}
is a two-dimensional covering system if

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}S(u_{i},v_{i},r_{i})=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}.

The covering system is
minimal
if no proper subset covers all of
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 1}
. A covering system is
reduced
if it is made up of reduced triples.
Two dimensional covering systems have a rich history
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
9
]

but not in our context.

If
Q=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
is the product of distinct primes then

\{(m,n):(a^{m}-b^{n},Q)>1\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\{(m,n):a^{m}\equiv b^{n}\pmod{p_{i}}\}.

Now if
p_{i}\nmid ab
then Proposition
1
implies that there is a reduced integer triple
(m_{i},n_{i},r_{i})

for which

\{(m,n):a^{m}\equiv b^{n}\pmod{p_{i}}\}=S(m_{i},n_{i},r_{i}),

where
r_{i}=[\text{ord}_{p_{i}}(a),\text{ord}_{p_{i}}(b)]
and
m_{i}=\frac{r_{i}}{\text{ord}_{p_{i}}(b)}=\frac{\text{ord}_{p_{i}}(a)}{(\text{ord}_{p_{i}}(a),\text{ord}_{p_{i}}(b))}
.
Piecing these together we deduce the following:

Corollary 1
.

Let
Q=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
be the product of distinct primes coprime to
ab
. Then

\{(m,n):(a^{m}-b^{n},Q)>1\}\supset\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}

if and only if

\{(m_{i},n_{i},r_{i}):i=1,\dots,k\}
(as given just above) is a two-dimensional covering system.
Moreover
Q
is minimal (in that no proper divisor has a common factor with all
a^{m}-b^{n}
) if and only if the covering system is minimal.

Linear forms in logarithms

are given and coprime, and
m,n\geqslant 1
vary. We will use Baker’s theorem
[
1
]
in the following form to show that the above has finitely many solutions: There exists a number
C=C(a,b)
such that if
m,n\geqslant 1
then

|m\log a-n\log b|\geqslant(m+n)^{-C}

Taking exponentials of both sides and multiplying through by
b^{n}
we get

(3.1)

|a^{m}-b^{n}|\gg b^{n}(m+n)^{-C}

and this goes to
\infty
as
a^{m},b^{n}\to\infty
.
Thus if there is a two-dimensional covering system then
|a^{m}-b^{n}|
takes only finitely many prime values and each of them for only finitely many pairs
m,n\geq 1
.

One can even do better and rule out all but finitely many prime powers, that is
a^{m}-b^{n}=p^{\ell}
. This follows from work of Siegel
[
8
]
who essentially showed that there are only finitely many solutions to
a+b=c
in coprime integers
a,b,c
whose prime factors all come from a given finite set.

We speculate that there may be a universal upper bound
B
for the number of prime values taken by any

\{|a^{m}-b^{n}|:m,n\geqslant 1\}
if it is covered by a reduced two-dimensional covering system.

4.
Heuristic for the number of primes
a^{m}-b^{n}

We first estimate
\#\{(m,n):|a^{m}-b^{n}|\leqslant x\}
and use the Cramér heuristic to guess at the number of prime values. We then
adjust this with local probabilities to guess at the number of primes.

4.1.
The number up to
x

We wish to count the number of
m,n\geqslant 1
with
0<a^{m}-b^{n}\leqslant x
.
If
m\leqslant\frac{\log x}{\log a}
and
n\leqslant\frac{\log a^{m}}{\log b}
then
b^{n}<a^{m}\leqslant x
, and the number of such pairs is

\sum_{m\leqslant\frac{\log x}{\log a}}\bigg\lfloor\frac{\log a^{m}}{\log b}\bigg\rfloor=\sum_{m\leqslant\frac{\log x}{\log a}}\bigg(m\frac{\log a}{\log b}+O(1)\bigg)=\frac{(\log x)^{2}}{2\log a\log b}+O\bigg(\frac{\log x}{\log a}+\frac{\log x}{\log b}\bigg).

Now we consider
m>\frac{\log x}{\log a}
and let
n_{m}
be maximal with
b^{n_{m}}<a^{m}
.
Equation (
3.1
) yields that

x\geqslant a^{m}-b^{n}\gg a^{m}m^{-C}

and so
m\leqslant\frac{\log x}{\log a}+O(C\log\log x)
and therefore there are
\ll C\log\log x
such pairs

(m,n_{m})
. If
n\leqslant n_{m}-1
and

x\geqslant a^{m}-b^{n}\geqslant a^{m}-b^{n_{m}}/b>a^{m}(1-1/b),

so
a^{m}\leqslant\frac{b}{b-1}x
and therefore
m\leqslant\frac{\log x+O(1/b)}{\log a}
. This yields at most one value of
m
, and so the number of such pairs
m,n
is
\ll\frac{\log x}{\log b}
(which can be attained if
a^{m}=x+1
).

Adding these estimates, together with the analogous argument for when
0<b^{n}-a^{m}\leqslant x
we obtain

\#\{m,n\geqslant 1:|a^{m}-b^{n}|\leqslant x\}=\frac{(\log x)^{2}}{\log a\log b}+O\bigg(\frac{\log x}{\log a}+\frac{\log x}{\log b}\bigg).

4.2.
The Cramér heuristic

A randomly chosen integer near
x
is prime with probability about
\frac{1}{\log x}
. If a set of integers is chosen more-or-less randomly then a first guess at the number of primes in the set can be obtained by applying this heuristic.

Now for each
n\leqslant n_{m}
we have the “probability” that
a^{m}-b^{n}
is prime is about

\frac{1}{\log(a^{m}-b^{n})}=\frac{1}{\log(a^{m}m^{O(1)})}\sim\frac{1}{\log a^{m}}

while the number of such
n
is
\frac{\log a^{m}}{\log b}+O(1)
and so the“expected’ number of primes in

\{a^{m}-b^{n}:1\leqslant n\leqslant\frac{\log a^{m}}{\log b}\}
is
\sim\frac{\log a^{m}}{\log b}\cdot\frac{1}{\log a^{m}}\sim\frac{1}{\log b}
.
Summing this up over all
m
with
a^{m}\leqslant x
(or
a^{m}\leqslant\frac{b}{b-1}x
) we expect
\sim\frac{\log x}{\log a\log b}
primes.

Combining what we get here with the analogous argument for
a^{m}<b^{n}
we obtain the guess:

\#\{m,n\geqslant 1:|a^{m}-b^{n}|\text{ is prime and }a^{m},b^{n}\leqslant x\}\sim\frac{2\log x}{\log a\log b}.

We will need to adjust the constant to take account of divisibility by small primes, but still we believe that if
x=a^{y}
then the number of primes should be linear in
y
. We test this next.

4.3.

1<a<b
and that there is no covering system for

Since
a^{m}-b^{n}
is coprime to

to obtain the new guess

\#\{m,n\geqslant 1:|a^{m}-b^{n}|\text{ is prime and }a^{m},b^{n}\leqslant x\}\sim\frac{2ab\log x}{\phi(ab)\log a\log b}.

4.4.
Local adjustments, and special form adjustment

It is usual, when guesstimating the number of primes in a given set of integers, to adjust one’s guesstimate depending on how the set of integers is distributed mod
p
for each prime
p
. If the set is the set of values of a polynomial, then the distributions mod
p
are independent for different
p
, since the values are periodic mod
p
with period
p
.
However this is not so in our case. For example we see that
3
divides
2^{n}-1
if and only if
n\equiv 0\pmod{2}
and

5
divides
2^{n}-1
if and only if
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, so that
15
divides
2^{n}-1
if and only if
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

Instead of working mod
p
, we need to work modulo a sequence of composite integers
Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots
such that every prime
p
divides
Q_{j}
for all
j\geqslant j_{p}
. One idea is to have
Q_{j}
be the product of the
j
smallest primes (which is essentially the usual choice), but we saw in
[
4
]
that other choices might be more natural.

Potentially there is a second complicating issue. If
g:=(m,n)>1
then
a^{m/g}-b^{n/g}
divides
a^{m}-b^{n}
and so
a^{m}-b^{n}
is composite unless

a^{m/g}-b^{n/g}=\pm 1
. Mihailescu’s theorem
[
5
]
(which was Catalan’s conjecture) implies that either

\{a^{m/g},b^{n/g}\}=\{3^{2},2^{3}\}
or
m/g=1
or
n/g=1
.
If
n=g
then
n
divides
m
, say
m=nk
where
b=a^{k}\mp 1
;
if
m=g
then
m
divides
n
, say
n=m\ell
where
a=b^{\ell}\pm 1
.
However in all three cases this plays a role for
O(N)

(m,n)
-pairs with
m,n\leqslant N
, whereas there are
\asymp N^{2}
pairs, so these cases effect a vanishing proportion of pairs
(m,n)
, so can be ignored.

The prime factors of
ab
never divide
a^{m}-b^{n}
with
m,n\geqslant 1
but all other primes do for some
m,n
values (since
p|a^{p-1}-b^{p-1}
).
Let
P_{i}
be the
i
th smallest prime power,

r_{k}=[P_{1},\cdots,P_{k}]\text{ and }q_{k}=(a^{r_{k}}-1,b^{r_{k}}-1).

The proportion of
a^{m}-b^{n}
values that have no factor in common with
q_{k}
is given by

\frac{1}{x^{2}}\#\{m,n\leqslant x:(a^{m}-b^{n},q_{k})=1\}\sim\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}\#\{m,n\leqslant r_{k}:(a^{m}-b^{n},q_{k})=1\}.

We wish to incorporate the criterion
(m,n)=1
. This fits best in our approach if we work instead with
(m,n,r_{k})=1
since that will find all common factors of any given
m
and
n
once
k
is sufficiently large, and the criteria is now also
r_{k}
-periodic. Therefore
the proportion of
a^{m}-b^{n}
values that have no factor in common with
q_{k}
and with
(m,n)
coprime with
r_{k}
is given by

\frac{1}{x^{2}}\#\bigg\{m,n\leqslant x:{(a^{m}-b^{n},q_{k})=1\above 0.0pt\&\ (m,n,r_{k})=1}\bigg\}\sim\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}\#\bigg\{m,n\leqslant r_{k}:{(a^{m}-b^{n},q_{k})=1\above 0.0pt\&\ (m,n,r_{k})=1}\bigg\}

As
k\to\infty
this incorporates divisibility by small primes as well as
(m,n)=1
, as desired.
For regular integers, the analogous probability is
\phi(q_{k})/q_{k}
, and so the adjustment to the Cramér heuristic, taking into account divisibility by the small primes, is

\kappa_{a,b}(k):=\frac{q_{k}}{\phi(q_{k})}\cdot\frac{1}{r_{k}^{2}}\#\bigg\{m,n\leqslant r_{k}:{(a^{m}-b^{n},q_{k})=1\above 0.0pt\&\ (m,n,r_{k})=1}\bigg\}

hopefully becoming more accurate as
k
increases.
Indeed we believe that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\kappa_{a,b}(k)\text{ exists, and equals a non-zero constant }\kappa_{a,b},

and therefore we conjecture that

\boxed{\#\{|a^{m}-b^{n}|\leqslant x:|a^{m}-b^{n}|\text{ is prime}\}\sim\frac{2ab\,\kappa_{a,b}}{\phi(ab)\log a\log b}\cdot\log x;}

and so, in the introduction, we have
c_{a,b}:=\frac{2ab\,\kappa_{a,b}}{\phi(ab)\log a\log b}
.
