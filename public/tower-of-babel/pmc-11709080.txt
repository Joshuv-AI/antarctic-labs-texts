Influence of perceived impostorism on self-esteem and anxiety among University Nursing Students: Recommendations to implement mentorship program
Authors: Devendra Bhardwaj, Rajesh Kumar, Yogesh Bahurupi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The imposter phenomenon (IP) involves feelings of intellectual fraudulence and phoniness in challenging new roles. IP feelings are associated with low self-esteem and anxiety in nursing students, especially during transition or early stages of nursing training. This research aims to find out the prevalence of IP and its relation with self-esteem and anxiety among nursing undergraduates.Material and Methods:A cross-sectional survey randomly selected nursing students from the six established nursing institutions in Uttarakhand state. Three hundred eight (n = 308) participants completed the survey questionnaires, which included a structured socio-demographic sheet, the Clance Imposter Phenomenon Scale (CIPS), the Generalized Anxiety Scale-7 (GAS-7), and the Rosenberg Self-esteem Scale (RSES). Appropriate descriptive and inferential statistics were used to generate the results.Results:The mean age of the students was 20.93 (SD = 1.09) years. The mean CIPS score was 58.57 (SD = 10.69). Frequent to intense feelings of impostorism were reported by 43.4%, and 51.3% reported at least moderate IP feelings. Further, second-year students than third year, on average, significantly reported higher scores on imposter phenomenon (P < 0.001), anxiety (P = 0.006), and lower scores on self-esteem (P = 0.018). As predicted, CIPS scores correlated positively with GAS-7 (r = -.340, P < 0.01) and negatively with RSES (r = -.307, P < 0.01). A multiple regression model predicting the imposter phenomenon with self-esteem demonstrated a negative main effect of self-esteem (P < 0.001) and significant two-way interaction.Conclusions:A higher proportion of the students reported feelings of imposter phenomenon. Low self-esteem was reported as a strong predictor of IP. Institutional strategies such as mentorship, supervised education, and early screening of psychological issues could be preventive and promotive strategies. Future research is recommended to identify the cause of poor IP in students.

Introduction

Nursing is a health discipline responsible for delivering the best quality care to sick and wounded people. Nursing is not scientific knowledge and technical skill but a profession based on human values.[1] Nursing students experience tremendous stress during different transitions from school to life in the nursing course and deal with other uneventful additional life adjustments as per the needs of theory and clinical experiences concerned.[2] Nursing profession hammers the think tank of nursing students to utilize their best theoretical knowledge in clinical training to achieve the necessary set of skills and competencies.[3] Students must undergo training in varied clinical areas to be trained in the various aspects of nursing care.[4]

Nursing students are prone to bear high stress and are vulnerable to developing many psychological problems, including anxiety, poor self-esteem, symptoms of depression, and poor concentration in the study. This is likely because nursing students face powerful stress in daily life, including dealing with a variety of critical patients, experiencing death and dying situations, caring for ventilator patients, and adjusting to health team members.[45] Failure to cope with overwhelming study burdens and clinical requirements develops a feeling of intellectual phoniness, self-doubt, and lack of confidence in one’s ability, leading to the imposter phenomenon (IP).[67]

Imposter phenomenon (IP), impostorism, fraud syndrome, or imposter experience is a psychological state of doubt about their ability to complete a task even when they have evidence to the contrary.[5] Imposter phenomenon found a close link with burnout, despair, higher stress, anxiety, low self-esteem, and perfectionism and leads to mental disorders that can severely affect the academic performance and the mental well-being of the students.[89] On the contrary, low IP found a strong link with higher self-esteem and favorable efficient performance and clinical practices among students.[10]

Further, earlier research proved a strong relationship between IP and high neuroticism, low self-esteem, and low conscientiousness.[1112] The students with higher IP reported developing more avoidant coping strategies and experiencing more emotional exhaustion and low job satisfaction later in life.[13] As a vulnerable group, nursing students are prone to develop imposter phenomena that affect not only their mental health but can have an untoward impact on academic and clinical performances. Low self-esteem and emotional exhaustion could be potential causes of low performance due to higher IP among students.

In order to improve the academic and clinical performance of future budding nurses, the prevalence of and associated factors to IP need to be understood. Thus, this research aimed to study the prevalence of IP and its association with self-esteem and anxiety in nursing students.

Material and Methods

A cross-sectional descriptive correlation survey was completed on December 23-January 24 in six established conveniently selected nursing institutions in Uttarakhand, India. The researcher uses simple random sampling, a lottery method, to enroll the survey participants. Students studying in 2nd and 3rd year and willing to participate in data collection are included in the survey. Participants diagnosed with any physical and mental illness and under treatment were excluded from the participation. Questionnaires, consent forms, and informant participant sheets (IPS) were administered to the students to complete the questionnaires. The questionnaire was distributed to 525 students and returned by 490 with a response rate of 86.73%. Finally, after carefully scrutinizing the filled questionnaires, it was decided to include 308 questionnaires in the analysis of this study.

Data instruments

Socio-demographic Profile Sheet: A structured, validated, and pretested sheet used to collect information on the personal and professional characteristics of the participants. The sheet consists of information on age (years), year of study, religion, habitat, monthly family income (INR), presently staying in the hostel, occupation, and education of the father and mother, and presence of family members in the nursing profession.

Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale: The scale is a 20-item self-reported 5-point Likert scale, with 1 (not at all true) to 5 (very true).[14] Scale reported that a total point of 40 or less indicates few, 41 to 60 indicates moderate, 61 to 80 indicates frequently, and higher than 80 indicates intense impostor experiences. A higher score on the scale suggests severe symptoms of the imposter phenomenon and vice versa. The original scale comprises three dimensions: fake (self-doubt, concern about intelligence and ability), luck (thought of having accomplished things by chance or error rather than through ability), and discount (the inability to acknowledge praise and good performance). The scale has excellent construct validity and was used in a similar population[15] and reported satisfactory excellent internal consistency[16]; Cronbach’s α in the current study = 0.891.

General Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-7): A 7-item self-administered questionnaire used to screen out the symptoms of anxiety among nursing students.[17] Participants were asked to rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale, 3 (nearly every day) to 0 (not at all), with a total score range of 0-21. The internal consistency of the scale is 0.92, and the reliability coefficient Cronbach’s α for the scale is 0.90.[18]

Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES): Self-esteem was measured using RSES developed by Rosenberg in 1965.[19] The scale consists of 10 items (five positive and five negative statements) on a four-point Likert scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = agree, 4 = strongly agree. For positive items (1, 3, 4, 7, and 10), the responses were rated as follows: strongly disagree, 1; disagree, 2; agree, 3; and strongly agree, 4. The negative items’ responses (2, 5, 6, 8, and 9) were reverse coded. The scale has total score ranges from 10 to 40, where higher scores indicate higher self-esteem and vice versa. The scale is widely used in similar studies and has been reported to have excellent reliability.[2021]

Ethical Consideration: The Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC) approved the research project with a letter No. AIIMS/IEC/23/384. All methods and procedures are carried out per the updated ethical guidelines and regulations for human participants in research.[22] Researchers explained the study’s purpose and objectives to the participants before obtaining consent, and relevant explanations were given for doubts and other queries. The participants were ensured to maintain confidentiality and privacy during each stage of the research process.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the population size of the nursing students (n = 600) studying in major nursing institutions attached to the medical colleges. We took an absolute precision of 5%, design effect, (d = 1) at 95% confidence interval using the formula, n = [DEFF*Np (1-p)]/[(d2/Z21-α/2*(N-1) +p*(1-p)]. The sample size for the study was reported to be 96. However, considering the multi-centric site, researchers decided to take a larger sample size (n = 308) to ease the generalization of the findings on the whole population in the state.

Statistical analysis

A total of 308 questionnaires were included in the final analysis and transferred to Microsoft Excel. The data are analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.[23] Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage, are used to depict the participants’ characteristics. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check the data distribution, and considering the nonnormal distribution of data, Spearmen’s rho (ρ) was used to compute the relationship between variables. Median and interquartile range (IQR) are used to compare the findings between different variables. Multilinear regression analysis was performed to predict the factors associated with the imposter phenomenon. Odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to quantify the strength of the association between participants’ characteristics and the imposter phenomenon. All hypotheses are tested at P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Discussion

The study aimed to determine the levels of imposter phenomenon, examine the relationships between imposter phenomenon, self-esteem, and anxiety in nursing students, and investigate the difference in the variables between classes. Our study demonstrated that the imposter phenomenon is common among nursing students, with 43.4% of students’ scores falling in frequent to intense IP experiences and 51.3% of students reporting at least moderate IP status. Our study findings agree with many previously published studies. The prevalence of IP remains more or less the same in previously published literature on a similar population, with 43% of students reporting moderate and 40.7% of students experiencing frequent symptoms of IP.[24] Also, an international collaborative study on final-year nursing students from Australia, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom demonstrated similar findings on the imposter phenomenon with 41.7%.[25] Several other studies also reported similar findings on the variables among nursing students.[2627]

The comparison of the findings of our study with many previous studies on IP indicates the strongest replication pattern. In addition, the mean CIPS score in the present sample was 58.57 (10.69), which is in concurrence with the previous studies conducted on undergraduate samples[2829] and indicates that every sample in the present investigation has features of imposter phenomenon. The present study replicates the findings of many previous studies to report the status of the imposter phenomenon in nursing undergraduates. Intuitively, the sudden transition from school life to the nursing profession, fear of uncertainty, infection, performance pressure, and exposure to death, and other critical conditions probably lead to higher impostorism among students.[30] However, the authors recommend an exploratory study to pinpoint the factors associated with IP among nursing students in the future.

In the present study, the imposter phenomenon is examined concerning self-esteem among undergraduate samples. As predicted, the imposter phenomenon demonstrated a significant negative correlation with anxiety and positively correlated with self-esteem in both classes of study. Findings promisingly support the idea that students with low self-esteem are more predisposed toward developing the imposter phenomenon. The study findings on the relation of self-esteem to the imposter phenomenon strongly replicated many previous findings and proved that students with low self-esteem tend to develop more symptoms of impostorism.[8–1027] Our study findings replicated many of the earlier studies on the relation between self-esteem and imposter phenomenon and provided sufficient ground to conclude that self-esteem is an important strong predictor that tends to lead to high impostorism. Likewise, the study findings replicated the facts from the standard published literature to report a link between psychological problems and the imposter phenomenon.[25831] These findings also match the defining attributes of the imposter phenomenon, including fear of failure, chronic self-doubt, and performance anxiety.[27]

Previous research suggested that individuals with high impostorism phenomenon suffered more psychological distress, including performance anxiety, burnout, stress, depression, and perfectionism, which may affect training and future prospectus.[932] In this study, a positive relationship was reported between anxiety and the imposter phenomenon in our samples. These findings are consistent with many previous studies examining the association between anxiety and impostorism in different kinds of populations, including undergraduate medical.[83334] Specifically, the findings that anxiety was predictive of IP suggest that individuals with higher anxiety attempt to feel. This might be due to an unstable sense of self-worth, intense fear of failure, or a sense of inferiority rejection, which can lead to escapism, self-doubt, and self-handicapping.[34]

In addition, the findings of this study demonstrated significantly higher odds of the imposter phenomenon among junior students than their counterparts. This finding is consistent with many other previous studies that reported that impostorism features are common in students transitioning from school life to a medical professional.[1035] During the transition, students are relatively more stressed and demonstrate lower confidence in previous similar work.[103536] It has been established in earlier studies that moving from pre-clinical to clinical training can be more stressful and challenging for nursing students. In response to this transition, students may have frequent spells of low mood, depression, thoughts of dropping out, anxiety attributed to academic pressure, poor family and friend support, or financial issues.[3536]

Strengths, limitations, and recommendations

The study has several good points, including using large, randomly selected samples from state-wide nursing institutions. Researchers also used the globally accepted scales for study variables, such as CIPS, RSES, and GAD-7. However, there are several limitations to work upon, including using a cross-sectional survey design, which may impede the generalization of the findings. Similarly, in all cross-sectional surveys, causal inference is unwarranted. However, the findings of the present study and previous research reported self-esteem as an important vulnerability factor, which may be along with many other factors that trigger the imposter phenomenon in students. Researchers also point out the overlapping of the examination period with data collection, so attendance of the students could have been higher, which may only represent part of the student body. However, future research using experimental and longitudinal designs may help study the impact of self-esteem on the imposter phenomenon or vice versa. Future research involving all class students from entry to exit would better help to understand the initiation and phases of development of the imposter phenomenon.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study strongly support the idea that self-esteem is a significant antecedent of the imposter phenomenon. IP may subsequently lead to many psychological problems, including anxiety. Findings support the idea that students with lower self-esteem are more prone to developing impostorism during the early stage of the course.
