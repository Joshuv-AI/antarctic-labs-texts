Spatiotemporal deformation patterns induced by ultradeep excavation in soft soil areas
Authors: Xuesong Cheng, Wenkai Wang, Qinghan Li, Jianwei Jia, Zihao Wang, Chenkai Li, Tong Wang, Gang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid development of underground space in soft soil areas necessitates improved understanding of the deformation patterns and mechanisms associated with ultradeep excavations. This study investigated the spatiotemporal deformation patterns induced by a 34.9 m-deep subway station excavation in Tianjin, China, constructed using the top-down (TD) method. The difference in deformation patterns between excavations using the bottom-up (BU) method and those using the TD method is discussed through field measurements and numerical simulations. In TD excavation, the magnitude of diaphragm wall (DW) horizontal displacement is significantly smaller than that in BU excavation. Furthermore, the rate at which the depth of maximum horizontal displacement increases with excavation depth is slower than that observed in BU excavation. Under stratum heave effects, the DW exhibited an uplift of up to 20 mm, generating differential displacements exceeding 10 mm between the wall and column. Stratum heave further exacerbated the differential settlement of adjacent buildings, causing the far-side foundation to experience uplift while settlement persisted on the near side. After accounting for wall uplift, the ratio of the maximum ground surface settlement to maximum lateral wall displacement ranged from 0.4 to 2.0, consistent with previous studies. The progressive development of DW deformation in both horizontal and vertical directions was summarized. The growth curve of TD excavations exhibits a convex upward shape, whereas that of BU excavations exhibits a convex downward shape. These findings provide guidance for the stage-specific deformation control of comparable complex projects in soft soil areas and highlight the need to incorporate stratum heave effects into the deformation analysis and structural design of ultradeep excavation support systems.

Introduction

Global urbanization and three-dimensional urban development have driven exponential growth in underground space utilization, with projects trending toward greater depth, scale, and span. Deep excavations in high-density built environments pose challenges for deformation control and mitigation of environmental impacts, requiring high-precision analysis and control strategies1–7. Globally, many rapidly developing megacities with high population densities, including Tianjin in China, are predominantly located in coastal, lakeshore, and fluvial plains underlain by soft soil strata. Soft soil strata are typically composed of cohesive soils, silt, and organic soils with high fine-grained contents. Soft soils tend to exhibit high compressibility and thixotropy. Excavations in soft soil deposits encounter geotechnical challenges that may significantly compromise both structural performance and the surrounding environment. The low shear strength and high compressibility of soft clays and silts often lead to substantial basal heave and large lateral wall deflections8–11, differential settlements and tilting of adjacent buildings12–14, as well as increased safety risks.

Excavation methods can be classified into top–down (TD) and bottom–up (BU) methods. The BU method is economical and easy to operate; however, the temporary support system exhibits low stiffness, which often results in significant deformation of the diaphragm walls (DW)15–17. By contrast, the TD method can effectively control deformation and shorten the construction period. Therefore, the TD method has been widely used for ultradeep excavations in recent years, particularly in urban projects18–20. The present case study adopts the TD method, in which floor slabs are rigidly connected to DW, enabling deformation control via synergistic effects between the main structure and DW. However, cast-in-place floor slabs are less efficient than temporary supports, and the presence of previously constructed floor slabs restricts soil transportation from the lower levels. During this stage, the risk of DW deformation significantly increases owing to the incomplete closure of the support system. Therefore, enhancing real-time deformation monitoring and control throughout the excavation process is essential to ensure construction safety and quality.

Extensive studies have addressed the structural and environmental deformation induced by deep excavation, leading to the development of deformation mechanisms and control measures. Various technical approaches have been developed for determining the deformation characteristics and control measures of deep excavation, including theoretical analysis21–26, numerical simulations11,27–29, and field measurement methods30–33. For excavations with depths < 20 m, the body of research is relatively mature. Ou et al.34 analyzed ten deep excavation projects in Taipei, China, revealing the lateral deformation patterns of DW and their relationship with ground settlement. Zheng et al.11 investigated soil–structure interaction during a 14.7 m-deep staged excavation in Tianjin, China, and evaluated the optimal depth of the first excavation stage under different site conditions. Meng et al.35 systematically examined deformation induced by an 18 m-deep excavation in Shenzhen, China, including its interaction with adjacent tunnels, and proposed relationships among the excavation sequence, excavation length, and deformation. At such depths, excavation-induced base heave becomes significant, and extensive studies have addressed its stability. Goh36 applied finite element analysis to evaluate the base heave stability in circular excavations supported by DW in clay and proposed a simplified method for axisymmetric conditions. Abdi and Ou37 conducted three-dimensional finite element analyses to assess the effects of ground improvement on the safety factor against base heave and proposed a simplified evaluation method for cases with and without ground improvement. For excavations in this depth range, research on excavation and support techniques as well as deformation patterns is relatively mature. The phenomenon of heave has also received attention from researchers. However, existing studies on heave have focused primarily on the influence of basal heave on support system stability. The overall heave of the stratum induced by excavation has not yet been clearly observed or systematically investigated.

With increasing underground space utilization, excavations exceeding 20 m have become widespread, prompting further investigations on large-scale excavation behavior. Cheng et al.38 conducted comprehensive field monitoring of a 30.2 m-deep excavation in Hangzhou, China, summarizing deformation patterns of retaining structures, support systems, and surrounding pipelines. Tan et al.39 analyzed field data from a 30 m-deep large-scale excavation in Suzhou, China, and established deformation patterns induced by excavation and dewatering. Tan et al.40 observed upward soil movement behind and beneath the DW and discussed differences between BU and TD excavations. Chen et al.41 investigated the lateral deformation of DW in a 29 m-deep excavation for a commercial complex in Hangzhou, China, and compared the performance of prestressed steel struts and concrete strut systems. Regarding deep excavations exceeding 20 m in depth, relevant studies on excavation-induced deformation are available. Nevertheless, the construction and deformation control of such challenging excavations remain a practical difficulty, and experience with excavations deeper than 30 m is particularly lacking. Furthermore, research on the stratum heave effect induced by ultra-deep excavations is even more insufficient.

Narrow, elongated metro station deep excavations, characterized by a high depth-to-width ratio and large span, have attracted extensive attention regarding their deformation behavior. Tan et al.19 investigated deformation characteristics of a semi-top-down (STD) excavation for a metro station in Shanghai, China, and examined the performance and advantages of the STD method. Wu et al.42 systematically studied the effects of dewatering during metro station excavation in central Tianjin, China, on surrounding building settlement. Zheng et al.43 analyzed the influence of width-to-depth ratio (B/H) and DW embedment ratio (D/H) on base heave failure, and established a quantitative relationship between sliding radius and excavation geometry. For deep excavations of metro stations, it is widely recognized in practice that the TD construction method achieves better deformation control than the BU method. However, quantitative comparisons between the two methods, particularly detailed comparisons of deformation indices throughout the entire excavation process, have not yet been systematically conducted.

As the scale and depth of excavation increase, the combination of field data analysis and numerical simulation is essential to better reveal the underlying mechanisms. Thorough case studies should be conducted to provide references for similar engineering projects. In light of the above, the deformation characteristics of highly compressible soft soils in construction-induced environments and ultradeep (> 30 m) narrow strip excavations remain insufficiently understood. The observed upward stratum displacements beneath excavations in Tianjin and similar soft grounds induce anomalous deformations in support systems and the surrounding ground, inducing coupled vertical movements of the retaining structures, interior columns, and adjacent ground that may exacerbate differential settlements. However, this phenomenon remains insufficiently investigated in current design and construction practice.

This study integrated field monitoring data with finite difference method numerical simulation and explicitly incorporated stratum heave to systematically characterize the deformation responses of DW, columns, the adjacent ground surface, and utilities during ultradeep excavation in typical soft soil. A series of empirical relationships and deformation characteristics for deep excavation in soft soil areas were established to advance engineering practice.

Project and monitoring scheme

The project is a Tianjin Metro transfer station located north of Wei-Jin-Nan Road and the Bin-Shui-Xi Road intersection, extending north–south along Wei-Jin-Nan Road, with the Senior Activity Center to the west and Tianjin Cancer Hospital to the east in a densely trafficked urban environment.

The station adopts a four-level underground island platform with external dimensions of 187.5 m × 24.1 m, a platform track depth of 32.631 m, roof cover thickness of approximately 5.44 m, excavation depths of approximately 34.89 m in the standard section and 36.76 m at the end shaft, and a TD construction method supported by 1.2 m-thick DW with lengths of 69.187 and 69.887 m constructed with C35 concrete and I-beam connections. Floor slabs functioned as excavation supports with thicknesses of 1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.5, and 1.4 m for the roof, levels 1–3, and base slab, respectively. Three and four layers of 1200 mm × 1000 mm reinforced concrete temporary struts were installed in the standard section and end shaft, and the end shaft included an additional temporary strut layer and a lower base slab elevation. Excavation-induced deformation comprises structural deformation and surrounding environmental deformation. Structural deformation includes lateral and vertical displacements at the DW top, deep lateral displacement of the DW measured via inclinometry, and column settlement, whereas environmental deformation includes settlements of the surrounding ground surface, relocated underground pipelines during construction, and adjacent buildings. At most monitoring points, a large δh occurs at the wall top and between slabs, whereas δh remains small at slab levels owing to the use of the TD method. Temporary supports exhibit lower stiffness than floor slabs, whereas the top and bottom slabs offer the highest stiffness and support; the excavation base is additionally constrained by DW anchorage. Consequently, δh is minimized at the bottom and top slab levels and amplified in the upper and lower-middle wall sections. The wall top shows continuous inward displacement during excavation, attributed to the delayed installation of the first-level strut, which fails to provide timely support.

At midpoints along the long side, ZQT6 and ZQT17 exhibit contrasting deformation patterns due to site-specific conditions. On the east side near Tianjin Cancer Hospital, limited soil volume restricts compression, causing ZQT6 to record a negative horizontal displacement (δh) during early excavation and a reduced δh after E4 relative to E3. On the west side adjacent to the temporary Wei-Jin-Nan Road, traffic-induced loading promotes soil compression toward the excavation, resulting in a progressively intensifying inward S-shaped displacement at DBC17. Sectional excavation proceeds from end shafts to the center, causing end walls to displace inward after each stage while mid-span walls may deflect outward due to higher stiffness; the load imbalance further increases western mid-span wall displacement and drives east-side panels westward. At the end shafts, ZQT11 exhibits an S-shaped curve with increasing deformation as excavation deepens, whereas ZQT22 shows initial linear tilting during E1–E3 followed by pronounced bulging at E4. Tunnel grouting improves soil integrity within 0–26 m, yielding small uniform deformation up to the third excavation layer (25.7 m); after E4, uneven deformation develops under soil pressure. Large deformation at ZQT11 induces smaller deformation at ZQT12 due to the corner effect, whereas ZQT22 shows marginal deformation and ZQT21 exhibits larger deformation under external soil pressure.

Maximum lateral deformation and position development characteristics

Figure 6 shows the evolution of the maximum lateral deformation δm, displacement position Hmax, and increment ∆δm at ZQT17 (standard section midpoint) and ZQT11 (north end shaft) during excavation. δm progressively increased with staged slow growth and partial retraction.Fig. 6Variations in δm, ∆δm, and Hmax at ZQT17 and ZQT11 with the excavation process.

Variations in δm, ∆δm, and Hmax at ZQT17 and ZQT11 with the excavation process.

Owing to stratum variation and excavation geometry, δm at the end shaft exceeded that at the standard section midpoint during early excavation, and ∆δm exhibited differing or opposite trends. By E3, δm and ∆δm at both locations became similar, reflecting earlier unloading and longer excavation–support intervals at the end shaft under sectional excavation.

The time nodes correspond to completion of excavation at each layer, and the displacement exhibits a V-shaped evolution. During the early and middle stages, δm decreases under TD construction and sectional excavation, whereas the completion of floor slabs concentrates deformation above it. Subsequent excavation induces inward deformation below the slab under soil pressure, whereas upper deformation retracts owing to slab support and wall stiffness. Segmented excavation from both ends causes earlier inward movement at the end walls and temporary mid-span retraction. The deformation profile remains S-shaped, and Hmax shifts downward with increasing excavation depth, stabilizing near the excavation face.

Based on 10 excavation case histories in Taipei, Taiwan, Ou et al. demonstrated that in Taipei soft clay excavations, Hmax typically occurs near the excavation surface (He) 34. Tan et al. reported that in Shanghai projects, Hmax ranges between Hmax = He – 15 m and Hmax = He + 7 m. Figure 7 compares the Hmax–He relationship in this study with other cases. In the cases investigated by Ou et al. and Tan et al., the BU method was adopted, and the Hmax/He ratios ranged between Hmax = He + 5 and Hmax = He – 10. For an excavation depth less than 25 m, the growth rate follows ∆Hmax = ∆He. By contrast, TD cases, including the present study and those by Tan and Li, exhibit larger Hmax and a decreasing growth rate with increasing He44. Tan and Li proposed that when the excavation depth He exceeds 16 m, the maximum displacement Hmax tends to occur above the excavation depth, Hmax/He < 1. In this case, the excavation depth approaches 35 m, and this trend becomes more pronounced and consistent with the TD case data analyzed by Tan and Li. For these two TD cases, the coordinates of Hmax – He ranged between Hmax = 0.55He + 3 and Hmax = 0.55He + 15. The growth rate of Hmax is more consistent with ∆Hmax = 0.55∆He.Fig. 7Relationship between Hmax and He.

Relationship between Hmax and He.

In TD excavations, floor slabs rigidly connected to the DW provide lateral restraint at each slab level, limiting wall deformation above these elevations. At the initial stage, most of the DW remains below the excavation depth, and unloading-induced soil movements outside and at the base govern wall displacement, whereas the deformation induced by active earth pressure above the excavation level is limited; therefore, Hmax is located below the excavation surface. As excavation proceeds, the embedded depth decreases and the active earth pressure above the excavation surface increases, causing Hmax to shift upward and concentrate above the excavation level with a reduced growth rate. Compared with BU excavation, immediate slab casting restrains soil heave below the excavation surface, leading to improved control of the wall deformation below the excavation level and a greater concentration of Hmax above it, ultimately resulting in a lower growth rate with the excavation depth. This mechanism is corroborated by the numerical simulation results presented in Section "Conclusion".

Figure 8 presents the relationship between the maximum lateral displacement δm of the DW and excavation depth He at different monitoring points, including comparative data from Tan et al.19,45, Tan and Li44, and Ou et al.34. As He increases, δm exhibits an increasing trend, with an expanding distribution range that generally falls within 0.03%He ≤ δm ≤ 0.2%He. This range is narrower than the upper bounds of δm = 1.0%He obtained by Peck46 and δm = 0.5%He obtained by Clough and O’Rourke47; however, the upper boundaries of data obtained from other BU and TD excavations are close to δm = 0.5%He. The results obtained in the present case study are similar to those reported by Tan et al.19 where δm ≤ 0.22%He. Compared with the TD case reported by Tan and Li, the deformation in the present study is smaller. This difference is attributed to the DW thickness of 1.2 m adopted in this study, which exceeds the 1.0 m thickness in the case studied by Tan and Li, but is consistent with the 1.2 m thickness used in the BU cases shown in the figure. This result indicates that TD excavation provides more effective control over the lateral displacement of the DW than in BU excavation.Fig. 8Relationship between δm and He.

Phased development pattern of the lateral deformation of underground DW

Figure 9 shows the development ratio of δm at each point relative to its final value for each excavation stage. On the basis of the development patterns of δm, the monitoring points can be grouped into Types A and B. In Type A, δm peaks in stage E3 and either retracts or stabilizes in E4, whereas in Type B, the maximum δm is attained in Stage E4. Specifically, Fig. 9a and b show the distributions of developmental ratios for Type-A and Type-B points, respectively, and Fig. 9c presents a mapping of the spatial locations of all points attributed to both categories.Fig. 9Development ratios of δm at all construction stages.

Development ratios of δm at all construction stages.

Type-A monitoring points exhibited peak δm at stage E4, with lateral displacements at E1 and E4 significantly higher than at other stages. Conversely, δm at Type-B points concentrated mainly in stage E1, making E1 the critical stage for deformation control of the DW. Points along the end shaft wall (e.g., ZQT1, ZQT10, ZQT12) showed rapid displacement accumulation during E1–E3 (Fig. 9c), closely associated with the corner effect. The massive bottom slab constructed at the end of E4 provided stiff restraint suppressing further inward movement of the lower wall, while additional temporary struts at the fourth basement level increased support stiffness, sharing earth pressure and reducing wall load. With increasing depth, the three-dimensional corner effect mobilized greater shear resistance via soil arching, limiting lateral displacement near wall ends. Concurrently, progressive inward displacement of adjacent standard-section walls during earlier stages partially relieved earth pressure on end shaft walls. These mechanisms explain reduced deformation accumulation of end shaft walls during the final stage, whereas the end shaft ends and standard section continued developing displacement at higher rates. ZQT21 deviated from the “E3-peak” pattern observed at ZQT1, ZQT10, and ZQT12. The adjacent existing building constrained the wall at ZQT22, delaying inward displacement and attenuating the corner effect at ZQT21. Simultaneously, persistent vibration loads from the temporary west-side road progressively drove western wall panels inward, causing the deformation history of ZQT21 to diverge from geometrically similar counterparts.

Analysis of the relationship between ground surface settlement and lateral displacement of DW

The vertical ground displacement δv increased with the excavation depth, which is contrary to the results based on the soil loss owing to the lateral displacement of the DW. No discernible correlation was observed between the maximum vertical ground settlement, δvm, and the maximum lateral wall deflection, δm. Given that the stratum experienced uplift owing to unloading during excavation, the upward movement of the DW during excavation can be considered a reflection of the stratum heave. Moreover, the uplift of the stratum owing to excavation unloading is holistic; the attenuation of the heave magnitude with increasing distance from the excavation site is temporarily disregarded. Therefore, taking the vertical settlement dv at the top of the DW as the benchmark, the effective vertical ground settlement δvm’ was calculated as δvm’ = δvm – dv, and the relationship between δvm’ and δm, dv was determined (Fig. 15).Fig. 15Relationships between δvm, δvm’, and δm.

Relationships between δvm, δvm’, and δm.

Figure 15 shows the coordinate values of different monitoring points at various excavation stages, which were used to analyze the distribution relationship between the two variables. The data points ranged between δvm’ = 0.4δm and δvm’ = 2δm. Mana et al.48 observed that the two variables have the relationship, δvm’ = δm. The data points are distributed around this line, indicating good agreement between the ground vertical displacement referenced to the DW and previous empirical observations. This consistency suggests synchronous responses of the DW and ground surface vertical displacements, confirming that the deformation represents overall stratum heave rather than isolated bottom or local soil uplift. The heave was caused by excavation-induced unloading, which caused elastic expansion of the soil skeleton as the confining pressure decreased, combined with upward movement of the impermeable layers driven by the confined groundwater pressure. The combined effects produced upward stratum displacement influencing both support structures and ground vertical deformation. The DW–referenced method for analyzing the ground vertical displacement in this case provides a useful reference for similar projects.

The lower bound of δvm′ in the present study exceeds that reported by Tan et al.49, whereas the upper bound is comparable, indicating a generally larger ground settlement but a consistent δvm′–δm relationship. Compared with building excavations reported by Tan et al. and Cheng et al.38, which exhibited narrower ranges of 0.3δm–1.5δm and 0.6δm–1.18δm, respectively, the wider distribution observed in this study and similar case studies indicates greater variability in subway excavations. This difference results from the spatial variability of deformation mechanisms in long and narrow excavations. At the end shaft, the lateral displacement of the DW provides limited dissipation of ground settlement, whereas in the standard section, it provides more dissipation of the ground settlement, leading to different δm/δvm’ values between the excavation center and ends. In addition, the large span of narrow excavations increases the lateral displacement freedom of the central DW, while external loading and stratigraphic heterogeneity further intensify the spatial variability of deformation mechanisms.

As excavation deepens, unloading triggers stratum heave that progressively offsets initial settlement at farther locations, causing uplift or reduced settlement. More pronounced heave at the far side indicates the integral nature of stratum heave. Near the excavation, soil loss dominates, causing settlement; at the far side, stratum heave dominates, yielding smaller settlement and eventual uplift.

Numerical validation of the development pattern of the DW

A finite difference model was established using FLAC3D to validate the development pattern proposed above; that is, the growth rate of Hmax decreases with increasing excavation depth using the TD method in ultradeep excavations. Referring to the actual construction geological conditions and structural parameters, we established and validated a numerical model for the retaining structure of the standard section of the subway station excavation (Fig. 18). The soil mass was simulated utilizing zone elements with the plastic-hardening constitutive model50,51. The plastic-hardening model is a double-hardening constitutive framework incorporating two yield surfaces: a shear hardening surface for deviatoric loading and a cap surface for compressive hardening. This model requires additional parameters to represent stress-dependent stiffness, including the reference secant modulus E50, the reference unloading/reloading modulus Eur, and the reference oedometer modulus Eoed, enabling representation of the higher stiffness during unloading/reloading relative to the primary loading. At small to medium strain levels (0.01%–1%), the plastic-hardening model more accurately represents stiffness variation with stress level and stress path compared with linear elastic models such as the Mohr–Coulomb and Drucker–Prager models. The parameters for the plastic-hardening model were determined based on the project investigation report and existing research52,53. The initial groundwater level was set to 1.0 m below the ground surface. The retaining system, comprising floor slabs, four levels of horizontal concrete struts, DW, columns, and pile foundations beneath columns, was modeled using shell, beam, and liner elements. The specific parameters for structural elements are presented in Table 5. The excavation sequence is illustrated in Fig. 18. The established numerical model is depicted in Fig. 19a. Standard fixed boundary conditions were adopted: the top surface was set as a free surface, lateral boundaries were restricted against horizontal displacement, and the bottom boundary was constrained against both horizontal and vertical displacements. Measured data from monitoring point ZQT-17 of the excavation were used as a reference for model validation. The results show good agreement between simulated and measured values upon completion of the four-stage excavation, as shown in Fig. 19b. The maximum lateral displacement and its location in the DW from the simulation results are generally consistent with field measurements. Nevertheless, significant discrepancies are observed between the simulated and measured lateral displacements at the wall top, primarily attributed to the delayed installation of the first-level strut in the actual project, which reduced its effectiveness and resulted in large wall-top displacement. Minor discrepancies in the peak displacement between the simulated and measured curves at excavation stages E3 and E4 may result from the limitations of the plastic-hardening constitutive model in capturing the time-dependent and strain-softening behavior of soft clay under large unloading, simplified boundary and initial stress conditions neglecting local heterogeneity and three-dimensional excavation effects, and measurement uncertainties due to instrumentation tolerances and installation disturbances. Conclusion

This study investigates the deformation behavior of an ultradeep excavations in the soft soil of Tianjin, China, through numerical simulations and analysis of field monitoring data. The following conclusions are drawn.The deformation patterns differ between excavations constructed using the BU and TD methods. The rate of increase in Hmax under TD excavation generally followed a growth relationship of ΔHmax = 0.55ΔHe, lower than that observed under BU excavation. For excavations with the same depth and DW thickness, the horizontal deformation of the DW in the BU method is approximately 30% larger than that in the TD method.The heave caused asynchronous upward movement of the column and the DW, leading to differential uplift. Vertical displacements of the DW exhibited a pronounced upward trend under the influence of stratum heave. The standard section showed initial settlement followed by heaving, whereas the end shafts exhibited progressive heave owing to the “shaft-first” excavation scheme. The DW uplift occurred earlier and attained greater magnitudes than the column uplift, exceeding the column uplift by more than 10 mm during E3 and E4. This discrepancy may alter the internal forces of horizontal beams and floor slabs, and hence, requires greater attention.The vertical displacements of the ground surface and adjacent buildings outside the excavation exhibit a distinctive deformation pattern under the effect of ground heave. Throughout the excavation, the vertical ground surface displacement exhibited a pronounced upward trend. The ratio of maximum surface deformation minus the uplift value of the DW to the maximum lateral displacement of the DW was within 0.4δhm < δvm′ < 2δhm, consistent with findings of previous research. For buildings located adjacent to the excavation, significant settlement is observed in close proximity to the excavation, whereas substantial uplift occurs at farther distances. As the excavation depth increases, the vertical displacement of buildings becomes more significantly influenced by ground heave.Quantitative benchmarks for DW lateral and vertical deformation progression at each excavation stage were established. Cumulative average lateral deformation reached 43.0%, 65.2%, 82.9%, and 88.6% of the final value after excavating the first to fourth floors, respectively. Under the TD method, deformation growth rate is higher in early stages; under the BU method, it is higher in late stages. For vertical deformation, the standard section showed initial settlement contributions of –25.69% (E1) and –13.83% (E2), followed by heave contributions of 33.24% (E3) and 100.0% (E4); end shafts exhibited progressive heave contributions of 3.16%, 18.59%, 66.71%, and 95.31% across the four stages. These stage-specific deformation ratios provide practical references for similar ultra-deep excavations in soft soil areas.

In addition, quantitative assessments of the influence of stratum heave on differential settlements of nearby buildings are required. These topics will constitute the primary focus of our research group in the next phase.
