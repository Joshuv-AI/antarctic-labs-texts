Bereaved parents' experiences of anger as a part of their grieving process: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Anita Fjærestad, Atle Dyregrov, Pål Kristensen, Signe Hjelen Stige
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Grief following the loss of a child is one of the most profound stressors a person can endure. Emotions such as sadness, yearning and despair are commonly reported in clinical and research literature, while anger, which is also a common response to grief, especially when the loss is violent or traumatic, is more often overlooked.Objective: To explore in depth how bereaved parents following the 2011 Utøya terror attack have experienced anger as a part of their grieving process.Method: Bereaved parents (n = 22) were interviewed nine years post loss. The interview guide had questions about their experiences with grief and trauma, the impact of other life events and their experience of professional help and treatment. Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.Results: Drawing on in-depth interviews, we found three themes: (1) Anger has not been part of my grieving process, (2) Anger has been a natural part of my grieving process and (3) Anger has been a complicated part of my grieving process.Conclusion: This study demonstrates the multifaceted nature of anger in bereavement following the Utøya terror attack. Anger was experienced in different ways: absent, natural, or complicating, highlighting its complexity as a grief response. Particularly, self-directed anger emerged as a marker of psychological distress, consistent with prior research on complicated grief. Although the interview data did not explicitly address the influence of cultural and societal norms, the broader collective response to the attack may have shaped emotional expression among the bereaved. The absence of anger in some participants, coupled with their concern about this absence, suggests an internalisation of normative grief expectations. This underscores the need for future research to examine how collective identity and cultural framing influence emotional regulation in traumatic bereavement.KEYWORDS: Bereavement, bereaved parents, grief, terrorism, anger

Introduction

On 22 July 2011, a Norwegian right-wing extremist first detonated a bomb in the governmental quarter in Norway, killing eight people, before travelling to Utøya, a small island outside of Oslo, where the Norwegian Labor Party had their annual youth camp. Upon arriving at the island, he started a mass shooting, killing 69 of the 550 people present at the island. Many of them were children, leaving many bereaved parents across Norway. In the aftermath of the terror attack, the Prime Minister at the time, Jens Stoltenberg, stated in a speech that the attack should be met with more democracy, openness and humanity, and the phrase ‘to meet hate with love’ was repeated across the country. People gathered in peaceful demonstrations to show their compassion known as ‘rosetog’ (marches where people were carrying roses as a symbol of love and solidarity) (Christensen & Grønvik, 2020). This collective response to the attack might have contributed to shaping norms around what emotional response was publicly acceptable. Expressions of anger were not a part of the collective response and were not encouraged. This might have led to bereaved parents feeling a discrepancy between their private emotional reality and collective expectations, leading to certain emotions, such as anger, being seen as unacceptable.

Grief following the loss of a child is one of the most profound stressors a person can endure (Djelantik et al., 2020). Emotions such as sadness, yearning and despair are commonly reported in clinical and research literature. Anger is also a common response to grief, especially when the loss is violent or traumatic, although it is more overlooked (Nijborg et al., 2025; Rynearson, 2013). Recent research has highlighted that different forms of anger, particularly self-directed anger, may be strongly associated with complicated grief and post-traumatic stress (Nijborg et al., 2025). Anger directed inwards has been shown to be particularly harmful, potentially diverting attention from the emotional pain and hindering the processing of loss (Nijborg et al., 2025). Cultural norms on what is considered appropriate or normal in grief can influence if or how anger is expressed as a part of grief (Neimeyer et al., 2014; Walter, 1996). Walter (1996) and Neimeyer et al. (2014) argue that grief is not only a psychological process but also a socially constructed experience, influenced by what is deemed acceptable within a given cultural context. Silverman et al. (2021) further highlights how collective rituals and societal expectations can suppress certain emotions, such as anger, in favour of more socially acceptable expressions, such as sadness or yearning. In the Norwegian context, the emphasis on unity and compassion following the terror attack may have contributed to a cultural climate where anger was seen as inappropriate. According to Drury et al. (2019), shared social identity in emergencies fosters emotional consensus, which can promote resilience but may also constrain emotional diversity. Lebel and Ronel (2009) describe how grief reactions can be changed through societal expectations, shifting anger into socially acceptable forms or suppressing it altogether. These perspectives suggest that the cultural framing of grief in Norway may have influenced which emotions bereaved parents felt allowed to express. Grief-related anger is not specific to terrorism and has been observed in other collective traumatic events. Research following the Sewol ferry disaster in South Korea, where human error was a central factor, revealed how bereaved expressed intense anger and bitterness towards authorities (Chae et al., 2018; Choi & Cho, 2019; Kim et al., 2020). These findings suggest that anger in grief may be particularly pronounced when the loss is perceived as preventable or caused by human error. This highlights the importance of understanding grief not only as an individual psychological process, but also as a culturally and contextually embedded experience. One of the most widely accepted and tested models of grief, the dual process model of grief (Stroebe & Schut, 2010), provides a framework for understanding how individuals oscillate between confronting and avoiding grief-related stressors, and how emotions like anger may be part of both loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping. Despite these insights, anger remains underexplored in grief research, particularly in the context of traumatic loss. The aim of this paper is therefore to explore how bereaved parents who lost a child in the Utøya terror attack experienced anger as part of their grieving process.

Materials and methods

Design

This study is a part of the nationwide longitudinal study of bereaved parents, siblings and close friends after the Utøya terror attack in 2011 (for further details, see Fjærestad et al., 2023). In this article, we draw on in-depth interviews nine years after the terror attack to explore bereaved parents’ descriptions of their lived experiences with anger as a part of their grieving process.

Participants

Bereaved parents were recruited from the sample who participated in the quantitative data collection of the longitudinal study in 2019 (n = 90). Of these 90 participants, 42 parents agreed to be contacted for an interview. We included 26 of those who consented for the qualitative data collection. Participants were included based on background variables (gender, location of living, scores on Inventory of Complicated Grief). For gender and location of living we sought to have as much variability as possible, and for the Inventory of Complicated Grief (ICG) we chose participants who scored above 30 six months prior to the interviews, to gain insight into the experiences of those who continued to struggle with their grief. Of the 26 participants we chose for the qualitative data collection, four withdrew their consent, leaving a sample of 22 bereaved parents (13 mothers, 9 fathers), representing 19 of the deceased children.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview guide was developed prior to data collection and was tested on one participant and reviewed before the other interviews were conducted. We had questions we asked all participants, but we also allowed them to elaborate on topics that they felt were important. All interviews opened with an invitation to say something about the child they had lost. The interview guide consisted of three main themes: (1) General functioning – grief and trauma, (2) The impact of other life events and (3) Coping, support and professional help. The data was collected during the COVID-19 pandemic and consequently 14 of the 22 interviews were conducted digitally. There was no notable difference in the data quality between physical and digital interviews, other than the physical interviews being longer, probably due to more small talk and a natural conversation style, since physical interviews took place in the participants’ homes. Interviews were conducted by the first author (n = 15), the third author (n = 6) and an experienced psychologist from the Centre for Crisis Psychology (n = 1). All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author.

Material used in this article

The data material has been utilised for two previous papers exploring parents’ experienced grief trajectories (Fjærestad et al., 2023) and their experiences with the helping system (Fjærestad et al., 2025). Part of the process in this paper was to find an analytical focus where the material was still rich enough to contribute to the field of bereavement research. There is no overlap of the parts of material analysed for this article and the previous publications. During the qualitative interviews, participants were asked if they had experienced any feelings of anger through their grieving process and what their experiences with anger had been. The first author became interested in the topic through the interviews when the participants portrayed different descriptions of how their anger had been a part of their grieving process. The interest in further analysing their experiences grew through the re-reads of the material. Unfortunately, we did not ask about their experiences regarding the impact of the collective response to the terror attack at T4, as was asked in the interviews at T2. The material, therefore, does not elaborate on how bereaved parents’ experiences with anger were affected by the cultural and collective norms on how one should feel or behave after the terror attack nine years post loss. We also looked at scores on items measuring anger in the quantitative data and found that anger is reported by many of the bereaved parents (64%) even nine years post loss, which further inspired the analytical focus of this paper.

Data analysis

Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, as described by Braun and Clarke (2006). Nvivo 12 software (QSR, 2018) was used for technical support during the analysis. In line with the articulated phases of reflexive thematic analysis, we first read through the interviews and got familiarised with the data material. After reading and rereading the material, we met to discuss the analytical focus. Through several discussions, we decided to explore: how do bereaved parents experience anger as a part of their grieving process? Transcribed interviews were then coded by the first author and segments of the text that were related to anger's placement in their grieving process and how they have experienced and coped with feelings of anger were sorted and named. After the coding process, the authors had several meetings to further discuss and review the codes and sort them into meaning units describing how the bereaved parents had experienced anger as a part of their grieving process.

Researchers

The authors of this paper are all clinical psychologists who have combined clinical work with research at the Faculty of Psychology at the University of Bergen. The first author is conducting a PhD and a specialisation in clinical psychology. The other authors are all professors with many years of experience. The second and third authors have dedicated their careers to the bereavement field, and the fourth author has a special competence in trauma and qualitative research.

Ethics

The study was conducted in line with the Helsinki Declaration and approved by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK Øst) in Norway (2018/2174). Participants were invited to participate and signed an informed consent form to be included in the study. They received information on all parts of the study and were told that they could withdraw their consent at any time after signing the form, without giving a reason. None of the interviewed participants decided to withdraw their consent. During the interview, all participants were told that they could take breaks or decide not to answer questions if they did not feel that they wanted to share information. If participants needed help following participation in the study, specially trained coordinators were able to refer them to the health care they needed. Of the 22 participants, two were referred for follow-up after the interview through these coordinators.

Results

Through the reflexive thematic analysis, we identified how bereaved parents described their experiences with anger as a part of their grieving process, constructing a thematic structure of three main themes representing and exemplifying the different roles anger had played in the participants’ grieving process: (1) Anger has not been part of my grieving process, (2) Anger has been a natural part of my grieving process and (3) Anger has been a complicated part of my grieving process. Participants contributing to the first theme did not describe any feelings of anger. For participants contributing to the second and third themes, anger was described as a part of their grieving process, and regardless of which theme they were categorised into, they expressed how the anger was directed towards the terrorist and the police or the government for not doing their job. Some reported feeling angry because the island was not secured enough, and some of them reported anger towards the media attention the terrorist got after the attack. Anger, described as self-blame, guilt, or shame, was only described by participants in theme 3.

Theme 1: anger has not been part of my grieving process (n = 6)

Six of the 22 participants had not experienced anger as a part of their grieving process. Some of them described how they were usually optimistic people who did not see any reason for anger towards anyone following the attack and explained how their positive outlook on life in general was helpful. When probed about feelings of anger, one mother said: ‘I have always had a positive view on life and have always been oriented towards solutions and not focused on problems and negativity; anger is not a part of who I am!’ (ID 132). Some participants described how they were occupied with other emotions, while others described how they, for a while after the terror attack, were waiting for the anger to show up – without this ever occurring. They had the idea that they should feel angry and felt that something must be wrong with them, as they did not seem to experience it:

A lot could have been different, but I have not experienced any anger. I remember asking people when my anger would turn up (..) I was worried about not being angry. I expected to explode but I never did (..) I was really worried because I did not feel any anger. I have not felt any anger at all. (ID 291)

Some participants explained how their lack of anger might be because their child was among the first to be killed in the attack. As one father said: ‘My son was killed very early … so if the police had been there, say twenty minutes earlier, it would not have mattered, he would still be dead’ (ID 51). Another father shared this view and said:

The reason I am not angry is that (…) I know the time of my son’s death, and for me there is no reason to be angry because he died early on and could not have been saved no matter what the police did or did not do (ID 117).

Some further described that even though their child’s death could not have been prevented, they thought about the other children that could have been saved. As one mother said: ‘For us there would not have been any difference, but there were other children that might have made it if the police had done their job’ (ID 115), showing compassion and understanding for other bereaved parents' experience of anger.

Theme 2: anger has been a natural part of my grieving process (n = 8)

Eight out of the 22 participants described anger as a natural part of their grieving process and shared how they accepted anger as a part of grieving and had been able to move in and out of anger, similar to moving in and out of other reactions. As one father said: ‘You have to accept the anger as a part of it and move on’ (ID 144). A mother further described how anger could arise on special occasions, only to subside again afterwards: ‘Anger is not always present, but it can be triggered, especially around particular dates such as the 22 of July and then it goes away again’ (ID 9). Other participants explained how they were able to feel angry but not to let the anger take over their everyday lives or disturb their grieving process. One mother said:

It is like … you cannot let the anger take over and I do not let it, but I feel angry for everything that could have been avoided and everything that should have been done and what went wrong. Those who could have lived … if something was different … It makes me bitter but I don’t let it take over my life. (ID 3)

There was also one participant who described being very angry in the first two weeks but then experienced how the feeling calmed down and disappeared. She said:

I have had a lot of anger towards the police because they have not done … we saw them on TV and … it was really bad. The anger lasted for two weeks, and I couldn’t sleep and … then it just went away, I was done with it. It was nice when it went away because it was … it took a lot of energy over those weeks. (ID 284)

Although these participants described anger as a part of their process in a way that seemed similar to other emotions, they all talked about anger towards the terrorist and some of them even mentioned having thoughts of revenge. As one father said: ‘If he ever gets out of prison and I find his address, I would be happy to kill him’ (ID 204). Similar statements were made by many of the bereaved parents.

Theme 3: anger has been a complicating part of my grieving process (n = 8)

Eight out of the 22 participants described how anger had been a difficult part of their grieving process. They talked about anger standing in the way of their grief or how they avoided feelings of anger because it was overwhelming, or they felt they had to move on or get rid of it somehow. One father said how he felt like there was nothing he could do about his anger:

No … I am a quite calm person, and I don’t get angry easily … but this … I am angry … very angry … there is a lot of anger … but I know that I can’t do much about it. So, it is just there, and I try not to think about it. (ID 91)

For some, anger was an emotion that took up more space than other emotions or stood in the way of other grief reactions or made them feel stuck in their grieving process. One mother said: ‘I couldn’t feel any grief, I didn’t feel anything … I was so angry, and I hated so much … ’ (ID 31), showing how anger became an overwhelming feeling. Some of them further described feeling stuck in guilt and being angry with themselves. As one mother said: ‘Yes, shame and guilt … I feel stupid and I am stuck (ID 286). Another one elaborated:

I struggle with guilt … I was the one who allowed my child to go there, who drove her up there … I shouldn’t have. She was so young, I should have said she couldn’t go. I am not angry with anyone else but myself. (ID 209)

Being angry with the police for them not doing their job was common in this group of participants. As one mother said: ‘I am pissed off because the police saved themselves and not our kids. I will never make peace with it; it makes me really angry’ (ID 45). The terrorist getting coverage in the media was described as another source of anger. As one father said:

I remember … the first couple of years … I got very angry. It felt like NRK and TV2 (news channels) had a microphone for the mass murderer … he got so much time on display to promote his views. It affected me very much; I got really angry (ID 36).

These participants described different types of conflicting feelings around the anger they experienced. Some of them described how anger held them back in their grieving process. As one father said:

It doesn’t help to get angry but (…) you have to move on and not think about … can’t go around being angry and complaining, you have to move on even though you are stuck (…) I am stuck in that … everyone moves on … time goes on but I am stuck in anger. (ID 270)

This father's description also shows how anger seems to be a feeling that is not allowed and should be avoided, when he stated, ‘you can’t go around being angry’, as if the feeling of anger is voluntary.

Discussion

This study shows differences in the individual experience of anger as part of grief for bereaved parents after the Utøya terror attack. Some parents had not experienced anger at all, which for some was experienced as problematic in itself. For those parents who did experience anger, they reported anger pointed at the same sources (the perpetrator, the government, the media, the police) regardless of how they described their anger in their grieving process. For the group describing anger as a complicating part of their grief, anger directed at oneself as shame or guilt was also expressed, suggesting that specific types of anger are more likely to complicate grief. The study by Nijborg et al. (2025) supports this notion and shows how different types of anger after traumatic loss have different influences on mental health, and that self-directed anger is associated with complicated grief. For bereaved parents who described anger as a natural part of their grieving process, their descriptions are similar to what is described in previous research about coping with grief after violent or traumatic loss (Rynearson, 2013). They stated how they were able to move in and out of their anger, similar to how Stroebe and Schut (2010) describe the oscillation in and out of moving towards or away from their grief, as a way of coping with loss. Further, they said how their anger was more prominent on special memorable days, such as the 22nd of July, which is also described in previous research regarding other emotional responses present in grief (Worden, 2009). Similarly, for those who described how anger was a complicating part of their grief, their descriptions were concurrent with symptoms described as complicated or prolonged grief. They portrayed how they were stuck in their grief either due to feelings of anger towards themselves and thoughts of what they could have done or should have done, or because they found it difficult to allow anger to take up space. They talked about not knowing what to do about the feeling and how they felt they had to shove it away and avoid it. Our results do not reveal why they felt like they had to suppress it, but we hypothesise that it might be related to how cultural norms and collective rituals can regulate which emotions are seen as acceptable in grief (Silverman et al., 2021). In light of this, the Norwegian collective response after the terror attack, which focused on unity and compassion, could have contributed to bereaved parents suppressing feelings of anger. The avoidance of anger might have been present due to the collective expectation that one should not entertain those feelings. Interestingly, some of our participants expressed concern over the absence of anger, indicating self-critical awareness of their grieving process. This suggests that cultural scripts about ‘normal grief’ may lead individuals to monitor and evaluate their emotional responses, potentially fostering feelings of inadequacy in their grieving process. Neimeyer et al. (2014) describe grief as a socially constructed experience, and our findings support the notion that bereaved individuals may internalise societal expectations about how one ‘should’ grieve, including whether anger is appropriate or necessary. Studies from other contexts, such as the Sewol ferry disaster in South Korea (Chae et al., 2018; Choi & Cho, 2019; Kim et al., 2020), show how bereaved families expressed intense anger and bitterness towards authorities due to perceived negligence and betrayal. These findings suggest that anger in grief may be particularly pronounced when the loss is attributed to human error. Although the Utøya attack was an act of terrorism, some bereaved parents in our study expressed similar anger towards the police and government for not doing their job in the aftermath, indicating that perceived preventability and accountability may be key factors in the emergence of anger. Thoughts of revenge were also described by some parents unrelated to how they described their experience of anger, which may indicate that those thoughts can be something separate from the direct expression of anger or the experience of the emotion. Thoughts of revenge may not necessarily be linked to the feeling of anger but could also be an expression of wanting justice to be served or an expression of the inhumane act of terror. It may also be that cultural norms on what is considered normal influence what bereaved parents deem appropriate to talk about or feel, as described in previous literature (Neimeyer et al., 2014; Walter, 1996).

Clinical implications

The three themes found in this study illustrate how anger as a grief response can be experienced in different ways. Some of the bereaved parents in this study described an integrated grief, where emotional responses remain flexible and do not interfere with adaptation (Shear, 2015), while others described anger as a complicating part of their grief, which is seen as a risk of prolonged grief disorder (Nijborg et al., 2025). Clinically, these distinctions highlight the importance of assessing not only whether anger is present but also the function it has for the individual and their grieving process.

Limitations

One notable limitation of our study is that we did not include specific questions about how participants perceived the cultural and collective response to the terror attack, nor how these societal norms may have influenced their emotional experiences. As a result, our findings do not capture the full extent to which the broader cultural context shaped expressions of anger. Qualitative research is further limited in that the analysis can lead to different analytical foci based on who conducts the analysis, but this limitation is accounted for through several discussions between the four authors of this paper.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the multifaceted nature of anger in bereavement following the Utøya terror attack. Anger was experienced in different ways: absent, natural, or complicating, highlighting its complexity as a grief response. Particularly, self-directed anger emerged as a marker of psychological distress, consistent with prior research on complicated grief. Although the interview data did not explicitly address the influence of cultural and societal norms, the broader collective response to the attack may have shaped emotional expression among the bereaved. The absence of anger in some participants, coupled with their concern about this absence, suggests an internalisation of normative grief expectations. This underscores the need for future research to examine how collective identity and cultural framing influence emotional regulation in traumatic bereavement.
