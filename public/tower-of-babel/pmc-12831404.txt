Baricitinib ameliorates skin fibrosis via direct fibroblast suppression and endothelial exosome-mediated paracrine signaling
Authors: Zhanying Hou, Xiufeng Zhao, Fang Liu, Shi Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNovel therapies are urgently needed for diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis (dcSSc). Aberrant JAK/STAT signaling drives fibrosis, but JAK inhibitors’ effects on vascular pathology and potential synergy with endothelial-derived exosomes remain unclear.MethodsUtilizing bleomycin (BLM)-induced human skin fibroblast (HSF) activation and hypoxia-impaired human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) models, we investigated the anti-fibrotic mechanisms of the JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor Baricitinib. Functional assays assessed proliferation, migration, tube formation, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and cytokine production. Conditioned media (CM) and exosomes from Baricitinib-treated HUVECs were applied to BLM-HSFs to evaluate paracrine/exosomal roles.ResultsBaricitinib (6 nmol/L) directly inhibited BLM-induced HSF proliferation, Collagen I/III, α-SMA expression, and actin polymerization. In hypoxia-damaged HUVECs, Baricitinib (54 nmol/L) restored proliferation, migration, tube formation, and suppressed JAK/STAT3 hyperactivation. Crucially, CM from Baricitinib-treated HUVECs significantly attenuated BLM-HSF fibrotic activation, an effect abolished by exosome inhibitor GW4869. Exosomes from Baricitinib-treated HUVECs exhibited enhanced uptake by HSFs and superior suppression of proliferation, cytoskeletal reorganization, and profibrotic marker expression compared to control exosomes; these effects were diminished by exosome disruption.ConclusionBaricitinib alleviates skin fibrosis via a dual mechanism: direct suppression of fibroblast activation and indirect mitigation through the “reprogramming” of HUVEC-derived exosomes. By rescuing endothelial dysfunction and enhancing the anti-fibrotic cargo of endothelial exosomes, Baricitinib interrupts the vascular damage-fibrosis cycle in dcSSc. This identifies endothelial exosomes as key mediators of JAK inhibitor efficacy, offering a novel therapeutic paradigm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03710-9.

Introduction

Systemic sclerosis (SSc), particularly diffuse cutaneous SSc (dcSSc), is a devastating autoimmune disease characterized by widespread vascular lesions, immune dysregulation, and progressive fibrosis of skin and internal organs. It is associated with high mortality and limited treatments [1]. Rapidly progressive skin fibrosis, digital ulcers, and visceral involvement in dcSSc severely impair quality of life [2]. Existing therapies like methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, or mycophenolate mofetil have controversial efficacy, and while novel biologics show potential in improving modified Rodnan skin score (mRSS), they lack statistical significance, highlighting the urgent need for novel anti-fibrotic therapies [3–6].

SSc fibrosis involves complex pathway interactions, with aberrant JAK/STAT signaling being a key driver [7]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and the potent pro-fibrotic factor TGF-β1/2 activate JAKs (especially JAK2), coordinating inflammatory cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, leading to excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition [8]. TGF-β1/2 directly activates JAK2, strengthening the rationale for targeting JAK in anti-fibrotic/anti-inflammatory therapy [9]. However, the impact of JAK inhibitors on SSc vascular lesions remains unclear.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) are a potential SSc therapy due to immunomodulatory, pro-angiogenic, and anti-fibrotic properties [10]. However, systemically infused HUVEC poorly home to target tissues; their effects stem primarily from paracrine mechanisms. Exosomes, key paracrine carriers from HUVEC rich in bioactive molecules, exhibit therapeutic effects comparable or superior to intact HUVEC in fibrotic disease models, avoiding cell therapy risks like immune rejection and tumorigenesis [11]. They are also more stable for storage. Nevertheless, HUVEC-derived exosomes require rigorous in vivo validation of their anti-fibrotic efficacy in SSc.

JAK inhibitors (JAKi) like tofacitinib and Baricitinib are effective in autoimmune diseases like rheumatoid arthritis [12]. Case reports and small studies suggest potential for improving SSc skin hardening, digital ulcers, and SSc-associated interstitial lung disease (ILD) [13]. Preclinical studies support that targeting JAK2 improves skin fibrosis in SSc models [14].

This study focuses on the JAK inhibitor Baricitinib. Using a bleomycin (BLM)-induced skin fibrosis cell model, it aims to elucidate whether Baricitinib exerts anti-fibrotic effects by regulating exosomes secreted via HUVEC-mediated mechanisms, providing new insights for SSc treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell source and ethics

Human skin fibroblasts (HSFs; #CP-H103, Procell, China) were cultured in the manufacturer’s complete medium (#CM-H103, Procell) containing 0.5 ng/mL EGF and 5 ng/mL bFGF. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, #JY-J992) were obtained from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and maintained in Endothelial Cell Medium (#1001; ScienCell Research Laboratories, USA). This study was conducted exclusively with commercially acquired human cell lines. Therefore, in accordance with our institutional policy and because no human participants or animals were involved, ethical committee approval was not applicable for this research.

Establishment of fibrosis model in HSFs

To determine the optimal concentration of bleomycin sulfate (BLM; #HY-17565, MCE) for inducing fibroblast activation, HSFs were treated with various concentrations of BLM (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 ng/mL) for different durations (24, 48, and 72 h), as previously described [15].

To investigate the effects of JAK inhibition on BLM-induced HSF proliferation and fibrosis, HSFs were first treated with 40 ng/mL BLM for 24 h, followed by incubation with Baricitinib (#HY-15315, MCE) at varying concentrations (1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 nmol/L). The Baricitinib stock solution was prepared in DMSO and solubilized by sonication with applied heat. Following 24-hour treatments, total RNA and protein were extracted from HSFs to assess gene and protein expression.

Establishment of hypoxia model in HUVECs

To assess hypoxia-induced endothelial dysfunction, HUVECs were placed in a sealed chamber with an anaerobic gas-generating pouch (#C-35, Mitsubishi Gas Chemical, Japan) and incubated under hypoxic conditions (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, 37 °C) for 0 (normoxic control), 2, 4, 6, 12, or 24 h. The 0-hour control group was maintained under standard normoxic conditions (21% O₂, 5% CO₂, 37 °C) without chamber placement.

To evaluate the effects of JAK inhibition, parallel hypoxia-induced HUVECs (exposed to 6-hour hypoxia) were treated with Baricitinib (13.5, 27, 54, 108, or 216 nmol/L) for 24-hour treatments during hypoxic exposure.

Isolation and characterization of exosomes

Exosomes were isolated from HUVEC cultures using the Total Exosome Isolation Kit (#4478359, Invitrogen, USA). Briefly, HUVECs were cultured in serum-free medium for 48 h. The conditioned medium was collected and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22-µm pore membrane. Exosome Isolation Reagent was added to the clarified supernatant at a 1:4 (v/v) ratio, mixed thoroughly by inversion, and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The resulting exosome pellet was washed with PBS and resuspended in PBS.

The Morphology of isolated exosomes was assessed by transmission electron microscopy (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan); Particle size distribution and concentration were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight NS300, Malvern Panalytical, UK); Exosomal identity was confirmed by Western blot analysis of canonical exosomal markers.

Experimental groups

To validate the anti-fibrotic effects of HUVECs-derived conditioned media (CM), HUVECs were treated with 54 nmol/L Baricitinib and/or 10 µM GW4869 (an exosome secretion inhibitor) in endothelial cell medium for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Following incubation, the CM was collected and used to treat BLM-activated HSFs for 24 h; subsequently, HSFs were harvested for functional assays.

To evaluate the anti-fibrotic effects of HUVECs-derived exosomes (EXO), HUVECs were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in endothelial cell medium supplemented with 54 nmol/L Baricitinib. After incubation, EXOs were isolated and then applied to BLM-activated HSFs for 24 h before harvesting the HSFs for functional assays.

Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The cell supernatant samples were collected from the various groups. The inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (#E-HSEL-H0001), IL-6 (#E-HSEL-H0003), and TNF-α (#E-EL-H0109) were measured with ELISA. All ELISA kits were purchased from Elabscience Company and used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 kits exhibit detection sensitivities of 0.3, 0.49, and 0.94 pg/mL with working ranges of 0.63-40, 0.78-50, and 1.56–100 pg/mL, respectively.

Reverse Transcription-quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

For gene expression analysis by RT-qPCR, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well. After overnight adhesion, the cells were treated as specified and harvested at the indicated time points post-treatment for total RNA extraction. Total cellular RNA was isolated using TRIzol™ Reagent (#15596026CN, Invitrogen). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (#RR055A, Takara, JAPAN). qPCR analysis was conducted on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System using SYBR Green-based master mix (Takara Bio) under the following cycling conditions: denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were quantified using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt), normalized to GAPDH expression as the housekeeping gene. The primer sequences used are listed in Table 1 (Supporting Information).

Western blot analysis

For protein analysis by Western blot, cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Post-adhesion, the cells were exposed to the experimental treatments, and cellular proteins were subsequently extracted by lysis at the designated times. Then cell protein extract was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (P0009, Beyotime, China). Western blot assay was carried out as previously described [16]. Utilizing the primary antibodies listed below: Collagen I (ab138492, 1:1000, abcam, USA), Collagen III (ab184993, 1:1000, abcam), α-SMA (ab7817, 1 µg/mL, abcam), JAK1 (#3344, 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), phosphorylated form of JAK1 (ab138005, 1:1000, abcam), JAK2 (ab108596, 1:5000, abcam), phosphorylated form of JAK2 (ab32101, 1:1000, abcam), STAT3 (ab68153, 1:2000, abcam), phosphorylated form of STAT3 (ab76315, 1:2000, abcam), and GAPDH (ab8245, 1:5000, abcam). Chemiluminescence was visualized and quantified by Tanon 5200 Automatic Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

After seeding in 24-well plates overnight at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well, the adherent cells were treated as required and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in for 10 min, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with Alexa Fluor®555-conjugated anti-α-SMA antibody (ab202509, abcam), Alexa Fluor®488- conjugated anti-Collagen III (ab237238, abcam), and Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated phalloidin (C2201S, Beyotime). After washing, Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. The cells were observed under the fluorescence microscope (CK53, Olympus, Janpan).

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well, allowed to adhere overnight, and treated with experimental conditions. Following treatment, CCK-8 reagent (#40203ES60, YEASEN, China) was added directly to each well. Plates were gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

EdU staining

After being seeded on coverslips at a density of 0.5 × 10⁵ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight, cells were subjected to the experimental treatments. Subsequently, they were pulsed with 10 µM EdU reagent (#C0075S, Beyotime) for 2 h at 37 °C. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100. The Click reaction cocktail (containing 555-azide, CuSO₄, and Click Additive solution) was applied for 30 min at RT (protected from light). Samples were washed three times with PBS, counterstained with DAPI (5 µg/mL, 10 min) for nuclear visualization, mounted with antifade medium, and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Scratch wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Following adhesion, they were subjected to the experimental treatments. Upon reaching confluence, a uniform scratch wound was generated in the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip.Then gently washed three times with PBS to remove dislodged cells, and replenished with fresh serum-free medium. Cells were incubated under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), with wound closure monitored by phase-contrast microscopy at 0 and 30 h post-scratching. Migratory capacity was quantified through residual wound width measurements using ImageJ software, with percentage closure calculated as [(Width0h– Width30h) / Width0h] × 100.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (#356234, Corning, USA) was thawed overnight at 4 °C. Aseptically, 50 µL was coated per 96-well well (avoiding bubbles), polymerized at 37 °C/5% CO₂ (30 min). HUVECs (1 × 10⁴ cells/mL) in 100 µL were seeded onto Matrigel and cultured overnight. After medium removal, cells were stained with 100 µL Calcein AM (#C2012, Coring) away from light, replaced with fresh medium (37 °C, 30 min, dark), washed with PBS (3×), and imaged in serum-free medium. Tubule networks were quantified using Image J.

Exosome uptake assay

Purified exosomes were labeled with 2 µM PKH26 fluorescent dye (#40780ES20, YEASEN) for 5 min at RT, with staining quenched by adding 1% BSA. Labeled exosomes were washed via ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 70 min, 4 °C), resuspended in PBS, and added to HSFs pre-cultured to 70% confluence. After 12 h incubation (37 °C, 5% CO₂), cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA (15 min), counterstained with DAPI, and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (v.9.0c) software was used for the data analysis. All values are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Unpaired Student’s t-test was used for comparison differences between two groups. One-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was used to compare multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined as P value < 0.05.

Results

Baricitinib inhibits skin fibroblasts proliferation and ECM deposition

To determine the optimal concentration of BLM for inducing fibroblast activation, HSFs were treated with various concentrations of BLM (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 ng/mL) for 24, 48, or 72 h. Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 1A, HSF proliferation peaked at a BLM concentration of 40 ng/mL. Consequently, 40 ng/mL BLM was used to establish the HSF proliferation model in subsequent experiments. We next evaluated the effect of BLM on the profibrotic phenotype of HSFs in vitro. Protein expression of Collagen I, Collagen III (reflecting ECM deposition), and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (a hallmark of fibroblast activation) was primarily assessed. BLM treatment significantly increased the protein levels of Collagen I, Collagen III, and α-SMA (Fig. 1B). This finding was corroborated by FITC-phalloidin labeling, which revealed enhanced actin filament formation (Fig. 1C). Given that skin fibrosis is accompanied by inflammation, we measured cytokine production. As JAK proteins are characteristic of SSC tissues, we examined their expressions. BLM-induced HSF activation also led to a significant increase in JAK1 and JAK2 gene expression (Fig. 2A-B). We then evaluated Baricitinib, a selective JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor, for its ability to modulate BLM-induced fibroblast activation. Baricitinib treatment inhibited HSF proliferation, with the most potent inhibitory effect observed at 6 nmol/L (Fig. 2C). Therefore, 6 nmol/L Baricitinib was selected for subsequent functional assays. Baricitinib treatment significantly reduced the BLM-induced upregulation of both JAK1 and JAK2 gene expressions (Figs. 2D-E). Consistent with this, FITC-phalloidin labeling showed a marked reduction in actin filaments in Baricitinib-treated cells compared to BLM-stimulated controls (Fig. 2F). Finally, we investigated the effect of Baricitinib on profibrotic factor expression. Baricitinib significantly attenuated the BLM-induced increase in Collagen I, Collagen III, and α-SMA protein levels (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that Baricitinib suppresses BLM-induced HSF proliferation, ECM production, and profibrotic activation.

Baricitinib facilitated cell proliferation, migration and tube formation in hypoxia-exposed HUVECs

Vascular endothelial cell dysfunction, a key feature of SSc, is primarily driven by hypoxia. Compared to normoxia control, hypoxia treatment significantly reduced HUVEC proliferation capacity (Fig. 3A-C) and tube formation ability (Fig. 3D) in a time-dependent manner.

To investigate Baricitinib’s role in hypoxia, HUVECs were treated with varying concentrations of Baricitinib under hypoxic condition. Baricitinib ameliorated hypoxia-induced cell injury in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4A). Among the concentrations tested, 54 nmol/L Baricitinib most effectively reversed the hypoxia-mediated reduction in cell viability (Fig. 4A-B) and was therefore selected for subsequent mechanistic studies. Baricitinib (54 nmol/L) significantly restored hypoxia-impaired tube formation (Fig. 4C) and migration capacity (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, hypoxia activated the JAK/STAT3 pathway compared to normoxia controls, an effect markedly suppressed by Baricitinib treatment (Fig. 4E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Baricitinib attenuates hypoxia-induced endothelial dysfunction in HUVECs by restoring proliferative, angiogenic, and migratory capacities while inhibiting JAK/STAT3 hyperactivation.

Baricitinib attenuates BLM-induced HSF fibrotic activation via HUVEC paracrine signaling

To investigate paracrine mechanisms, CM were collected from HUVECs treated with Baricitinib, GW4869, or their combination and applied to BLM-induced HSFs (Fig. 5A). CM derived from untreated HUVECs significantly suppressed the proliferation of BLM-induced HSFs. Notably, CM from Baricitinib-treated HUVECs (Bar-CM) further inhibited HSF proliferation. Conversely, CM pre-incubated with GW4869 (GW4869-CM) attenuated the anti-proliferative effects of both control CM and Bar-CM, enhancing HSF proliferation (Fig. 5B). Similarly, HUVEC-derived CM significantly reduced actin filament fluorescence intensity in BLM-induced HSFs. Bar-CM induced a more pronounced reduction in actin cytoskeletal levels. However, GW4869-CM partially reversed the suppression of actin filaments by both control CM and Bar-CM, restoring actin expression (Fig. 5C). Consistent with these functional changes, protein levels of profibrotic markers (Collagen I, Collagen III, and α-SMA) mirrored the observed patterns (Fig. 5D-G). Collectively, these data demonstrate that Baricitinib attenuates BLM-induced HSF fibrotic activation by modulating HUVEC paracrine signaling. Baricitinib mitigates BLM-induced HSF fibrotic activation via HUVEC exosome secretion

To elucidate the role of HUVEC paracrine signaling in Baricitinib-mediated inhibition of BLM-induced HSF fibrosis, exosomes were isolated from HUVEC cultures. Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed a monodisperse population of exosomes with a peak diameter of 165.4 nm (Fig. 6A). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed classical cup-shaped morphology with intact bilayered membranes (Fig. 6B). Immunoblotting demonstrated strong enrichment of exosomal surface markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 (Fig. 6C). These results collectively validate successful exosome isolation. To assess functional effects, HUVEC-derived exosomes (EXO), exosomes isolated from Baricitinib-treated HUVECs (Bar-EXO), or Tween-20 (breaker exosomes) were added to BLM-induced HSFs (Fig. 6D). EXO significantly suppressed BLM-induced HSF proliferation, while Bar-EXO exerted a stronger anti-proliferative effect. Tween-20 disruption attenuated Bar-EXO-mediated proliferation inhibition (Fig. 6E). To evaluate cellular uptake, PKH26-labeled exosomes were co-cultured with HSFs. Both EXO and Bar-EXO accumulated abundantly in HSF perinuclear regions, whereas Tween-20 pretreatment markedly reduced exosome internalization (Fig. 6F). Parallel effects were observed on cytoskeletal organization: EXO reduced actin fluorescence intensity, while Bar-EXO induced more pronounced cytoskeletal suppression. Tween-20 disruption partially reversed Bar-EXO-mediated actin inhibition (Fig. 6G). Consistently, protein levels of profibrotic markers (Collagen I, Collagen III, α-SMA) mirrored this functional pattern: Bar-EXO most potently suppressed expression, while Tween-20 restored fibrotic protein levels (Fig. 6H-K). Collectively, these data demonstrate that HUVEC-derived exosomes particularly those from Baricitinib-treated cells attenuate BLM-induced HSF fibrotic activation.

Discussion

This study confirmed that Baricitinib ameliorates BLM-induced skin fibrosis through a dual mechanism: direct inhibition of fibroblast activation and indirect regulation of endothelial cell-derived exosomal signaling. Core findings include: Baricitinib directly inhibited HSF proliferation and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition; it restored endothelial cell function impaired by hypoxia; paracrine factors from HUVECs mediated Baricitinib’s anti-fibrotic effects on HSFs; and exosomes derived from Baricitinib-treated HUVECs were the key carriers mitigating fibrotic activation. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized endothelial-dependent pathway for the anti-fibrotic action of JAK inhibition.

Data demonstrated that Baricitinib significantly reduced TGF-β-stimulated HSF proliferation and collagen synthesis. This aligns with the established understanding that JAK/STAT signaling is hyperactivated in SSc fibroblasts and synergizes with TGF-β1/2 to drive fibrosis [17]. The classical Smad pathway and non-classical JAK2 activation by TGF-β1/2 collectively amplify ECM gene expression [18]. Baricitinib’s interference with JAK/STAT transduction likely blocks this crosstalk – evidenced by the downregulation of COL1A1, α-SMA, and fibronectin mRNA [19]. Notably, the dose-dependent reduction in fibroblast metabolic activity suggests Baricitinib may inhibit hyperproliferation via cell cycle arrest [20]. These direct anti-fibrotic effects provide a mechanistic basis for the clinical observation of JAK inhibitors improving mRSS in dcSSc patients.

A salient discovery is Baricitinib’s reversal of hypoxia-induced HUVEC dysfunction. Baricitinib treatment restored these parameters to near-normoxic levels, indicating its potent pro-angiogenic activity. This likely stems from JAK/STAT regulation of HIF-1α stability and VEGF signaling. Hypoxia triggers JAK2/VHL complex formation, disrupting HIF-1α stability and impairing angiogenesis [21]. By inhibiting JAK2, Baricitinib may maintain HIF-1α function, enhancing VEGF-A secretion [22]. The improvement in endothelial migratory capacity further substantiates its vascular reparative effect, which is crucial for reversing SSc-associated vasculopathy – vascular regeneration impairment is a precursor event to fibrosis [23].

This study is the first to reveal that Baricitinib indirectly inhibits fibroblast activation by repairing hypoxia-damaged endothelial function. Although proteomic analysis of exosomes was not performed, functional experiments with conditioned medium (CM) provided key mechanistic clues: Baricitinib-treated HUVEC-CM significantly reduced HSF collagen synthesis, an effect completely abolished upon exosome depletion, confirming the HUVEC secretome as the core mediator of the anti-fibrotic action.

This study found that exosomes from Baricitinib-pretreated HUVECs (Bar-Exo) exhibited stronger anti-fibrotic efficacy compared to the control group (Ctrl-Exo) – significantly suppressing α-SMA and COL3A1 expression in HSFs. This phenomenon resonates with recent clinical findings: serum exosomes from SSc-ILD patients carry specific protein biomarkers whose levels negatively correlate with fibrosis severity [24]. We thus speculate Bar-Exo may be enriched with similar regulatory molecules. However, it must be acknowledged that the current study has key limitations: lack of direct analysis of exosomal components (proteins/RNA), preventing identification of the active constituents and their targets; and inability to fully exclude the independent contribution of soluble factors. Future work requires integrating aptamer-based proteomics to compare differential protein profiles between SSc patient serum exosomes and Bar-Exo, focusing on screening translational medicine targets such as TGFβ pathway inhibitors.

The unique value of Baricitinib in SSc treatment lies in its dual capacity to synergistically intervene in vascular pathology and fibrosis [25]. The endothelial function restoration and inflammatory factor downregulation discovered herein align well with early SSc pathological features, providing a mechanistic explanation for the mRSS improvement observed in clinical trials. Compared to cell therapies, Baricitinib’s strategy of inducing endogenous endothelial cells to secrete “reprogrammed exosomes” holds greater clinical feasibility. This model of activating the host’s own repair system via an oral drug represents a novel paradigm that breaks away from traditional single-target anti-fibrotic therapies.

Key shortcomings of this work must be addressed: The bleomycin model only partially simulates the complex pathology of SSc, necessitating validation of conclusions in models like Tsk-1 that more closely resemble human autoimmune features [26]; the mechanism of exosome action remains incompletely elucidated, particularly the specific uptake pathways by fibroblasts and their in vivo biodistribution; and the failure to incorporate SSc patient-derived primary endothelial cells to validate Baricitinib’s regulatory effects is a significant omission. Future research should focus on three directions: First, establishing exosome tracing technology to dynamically observe the homing and delivery process of Bar-Exo in fibrotic tissues; second, utilizing CRISPR technology to screen for key effector molecules within exosomes; and finally, conducting exosome sequencing from SSc patient-derived endothelial cells to identify biomarkers predictive of Baricitinib efficacy.

In summary, Baricitinib alleviates skin fibrosis through a novel mechanism of “endothelial exosome reprogramming.” By rescuing endothelial dysfunction and transforming HUVEC exosomes into anti-fibrotic carriers, it interrupts the core SSc pathology – the vicious cycle of vascular damage and fibroblast activation. This work establishes endothelial exosomes as dynamic mediators of JAK inhibitor efficacy, opening new avenues for exosome-targeted therapy in SSc.
