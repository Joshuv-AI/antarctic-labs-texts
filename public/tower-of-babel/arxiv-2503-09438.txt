Abstract
. In this paper we deal with the following weakly coupled nonlinear Schrödinger system { − Δ α ​ u + ω ​ u = | u | 2 ​ u + β ​ u ​ | v | 2 in ​ ℝ 2 , − Δ ​ v + ω ~ ​ v = | v | 2 ​ v + β ​ | u | 2 ​ v in ​ ℝ 2 , \displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\alpha}u+\omega u=|u|^{2}u+\beta u|v|^{2}&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},\\ -\Delta v+\tilde{\omega}v=|v|^{2}v+\beta|u|^{2}v&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},\end{cases} where − Δ α -\Delta_{\alpha} denotes the Laplacian operator with a point interaction, ω \omega greater then a suitable positive constant, ω ~ > 0 \tilde{\omega}>0 , and β ⩾ 0 \beta\geqslant 0 . For any β ⩾ 0 \beta\geqslant 0 this system admits the existence of a ground state solution which can have only one nontrivial component or two nontrivial components and which could be regular or singular. We analyse this phenomenon showing how this depends strongly on the parameters. Moreover we study the asymptotic behaviour of ground state solutions whenever β → ∞ \beta\to\infty .

1.
Introduction

In last years, great attention has been posed to systems of weakly coupled nonlinear Schrödinger equations
of the type

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta u+\omega_{1}u=|u|^{2}u+\beta u|v|^{2}&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N},\\
-\Delta v+\omega_{2}v=|v|^{2}v+\beta|u|^{2}v&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N},\end{cases}

where
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}>0
and
\beta\geqslant 0
are fixed parameters.
Such systems describe physical phenomena such as the propagation in birefringent optical fibres,
Kerr-like photorefractive media in optics and Bose–Einstein condensates (see, for example,
[
19
,
24
,
25
]
).
Observe that if
u
is a nontrivial solution of the single nonlinear Schrödinger equation

-\Delta u+\omega_{1}u=|u|^{2}u\qquad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}^{N}},

then
(u,0)
is a nontrivial solution of the system (
1.1
). Analogously
(0,v)
is a nontrivial solution of the system (
1.1
) if
v
is a nontrivial solution of

-\Delta v+\omega_{2}v=|v|^{2}v\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N}.

Such types of solutions are usually called
scalar solutions
of (
1.1
), while a solution
(u,v)
is called
vector
if both the components are nontrivial. So, it is interesting to study the existence of ground state solutions associated to (
1.1
) understanding if such solutions are scalar or vector. Actually, this depends on
\beta
and the ground state solution is scalar under a certain value of
\beta
and, after that, it becomes vector. See, for instance,
[
22
,
23
,
27
]
.

A similar phenomenon occurs also for systems of nonlinear Schrödinger
equations with three wave interaction of the following type

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta u+\omega_{1}u=|u|^{2}u+\beta vz&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N},\\
-\Delta v+\omega_{2}v=|v|^{2}v+\beta uz&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N},\\
-\Delta z+\omega_{3}z=|z|^{2}z+\beta uv&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{N},\\
\end{cases}

where, as before,
\beta\geqslant 0
and
\omega_{i}>0
, for
i=1,2,3
. These systems describe the Raman amplification used in optical telecommunication (see
[
18
]
). The existence of scalar and vector ground state solutions has been studied in
[
21
,
26
]
.
In
[
21
]
, moreover, the asymptotic behaviour of ground states solutions whenever
\beta
goes to
\infty
has been considered.

On the other hand, in the presence of defects
or impurities, the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with point interaction has been recently proposed as an effective model for a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC). See
[
29
,
30
]
for the physical background.
In the 1D case,
there has been a lot of works for the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with point interaction,
such as the existence of a ground state solution
and the (in)stability of standing waves;
we refer to
[
9
,
14
,
15
,
20
]
and references therein.
Instead, the higher dimensional case is less studied. In presence of a pure power nonlinearity, the
2D problem has been considered in
[
1
,
16
]
,
while the 3D problem in
[
2
]
. Instead
[
28
]
deals with a more general nonlinearity in
2
and
3
dimensions. Concerning with time-dependent problems in higher dimensional case,
we refer to
[
10
,
11
,
13
,
17
]
and references therein. See also
[
12
]
for related problems.

Formally, in the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with point interaction, the classical Laplacian operator is replaced by
-\Delta_{\alpha}
.
Here
-\Delta_{\alpha}
can be thought as the two or three dimensional version of the Schrödinger operator with the Dirac delta concentrated in zero
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\alpha\delta_{0}
.

A rigorous formulation is given through the self-adjoint extension
of the operator
-\Delta|_{C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\})}
.
Then it is known that there exists a family
\{-\Delta_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}

of self-adjoint operators which realize all point perturbations of
-\Delta
;
see
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
As a consequence, the domain of
-\Delta_{\alpha}
is given by

\displaystyle D(-\Delta_{\alpha})

\displaystyle:=\big\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2}}):\text{there exist}\ q\in\mathbb{C}\text{ and }\lambda>0\text{ s.t. }

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\phi_{\lambda}:=u-q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\in H^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})\text{ and }\phi_{\lambda}(0)=(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})q\big\},

where
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is the Green’s function of
-\Delta+\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(1.3)

\theta_{\lambda}:=\displaystyle\frac{\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}}{2}\right)+\gamma}{2\pi}

and
\gamma
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.
Moreover the action is defined by

-\Delta_{\alpha}u:=-\Delta\phi_{\lambda}-q\lambda\mathcal{G}_{\lambda},\quad\text{for all }u=\phi_{\lambda}+q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\in D(-\Delta_{\alpha}).

It is also known that
\sigma_{ess}(-\Delta_{\alpha})=[0,\infty)
.
Moreover

-\Delta_{\alpha}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue
-\omega_{\alpha}

which is given by

(1.4)

\omega_{\alpha}:=4e^{-4\pi\alpha-2\gamma}.

By the definition of
\theta_{\lambda}
in (
1.3
),
we find that
\alpha+\theta_{\lambda}>0
for any
\lambda>\omega_{\alpha}
.

The function
u\in D(-\Delta_{\alpha})
consists of a
regular part

\phi_{\lambda}
,
on which
-\Delta_{\alpha}
acts as the standard Laplacian,
and a
singular part

q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
,
on which
-\Delta_{\alpha}
acts as the multiplication by
-\lambda
.
These two components are coupled by the boundary condition

\phi_{\lambda}(0)=(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})q
.
The strength
q=q(u)
is called the
charge
of
u
.
In particular, we have that

\langle-\Delta_{\alpha}u,u\rangle=\|\nabla\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}-\lambda\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})|q|^{2}.

Here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard
L^{2}
-inner product.
As observed in
[
1
, Remark 2.1]
,
\lambda
is a free parameter
and it does not affect the definition of
-\Delta_{\alpha}

nor the charge
q(u)
.

The energy space associated with
-\Delta_{\alpha}
is defined by

H^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{2}}):=\left\{u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}^{2}}):\text{there exist }q\in\mathbb{C}\text{ and }\lambda>0\text{ s.t. }\phi_{\lambda}:=u-q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})\right\}.

We remark that even if we work on this low regularity space,

q(u)
is independent of
\lambda
and uniquely determined (see Lemma
2.1
below).
Therefore, in the definition of
H^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
,
we do not stress the dependence of
q(u)
with respect to
\lambda
.

For any
\omega>\omega_{\alpha}
, we define the related quadratic form by

\langle(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\omega)u,u\rangle=\|\nabla\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+(\omega-\lambda)\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})|q|^{2},

for
u=\phi_{\lambda}+q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\in H^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
.
We also put

\|u\|_{H^{1}_{\alpha,\omega}}^{2}:=\langle(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\omega)u,u\rangle=\|\nabla\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+(\omega-\lambda)\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})|q|^{2}.

Clearly if
q(u)=0
, then
\|u\|_{H^{1}_{\alpha,\omega}}
coincides with
the norm
\|u\|_{H^{1}}
.
Moreover it also holds that

(1.5)

\|u\|_{H^{1}_{\alpha,\omega_{1}}}\sim\|u\|_{H^{1}_{\alpha,\omega_{2}}}\quad\text{for}\ \ \omega_{\alpha}<\omega_{1}<\omega_{2}.

See
[
16
]
for details.

Motivated by all this, in the present paper we consider the following weakly coupled nonlinear Schrödinger system

(
\mathcal{P}_{\beta}
)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\alpha}u+\omega u=|u|^{2}u+\beta u|v|^{2}&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},\\
-\Delta v+\tilde{\omega}v=|v|^{2}v+\beta|u|^{2}v&\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},\end{cases}

where
\omega>\omega_{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\omega}>0
, and
\beta\geqslant 0
.

We want to attach this problem by means of variational techniques. So, let us introduce the functional framework.
We set

\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}:=H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Unless otherwise stated, we fix
\omega_{\alpha}<\lambda\leqslant\omega
. Solutions of (

P
β

) will be found as critical points of the functional
I_{\beta}:\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}\to\mathbb{R}
, where

\displaystyle I_{\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\omega)u,u\rangle+\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}(\|u\|_{4}^{4}+\|v\|_{4}^{4})-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|uv|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+(\omega-\lambda)\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})|q|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\ +\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}(\|u\|_{4}^{4}+\|v\|_{4}^{4})-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|uv|^{2},

for any
u=\phi_{\lambda}+q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\in H^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
and
v\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
.

We will look for ground state solutions and so we need to define the ground state level

c_{\beta}:=c_{\beta}(\omega,\tilde{\omega}):=\inf_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}}I_{\beta}(u,v),

where
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
is the Nehari manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\beta}:=\big\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\mid G_{\beta}(u,v)=0\big\},

with

\displaystyle G_{\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\langle(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\omega)u,u\rangle+\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}-(\|u\|_{4}^{4}+\|v\|_{4}^{4})-2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|uv|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+(\omega-\lambda)\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})|q|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\ +\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}-(\|u\|_{4}^{4}+\|v\|_{4}^{4})-2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|uv|^{2}.

As observed, for example, in
[
1
]
,
I_{\beta}

does not depend
on the choice of
\lambda
and so also the Nehari manifold
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}
and the ground state level
c_{\beta}
.

We will need to compare the functional
I_{\beta}
with the corresponding one in absence of the point interaction. Namely we introduce
I_{\beta}^{0}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{R}
where, for any
(u,v)\in\mathbb{H}:=H^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})\times H^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
,

\displaystyle I_{\beta}^{0}(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}(\|u\|_{4}^{4}+\|v\|_{4}^{4})-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|uv|^{2}.

Analogously as before, we define its associated ground state level

\displaystyle c_{\beta}^{0}:=c_{\beta}^{0}(\omega,\tilde{\omega}):=\inf_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\beta}^{0}}I_{\beta}^{0}(u,v),

where
\mathcal{N}_{\beta}^{0}
is the Nehari manifold

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\beta}^{0}:=\big\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\mid G_{\beta}^{0}(u,v)=0\big\},

with

\displaystyle G_{\beta}^{0}(u,v):=\|\nabla u\|_{2}^{2}+\omega\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}-(\|u\|_{4}^{4}+\|v\|_{4}^{4})-2\beta\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|uv|^{2}.

It’s well known that the ground state for
c_{\beta}^{0}
is attained. See, for example,
[
8
,
22
,
27
]
and references therein.

Analogously, we have to introduce the corresponding functionals, Nehari manifolds and ground states levels for the single nonlinear Schrödinger equation both in presence of the point interaction and without the point interaction. More precisely we set
S_{\omega}:H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
where

\displaystyle S_{\omega}(u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\langle(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\omega)u,u\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\|u\|_{4}^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\phi_{\lambda}\|_{2}^{2}+(\omega-\lambda)\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(\alpha+\theta_{\lambda})|q|^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\|u\|_{4}^{4},

for any
u=\phi_{\lambda}+q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}\in H^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
. Moreover we denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\omega}:=\big\{u\in H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\setminus\{0\}\mid\langle(-\Delta_{\alpha}+\omega)u,u\rangle=\|u\|_{4}^{4}\big\},

the associated Nehari manifold and

\displaystyle d(\omega):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{M}_{\omega}}S_{\omega}(u),

the ground state level.
As shown in
[
1
, Theorem 1.11]
,
d(\omega)
is attained and any minimizer is singular.

Finally, we set
S_{\tilde{\omega}}^{0}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for any
v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,

\displaystyle S_{\tilde{\omega}}^{0}(v):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}\right)-\frac{1}{4}\|v\|_{4}^{4},

and

\displaystyle d^{0}(\tilde{\omega}):=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\omega}}^{0}}S_{\tilde{\omega}}^{0}(v),

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\omega}}^{0}:=\big\{v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\setminus\{0\}\mid\|\nabla v\|_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}\|v\|_{2}^{2}=\|v\|_{4}^{4}\big\}.

It’s well known that
d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
is attained.

In the sequel we need also to introduce

(1.6)

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle:=\beta_{0}(\omega,\tilde{\omega}):=\max\{\beta\geqslant 0\mid c_{\beta}^{0}=c_{0}^{0}\},

(1.7)

\displaystyle\beta^{*}

\displaystyle:=\beta^{*}(\omega,\tilde{\omega}):=\max\{\beta\geqslant 0\mid c_{\beta}=c_{0}\}.

Observe that one can show that
\beta_{0}>0
and it is attained arguing as in
[
21
]
. Also
\beta^{*}
is positive and attained (see Lemma
2.21
and Lemma
2.23
).

Our first existence result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega>\omega_{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\omega}>0

and
\beta\geqslant 0
. Then there exists a ground state
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
of (

P
β

).

Our main aim in this paper is not only the existence of a ground state solution, but also the nature of such solution.

First of all, observe that if
u\in H^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
is a nontrivial solution of

-\Delta_{\alpha}u+\omega u=|u|^{2}u\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

then
(u,0)
is a nontrivial solution of (

P
β

). Analogously, if
v\in H^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{2}})
is a nontrivial solution of

-\Delta v+\tilde{\omega}v=|v|^{2}v\quad\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{2},

then
(0,v)
is a nontrivial solution of (

P
β

), too.

In order to distinguish these
semi-trivial
solutions from those with both the two components different from zero, we need the following definition.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
(u,v)\in\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
be a nontrivial solution of (

P
β

). We say that it is a
scalar solution
if
u=0
or
v=0
. Conversely, we say that it is a
vector solution
if
u\neq 0
and
v\neq 0
.

Moreover, we would like to investigate if the first component of the solutions contains or not a singular part.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(u,v)\in\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
. Then there exist
\phi_{\lambda}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
q\in\mathbb{C}
such that
u=\phi_{\lambda}+q\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
.
We say that
(u,v)
is regular if
q=0
. While we say that
(u,v)
is singular if
q\neq 0
.

We will see how all these properties of the solutions are strictly related to the parameter
\beta
and to all the mountain pass levels introduced before. Therefore we need to study carefully all the possible different scenarios. Actually the situation is richer and more delicate in comparison with problems without a point interaction such as (
1.1
) and (
1.2
).

We start with the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega>\omega_{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\omega}>0

and
\beta\geqslant 0
.
Then if
c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
, then there exists a ground state
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
of (

P
β

) such that
q_{0}\neq 0
, where
q_{0}
is the charge of
u_{0}
.
Namely,
c_{\beta}
has a singular minimizer.
Moreover,
\phi_{\lambda,0}\neq 0
holds, where
u_{0}=\phi_{\lambda,0}+q_{0}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
.

From the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we find that
(u,v)
is a minimizer for
c_{\beta}
if and only if
(u,v)
is a ground state of (

P
β

).
So in what follows, we use the terms “ground state” and “minimizer” without any distinction.

In Lemma
2.10
and Lemma
2.12
, we give some conditions which assure that
c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
and so we have the following.

Corollary 1.5
.

Assume that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\omega>\omega_{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\omega}>0

and
\beta>0
. Then if we assume either
\omega\leqslant\tilde{\omega}
or
\beta>\beta_{0}
, then there exists a ground state
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
of (

P
β

) such that
q_{0}\neq 0
, where
q_{0}
is the charge of
u_{0}
.
Namely,
c_{\beta}
has a singular minimizer.
Moreover,
\phi_{\lambda,0}\neq 0
holds, where
u_{0}=\phi_{\lambda,0}+q_{0}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
.

Now we show that for
small
value of
\beta
, the ground state is scalar and, in this case, the parameters
\omega,\tilde{\omega}
play a crucial role. More precisely, the following holds.

Theorem 1.6
.

If
0\leqslant\beta<\beta^{*}
then for any minimizer
(u_{0},v_{0})
for
c_{\beta}
is scalar. If, in addition,
d(\omega)>d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
, then
u_{0}=0
and so
(u_{0},v_{0})
is scalar and regular; while if
d(\omega)<d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
, then
v_{0}=0
and
(u_{0},v_{0})
is scalar and singular.
If
d(\omega)=d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
, then it follows that
u_{0}=0
or
v_{0}=0
.

Remark 1.7
.

By the scaling argument,
d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})=d^{0}(1)\tilde{\omega}
holds. Therefore, the followings hold:

(i)

if
\tilde{\omega}>d(\omega)/d^{0}(1)
, then
d(\omega)<d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
holds;

(ii)

if
\tilde{\omega}=d(\omega)/d^{0}(1)
, then
d(\omega)=d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
holds;

(iii)

if
\tilde{\omega}<d(\omega)/d^{0}(1)
, then
d(\omega)>d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
holds.

The next result shows that (

P
β

) does not admit the existence of vector and regular ground state.

Theorem 1.8
.

For any
\beta\geqslant 0
, there is no vector and regular minimizer for
c_{\beta}
.

Remark 1.9
.

From Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.6
, it follows that
if
0\leqslant\beta<\beta^{*}
and
c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
hold, then all minimizers for
c_{\beta}
are scalar and singular.

Our aim is to prove that for
\beta
sufficiently large all the ground states are vector and singular. In order to do this, first we need the following results.

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
\beta\geqslant 0
and assume that
c_{\beta}=c_{\beta}^{0}
. Then

\omega>\tilde{\omega},\qquad\beta\leqslant\beta^{*}\leqslant\beta_{0},\qquad c_{\beta}=c_{\beta}^{0}=c_{0}^{0}=c_{0}=d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})\leqslant d(\omega).

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle c_{\beta^{\prime}}=c_{\beta^{\prime}}^{0}\quad\text{for any}\ 0\leqslant\beta^{\prime}\leqslant\beta^{*},

\displaystyle c_{\beta^{\prime}}<c_{\beta^{\prime}}^{0}\quad\text{for any}\ \beta^{\prime}>\beta^{*}.

Remark 1.11
.

By Theorem
1.10
we deduce that either
c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
, for any
\beta\geqslant 0
, or
c_{\beta}=c_{\beta}^{0}
, for any
0\leqslant\beta\leqslant\beta^{*}
, and
c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
, for any
\beta>\beta^{*}
. In any case, we have that
c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
, for any
\beta>\beta^{*}
.

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
\beta\geqslant 0
. Then the followings are equivalent:

(i)

c_{\beta}=c_{\beta}^{0}
;

(ii)

d(\omega)\geqslant d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
and
\beta\leqslant\beta^{*}
.

We can easily find that the following corollary is correct by contraposition.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
\beta\geqslant 0
. Then the followings are equivalent:

(i)

c_{\beta}<c_{\beta}^{0}
;

(ii)

d(\omega)<d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
or
\beta>\beta^{*}
.

Finally we can say that for
large

\beta
the ground state solution of (

P
β

) is always vector and singular.

Theorem 1.14
.

If
\beta>\beta^{*}
, all minimizers for
c_{\beta}
are vector and singular.

Remark 1.15
.

Assume
c_{\beta}=c_{\beta}^{0}
and so
0\leqslant\beta<\beta^{*}
.
In this case,
d(\omega)\geqslant d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
holds by Theorem
1.10
. Then from Theorem
1.6
, the followings hold.

(i)

If
d(\omega)=d^{0}(\tilde{\omega})
, then

\mathcal{E}_{\beta}=(\mathcal{E}(\omega)\times\{0\})\cup(\{0\}\times\mathcal{E}^{0}(\tilde{\omega}))
,
where
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
is the set of minimizers for
c_{\beta}
,
\mathcal{E}(\omega)
is the set of minimizers for
d(\omega)

Lemma 2.17
