Co-amorphization for solubility and dissolution rate improvement: the case of low-crystallization-tendency drugs
Authors: Arif Budiman, Naila Dwi Rahmaharva, Salma Chiara Putri, Stella Aurelia Huang, Justine Frederica, Marshella Dhanu Ardhita, Melsa Destia, Jeremy Marcelino, Salma Amaliah, Laila Subra, Diah Lia Aulifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoor aqueous solubility and limited oral bioavailability remain major challenges in drug development. Although amorphous forms can improve solubility and dissolution, their physical stability is often compromised by recrystallization. Co-amorphous systems (CAMS) have therefore been extensively investigated as a strategy to stabilize amorphous drugs and enhance their performance. Nevertheless, compounds classified as Type III according to Baird et al. (2010) are generally considered good glass formers with inherently low recrystallization tendencies, raising questions regarding the necessity and additional benefits of CAMS for this type of compounds. This review aims to evaluate the relevance and effectiveness of CAMS for drug classified as good glass formers, with particular emphasis in their impact on solubility, dissolution behavior, and physical stability. Relevant publications were systematically identified through major scientific databases and search engines using keywords “co-amorphous system” and “coformer”. A total of 18 studies involving Type III drugs were included and critically analyzed. Analysis of the included studies revealed that CAMS improved aqueous solubility by approximately 1.7-fold to more 3900-fold and significantly enhanced dissolution performance, with reported increases in dissolution parameters ranging from 1.75-fold to 255-fold. In addition, several formulations exhibited rapid and near complete drug release as well as prolonged physical stability of up to seven months. These effects were mainly driven by intermolecular interactions that suppressed recrystallization and enhanced supersaturation. Overall, CAMS improved solubility, dissolution, and physical stability, indicating benefits beyond amorphous stabilization even for good glass formers.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Co-amorphous system (CAMS), Poorly water-soluble drugs, Glass-forming ability, Solubility, Dissolution

Introduction

Poor aqueous solubility remains a major challenge in pharmaceutical development, with approximately 40% of marketed drugs and nearly 90% of drug candidates classified as poorly water-soluble drugs (PWSDs) (Xie et al. 2024; Budiman et al. 2019). Solubility is a key determinant of drug dissolution, pharmacokinetics and efficacy. Limited solubility can restrict the dissolution rate and hinder the achievement of optimal plasma concentration, often resulting in poor and erratic oral bioavailability as well as disproportionate systemic exposures (Rodriguez-Fernandez et al. 2025; Fink et al. 2020; Nyamba et al. 2024). Although dose escalation may partially compensate for limited absorption, it can also increase the risk of adverse effects, treatment cost and reduce patient compliance (Chavan et al. 2016; Xie et al. 2024). Various pharmaceutical approaches have been explored to overcome poor aqueous solubility, including particle size reduction (micronization and nanonization), salt formation, complexation with cyclodextrins, lipid-based formulations, and solid dispersion techniques such as amorphous and co-amorphous system (Nyamba et al. 2024; Budiman et al. 2023). Among these, solid-state approaches that convert drugs into a disordered amorphous form have gained particular interest due to their ability to dramatically improve dissolution rates without chemical modification.

Amorphous systems have been recognized as an effective formulation strategy to improve the dissolution rate and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble active pharmaceutical ingredients (API) (Liu et al. 2024; Dong et al. 2024a). Their enhanced dissolution behavior is mainly attributed to the higher Gibbs free energy of the amorphous state compared with the crystalline state (Budiman and Aulifa 2022a). However, amorphous systems are thermodynamically unstable and may undergo recrystallization during storage or dissolution, which can compromise their biopharmaceutical advantages (Petkov et al. 2024; Budiman et al. 2025). Therefore, stabilization strategies, including the incorporation of polymer, excipient, or low-molecular-weight coformers, are required to maintain the amorphous state and preserve dissolution performance is crucial to improve the stability of amorphous systems (Baghel et al. 2016; Srividya and Ghosh 2025).

Co-amorphous systems (CAMS) have gained increasing interest as single-phase amorphous mixtures composed of an API and one or more low-molecular-weight coformers, such as organic acids, amino acids, or other small molecules (Blaabjerg et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2021). Compared with polymeric amorphous solid dispersions (ASDs), CAMS allow for higher drug loading, reduced amounts of excipients, and improved maintenance of supersaturation (Mishra et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019; Aragón-Aburto et al. 2022). Their performance is strongly influenced by rational coformers selection, particularly in terms of miscibility, chemical complementarity, and the ability of establish stabilizing non-covalent interactions with the API (Kasten et al. 2019). These stabilizing interactions are mainly non-covalent in nature, including hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions. Rather than forming new covalent bonds, these interactions modulate API mobility and molecular packing, with their strength and persistence depending on the drug-coformer pair, preparation method, storage condition, and dissolution environment. Accordingly, understanding the molecular basis of drug-coformer interactions is essential for designing CAMS with improved solubility, dissolution behavior, and physical stability (Karagianni et al. 2018a; Chieng et al. 2019; Budiman et al. 2021; Shelke et al. 2024).

In addition to coformer selection, the intrinsic solid-state properties of the drug substance play a critical role in determining the performance of CAMS. Baird et al. (2010) classified compounds according to their crystallization tendency from the undercooled melt as shown in Fig. 1. Type I compounds crystallize readily and are poor glass formers, Type II compounds form amorphous solids upon cooling but recrystallize upon reheating above the glass-transition temperature (Tg), and Type III compounds resist crystallization during both cooling and reheating, making them good glass formers (GGF) (Budiman and Aulifa 2022b). This classification provides mechanistic insight into the glass-forming ability (GFA) and glass stability (GS), both of which influence the feasibility of amorphous formulations and the design of solid dispersion systems (Baird et al. 2010; Kawakami and Ohba 2017). Type III compounds are generally considered physically stable in the amorphous form because of their low crystallization tendency. Unlike Type I compounds, which crystallize readily during cooling, and Type II compounds, which recrystallize upon heating above Tg, Type III compounds exhibit high glass-forming ability and remain amorphous during both cooling and reheating. Consequently, their formulation challenges differ from those of Type I and Type II drugs, as the primary objective is often not merely recrystallization inhibition but also enhancement of dissolution behavior and maintenance supersaturation. However, several studies have reported that the addition of a coformer can further improve the solubility, dissolution profile and physical stability of these compounds through molecular-level interactions such as hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking (He and Ho 2015; Dengale et al. 2016; Budiman et al. 2021; Pešić et al. 2021). Although CAMS have been extensively investigated as a strategy to enhance the solubility and dissolution rate of PWSDs, their specific contribution to Type III drugs remain less clearly refined. This is particularly relevant because Type III compounds are intrinsically amorphous-stable and therefore provide a useful model for evaluating whether co-amorphization offers benefits beyond recrystallization inhibition, including improved dissolution behavior, supersaturation maintenance, and optimized drug-coformer interaction-mediated performance. Therefore, this review aims to evaluate the relevance and effectiveness of CAMS for Type III poorly water-soluble drugs with low crystallization tendency. Specifically, this review examines how co-amorphization influences solubility, dissolution behavior, physical stability, and the molecular interactions that contribute to CAMS performance in compounds that already possess intrinsic amorphous stability.

Methods

This review employs a systematic narrative methodology to examine and synthesize the recent advancements in the development of CAMS aimed at enhancing solubility and dissolution rate. Relevant publications were systematically identified through major scientific databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar, using the keywords “co-amorphous system” and “coformer”. The literature review included studies published between 2015 and 2025. Duplicate records were initially identified through manual comparison of the title, authors, publication year, journal, and DOI of each retrieved article. The remaining references were subsequently imported into Mendeley References Manager, where the built-in duplicate detection function was used to identify and remove any residual duplicate records. The deduplication results were then reviewed by the research team prior to screening process to ensure consistency. Titles and abstracts were subsequently screened by six members of the research team. Any discrepancies regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Only full-text articles published in English and available online were included. Studies were excluded if they were irrelevant to co-amorphous systems, lacked experimental or formulation data, or failed to present quantifiable outcomes pertaining to solubility or dissolution performance, or did not involve low crystallization tendency (Type III) drugs, which constitute the specific focus of this review. Following the screening process, 18 studies met the predefined eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis, comprising four studies on solubility enhancement and fourteen on dissolution improvement. The collected data were systematically categorized to highlight formulation strategies, molecular interactions, and performance outcomes. Co-Amorphous Systems (CAMS)

CAMS are defined as homogeneous, single-phase amorphous mixtures consisting of two or more low-molecular-weight components (Fig. 3), most commonly a drug in combination with a second drug or a small-molecule coformer (Shi et al. 2019; Deng et al. 2022). In contrast to conventional polymer-based ASDs, CAMS offer several potential advantages, including higher drug loading, reduced excipient requirements, and the ability to achieve strong intermolecular stabilization through low molecular weight coformers. Polymeric systems are widely used to enhance solubility and maintain supersaturation. However, the large amount of polymer required may limit drug loading and increase formulation complexity. In contrast, CAMS achieve stabilization through specific drug-coformer interactions while maintaining a relatively high proportion of API (Karagianni et al. 2018b; Liu et al. 2021; Khorami et al. 2024; Mahmoud et al. 2025). Stabilization of the amorphous phase is conferred through multiple molecular mechanisms, including amorphous salt formation, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, or intimate molecular mixing, each of which disrupts the tendency of drug molecules to align into a crystalline lattice (Zhang et al. 2024). These stabilizing interactions collectively reduce molecular mobility and inhibit recrystallization (Hiew and Taylor 2022).

Co-amorphous solid system

The rational selection of an appropriate coformer is therefore critical, guided by considerations of miscibility, physicochemical complementarity, and in the case of drug–drug systems, therapeutic compatibility. Furthermore, the method of preparation ranging from mechanochemical activation (e.g., ball milling) to thermodynamic approaches such as melt-quenching, spray drying, or solvent evaporation exerts a decisive influence on both the formation and stability of the co-amorphous phase, with the same drug–coformer pair displaying divergent outcomes under different processing conditions (Martínez et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023; Kamruzzaman et al. 2024). By sustaining supersaturation and enhancing dissolution kinetics, CAMS significantly improve the oral bioavailability of PWSDs, thereby emerging as a robust alternative to polymer-based dispersions in modern solid-state pharmaceutical design (Laitinen et al. 2017; Ruponen et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021).

The formation of CAMS occurs when crystalline drug and coformer molecules are converted into a single disordered phase through energy input, such as mechanical stress or thermal treatment (Goetz and Mahamid 2020; Amini et al. 2021). This process breaks the long-range crystalline order, resulting in a disordered amorphous matrix where molecules are randomly oriented, forming a high-energy configuration that facilitates rapid wetting and dissolution. As a result, CAMS promotes rapid drug release and maintains a stable supersaturated state during dissolution, thereby improving solubility, dissolution rate, and overall bioavailability of PWSDs (Saberi et al. 2023).

Recent studies demonstrate that CAMS effectively enhance solubility and improve pharmacokinetic outcomes. Co-amorphous formulations of poorly soluble drugs, such as atorvastatin and curcumin, demonstrate significant increases in plasma exposure relative to their crystalline forms (Edueng et al. 2019). The observed improvements are primarily due to CAMS’s capacity to sustain a stable supersaturated state during dissolution, facilitating elevated drug concentrations in the gastrointestinal tract and enhancing absorption. The results demonstrate that CAMS are effective in vitro and provide significant therapeutic benefits in vivo (Kasten et al. 2019).

Low crystallization tendency

The crystallization tendency of pharmaceutical compounds, as classified by Taylor’s group, follows the order type IA > type IB > type II > type III. Type I and II compounds show a strong tendency to crystallize (Shi et al. 2020). Type I compounds crystallize during cooling from the undercooled melt, whereas Type II compounds do not crystallize during cooling but recrystallize upon reheating above their glass transition temperature (Tg) (Kawakami 2019; Elkhashab et al. 2025). This behavior in Type II molecules is likely related to nuclei formed during cooling that become "quenched-in" within the glassy matrix and subsequently promote crystal growth during reheating. Representative examples of Type I compounds include haloperidol, carbamazepine, and naproxen, while nifedipine, griseofulvin, and tolbutamide are common Type II examples (Baird et al. 2010). In contrast, Type III compounds exhibit high glass stability and recrystallize very slowly, allowing them to melt again during reheating without prior recrystallization (Huang et al. 2019). However, the distinction between Type II and Type III drugs should not be interpreted as absolute, as some molecules may display borderline Type II/III behavior with an intermediate crystallization tendency. Therefore, crystallization tendency should be interpreted in a gradual rather than strictly categorical manner.

The concept of a "good glass-former" is crucial for understanding the physical stability of amorphous drugs. A substance is classified as such if it has a high glass-forming ability (GFA), indicating its tendency to bypass crystallization after cooling from a melt or rapid solvent evaporation, thereby solidifying into a disordered, glassy form (Wong et al. 2020). This process is kinetically controlled, as molecular mobility decreases before the molecules can reorganize into an ordered crystal lattice. High GFA is often associated with molecular features such as higher molecular weight, structural complexity or rigidity, and strong intermolecular interactions, all of which hinder the molecular rearrangement required for crystallization (Ma and Horike 2022). Thus, crystallization-tendency classification is not merely an empirical label but reflect intrinsic molecular properties that govern glass formation and amorphous stability (Baird et al. 2010; Blaabjerg et al. 2016).

Type III compounds are therefore frequently considered suitable candidates for amorphous formulation strategies because of their intrinsic resistance to nucleation and crystal growth. Their physical stability is associated with high GFA, reduced molecular mobility, and stabilizing intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding or π–π stacking, which collectively hinder the alignment into a crystalline lattice (Baird et al. 2010; Alhalaweh et al. 2015; Blaabjerg et al. 2016). This inherent resistance to crystallization makes Type III compounds useful model systems for evaluating whether formulation strategies, including ASDs and CAMS, provide benefits beyond physical stabilization, particularly in improving dissolution behavior and maintaining supersaturation (Panini et al. 2019a; Panini et al. 2019b; Pantazos et al. 2025). Advances in analytical and predictive tools, including differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (SS-NMR), molecular simulation, and machine-learning approaches, have further supported the mechanistic understanding of amorphous stability and drug-coformer interactions (Bhatta et al. 2025). To facilitate comparison among studies, the reported co-amorphous systems were categorized according to their primary performance outcomes, namely solubility enhancement, dissolution improvement, and physical stability. This grouping enables a clearer evaluation of the contribution of co-amorphization to the performance of Type III drugs.

The studies compiled in Table 2, 3, 4 indicate that the performance of CAMS in Type III drugs is highly system-dependent. Solubility and dissolution studies

Improving the solubility and dissolution rate of PWSDs remains a major challenge in solid dosage development. Although cocrystal approaches cam enhance apparent solubility by modifying crystal packing and lattice energy, their ability to maintain supersaturation may be limited by precipitation or recrystallization during dissolution. In contrast, CAMS preserve drugs in a disordered high-energy amorphous state, thereby enabling faster dissolution and more sustained supersaturation in the gastrointestinal milieu. This advantage is strongly modulated by the crystallization tendency of the drug itself, as described by the Baird–Taylor classification. As presented in Table 1 and summarized in Tables 2 and 3, the drugs investigated in this review are classified as Type III compounds, which are GGFs characterized by low crystallization tendency and high glass stability. Despite their extremely poor aqueous solubility (mostly ≤ 0.05 mg/mL), these compounds such as indomethacin, ketoconazole, and pimozide possess strong potential for amorphous stabilization. Their inherent resistance to recrystallization allows them to retain the amorphous state under various processing conditions, making them ideal candidates for co-amorphous formulation approaches aimed at improving solubility, dissolution, and long-term stability.

In terms of solubility enhancement, the ketoconazole–fumaric acid CAMS showed the highest solubility improvement, increasing from 0.060 mg/mL (crystalline form) to 11.652 mg/mL in its co-amorphous state, an almost 190-fold increase (Indra et al. 2025). Similarly, the loratadine–citric acid CAMS exhibited a significantly higher solubility of 51.16 ± 2.44 µg/mL compared with 0.95 ± 0.01 µg/mL for crystalline loratadine (Wang et al. 2017). Collectively, these findings suggest that intermolecular interactions between drugs and coformers contribute to enhanced solubility and dissolution performance by stabilizing the amorphous phase and promoting favorable dissolution behavior. The underlying mechanisms are discussed further in Section 6.

From the perspective of dissolution performance, the indomethacin–arginine CAMS prepared by spray-drying exhibited the most remarkable enhancement. The intrinsic dissolution rate reached 10.20 ± 1.47 mg·min−1·cm−2, nearly 200-fold higher than crystalline indomethacin (0.04 ± 0.00 mg·min−1·cm−2). This extraordinary improvement arises from strong ionic interactions between the carboxyl groups of indomethacin and the amino groups of arginine, which effectively disrupt lattice assembly and stabilize the amorphous state. The synergistic contribution between its low crystallization tendency (as a Type III drug) and ionic stabilization provided by the coformer results in superior dissolution kinetics and sustained supersaturation. This finding reinforces that co-amorphization of Type III drugs provides a kinetic advantage by maintaining the amorphous phase during dissolution, enabling faster and more consistent drug release compared to conventional crystalline or cocrystal forms.

A comparative evaluation of the studies reveals that the magnitude of solubility enhancement is strongly dependent on the nature of the intermolecular interactions established within the co-amorphous matrix. Systems involving strong hydrogen bonding or ionic interactions generally exhibited greater performance improvements than systems relying primarily on molecular mixing. For example, ketoconazole–fumaric acid, ketoconazole–glycyrrhizin acid, and indomethacin–arginine demonstrated among the highest improvements in solubility and dissolution, suggesting that interaction strength plays a critical role in maintaining supersaturation and preventing molecular aggregation during dissolution.

Another notable trend is that even Type III drugs, which inherently possess high GFA and low crystallization tendency, still benefited substantially from co-amorphization. This finding suggests that the role of coformers extends beyond physical stabilization. Instead, coformers may modify molecular packing, improve wettability, and promote supersaturation generation, leading to enhanced dissolution performance despite the already favorable amorphous stability of Type III compounds.

Stability studies

Regarding physical stability, several CAMS exhibited clear evidence of amorphous stabilization as shown in Tables 2 and 3. The ritonavir–saccharin CAMS demonstrated enhanced thermal and kinetic stability, maintaining supersaturation up to 300 min when incorporated into mesoporous silica, while pure amorphous ritonavir recrystallized within four days at 60 °C. This improvement is attributed to hydrogen bonding between the thiazole nitrogen of ritonavir and the amine proton of saccharin, which restricts molecular mobility and delays recrystallization (Budiman et al. 2021). Similarly, the ketoconazole–fumaric acid CAMS exhibited a significantly reduced melting enthalpy (13.93 J/g) compared to its crystalline counterpart (87.13 J/g), indicating substantial crystal lattice disruption and reduced crystallinity, a hallmark of enhanced amorphous stability (Indra et al. 2025). Meanwhile, the indomethacin–arginine system also maintained its amorphous structure during dissolution, owing to its inherently low crystallization tendency as a type III compound. These results collectively confirm that all type III drugs reviewed exhibit strong amorphous stability when co-amorphized with complementary coformers through hydrogen or ionic bonding, leading to robust solid-state formulations with prolonged resistance to recrystallization (Jensen et al. 2016).

Across the reviewed studies, physical stability improvements were consistently associated with the presence of specific intermolecular interactions, particularly hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions. Most co-amorphous systems remained amorphous for periods ranging from several weeks to several months, whereas the corresponding pure amorphous drugs frequently recrystallized within days or weeks. These findings indicate that coformers not only contribute to dissolution enhancement but also act as molecular mobility suppressors, thereby increasing resistance to recrystallization during storage.

Interestingly, although Type III drugs already exhibit low crystallization tendencies, the addition of suitable coformers further prolonged amorphous stability. This observation highlights that co-amorphization can provide additional kinetic stabilization even for compounds classified as good glass formers, supporting its relevance beyond poorly stable amorphous drugs.

Mechanistic insights into the performance of CAMS

The dissolution process of pharmaceutical solids is governed by the interrelation between thermodynamic stability, molecular mobility, and the intermolecular interactions. In crystalline solids, molecules are arranged in a long-range ordered lattice, resulting in low Gibbs free energy and high thermodynamic stability. However, this ordered lattice also creates an energetic barrier to dissolution and contributes to low apparent solubility. The absence of the energy barrier enables the amorphous form to display rapid dissolution rates and high solubility. In contrast, amorphous solid lack long-range molecular order and exist in a higher-energy state, allowing faster dissolution and higher apparent solubility. Nevertheless, amorphous solids have tendency to undergo molecular rearrangement and recrystallization during storage or upon contract with dissolution media. For Type III compounds, the mechanistic contribution of CAMS cannot be attributed solely to physical stabilization. These compounds already possess good GFA and inherently low crystallization tendency, suggesting that the performance improvements observed after co-amorphization are also driven by additional mechanisms. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain these performance improvements, including improved wettability, disruption of molecular packing, enhanced molecular dispersion within the amorphous matrix, and maintenance of transient supersaturation during dissolution, all of which have been reported to contribute to the improved performance of specific co-amorphous formulations (Jackson et al. 2016; Dong et al. 2024b; Hiew et al. 2025). In the context of CAMS, the enhanced solubility values reported in the literature often reflect apparent solubility associated with amorphization and supersaturation phenomena rather than a permanent increase in intrinsic equilibrium solubility. Apparent solubility refers to the transient increase in dissolved drug concentration resulting from supersaturation, whereas true equilibrium solubility represents the maximum thermodynamically stable concentration of the crystalline drug under equilibrium conditions. Although some Type III compounds exhibit relatively high glass-transition temperatures (Tg), their amorphous stability is primarily associated with slow nucleation kinetics and reduced molecular mobility rather than Tg alone. Therefore, co-amorphization may provide additional kinetic and dissolution advantages even when extensive stabilization against recrystallization is not the main requirement. Therefore, the performance advantages associated with CAMS should not be interpreted solely in terms of amorphous stabilization. The observed improvements may involve different but interrelated phenomena, including solubility enhancement, dissolution rate improvement, and supersaturation maintenance.

It is important to distinguish among solubility enhancement, dissolution rate improvement, and supersaturation maintenance when interpreting the performance of CAMS. Solubility enhancement refers to an increase in the equilibrium concentration of dissolved drugs, whereas dissolution rate improvement describes the acceleration of drug release into the dissolution medium. In contrast, supersaturation maintenance reflects the ability of the system to sustain drug concentrations above equilibrium solubility by inhibiting recrystallization. Although these phenomena are interrelated, they governed by different mechanism and may not necessarily improve to the same extent within a given co-amorphous formulation.

Borderline Type II/III molecules provide a useful framework for interpreting the mechanistic performance of CAMS, as their crystallization behavior may be governed by the balance between intrinsic GFA and residual molecular mobility. Unlike typical Type III drugs, which generally exhibit a low crystallization tendency, borderline Type II/III compounds may still undergo molecular rearrangement and nucleation under specific storage or dissolution conditions. In such systems, coformer selection becomes particularly important because drug–coformer interactions, most commonly hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, and in certain systems π–π interactions, have been widely reported to reduce molecular mobility, disrupt ordered molecular packing, and increase the kinetic barrier to crystallization in CAMS (Allu et al. 2019; Pagliari et al. 2022; Song et al. 2024). However, the dominant stabilization mechanisms vary among formulations and depends on the molecular characteristics of both the API and the conformer.

These considerations highlight that the performance of CAMS depends not only on converting the drug into an amorphous state but also on the quality and balance of drug-coformer interactions. Complementary intermolecular interactions have been reported to suppress molecular mobility, increase the nucleation barrier, and consequently enhance the physical stability of the amorphous form (Liu et al. 2022; Song et al. 2024). However, the magnitude of performance enhancement may also be influenced by factors such as particle morphology, particle size distribution, and processing conditions employed during CAMS preparation. Consequently, the extent of solubility and dissolution enhancement reported in the literature varies substantially among CAMS formulations, ranging from modest improvements to several thousand-fold increases. This variability highlights that CAMS performance is highly formulation-dependent and influenced by multiple factors, including the physicochemical properties of the drug, coformer selection, formulation composition, intermolecular interactions, and preparation method. Therefore, the reported benefits should be interpreted within the context of each specific formulation rather than generalized across all CAMS. As conceptually shown in the mechanistic scheme (Fig. 4), crystalline solid generally exhibit low dissolution rates but high physical stability, amorphous solid show faster dissolution rates but suffers from recrystallization over time, whereas the co-amorphous solid are designed to combined rapid drug release with prolonged supersaturation, and reduced recrystallization tendency. From a practical viewpoint, the objective of the CAMS is therefore to retain the dissolution advantage of the amorphous solid while improving the kinetic stability of the supersaturated system.

Mechanism of drug dissolution and crystallization behavior in different solid states

From a formulation perspective, the molecular ratio between the drug and stabilizing component is a critical determinant of CAMS performance. In co-amorphous systems, this generally refers to the drug–coformer molar ratio rather than a conventional drug–polymer ratio. An insufficient amount of coformer may fail to provide adequate molecular interaction sites, leading to incomplete amorphization, poor miscibility, or limited recrystallization inhibition. Conversely, excessive coformer content may reduce drug loading, alter dissolution behavior, or promote phase separation and crystallization of the coformer itself. Therefore, the optimal ratio should provide sufficient molecular interaction between the API and coformer while maintaining acceptable drug loading, physical stability, and dissolution performance. In addition to ratio optimization, formulation and safety considerations should include coformer toxicity, pharmacological activity, dose compatibility, hygroscopicity, chemical stability, excipient compatibility, and scalable processing feasibility. These factors are essential to ensure that CAMS not only improve solubility and physical stability but also remain suitable for practical oral dosage form development.

The ritonavir-saccharin system provides a representative example of this mechanism. Crystalline ritonavir exhibits poor aqueous solubility and limited dissolution, whereas its amorphous form can generate supersaturation but may remain vulnerable to physical instability depending on storage and dissolution condition. In the ritonavir-saccharin CAMS, hydrogen bonding between the thiazole nitrogen of ritonavir and the amine proton of saccharin reduces molecular mobility and completely inhibit crystallization (Budiman et al. 2021). This interaction supports the maintenance of supersaturation and demonstrates how coformer-mediated molecular stabilization can enhance the dissolution behavior of a poorly soluble API. However, this behavior should not be generalized to all Type III drugs, since CAMS performance depends on the molecular complementarity of the drug-coformer pair, the strength and orientation of intermolecular interactions, and the preparation method. Consequently, the magnitude of improvement observed in one CAMS formulation should not be considered representative of all co-amorphous systems.

Overall, the performance of CAMS is determined by an interaction balance rather than by the mere presence of intermolecular interactions. Weak or poorly oriented intermolecular interactions may be insufficient to inhibit nucleation, whereas excessively strong interactions can hinder molecular detachment and reduce drug release at the solid–liquid interface (Ibrahim et al. 2026; Cousineau et al. 2026). Optimal co-amorphous formulations therefore require an intermediate interaction window that sustains supersaturation without sacrificing interfacial release. Within Baird’s glass-forming framework, CAMS should not be viewed as thermodynamically reclassifying an API into an intrinsic GGF. Instead, CAMS functionally improve GGF like performance at the kinetic level by restructuring molecular mobility, increasing nucleation and growth barriers, stabilizing the supersaturated state during dissolution. Thus, co-amorphization represents a promising molecular strategy to balance solubility enhancement and physical stability. Nevertheless, the extent of performance improvement depends strongly on formulation-specific factors, and successful CAMS development requires careful optimization of drug–coformer combinations and preparation conditions.

Author perspectives

For Type III good glass-forming drugs, the future development of CAMS should move beyond the conventional objective of recrystallization inhibition. Since these APIs already possess relatively low crystallization tendency, the main value of co-amorphization lies in its ability to optimize dissolution behavior, maintain supersaturation, and improve performance through drug–coformer interactions. From this perspective, CAMS should not be viewed only as a stabilizing platform, but also as a molecular design strategy for enhancing the functional performance of APIs that are already intrinsically stable in the amorphous state. The literature compiled in this review indicates that the benefits of CAMS for Type III drugs are not uniform across all systems. Some formulations showed only modest improvements in solubility or dissolution, whereas others demonstrated marked enhancement, particularly systems involving strong and complementary drug–coformer interactions. The largest improvements were generally observed in systems where the coformer contributed not only to amorphous stabilization but also to supersaturation maintenance, molecular dispersion, or improved wetting behavior. This suggests that the performance of CAMS in Type III drugs cannot be predicted solely from the intrinsic GFA of the API. Instead, the functional outcome depends on the combined contribution of API properties, coformer chemistry, preparation method, and the balance between stabilization and drug release. This interpretation is important because Type III drugs provide a distinct formulation context. In poorly stable amorphous systems, the primary function of the coformer is often to suppress recrystallization. In Type III systems, however, recrystallization is already intrinsically limited, so the additional value of CAMS is more closely related to performance optimization. The reviewed studies suggest that co-amorphization may improve dissolution and supersaturation even when extensive recrystallization inhibition is not the dominant requirement. Therefore, future studies should evaluate CAMS not only by their ability to maintain amorphous stability, but also by their capacity to generate and sustain therapeutically relevant supersaturated concentrations.

Future research should place greater emphasis on predictive coformer selection. At present, coformer screening remains largely empirical, although CAMS performance is strongly influenced by molecular complementarity, interaction strength, miscibility, and the balance between stabilization and drug release. A more systematic integration of experimental solid-state characterization, molecular simulation, and data-driven prediction may help identify coformers that improve dissolution and maintain supersaturation without excessively restricting molecular detachment at the solid–liquid interface. Such approaches would be particularly useful for Type III drugs, where the formulation objective is not simply to prevent crystallization but to optimize biopharmaceutical performance. Another important direction is the translation of in vitro performance into in vivo relevance. Many CAMS studies report improved solubility, dissolution, and supersaturation under laboratory conditions, but the relationship between these improvements and oral absorption remains insufficiently established. Future investigations should therefore include biorelevant dissolution testing, permeability assessment, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and, where possible, in vitro–in vivo correlation. This would help determine whether the enhanced dissolution behavior of Type III CAMS can consistently translate into improved bioavailability.

From a product development standpoint, greater attention should also be given to formulation scalability, process robustness, and long-term stability under realistic storage conditions. Preparation methods such as milling, spray drying, melt quenching, and solvent evaporation may generate different molecular arrangements, even for the same drug–coformer pair. Therefore, future CAMS development should consider not only molecular interaction profiles, but also manufacturability, batch reproducibility, hygroscopicity, excipient compatibility, and regulatory acceptability. Addressing these factors will be necessary for advancing CAMS from a promising solid-state strategy toward practical supersaturating oral dosage forms. CAMS for Type III drugs should be positioned as a performance-optimization approach rather than only a recrystallization-prevention strategy. The integration of rational coformer selection, mechanistic characterization, predictive modeling, and translational evaluation may provide a more refined platform for improving the solubility, dissolution behavior, and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs that already possess intrinsic amorphous stability.

Despite the promising findings summarized in this review, several limitations should be acknowledged. The number of studies specifically investigating CAMS in Type III compounds remains relatively limited, and substantial variability exists among the reported drug-coformer combinations, preparation methods, and performance evaluation parameters. In addition, most available evidence is derived from in vitro studies, whereas comprehensive in vivo and clinical evaluation remain scarce. Furthermore, differences in experimental design and reporting standards among studies limit direct comparisons across systems. Therefore, the conclusions presented herein should be interpreted as a qualitative synthesis of the currently available evidence, and further standardized investigations are required to establish broader design principles for CAMS in Type III drugs.

Conclusion

The reviewed studies demonstrate that CAMS represent a promising strategy for improving the solubility, dissolution behavior, and physical stability of Type III drugs. However, the extent of these improvement varies considerably among formulations and depends strongly on factors such as drug-coformer compatibility, intermolecular interactions, molecular miscibility, formulation composition, and preparation method. Specific intermolecular interactions may suppress molecular mobility, increase nucleation barriers, and facilitate supersaturation maintenance, although their effectiveness is highly systems-dependent.

Key challenges remain, including predictive coformer selection, translation of in vitro supersaturation to in vivo performance, and process scalability. Addressing these gaps will be essential for advancing CAMS into a fully deployable and broadly applicable formulation platform in modern solid-state pharmaceutics.
