Natural biostimulant formulations enriched with honey, iodine, and silymarin enhance drought tolerance and yield in faba bean (Vicia Faba L.) through antioxidant and hormonal regulation
Authors: Mostafa M Rady, Amr Elkelish, Martin L Battaglia, Mona AA Abdel-Kawy, Gamal F Mohamed, Hanan A A Taie, Ragab S Taha, Haifa AS Alhaithloul, Mohamed A M Ali, Abdelghafar M Abu-Elsaoud, Tapsoba François, Ali A S Sayed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought stress is a major limitation to legume productivity in arid and semi-arid regions. This study investigated the effectiveness of two novel natural biostimulant formulations (NBFs), composed of bee honey, potassium iodate, and silymarin selected for their complementary antioxidant and osmoprotective functions in enhancing drought tolerance in faba bean (Vicia faba L.). Plants were subjected to deficit irrigation (withholding one irrigation after every two cycles) and foliar-sprayed with NBFs containing either 7.5 g L⁻¹ (F1) or 15 g L⁻¹ (F2) bee honey combined with 25 mg L⁻¹ potassium iodate and 0.2 mg L⁻¹ silymarin. Deficit irrigation markedly reduced growth, photosynthetic pigments, water status, and yield, while elevating oxidative stress markers. Application of NBFs significantly alleviated these adverse effects by enhancing osmolyte accumulation, improving antioxidant defenses, and restoring photosynthetic efficiency. Hormonal regulation was also improved, with increases in auxin, gibberellins, and cytokinins, alongside reduced abscisic acid levels. Notably, F2 was more effective than F1 in maintaining leaf integrity and maximizing pod yield under drought. These results highlight the potential of honey–iodine–silymarin formulations as eco-friendly, cost-effective biostimulants to promote crop resilience and productivity under water-limited conditions, contributing to sustainable agriculture.Keywords: Agricultural sustainability, Antioxidant defense systems, Drought, Natural biostimulants, Vicia faba

Introduction

Faba bean, Vicia faba L., is a highly nutritious and widely cultivated winter legume. It is an important food source for both humans and animals [1–3]. Egypt ranks sixth among the countries with the highest production of faba bean, contributing 3.9% of the global output. The five most productive countries ahead of Egypt are: China (34.6%), Ethiopia (16.9%), the United Kingdom (7.6%), Australia (6.7%), and France (5.4%) [4]. Faba bean seeds are a valuable source of protein, essential amino acids, and various minerals. They also contain dietary fiber and various antioxidants, including phenolic compounds and flavonoids. Bioactive compounds have also been found in the seeds, including folic acid (Vitamin B9), L-DOPA (a precursor to dopamine), and GABA (which helps regulate blood pressure and heart rate, preventing certain diseases) [5, 6]. Moreover, plant roots symbiotically fix atmospheric nitrogen into the soil. This vital process increases the N content in the soil and reduces the need for chemical N additives [28, 76]. However, faba bean plants are more sensitive to water-withholding (drought) stress than other legume crops [7–9].

The major concern in Egypt is the increasing demand for arable land to feed the growing population, coupled with limited irrigation water levels. Fresh water is available only from the Nile River (55.5 billion m3) [10], and minimal amounts from rainfall and wells. These limited amounts of water are allocated to all life activities, including agriculture. Consequently, crop cultivation, including faba beans, is negatively affected.

Producing more food using less water is the biggest challenge facing agriculture. This problem can be addressed by maximizing water use efficiency. This strategy is important for the Mediterranean region, including Egypt. This region is classified as an arid environment and is more vulnerable to the negative impacts of climate change [11–13]. In such a region, high temperatures increase evapotranspiration rates, causing drought leading to lower crop yields [14–16]. In addition, Implementing a withholding irrigation strategy throughout the growing season (D-IR) can save water. However, it can impede plant cell metabolism and development. It also carries significant risks and can have serious consequences if not managed carefully [17, 18]. Studies have concluded that the D-IR strategy may hinder nutrient absorption, transport, and metabolism, impairing plant growth in the plant. It can inhibit photosynthesis, impair nutrient uptake, and over-produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). As a result, plant cell expansion and division, cell membrane, growth, reproductive development, and root structure are impaired. Therefore, the plant becomes more susceptible to other stresses, accelerating plant death [19–21].

Available water can be managed effectively, maximizing its use efficiency through optimal agricultural practices [22]. Additionally, plants can adopt various strategies to cope with D-IR, including stress avoidance, tolerance, and escape [13, 23]. For avoidance, plants can increase their water uptake rates while reducing leaf area, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rate. For drought tolerance, plants generally use a combination of osmotic adjustment, maintaining tissue turgor, and keep plant growth under certain levels of drought stress. Finally, drought escape involves enhancing growth under different levels of drought stress to secure plant survival [24].

Recently, the use of sustainable agricultural practices, including natural biostimulants (NBs), has been explored to enhance crop defense mechanisms against environmental (eco-) stresses, including drought and salinity [23, 25, 26]. The use of NBs has been reported to make plants more stress-tolerant, allowing them to maintain acceptable growth and yield even under eco-stress conditions, contributing to the construction of more sustainable and resilient agricultural systems [27, 28]. Additionally, NBs are more readily available, less expensive, and an eco-friendly alternative to synthetic growth simulators [28–30]. They have been identified as enhancers of photosynthetic efficiency and antioxidant activities (enzymatic and non-enzymatic), as well as secondary metabolites involved in plant physiological responses [31, 32].

A dilute solution of bee honey (DBH) shows promise as an efficient natural biostimulant for enhancing plant tolerance to eco-stress impacts. It provides readily available bioactive compounds that directly enable the plant to combat the physiological challenges posed by eco-stressors. These compounds make the DBH a promising and efficient natural biostimulant for sustainable agriculture [28]. DBH contains simple sugars, proteins, organic and inorganic acids, lipids, enzymes, flavonoids, phenolic acids, and essential minerals. These bioactive compounds enable the DBH to have dual major mechanisms: an osmoregulator (OR) and a multi-antioxidant. Through these mechanisms, ROS and oxidative damage can be minimized, while plants can tolerate eco-stresses, including salinity and drought [33–35].

Iodine (I) is an essential micronutrient for the health of humans, animals, and plants [30]. It is required for the synthesis of thyroid hormones and plays a crucial role in regulating metabolism, growth, and development [36]. Insufficient iodine intake in humans can lead to a series of thyroid diseases [37]. Although iodine is not considered an essential nutrient for all terrestrial plants, there is evidence that it plays beneficial roles when applied in appropriate concentrations. One of its major benefits, is its ability to stimulate the biosynthesis and accumulation of many health-promoting bioactive compounds in plants, especially leafy vegetables. These compounds, including various phenolic compounds and vitamin C, are beneficial to human health [38, 39]. Studies indicate that iodine can increase plant antioxidant content, a critical mechanism for enhancing adaptive responses and plant tolerance to stress [40, 41]. Plants are a good source of iodine, with approximately 80% of all the iodine in human diets originating from plants. Furthermore, iodine levels in plants can be increased by biofortification methods and the addition of fertilizers [42]. Foliar application of iodine to plants is more efficient than soil application for biofortifying plants, especially leafy vegetables and grain crops such as wheat, rice, and maize [43].

Silymarin (SM) is a main bioactive compound in the fruit of the milk thistle (Silybum marianum L.) plant. Besides flavonolignans, SM consists of a mixture of flavonoids, including silibinin, silydianin, isosilychristin, isosilybin, silychristin, and toxifolin [44]. When accumulated in plant tissues, silymarin effectively responds to eco-stresses due to its antioxidant properties. It directly prevents the formation of ROS by inhibiting the enzymes involved in their production. Under severe D-IR, S. marianum plants can accumulate high levels of SM, leading to increased antioxidant enzyme activities and chalcone synthase gene expression, favoring plant tolerance [45]. Studies indicate that SM or SM-enriched NBs have recently been used to enhance plant tolerance to eco-stresses. This is because SM helps maintain mitochondrial integrity and cell redox balance by activating various antioxidants [46, 47].

To date, only a few studies have been performed on the use of SM alone or SM-enriched biostimulants. Moreover, to our knowledge, no study has been conducted using DBH enriched with iodine (KIO3) and SM as a natural biostimulant formulation (NBF) to promote faba bean plant growth and productivity under D-IR stress. This research hypothesized that foliar feeding with NBF would effectively minimize the harmful effects of over-produced ROS in D-IR-stressed faba bean plants. Additionally, the NBF treatment can reinforce the plants’ tolerance to D-IR stress by maintaining leaf water status, and hormonal and antioxidant redox balance. Therefore, this work aimed to detect the impacts of leaf treatment with NBF on the following estimations in D-IR-stressed faba bean plants: (i) oxidative stress marker levels and damage to the plasma membranes due to stress, (ii) photosynthesis efficiency and antioxidant redox homeostasis, (iii) upregulation of osmo-protection, various antioxidants, and enzyme activities, and (iv) plant growth and yield components. The combination of bee honey, potassium iodate, and silymarin in the present study was strategically selected based on their complementary physiological roles in mitigating abiotic stress. Bee honey is a rich source of osmoprotectants, antioxidants, and trace minerals that enhance osmotic adjustment and redox balance; iodine (as potassium iodate) stimulates antioxidant enzyme systems and improves nutrient metabolism and hormonal regulation; while silymarin, a potent flavonolignan complex, reinforces antioxidant defenses and stabilizes cellular membranes under stress. The integration of these bioactive agents into a single natural biostimulant formulation was hypothesized to generate a synergistic effect—strengthening drought resilience, maintaining photosynthetic efficiency, and improving yield performance in faba bean plants.

Materials and methods

Preparation of the natural biostimulant formulations and induction of drought stress

Pure clover bee honey (BH) was obtained from the apiaries of the Faculty of Agriculture, Fayoum University (29°17’N; 30°53’E). Two accurate weights, 7.5 and 15 g of BH, were taken; each weight was thoroughly dissolved in 1 L of sterile distilled water (Sd-H2O) at room temperature (24 ± 1 °C), to obtain two dilute BH solutions, DBH1 and DBH2, respectively. To prepare natural biostimulant formulations (NBFs), each DBH (DBH1 and DBH2) was enriched with 25 mg of potassium iodate (KIO3) and 0.2 mg of SM to produce F1 and F2, respectively. Therefore, F1 contained 7.5 g BH, 25 mg KIO3, and 0.2 mg SM L− 1 of Sd-H2O, and F2 contained 15 g BH with the same concentrations of KIO3 and SM L− 1 of Sd-H2O. The specific concentrations of BH (7.5–15 g BH), KIO3 (25 mg), and SM (0.2 mg SM) L− 1 of Sd-H2O were selected to prepare NBFs based on our preliminary study [1, 2]. In this study, drought stress (D-IR) was induced by withholding irrigation after every two consecutive irrigations. This water-saving strategy was followed throughout the experiment, from seed sowing to pod harvest.

Experimental dating, plant materials, and growing conditions

Two trials began on October 25 and ended on March 1 for the 2021/2022 and 2022/2023 winter seasons. Faba bean seeds (cv. Giza 716) were obtained from the Agricultural Research Center, Giza, Egypt. A 5% NaOCl solution was used to sterilize the surface of the intact seeds, which were then washed with Sd-H2O. One hundred and twenty plastic pots (38 cm diameter × 35 cm depth) were used for planting/growing faba bean seeds/plants. Each pot was filled with 10 kg of composite growth medium (CGM). The CGM contained three main components: pure sand (20.0%), vermiculite (30.0%), and peat moss (50.0%) by weight. The sand was treated with 10% commercial HCl, rinsed three times over three days to remove toxic and salt ions, and then washed several times with Sd-H2O. The CGM components were enriched with humic acid (10 mg kg− 1). Then, they were disinfected with the fungicide Moncut SC (Central Glass Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at a rate of 10 mg kg− 1. Afterwards, the CGM was fertilized with 0.42 g NH4NO3 + 0.5 g CaH4P2O8 + 0.33 g K2SO4 + 0.83 g MgSO4 L–1. A 1.25 g CaCO3 L− 1 was added to adjust pH, and 2% acidified compost was also added to increase CGM fertility [48]. After filling the pots with the CGM, they were placed in an open (net) greenhouse under natural conditions: an average temperature of 23 ± 3 °C, a humidity of 62 ± 5%, a day/night photoperiod of 11/13 h, and natural sunlight intensity. The seeds were then sown at a rate of three seeds pot− 1.

Treatments and trial setup

Fifteen days after sowing (DAS), the pots were splitted into two equal sections, each containing 60 pots. The first section was specified for full irrigation (F-IR), while the second section was specified for irrigation withholding (drought) stress (D-IR). Each section was divided into three equal subsections, each containing 10 pots. In both sections, the first subsection was assigned for foliar spraying with Sd-H2O, the second subsection was assigned for foliar spraying with F1, and the third subsection was assigned for foliar spraying with F2. Therefore, the treatments were as follows: (1) foliar spraying with Sd-H2O + F-IR, (2) foliar spraying with F1 + F-IR, (3) foliar spraying with F2 + F-IR, (4) foliar spraying with Sd-H2O + D-IR, (5) foliar spraying with F1 + D-IR, and (6) foliar spraying with F2 + D-IR.

Proline and ascorbate contents were estimated according to the methods of Bates et al. [50] and Kampfenkel et al. [51], respectively. Soluble sugar contents were evaluated [52], and an HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography) system was used to assess sugar fractions in BH (concentration of fructose, glucose, maltose, and sucrose, %) according to Bogdanov and Baumann [53]. Nutrient contents (calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, manganese, and copper) were measured using the methods of Chapman and Pratt [54]. The blue color method of Jackson [61] was applied to evaluate phosphorus content. A Perkin-Elmer flame pycnometer, Model 52-A (Glenbrook, Stamford, CT, USA), was used to assess potassium content [55]. The iodine content in BH was estimated by incubation with TMAH (25%), and then the standard analytical method of Jerše et al. [56] was applied. To ensure consistency between experiments, all honey batches were sourced from a single clover apiary and verified for physicochemical properties (moisture ≤ 18%, total soluble solids >80%, antioxidant activity ≥ 85%). Such quality control parameters are recommended for future standardization.

Plant sampling for estimations

The first sampling was implemented 75 DAS, including whole plants to examine vegetative growth characteristics using the root and shoot systems. The fully expanded upper third leaves were used for all physio-biochemical measurements and antioxidant system components (enzymatic and non-enzymatic). Subsequently, between 114 and 126 DAS (harvest stage), green pods were collected to assess pod yield components. During this period, pods were collected five times, 3 days apart, and counted and weighed for each plant. In all cases, samples were collected at 7:00 a.m. (11 ± 1 °C) to avoid the influence of environmental factors. They were immediately placed in insulated boxes refrigerated at 4 °C and quickly transported to the laboratory for various analyses. To evaluate all physio-biochemical determinations and antioxidant system components, samples from 3 replicates were used, and samples from 5 to 15 replicate plants were used to evaluate growth traits and pod yield components, respectively.

Estimation of osmoregulator contents and leaf integrity

Free proline content (µM g‒1 DW) was measured following the method of Bates et al. [50]. The supernatant obtained from centrifugation of the extract was mixed with a solution of acid-ninhydrin (freshly prepared). After incubation for 0.5 h at 90 °C, the reaction was terminated in an ice bath. The samples were extracted again with toluene to separate both the toluene and aqueous phases. Using a UV-160 A UV–vis spectrometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), absorbance was measured at 520 nm, and the free proline content was calculated using a standard curve prepared with proline. The procedure of Irigoyen et al. [52] was used to determine the content of total soluble sugars (mg g− 1 DW). The supernatant obtained from centrifugation of the extract was collected to mix with anthrone (freshly prepared). The mixture was incubated for 10 min at 100 °C. Using a UV-160 A UV–vis spectrometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), absorbance was measured at 625 nm, and the total soluble sugar content was calculated using a standard curve prepared with glucose. The contents of both choline and glycine betaine (GB) were evaluated using the procedures of Bessieres et al. [57] and Subbarao et al. [58], respectively, using Shimadzu-V HPLC. A 0.5 g of liquid N-frozen sample was homogenized in a 4 ml mixture of water, chloroform, and methanol (3:5:12, v/v/v). The homogenate was then incubated at 4 °C overnight. Using BioRad AG1-X8 ion exchange resins, 1 ml of the upper methanol phase was taken from the extract, purified, and then centrifuged (5000 × g for 10 min) to remove the ion exchange resin. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter before loading into the HPLC system. Using a Nucleogel RP column (RP-S 100-8, 300 × 7.7 mm) preceded by a guard column, the mobile phase (15 mM KH2PO4) was delivered by an analytical isocratic pump at a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min at 70 °C. The GB and choline contents were detected by a UV detector at 230 nm, and then quantified by comparing the peak surface areas (2.5 min and 3.5 min) with those obtained with pure GB and choline standards, respectively. The content of K+ (mg g− 1 DW) was evaluated by applying the procedure of Bilal et al. [59]. Briefly, HNO3 and H2O2 were used for suspension and digestion of the samples, and Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS; Optima 7900DV, Perkin-Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was utilized to quantify the content of K+.

Leaf-blade discs with a 2-cm diameter were prepared to determine the relative water content (RWC). Fresh, turgid, and dry weights (FW, TW, and DW, respectively) were recorded. FW was taken immediately after preparing the discs, TW was taken after discs’ water saturation for 24 h, and DW was taken after drying for 48 h at 70 °C. Estimation of photosynthetic efficiency

The procedures of Konrad et al. [61] were utilized to evaluate instantaneous carboxylation efficiency (iEC; µM m‒2 s‒1). After the extraction of photosynthetic pigments using fresh leaves and pure acetone as a solvent, the leaf total chlorophyll and carotenoid contents (mg g‒1 FW) were evaluated following the procedures described in [62]. Leaf photochemical activity was estimated using the potassium cyanide technique described in [63]. The method of Konrad et al. was used to estimate chlorophyll ‘a’ fluorescence (Fv/Fm) using a fluorometer (PAM-2000, Heinz-Walz).

Estimation of oxidative stress biomarkers

The procedure of Kubiś [64] was used to determine the level of superoxide radical (O2•−) in the leaves, and the method of Velikova et al. [65] was used to estimate the level of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). To evaluate lipid peroxidation,, as indicated by malondialdehyde (MDA) content [66], the supernatant was obtained after homogenizing the leaves with Na-phosphate buffer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 25 min. Absorbance was measured at 532–600 nm to quantify the MDA content.

Twenty leaf discs were prepared to determine the electrolyte leakage (EL). The electrical conductivity of the solutions, EC1, EC2, and EC3, was recorded. EC1 was taken immediately after preparing the discs, EC2 was taken after the discs had been heated for 0.5 h at 45 °C – 55 °C, and EC3 was taken after boiling for 10 min at 100 °C. Estimation of non-enzymatic antioxidant activities

The detailed procedures of Kampfenkel et al. [51] and Griffith [67] were used to determine ascorbate (AsA) and glutathione (GSH) contents (µM g‒1 FW) and redox states (%). The content of the antioxidant α-tocopherol (µM g− 1 DW) was determined by using the HPLC system described in Konings et al. [68] and Ching and Mohamed [69]. The total leaf content of phenols was evaluated following the Folin-Ciocalteu procedure of Wolfe et al. [70] with modifications made by Ali et al. [71]. To determine total flavonoid content, the method of Sultana et al. [72] was used. To evaluate iodine content, dried leafy samples were incubated with tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH, 25%) before following the iodine assaying standard method of Jerše et al. [56]. The SM content was determined as described in Arampatzis et al. [73] using a Soxhlet apparatus. For extraction, methanol (200 mL) was used, and the extractant was then evaporated to dryness. The resulting sample was reconstituted in HPLC grade methanol (25 ml), and the reconstituted sample was diluted with methanol to assess SM content (µg g–1 DW) using the HP 1100 Liquid Chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA). Antioxidant activity was determined using the 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay [74].

Estimation of enzymatic antioxidant activities

The procedure detailed in [75] was used to measure superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity by observing the readings of inhibiting the photochemical reduction of the nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) at 560 nm. The enzyme amount that inhibits 50% of the NBT reduction was calculated as 1 SOD unit. The procedure of guaiacol oxidation was used to estimate the activity of peroxidase (POD) using an enzyme extract and phosphate buffer, with guaiacol and H2O2 as reaction substrates. The full procedure of Ali et al. [71] was used to determine the activity of catalase (CAT) using an enzyme extract and phosphate buffer, with H2O2 as a reaction substrate. The activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) was assayed using the method of Nakano and Asada [76]. For this purpose, an enzyme extract and phosphate buffer, with ascorbate as a reaction substrate, were used. The method in [77] was used to assess soluble protein content.

Extraction and quantification of phytohormones

Phytohormone contents were quantified utilizing an HPLC system (Waters Alliance 2695, Waters Corp.). Briefly, 0.2 g of fresh leaf was extracted in 2 mL of distilled water and centrifuged at 12,500 rpm at 4 °C. The pH of the recovered supernatants was adjusted to 2.8–3.2 using 30% acetic acid. The acidified water extract was partitioned twice against 2 mL of diethyl ether. The organic layer was recovered and evaporated under a vacuum in a centrifuge concentrator (Speed Vac, Jouan). The dry residue was then suspended in a water: methanol (9:1, v/v) solution. The resulting solution was filtered and directly injected into the HPLC system. Using MeOH, gibberellic acid (GA3) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) were separated [78]. Following the procedure of Novák et al. [79], leaf samples were frozen in liquid N and then ground to extract and analyze total cytokinins (CKs). The abscisic acid (ABA) level was determined after extraction and purification using the method of Kettner and Dörffling [80].

Leaf anatomy for stomatal features

To estimate the guard cell dimensions, stomatal aperture area, and stomatal density measurements, 9 plants were randomly selected from each treatment. The measurements were performed for a fully expanded leaf on the 4th node from the shoot apex of each plant. From the lower surface, the sample of epidermal cells was obtained (abaxial side) with the nail varnish technique [81]. A small area of abaxial was then observed through a light microscope (BX60, Olympus, Hamburg, Germany), equipped with a digital camera (Camedia C4040, Olympus, Hamburg, Germany). Stomata dimensions, stomatal aperture area, and stomata density were assessed with the software AnalySIS®3.2 program for image analysis (Olympus, Hamburg, Germany) and their frequency (n mm–2).

Estimation of plant growth and yield components

After separating the plants into shoot and root systems, they were weighed for fresh weight. They were dried at 70 °C for 48 h until the weights were constant, and then the dry weights were recorded. Pods were counted and weighed for each plant. Seeds were then extracted from the pods and weighed for each plant, and 100 seeds were weighed for the specified replicates.

Statistical analysis

The two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) technique was executed after testing for homoscedasticity. Treatment means were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) test at p ≤ 0.05 with CoStat software (version 6.29; CoHort Software). Statistical computations were carried out using NCSS and Microsoft Excel® (2013).

Results

Plant growth traits

Our findings indicate that withholding one irrigation after every two consecutive irrigations until the end of the growing season (D-IR) resulted in significant negative impacts on growth traits in faba bean plants (Table 3). D-IR reduced shoots and root fresh weights by 47% and 44%, and shoot and root dry weights by 47% and 43, respectively, compared to the control (F-IR; full irrigation). When F-IR was provided across the complete growth cycle of faba bean plants, foliar feeding with F1 (F-IR, F1) or F2 (F-IR, F2) significantly increased all the abovementioned growth traits compared to the F-IR with just distilled water application (F-IR, F0), with no significant differences between biostimulators F1 and F2 across all comparisons.

Osmo-regulatory substances (O-RSs) and leaf integrity

Compared to the no water stress treatments, application of D-IR treatments (averaged across the three biostimulants treatments F0, F1, and F2), increased some of the O-RSs in faba bean plants, including proline, glycine betaine (GB), and soluble sugars 51%, 38%, and 37%, respectively, but decrease K+ concentration by an average 27%. Across all comparisons, the no water stress and no biostimulant treatment (F-IR, F0) and the deficit irrigation stress with the double rate of BH biostimulant (D-IR, F2) resulted in the lowest and highest values of proline, GB and soluble sugars across all comparisons, respectively (Fig. 1). Compared to F-IR, F0, applying either biostimulant with or without stress or no applying biostimulant under stress, increased proline, GB and soluble sugars between 14 and 93% as a drought-response mechanism. Across all comparisons, lowest K+ values were found in the D-IR, F0 while highest K+ values when either biostimulant was applying under no water stress (F-IR, F1 and F-IR, F2) (Fig. 1).

Hormonal status

Application of D-IR, F0 and D-IR, F1 treatments induced negative impacts on the hormonal levels of IAA, GA3, and CKs in faba bean plants (Fig. 6). D-IR, F0 significantly decreased the contents of IAA, GA3, and CKs by 41, 43, and 45%, respectively, while increasing ABA levels by 82% compared to the F-IR control. Under F-IR conditions, foliar spraying with F1 or F2 significantly increased the contents of IAA, GA3, and CKs, while the content of ABA decreased compared to the F-IR control, with no significant differences between the values obtained from both F1 and F2 application. Under D-IR, foliar spraying with F1 or F2 markedly increased the contents of IAA, GA3, and CKs, while decreasing the content of ABA. In this case, however, F2 noticeably outperformed F1, increasing the contents of IAA, GA3, and CKs by 71, 73, and 86%, respectively, while decreasing the content of ABA by 45% compared to the D-IR control (Fig. 6).

Application of a D-IR, F0 treatment stimulated significant negative impacts on yield components of faba bean plants, significantly reducing the number of pods plant–1, pods weight plant–1, seeds yield plant–1, and 100-seed weight by roughly 25, 23, 33, and 33%, respectively, compared to the F-IR, F0 control (Table 6). Discussion

Reduced growth rates in crops exposed to drought stress, as induced in our study by withholding irrigation (D-IR), have been widely reported in the literature [7, 9, 82, 83]. In the present study, drought markedly suppressed faba bean growth and yield, particularly under D-IR without biostimulant treatment (D-IR, F0). In contrast, foliar application of the honey-iodine-silymarin formulations (F1 and F2) substantially mitigated these reductions, with F2 being consistently superior under stress conditions. This indicates that doubling the bee-honey concentration from 7.5 g L⁻¹ in F1 to 15 g L⁻¹ in F2 enhanced drought tolerance and plant vigor. The improved performance can be attributed to the rich composition of bee honey, which contains osmoprotectants (proline, soluble sugars, maltose, sucrose, glucose, and fructose), antioxidants (ascorbate, iodine), and essential nutrients (Mg, K, Ca, P, Fe), collectively supporting osmotic balance, redox regulation, and cellular integrity under stress [34, 84].

Moreover, application of F1 and particularly F2 strengthened both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant defenses (Fig. 5; Tables 4 and 5), thereby limiting oxidative damage and maintaining metabolic activity [85, 86]. These effects were reflected at multiple physiological levels, including improved phytohormonal balance (Fig. 6), photosynthetic efficiency (Fig. 3), relative water content and membrane stability (Fig. 2), and enhanced osmolyte accumulation (Fig. 1). Consequently, these integrated improvements culminated in significantly higher pod yield and yield components in biostimulant-treated plants, demonstrating the synergistic role of bee honey, iodine, and silymarin in promoting faba bean resilience to drought (Table 6).

D-IR-exposed faba bean plants treated with F1 or F2 accumulated higher levels of phytohormones particularly total cytokinins; CKs (Fig. 6). This could be due to the wealth of plant growth-encouraging substances in BH (Table 1). Phytohormone levels enhancement observed in BH-treated plants were similarly reported under drought and nitrogen stresses [87]. The increased CKs, in this study due to BH application, contributed to promote cell division and elongation, induce carbohydrate metabolism and chlorophyll biosynthesis, and generate new source-sink relations, which resulted in increased dry matter accumulation in plants [88]. Enriched-BH also increased IAA and GA3 levels in D-IR-exposed faba bean plants (Fig. 6), which reflected in better D-IR management, as endogenous plant hormones are essential prerequisites for improving plant self-defense system against D-IR [89–91].

Photosynthetic efficiency (in terms of total chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, PSII efficiency; Fv/Fm, and photochemical activity) is an accurate indicator of overall plant health, and exposure to abiotic stress reduces photochemical quenching capacity within PSII [92–94]. The results showed reductions in photosynthetic efficiency indices (Fig. 3), indicating degradation of chlorophyll and photo-inhibition of PSII, which is possibly due to the decrease in leafy water status (RWC and MSI) (Fig. 2) needed for photosynthesis and repairing the negative influences of ROS on chloroplasts [51]. The F1 or F2-treated faba bean plants were able to maintain higher photosynthetic efficiency indices under D-IR conditions. These results may be attributed to an improvement in leaf RWC and cell membrane integrity (MSI), while reducing EL by F1 or F2 treatment (Fig. 2). The results of this study agree with those of several authors [32, 36] who demonstrated that BH application was able to protect the photosynthetic apparatus to acceptable levels under drought, salinity, and excessive N conditions in comparison to untreated plants. BH is rich in Cu, Zn, Mn, Fe, Ca, Mg, P, and K to sustain intercellular ion balance, photosynthesis, accordingly promoting the efficiency of photosynthetic machinery of the D-IR-exposed faba bean plants.

An essential mechanism of protection against eco-stresses comprises the synthesis of osmo-regulatory compounds (O-RSs) to protect cellular membranes and proteins against damage along with eliminating ROS [95, 96]. The untreated stressed plants significantly (p ≤ 0.05) accumulated O-RSs like soluble sugars, glycine betaine (GB), and proline in comparison with unstressed untreated (the control) plants (Fig. 1). F1 or F2-treated faba bean plants maximized their O-RSs in comparison with untreated plants contributing to an enhanced osmotic adjustment and providing greater protection to cells from D-IR.

As main organic O-RSs, proline and GB accumulate in a range of plant species in response to D-IR [97, 98]. Since it directly contributes to osmotic adaptation, proline acts as a compatible solute. It also functions as an enzyme protectant and a scavenger of free radicals [99, 100]. In addition, soluble sugars are a major group of O-RSs, playing a decisive role in alleviating the adverse effects of abiotic stresses through specific mechanisms, including resistance to desiccation of plant cells through osmotic modification [101, 102]. These accumulated O-RSs (proline, GB, and soluble sugars), due to F1 or F2 application, participated significantly in alleviating the harmful D-IR impacts to induce faba bean plants to respond to D-IR stress by osmoprotectant up-regulations (Fig. 1).

Exposure of faba bean plants to D-IR persuades cellular redox balance disturbance and induces excessive production of oxidative stress markers (O2•− and H2O2) and oxidative damage (EL and MDA) (Fig. 4), which may cause direct damage to metabolic processes and cellular structures. Plants are equipped with defense machinery that includes the synthesis of enzymatic (APX, POD, CAT, and SOD) and non-enzymatic (α-tocopherol; αToC, phenolic, flavonoids, GSH, and AsA) antioxidants to mitigate ROS, and overcome oxidative damage to plant cells. Increasing the activities of enzymatic antioxidants is an urgent necessity for D-IR-exposed plant cells. After dismutating the O2•− radicals into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by SOD, CAT degrades the intracellular H2O2 [103–105]. In comparison with untreated stressed faba bean plants, these degrading mechanisms were markedly higher in F1 or F2-treated plants, in which the high activities of SOD and CAT significantly connected with great suppressions of O2•− and H2O2, and thus suppression of oxidative damage (Fig. 4).

AsA, GSH, αToC, phenolics, and flavonoids represent essential non-enzymatic antioxidant mechanisms in different stressed crop plants. Their endogenous levels were significantly increased by the foliar application of F1 or F2 on faba bean plants grown under normal or D-IR conditions. Consequently, AsA and GSH redox states increased with increased antioxidant activity (DPPH-radical scavenging activity; DPPH-RSA) in stressed plants (Fig. 5; Table 4). AsA, GSH, and αToC play crucial roles in enhancing plant tolerance to eco-stress conditions through improving the scavenging ability (DPPH-RSA) to get rid of excessive ROS [72, 106, 107]. The richness of BH in antioxidants accompanied by its high antioxidant activity (Table 1) enabled faba bean plants to cope with D-IR condition. Overall, the findings of this study suggest that the ameliorative effect of F1 or F2 foliar application (with a significant preference for F2) protected faba bean plants against the damaging influences of D-IR by ROS-scavenging and osmotic adjustment [85, 108, 109] through the up-regulation mechanisms of O-RSs (Fig. 1) and different antioxidants (Fig. 5; Tables 4 and 5), positively reflecting in high faba bean pod yield and yield components (Table 6).

BH is a rich source of O-RSs, different nutrients, various vitamins, etc., and possesses high antioxidant activity (87.5%) (Table 1). Thus, it possesses several mechanisms for D-IR tolerance in faba bean plants, including the prevention of lipid peroxidation. In agreement with this report, BH contributes to ameliorating the negative influences stimulated by D-IR on plant growth and productivity because it possesses mechanisms to overcome D-IR [6]. This may be due to the growth-linked metabolites of the BH bioactive compounds, including vitamins, O-RSs, and nutrients. These bioactive compounds enable the plant to restore its normal growth and productivity under D-IR. Besides, BH is a potent osmo-regulator (contains a high percentage of soluble sugars; Table 1) that maintains cell membranes, cellular wall elasticity, and leafy integrity, including high leaf RWC and MSI, and low levels of MDA and EL. These mechanisms induce maximization of nutrient contents, various antioxidants and photosynthetic efficiency, and suppression of H2O2 and O2•‒ levels to minimize D-IR-induced oxidative damage [32, 110].

The results (Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7; Tables 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7) indicate that supplementation of the BH with iodine and silymarin; Sm (F2) led to significant increases in faba bean plant growth traits and yield components through improved physio-biochemical attributes and antioxidant defense systems under D-IR, all of which were improved through several mechanisms by BH, iodine, and Sm.

Physio-biochemical mechanisms of iodine interaction on plant uptake of essential nutrients have not been explored. Also, in some reports the impact of iodine on photosynthesis machinery is not predicted. However, iodine can significantly improve the growth traits, yield components, and photosynthesis parameters, as well as plant antioxidant status, providing more plant tolerance to D-IR influences. Previous reports indicate a synergistic or antagonistic iodine effect on plant mineral nutrition [39, 41]. By adding to irrigation water (a cost-effective and beneficial method), KIO3 provides a significant increase in plant nutrient content and plant growth (biomass) as it optimizes soil nutrient levels. It also increases antioxidant amounts to induce greater tolerance/resistance to eco-stresses and enhance plant growth and productivity. Through foliar spraying, IO3− (lower toxicity to plants) is transported from plant leaves to roots and edible parts and is reduced to I− [111, 112]. The lower IO3− toxicity can be elucidated based on that the IO3− is an alternative substrate to many enzymes, including nitrate reductase, or it activates IO3− reductase through its stimulation of other responses connected with redox signaling and iodine metabolism in plants, besides the IO3− reduction. The addition of IO3− in nutrient solutions increases leafy vegetable biomass. The concentration ranges of 00.5− 2 × 10− 3 M KIO3 added to the faba bean and strawberry nutrient solutions lead to significant increases in plant biomass [113]. Positive influences on food crop biomass have been observed. Although the global concentration of iodine averages at 2.6 mg per kg soil, applications of iodine up to 10 mg per kg soil enhance plant growth and productivity [30]. In most cases, iodine application as IO3− is better than I−, particularly for the synthesis of the antioxidant compounds. KIO3 supplementation (7.88 µM IO3−) increases the activities and contents of APX, SOD, AsA, and phenolics in plants. The application of KIO3 (20 and 40 µM IO3−) increases AsA, GSH, and phenolic accumulations, as well as SOD, APX, and CAT activities, and antioxidant potential. In this report, the beneficial impact of KIO3− application on plants (as a component of F1 or F2) may be related to its inclusion in plant proteins, thus stimulating plant growth, development, and productivity [114, 115].

Silymarin (Sm) has diverse biological activities and antioxidant effects mediating different mechanisms for promoting ROS removal. The Sm antioxidant influences are due to the bio-transformation of “silybin diastereomers” to create “glucuronide derivatives”. It also exerts anti-inflammatory impacts by promoting CKs production and preventing inflammatory pathways. These essential mechanisms can inhibit oxidative stress damage in stressed crop plants. Thus, Sm can be applied to stressed crop plants to improve plant productivity and overall agricultural sustainability through improving plant protection against stress [116–118]. Sm can prevent lipid peroxidation in stressed plants due to ROS- scavenging, increase the cellular GSH content, regulate cell membrane permeability, increase cell membrane stability, and regulate nuclear expressions. The “silibinin” component of Sm is a potent Fe chelator, thus inhibiting linoleic acid oxidation catalyzed by Fe2+. In addition, Sm maintains the normal fluidity of cell membranes by direct interaction with cell membrane ingredients and preventing alterations in lipid fraction contents [119].

Exogenous application of Sm (to enrich the HB) mitigates salinity (10.0 dS m–1) stress damage and improves plant growth, physio-biochemical traits, yield, K+/Na+ ratio, non-enzymatic and enzymatic antioxidant activities, O-RSs levels, hormonal balance, and gene expressions, all due to decreased toxic (Na+ and Cl–) ion contents, oxidative stress markers, oxidative damage, and ABA level. In addition, Sm improves plant growth and biomass accumulations, photosynthesis efficiency, different antioxidant systems, antioxidant redox balance, regulation of endogenous polyamines (putrescine, spermidine, and spermine) and the activities of polyamine metabolic enzymes (arginine decarboxylase and ornithine decarboxylase), hormonal homeostasis, and thus antioxidant defense mechanisms under cadmium (Cd) stress conditions. Therefore, Sm effectively mitigates lipid peroxidation, reduces ionic leakage and Cd2+ buildup and content, and thus protects against oxidative damage catalyzed by ROS. These positive mechanisms of Sm against eco-stresses collectively preserve the cell membrane integrity in favor of satisfactory growth and productivity under eco-stress conditions [120, 121].

Stomatal traits are important inductors that associated with stress tolerance ability in plants and have critical role in nutrients and water movement in plants. Foliar application of F1 or F2 improved the leaf anatomical traits particularly stomatal aperture area, stomatal length, and stomatal width. Foliar application of F1 or F2 has an important role in strengthening the cell wall and maintaining cell expansion for improving the stomata anatomical traits and protecting the chloroplast structure. This improvement in stomatal traits may be due to the role of F1 or F2 in activation of phytohormones that control maintenance of cell turgor pressure and activity of antioxidant defense system, as well as control pro-cambial of vascular tissues [81, 122]. The current results highlighted the great importance of F1 or F2 in improving nutrients and water translocation into active parts of the Vicia faba plants through enhancing stomatal performance (Fig. 7; Table 6). Overall, the results of this study present new good insights into the role of the application of biostimulant formulations (F1 and F2) in supporting yield traits through improving stomatal system for enhancing Vicia faba growth and development. The proposed biostimulant formulations are cost-effective and scalable for field application. All ingredients—bee honey, potassium iodate, and silymarin—are commercially available at low cost, and foliar application can be achieved using standard spraying systems without specialized equipment. The total cost per hectare is estimated to be comparable to or lower than other natural biostimulants, making this approach feasible for sustainable agriculture in arid regions.

It should be noted that natural honey composition can vary depending on floral source and environmental factors. To minimize this variability in future applications, standardized quality parameters such as total soluble solids, sugar profile (fructose–glucose ratio), and antioxidant activity should be determined prior to formulation. Developing quality specifications for biostimulant-grade honey will be essential for reproducibility and regulatory approval.

Although this study was conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions, the findings provide a strong foundation for field-scale implementation. Future field trials are warranted to evaluate formulation performance under variable environmental and soil conditions. Given the low cost and bio-based nature of the inputs, the formulations are economically viable for adoption in drought-prone regions where sustainable intensification is needed.

Conclusion

Environmental stresses, including drought, are prime abiotic threats, which constrain crop performance (growth and output) worldwide. In the current study, drought significantly decreased growth and pod yield components, as well as physio-biochemical characteristics of faba bean plants compared to unstressed plants. Foliar application of F1 (7.5 g bee honey + 25 mg potassium iodate + 0.2 mg silymarin per liter distilled water) or F2 (15 g bee honey + 25 mg potassium iodate + 0.2 mg silymarin per liter distilled water) succeeded in alleviating the damaging impacts of drought stress. Surpassing F1, F2 exhibited greater tolerance in faba bean plants to drought stress inspected by decreasing oxidants (O2•− and H2O2) and oxidative damage (MDA and EL) levels through enhanced non-enzymatic (AsA, GSH, α-tocopherol, phenolic, flavonoids), enzymatic (SOD, CAT, POD, and APX), and antioxidant activities, as well as improved osmo-regulatory substances (soluble sugars, glycine betaine, proline, and K+) contents, photosynthetic efficiency (total chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, PSII efficiency; Fv/Fm, and photochemical activity), and phytohormone levels. Foliar feeding with the multi-biostimulator F2 should, in the future, be widely used to promote crop growth and yield under eco-stresses.
