Are we measuring neighborhood disadvantage wrong? A methodological reassessment of its structure in the United States
Authors: Philippa Clarke, Kimberly Rollings, Robert Melendez, Marilyn Sinkewicz, Kate Duchowny, Lindsay Gypin, Grace Noppert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeighborhood disadvantage (or deprivation) indices are increasingly used in population health research and policy. In the United States, many frequently used neighborhood disadvantage indices were originally derived in the late 1990s and early 2000s. However, the United States has undergone dramatic economic and social shifts in the past three decades, including the Great Recession, growing wealth inequality, and the changing nature of work in the “gig” economy. Yet few studies have re-evaluated the structure of neighborhood disadvantage to determine if the items included in existing indices continue to capture the contemporary US context. The purpose of this paper was to re-evaluate the construct of neighborhood disadvantage using national data on United States census tract characteristics from the 2018-2022 American Community Survey. We found that neighborhood disadvantage has evolved from its early 21st Century construct of the co-absence of economic, social, and family resources in the local neighborhood to more recently becoming a construct better captured only by economic indicators of poverty, public assistance income, and low income. We also found evidence for neighborhood affluence as a distinct construct from disadvantage. We recommend simplifying neighborhood disadvantage indices by including only a parsimonious set of economic items. We also recommend the use of a distinct neighborhood affluence index derived from items that are better related to this construct.Keywords: Neighborhood, Socioeconomic status, Disadvantage, Affluence, Deprivation, Factor analysis, United States

Introduction

Despite the recent focus on neighborhood disadvantage (also termed area-level deprivation or vulnerability) in United States health policy and research, the construct of neighborhood disadvantage itself is rarely questioned. Area-level disadvantage is typically measured using census-based information on the sociodemographic composition of neighborhoods (e.g., census tract proportion of people in poverty). While some research has relied on a single indicator to capture disadvantage, composite measures that combine multiple social, economic, housing, and demographic variables in a single index have been shown to be more strongly associated with health outcomes (Lian et al., 2016; Trinidad et al., 2022). Many of the composite disadvantage indices in widespread use today were derived from indices developed in the late 1990s and early 2000s (Flanagan et al., 2011; Singh, 2003). However, current research does not question whether the components included in such disadvantage indices are outdated. By re-evaluating the structure of disadvantage in the contemporary US context, this paper seeks to inform the accurate measurement of neighborhood disadvantage in clinical and population health research.

Neighborhood socioeconomic structure: disadvantage and affluence

Neighborhoods have long been studied by social scientists as bounded spatial systems shaping social processes that influence individual behavior, attitudes, and opportunities (Brooks-Gunn et al., 1993; Shaw, & McKay, 1942). While the spatial segregation of people in poverty has existed since the Industrial Revolution (Massey, 1996), the sociological study of disadvantage focused on the co-absence of economic, social, and family resources in the local neighborhood (Massey, 1990; Massey et al., 1998; Massey et al., 1994; Ross et al., 2001; Sampson & Raudenbush, 1997; Wilson, 1985, 1987). Social disorganization and social isolation theories posited that the scarcity of resources in disadvantaged neighborhoods fostered a sense of mistrust and a lack of collective efficacy, which resulted in higher rates of violent crime and poor mental health among residents (Aneshensel et al., 2007; Morenoff et al., 2001; Ross et al., 2001; Sampson & Groves, 1989; Sampson & Raudenbush, 1997). Concentrated unemployment in disadvantaged neighborhoods was hypothesized to create a collective environment that isolated people from the norms of work and informal knowledge of job opportunities that had lasting impacts on youth educational attainment and employment outcomes (Garner & Raudenbush, 1991; Massey & Shibuya, 1995; Wilson, 1987). The concentration of single parents in economically disadvantaged neighborhoods was hypothesized to weaken informal social control mechanisms, with adverse consequences for child and youth outcomes (Brooks-Gunn et al., 1993). In essence, the neighborhood defined opportunity structures and operated as an informal system of social control, which broke down when residents had few economic resources (Boardman et al., 2001; Duncan, Brooks-Gunn, and Aber, 2000; Ginther & Wolfe, 2000; Ross, 2000; Ross et al., 2001; Ross & Mirowsky, 2001).

Conversely, neighborhoods with a high proportion of residents with college degrees and high incomes anchored community institutions and provided organizational and economic resources (Browning & Cagney, 2003; Morenoff et al., 2001; Wen et al., 2003). Distinguished from non-disadvantaged neighborhoods by their large share of highly educated adults in professional occupations, affluent neighborhoods had the social and economic resources to promote and sustain local institutions that benefit the health of the neighborhood as a whole (Browning & Cagney, 2003; Wen et al., 2003). This upper end of the neighborhood socioeconomic continuum, neighborhood affluence, has often been shown to be more relevant for health outcomes than neighborhood disadvantage (Clarke et al., 2012; Massey, 1996; Massey et al., 1998; Wen et al., 2003). Yet, many disadvantage indices include indicators of affluence in their construction (e.g., the Area Deprivation Index includes a variable for white collar occupations (Kind & Buckingham, 2018); the Child Opportunity Index includes variables for college education and high-skill employment (Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2020)), preventing the ability to distinguish the separate effects of disadvantage and affluence on population health outcomes.

Contemporary measures of neighborhood socioeconomic structure

There are at least 33 publicly available disadvantage indices in the US (Franchi et al., 2023; Breslau et al., 2022; Trinidad et al., 2022). They all include multiple economic, social, and housing items (ranging from 7 to 29 items) at the local area level (e.g., census tract, block group), which are combined to create a single multidimensional index of neighborhood disadvantage (Trinidad et al., 2022). For example, the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), one of the most widely used indices in clinical, public health, and healthy policy work (Powell et al., 2023), includes 17 census block group indicators capturing economic characteristics (family income, poverty), social characteristics (education, unemployment, single parent families) and housing characteristics (home ownership, home value, mortgage and rent, household crowding, and multiple housing characteristics, e.g., percent of households without complete plumbing or a vehicle) (Kind & Buckingham, 2018). Other commonly used indices, such as the Childhood Opportunity Index (COI) (Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2020), the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) (Flanagan et al., 2011), and the Social Deprivation Index (SDI) (Butler et al., 2013), include similar economic, social, and housing items in their index construction plus other sociodemographic and environmental indicators (e.g., age structure, transportation, industrial pollutants).

A recent scoping review summarized the range of variables included in 15 national disadvantage indices used in US health outcomes research (Trinidad et al., 2022). While most indices were developed to quantify socioeconomic disadvantage, others were specifically intended to identify communities most in need of support during natural disasters (SVI, Flanagan et al., 2011 (Flanagan et al., 2011)), to evaluate community healthcare needs (Community Need Index (CNI), Huang et al., 2019), or to identify factors that impact childhood growth and opportunity (COI, Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2020). Every one of the 15 indices reviewed included a measure of poverty. Educational attainment (adults with less than a high school degree or with a college degree) was included in 12 of the 15 indices. The proportion of adults unemployed (or employed) was included in all but 4 of the indices. Housing characteristics (median home value, median rent, mobile homes, crowding, renter vs owner-occupied housing, and vacant housing) were present in all but 3 of the indices. Four indices included households receiving public assistance income, and over half (eight) included single-parent or female-headed families. Other variables, such as race/ethnicity and households without a vehicle, were less consistently included across indices and their relevance for disadvantage may be questionable (Butler et al., 2013; Flanagan et al., 2011; Huang et al., 2019; Kind & Buckingham, 2018; Sullivan et al., 2018). For example, the spatial concentration of low income non-Hispanic Black residents in a neighborhood represents the consequence of historical residential and sociopolitical policies, not socioeconomic disadvantage per se. As Trinidad and colleagues (2022) cautioned, there is a need to critically consider how each variable included in such indices relates to disadvantage.

The methodology used in index construction also varies widely. Factor analysis or principal components analysis are typically used as a data reduction technique, but the variables are inconsistently standardized or ranked across indices prior to analyses. The resulting indices are either expressed as a value or a rank (within the nation, states, or quintiles) (Trinidad et al., 2022). Finally, the spatial definition of neighborhood varies across indices. Most use census tracts, while others (CNI (Huang et al., 2019) and Distressed Communities Index (Mehaffey et al., 2020)) use the larger ZIP code tabulation area. The ADI is the only index to use smaller block group areas. However, due to their smaller population, block group data are frequently suppressed (missing) by the US Census Bureau, particularly for income-related measures, requiring imputation for indices created at the block group level. For example, over 50% of block groups have at least one missing item in the construction of the 2022 ADI (K. Rollings, Noppert, et al., 2024).

The plethora of indices available and their differences in construction have limited comparability and reproducibility across studies. The ADI, SVI, and Social Deprivation Index (SDI, Butler et al., 2013) are some of the most used indices and are recommended for studying population health outcomes (Franchi et al., 2023; Breslau et al., 2022).1 But research indicates that the ADI and SVI are only modestly correlated (Spearman Correlation Coefficient = 0.49-0.57 at the census tract and block group levels, respectively) (K. A. Rollings, Noppert, et al., 2024), with notable disagreement in identifying which neighborhoods are the most (or least) disadvantaged across the US (e.g., only 35% of US census tracts classified as the most disadvantaged (highest decile) by the ADI were similarly classified as most disadvantaged by the SVI) (Rollings et al., 2023)). Index differences also manifest as inconsistencies in rates of mortality and adverse health outcomes by neighborhood disadvantage (Azar et al., 2023; Berg et al., 2021; Gladish et al., 2026; Hannan et al., 2023; Limburg et al., 2025; Rehkopf & Phillips, 2023; K. A. Rollings, Noppert, et al., 2024). For example, in a study of Medicare beneficiaries following coronary artery bypass graph surgery, Rollings, Noppert, et al. (2024) found a 0.9% difference in 30-day readmission rates in US census tracts classified as the most disadvantaged by the ADI versus the SVI. Similarly, in a cohort of primary care patients, odds ratios for diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and mortality were consistently lower in the least (vs. most) disadvantaged census tracts as classified by the ADI versus the SDI (Limburg et al., 2025).

Contemporary neighborhood disadvantage indices have recently been used in US health policy to inform vaccine distribution (Srivastava et al., 2022) and to provide payment adjustments to healthcare providers in disadvantaged areas (Azar et al., 2023; Phillips et al., 2023; Breslau et al., 2022; Robst et al., 2024). Until 2024, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Accountable Care Organization Realizing Equity, Access, and Community Health (ACO REACH) program used the ADI to identify the most disadvantaged individuals and communities in order to provide additional resources to health care providers serving these areas (Liao & Navathe, 2022; Navathe & Liao, 2023). However, the ADI's over-emphasis on home value in its index construction was found to introduce bias by underestimating disadvantage in areas that were otherwise disadvantaged according to poverty and income indicators (Petterson, 2023). As a result, CMS commissioned and began using an alternative index (the Community Deprivation Index (CDI)) in 2025 (Robst et al., 2024). Others have the addressed methodological problems of the ADI by releasing alternative versions that standardize home value, impute missing data, and update schooling thresholds to represent contemporary high and low education categories (Duke University School of Medicine, 2022; Gladish et al., 2026; K. Rollings, Noppert, et al., 2024). But the 17 component items included in the ADI remain unquestioned.

Why we need to revisit the structure of neighborhood disadvantage

Despite calls for continually re-examining the components of neighborhood disadvantage over time (Rehkopf & Phillips, 2023), the internal validity of disadvantage indices has rarely been assessed. A small number of studies have re-evaluated the single factor structure of the ADI, which was derived from 1990 census tract data (Singh, 2003), using more recent data from the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) (Berg et al., 2021; Gladish et al., 2026; Petterson, 2023). Petterson (2023) re-estimated the factor loadings using five-year estimates from the 2016-2020 ACS data and Gladish et al. (2026) did the same with 2018-2022 ACS. In both analyses, there was consistent evidence that housing (crowding, motor vehicle, plumbing), family (single parent households), and unemployment variables had progressively weaker loadings on the ADI factor structure over time. For example, the factor loading for the proportion of people unemployed was 0.71 in Singh's analysis of 1990 Census data, but fell to 0.36 in Petterson's 2020 analysis, and 0.31 in Gladish et al., ’s 2022 analysis. Similarly, factor loadings for household crowding, lack of a motor vehicle, and households without complete plumbing ranged from 0.21 to 0.34 in the 2022 analysis compared to Singh's 1990 factor loadings (range 0.49 to 0.78).

In contrast, the income-related variables continued to have strong loadings across this 30-year period. The 2022 census tract factor loadings for families below poverty (0.78) and median household income (−0.94) (Gladish et al., 2026) were consistent with the loadings for those variables in 1990 (0.86 and −0.86, respectively) (Singh, 2003). Recent research found that area-level poverty and education are largely driving neighborhood effects on physical health, mental health, and mortality outcomes, regardless of geographic area (Lian et al., 2016; Lou et al., 2023). Items such as housing, resources, and environment were less strongly related, suggesting that disadvantage could be better operationalized using fewer indicators. Indeed, Singh's (2003) original analysis of the ADI found that of all the 17 indicators in the ADI, poverty, income, and education had the largest relative weights in generating the 1990 index.

Collectively, this evidence suggests that neighborhood disadvantage in the US may be evolving away from a construct based on social deprivation (captured by unemployment, household crowding, households without plumbing, or without a vehicle) towards a more economic-focused construct better represented by low income and poverty variables. The US has undergone dramatic economic and social shifts in the past five decades, including the Great Recession, growing wealth inequality, rapid gentrification, and the social and economic fallout from the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic (Dudel & Myrskylä, 2017; Pugh et al., 2022; Richardson et al., 2019). Since 1970, the distribution of household income is increasingly concentrated at the extremes (upper and lower income), and the proportion of middle-income households is declining (Pew Research Center, 2026). Marriage is less common, and single parent families are on the rise (children are about 20% less likely to be living with two married parents in 2024 compared to 1970). A high school education has become almost ubiquitous (the share of adults without a high school diploma decreased from 42% in 1970 to 10% in 2024) (United Health Foundation, 2026), making it less of distinguishing characteristic for disadvantage indices.

In 2019 the University of Minnesota Law School published a report on neighborhood change in US census tracts between 2000 and 2016 (Institute on Metropolitan Opportunity, 2019). In the middle of the 20th Century, wealth was concentrated in the suburbs leaving growing poverty in the inner city. But from 2000 to 2016, growth, gentrification, poverty, and decline were seen across urban, suburban, and exurban communities, signaling the “emergence of a totally different paradigm of neighborhood change” (Institute on Metropolitan Opportunity, 2019; pg. 3). The two common trends observed were a growing spatial concentration of low-income residents in economically declining tracts, and a growing concentration of higher income residents (displacing the low-income population) in economically expanding neighborhoods. Thus, concentrated spatial disadvantage may be transforming towards a purely economic phenomenon that distinguishes the “haves and the have-nots”.

Objective and hypotheses

The purpose of this paper was to re-evaluate neighborhood disadvantage in the contemporary US context to see if it is still a multidimensional construct represented by housing, family, education, unemployment, and economic indicators. We examined the internal validity of neighborhood disadvantage using latent variable measurement models with nation-wide census tract data from the 2018-2022 American Community Survey (ACS). We hypothesized that the structure of “neighborhood disadvantage” has simplified from multidimensional to primarily economic. We also hypothesized that “affluence” exists as a separate construct distinct from disadvantage. Re-evaluating the components that make up disadvantage indices is critical if we are to accurately capture the contemporary US context for health research and policy (Phillips et al., 2016; Rehkopf & Phillips, 2023).

Methods

Overview

We take a latent variable approach within a measurement theory framework to examine the socioeconomic structure of neighborhoods in the contemporary US context. Neighborhood disadvantage and neighborhood affluence are inherently unobservable, and thus not directly measurable. However, within a latent variable approach, the constructs of disadvantage and affluence can be captured through characteristics of neighborhoods that can be directly measured (with error). We first conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to identify a parsimonious set of factors and variables that capture the dimensionality of neighborhood socioeconomic structure. We then tested the factor structure using a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

Data and measures

We used the census tract as the unit of measurement since it has historically been the most commonly used spatial area in health research. Census tracts generally have a population size between 1200 and 8000 people, with an average size of 4000 people. They encompass spatial areas that are large enough (vs. census block or block group) to capture the space through which neighborhood socioeconomic processes operate (Aneshensel et al., 2007), but also small enough (vs. county) to capture areas that are relatively homogenous according to the US Census. Census tracts are also the preferred geographic level for studying the health effects of neighborhood SES (Franchi et al., 2023).

The National Neighborhood Data Archive (NaNDA) includes publicly available nation-wide measures of socioeconomic characteristics at the census tract level (Clarke et al. 2022; Duncan and Aber [1997], 2000). We used NaNDA's 2018-22 ACS 5-year estimates for 84,539 census tracts (at 2020 census geography) with non-zero population in the 50 states, District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. We included 17 census tract variables (listed in Table 1) identified in previous theory and research (Duncan and Aber [1997]; Singh, 2003), which are commonly used in contemporary neighborhood disadvantage measures (Flanagan et al., 2011; Kind & Buckingham, 2018), including race, ethnicity, education, income, poverty, public assistance income, unemployment, occupation, marriage and family structure, age structure, foreign born, English proficiency, and housing ownership. All variables were measured as proportions (range 0-1) (e.g., proportion of population below poverty = number of people below poverty/total population in each census tract). We restricted our analyses to 84,211 tracts (99.61% of the total number of census tracts) with complete data on all 17 variables, dropping 328 (0.39%) tracts with missing data on at least one variable.2

Statistical analyses

To conduct the factor analyses we split the ACS 2018-22 data into two random half samples. One half was used for an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with all 17 census tract variables using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors (to address skewness and kurtosis). A goemin oblique factor rotation was used (Hattori et al., 2017). A series of iterative models was conducted to identify the best fitting model. Variables were sequentially dropped if they had weak loadings (lambda<|0.5|) of if they showed evidence of cross-loading across factors (i.e., loading on one factor was less than twice the loading on another factor). The best fitting models from the EFA were then evaluated using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with the second random half sample of the ACS 2018-22 data. Factor loadings, factor variances, and item residual variances were permitted to be free parameters. Residual covariances and factor means were fixed to zero. To establish the scale of the latent factor, the factor loading of one variable was fixed to 1.0.

The EFA models were evaluated based on the interpretability of factors and the number of factors with an eigenvalue >1. For both EFA and CFA, nested models were compared based on four goodness of fit indices. The Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) assess fit relative to other models; values of 0.90 or greater are indicative of good fitting models (Bentler, 1990; Hu & Bentler, 2009). The Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) measures the difference between observed and fitted variances and covariances; values of 0.05 or less are desired. Finally, the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) produces a measure of model misspecification per degree of freedom. A simulation study conducted by Hu and Bentler (Hu & Bentler, 2009) suggested that an RMSEA smaller than 0.06 indicates relatively good model-data fit. All analyses were conducted in MPlus (Version 8.11).

Results

Table 1 reports the means, standard deviation (SD), skewness, and kurtosis for the 17 census tract variables (ACS 2018-2022). On average, 33% of people in US census tracts had a bachelor's degree or higher (mean proportion = 0.329, SD = 0.198, Table 1) and 14% had incomes below the federal poverty level (mean proportion = 0.139, SD = 0.115). The mean proportion of unemployed adults in US census tracts was 0.057 (SD = 0.047), or 5.7%. Evidence of skew and/or kurtosis indicates significant departure from normality for some of the indicators, including unemployment, proportion of people age 70 and over, and with limited English proficiency, which is addressed in the analyses using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors.

Confirmatory factor analysis

We restricted the CFA models to the 10 variables in the disadvantage and affluence factors, excluding the 3 variables (proportion Hispanic, foreign born, and with limited English) that were distinct from the socioeconomic factors. We first tested a single overall socioeconomic factor and then tested two different 2-factor models with separate (correlated) affluence and disadvantage factors. The first two-factor model included all 10 SES variables (3 affluence items and 7 disadvantage items). The second two-factor model used only the 6 variables with the strongest loadings in the EFA: 3 items for affluence (high income, college education, professional/managerial occupations) and the 3 economic items for disadvantage (low income, poverty, public assistance).

Table 5 presents the fit statistics across the iteration of CFA models. The single socioeconomic factor model (Model 1, Table 5) did not fit the data well (RMSEA = 0.180, SRMR = 0.089; CFI = 0.691, TLI = 0.603). A two-factor model with all 10 variables (Model 2, Table 5) specifying separate (correlated) factors for affluence and disadvantage, had better fit (RMSEA = 0.118, SRMR = 0.073; CFI = 0.871; TLI = 0.829). Fig. 1 illustrates the factor structure (standardized loadings) for this two-factor model. Neighborhood affluence was represented by 3 indicator variables (standardized factor loadings range from 0.857 to 0.936): proportion of adults with a bachelor's degree or higher; proportion of people in the civilian labor force who are employed in professional or managerial occupations; proportion of families with annual income >$125,000. Neighborhood disadvantage was represented by 7 indicator variables, with three economic variables having factor loadings ranging from 0.844 to 0.923: proportion of people with annual income below federal poverty level; proportion of households receiving public assistance income or food stamps; proportion of families with annual income <$40,000. The other 4 social indicator variables (less than high school education, single parent families, unemployed, and owner-occupied housing) had much lower loadings (ranging from −0.530 to 0.681). Given their weaker loadings, we subsequently tested a second two-factor model without these four social indicator variables.

Discussion

Ever since the Industrial Revolution, people have been sorted into neighborhoods by socioeconomic position. From the slums of London's East End (1850-1930) to the tenements of New York City (circa 1900) and the favelas of current-day Brazil, poverty has been concentrated in place. In the late 20th Century social characteristics in the form of concentrated unemployment, welfare dependency, and female-headed families became part of the characterization of disadvantaged neighborhoods (Garner & Raudenbush, 1991; Sampson & Groves, 1989; Wilson, 1985). Contemporary measures of neighborhood disadvantage have since integrated both social and economic characteristics of the local neighborhood in their index construction (Breslau et al., 2022; Trinidad et al., 2022). But little research has assessed the degree to which such indices continue to capture the contemporary socioeconomic context of the United States.

In this paper, we re-evaluated neighborhood socioeconomic structure using latent variable measurement models with nation-wide census tract data from the 2018-2022 ACS. We found that neighborhood disadvantage has evolved from the late 20th Century construct of the co-absence of economic, social, and family resources in the local neighborhood (Massey, 1990; Massey et al., 1994, 1998; Ross & Mirowsky, 2001; Singh, 2003; Wilson, 1987) to becoming a construct better captured by economic indicators of poverty, public assistance income, and low income. Existing neighborhood disadvantage indices in widespread use in US health policy and research include multiple economic, social, demographic, and housing items (Franchi et al., 2023; Breslau et al., 2022; Trinidad et al., 2022). The five most common characteristics measured at the local area level are income, education, housing, family structure, and transportation (Trinidad et al., 2022). Yet, our analyses provided compelling evidence that social indicators, including unemployment, housing characteristics, and single parent families, may not be the best markers of neighborhood disadvantage in the contemporary US context.

The evolution of neighborhood disadvantage

Over 30 years ago, the growth of concentrated poverty in inner cities clustered with other indicators of social disadvantage, such as male joblessness, female headed families, and high school dropouts. In The Truly Disadvantaged, William Julius Wilson (1987) argued that people living in neighborhoods of concentrated poverty were disadvantaged not because they were poor but because the social disadvantage in these neighborhoods offered limited resources and role models necessary for positive life outcomes. Whereas poor neighborhoods in the first half of the 20th century (prior to the deindustrialization of the US economy) had relatively low levels of unemployment, single parent families, and welfare dependency, the levels of joblessness, teenage pregnancies, female-headed families, welfare dependency, and serious crime reached “catastrophic proportions” in high poverty neighborhoods in the mid-1970's (Wilson, 1985) (pg. 541). Because poverty clustered with other forms of social disadvantage, social scientists argued that these neighborhood sociodemographic characteristics should be studied to understand the mechanisms by which impoverished neighborhoods led to poor individual outcomes (Brooks-Gunn, Duncan, and Aber [1997] 2000).

Our results provide evidence that the meaning of concentrated disadvantage is shifting yet again. In contemporary US neighborhoods, poverty is no longer clustering with markers of social disorganization, such as unemployment and single parent family structure. While joblessness was a defining characteristic of economically disadvantaged neighborhoods in the middle of the 20th Century (Wilson, 1985, 1987), unemployment is becoming more diffuse as the nature of employment and work itself are changing in the contemporary US context. Stable long-term careers are being replaced by nonstandard, often short-term work, with millions participating in independent, contract, or “gig” work (Tan et al., 2021; Wright, 2018). Following the 2008 global financial crisis, the market for temporary, contractual labor expanded substantially as American companies began hiring workers without offering them formal employment. Many of these freelance workers are highly skilled professionals working the equivalent of full-time hours but are classified as unemployed in official employment statistics (Bracha & Burke, 2021; Burke & Cowling, 2020). Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston indicated that up to 15% of people counted as officially “unemployed” engage in some gig work (Bracha & Burke, 2023). Many workers also use the gig economy to supplement their income during an unemployment spell. Unemployment may therefore be more of a fluid state and not be as precarious as it was 50 years ago. Or perhaps limitations in the way unemployment is measured in contemporary US surveys have rendered it an unreliable indicator in disadvantage indices (Bracha & Burke, 2023).

The concentration of single parent families in economically disadvantaged areas was also a characteristic of late 20th America (Ross et al., 2001), but our results suggest that this may be outdated. The US has the world's highest percentage of children in single parent families (representing one-third of all children in America), the majority of which are single mother families (Kramer, 2019). While single parent families were typically characterized by poverty, low education, and job insecurity in the mid-20th century, they are increasingly represented by higher educated individuals of both sexes (Yorks, 2022).

As such, we need to critically think about what these social characteristics mean in the contemporary US context and whether these variables continue to represent disadvantage. While concentrated unemployment in disadvantaged neighborhoods was hypothesized to decrease access to job information network systems and to create a collective environment that isolated people from the norms of work (Garner & Raudenbush, 1991; Massey & Shibuya, 1995; Wilson, 1987), neighborhoods may no longer be the defining opportunity structure for work. Today, job information networks, role models, and behavioral norms are likely to come from social media and the internet rather than the sociodemographic characteristics of one's neighbors. Similarly, social ties and local systems of social control that prevent “disorderly behavior” (Wilson, 1996) are no longer dependent on the concentration of two-parent families in local neighborhoods.

The persistence of neighborhood affluence

We also found clear evidence for neighborhood affluence as a distinct construct from neighborhood disadvantage. Neighborhood affluence emerged in the social science literature in the late 1980's and 1990's as scholars anticipated that the growing spatial concentration of poverty in the US would simultaneously generate enclaves of affluence as the rich sought to distance themselves physically and socially from the socioeconomically disadvantaged (Massey, 1990, 1996). Scholars argued that affluent neighborhoods provided the necessary social and institutional resources to protect against crime (Morenoff et al., 2001) and foster better developmental outcomes for children and youth (Brooks-Gunn et al., 1993; Sampson et al., 1999). For example, affluent neighborhoods could offer better networks for obtaining higher paying jobs, and affluent neighbors served as positive role models for school completion (Brooks-Gunn et al., 1993). This body of research emphasized that concentrated affluence was more than just the absence of disadvantage, and that affluent neighbors had more of an impact on fostering child development than the absence of low-income neighbors (Brooks-Gunn et al., 1993; Sampson et al., 1999).

In our results, the disadvantage and affluence factors were not perfectly correlated (−0.67, Fig. 2), reaffirming affluence as a distinct neighborhood construct in the contemporary US. Yet, current research conflates affluence and disadvantage by using indices that include indicators of college education and white collar/professional occupations (Acevedo-Garcia et al., 2020; Kind & Buckingham, 2018), preventing an understanding of how affluence may affect individual outcomes in distinct ways from disadvantage. A recent re-estimation of the factor structure of the ADI (with 2005–2009 and 2013–2017 ACS data) found evidence of a separate affluent factor (including income, home value, white collar occupations) that was distinct from economic disadvantage (poverty, no vehicle, unemployment), and education/crowding (Berg et al., 2021). Continuing to re-evaluate the structure of contemporary indices is critical if we are to appropriately distinguish between affluence and disadvantage in the measurement of neighborhood context.

Recommendations for future work

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, when research on the effects of neighborhood disadvantage on child and adolescent development, joblessness, and mental and physical health was growing, scholars disagreed about which features of neighborhood socioeconomic composition were most important for individual outcomes. Some focused on the number of female-headed families in a neighborhood and its relationship with youth fertility outcomes (Hogan & Kitagawa, 1985); others focused on the number of people employed in professional/managerial occupations and the association with high school dropout rates (Crane, 1991); still others focused on neighborhood unemployment as the critical factor influencing youths’ social mobility (Wilson, 1991). Some of these choices may be considered controversial today. But the point is that scholars were thoughtfully considering what neighborhood measures were most relevant for the particular outcome they were studying. This paper is a reminder to scrutinize the variables included in each neighborhood index and consider whether they are capturing the intended exposures of interest.

Our findings suggest that neighborhood disadvantage indices could be simplified by including a parsimonious set of three economic items. We recommend prioritizing poverty, public assistance income, and low-income indicators within existing disadvantage indices (e.g., ADI, CDI). While the transportation, social, and housing characteristics of a neighborhood are unequivocally important for individual health outcomes, they may be weakly patterned by economic disadvantage indicators. Disentangling housing and other social structural domains from economic disadvantage is critical for understanding their distinct effects on population health outcomes. Health outcomes research should also consider neighborhood affluence in addition to disadvantage. Although the correlation between affluence and disadvantage may pose problems when including them both in statistical models, separate models with each construct could provide insights into their distinct effects.

While other existing indices include measures of English language proficiency (SVI (Flanagan et al., 2011) and Community Need Index (Huang et al., 2019)), our results indicate that this characteristic is not an indicator of economic disadvantage nor affluence. Instead, language is part of a separate construct that is simply capturing the concentration of people who are Hispanic, foreign born, and with limited English proficiency in local areas. Note that in Singh's (2003) original factor analysis of 21 indicators, English language proficiency and percentage of immigrant population had such weak loadings (<0.25) that they were dropped from the subsequent ADI index creation. We therefore recommend caution in using language and foreign-born items in contemporary disadvantage indices.

Limitations

While the strengths of this study include the large nationwide analysis of all residential census tracts in the United States, and the rigorous evaluation of internal validity of neighborhood disadvantage indices in 2022, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the factor structure was evaluated nationally consistent with other neighborhood disadvantage indices in widespread use (e.g., ADI, SVI). However, the factor structure could vary by US region, state, or across rural versus urban areas; testing factorial invariance of these measures is therefore called for in further research. Second, we estimated the factor structure in US census tracts, consistent with other work (Singh, 2003). However, the relative importance of the factor items may vary at other spatial scales (e.g., block group, ZIP code tabulation area) (Krieger, 2002; Lian et al. 2016; Duncan and Aber [1997], 2000), and merits further investigation. Third, the factor analysis was based on 17 census tract variables identified in previous theory and research (Duncan and Aber [1997]; Singh, 2003); however further research could evaluate the factor structure with other potentially important indicators. Fourth, the CFA model did not include correlated residuals because we had no a-priori theoretical justification for expecting a shared method variance (e.g., similar question wording for items or identical response scales) and adding correlated error can result in biased estimates of factor loadings and factor covariances (Brown, 2015). Finally, we caution that when using disadvantage indices in a longitudinal context, differences in index components can create challenges for comparability.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of this work indicate that neighborhood disadvantage in the US is evolving from a multidimensional construct of social and economic disadvantage to an economic construct distinguished by neighborhood disadvantage or neighborhood affluence. Further research is needed to evaluate the external validity of these indices with respect to population health outcomes (Clarke et al., 2026). Recognizing that neighborhoods inherently change with time (Petterson, 2023), our findings emphasize the importance of continually re-examining the components of neighborhood disadvantage measures and also raise the importance of considering the often overlooked construct of neighborhood affluence.
