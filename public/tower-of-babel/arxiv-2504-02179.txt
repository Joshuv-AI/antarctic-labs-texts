Homes’ Law and Universal Planckian Relaxation
A. Shekhter, M. K. Chan, R. D. McDonald, N. Harrison

Abstract
According to Zaanen’s interpretation of Homes’ empirical law [Zaanen, Nature 430 , 512 (2004)], the superconducting transition temperatures in the cuprates are high because their metallic states are as viscous as quantum mechanics permits. Here, we show that Homes’ law in fact implies three key points: (i) the resistivity is linear in temperature in the normal state near the transition temperature; (ii) the dimensionless coefficient of proportionality of the relaxation rate with temperature is of order unity—the so-called universal Planckian relaxation rate; and (iii) the logarithmically broad applicability of this law arises from an unusually wide range of effective masses throughout the cuprate phase diagram. In fact, a universal Planckian relaxation rate implies Homes’ law only if the mechanism of mass renormalization is independent of the Planckian relaxation.



In the high-temperature superconducting cuprates and strongly coupled conventional superconductors, the superfluid density scales with the product of the transition temperature and the electrical conductivity just above it, a relationship known as “Homes’ law”
Homes et al. 2004
. Immediately following this observation, Zaanen noted that this empirical law connects two previously unrelated facts: the highest achievable superconducting transition temperatures, and a “strange” metallic state
Anderson 1988
that is as viscous as permitted by quantum mechanics
Zaanen 2004
. Homes’ law has since been shown to apply to a broader variety of unconventional superconductors, as well as strongly coupled conventional superconductors
Homes et al. 2005
;
Dordevic et al. 2013
.

Here, we point out that Homes’ law is, in fact, a simple manifestation of the linear-in-temperature resistivity (
\rho=AT
) in the strange metal, which is its defining characteristic. This is understood a a
T
-linear dependence of the scattering rate,
\hbar/\tau=\alpha k_{\rm B}T
, where
\alpha
is a universal dimensionless quantity of order unity
Legros et al. 2019
;
Shekhter et al. 2024
. In the cuprates, this law implies a universal ‘Planckian’ relaxation rate associated with the strange metal state that is independent of doping. The law spans many orders of magnitude because of enormous variation of the effective mass with composition
Legros et al. 2019
;
Shekhter et al. 2024
. Although though some of the data used by Homes and coworkers
Homes et al. 2004
lies outside the strange metal region, the law still applies to logarithmic accuracy.

It is important to note that both the superfluid density and the conductivity are proportional to the ratio
n/m^{\ast}
, of the carrier density to the effective mass
Homes et al. 2004
. Thus, Homes’ law tacitly implies that in the materials where Homes’ law applies, the relaxation time,
\tau
, entering the conductivity is inversely proportional to temperature,
T
, which is a defining characteristic of a strange metal.
Based solely on a dimensional analysis, Zaanen argued
Zaanen 2004
that
\hbar/\tau\sim k_{\rm B}T
at
T=T_{\rm c}
, where the scaling is determined by Boltzmann’s constant,
k_{\rm B}
, and Planck’s constant,
\hbar
(here divided by
2\pi
). In this particular context,
\hbar/\tau\sim k_{\rm B}T
has come to be known as the Plackian relaxation (or dissipation) rate
Hartnoll and Mackenzie 2022
;
Phillips et al. 2022
, and applies at all temperatures (not just
T_{\rm c}
). Empirically, Homes’ law gives
\alpha\approx
2.6. This is consistent with a wider range of measurements
Shekhter et al. 2024
.

It is important to note that Homes’ law has logarithmic accuracy. In the cuprates, for example,
\hbar/\tau=\alpha\,k_{\rm B}T
accurately describes the resistivity,
\rho(T)=1/\sigma(T)
, in the strange metal state, which occupies a funnel-shaped region in the temperature-versus-doping phase diagram
Shekhter et al. 2024
;
Ando et al. 2004
. With the exception of a range of dopings close to a critical doping
p^{\ast}\sim 0.2
,
T_{\rm c}
lies below the funnel region, meaning that the resistivity in the immediate vicinity of
T_{\rm c}
departs from the Planckian form given by
\hbar/\tau=\alpha\,k_{\rm B}T
. However, because the resistivity on the approach to
T_{\rm c}
below the funnel region deviates from the linear form by a numerical factor (of three or less), this departure has no significant impact on the logarithmic scale in Fig.
1
.

Given the dependence of both the superfluid density and electrical conductivity on the ratio
n/m^{\ast}
, the variation in this quantity is the sole reason why Homes’ law spans multiple decades in Fig.
1
. Hence, it is the large variation in
n/m^{\ast}
makes Homes’ law span so many decades. In the cuprates, for example,
n/m^{\ast}
within the
a,b
-planes changes by roughly an order of magnitude as a function of doping
Legros et al. 2019
;
Shekhter et al. 2024
. Furthermore, although the superfluid density and conductivity are strongly anisotropic in the cuprates
Homes et al. 2004
, Homes’ law continues to hold because these anisotropies can be expressed in terms of an anisotropy in the effective mass,
m^{\ast}
. Finally, in non-cuprate superconductors,
m^{\ast}
is much larger in heavy fermion superconductors
Hewson 1993
than in conventional superconductors
Carbotte 1990
;
Bruin et al. 2013
. Hence, Homes’ law clearly demonstrates that superconductivity arises from an instability that occurs regardless of the underlying effective mass and the mechanism by which it is enhanced.
From this perspective, the wide applicability of Homes’ law across a logarithmically broad range of superfluid densities, conductivities, and transition temperatures suggests that the fluctuations responsible for the linear-in-temperature relaxation rate are largely independent of the electronic correlations responsible for enhancing the effective mass. This is discussed in some detail in Ref.
Shekhter et al. 2024
.

In the cuprates, the
T
-linear relaxation rate is accompanied by a linear-in-energy relaxation rate, which is the result of a competition between energy and temperature associated with quantum criticality
Valla et al. 2000
;
Kaminski et al. 2005
;
Bok et al. 2016
;
Basov and Timusk 2005
;
Armitage 2018
. By contrast, phonon fluctuations in conventional metals do not exhibit such competition. Whereas the phonon-scattering relaxation rate depends on temperature, it does not depend significantly on energy
Ziman 1960
. The fact that the values of
\alpha
in strongly-coupled conventional superconductors are close to unity is the result of strong charge screening in these systems
Carbotte 1990
.
In heavy fermion systems (e.g. CeCoIn
5
), the Planckian relaxation rate may be caused by quantum criticality in a similar manner to the cuprates
von Löhneysen et al. 2007
;
Gegenwart et al. 2008
. An alternative possibility is that this results from antiferromagnetic fluctuations behaving similarly to phonons
Monthoux et al. 2007
.

This brings us to the question: what sets
\alpha
close to unity in strange metals? One possibility is that
\alpha\sim 1
is the natural value for such a dimensionless coefficient. Another possibility, as suggested in Ref.
Zaanen 2019
, is that
\alpha\sim 1
represents the maximum possible value in a system with extensive many body quantum entanglement. Circumstantial evidence for such a possibility has been reported in viscosity studies of the quark-gluon plasmas
Busza et al. 2018
and the unitary regime of cold-atom Fermi gases
Cao et al. 2011
.

It is finally important to note that because
\hbar/\tau=\alpha k_{\rm B}T_{\rm c}
sets the magnitude of the normal-state conductivity at
T_{\rm c}
, Homes’ law does not itself depend on
T_{\rm c}
. Hence, while a Planckian relaxation rate in the normal state is a prerequisite for high values of the superconducting transition temperature
Zaanen 2004
, it does not, in fact, provide an absolute bound on the value of
T_{\rm c}
as claimed by Zaanen
Zaanen 2004
. In other words, the scale-invariance of the Planckian relaxation cannot determine
T_{\rm c}
. If anything, it is the magnitude of
n/m^{\ast}
that determines
T_{\rm c}
in strange metal systems
Emery and Kivelson 1995
;
Baskaran et al. 1987
. In this sense, the high
T_{\rm c}
observed in Homes’ materials is simply a consequence of the fact that strange metals, due to their many body highly entangled state, become unstable to superconductivity at atypically high temperatures
Zaanen 2018
.
