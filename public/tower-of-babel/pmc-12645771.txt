Simultaneous anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with valgus high tibial osteotomy improves mid- to long-term clinical outcomes without increasing the complication rates in young anterior cruciate ligament deficient patients with varus malalignment: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Guoyi Han, Fei Li, Qinglei Xu, Peng Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study is to systematically review and quantitatively analyze the mid- to long-term clinical outcomes of simultaneous anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) and valgus high tibial osteotomy (HTO) in the treatment of ACL injury with varus malalignment of the knee.MethodsThis study was performed in line with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) and AMSTAR (Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews) Guidelines. PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases were searched to identify studies reporting clinical outcomes of simultaneous ACLR and valgus HTO surgery. Data on follow-up duration, patient-reported outcome measures, clinical stability assessment, radiological outcomes, return to sports level evaluation, and complication rate were extracted. The Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Study scores was used for quality assessment. Review Manager and R statistical software were used to perform the statistical analysis.ResultsFifteen studies with a total of 463 knees in 461 patients were included. Patients were predominantly male (75%). After mid- to long-term follow-up, the weighted mean of mechanical femoral tibial angle (mFTA) increased from 174.6° to 182.0° (weighted mean difference = −7.4; p < 0.00001) after the simultaneous HTO and ACLR procedure. Significant improvements (p < 0.00001) were identified in the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective score (weighted mean: 51.8 vs. 69.7), Lysholm score (weighted mean: 59.8 vs. 87.3), and Tegner activity score (weighted mean: 3.5 vs. 4.8). The proportion of patients returned to pre-injury sports level ranged from 31.43% to 50% with an overall pooled rate of 31.8%. The incidence of complications ranged from 0% to 75% with an overall pooled risk of 13.4% (95% CI = 4.9%–25.2%). Two patients (0.46%) required revision surgery due to graft failure. Five studies compared the clinical outcomes of simultaneous valgus-producing HTO with ACLR and HTO alone procedures. The pooled analysis showed that the combined procedure group had a significantly higher Lysholm score (weighted mean: 72.4 vs. 78.7; WMD = −6.3; p = 0.02). As for the radiological anterior tibial translation (ATT) measurement (weighted mean: 3.50 vs. 2.26, p = 0.19), the Kellgren-Lawrence OA grading score (weighted mean: 0.47 vs. 0.32, p = 0.12) and the incidence of complications (10/49 [20.4%] vs. 9/47 [19.1%]; OR = 0.71; P = 0.75), no significant difference between combined procedure group and HTO alone group were found.ConclusionThis review showed that the simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO could achieve improvement of clinical outcomes in terms of PROMs, knee stability, and return to pre-injury level. The indication of this procedure is physically active ACL-D patients with knee varus resulting from medial compartment OA and/or medial meniscus insufficiency, rather than ACL injury with constitutional knee varus. Compared with HTO alone, the combined procedure improved knee function, but did not reduce the incidence of radiological OA progression or complication risk.Level of evidenceLevel IV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06476-7.Keywords: Valgus high tibial osteotomy, Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, Medial compartment osteoarthritis, Knee varus, Patient-reported outcome measures

Introduction

The treatment of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) deficient patients with knee varus has been a great dilemma for orthopedic surgeons [1]. Gait analysis suggested that in ACL-deficient (ACL-D) knee, the knee adduction moment (KAM) would become larger and could bring the load of the knee joint more medially and aggravate the cartilage wear of the medial compartment [2–4]. Medial articular cartilage wear would furtherly increase the knee varus malalignment, enlarge the lever arm of foot-floor force during walking or running, and make the KAM even larger [2]. This forms a vicious cycle, and this scenario should be given more attention when making decisions for the surgical strategy of ACL-D patients with medial compartment osteoarthritis (OA) [5].

The ACL reconstruction (ACLR) surgery could restore the stability of the knee joint and consequently protect the meniscus [4, 6–8] and articular cartilage from OA progression [9, 10]. However, biomechanical studies have shown that varus knee alignment would bring a greater load to the ACL graft and result in a higher failure rate of ACLR surgery [11]. Although ACLR with bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft showed biomechnical advantages over hamstring [4] and peroneus longus tendon [12, 13], it might also result in potential various complications including bone mineral content loss [14]. Furthermore, Noyes et al. [15] found that a high adduction moment could produce more tensile force on the posterolateral complex (PLC) and lead to abnormal lateral joint opening. Therefore, the concept of the double varus and triple varus knee was introduced with great originality, and early high tibial osteotomy (HTO) surgery was developed, with simultaneous or staged ACLR to address the dilemma of ACL-D with medial OA as well as varus malalignment [15, 16].

So, this study aims to investigate the mid- to long-term clinical outcome of simultaneous ACLR and HTO surgery in treating ACL-D patients with medial OA and knee varus malalignment. This study hypothesized that this combined procedure would yield satisfactory clinical outcomes in terms of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) through mid-term to long-term follow-up.

Materials and methods

This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024540857). This work was conducted in line with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses), AMSTAR (Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews) Guidelines [17], and TITAN (Transparency and International Transferability in Artificial Intelligence for Neuroimaging) Guideline [18].

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

The search strategy was used to identify all publications eligible for inclusion in the review. PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases were searched in July 2025. The search terms are listed in Table 1. Specific search strategies were developed for each database, and references of the identified studies were checked for potential eligibility.

The following inclusion criteria were used to identify eligible studies: (1) publications reporting on the clinical outcomes of ACLR in patients with medial OA and knee varus in terms of PROMs, (2) adding valgus-producing HTO procedure to address medial OA and correct knee varus simultaneously, (3) prospective or retrospective cohort study with an average follow-up of at least 60 months and clinical outcome parameters in terms of PROMs, and (4) comparative studies designed to compare clinical outcome of concomitant ACLR and HTO procedure with HTO alone. Furthermore, this study excluded non‐English language reports, case reports, conference abstracts/posters, or reviews. After the removal of duplicates, two orthopedic surgeons independently reviewed the titles and abstracts to screen for potentially eligible studies. Full texts were then assessed independently by the same two reviewers to identify the final list of publications suitable for inclusion in the current study. If disagreement occurred, a third senior orthopedic surgeon was consulted for final assessment and consensus.

Data extraction

Each included article was abstracted regarding study features, patient characteristics, surgical information, and outcome measures. The study characteristics included the author’s name, publication date, study design, level of evidence, and number of patients/knees. Patient data included sex, age, and duration of follow-up. Surgical information covered in the included studies regarding the indication of simultaneous ACLR with valgus producing HTO and surgical techniques such as the osteotomy approach (medial open wedge or MOW, lateral close wedge or LCW, and other options), graft choice (autograft, allograft or other options) and fixation method of osteotomy sites (staples, plates or angle-stable locking plate fixators) was summarized. Other additional procedures for knee stability (lateral extra-articular tenodesis or LET, meniscus allograft transplantation or MAT, microfracture chondroplasty) were also recorded.

Outcome measures consisted of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), radiological parameters, and physical examination results. PROMs included Lysholm score, International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective knee form, and Tegner activity scale (TAS). Post-operative complications (including graft failure event) were documented. Radiological parameters were mechanical femoral tibial angle (mFTA), weight bearing line (WBL) ratio, single leg stance measurement, posterior tibial slope (PTS) angle, and patellar height measurements. Physical examination results included Lachman test evaluation, subjectively or objectively with the side-to-side difference (SSD) of KT-1000, KT-2000, or TELOS measurements, and subjective grading of the pivot shift test. The data extraction of the included studies was conducted independently by two reviewers.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers. In this regard, the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Study (MINORS) [19] for cohort studies was used. Items on the questionnaire were scored as follows: 0 if not reported, 1 if reported but inadequate, and 2 if reported and adequate. The maximum score for noncomparative studies was 16, with a score of 13–16 being considered the low risk of bias. The maximum score for comparative studies was 24, with scores of 21–23 considered to be at low risk of bias. Otherwise, studies were considered to have a high risk of bias. When disagreement occurred, a third senior orthopedic surgeon was consulted for final consensus. The intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated in SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM) and was used to evaluate the reliability of the publication bias assessment.

Statistical analysis

Review Manager (version 5.3) and R statistical software were utilized for the statistical analysis, with p < 0.05 as the threshold of significance. The mean-variance estimation method by Luo et al. [20] and Wan et al. [21] was employed to estimate means and standard deviations from median and interquartile ranges in some studies. For continuous data with standard deviation, meta-analysis calculated the weighted mean difference (WMD) with 95% CIs using the inverse variance method. When comparing dichotomous data, the odds ratio (OR) was calculated using the Mantel-Haenszel method. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistics and Q test. Studies with I2 < 50% and p >0.05 were considered minimally heterogeneous, using a fixed-effects model. If I2 >50% or p < 0.05, indicating high heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. Subgroup analysis was conducted to explore the sources of heterogeneity. Occurrences of complications were determined using R statistical software. Other results were presented descriptively.

Results

Literature search and quality assessment

A total of 1056 studies were identified in the initial search, and 15 articles were finally included for analysis (Fig. 1). There are 6 comparative studies, 4 prospective cohort and 5 retrospective cohort studies. The median MINORS scores of the two reviewers were 23 (range: 23–24) and 24 (range: 22–24) for comparative studies and 14 (range: 13–16) and 15 (range: 12–16) for noncomparative studies, respectively. All cohort studies had a mean follow-up time of more than 5 years. The characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 2.

Patient characteristics

A total of 463 knees in 461 ACL-D patients with medial OA and knee varus alignment were included. Males were more likely to develop ACL-D with knee varus than females (75% [321/428] vs. 25% [107/428]). The mean age of patients ranged from 30.0 to 52.3 years. The pre-operative average mechanical femoral tibial angle (mFTA) ranged from 171.5° to 177°. The mFTA was defined as the angle formed between the mechanical axis of the femur and that of the tibia. The mechanical axis of the femur was defined as the line joining the center of the femoral head and the intercondylar notch of the femur. The mechanical axis of the tibia was defined as the line connecting the center of the proximal tibia and the center of the tibial plafond. An mFTA < 180° indicates varus alignment, and a value > 180° indicates valgus alignment.

Surgical indication and technique

The surgical indications for simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO in the included studies were not identical. The commonly accepted indications included ACL-D associated with medial OA with knee varus, resulting from medial meniscus insufficiency or previous medial meniscus resection. ACL injury with constitutional knee varus is not an indication for simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO. However, 5 studies included only mild to moderate medial OA patients [22–26], whereas the other 4 studies also included severe OA patients [27–30], with the whole proportion of severe OA as 8.9% (35/392). As for the condition of the medial meniscus, the incidence of medial meniscus insufficiency from 10 relative studies was as high as 74% (185/250). Eight studies included revision ACLR cases, and the proportion was 31.2% (62/199), while the other 4 studies included only primary ACLR cases (Table 3).

Patient-reported outcomes

Five different PROMs were utilized among the included studies: the IKDC subjective score (4 studies), Lysholm score (3 studies), and Tegner activity scale (5 studies). The pooled analysis showed that the IKDC subjective score (weighted mean: 51.8 vs. 69.7; WMD = − 17.9; p < 0.00001), Lysholm score (weighted mean: 59.8 vs. 87.3; WMD = − 27.5; p < 0.00001), and Tegner activity scale (weighted mean: 3.5 vs. 4.8; WMD = − 1.3; p < 0.00001) improved significantly post-operatively, compared with the pre-operative scores (Fig. 2).

Radiological outcomes

The data of pre-operative and post-operative mFTA were reported in nine studies, and the pooled analysis showed the pre-operative and post-operative mFTA range from 171.5° to 177° (weighted mean: 174.6°) and from 180.8° to 185° (weighted mean: 182.0°), respectively. The difference was statistically significant (WMD = -7.4°; p < 0.00001). Six studies reported the peri-operative PTS angle changes and the pooled analysis showed that the pre-operative and post-operative PTS angle ranged from 5.17° to 11° (weighted mean: 7.7°) and from 5.2° to 10.2° (weighted mean: 7.8°), with no significant differences between the two statuses (WMD = -0.13; p = 0.58). In addition, the pooled analysis of five articles found that the patellar height after the concomitant ACLR with HTO procedure did not change significantly compared with that of pre-operative (Fig. 3).

Clinical examinations

As for the ATT, three studies [22, 24, 31] reported their results using TELOS at 150 N, the ATT at 20–30° knee flexion ranged from 7.45 mm to 9.90 mm pre-operatively and from 1.12 mm to 3.80 mm post-operatively, decreased significantly (weighted mean: 8.33 vs. 3.12; WMD = 5.21; p < 0.00001) after simultaneous ACLR with valgus producing HTO surgery. Another two studies [24, 27] evaluated ATT by the radiological single leg stance method, ranging from 6.3 mm to 6.8 mm pre-operatively and from 3.2 mm to 3.4 mm post-operatively, also decreased significantly (weighted mean: 6.52 vs. 3.31; WMD = 3.20; p < 0.0001) after the one stage ACLR with valgus producing HTO surgery. Four studies reported the rate of residual pivot-shift before and after surgery [22, 24, 27, 30]. The pooled analysis showed that the rate of residual pivot shift grade significantly decreased after surgery (108/119 [90.7%] vs. 29/119[24%], OR = 32, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4).

Return to sports and IKDC objective score

Four studies reported the proportion of return to sports at the pre-injury level [24, 25, 28, 32], ranging from 31.43% to 50%, with an overall pooled rate of 31.8% (95% CI = 22.6%–41.1%) (Fig. 5).

Complications

A total of nine studies reported the incidence of perioperative complications in 438 procedures. The major complication rate of included studies ranged from 0 to 75% with an overall pooled risk of 11.2% (95% CI = 4.9%–25.2%) (Fig. 6). The most common complication is knee stiffness in the short term after surgery (stiffness/adverse cases: 8/35 [22.9%]) [33]. Two studies [24, 29] respectively reported 1 case of ACL graft failure and 1 case of revision ACLR, resulting in an overall ACLR failure rate of 0.46% (2/438). The other two studies [23, 24] reported 1 delayed union and 1 non-union of the osteotomy sites. The additional complication events include 1 severe OA requiring TKA, 1 patellar baja, 1 extension deficit, and 1 flexion contracture [24, 27, 29, 30].

Simultaneous ACLR with HTO versus HTO alone

Five studies compared the PROMs, objective ATT, radiological OA changes, and the incidence of post-operative complications between the concomitant ACL-R with valgus producing HTO and isolated HTO surgery [31, 33–36]. The pooled analysis showed that the combined procedure group had a significantly higher Lysholm score than that of the HTO alone group (weighted mean: 72.4 vs. 78.7; WMD = −6.3; p = 0.02) (Fig. 7a). Regarding ATT measurement, the pooled analysis showed the ATT at 20–30° knee flexion ranged from 2.22 mm to 2.30 mm in combined procedure and from 2.30 mm to 4.32 mm in HTO alone (weighted mean: 3.50 vs. 2.26; WMD = 1.24; p = 0.19), but with no statistical significance (Fig. 7b). As for the radiological evaluation of OA progression, two studies [31, 34] reported the results with K-L grading scores. The pooled analysis showed the increase of K-L score ranged from 0.35 to 0.61 in the combined procedure and from 0.26 to 0.49 in HTO alone (weighted mean: 0.47 vs. 0.32; WMD = -0.15; p = 0.12), with no statistical significance (Fig. 7c). Meta-analysis of 3 comparative studies suggested that the complication rates of concomitant ACLR with HTO had no significant difference compared with that of HTO alone (10/37[27%] vs. 8/34[23.5%]; OR = 0.87; p = 0.93; Heterogeneity: I2 = 61%, P = 0.08) (Fig. 7d).

Discussion

The most important findings of this study were that the simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO could achieve improvement of clinical outcomes in terms of PROMs, knee stability, and return to pre-injury level. The indication of this procedure is physically active ACL-D patients with knee varus resulting from medial compartment OA and/or medial meniscus insufficiency, rather than ACL injury with constitutional knee varus. Compared with HTO alone, the combined procedure improved knee function, but did not reduce the incidence of radiological OA progression or complication risk.

The simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO procedure was first reported by O’Neil and James [37] as a salvage surgery in the 1980s, for young patients with chronic ACL-D, all of whom had previous medial meniscectomy. The results suggested that all ten patients, with an average age of 41 years, had relief of instability and decreased pain, but only one patient returned to full activity. Noyes et al. [2] introduced gait analysis to elucidate the mechanism of this combined procedure and found that varus alignment in conjunction with medial compartment OA, cartilage damage, or loss of medial meniscus could increase the adduction moment during walking and HTO could decrease the adductor moment and be a potential solution for ACL-D patients with varus malalignment, deriving from medial cartilage abrasion or medial meniscus deficiency. Marriott et al. proposed from their serial research [26, 35] that one-stage HTO and ACLR could result in substantial changes in gait biomechanics with a substantial decrease in the KAM in the surgical limb and a slight increase in the nonsurgical limb from pre-operatively to 5 years post-operatively. From this systematic review, most of the authors agreed that the surgical indication of the simultaneous ACLR and valgus HTO surgery should be ACL-D patients with knee varus derived from medial OA cartilage wear and/or medial meniscus insufficiency, rather than ACL injury with constitutional varus knee. Moreover, some authors [15, 24, 27–31] insisted that HTO with ACLR, one stage or two stage, should be indicated only to those patients with giving-way symptoms or having a high demand to return to a high level of sports, to alleviate the concern about the high rate of complications.

On the other hand, Won et al. [38] designed a level III case-control study and reported that the revision ACLR group, compared with the primary ACLR group, had more frequent varus malalignment in terms of the proportion of knees with more varus mechanical tibiofemoral angle than 5°. The conclusion was that revision ACLR surgery may be a reasonable candidate for concurrent HTO to address concomitant alignment and OA issues in the medial compartment. Nevertheless, Bonin et al. [27] excluded constitutional varus cases without medial OA or medial meniscus deficiency from their study, while Schneider et al. [24] did not include constitutional varus even larger than 6° as an indication for combined ACLR with HTO. Based on these publications, we conclude that simultaneous ACLR and HTO should be considered as a kind of salvage procedure for physically active young ACL-D patients with varus malalignment, deriving from medial compartment OA or previous medial meniscectomy, especially for revision ACL cases. An ACL injury with constitutional knee varus and no medial OA should not be indicated to undergo simultaneous ACLR with HTO.

As for the necessity of plus ACLR to valgus HTO, the meta-analysis suggested that simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO demonstrated better Lysholm score and no significant difference in terms of complication rate compared with that of HTO alone. However, in terms of post-op OA progression and radiological measurement of ATT, the additional ACLR to valgus HTO showed no advantage in comparison with HTO alone. In 1993, Noyes et al. [15] reported that HTO with ACLR group could result in better anterior and rotatory stability than HTO alone, but the post-op clinical score showed no significant difference. The conclusion drawn from the study was that HTO alone could be enough for some ACL-D with medial OA and varus knee patients. Nevertheless, Williams et al. [36] published a comparative study between HTO alone and one-stage valgus HTO plus ACLR in 2003, with encouraging results about significant improvement of Lysholm knee score and Tegner activity score of combined surgery compared with that of the HTO alone group. Therefore, the advantage of additional ACLR to valgus HTO over HTO alone remains controversial and needs further high-level investigations to elucidate.

The target al.ignment of HTO is controversial. Some authors proposed using the WBL ratio (%) [16, 29, 31], which was defined as the percentage of the WBL crossing point of the proximal tibial plateau with the medial tibial edge as 0% and lateral as 100%, and set the target WBL ratio at 62% tibial plateau to slightly valgus alignment. While Schneider et al. [24] put the post-op target to 0–3° of valgus, despite the WBL ratio. Kim et al. [31] suggested target WBL ratio should be 55% to avoid valgus overcorrection in simultaneous HTO with the ACLR group. Moreover, chondral resurfacing [29] and meniscus allograft transplantation (MAT) [25] procedures were also reported to be added to concomitant HTO plus ACLR surgery, making the WBL ratio from 68% to 55% and more approaching the physiological coronal alignment of the lower limb [25, 39]. Whatsoever, the mean post-operative HKA of the included studies was 182° (180.8°-185°), without any overcorrection to unacceptable valgus. Wang et al. [40] demonstrated, from a biomechanical cadaver study, that joint line obliquity (JLO) change of 4° could cause significant mediolateral tibiofemoral subluxation during weight bearing, which could alter contact areas and meniscal loading. Clinically, poor outcomes were more evident in over-corrected MPTA (>95.2°) than in mildly under-corrected or properly corrected MPTA (< 95.2°) in MOW-HTO [41]. In the ESSKA Formal Consensus about osteotomy around the painful degenerative knee, Dawson et al. recommended WBL be set between 50% and 68% in an individualized way. The lower range of corrections would make future partial or total knee arthroplasty more feasible [42].

The impact of changes in sagittal force alignment after HTO has also received attention. Arun et al. [32] reported that decreasing the tibial slope >5° from pre-operation could achieve a more favorable effect on ACLR graft and better clinical outcome than that of decreasing the tibial slope < 5°. This supported that, when performing the valgus HTO, decreasing the PTS at the sagittal plane is beneficial to the ACLR graft. Similarly, Imhoff et al. [43] suggested that combined varus and tibial slope modification might result in ATT decreasing in both ACL-D and ACLR knees. The pooled analysis of this study showed that the pre-op PTS angle ranged from 5.17° to 11° (weighted mean: 7.79°) and post-op from 5.2° to 10.2° (weighted mean: 8.12°), with no significant statistical differences (WMD = -0.33; p = 0.28). Another parameter about the sagittal plane alignment is patellar height. Although it seems to be widely accepted that LCW-HTO tends to increase patellar height while MOW-HTO decreases patellar height, Ollivier et al. [44] recommended in the recently published ESSKA consensus that this appears negligible for routine corrections. From the pooled analysis performed in this study, both the Insall-Salvati ratios (weighted mean: 1.06 vs. 0.96; WMD = 0.10; p = 0.40) and the CD index (weighted mean: 0.98 vs. 0.95; WMD = 0.03; p = 0.63) showed no statistically significant changes after simultaneous ACLR with HTO.

Whether adding ACLR to valgus HTO would help to improve return to sports performance has always been a focus. Some authors reported that ACL injury in athelete would result in quadriceps force fluctuation and lowered functional performance [45], while both dry land and aquatic rehabilitation after ACLR could be helpful equally [46, 47]. In this study, cohort studies showed the rate of returning to pre-injury level of sports activity ranged from 31.43% to 50%, with an overall pooled rate of 31.8% (95% CI = 22.6%–41.1%). Although post-op Tegner activity score (TAS) improved significantly than pre-op score (weighted mean: 3.36 vs. 4.73; WMD = − 1.37; p < 0.00001), post-op TAS could not return to the pre-injury level (weighted mean: 5.8 vs. 4.6; WMD = 1.12; p = 0.003), In the 1990s, Dejour et al. [48] and Neuschwander et al. [49] proposed that return to the high level of activity was unrealistic after combined ACLR with valgus HTO surgery from their clinical study. Nowadays, clinical evidence only supports that simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO could achieve a predictable return to recreational sports [25, 31, 50]. Since the lack of reliable data from the comparative studies included in this study, the comparative advantages of one-stage ACLR plus HTO over HTO alone in terms of return to pre-injury level in competitive sports could not be confirmed.

The potential risk of long-term OA progression and complications, like stiffness, ACL graft failure, and osteotomy site delay union or nonunion, from additional ACLR to HTO has also been of concern. Firstly, Agostinone et al. [23] reported the percentage of IKDC radiologic medial compartment C and D osteoarthritis grade was 59% (19/32) before combined HTO with ACLR operation, 75% (24/32) at mean 6.5 years mid-term follow-up (p = 0.02, compared with pre-operative) and 87% (20/23) at mean 14.3 years final long-term follow-up (p = 0.12, compared with mid-term follow-up). The changes might be attributed to the process of natural OA progression. D’Ambrosi et al. [51] reported that ACLR could result in mild OA compared with contralateral healthy knees in the long term in most of the patients. Furthermore, additional ALC procedures to ACLR would reduce the failure rate without increasing the number of complications at the mid-term follow-up [52]. The meta-analysis in this study suggested that the K-L grading score changes between the HTO alone group and the simultaneous HTO plus ACLR group (weighted mean: 0.47 vs. 0.32; WMD = -0.15; p = 0.12) had no significant difference. Besides, among the complications in 438 procedures, the overall pooled risk was 13.4% (95% CI = 4.9%–25.2%) and 25.7% (9/35) of the complications were knee stiffness in the short term after surgery, followed by ACL graft failure 11.4% (4/35) and surgical site infection or hyperesthesia 11.4% (4/35). There was a 5.7% (2/35) extension deficit and 2.9% (1/35) flexion contracture, patellar baja, HTO site delay union, nonunion, DVT, and OA progression to TKA, respectively. This relatively high incidence of stiffness might be from non-rigid internal fixation to HTO and additional post-operative cast immobilization, which was quite common in studies performed before the year 2000. The survival rate after 10 years of follow-up reached to 90% and 75% in patients after both open and closed wedge osteotomy without significant OA progression [42]. Therefore, simultaneous HTO plus ACLR procedure is a safe option for young ACL-D patients with varus malalignment from mild to moderate medial OA and/or medial meniscus insufficiency.

This study has limitations. First, the 15 included studies have a long period from 1995 to 2023. The technique, rationale, and surgical volumes of both osteotomy and ACLR have undergone significant evolution and updates during these 28 years, resulting in great heterogeneity when analyzing data about clinical outcomes and complication rates. For example, the IKDC score used in studies published before the year 2000 was the IKDC 1993 version [53], while recently studies have adopted the IKDC 2006 version [54]. Likewise, the internal fixation device of HTO was staples or non-locking plates, and ACLR was performed in open or mini-open ways before the year 2000, while recent studies have used arthroscopic ACLR and rigid locking plates in HTO surgery. All the above have an influence on rehabilitation protocol, complication rates, and clinical outcomes. Second, the number of comparative studies included in this study was limited, and the score system for clinical outcomes varied greatly among these studies. This might make it difficult to reach persuasive conclusions about the advantages of one-stage HTO combined with ACLR in comparison to HTO alone, which needs future comparative studies with a high level of evidence to elucidate.

Conclusion

This mid- to long-term follow-up results showed that the simultaneous ACLR with valgus HTO could achieve improvement of clinical outcomes in terms of PROMs, predictable restoration of knee stability, and a high proportion of return to pre-injury level of recreational rather than competitive sports. The indication of this procedure is physically active ACL-D patients with knee varus resulting from medial compartment OA and/or medial meniscus insufficiency, rather than ACL injury with constitutional knee varus. Compared with HTO alone, concomitant ACLR with HTO showed significantly better Lysholm knee score, no higher risk of radiological OA progression, or complication rates.
