Reduction of zinc and iron in dust removal ash by H2 generated from lignite pyrolysis
Authors: Zihan Wang, Jianfeng Dong, Jing Guo, Hanjie Guo, Bin Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe article describes a process based on the hydrogen recovery of dust removal ash from lignite pyrolysis. The high volatile components in lignite will produce a large amount of H2 during the pyrolysis process, which can replace part of C to reduce zinc and iron oxides in dust removal ash, thereby achieving the goal of reducing carbon consumption. Two methods were used to study the different pyrolysis behaviors of lignite, and it was found that the peak of H2 release was reached at 700 °C. After 1000 °C, the pyrolysis of lignite basically ended, and the final pyrolysis gases were mainly H2, CO, CO2, CH4, and a small amount of CxHy. In addition, the proportion of H2 generated by the full pyrolysis of lignite at 1000 °C in H2–H2O is 84.4 %, and the proportion of CO in the CO–CO2 system is 74 %, which can meet the reduction of zinc and iron oxides in dust removal ash. Based on the principle of minimum free energy and laboratory experiments, zinc oxide is completely reduced to zinc vapor above 906 °C, while the Fe obtained from iron oxide reduction increases with the increase of H2/CO in the system above 810 °C, further confirming the feasibility of this process. The effects of temperature, lignite added amount and holding time on iron reduction and zinc volatilization during the reduction process of dust removal ash was investigated. The results indicate that due to the fast diffusion rate of H2, the reaction rate is greatly increased, which can quickly increase the metallization rate of dust removal ash to over 95 % and the dezincification rate also increases from 19.60 % to about 98.76 % with the increase of carbon content under low temperature (1000 °C) and low carbon content (10 wt %) conditions.Keywords: Coal pyrolysis, Coal based hydrogen reduction, High-temperature roasting, Waste recovery, Minimum free energy

Introduction

During the production and operation of a steel companies, a large amount of dust removal ash (DRA) is produced, including: sinter DRA, blast furnace DRA, converter DRA, electric furnace DRA and so on. Because of the high iron and zinc content in DRA, it has a high recycling value [1,2]. However, the recycling of DRA will further enrich the Zn and affect the quality of subsequent products [3]. As the global production of pig iron continues to increase, the large amount of DRA puts a huge pressure on the environment and the sustainable development of the global economy [4]. Therefore, the separation and recovery of iron and zinc has important practical significance for the multi-purpose and harmless treatment of DRA. The traditional methods for treating dust containing zinc are wet metallurgy and pyrometallurgy [5,6]. In wet metallurgy, the wet process is sensitive to raw materials and the process is difficult to optimize for large-scale use [7]. Therefore, the current treatment of zinc-containing metallurgical dust is mainly focused on pyroprocessing. The direct reduction method consisting of rotary kiln method and rotary bottom furnace method has been widely used in many steel companies in the world for the treatment of DRA. However, there are still disadvantages such as low volatility of zinc and lead, high energy consumption, difficulty in recovering iron, and a large amount of toxic heavy metal residue [8,9].

So far, there have been few research reports on the smelting and reduction of DRA, and most of them require pelletizing, which is a complex process [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]], and it is important to note in particular that little attention has been paid to the recovery of iron. N. Nargin et al. showed [15] that the optimum temperature for reduction of oxide pellets using lignite was 1000 °C with 93 % metallization. Guo et al. [16] studied the hydrogen reduction of Zn-containing dust pellets and showed that conditions such as reduction temperature, time, and H2 flow rate had a significant effect on pellet dezincification. Li et al. [17] studied in order to recover the valuable metals from the electric furnace dust. The optimum melting conditions were determined as temperature of 1450 °C, sodium carbonate addition of 4 %, C/O of 0.8, holding time of 30 min, and alkalinity of 0.8. Mamdouh [18] studied the method of microwave heating to simultaneously recover zinc from steelmaking dust and blast furnace sludge, and found that the mixture with high Calcium oxide content and low iron oxide content had the best zinc removal effect.

In previous studies, it was found that hydrogen has good thermal conductivity, low density and low viscosity, and an appropriate increase in the proportion of hydrogen in the reducing gas has great potential to improve the reducing kinetic conditions and promote the reduction of iron oxides [18]. Considering that it is difficult to meet the conditions of direct H2 reduction in domestic steel plants nowadays, coal with high volatile content can be an excellent reducing agent for dust ash reduction by pyrolysis to produce H2 and CO. This process of using high volatile coal pyrolysis to produce H2 to participate in reduction is known as coal-based hydrogen metallurgy. In this study, lignite with high volatile matter was selected to reduce the DRA powder by using the H2 and CO produced by its pyrolysis together. The effects of reduction temperature, reduction time, and carbon allocation on the dezincification rate and iron metallization rate were investigated, and the optimal operating parameters were determined. With the minimum free energy principle and experimental results, a possible reaction mechanism was proposed.

Experimental methods

In order to recover iron and zinc oxides from DRA, a high-temperature roasting method was used for treatment. The experiment was conducted in a muffle furnace, and the experimental device is shown in Fig. 2. Before the experiment starts, the dried lignite and DRA are mixed evenly according to the set carbon content (10 wt%, 15 wt%, 20 wt%) and placed in an alumina crucible. To ensure that the experimental sample will not be oxidized during cooling, the alumina crucible containing the experimental sample is placed inside the graphite crucible. The small hole in the upper part of the graphite crucible is used to balance the pressure inside and outside the crucible during the heating process. The experiment was conducted under isothermal conditions. After the muffle furnace reached the expected temperature (1000 °C, 1100 °C, 1200 °C), the reactor was placed in the furnace and kept warm for 5, 10, and 20 min. After the high-temperature roasting reduction is completed, the sample cooled to room temperature (25 °C) is taken out, and the chemical composition of the reduced sample is analyzed using XRF. At the same time, the TFe and MFe amounts of the reduced sample are determined by chemical titration according to GB/T 6730.6-1986, in order to determine the zinc removal rate and iron metallization rate of the reduced sample.

The process of recovering dust is to recover valuable metals such as Zn and Fe, and the dezincification rate and metallization rate are different indicators for zinc and iron, respectively. Because the boiling point of zinc is only 906 °C, when the reduction temperature is above 906 °C, the zinc in the sample will be reduced in the form of gas. The reduced zinc vapor will leave the system, so the dezincification rate is used as an indicator to characterize zinc reduction. The higher the dezincification rate, the more thorough the reduction of zinc in the system. The reduction of iron follows the rule of Fe2O3 → Fe3O4 → FeO → Fe, and regardless of the temperature range set in the paper, the reduction products exist in solid form. The final reduction product is commonly known as sponge iron. Therefore, the higher the metallization rate of iron, the higher the degree of reduction of iron in the system.

Phase analysis of the reduced samples was performed using X-ray diffraction (XRD). Zeiss ULTRA + field emission Scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) was used to analyze the microstructure and elements in the micro area.

Two methods were used for experimental detection of lignite pyrolysis, with the difference being the different detection methods for pyrolysis gases. Method one is to keep the lignite sample at the final temperature for a certain period of time and collect all gases for subsequent mass spectrometry analysis. Method two is to use mass spectrometry to detect the composition and proportion of the collected gases in real-time. The specific experimental process is as follows:

Method 1: Heat a dry coal sample with a particle size less than 0.15 mm to 1200 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under air isolation conditions, and maintain at this temperature for 30 min. Collect pyrolysis products and calculate the yields of coke, tar, and pyrolysis gas.

Method 2: The lignite with particle size less than 0.15 mm was loaded in an Al2O3 fixed bed and purged with argon gas for 5 min at room temperature with a gas flow rate of 300 ml/min, and then increased from room temperature to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The gas produced by pyrolysis was condensed in an ice-water and dry ice bath and then entered the mass spectrometer, where the intensity of gas production was recorded simultaneously. Pyrolysis tests were performed using a Thermo plus EV2/thermo mass photo under Ar atmosphere.

Besides, took 20g of lignite with a particle size less than 100 mesh (0.15 mm) and placed it in a 105 °C oven. Weighed it every 1 h until the weight of the lignite remained unchanged. The weight loss ratio of lignite before and after the experiment was the moisture content of the lignite.

H2 is a key elemental component as far as the pyrolysis reaction is concerned. H2 production starts in large quantities at 300 °C, with a rapid increase in instantaneous production, reaching a peak rate at 700 °C, and then the release is completed by 1000 °C. The production of H2 is a result of the aromatic structure condensation of coal molecules and therefore occurs mainly in the secondary pyrolysis stage [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. It can be seen that due to the high volatile content of lignite, a large amount of H2 is generated during the warming process, which lays the foundation for the subsequent hydrogen reduction.

It can be seen that at temperatures below the boiling point of metallic zinc, 906 °C, Fe can be reduced within the system and the final reduction product of zinc oxide is ZnO. When the temperature is greater than 906 °C, Zn is reduced in the form of gas. With the increase of temperature, the reduction history of ZnFe2O4 with H2 is ZnFe2O4→Fe3O4 + ZnO→FeO + ZnO→ Fe + ZnO→Fe + Zn(g), and Zn also starts to be reduced by H2 in the form of gas only when the temperature is higher than 906 °C. At the temperature T < 827 °C, Fe2O3 is preferentially reduced by CO, and when the temperature T > 827 °C, the reduction of H2 dominates at the same temperature conditions. From Tables 3 and it can be seen that at 1000 °C, the percentages of H2 and CO at full pyrolysis of lignite are 84.4 % and 74.0 %, respectively, which are labeled with those in Fig. 6(c) and (d). ZnO will not be reduced by CO produced after pyrolysis, but can be reduced to Zn by H2. In addition, in the reduction of Fe2O3, the molar ratios of gases produced by lignite pyrolysis are all higher than the equilibrium line of FeO–Fe in Fig. 6(d), which possesses the ability to reduce FeO to Fe. Establishment of thermodynamic coupling model

The key to the coal-based hydrogen metallurgical reduction process is the H2O and CO2 contained in the coal pyrolysis gas. Under the action of coal pyrolytic carbon, gas circulation or self-reforming can be realized, which can be transformed into H2 and CO through water gas reaction and carbon fusion loss reaction, providing a continuous source of reduction potential energy. It can be seen that in the coal-based hydrogen metallurgy reduction system, the coal pyrolysis process and the DRA reduction process produce a coupling effect, which can produce sufficient amount of reducing gas under the action of pyrolysis carbon and achieve a continuous cycle of H2 and CO to provide a continuous reduction potential energy [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a coupled thermodynamic model of lignite coal-based hydrogen reduction.

Using the principle of minimum free energy to establish a coupled thermodynamic model, it is assumed that in a multiphase closed system, the total free energy is minimum when the system reaches thermodynamic equilibrium. There are seven initial phases, which are ZnFe2O4, ZnO, Fe2O3, C, H2, H2O and CH4. The amount of pyrolysis products obtained from lignite pyrolysis can be obtained by giving the masses of initial DRA and lignite, and then the total amount of each element in the system can be determined, and finally the content of each group element at equilibrium is calculated. Results of high temperature roasting reduction experiments

Effect of reduction temperature

Temperature is an important thermodynamic factor to control the chemical reaction. Fig. 11 shows the effect of adding 20 % lignite and holding it for 5 min at different temperatures (1000 °C, 1100 °C, 1200 °C) on the reduction of DRA. Among them, Fig. 11(a) shows the changes of dezincification rate and metallization rate of DRA. The experimental results show that the dezincification rate increases from 25.66 % to 98.76 % when the temperature increases from 1000 °C to 1200 °C, and the dezincification rate increases rapidly when the temperature increases from 1000 °C to 1100 °C. When the carbon content is 20 % and the reduction time is 5 min, the metallization rate of the sample increases from 59.48 % to 98.23 % with the increase of temperature.

Changes of DRA properties with temperature; (a) metallization rate and dezincification rate; (b) XRD pattern.

According to the analysis in Fig. 4, it can be seen that the release of H2 in lignite tends to end at 1000 °C. When hydrogen is involved in the reduction reaction, the diffusion ability of hydrogen is 3.74 times higher than that of CO, which makes the transfer of hydrogen to the reaction interface through the diffusion of macro-microporosity inside the sample faster and the reduction rate of hydrogen reaction is also faster. From Fig. 6(c), it is known that the higher the temperature, the stronger the ability of H2 to reduce iron oxides, and the greater the metallization and dezincification rates of the reduced samples.

The physical phase analysis of the reduced sample was performed using XRD, and the results are shown in Fig. 11(b). The main physical phases in the reduced sample are found to be FeO and metallic Fe. The diffraction peak of FeO gradually decreases with the increase of temperature, and the peak of FeO disappears at 1200 °C and almost all of it is transformed into the peak of Fe. The absence of Zn is due to the fact that ZnFe2O4 in the DRA is almost completely reduced to Zn vapor, which escapes as a gas.

Effect of reduction time

Fig. 12 shows the effect of reduction time on the DRA properties at 1000 °C and lignite addition of 10 wt %. From Fig. 12(a), it can be seen that the dezincification rate increases rapidly from 18.60 % to 86.75 % as the reduction time increases from 5 min to 10 min, but the metallization rate of Fe decreases with the increase of reduction time from 95.21 % to 54.15 %, and the XRD pattern of Fig. 12(b) supports this statement. The diffraction peaks of Fe are strongest at a holding time of 5 min. With the increase of reduction time, the diffraction peaks of Fe gradually decrease and the diffraction peaks of FeO gradually increase. This indicates that the increase of reduction time at low temperature will promote the reduction of Zn and depress the formation of Fe at the same time. It is known that the pyrolysis of lignite tends to end at 1000 °C, when the amount of H2 in the system reaches its peak. Combined with Fig. 6(c), it can be seen that Fe2O3 will be reduced to Fe at 1000 °C when φCO＞59.45%. As the reaction time increases, the amount of H2 in the system gradually decreases and the amount of CO increases, at which time the reduction product of Fe2O3 changes to FeO. Therefore, under low temperature conditions, the metallization rate of Fe decreases with the increase of reaction time when the amount of lignite added is certain.

Changes of DRA properties with reduction time; (a) metallization rate and dezincification rate at 1000 °C; (b) XRD pattern of (a); (c) metallization rate and dezincification rate at 1200 °C.

As shown in Fig. 12(c), when the reduction temperature is 1200 °C and the lignite addition is 10 wt %, the rate of iron metallization and dezincification increases with the increase of reaction time. With the increase of the reduction reaction temperature, the amount of CO in the system increases. When the temperature increases, both H2 and CO will participate in the reduction of iron oxide, while the increase in temperature will also increase the reduction rate of iron oxide and the reduction of reduced iron. At 1200 °C, the Dezincification rate can reach 98.12 %, which can obtain high-quality iron-containing raw materials. This indicates that high temperature roasting reduction can effectively remove metallic zinc from the DRA.

Effect of lignite addition

Fig. 13 shows the effect of the amount of lignite added on the properties of DRA after insulation at 1000 °C and 1200 °C for 5 min. From Fig. 13(a), it can be seen that the dezincification rate increases from 18.60 % to 25.66 % and the metallization rate decreases from 95.21 % to 59.48 % with the increase of lignite addition from 10 wt% to 20 wt%. According to the XRD pattern of Fig. 13(b), it can be found that the intensity of the diffraction peak of Fe gradually decreases and that of FeO increases with the increase of the carbon allocation. It indicates that under low temperature conditions, an increase in the amount of lignite is beneficial for the removal of zinc. However, due to the short reaction time, the increase in zinc removal rate is very small. In addition, the decrease in metallization rate indicates that an increase in the amount of lignite will inhibit the reduction of Fe.

At the same time, the presence of H2 can also lower the reaction temperature and reduce energy consumption. Conclusions

Through theoretical calculations and experimental studies, a treatment method for the recovery of Fe and Zn from DRA using lignite pyrolysis separation was explored, and the following main conclusions were drawn.

1.The main phases in the DRA are ZnFe2O4 and Fe2O3. The main components of lignite pyrolysis gas at 1000 °C are H2, CO, CO2, CH4 and a small part of hydrocarbons. The reducing gases H2 and CO produced by sufficient pyrolysis of lignite can meet the reduction conditions of ZnFe2O4 and Fe2O3. ZnFe2O4 can be reduced to ZnO at any temperature, and the starting temperature of ZnO reduction is 906 °C. At the same temperature, H2 reduces ZnO more easily than CO.

ZnFe2O4 can be reduced to ZnO at any temperature, and the starting temperature of ZnO reduction is 906 °C. At the same temperature, H2 reduces ZnO more easily than CO.

2.The coupled thermodynamic model of lignite reduction of dusty ash using the principle of minimum free energy indicates that when the system reaches equilibrium, the content of the product Fe in the system increases with the total amount of gas. As long as the boiling point of Zn is reached and the reducing gas is sufficient, the Zn oxide can be completely reduced to Zn vapor. During the reduction process, the phase of Fe becomes ZnFe2O4→Fe2O3→Fe3O4→FeO→Fe, and the phase of ZnFe2O4 becomes ZnFe2O4→ZnO→Zn(g).
