Growing pains: adult subspecialist perspectives on improving transition from paediatric to adult care for youth with complex health conditions
Authors: Sarah Malecki, Chioma Nwebube, Julia Orkin, Ilana Halperin, Naba Khan, Tomisin John, Hans Katzberg, Amanda Steiman, Rano Matta, Tarek Abdelhalim, Danielle M Andrade, Natasha Bollegala, Shaelynne Bush, Clodagh Ryan, Kimberly Colapinto, Eleni Dimaraki, Hanna Faghfoury, Jennifer Fu, Natasha Gakhal, Kate Gent, Chana Korenblum, Perla Lansang, Iliana C Lega, Mary Jane Lim-Fat, Jessica Liu, Susan Miranda, Geetha Mukerji, Christian Pagnoux, Eryn Vandepoele, Ophir Vinik, Karla Wentzel, Darryl Yates, Eyal Cohen, Alene Toulany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransition from paediatric to adult healthcare is particularly challenging for youth and their caregivers with complex health conditions, in part due to loss of paediatric supports and fragmentation of care, contributing to adverse outcomes. To date, transition research and policy has been largely driven by paediatric providers. Adult provider insights are critical to inform gaps in care, transition planning and enable innovative and sustainable interventions.ObjectiveTo describe adult subspecialist perspectives on barriers, facilitators and opportunities to improve the transition from paediatric to adult care for youth with complex health conditions.MethodsAn adult subspecialty advisory panel was convened as part of a quality improvement initiative, with physicians from the University of Toronto’s Department of Medicine who participated in three virtual meetings through nomination and stakeholder mapping. The first meeting focused on identifying current challenges and opportunities in transition to adult care; the second generated recommendations based on experiential knowledge and the third reviewed case studies to identify gaps and potential solutions. Discussions were recorded, transcribed and analysed using thematic qualitative analysis.ResultsAdult subspecialists identified five key areas for improvement: (1) preparing patients and families for the realities of adult care; (2) bridging communication gaps between paediatric and adult systems; (3) moving beyond age-based transition toward flexible, readiness-informed care; (4) strengthening collaboration among adult providers caring for young adults; and (5) addressing structural and infrastructure barriers within adult care systems.ConclusionAdult care physicians expressed and validated that young adults have unique care needs during their transition to adult care. They highlighted the need for stronger cross-system collaboration, resources and investment to ensure effective transition. These findings highlight barriers in implementing current paediatric-led transition standards and provide a foundation for system-level, disease-agnostic approaches to improve continuity of care across the paediatric–adult divide.Keywords: Transition to Adult Care, Complexity, Adolescent, Chronic disease management

Introduction

Children with complex chronic health conditions (long-term conditions affecting multiple organ systems/requiring subspecialty care and high healthcare utilisation) are on the rise and increasingly surviving to adulthood,1 2 leading to an emerging group of young adults with complex healthcare needs. While transitioning from paediatric to adult care is known to be a challenging process for most, those with chronic health conditions are particularly vulnerable due to the loss of paediatric supports and fragmentation in care, leading to increased risk for poor outcomes, including care delays, medical complications and increased hospitalisation rates.3–7

Paediatricians have spearheaded recent recommendations to improve transition to adult care (TAC) and have held an important seat at the table in developing national guidelines and provincial quality standards for TAC.7–9 However, the paediatric focus of most transition advocacy, research and interventions to date10–12 has created challenges in implementing these standards in the adult healthcare system, as well as gaps in engagement, responsibility-sharing and resources across sectors. Differences in care models between paediatric and adult healthcare further complicate implementation, particularly for youth with complex conditions.13

Adult care physicians play a critical role in the transition process, delivering initial and ongoing care necessary for successful ‘landing’ and transition success.9 14 15 Their insights are thus critical to inform gaps in care, transition planning and enable innovative and sustainable interventions. Prior work summarising adult care physician perspectives is relatively sparse16 and generally not focused on complex health conditions,17–19 or has been condition-specific.20 This quality improvement (QI) initiative aimed to address this gap by synthesising adult subspecialist perspectives on improving the TAC for youth with complex health conditions in a multi-institutional adult care setting and to describe future directions.

Methods

Adult subspecialty advisory panel and recruitment

This study was conducted as a QI initiative within the TAC programme at The Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids). The TAC programme was developed by paediatricians and allied paediatric healthcare providers to support youth and their caregivers at highest risk for poor outcomes.13 21 SickKids is Canada’s largest children’s hospital providing emerging state of the art care for complex rare diseases. The academic hospital serves as a hub for tertiary and quaternary paediatric care in Toronto up until age 18 when transfer to adult care is mandated. Disease-agnostic multidisciplinary complex care models have evolved to address unmet care needs for children with complex health conditions in recent years.22 In contrast to the model at SickKids, adult subspecialty care in Toronto is often delivered at multiple hospitals across the city and no adult equivalent to a complex care programme currently exists. Although strides are being made within certain subspecialties,23 most transition programmes are disease-specific (eg, vasculitis, sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis, diabetes)10 24 25 with limited resources, infrastructure and funding, creating inequities in meeting the needs of this population.26

In 2024, an adult subspecialist advisory panel was developed to understand adult physician perspectives on gaps and barriers in TAC more generally and to inform sustainable implementation strategies in transitioning to adult subspecialty services.13 Adult care physician panel members were recruited from the University of Toronto’s Department of Medicine using purposive sampling techniques and nomination process. The Department of Medicine Chair compiled a list of practicing subspecialists with interest or experience in transition work who then, in turn, were invited to include other experts in their network. Each panel meeting was structured to build on key themes and address both individual and system-wide challenges related to the TAC.

Meetings

Three panel meetings were conducted virtually between January 2024 and January 2025 and were facilitated by members of the QI team affiliated with the SickKids TAC programme. Each meeting was co-led by two paediatric and one adult physician to support cross-system perspectives. The first meeting was introductory with the aim of assessing the current state around transition challenges and opportunities for improvement. During the second meeting, the panel formulated recommendations for improving TAC based on experiential knowledge. In the final meeting, the panel reviewed sample case studies to identify potential gaps and areas for healthcare advancement.

Meeting synthesis and thematic generation

Detailed meeting notes were taken for the first meeting and subsequent panel meetings were recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service. All meeting notes and transcripts were uploaded to the qualitative data analysis software NVivo V.1527 for analysis.

A thematic qualitative analysis was conducted to analyse the discussion points,28 focusing on adult physician perspectives across the three meetings as a whole. Thematic analysis was chosen as an analytical approach because it provides a flexible and accessible method for identifying, synthesising and interpreting patterns across qualitative data while allowing themes to be generated without reliance on a specific theoretical framework.29 The transcripts were coded independently by two coders (CN and NK, medical student and researcher) who were not involved in the panel meetings, with each coder initially familiarising themselves with the data, then individually coding the data. The codes generated were compared and used to co-develop a preliminary codebook. This codebook was then applied to the data and refined iteratively throughout the coding process, with input from SMa, a general internal medicine specialist involved in the panel meetings, and other members of the panel. After coding, members of the research team individually constructed themes. These themes were then discussed with the research team and used to reach alignment and consensus on the final themes.

Institutional approval was obtained through a QI review process by the Quality Management Department at SickKids and was not subject to a Research Ethics Board review. Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) reporting guidelines30 were followed.

Findings

Demographics

There were 38 attendees overall across the three meetings, with numbers of attendees varying at each meeting. Panel members included hospital leaders and clinicians. A diverse range of subspecialties was represented among the 24 adult physicians, including general internal medicine, neurology, gastroenterology, endocrinology, rheumatology, dermatology, and genetics.

Themes

Theme 1: preparing patients and families for the realities of adult care

Adult care physicians expressed that it was the responsibility of the paediatric providers to equip patients and families with the resources necessary for the TAC. Physicians identified that self-management documents prepared by the paediatric care team and carried by the patient/family across diverse care settings (eg, on their phone) describing typical signs of acute exacerbations of chronic conditions, suggested management and care team contacts would be helpful, especially those with rare, paediatric-onset conditions that adult providers are less familiar with. Physicians noted difficulty in initial interactions and relationship building with patients and families related to prior negative experiences with the healthcare system and negative perceptions about adult care. These perceptions were primarily due to a lack of resources (eg, a comprehensive clinic hub with dedicated allied health) to support equivalent care that they received in paediatrics. This can result in challenges with appointment attendance and firm attachment to adult care. Physicians felt that adequate preparation about what to expect in adult care and choosing a provider sensitive to the unique needs of this patient population were important steps to improve this.

Theme 2: bridging communication gaps between paediatric and adult systems

The importance of continued collaboration between paediatricians and adult care physicians was emphasised by adult care physicians. They proposed a comprehensive transfer document, with information relevant to the receiving physician, that would provide a summary of the patient’s medical history. This document, alongside the proposed self-management documents, would serve the purpose of furthering an adult physician’s understanding of a rare disease process and allowing them to provide adequate care to that patient. Physicians were in support of anticipatory care planning and having paediatricians identify care centres that were best positioned to meet a patient’s needs prior to transfer. They also discussed the benefits of coordinating early referrals while patients are still in paediatric care, as it would facilitate a smoother transition. However, participants acknowledged adult physicians may be better to refer in some cases due to their knowledge and experience as well as changing patient priorities.

Theme 3: moving beyond age-based transition toward flexible, readiness-informed care

Adult care physicians believed that they have a responsibility to advocate for policy-level change to support youth with complex chronic conditions transitioning to adult care. They stressed the uncertainty that occurs when discussing what constitutes the beginning and end of adult care transition. Physicians felt that having 18 years old as the fixed age that signifies the end of paediatric care is not patient-centred. They advised that having a flexible and tailored approach would better support those with complex conditions who require more engagement with healthcare services. Some adult physicians thought that paediatric hospitals should be able to provide care to patients who are over 18 years old in some cases. Many felt that because paediatricians have increased knowledge of certain rare, paediatric-onset conditions, they are more equipped and better resourced to provide quality care to these patients.

TAC was identified as an ongoing process that should be dictated by a patient’s/family’s readiness. As such, adult physicians expressed a desire to clearly identify metrics that signify a successful transition. This would allow for physician clarity regarding when transition-related supports are no longer required. There was additional interest in implementing healthcare guidelines that outline the standard of care when transitioning patients to adult care. Specifically, physicians identified the need for this standard of care to further address the ongoing needs of those with rare and complex medical conditions.

Theme 4: strengthening collaboration among adult physicians caring for young adults

Many adult care physicians emphasised the responsibility of paediatric and adult healthcare clinicians in collaborating with one another when coordinating care for youth with complex chronic conditions transitioning to adult care. Adult physicians discussed the difficulty in identifying specialists who are both interested and equipped to care for youth with complex chronic conditions. Many rely on their own personal networks, recognising the difficulty branching out to adult specialists who may be better suited to care for a patient’s needs. It was noted that both adult physicians and patients face struggles when primary care is not accessible during this time. If primary care was provided by a community paediatrician before the age of 18, there may be difficulty with attachment to adult primary care, or the lack of longitudinal relationship in new attachment with primary care may make care coordination more challenging. There was interest in the development of a network of adult healthcare professionals, including physicians and other allied healthcare providers, who are willing and able to support these youth. Acknowledging that not every patient requires access to quaternary care, adult physicians additionally recommended leveraging connections and creating pathways with community physicians who are able to provide care that is more accessible to patients.

Theme 5: structural and infrastructure barriers within adult care systems

Adult care physicians discussed the inadequacies in the adult care infrastructure, particularly in supporting youth with rare, complex conditions. They noted that in many instances, TAC is associated with decreased capacity to meet these patients’ needs. For example, adult physicians described instances where their patients lost access and funding for complex care treatments on entering the adult system. Adult physicians also expressed that a lack of interdisciplinary supports made it difficult to address all determinants of a patient’s health. Adult physicians were unclear about how and where they could provide holistic care given the unanimous acknowledgement of the lack of infrastructure and funding to support coordinated multidisciplinary programmes to match supports offered in paediatrics. There was overall a positive response to the idea of shared disease-agnostic resources offered by the TAC programme. However, discussions focused on the challenges of sharing these resources among diverse subspecialists without a local or generalist ‘quarterback’, and concerns regarding gaps once these limited paediatric resources ran out. Structural problems and resource constraints emerged as a main topic of conversation in later meetings, with discussions about designing new care pathways, including generalist-led adult complex care models in tertiary hospitals with sustainable financial support.

Discussion

This QI initiative provides novel insights into adult subspecialist perspectives on supporting youth with complex health conditions during TAC. Across five key themes, participants highlighted gaps in preparation, communication, accountability and system infrastructure, underscoring the need for coordinated, cross-system approaches to transition.

Adult care physicians suggested it was the responsibility of the paediatric care team to prepare patients and families for transition, provide detailed self-management and transition documents and attempt to identify physicians with interest and expertise relevant to the patient’s ongoing care needs. However, there was acknowledgement that both paediatricians and adult physicians may share responsibility in identifying adult physicians with interest and expertise in this population. There was uncertainty in what age might be appropriate to transfer care to the adult physician, and a request for paediatricians to be more flexible in the age of transition extending beyond age 18. Adult care physicians recognised the importance of increasing collaboration between paediatric and adult care, as well as between adult physicians in the care of these patients. Adult physicians felt that new disease-agnostic multidisciplinary care models (eg, with nurse navigators, social workers and others) may help overcome some of the structural barriers and resource constraints in providing care to youth with complex chronic conditions transitioning to adult care, but more institutional support will be required.

Many of the perspectives raised by adult care physicians in our panel align with findings from the limited prior transition research conducted in other jurisdictions, including studies examining transitions broadly17 or within specific disease groups, such as type 1 diabetes.20 Similar to our panel meetings, conversations were focused on steps paediatricians can take to improve the process. This suggests that the perspectives of the adult physicians outlined here are generalisable to different healthcare contexts. We found that discussions related to where and how these patients can be supported by adult care physicians were less action-oriented, reflecting the lack of current infrastructure, funding and solutions to make sustainable change. Echoing a recent call to action from organisations supporting youth with disabilities,31 our work also suggests that adult care physicians, with government support, need to feel empowered to take responsibility in crafting sustainable solutions across different conditions. Concerns raised by the specialists in our panel echo the same concerns of internists over a decade ago in the USA18 and neurologists globally24 but as the population of complex young adults continues to grow, calls to action from paediatricians and families have begun to change the political climate and significant momentum is building.

Importantly, our findings extend beyond prior literature by identifying system-level barriers within adult care that limit implementation of existing transition recommendations and quality standards.9 For example, our themes of preparing for the realities of adult care, bridging communication gaps, increasing flexibility between paediatric and adult systems and strengthening collaboration between adult physicians align with opportunities to achieve the quality standards of transition readiness, information-sharing and planned and coordinated transitions.9 While previous work has emphasised paediatric-led strategies, our results highlight the need for greater engagement, ownership and capacity-building within adult healthcare systems to achieve sustainable improvements.

In response to gaps identified through this initiative, the TAC programme has implemented several changes within paediatric care, including earlier identification of transition needs, more explicit expectation-setting with patients/youth and families, development of self-management tools, proactive referral to adult subspecialists and primary care and clearer pathways to community versus tertiary services. Importantly, ongoing collaboration with adult medicine partners is underway to co-design and test new care pathways that extend beyond the paediatric system and support continuity into adulthood. In developing local solutions, leveraging aspects of successful existing programmes in future Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles will be essential. For example, although an adult complex care programme does not currently exist in Canada,10 the Transitional and Lifelong Care programme in London, Ontario begins at age 14 years and provides disease-agnostic multidisciplinary care through a rehabilitation lens well into adulthood.10 32 33 Incorporating insights from other adult care physicians often involved in the care of these patients, for example, family doctors, surgeons and psychiatrists, will also be important. Primary care providers offer generalist perspectives that apply across a broad range of diseases34 35 and often provide care in outpatient/community settings. This care should be integrated with that provided by adult specialists in hospitals, especially when adult care is spread across many specialties and institutions.

Strengths and limitations

This QI initiative used qualitative methods to synthesise adult physician perspectives on gaps in care for youth with complex health conditions, as part of a broader body of work focused on transition at a tertiary paediatric centre in Toronto, Ontario, Canada. Our focus on adult healthcare physicians in this context helps fill an important gap in the literature. The themes we identified have led to new areas of focus and solutions that are currently in the design and testing phase. There are also limitations. Findings, including perspectives, are specific to the local context, and although the literature suggests consistency with other sources, the results may not be broadly generalisable. The panel was drawn from a group of interested physicians at academic healthcare institutions in Toronto, limiting transferability to other settings, for example, non-academic or community based, where interests, resources, incentives and care models differ. To limit the number of meeting attendees and focus the conversations, predefined meeting agendas were set, which may have limited the discussion. Contribution from attendees was uneven, leading to some voices being represented more strongly in the results. Only adult specialists from the department of medicine were included in this panel. Not all disciplines involved in transition care were represented (ie, primary care, surgeons, psychiatrists, interdisciplinary members and community based providers). As a result, the findings and solutions may have been different if patients and other care providers were included in these meetings, instead of in separate discussions.13

Conclusion

The perspectives shared by adult care physicians through this subspecialty advisory panel represent a critical contribution to advancing more coordinated, equitable and sustainable transition approaches for young adults with paediatric-onset complex conditions. As the prevalence and survival into adulthood continues to increase for youth with complex health needs, physicians, and healthcare systems need to be better equipped to respond to conditions that have historically been managed within paediatrics. Strengthening adult physician comfort, capacity, collaboration and support, particularly in the care of rare and complex diseases, will be essential to ensuring continuity and quality of care across the lifespan. Achieving this will require intentional investment in adult-system infrastructure and personnel to create medical homes for these younger adults with unique needs amidst ever-increasing age-related complexity. Appropriate physician compensation and shared accountability across institutions and policymakers will also be essential.

Together, these findings highlight the urgent need for cross-system, disease-agnostic models of transition care that extend beyond paediatric settings and support young adults during a period of heightened vulnerability. Meaningful progress will depend on sustained partnership between paediatric and adult physicians and allied health professionals, health system leaders and government stakeholders to ensure that youth with complex healthcare needs do not experience avoidable gaps in care and poor outcomes as they enter adulthood.
