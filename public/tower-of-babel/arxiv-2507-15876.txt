Re-evaluating Short- and Long-Term Trend Factors in CTA Replication: A Bayesian Graphical Approach
Eric Benhamou, Jean-Jacques Ohana, Alban Etienne, Béatrice Guez, Ethan Setrouk, Thomas Jacquot

Abstract
Commodity Trading Advisors (CTAs) have historically relied on trend‑following rules that operate on vastly different horizons—from long‑term breakouts that capture major directional moves to short‑term momentum signals that thrive in fast‑moving markets. Despite a large body of work on trend following, the relative merits and interactions of short- versus long‑term trend systems remain controversial. This paper adds to the debate by (i) dynamically decomposing CTA returns into short‑term trend , long‑term trend and market beta factors using a Bayesian graphical model, and (ii) showing how the blend of horizons shapes the strategy’s risk‑adjusted performance.

Introduction & Motivation

Professional trend followers range from traditional long-term 500-day breakout systems to intraday futures scalpers. Academic work likewise presents mixed conclusions about the effectiveness of trend-following strategies and the optimal trend horizons. For instance,
(
Moskowitz et al. 2012
;
Baltas and Kosowski 2013
)
document sizable profits from 1–12‑month trends, whereas
(
Jegadeesh et al. 2022
)
highlight the fragility of ultra-short signals, and
Clenow 2023
advocates for very short term trends. Other industry studies emphasize the benefit of
multi‑horizon
mixes
(
Baz et al. 2015
;
Baltas and Kosowski 2020
)
. Practitioner commentary reflects both the current challenges these strategies face in regaining past success
(
Kilburn 2024b
;
Tzotchev 2018
)
and the promise of shorter-term approaches for delivering more dynamic and convex return profiles
(
Kilburn 2024a
)
. This perspective is reinforced by the 2025 “tariff test” episode, during which a new variant—dubbed the risk-off CTA—demonstrated its ability to mitigate drawdowns amid sharp market reversals
(
Bartholomew 2025
)
. Thought leaders like Cliff Asness also stress the inherently statistical nature of trend-following and its ongoing integration with advanced AI techniques
(
Asness 2024
)
. Despite these contributions, a unified quantitative framework for blending horizons remains undeveloped. To address this gap, we investigate four questions:

1.

Can CTA-style strategies be replicated using only liquid futures?

2.

How do short- and long-term trend factor risk–returns compare over the past decade?

3.

Does mixing horizons materially improve drawdowns and diversification?

4.

Are short-term trends valuable or should we rely on longer trend horizons?

Key Contributions and Innovation.

This study makes
two
novel contributions to the CTA-replication literature:

(i)

We introduce a

that

.

(ii)

We document how a
short-term-trend
+
raw-beta sleeve
outperforms classic multi-month breakouts on both Sharpe and drawdown efficiency over 2010–2025—even after realistic transaction and roll costs.

Literature Review

Classical trend following.

Post-crisis evidence continues to affirm the economic and statistical significance of
3–12-month
time-series momentum.
Using large cross-asset panels,
Lempérière et al. 2014
,
Baz et al. 2015
and
Baltas and Kosowski 2020
document Sharpe ratios that are broadly comparable to the pre-2008 period.

Short-term momentum and reversals.

The economics of very short-horizon signals are far less stable.

Narayan et al. 2015
show that daily momentum mean-reverts strongly once micro-structure frictions are accounted for, while
Boons and Prado 2019
and
Jegadeesh et al. 2022
attribute much of the apparent weekly alpha to bid–ask bounce and dealer-inventory effects.
Adaptive filters that down-weight periods of elevated noise partly mitigate—but do not eliminate—this performance drag.

CTA replication and factor models.

Responding to fee pressure, a growing strand of work evaluates cost-effective CTA replication.
Linear factor mimickers (like in
Braun et al. 2024
), or Kalman filter replication (like in
Benhamou 2018b
;
Benhamou 2018a
) achieve correlations of
0.70
–
0.80
to the
SG Trend
index while retaining most downside protection, implying that investors can capture the bulk of the systematic-trend payoff at a fraction of traditional management and incentive fees.

Bayesian and machine-learning trend decomposition.

Recent studies frame trend signals within explicitly time-varying statistical structures.
Bayesian state-space decompositions and deep neural networks (
Gu et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2019
) uncover latent regime shifts in both signal strength and asset-class exposure.
These approaches highlight that the trend premium is not static but oscillates with macro-volatility and liquidity conditions, opening the door to dynamic allocation across “trend regimes” rather than binary on/off timing rules.

While the post‑2015 literature recognises the value of mixing horizons, few papers explicitly decompose CTA returns into distinct short‑ and long‑term trend factors—leaving the debate unresolved.

Trend Factor Design: Lookback Straddles

Inspired by the payout symmetry of a
lookback straddle
—a combination of a lookback call and put that captures the
ex‑post
maximum excursion of the underlying—we engineer horizon‑specific trend factors by replicating the
delta
of a lookback straddle across multiple windows as detailed in
Fung and Hsieh 2001
.

For each contract price series
S_{t}
and window length
n
(in days) we define the running maximum, running minimum and volatility:

\displaystyle M_{t,n}

\displaystyle=\max_{t-n+1\leq j\leq t}S_{j},

(1)

\displaystyle m_{t,n}

\displaystyle=\min_{t-n+1\leq j\leq t}S_{j},

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=t-n+1}^{t}\Bigl[\ln\!\bigl(S_{j}/S_{j-1}\bigr)\Bigr]^{2},

(3)

Let
\Phi(\cdot)
denote the standard normal CDF. The
lookback‑straddle trend score
for horizon
n
is then

T_{t,n}\;=\;\Phi\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\ln(S_{t}/m_{t,n})}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)-\Phi\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\ln(M_{t,n}/S_{t})}{\sigma_{n}\sqrt{n}}\Bigr).

(4)

This score is
convex
in extreme up or down moves, generating positively skewed payoffs reminiscent of managed‑futures “crisis alpha.”

Short- vs. Long‑Horizon Sets.

We implement two buckets of windows:

•

Short‑Term (ST):

n\in\{10,\,20,\,40,\,60\}
. These capture fast breakouts and reversals.

•

Long‑Term (LT):

n=500
. This captures the classic two-year long term trend followers.

For each
n
we compute
T_{t,n}
via (
4
).
The composite trend factor is simply the mean across horizons:

F^{\text{Trend}}_{t}\;=\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}T_{t,n},

(5)

so that every look-back window contributes equally.

Complementary Market‑Beta Factor.

Beyond the trend scores, we retain the
raw daily return
for each contract,
r_{t}^{m}
, which will act as the market‑beta factor in Eq. (
17
). Together,
\{T_{t,n}\}_{n\in\mathcal{N}}
and
r_{t}^{m}
span both directional momentum and baseline market exposure, enabling the Bayesian model to disentangle pure trend alpha from beta‑like moves.

4
Theoretical Foundations

This section lays out the key analytical results in two stages:
(i) we prove that the look-back–straddle trend score
T_{t,n}
equals the delta of a fixed-window look-back straddle under geometric Brownian motion, providing an option-pricing–based drift filter;
(ii) we derive an explicit formula for the absolute Sharpe ratio of any portfolio that mixes short- and long-horizon trend factors, isolate the diversification term, and obtain a closed-form optimal weight that clarifies when short-term momentum should be added and confirms that the allocation should lean more heavily toward the long-term trend factor.

4.1
Look-Back–Straddle Delta as a Drift Filter

Assume that the log-price follow a geometric Brownian motion

\mathrm{d}X_{t}\;=\;\mu\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x,

(6)

with constant drift
\mu
and volatility
\sigma
.
For any fixed window of length
n
define the running maximum and minimum as in equations (
1
) and (
2
) and the
fixed-window look-back straddle
maturing at
t
whose payoff is:

L_{t,n}\;=\;M_{t}-m_{t}.

(7)

Throughout this subsection we work under the risk-neutral measure
(
\mu\!=\!0
) when pricing, and return to the physical measure when we
interpret the result as a drift filter.

Delta of the straddle.

Under the risk-neutral measure, the fixed-window look-back straddle is valued as the discounted conditional expectation
V(s,X_{s},M_{s},m_{s})=e^{-r(t-s)}\mathbb{E}[\,M_{t}-m_{t}\,|\,X_{s},M_{s},m_{s}]
; a closed-form expression is provided by
Goldman et al. 1979
;
Conze and Viswanathan 1991
). Differentiating that price with respect to the current log-price and letting
s\rightarrow t
yields the delta

\Delta_{t,n}=\Phi\!\Bigl(\tfrac{X_{t}-m_{t}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\Bigr)-\Phi\!\Bigl(\tfrac{X_{t}-M_{t}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\Bigr),

(8)

which coincides exactly with our trend score
T_{t,n}
, thereby interpreting the score as an option-pricing–based drift filter.

Equality with the trend score.

Equation (
4
) of the main text defines the
look-back–straddle
trend score

T_{t,n}\;=\;\Phi\!\bigl(z_{t}^{+}\bigr)-\Phi\!\bigl(z_{t}^{-}\bigr),\;z_{t}^{\pm}:=\tfrac{X_{t}-m_{t}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}},\;\tfrac{X_{t}-M_{t}}{\sigma\sqrt{n}},

which is identical to the right-hand side of (
8
).
Hence

T_{t,n}=\Delta_{t,n},

(9)

establishing that the proposed trend score is precisely the
delta

of a fixed-window look-back straddle.

Interpretation as a drift filter.

Because the delta of any option captures the instantaneous change in its
fair value per unit change in the underlying,
T_{t,n}
inherits two key
properties:

(a)

Scale-free sensitivity.
Through the

z
-scores
z_{t}^{\pm}
the filter rescales distances to the
running extrema by the diffusion scale
\sigma\sqrt{n}
, making
it comparable across assets and horizons.

(b)

Directional signal.
When
X_{t}
is near the upper
(lower) boundary,
T_{t,n}\!\approx\!+1
(
-1
), signalling a
strong upward (downward) drift over the past
n
days, whereas a
value near
0
indicates sideways price action.

Thus the look-back–straddle trend score functions as an
option-pricing–based, horizon-specific drift detector whose magnitude is
directly linked to the expected P&L of a dynamically delta-hedged
straddle over the window
n
.

4.2
Sharpe–Ratio Decomposition

Let
F^{\mathrm{ST}}
and
F^{\mathrm{LT}}
be the short– and long–horizon factors and assume that there are correlated:

\rho\;=\;\operatorname{corr}\bigl(F^{\mathrm{ST}},F^{\mathrm{LT}}\bigr).

(10)

We form a one–unit–budget portfolio

P\;=\;\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\,F^{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}\,F^{\mathrm{LT}},\qquad\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1.

(11)

Denote
\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[F^{i}]
and

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}
, the first two moments of the two short– and long–horizon factors. Then we can compute
S(\omega)
, the
absolute
Sharpe ratio (i.e. the Sharpe ratio assuming a zero risk-free rate, equivalently the ratio of expected return to volatility), as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[P]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(P)}}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}}{\sqrt{\omega^{\top}\Sigma\,\omega}}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}}{\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}^{2}+2\,\rho\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}}}.

(13)

Using
\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
one checks that

\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}^{2}=1-2\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}},

(14)

so that

\operatorname{Var}(P)=1-2\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}+2\,\rho\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1+2(\rho-1)\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}},

(15)

i.e. the familiar
2(\rho-1)\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}
variance‐reduction from diversification.

Proposition 1
.

The weight
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
that maximizes the absolute Sharpe ratio (as we do not subtract in the numerator the risk free rate) of the portfolio denoted by
\mathcal{S}(P)
, subject to the budget constraint
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1
, is given by

\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*}=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\rho\,\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}}{(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})(1-\rho)},

(16)

Proof.

Differentiating
\mathcal{S}(P)
with respect to
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
and enforcing
\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
yields the closed‐form optimum. The detailed derivation is given in Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 1

(Short- vs. Long-Term Trends)
.

The Sharpe-maximizing weight is strictly positive whenever
\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}>\rho\,\mu_{\mathrm{LT}},
. Moreover, if
\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}
and
\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}
are equal, then
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*}=50\%
, implying equal weighting of the short- and long-term trend factors.

Application 1
.

Using Proposition
1
with the sample moments from Table
3
gives

\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*}\approx 17\%,\hskip 56.9055pt\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}^{*}\approx 83\%,

confirming that one should allocate a larger weight (almost five times i.e.
83\div 17
) to the Long-Term than to the Short-Term trend factor.

Methodology

We model the daily excess return of the SG CTA benchmark,
r_{t}^{\text{CTA}}
, as a
market-by-market
mixture of three horizon-specific factors:

\displaystyle r_{t}^{\text{CTA}}\!=\!\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\Bigl(

\displaystyle\beta_{t,m}^{\text{ST}}f_{t,m}^{\text{ST}}\!+\!\beta_{t,m}^{\text{LT}}f_{t,m}^{\text{LT}}\!+\!\beta_{t,m}^{\text{MKT}}r_{t}^{m}\Bigr)\!+\!\varepsilon_{t},

(17)

\displaystyle(\beta_{t,m}^{\cdot}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\beta_{t-1,m}^{\cdot},\,\sigma_{\beta}^{2}\bigr),\,\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}).

(18)

Here
m
indexes the 24 liquid futures in the investment universe—spanning equity indices, government bond futures, major currency pairs, and key commodity contracts— and each
\beta_{t,m}^{\cdot}
is a
time‑varying, contract‑specific
loading. The factors for a given market
m
are constructed as follows:

•

f_{t,m}^{\text{ST}}
— short-term trend score of market
m
(look-back straddle, horizons 10, 20, 40 and 60-day window),

•

f_{t,m}^{\text{LT}}
— long-term trend score of market
m
(500-day window),

•

r_{t}^{m}
— raw daily return of market
m
(captures market beta).

This formulation separates
cross-asset allocation
(over
m
) from
horizon allocation
(ST vs. LT vs. Market), letting the Bayesian filter infer both dimensions simultaneously.

5.1
Graphical Model Primer

Graphical models provide a transparent representation of conditional dependencies among variables, encoding them as nodes (random variables) and edges (probabilistic links). Our dynamic factor model can be written as a time‑unrolled Bayesian network (Figure
1
), with two node layers per date: latent
states
(factor weights) and observable
emissions
(CTA returns).

To illustrate the machinery, consider the more granular asset‑class version used for live daily decoding. Let
\widehat{\text{NAV}}_{t}
be the estimated net‑asset value on day
t
and
r^{k}_{t}
the return of asset class
k\in\{\text{Eq},\text{Fx},\text{Bd},\text{Co}\}
, where
Eq
denotes an Equity Index Future,
Bd
a Bond Future,
Fx
a Currency Future, and
Co
a Commodity Future. Denote their time‑
t
weights by
w^{k}_{t}
. The generative relationship is

\widehat{\text{NAV}}_{t}=\widehat{\text{NAV}}_{t-1}\Bigl(1+\sum_{k}w^{k}_{t-1}r^{k}_{t}\Bigr),

(19)

mirroring a one‑step portfolio update in continuous compounding. The weights follow a first‑order Gaussian random walk
w^{k}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(w^{k}_{t-1},\sigma_{w}^{2})
and are
a‑priori
correlated across asset classes, enabling interaction effects beyond a Kalman filter’s diagonal covariance.

Inference pipeline.

Daily estimation proceeds via message‑passing: (i) a prediction step propagates the weight distribution forward, (ii) a correction step conditions on the new NAV observation, and (iii) backward smoothing refines the entire path. This scheme is analogous to a Kalman filter but leverages full asset‑interaction structure.

1.

State‑Space Representation:
latent weights
w^{k}_{t}
constitute the hidden state; NAV returns are emissions.

2.

Dynamic Inference:
FFBS or particle‑filtering yields the posterior
p(w_{1:T}\mid\text{NAV}_{1:T})
.

3.

Interaction Modeling:
joint Gaussian priors across
k
capture cross‑asset spill‑overs often ignored by independent factor models.

4.

Online Updating:
as new prices arrive, the entire posterior is updated recursively, ensuring rapid adaptation.

The graphical‑model framework thus unifies factor replication and style‑drift tracking in one coherent probabilistic engine—an approach long standard in speech recognition
(
Murphy 2012
)
yet still under‑exploited in finance.

5.2
Bayesian Filter as a Sparse Kalman Upgrade

Setting
\Sigma_{\beta}=\sigma_{\beta}^{2}I
and dropping the cross-asset prior
correlations in Section 5 collapses our state evolution to a diagonal‐covariance
Gaussian random walk—
exactly
the standard Kalman filter used in

Harvey 1990
;
Harvey and Shephard 1993
. Retaining the full covariance matrix while placing
a sparsity-inducing Horseshoe prior on off-diagonal elements
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
) lets the data decide which cross-horizon or
cross-asset interactions are worth tracking, yielding a filter that nests the
Kalman as a special case yet requires only
\mathcal{O}(K^{2})
rather than

\mathcal{O}(K^{3})
operations thanks to block-diagonal structure.

Take-away.

The analytical results underpin the empirical findings: (i) the look-back
delta naturally filters drift; (ii) Sharpe improvements stem from both timing

and
convexity; (iii) the Bayesian network offers a computationally
tractable, theoretically sound generalization of classical linear filters.

6.2
Cost Framework (summary)

We model three layers of implementation cost:

(a)

Transaction cost (Tx. Cost).
Round-turn execution expense that bundles bid–ask, brokerage, exchange and clearing fees plus a small slippage buffer; see Table
1
for contract-level figures.

(b)

Replication (roll) cost.
Systematic drag when the front-month future is rolled; measured as the 2000–2025 average front-to-next calendar spread.

(c)

Management fee.
Flat
0.5\%
per-annum charge on AUM.

A complete derivation of all-in costs—including leverage assumptions, per-year round-turn counts and the resulting 1.38–1.68 % p.a. range is provided in Appendix
C
.

6.3
The Various Strategies

In this part, we compare seven factor‐based portfolios plus the SG CTA Trend Index benchmark. Each strategy is built from our horizon‐specific lookback‐straddle scores with/or without the raw market returns factors and is identified by the following letter codes:

•

LTT
: The
Long‐Term Trend
factor averages lookback‐straddle deltas over a 500-day window to capture persistent directional moves, emulating classic two‐year breakout systems.

•

MKT
: The
Markets
factor aggregates the raw daily returns of all liquid futures contracts, serving as a pure market‐beta proxy without momentum filtering.

•

STT
: The
Short‐Term Trend
factor averages lookback‐straddle deltas across 10, 20, 40 and 60‐day horizons, targeting high‐frequency breakouts and rapid market shifts.

•

STT+LTT
: This
Multi‐Horizon Trend
(denoted by STT+LTT) factor combines short- and long-term trends on an equal‐weight basis to capture both fast and slow trend dynamics.

•

MKT+STT+LTT
: The
Markets + Multi‐Horizon Trend
factor allocates equally to raw market returns, short- and long-term trends, integrating baseline beta with diversified momentum drivers.

•

MKT+STT
: The
Markets + Short‐Term Trend
factor pairs raw returns with only the short‐horizon trend factor, emphasizing rapid trend capture alongside fundamental market exposure.

•

SGCTAT
: The
SG CTA Trend index
with ticker NEIXCTAT, is the industry benchmark for systematic trend-following. Maintained by Société Générale Prime Brokerage, it tracks the net performance of the 10 largest, diversified CTAs trading primarily futures, selected based on correlation to peers and daily return reporting. The index is equally weighted, rebalanced annually, and has been live since January 2000.

6.6
Key Take-Aways

•

Short-Term Trend (STT).
On its own, the STT factor underperforms (Sharpe = 0.20; Return/MaxDD = 0.26). Frequent whipsaws lead to “false starts,” so STT primarily adds diversification rather than standalone alpha.

•

Long-Term Trend (LTT).
The LTT factor improves risk-adjusted returns (Sharpe = 0.39) relative to STT but still lags mixed-horizon approaches.

•

Multi-Horizon Blend (STT+LTT).
Combining STT and LTT raises the Sharpe/MaxDD efficiency to 2.37, reflecting the asymmetric payoff benefits of mixing fast and slow trend signals.

•

MKT + STT.
Integrating raw market returns with the short-term trend factor yields the strongest performance: highest Sharpe (0.49), best Return/MaxDD (0.48), and shallowest max drawdown (14.9%).

Extended‑sample evidence.

•

Five‑year window (2020–2025).

The same sleeve maintains leadership with a Sharpe/MaxDD of 4.94 and a Return/MaxDD of 0.64, demonstrating that the short‑horizon/market‑beta blend is not a recent artefact but a persistent source of convex carry.

7
Evaluating strategies through performance and correlation

7.1
Motivation

When allocating to a replication strategy, investors generally weigh two key issues simultaneously:

(1)
How much profit does the strategy generate per unit of risk?
(2)
How closely does it track the benchmark it aims to replicate?

We measure the first aspect by the ratio

y=\mathrm{Return}/\mathrm{MaxDD}
.
It captures the capacity to create gains relative to the single worst loss an
investor would have endured.
The second aspect is simply the linear correlation

\rho
with the SG CTA Trend index, which we
also
wish to maximise,
because higher correlation means better replication quality.

Our goal is therefore to decide whether one strategy is
better
,

worse
or
equivalent
to another, given
the
(\rho,y)
-coordinates of each point on the chart.

7.2
Utility functions as a comparison tool

To aggregate the two desirable attributes in a single preference score we use
bivariate utility functions. Two classical forms suffice:

Cobb–Douglas
(first formulation, 1927).

For positive inputs
x_{1},x_{2}
the Cobb–Douglas utility is

U_{\text{CD}}(x_{1},x_{2})=A\,x_{1}^{\alpha}x_{2}^{1-\alpha}
with
0<\alpha<1
.
It is homothetic, exhibits unit elasticity of substitution and assigns a clear
economic meaning to
\alpha
: the relative weight of the first attribute.

CES.

The Constant-Elasticity-of-Substitution family generalises this idea to

U_{\text{CES}}(x_{1},x_{2})=\bigl(\alpha x_{1}^{\rho}+(1-\alpha)x_{2}^{\rho}\bigr)^{1/\rho}
,
where
\rho
tunes the elasticity

\sigma=1/(1-\rho)
; Cobb–Douglas is the limiting case
\rho\to 0
.

Because we only need a single, transparent parameter to pivot the trade-off,
the Cobb–Douglas specification

U(\rho,y)=\rho^{\alpha}y^{1-\alpha}

is retained for the rest of the analysis.

7.3
Iso-utility curves

Fixing a utility level
U
gives the indifference locus

U=\rho^{\alpha}y^{1-\alpha}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=U^{1/(1-\alpha)}\,\rho^{-\alpha/(1-\alpha)},

a smooth, strictly convex line in the
(\rho,y)
-plane. Moving
north-east across such a curve always raises utility.

7.4
Pareto-dominant points on the chart

Among the strategies displayed,
MKT+STT

(\rho=0.80,\,y=0.47)
and

MKT+STT+LTT

(\rho=0.85,\,y=0.38)

dominate all other candidates: no alternative offers a higher
correlation or a higher Return/Max-DD ratio (excluding the SGCTAT benchmark, which by definition has a correlation of 1 with itself). These two points therefore lie
on the
Pareto frontier
of the sample.

7.5
The
\alpha
that makes the two dominant strategies indifferent

Setting
U(\rho_{1},y_{1})=U(\rho_{2},y_{2})
for the pair above yields a single
solution

\alpha=\frac{\ln(y_{2}/y_{1})}{\ln(y_{2}/y_{1})+\ln(\rho_{1}/\rho_{2})}\simeq 0.78,\qquad U\simeq 0.71.

Hence one—and only one—Cobb–Douglas indifference curve passes through
both Pareto-dominant strategies.

Figure 2:
Cobb–Douglas indifference curve
(
\alpha=0.78,\;U\approx 0.71
)
that passes through the two Pareto-dominant strategies

MKT+STT
and
MKT+STT+LTT

in the
(\rho,\;\mathrm{Return}/\mathrm{MaxDD})
-plane.

In other words, the two strategies are indifferent only if roughly 78% of the utility weight is placed on correlation.

. As

Across all three previously mentioned specifications the

—would the

8
Conclusion

Framing Managed-Futures performance as a Bayesian state-space system gives a clear, daily-granular view of how
short-term trend
(STT),
long-term trend
(LTT) and
market beta
(MKT) combine to drive returns and allows us answering the four questions of the introduction. As a result, three insights emerge:

1.

Diversification value of STT.

Although STT is the component with the lowest standalone Sharpe, it is the least correlated with SGCTAT, and its convex payout profile dampens drawdowns after abrupt price shocks—behaving like a low-cost “insurance layer” against regime shifts.

2.

Power of horizon blending.

Mixing horizons is materially more efficient than choosing one.
A naïve 50/50 STT–LTT allocation raises the Sharpe/Max-DD efficiency ratio to
\,2.37
(vs.
1.34
for STT and
2.09
for LTT), translating into smoother equity curves and smaller peak-to-trough losses.

3.

MKT + STT dominates.

Pairing raw market returns with the short-term trend factor produces a point
(\rho=0.80,\;\text{Return}/\text{MaxDD}=0.47)
that lies on the empirical Pareto frontier as illustrated in subsection
7.6
.

Practical implications and future work.

Short-horizon trend signals can feel frustrating when they whipsaw, yet our results show that their asymmetry is exactly what counter-balances the more linear risk of long-horizon rules. In practice, the Bayesian filter gives portfolio managers a live read-out of how each horizon is contributing to return and drawdown. Future next steps are to let the model choose its own look-back windows, compare deep-learning trend factors, and extend the study to a wider set of futures.

Appendix A
Proof of the Optimum Weight Formula

We wish to maximise the absolute Sharpe ratio

\mathcal{S}(\omega)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[P]}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(P)}}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}}{\sqrt{\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}^{2}+2\,\rho\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}}}

(20)

subject to
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}+\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1
. Set

\omega_{\mathrm{LT}}=1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
, and define

N(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})=\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+(1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})\mu_{\mathrm{LT}},\quad D(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})=\sqrt{1+2(\rho-1)\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}(1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})},

(21)

so
\mathcal{S}(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})=N/D
.

Differentiating with respect to
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
gives

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}}{\partial\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}}=\frac{N^{\prime}(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})\,D(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})-N(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})\,D^{\prime}(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})}{D(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})^{2}},

(22)

where

N^{\prime}(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})=\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}},\quad D^{\prime}(\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})=\frac{(\rho-1)\,(1-2\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})}{\sqrt{1+2(\rho-1)\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}(1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})}}.

(23)

Setting the numerator to zero and solving yields

(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})\bigl[1+2(\rho-1)\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\,(1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})\bigr]=\bigl[\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\,\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+(1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})\,\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}\bigr](\rho-1)\,(1-2\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}).

(24)

or equivalently,

LHS

\displaystyle=(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})\bigl[1+2(\rho-1)\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}-2(\rho-1)\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}\bigr]

(25)

\displaystyle=(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})+2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}-2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2},

(26)

RHS

\displaystyle=\bigl[\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+(1-\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}\bigr](\rho-1)(1-2\omega_{\mathrm{ST}})

(27)

\displaystyle=(\rho-1)\,\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}+(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-3\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}-2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})\,\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}.

(28)

By matching coefficients of
1
,
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}
, and
\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{2}
, we obtain:

\begin{cases}\text{const. term:}&\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}=(\rho-1)\,\mu_{\mathrm{LT}},\\[5.0pt]
\text{linear term:}&2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})=(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-3\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}),\\[5.0pt]
\text{quadratic term:}&-2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})=-2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}).\end{cases}

(29)

The quadratic term cancels out and we are left with:

\underbrace{(\mu_{\rm ST}-\mu_{\rm LT})-(\rho-1)\,\mu_{\rm LT}}_{\text{constant part}}\;=\;\underbrace{(\rho-1)(\mu_{\rm ST}-3\mu_{\rm LT})-2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\rm ST}-\mu_{\rm LT})}_{\text{coefficient of }\omega_{\rm ST}}\;\omega_{\rm ST}.

(30)

Solve for
\omega_{\rm ST}
:

\omega_{\rm ST}=\frac{(\mu_{\rm ST}-\mu_{\rm LT})-(\rho-1)\,\mu_{\rm LT}}{(\rho-1)(\mu_{\rm ST}-3\mu_{\rm LT})-2(\rho-1)(\mu_{\rm ST}-\mu_{\rm LT})}=\frac{\mu_{\rm ST}-\rho\,\mu_{\rm LT}}{-(\rho-1)\,(\mu_{\rm ST}+\mu_{\rm LT})}.

(31)

Or equivalently, the unique maximizer

\omega_{\mathrm{ST}}^{*}=\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}-\rho\,\mu_{\mathrm{LT}}}{(\mu_{\mathrm{ST}}+\mu_{\mathrm{LT}})(1-\rho)},

(32)

which is exactly formula (
16
). ∎

Appendix C
Cost–Calculation Details

We provide here the cost framework used in
Section
6.2
. We move from
contract-level inputs
to
the
all-in portfolio drag
quoted in basis points (bp) of assets under
management (AUM) per annum.

C.1
Implementation Cost Components

C.1.1
Transaction Cost per Round-Turn

For each futures contract
i
the execution cost of a
round-turn

(open+close) is expressed in bp of notional:

C^{\text{Tx}}_{i}\;=\;\frac{\tfrac{\text{Bid–Ask Spread}}{2}\;+\;\text{Brokerage}\;+\;\text{Exchange \& Clearing Fees}\;+\;\text{Slippage Buffer}}{\text{Contract Notional}}\times 10^{4}.

•

Bid–Ask spread.
Daily median quoted half-spread (ticks) from
Bloomberg TOMS over 2000-2025.

•

Brokerage.
Volume-weighted average commission reported by
three tier-one FCMs.

•

Exchange/clearing fees.
Schedule as of 2025 Q1.

•

Slippage buffer.

0.5\,\sigma_{\text{1-min}}
where

\sigma_{\text{1-min}}
is the one-minute return volatility,
capturing impact in stressed tape.

Why a 2 bp round-turn cost?

We illustrate the calculation for the E-mini S&P 500 (ES) contract:

\displaystyle\text{Half-spread}

\displaystyle=\,0.25\,\text{pt}=\$12.50,

\displaystyle\text{Brokerage}

\displaystyle=\,\$1.18,

\displaystyle\text{Fees}

\displaystyle=\,\$0.85,

\displaystyle\text{Slippage buffer}

\displaystyle=\,\$3.00,

\displaystyle\text{Notional}

\displaystyle=\,\$50\times\underline{4\,800}=\$240\,000.

\displaystyle\therefore C^{\text{Tx}}_{\text{ES}}

\displaystyle=\frac{12.50+1.18+0.85+3.00}{240\,000}\times 10^{4}\approx\mathbf{0.75\;bp}.

Across all 24 contracts the
volume-weighted
average is

\bar{C}^{\text{Tx}}=2\,\text{bp}
per round-turn, matching
Table
1
. We therefore adopt

\bar{C}^{\text{Tx}}=2\,\text{bp}
as a single, all-in execution hair-cut to keep things simple, though quite realistic—if anything conservative.

C.1.2
Roll Cost per Contract

Replication drag arises when the front-month future
(F_{0})
is replaced by
the next-month
(F_{1})
. We proxy the carry over 2000-2025 by

C^{\text{Roll}}_{i}\;=\;\bigl[\ln(F_{1}/F_{0})\bigr]_{\text{avg}}\times 10^{4}\;\text{bp}.

Values cluster around
12\,\text{bp}
for rates and metals,
15\,\text{bp}

for equity indices and energies, and
2\,\text{bp}
for major currency pairs
(where the front-next basis is negligible).

C.1.3
Management Fee

We charge a flat

C^{\text{Mgmt}}=50\,\text{bp \; p.a.}

on AUM—roughly one-third of the classic “2%+20%” fee stack still common
among discretionary CTAs.

C.2
Putting the Numbers in Context

The strategies presented in the paper employ an average gross leverage of
4×
AUM and—based on empirical fills from our execution stack—generate between
20 and 35
round-turns per year, depending on the horizon.

•

Transaction cost drag
:
20\text{–}35\times 2\,\mathrm{bp}=40\text{–}70\,\mathrm{bp}
per year.

•

Roll drag
:
4\times 12\,\mathrm{bp}=48\,\mathrm{bp}
per year (on average).

•

Management fee
: fixed 0.50% p.a. (50 bp).

Combining these elements yields an
all-in
cost of

40+48+50=138\,\mathrm{bp}\quad\text{to}\quad 70+48+50=168\,\mathrm{bp},

i.e.
1.38% to 1.68%
per annum, reflecting the costs associated with transactions, roll drag, and moderate management fees in each tested strategy.
