Development of an interpretable machine learning model-based online tool for risk prediction of falls and fall-related injuries in Chinese middle-aged and older adults with depressive symptoms-a longitudinal study based on the CHARLS database
Authors: Mei Yuan, Siran Bi, Ziyi Chen, Xiaoli Fan, Xiyu Chen, Kun Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to establish and validate interpretable Machine Learning (ML) models for predicting falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults with depressive symptoms (DS) and to develop relevant online computational tools.MethodsUsing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) survey from 2015 to 2018, 32 predictor variables related to the risk of falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults with DS were included based on five dimensions of the health ecology model, and the important predictor variables were screened using Principal Component Analysis and LASSO regression at the same time. We further developed eight ML algorithms-Logistic Regression(LR), Support Vector Machine(SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Neural Network (NN), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost)-to construct the risk prediction model, and selected the best predictive variables based on grid search and 10-fold cross-validation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) was used for personalised interpretation of the models. In addition, we further performed stratified analyses by dividing participants into two age groups: 45–59 years and 60 years and older.ResultsAmong 3,664 middle-aged and older adults with DS, the incidence rate of falls and fall-related injuries after three years of follow-up was 20.36% and 8.92%, respectively. Among all models, LightGBM had the best performance. LightGBM performed the best, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.821 (95% CI: 0.802–0.841) for the fall risk test set and an AUC of 0.905 (95% CI: 0.892–0.919) for the fall injury risk test set. We identified important risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults with DS. The optimal predictive model and risk predictors differed from those identified before stratification by age. SHAP visualises the specific contributions of these risk factors, thereby enhancing the model’s value for application. Online tools to implement the model are available at https://riskpredictiontool.shinyapps.io/falls_prediction_tool/ and https://riskpredictiontool.shinyapps.io/fall_related_injuries_prediction_tool/.Discussion and conclusionsThe results can help predict risk of falls and fall-related injuries among middle-aged and older adults with DS. These findings provide an important guide for the development of public health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27754-x.

Introduction

Globally, population ageing is rapidly impacting social ecology, and countries are increasingly facing a growing burden of disease. Among the various health issues, depressive symptoms (DS) are particularly significant among middle-aged and older adults. DS are negative affective disorders that arise from an individual’s inability to cope with stressful life events. They are typically characterised by a depressed mood, diminished interest in activities, pessimism and a lack of initiative [1]. A study conducted by Zhang et al. in 2024 revealed that the incidence of DS among middle-aged and older adults in China is as high as 27.43% [2]. In addition to heightening the risk of diseases such as anaemia and sarcopenia in this population, individuals experiencing DS are also at a greater risk of falls [3, 4].

Falls are a major cause of morbidity and disability globally [4] and may result in severe consequences such as soft tissue contusions and fractures [5]. In a study by Jiang et al., the overall incidence of fall-related injuries in the elderly population over 60 years of age was as high as 54.95% [6]. Fall-related injuries occurring after a fall are moreover a major cause of long-term pain and dysfunction [7], which not only threaten the physical and mental health of middle-aged and older adults to a great extent, but also impose a heavy financial burden on individuals, families and the society due to the high costs associated with long-term rehabilitation and care needs. DS have been widely recognised as an important risk factor for falls [8, 9]. Andrea Iaboni and other researchers believe that this may be due to factors such as gait disturbance, distraction, and adverse effects of antidepressants due to depression [10, 11]. Meanwhile, Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs), a common antidepressant medication, have been shown to increase the risk of fracture by 70% [12]. This evidence suggests that individuals with DS are generally at higher risk of falls and fall-related injuries. Therefore, there is an urgent need for early risk assessment and timely intervention regarding falls and fall-related injuries among individuals with DS.

Machine learning (ML), as a key subfield of Artificial Intelligence (AI), enables computer systems to make predictions or decisions based on data by autonomously learning patterns and optimising performance [13]. In clinical applications, ML technology can realise accurate prediction of an individual patient’s symptom development by establishing prediction functions or decision rules through in-depth analysis of a large amount of clinical case data. In recent years, ML technology has been widely used in several clinical scenarios, such as disease screening and prediction, imaging diagnosis, and pharmacological research [13, 14]. Notably, although the feasibility of ML in predicting falls has been demonstrated in previous studies [15], there is still a noticeable gap in its application to predicting the risk of falls and fall-related injuries in Chinese middle-aged and older adults with DS.

Although ML models have demonstrated superior performance in clinical prediction tasks, the opacity of their decision-making process has severely restricted their practical application [16]. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) effectively breaks this “black box” problem of ML models by its powerful explanatory ability. This technique is based on the Shapley value principle in cooperative game theory, which enables clinical workers to understand the basis of prediction and the logic of the decision-making process more intuitively by quantifying the marginal contribution of each characteristic variable to the individual prediction results [17]. Therefore, researchers typically introduce the SHAP interpretability framework to quantify the risk prediction of outcomes by important predictors, thereby providing an intuitive basis for clinical decision-making [18].

In summary, this study utilised the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative database, to investigate predictors of falls and fall-related injuries among middle-aged and older adults with DS. The Health Ecology Model (HEM) was employed to categorise these predictor variables. We applied eight ML algorithms-Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Neural Network (NN), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost)-to develop risk prediction models for falls and fall-related injuries. Additionally, we performed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analyses to enhance the interpretability of the models. Ultimately, we created an online computational tool based on the model results. The findings of this study can assist clinicians and related professionals in identifying patients who are at high risk for falls and fall-related injuries, which is essential for promoting healthy ageing.

Materials and methods

Data source

CHARLS is a national prospective tracking survey project hosted by the National Development Research Institute of Peking University, aiming to systematically collect multidimensional data on the health status, socio-economic characteristics, and pension security of China’s middle-aged and older adults aged 45 years and above, and to provide a high-quality empirical basis for research on ageing. CHARLS employs a multi-stage probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) method, encompassing 150 counties and 450 communities across 28 provinces, ensuring a high degree of reliability and representativeness. CHARLS has been approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants or their legal guardians have signed written informed consent forms. This study involves a secondary analysis of prospective cohort data from the publicly available CHARLS dataset and therefore does not require additional ethical approval. CHARLS survey sampling weights were used to correct for biases resulting from the complex sampling design, ensuring that the results are nationally representative. All data and questionnaire content can be downloaded through the CHARLS official data platform (https://opendata.pku.edu.cn/dataverse/CHARLS) [19].

The study is based on data from the 2015–2018 waves of the CHARLS surveys. The detailed inclusion and exclusion procedures are as follows: (1) Baseline assessment in 2015: exclude participants younger than 45 years of age, or those with missing DS data or non-DS data; (2) ID-based matching: eligible participants from the 2015 baseline and 2018 follow-up were matched at a 1:1 ratio based on ID, and participants lost to follow-up were excluded; (3) Exclusion of participants who have experienced a fall or fall-related injury at baseline: those who reported falls and fall-related injuries at the 2015 baseline were excluded. (4) We assessed missing values in the remaining participants’ demographic variables and excluded 1,863 individuals with a missing rate of ≥ 20% to avoid bias in the imputed results. For the remaining individuals with a missing rate of < 20% for the characteristic variables, after confirming through missing pattern analysis that the data met the characteristics of missing at random (MAR), we employed the Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) method for multiple imputation [20]. A total of 3,664 participants were ultimately included in the sample; the sample screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

The minimum sample size required for the study was calculated using the events per variable (EPV) method, a widely recognised approach in developing clinical prediction models [21]. Its primary function is to assess the balance between the sample size and the number of variables, thereby preventing model overfitting and unstable results caused by including too many features. Based on evidence from previous studies, each predictor needs to satisfy at least 10 event counts to ensure the stability of the model. And the model showed better predictive performance when the event count was increased to 20. Therefore, we set the EPV threshold at 20. A total of 32 predictors were included in our study. The incidence rates of falls and fall-related injuries among Chinese middle-aged and older adults with DS in the study were approximately 20.36% and 8.92%, respectively. When these rates were substituted into the formula, the minimum sample sizes required for the study were calculated to be 804 and 703, respectively.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

In our study, DS assessed during the 2015 baseline survey were used as a baseline grouping variable to define the study cohort. This study focuses exclusively on middle-aged and older adults who tested positive for DS as the target population for prediction. DS in the middle-aged and older adults were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression scale (CESD-10). The scale consists of 10 entries, including: feeling worried about minor issues, feeling unable to concentrate when doing something, feeling depressed, feeling everything was an effort, feeling fearful, having difficulty sleeping, feeling lonely, feeling unable to get going, feeling hopeful about the future and feeling happy. Subjects were asked to rate each entry on a four-point scale (1–4) based on how they felt during the past week: (1) rarely or never (less than 1 day per week); (2) occasionally (1–2 days per week); (3) sometimes (3–4 days per week); and (4) often (5–7 days per week). The items “feeling hopeful about the future” and “feeling happy” are scored in reverse, while the remaining items are scored normally. The total score ranges from 0 to 30 points, and we define a total score of ≥ 10 as indicative of DS [22]. The reliability and validity of the CESD-10 scale and the ≥ 10 cutoff have been well established in Chinese populations [22, 23].

Assessment of falls and fall-related injuries

We excluded participants who reported a history of falls or fall-related injuries in 2015 and conducted a further analysis of self-reported falls and fall-related injuries among participants in the 2018 follow-up wave. A fall is defined as an unexpected event in which an individual suddenly loses balance due to unforeseen causes and comes into contact with the ground or a lower surface [24]; all related determinations are based on participants’ self-reports. Fall-related injuries are similarly defined based on participants’ self-reports, with reference to relevant assessment criteria, and are determined based on whether the participant reports having received professional medical intervention [25]. Based on the CHARLS questionnaire, the study population was screened using the following procedure: firstly, the initial screening was based on the question “Have you had a fall in the last 12 months?” and the responses were categorised as “yes” and “no”. Participants who answered “yes” were further screened by the question “Did you seek medical attention after the fall?”. Participants who met both the ‘had a fall’ and ‘required medical attention’ criteria were defined as having both a fall and a fall-related injury outcome.

Predictor variables

As a theoretical framework, HEM offers a comprehensive understanding of the various factors that influence health and their interrelationships [26]. Based on a review of existing evidence, we considered 32 variables related to the risk of falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults with DS for inclusion in the study based on the HEM from five dimensions: innate personal traits, personal psychological-behavioural lifestyle, interpersonal networks in the family and community, working and living conditions and local, national and global policy environments. The detailed assignment of all variables is shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables that did not fit the normal distribution were described as median and quartiles (M (Q₁, Q₃)), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (n(%)). The Mann-Whitney test and chi-square test were used to compare the distributions of the variables. The dataset was then randomly divided into training and testing sets in a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, we used the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to address the data imbalance. A LASSO regression model is constructed using the raw data, and an appropriate regularization parameter is selected via cross-validation to obtain a sparse regression model in which the coefficients of some features are zero. These features with zero coefficients are considered unimportant and can be removed [27]. Simultaneously, eigenvalues are obtained through principal component analysis. Research indicates that when the cumulative variance explained by the principal components reaches 70%, the majority of the useful information in the original data is captured, thereby meeting the requirements for dimensionality reduction [28]. Therefore, based on the magnitude of the eigenvalues, the first k principal components are selected when the cumulative variance explained reaches 70%. The original high-dimensional data is then projected onto the low-dimensional space formed by these k principal components, yielding the reduced-dimension data. Finally, the intersection of the results from these two methods is taken to determine the final feature variables. We then used eight machine learning algorithms—LR, SVM, GBM, NN, XGBoost, AdaBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost—to build predictive models. To ensure effective feature selection, grid search and 10-fold cross-validation were performed on the entire dataset. The Area Under the Curve (AUC), precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and Brier score were used to evaluate model performance. Additionally, we plotted the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curve (CC) and decision curve (DC) for the model. The best predictive model was interpreted using SHAP analysis. To compare the performance of the models built before and after screening the feature variables, we use the data before screening the feature variables to construct the model through the above steps. The specific model construction flowchart is shown in Fig. 2.

Considering the potential age gradient in the risk of falls and fall-related injuries, we conducted stratified analyses. According to the age stratification criteria defined by the WHO, models were separately constructed for individuals aged 45–59 years and those aged 60 years and older.

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.0, and P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

A total of 3,664 participants were included in the study, with a median age of 60.00 (52.00, 66.00). Among them, 2,213 (60.40%) were female. The incidence rate of falls among middle-aged and older Chinese patients with DS was 20.36%, and the incidence rate of fall-related injuries was 8.92%. The Mann-Whitney test revealed that age was significantly different between individuals with and without falls, and that age and BMI were significantly different between individuals with and without fall-related injuries. The incidence of falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and elderly populations varies across different dimensions of the HEM. Detailed results are shown in Tables 1 and 2.

Screening of predictor variables

The dataset was randomly divided into training and test sets on a 7:3 basis. Due to the imbalance in the sample data for falls and fall-related injuries (the number of participants who experienced falls or fall-related injuries was far smaller than the number of participants who did not), we used the SMOTE algorithm in the training set to balance the data, bringing the ratio of samples in the two categories to 1:1. Lasso regression analyses identified 24 significant factors associated with falls (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B) and 25 significant factors related to fall-related injuries (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), and the percentage of cumulative variance of the top 17 variables for falls and fall-related injuries in the PCA exceeded 70 per cent for each variable, thus identifying 17 significant factors associated with falls and fall-related injuries, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 2C) [28]. We selected a common subset of the characteristic variables identified by both methods as the most important predictors for falls, which included abdominal obesity, nap, lumbar pain, self-reported health, visual impairment, alcohol consumption, smoking, age, night sleep duration, exercise and gender. Similarly, for fall-related injuries, the significant predictor variables included age, gender, sleep quality, hearing impairment, night sleep duration, hypertension, abdominal obesity, alcohol consumption, exercise, nap, backache and self-reported health.

Model performance

In the training set, we used the important variables identified through screening to construct the risk prediction model. The model was validated on the test set, and the model fit metrics for both the training and test sets were obtained, as shown in Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Table 3. Among these, the LightGBM model demonstrated strong predictive performance on both the training and test sets. On the training set, the AUC for the fall prediction model was 0.872 (95% CI: 0.861–0.883) (Supplementary Fig. 3A), and for the fall-related injury prediction model, the AUC was 0.946 (95% CI: 0.940–0.953) (Supplementary Fig. 4A), indicating high predictive accuracy. In the test set, the AUC for the LightGBM model in predicting falls was 0.821 (95% CI: 0.802–0.841) (Supplementary Fig. 3B), and the AUC for predicting fall-related injuries was 0.905 (95% CI: 0.892–0.919) (Supplementary Fig. 4B). The difference in prediction performance between the training and test sets is minimal, with no significant performance degradation, which fully confirms the robustness and reliability of the internal validation results and clearly demonstrates the model’s excellent generalization ability. The LightGBM model outperforms other models in metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, and Brier score. The ROC curve results show that the LightGBM model has the strongest ability to discriminate between positive and negative samples. CC demonstrated a high degree of consistency between the predicted and actual results of the LightGBM model (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D, Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). DC show that the net benefit of the LightGBM model is significantly higher than the baseline when the threshold is between 0.0 and 1.0 (Supplementary Fig. 3E, Supplementary Fig. 4E). On the test set, the model also demonstrated good net gains (Supplementary Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 4F). This indicates that the model is not limited to the training dataset used in this study, but can be effectively extrapolated to similar populations of middle-aged and older adults in China with DS, demonstrating stable potential for clinical prediction and application. In summary, we conclude that the LightGBM model is the best risk prediction model for predicting falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and elderly Chinese patients with DS.

Results after age startification

Although the sample size of this study is limited, we have further considered stratifying Chinese middle-aged and older adults with DS by age and conducting exploratory construction of risk prediction models across different age subgroups, with the aim of providing valuable insights for future research. The incidence of falls was 18.65% in the 45–59 years group and 22.06% in the ≥ 60 years group, respectively (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Table 5). The incidence of fall-related injuries was 7.66% and 10.19% in the two groups, respectively (Supplementary Table 6, Supplementary Table 7). The GBM model performed best in predicting fall risk among the 45–59 age group (Supplementary Table 8, Supplementary Fig. 5), while the LightGBM model performed best in the 60 and older age group (Supplementary Table 9, Supplementary Fig. 6). Alcohol consumption, self-reported health, hearing impairment, and exercise were common predictors across both age groups. Regarding the prediction of fall-related injury risk, the GBM model performed best in both age groups (Supplementary Table 10, Supplementary Table 11, Supplementary Fig. 7, Supplementary Fig. 8). Gender, sleep quality, BMI, night sleep duration, abdominal obesity, self-reported health, and nap were common risk predictors across both groups. The results of the age-stratified SHAP analysis are shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, 10, 11, and 12. The results indicate that there are age-related differences in both the optimal prediction model and the risk predictors of falls and fall-related injuries.

Discussion and conclusions

Abdominal obesity is a significant risk factor associated with falls in middle-aged and older adults with DS. Simoneau et al. demonstrated that obesity may lead to a shift in the centre of mass, which is further away from the ankle joint in obese individuals compared to those without obesity, and that the ankle joint requires greater corrective torque [29]. All of these factors can influence the occurrence of falls. Not napping also increases the risk of falls. It has been demonstrated to improve cognitive deficits resulting from insufficient sleep duration in middle-aged and older adults [30]. Patients who do not nap lack this way of improving health and regulating stress, and thus may have a higher risk of falls. In our study, lumbar pain was a high-risk factor for falls. This may be due to changes in the processing of afferents and sensory centres of the paravertebral muscle spindle in patients with lumbar pain [31], which leads to impaired postural sensation and consequently balance disorders. Poor self-reported health also increases the risk of falls in middle-aged and older adults with DS. Poorer self-reported health tends to imply poorer physical health and higher psychological stress, which is also associated with a higher risk of falls [32]. In our study, visual function was a significant factor influencing the occurrence of falls in patients with DS. Impaired visual function significantly reduces an individual’s spatial perception of the environment, leading to a reduced ability to recognise potential hazards (e.g., ground obstacles, irregular road surfaces, etc.). This lack of visual information input not only directly affects balance maintenance function but also triggers compensatory gait adjustments (e.g., shortened stride length, reduced stride speed), which further increases the probability of falls occurring. Alcohol consumption and smoking also influence the occurrence of falls in middle-aged and older adults with DS. Alcohol consumption affects signalling in the brain, causing a transient reduction in muscle coordination [33], which is directly associated with falls. Smoking, on the other hand, leads to increased serum concentrations of cholesterol, very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and triglycerides [34], which exacerbates the cardiovascular burden in patients and increases the risk of developing atherosclerosis. We speculate that standing instability due to cardiovascular symptoms, such as angina, indirectly caused by smoking, is a possible reason for the increased risk of falls in middle-aged and older adults with DS. In our study population, age is a significant factor influencing falls in middle-aged and older individuals. Along with age, physiological functions such as musculature and metabolism are altered [35], which may affect the occurrence of falls. Falls are also affected to varying degrees by the night sleep duration. Sleep is involved in a variety of bodily processes, including immunity and metabolism, and is directly related to the recovery of physiological functions. A study has shown that sleep duration is a potential trigger for sarcopenia [36]. All of which affect the risk of developing falls. Previous studies have suggested that exercise interventions can reduce the risk of falls among older adults [37]. However, in our study, exercise was also found to increase the risk of falls among middle-aged and older adults with DS. We speculate that this may be due to the fact that this population often experiences impaired attention and executive function, and may also have multiple chronic conditions and be taking related medications; these factors alone already increase their risk of falling [10, 11]. Furthermore, this population tends to engage in more outdoor activities, walk for longer periods, and have greater exposure to environments associated with falls, thereby increasing the likelihood of falling [38, 39]. In addition, our study confirms that among middle-aged and older adults with DS, women show a higher risk of falls than men. A possible explanation is that men typically have better visuospatial cognitive abilities [40], which can help them avoid obstacles that may cause falls more effectively and predict the risk of falls.

In our study, poor sleep quality, hearing impairment, hypertension and backache increased the risk of fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults with DS. However, they were not high-risk factors for causing falls. Poor sleep quality leads to a decrease in body function and quality [41], and the likelihood of developing an organic injury after a fall increases. The ears of patients with hearing impairment may be more fragile than those of healthy individuals, and a fall impact may result in a more serious outcome. Therefore, strengthening awareness of physical examination, timely detection of hearing impairment, and timely intervention, such as the use of assistive listening tools like hearing aids, can effectively predict the risk of fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults. There are numerous complications of hypertension, including congestive heart failure, cerebral haemorrhage, renal failure, myocardial infarction, thrombotic stroke, etc. [42], and falls may lead to a further increase in cardiovascular burden and cause more serious injuries. Falls in patients with back pain may cause secondary injuries to the back, which may also increase the risk of fall-related injuries. In addition, unlike falls, men and patients who nap have a higher risk of fall-related injuries. Possible explanations for this are that men are more physically active, have more bad habits such as smoking than women [43], and are in poorer physical condition, and therefore have a higher risk of fall-related injuries such as fractures. Napping, on the other hand, may be a clinical manifestation of poor health, and injuries caused by falls are more severe in those with poor physical fitness.

After age stratification, the optimal prediction model and important risk predictors differed from those obtained in the total sample analysis. In the fall risk model, alcohol consumption, self-reported health, hearing impairment, and exercise were common predictors across both age groups; in the fall injury model, gender, sleep quality, BMI, night sleep duration, abdominal obesity, self-reported health, and nap were common risk predictors across both groups. The results indicate that risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries exhibit significant age-related heterogeneity among middle-aged and older adults with DS in China. This difference may reflect the physiological and functional changes that occur in this population as they age. We speculate that possible causes may be related to physiological differences associated with aging in patients with DS and the cumulative effects of the disease course of DS [44].

In the complex field of healthcare, ML technology has great potential for clinical decision-making, assisted diagnosis and prognostic assessment with its superior data processing and analysis capabilities. In our study, LightGBM is the model with the best predictive performance. LightGBM is an efficient gradient boosted decision tree (GBDT) algorithm. The algorithm adopts a histogram-based feature discretisation method to deal with continuous variables, replaces the traditional layer-by-layer growth with a leaf-by-leaf growth strategy, and incorporates a maximum depth restriction, which not only drastically reduces the memory consumption and significantly improves the computational efficiency, but also effectively controls the complexity of the model, and enhances the model’s generalisation ability while guaranteeing a high prediction accuracy [45]. The core value of CC is to assess the reliability of model predictions, i.e., how well the predicted probability agrees with the actual probability of occurrence. The LightGBM model in this study demonstrated optimal calibration, which suggests that clinical workers can implement risk stratification management and formulate precise intervention plans based on specific risk prediction values in clinical decision-making. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) demonstrates the usefulness of the LightGBM model in real clinical scenarios. In our study, the LightGBM model produced a significant advantage in terms of combined clinical net benefit under most clinical decision thresholds. This suggests that when healthcare workers implement targeted interventions informed by model predictions, they may not only better identify high-risk individuals but also reduce unnecessary interventions among low-risk populations. This may contribute to more rational resource allocation and help improve the overall efficiency of clinical interventions. In addition, in order to solve the inherent “black box” problem of ML, this study used SHAP to visualise and analyse the prediction results of the LightGBM model, which can visually present the direction and intensity of the influence of each risk factor, and quantify the effect of the variable on specific risk factors by rigorously calculating the marginal contribution of each feature in different feature subset combinations. In our study, SHAP-based interpretable analyses enable clinicians to accurately identify the core intervention targets for falls and fall-related injuries in middle-aged and older adults with DS, clarify the degree of contribution of each factor, and thus provide rational guidance for the development of evidence-driven, individualised intervention programmes.

The data on exposures, outcomes, and covariates included in this study are all based on participants’ self-reports; such self-reported data hold significant validity and practical value in epidemiological and social survey research [46]. Most indicators related to health behaviors, lifestyle, psychological status, social support, and subjective experience are difficult to measure directly with objective instruments, and thus are generally collected through self-reports. With standardized study design and validated questionnaires or scales, self-reported data can reflect individuals’ cognitive characteristics, behavioral habits, and health perceptions to a certain extent, and also have the advantages of relatively low cost, wide coverage, and feasibility in large-scale surveys. However, it cannot be denied that self-reporting may lead to discrepancies between questionnaire results and reality due to factors such as individual memory bias, cognitive decline, subjective judgments, and social expectations. It may potentially affect the quality of the model’s training data. This, in turn, may affect the model’s fit and predictive accuracy, thereby limiting its application in real-world clinical settings. Future studies could incorporate objective measurement indicators, such as device monitoring data, medical records, and clinical test results, to further enhance the model’s predictive capabilities.

This online tool is designed for use by a diverse range of users, including clinicians, community health workers, and middle-aged and older adults with DS and their caregivers. It provides a convenient, visual risk assessment for falls and fall-related injuries among middle-aged and older adults with DS in China. Built around a ML model, the tool supports the direct entry of measured data for feature variables and intuitively displays risk prediction scores along with the weighting of key risk factors, thereby offering a feasible pathway for the clinical implementation of the predictive model. In clinical and primary healthcare settings, this tool can be integrated into outpatient screening workflows or linked with electronic health record systems, enabling efficient integration with existing clinical assessment frameworks. It assists users in rapidly completing risk assessments, identifying high-risk individuals early, and initiating targeted interventions. This online tool fills a gap in China regarding risk prediction tools for falls and fall-related injuries among middle-aged and elderly patients with DS. It provides more precise and efficient support for individualized fall prevention and control in clinical settings, holding significant practical value and public health implications.

To address the problem of falls and fall-related injuries among Chinese middle-aged and older adults with DS, this study compared eight ML models for risk factor identification and risk prediction based on the CHARLS 2015–2018 cohort data. The results identified LightGBM as the optimal model for prediction. SHAP analysis explained the model prediction mechanism, quantified feature contributions, and improved model transparency and applicability. The development of online tools will enable healthcare workers and related personnel to predict disease risk more conveniently and formulate targeted preventive strategies. The research findings provide an online predictive tool for falls and fall-related injuries among middle-aged and older adults with DS in China. This tool can be applied in primary care screening for patients with mental health conditions, offering early warning, aiding in the development of intervention measures, and providing a scientific basis for optimizing public health strategies.

Limitations

First, the data in this study were primarily derived from self-reports by the respondents themselves or their proxies; therefore, the impact of recall bias and social desirability bias regarding depressive symptoms, falls, fall-related injuries, and covariates on the study results cannot be overlooked. Second, due to limitations in the survey content and measurement methods of the database, some potentially important predictors (such as congenital diseases) and certain objective biomarkers (such as bone density and specific nutrients) were not included in the study. Furthermore, due to the limited scope of data collected in the CHARLS database, our study lacks objective biomarkers that could enhance the predictive power of the model. Finally, although the predictive model was developed using a nationally representative dataset, the model’s performance may still require further validation using independent external cohort data or field testing and usability assessments in real-world primary care settings.
