Plasma metabolomics disentangles T2DM- and CAD-specific dysmetabolism and identifies potential biomarkers for CAD risk escalation in diabetic patients
Authors: Ting Hu, Wen Zhang, Xian Ding, Feifei Han, Zhuoling An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major driver of coronary artery disease (CAD). Prior studies often conflate T2DM- and CAD-specific metabolic alterations, limiting insights into CAD pathogenesis in T2DM. This study aimed to distinguish CAD-unique signatures from T2DM-specific dysmetabolism, and to identify potential metabolic biomarkers for CAD risk escalation in T2DM patients.MethodsWe performed an untargeted plasma metabolomic study with 123 healthy controls (HCs), 50 T2DM patients without CAD, and 155 T2DM patients with CAD. T2DM_CAD was defined as T2DM diagnosed at least 5 years prior to CAD, with coronary angiography-confirmed stenosis (> 30%) in major coronary arteries. Differential metabolites were identified via intergroup comparisons, with T2DM-specific and CAD-specific signatures distinguished based on unique expression patterns. Machine learning models were developed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of these metabolites for CAD.ResultsPlasma metabolomic profiling identified distinct metabolic patterns across the three cohorts. Metabolites specific to T2DM were enriched in carbohydrates and certain lipid species, reflecting disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism. CAD-specific metabolites were predominantly lipids and organic acids, with notable involvement in amino acid and fatty acid metabolic pathways. Several metabolites changed progressively from HCs through T2DM to T2DM_CAD, reflecting advancing metabolic dysregulation, whereas others showed opposing trends, suggesting compensatory or protective adaptations. Integration of key metabolites with clinical parameters in machine learning models effectively distinguished between study groups, demonstrating promising performance for CAD risk assessment in T2DM patients.ConclusionsThese findings disentangle T2DM- and CAD-specific metabolic disturbances and identify escalation/de-escalation features of CAD risk in diabetic patients, which are potential candidates for future risk stratification pending validation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02981-5.

Introduction

According to the International Diabetes Federation’s 2021 report, approximately 537 million adults worldwide are living with diabetes, with over 90% of whom have type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [1]. Over the past two decades, substantial progress has been made in T2DM management, with current therapies effectively controlling acute hyperglycemia and reducing the risk of certain microvascular complications [2–5]. However, their impact on macrovascular outcomes remains modest, leaving T2DM patients at persistently high risk for cardiovascular events [4, 6]. Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the predominant cause of mortality in T2DM patients, accounting for nearly two-thirds of all diabetes-related deaths [7].

T2DM complicated by CAD is marked by insidious onset, aggressive clinical manifestations, multiple comorbidities, and poor prognosis. Notably, the mortality rate following myocardial infarction (MI) is 4–5 times higher in T2DM-CAD patients than in non-diabetic MI patients [8]. Early clinical diagnosis and intervention are therefore paramount to preventing fatal outcomes. Yet current clinical diagnosis of T2DM with CAD primarily relies on post-myocardial injury changes in electrocardiograms and serum biomarkers, which exhibit significant lag and are thus unsuitable for early clinical warning and management [9, 10]. Exploring biomarkers for early risk prediction of CAD in T2DM patients is therefore of great clinical significance. However, the specific metabolic perturbations that distinguish T2DM alone from T2DM complicated by CAD remain incompletely characterized, impeding the development of precision biomarkers and targeted interventions for risk stratification.

Metabolomics, which enables global profiling of endogenous small molecules, has emerged as a powerful tool to unravel the metabolic underpinnings of complex diseases and to discover diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [11, 12]. Recent advances have yielded unprecedented insights into the metabolic dysregulation driving both CAD and T2DM [13–17]. Distinct metabolic signatures correlated with disease progression and risk stratification— notably, acylcarnitine imbalances represent a shared feature across major cardiovascular events, including heart failure, MI, and stroke [13, 16]. In T2DM, elevated branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and specific lipid species are strongly linked to CAD complications [17]. Critically, metabolomic biomarkers significantly outperform conventional risk factors in predictive power, especially in high-risk populations with T2DM and established or incipient CAD. Together, these findings underscore metabolomics’ potential to uncover novel biomarkers and refine personalized risk assessment in cardio-metabolic disease.

Previous studies have identified metabolic signatures associated with T2DM or CAD individually, exploring metabolic perturbations and pathway dysregulations induced by these conditions [13, 18–20]. However, a critical limitation of prior studies is the frequent conflation of T2DM-related and CAD-specific metabolic changes. Most investigations either compare healthy controls (HCs) directly with CAD patients (without stratifying by diabetes status) or treat T2DM and CAD as overlapping phenotypes, thereby failing to disentangle metabolites that specifically drive CAD progression in the context of pre-existing T2DM. This gap is consequential, as metabolic features “associated with CAD” in T2DM may simply reflect underlying diabetic dysmetabolism rather than true CAD-specific processes, reducing their utility as diagnostic or prognostic markers.

To address this, the present study employs untargeted plasma metabolomics to systematically compare three well-defined cohorts: HCs, T2DM patients without CAD (metabolically matched controls, MMCs), and T2DM patients with CAD (T2DM_CAD). The control groups should be matched as closely as possible to the T2DM_CAD group in terms of baseline characteristics. By applying stringent criteria to distinguish T2DM-specific metabolites from CAD-specific metabolites, we aim to (1) characterize the core metabolic disturbances inherent to T2DM, independent of CAD; (2) identify novel CAD-specific metabolic signatures that emerge in T2DM patients with coronary atherosclerosis; (3) define dynamic “escalation” (progressive changes from HC to T2DM to T2DM_CAD) and “de-escalation” (reversed changes between T2DM and T2DM_CAD) features that correlate with CAD severity; and (4) develop machine learning models integrating key metabolites and clinical parameters to improve CAD risk prediction in T2DM. By disentangling these distinct metabolic profiles, this study seeks to enhance understanding of T2DM- and CAD-specific metabolic reprogramming, as well as to identify potential biomarkers associated with CAD risk in T2DM patients.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Methanol, acetonitrile, and isopropyl alcohol of mass spectrometry (MS) grade were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA, USA). Formic acid of MS grade was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Metabolite standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, Bide pharm (Shanghai, China), Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) or Steraloids (Newport, RI, USA). Stable isotope-labeled internal standards (ISs) were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (Cambridge, MA, USA), Cayman Chemical or Steraloids. Ultrapure water was prepared using a Milli-Q purification system (Bedford, MA, USA).

Ethical approval

All procedures involving human participants in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (Ethics Code: 2020-Research-416). According to the regulations of the ethics committee of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital regarding exemption of informed consent, the use of residual blood samples collected after routine clinical testing for research purposes may be exempted from informed consent. This study met the criteria for an exemption from informed consent and was approved by the Ethics Committee for waiver of informed consent.

Patient enrollment and sample collection

Participants were consecutively recruited from Beijing Chaoyang Hospital between January 2021 and February 2023, comprising three cohorts: 123 HCs, 50 T2DM patients without CAD (MMCs), and 155 T2DM patients with CAD (T2DM_CAD). The T2DM and T2DM_CAD cohorts were primarily enrolled from the Cardiology Center (a national key discipline specializing in cardiovascular diseases, with high patient volume for these comorbid conditions), while the HC cohort was recruited from both the hospital’s health check-up program ensure sufficient access to individuals meeting strict healthy criteria.

For the HC cohort, inclusion required freedom from metabolic disorders (e.g., dysglycemia, dyslipidemia), CAD, pregnancy, malignancy, and other chronic comorbidities, confirmed by rigorous screening (including physical examination, laboratory tests for fasting glucose/lipids, and imaging evaluations as needed). Additionally, HC participants had no family history of cardiovascular diseases (in first-degree relatives) to exclude potential genetic confounding factors associated with CAD.

The metabolically matched T2DM control group (without CAD) included patients diagnosed with T2DM according to the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or 2-h postprandial glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] ≥ 6.5%). Key exclusion criteria for this group were: (1) CAD (confirmed by coronary angiography or computed tomography angiography). These strict criteria ensured isolation of the metabolic effects of T2DM independent of CAD and minimized within-group variation; (2) history of hypertension or hyperlipidemia; (3) pregnancy, malignancy, autoimmune disease, or active infection; (4) severe liver or kidney dysfunction (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 or alanine transaminase > 2 × upper limit of normal [ULN]).

The T2DM_CAD group included T2DM patients with confirmed CAD, with two core inclusion criteria: (1) the diagnosis of T2DM preceded the diagnosis of CAD by at least 5 years (to establish a temporal relationship consistent with T2DM-related CAD pathogenesis); and (2) coronary angiography -confirmed stenosis (> 30%) in at least one of the three major coronary arteries (left main, left anterior descending, left circumflex, or right coronary artery) — this criterion ensured inclusion of a broad spectrum of CAD severity (mild to severe) to enhance cohort representativeness and diversity. Key exclusion criteria for this group were: (1) pregnancy, malignancy, autoimmune disease, or active infection; (2) Severe liver and kidney dysfunction.

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants. To minimize participant burden, residual clinical specimens (remaining after completion of routine diagnostic tests) were utilized. All samples were processed within 8 h of collection: EDTA-anticoagulated blood was centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma, which was stored at -80 °C until metabolomic analysis.

Clinical data collection

Demographic information, including sex and age, was collected for all participants. Clinical parameters such as blood glucose levels and blood lipid levels were recorded. The blood glucose parameters included fasting blood glucose (GLU) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). The blood lipid parameters included serum free fatty acids, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), and lipoprotein(a). Other important laboratory parameters collected included homocysteine (HCY), uric acid, liver function indicators (aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], alkaline phosphatase [ALP], direct bilirubin [DBIL], indirect bilirubin [IBIL], total bilirubin [TBIL], and total bile acids [TBA]), and renal function indicators (urea and creatinine [CREA]). All laboratory parameters were obtained from official laboratory reports issued by the hospital’s clinical laboratory.

Plasma sample pretreatment for metabolomics analysis

Plasma samples were processed using a protein precipitation method. A 100 μL aliquot of plasma was transferred into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. Subsequently, 10 μL of pre-cooled mixed internal standard solution and 290 μL of pre-cooled methanol were added to the plasma. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min to ensure thorough homogenization. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and transferred to a new tube, where it was dried under a stream of nitrogen gas at room temperature. The dried residue was stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

For reconstitution, the dried samples were reconstituted with 100 μL of a solution containing acetonitrile and water (2:98, v/v). The mixture was vortexed for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution of the residue dissolve. The reconstituted samples were then centrifuged again at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to an autosampler vial for UHPLC-HRMS/MS analysis.

A plasma quality control (QC) sample was prepared by pooling 20 μL of plasma from each subject. This QC sample was processed using the same procedure as described above. During the analytical run, the QC sample was inserted after every 10 samples to monitor the stability and reproducibility of the analytical system.

Untargeted metabolomics study

Untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed using an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive MS equipped with an electrospray ionization source. Separation was achieved on a Waters HSS T3 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 3.5 μm) maintained at 35 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B), both containing 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was set at 0.25 mL/min, and the injection volume was 5 μL. The autosampler temperature was kept at 4 °C. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–1.5 min, 2% B; 1.5–15 min, linear gradient from 2 to 100% B; 15–22 min, 100% B; 22–22.1 min, linear gradient from 100 to 2% B; 22.1–30 min, 2% B.

The MS parameters were optimized as follows: capillary temperature was set at 320 °C; sheath gas flow rate was 45 arbitrary units, and the auxiliary gas flow rate was 10 arbitrary units; the RF lens was set at 40%. For MS1 full-scan acquisition, the resolution was 70,000 with a maximum injection time (IT) of 100 ms and a mass range of m/z 70–1050. For MS2 acquisition, the resolution was 17,500, with a maximum IT of 50 ms. The loop count was set to 10, and the TopN was 10. The isolation window was 1.0 m/z, and the normalized collision energy (NCE) was set to 20, 30, and 45. Dynamic exclusion was set at 4.0 s. The total run time for each sample was 22 min.

To optimize analysis efficiency and enhance ion detection sensitivity, biological samples were analyzed using a simultaneous positive/negative ion full-scan mode without acquiring data-dependent acquisition (DDA) MS2 data for all samples. To facilitate subsequent metabolite identification, mixed QC samples were analyzed in either positive ion full scan + DDA or negative ion full scan + DDA mode. This strategy reduced overall data acquisition time while ensuring the availability of MS2 spectra for metabolite annotation.

Metabolomics data pre-processing, normalization, and metabolite identification

The raw mass spectrometry data obtained from the LC–MS analysis were first converted into the mzXML format using ProteoWizard software. Next, the mzXML files were imported into the R environment, and the XCMS package was employed for comprehensive data pre-processing. This included retention time correction to account for any drifts in the chromatographic system, followed by peak detection, extraction, integration, and alignment across all samples. The resulting data matrix contained detailed information on the m/z, retention time, and peak intensity for both positive and negative ion modes. This matrix served as the foundation for further data normalization and statistical analysis. Detailed code for XCMS workflow is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

To mitigate systematic errors and ensure data quality in large-scale, longitudinal metabolomics studies, metabolite peak intensities were normalized using the Iterative Surrogate-Based Weighted Support Vector Regression (Norm ISWSVR) method, a novel data normalization algorithm developed by our research group [21]. QC samples were analyzed across batch runs to assess reproducibility, and only features with a coefficient of variation (CV) < 30% were retained for downstream analysis. For metabolite identification, the data from the DDA scans of the QC samples were imported into Compound Discoverer v3.1. The software was used to extract peak features from the MS and MS/MS data. These peak features, along with their associated MS/MS spectra, were then searched against multiple databases, including an in-house standard database, the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) database, mzCloud, and the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) public spectral database. The search results were used to assign molecular formulas and propose structures for the detected metabolites, thereby facilitating the identification and annotation of the metabolites in the study.

Finally, 658 unique metabolites were annotated in this study. The identification of plasma metabolites was categorized into four levels according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) recommendations [22, 23]. Level 1 (MS1): Metabolites were identified by matching retention time, MS, and MS/MS spectra to authentic standards in the in-house database, representing the highest confidence. Level 2 (MS2): Metabolites were identified by matching MS and MS/MS spectra to reference compounds in public databases (e.g., NIST, mzCloud, HMDB), with moderate confidence due to the lack of retention time matching. Level 3 (MS3): Metabolites are assigned to chemical classes using characteristic physicochemical properties or spectral similarities. This helps capture metabolic trends and pathway-related patterns. Level 4 (MS4): Metabolites with only detectable ion features. Lacking classification and names, they can still be differentiated and quantified by spectral data, potentially indicating novel metabolic processes.

Statistical analysis

Differential metabolite screening was performed using the limma package in R (version 4.2.1). To account for baseline age differences among cohorts and minimize confounding, a linear model incorporating age as a covariate was constructed for pairwise comparisons. Prior to analysis, metabolomic data were log₂-transformed and Z-score normalized to stabilize variance and improve data distribution. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Metabolites with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.​

Multivariate statistical analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA), were performed on log₂-transformed metabolomics data using SIMCA-P software (version 14.0; Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden) to visualize global metabolic profile differences among groups and identify latent clustering patterns.

Pathway enrichment analysis of the identified differential metabolites was conducted using the web-based platform MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/), with pathway significance determined by combined scores integrating enrichment and pathway impact values.​ Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were used to assess relationships between metabolites and clinical parameters, with P-values adjusted for FDR using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Chord diagrams, generated using the circlize package in R, were used to visualize metabolite–clinical phenotype correlations. Correlation networks among differential metabolites were constructed and visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.9.1), with edge weights representing correlation strength. Detailed code for data processing and visualization is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Model development

Machine learning models were developed to evaluate the classification performance of different feature sets, including clinical indicators, identified differential metabolites, and metabolic modules. Using the caret package and its associated tools in R (with code provided in the Supplementary Materials), four different machine learning models were constructed and compared: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Naive Bayes (NB), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). These models were used to distinguish between Controls and different degrees of coronary artery stenosis, as well as to differentiate Controls and CAD patients with varying severities of clinical symptoms.

The datasets was randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets. Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to address the issue of imbalanced datasets. During model training, fivefold cross-validation repeated three times was used to select the optimal parameters. The top 10 most important clinical indicators, metabolites, and metabolic modules — ranked by their importance scores — were selected to construct the final classification models.

Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Kappa coefficient, F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Finally, a combined predictive model was built using the top 10 most important clinical indicators and metabolites. Visualizations, including bar plots, density plots, confusion matrix heatmaps, variable importance lollipop plots, and ROC curves, were generated using the ggplot2 package and its extensions. The complete code for all machine learning analyses is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Coronary angiography results revealed that 21% of participants in the T2DM_CAD group exhibited mild coronary stenosis (30–70% stenosis in three vessels), while 79% had severe stenosis (> 70%). Among T2DM_CAD patients, 26% reported no cardiac-related clinical symptoms (“Free”), 41% presented with angina pectoris (“AP”), and 33% had experienced MI.​

Regarding biochemical indicators, substantial differences were observed across the three groups in parameters related to blood glucose, lipids, liver function, and renal function. The HC group exhibited significantly lower levels of HbA1c and GLU compared to the two T2DM groups. Moreover, serum glucose levels in T2DM_CAD patients were significantly higher than those in T2DM patients without CAD, suggesting that poor glycemic control in T2DM may contribute to increased CAD risk. HCY, a known potential risk factor for CAD, did not show significantly elevated levels in T2DM_CAD patients in this study. Lipid parameters, including FFA, TC, LDL-C, and TG, differed significantly among the three groups. However, as traditional CAD risk factors, these lipids were not significantly higher in T2DM_CAD patients compared to controls, which we speculate may be related to the widespread use of lipid-lowering medication use in the T2DM_CAD group. HDL-C, a traditional protective factor against CAD, showed a progressive decline across the HC, T2DM, and T2DM_CAD groups, indicating that lower serum HDL-C levels are indeed associated with elevated CAD risk.

Liver function-related indicators, including ALT, ALP, IBIL, and TBIL, were significantly lower in the T2DM_CAD group compared to controls. In contrast, renal function-related indicators, including urea and CREA, were significantly higher in the T2DM_CAD group, potentially attribute to renal impairment resulting from poorer glycemic control in T2DM_CAD patients compared to the MMC group.​ Overall, these findings demonstrate significant differences in clinical phenotypes and laboratory parameters across the HC, T2DM, and T2DM_CAD groups, underscoring the importance of comprehensive clinical assessment in understanding CAD progression in diabetic cohorts.

To elucidate the biological relevance of these CAD-specific metabolites, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. As highlighted in Fig. 3C, key enriched significant pathways included: valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis; arginine biosynthesis; linoleic acid metabolism; and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids. Notably, L-isoleucine, L-leucine, and L-valine belong to the BCAAs. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed on the 223 annotated CAD-specific metabolites, adjustment for age and HbA1c. A total of 183 metabolites remained significantly associated with CAD risk, as shown in the forest plot (Supplementary Figure S5). Among them, 106 were identified as risk factors and 77 as protective factors. The top five metabolites most strongly associated with CAD risk were: ascorbic acid (risk factor, OR = 8.04), cortisol (protective factor, OR = 0.20), glutamic acid (risk factor, OR = 6.85), glycylproline (protective factor, OR = 0.13), and ( +)-15,16-dihydroxyoctadecanoic acid (protective factor, OR = 0.23).​ These CAD-specific metabolomic signatures not only distinguish the metabolic underpinning of CAD from those of T2DM but also provide novel insights into potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for CAD in diabetic populations.

To further clarify the clinical relevance of these CAD-specific metabolites in cardiovascular disease, we systematically summarized relevant completed clinical trials (detailed in Supplementary Table S2) and analyzed their alignment with our findings. Most of these clinical studies support the potential of our identified metabolites as CAD-related biomarkers or therapeutic targets. For instance, trials focusing on polyunsaturated fatty acids metabolism confirm that reduced plasma DGLA levels correlate with increased coronary plaque burden. Studies on amino acid metabolism demonstrate that lower glutamine concentrations are associated with elevated CAD risk—reinforcing the utility of DGLA and glutamine as potential prognostic biomarkers for CAD.

Machine learning models for classifying HC, T2DM, and T2DM_CAD were developed using annotated differential metabolites and clinical indicators.​

Discussion

T2DM accelerates coronary atherosclerosis through multiple pathways, with 60%–70% of T2DM patients ultimately succumbing to cardiovascular disease [24, 25]. A critical limitation of prior research is that most studies exploring metabolic features of CAD have relied on direct comparisons between HCs and CAD patients [26, 27]. This approach risks misattributing T2DM-related metabolic disturbances to CAD itself, as many “CAD-associated” metabolites may already differ significantly between HC and metabolically matched T2DM groups. The present study addresses this gap by rigorously distinguishing T2DM-specific dysmetabolism from CAD-unique signatures in a cohort of HC, T2DM patients without CAD, and T2DM patients with CAD (T2DM_CAD). By isolating these distinct metabolic profiles, we aim to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of CAD pathophysiology in diabetic populations and highlight potential targets that may help dissect the mechanisms by which T2DM promotes CAD.

Our identification of 100 T2DM-specific metabolites—defined by significant differences between T2DM and HC, but not between T2DM and T2DM_CAD—may reflect metabolic disturbances inherent to T2DM that appear not to be further modulated by the presence of CAD. Among the key annotated metabolites are elevated levels of fructose, glucose, and lipid species (including cervonoyl ethanolamide, palmitoleoyl ethanolamide, lysoPE(18:3(6Z,9Z,12Z)/0:0), and octadec-9-ynoic acid), alongside reduced levels of L-pipecolic acid and mannoheptulose. These alterations are associated with enrichment in starch/sucrose metabolism and galactose metabolism (Fig. 1). These observations are consistent with the prevailing view that T2DM may initiate a cascade of metabolic derangements — including carbohydrate intolerance and dysregulated lipid signaling — which could contribute to establishing a pro-atherogenic milieu [5, 28, 29].​

Notably, fructose emerged as a potentially robust risk factor for T2DM (OR = 7.95), independent of age and HbA1c levels, which is consistent with prior evidence suggesting a role for fructose in promotion of insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis [30, 31]. Elevated LysoPE(18:3(6Z,9Z,12Z)/0:0) and palmitoleoyl ethanolamide appear to be involved in inflammatory signaling [32, 33]. Conversely, the protective association of L-pipecolic acid (OR = 0.31) may suggest a potential link to gut microbial metabolism, as this metabolite has been associated with immune homeostasis and microbiota-host interactions [34–36]. Intriguingly, the associations of palmitoleoyl ethanolamide, leucine, leucyl-glutamate, glucose, and mannoheptulose with T2DM lost significance after adjusting for age and HbA1c, which may indicate that their associations with T2DM are mediated, at least in part, by traditional glycemic risk factors. Collectively, these findings may not only help elucidate the distinct metabolic signatures inherent to T2DM but also provide a basis for identifying potential therapeutic targets and candidate biomarkers that could contribute to a better understanding of the metabolic underpinnings of T2DM and its progression.

A key strength of this study lies in the identification of 223 CAD-specific metabolites—features not linked to T2DM alone but potentially uniquely associated with coronary atherosclerosis in diabetic patients. These metabolites—predominantly lipids and organic acids—were primarily enriched in valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis; arginine metabolism; and n-6 fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 3). These CAD-specific metabolomic signatures not only help distinguish the metabolic disturbances associated with CAD from those inherent to T2DM but also appear broadly consistent with some existing clinical evidence (Supplementary Table S2), potentially offering novel insights into candidate biomarkers for CAD risk stratification and possible therapeutic targets. Within the valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis pathway, BCAAs (L-isoleucine, L-leucine, L-valine) were all elevated in T2DM_CAD and showed strong positive intercorrelations, which may reflect coordinated accumulation. This observation is broadly in line with prior evidence suggesting that BCAAs could contribute to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and vascular inflammation — processes that may play key roles in atherosclerotic plaque progression [37–40]. In the arginine metabolism pathway, upregulation of oxoglutaric acid and glutamic acid, alongside reduced glutamine, may suggests impaired nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability [41]. Glutamine depletion could potentially limit endothelial NO synthesis, while accumulation of oxoglutaric acid might exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction — together, these alterations may collectively contribute to impaired vascular relaxation [42–44].​

Notably, perturbations in n-6 fatty acid metabolism appear to emerge as a potential hallmark of CAD-specific dysregulation [45]. Straight-chain n-6 fatty acids (palmitic acid, oleic acid, linoleic acid, gamma-linolenic acid, arachidonic acid) and oxidized derivatives (13-L-hydroperoxylinoleic acid, 9,10-epoxyoctadecenoic acid) were significantly elevated in T2DM_CAD (Fig. 3 and Figure S4). As polyunsaturated fatty acids, n-6 species can be metabolized into oxidized intermediates and eicosanoids (e.g., prostaglandins, leukotrienes), which are known to contribute to inflammatory responses—a process thought to be central to all stages of atherosclerosis [46, 47]. Their accumulation may potentially exacerbate endothelial injury, thereby facilitating lipid deposition and plaque formation [48–50]. In contrast, DGLA —a precursor to the anti-inflammatory prostaglandin E1—was the only n-6 fatty acid found to be reduced in T2DM_CAD [51, 52]. This observation is broadly consistent with prior reports suggesting that lower DGLA levels may correlate with higher all-cause mortality in elderly patients with MI [53], and that DGLA supplementation has been associated with reduces atherosclerotic burden in animal models [54]. Its depletion in T2DM_CAD may reflect a diminished capacity for anti-inflammatory protection, which could further promote atherogenesis. These divergent n-6 fatty acid profiles may underscore the context-dependent nature of their roles in CAD.

Ascorbic acid (OR = 8.04) and cortisol (OR = 0.20) emerged as among the top metabolites associated with CAD. The elevation of ascorbic acid may reflect a compensatory response to heightened oxidative stress in advanced CAD, while the apparent protective association of cortisol could be relate to adaptive stress responses or potential vascular anti-inflammatory effects [55]. The identification of CAD-specific metabolic signatures, distinct from those attributable to T2DM, may represents a meaningful step toward a more refined understanding of the metabolic underpinnings of CAD. These distinct metabolic alterations appear intrinsic to CAD and could provide a basis for identifying potential therapeutic targets and candidate biomarkers. Further exploration of these CAD-specific metabolites and pathways may facilitate a deeper appreciation of the metabolic mechanisms involved in CAD pathogenesis.

The identification of five metabolites exhibiting a consistent pattern of escalation across the disease continuum — from HC to T2DM to T2DM-CAD — along with 18 metabolites showing de-escalation (defined by opposing trajectory shifts between the HC vs. T2DM and T2DM vs. T2DM-CAD transitions) — may help elucidate the dynamic metabolic reprogramming that occurs during CAD development in individuals with T2DM (Fig. 4). Notably, four of the escalating metabolites (N2248, N3878, N4100, N2171) demonstrated significant positive correlations with glycemic indices (GLU and HbA1c) as well as CAD severity, including degree of coronary stenosis and severity of cardiac symptoms. These observed associations may suggest a potential mechanistic link between these metabolites, chronic hyperglycemia, and accelerated atherosclerosis. As such, they could serve as potential biomarkers for CAD risk stratification in T2DM patients, potentially aiding in the identification of at-risk individuals prior to clinical manifestation.

In contrast, the majority of CAD de-escalating metabolites showed inverse correlations with CAD severity. In particular, ricinoleic acid and tetranor-12(R)-HETE exhibited the strongest negative associations with severity of coronary stenosis. These lipid species, which have been implicated in the modulation of inflammatory pathways, were elevated in T2DM relative to HC but significantly reduced in T2DM_CAD. This observed downregulation may reflect either the influence of pharmacological interventions (e.g., statin or anti-inflammatory therapy) or compensatory metabolic adaptations in response to established atherosclerotic disease. Their inverse relationship with stenosis severity could suggest a potential protective role or possible utility as indicators of disease stabilization. However, longitudinal studies would be needed to validate their prognostic relevance and to further explore whether these metabolic shifts are causally involved in disease progression or instead represent secondary epiphenomena.

Machine learning models integrating clinical indicators and metabolites—particularly SVM and Random Forest—achieved high accuracy in distinguishing T2DM_CAD from controls, with CAD-specific metabolites (e.g., glycylproline, cortisol) and glycemic markers (HbA1c, GLU) emerging as key contributors. Notably, these models appeared to outperformed traditional lipid markers (e.g., LDL-C), which did not shown significant elevation in T2DM_CAD (Table 1)—likely due to the widespread use of lipid-lowering therapies.​ While classification accuracy for T2DM alone was more modest (70–86.8%), the relatively strong performance in detecting CAD supports the possibility that the identified metabolite panel could aid in earlier CAD identification among individuals with T2DM. This may hold clinical relevance, as many patients with T2DM remain asymptomatic until CAD progresses to an advanced stage—underscoring the potential value of more sensitive biomarkers for guiding timely preventive interventions.

In contrast to prior metabolomics studies of CAD— which typically analyze mixed CAD populations without stratification by diabetes status [13, 15, 16], our three-cohort design (HC → T2DM-only → T2DM-CAD) was structured to help identify metabolic features potentially specific to CAD in the context of pre-existing T2DM. This approach enabled the detection of metabolites exhibiting “escalation” or “de-escalation” trends across the disease continuum. Clinically, our machine learning model demonstrated encouraging discriminative performance in distinguishing T2DM-CAD from T2DM-only individuals (AUC > 95% in our cohort), a level of performance that appears broadly comparable to — and in some aspects may exceed — that reported for general CAD prediction models in the literature. These findings may tentatively suggest potential utility in cardiovascular risk stratification among T2DM patients; however, broader validation in larger, independent cohorts would be essential before any consideration of clinical translation.

Building upon our metabolomic findings and proposed functional validation studies, our future research will seek to explore whether these insights might inform potential therapeutic strategies for CAD in patients with T2DM. First, we intend to investigate whether dietary restriction of BCAAs, or pharmacologic modulation of BCAA catabolic enzymes, could potentially ameliorate vascular dysfunction or attenuate plaque progression in preclinical models. Second, we plan to examine whether supplementation with DGLA — either alone or in combination with prostaglandin E1 analogs — might help restore anti-inflammatory signaling and support endothelial barrier function in vivo. Third, we aim to assess the feasibility of modulating the glutamine–glutamate axis — for example, through glutamine repletion or targeted regulation of glutamine synthetase — as a possible approach to enhance nitric oxide bioavailability. By integrating these exploratory interventions with multi-omics profiling (metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics), we hope to gain a more systems-level perspective on the pathogenesis of CAD in the context of T2DM. Such efforts may, in time, contribute to the identification of novel, more personalized approaches for managing cardiovascular risk in high-risk individuals — though substantial validation and translational work would remain necessary.

Limitations

Our pathway analysis, conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0, is subject to important interpretative limitations, as noted by Lee et al. [56], which may constrain the biological interpretability of our findings. First, metabolites within enriched pathways often show opposing concentration trends (e.g., some elevated, others reduced). Without metabolic flux data, it remains unclear whether these shifts reflect pathway activation, impaired utilization, or compensatory regulation. Second, plasma metabolite profiles lack tissue resolution. For example, enrichment in “BCAA biosynthesis” — inferred from elevated plasma BCAAs — may reflect reduced peripheral catabolism (e.g., in muscle or heart), not increased hepatic synthesis. Similarly, changes in “n-6 fatty acid metabolism” could stem from adipose tissue (less CAD-relevant) or vascular endothelium (directly relevant); our data cannot distinguish between these sources. Third, many metabolites map to multiple pathways (e.g., glutamic acid in both “arginine biosynthesis” and the “citrate cycle”), introducing subjectivity in pathway prioritization and weakening causal inference. Critically, it should be emphasized that our analysis identifies only statistical enrichment of metabolites within annotated pathways, not actual functional activation or inhibition of those pathways.

The incomplete metabolite identification, resulting from limited DDA MS2 coverage across all samples, affects the confidence of annotations and may have excluded potentially important novel or low-abundance metabolites. Given that over 95% of the detected unannotated peaks were omitted from further analysis, it is likely that additional metabolic pathways—particularly those involving low-abundance or less commonly studied metabolites—remain unexplored in the current dataset.

The cross-sectional study design inherent to this work precludes any causal inference between metabolites and disease progression. To address this, future longitudinal studies are essential to track metabolic trajectories from the onset of T2DM to the development of CAD, while mechanistic investigations should further explore how CAD-specific metabolites—especially those displaying dynamic escalation or de-escalation patterns—contribute to atherogenesis in the context of diabetes. Additionally, two key data-related limitations merit attention: the lack of detailed medication history and comorbidity data, which may confound metabolic profiles and hinder our ability to fully adjust for these critical clinical variables; and the substantially smaller sample size of the T2DM-only group (n = 50) compared to other cohorts. While rigorous cross-validation and permutation testing were employed to mitigate bias, this discrepancy in sample size remains a potential source of statistical bias.

Beyond study design and data constraints, the clinical utility of the identified metabolites is further limited by their lack of disease specificity. Although the T2DM- and CAD-associated metabolites demonstrated statistically significant associations with their respective conditions in our cohort, they participate in fundamental biological processes (e.g., oxidative stress, lipid metabolism) and may also be altered in other clinical contexts, such as obesity or non-diabetic atherosclerosis. Moreover, metabolite concentrations are inherently dynamic, susceptible to fluctuations driven by non-disease factors (e.g., dietary intake, circadian rhythm) as well as pharmacological interventions. Collectively, these issues mean the utility of these metabolites (whether used individually or in combination) for distinguishing T2DM patients with versus without CAD remains preliminary. To validate their specificity, reproducibility, and translational potential, future studies should employ larger, multi-center, ethnically diverse cohorts with stringent phenotyping—with particular attention to controlling for concurrent inflammation, medication use, and recent dietary changes.

Conclusions

In this study, we systematically disentangled metabolic alterations specific to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) from those uniquely associated with coronary artery disease (CAD) in the context of T2DM. This distinction represents a critical advance over prior investigations, which frequently conflated diabetes-related metabolic perturbations with those directly implicated in cardiovascular pathogenesis. The identified CAD-specific metabolites and the escalation/de-escalation features of CAD risk represent promising candidates for further studies of disease progression. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning models demonstrates promising utility for the discrimination between HC, T2DM, and T2DM_CAD. While these findings advance the understanding of the metabolic interplay driving cardiovascular complications in diabetes, we acknowledge that external validation in independent, multi-center cohorts remains essential to confirm generalizability. Collectively, these findings enhance our understanding of the metabolic interplay driving cardiovascular complications in diabetes and provide potential candidates for developing personalized risk stratification and preventive interventions in this high-risk population.
