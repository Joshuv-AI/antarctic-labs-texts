Factors Affecting Changes in CD4+ T-cell Count Among People Living with HIV (PLWH): A Retrospective Cohort Study in Kermanshah, Iran
Authors: Ayda Mohammadnezamian, Keyghobad Ghadiri, Mosayeb Rostamian, Leila Solouki, Manizheh Azadian, Bahare Andayeshgar, Shahla Shahbazi, Roya Chegene Lorestani, Etrat Javadirad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveConsidering the crucial role of CD4+ T-cell counts in disease control and management, the present study aimed to investigate changes in CD4+ T-cell counts among People Living with HIV (PLWH) at the Behavioral Diseases Counseling Center in Kermanshah Province. The study also assessed the impact of demographic and clinical factors, including age, gender, duration of infection, duration of antiretroviral therapy (ART), and CD4+ T-cell count, on changes in CD4+ T-cell counts during ART among PLWH.MethodsIn this retrospective study, the medical records of 2909 PLWH who had attended the Kermanshah Behavioral Diseases Counseling Center between 2012 and 2023 were reviewed. Demographic, behavioral, and clinical data were extracted from the records. Ultimately, 728 individuals whose CD4+ T-cell counts were documented both at diagnosis and after treatment were included in the analysis. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21. As CD4+ T- cell count changes were not normally distributed, robust linear regression was employed. Collinearity among independent variables and autocorrelation of errors were assessed and confirmed. The assumption of homoscedasticity of errors was also evaluated and satisfied.ResultsAmong the 728 PLWH, 519 (71.3%) were male and 209 (28.7%) were female. The mean age of the PLWH was 37.2 ± 11.5 years. The mean CD4+ T-cell count at diagnosis was 336.4 cells/µL, which increased to 526.4 cells/µL after treatment. The average duration of treatment was 57.8 months (SD = 34.5), and the average number of follow-up visits was 48.6 (SD = 36.9). Robust linear regression, with ART duration entered as a four-level category, showed that longer ART duration (1–5 years: B=111.9; 5–10 years: B=267.5; >10 years: B=527.3, all vs <1 year, p<0.001), higher education (B=302.6, 95% CI: 171.5–433.7), older age (B=5.3, 95% CI: 2.4–8.3), and sexual transmission (B=101.6, 95% CI: 18.0–185.2) were significantly associated with improved CD4+ T-cell recovery (p<0.05). Female sex, more advanced clinical stage (III–IV), and certain occupational categories were also significantly associated with CD4+ T-cell recovery, while a longer duration of infection was associated with lower CD4+ T-cell recovery (all p<0.05).ConclusionConsistent with the well-established effect of antiretroviral therapy on CD4+ T-cell recovery, this study suggests that immune recovery is influenced by both treatment duration and patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics. These findings emphasize the importance of individualized HIV care, with closer monitoring and adherence support for patients at risk of poorer immune recovery.Keywords: HIV, CD4+ T cell counts, retrospective, Iran

Introduction

HIV, the virus that causes acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), has a relatively low prevalence in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region; however, the number of infections is increasing. According to the UNAIDS 2024 report, the number of new HIV infections increased from 12,000 in 2010 to 23,000 in 2024, while AIDS-related deaths have declined only slightly. Currently, an estimated 240,000 people in the region are living with HIV, but fewer than two-thirds are aware of their HIV status, and fewer than half are receiving treatment.1 In Iran, approximately 40,000 people were living with HIV in 2024; in the same year, 2100 new infections and 1700 AIDS-related deaths were occurred. Thus, HIV remains a global public health challenge.2

HIV specifically targets and destroys CD4+ T-cells, and the progressive depletion of these cells is a major contributor to disease progression and immune system dysfunction.3 CD4+ T-cells play a central role in immune homeostasis by coordinating adaptive immune responses, including assisting B cells in antibody production, activating macrophages, and regulating the recruitment of immune cells through cytokine production. These cells are essential for host defense against a wide range of pathogens. CD4+ T-cells originate in the bone marrow, mature in the thymus, and circulate in peripheral blood to maintain immune function. In healthy adults, CD4+ T-cell counts typically range from approximately 500 to 1500 cells/mm3. HIV infection gradually depletes these cells, leading to impaired immune responses and increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections. Clinically, a CD4+ T-cell count below 200 cells/mm3 is associated with severe immunosuppression and an AIDS-defining condition.4,5

The use of combination ART has had a significant impact on controlling the progression of HIV infection. This approach has not only helped control the course of the disease but has also increased CD4+ T-cell counts and reduced viral replication. As a result, morbidity and mortality among PLWH have been significantly reduced.6

CD4+ T-cell count and viral load (VL), as reliable markers for monitoring disease progression and predicting treatment outcomes, play a crucial roles in HIV management and assist both physicians and PLWH in making informed treatment decisions.7

These indicators can be affected by a variety of factors, including co-infection with other viruses, racial and geographical differences, genetic characteristics, pregnancy, stress levels, treatment adherence, individual lifestyle and habits, age, primary source of infection, substance use, cancer, use of immunosuppressive drugs, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare services.8,9

Understanding regional conditions is essential for healthcare teams to develop strategies that optimize treatment outcomes. However, to date, no study has investigated the factors influencing CD4+ T-cell counts among PLWH in this region. In our previous study investigating factors influencing survival in PLWH, the results demonstrated that CD4+ T-cell count plays a crucial role in survival, with a low CD4+ T-cell count being one of the key factors associated with an increased risk of mortality.10 Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective study to evaluate changes in CD4+ T-cell counts among PLWH in Kermanshah, western Iran. The findings of this study provide new and specific insights into the factors that contribute to immune system enhancement and may be useful in optimizing antiretroviral treatment strategies and improving treatment outcomes among PLWH in Kermanshah and similar regions.

Method

This retrospective study was conducted among PLWH who attended the Kermanshah Behavioral Diseases Counseling Center between 2012 and 2023, including a total of 2909 individuals. The inclusion criteria were confirmed HIV/AIDS diagnosis and availability of CD4+ T-cell counts at the time of diagnosis (baseline) and the most recent follow-up CD4+ T-cell count after initiation of antiretroviral therapy, as recorded in medical records. The follow-up period ranged from 1 to 194 months, with a mean of 48.6 months. PLWH with incomplete medical records were excluded from the study. Specifically, patients with only a single available CD4+ T-cell measurement were excluded, and only those with both baseline and post-treatment CD4+ T-cell values were included in the final analysis. Data were extracted on age, gender, duration of infection, duration of ART, baseline CD4+ T-cell count, and the most recent post-treatment CD4+ T- cell count.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables, such as age and CD4+ T-cell count, were summarized using the mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data and the median and interquartile range (IQR) for non‑normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Given the non‑normal distribution of ΔCD4+, a linear model was fitted with heteroscedasticity‑consistent (Huber–White sandwich) standard errors, implemented via the Robust Covariance Estimator option of the SPSS GENLIN procedure (SPSS v21). Collinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), which indicated no multicollinearity. Additionally, the Durbin–Watson test was used to assess the assumption of error autocorrelation, yielding a value of 1.986, which indicates the absence of autocorrelation. Because records were extracted and analyzed in a fixed chronological order, the Durbin–Watson statistic was used as a general diagnostic for residual autocorrelation that could reflect unmodeled secular trends over the 2012–2023 study period. The assumption of constant error variance (homoscedasticity) was also examined. A scatter plot of residual values against predicted values showed no pattern, indicating that the error variance was constant.

As this was a retrospective analysis of existing records, no a priori power calculation was performed; however, the analytic sample (N=495–728 depending on covariate completeness) exceeds established minimum-sample-size heuristics for multivariable regression with 24 parameters (Green, 1991; Table S1).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21, with a p‑value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethical Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1402.231).

Results

In this study, all medical records of individuals diagnosed with HIV/AIDS who had attended the Behavioral Diseases Counseling Center in Kermanshah Province were reviewed. A total of 2909 records were available, of which 728 PLWH with documented CD4+ T-cell counts both at diagnosis and after treatment were included in the analysis.

The mean age of the participants was 37.2 years (SD=11.5). The majority of PLWH were male (71.3%) and unemployed (63.6%). Regarding marital status, 35.9% had never been married. Additionally, 41.8% of participants had a history of incarceration, and 62.6% had a history of substance use. The most common route of HIV transmission was injection drug use (48.6%). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) clinical staging, the majority of PLWH (70.2%) were in stage II of the disease (Table 1). The mean CD4+ T-cell count before treatment was 336.4 cells/µL (SD=309.5), which increased to 526.4 cells/µL (SD= 409.3) following treatment.

To further explore the effect of treatment duration on immune recovery, ART duration was stratified into four clinically relevant categories: <1 year, 1–5 years, 5–10 years, and >10 years. Among the 728 PLWH included in the study, 627 had complete data on ART duration and were included in this subgroup analysis. The remaining 101 participants had missing ART-duration data and were therefore excluded from this descriptive analysis. As shown in Table 3, CD4+ T-cell recovery increased progressively with longer ART exposure. Participants treated for less than one year showed virtually no improvement in CD4+ T-cell count (mean ΔCD4+=−12.6 cells/µL), whereas those treated for 1–5 years, 5–10 years, and more than 10 years demonstrated mean increases of 175.3, 358.7, and 502.3 cells/µL, respectively. Likewise, the median change in CD4+ T-cell count increased across treatment-duration categories, from −20.5 cells/µL in the <1-year group to 441.0 cells/µL in the >10-year group.

Multivariable Regression Analysis of Factors Affecting Changes in CD4+ T- Cell Count in PLWH

Education was one of the strongest predictors of changes in CD4+ Tcell counts. PLWH with higher level of education demonstrated the largest CD4+ recovery relative to those with no formal education (B=302.6, 95% CI:171.5 to 433.7, p<0.001). A graded, dose-dependent pattern was seen across lower educational levels: high school (B=186.1, 95% CI:83.2 to 289.0, p<0.001), secondary (p=0.009), and primary education (B=105.1, 95% CI:9.5 to 200.8, p=0.031), each compared with illiterate PLWH.

The route of transmission was also a significant predictor of immune response to treatment. Compared with PLWH who acquired HIV through injection drug use (IDU), those infected through sexual transmission showed significantly greater CD4+ T-cell recovery (B=101.6, 95% CI:18.0 to 185.2, p=0.017). Mother-to-child transmission was not significantly different from IDU (B=142.2, 95% CI: −119.1 to 403.5, p=0.286). Age also significantly influenced treatment response, with each additional year of age associated with an average increase of B=5.3 units in CD4+ T-cell count change (95% CI:2.4 to 8.3, p<0.001). PLWH on ART for 1–5 years, 5–10 years, and >10 years had significantly greater CD4+ T-cell recovery than those on ART <1 year (B=111.9, 267.5, and 527.3 cells/µL respectively; all p < 0.001.

The clinical status of PLWH at diagnosis was significantly associated with the degree of CD4+ T-cell recovery after treatment. Compared with PLWH in stage I, those in stage III (B=−193.7, 95% CI: −355.6 to −31.8, p=0.019) and stage IV (B=−230.9, 95% CI:−414.6 to −47.3, p=0.014) showed significantly smaller increases in CD4+ T-cell counts, indicating a poorer immunological response with more advanced disease at diagnosis. PLWH in stage II did not differ significantly from those in stage I (B=−86.0, 95% CI:−241.6 to 69.6, p=0.279).

Sex was significantly associated with CD4+ T-cell recovery, with female PLWH demonstrating a greater increase in CD4+ T-cell counts compared with males (B=−110.5 for males vs females, 95% CI:−200.0 to −21.1, p=0.015). Occupation was also associated with CD4+ T-cell recovery, with PLWH working in high-risk environments (B=229.6, 95% CI: 31.8 to 427.3, p=0.023) and those in other occupations (B=141.4, 95% CI: 14.9 to 267.9, p=0.028) showing significantly greater CD4+ recovery compared with laborers, while unemployed (p=0.091) and student (p=0.580) categories did not differ significantly from laborers.

History of addiction (p=0.441), incarceration (p=0.250), and marital status (all categories, p=0.69–0.84) did not show a significant effect on changes in CD4+ T-cell counts. History of extramarital sexual contact could not be evaluated in the multivariable model because of substantial missing data (45%) and was therefore excluded. In contrast, longer duration of HIV infection prior to CD4+ assessment was associated with smaller CD4+ T-cell recovery (B=−0.49 per month, 95% CI: −0.84 to −0.13, p=0.007). The wide range of follow-up visits (1–194) did not independently predict CD4+ T-cell recovery in the multivariable model (B=0.59, 95% CI:−0.21 to 1.37, p=0.147), indicating that once ART duration is accounted for, heterogeneity in visit frequency does not independently confound CD4+ T-cell recovery estimates (Table 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the factors associated with changes in CD4+ T-cell counts in PLWH after ART initiation using robust linear regression analysis.

Although the positive association between ART and CD4+ T-cell recovery is well established, the present study highlights the importance of education level, age, and HIV transmission route that influence response to treatment and thus contribute to improved immune recovery among PLWH.

The findings of this study indicated that PLWH with higher level of education demonstrated significantly greater CD4 T-cell recovery, which may be attributed to greater literacy and awareness, which may facilitate better treatment adherence. Similar findings were reported by Kalichman et al, who identified a strong and significant association between health literacy and treatment adherence; individuals with lower levels health literacy were more likely to take their medications irregularly or less frequently than those with higher level health literacy.11

In another study by the same author, individuals living with HIV with lower health literacy had lower CD4+ T-cell counts, higher VL, and were less likely to initiate ART.12 A European report also emphasized that PLWH with lower levels of education exhibited poorer responses to ART.13 In Woldesenbet’s study, the duration of ART initiation and educational status at primary and university levels were positively associated with changes in CD4+ T-cell counts, while factors such as hospitalization, poor adherence, WHO clinical stage II, and low baseline weight had negative effects.14

In addition, factors related to the mode of viral transmission also play a significant role in treatment response. In the present study, the route of transmission was identified as a significant predictor of immune response to therapy. Compared to PLWH who acquired the virus through injecting drug use (IDU), those whose route of transmission was sexual exhibited a significant improvement in CD4+ T-cell counts. This finding is consistent with the results of a study by Suarez-Garcia et al, who reported that PLWH infected with HIV through injecting drug use are at higher risk of late presentation, delayed initiation of ART, and progression to AIDS and death compared to those infected via sexual transmission.15 Similarly, a cohort study by Keiser et al in Switzerland demonstrated that single migrants, older heterosexual men, and drug users exhibited poorer responses to treatment, were more likely to discontinue therapy, and had higher mortality rates compared to older homosexual men.16 These findings are consistent with the results of a large study by Murray et al, which was conducted in Canada, Europe, and the United States and showed that HIV-infected people with a history of IDU experienced higher rates of AIDS and mortality after starting combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) compared with non-IDU PLWH. Although baseline CD4+ T-cell counts were similar between IDU and non-IDU at the start of treatment, CD4+ T cell counts remained significantly lower among IDUs at 6 and 36 months after treatment. These differences were attributed to co-infection with hepatitis C virus (HCV) and lower adherence to treatment among IDUs.17 Therefore, the association observed in the present study may reflect underlying behavioral and clinical differences between transmission groups rather than a direct effect of transmission route on immune recovery.15–17

The age was another variable significantly associated with immune recovery, as older PLWH showed a greater average increase in CD4+ T-cell counts, 5.3 units per year of age. This finding is consistent with a meta-analysis by Ghidei et al, which showed that older PLWH had better adherence to ART and less likely to discontinue treatment compared with younger PLWH. This factor may play an important role in improving treatment outcomes among older PLWH.18 Silverberg et al also reported that although PLWH aged 50 years or older were more likely to experience side effects from the drug, they demonstrated high treatment adherence. This high adherence was associated with better viral suppression and compensated for their weaker CD4+ T-cell response compared with younger PLWH.19 In contrast, the study by Fiseha et al showed that older age, male gender, and initial CD4+ T-cell count were the dominant factors in poor recovery of CD4+ T-cell count.20 Similarly, Kaufmann et al reported that older age, lower baseline CD4 count at treatment initiation, and longer duration of HIV infection were significantly associated with having a CD4+ T-cell count below 500 cells/µL.21 Therefore, the results of these studies show that although older PLWH may have a weaker immunologic response than younger PLWH, higher treatment adherence reported in previous studies can compensate for this deficiency.

Also, the duration and continuity of treatment are key factors in improving CD4+ T-cell counts. The findings of this study indicated that CD4+ T- cell counts increased significantly after the initiation of ART compared to pre-treatment levels. Regression analysis also showed a clear, stepwise positive effect of treatment duration on CD4+ T-cell recovery, with PLWH treated for 1–5, 5–10, and >10 years demonstrating significantly greater increases in CD4+ T‑cell counts compared with those treated for less than one year (B = 111.9, 267.5, and 527.3 cells/µL, respectively; all p < 0.001). This upward trend is consistent with the findings of Bishop et al, who reported that CD4+ T-cell counts in PLWH receiving continuous ART continued to increase for at least 10 years.22 Similarly, Oliveira et al showed that PLWH with high adherence was associated with higher CD4+ T-cell counts and undetectable VL.23 The results of the study by Kaufmann et al showed that 39% of PLWH treated with ARTs were able to achieve an adequate CD4+ T-cell level (500 or more). Treatment interruption was one of the negative factors affecting CD4+ T-cell recovery.21 The results of these studies actually emphasize the importance of ongoing treatment.

Disease progression stage can influence the magnitude of the immune response. accordingly, the findings of this study demonstrated an inverse relationship between clinical stage and CD4+ T-cell response. These differences were statistically significant, indicating that PLWH in more advanced clinical stages (III and IV) had a significantly poorer immunological response to treatment than those diagnosed at stage I disease. This result was consistent with the results of other studies, For instance, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia by Edathodu et al reported a correlation between clinical disease stage and CD4+ T-cell counts, showing a progressive decrease in the mean CD4+ T- cell count from stages I to IV.24 Baveewo et al also found a significant inverse correlation between CD4+ T-cell counts and WHO clinical stages, with CD4+ T-cell numbers significantly decreasing as clinical progression advanced.25 A study conducted in Iran by Afrashteh et al demonstrated that the clinical stage of the disease is a significant factor influencing CD4+ T-cell counts.26 The results of these studies indicate that PLWH in advanced clinical stages may have poorer CD4+ T-cell recovery.

Finally, gender differences were also observed in treatment response. In this regard, the results of the present study showed that female PLWH had a significantly greater increase in CD4+ T-cell counts than male PLWH (B=−110.5 for male vs female, 95% CI:−200.0 to −21.1, p=0.015). This finding is consistent with several previous studies demonstrating a better immunologicresponse to ART among women. For example, the study by Maman et al reported that women had better long-term responses to ART and reached optimal CD4+ T-cell counts more quickly.27 Maskew et al also found that women consistently showed a better immune response than men.28 Similarly, Bastard et al, in a long-term study of PLWH receiving HAART, observed that women had better CD4+ T-cell recovery and lower mortality compared with men.29 This may be due to the unequal gender distribution in the study population and other unmeasured factors.

This study suggests that targeted monitoring and supportive interventions, particularly for individuals with lower level educational attainment, younger age, and a history of injecting drug use, may improve ART outcomes and CD4+ T-cell recovery when combined with strengthened patient education, adherence counseling, and structured follow-up strategies. In addition, routine consideration of demographic and behavioral factors may help identify high-risk patients and facilitate early, targeted interventions. These findings may also support HIV care programs in western Iran and similar settings.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective nature and the inclusion of PLWH with CD4+ T-cell counts measured at baseline and after treatment. Although this design allowed for comparison of changes in CD4+ T-cell counts before and after ART initiation, it limited the ability to assess precise longitudinal trajectories of CD4+ T- cell recovery over time.

In addition, plasma viral load, virological suppression status, ART regimen/class, treatment interruptions, and antiretroviral drug-resistance data were not consistently available in the medical records and therefore could not be included as covariates in the analysis. Consequently, the observed associations with CD4+ T-cell recovery should be interpreted in the absence of adjustment for these important virological and treatment-related factors. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive virological monitoring together with detailed treatment information are warranted to better characterize the determinants of CD4+ T-cell recovery.

Conclusion

Consistent with the well-established effect of antiretroviral therapy on CD4+ T-cell recovery, this study suggests that immune recovery is influenced by both treatment duration and patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics. These findings emphasize the importance of individualized HIV care, with closer monitoring and adherence support for patients at risk of poorer immune recovery.
