Endovenous reconstruction of congenital iliocaval anomalies
Authors: Joel Hlavaty, Grant Hubbard, Justin Smith, Karem Harth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis series demonstrates the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of endovascular reconstruction in three patients with congenital atresia of the inferior vena cava (IVC) or iliac veins.MethodsThis is a case series of three patients with central venous anomalies treated at a tertiary referral center. Two patients presented with acute deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and a third patient presented with chronic venous claudication and pelvic discomfort. All patients were categorized based on the 2021 SVP Classification based on their Symptoms, location of Varices, and the underlying Pathophysiology. The etiologies and endovenous management strategies are discussed in detail.ResultsTechnical success was achieved in all cases, with patency maintained on follow-up duplex and cross-sectional imaging. Two patients presented with symptomatic lower extremity DVT in the setting of segmental IVC atresia, whereas another patient presented with venous claudication and long-segment common iliac vein atresia with duplicated IVC. The two patients who initially presented with DVT were discharged on therapeutic Lovenox and clopidogrel after stenting, whereas one patient who did not present with thrombosis was discharged on clopidogrel. All patients achieved resolution of lower extremity pain, swelling, and venous claudication.ConclusionsThrough careful selection and understanding of the anatomy, etiology, and management strategies, patients can be successfully reconstructed with an endovascular approach.

Central venous anomalies leading to atresia are rare conditions in which components of a patient’s central venous structures (ie, inferior vena cava [IVC], iliac veins, and subclavian veins) develop poorly after embryonal development.1 The pathogenesis may arise in the setting of chronic perinatal thrombosis/fibrosis of the central veins or as a failure of fusion, formation, or involution of embryonic venous structures.2 Patients with central venous anomalies may live for decades without knowledge of their aberrant anatomy because of the development of rich collateral venous drainage and/or compensated venous beds.3,4 Case reports over the past 3 decades describe a variety of presentations with or without the presence of an associated deep venous thrombosis (DVT). Anomalies of the iliac veins may develop by the same mechanism as IVC anomalies, though there is less available literature, potentially because of a larger proportion of patients remaining asymptomatic or the lesion being misidentified as a post-thrombotic-related occlusion.

There is no current consensus on management of these patients because of the overall low frequency of symptomatic congenital central venous anomalies. Open surgical management has been described but presents significant morbidity with reports of early thrombosis and suboptimal long-term patency.5,6 With advances in techniques and dedicated devices, stenting of the IVC and iliac veins for reconstruction has become increasingly adopted with good safety, technical, and patency results.7, 8, 9 This report discusses, with their consent, the details of three patients with central venous anomalies: two involving the IVC and one the LCIV. Description of cases

Patient 1: SVP classification: S3c, V1, P IVC,O,C; BCIV,O,T; BEIV,O,T

The patient is an otherwise healthy female in her 20s presenting to the emergency department with 1 week of left greater than right leg pain, swelling, and numbness. She was recently started on oral contraceptives but otherwise had no significant medical history nor preceding symptoms. Venous duplex diagnosed bilateral extensive lower extremity DVT from the external iliac vein (EIV) to the tibial veins. Computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast demonstrated thrombosis through both common iliac veins extending cephalad to the level of the pararenal IVC, followed by a long atretic segment from the pararenal to the retrohepatic IVC. Hypercoagulable workup was negative. Presentation and workup were consistent with a congenital venous anomaly with DVT development in the setting of oral contraceptive pill onset.

Oral contraceptives were stopped, and a heparin drip and lower extremity compressions were initiated. Given the extent of thrombosis and persistent limb pain, percutaneous venous thrombectomy and endovenous reconstruction were offered to the patient. Access points included the right internal jugular (RIJ) and bilateral popliteal veins. Venography and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) demonstrated a markedly atretic retrohepatic IVC with occlusion of the suprarenal and pararenal IVC and extensive IVC and bilateral iliofemoral thrombosis (Fig 1). Following wire crossing of the lesion, bilateral suction thrombectomy was performed with good results. The IVC atresia was crossed in an antegrade fashion, and initial angioplasty created a channel for further work completed over an Amplatz wire (Boston Scientific). The recanalized IVC was serially dilated with noncompliant balloon angioplasty to 14 mm. Due to a lack of appropriate stent availability, the decision was made to bridge the patient with enoxaparin as an inpatient until adequately sized stenting was acquired.Fig 1Intraoperative fluoroscopy images of atretic inferior vena cava (IVC) and crossing the occlusion. IJV, Internal jugular vein.

Intraoperative fluoroscopy images of atretic inferior vena cava (IVC) and crossing the occlusion. IJV, Internal jugular vein.

She was brought back to the operating room for procedural completion within 72 hours. A venogram and IVUS showed residual subacute thrombosis at the renal confluence that was not cleared with previous suction thrombectomy. Mechanical thrombectomy was performed through bilateral common femoral vein (CFV) access with improved luminal clearance on IVUS. Balloon and stent sizing were based on IVUS measurement of inflow and outflow patent IVC.11 Prestenting and poststenting dilation was performed with a 20-mm noncompliant balloon followed by stenting of the IVC with a 20 × 150 mm Abre venous dedicated stent (Medtronic). Stented segments included the infrarenal IVC to the retrohepatic IVC just prior to the hepatic vein confluence. Enoxaparin and clopidogrel were initiated in the recovery area, and she was discharged that day.

Surveillance consisted of clinical visits and noninvasive duplex imaging to evaluate venous and stent patency. Duplex at 1 month showed respirophasic flow throughout the IVC stent and bilateral iliofemoral segments. Enoxaparin was replaced by oral apixaban, and Plavix was stopped after 3 months. Follow-up CT of the abdomen and pelvis within 1 year demonstrated a patent IVC stent and bilateral iliofemoral segments (Fig 2). At current 2-year follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic with a patent IVC stent.Fig 2Follow-up computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrating patency of the inferior vena cava (IVC) stent.

Patient 2: SVP classification: S3c, V1, PIVC,O,C, PIVC,O,T, PBCIV,O,T

A woman in her 20's presented with 2 days of bilateral leg pain, numbness, and swelling. She had a history of mild lower extremity swelling managed with compression stockings and started oral contraceptives 1 week prior for management of polycystic ovarian syndrome. She had a body mass index of 44 but had no other identifiable risk factors for DVT. Duplex ultrasound on presentation demonstrated extensive bilateral lower extremity DVT, and CT abdomen and pelvis showed atresia of the IVC with renal hilar varices and a prominent hemiazygos and azygos vein. Presentation and workup were consistent with a congenital venous anomaly with DVT development in the setting of oral contraceptive pill initiation.

She was initially managed with 48 hours of catheter-directed thrombolysis with the goal of clearing acute thrombus and restoring her native outflow and collaterals. Given no limb threat, she was discharged home with the plan to monitor her symptoms, continue anticoagulation, compression, and initiate weight loss medications. Over the next 10 months, the patient successfully lost 55 lbs, and despite this and anticoagulation, she had persistent lower extremity pain and venous claudication. Repeat CT abdomen and pelvis and diagnostic venography demonstrated maintained patency of bilateral iliofemoral segments, reconstitution of the retrohepatic IVC near the hepatic vein confluence, and what appeared to be a long string sign remnant of her atretic IVC. She was offered endovenous reconstruction because of ongoing venous claudication and overall poor quality of life.

In the operating room, three-point access was obtained including the RIJ and bilateral femoral veins. Through-and-through wire access was obtained bilaterally, and balloon angioplasty was utilized to open up a channel and body floss the wires for maximal support. IVUS was performed, and the IVC was sequentially dilated up to 14 mm kissing balloon angioplasty, which was started at the retrohepatic IVC and carried down to the CFV. IVUS allowed for stent size selection based on normal outflow IVC measurements and defined landmarks for stent placement by identifying the profunda and femoral confluence for inflow and the hepatic vein confluence for outflow. Two 25 mm × 5 cm Cook Z stents were deployed (via the RIJ) caudal to the hepatic veins in the retrohepatic IVC segment and postdilated to 25 mm. Then, beginning at the CFV, we double barreled 14 mm stents into the Z-stents with 1 cm overlap and post dilated to 14 mm percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (Fig 3). Completion venogram and IVUS were satisfactory with resolution of collaterals. Enoxaparin and Plavix were started in the recovery area, and the patient was discharged home after an overnight observation.Fig 3Abre stents placed in bilateral iliac veins and then in kissing fashion up to the caval Cook Z-stent.

Abre stents placed in bilateral iliac veins and then in kissing fashion up to the caval Cook Z-stent.

Surveillance included duplex ultrasound at 2 weeks and then at 3, 6, and 12 months. She was transitioned to oral anticoagulation after 4 weeks on enoxaparin. She noted improvement in swelling and venous claudication but did have significant pain attributed to stent implantation, which was well managed with pain management consultation. Her stents remain patent at 1-year follow-up.

Patient 3: SVP classification: S2, S3c, V0, PLCIV,O,C

This patient is a female in her 70s who presented to the outpatient office with a 1-year history of left leg lifestyle-limiting venous claudication, pelvic discomfort, and dependent left lower extremity discoloration. Her medical history was notable for a remote lumbar fusion and a splenectomy for splenomegaly during an evaluation for suspected malignancy, which was ruled out. CT scan was performed and was notable for atresia of the entire length of the left common iliac vein (LCIV) and a duplicating IVC draining into the left renal vein directly from the left external iliac vein. A venogram demonstrated this and prominent left to right presacral vein drainage (Fig 4). Given her symptoms, endovenous reconstruction was offered. In the operating room, access points included the left femoral vein and RIJ vein. Retrograde crossing was unsuccessful. The wire was maintained in the IVC to serve as a target for successful antegrade crossing with a stiff-angled glidewire and a Berenstein catheter. An Amplatz support wire was exchanged, IVUS performed, and the iliofemoral segment was serially dilated to 14 mm with noncompliant balloons. A 14 × 150 mm Abre stent was deployed in the LCIV through EIV and then postdilated 1:1 to 14 mm balloon angioplasty (Fig 4). The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 1 on clopidogrel.

The same surveillance protocol was instituted as with prior patients, and follow-up at 12 months was notable for a patent stent with respirophasic flow throughout and complete resolution of the patient’s lower extremity and pelvic symptoms. The patient was switched to 81 mg daily aspirin at 3 months.

Discussion

Three cases of endovenous reconstruction for symptomatic iliocaval congenital atresias have been presented. The first two cases involved concomitant extensive DVT as the first presenting symptom leading to the diagnosis of their venous anomaly, whereas the third case presented with symptoms and a well-described LCIV atresia with a duplicating IVC. Each involved a stent-based reconstruction providing a technically and clinically successful result. This approach demonstrates that endovenous strategies offer a safe and effective solution among properly selected patients with central venous atresias.

Review of the embryogenesis of major central veins is key to understanding the classifications of the presented cases. There are four segments of the IVC: the hepatic, suprarenal, renal, and infrarenal segments.4,12,13 These develop from the fusion of three separate embryologic venous structures.3 The suprarenal segment is formed from the right subcardinal vein, the renal segment from a fusion of the right and left supracardinal veins, and the infrarenal segment from the right supracardinal vein. The hepatic segment forms as an extension of the right subcardinal vein. Because the IVC develops from multiple embryologic segments, it is uncommon to see agenesis of the entire length of the IVC, though it is described.12,13 The proximal iliac veins are formed from the right and left posterior cardinal veins, and atresia of these can occur as with the IVC. Our patient presentations were consistent with these patterns of one or multiple atretic venous segments corresponding to specific embryologic sources.

Much like two of our cases, patients with venous agenesis typically present as young (<30 years old) healthy adults with bilateral DVT and no hypercoagulable disorder or family history.14,15 Our third case presented later in life but did not have a thrombotic or injury history to explain her LCIV occlusion. In addition, she had a well-described venous variant with a duplicating IVC, pointing to an underlying congenital condition. Caval thrombosis can occur in the setting of IVC agenesis: 4% to 15% of patients with more distal DVT progress to thrombosis of the IVC.4 When patients with IVC agenesis develop DVT, more than 50% will have bilateral DVT, compared with less than 10% in patients with DVT and normal IVC.4 Diagnosis of central venous agenesis is typically achieved through a combination of venous duplex, CT scan, and conventional venography.3,14 Imaging to diagnose DVT and evaluate venous patency includes venous duplex of the lower extremities, whereas CT provides information regarding thrombus extension into the IVC or any congenital venous abnormality (independent of DVT diagnosis). Venography is critical in both diagnosis and therapeutic interventions. Principles of reconstruction in these patients currently follow the same principles applied among noncongenital cases. Patients should have adequate inflow and outflow vessels for stent landing. Vessel preparation with a 1:1 prestenting and poststenting percutaneous transluminal angioplasty is important for stent deployment and expansion. Reference vessel diameters and area have been previously described and serve to guide stent size selection.11, 16 IVUS becomes essential to anatomic evaluation as well as landing and stent sizing. Until 2026, there were no Food and Drug Administration-approved IVC stents, and in 2019, the first dedicated venous stents were approved for iliac and CFV treatment. Head-to-head comparisons of the various available stents for iliocaval reconstruction, nor their ideal technique for deployment, are available. These cases provide examples of some of the available described techniques. In our case, and specific to the IVC, stent sizing was based on outflow IVC sized by IVUS and stent availability at that time. As technology continues to evolve, larger studies evaluating reconstruction techniques will be important. Venoplasty of congenitally aplastic portions of the iliac veins or IVC does carry a risk of venous rupture; however, there is a dearth of literature related to this topic or its overall incidence and clinical significance. Although infrequent, IVC or iliac vein rupture during endovascular procedures can occur but generally does not produce hemodynamic instability and can be managed with balloon occlusion or stenting. In the case of hemodynamic instability, placement of uncovered stents restores inline low-pressure outflow, with the placement of a covered stent in scenarios of hemodynamic instability.

Case reports over the past several decades have described the management of congenital venous anomalies in a variety of ways. Nonoperative management with anticoagulation and compression has proven successful for a subset of patients with mild symptoms.13,16 Several earlier reports have described open venous bypasses for reconstruction, typically with ring-supported polytetrafluoroethylene.5,6,17 These bypasses may or may not be constructed with an iatrogenic arteriovenous fistula to augment forward flow and improve patency.5,6 The downside of these bypasses has included the morbidity of open surgery and inferior primary patency rates: as low as 53% at 41 months.5,6 In the era of endovascular therapy, percutaneous thrombectomy and thrombolysis are often used in the setting of acute DVT.1,2,12,18 Following thrombus removal, these patients may undergo endovascular deep venous reconstruction with angioplasty and/or stenting.8,19,20 Iliocaval reconstructions have reported rates of technical success in 80% to 90% of patients and primary patency rates of 85% to 90% at 2-year follow-up.7, 8, 9 Our patients underwent successful endovenous reconstruction with excellent clinical results and all with excellent primary patency rates to date.

Conclusions

In this report, we presented three symptomatic patients with congenital central venous anomalies successfully treated with endovenous reconstruction. Our experience with these cases is similar to that reported in the literature and highlights the advantages of an endovascular approach in the appropriately selected patient. Future work in this area will continue to evaluate the long-term outcomes and provide further insight into how patients will continue to benefit from evolving endovenous therapies.
