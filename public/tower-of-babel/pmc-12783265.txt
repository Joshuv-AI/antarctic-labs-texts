Ventilation and buffering capacity effects on ocean acidification in low oxygen environments
Authors: Liang Xue, Christopher Sabine, Jianfang Chen, Siv K. Lauvset, Qinsheng Wei, Kuiping Li, Wei-Jun Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ocean acidification results from oceanic uptake of anthropogenic CO2 (ΔCant). Weak carbonate buffering capacity (high Revelle factor, RF) amplifies acidification, but its role in persistently low-oxygen, poorly ventilated regions is unclear. Here we compare preindustrial to present changes in partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2), hydrogen ion concentration ([H+]), pH, aragonite saturation state (Ωara), and RF within permanent oxygen minimum zones (OMZs) versus well-ventilated regions. We find that acidification is negligible in the least-ventilated, poorly buffered lower OMZs, but detectable in moderately ventilated upper OMZs. In upper OMZs, pCO2 and [H+] increase faster while Ωara, pH, and RF change more slowly than in adjacent well-ventilated regions. Our analysis reveals that limited ΔCant delivery by ventilation ultimately constrain acidification in low-oxygen regions. Accordingly, low-oxygen regions with poor ventilation will experience less acidification than well-ventilated regions, and different metrics (notably [H+] versus Ωara) respond distinctly due to their different definitions and sensitivities.

Introduction

The ocean plays an important role in alleviating the rise of carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration in the atmosphere and thus the rate of global warming, annually absorbing 25 ~ 30% of the global CO2 emissions by human activities (that is, anthropogenic CO2, CO2ant)1–4. At the same time, however, the ocean’s uptake of CO2ant (ΔCant) has induced a shift in the ocean carbonate chemistry, commonly referred to as ocean acidification5–9. This presents as increases in seawater CO2 partial pressure (pCO2) and hydrogen ion concentration ([H+]), simultaneously decreasing pH, carbonate ion concentration ([CO32−]) and the saturation state with respect to calcium carbonate minerals such as aragonite (Ωara). Acidification may exert negative influences on many marine organisms that inhabit surface and deep waters5–7, and alter global biogeochemical cycles that are important to ecosystem function and future climate changes8–10.

Ocean acidification is caused by ΔCant, but its rate of change is moderated by the initial state of the ocean carbonate system or buffering capacity (Fig. 1), which is often characterized by the Revelle factor (RF)11–15. A higher RF typically indicates a weaker buffering capacity and, for example, a stronger pH response to additional CO2 input11,16. Accordingly, many studies have shown that low-oxygen waters in both coastal and open ocean regions are more vulnerable to acidification due to their higher concentrations of biologically produced CO2 and lower buffering capacities11,16–21. While it is well justified to highlight the role of buffering capacities in amplifying the anthropogenic acidification, its role is not sufficiently understood, especially in regions with permanent low oxygen. This is because acidification results from the combined action of ΔCant and RF, which together produce varied and complex outcomes across the global ocean (Fig. 1). Indeed, in coastal hypoxic regions, ocean acidification can be greatly amplified11,16,18,22, as bottom waters are seasonally stagnant from spring to summer but ventilated during winter mixing, which brings CO2ant on an annual timescale, and biological CO2 addition is intense and rapid (Fig. 1). However, we argue that the poorly ventilated ocean interior oxygen minimum zones (OMZs) do not necessarily experience fast acidification due to the limited delivery of CO2ant23–25, despite their higher concentrations of biologically produced CO2 and lower buffering capacities26 (Fig. 1). This is because ventilation, broadly defined as the transport of surface water to depths27,28, plays a dominating role in the ocean’s uptake of CO2ant and, consequently, in driving acidification also in the interior ocean3,4,29–32. For example, in well-ventilated mode and intermediate waters, significant acidification due to faster downward transport of CO2ant has been documented through numerous observational and modeling studies31,33–37. Therefore, we expect that the acidification in the poorly ventilated OMZs will be quite limited. To better understand ocean acidification in low-oxygen marine environments with long ventilation timescales, we examine ΔCant-induced changes in a suite of acidification metrics, including pCO2, [H+], pH, Ωara, and RF (ΔpCO2, Δ[H+], ΔpH, ΔΩara, and ΔRF) within globally typical OMZs compared to well-ventilated regions along three meridional sections of the Pacific, Indian, and Atlantic Oceans (P16, I9N8S, and A16; see Supplementary Fig. 1) using the GLODAPv2.2016b mapped product by Lauvset et al.38. Although RF is a measure of seawater’s buffering capacity, it also changes in response to ΔCant inputs, albeit more complexly than the other four metrics (e.g., first increase to a maximum and then decline with CO2 addition39,40). Thus, we include it as part of the ocean acidification metrics. In this work, the OMZs are defined as regions where dissolved oxygen concentrations are below 90 µmol kg−1, which is a more lenient oxygen threshold for delineating the boundaries of OMZs23,41. Note that the OMZs, which comprise about 8% of the total oceanic area41, are also important for ocean biogeochemical cycles like nitrogen and carbon26,42,43, and could provide a habitat for organisms specifically adapted to low oxygen conditions, though they are considered inhospitable to most species44. In this study, we also utilize mean water age to characterize the relative strength of ocean ventilation in space, allowing us to assess the influence of ocean ventilation on ocean acidification. Our results reveal that since the Industrial Revolution, changes in acidification metrics are negligibly minor in the lower OMZs, whereas within the upper OMZs, they are detectable, with pCO2 and [H+] rising more rapidly but Ωara, pH, and RF showing slower changes than those in neighboring well-ventilated waters at similar depths. We demonstrate that even in poorly-buffered low-oxygen environments, acidification rates are still mainly limited by ΔCant through ventilation, and the different responses seen across acidification metrics arise from metric-specific chemical nature or definition (e.g., [H+] vs. Ωara). This view is more comprehensive than the hitherto research focused on moderately ventilated low oxygen and pH subsurface waters in the open ocean18–20,45,46 or seasonally ventilated hypoxic waters in the coastal oceans11,16,47,48, and may offer a more balanced and generalized understanding of the mechanisms underlying CO2ant-induced acidification in low-oxygen marine environment.

Acidification within OMZs versus well-ventilated waters

We calculated ΔpCO2, Δ[H+], ΔpH, ΔΩara, and ΔRF since the Industrial Revolution using data from the GLODAPv2.2016b mapped product by Lauvset et al38. We find that vertically, the spatial patterns of ΔCant and the resultant changes in these acidification metrics generally resemble those of the mean age of water masses, with well-ventilated regions exhibiting stronger acidification than more poorly ventilated regions (Fig. 2). In particular, we compare changes occurring within the OMZs with those in well-ventilated regions at similar water depth. For convenience, we define the upper OMZ as the part with water ages less than 300 years and the lower OMZ as the part with water ages greater than 300 years, since the upper part of the OMZ is younger than the lower part (Fig. 2a–c). Despite previously reported low-oxygen waters being more vulnerable to acidification due to lower buffering capacity11,16–21, we find that changes in all acidification metrics remain negligibly minor in the lower OMZs (Fig. 2j–x, Table 1). This is because here ventilation is restricted, with average water ages exceeding 550 years in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, and over 350 years in the Atlantic (Fig. 2a–c), resulting in minimal accumulation of CO2ant in the ocean (Fig. 2g–i). Specifically, along sections P16 and I9N8S, the average ΔCant values are 3 and 4 µmol kg−1 (Fig. 2g–h), respectively, which are close to its calculation uncertainty. Therefore, despite the very weak buffering capacity with average RF values ~ 18 (Fig. 2d, e), the changes in acidification metrics here are not significant. In the upper OMZs, due to moderate ventilation and thus moderate CO2ant accumulation, acidification occurs, but the magnitudes of acidification metrics are either lower or higher than those in well-ventilated waters and the upper 50 m of the ocean (Fig. 2g–x, Table 1). Specifically, the magnitudes of ΔΩara and ΔpH in the upper OMZs are lower than those observed in well-ventilated waters and the upper 50 m. For instance, the average water age in the upper OMZs (120–160 years) is older than that in the upper well-ventilated waters ( < 25 years) and at the upper 50 m (< 5 years, Fig. 2a–c), thus the average ΔCant values in the upper OMZs (18–24 µmol kg−1) are smaller than those in the upper well-ventilated waters (40–50 µmol kg−1) and at the upper 50 m ( ~ 60 µmol kg–1, Fig. 2g–i). Correspondingly, on average Ωara decreases by 0.11–0.19 units in the upper OMZs, while it drops by 0.34–0.45 units in well-ventilated waters and by 0.59–0.64 units at the upper 50 m (Fig. 2s–u). Notably, Ωara experiences a fast decline of 0.45 units in the upper well-ventilated waters of the North Atlantic (Fig. 2u), associated with the Subpolar Mode Water33,34. Coincidently, the average magnitude of pH decrease of 0.06 units in the upper OMZs is also smaller than that of 0.09–0.10 units in the upper well-ventilated waters and at the upper 50 m (Fig. 2p–r). In contrast, ΔpCO2 and Δ[H+] are greater in the upper OMZs than in both well-ventilated waters and the upper 50 m (Fig. 2j–o, Table 1). For example, along section P16, the mean increases of pCO2 and [H+] in the upper OMZ are 132 ± 48 µatm and 2.72 ± 0.99 nmol kg−1, respectively, indicating a faster rate compared with the upper well-ventilated waters, which show increases of 88 ± 9 µatm and 1.84 ± 0.18 nmol kg−1. The large changes in pCO2 and [H+] within the upper OMZs due to the amplification by weak buffering capacity are in good agreement with early studies in the eastern tropical Pacific oxygen-depleted waters19,20.

From the preindustrial era to the present, RF within the upper OMZs also has changed, but the mean ΔRF along section P16 is smaller (0.2 ± 0.4) than along sections I9N8S and A16 (0.8 ± 0.4 and 0.9 ± 0.3, respectively, Fig. 2v–x, Table 1). This difference arises because RF is a complex property— it is a ratio of two relative changes (αlnpCO2 / αlnDIC, DIC stands for dissolved inorganic carbon) and can increase to a maximum and then decline39,40. Although RF has reached that peak in some very poorly buffered coastal hypoxic waters (for example, Chesapeake Bay bottom waters11), it remains far below that level in most of the open ocean—except in a few extremely O2-depleted zones with unusually high RF (e.g., >18.8), such as parts of section P16 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We also find that when atmospheric CO2 reaches 560 parts per million (ppm) in the future —corresponding to an approximate threefold increase in ΔCant relative to present—the pattern of acidification in the OMZs remains similar to today’s, but the ΔCant signal and associated acidification will penetrate to greater depths (Fig. 3f–j). For example, currently a ΔCant of ~6 µmol kg−1 appears at 900 m, but after a threefold increase in ΔCant, a value of ~8 µmol kg−1 would instead appear at 1100 m (Fig. 3f). Also, in the future the upper OMZs will show much broader areas with negative ΔRF (Fig. 3j). Additionally, we observe that ΔpCO2 and Δ[H+] do not always track ΔCant. This mismatch appears across different waters as mentioned above—for example, between upper OMZs and well-ventilated regions (Fig. 2g–o, Table 1)—and also in the waters above the OMZs (Fig. 3). From the sea surface down to the OMZ upper boundary, the magnitudes of ΔCant, ΔΩara, and ΔpH generally decrease with depth, whereas the magnitudes of ΔpCO2 and Δ[H+] generally increase (Fig. 3f–i). In summary, ΔΩara and ΔpH almost always track ΔCant, while ΔpCO2 and Δ[H+] only sometimes do (e.g., in the lower OMZs). This unusual phenomenon may confound acidification assessments, which will be examined next in terms of the influence of ventilation, buffering capacity, and the specific chemical nature or definitions of acidification metrics.

Influences of ventilation, buffering capacity and sensitivities of acidification metrics

The correlation between ΔCant and water age indicates that ventilation, which carries surface waters rich in CO2ant into the ocean interior, predominantly governs the spatial pattern of ΔCant in the ocean interior (see the insert in Fig. 4a), consistent with previous reports3,4,29, even though air-sea exchange initially transfers CO2ant from the atmosphere into the ocean. Thus, it suggests that ventilation plays an essential role in influencing the changes of acidification metrics in the ocean interior. Specifically, both ΔΩara and ΔpH show a strong positive correlation with water age, with determination coefficients (r²) nearing 0.90 or greater (Fig. 4a, b), suggesting the dominant control of ocean ventilation or ΔCant over the declines in Ωara and pH and a relatively minor role for buffering capacity in these changes. However, the correlations of water age with ΔpCO2 and Δ[H+] are not as strong, with r² values around 0.60 but still statistically significant (Fig. 4c, d). This indicates that although ΔpCO2 and Δ[H+] are dominated by ocean ventilation, they are still influenced by buffering capacity. For example, two waters with similar ages of approximately 80 years and ΔCant values of around 29 µmol kg−1 demonstrate divergent acidification outcomes based on different initial RF values (Fig. 4). From the preindustrial era to 2002, waters with an RF of 14 experienced a pCO2 increase of 130 µatm, a [H+] increase of 2.50 nmol kg−1, decreases in pH and Ωara of 0.07 and 0.23 units, respectively. In contrast, waters with an RF of 19, with higher biologically produced CO2, experienced a pCO2 increase of 278 µatm, a [H+] increase of 5.75 nmol kg−1, and reductions in pH and Ωara of 0.10 and 0.14 units, respectively. To clearly see the interaction between ΔCant, which generally decreases with depth, and RF, which generally increases with depth (e.g., Δ[H+] = ΔCant × Huc), we consider the waters from the surface to the upper OMZs as a mixture between surface and deep waters (see “Methods”). The differences in sensitivity for these four acidification metrics are associated with the chemical nature or definitions of the metrics. As analyzed by Orr50, we find that these differences stem from the fact that waters with higher RF values have lower initial concentrations of CO32− and borate ions (B(OH)4−), and vice versa52 (see Supplementary Fig. 7). In waters with higher RF, less CO32− is available to neutralize the incoming CO2 and thus more CO2 is available to produce H⁺. Consequently, for equivalent ΔCant, there are smaller decreases in [CO32−] and Ωara and larger increases in pCO2 and [H⁺] in higher RF waters than in lower RF waters. Therefore, when RF values are higher, pCO2 and [H⁺] show greater sensitivity to CO2 additions, whereas Ωara shows weakened sensitivity. And pH must be less sensitive than [H+] due to the logarithmic definition of pH (pH = −log[H+]), as pointed out by Fassbender et al53,54. In reality, CO2-induced chemical reactions are more complex than described above. In the “Methods” section, we quantify the sensitivity differences among the four acidification metrics by calculating how seawater CO2 species and [H+] change after a 1 µmol kg−1 DIC addition.

Acidification mechanisms in well-ventilated versus poorly-ventilated waters

The contrasting mechanisms of acidification in well-ventilated waters—such as mode and intermediate waters—versus poorly-ventilated waters, such as the permanent OMZs, are illustrated in Fig. 6. Our analysis shows that vertically, the strength of ventilation controls the interior-ocean pattern of ΔCant and thus shapes the resulting acidification pattern. Ocean ventilation acts as a delivery mechanism, pumping CO2ant into deep waters and driving the interior-ocean acidification. Accordingly, significant acidification of the ocean interior has occurred in mode and intermediate waters31,33–37,46, which are generally well ventilated55. In the upper OMZs, where both ventilation and ΔCant levels are moderate, acidification is detected. However, the apparent rate of acidification is also associated with the choice of acidification metrics, as buffering capacity modulates each metric differently. If Ωₐᵣₐ and pH—two commonly used metrics—are considered, acidification appears slower in the upper OMZs than in the adjacent well-ventilated waters. In contrast, when pCO2 and [H⁺] are used, acidification appears more rapid in the upper OMZs. In the lower OMZs, where both ventilation and ΔCant are minimal, acidification is negligibly minor regardless of the metric used—highlighting that when no or little ΔCant is present, there will be limited acidification.

We therefore categorize acidification in low-oxygen waters into two types based on ventilation strength. The first type occurs in regions with high RF values and moderate to rapid ventilation—such as the upper OMZs and seasonal low-oxygen (including hypoxic) zones, where significant acidification arises from the interplay between ΔCant and low buffering capacity. For example, many coastal low-oxygen waters experience rapid acidification (Fig. 1), driven by elevated RF values and low buffering due to elevated CO2 concentration from biological respiration and/or enhanced solubility at low temperatures11,16,56,57. These waters are typically stratified in summer but well ventilated in winter through mixing with surface and offshore waters, facilitating the influx of CO2ant rich waters. The second type is characterized by similarly high RF values but limited ventilation. Acidification in the lower OMZs typifies this class, exhibiting slow and minimal change due to restricted ΔCant delivery. Furthermore, we propose that in the water column, if the increase in acidification caused by a weaker buffering capacity is larger than the decrease in acidification caused by weaker ventilation, then the observed trends in pCO2, [H+], and pH will track changes in RF or buffering capacity (Supplementary Fig. 8). Otherwise, the observed trends in these acidification metrics will instead track changes in ΔCant or ventilation. We can apply this principle to determine whether changes in acidification metrics track buffering capacity or ventilation.

While this study addresses the impact of the relative strength of ventilation in space on CO2ant-induced acidification in the ocean, future research should prioritize the role of temporal variability and shifts in ventilation intensity driven by climate change, such as enhanced stratification58, which has already been documented in multiple regions31,32,46,59–61. Furthermore, in real ocean, trends of acidification metrics are shaped not only by ΔCant, but also by climate- and human-driven processes like warming, eutrophication, and changes in winds and biological productivity62–67. As such, sustained and expanded observational efforts will be critical for accurately tracking the evolving nature of ocean acidification.

Our work also implies that the choice of acidification metrics can yield different and even conflicting understandings of acidification progression when their absolute changes are used. In our review of existing studies about the acidification influence on marine organisms and ecosystems, we find that nearly all four acidification metrics are used: some researchers focus on pH8,9,68, others on Ωara6,69,70, and still others on [H+]71 or pCO272,73, while some employ multiple metrics74. Yet, others even include RF21,40. This could pose a challenge for assessing ecological consequences, as the sensitivity of organisms, elemental cycles, and processes may vary depending on the metric. For instance, it is reported that the development and growth of some marine larval shells depend more on seawater Ωara than on pCO2 or pH75. In addition, it is recommended to consider whether expressing ocean acidification as a relative change from the initial state is more appropriate, since the metrics’ relative changes are much more similar than their absolute changes (Supplementary Fig. 6). Thus, it is essential to further investigate which acidification metrics most accurately reflect their impacts on marine organisms, ecosystems, and biogeochemical cycles in future research.

Methods

Data source

In this study, the data used to calculate changes in acidification metrics attributed solely to ΔCant come from the GLODAPv2.2016b mapped product, detailed by Lauvset et al38. This product is a mapped climatology of ocean biogeochemical variables generated using the Data-Interpolating Variational Analysis (DIVA) mapping method, based on the quality-controlled and internally consistent GLODAPv2 dataset76. It provides global climatologies at a resolution of 1° × 1°, covering 33 standard depth surfaces. The dataset includes not only salinity, temperature, oxygen, phosphate, silicate, and TA, but also includes ΔCant between 1800 and 2002, as well as DIC normalized to the year 2002. According to Lauvset et al38., the ΔCant since the Industrial Revolution was calculated using the transit time distribution (TTD) method77,78, based on chlorofluorocarbon-12 (CFC-12) observations available in GLODAPv2.

The mean age of water mass data from Jeansson et al.79, was utilized, where age refers to the time since a water parcel last interacted with the atmosphere. This age was calculated using the TTD method, as detailed by Waugh et al.80, applied to the GLODAPv2 data synthesis product76, based on measured concentrations of transient tracers such as CFC-12. For the TTD calculations, it was assumed that transient tracers were fully (100%) saturated upon subduction. This assumption may introduce a bias towards older ages, particularly in regions of dense water formation, where saturation is often less than 100%. Additionally, the solution to the Green’s function was assumed to follow an Inverse Gaussian distribution. The analysis also presumed a balance between advection and mixing, maintaining a unity ratio between the width and the mean age of the TTDs.

The difference in sensitivity of four acidification metrics (pCO2, [H+], pH, and Ωara) is fundamentally related to the chemical reactions induced by CO2 addition and the definitions of these metrics. This dissociation generates 959.405 nmol kg−1 of H⁺, of which 959.380 nmol kg−1 is consumed by CO32−, B(OH)4−, and other minor alkaline species. This consumption results in a decrease in [CO32−], leading to an Ωara reduction of 0.015 units. Importantly, different RF values (RF = αlnpCO2 / αlnDIC) also reflect variations in the concentrations of seawater CO32−and B(OH)4−, which subsequently influence the sensitivity of acidification metrics. With increasing RF values, concentrations of CO32− and B(OH)4− in water columns decline rapidly61, in the subsurface primarily due to enhanced biological CO2 production (see Supplementary Fig. 7). For equivalent additions of CO2*, lower [CO32−] in the water column diminishes the rightward progression of the reaction Eq. (4a), resulting in a slower decrease of [CO32−] and Ωara, as well as reduced consumption of H⁺. And the reduced H⁺ consumption does not favor the rightward movement of this chemical reaction (Eq. (3)), leading to higher concentrations of undissociated CO2*. This increase results in an elevated pCO2, as described by the equation pCO2 = CO2*/K0, where K0 is the CO2 solubility in seawater. Although waters with higher RF values exhibit a reduction in CO2 dissociation, producing less H⁺, the residual H⁺ concentration can remain elevated due to less consumption of H⁺. Thus, for equal additions of CO2, a rapid increase in [H⁺] is observed in higher RF waters. For instance, in the upper OMZ along section P16, where the average RF is 18 and [CO32−] is 61 µmol kg−1, adding 1 µmol kg−1 of CO2* leads to the dissociation of approximately 0.66 µmol kg−¹ of CO2*, leaving 0.34 µmol kg−1 undissociated, producing a larger pCO2 increase of ~7 µatm. The CO2 dissociation yields 657.734 nmol kg−1 of H⁺, with about 657.584 nmol kg−1 consumed by CO32−, B(OH)4−, and other minor alkaline species. Thus, the same addition of CO2 in the upper OMZ along section P16 results in a smaller Ωara reduction of 0.009 units, while the residual H⁺ concentration rapidly increases to 0.150 nmol kg−1, causing a pH increase of 0.0032 units. By comparing these two scenarios, we find that with higher RF values, both pCO2 and [H⁺] demonstrate greater sensitivity to CO2 additions compared to Ωara.

Simulating the influence of different metric sensitivity on acidification in the OMZs

To simplify our analysis, we conceptualize the waters from the surface to the OMZs as a mixture between surface (30 m) and deep waters (1000 m), allowing us to simulate the influence of different acidification metric sensitivities on acidification within the OMZs. We first computed the mixing values of DIC, TA, T, S, phosphate, and silicate using a two-endmember mixing model, as previously described by Xue et al93.
