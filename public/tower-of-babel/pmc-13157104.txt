A quantitative framework for subsurface uncertainty assessment using multidisciplinary geoscientific data
Authors: Konstantinos Chavanidis, Israa S Abu-Mahfouz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces the Integrated Model Coherence Index (IMCI), a novel conceptual framework for quantifying the reliability of multi-method subsurface geoscientific models. While joint inversion techniques focus on identifying a best-fit model that minimizes global error, they often show disagreements between independent datasets. The IMCI addresses this limitation by focusing on interpretational convergence, measuring how different data streams (geological/remote sensing, geophysical, and geochemical/borehole) predict the same subsurface properties, thereby transforming qualitative, multidisciplinary observations into a quantitative, risk-based metric. The output is a 3D confidence cube, in which each voxel is assigned a normalized reliability score. High-confidence zones indicate mutual reinforcement between data streams, while low-confidence areas highlight ambiguity. This can guide targeted data acquisition and help de-risk exploration in fields such as natural resource exploration and management, as well as hazard assessment.Keywords: Geosciences, Subsurface exploration, Data integration, Risk mitigation, Integrated model coherence index

Background

Subsurface characterization is the multidimensional determination of structural geometry, lithological composition, and the physical state of the Earth’s interior, as expressed by fluid content, thermal regimes, and stress fields. It has evolved from one-dimensional (1D) well-log analysis and traditional two-dimensional (2D) geological and geophysical profiles into sophisticated three-dimensional (3D) volumetric and four-dimensional (4D) time-lapse models. Single-method studies remain constrained by inherent limitations, though. For instance, geophysical datasets are often characterized by non-uniqueness and high signal-to-noise ratios, while geological and remote sensing interpretations are frequently limited by outcrop dependence and surface indications. Although joint inversion techniques are widely applied to mitigate these ambiguities, the final models frequently lack an objective measure of reliability due to the fundamental difference in resolution and sensitivity between different data types, particularly in cases where direct subsurface-truthing (through borehole drilling) is not available.

To address this challenge, the Integrated Model Coherence Index (IMCI) is introduced as a universal metric to quantify the reliability of the resulting integrated models. While conventional uncertainty quantification frameworks, such as Bayesian posterior probability, effectively communicate the non-uniqueness of models relative to a specific dataset, they often fail to effectively address situations where a model is consistent with the data mathematically but contradicts independent physical or geological information. The IMCI is a novel conceptual technique designed to quantify multi-method model uncertainty by integrating data across different scales, from regional satellite observations to point-source geochemical and borehole sampling, representing a necessary evolution in Earth Science data integration. Unlike existing joint inversion metrics that typically focus on minimizing a global objective function, the IMCI is introduced as a hierarchical meta-metric, shifting the analytical focus from the data misfit to the interpretational coherence. By measuring the convergence across independent geological, geophysical, and geochemical inputs, the IMCI transforms multidisciplinary qualitative interpretations into a quantifiable, risk assessment tool and metric of subsurface reality, offering a robust pathway to minimizing uncertainty in resource and hazard assessments.

The IMCI can be estimated across a broad set of applications, where geoscientific data are used for various purposes. Examples where IMCI can be used to assist decision-making include the geothermal and hydrocarbon exploration and production (delineation of thermal sources, reservoir traps, and fluid conduits (e.g., faults, contacts), production monitoring), mineral exploration (targeting structural controls, alteration haloes, and high-density/conductive ore bodies), groundwater modeling (mapping aquifer geometry, saltwater intrusions, and preferential flow pathways), geo-environmental management (identifying buried waste and contaminant pathways, monitoring CO2 storage), infrastructure and hazard assessment (identifying unstable ground conditions for engineering projects, assessing seismic hazard and landslide risk), and archaeological studies (mapping of historical features).

The implementation of IMCI can significantly contribute to de-risking exploration by identifying high-confidence zones where independent data streams converge. This can guide the selection of more precise drilling locations (e.g., in geothermal and hydrocarbon exploration), thereby reducing capital expenditure on low-probability targets. Moreover, low-confidence voxels highlight areas of high ambiguity, justifying additional, targeted data acquisition. Finally, when applied to time-lapse datasets, the IMCI can track temporal changes in model confidence as reservoir pressure or fluid saturation evolves, providing a dynamic tool for monitoring production or CO2 storage integrity.

Method details

The primary challenge in integrated subsurface characterization is the objective quantification of reliability. Joint inversion methodologies typically aim to identify a best-fit deterministic model that minimizes global error across multiple data streams. While mathematically efficient, this approach often masks fundamental disagreements between independent interpretations, potentially leading to a false sense of certainty in high-ambiguity zones.

Instead of seeking a model that satisfies all physical constraints, the IMCI quantifies model reliability by measuring the degree of interpretational convergence across three distinct data domains: geology/remote sensing (Stream A - SA), geophysics (Stream B - SB), and in-situ (geochemistry and borehole) measurements (Stream C - SC). More specifically, SA utilizes surface-based observations to extrapolate structural and compositional features, SB maps volumetric physical properties contrasts, which are then translated into lithological or fluid interpretations, and SC provides the real-Earth constraints derived from direct sampling and high-resolution borehole measurements (logging and core analysis).

A significant distinction should be made between the IMCI and standard measures of model uncertainty, such as posterior probability volumes or standard deviation maps derived from Bayesian inversion. Standard posterior probability quantifies the mathematical fit between a specific model configuration and the input data. A high posterior probability indicates that a model successfully honors the observed physical measurements. However, it does not account for whether that fit is geologically plausible or if it contradicts independent physical properties measured within the same volume. In contrast, the IMCI measures interpretational convergence. It is a cross-methodological validation tool that functions at the interpretational layer rather than the raw data layer. For example, a joint geophysical inversion may produce a high-probability best-fit for a high-density body, even if the geological stream identifies a low-density body in the same region. Conventional frameworks often average out or mask such interpretational conflicts through global error minimization. The IMCI explicitly identifies these conflicts as low-confidence zones. Ultimately, the IMCI is not a substitute for posterior probability but a meta-metric. It identifies voxels where independent physical parameters (e.g., density, resistivity, and structural geometry) reinforce a singular, multidisciplinary conclusion, providing a level of reliability that single-source probability volumes cannot achieve.

The principle of the IMCI is that each data source must be independently interpreted to predict the same fundamental subsurface property, such as lithofacies (rock type) or fluid state. This enables a voxel-by-voxel comparison of predictions, allowing for a co-located analysis. The IMCI calculation is based on widely applied statistical methods and tools, such as Bayesian inversion and Machine Learning (ML) classification [e.g., 1,2], and its value for any given voxel is maximized where the three independent streams mutually reinforce a singular conclusion.

Since the main data sources measure different physical properties (e.g., density, resistivity, elemental composition), they cannot be directly compared. However, cross-methodological calibration requires the definition of a consistent, shared model parameter. This can be appropriately represented by the lithofacies classification, which encompasses rock type and fluid saturation state. High-resolution direct measurements from geophysical logging and core analysis serve as the absolute ground truth; thus, as a first step in the coherence calculation process, every measured depth interval in the boreholes is assigned a categorical lithofacies label (e.g., brine-saturated sandstone, fractured basalt, clay-rich shale).

Next, for each of the two large-scale data streams (SA and SB), a separate interpretational model is built using the borehole data (if available) as the training output. This model learns to predict lithofacies based solely on local physical properties derived from a specific source. The training phase involves constructing independent interpretational models for the geophysical and geological/remote sensing datasets, using the benchmark lithofacies as the labeled output. The models typically employ multivariate regression or advanced ML classifiers, such as Random Forest or Artificial Neural Network (ANN) [e.g., 3,4]. The geophysical models are trained on the combined local physical properties (e.g., seismic velocity, electric resistivity, density, magnetic susceptibility) extracted specifically at the borehole locations, while for the geological/remote sensing data, extrapolation or geometric classifier models trained on structural inputs are utilized, such as distance to major faults, dip angles, and surface alteration signatures from hyperspectral imagery. The goal is for the final models to predict the most likely lithofacies and, most importantly, an associated measure of uncertainty for every point in the 3D volume, providing a probabilistic interpretation of composition and structure based on subsurface and surface constraints. Both models must output probability distributions over the possible lithofacies categories, not singular deterministic predictions, to enable the subsequent calculation of coherence.

In more detail, the reliability of geological (SA) and geophysical (SB) extrapolations depends on their proximity to high-resolution in-situ constraints, rapidly decreasing with increasing distance from the latter. The intrinsic reliability (wbase) is a spatial variable that represents the starting reliability of a method at the in-situ point and is the maximum possible value that a weight can take before it begins to decay as we move away from the ground-truth data. The selection of σ is constrained by the geostatistical characteristics of the study area, specifically the geological correlation derived from variogram analysis of the target lithofacies. It can be objectively calibrated through a "blind-well" validation strategy, in which a subset of borehole/in-situ data is withheld during the initial calculation, and the optimal σ is determined by minimizing the discrepancy with the actual lithological match at these locations. Additionally, conducting a sensitivity analysis by varying σ enables a quantifiable assessment of how spatial uncertainty propagates from ground-truth points, ensuring the model remains realistic in highly complex or compartmentalized geological settings.

Regardless of the voxel’s spatial position, a methodological reliability index (wmeth) is defined for each geoscientific method based on its sensitivity and resolution limits. This index represents the static, qualitative confidence assigned to a specific method before accounting for spatial or contextual factors and serves as the baseline weight, reflecting how well a particular technique can resolve the target property under ideal conditions. It is defined by a combination of the theoretical sensitivity of the method (how strongly the measured physical property responds to the target feature), the depth resolution (scale factor) and the degree of non-uniqueness of the method (e.g., the seismic reflection method presents higher wmeth due to its ability to resolve thin layers and discretize faults, the gravity and magnetic methods have lower wmeth due to the inherent non-uniqueness of their interpretations, and the Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR) method has high wmeth in very shallow subsurface applications due to its high resolution imaging ability). The index is calibrated against the empirical performance of the interpretation ML models during the initial training phase. Specifically, it is adjusted proportionally to the classification accuracy, or F1-score achieved at borehole/in-situ locations, ensuring that the index reflects demonstrated predictive reliability. Furthermore, the scientific stability of the estimated methodological indices is evaluated through a comprehensive sensitivity analysis. The consensus interpretation class (Icons) is the reference model derived from a standard process, such as joint inversion. It represents the most likely lithofacies category for a given voxel after all data streams have been mathematically reconciled to minimize global error. In general, Icons acts as the target for comparison, while IMCI measures the interpretational conflicts surrounding that target. For instance, the joint inversion will identify a voxel as 'sandstone', but the IMCI will reveal any discrepancy between the seismic and resistivity data. Using this example, the coherence score (Ri(v)) answers the question: “Given that the best integrated model (Icons) claims a specific voxel is a 'sandstone', what is the probability (P) that Stream i would have reached that same conclusion independently?”

The confidence score of the IMCI (C(v)) is finally calculated using Eq. (1). The method's output is the visualization of the confidence score as a confidence cube (uncertainty volume), alongside traditional models. Every voxel in the final 3D cube is accompanied by a normalized confidence index, ranging from 0 (maximum disparity/low confidence) to 1 (perfect convergence/high confidence). In general, confidence is maximized where the interpretations of the independent datasets reinforce a singular conclusion. High confidence is a significant indication for further project development, while low confidence highlights the need for new and/or additional data acquisition and integration. The confidence cube visualizes the internal stability of the integrated model, providing information not only about the subsurface regime but also about the general confidence of the multidisciplinary interpretation.

Method validation

Different operational scenarios and examples illustrate the significance of the IMCI, demonstrating how different methods are translated into unified interpretational outputs to determine the final confidence metric. To validate the IMCI against standard uncertainty metrics, it is essential to distinguish between a high posterior probability and a high coherence index.

1.Scenario 1: Based on surface lineament analysis and mineral spectral signatures that are derived from geological/remote sensing data sources (e.g., structural geology mapping, InSAR deformation signatures, and hyperspectral alteration mapping), a voxel is predicted to be part of a hydrothermal alteration zone or an active fault. Using geophysical data, the given voxel is interpreted to belong to a high-velocity and low-resistivity zone (e.g., by integrating seismic velocity models with MT resistivity results). Within a geothermal context, this combination of properties usually signifies a dense, fractured lithological unit saturated with high-temperature saline fluids. In-situ measurements in a drilled borehole (e.g., geophysical neutron and resistivity logs combined with core geochemistry) predict an effective porosity of 20% and high brine saturation at a specific depth interval. In such a case, the IMCI will be close to 1, providing high confidence in the final interpretation (e.g., presence of a porous, fractured reservoir).

While the current validation utilizes controlled conceptual scenarios to discuss the IMCI framework's mathematical stability, a critical next step in validating the IMCI is its application to existing, well-documented multidisciplinary datasets. By retrospectively applying the IMCI to known exploration case studies, the generated confidence cube can be compared against actual production or drilling outcomes. This validation process is essential for calibrating the coherence thresholds and demonstrating the framework's efficacy in real-world risk mitigation before it is deployed in higher-risk exploration scenarios.

Limitations

While the IMCI provides a robust mathematical framework for quantifying subsurface reliability, its efficacy is fundamentally governed by the quality, variety, and spatial distribution of the input data. In data-scarce environments, this distribution often results in uneven data density, which can introduce a spatial bias where regions proximal to high-resolution constraints appear disproportionately reliable compared to more distal areas. This could potentially lead to a localized overestimation of model stability.

The most significant constraint of the IMCI is its heavy reliance on in-situ measurements, specifically borehole data and core analysis. Since coherence scoring relies on models trained against direct physical measurements, the accuracy of the resulting confidence cube is inherently linked to the availability of well-documented calibration points. In areas where drilling data is sparse or non-existent, the framework must rely on lower-weight analogues or theoretical models, which significantly increases the uncertainty of the IMCI itself.

In addition, the index is most effective when it integrates data from the three independent streams (geology/remote sensing, geophysics, and in-situ measurements). When the input is limited to only one or two methods of the same stream (for instance, seismic reflection and gravity), the lack of independent physical cross-checks limits the index's ability to resolve fundamental ambiguities, such as fluid state and lithological contrast.

The IMCI framework faces additional challenges related to model dependency; the resulting coherence score is sensitive to the specific algorithms, inversion parameters, and mathematical architectures chosen for the independent interpretation models. Consequently, the IMCI may reflect the internal biases of the chosen modeling approach rather than an absolute measure of physical reality. Also, for the IMCI to be meaningful, the integrated methods should be sensitive to the same target property but through different physical mechanisms. If the available methods are redundant or share the same physical biases, the IMCI may produce a high confidence score that might be inaccurate or misleading.

Finally, the transformation of qualitative data into a quantitative metric requires a high degree of standardized processing. This process is subject to ML training assumptions, particularly the requirement that the physical relationships observed at training sites are geologically representative of the entire 3D volume. In complex or heterogeneous settings, a lack of stationarity, where the relationship between physical properties and lithofacies changes spatially, can lead to model overfitting and the propagation of training errors into the final IMCI. The independent interpretation of the multidisciplinary data demands that each data stream is processed to a level where it can output a probabilistic prediction. This adds to the computational time necessary for the calculation of the index, while it also requires close collaboration across different disciplines to ensure that a common interpretation ground (the interpretation of lithofacies) is consistently defined.
