Unexpectedly large myoepithelioma-like tumor of the vulvar region with two local recurrences: A rare case report and review of the literature
Authors: Soheila Aminimoghaddam, Soheila Sarmadi, Mahdis Kashian, Niloufar Sarchami
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We report, for the first time, a case of MELTVR with a very large size compared to previous reports, with two recurrences less than 10 years after surgery.•The presence of two separate but connected lesions (one in the labia majors and the other in the mons pubis) was a unique feature of this case.•This case highlights the local aggressiveness of MELTVR and the necessity of resection with an adequate margin.Keywords: Myoepithelioma-like tumor of the vulvar region, Labia major, Mons pubis, Recurrence

Introduction

In 2015, Yushida et al., reported a new entity termed “myoepithelioma-like tumor of the vulvar region (MELTVR)” as a rare mesenchymal neoplasm arising in the adult female vulva (Yoshida et al., 2015, Xu et al., 2020). While MELTVRs resemble soft tissue myoepitheliomas and extraskeletal myxoid chondrosarcomas in histopathologic examinations, they contain a discriminating immunohistochemical profile, entailing positive epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) vimentin, and negative S100 protein and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Xu et al., 2020, Zhang and Wang, 2019). Loss of INI1/SMARCB1 expression is also evident in MELTVR. The tumor generally has a clear boundary and comprises epithelioid and spindle morphologies. The range in size of the tumor was 2–7.7 cm in the 9 cases reported by Yushida et al. At a mean follow-up of 66 months, the tumor regrew or recurred in three patients (33.3 %) managed with wide local excision (Yoshida et al., 2015).

In this report, for the first time, we present a case of unexpectedly large MELTVR, which showed two consecutive recurrences following the wide local excision of tumor mass, necessitating a wide resection in the third operation for local tumor control.

Consent was obtained from the patients for the publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

A 46-year-old Female with a body mass index (BMI) of 27.3 kg/m2 was referred to our gynecology clinic with a two-year history of subcutaneous nodules in the vulvar region, which showed an obvious increase in size during the last three months. The patient reported feeling discomfort in the vulva, with no pain, itching, or bleeding. The patient had no history of alcohol consumption, smoking, and comorbidity. The patient had no family history of malignancy.

In the history taking, the patient did report a hx of MELTVR. The original lesion was detected ten years before the current referral (2013) and recurred six years later, in 2019. Both the primary lesion and recurrence were managed with wide local excision in another center by a gynecological surgeon not specialized in oncology. The patient also had a history of two caesarian sections, giving birth to two live babies.

Five years after the birth of the second baby. In the clinical examination, a large palpable mass was noticed in the right labia majora, extending to the mons pubis (Fig. 1).

Photograph of the mass before the operation.

Sonographic evaluation of the pelvis revealed two cystic masses within the vulva region, suggesting a benign neoplasm such as leiomyoma or lipoblastoma.

Considering the prior history, the patient underwent elective surgery with the diagnosis of MELTVR recurrence. The surgery (wide local excision) was done by a gynecologic oncologist.

The surgical approach included the removal of the epidermis, dermis, 2 to 3 mm of the underlying fat tissue and a 2 cm margin due to frequent recurrences of the tumor.(Helm) After exposure, two large masses were found in the vulvar region, approximately 8 cm and 4 cm.

The two lesions were attached. The larger mass was located in the labia majora, while the smaller lesion was located in the pubis. Both lesions were extracted using wide resection with a 2 cm margin in all directions. Extracted masses (Fig. 2) were sent for histologic and immunohistochemistry (IHC) evaluations. No adjuvant therapy was performed for the patient.

Photograph of the gross specimens.

Macroscopic pathologic evaluations reported two gray-tan colored semi-spherical shaped well-defined masses with the sizes of 10*9*7 cm and 9*7*3 cm, with no clear capsule. The masses had solid cut surfaces in sections with firm and myxoid consistency. Two separate lipoconnective tissues were also seen. Microscopic evaluation revealed myxoid mesenchymal tissues suggesting the recurrence of MELTVR. A mild to moderate atypia was observed. But, no mitosis or necrosis was detected. One of the dissected lymph nodes was free of tumor (Fig. 3).

IHC examination was positive for PR, ER, and vimentin and negative for S100, alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and INI1/SMARCB1 expression. Scattered positive MDM2 tumor cells were noticed. Based on the history, microscopic histology, and IHC results, the recurrence of MELTVR was confirmed.

The patient was discharged from the hospital in good health and followed up. After discharge, the patient was treated with antibiotics (cephalexin 500 mg) for two weeks. The patient is still disease-free at 12, 24, and 36 months after the surgery. Also, no complication was recorded during the 36-month follow-up of the patients.

Discussion

Since MELTVR is a new and rare tumor, several aspects of this lesion, including the best surgical procedure and recurrence potential, are not yet acknowledged (Yorozu et al., 2021, Fen et al., 2020). The number of available reports is very limited. In the largest available series, Yushida et al. reported nine cases of MELTVR, with the largest size of 7.7 cm. Most of the lesions (5/9) were located in the labia majora. Most lesions were initially managed with procedures other than wide resection and required wide resection sometime later. The lesion regrew in two patients and recurred in one patient. Recurrence occurred 12 years after index surgery with positive margins. Vimentin was positive in 6 of 9 patients. Cytokeratin was only positive in two patients. Epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and ER were positive in all cases, while S100, GFAP, and CD34 were negative in all tumors. PR was positive in all but one case. Also, all lesions showed loss SMARCB1 (Yoshida et al., 2015). The present case was larger than all cases reported by Yushida et al. In addition, our case recurred two times, while no patient in the series of Yushida et al. had two recurrences. The immunohistochemical profile of our patients was similar to that reported by Tushida et al (positive for vimentin, ER, PR, and loss SMARCB1).

Kaku et al. reported a case of slow-growing MELTVR, histologically mimicking a solitary fibrous tumor, in a 31-year-old healthy woman. The lesion was approximately 2 cm in size and was first enucleated and then widely re-excised, with no local recurrence or metastasis during the 11-month follow-up. IHC, the tumor was positive for ER, PR, EMA, SMA, Bcl-2, and CD99, but negative for S100 protein, GFAP, and CD34. Loss of SMARCB1 protein expression was also evident (Kaku et al., 2016). The present case was significantly larger than the case presented by Kaku et al. The immunohistochemical profile of the two cases was almost similar; however, we did not evaluate CD34, CD99, and Bcl-2. Instead, our case revealed MDM2-positive cells, which was not reported by Kaku et al.

Kojima et al. reported a case of MELTVR with infiltrative growth and harboring only a few ER-positive cells in the groin of a 70-year-old woman with a history of breast carcinoma. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were positive for EMA and aSMA and were focally (less than1 %) but strongly positive for ER. PR-positive cells were also focally distributed. Cells were completely negative for pan-cytokeratin, desmin, S100 protein, GFAP, and CD34. INI1/SMARCB1 was also lost in all tumor cells. The tumor was managed with wide local excision and showed no recurrence within one year of follow-up (Kojima et al., 2019). The location of the tumor was different in our case. ER-positive cells were scattered in our case, but focally distributed in the case reported by Kojima et al. Unlike the case of Kojima et al., wide local excision led to two consecutive recurrences in our case. This inconsistency suggests different aggressiveness for MELTVR, which may be justified by the different IHC profiles, such as the level of ER positiveness (Baumgarten and Frasor, 2012, Reinert et al., 2022). However, this deduction needs a high level of evidence to be confirmed.

Xu et al. reported a case of MELTVR in the perianal region of a 65-year-old female, which was managed with wide resection and remained disease-free until the last follow-up (8 months). The lesion was weekly positive for ER, partially positive for EMA, focally positive for Bcl-2 and CD99, and negative for S100, cytokeratin, GFAP, CK7, SOX10, CD31, CD34, desmin, MyoD1, myogenin, SMA, CD117, β-catenin and MUC4 (Xu et al., 2020). In our case, the tumor cells were strongly positive for ER, further supporting the role of immunohistochemical profiling, particularly ER positiveness, in the aggressiveness of MELTVR.

Liu et al. reported a case of MELTVR in front of the pubis tubercle of a 43-year-old female. The lesion was located in the pubic tubercle, and not the vulva. The patient remained disease-free during the eight-month follow-up. IHC, the tumor cells were positive for ER, PR, vimentin, EMA, and calponin and partially positive for cathepsin k. INI1/SMARCB1 expression was also deficient. The uniqueness of this case was its location (Liu et al., 2022). In the present report, we observed two separate lesions, none of which were located in the pubic tubercle.

Zhang and Wang also reported a case of MELTVR in the mons pubis of a 34-year-old female, almost 30 mm in diameter. The surgical margin was positive, but no recurrence occurred during the follow-up period. The tumor cells showed strong focal ER expression in the myxoid area, and PR-positive cells focally distributed in the peripheral area of the tumor. The tumor cells reacted focally for EMA and were completely negative for pan-cytokeratin, desmin, S100 protein, GFAP, and CD34. Loss of expression of INI1/SMARCB1 was also noticed (Zhang and Wang, 2019). Despite a positive surgical margin in the case reported by Zhang and Wang, no recurrence further supports the hypothesis of different aggressiveness for MELTVRs, requiring an individualized surgical strategy.

Lin et al. also reported a case of MELTVR in the right mons pubis of a 49-year-old woman, but unfortunately, we could not access the full article to summarize the characteristics of the case. A literature review reveals that MELTVR mainly involves the labia majora and mons pubis. The lesion could be locally aggressive, and wide resection with adequate margin could be regarded as the selected choice of treatment to prevent local recurrence. However, future studies are required to determine the best treatment for MELTVR. While wide local excisio has successfully treated MELTVR in some earlier studies, it led to two consecutive recurrences in our case. This inconsistency also suggests some case-by-case differences in the local aggressiveness of MELTVR, which the differences in immunohistochemical properties might justify. This hypothesis also needs to be supported by a larger amount of evidence.

The uniqueness of the present case was its large size compared to the MELTVRs reported in earlier studies, the presence of two separate but attached lesions, and two recurrences within ten years after initial treatment. The large size of the lesion could be attributed to the late referral of the patient. It took a long time between the date mass was noted and the date of referral, maybe because the lesion was small in the first months and progressively increased size was observed in the last three months.

Finally, the MELTVR diagnosis could be deceiving, as it mimics several other lesions of the vulva. Therefore, more awareness is required regarding the differential diagnosis of MELTVR from other lesions such as the solitary fibrous tumor (Kaku et al., 2016).

The present study was not without limitations. As the main limitation, we did not perform fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to investigate EWSR1 and FUS1 gene rearrangements.

Our report shows,MELTVR can be locally aggressive and should be excised with an adequate margin. Resection with inadequate margin could result in multiple recurrences, as observed in the present case. We suggest treatment of MELTVR by a more specialized surgeon, such as a gynecological oncologist, to obtain a better clinical and oncological outcome. Considering the rare incidence of MELTVR, more awareness is required regarding the differential diagnosis of MELTVR with other lesions of the vulva, particularly when located in the labia majora and mons pubis.
