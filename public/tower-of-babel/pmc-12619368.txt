Cardiac arrest triggers IL-17-mediated neuroinflammation and astrocyte polarization: insights into pathogenesis and intervention
Authors: Shumei Li, Lei Wang, Qiqi Luo, Maiying Fan, Yixiao Xu, Xiehong Liu, Yiyuan Zhang, Lianhong Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCardiac arrest (CA) is a life-threatening emergency with a global one-year survival rate of 2%-10%. Brain injury significantly impacts CA outcomes, and neuroinflammation is a key mediator of cerebral damage. Interleukin-17 (IL-17) has been implicated in multiple inflammatory disorders, yet its contribution to CA-induced cerebral damage remains undefined.ObjectiveTo elucidate the role of the IL-17 axis in CA-triggered neuroinflammation and to determine whether IL-17 blockade can attenuate hippocampal injury and improve neurologic recovery.MethodsAsphyxial CA was induced in adult Sprague-Dawley rats followed by cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity, Th17 infiltration, astrocyte polarization, and downstream signaling were assessed by flow cytometry, RNA-seq, qRT-PCR, ELISA, immunofluorescence, and western blotting. IL-17 A or IL-17RA was neutralized in vivo with specific antibodies, and human SVGP12 astrocytes were employed for mechanistic validation.ResultsCA promotes Th17 cell differentiation and enhances blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, facilitating the infiltration of Th17 cells and their secreted IL-17 A/F into the hippocampus. IL-17 A/F specifically binds to IL-17RA/RC on astrocytes, activating NF-κB, and MAPK pathways, which drive A1 polarization of astrocytes and exacerbate neuroinflammation. IL-17 A neutralization reverses A1 polarization of astrocytes, reduces neuronal apoptosis, improves 24-hour neurologic deficit scores, and enhances survival in CA rats. In vitro, IL-17 A induced A1 polarization and inflammatory cytokine release in astrocytes, effects abolished by IL-17RA blockade.ConclusionOur study elucidates the mechanisms underlying CA-induced neuroinflammation and identifies the IL-17 A pathway as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating neurological injury following cardiac arrest.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03600-6.

Introduction

Cardiac arrest (CA), characterized by abrupt cessation of cardiac function, constitutes a life-threatening medical emergency. It ranks as a leading global cause of mortality, with only a 2%−10% 1-year survival rate [1]. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is an emergency technique to achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in cardiac and respiratory arrest patients. However, ischemia-reperfusion injury during CPR can cause and exacerbate post-CA syndrome (PCAS) in successfully resuscitated patients. PCAS includes persistent precipitating pathophysiology, systemic ischemia-reperfusion injury, myocardial dysfunction and brain injury [2]. Among them, hypoxic brain injury constitutes a primary contributor to neurological impairment, cognitive dysfunction, and even death in patients with CA [3]. Alleviating brain tissue injury can serve as an effective strategy to improve the prognosis of patients with CA and reduce the mortality rate.

Inflammation is a crucial pathological mechanism of brain injury induced by CA [4]. Systemic ischemia-reperfusion injury during ROSC significantly elevates cytotoxic T cells, dendritic cells, monocytes and macrophages in the blood, triggering systemic inflammatory responses [5]. During ROSC after CA, pro-inflammatory immune cells and cytokines of the systemic inflammatory response infiltrate the brain parenchyma through compromised blood-brain barrier (BBB), activating resident immune cells microglia and astrocytes, and exacerbating neuroinflammation-mediated brain injury [6–9]. Therefore, inhibiting the neuroinflammation can alleviate brain injury in patients with CA and improve the prognosis. However, the mechanisms of neuroinflammation caused by CA has not been fully elucidated.

Interleukin-17 (IL-17), a pro-inflammatory cytokine primarily produced by T helper 17 cells (Th17), mediates pathogenesis in multiple autoimmunity and inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [10], multiple sclerosis (MS) [11], psoriasis [12], systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [13], and asthma [14]. TGF-β and IL-6 activate STAT3 signaling and induce RORγt expression, driving CD4+ T cell differentiation into Th17 cells that secrete IL-17 [15]. Additionally, CD8+ T cells, γδ T cells, NK cells, mast cells, macrophages and neutrophils have been identified as the sources of IL-17. The IL-17 family has six cytokines, including IL-17 A, IL-17B, IL-17 C, IL-17D, IL-17E(IL-25) and IL-17 F [16]. Among them, IL-17 A and IL-17 F are the primary effector cytokines and share approximately 55% sequence structural homology [16]. The IL-17 receptor family contains five members (IL-17RA, IL-17RB, IL-17RC, IL-17RD, IL-17RE). The IL-17 cytokines exert their biological effects through binding their homo-/heterodimeric receptor complexes. IL-17 A/F homodimers and heterodimers bind to IL-17RA/IL-17RC receptor complexes, This interaction activates MAPK, NF-κB and C/EBP pathways, driving the transcription and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNFα, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, GM-CSF) and chemokines (e.g., CXCL1,CXCL8) [13, 17, 18]. Consequently, innate immune cells such as neutrophils are recruited, amplifying the inflammatory response. Furthermore, several studies have shown that neutralization of IL-17 reduces neutrophil neuroinfiltration, alleviates stroke-induced neuroinflammation, and improve prognosis [19–21]. However, the role of IL-17 in PCAS remains poorly understood. A study on the prognosis prediction of CA indicates that plasma levels of IL-17 and IL23 can predict PCAS patients mortality, and are positively correlated with Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE)-II score and ROSC [22]. However, the role and mechanisms of IL-17 in CA induced neuroinflammation remain to be elucidated.

Using an asphyxial CA model, we demonstrate that CA promoted Th17 cell differentiation and increased the permeability of BBB, facilitating the infiltration of Th17 cells and Th17 cell secreted IL-17 A/F into the hippocampus. Subsequently, IL-17 A/F bound to IL-17RA/RC on astrocytes in the hippocampus, activating the NF-κB, and MAPK pathways, promoting A1 polarization of astrocytes, enhancing the expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α and GM-CSF), and mediating the post-CA neuroinflammation. Moreover, the IL-17RA neutralizing antibody reversed A1 polarization of astrocytes and effectively ameliorated the neurological and neuroinflammatory outcomes in the asphyxial CA rat model. In summary, this study elucidates mechanisms underlying CA-induced neuroinflammation and suggested IL-17 A pathway as a potential therapeutic target for alleviating the neurological functional injury caused by CA.

Methods

Animal

Sprague-Dawley rats were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co and housed in a pathogen-free facility under standard 12-h light/dark cycle conditions. All rats used were between 12 and 16 weeks of age. Carbon dioxide administered for 2 min from a compressed gas tank followed by cervical dislocation was used as the method of euthanasia. All experiments were performed in accordance with the approved protocols by the Medical Ethics Review Committee of Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (approval No.20190218175).

Asphyxial cardiac arrest rat model

Following intraperitoneal anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), Sprague-Dawley rats underwent tracheal intubation and femoral artery cannulation. Hemodynamic parameters (heart rate [HR], mean arterial pressure [MAP], electrocardiogram [ECG]) were continuously monitored using a Biological Function Recording system (BL-420 S, Chengdu Taimeng, China). Upon stabilization of hemodynamic parameters, the tracheal tube was clamped at end-expiration to induce 7-minute asphyxia. CA was defined by MAP < 30 mmHg with concurrent ECG evidence of asystole, ventricular fibrillation, or pulseless electrical activity. Seven minutes after inducing asphyxia, the tracheal catheter was unclamped, and an animal ventilator (HX-300, Chengdu Taimeng, China) was turned on for mechanical ventilation. Ventilation parameters were set as follows: tidal volume of 1 ml per 100 g body weight, expiratory-inspiratory ratio of 5:4, and respiratory frequency of 90–110 breaths/min. CPR was initiated with epinephrine (0.02 mg/kg), sodium bicarbonate (1 mEq/kg), and normal saline (0.5 mL) injections, accompanied by manual chest compressions at 200/min. Resuscitation success required: (1) sustained ROSC > 10 min, (2) supraventricular rhythm with normal QRS complexes, and (3) MAP maintained ≥ 60 mmHg. Failure to restore ROSC within 3 min of CPR was defined as resuscitation failure. Successfully resuscitated rats received wound closure 20 min post-ventilation and were transferred to individual cages. Sham-operated rats underwent exactly the same surgical preparation as cardiac-arrest animals but no asphyxia or cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was induced. For IL-17RA neutralization experiments, successfully resuscitated rats received intraperitoneal injection of anti-IL-17 A antibody (0.2 mg/100 g; Selleck, Cat# A2120) or equivalent IgG isotype control (0.2 mg/100 g; Selleck, Cat# A2106). At 24 h post-resuscitation, blood and brain tissue samples were collected.

Evaluation of neurological deficits

A previously validated scale of neurologic deficit score (NDS) [23] was used to assess the neurological outcome at 24 h after ROSC, which involves the scoring of 19 items including arousal, reflex, motor, sensory, and balance responses, and was conducted by 2 investigators who were blinded to animal grouping (Supplementary Table 1). An NDS of 80 was considered neurologically normal, whereas an NDS of 0 indicated brain death. In this study, dead rats were excluded from the NDS assessment.

Bulk RNA sequencing & data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampal tissues (CA group, n = 3; sham group, n = 3) using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 10296028CN). RNA purity (A260/A280), concentration, and integrity (RIN > 8) were assessed using Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer and 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Then, Qualified RNA were used for library construction. The procedures of library construction including cDNA synthesis, fragmentation, adapter ligation and amplification. Sequencing was performed by illumina sequencing platform of Biomarker Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Clean data with high quality was obtained by filtering Raw data, which were further mapped to pre-defined reference genome generating mapped data. Subsequent analysis was conducted in R studio version 3.5.1 (R studio Inc., Boston, MA, USA). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between CA group and sham group were identified using thresholds of fold change ≥ 2 or < 0.5 and P < 0.05. Volcano plots were made with the RNA sequencing data using the R program for visualizing the differential gene expressions. Significant DEGs (|log2 FC| ≥ 1, P < 0.05) were colored: red = upregulated, blue = downregulated. And non-significant genes were colored gray. Hierarchical clustering of the top 20 DEGs sorted based on log2 FC was generated to visualize using pheatmap package in R. KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs were analyzed using the clusterProfiler R package, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Preparation of hippocampal single-cell suspension

Hippocampal tissues were cut into small pieces and digested with 0.25% trypsin (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-129047) and 0.2% collagenase (sigma #C1889) at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the digestion was terminated using DMEM/F12 medium (Procell, Cat# PM150312) with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GIBCO, Cat# A5669401). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 250×g for 5 min and the supernatant was discarded. Then, the cells were resuspended in DMEM/F12 containing 20% FBS and filtered through a 200-mesh sieve. Finally, Cell concentration and viability were quantified using a Brightfield Cell Counter (CellDrop BF UNL TD, DeNovix, China) and trypan blue exclusion (to distinguish live/dead cells). Cell density (≤ 10^7 cells/ml) was adjusted to by diluting with pre-warmed DMEM/F12 containing 20% FBS using the formula: Total volume needed = (Initial concentration × Initial volume)/Target concentration.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) preparation

2 - 4 ml peripheral blood was collected at 24 h post-ROSC (CA group) or 24 h post-procedure (sham group) from anesthetized rats in a 15-mL EDTA-coated sterile centrifuge tubes (final EDTA 2 mM). Then, The Peripheral Blood was diluted 1:1 with sterile, room-temperature PBS. 4 ml of Ficoll - Paque™ density gradient medium (Cytiva, Cat# 17144003) were pipetted into a 15 mL sterile centrifuge tube and carefully overlay with the diluted blood. After Centrifuging at 300–400 × g for 20–30 min, the opaque mononuclear band was aspirated, transferred to a new tube, and washed twice with 10 mL PBS (300 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL PBS. Subsequently, cell concentration and viability were quantified using a Brightfield Cell Counter (CellDrop BF UNL TD, DeNovix, China) and trypan blue exclusion (to distinguish live/dead cells). Cell density was adjusted to ≤ 10^7 cells/ml.

Flow cytometry

As aforementioned, a single-cell suspension of hippocampus or PBMC was prepared. Cells were stimulated in 1640 medium with PMA (50 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 chamber. After incubation, wash the cells once with PBS and fix them with 4% paraformaldehyde (Aladdin, Cat# P395744). Next, cells were permeabilized using 0.15% Triton (Acmec, Cat# 9002-93−1) for 5 min and blocked with 3% goat serum (Aladdin, Cat# G7521234) for 30 min. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were incubated with IL-17 A-PE antibody (Invitrogen, eBioscience, Cat# 17B7) for 50 min, then followed by incubation of CD4-FITC antibody (BD Pharmingen™, Cat# 554837), CD3-PerCP antibody (elabscience, Cat# E-AB-F1228J) and CD45-Alexa Fluor@647 antibody (BD Pharmingen™, Cat# 565465) at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, cells were washed once with PBS and examined using a flow cytometry (Beckman, Coulter, CytoFLEX). Data were analyzed using CytExpert software (v2.4). Compensation was performed using compensation beads and single-stained controls prepared on the same day. Doublets were excluded by gating on single cells based on FSC-H vs. FSC-A. Viable leukocytes were gated as 7-AAD- (fluorescence < 10²) and based on FSC-A vs. SSC-A to identify the lymphocyte/monocyte population. Leukocytes were further confirmed as CD45+. CD3+CD4+ T cells were identified from the leukocyte population. IL-17 A+ cells were defined based on an isotype control, with the positive threshold set at the 99th percentile. The percentage of Th17 cells was calculated as (IL-17 A+CD3+CD4+ events/total CD3+CD4+ events) × 100.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Extract RNA from hippocampal tissues or SVG-P12 cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 10296028CN). The RNA integrity was detected using a qubit 2.0 (Agilent). Then, reverse transcription was performed to synthesized cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (QIAGEN, Cat# RT31-020). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was determined relative to Gapdh and fold change calculated by using the 2−ΔΔCT threshold. A list of primers is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Cytokine measurement

Supernatants from homogenized hippocampal tissues and serum were collected after centrifugation. IL-1β (elkbiotech, Cat# ELK1272), IL-6 (elkbiotech, Cat# ELK1158), IL-10 (elkbiotech, Cat# ELK1144), TNF-α (elkbiotech, Cat# ELK1396), GM-CSF (elkbiotech, Cat# ELK1030), IL-17 A (Elabscience, Cat# Rat IL-17 A) levels were measured by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human astrocytes SVGP12 were purchased from Xiamen Yimo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Human astrocytes SVGP12 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Procell, Cat# PM150312) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, GIBCO, Cat# A5669401) and 2% penicillin-streptomycin (MCE, Cat# HY-K1006) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. To elucidate the effects and mechanisms of IL-17 A on human astrocytes SVGP12, human astrocytes SVGP12 were divided into control group, IL-17 A group and IL17A + anti-IL17RA group. The control group was treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the IL17A group was treated with 100 µg/mL of IL17A, and the IL17A + anti-IL17RA group was treated with a combination of 100 ng/mL of IL17A (MCE, Cat# HY-P70527) and 100 µg/mL of anti-IL17RA (Selleck, Cat# A2032). Twenty-four hours later, human astrocytes SVGP12 were collected. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was used to detect the contents of A1 astrocyte markers (C3, FbIn5, Ligp1, Serping1) and A2 astrocyte markers (Cd109, Tgm1, S100a10, Clcf1, Ptx3), as well as the expression levels of IL1β, IL6, IL10, TNF-α, and GM-CSF mRNA. Western blotting was employed to analyze the activation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at room temperature (RT), processed for sectioning (6 μm thickness), and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at RT. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 0.3% H2O2/PBS (15 min at RT), followed by blocking of non-specific binding sites with TNB buffer (1 h, RT). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: GFAP (1:250, Abcam, Cat# ab7206), IBA1 (1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab178846), NeuN (1:300, Abcam, Cat# ab177487), IL-17RA (1:200, Biorbyt, Cat# orb1536865), p-P65 (1:200, Zenbio, Cat# 310013), p-ERK (1:200, Zenbio, Cat# 310065), CD31 (1:250, Abcam, Cat# ab81289), ZO-1 (1:100, Abcam, Cat# ab307799). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2) for 30–60 min at RT. Then, the tyramide signal amplification (TSA) reagents (Yeasen, Cat# 60405ES03) were employed to amplify the signals, whose amplification relies on horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-catalyzed covalent deposition of fluorescent tyramide derivatives at the antigen site. Unbound reagents were removed by 5 min x 3 times TBST wash. Nuclei were counterstained using 1 µg/ml DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D9542). The slides were imaged using a Digital Pathology Syestem (3D HISTECH, Pannoramic SCAN II). Fluorescence intensity quantification of CD31 and ZO-1 in the hippocampus was performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). Regions of interest (ROIs) were delineated along hippocampal vascular structures. Following channel separation and scale calibration, the total integrated density (sum of pixel intensities within the ROI) and the ROI area (µm²) were measured. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was calculated as the ratio of the total integrated density to the ROI area.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and Nissl body staining

The brain tissues were fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at RT. Then, the fixed brain samples were embedded in paraffin. Using a microtome (Thermofisher scientific, SHANDON FINESSE 325), paraffin-embedded brains tissue is cut into thin sections (6 μm thickness), which are then mounted on glass slides. Subsequently, the deparaffinization of brain tissue sections was performed by successive washes in xylene (10 min x 3 times), followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (anhydrous ethanol, 95% ethanol, 90% ethanol, 80% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 5 min each) to water. For HE staining, brain tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin for 4 min and washed with tap water for 2 min. Then, the brain tissue sections were differentiated with 0.8 hydrochloric acid alcohol for 2 s and was washed with tap water. Next, the sections were stained with eosin solution for 2 s. For Nissl body staining, sections were stained with preheated (50 °C), 1% toluidine blue aqueous solution in incubator at 56 °C for 20 min and washed with tap water. Then, sections were differentiated with 95% ethanol. Finally, the section of HE and Nissl body staining were dehydrated in ascending ethanol (70%−100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam, followed by examination with a light microscope (Olympus, CX31).

TUNEL staining assay

Following perfusion-fixation and overnight post-fixation in 4% PFA, brain samples were embedded in paraffin wax and cut into 6-µm sections with a microtome (Thermofisher scientific, SHANDON FINESSE 325) and adhered to poly-l-lysine-coated glass slides. Serial sections were deparaffinized in mixed xylenes (10 min x 3 times) and rehydrated in graded ethanol solutions and deionized water. Antigen retrieval was performed by incubating slides in Dako Target Retrieval Solution, pH 9 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) at 95 °C for 20 min. Slides were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at RT. Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 0.3% H2O2/PBS (15 min at RT), followed by blocking of non-specific binding sites with TNB buffer (1 h, RT). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: NeuN (1:300, Abcam, Cat# ab177487), Subsequently, primary antibodies were detected using Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (ThermoFisher, Cat# A11008). TUNEL kit was purchased from Vazyme (Cat# D9542) and performed according to manufacturer’s instructions on NeuN-stained sections. Nuclei were counterstained using 1 µg/ml DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# D9542) and the slides were imaged using a Digital Pathology System (3D HISTECH, Pannoramic SCAN II). All images were analyzed by two observers blinded to group assignment. The apoptosis rate was calculated by the formula: apoptotic cells/all cells in a field × 100%.

Western blotting

Hippocampal tissues and cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Thermofisher scientific, Cat# A32959). The protein concentration is determined using BCA assay (Biosharp, Cat# BL1054C). Protein samples were combined with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Solarbio, Cat# P1040) in a 1:4 ratio. The mixtures were denatured by heating at 95 °C for 10 min in a dry bath (Thermo Fisher, Cat# FB15001) and then immediately transferred to ice to minimize protein refolding. After a brief centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 30 s to collect any condensation, the samples were subjected to protein separation via sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Then, the separated proteins were transferred from the gel noto 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (Merck millipore, Cat# IPVH00010), followed by blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at RT. Incubation with primary antibody was conducted overnight at 4 °C. After 10 min x 3 times TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:4000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2) for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Thermofisher scientific, 34580) reagents, and the signal was detected and captured on a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc™ MP system. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab Software (Version 6.1, Bio-Rad), normalized to GAPDH loading control (1:4000, Zenbio, Cat# R380626). Blots were probed with antibodies against CD4 (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# R380870), IL-17 A (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab318150), IL17-RA (1:1000, Biorbyt, Cat# orb1536865), IL17-RC (1:1000, Biorbyt, Cat# orb215328), GFAP (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab7206), S100B (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 380829), p-P65 (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 310013), P65 (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 250060), p-ERK (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 310065), ERK (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 343830), p-IKKα/β (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 530546), IKKα (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 381289), IKKβ (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 381592), p-MEK (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 310050), MEK (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# R24949), p-P38 (1:1000, Huabio, Cat# ET1702-65), P38 (1:1000, Huabio, Cat# ER2001-52), CD4 (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# R380870), RORγt (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab207082), IL-22 (1:1000, Zenbio, Cat# 252382), Claudin-5 (1:1000, CST, Cat# 49564 T), ZO-1(1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab307799), Occludin (1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab216327).

Statistical analysis

Experiments were performed with ≥ 3 independent biological replicates. Data were presented as the mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM). Homoscedasticity (homogeneity of variance) was tested for all datasets. Where variances were equal, we performed unpaired t-tests to compare the differences between two experimental groups. For data with unequal variances, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied. All statistical analyses of the data were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Cardiac arrest-induced hippocampal injury

To investigate the role and mechanism of CA - induced brain injury, an asphyxia CA rat model was established (Fig. 1A). Hemodynamic trajectories confirmed model validity (Supplementary Fig. 1 A, B). CA rats exhibited significantly reduced 24-h neurological deficit scores (NDS) versus sham controls (Fig. 1B), indicating that CA induced neurological impairment. The hippocampus is one of the most important regions in the brain, responsible for functions such as learning, memory, spatial navigation, neural plasticity, emotional regulation, and processing of abstract knowledge and rules [24]. Astrocytes and microglia are involved in the pathological processes of neuroinflammation [25]. We employed immunofluorescence (IF) to assess the expression of the astrocytic marker GFAP and microglia marker IBA-1 in hippocampal tissue. The results revealed elevated GFAP+ astrocyte and IBA1+ microglia density in CA hippocampus (Fig. 1C, D). Next, we evaluated the impact of CA on hippocampal neurons. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed cellular necrosis, edema, vacuolization, and nuclear pyknosis (Supplementary Fig. 2 A), with concomitant NeuN+ neuron loss in CA1, CA3, and DG subfields of hippocampus in CA rats (Supplementary Fig. 2B, C). Nissl staining demonstrated significantly reduced Nissl body density in hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG subfields of CA rats versus sham controls (Fig. 1E, F). Complementarily, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assays confirmed marked neuronal apoptosis in corresponding hippocampal subregions (Fig. 1G, H). These findings demonstrate CA-induced hippocampal glial activation and neuronal damage.

Cardiac arrest induces Th17 cell differentiation

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying hippocampal injury induced by CA, we performed transcriptomic analysis on hippocampal tissues of rats in the CA and sham groups at 24 h post ROSC. Based on the criteria of |log₂FC| >1 and P < 0.05, we identified 930 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups (Supplementary Table 3). Volcano plot Visualization revealed 515 upregulated and 415 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical clustering of top 20 DEGs demonstrated clear group segregation (Fig. 2B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of the IL-17 pathway and activation of NF-κB and MAPK pathways, indicating their crucial role in CA-induced hippocampal injury (Fig. 2C).

IL-17 derived from T helper 17 (Th17) cells involved in pathogenesis of various autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [10–14]. STAT3 activation medicated by transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and IL-6 enhanced RORγt expression, contributing to the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th17 cells and secretion of IL-17 cytokines [15]. We hypothesize that CA induces Th17 cell differentiation. Consistently, qPCR showed elevated CD4, RORγt, and IL-22 in the hippocampal tissues of CA rats (Fig. 2I). These findings further confirm that CA promotes Th17 cell differentiation.

Cardiac arrest increases blood-brain barrier permeability

The blood-brain barrier (BBB), a highly selective semipermeable membrane composed of tightly connected endothelial cells, astrocytes, pericytes, and basement membrane, regulates substance exchange between blood and brain parenchyma to maintain the homeostasis of the cerebral environment [26]. Multiple studies have shown that CA impairs BBB integrity, facilitating peripheral immune cell infiltration that exacerbates post-CA brain injury [6, 27]. Our previous findings demonstrated elevation of Th17 cells in PBMCs (Fig. 2D, E) and hippocampus (Fig. 2F, G) of CA rats, leading us to hypothesize BBB disruption enables hippocampal Th17 infiltration. we therefore assessed the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Claudin-5, and Occludin in hippocampal tissues. Western blot analysis demonstrated significantly reduced protein levels of Occludin, and Claudin-5 in CA rats’ hippocampal tissue (Fig. 3A and B). Consistent mRNA downregulation was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. 3C). IF staining further revealed decreased fluorescence intensity and compromised integrity of CD31 and ZO-1 in the hippocampus of CA rats (Fig. 3D-F). Collectively, these data establish CA-induced BBB breakdown as a critical mechanism enabling peripherally differentiated Th17 cells to infiltrate the hippocampus and drive hippocampal injury.

Cardiac arrest promotes IL-17 A/F expression and secretion, upregulates hippocampal astrocyte IL-17RA/C, and ultimately induces inflammation

The IL-17 family comprises IL-17 A-F [16]. qPCR screening of IL-17 isoforms revealed significant upregulation of IL-17 A, IL-17D, and IL-17 F in the hippocampus of CA rats (Fig. 4A). Given the high homology between IL-17 A and IL-17 F, and their role as the primary Th17 cell effector cytokines, we focused on IL-17 A in subsequent studies [16]. Subsequently, ELISA was performed to quantify IL-17 A levels. Consistent with expectations, IL-17 A levels were elevated in both hippocampus and serum of CA rats (Fig. 4B, C). IL-17 A/F homodimers/heterodimers activate MAPK, NF-κB, C/EBPs pathways via IL-17RA/RC binding to induce proinflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, GM-CSF) and drive inflammatory responses [13]. To investigate the effect of CA on the expression levels of IL-17RA and IL-17RC, we first measured the mRNA levels of IL-17RA-RE in the hippocampus. qPCR revealed selective upregualtion of IL-17RA and IL-17RC mRNA in CA hippocampus, whereas IL-17RB, IL-17RD, and IL-17RE remained unchanged (Fig. 4D). Consistently, Western blotting confirmed the elevation of IL-17RA and IL-17RC in hippocampus of CA rats (Fig. 4E). Although IL-17RA/RC are expressed in microglia, astrocytes and neurons, their CA-responsive cellular localization was undefined. In the present study, we performed double immunofluorescence assays to further clarify the cellular expression and localization of IL-17RA. The results showed that specific co-localization of IL-17RA with the astrocyte marker GFAP, whereas co-localization with the microglia marker Iba1 and neuron marker NeuN was negligible (Fig. 4F). Subsequently, we measured the levels of cytokines in hippocampal tissue and serum. Compared with sham group, CA rats exhibited increased protein (Fig. 4G) and mRNA (Fig. 4H) levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and GM-CSF in the hippocampus. Additionally, serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α and GM-CSF were statistically higher in CA rats (Fig. 4I). Collectively, these data suggest that IL-17 A mediates CA-induced neuroinflammation via binding with membrane proteins IL-17RA/IL-17RC on hippocampal astrocyte.

Cardiac arrest leads to astrocyte polarization and activation NF-κB and MAPK pathway in hippocampus

CA-induced Th17 cells infiltrate the hippocampus via BBB disruption. The specific receptors IL-17RA/RC for IL-17 A/F are highly expressed in the hippocampus of CA rats. Meanwhile, IL-17RA specially co-localizes with astrocyte marker GFAP, indicating that astrocytes are involved in IL-17 A signaling-mediated neuroinflammation following CA. To further clarify the role of astrocyte in this process, we first examined the astrocytes activation. Compared to the sham group, GFAP and S100A, as the markers of astrocyte activation, were significantly increased in hippocampus of CA rats (Fig. 5A, B), suggesting that CA promotes astrocyte activation. Reactive astrocytes polarize toward neurotoxic “A1” phenotype or neuroprotective “A2” phenotype [28]. To further investigate the polarization phenotype of astrocytes, qPCR revealed that mRNA levels of A1-related markers C3, FbIn5, Ligp1, and Serping1 were significantly increased in the hippocampus of CA rats (Fig. 5C). Conversely, the expression of A2-related markers Clcf1 and Tgm1 was decreased, while Cd109, Ptx3, and S100A10 showed no significant changes (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that CA promotes the polarization of hippocampal astrocytes into neurotoxic A1 phenotype.

IL-17 A neutralizing antibody ameliorates cardiac arrest-induced hippocampal injury

Our previous study demonstrated that CA induced the differentiation of Th17 cells. And Th17 cells infiltrate the brain parenchyma via the disrupted BBB and secrete IL-17 A/F. These cytokines bind to IL-17RA/RC on hippocampal astrocytes, activating NF-κB and MAPK pathways to promote astrocyte polarization into A1 neurotoxic phenotypes that exacerbate neuroinflammation. Subsequently, we investigated whether the IL-17 A signaling could serve as a therapeutic target for improving CA induced hippocampal injury. CA rats received intravenous anti-IL-17 A (2 mg/kg) or IgG control at ROSC (Fig. 6A). Hemodynamic parameters remained comparable (Supplementary Fig. 5 A, B), confirming equivalent hippocampal injury. Compared to the IgG treated control, Anti-IL-17 A improved NDS at 24 h post-CA (Fig. 6B) and reduced GFAP+ astrocyte density (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, hippocampal GFAP/S100B proteins significantly decreased (Fig. 6E), with concordant mRNA reductions (Fig. 6F), confirming that anti-IL-17 A reduces astrocyte activation induced by CA. Next, we explore the the effect of anti-IL-17 on astrocyte polarization, qPCR revealed that anti-IL-17 A significantly decreased mRNA levels of neurotoxic A1 markers (C3, FbIn5, Ligp1, Serping1) in the hippocampus of CA rats (Fig. 6G), while increasing mRNA levels of neuroprotective A2 markers (Tgm1, Ptx3, S100A10), with no significant changes in Clcf1 and Cd109 (Fig. 6H), indicating that anti-IL-17 A reverses CA-induced polarization of hippocampal astrocytes toward the neurotoxic A1 phenotype.

IL-17 A neutralization potently suppresses NF-κB/MAPK signaling activation in hippocampal astrocytes of CA rats and attenuates the inflammation

Our previous findings showed that anti-IL-17 A therapy attenuated CA-induced hippocampal injury and reversed A1 astrocyte polarization. We posited this occurs via suppression of NF-κB/MAPK signaling and downstream cytokine storm. Indeed, anti-IL-17 A abrogated CA-induced phosphorylation: p-p65, p-IKKα, p-IKKβ in NF-κB pathway; p-ERK, p-MEK, p-P38 in MAPK pathway (Fig. 8A, B). Double IF staining confirmed reduced p-P65/p-ERK co-localization with GFAP+ astrocytes (Fig. 8C), with 25.87% and 17.90% reductions in double-positive cells respectively (Fig. 8D), indicating that IL-17 A neutralization attenuates the activation of NF-κB/MAPK pathways in hippocampal astrocytes after CA. ELISA assays showed that IL-17 A neutralizing antibody significantly inhibited the secretion of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, GM-CSF, and TNF-α in both hippocampal tissues and serum of CA rats compared with the IgG-treated group (Fig. 8E, Supplementary Fig. 6 A). Consistently, mRNA levels of these proinflammatory cytokines were also decreased in hippocampus of anti-IL-17 A-treated CA rats (Supplementary Fig. 6B), confirming improved inflammatory status after IL-17 A neutralization. Collectively, these findings indicate that the IL-17 pathway is a potential therapeutic target for reducing hippocampal injury after CA.

Anti-IL17RA mitigates IL17A-induced astrocyte polarization, suppresses NF-κB/MAPK pathway activation, and attenuates inflammation in SVG P12 cells

To further confirm that Th17 cell-derived IL-17 A specifically binds to IL-17RA/RC receptors on astrocytes, activates NF-κB/MAPK pathway, regulates astrocyte activation and polarization, promotes inflammation, and mediates hippocampal injury following CA, we treated human astrocyte SVG P12 cells with IL-17 A recombinant protein alone or in combination with an anti-IL-17RA. Compared to the PBS control group, IL-17 A upregulated GFAP and S100B expression in SVG P12 cells (Fig. 9A, B), increased the mRNA levels of neurotoxic A1 reactive astrocyte markers (C3, FbIn5, Ligp1, Serping1) (Fig. 9C), indicating that IL-17 A promotes astrocyte activation and neurotoxic A1 polarization. In contrast, no significant changes were observed in the neuroprotective A2 markers (Cd109, Clcf1, S100A10, Tgm1), with the exception of PTX3 (Fig. 9D). Furthermore, anti-IL-17RA attenuated IL-17 A-induced astrocyte activation and A1 polarization. Additionally, IL-17 A treatment significantly increased the phosphorylation of p-P65, p-IKKα, p-IKKβ, p-ERK, p-MEK, and p-P38 in SVG P12 cells (Fig. 9E, F), suggesting IL-17 A activates NF-κB/MAPK pathway in astrocytes, which was reversed by anti-IL-17RA. Furthermore, IL-17 A increased mRNA expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and GM-CSF, while IL-17RA neutralization reduced IL-17 A-mediated upregulation of these proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 9G), indicating IL-17 A induces inflammation dependent on binding with IL-17RA in astrocytes. In conclusion, our study elucidates that IL-17 A binding to IL-17RA activates NF-κB/MAPK pathway, drives neurotoxic A1 polarization of reactive astrocytes, and contributes to neuroinflammation after CA, providing a potential therapeutic target for CA induced hippocampal injury.

Discussion

Neuroinflammation is a critical driver of cognitive impairment following CA/CPR [30, 31]. However, the specific role of IL-17 A in interacting with brain-resident glial cells during CA-induced neuroinflammation remains poorly defined. Here, we demonstrate that CA activates the IL-17 A signaling pathway. Specifically, Th17 cells and their secreted IL-17 A infiltrate the hippocampus via a dysfunctional BBB in a rat model of CA. Subsequently, IL-17 A interacts with IL-17RA/RC heterodimers expressed on astrocytes. This ligand-receptor interaction activates the NF-κB, and MAPK signaling pathways, promoting neurotoxic A1 astrocyte polarization and exacerbating CA-induced neuroinflammatory responses. Critically, administration of an IL-17 A neutralizing antibody alleviated CA-induced neuroinflammation and neurofunctional injury, and improves the survival rates of CA rats, highlighting the IL-17 A signaling pathway as a promising therapeutic target for improving CA prognosis.

The KEGG pathway analysis also indicated significant enrichment in the “neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction” and “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” pathways, suggesting extensive intercellular crosstalk within the hippocampus in rats following cardiac arrest. The finding that Th17-derived IL-17 A promotes A1 astrocyte polarization and neuroinflammation via binding to IL-17RA provides experimental validation of such cellular interactions in the post-cardiac arrest hippocampus. While there is limited literature specifically addressing intercellular interactions in the hippocampus after cardiac arrest, a recent single-cell RNA sequencing study in a porcine model of cardiac arrest revealed notable interactions between neutrophils and microglia in hippocampal tissue [6]. Moreover, substantial evidence from studies on cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury supports the presence of robust intercellular communication in the brain under hypoxic-ischemic conditions [32–34]. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that hippocampal cellular crosstalk constitutes an important component of the pathological process following cardiac arrest, warranting further focused investigation in future studies.

IL-17 A originates primarily from Th17 cells and infiltrates the brain parenchyma following BBB disruption. A previous study on migraine demonstrates that nitroglycerin (NTG) enhances BBB permeability, promotes IL-17 A infiltration into the medulla, and triggers microglia-mediated neuroinflammation [35], and Th17 cells are known to penetrate the BBB in multiple sclerosis (MS), activating inflammatory cells and driving neurodegeneration [36]. In line with these findings, our study revealed elevated levels of Th17 cells and IL-17 A/F in the blood and hippocampus following asphyxial CA, accompanied by increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, GM-CSF). This implicates the IL-17 A signaling pathway in both systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation post-CA. Moreover, we observed decreased expression of the tight junction proteins Occludin, Claudin-5, and ZO-1 in the hippocampus, confirming CA-induced BBB disruption. Consequently, the infiltration of Th17 cells and IL-17 A/F through this compromised BBB exacerbates CA-induced neuroinflammation.

Astrocytes, constituting approximately 30% of CNS cells [37], are essential for CNS homeostasis [38], regulating synaptic development [39], neuronal metabolism, BBB integrity [40], and clearance of metabolic byproducts and proteins [41, 42]. Following CNS injury, astrocytes become reactive [43] and exhibit phenotypic heterogeneity, broadly categorized into neurotoxic A1 and neuroprotective A2 subtypes that function at distinct disease stages [44]. Building on established evidence, A1 astrocytes activated early in cerebral ischemia secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ), driving neuroinflammation and neuronal damage [45–47]. In this study, we observed significant upregulation of A1 astrocyte markers (C3, Fbln5, Ligp1, Serping1) in the hippocampus at 24 h post-CA. This A1 astrocyte activation was accompanied by increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, GM-CSF). Notably, markers of the A2 phenotype (Cd109, Clcf1, Ptx3, S100A10, Tgm1) showed no significant changes compared to controls. These findings demonstrate that astrocytes predominantly adopt a neurotoxic A1 phenotype during the early phase of CA-induced CNS injury, mediating neuroinflammation. Given the established role of A2 astrocytes in enhancing neuronal survival during later disease stages [48], future studies should explore whether this neuroprotective phenotype emerges in the later phases of CA-induced brain injury.

IL-17 A/F binding to IL-17RA/IL-17RC heterodimers activates NF-κB, MAPK, and ERK pathways, upregulating pro-inflammatory chemokines/cytokines to drive inflammation [13]. These receptors are expressed on neurons, astrocytes, and microglia in the CNS [49], implicating IL-17 A signaling in neuroinflammation. In EAE mice, astrocytic/microglial IL-17RA upregulation identifies it as a therapeutic target [50]. We demonstrate upregulated IL-17RA/RC expression in the hippocampal astrocytes following asphyxial CA. Peripheral IL-17 A activates microglia in hyperammonemia [51] and accelerates PD neurodegeneration [52], indicating that IL-17 A involved in neuroinflammation. In stroke, γδT cell-derived IL-17 A/TNF-α co-induce astrocytic CXCL-1, promoting neutrophil infiltration [20], while gut microbiota-targeted IL-17 A blockade improves outcomes [19, 21]. In CA, Th17-derived IL-17 A/F binds astrocytic IL-17RA/RC, activating NF-κB/MAPK pathways. This cascade promotes neurotoxic A1 polarization and increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, GM-CSF), exacerbating neuroinflammation. Additionally, IL-17 neutralization reversed A1 polarization, attenuated neuroinflammation, improved hippocampal injury and survival rates of CA rats.

In conclusion, CA induces Th17 cell/IL-17 A/F CNS infiltration via disrupted BBB. Subsequent IL-17 A/F binding to astrocytic IL-17RA/RC heterodimers activates NF-κB/MAPK pathways, enhancing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression. This exacerbates CA-induced neuroinflammation and hippocampal injury. Critically, IL-17RA neutralization alleviates these pathologies, offering a targeted strategy to improve CA prognosis (Fig. 10).
