Effect Modification by Exercise Habit on the Association Between Participation in Community Gathering Places and Cognitive Disability
Authors: Kazuki Uemura, Tsukasa Kamitani, Koutatsu Nagai, Tetsuya Ueda, Emi Fukuda, Masafumi Kuzuya, Minoru Yamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCommunity gathering places in Japan, supported by local governments, are expected to promote physical and cognitive health through physical activity and social interaction, in line with the World Health Organization's concept of age‐friendly environments. However, evidence regarding their effects on dementia prevention remains inconsistent. This study aimed to assess whether habitual exercise modifies the association between participation in community gathering places and incident cognitive disability in older adults.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the Public Survey of Long‐Term Care Prevention in Habikino City, Japan, and included community‐dwelling adults aged ≥ 65 years without certified care needs. Participation in community gathering places was investigated at baseline and incident cognitive disability was tracked from January 2020 to January 2024. We used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) after adjusting for demographics and health status. To test the effect modification by exercise habit, product terms between participation and exercise status were included in the model. We addressed missing baseline data using multiple imputation.ResultsA total of 2758 eligible older adults were included in the primary analysis. We observed a potential effect modification by exercise habit status (p for interaction = 0.092). Participation in community‐gathering places was associated with a lower hazard of cognitive disability among individuals without exercise habits (adjusted HR [95% CI], 0.51 [0.27, 0.97]), whereas no such association was observed among those with exercise habits (1.09 [0.57, 2.09]).ConclusionsThe association between participation in community gathering places and cognitive disability may differ according to habitual exercise status. These findings suggest that municipally supported community gathering places may represent an accessible form of participation associated with a lower risk of cognitive disability among older adults without established exercise habits.

Introduction

Recent evidence suggests that nearly 40% of dementia cases may be preventable by addressing a range of modifiable risk factors [1]. The 2024 Lancet Commission highlighted the importance of age‐friendly and supportive community environments as part of population‐based strategies for dementia risk reduction [1]. This perspective aligns with global public health strategies, including the World Health Organization's (WHO) Global Age‐friendly Cities Guide [2], and is reflected in various policy and community‐level initiatives that facilitate older adults' participation in group activities [2, 3, 4, 5].

A defining feature of the Japanese long‐term care prevention system's 2015 transition toward a population‐based approach was the municipal promotion of kayoi‐no‐ba, which are locally organized group activities led by older residents. Community gathering places share conceptual similarities with community‐based group activities for older adults described in other countries, as both promote social participation and regular group engagement, although the former have been developed within Japan's long‐term care prevention framework [6, 7]. Nationwide coverage reached 98.2% of municipalities by 2023, and approximately 6.7% of older adults were estimated to participate in these programs [7]. A key principle behind these community initiatives is inclusiveness, and activities, such as simple stretching, seated exercises, or light resistance training, are designed in a way that even older adults with mild disability or frailty can enjoy safely [8]. Although exercise‐based programs are the most prevalent (54.6%), several gatherings also contribute to well‐being by encouraging social interaction, which can increase opportunities for diverse forms of engagement and help prevent depressive symptoms [9].

Previous studies on community gathering places have primarily examined functional disability and long‐term care outcomes [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. However, evidence regarding cognitive outcomes remains limited and inconsistent, with one study reporting a protective association [15] and another finding no significant relationship [9]. Recent research has suggested heterogeneity in program effects by sociodemographic factors, with greater benefits reported among socially and demographically disadvantaged groups [11]; however, whether such heterogeneity is explained by differences in health behaviors has not been well examined. Given that regular physical activity is an established protective factor against dementia [1, 16, 17], exercise habits may modify the cognitive benefits associated with participation in community gathering places. Because community gatherings typically involve physical activity and social interaction, individuals already engaging in regular exercise may derive limited additional cognitive benefits.

Given these considerations, we hypothesized that the association between participation in community gathering places and cognitive disability would differ according to exercise habits. Consequently, we aimed to examine the effect modification by exercise habit on the association between participation in community gathering places and incident cognitive disability in community‐dwelling older adults. Exploring heterogeneity in exercise habits, an established individual‐level protective factor for dementia, may help interpret the relevance of these municipally supported, inclusive community programs across older adults with diverse health behaviors.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

This retrospective cohort study used data from a population‐based survey conducted in Habikino City, Osaka Prefecture, Japan. As of 2020, the city had approximately 108,700 residents, with individuals aged 65 years and older comprising 31% of the total population. Located in the Osaka Metropolitan Area, Habikino City is a suburban residential city within a larger urban region. We used data from the “Public Survey of Long‐Term Care Prevention and Needs in Spheres of Daily Life”, a survey conducted by municipalities as part of Japan's long‐term care prevention policy to identify community‐level needs and challenges [18]. A postal, self‐administered questionnaire was distributed between January 20 and February 7, 2020. It targeted a random sample of community‐dwelling adults aged 65 years and older who had not been certified as requiring long‐term care services (i.e., care level 1 or higher) under Japan's Long‐Term Care Insurance (LTCI) system, thereby identifying them as physically and cognitively independent at the time of sampling. A total of 3511 individuals who met the criteria were selected. According to the eligibility criteria, individuals who received a new LTCI care certification or were found to have developed cognitive impairment between the sampling date and the time of the survey were excluded from the present analysis. Certification status was verified using administrative records from Habikino City. All survey responses were linked to the LTCI data using anonymous identification codes to ensure confidentiality.

Habikino City granted Osaka Metropolitan University access to these data for academic research purposes. The use of data in this study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Rehabilitation Science, Osaka Metropolitan University (Approval No. 2023–120). Owing to the study's retrospective design and the anonymized nature of the dataset, individual informed consent was not required.

Exposure: Participation in Community Gathering Places

Individuals who reported attending any community gathering place at least once a week were defined as “participants”, while those with less than weekly attendance were categorized as “non‐participants”. The definition was based on the following baseline questionnaire item: “How often do you participate in “Kayoi‐no‐ba”, such as “Habikino Wellness”, “the Senior Citizens' Salon”, and “the Ikiiki‐Hyakusai Exercise”, for frailty prevention?” Response options were: (1) “4 or more times a week”, (2) “2–3 times a week”, (3) “once a week”, (4) “1–3 times a month”, (5) “a few times a year”, and (6) “never”. Participation was defined as attending at least once per week, as community gathering place programs supported by Habikino City are generally organized on a weekly basis. Further details are provided in Text S1.

Outcome: Cognitive Disability

We used data from the Activities of Daily Living Independence Assessment Criteria for Elderly Individuals with Dementia, developed by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare (MHLW), as a measure of cognitive disability and a proxy for dementia [19]. These data were obtained through a standardized in‐home assessment conducted under the national LTCI scheme established in 2000 [20]. All individuals in the study were aged 65 years or older and were enrolled in the national LTCI, which is a mandatory system for all residents aged 40 and older. Those who applied for long‐term care services underwent eligibility assessment conducted by trained personnel who visited their homes. During these home visits, a standardized protocol was used to evaluate the functional status, including basic and instrumental activities of daily living, cognitive function (e.g., memory, orientation, and communication), and the presence of mental or behavioral symptoms. Based on this assessment, individuals were categorized into eight cognitive disability levels: 0, I, IIa, IIb, IIIa, IIIb, IV, and M, where higher levels indicated increasing severity (0 = independent to M = requiring specialized medical treatment). Level II indicates that individuals may experience some difficulty with communication or behavioral symptoms that can interfere with daily functioning, although they remain largely independent with minimal support. We defined incident cognitive disability as any classification above level IIa, based on a previous validation study demonstrating the appropriateness of this threshold [21]. This definition has been adopted in previous epidemiological studies [22, 23, 24].

The resulting index of cognitive disability has shown a strong inverse correlation with the Mini‐Mental State Examination (Spearman's ρ = −0.74) [25]. In our study, we used the in‐home assessment of cognitive disability, which has previously been shown to be highly consistent with independent medical assessments conducted by a physician panel (Pearson's r = 0.80) [26]. The initial certification is, in principle, valid for 6 months, and subsequent reassessments are generally conducted every 12 months.

Effect Modifier: Exercise Habit

Following the definition provided by the National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan [27], we assessed participants' exercise habits at baseline using the following question: “Have you been engaging in exercises that cause sweating for more than 30 min, twice per week, for over a year?” The responses were recorded as binary variables (yes or no). This question aims to capture habitual moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity. Exercise habits are generally characterized by four components: frequency, duration, intensity, and continuity. The present criterion corresponds to individuals engaging in exercise at least twice per week for 30 min or more over a period of at least 1 year. This approach, established by the MHLW, has been adopted as a target indicator in the National Health Promotion Policy, Health Japan 21, and is widely used as a standard method for assessing exercise habits in Japan [28].

Confounders

Information on age, sex, and LTCI certification status for support levels 1–2 was obtained from administrative records provided by the Habikino City. LTCI certification for support levels 1–2 is granted to individuals with mild disabilities who remain relatively independent compared with those classified as needing care (i.e., care level 1 or higher) but still require some assistance with daily activities [29]. All other variables were collected via the baseline postal questionnaire and were considered potential confounders in the association between community gathering place participation and cognitive impairment. The questionnaire‐based covariates included: living arrangement (living alone vs. with others), subjective economic status (responses of “somewhat poor” or “very poor”), smoking and drinking status (current vs. never/past), chronic conditions (the presence or absence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia), potential risk of functional disability, self‐rated health (responses of “poor” or “very poor”), and depressive symptoms. Potential risk of functional disability was evaluated using the Kihon Checklist, focusing on domains such as physical function, nutrition, and housebound status [30]. These variables were included as covariates to account for baseline functional vulnerability and were not used as outcome measures. In particular, item 17, which addresses reduced frequency of going out, was used as an indicator of risk for being homebound. Subjective cognitive decline was defined as a response indicating cognitive concern for either item 19 or item 20 of the Kihon Checklist. Depressive symptoms were assessed using a two‐question case‐finding instrument [31].

Statistical Analyses

We summarized baseline characteristics using descriptive statistics. We presented continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SDs) and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Additionally, we examined the distribution of baseline characteristics according to participation in community gathering places and exercise habits among individuals with complete exposure and effect modifier data. We also described the baseline characteristics separately for individuals with and without missing data.

For the time‐to‐event analysis, we defined the start of the follow‐up as the final day of the baseline survey period (February 7, 2020), followed by incident cognitive disability, relocation outside the city, death, or the end of the observation period (January 18, 2024), whichever occurred first. We estimated the cumulative incidence of cognitive disability using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared participants and non‐participants in community gathering places within subgroups defined by baseline exercise habits using log‐rank tests. Individuals who died before cognitive disability occurred were censored at the time of death.

First, we fitted a model without including the product term to estimate the overall association between participation in community gatherings and incident cognitive disability. We then evaluated effect modification by exercise habits using a product term between participation status and exercise habit status in the cause‐specific Cox proportional hazards models. The statistical significance of the product terms was tested using the Wald test [32]. In multivariable models, we adjusted for age, sex, LTCI certification status (support levels 1–2), living arrangement, low economic status, smoking and drinking status, chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia), potential risk of functional disability (Kihon Checklist domains), poor self‐rated health, and depressive symptoms. The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for participation in community gathering places by exercise habit status were calculated from Cox regression models using the postestimation command “lincom” in Stata. We evaluated proportional hazards assumptions using Schoenfeld residuals.

We addressed the missing baseline data using multiple imputation with chained equations under the missing‐at‐random assumption. We generated 20 imputed datasets, fitted the regression models within each dataset, and pooled the estimates using Rubin's rules [33, 34]. We treated analyses based on multiple imputation as the primary analysis.

We conducted multiple sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness. Specifically, we (1) performed complete‐case analyses; (2) excluded cases occurring within the first year of follow‐up to reduce potential reverse causation; (3) applied alternative exposure definitions based on participation frequency; (4) estimated 4‐year risk ratios using modified Poisson regression; (5) fitted Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models to account for death as a competing event; and (6) examined potential effect modification by physical functional decline (defined as meeting the criteria for decline in the physical function domain of the Kihon Checklist). All sensitivity analyses except for the complete‐case analysis were conducted using multiple imputation datasets. We also calculated E‐values within each exercise habit stratum to assess the robustness of the observed associations with potential unmeasured confounding factors using a publicly available calculator [35, 36].

All analyses were performed using Stata software (version 18.0; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). We considered two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 to be statistically significant. Because statistical tests for interaction generally have lower power, we interpreted p‐values < 0.10 as suggestive of potential effect modification, to avoid overlooking potentially meaningful interactions [37, 38, 39].

We conducted multiple sensitivity analyses, and the results were generally consistent with those of the primary analysis. We performed complete case analyses and excluded cases that occurred within the first year of follow‐up (n = 72) to reduce potential reverse causation, both of which showed estimates comparable to those of the primary analysis (Tables S3 and S4). We examined alternative definitions of participation frequency and observed that the association was weakened at lower participation frequencies (Table S5). We found that the results from the modified Poisson regression and Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models were generally consistent with those of the primary analysis (Tables S6 and S7). Additionally, we examined the effect modification by physical functional decline and observed a stronger association among individuals with physical functional decline (Table S8). We also calculated the E‐values for stratum‐specific hazard ratios to assess robustness to potential unmeasured confounding factors (Table S9).

Discussion

The association between participation in community gathering places and cognitive disability differed according to exercise habits, suggesting a possible effect modification. Participation in these programs was associated with a lower hazard of cognitive disability among older adults without regular exercise habits, whereas no such association was observed among those with existing exercise habits. Sensitivity analyses yielded generally consistent results, supporting the overall robustness of our findings.

The cognitive benefits of participating in community groups, including those beyond the local government‐supported gathering places examined in this study, have been reported in previous studies [22, 40, 41], with findings that are partially aligned with ours in supporting the potential cognitive benefits of social engagement. However, an outcome‐wide epidemiological study found no significant association between social participation (broadly defined as involvement in any community organizations or committees) and cognitive disability, nor any effect modification by demographic factors such as age or sex [42]. We also did not observe a statistically significant association between participation in community gathering places and cognitive disability in the overall analysis. In contrast, our study examined potential heterogeneity based on lifestyle‐related factors and suggested that the association between participation in community gathering places and cognitive disability may differ according to habitual exercise status. Here, exercise habits represent individual‐level behavior, whereas community gathering places reflect municipally supported community initiatives. Their interaction helps clarify the role of community gathering places in relation to established individual exercise behaviors within population‐level dementia prevention.

Our results suggest that individuals more susceptible to functional or cognitive decline may particularly benefit from participating in community gathering places. This interpretation is conceptually consistent with previous findings showing that the protective effects of such participation against functional disability were observed predominantly among older adults with frailty, but not among those who were robust or pre‐frail [10]. Additional analyses stratified by physical functional decline showed a similar directional pattern. Because most community gathering places offer light‐intensity exercise to accommodate even frail older adults [8], the physical component may provide limited additional cognitive benefit for those already regularly active. In contrast, for individuals without established exercise habits, even low‐threshold activity—combined with the social and psychological benefits of group participation, such as strengthened social ties and improved mood [9, 43]—may contribute to cognitive health.

These findings highlight the potential role of community gathering places as an accessible approach to population‐level dementia prevention, particularly for older adults who face barriers to maintaining regular moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity. The attenuation of estimates at lower participation frequencies (Supplementary Table S5) suggests a potential dose–response pattern; however, the persistence of the directional association, even with less frequent participation, underscores the low‐threshold nature of these programs. Designed to be inclusive, these programs may promote participation across diverse functional levels [8]. In this context, community gathering places align with broader public health goals related to social participation and healthy aging emphasized in international and national initiatives, such as the WHO's Decade of Healthy Aging initiative [44] and Health Japan 21 [45].

This study has several limitations. First, cognitive disability in this study was not based on a clinical diagnosis of dementia. Nevertheless, the cognitive disability classification used in this study—based on Japan's LTCI system—is nationally standardized and has shown reasonable concordance with widely used cognitive assessments such as the Mini‐Mental State Examination [25]. This operational definition has also been applied in prior population‐based research [9, 22, 23, 40, 42]. Second, although the survey achieved a relatively high response rate with minimal attrition during follow‐up, 591 participants (21.4%) had missing values for at least one variable. Although selection bias may have arisen if those with missing data differed systematically from the complete cases, the consistency of the multiple imputation results suggests that this bias was likely minimal. Third, because this study was conducted in a single municipality, the generalizability of the findings to other regions may be limited. Fourth, because participation status was assessed at baseline rather than newly initiated, individuals who were already healthier or more active may have been more likely to participate, potentially leading to a selection bias. In addition, baseline differences between participants and non‐participants may not have been fully accounted for despite statistical adjustment. Given the observational design of this study, residual confounding factors cannot be excluded. Exercise habits and other covariates were assessed at baseline, and the temporal order between exposure, effect modifiers, and confounders could not be fully established. Finally, certain unmeasured confounders including educational attainment may have affected the observed associations. While our analysis accounted for economic conditions, it did not adjust for participants' educational background, which may have contributed to residual confounding. Future longitudinal research should consider incorporating these variables into the design to better clarify causal relationships.

In conclusion, this study found that the association between participation in community gathering places and cognitive disability differed according to the habitual exercise status of older adults. Although the intensity of physical activity in these settings is generally low, participation is associated with cognitive benefits in individuals who do not exercise regularly. These findings suggest that community gathering places may function as accessible points of engagement for older adults without established exercise habits and underscore their inclusive potential within community‐based prevention.
