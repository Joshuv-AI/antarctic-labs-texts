Optimal Depth-Three Circuits for Inner Product
Mohit Gurumukhani, Daniel Kleber, Ramamohan Paturi, Christopher Rosin, Navid Talebanfard

Abstract
We show that Inner Product in 2 ​ n 2n variables, 𝐈𝐏 n ​ ( x , y ) = x 1 ​ y 1 ⊕ … ⊕ x n ​ y n {\mathbf{IP}}_{n}(x,y)=x_{1}y_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus x_{n}y_{n} , can be computed by depth-3 bottom fan-in 2 circuits of size 𝚙𝚘𝚕𝚢 ⁡ ( n ) ⋅ ( 9 / 5 ) n {\mathtt{poly}}(n)\cdot(9/5)^{n} , matching the lower bound of Göös, Guan, and Mosnoi (Inform. Comput.’24). Our construction is given via the following steps. 1. We provide a general template for constructing optimal depth-3 circuits with bottom fan-in k k for an arbitrary function f f . We do this in two steps. First, we partition the accepting inputs to f f into several carefully defined orbits . Second, for each orbit, we construct one k k -CNF that (a) accepts the largest number of inputs from that orbit and (b) rejects all inputs rejected by f f . This partitioning of inputs into orbits is based on the symmetries of f f . 2. We instantiate the template for 𝐈𝐏 n {\mathbf{IP}}_{n} and k = 2 k=2 . Guided by a modularity principle that these optimal 2-CNFs may be constructed by composing variable-disjoint copies of small formulas, we use computer search to identify a small set of 2-CNFs each on at most 4 variables which we call building blocks . 3. We then use analytic combinatorial techniques to determine optimal ways to combine these building blocks and construct these optimal 2-CNFs. We believe that the above steps can be applied to a wide range of functions to determine their depth-3 complexity.

Introduction

A
\Sigma_{3}
circuit, is a depth-3 OR-AND-OR circuit with unbounded fan-in. Despite their simplicity, these circuits are surprisingly powerful. Any
n
-variate Boolean function can be computed by a
\Sigma_{3}
circuit of size
O(2^{n/2})

[
6
]

2^{O(n/\log\log n)}

2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
and beating this barrier remains a major open problem in the field
[
12
]
.

Even a further restriction of depth-3 circuits shows surprising power. Let
\Sigma^{k}_{3}
to be the class of
\Sigma_{3}
circuits in which every bottom OR gate is connected to at most
k

k
-CNFs and the number of these formulas serves as a natural measure of size for the circuit. We denote the smallest number of
k
-CNFs needed to express a function

by
\size^{k}_{3}(f)

Near-maximal lower bound arguments.

Paturi, Saks, and Zane

In order to prove strong

An affirmative answer yields a near-maximal

Depth-3 complexity of Inner Product.

The Inner Product modulo 2 function

n

{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}(x,y)=x_{1}y_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{n}y_{n}

{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}

{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}=\bigvee_{I\subseteq[n],|I|=\text{odd}}(\wedge_{i\in I}(x_{i}\wedge y_{i})\wedge_{i\not\in I}(\neg x_{i}\vee\neg y_{i}))

{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
and characterizing the formulas that maximize the probability of accepting an input under this restriction. For the upper bound, they used the above construction to cover solutions with small weight, i.e., the number of coordinates on which both
x
and
y

matching
formulas. We will discuss these formulas later as we will also use them in our construction.

Amano

{\mathbf{IP}}_{b}

To find such depth-3 circuits for a fixed

A recipe for constructing depth-3 circuits

Let
f:\Q^{n}\rightarrow\Q
be an arbitrary Boolean function and consider the task of proving an upper bound on
\size_{3}^{k}(f)
, the size of the smallest
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuit computing
f
.
In this section, we will provide a general framework for tackling such a task and will instantiate it to construct optimal circuits for
{\mathbf{IP}}
. We find it valuable to abstract out this framework since it provides a systematic way of constructing near optimal circuits for many classes of Boolean functions and also without this framework, our construction may seem to be constructed out of the blue. Our framework reduces the task of constructing
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuits for
f
to the task of constructing many different
k
-CNFs, each
consistent
with
f
, and each one maximizing the number of satisfying assignments with a certain property. We say a
k
-CNF
F
is consistent with the function
f
if for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, it holds that
F(x)=1\implies f(x)=1
, i.e.,
\mathbf{sol}(F)\subseteq f^{-1}(1)
, where
\mathbf{sol}(F)
is the set of satisfying assignments of
F
.

To describe our framework, we need to define the notion of automorphism group and orbit of
f
.

Definition 2.1

(Automorphism group and orbits)
.

For any
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, define the
automorphism group of
f
,
\aut_{f}
, to be the group of permutations
\pi
such that for all
x\in\Q^{n}
,
f(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. Consider the action of
\aut_{f}
on
f^{-1}(1)
and denote the set of orbits of this action by
\orb_{f}
.

Note that for each
S\in\orb_{f}
we have
S\subseteq f^{-1}(1)
.
We now define the following quantities associated with orbits of
f
and how
k
-CNFs capture it.

Definition 2.2
.

For any
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and
S\in\orb_{f}
, define

\mu_{f,S,k}:=\max_{F:F\text{ is a }k\text{-CNF on }n\text{ variables consistent with }f}\{|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|\},

Furthermore, define
\rho_{f,S,k}:=|S|/\mu_{f,S,k}
, and
\rho^{*}_{f,k}=\max_{S\in\orb_{f}}\{\rho_{f,S,k}\}
.

In words,
\mu_{f,s,k}
is the largest number of assignments in the orbit that are accepted by a
k
-CNF which is consistent with
f
,
\rho_{f,s,k}
is the contribution of that orbit to
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuit for
f
and
\rho_{f,k}^{*}
is the maximum such contribution.
With this, we present the main result of our framework that
\rho^{*}_{f,k}
along with the number of orbits, governs the size of optimal
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuits for
f
. Formally,

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
and all functions
f:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, we have
\rho^{*}_{f,k}\leq\size^{k}_{3}(f)\leq O\left(n\cdot|\orb_{f}|\cdot\rho^{*}_{f,k}\right)
.

We prove this lemma in
Section

2.1
.
Notice that when
\left\lvert\orb_{f}\right\rvert
is bounded by a polynomial, then
\size^{k}_{3}(f)
and
\rho^{*}_{f,k}
are within a polynomial factor of each other. Therefore, to determine the
\Sigma^{k}_{3}
complexity of such a function, it is enough to find for each orbit
S
, a
k
-CNF consistent with the function with maximum number of solution in
S
. This is indeed the case with
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
and is exactly the strategy we will use to construct optimal
\Sigma_{3}^{2}
circuits for
{\mathbf{IP}}
.

2.1
Depth-3 upper bound - proof of
Lemma

2.3

We will need the following claim that shows how we can use a single
k
-CNF consistent with
f
that covers some fraction of an obit
S
of
f
and use it to find not too many
k
-CNFs that completely cover the orbit
S
of
f
.

Claim 2.4
.

Let
S\in\orb_{f}
and let
F
be an
n
-variate
k
-CNF consistent with
f
. There are
k
-CNFs
F_{1},\ldots,F_{t}
all consistent with
f
such that
S\subseteq\mathbf{sol}(F_{1})\cup\ldots\cup\mathbf{sol}(F_{t})
and
t=O(n|S|/|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|)

We will prove this claim at the end of this section. We first show how the proof of our desired lemma regarding the construction of optimal circuits for

follows using the claim.

Proof of
Lemma

2.3
.

We prove the lower bound and the upper bound part of the inequality separately.

For the lower bound, fix any
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuit
C
for
f
.
We express
C=\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and each
F_{i}
is a
k
-CNF. We will show that for all
S\in\orb_{f}
, it holds that
m\geq\rho_{f,S,k}
, which implies
m\geq\rho^{*}_{f,k}

Fix any
S\in\orb_{f}
.
We observe that each
F_{i}
must be consistent with
f
and so for all
i\in[m]
, we must have that
\left\lvert\mathbf{sol}(F_{i})\cap S\right\rvert\leq\mu_{f,S,k}
.
We next observe that for all
S\in\orb_{f}
, each assignment in
S
must be covered by some
F_{i}
.
Indeed, for each
\alpha\in S
, since
C(\alpha)=f(\alpha)=1
and
C=\lor_{i=1}^{m}F_{i}
, there must exist some

such that
F_{i}(\alpha)=1
.
Hence, we must we have
m\geq|S|/\mu_{f,S,k}=\rho_{f,S,k}
as desired.

For the upper bound, we construct our desired circuit
C
for
f
as follows.
For all
S\in\orb_{f}
, we let
F_{S}
be the
k
-CNF consistent with
f
that maximizes
\left\lvert\mathbf{sol}(F_{S})\cap S\right\rvert
.
By definition, each
F_{S}
accepts
\mu_{f,S,k}
many assignments from
S
.
We now apply
2.4
and construct
k
-CNFs
F_{S,1},\ldots,F_{S,t}
consistent with

which cover all
S
where
t=t(S)=O(n|S|/|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|)=O(n\rho_{f,S,k})
.
Our final depth-3 circuit

C=\lor_{S\in\orb_{f},i\in[t(S)]}F_{S,i}
.
Hence,
C
accepts all assignments from each of the orbits

Since each

f

The total number of
k

O(n\sum_{S\in\orb_{f}}\rho_{f,S,k})=O(n\cdot|\orb_{f}|\cdot\rho^{*}_{f,k})

∎

We finally prove our remaining claim of using a single
k
-CNF covering small fraction of an orbit to construct many different

-CNFs that entirely cover an orbit.

Proof of
2.4
.

Let
t=2|S|\ln|S|/|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|=O(n|S|/|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|)
. For each
i\in[t]
define
F_{i}=F^{\pi_{i}}
to be the permutation of
F
under a random permutation

. Fix
\alpha\in S
. It follows from the Orbit-Stabilizer theorem that for a random
\pi\in\aut_{f}
,
\Pr[\alpha\in\mathbf{sol}(F^{\pi})]=|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|/|S|
.
We then compute

\displaystyle\Pr[\alpha\not\in\mathbf{sol}(F_{1})\cup\ldots\cup\mathbf{sol}(F_{t})]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|/|S|\right)^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-t\cdot|\mathbf{sol}(F)\cap S|/|S|\right)

\displaystyle<1/|S|

where the last inequality follows by the choice of

and the fact that
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\leq 2^{n}
.
By a union bound over all
\alpha\in S
, we conclude that there indeed exists a choice of
F_{i}
s covering all assignments in
S
as desired.
∎

3.1
Orbits of IP and spectra of 2-CNFs

We now describe the orbits of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
and also introduce an associated quantity with
2
-CNFs - their spectra.

We first observe that the set
\aut_{{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}}
consists of all permutations that permute the
n
coordinates, and within each coordinate permute
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
. Thus
|\aut_{{\mathbf{IP}}}|=n!2^{n}
.
We now describe the orbits of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
.
For input
(x,y)\in(\{0,1\}^{n})^{2}
, define the following quantities:

1.

d_{2}(x,y)=\left\lvert\{i\in[n]:x_{i}=y_{i}=1\}\right\rvert
.

2.

d_{1}(x,y)=\left\lvert\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}\right\rvert
.

3.

d_{0}(x,y)=\left\lvert\{i\in[n]:x_{i}=y_{i}=0\}\right\rvert
.

We see that
d_{2}(x,y)+d_{1}(x,y)+d_{0}(x,y)=n
.
More importantly, when a permutation from
\aut_{{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}}
acts on
(x,y)
, the resultant
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in(\{0,1\}^{n})^{2}
is such that
d_{j}(x,y)=d_{j}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
for
j\in\{0,1,2\}
.
We also see that for any
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in(\{0,1\}^{n})^{2}
such that
d_{j}(x,y)=d_{j}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
for
j\in\{0,1,2\}
, there exists a permutation
\pi\in\aut_{{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}}
such that
\pi(x,y)=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Hence, these quantities precisely determine the orbits of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
.
In particular we have
|\orb_{{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}}|=O(n^{2})
.

We parameterize the orbits of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
as follows:

Definition 3.2

(Orbits of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
)
.

For
d_{2},d_{1},d_{0}\in[n]
such that
d_{2}+d_{1}+d_{0}=n
, let
S(d_{2},d_{1},d_{0})\subseteq(\{0,1\}^{n})^{2}
denote the set of assignments
(x,y)\in(\{0,1\}^{n})^{2}
such that
d_{j}(x,y)=d_{j}
for all
j\in\{0,1,2\}
.

For any
(x,y)\in S(d_{2},d_{1},d_{0})
, we have that
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}(x,y)=d_{2}\mod 2
. Observe that the sets
S(d_{2},d_{1},d_{0})
capture all the orbits of
{\mathbf{IP}}
as well as
\neg{\mathbf{IP}}
. We record the following fact regarding size of each of the orbits:

Fact 3.3

|S(d_{2},d_{1},d_{0})|=\binom{n}{d_{2}}\binom{n-d_{2}}{d_{0}}2^{n-d_{2}-d_{0}}=\binom{n}{d_{2}}\binom{d_{1}+d_{0}}{d_{1}}2^{d_{1}}

We define one more quantity associated with a
2
-CNF
F

{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}

where the monomials represent an orbit of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
and the coefficient for the monomials will represent the number of satisfying assignments of

Definition 3.4

(Spectrum of 2-CNF)

For a
2
-CNF
F

2n

G(x,y,z)=\sum_{p,q,r\in\mathbb{N}}C_{p,q,r}x^{p}y^{q}z^{r}

Notice that the spectrum of

5.1
Open Questions

The immediate question left open is to determine
\size^{k}_{3}({\mathbf{IP}}_{n})
for every
k\geq 3
. We believe that a similar methodology should work for upper bounds. However, the challenge is to find an argument that scales with
k
. It also remains open to find a non-trivial lower bound argument for any
k\geq 3
.

Another related open question is to construct an explicit degree
2
polynomial that requires
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuits of size
2^{n-o(n)}
for all constant
k
; this is open even for
k=2
. Existentially, Impagliazzo, Paturi, and Zane showed that a random degree
2
polynomial requires maximal
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuits
[
14
]
.

5.2
How did we discover these circuits?

We here document our journey of constructing optimal circuits for
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
. We hope this inspires future works that try to find small
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuits for various explicit functions.

Modular constructions

As a starting point, we believed that optimal circuits for
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
must be modular, i.e., obtained by combining disjoint copies of smaller
2
-CNFs. Our reasons for such a belief were twofold: first that all previous best known constructions of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
had this property; and second that optimal (and conjectured to be optimal)
\Sigma_{3}^{k}
circuits for other simple functions such as Parity and Majority have this property.

Searching for better circuits for small
n
.

Our first improvement came from examining the construction of
\Sigma_{3}^{2}
circuits for
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
by Amano
[
1
]
.
To construct such circuits, the paper used an integer programming solver on a set of constraints to find a disjunction of 7
2
-CNFs that compute
{\mathbf{IP}}^{1}_{4}
, and another disjunction of 7
2
-CNFs that compute
{\mathbf{IP}}^{0}_{4}
. To obtain the final circuit, the paper combines these small circuits to construct such a circuit larger
n
.

We used a similar set of constraints, solving using Kissat SAT solver, to identify a disjunction of 14
2
-CNF formulas that compute
{\mathbf{IP}}^{1}_{5}
, and another disjunction of 13
2
-CNF formulas that compute
{\mathbf{IP}}^{0}_{5}
; repeated use of these in Amano’s construction gives a slightly improved construction of circuits for
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
with size
2^{cn}
where
c\approx 0.9509
. This inspired us to seek further improvements.

Search for small Pareto-optimal building blocks

Suppose
A
and
B
are
2
-CNFs consistent with
{\mathbf{IP}}^{b}_{n}
for some
b\in\{0,1\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, we say
A
dominates
B
if each coefficient in
A
’s spectrum is at least as large as the corresponding coefficient in
B
’s spectrum.
We performed an exhaustive computer search for Pareto-optimal (non-dominated) 2-CNF building blocks for
{\mathbf{IP}}^{0}_{2}
that identified three distinct Pareto-optimal building blocks:
\mathbf{Matching},\mathbf{2Imp}
and two copies of
\ \mathbf{Nand}
. In particular,
\mathbf{2Imp}
was a new building block first identified by this exhaustive search, and was crucial in our optimal constructions. We also identified many more such Pareto-optimal building blocks for other small values of
n
that we did not end up using for our construction.

Search for compositions of building blocks

After identifying building blocks, we next empirically tried to find out for some large fixed values of
n
, for each orbit of
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
, how to combine our Pareto-optimal building blocks to construct a
2
-CNF consistent with
{\mathbf{IP}}_{n}
that maximizes the number of solutions from that orbit.
To do this, we enumerated all distinct compositions of these building blocks. This exercise not only pointed us the best composition for each orbit but also let us identify the hardest orbit for our compositions (the one with that maximizes the ratio of size of the orbit to the number of solutions from that orbit).

We did this for
n=50
,
n=100
, and
n=200
, as well as restricted enumeration for
n=400
and
n=800
near our conjectured hardest orbits.
These identified best compositions using
\mathbf{Matching},\mathbf{2Imp},\mathbf{Nand}
, similar to the constructions later used in our proof. The compositions found by enumeration correspond to depth-3 circuits of size
{\mathtt{poly}}(n)\cdot 2^{cn}
, with:

n=50

c=0.8344320

n=100

c=0.8414042

n=200

c=0.8447647

n=400

c=0.8463819

n=800

c=0.8471913

A least-squares fit to the data yields
0.8479999-0.6470509/n
, suggesting eventual convergence to
0.8479999
for large
n
. This is extremely close to
\log(9/5)=0.8479969
, which would match the lower bound from
[
11
]
, motivating us to pursue an upper bound with
c=\log(9/5)
.

Restricted compositions to cover regions

After this, we analytically solved for optimal constructions using
\mathbf{Matching},\mathbf{2Imp},\mathbf{Nand}
using saddle point methods. Doing this led us to identifying regions 2, 3, 4 from
Definition

3.11
and pointed us to the fact that optimal constructions from these regions use at most
1
or
2
building blocks.
We then performed a search for optimal compositions (using at most 2 types of building blocks) for each of these regions for
n=200
.
During this search, we wanted to minimize the number of distinct compositions required to ‘cover’ a region so that the ratio of size of orbit to number of solutions from that orbit is at most
(9/5)^{n}
.
This search helped us successfully identify one or two compositions (involving only 1 or 2 gates) that we used in our constructions.

6
Preliminaries and notation

We use
\log(x)
to denote logarithm of
x
with base
2
; we will use
\ln(x)
to denote natural logarithm, i.e., logarithm of
x
with base
e
. We will also often make use of the well known binary entropy function
H:[0,1]\to[0,1]
defined as
H(x)=x\log(1/x)+(1-x)\log(1/(1-x))

We will utilize the following well known approximation for binomial coefficients (see for instance equation 7.14 from

Theorem 6.1

For all

such that

\frac{1}{m+1}2^{mH(k/m)}\leq\binom{m}{k}\leq 2^{mH(k/m)}

where

\frac{f(u_{i},v_{i})}{2\pi u_{i}^{p+1}v_{i}^{q+1}\sqrt{\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{2}}}\right)\right).

Since
p^{2}+q^{2}\geq 4
, and
u_{i},v_{i}\leq n
, and that the entries of
\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}
are bounded by
{\mathtt{poly}}(u_{i},v_{i})
, we infer that there exists a universal constant
K_{1}
such that the coefficient is at least

n^{-K_{1}}\cdot\frac{f(u_{i},v_{i})}{u_{i}^{p/2}v_{i}^{q}}=n^{-K_{1}}\cdot\frac{(u_{i}+(v_{i}+1)^{2})^{B_{i}}(2v_{i}+1)^{2C_{i}}}{u_{i}^{p/2}v_{i}^{q}}

∎

8.3
Bound for region 4

In this subsection, we obtain asymptotic bound on coefficient of generating function from
\mathcal{R}_{4}
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.5
.

We first prove that
Q_{i}
has a unique real nonnegative root.
First, since
Q_{i}
has degree
3
, it has at least one real root.
Let
x_{0}
be any such real root.
Since
Q_{i}(x_{0})=0
, we rearrange to infer that
x_{0}
must satisfy:

\frac{4C_{i}x_{0}}{2x_{0}+1}+\frac{2x_{0}^{2}(2A_{i}-p)}{2x_{0}^{2}+1}=q

We see that the left side at
x=0
equals
0

and as
x\rightarrow+\infty
, it approaches
2C_{i}+2A_{i}-p=q+r
(this is because
n=2A_{i}+2C_{i}=p+q+r
).
Since
q
and
r
are nonnegative and right side equals
q
, by intermediate value theorem, there must exist a unique value
y\geq 0
such that left side equals
q
, proving our result.

We now prove the asymptotic bound.
For
i\in[2]
, let
f_{i}(u,v)=P_{i}(u^{1/2},v,1)
. Since
x
appears in squared form,
f_{i}
is still product of three polynomials. Furthermore, since
2A_{i}+2C_{i}=p+q+r=n
, and both the polynomials are homogeneous, we infer that the coefficient of
u^{p/2}v^{q}
in
f_{i}(x,y,z)
equals the coefficient of
x^{p}y^{q}z^{r}
in
P_{i}(x,y,z)
.

Define
h_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
by
h(u,v)=\ln(f_{i}(u,v))-(p/2)\ln(u)-q\ln(v)
.
We will find non-negative
(u_{i},v_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\nabla_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})=0
.
We compute that
\frac{\partial h_{i}}{\partial u}=\frac{A_{i}}{u+2v^{2}+1}-\frac{p}{2u}
.
Setting this to
0
and rearranging, we infer that

\displaystyle u_{i}=\frac{p(2v_{i}^{2}+1)}{2A_{i}-p}

(
1
)

We next compute that
\frac{\partial h_{i}}{\partial v}=\frac{4C_{i}}{2v+1}+\frac{4vA_{i}}{u+2v^{2}+1}-\frac{q}{v}
.
Setting this to
0
, rearranging, substituting in
Equation

1

,
and using the fact that
8C_{i}+4(2A_{i}-p)-4q=4r
(since
2A_{i}+2C_{i}=p+q+r=n
),
we infer that
v_{i}
satisfies the following cubic equation:

(4r)\cdot v_{i}^{3}+(4A_{i}-2p-2q)\cdot v_{i}^{2}+(4C_{i}-2q)\cdot v_{i}-q=0

In other words,
Q_{i}(v_{i})=0
.
From above, we know that there is a unique nonnegative root of
Q_{i}
and we will let
v_{i}
be that.

With this value of
u_{i},v_{i}
, we show that

\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})
is positive definite where
\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}
is the the Hessian of
h_{i}
. For this, let
\alpha_{i}(x,y)=h_{i}(\exp(x),\exp(y))
. Using the fact that
h
has nonnegative coefficients in its power series, we use the well known result (see section 4.5 from
[
3
]
) that the function
\alpha_{i}
as we defined is convex.
Also by picking any three non-zero terms of the power series and checking that they don’t lie in single line, we see that
\alpha_{i}
will be strictly convex and so
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})
is positive definite. Using the fact that
\nabla_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})=0
and chain rule, we obtain that
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})=D\cdot\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})\cdot D
where
D
is the diagonal matrix with entries
(u_{i},v_{i})
.
Since
D
is invertible, we infer that
\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})
is indeed positive definite.

We next apply the triangle inequality to infer that in the complex torus with
\left\lvert u\right\rvert=u_{i},\left\lvert v\right\rvert=v_{i}
, the unique global maximum for
f_{i}
occurs at
(u_{i},v_{i})
(carefully checking that the values at
(\pm u_{i},\pm v_{i})
are smaller).
Also since
r\leq n-8
,
p+q+r=n
and
n\geq K_{0}
where we will set
K_{0}
to be large enough universal constant, it must be the case that
(p/2)^{2}+q^{2}\geq 4
.
Therefore we satisfy all conditions to apply
Lemma

8.1
with
(u_{0},v_{0})=(u_{i},v_{i})
, we infer that the coefficient of
u^{p/2}v^{q}
in
f_{i}(u,v)
equals

\frac{f(u_{i},v_{i})}{2\pi u_{i}^{p+1}v_{i}^{q+1}\sqrt{\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}(u_{i},v_{i})}}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{2}}}\right)\right).

Since
p^{2}+q^{2}\geq 4
, and
u_{i},v_{i}\leq n
, and that the entries of
\mathcal{H}_{h_{i}}
are bounded by
{\mathtt{poly}}(u_{i},v_{i})
, we infer that there exists a universal constant
K_{1}
such that the coefficient is at least

n^{-K_{1}}\cdot\frac{f(u_{i},v_{i})}{u_{i}^{p/2}v_{i}^{q}}=n^{-K_{1}}\cdot\frac{(u_{i}+v_{i}^{2}+1)^{A_{i}}(2v_{i}+1)^{2C_{i}}}{u_{i}^{p/2}v_{i}^{q}}

∎

Appendix A
Proving coefficient asymptotics

In this section, we will prove our required lemma regarding bounding coefficients of power series of an analytic function -
Lemma

8.1
.

To help prove this, we will require the following well known result that coefficient expansion of a generating function can be expressed as a Cauchy integral (see equation 1.5 from
[
20
]
):

Theorem A.1

(Bivariate Cauchy integral formula)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be analytic on a compact neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
around
(0,0)
. Let
f(u,v)=\sum_{r=(r_{1},r_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}C_{r}u^{r_{1}}v^{r_{2}}
where for all
i\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
,
C_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
C_{i}\geq 0
. Then, for all
r\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
, it holds that

C_{r}=\oint_{x\in\mathcal{N}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{2}f(x)\cdot x_{1}^{-r_{1}-1}\cdot x_{2}^{-r_{2}-1}\,dx.

We will utilize the following result regarding bounding Cauchy integral:

Theorem A.2

(Theorem 5.2 from
[
20
]
, specialized)
.

Let
A,\phi:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be analytic on a compact neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
around
(0,0)
. Furthermore assume that the real part of
\phi
is nonnegative on
\mathcal{N}
and vanishes only at
(0,0)
, and that the Hessian matrix
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}
of
\phi
is such that
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0)
is nonsingular. Let,
I(\lambda)=\int_{\mathcal{N}}A(z)e^{-\lambda\phi(z)}
. Then for
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we have that:

I(\lambda)=A(0,0)\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\det(\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0))}}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\right).

Using these, we obtain the following estimate regarding estimating coefficient of an analytic function:

Proof of
Lemma

8.1
.

We apply
Theorem

A.1
with the contour
\mathcal{C}
being product of circles with
\left\lvert u\right\rvert=u_{0},\left\lvert v\right\rvert=v_{0}
so that

C_{p,q}=\frac{-1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{(u,v)\in\mathcal{C}}f(u,v)u^{-p-1}v^{-q-1}dudv.

For
\theta=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})\in[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
, we perform change of variables to express the points in the contour as
u=u(\theta)=u_{0}e^{i\theta_{1}},v=v(\theta)=v_{0}e^{i\theta_{2}}
.
We see that
du=u_{0}ie^{i\theta_{1}}d\theta_{1}
and
dv=v_{0}ie^{i\theta_{2}}d\theta_{2}
.
We also use the fact that
\exp(h(u,v))=\frac{f(u,v)}{u^{p}v^{q}}
to obtain that

C_{p,q}=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\theta\in[\pi,\pi]^{2}}\exp(h(u(\theta),v(\theta)))d\theta.

Let
\lambda=\sqrt{p^{2}+q^{2}}
. We perform one last change of variables where we let
h^{\prime}(u,v)=h(u,v)/\lambda
to express the integral as:

C_{p,q}=\frac{\exp(h(u_{0},v_{0}))}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{\theta\in[\pi,\pi]^{2}}\exp\left(\lambda\cdot\left(h^{\prime}(u(\theta),v(\theta))-h^{\prime}(u_{0},v_{0})\right)\right)d\theta.

We will apply
Theorem

A.2
with
A(\theta)=1
,
\phi(\theta)=h^{\prime}(u_{0},v_{0})-h^{\prime}(u(\theta),v(\theta))
.
We claim the following:

Claim A.3
.

\det(\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0))=\left(\frac{u_{0}v_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\det(\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0})).

We will prove this at the end and for now will just assume it.

We first show that we satisfy all of the preconditions laid out by
Theorem

A.2
.
First, we have that
\phi(0,0)=0
and by unique maximality of
u_{0},v_{0}
that for all
\theta\neq(0,0)
, it follows that
\phi(\theta)>0
.
Second, we show that
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0)
is non-singular.
By assumption, we know that
\mathcal{H}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})
is positive definite and so must be true for
\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0})
, making it singular.
Therefore by
A.3
, it follows that
\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0)
is non-singular as well.
Lastly, by assumption we have that
\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Hence, we can indeed apply
Theorem

A.2
with the same
\lambda
to infer that

C_{p,q}=\frac{\exp(h(u_{0},v_{0}))}{4\pi^{2}}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\det({\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0)})}}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\right).

Using
A.3
and the fact that
\exp(h(u_{0},v_{0}))=\frac{f(u_{0},v_{0})}{u_{0}^{q}v_{0}^{q}}
, our desired bound on
C_{p,q}
follows.

We lastly prove our helper claim that relates
\det(\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0))
and
\det(\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0}))
.

Proof of
A.3
.

Let
g(\theta)=-h^{\prime}(u(\theta),v(\theta))
so that
\phi(\theta)=h^{\prime}(u_{0},v_{0})+g(\theta)
.
Since
h^{\prime}(u_{0},v_{0})
is a constant, we have that
\mathcal{H}_{g}(0,0)=\mathcal{H}_{\phi}(0,0)
.
Hence, it suffices to show that
\det(\mathcal{H}_{g}(0,0))=\left(\frac{u_{0}v_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\det(\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0}))
.
Using chain rule, we see that for
i\in[2]
:

\frac{\partial g}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\frac{\partial h^{\prime}}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta_{i}}+\frac{\partial h^{\prime}}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial\theta_{i}}

When we evaluate this at
(0,0)
, we will evaluate
\frac{\partial h^{\prime}}{\partial u}
and
\frac{\partial h^{\prime}}{\partial v}
at
(u_{0},v_{0})
.
By assumption
\nabla_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})=0
and so
\nabla_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0})=0
.
Using above, this implies that
\nabla_{g}(0,0)=0
.

Using chain rule, the fact that
\nabla_{g}(0,0)=0
, and recalling the definition of
u(\theta)
and
v(\theta)
, we obtain that

\mathcal{H}_{g}(0,0)=D\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0})D

where
D
is the
2\times 2
diagonal matrix with entries
iu_{0},iv_{0}
.

With this we finally compute that

\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{H}_{g}(0,0))

\displaystyle=\det(D\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0})D)

\displaystyle=\det(D)\cdot\det(\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0}))\cdot\det(D)

\displaystyle=(u_{0}v_{0})^{2}\det(\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0}))

\displaystyle=\frac{(u_{0}v_{0})^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\det(\mathcal{H}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0}))

where for the last equality we used the fact that
h^{\prime}=h/\lambda
, which implies
\mathcal{H}_{h^{\prime}}(u_{0},v_{0})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathcal{H}_{h}(u_{0},v_{0})
.
∎
