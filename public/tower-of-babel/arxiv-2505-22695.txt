LLM-ODDR: A Large Language Model Framework for Joint Order Dispatching and Driver Repositioning
Tengfei Lyu, Siyuan Feng, Hao Liu, Hai Yang

Abstract
Ride-hailing platforms face significant challenges in optimizing order dispatching and driver repositioning operations in dynamic urban environments. Traditional approaches based on combinatorial optimization, rule-based heuristics, and reinforcement learning often overlook driver income fairness, interpretability, and adaptability to real-world dynamics. To address these gaps, we propose LLM-ODDR, a novel framework leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for joint Order Dispatching and Driver Repositioning (ODDR) in ride-hailing services. LLM-ODDR framework comprises three key components: (1) Multi-objective-guided Order Value Refinement, which evaluates orders by considering multiple objectives to determine their overall value; (2) Fairness-aware Order Dispatching, which balances platform revenue with driver income fairness; and (3) Spatiotemporal Demand-Aware Driver Repositioning, which optimizes idle vehicle placement based on historical patterns and projected supply. We also develop JointDR-GPT, a fine-tuned model optimized for ODDR tasks with domain knowledge. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets from Manhattan taxi operations demonstrate that our framework significantly outperforms traditional methods in terms of effectiveness, adaptability to anomalous conditions, and decision interpretability. To our knowledge, this is the first exploration of LLMs as decision-making agents in ride-hailing ODDR tasks, establishing foundational insights for integrating advanced language models within intelligent transportation systems. While the current framework incurs higher computational costs than traditional methods, we show that parallel decomposition and model distillation can reduce latency to production-viable levels for deployment.

While traditional methods provide foundational insights, their rigidity in dynamic environments has motivated researchers to explore data-driven RL paradigms. These approaches aim to overcome scalability constraints by learning adaptive policies through environmental interactions.

Problem Formulation

In this paper, we consider a ride-hailing platform operating within a specified urban region
\mathcal{G}
where passengers submit ride requests and idle vehicles await dispatch. The system executes an order dispatching process at one-minute intervals to assign available vehicles to incoming passenger requests. Following the order dispatching phase, vehicles remaining idle beyond a predefined maximum idle time threshold are strategically repositioned to nearby areas to increase their likelihood of matching with future requests.

(3)

(4)

x_{ij}^{(t)}\in\{0,1\},\quad\forall(r_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}^{(t)}

(5)

y_{jk}^{(t)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if vehicle $v_{j}$ is repositioned to region $g_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{j}$}\\

(8)

Constraint (
7

Joint ODDR Optimization Objective

The objective of the joint ODDR problem is to maximize a multi-criterion function that balances platform revenue, service quality, and operational efficiency.
The system state at time
t+1
is determined by the previous state and the combined effect of both decision matrices:

S(t+1)=\Gamma(S^{(t)},\mathbf{X}^{(t)},\mathbf{Y}^{(t)})

(9)

Where
\Gamma
represents the state transition function.

This Joint ODDR Optimization captures two critical dependencies.
(1) Current dispatching decisions
\mathbf{X}^{(t)}
directly influence the future distribution of idle vehicles by determining which vehicles become occupied and their subsequent locations after completing trips.
(2) Repositioning decisions
\mathbf{Y}^{(t)}
strategically redistribute idle vehicles to areas with anticipated future demand, improving the likelihood of successful matches in subsequent time periods.

Multi-objective-guided Order Value Refinement

We begin by acquiring the current state of an experimental area
\mathcal{G}
and processing the data to obtain order set
\mathcal{R}
and driver information
\mathcal{V}
. To enable orders to incorporate multi-objective constraints, we developed the Multi-objective-guided Order Value Refinement module, which comprises two key components: an order value scorer and an order value reviewer.
The workflow of this multi-objective-guided refinement process is outlined in Algorithm

IV-B
1
Order Value Scorer

In the order value scorer, we design a tailored prompt to instruct LLMs to evaluate the overall value of each order by considering multiple objectives. For each order
r_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{(t)}
, we extract and provide order attributes
x^{r}_{i}
including origin location
o_{i}
, destination location
d_{i}
, waiting time
t^{w}_{i}
, and immediate reward
w_{i}
. Additionally, we incorporate the destination area’s future advantage value, which represents potential future earnings for drivers.

The future advantage value is quantified by calculating the historical order density in each region over a preceding time window:

f^{(t)}_{n}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t-T}^{t}\mathcal{N}(R^{(t^{\prime})},\mathcal{D})-\sum_{t^{\prime}=t-T}^{t}\mathcal{N}(R^{(t^{\prime})},\mathcal{O})

(10)

where
T
denotes the time window (set to 60 minutes in our model),
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{O}
denotes the destination area and original area, respectively.
R^{(t^{\prime})}
represents the set of orders at time
t^{\prime}
, and
\mathcal{N}(R^{(t^{\prime})},n)
corresponds to the number of orders at time
t^{\prime}
in region
n
.

We formulate a set of multi-objective constraints
C_{s}
to guide the LLM’s reasoning process:

C_{s}=\{o_{1},o_{2},f^{(t)}_{n}\}

(11)

where
o_{1}
represents the constraint that orders with longer waiting time
t^{w}_{i}
and higher immediate rewards
w_{i}
should receive higher overall value, since prolonged waiting indicates higher service urgency and higher risk of abandonment. The constraint
o_{2}
specifies that orders destined for areas with greater future advantage value
f^{(t)}_{n}
should receive elevated overall value to promote long-term system efficiency and improved driver utilization.

The order value scorer then computes an overall value
v_{i}
for each order by employing the LLM to reason about these objectives and constraints:

v_{i}=LLM(P_{scorer},\{x^{r}_{i},f^{(t)}_{n},C_{s}\})

(12)

where
P_{scorer}
is the designed prompt template that structures the inputs and constraints for the LLM to process.

This scoring mechanism creates a holistic evaluation that balances immediate rewards with future potential, thereby addressing the platform’s need to optimize for both short-term gains and long-term efficiency. The resulting order values
v_{i}
are then used in subsequent order dispatching decisions.

Order Value Reviewer

To enhance the accuracy of order valuations, we designed an order value reviewer that systematically evaluates the reasonableness of the order value scorer’s assessments. This module examines each order’s estimated overall value and provides feedback for further refinement if necessary. The review process can be formalized as:

\{s_{i},F_{i}\}=LLM(P_{reviewer},\{x_{i}^{r},v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{R}^{(t)}|})

(13)

r_{i}

F_{i}

After meeting the stopping criteria, the final refined values

Following the evaluation of each order’s overall value through multi-objective-guided refinement, we conduct order dispatching while supporting the platform’s sustainable development. The workflow of this part is outlined in Algorithm

For each order

\mathcal{D}_{r_{i}}\subset\mathcal{V}^{(t)}
based on availability and proximity:

\mathcal{D}_{r_{i}}=\{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}^{(t)}\mid s_{j}=0\land\text{dist}(cl_{j},o_{i})\leq\tau_{dis}^{max}\}

By considering drivers’ historical income distribution, the system gives preference to drivers with lower historical earnings, promoting income equality across the driver pool.

Instead of directly optimizing through traditional algorithms, we leverage the LLM’s reasoning capabilities to determine the assignment:

\mathcal{X}^{(t)}=\{(r_{i},v_{j})\mid x_{ij}^{(t)}=1,(r_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}^{(t)}\}.

(17)

This formulation allows the LLM to balance immediate platform revenue (through order overall values

IV-D

Spatiotemporal Demand-Aware Driver Repositioning

The driver repositioning module represents the culmination of our joint optimization framework, where dispatching decisions directly inform repositioning strategies through predicted vehicle arrivals, while repositioning outcomes feed back to influence future dispatching through area value updates. This bidirectional information flow ensures that both components work in concert rather than in isolation.

Following order dispatching, idle vehicles exceeding a predefined idle time threshold are repositioned to adjacent regions to enhance operational efficiency. The workflow of this idle driver repositioning is outlined in Algorithm
4
.
For each vehicle
v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{\text{idle}}^{(t)}=\{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}^{(t)}\mid s_{j}=0\land\tau_{j}^{\textit{idle}}>\tau^{\max}\}
, we identify candidate repositioning regions
\mathcal{N}_{j}\subset\mathcal{G}
within a two-neighborhood radius of the vehicle’s current region.

To determine optimal repositioning strategies, we integrate three key spatiotemporal factors based on
\mathcal{X}^{(t)}
that characterize the supply-demand dynamics of each candidate region
g_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{j}
:

D_{k}^{hist}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t-15}^{t}|\mathcal{R}_{g_{k}}^{(t^{\prime})}|

(18)

where
D_{k}^{hist}
represents the total number of order requests originating from region
g_{k}
in the past 15 minutes, indicating the historical demand intensity,

M_{k}^{hist}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t-15}^{t}|\mathcal{X}_{g_{k}}^{(t^{\prime})}|

(19)

where
M_{k}^{hist}
denotes the number of successfully matched orders in region
g_{k}
during the past 15 minutes, revealing the historical order fulfillment capacity.

V_{k}^{fut}=\sum_{(r_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{X}^{(t)}}\mathbf{1}[d_{i}\in g_{k}]\cdot f(tt_{i})

(20)

where
V_{k}^{fut}
quantifies the number of vehicles projected to arrive in region
g_{k}
in the next 15 minutes based on the current dispatching decisions
\mathcal{X}^{(t)}
. Here,
\mathbf{1}[d_{i}\in g_{k}]
is an indicator function that equals 1 if the destination of order
r_{i}
belongs to region
g_{k}
, and

is a time-based weighting function that accounts for the expected arrival time.

We design a specialized prompt

y_{jk}^{(t)}=LLM(P_{repo},\{v_{j},\mathcal{N}_{j},\{D_{k}^{hist},M_{k}^{hist},V_{k}^{fut}\}_{g_{k}\in\mathcal{N}_{j}}\})

where

indicates whether vehicle

g_{k}
at time

This enables the integration of both historical patterns and future projections derived from current dispatching decisions, thereby capturing the dynamic spatiotemporal dependencies that characterize urban transportation systems. By balancing these factors, the LLM produces strategically sound repositioning decisions that optimize overall system performance while providing transparent reasoning about the decision process.

IV-E

Hybrid Instruction Fine-Tuning

To develop a more accurate model, we utilize order dispatching and driver repositioning results (generated by GPT-4, cA2C, and TVal) to fine-tune the open-source LLM Llama3.1-70B via the Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) strategy
[
35
]
, resulting in enhanced performance, named JointDR-GPT. We employ mixed-task instruction-tuning
[
60
]
to enhance the LLM’s proficiency in handling both order dispatching and driver repositioning tasks.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}
denote the base language model, where
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{\theta}}(y|x)
represents the response probability distribution given instruction
x
. We consider the order dispatching task
\mathcal{T}_{dis}
and driver repositioning task
\mathcal{T}_{rep}
. Given that both instructions and target outputs employ natural language formatting, we unify training through an end-to-end sequence-to-sequence framework. The optimization objective maximize the language model’s loss:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{T}_{dis}}[\log\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{\theta}}(y|x)]+\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{T}_{rep}}[\log\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}_{\theta}}(y|x)]

(22)

where
x
and
y
represent the instruction input and instruction output of order dispatching task
\mathcal{T}_{dis}
or driver repositioning task
\mathcal{T}_{rep}
, respectively.

To evaluate our proposed JointDR-GPT and baseline methods, we utilize a ride-hailing simulator
[
61
]
that simulates key operational components, including order generation, order dispatching, and idle vehicle repositioning.

At each simulation time step

t

In the final stage, drivers exceeding a predefined idle time threshold are strategically relocated to enhance service efficiency.
Once dispatching and repositioning actions are finalized, drivers actively servicing orders follow shortest-path routes to their destinations, while idle drivers remain stationary or relocate to designated grids. All matched order information is saved in a table and used to calculate the rewards for the current observation. Subsequently, the simulator returns a new observation and initiates a new cycle of order dispatching and driver repositioning.

V-B

Overall Performance

We compare the performance of various models, including rule-based models, RL-based models, and models leveraging different large language models. The overall experiments performance are summarized in Table
I
.

As demonstrated by the experimental results, the proposed JointDR-GPT outperforms all eight baseline models across the three datasets. Specifically, regarding the overall GMV metric, the JointDR-GPT achieves improvements of 3.73%, 12.52%, 23.27%, and 38.47% compared to the GRC, cA2C, TVal, and KM models, respectively, on the small-100 dataset. On the small-200 dataset, these improvements are 9.42%, 11.50%, 15.78%, and 45.44%, respectively, while on the large-500 dataset, they are 4.61%, 9.64%, 14.64%, and 21.51%, respectively.
These findings indicate that our proposed JointDR-GPT exhibits strong performance, effectively integrating multiple objectives into an overall scoring mechanism for each order. Consequently, it achieves more efficient order dispatching and driver repositioning.
Meanwhile, we observed that during the morning, noon, and evening peak hours, our proposed model outperforms traditional approaches. For instance, in the morning peak period on the small-100 dataset, our method improves the GMV metric by 8.60%, 19.55%, 62.7%, and 78.26% compared to the GRC, cA2C, TVal, and KM models, respectively. This demonstrates that when faced with supply-demand imbalances during peak hours, our model can adapt more quickly to the environment and make appropriate adjustments, thereby enhancing the overall operational efficiency of the platform. A detailed analysis in our case study further substantiates this finding.

Compared with other LLMs, the fine-tuned JointDR-GPT demonstrates significant improvements in overall performance. Among general-purpose models, GPT-4o achieves the highest performance, while Llama3.1-8B performs the weakest. These experimental results suggest that generalist LLMs may lack domain-specific expertise in traffic management, underscoring the need to develop specialized models for such applications. Furthermore, although LLMs employing our inference pipeline (e.g., Llama3.1-70B and Qwen2.5-72B) underperform compared to our fine-tuned JointDR-GPT, they still surpass traditional methods (KM, TVal, cA2C). These findings emphasize the benefits of multi-objective integration for holistic order evaluation in order dispatching and driver repositioning, ultimately enhancing long-term platform profitability. However, they also expose critical performance limitations, particularly in balancing regional value allocation, driver income fairness, and spatiotemporal supply-demand equilibrium.
To bridge this gap, we propose three core innovations: Multi-Objective-Guided Order Value Refinement, Fairness-Aware Order Dispatching, and Spatiotemporal Demand-Aware Driver Repositioning. Combined with optimized JointDR-GPT, our integrated approach achieves state-of-the-art performance in joint order dispatching and driver repositioning tasks.

V-F

Limitations and Discussion

While LLM-ODDR demonstrates strong performance, we acknowledge several limitations inherent to LLM-based decision systems. First, regarding output reliability, LLMs may occasionally produce implausible decisions due to hallucination.
Second, concerning consistency, identical inputs may occasionally yield different outputs due to the stochastic nature of LLM inference. While low temperature settings reduce this variability, complete determinism remains challenging.
Third, computational overhead is significant; LLM-based approaches require substantially more processing time than traditional methods, though batch processing considerably improves throughput for practical deployment. Importantly, the gap between LLM inference latency and the strict real-time requirements of production dispatch systems may result in increased rider cancellations and reduced platform efficiency, representing a critical barrier to deployment at scale.
Finally, our evaluation is limited to Manhattan taxi data, and generalization to other geographic regions, transportation modes, or novel scenarios not represented in training warrants further investigation. Despite these limitations, our experimental results suggest that the performance gains and interpretability benefits of LLM-ODDR justify its application in many practical scenarios, while the identified issues point to important directions for future research.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented LLM-ODDR, the first LLM-based framework that jointly optimizes order dispatching and driver repositioning in ride-hailing services. By leveraging the reasoning capabilities of LLMs through our novel Multi-objective-guided Order Value Refinement, Fairness-aware Order Dispatching, and Spatiotemporal Demand-Aware Driver Repositioning components, LLM-ODDR effectively addresses the limitations of traditional approaches in handling multi-objective optimization, abnormal scenarios, and decision interpretability. Our comprehensive experiments on real-world Manhattan taxi datasets demonstrate that LLM-ODDR significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, including reinforcement learning approaches and other LLM-based models. The framework demonstrates exceptional adaptability to surge demand events and promotes equitable income distribution among drivers, while providing interpretable rationales for dispatching and repositioning decisions. Through the release of the pre-trained JointDR-GPT, we aim to facilitate further research in this domain and believe JointDR-GPT represents a significant advancement in leveraging LLMs for complex transportation decision-making, with potential implications for other logistics domains requiring multi-objective optimization, adaptability, and interpretability.

However, it is important to acknowledge that LLM-based approaches incur substantially higher computational costs compared to traditional methods, with inference times of approximately 17 to 30 seconds for large LLMs per decision cycle. Future work should focus on model optimization techniques such as knowledge distillation, quantization, and efficient inference strategies to reduce latency while maintaining the superior performance and interpretability benefits of LLM-based decision-making.

Appendix C
Fine-tuning Procedures and Hyperparameters

This section provides a description of the fine-tuning procedures, training data construction methodology, and hyperparameter configurations employed to develop JointDR-GPT. Our approach leverages Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to efficiently adapt the Llama3.1-70B base model for joint order dispatching and driver repositioning tasks.

C.1 Training Data Construction

The training corpus for JointDR-GPT was constructed from historical ride-hailing transaction records to ensure comprehensive coverage of diverse operational scenarios. Our data construction pipeline comprises four principal stages.

Stage 1: Raw Data Collection.

We collected ride-hailing transaction records spanning a two-week period (July 13–26, 2015) from the Manhattan area of New York City. This temporal window was strategically selected to precede our evaluation period (July 27–31, 2015), thereby preventing data leakage while ensuring temporal proximity for demand pattern consistency.

Stage 2: Scenario Sampling and Filtering.

Training scenarios were sampled using stratified temporal sampling to ensure coverage of all time-of-day patterns (morning peak, midday off-peak, evening peak, overnight) and all five weekday types. For each sampled time step, we retained only scenarios with at least 3 active orders and at least 3 available drivers, as trivial scenarios provide no meaningful learning signal for multi-objective reasoning. This yielded 20,160 training scenarios out of 25,200 candidate time steps (80% retention rate). Filtering statistics are summarized in Table

Stage 3: Label Generation and Quality Control.

We apply a two-stage quality filter to ensure label reliability.

(i) Dispatching label quality.

Only scenarios where the offline combinatorial optimization (Hungarian algorithm) achieves at least 70% Order Response Rate are retained as dispatching supervision, ensuring labels reflect genuinely solvable instances.

(ii) Reasoning trace verification.

GPT-4o reasoning traces are verified for structural completeness (presence of all required XML tags) and logical consistency (the stated driver assignment matches the tag-extracted decision). For GPT-4o-generated traces, any sample where parsing fails or dispatching decisions violate 1-1 matching constraints is discarded and re-queried (up to 3 retries); approximately 4.2% of initial samples were discarded through this process.
Traces that pass parsing but contain internally inconsistent reasoning (e.g., the analysis favors Driver A but the tag assigns Driver B) are identified by a secondary LLM-based consistency check and excluded, affecting approximately 1.8% of samples.

Appendix D
Output Parsing and Tag Extraction

To integrate LLM outputs into the operational ride-hailing system, we employ a robust structured parsing mechanism that extracts actionable decisions from natural language responses. This section details the parsing strategy, validation procedures, and failure handling mechanisms.

D.1 Structured Output Format Design

Each LLM module produces outputs containing both natural language reasoning and structured decision tags. We adopt an XML-style tagging convention that enables reliable extraction while preserving the interpretability benefits of natural language explanations. The general output structure follows this pattern:

[Step-by-step analysis and reasoning...]
Final Decisions:
<dispatch>ORDER_ID:DRIVER_ID</dispatch>
...

This hybrid design offers several advantages over purely structured (JSON) or purely unstructured outputs.

Separation of Concerns
: The reasoning component provides transparency and auditability, while decision tags enable unambiguous action extraction.

Robust Parsing
: XML-style tags with explicit delimiters are less susceptible to parsing errors than JSON, which can fail due to unescaped characters or formatting inconsistencies common in LLM outputs.

Partial Result Recovery
: Unlike JSON parsing which fails entirely on malformed input, our tag-based approach can extract valid decisions even when some outputs are malformed.

Graceful Degradation
: When parsing fails completely, the system falls back to baseline methods (KM algorithm) to ensure operational continuity.

Third, among the high-performing methods, Llama3.1-70B and GPT-4o-mini achieve the lowest IDR (both 0.262), reflecting the tightest income range. JointDR-GPT has a moderately higher IDR (0.297), which reflects its dispatching strategy: by proactively routing drivers to high-value areas through spatiotemporal repositioning, JointDR-GPT raises the income ceiling (max

This means that even the lowest-earning driver under JointDR-GPT earns more than the lowest-earning driver under any other method. From the max-min fairness perspective, JointDR-GPT therefore achieves a strict Pareto improvement: no driver is worse off in the worst case, and the overall system generates significantly more revenue.

Fourth, it is noteworthy that GPT-4o, despite achieving the second-highest GMV (69,853), has a relatively higher IDR (0.382) and a lower FS (557.0) compared to Llama3.1-70B and GPT-4o-mini. This suggests that stronger general-purpose reasoning does not automatically translate to better fairness outcomes; rather, the balance between efficiency-seeking and fairness-encoding depends on the specific model’s response to prompt instructions. JointDR-GPT, through fine-tuning on ODDR-specific data that explicitly includes fairness-aware decisions, achieves the most favorable combination of both objectives.

As shown in the dispatching prompt (Appendix A.3), driver attributes including historical income (current overall reward), number of finished orders, and idle time are presented alongside order information, with explicit instructions to consider “ensuring fairness among drivers” as a priority factor. This prompt-based approach enables flexible integration of fairness criteria without requiring algorithmic redesign. Among all frameworks evaluated, JointDR-GPT achieves the best results on efficiency (GMV), fairness floor (FS), and income level (median and mean), underscoring its outstanding performance in joint order dispatching and driver repositioning tasks.
