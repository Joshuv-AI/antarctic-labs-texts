Global patterns of pertussis incidence and vaccination strategies: implications of divergent surveillance data
Authors: Kangguo Li, Yulun Xie, Jiadong Wu, Yunzhi Zenghuang, Tao Chen, Juan Li, Sha Chen, Ziyan Liu, Honglian Liu, Jia Rui, Zeyu Zhao, Mingzhai Wang, Kaiwei Luo, Yanhua Su, Tianmu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pertussis remains a global threat for infants, and recent increases in notifications have renewed interest in optimising vaccination strategies and improving vaccines. At the biological level, the rationale for maternal pertussis immunization extends beyond passive antibody transfer alone and may also involve broader maternal–infant immune interactions; however, at the population level these mechanisms are operationalized through policy adoption, timing recommendations, and coverage. We compared incidence constructs from WHO routine notifications and Global Burden of Disease (GBD) modelled estimates and assessed how associations with vaccination policy indicators change across outcome definitions. Trends were characterised with Joinpoint regression; policy associations were estimated using Bayesian hierarchical models fitted separately to each dataset with Universal Health Coverage stratified random intercepts. Incidence levels and trends differed markedly between WHO and GBD. Unadjusted analyses showed heterogeneous, sometimes opposing, associations for maternal immunization and schedule timing. After adjustment, most schedule parameters were small and imprecise, whereas DTP3 coverage remained strongly inversely associated with incidence in GBD but not in WHO. Surveillance and modelled estimates should not be interpreted interchangeably; harmonized constructs and routine implementation and ascertainment metadata are needed for robust cross-country inference.

Introduction

Pertussis, caused by Bordetella pertussis, remains a persistent global health challenge despite decades of routine childhood immunization1. Infants, particularly those under two months of age who are too young to complete the first vaccine dose, face the highest risk of severe complications, including pneumonia, hospitalisation, and death2. This vulnerability reflects not only the absence of completed active immunization, but also the distinctive immunologic context of early infancy, in which protection remains incomplete and infant responses can be modified by maternally derived antibodies, potentially blunting responses to primary pertussis vaccination1,3,4. In high-transmission settings and during outbreaks, incidence among this age group has exceeded 1000 per 100,0005.

Despite high national vaccine coverage in many countries, pertussis has recently shown signs of a resurgence. Several surveillance systems have reported a sharp increase in cases, beginning in late 2023 and continuing into 2024. For example, the USA noted that the 2024 cases were more than six times the number reported in 20236. In Europe, similar patterns have emerged, with many member countries reporting incidence by early 2024 that is multiple times higher than during 2016–2019. In 2024, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) issued an alert regarding the increase in global pertussis activity, urging countries to review their vaccination strategies7.

Recent increases in reported pertussis incidence may reflect both epidemiologic changes (e.g., waning immunity after the shift from whole-cell to acellular vaccines, pandemic-related disruptions and delayed boosters, increased susceptibility, and potential pathogen changes) and changes in ascertainment driven by strengthened surveillance and expanded access to sensitive molecular diagnostics8–11. In response, many countries have revised their national immunization schedules for 2024–25. These include increasing the number of primary doses, adjusting the timing of both initial and final childhood doses, and recommending maternal vaccination during pregnancy to protect infants during their most vulnerable period12–14. The protective rationale for maternal immunization is most often framed in terms of trans-placentally acquired antibodies but may also reflect broader maternal–infant immune interactions that shape susceptibility in early life3,4; in the present study, however, we examine these issues at the level of national policy indicators rather than individual immunologic endpoints.

Understanding the effectiveness of these strategies requires accurate and reliable data. Currently, global assessments of the pertussis burden rely primarily on two sources: the World Health Organization (WHO) surveillance data, which are based on country-reported notifications, and the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation’s Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, which produces modelled estimates that adjust for underreporting and data gaps15,16. Although both sources are widely used in global health research and policy, they differ significantly in methodology, input data, and assumptions, resulting in notable disparities in reported incidence and trends. These inconsistencies have also been documented for other infectious diseases. For example, comparative analyses of neglected tropical diseases in China have found that GBD estimates often exceed national surveillance figures by several orders of magnitude17. While such discrepancies are increasingly recognised, little is known about their implications for evaluating the effectiveness of vaccination strategies. To date, no study has systematically compared WHO surveillance and GBD estimates in relation to national vaccine policy variables for pertussis. More importantly, the implications of construct mismatch (crude vs age-standardized), policy–outcome time misalignment, and ascertainment heterogeneity for cross-country policy inference remain under-characterized.

This study aimed to address this gap. We compared global and regional pertussis incidence trends from 2000 to 2024 and assessed how vaccination strategy components (number of primary doses, timing of first and last scheduled doses, and maternal vaccination recommendations) were associated with incidence within each dataset. Our findings suggest that not only do incidence levels and trends diverge between the two sources, but also that conclusions regarding the effectiveness of vaccination strategies may depend on the data source selected for analysis. These results highlight the importance of data source transparency, harmonisation of surveillance and modelling frameworks, and improved integration of implementation metrics in global vaccine policy evaluation.

Results

Global and regional pertussis incidence

Regional comparisons reveal marked heterogeneity. In the African Region (Fig. 1B), GBD estimates were the highest among all regions in 2000 (peaking near 675.66 per 100,000), while WHO data remained below 113.2 per 100,000 throughout. In the Eastern Mediterranean, incidence remained sustained, except for a notable spike in WHO-reported incidence in 2004 (154.70 per 100,000), which was significantly higher than in other years (typically <30.00 per 100,000) (Fig. 1C). The Americas region (Fig. 1E) exhibited a U-shaped GBD trend, with incidence declining from 233.36 to 172.00 before increasing again after 2010, reaching a peak of 269.48 in the 2019. In contrast, WHO data for this region displayed more fluctuation, including a spike around 2013 and again in 2024. In the European Region, WHO incidence increased gradually over the period, reaching 74.50 per 100,000 in 2019, while GBD estimates showed a similar trend to the Region of the Americas (Fig. 1D, E). The Southeast Asia Region showed the most significant difference in AAPC direction between data sources (Fig. 1F). WHO data indicated slight increases prior to 2010, whereas GBD estimates showed consistent and significant declines. The Western Pacific Region had low WHO-reported incidence compared to other regions, with values rising to 33.20 per 100,000 in 2019, while GBD estimates displayed greater fluctuation (Fig. 1G).

WHO-based AAPC values were positive in multiple regions and time periods, including the Eastern Mediterranean (+68.22% during 2000–2004) and the Americas (+17.45% during 2000–2004, +19.01% during 2010–2014), while GBD-based AAPCs were negative or near-zero. Significant downward trends were recorded in the African Region (–11.89% during 2000–2019) and Southeast Asia (–27.61% during 2015–2019) in WHO data, and in the European Region (–8.21% during 2005–2009) and Western Pacific Region (–5.65% during 2000–2004) in GBD data. Several regions showed contrasting AAPC directions between datasets for the same period (Fig. 1H, I).

To assess the effect of age standardization, we compared the temporal trends using both GBD age-standardized and original incidence data. The AAPC estimates for both datasets were remarkably similar, with minimal divergence in the global trend direction (Supplementary Fig 1). This suggests that age standardization did not significantly alter the overall trends in pertussis incidence across regions. The AAPC for both datasets showed consistent declines in incidence from 2000 to 2019, with a sharp drop in 2021, reinforcing the robustness of the observed trends.

Vaccination strategy landscape

Among 214 countries, most reported administering four (n = 61) or five doses (n = 61) in the routine childhood schedule, while 57 countries used three doses and 35 used six doses (Fig. 2A). Three-dose schedules were more common in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, while higher-dose schedules predominated in Latin America, South and East Asia, and parts of Europe. Regarding first-dose timing (Fig. 2B), 125 countries scheduled the first dose between 1.5 and 2.5 months of age. Seventy-two countries administered the first dose earlier (between 1.0 and 1.5 months), while only 17 countries initiated vaccination after 2.5 months. Early initiation was more common in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, whereas later starts were observed in several high-income countries. Additionally, the timing of the final dose also showed substantial variation (Fig. 2C). Fifty-five countries scheduled the last routine dose between 3 and 6 months of age, 44 between 1 and 2 years, 46 between 6 and 12 years, and 32 extended the final dose to ages 13–18. As of 2024, 61 countries officially recommended maternal vaccination (Fig. 3A). These countries spanned most of the Americas, Western Europe, Oceania, and selected parts of Asia and Africa. In contrast, large parts of Africa, Eastern Europe, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia had no recommendations. Among countries with recommendations, gestational timing varied (Fig. 3B, C). To capture implementation beyond policy presence, we also summarize the most recent publicly available maternal uptake (coverage) estimates by country. Maternal uptake data were available for 65 countries overall; the most recent reported uptake spanned the full range from 0 to 100% (Fig. 3C). Among countries with an official recommendation, uptake was heterogeneous, with several settings reporting remarkably high uptake (e.g., Costa Rica 95.42%, Peru 93.21%, Oman 92.98%, Spain 91.83%, Bahamas 92.34%) alongside low uptake in others (e.g., Thailand 0%, Saudi Arabia 3.70%, Slovenia 6.50%) (Supplementary Table 1).

Multiple vaccination strategy dimensions were significantly associated with variations in pertussis incidence, although patterns differed between data sources and time points. Notably, the 2021 WHO data showed trends consistent with the GBD 2019 and 2021 estimates, particularly in relation to maternal immunization and the timing of the final dose. However, the 2019 and 2024 WHO data often showed inverse associations.

Routine dose number was associated with pertussis incidence in several datasets. In the WHO 2019 and 2024 reports (Fig. 4A, I), incidence was significantly lower in countries administering three doses compared with those using six-dose schedules (P < 0.001). Age at first dose was associated with incidence in several datasets. WHO 2019 and 2024 data (Fig. 4B, J) indicated a higher incidence in countries initiating vaccination at 2.5–3.0 months compared to 1.0–1.5 months (P = 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively), suggesting a potential benefit from earlier initiation. However, the WHO 2021 and GBD datasets (Fig. 4F, N, R) showed inconsistent patterns, with the highest incidence in the earliest-start group (1.0–1.5 months) and a decline across later-start groups, indicating no uniform trend.

The timing of the final scheduled dose yielded contrasting patterns between data sources. WHO 2019 and 2024 data (Fig. 4C, K) showed a significantly higher incidence in countries where vaccination was completed at 13–18 years compared to those completed by 3–6 months or 1–2 years of age. Conversely, GBD 2019 and 2021 data (Fig. 4O, S) showed the lowest incidence among countries with last-dose timing of 1–2 years, which was significantly lower than those with last-dose timing of 3–6 months (P < 0.01). WHO 2021 data (Fig. 4G) showed no significant differences across groups but followed a similar trend to GBD.

Maternal immunization policy was the most consistently associated factor across datasets. GBD 2019 and 2021 data (Fig. 4P, T) showed a significantly lower incidence in countries with maternal vaccination recommendations (P < 0.01). WHO 2021 data (Fig. 4H) showed no significant difference, though the trend was directionally consistent. In contrast, the WHO 2019 and 2024 data (Fig. 4D, L) reported a higher incidence in countries with maternal immunization policies (P < 0.001 and P = 0.01), representing a stark divergence from the GBD estimates.

In 2021, both antigens shifted towards lower coverage. Median DTP1 decreased to 94% (IQR 86–98%) and median DTP3 to 91% (IQR 78–96%) (Fig. 5B, E). Cross-country dispersion increased, particularly for DTP3 (variance 0.0198 in 2021 vs 0.0148 in 2019), consistent with the visibly broadened density curve in the inset plots. The number of countries with DTP3 ≥ 90% decreased from 124/194 (2019) to 104/194 (2021), while countries with DTP3 < 80% increased from 35/194 to 50/194. Minimum values in 2021 reached 41% (DTP1) and 32% (DTP3).

By 2024, DTP1 partially rebounded (median 95%, IQR 87–98%), whereas DTP3 remained at a median of 91% (IQR 80–96%). Dispersion in 2024 remained higher than in 2019 (DTP3 variance 0.0170), and 47/194 countries still had DTP3 < 80% (Fig. 5C, F). Countries achieving DTP3 ≥ 90% increased to 110/194 but did not return to the 2019 level. Across all years, high-coverage regions remained extensive, but areas of lower DTP3 coverage persisted across 2021–2024, consistent with the lower-tail expansion observed in the density distributions (Fig. 5).

Dose-number effects did not exhibit a consistent gradient across data sources (Fig. 7A). WHO-based models were broadly compatible with null associations for higher-dose schedules (e.g., WHO 2024: 5 doses RR = 1.26, 95% CrI 0.50–3.23; 6 doses RR = 1.15, 95% CrI 0.45–2.95). GBD estimates suggested at most modest protection in some contrasts without a monotonic pattern (e.g., GBD 2021: 6 doses RR = 0.65, 95% CrI 0.32–1.31). Timing parameters were similarly uncertain (Fig. 7B, C). For first-dose timing, associations were near null and imprecise (WHO 2024: 2.5–3.0 months RR = 1.40, 95% CrI 0.55–3.70; GBD 2021: 2.5–3.0 months RR = 0.78, 95% CrI 0.38–1.60). For the last scheduled dose, WHO point estimates tended to be higher for later completion but remained uncertain (WHO 2024: 13–18 years RR = 1.74, 95% CrI 0.69–4.45), whereas the strongest GBD signal suggested lower incidence when completion occurred at 3–5 years in 2021 (GBD 2021: RR = 0.53, 95% CrI 0.28–1.00), without a consistent gradient across timing categories. Maternal vaccination recommendation showed consistently positive but uncertain associations in WHO datasets (WHO 2019: RR = 2.00, 95% CrI 0.82–4.93; WHO 2024: RR = 1.81, 95% CrI 0.71–4.46), while remaining closer to null in GBD (GBD 2019: RR = 0.94, 95% CrI 0.74–1.21; GBD 2021: RR = 1.21, 95% CrI 0.71–2.04) (Fig. 7D).

Including DTP3 coverage (scaled per 10-percentage-point increase) produced the clearest divergence by source (Fig. 7E). In GBD models, higher DTP3 coverage was strongly and consistently associated with lower incidence (GBD 2019: RR = 0.63, 95% CrI 0.57–0.69; GBD 2021: RR = 0.63, 95% CrI 0.50–0.77), whereas WHO models remained compatible with no association (WHO 2021: RR = 1.13, 95% CrI 0.59–2.11; WHO 2024: RR = 1.13, 95% CrI 0.58–2.17). Sensitivity analyses using GBD non–age-standardized incidence yielded similar directionality for key policy terms (e.g., maternal recommendation), suggesting that age-standardization did not materially alter the main policy interpretations (Supplementary Fig 3).

Discussion

This study shows that inferences about pertussis vaccination strategies can change materially as a function of how incidence is defined. Comparing WHO routine surveillance notifications with GBD modelled estimates yielded clear differences in incidence levels and temporal patterns, as well as in the direction and strength of associations with policy indicators. This creates a core constraint for cross-country policy evaluation: when outcomes are generated by non-equivalent measurement systems, ecological policy-incidence relationships can weaken, change magnitude, or reverse. WHO incidence is typically based on country-reported notifications that depend on testing availability, case definitions, and reporting completeness. By contrast, GBD estimates are produced by statistical models that combine multiple inputs, adjust for under-ascertainment, and are commonly reported as age-standardized rates16. Because these constructs are not interchangeable, associations observed under one framework should not be interpreted as direct confirmations, or refutations, of associations observed under the other.

In unadjusted analyses, schedule policy indicators showed heterogeneous and sometimes opposing associations with incidence, and these patterns varied by data source and year1,18,19. This should not be interpreted as evidence that vaccination schedules are ineffective. A more plausible explanation is that policy descriptors alone do not capture the dominant drivers of cross-country differences in measured incidence, particularly when surveillance sensitivity varies widely20,21. Consistent with this interpretation, adjusted Bayesian hierarchical models that incorporated UHC grouping to partially reflect health-system heterogeneity yielded imprecise schedule-policy effects that were often compatible with no strong independent association. These results indicate limited ability to disentangle policy effects from reporting and broader system-level factors using cross-country ecological contrasts alone.

The maternal vaccination indicator illustrates these interpretability challenges. In some unadjusted contrasts using GBD estimates, the presence of maternal vaccination policy appeared protective, whereas WHO notifications sometimes suggested a positive association. A direct causal harm of maternal immunization is biologically implausible. This pattern is more consistent with ascertainment differences and with policy adoption being correlated with surveillance capacity20,22. In our dataset, only 61 countries recommended maternal pertussis vaccination in 2024, and large-scale monitoring systems have historically been concentrated in a small number of settings, while many countries rely on limited subnational studies or incomplete routine data. Under these conditions, countries introducing maternal immunization policies may simultaneously expand diagnostic infrastructure and reporting, increasing notifications even if underlying transmission is unchanged23. Conceptually, maternal immunization is best understood as a bridging strategy: it provides early-life protection during the period before infants can mount and complete their own vaccine-induced immunity1,3. This protection likely depends mainly on passively acquired antibodies in early life, whereas durable protection ultimately relies on infants’ own immune priming, booster completion, and the overall performance of routine immunization programmes24,25. The bridge may also be weaker in preterm infants because reduced transplacental antibody transfer can lower and shorten passive protection5. Although breast milk-derived mucosal factors may partly compensate, they are unlikely to match transplacental IgG and may vary with breastfeeding and neonatal care25.

Reverse causation and timing misalignment are also plausible explanations. Maternal immunization policies may be adopted in response to higher baseline burden, producing policy-burden correlations rather than true policy effects. In several settings, determinants of maternal immunization adoption included infant mortality and public acceptability26,27. This concern is amplified when policy exposure is coded as of 2024, but outcomes are drawn from 2019 to 2021, because such misalignment can introduce exposure misclassification and bias associations. Because we do not directly measure surveillance intensity or diagnostic testing volume, these mechanisms should be treated as plausible explanations rather than confirmed causes9. More broadly, measured incidence reflects not only vaccine biology but also implementation fidelity, including whether written policy translates into timely and equitable uptake, together with health-system capacity and data collection practice28. While the UHC index may partially proxy system performance, it does not capture key drivers of notification artefacts such as testing volume, case-definition changes, reporting completeness, or outbreak investigation intensity, leaving scope for residual confounding29,30. In addition, policy presence does not measure maternal vaccine uptake. Low uptake or recent implementation could attenuate true infant benefits while surveillance improvements raise reported cases, producing paradoxical signals, especially in low- and middle-income countries31,32.

Our findings should also be interpreted in the context of renewed pertussis resurgence. Between 2023 and 2024, multiple countries reported sharp increases, including large rises in China, the United States and other counties2,6,25. These observations are best viewed as notification surges that may reflect both changes in transmission and shifts in ascertainment, reporting timeliness, and diagnostic practice. Several programmatic and epidemiological factors may contribute, including reduced natural boosting during COVID-19 restrictions and the accumulation of immunologically naive cohorts8,9. Immunity after acellular vaccination is known to wane over approximately 4 to 6 years, which can shift burden toward adolescents and adults who may have milder illness but can transmit to infants10,11,33. In addition, country-specific transitions from whole-cell to acellular vaccines and pathogen evolution, including antigenic shifts and reports of macrolide-resistant strains, may shape longer-run trends10,11. Because these forces are tied to vaccine product type and transition timing, schedule descriptors such as total dose count may conflate immunological and programmatic pathways if product type and transition timing are not explicitly modelled. Beyond passive protection alone, another unresolved issue is whether maternal immunization may shape the infant’s own developing cellular immune responses. Although our study does not include infant immunophenotyping, maternally derived antibodies and other transferred immune signals could plausibly influence the context in which neonatal CD4+ T-cell responses are primed after infection or routine vaccination1,10,34. Whether these influences merely modulate early responsiveness or meaningfully alter longer-term cellular immunity remains uncertain and cannot be resolved using national surveillance indicators alone.

A key methodological implication is that surveillance-based and modelled estimates answer different questions. WHO incidence relies on national notification systems with large cross-country variation in diagnostic infrastructure, age disaggregation, and reporting accuracy31,35. GBD models attempt to reconstruct likely burden by correcting for missingness and underreporting, but these corrections rely on assumptions that may not hold uniformly across settings16. Age standardisation can further alter cross-country rankings and trend magnitudes compared with all-age incidence, complicating direct policy comparisons36,37. Our construct-harmonisation sensitivity analysis using GBD all-age incidence was intended to separate the impact of age standardisation from other framework differences. The persistence of divergence after this alignment suggests that factors beyond demography, including ascertainment heterogeneity and framework-specific modelling and adjustment assumptions, may contribute to observed discrepancies. In settings with recent or incomplete implementation, a binary policy-present indicator may also be poorly aligned with effective coverage, further limiting interpretability.

The divergence in coverage-incidence relationships reinforces this point. Across countries, GBD estimates showed a consistent inverse relationship between DTP3 coverage and incidence, whereas WHO routine data, particularly in 2024, showed weak, null, or sometimes positive associations at high coverage. This pattern is unlikely to indicate vaccine failure. It is compatible with differential ascertainment, because high-coverage countries also tend to have stronger diagnostic capacity and more complete reporting38,39. To reduce the risk of misleading cross-country inference, routine incidence reporting should be accompanied by a minimal set of surveillance and implementation metadata: diagnostic modality and testing intensity (for example PCR availability, tests performed, positivity), case definition and reporting system changes over time, completeness and timeliness metrics (including reporting delays), and implementation indicators such as maternal Tdap uptake, infant dose timeliness, and equity gaps23,40–42. These additions would also support established delay-adjustment and under-ascertainment correction approaches, including reporting-delay nowcasting and calibration studies, where data permit.

These surveillance limitations are also relevant when interpreting concerns about potential blunting after maternal immunization3. Even where available evidence suggests that any attenuation of infant vaccine responses is modest at the clinical level, routine surveillance systems are typically optimized to capture laboratory-confirmed cases, severe disease, outbreaks, or hospitalization rather than milder or partially modified clinical presentations24,43. As a result, surveillance-based analyses may be relatively insensitive to subtle shifts in disease phenotype or severity distribution32. In other words, the absence of an observed increase in severe pertussis should not automatically be interpreted as proof that no milder clinical impact exists; rather, it indicates that any such impact is likely to be small relative to the overall protective benefit of maternal vaccination and difficult to detect in routine cross-country datasets39.

This study has several limitations. First, its ecological design precludes causal inference and is vulnerable to the ecological fallacy: we analyzed national-level policies rather than real-world implementation, and we could not capture subnational heterogeneity in coverage, timeliness, or disease burden. Nor could we evaluate individual-level immunologic mechanisms potentially relevant to maternal immunization, such as the quality of passive transfer, early mucosal protection, immune blunting, or infant cellular priming3,5,24,25; these issues can therefore only be discussed here as biologically plausible interpretive frameworks rather than directly tested mechanisms. In particular, “policy presence” does not equate to programme performance, and we lacked direct indicators of implementation fidelity (e.g., maternal Tdap uptake among pregnant people, timeliness of infant doses, booster delivery in adolescents/adults, and equity gaps across subpopulations)34,44,45. Second, missing data for some countries and years may introduce selection bias. We chose not to impute missing values to avoid spurious precision, though this may reduce generalizability. Third, WHO notifications and GBD estimates differ in provenance and construction (reported crude incidence versus modelled age-standardized incidence), limiting direct quantitative reconciliation; consequently, we cannot infer the “true” underlying incidence within this design17. Relatedly, we did not have harmonized indicators of ascertainment (e.g., diagnostic testing intensity, PCR availability, case-definition changes, reporting completeness, or outbreak investigation practices), which likely contribute to paradoxical associations in notification-based analyses46,47. Fourth, residual confounding is likely, including differences in vaccine product composition, booster recommendations beyond the childhood schedule, prior infection history, demographic structure, mobility, and pandemic-era disruptions affecting both immunization delivery and surveillance1. Although UHC grouping partially addresses health-system heterogeneity, it cannot capture surveillance sensitivity or diagnostic practices in detail. Finally, policy–outcome timing alignment is imperfect: policies may be adopted in a given year, whereas effects may occur with lag and vary by age group23; we did not explicitly model lagged effects or age-specific outcomes, which could dilute or obscure true programme effects.

This study shows that cross-country conclusions about pertussis vaccination strategies can change, and may even reverse, depending on whether WHO notifications or GBD modelled estimates are used. These discrepancies reflect differences in ascertainment, health-system capacity, and implementation fidelity, and should be explicitly incorporated into policy evaluation. As pertussis re-emerges globally, evaluation should move beyond static schedule descriptors to integrated frameworks that report comparable incidence metrics with clear distinction between crude and age-standardized measures, alongside surveillance sensitivity indicators and implementation measures including maternal uptake. Such harmonized reporting would enable more robust interpretation of policy–incidence relationships and better-informed programme adjustments.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized a merged dataset that combined national-level pertussis incidence data with vaccination strategy characteristics. Pertussis incidence data were obtained from two primary global sources: the WHO and GBD Study 2021. WHO data were downloaded from the Immunization Data Portal, which includes both case counts and estimated country population sizes by year15. Population estimates (defined as the total mid-year population) were obtained from the United Nations Population Division48. WHO incidence data were available for most countries from 2000 onwards, although data completeness varied, particularly in low-income settings. In contrast, GBD incidence data were sourced from IHME’s GBD 2021 dataset, which provides modelled estimates of age-standardized and original incidence16. Because WHO and GBD incidence metrics differ, analyses were conducted separately by source and year without pooling.

National childhood pertussis-containing vaccination schedule information (dose count and timing) and policy indicators were obtained from country-reported submissions through the WHO/UNICEF Joint Reporting Form on Immunization (JRF) and related WHO schedule documentation15. From these schedule entries, we derived (i) total scheduled dose count (primary series plus routine childhood boosters), (ii) timing of the first scheduled dose, and (iii) timing of the last scheduled childhood dose, harmonizing all ages to months. Where ages were reported as ranges, we used the earliest value for first-dose timing and the latest value for last-dose timing; where multiple valid routine schedules were reported within a country-year, we selected the schedule with the highest dose count (ties resolved by later last-dose timing, then earlier first-dose timing). All derived schedule indicators were manually verified against the original JRF schedule text by two reviewers with third-reviewer adjudication. Maternal immunization policy data were compiled through multiple independent searches of national health websites and documents, and cross-referenced with published reviews and reports3,24,49. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer who verified the data against public health websites and official reports.

To capture implementation beyond policy presence, we extracted annual country-reported infant DTP vaccination coverage indicators (DTP1 and DTP3) from the WHO Immunization Data Portal. These data represent administrative and official DTP coverage reported annually through the WHO/UNICEF JRF, and the WHO/UNICEF Estimates of National Immunization Coverage are also displayed and updated annually50. For each reference year used in cross-sectional analyses, we linked DTP1 and DTP3 coverage for the corresponding country-year. Additionally, maternal vaccination uptake (coverage) was extracted from published literature and official national reports and websites for countries where these data were publicly available23,51. Detailed country-level maternal pertussis vaccination policy, recommended timing windows, and the most recent maternal uptake estimates are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

In addition, we incorporated the WHO Universal Health Coverage (UHC) service coverage index as a proxy for health-system capacity. Annual UHC index values were obtained from the WHO Global Health Observatory indicator dataset. For cross-sectional analyses, we used year-matched UHC values when available; because 2024 values were not available for all countries at the time of extraction, 2021 UHC index values were carried forward to 2024 (as in our pre-specified analysis plan) to enable consistent adjustment. The UHC index was categorized into five strata following WHO guidance: Very low (<20), Low (20–39), Medium (40–59), High (60–79), and Very high (≥80). Countries with missing UHC data were retained and coded as “Unknown”30.

Trend analysis

To assess long-term incidence trends, we calculated the Average Annual Percentage Change (AAPC) in pertussis incidence using joinpoint segmented regression. We implemented these models using the National Cancer Institute’s Joinpoint Regression Software Command Line Interface (version 5.2.0, Bethesda, MD, USA), which we accessed via the nih.joinpoint R package. This method identifies statistically significant changes in trends over time and fits a series of joined log-linear segments. We fitted separate models for WHO-reported crude incidence and GBD-derived age-standardized incidence, using the natural logarithm of incidence as the dependent variable in both models. The maximum number of joinpoints allowed was five, with model selection based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the parametric method. For WHO and GBD data, the AAPC was computed for four pre-specified periods: 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. Following trend analysis, we evaluated cross-sectional associations between vaccination strategy parameters and pertussis incidence in five reference source–year combinations: WHO-derived incidence in 2019, 2021, and 2024, and GBD-estimated age-standardized incidence in 2019 and 2021. The primary outcome for these analyses was pertussis incidence per 100,000 population in each reference year. The main explanatory variables included: (i) the total number of scheduled childhood pertussis-containing doses, (ii) the timing of the first scheduled childhood dose, (iii) the timing of the last scheduled childhood dose, and (iv) the presence or absence of a national maternal pertussis vaccination recommendation.

Exploratory analysis

We first conducted an exploratory, non-parametric comparison of incidence across strategy groups using resampling-based methods. For each combination of reference year and strategy variable, we applied bootstrap resampling with 1000 replicates to estimate group-specific mean incidence and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Incidence values were analysed on the original scale (per 100,000 population) in these exploratory analyses. To assess the statistical significance of pairwise differences between strategy groups, we performed permutation tests with 5000 replicates per comparison, computing the difference in mean incidence between groups under random reassignment of group labels. Resulting p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. These resampling-based procedures are robust to small group sizes and do not rely on normality assumptions.

In a separate exploratory analysis, we examined the relationship between DTP1 and DTP3 vaccine coverage and pertussis incidence. For each year and data source (WHO and GBD), we first described the continuous association using scatter plots with non-parametric smoothers and Spearman correlation coefficients. We then categorised DTP1 and DTP3 coverage into four groups (<60%, 60–79%, 80–89%, ≥90%) and repeated the same resampling-based procedures described above to compare mean incidence across coverage categories, providing bootstrap-based 95% CIs and permutation-based p values with Holm adjustment for multiple testing. These resampling-based procedures are robust to small group sizes and do not rely on normality assumptions.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis to assess the effect of age standardization on the reported incidence trends. For this, we used GBD original incidence data alongside the age-standardized estimates. The crude incidence data were derived directly from the GBD 2021 dataset without age structure adjustments. The sensitivity analysis focused on comparing the temporal trends (AAPC) and the associations between vaccination strategies and incidence. The results from the crude incidence analysis were compared with those from the age-standardized estimates to evaluate whether the standardization process significantly affected the observed trends and policy-related associations.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2), the Joinpoint Regression Software Command Line Interface (version 5.2.0) via the nih.joinpoint R package, and Stan (version 2.32.7). Differences were considered statistically significant at a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 for frequentist tests; for Bayesian models, we primarily interpreted 95% CrIs and posterior probabilities rather than relying on arbitrary probability thresholds.
