Association between multiple discrimination experiences and disordered eating behavior among Brazilian university students
Authors: Gabriela Pacheco Carniel, Mariane da Silva Dias, Angela Lautert Monteiro, Francine Silva dos Santos, Raquel Canuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmerging evidence suggests that discrimination may shape eating-related behaviors through psychosocial pathways. This study aimed to analyze the association between multiple forms of discrimination and disordered eating behaviors among undergraduate students in southern Brazil.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 1,502 undergraduate students from a public university in Porto Alegre, Brazil. Eating behaviors were assessed using the Adult Eating Behavior Questionnaire, generating food approach and food avoidance scores. Perceived discrimination based on weight, race, gender, and sexual orientation was self-reported and operationalized as both individual exposures and 16 intersectional discrimination strata. Associations were assessed using linear regression models adjusted for age.ResultsThe highest food approach scores were observed among those reporting discrimination based on weight and sexual orientation (β = 9.1; 95% CI: 5.3, 12.8), weight and gender (β = 9.1; 95% CI: 7.0, 11.2), and weight and race (β = 9.0; 95% CI: 5.5, 12.4). For food avoidance, positive associations emerged for discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender (β = 5.2; 95% CI: 3.0, 7.4), while negative associations were found for weight and gender (β = −3.1; 95% CI: −5.0, − 1.2), weight and sexual orientation (β = −3.6; 95% CI: −6.9, − 0.2), and weight alone (β.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that perceived discrimination is associated with disordered eating behaviors in distinct and patterned ways, with weight-based discrimination playing a central role. An intersectional approach is essential to capture how multiple, overlapping forms of discrimination intensify vulnerability to disordered eating behaviors.

Background

Eating behavior is a broad term that encompasses food choice and motives, feeding practices, dieting, and eating-related problems such as obesity, eating disorders, and feeding disorders [1] Disordered eating behaviors are variations in regular eating patterns and behaviors and might include symptoms and behaviors of an eating disorder with a lower level of severity [2], although they do not necessarily meet the diagnostic criteria for a clinical eating disorder [3]. Examples of disordered eating behaviors include binge eating, restraint, emotional eating, disinhibition, strict dieting, and controlling body weight and shape through inappropriate compensatory behaviors [4]. In this article, we draw on the concepts of food approach and food avoidance to investigate potential disordered eating behaviors. Food approach refers to thoughts and behaviors involving movement toward or a desire for food, such as overeating, emotional eating, and eating in the absence of hunger [5]. In contrast, food avoidance refers to eating behaviors involving withdrawal from or avoidance of food, such as food neophobia, picky eating, slow eating, and emotional undereating [5].

Disordered eating behaviors have multiple causes and are common among young people [6], who constitute a particularly susceptible group to mental health issues [7]. In this context, disordered eating behavior may be influenced by stress, anxiety, and depression [6, 7] as well as by the pursuit of beauty standards as a means of social belonging and acceptance within specific groups [8]. In Brazil, a meta-analysis of university students reported that approximately 14.9% screened positive for symptoms of eating disorders [9].

Furthermore, recent literature has identified discrimination as a potential factor associated with the development of disordered eating behaviors. A systematic review by Rodrigues et al. (2022) revealed that perceived racial discrimination was associated with disordered eating behaviors, such as emotional eating, binge eating, loss of control eating, restrained eating, excessive snacking, disinhibition, and other harmful eating behaviors and attitudes (e.g., restriction, concern about eating, and concern about body shape and weight) [10]. Similarly, Gordon et al. (2023) reported that young adults from sexual minority groups who experienced weight-based discrimination were at a greater risk of disordered eating behaviors than those who did not [11]. Additionally, a meta-analysis examining the association between discrimination and eating disorder psychopathology found small to moderate effect sizes, consistent across all domains analyzed, including global eating disorder symptoms, body dissatisfaction, binge eating, and dietary restraint [12].

Discrimination is defined as any distinction, exclusion, restriction, or other differential treatment of groups identified by social markers, with the intent or effect of nullifying or impairing fundamental freedom in the political, economic, social, cultural, or any other public spheres [13–15]. Discriminatory practices may stem from various social attributes or acquired characteristics, such as race, ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, and body weight [16, 17]. Although each form of discrimination has distinct historical, legal, and cultural roots, all involve unjust treatment that negatively affects individuals from subordinate groups while benefiting those in dominant groups. From an intersectional perspective, discrimination is embedded within interrelated systems of power, namely as racism, patriarchy, gender binarism, heterosexism, and weight stigma, that operate simultaneously and reinforce one another. Intersectionality seeks to capture the structural and dynamic consequences of the interaction between two or more axes of subordination [18]. Thus, individuals may experience discrimination not only across multiple social markers but through their interaction, producing qualitatively distinct forms of disadvantage [19]. In this sense, individual diversity reflects both intersecting identities and their positioning within broader structures of inequality, shaping differential exposure to discrimination and its health-related consequences [16, 17, 20].

Krieger (2014) emphasizes that discrimination affects health through multiple interconnected mechanisms that generate and perpetuate social health inequities [14]. One of these pathways involves perceiving discrimination as a daily social stressor that triggers physiological and psychological responses [21–23]. One such response includes health behavior changes, such as eating behaviors [24]. In this context, the literature suggests that discrimination may foster the internalization of negative social attitudes, as well as the development of maladaptive coping mechanisms and social isolation, which, in turn, increase psychological distress and ultimately heighten vulnerability to eating disorder psychopathology [12].

The majority of studies examining the association between perceived discrimination and disordered eating behavior have been conducted in the United States (U.S.) [10]. Furthermore, different forms of discrimination are often analyzed in isolation or indirectly through sociodemographic characteristics that indicate membership in historically oppressed social groups [12, 25, 26], rather than explicitly investigating the intersection of multiple forms of perceived discrimination as exposures. Incorporating the concept of intersectionality is therefore essential to more fully understand these intersections.

This study aimed to analyze the association between various experiences of discrimination and disordered eating behaviors among undergraduate students in southern Brazil. Given that simultaneous forms of discrimination, such as those based on gender, race, sexual orientation, and weight, may cumulatively impact eating behavior, both individual and intersecting forms of discrimination were considered. From an intersectional perspective, it was hypothesized that individuals exposed to multiple and overlapping forms of discrimination would present higher levels of food approach behaviors compared to those exposed to single or no forms of discrimination.

Methods

Study design, population, and sample

This cross-sectional study included undergraduate students from a public university in Porto Alegre, Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul in accordance with Resolution No. 466/2012 of the Brazilian National Health Council and the Declaration of Helsinki (protocol no. 5,864,653). All participants provided informed consent.

The university has approximately 31,000 undergraduate students [27], all of whom were eligible to participate. Students aged ≥ 18 years were invited via email sent twice by the University Office of Student Affairs between February 2023 and April 2024. Recruitment was complemented by posters with a QR code displayed on campus and announcements on the university’s social media.

Participants completed a self-administered online questionnaire. As access to the survey required an institutional email address, each student could complete the questionnaire only once.

Variables

Disordered eating behavior

Eating behavior was assessed using the Adult Eating Behavior Questionnaire (AEBQ) developed by Hunot et al. (2016) [28] and translated and validated for use in Brazil by Dantas (2019) [29]. The AEBQ is a reliable instrument that provides a comprehensive, convenient, and easily applicable measure for Brazilian adults. The AEBQ is a 35-item Likert-type scale ranging from (0) “strongly disagree” to (4) “strongly agree.” This scale comprises eight dimensions divided into two categories: food approach behaviors (hunger, food responsiveness, emotional overeating, and enjoyment of food) and food avoidance behaviors (satiety responsiveness, emotional undereating, food fussiness, and slowness in eating). The mean score for each dimension and the sum of the food approach and avoidance dimensions were assessed as proposed in the original scale [28]. The internal consistency of the AEBQ was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha, yielding an overall coefficient of 0.7130, which indicates acceptable reliability [30].

Self-reported experiences of discrimination

A questionnaire specifically developed for this study, considering the university student population, was used to assess different experiences of discrimination. The instrument was based on the Experiences of Discrimination Questionnaire developed by Krieger et al. (2005) [31] and validated for use in Brazil (2016) [32]. It evaluates self-reported discrimination across nine specific situations and the response of the individual to unfair treatment.

This self-report instrument was designed to measure the perceived experiences of discrimination related to race/ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, and body weight that students encounter throughout their lives. For each category of discrimination, the first question was: “Have you ever felt discriminated against because of your race/ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, being a recipient of financial aid, body weight, or religious belief?” The response options were “yes” or “no.” For each type of discrimination reported, participants were asked to indicate the frequency with which they experienced that specific form of discrimination across different situations: at the university, when seeking employment, at work, during healthcare appointments, in stores or restaurants, in public spaces or on the street, or when engaging with the police or legal system. The response options were (0) never, (1) once, (2) two to three times, and (4) more than four times. Finally, participants were asked how they typically responded to these events: “If you feel you are being treated unfairly, do you usually (0) accept it as a fact of life, (1) try to do something about it, (2) talk to others about it, or (3) keep it to yourself ?”

A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess the construct validity of discrimination measures in this study population. The questionnaire was specifically developed for this study, and the CFA was performed to verify whether the items adequately represented the latent structure of each type of perceived discrimination investigated in this specific context (race, gender, sexual orientation, and weight). Items that had moderate factor loadings ( > = 0·6) were retained. The fit of both measurement and path models was assessed by means of fit indices, including the χ2 (P-value > 0·05), the comparative fit index (CFI > 0·95), the Tucker–Lewis index (TLI > 0·95), the fit index root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA = < 0·06) and the standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR < 0·08) [33]. Each type of discrimination was considered as a latent variable in the model. After excluding the item on experiencing discrimination in politics or the legal system for racial, sexual orientation, and weight discrimination, and the item on discrimination in stores for gender discrimination, goodness-of-fit indices were found to be satisfactory: X² (2) = 581.491, p < 0.001; RMSEA = 0.035; CFI = 0.978; TLI = 0.972; SRMR = 0.03.

Each form of discrimination was assessed as a dichotomous variable: having experienced discrimination in any of the reported situations (yes) or never having experienced discrimination (no). A variable representing the intersectional strata of discrimination was created, resulting in 16 combinations of discriminatory experiences as follows: (1) no discrimination; (2) weight discrimination; (3) racial discrimination; (4) sexual orientation discrimination; (5) gender discrimination; (6) weight and racial discrimination; (7) weight and sexual orientation discrimination; (8) weight and gender discrimination; (9) racial and sexual orientation discrimination; (10) racial and gender discrimination; (11) sexual orientation and gender discrimination; (12) weight, racial, and sexual orientation discrimination; (13) weight, racial, and gender discrimination; (14) weight, sexual orientation, and gender discrimination; (15) racial, sexual orientation, and gender discrimination; and (16) weight, racial, sexual orientation, and gender discrimination.

Sociodemographic characteristics and body mass index

Sex was assessed by the question “What sex were you assigned at birth?”, with response options male and female. Gender identity was assessed by the question “How do you identify your gender?”, with response options man, trans man, woman, trans woman, “travesti”, non-binary, and other. “Travesti” is a Latin American gender identity, particularly common in Brazil [34], understood as both a political-historical identity position and a transfeminine identity [35]. For analysis, gender identity was classified into four categories: cisgender men, cisgender women, transgender individuals, and non-binary/other. Sexual orientation was assessed by the question “What is your sexual orientation?”, with response options heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, asexual, pansexual, and other. For analysis, sexual orientation was classified into two categories: heterosexual and homosexual/bisexual/other. To ensure a more inclusive assessment, the option “other” captured less common gender identities and sexual orientations [36]. Age was assessed as a numerical variable and categorized into four age groups: 18–21, 22–25, 26–29, and ≥ 30 years. Race/skin color was assessed according to the question and categories used by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE), based on the question “How do you classify your skin color?”, with response options White, Yellow, Black, Brown, and Indigenous [37].

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on self-reported weight and height, and classified into two categories: <30 and ≥ 30 kg/m² [38]. Implausible values were excluded from the analysis, defined as weight < 20 or > 250 kg and height < 80 or > 220 cm, corresponding to values below the 1st percentile and above the 99th percentile of the observed data distribution.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata software version 18.0. For descriptive statistics, means and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the mean food approach and avoidance scores according to the intersectional strata of discrimination.

Simple and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between intersectional discrimination strata and eating behaviors. All models were adjusted for age. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of variable distributions, complemented by visual inspection of histograms. The variables included in the model met the assumptions of normality. No additional sociodemographic variables or indicators of nutritional status were included in the multivariable models, as these were conceptualized as social characteristics highly correlated with discrimination experiences rather than confounding factors. Some missing data were observed for age (n = 8) and BMI (n = 15). Linear regression analyses were conducted using complete-case analysis (listwise deletion), retaining only participants with complete data on all variables included in each model. Associations with p ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Out of approximately 31,000 undergraduate students enrolled at the university, a total of 1,502 students aged > 18 years or older responded to the survey. The mean age of the participants was 26.8 years (SD: 9.3), and 68.7% identified as female, 65.4% as cisgender women, 57.7% as heterosexual, 73.6% as White, and 79.4% presented with BMI < 30 kg/m² (Table 1).

The highest mean food approach scores were among individuals assigned female at birth, cisgender women, sexual minority participants, and individuals with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m². In contrast, the highest mean food avoidance scores were observed among individuals assigned female at birth, transgender individuals, participants aged 18–21 years, and individuals with a BMI < 30 kg/m² (Table 1).

The highest food approach scores were observed in groups that reported intersectional weight-based discrimination: weight and gender, weight and sexual orientation, and weight and race. These were followed by groups who reported weight-based discrimination alone and those who experienced discrimination based on weight, sexual orientation, and gender. The group that experienced both gender and sexual orientation discrimination had the highest mean food avoidance score (Table 2).

Discussion

Food approach

This study demonstrated that perceived discriminatory experiences are associated with food approach behaviors among young adult university students. Weight-based discrimination, both in isolation and within intersectional strata, showed the strongest and most consistent associations with food approach behaviors. Race in isolation was the only discrimination that did not show an association with the food approach.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between gender, sexual orientation, race, and weight-based discrimination and disordered eating behaviors in an interrelated form [12]. As the intersectionality theory posits that individuals may face multiple forms of oppression based on their social location, examining intersectionality is essential. Intersectionality rejects the notion that inequalities arise from distinct, isolated factors; instead, it emphasizes that overlapping processes lead to inequalities and aims to explain the structural and dynamic consequences of multiple, interacting axes of subordination [20, 39, 40].

Our findings underscore the significant role of weight-based discrimination in shaping disordered eating behaviors. When intersected with gender, sexual orientation, and racial discrimination, it was associated with higher food approach scores across all intersectional strata. Some studies have conducted intersectional analyses focusing on specific sociodemographic groups. Beccia et al. (2021) identified multiple marginalized status (e.g., across gender, sexual orientation, and weight status) in the social construction of eating disorder symptoms in the individuals of the Growing Up Today Study [41]. Gordon et al. (2023) found that three in ten young adults from sexual minority groups reported experiences of weight discrimination and were at increased risk of disordered eating behaviors, including unhealthy weight control practices, overeating, and binge eating [11]. Consistent with these findings, the present study showed that groups reporting weight discrimination in combination with sexual orientation or gender discrimination exhibited the largest magnitude of associations for food approach, reinforcing the notion that intersecting forms of discrimination may exacerbate disordered eating behaviors.

In addition, although no prior studies that jointly examined discriminatory experiences were identified in the literature, our findings are consistent with previously reported results on individual forms of discrimination. Three studies conducted among adults in the U.S. reported associations between weight discrimination and uncontrolled eating, emotional eating, weight control behaviors, and binge eating [42, 43], as well as overeating, fewer regular meals, and more frequent consumption of fast food and sugar-sweetened beverages [44]. In addition, a meta-analysis that synthesized the literature on discrimination and eating disorders found a higher effect size for weight discrimination and body image concerns compared to other types of discrimination [12]. Likewise, in the present study, individuals reporting weight discrimination demonstrated the highest magnitude of association with food approach.

In our study, gender and sexual orientation discrimination were associated with food approach. Prior studies have focused on membership in minoritized groups rather than perceived discrimination itself. Nagata et al. (2020) reported a higher lifetime prevalence of binge eating disorder among sexual and gender minority adults than among cisgender heterosexual adults in the U.S [45]. Beccia et al. (2021) observed that among young adults in the U.S., girls and women were more likely to report purging behaviors, binge eating episodes, and lifetime eating disorder diagnoses [41]. In this sense, our findings also indicate higher levels of food approach among cisgender women compared to other groups.

Unlike other forms of discrimination, we did not find an association between racial discrimination and eating behavior when examined in isolation, despite existing literature highlighting the relationship between racism and disordered eating behaviors [10, 46–48]. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence that racism is unrelated to disordered eating behaviors. Rather, it may reflect the specific configuration of social positions within this sample, in which individuals experiencing racial discrimination without co-occurring forms of disadvantage may not exhibit the same patterns. Importantly, racial discrimination was associated with food approach behaviors when combined with other forms of discrimination, mainly weight discrimination, reinforcing the relevance of an intersectional perspective. These results suggest that the effects of racism may be more pronounced when operating in conjunction with other systems of oppression.

One way to understand the relationship between discriminatory experiences and disordered eating behavior is to consider discrimination a social stressor that triggers psychological and physiological responses [49]. From an intersectional perspective, exposure to discrimination is not uniform but structured by the interaction of structural systems of oppression, such as racism, sexism, heteronormativity, and weight stigma, which are embodied through multiple, intersecting social positions (e.g., gender, sexual orientation, race, and body weight) [50]. These interlocking systems may intensify both the frequency and the cumulative burden of stressors. In this context, food may function as a self-regulatory strategy, thereby intensifying emotional eating behavior, which is characterized as the seeking of palatable food in response to negative feelings [49]. Sominsky and Spencer (2014) have proposed that food intake is suppressed through corticotropin-releasing hormone secretion immediately following a stressful event, redirecting physiological response from the less urgent need to eat toward fight, flight, or withdrawal responses necessary to cope with the stressor. However, this food-seeking behavior may resume following glucocorticoid activation in the subsequent hours. In cases of acute stress requiring a physical response, such as predator–prey interactions, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis modulates food intake to promote energy replenishment and confrontation of the stressful event [51]. Meanwhile, chronic psychological stress experienced during repeated instances of discrimination is associated with persistently elevated glucocorticoid levels and may lead to disordered eating behaviors.

Food avoidance

The group reporting discrimination against both gender and sexual orientation exhibited a positive association with food avoidance. Studies investigating the association between gender and sexual orientation discrimination and eating behaviors remain limited. Nagata et al. (2024) reported that experiencing sexual orientation discrimination was linked to a greater concern for weight gain and weight-related self-esteem, particularly among female adolescents in the U.S [52]. Parker and Harriger (2020) found higher rates of disordered eating behaviors, such as purging, fasting, and diet pill use, among LGBT+ youth than among heterosexuals [53]. Diemer et al. (2015) observed a higher prevalence of self-reported eating disorders and compensatory behaviors among transgender students than among their cisgender peers [54]. These findings support our results demonstrating higher mean food avoidance scores among transgender individuals than among cisgender women and men (Table 1). Therefore, food avoidance may be related to weight concerns, as suggested by Parker and Harriger (2020), who found that sexual minority adults reported a desire for thinness, binge eating, purging, and body dissatisfaction, all of which were correlated with excessive concern over body shape and size. Moreover, these behaviors occurred at higher rates than in heterosexual and cisgender male populations [53].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It is the first study to examine the association between different forms of perceived discriminatory experiences and disordered eating behavior using intersectional strata, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of social inequalities. The use of validated questionnaires to measure outcomes and exposures in the study population enhanced the reliability of the results. Additionally, this study was conducted in the Brazilian context, a setting still underexplored in this field of research, involving a sample of university students.

Nevertheless, this study also has some limitations. The sample consisted of young adult undergraduate students, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the general population. Although all eligible individuals in the target population were invited to participate, participation was voluntary, and thus the sample may be subject to self-selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. The cross-sectional design limited the ability to establish temporal relationships between exposures and outcomes. Additionally, discrimination measures were based on self-reporting and may not fully capture the scope of discriminatory experiences, including their structural dimensions [50]. Nonetheless, this form of measurement may provide insights into personal experiences that contribute to the embodiment of population health inequalities [50]. Finally, although our analytical strategy incorporated combinations of discrimination experiences through intersectional strata, it primarily reflects an additive approach and does not fully capture the complex, non-additive interactions between systems of oppression emphasized in intersectionality theory. More advanced approaches, such as MAIHDA (Multilevel Analysis of Individual Heterogeneity and Discriminatory Accuracy), may provide a more robust framework for modeling these dynamics and should be considered in future research [55].

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that different forms of perceived discrimination, both in isolation and in combination, are associated with changes in eating behavior among undergraduate students in southern Brazil. Weight discrimination, whether isolated or intersecting with gender, sexual orientation, or racial discrimination, showed the strongest association with increased food approach behaviors, indicating greater vulnerability to emotional or excessive eating. Conversely, combined gender and sexual orientation discrimination was associated with higher food avoidance scores, suggesting a pattern of more restrictive or avoidant eating behaviors.

Our findings highlight the importance of adopting intersectionality as an analytical approach to understand how multiple forms of discrimination combine to negatively impact health and eating behaviors. Furthermore, they highlight body weight as a social marker of inequality that remains largely unrecognized as a legitimate form of oppression. Weight-based discrimination is still widely prevalent in society and is often reinforced by stereotypes that associate obesity with laziness, gluttony, and lack of personal accountability [56–58]. Therefore, policies and interventions aimed at promoting healthy eating behaviors should incorporate strategies to address discrimination, particularly weight-based discrimination and its intersections with other social identities. Future longitudinal studies involving more diverse populations are needed to further explore the mechanisms linking discrimination to disordered eating behaviors and their implications for nutritional and mental health.
