Long-term clinical outcomes in patients aged 50 to 70 years old undergoing surgical aortic valve replacement in the United Kingdom
Authors: Jeremy Chan, Maria Comanici, Pradeep Narayan, Tim Dong, Gianni D. Angelini, CVD-COVID-UK/COVID-IMPACT Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn the last decade, there has been an increased use of biological prosthesis in patients aged 50 to 70 years old undergoing surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR). The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted health care services, which may have affected this trend. This study aims to evaluate the trends in prosthesis use and long-term clinical outcomes in patients aged 50 to 70 years old who underwent SAVR before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic in the United Kingdom.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data in all patients aged 50 to 70 years who underwent first-time, elective/urgent isolated SAVR from January 2013 to April 2025.ResultsA total of 11,010 patients underwent SAVR during the study period, with a median age of 63.4 years (first and third quartile: 58.6, 67.0 years), of whom 67% (n = 7359) were male. The use of biological prosthesis increased from 57.5% in 2013 to 71.3% in 2024. Patients who received a mechanical prosthesis when compared with a biological prosthesis had a lower all-cause mortality (hazard ratio, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.03-1.43; P = .021). The 1-, 5-, and 10-year survival rates were 98.02% versus 97.60%, 91.69% versus 91.66% and 81.08% versus 76.41%, respectively. Patients with a mechanical prosthesis had a greater cumulative incidence of cerebrovascular accident (adjusted subdistribution hazard ratio [sHR], 1.58; 95% CI, 1.13-2.22; P = .008) and major bleeding (sHR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.15-2.14; P = .005) but a lower incidence of infective endocarditis (sHR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.30-61; P < .001) and of repeat valvular intervention (sHR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.09-0.55; P < .001).ConclusionsThe use of biological prosthesis has increased incrementally since 2013 and persisted post-COVID-19 pandemic. Mechanical valves had a lower all-cause mortality, cumulative incidence of infective endocarditis, and need for repeat intervention. However, they were associated with a greater incidence of stroke and bleeding. The lifetime management of this group of patients remains complex, and long-term high-quality randomised controlled trials are required.

There has been a rapid expansion in the use of biological prosthesis in the United Kingdom and in Europe since the early 2000s.1 Despite equivalent short-term clinical outcomes, several studies have reported that biological prosthesis, when used for surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR), are inferior to mechanical prosthesis in long-term survival in patients between the ages of 50 to 70 years old.2, 3, 4 Other long-term clinical outcomes, such as incidence of infective endocarditis and repeat valvular interventions, also tend to favor mechanical prosthesis.4,5

The COVID-19 pandemic was declared in March 2020, and it had a significant impact on cardiac surgical practice and health care. Cardiac surgical volume has yet to return to prepandemic levels in the United Kingdom.6 We have previously reported an increasing trend in the use of biological prostheses from 1996 to 2019.1 The impact of COVID-19 on this trend is not fully understood. We aim to report the trend in prosthesis use in patients aged 50 to 70 years old undergoing SAVR before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic in the United Kingdom as well as the long-term clinical outcomes in patients aged 50 to 70 years old undergoing SAVR.

Methods

Data were accessed in the National Health Service (NHS) England's Secure Data Environment service, via the British Heart Foundation Data Science Centre's CVD-COVID-UK/COVID-IMPACT Consortium. All patients who underwent first-time isolated aortic valve replacement from January 2013 to April 2025 were identified from the UK National Adult Cardiac Surgery (NACSA) database. This was linked with the NHS Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) and the death registry from the Office for National Statistics to identify any readmission and dates of death (up to July 28, 2025) via anonymized patient IDs generated from NHS numbers. The NACSA dataset was previously described by Bridgewater.7 In brief, the NACSA collects data on preoperative characteristics, procedural data, and in-hospital outcomes such as in hospital mortality, cerebral vascular incident, renal-replacement therapy, and deep sternal wound infection before discharge. Readmission was defined as an admission after discharge to any English NHS hospital captured by the HES dataset. All readmissions within the latest HES available dataset (July 28, 2025) after the individual patient was discharged from the index operation were included. Patients who had active endocarditis, previous cardiac surgery, underwent emergency or salvage surgery, and had any concomitant procedures (coronary artery bypass grafting, major aortic and/or other cardiac surgical procedures) were excluded from the study. The flow diagram of cohort selection is shown in Figure E1.

All patients aged between 50 and 70 years old at the time of operation were divided into 2 groups: (1) those who received a biological prosthesis and (2) those who received a mechanical prosthesis. The trend of the use of biological or mechanical prosthesis across the study period, including various stages during the COVID-19 pandemic as defined by the UK government, was examined. The perioperative characteristics and postoperative outcomes across the whole study period were compared. The primary outcome includes all-cause death. Secondary outcomes include all-cause readmission, cerebral vascular accident, infective endocarditis (any cardiac heart valves, including replaced prosthetic valve), prosthetic valve thrombosis, bleeding events and repeat valvular intervention (both surgical and percutaneous).

Each readmission had a primary diagnosis and up to 19 secondary diagnoses. The primary and secondary diagnoses were reported using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Fifth Edition.8 The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Fifth Edition, codes used for cerebral vascular accident included I60, I61, I62, I63, and I64; for acute infective endocarditis (cardiac heart valves, including replaced prosthetic valve) included I33, T826, I38, and I398; for prosthetic valve thrombosis included T828; and for bleeding events included K29, K625, K922, N501, N93, I629, K228, R040, R041, R042, and N488. The procedures and interventions performed were identified using the OPCS Classification of Interventions and Procedures, Version 4.10. The classification is used by health care providers and commissioners throughout the NHS to support various forms of data collection for secondary uses. The Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures, Version 4.10 code used for repeating aortic valvular intervention includes K26 (plastic repair of aortic valve).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, pending the normality of the data. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. The normality of the continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Pearson χ2 test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to compare 2 categorical variables and for comparison between 2 means of continuous, independent samples, respectively.

To account for potential imbalance in baseline risk, a propensity score was calculated for each patient on the basis of a logistic regression model. All variables included in the model are listed in Table 1. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching (PSM) without replacement and with a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviations was performed. After PSM, standardized mean difference was used to assess balance of the covariates between the 2 groups. A value greater than 0.10 was considered to indicate the presence of residual imbalance among variables. The covariate balance before and after as well as the effectiveness of the PSM was visualized in the love plot (Figure E2). All variables have a standardized mean difference less than 0.1. Subsequent outcome analyses were performed on the matched cohort. The Cochran-Armitage test for trend was used to assess temporal trends in the proportion of patients receiving a biological prosthesis, as compared with a mechanical prosthesis, across the study Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to estimate overall survival. Survival time was calculated from the date of operation to the date of death from any cause, with patients censored at the last known follow-up. All-cause mortality was plotted using Kaplan-Meier curves, and survival probabilities were reported at prespecified time points. Comparisons between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

Furthermore, in the presence of competing risks, we performed a competing risk regression analysis using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model to estimate the cumulative incidence of primary and secondary outcomes. The cumulative incidence function was generated, and the Gray test was applied to compare the cumulative incidence functions between groups after generating plots using the package ggplot2. In addition, the Fine-Gray competing risks regression were used to estimate the subdistribution hazard of event of interest while accounting for the competing risk factor (death). Several articles have previously described the method in detail.9, 10, 11

Data completeness was satisfactory, with only 1% to 3% of missing data in each preoperative characteristic input into PSM. Missing data in all preoperative characteristics were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations. Several imputation methods were applied, including predictive mean matching, logistic regression and polytomous regression, depending on variable type (continuous, categorical and nominal categorical variables). Five imputed datasets were generated, and convergence of the imputation models was assessed visually using trace plots. Analyses were performed on the imputed datasets and pooled using Rubin's rules where appropriate.

This analysis was performed according to a prespecified analysis plan published on GitHub, along with the phenotyping and analysis code (https://github.com/BHFDSC/CCU007_15). R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and R Studio (version 1.4.1103; RStudio, PBC) were used for statistical analysis. Graphs and tables were created using R (version 4.2.3) and Microsoft Office 365 (version 16.0.14026, Microsoft Corporation).

Results

Trend in the Use of Prosthesis Before, During, and After the COVID-19 Pandemic

A total of 11,010 patients with a median age of 63.4 years (first and third quartile: 58.6, 67.0 years), of whom 67% (n = 7359) were male, were included in the study. During the study period, 69.31% (n = 7632) of patients received a biological aortic valve prosthesis. The use of biological prosthesis increased from 57.5% in 2013 to 71.3% in 2024 (Cochran-Armitage test for trend, P < .001). It first peaked in 2020 (71.3%) during the COVID-19 pandemic year, followed by a slight reduction in 2021 (67.5%); however, the increasing trend continued after the pandemic (Figure 1).Figure 1The use of biological prosthesis (against mechanical) for patients 50 to 70 years old who underwent isolated aortic valve replacement in the United Kingdom from 2013 to 2024.

The use of biological prosthesis (against mechanical) for patients 50 to 70 years old who underwent isolated aortic valve replacement in the United Kingdom from 2013 to 2024.

The breakdown of the use of biological prosthesis during different stages of lockdown is shown in Figure E3 and Table E1. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the use of biological prosthesis was greater than 65% across all stages of the pandemic. The second lockdown period (November 5, 2020 to December 2, 2020) had the greatest number of biological prostheses implanted (76.9%), whereas the third lockdown relaxation (March 8, 2021 to June 21, 2021) had the lowest (65.9%).

Specifically, examining an individual age group, we found that the use of biological prosthesis increased most in patients aged 60 to 64 years, from 48.7% in 2013, peaking at 75.2% in 2020, during the pandemic year, followed by a slight reduction to 75.1% in 2024. Nearly 90% of patients in the 65- to 70-year age group received a biological prosthesis, an increase from 80% in 2013 (Figure E4).

Long-Term Mortality Before and After PSM

The median follow-up for the overall cohort and the propensity-matched cohort was 54.9 (first and third quartiles: 26.9, 84.1 months, range: 0.1-150.8 months) and 56.1 months (first and third quartiles: 27.8, 84.9 months, range: 0.1-150.8 months), respectively. The overall survival at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 years of the whole cohort (before PSM) was 97.59%, 94.56%, 90.88%, 86.45%, and 77.33%, respectively. When we compared the survival in the 2 groups during the study period, patients who received a mechanical prosthesis had a lower all-cause mortality before (hazard ratio [HR], 1.34; 95% CI, 1.17-1.52) P = .021) and after PSM (HR, 1.22; 95% CI, 1.03-1.43) P = .021) (Figure 2 and Figure E5).Figure 2Kaplan-Meier curve of the propensity-matched cohort of patients aged 50 to 70 years who received mechanical (Mech) and biological (Bio) aortic valve prostheses.

Discussion

Our data demonstrated a sustained increase in the use of biological prostheses among patients aged 50 to 70 years undergoing SAVR in the United Kingdom, a trend that continued beyond the COVID-19 pandemic. Mechanical valves were associated with a lower all-cause long-term mortality and cumulative incidence of infective endocarditis and repeat valvular intervention. However, mechanical valves were also associated with a greater cumulative incidence of stroke and bleeding. The incidence of prosthetic valve thrombosis was similar in the 2 groups. The use of bioprosthesis has increased significantly in the United Kingdom1 and Europe12,13 since the early 2000s, reflecting both technological advances and patient-centric considerations. The rapid expansion of transcatheter aortic valve implantation has revolutionized the lifetime management of aortic valve disease. Hence, decision-making around aortic valve prosthesis selection in patients aged 50 to 70 years remains complex and should be individualized. In this age group, the choice between a mechanical and a biological prosthesis involves balancing the superior durability and lower risk of reintervention associated with mechanical valves against the lifelong requirement for anticoagulation and its attendant bleeding risk. Lifelong warfarin therapy after mechanical prosthesis entails regular international normalized ratio monitoring, potential dietary and drug interactions, and increased complexity for patients with limited access to health care or those who travel frequently.12,13 Conversely, biological prostheses avoid long-term warfarin therapy and may better align with patient preference and lifestyle but carry a greater risk of structural valve deterioration but also the potential need for future valve-in-valve or repeat intervention. Prosthesis choice should therefore, be guided by shared decision-making, considering life expectancy, comorbidity burden, bleeding risk, contraindications to anticoagulation, patient needs, and the feasibility of future transcatheter or surgical reintervention.

The COVID-19 pandemic may have further reinforced the pre-existing shift toward biological prostheses. In the United Kingdom, the pandemic disrupted cardiac surgical services, reduced capacity, increased case urgency, and constrained routine follow-up pathways, all of which may have favored prosthesis choices that avoided the lifelong anticoagulation monitoring required for mechanical valves. In our cohort, the use of biological prostheses remained greater than 65% throughout the pandemic phases, peaked in 2020, and continued to increase thereafter, suggesting that COVID-19 did not reverse this trend and may instead have accelerated an already established move towards bioprosthetic SAVR. However, as this was an observational registry analysis, the study cannot determine whether pandemic-related service pressures directly altered prosthesis selection or long-term comparative outcomes.

Consistent with our findings, multiple large cohort studies and meta-analyses have shown a survival advantage with mechanical valves in this age group.2,3,14, 15, 16, 17 Kyto and colleagues reported a greater 10- to 15-year survival rate for mechanical valves (eg, 10-year mortality: 18.6% mechanical vs 27.6% biological; 15-year survival: 59% mechanical vs 50% biological).14,16 Similarly, meta-analyses by Diaz and colleagues3 and Tasoudis and colleagues15 confirmed this benefit, with pooled HRs for long-term mortality ranging from 0.72 to 0.86 in favor of mechanical valves.

One of the conflicting findings when comparing our results with the current literature was the incidence of stroke. The 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline for the management of patients with valvular heart disease suggested a similar risk of stroke if patients with mechanical valves maintain appropriate anticoagulation.18 Several observational studies and propensity-matched analyses also reported an equivalence in stroke risk. In a propensity-matched cohort of 1001 patient pairs, the 15-year cumulative incidence of stroke was 8.6% for mechanical valves and 7.7% for bioprosthetic valves (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.75-1.43; P = .84), indicating no statistically significant difference.12 Other large registry studies and multicenter analyses confirm this finding, with HRs for stroke consistently close to unity and nonsignificant P values.13,16 This differs from our results, where the use of a mechanical prosthesis was associated with a higher cumulative incidence of cerebrovascular accident, with an adjusted sHR of 1.58.

Our finding of a greater incidence of infective endocarditis with bioprosthetic valves is strongly supported by existing literature. In a matched cohort of 2928 patients, the 10-year incidence of infective endocarditis was 7.3% for bioprosthetic valves versus 3.7% for mechanical valves (HR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.24-0.88).14 Another nationwide population-based study in Taiwan found a similar pattern, with bioprosthetic valves conferring a significantly greater risk of endocarditis (3.4% vs 1.9%; sHR, 1.78, 95% CI, 1.40-2.26).19 This association was further reinforced by a meta-analysis by Anantha-Narayanan and colleagues,5 which confirmed the greater risk of endocarditis with bioprosthetic valves, reporting pooled odds ratios ranging from 1.59 to 1.78 compared with mechanical valves.

The risk of major bleeding is consistently greater with mechanical valves, largely because of the need for lifelong anticoagulation. In a propensity-matched cohort of patients aged 50 to 69 years, Chiang and colleagues12 reported that the risk of major bleeding was almost twice as high with mechanical valves compared with bioprosthetic valves. Similar findings were reported in the multicenter European studies, where the risk of major bleeding in the mechanical group was significantly greater (HR, 0.65 for bioprosthesis vs mechanical, P = .004).13 Malvindi and colleagues20 reported the risk of hemorrhagic complications at 15 years of 16.2% for mechanical valves and 6.9% for biological valves (P = .001).

Our findings align with previous reports showing that prosthetic valve thrombosis is rare in both groups. For mechanical valves, the risk is estimated at 0.1% to 0.3% per year despite anticoagulation, whereas for bioprostheses it is even lower and generally considered uncommon in the absence of other risk factors.21,22 A large Swedish registry data confirmed that valve thrombosis represents only a small fraction of thromboembolic events in mechanical valve recipients.23 For bioprosthetic valves, thrombosis is rare but increasingly recognized as the result of improved imaging; most cases are subclinical, and the clinical impact is still debated.22

Undoubtedly, bioprosthetic valves are prone to structural valve deterioration and hence have a substantially greater likelihood of requiring repeat valvular intervention in the form of transcatheter valve-in-valve intervention or redo surgical aortic valve replacement. The 10-year reoperation rates are consistently greater for bioprosthetic valves (eg, 8.5% vs 1.4% for mechanical valves; HR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.12-0.74).14 This pattern persists across both large registry studies and meta-analyses, with hazard ratios for reoperation after bioprosthetic valve implantation ranging from 2.55 to 3.04 compared with mechanical valves.13,22,23 With the recent 2025 European Society of Cardiology and European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease, which lower the recommendation for transcatheter aortic valve replacement to 70 years old, the potential for a second intervention should be considered due to the risk of structural valve deterioration.24

An important additional consideration in the broader lifetime-management discussion is the Ross surgical procedure. Although it was outside the scope of the present registry-based comparison, contemporary series from expert centers suggest that, in carefully selected young and middle-aged adults, the Ross procedure can provide excellent hemodynamic and long-term survival that approaches that of the age- and sex-matched general population.24,25 In a multicenter cohort of 1431 adults undergoing the Ross procedure, survival was 95.1% at 10 years and 88.5% at 15 years,. Other contemporary propensity-matched comparative analyses have reported better long-term survival and freedom from valve-related complications than conventional prosthetic aortic valve replacement.26 However, the Ross procedure is technically demanding, dependent on surgeon and center expertise, and performed in a highly selected population; therefore, these outcomes cannot be directly extrapolated to an unselected national SAVR registry such as ours. The Ross procedure should therefore be recognized as an important option in selected patients, particularly at the younger end of the age spectrum, but its formal evaluation was beyond the remit of the present study, which was designed to compare real-world use of mechanical and biological prostheses in routine isolated SAVR practice.

Limitations

This national registry study, based on surgical, admission, and survival registries, provides broad real-world insights. However, certain limitations must be highlighted. First, as an observational design, the study can demonstrate associations but cannot establish causality. Second, despite the use of PSM, unmeasured preoperative variables like frailty or liver dysfunction cannot be accounted for, and the method does not fully capture the complexity of surgical decision-making or multidisciplinary team recommendations. Although active and chronic infective endocarditis at the index procedure were excluded, we could not reliably identify patients with a remote history of intravenous drug use from the available registry fields. These unmeasured factors may have contributed to residual confounding, particularly in relation to the observed cumulative incidence of prosthetic valve endocarditis. The registry did not capture prosthesis model or brand-specific details. As a result, potential differences in durability and structural valve performance between individual biological prostheses could not be examined, which may represent an additional source of heterogeneity within the biological prosthesis cohort. Although our data suggested an increase in the cumulative incidence of repeat valvular interventions beyond 10 years, the median follow-up was 55 months. In order to examine the clinical impact of biological prosthesis, especially in patients younger than 65 year old, further long-term studies are required to fully evaluate the impact since the introduction of transcatheter aortic valve implantation.

Conclusions

Our data demonstrate a persistent increase in the adoption of biological prosthesis in patients age 50 to 70 years old requiring SAVR over the last decade in the United Kingdom. This trend continues to persist post COVID-19 pandemic. The use of mechanical prosthesis was associated with lower all-cause mortality, a lower cumulative incidence of infective endocarditis, and fewer repeat valvular interventions. However, it was linked to a greater cumulative incidence of stroke and bleeding. A similar incidence of prosthetic valve thrombosis was found in both groups. The lifetime management of aortic valve disease in this group of patients remains complex, and patients should be fully informed about both the short-term and long-term clinical outcomes to tailor the prosthesis of choice.
