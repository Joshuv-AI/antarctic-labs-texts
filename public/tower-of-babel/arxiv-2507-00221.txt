Algebraic K \displaystyle K -theory of coherent spaces
Georg Lehner

Abstract
. We give a description of the value of a finitary localizing invariant, such as algebraic K \displaystyle K -theory, on the category of sheaves on a locally coherent space X \displaystyle X . This in particular includes all spaces that arise as spectra of commutative rings. As applications we discuss the connection between scissors congruence K \displaystyle K -theory and Topological Hochschild Homology of certain locally coherent spaces, as well as the algebraic K \displaystyle K -theory of a measure space.

Introduction

Recent progress made by Efimov
[

]
, Krause-Nikolaus-Pützstück

, Lurie, and Clausen among others have raised interest in the (continuous)
\displaystyle K
-theory of a class of large stable
\displaystyle\infty
-categories called
dualizable

\displaystyle\infty
-categories. An example is the
\displaystyle\infty
-category of sheaves on a locally compact Hausdorff space
\displaystyle X
. The result that is central here is the computation of the
\displaystyle K

Theorem 1.1

(
[
Efi25
]
Theorem 6.11, see also
[
KNP24
]
Theorem 3.6.1)

Let
\displaystyle X
be a locally compact Hausdorff space. Then

K^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathrm{Sp}))\simeq H_{cs}(X;K(\mathbb{S}))

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sp}
is the
\displaystyle\infty
-category of spectra and the right-hand side refers to compactly supported sheaf cohomology of
\displaystyle X
with respect to the local system given by the
\displaystyle K
-theory of the sphere spectrum.

This result is used as a starting point to import point-set topological techniques into the study of algebraic
\displaystyle K
-theory, not unlike one does in the setting of operator algebras and their (topological)
\displaystyle K
-theory. However, locally compact Hausdorff spaces are not the only class of spaces whose sheaf categories are dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-categories. Another class of spaces whose
\displaystyle\infty
-categories of sheaves are dualizable is the class of
locally coherent spaces
.

Definition 1.2
.

A space
\displaystyle X
is called
coherent
(sometimes also called a
spectral space
) if either of the following equivalent statements holds:

•

\displaystyle X
is a compact sober space together with a basis of compact open sets, closed under finite intersections.

•

\displaystyle X
is obtained as the spectrum of a commutative ring.

•

\displaystyle X
is obtained as an inverse limit of a diagram of finite posets equipped with the Alexandroff topology.

•

The frame of open sets of
\displaystyle X
is obtained as the Ind-completion of a bounded distributive lattice.

A space
\displaystyle X
is called
locally coherent
if either of the following holds:

•

\displaystyle X
is a sober space together with a basis of compact open sets, closed under finite intersections.

•

The frame of open sets of
\displaystyle X
is obtained as the Ind-completion of a lower bounded distributive lattice.

•

The frame of open sets of
\displaystyle X
is obtained from a finitary Grothendieck topology on a poset closed under finite meets.

We note that a coherent space
\displaystyle X
is Hausdorff iff
\displaystyle X
is a profinite set, also called a Stone space.

There is a Stone duality relating the category of locally coherent spaces with the category of lower bounded distributive lattices. Under this duality, a locally coherent space
\displaystyle X
is sent to the lattice
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{o}(X)
of compact open subsets. We have the equivalence

\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C})\simeq\mathrm{Sh}((\mathcal{K}^{o}(X),fin),\mathcal{C})

for any presentable
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
. Here, for the right-hand side we equip the poset of compact opens with the Grothendieck topology obtained by taking finite joins as coverings. Locally coherent spaces are special in the sense that if we choose a compactly generated base
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
, the
\displaystyle\infty
-category of sheaves
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C})
is again compactly generated. The central result that allows the computation of the
\displaystyle K
-theory of this category is the following. Denote the category of lower bounded distributive lattices as
\displaystyle\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}
.

(See Theorem
4.4

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a dualizable, stable
\displaystyle\infty

\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathcal{C}):\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\rightarrow\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}

Here with
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}
we mean the
\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle\infty
-categories. Any finitary localizing invariant
\displaystyle F:\mathrm{Cat}^{perf}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}

[

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle F^{cont}(\mathcal{C})=F(\mathcal{C}^{\omega})

Corollary 1.4

Let
\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cat}^{perf}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle THH

F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathcal{C})):\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}

This means the computation of the
\displaystyle K

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C})
under a finitary localizing invariant. For one, for any coherent space
\displaystyle X
, there is a natural map
\displaystyle X^{const}\rightarrow X
that equips
\displaystyle X
with a profinite topology, called the
constructible topology

Theorem 1.5

(See Theorem
6.1
and Corollary
6.4
)
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a coherent space. The natural map
\displaystyle X^{const}\rightarrow X
induces an equivalence

F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C}))\simeq F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X^{const},\mathcal{C}))

for any finitary localizing invariant
\displaystyle F
. In particular, if
\displaystyle F
has values in spectra, then for all
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\pi_{n}F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C}))\cong C(X^{const};\pi_{n}(F^{cont}(\mathcal{C}))

where
\displaystyle\pi_{n}(F^{cont}(\mathcal{C}))
is equipped with the discrete topology.

This means the computation of the
\displaystyle K
-theory of these categories reduces to the case of a compact Hausdorff spaces, assuming one understands the space corresponding to the constructible topology reasonably well. Since this might not always be useful in practice, we give a different description in terms of valuations.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be lower bounded distributive lattice and
\displaystyle A
an abelian group. An
\displaystyle A
-valued valuation on
\displaystyle D
is a function
\displaystyle\mu:D\rightarrow A
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\mu(0)=0

(2)

Modularity:
For all
\displaystyle U,V\in D
it holds that
\displaystyle\mu(U)+\mu(V)=\mu(U\vee V)+\mu(U\wedge V)
.

Given a lower bounded distributive lattice
\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\mu_{univ}:D\rightarrow M(D)

\displaystyle\mu:D\rightarrow A

\displaystyle M(D)\rightarrow A

\displaystyle\mu
along
\displaystyle\mu_{univ}

\displaystyle M(D)
the
module of D-motives

\displaystyle M(D)
can alternatively be described as the free abelian group
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[D]

(1)

\displaystyle[0]=0

(2)

\displaystyle[U]+[V]=[U\vee V]+[U\wedge V]
for all
\displaystyle U,V\in D

Write
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(D)
for the Moore spectrum corresponding to
\displaystyle M(D)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(D)

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a locally coherent space. Then

F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C}))\simeq\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{K}^{o}(X))\otimes F^{cont}(\mathcal{C})\simeq\bigoplus F^{cont}(\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle M(\mathcal{K}^{o}(X))

\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{Z}

\pi_{n}F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C}))\cong M(\mathcal{K}^{o}(X))\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{n}(F^{cont}(\mathcal{C})).

Remark 1.8
.

The above results indicate that locally coherent spaces can be thought of as zero dimensional objects from the perspective of
\displaystyle K
-theory. In some sense, this is disappointing, as the homotopy theory of these spaces, even just for finite posets, is very rich. (E.g. every homotopy type of a finite
\displaystyle CW
-complex can be obtained from a finite poset, see
[
McC66
]
.) It also means that no straightforward extension of Theorem
1.1
exists in the non-Hausdorff context, at least not framed in terms of sheaf cohomology.

Remark 1.9
.

We note that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is even compactly generated, the usage of dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-categories could in theory be ignored, as the category
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C})
is then also compactly generated for
\displaystyle X
locally coherent and we have

F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathcal{C}))\simeq F(\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathcal{C})^{\omega}).

However, the formal theory of dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-categories allows for a much cleaner, conceptual proof than a corresponding argument using the category
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cat}^{perf}
instead.

.

Locales and Grothendieck topologies on posets

We will use the language of frames and locales throughout this paper. For general resources, see
[
Joh82
]
and
[
PP12
]
. A join-semilattice
\displaystyle(D,\leq)
is a poset closed under finite joins (= coproducts). A distributive lattice
\displaystyle(D,\leq)
is a poset that admits joins of two elements, written as
\displaystyle U\vee V

\displaystyle U\wedge V

U\wedge(V\vee W)=(U\wedge V)\vee(U\wedge W)

\displaystyle U,V,W\in D

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle 1

A functor
\displaystyle f:D\rightarrow D^{\prime}
between distributive lattices is called a

if
\displaystyle f
preserves meet and joins. We denote the categories of lower bounded, respectively bounded, distributive lattices by

\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}~\text{ and }~\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd},

where morphisms are lattice homomorphisms that preserve the bottom element, respectively the bottom and top element.

A

is a poset
\displaystyle(F,\leq)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Frm}
of frames and frame homomorphisms. The category of locales is defined as the opposite category
\displaystyle\mathrm{Loc}=\mathrm{Frm}^{op}

\displaystyle F
of a locale
\displaystyle L
as the

of
\displaystyle L

\displaystyle F=\Omega(L)

Definition 2.1

Let
\displaystyle(P,\leq)

\displaystyle\{p\leq p\}

\displaystyle\{p_{i}\leq p~|~i\in I\}

\displaystyle q\leq p

\displaystyle\{p_{i}\wedge q\leq q~|~i\in I\}

\displaystyle\{p_{i}\leq p~|~i\in I\}

\displaystyle\{p_{ij}\leq p_{i}~|~j\in J_{i}\}

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle(P,\tau)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle f:P^{op}\rightarrow\mathbf{2}

\displaystyle\{p_{i}\leq p~|~i\in I\}

The locale
\displaystyle L(P,\tau)

\displaystyle(P,\tau)

\mathrm{Sh}((P,\tau),\mathbf{2})\subset\mathrm{Fun}(P^{op},\mathbf{2})

Any propositional sheaf
\displaystyle f:P^{op}\rightarrow\mathbf{2}
is determined by the downward closed set
\displaystyle f^{-1}(1)\subset P

\mathrm{Sh}((P,\tau),\mathbf{2})\subset\mathrm{Fun}(P^{op},\mathbf{2})

\displaystyle(-)^{sh}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\subset P

\mathcal{F}^{sh}=\{p\in P~|~\exists\text{ covering }\{p_{i}\leq p~|~i\in I\}\text{ s.t. }p_{i}\in\mathcal{F}\text{ for all }i\in I\}.

It can be checked that
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{sh}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

Lemma 2.4
.

The functor
\displaystyle(-)^{sh}:\mathrm{Fun}(P^{op},\mathbf{2})\rightarrow\mathrm{Sh}((P,\tau),\mathbf{2})

preserves finite meets.

Proof.

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle 1^{sh}=1

\displaystyle\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G}\subset P

(\mathcal{F}\wedge\mathcal{G})^{sh}\leq\mathcal{F}^{sh}\wedge\mathcal{G}^{sh}

simply because
\displaystyle(-)^{sh}
is a functor. Let
\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{F}^{sh}\wedge\mathcal{G}^{sh}

\displaystyle\{p_{i}\leq p~|~i\in I\}
and
\displaystyle\{q_{j}\leq p~|~j\in J\}

\displaystyle p_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
for all
\displaystyle i\in I
and
\displaystyle q_{j}\in\mathcal{G}
for all
\displaystyle j\in J

\displaystyle\{p_{i}\wedge q_{j}\leq p~|~i\in I,j\in J\}
is a covering with
\displaystyle p_{i}\wedge q_{j}\in\mathcal{F}\wedge\mathcal{G}
, by application of stability under base change and locality.
∎

U&\mapsto&h_{U}=\mathrm{Hom}_{F}(-,U)\end{array}

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Let us first verify that
\displaystyle h
is well-defined, i.e. for all
\displaystyle U\in F

\displaystyle h_{U}
satisfies the sheaf condition. Let
\displaystyle\{B_{i}\leq B~|~i\in I\}
be a covering; in other words,
\displaystyle\bigvee_{i\in I}B_{i}=B

\begin{array}[]{rccccccl}&h_{U}(B_{i})=1\text{ for all }i\in I&\text{iff}&B_{i}\leq U\text{ for all }i\in I&\text{iff}&B=\bigvee_{i\in I}B_{i}\leq U&\text{iff}&h_{U}(B)=1.\end{array}

Now define the functor

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\varphi:\mathrm{Sh}((P,\tau),\mathbf{2})&\rightarrow&F\\
f&\mapsto&\bigvee f^{-1}(1).\end{array}

Let us show that
\displaystyle\varphi
and
\displaystyle h
are inverse to another. Let
\displaystyle U\in F

\varphi(h_{U})=\bigvee_{B\leq U,~B\in P}B=U

since any open is obtained as a union of elements of
\displaystyle P

\displaystyle f
be a propositional sheaf. Recall
\displaystyle\varphi(f)=\bigvee f^{-1}(1)\in F

Let
\displaystyle B\in P

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}f(B)=1&\text{iff}&B\leq\bigvee f^{-1}(1)&\text{iff}&h_{\varphi(f)}(B)=1,\end{array}

(2)

(2)

\displaystyle 0

The equivalence of
\displaystyle(1)

\displaystyle(2)

\displaystyle(1)

To see

3.2.
Boolean algebras and profiniteness

We now turn to the subclass of coherent spaces given by Stone spaces, also called profinite sets. Algebraically speaking, this corresponds to considering Boolean algebras instead of bounded distributive lattices. Since the algebraic theory of Boolean algebras is obtained from the theory of bounded distributive lattices by adding the single operation of negation together with additional axioms, we have an adjunction

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{BAlg}}}

\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{forget}}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dashv}

\displaystyle\mathbf{2}
is Boolean, therefore we have for
\displaystyle D

\mathrm{pts}(D)=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}}(D,\mathbf{2})\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}}(\mathrm{Bool}(D),\mathbf{2})\cong\mathrm{pts}(\mathrm{Bool}(D)).

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle X

Given a

Conversely, given a finite frame
\displaystyle D

\displaystyle F

Proposition 3.25

Let
\displaystyle D
be a finite frame. Then

\displaystyle D

Proof.

We can write
\displaystyle D=\Omega(P_{\mathrm{Alex}})

\displaystyle P=\mathrm{pts}(D)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bool}(D)

\displaystyle p\in P

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bool}(D)

Define the

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle P

3.5.
Valuations and motives for distributive lattices

Definition 3.26
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be a lower bounded, distributive lattice and
\displaystyle A
an abelian group. An
\displaystyle A
-valued valuation on
\displaystyle D
is a function
\displaystyle\mu:D\rightarrow A
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\mu(0)=0

(2)

Modularity:
For all
\displaystyle U,V\in D
it holds that
\displaystyle\mu(U)+\mu(V)=\mu(U\vee V)+\mu(U\wedge V)
.

We denote the set of
\displaystyle A
-valued valuations on
\displaystyle D
by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Val}(D;A)
.

For a fixed abelian group
\displaystyle A
, the assignment of
\displaystyle D
to
\displaystyle\mathrm{Val}(D;A)
is a contravariant functor. If
\displaystyle f:D\rightarrow D^{\prime}
is a homomorphism of lower bounded distributive lattices and
\displaystyle\mu:D^{\prime}\rightarrow A
is a valuation, then the map
\displaystyle f^{*}\mu=\mu\circ f:D\rightarrow A
is again a valuation. This defines a map
\displaystyle f^{*}:\mathrm{Val}(D^{\prime};A)\rightarrow\mathrm{Val}(D;A)
. The expression
\displaystyle\mathrm{Val}(D;A)
is also clearly covariantly functorial in the abelian group
\displaystyle A
.

Given a lower bounded distributive lattice
\displaystyle D
, there exists a universal valuation
\displaystyle\mu_{univ}:D\rightarrow M(D)
, where
\displaystyle M(D)
is the free abelian group
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}[D]
modulo the relations:

(1)

\displaystyle[0]=0

(2)

\displaystyle[U]+[V]=[U\vee V]+[U\wedge V]
for all
\displaystyle U,V\in D
.

It is universal in the sense that whenever
\displaystyle\mu:D\rightarrow A
is a valuation, there exists a unique group homomorphism
\displaystyle M(D)\rightarrow A
extending
\displaystyle\mu
along
\displaystyle\mu_{univ}
. We call
\displaystyle M(D)
the
module of D-motives
. We summarize this in the following statement.

Proposition 3.27
.

The map
\displaystyle\mu_{univ}:D\rightarrow M(D)
induces for each abelian group
\displaystyle A
a natural bijection

\mathrm{Val}(D;A)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{Ab}}(M(D);A).

In particular, by the Yoneda embedding,
\displaystyle M:\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\rightarrow\mathrm{Ab}
is a functor, a fact that is also clear from the presentation.

Example 3.28

Let
\displaystyle B
be a Boolean algebra. Then
\displaystyle\mu:B\rightarrow A
is a valuation iff it is a

function, i.e.
\displaystyle\mu(0)=0
and
\displaystyle\mu(U\vee V)=\mu(U)+\mu(V)

\displaystyle U,V

\displaystyle U\wedge V=0
.

Let
\displaystyle S

The module of motives
\displaystyle M(D)

Theorem 4.4
.

The functor

\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathrm{Spc}):\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}\rightarrow\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{\omega}

Proof.

Corollary 4.10

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\infty

We would like to generalize Corollary

Definition 4.11
.

An exact sequence in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}
is a fiber-cofiber sequence

\mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{f_{!}}\mathcal{D}\xrightarrow{g^{*}}\mathcal{E}.

Concretely, this means that
\displaystyle f_{!}
is fully faithful and
\displaystyle g^{*}
is the (unique) Bousfield localization with
\displaystyle\mathrm{ker}(g^{*})=\mathcal{C}
.

Example 4.12
.

Let
\displaystyle L
be locale and
\displaystyle U
an open subset. Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a stable presentable
\displaystyle\infty
-category. Then the open-closed decomposition discussed in subsection
3.3
gives the sequence

\mathrm{Sh}(L_{/U},\mathcal{C})\xrightarrow{i_{!}}\mathrm{Sh}(L,\mathcal{C})\xrightarrow{c^{*}}\mathrm{Sh}(L_{U/},\mathcal{C}).

If
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(L,\mathcal{C})
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(L_{/U},\mathcal{C})
are in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}
then so is
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(L_{U/},\mathcal{C})
and the above sequence is an exact sequence in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}
.

We will need the following lemma due to Efimov.

Lemma 4.13

(
[
Efi25
]
, Proposition 1.67)
.

A filtered colimit of short exact sequences in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}
is again a short exact sequence.

Using this result, we can extrapolate to get a version of Corollary

for the lower bounded case as well.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
be a dualizable, stable
\displaystyle\infty
-category. The functor

\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathcal{C}):\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\rightarrow\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}

preserves filtered colimits. In particular, for any finitary localizing invariant
\displaystyle F:\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
, the functor

F(\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathcal{C})):\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}

preserves filtered colimits.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the theorem for the case
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{Sp}
. The filtered colimit preserving functor

\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathrm{Sp}):\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}\rightarrow\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}

canonically produces a filtered colimit presering functor

\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathrm{Sp}):{\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}}_{/\mathbf{2}}\rightarrow{\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}}_{/\mathrm{Sp}}.

Precomposing with the fully faithful left adjoint
\displaystyle(-)_{\infty}:\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\rightarrow{\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}}_{/\mathbf{2}}
still preserves filtered colimits. Now, for a given lower bounded distributive lattice
\displaystyle D
we have the short exact sequence

\mathrm{Sh}((D,fin),\mathrm{Sp})\rightarrow\mathrm{Sh}((D_{\infty},fin),\mathrm{Sp})\rightarrow\mathrm{Sp},

given by the open-closed decomposition of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(D_{\infty})
, see Display
1
, hence we can identify the functor
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}((-,fin),\mathrm{Sp})
we are looking for with the composite

\mathrm{DLatt}_{lb}\xrightarrow{(-)_{\infty}}{\mathrm{DLatt}_{bd}}_{/\mathbf{2}}\rightarrow{\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}}_{/\mathrm{Sp}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{ker}}\mathrm{Pr}^{L}_{dual}.

By Lemma
4.13
, short exact sequences are stable under filtered colimits, and hence we conclude the theorem.
∎

We will follow the definition of localizing invariants laid out in

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle\infty

5.
The ring spectrum of motives

We will also need a higher algebraic version of the module of motives. Given an abelian group
\displaystyle A
, write
\displaystyle HA
for the Eilenberg-Maclane spectrum of
\displaystyle A
. We call a connective spectrum
\displaystyle X
a
Moore spectrum
for
\displaystyle A
if
\displaystyle X\otimes H\mathbb{Z}\simeq HA
. Moore spectra for any given abelian group
\displaystyle A
exist and are unique up to equivalence. However, there exists no functor from abelian groups to spectra realizing the Moore spectrum of an abelian group. The Moore spectrum
\displaystyle X
of a free abelian group
\displaystyle A
is given as a wedge sum of sphere spectra
\displaystyle\bigoplus\mathbb{S}
, indexed by a choice of basis of
\displaystyle A
. Observe that the subcategory
\displaystyle\mathrm{MooreSp}\subset\mathrm{Sp}
spanned by Moore spectra (for arbitrary abelian groups) is closed under filtered colimits, as tensoring with
\displaystyle H\mathbb{Z}
and taking homotopy groups preserves filtered colimits. For more details, see
[
Sch
, II. 6.3]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\displaystyle D
be a lower bounded distributive lattice. The
spectrum of
\displaystyle D
-motives

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(D)
is defined to be the Moore spectrum associated to the module of motives
\displaystyle M(D)
.

This definition has two issues:

•

A priori, it is not clear if this is a functorial assignment, as the formation of Moore spectra is not functorial.

•

The module of motives
\displaystyle M(D)
has a (well-defined and non-unital) commutative ring structure given by
\displaystyle[U]\cdot[V]=[U\wedge V]
. If
\displaystyle D
has a top element as well, then
\displaystyle M(D)
is a commutative ring with unit given by
\displaystyle[1]
. This structure carries over to the Moore spectrum
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(D)
, a fact that is not obvious from the definition we gave.

We will rectify both of these shortcomings in this section. Before we do so, a few comments on algebra in a higher categorical setting. We call an
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
with finite products
cartesian
.

Definition 5.2

(See also
[
Joy08
]
,
[
Cra10
]
,
[
Ber19
]
, and
[
GGN15
]
, Appendix B)

An

, also called

, is a cartesian
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
together with a given object
\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{L}
that generates
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
under finite products. If
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is a cartesian category, a
model

\displaystyle M
for
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
is a finite-product preserving functor
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
. Write
\displaystyle\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{C})
for the full subcategory of
\displaystyle\text{Fun}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C})
consisting of the models.

Example 5.3
.

We have the following useful Lawvere theories.

•

The Lawvere theory for Boolean algebras is given by
\displaystyle\mathrm{Fin}_{2}
, the category of finite sets of cardinality
\displaystyle 2^{n}
for some
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

The Lawvere theory for bounded distributive lattices is given by the subcategory
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cubes}
of
\displaystyle\mathrm{FinPoset}
spanned by

, which are posets of the form
\displaystyle\mathbf{2}^{n}
for some
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
, with
\displaystyle\mathbf{2}=\{0\leq 1\}
.

•

The Lawvere theory for lower bounded distributive lattices is given by the subcategory
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cubes}_{root}
of
\displaystyle\mathrm{FinPoset}_{*/}
of finite pointed posets, spanned by
rooted combinatorial cubes
, which are the combinatorial cubes
\displaystyle\mathbf{2}^{n}
for some
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}
, together with the chosen basepoint being the initial object.

•

The Lawvere theory for join-semilattices is given by the category
\displaystyle\mathrm{FinRel}
of finite sets with relations as morphisms. This can be seen by observing that the free join-semilattice on
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{n})
of a
\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{m})
is determined by a choice of subset of
\displaystyle\mathbf{n}
for each
\displaystyle i\in\mathbf{n}

The Set-valued models for the above four Lawvere theories agree with the categories of Boolean algebras, bounded distributive lattices and lower bounded distributive lattices. Observe that we have cartesian functors

\mathrm{FinRel}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{P}}\mathrm{Cubes}_{root}\xrightarrow{forget}\mathrm{Cubes}\xrightarrow{forget}\mathrm{Fin}_{2},

Consider the
\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{Spc})

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

Theorem 5.4

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
be a Lawvere theory, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle y

\mathrm{Fun}^{L}(\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{Spc}),\mathcal{E})\simeq\mathrm{Fun}^{\amalg}(\mathcal{L}^{op},\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fun}^{L}
denotes the
\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle\mathrm{Fun}^{\amalg}

\displaystyle\infty

This is more or less directly handled by Lurie in

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle C\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(C)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(C)

\displaystyle\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{Spc})
for
\displaystyle C=\mathcal{L}^{op}

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(C)^{op}&\simeq\mathrm{Fun}^{L}(\mathrm{Spc},\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(C)^{op})&\simeq\mathrm{Fun}^{R}(\mathcal{P}_{\Sigma}(C)^{op},\mathrm{Spc})^{op}&\\

\displaystyle\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{Spc})

\displaystyle\infty

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
in
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle f:\mathcal{L}^{op}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
is finite coproduct preserving, then we have the induced adjunction

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{Spc})}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{E}}}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{!}}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f^{*}}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dashv}

where the right adjoint functor

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f^{*}:\mathcal{E}&\rightarrow&\text{Mod}_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathrm{Spc})\\
e&\mapsto&\mathrm{Map}_{\mathcal{E}}(f(-),e)\end{array}

Theorem 5.6

Let
\displaystyle B:\mathrm{Fin}_{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Spc}
be a model for the theory of Boolean algebras. Then the underlying space
\displaystyle B(\mathbf{2})
is discrete. Moreover, there is an equivalence of
\displaystyle\infty
-categories

\mathrm{BoolAlg}=\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{BoolAlg}}(\mathrm{Set})\simeq\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{BoolAlg}}(\mathrm{Spc}).

Proof.

Denote the one and two-element set by
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{2}
, respectively. The statement about the equivalence of
\displaystyle\infty
-categories follows immediately by observing that if
\displaystyle B
is a Boolean algebra object such that
\displaystyle B(\mathbf{2})
is discrete, then the value
\displaystyle B(\mathbf{2}^{n})=B(\mathbf{2})^{n}
is discrete for any
\displaystyle n\geq 0
.

Now let
\displaystyle B
be a Boolean algebra object in spaces. First note that restricting to the
\displaystyle(0,\vee)
-structure equips
\displaystyle B
with the structure of an
\displaystyle E_{\infty}
-monoid. By the Eckmann-Hilton argument, the operation
\displaystyle\vee
agrees with the usual group multiplication on
\displaystyle\pi_{n}(B(\mathbf{2}),0)
for any
\displaystyle n\geq 1
. However, for the operation
\displaystyle\vee
any element is idempotent, hence
\displaystyle\pi_{n}(B(\mathbf{2}),0)=1

We are now ready to prove the two main theorems of this paper.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a coherent space. Then the natural map
\displaystyle X^{const}\rightarrow X
induces an equivalence

F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X,\mathcal{C}))\simeq F^{cont}(\mathrm{Sh}(X^{const},\mathcal{C}))

for any finitary localizing invariant
\displaystyle F
and dualizable stable
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Write
\displaystyle D=\mathcal{K}^{o}(X)
for the bounded distributive lattice of compact open sets of
\displaystyle X
. The natural map
\displaystyle X^{const}\rightarrow X
corresponds under Stone-Duality to the homomorphism

\displaystyle D\rightarrow\mathrm{Bool}(D)
. We can write
\displaystyle D=\mathrm{colim}_{i\in I}D_{i}
with
\displaystyle D_{i}
finite frames, hence using Theorem
4.14
, it suffices to prove the theorem for the case of
\displaystyle D
being a finite frame. Then we have the natural commuting square

\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F(\mathrm{Sh}(D,\mathcal{C}))}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Bool}(D),\mathcal{C}))}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F(\mathcal{C})^{|\mathrm{pts}(D)|}}}
\displaystyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{F(\mathcal{C})^{|\mathrm{pts}(\mathrm{Bool}(D))|}}}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\displaystyle\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}

where the bottom map is an equivalence since
\displaystyle\mathrm{pts}(D)\cong\mathrm{pts}(\mathrm{Bool}(D))
and the vertical maps are equivalences by Example
4.6
and Corollary
4.19
.
∎

Given Theorem
6.1
, we will now extend the statement to locally coherent spaces. Note that if
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
is a presentable stable
\displaystyle\infty
-category, it is tensored over the
\displaystyle\infty
-category of spectra
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sp}
. For
\displaystyle A\in\mathrm{Sp}
and
\displaystyle c\in\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle A\otimes c
for their tensor. Also recall the existence of the spectrum-of-motives-functor
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}
provided by Theorem

.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a locally coherent space corresponding to the frame
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(D)
, where
\displaystyle D
is the lower bounded distributive lattice of compact open subsets of
\displaystyle X
. Then for any finitary localizing invariant
\displaystyle F
and dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
there is a natural equivalence

F(\mathrm{Sh}(X;\mathcal{C}))\simeq\mathcal{M}(D)\otimes F(C).

If the localizing invariant
\displaystyle F
takes values in the
\displaystyle\infty
-category of spectra, we have that

\pi_{n}F(\mathrm{Sh}(X;\mathcal{C}))\cong M(D)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\pi_{n}F(C).

Proof.

Let us first prove the case for
\displaystyle X
being profinite, with
\displaystyle X=\lim_{i\in I}X_{i}
for
\displaystyle X_{i}
finite discrete sets. In this case we have

\mathrm{Sh}(X;\mathcal{C})\simeq\text{colim}_{i\in I}\mathrm{Sh}(X_{i};\mathcal{C})\simeq\text{colim}_{i\in I}\bigoplus_{X_{i}}\mathcal{C}

in
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cat}^{dual}

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle X
is locally coherent space corresponding to the frame
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(D)

\mathrm{Sh}((D,fin);\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\mathrm{Sh}((D_{\infty},fin);\mathcal{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(D_{\infty})

3.36

7.
Applications

7.1.
Non-equivariant Scissors Congruence

An interesting application of the theory appears in the study of scissors congruence. Let
\displaystyle X
be a geometry, such as Euclidean space
\displaystyle E^{n}
, the sphere
\displaystyle S^{n}
or hyperbolic space
\displaystyle H^{n}
, of dimension
\displaystyle n
. A geometric
\displaystyle n
-simplex is the convex hull of
\displaystyle n+1
points of non-trivial measure. (Meaning the
\displaystyle n+1
-points do not lie on an
\displaystyle n-1
-dimensional subspace.) A polytope in
\displaystyle X
is a finite union of geometric
\displaystyle n
-simplices. (Note that this includes the empty set
\displaystyle\emptyset
.) Let
\displaystyle G
be a subgroup of the group of isometries. A central question is Hilbert’s third problem.

Question 7.1

(Hilbert’s third problem)
.

Given two polytopes
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
, when can
\displaystyle P
be cut up into finitely many pieces, moved around via isometries in
\displaystyle G
and reassembled into
\displaystyle Q
?

The question can be phrased differently. Consider the free abelian group
\displaystyle P(X,G)
generated by the polytopes in
\displaystyle X
modulo the relations

•

\displaystyle[P\cup Q]=[P]+[Q]
whenever the intersection of
\displaystyle P
and
\displaystyle Q
has measure zero,

•

\displaystyle[P]=[gP]
for any isometry
\displaystyle g\in G
.

The group
\displaystyle P(X,G)
is called
scissors congruence group
of
\displaystyle X
. Hilbert’s third problem can be reinterpreted as the question of finding an explicit description of this group (via invariants such as volume or the Dehn invariant).

Inna Zakharevich has generalized this abelian group into a spectrum
\displaystyle K^{sci}(X,G)
called Scissor’s congruence
\displaystyle K
-theory, whose zero-th homotopy group agrees with
\displaystyle P(X,G)
and whose higher homotopy groups provide a playing field for the question of finding “higher Dehn invariants”, see
[
Zak11
]
, also
[
Mal24
]
and
[
Kup+24
]
. Let us mention two central statements about the description of this spectrum. The first is about the non-equivariant case, i.e. when we let
\displaystyle G=\{1\}
be our group of isometries.

Theorem 7.2

(Malkiewich-Zakharevich,
[
Mal24
]
Theorem 1.10)
.

For any geometry
\displaystyle X
, there is an equivalence

K^{sci}(X,1)\simeq\bigoplus\mathbb{S},

where the wedge product is indexed by a choice of basis of the free (!) abelian group
\displaystyle P(X,1)
.

Furthermore, the spectrum
\displaystyle K^{sci}(X,1)
naturally inherits an action by any group of isometries
\displaystyle G
. This is used in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.3

(Bohmann-Gerhardt-Malkiewich-Merling-Zakharevich,
[
Mal24
]
Theorem 1.11)
.

There is an equivalence of spectra

K^{sci}(X,1)_{hG}\simeq K^{sci}(X,G).

This connects to the statements in this paper as follows. Note that the collection of polytopes in
\displaystyle X
forms a lower bounded distributive lattice
\displaystyle D(X)
, where joins are given by union and meets are given by intersection up to measure zero (so two polytopes that touch along a set of
\displaystyle(n-1)
-faces have empty meet). It is clear by definition that

M(D(X))\cong P(X,1).

The latter group is also referred to as the polytope module of
\displaystyle X
. Associated to
\displaystyle D(X)
, we have a locally coherent space
\displaystyle\mathrm{Coh}(X)
under Stone duality. An immediate corollary of Theorem
6.2
is the following, by noting that
\displaystyle\mathrm{THH}(\mathrm{Sp})\simeq\mathbb{S}
.

Corollary 7.4
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a geometry. There is an equivalence

\mathrm{THH}(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Coh}(X);\mathrm{Sp}))\simeq\mathcal{M}(D(X))\simeq K^{sci}(X,1).

If
\displaystyle G
is a group of isometries, there is a natural action of
\displaystyle G
on
\displaystyle\mathrm{Coh}(X)
via homeomorphisms (since
\displaystyle G
acts on
\displaystyle D(X)
via lattice isomorphisms). Theorem
7.3
seems to suggest that a form of assembly could hold for the dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-category
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Coh}(X);\mathrm{Sp})
together with its induced
\displaystyle G
-action. We are left with the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.5
.

Let
\displaystyle X
be a geometry and
\displaystyle G
a group of isometries of
\displaystyle X
. There is an equivalence

\mathrm{THH}(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Coh}(X);\mathrm{Sp})_{hG})\simeq K^{sci}(X,G).

This conjecture would imply that higher scissors congruence
\displaystyle K
-theory can be thought of as a special case of the study of topological Hochschild Homology, or equivalently traces, of a class of dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-categories. It would tighten the relationship between scissors congruence
\displaystyle K
-theory with algebraic
\displaystyle K
-theory and explain why several techniques have close analogues on both sides.

Remark 7.6
.

For a given polytope
\displaystyle P
, the lattice
\displaystyle D(X)_{/P}
is in fact Boolean. This means that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Coh}(X)
has a neighborhood basis of profinite spaces, closed under intersection. As a consequence we see that
\displaystyle\mathrm{Coh}(X)
is a locally compact Hausdorff space.

Using the fact that
\displaystyle K_{0}(\mathbb{S})=\mathbb{Z}
and
\displaystyle K_{1}(\mathbb{S})=\{\pm 1\}
, this means that for a localizable measure space
\displaystyle(X,\mu)
we have natural inclusions

\begin{array}[]{rcccccl}K_{0}(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Stone}(X);\mathrm{Sp}))&\cong&M(\mathcal{B})&\subset&L^{\infty}(X;\mathbb{Z})&\cong&K^{alg}_{0}(L^{\infty}(X;\mu))\\
K_{1}(\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Stone}(X);\mathrm{Sp}))&\cong&M(\mathcal{B})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\{\pm 1\}&\subset&L^{\infty}(X)^{\times}&\cong&K^{alg}_{1}(L^{\infty}(X;\mu)).\end{array}

which correspond to the inclusions of functions taking on finitely many values, or functions taking values in
\displaystyle\{\pm 1\}
, respectively.

In summary, the relation between the (algebraic)
\displaystyle K
-theory of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Stone}(X)
and the operator theory of
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
is very tight, related by forms of completions or passage to bounded elements. This suggests that one should think of the compactly generated category
\displaystyle\mathrm{Sh}(\mathrm{Stone}(X);\mathrm{Sp})
as the corresponding object in the setting of dualizable
\displaystyle\infty
-categories to the commutative von Neumann algebra
\displaystyle L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
in the setting of
\displaystyle C^{*}
-algebras.

A compact strictly localizable enhanced measurable space

\displaystyle X
