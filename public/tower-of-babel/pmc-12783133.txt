Hospital information system based psychological nursing improves maternal and neonatal outcomes in cesarean section patients
Authors: Yuping Ye, Hongmei Cai, Dan Xiao, Yongmei Yang, Hua Liang, Runzuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo evaluate the association between Hospital Information System (HIS)-based psychological nursing intervention models and maternal and neonatal outcomes among cesarean section patients during the perioperative period. A quasi-experimental before-and-after study with propensity score matching was conducted at Foshan Women and Children Hospital from January 2022 to December 2023. The control group (n = 284) received standard nursing care between January and June 2022, while the intervention group (n = 284) received HIS-based psychological nursing interventions from July 2022 to December 2023. Interventions included systematic psychological assessment, individualized counseling sessions (4–6 sessions, 30–45 min each), structured education, and continuous perioperative support. Propensity score matching was performed using maternal age, education level, parity, gestational age, and baseline anxiety levels. Primary outcomes included maternal psychological indicators (anxiety and depression scores) and clinical parameters (operative duration, blood loss, complications). Secondary outcomes encompassed neonatal health indicators. The intervention group was associated with improvements in psychological outcomes, with State-Trait Anxiety Inventory scores decreasing by 27.1% (mean difference: -13.1, 95% CI: -14.8 to -11.4, p < 0.001) and Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale scores reducing by 41.4% (mean difference: -3.6, 95% CI: -4.2 to -3.0, p < 0.001). Clinical outcomes showed reduced operative duration (mean difference: -6.6 min, 95% CI: -8.3 to -4.9, p < 0.001), decreased blood loss (mean difference: -57.3 mL, 95% CI: -72.4 to -42.2, p < 0.001), and shorter hospital stays (mean difference: -0.9 days, 95% CI: -1.1 to -0.7, p < 0.001). Neonatal outcomes showed higher 5-minute Apgar scores (mean difference: 0.4, 95% CI: 0.2 to 0.6, p < 0.001) and reduced asphyxia rates (3.5% vs. 8.8%, risk ratio: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.84, p = 0.013). HIS-based psychological nursing intervention models were significantly associated with improvements in maternal psychological well-being, clinical outcomes, and neonatal health indicators. However, the temporal separation between groups limits causal inference due to potential secular trends. These findings suggest that systematic psychological support protocols may be beneficial as standard care for cesarean section patients, pending confirmation by randomized controlled trials.Keywords: Hospital information system, Psychological nursing intervention, Cesarean section, Maternal outcomes, Neonatal outcomes, Perioperative careSubject terms: Psychology, Health occupations

Introduction

Cesarean section rates have exhibited a remarkable upward trajectory globally over the past two decades, with rates increasing from approximately 12% in 1990 to over 21% in 2021 worldwide, as reported by the World Health Organization1. The trend shows substantial regional and within-country heterogeneity, with very high cesarean section rates in some regions such as Latin America and Eastern Asia, while other regions like sub-Saharan Africa maintain persistently low rates, reflecting marked inequalities in access to surgical obstetric care2. The World Health Organization recommends that cesarean section rates should ideally remain between 10 and 15% of all births at the population level, yet many countries now report rates exceeding 30%, indicating a substantial deviation from evidence-based obstetric practice3.

The perioperative period of cesarean section represents a critical phase characterized by heightened psychological vulnerability for both mothers and their newborns. Maternal anxiety, depression, and fear during this period can significantly impact not only the immediate surgical outcomes but also long-term maternal-infant bonding and neonatal development4. Research has demonstrated that elevated maternal stress hormones, particularly cortisol, can cross the placental barrier and influence fetal neurodevelopment, potentially leading to adverse neonatal outcomes including altered sleep patterns, feeding difficulties, and compromised immune function5. Furthermore, untreated perioperative psychological distress may contribute to the development of postpartum depression, which affects approximately 10–20% of new mothers and can have lasting consequences for family dynamics and child development6,7.

The integration of Hospital Information Systems (HIS) into modern healthcare delivery has revolutionized nursing management and patient care coordination. These sophisticated digital platforms enable real-time monitoring of patient status, standardized care protocols, and enhanced communication among multidisciplinary healthcare teams8. In the context of obstetric care, HIS facilitates comprehensive documentation of maternal psychological assessments, risk stratification, and implementation of targeted interventions throughout the perioperative continuum9,10. The systematic collection and analysis of patient data through HIS provides unprecedented opportunities for evidence-based decision-making and quality improvement initiatives in maternal healthcare11,12.

The development of HIS-based psychological nursing intervention models represents a paradigm shift toward personalized, data-driven maternal care. Such models leverage the computational capabilities of information systems to identify high-risk patients, deliver timely interventions, and monitor treatment responses in real-time9. By integrating psychological assessment tools, intervention protocols, and outcome tracking mechanisms within a unified digital platform, healthcare providers can implement more consistent and effective psychological support strategies for cesarean section patients.

The necessity for implementing systematic psychological nursing interventions during the cesarean section perioperative period is underscored by mounting evidence linking maternal psychological well-being to both immediate surgical outcomes and long-term maternal-infant health trajectories. Traditional approaches to perioperative nursing care often lack standardization and may not adequately address the complex psychological needs of cesarean section patients11. Therefore, the development and evaluation of HIS-based intervention models that can systematically identify, monitor, and address psychological distress represents a critical advancement in obstetric nursing practice.

Research objectives and hypotheses

The primary objective of this quasi-experimental study is to evaluate the association between a HIS-based psychological nursing intervention model and maternal and neonatal outcomes among cesarean section patients. Our primary hypothesis is that the HIS-based psychological nursing intervention will be associated with improvements in maternal psychological well-being, specifically anxiety and depression levels, compared to standard care. Secondary hypotheses include: (1) the intervention will be associated with improved perioperative clinical outcomes, including reduced operative duration, blood loss, and complication rates; and (2) the intervention will be associated with improved neonatal health indicators, including higher Apgar scores and reduced asphyxia rates.

Innovation and significance

This study contributes to the existing literature by providing the first comprehensive evaluation of a HIS-integrated psychological nursing intervention model specifically designed for cesarean section patients. The innovative aspects of this research include the systematic integration of psychological assessment protocols within routine hospital information systems, the development of real-time risk stratification algorithms, and the implementation of standardized intervention pathways that can be replicated across different healthcare settings. The findings from this investigation will provide essential evidence for healthcare administrators, nursing leaders, and clinical practitioners seeking to optimize perioperative care quality and improve maternal-infant outcomes through technology-enhanced nursing interventions.

Theoretical foundation and literature review

Theoretical framework of perioperative psychological nursing care for cesarean section

The theoretical foundation of perioperative psychological nursing care for cesarean section is anchored in several well-established psychological and nursing theories that collectively provide a comprehensive framework for understanding and addressing maternal psychological responses during the surgical process. The stress response theory, originally conceptualized by Selye and later refined by Lazarus and Folkman, serves as the primary theoretical foundation for understanding how women respond to the psychological stressors associated with cesarean delivery13. This theory posits that individual responses to stressful events are mediated by cognitive appraisal processes, where the perceived threat of surgery and its potential consequences trigger a cascade of physiological and psychological responses that can significantly impact both maternal and neonatal outcomes14.

The application of social support theory in the context of cesarean section nursing care emphasizes the critical role of interpersonal relationships and social networks in buffering the adverse effects of perioperative stress15. According to this theoretical framework, effective social support mechanisms, including professional nursing support, family involvement, and peer interactions, can significantly reduce maternal anxiety levels and enhance coping mechanisms during the perioperative period. The theory distinguishes between different types of social support, including emotional, informational, instrumental, and appraisal support, each contributing uniquely to maternal psychological well-being and surgical recovery outcomes.

Self-efficacy theory, developed by Bandura, provides another crucial theoretical lens through which to understand maternal psychological responses to cesarean section16. This theory suggests that an individual’s belief in their capacity to execute behaviors necessary to produce specific performance attainments directly influences their emotional states, stress levels, and behavioral choices during challenging situations. In the context of cesarean section, maternal self-efficacy beliefs regarding their ability to cope with surgical procedures, manage postoperative pain, and successfully transition to motherhood significantly influence psychological adjustment and recovery trajectories.

The integrated mechanism of psychological stress response during cesarean section perioperative period, as illustrated in Fig. 1, demonstrates the complex interplay between environmental stressors, cognitive appraisal processes, and physiological responses that ultimately influence maternal and neonatal outcomes. The pathogenesis of preoperative anxiety in cesarean section patients typically involves a combination of anticipatory fear regarding surgical risks, concerns about neonatal well-being, and uncertainty about the recovery process17. These psychological stressors activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to elevated cortisol levels that can cross the placental barrier and potentially affect fetal neurodevelopment and behavioral outcomes.

Postoperative depression in cesarean section patients represents a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by hormonal fluctuations, physical discomfort, altered birth expectations, and potential complications related to surgical recovery18. The theoretical understanding of postoperative depression encompasses both biological and psychosocial factors, including the rapid decline in pregnancy hormones, inflammatory responses associated with surgical trauma, and psychological adjustment challenges related to unexpected changes in birth plans. The impact pathway of these psychological disturbances on maternal-infant outcomes involves both direct mechanisms, such as impaired maternal-infant bonding and reduced breastfeeding success, and indirect mechanisms, including compromised maternal self-care behaviors and delayed recovery from surgical interventions.

Current application status of hospital information systems in nursing interventions

The evolution of Hospital Information Systems (HIS) in nursing management has progressed through distinct developmental phases, beginning with basic electronic health record systems in the 1970 s and advancing to sophisticated integrated platforms that facilitate comprehensive patient care coordination and clinical decision support19. The initial implementation of computerized nursing information systems primarily focused on documentation efficiency and administrative tasks, but contemporary HIS platforms have evolved to encompass advanced functionalities including real-time patient monitoring, evidence-based care protocols, and predictive analytics for risk stratification and intervention planning.

The advantages of informatized nursing interventions are manifold, encompassing enhanced care standardization, improved communication among healthcare teams, and systematic quality monitoring capabilities. Impact mechanisms of psychological nursing interventions on maternal and neonatal outcomes

The impact mechanisms of psychological nursing interventions on maternal and neonatal outcomes operate through complex neurobiological and psychosocial pathways that interconnect maternal psychological well-being with physiological responses and subsequent birth outcomes. Psychological nursing interventions primarily influence maternal mental health status through cognitive restructuring, stress reduction, and enhancement of coping mechanisms, which subsequently modulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity and reduce circulating stress hormones such as cortisol and catecholamines24. This neuroendocrine modulation creates a cascade effect that influences multiple physiological systems, including cardiovascular function, immune response, and uterine contractility patterns during labor and delivery.

The physiological indicators most significantly affected by psychological nursing interventions include blood pressure variability, heart rate patterns, inflammatory markers, and pain perception thresholds. Research has demonstrated that structured psychological support during the perioperative period can reduce maternal systolic blood pressure by an average of 8–12 mmHg and decrease circulating interleukin-6 levels by approximately 25–30% compared to standard care protocols25. These physiological improvements translate directly into enhanced surgical outcomes, including reduced intraoperative complications, decreased analgesic requirements, and accelerated postoperative recovery times.

The influence of psychological nursing interventions on delivery outcomes encompasses both immediate birth-related parameters and longer-term maternal recovery indicators. Women receiving comprehensive psychological support demonstrate significantly lower rates of operative complications, reduced blood loss during cesarean section, and shorter hospital stays compared to control groups receiving routine care26. The mechanisms underlying these improvements include enhanced maternal cooperation during surgical procedures, reduced muscular tension that facilitates surgical access, and improved postoperative adherence to recovery protocols through increased self-efficacy and motivation.

Neonatal health outcomes are profoundly influenced by maternal psychological status through both direct and indirect pathways. Direct effects include transplacental transmission of maternal stress hormones that can affect fetal neurodevelopment, while indirect effects encompass improved maternal self-care behaviors and enhanced maternal-infant bonding that promote optimal neonatal care practices27. Infants born to mothers who received psychological nursing interventions demonstrate improved Apgar scores, reduced incidence of neonatal intensive care unit admissions, and enhanced breastfeeding initiation rates compared to those born to mothers receiving standard care.

Comparative analysis of different psychological intervention modalities reveals significant variations in effectiveness across various outcome measures. Cognitive-behavioral interventions appear most effective for reducing preoperative anxiety and enhancing coping skills, while supportive counseling approaches demonstrate superior outcomes for improving maternal-infant bonding and reducing postpartum depression risk. Multi-modal interventions that combine educational components with emotional support and relaxation techniques consistently demonstrate the most comprehensive benefits across all measured outcomes28. The duration and intensity of interventions also significantly influence effectiveness, with interventions lasting 4–6 weeks and including at least 3–4 individual sessions showing optimal results.

The optimal timing for psychological nursing interventions appears to be most critical during the antenatal period, ideally beginning 2–4 weeks before scheduled cesarean section to allow sufficient time for skill development and stress reduction. However, interventions initiated during the immediate preoperative period can still provide significant benefits for anxiety reduction and pain management. The most effective intervention methods combine individualized assessment of psychological risk factors with tailored intervention strategies that address specific maternal concerns and leverage existing support systems. Integration of family members and significant others into intervention protocols enhances effectiveness by strengthening social support networks and ensuring continuity of care beyond the immediate perioperative period.

Research methods

Research design and subject selection

This study employed a quasi-experimental before-and-after design with propensity score matching to evaluate the association between Hospital Information System (HIS)-based psychological nursing intervention models and maternal and neonatal outcomes among cesarean section patients. The HIS-based intervention was implemented as part of a quality improvement initiative and integrated into routine clinical care beginning in July 2022. This study retrospectively extracted and analyzed de-identified data from the hospital’s electronic health records to compare outcomes between patients who received care before (control group) and after (intervention group) implementation of the new model. This design was selected to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of intervention protocols systematically implemented within the hospital’s information system29.

The research framework, as illustrated in Fig. 2, encompasses a comprehensive evaluation process spanning from initial patient identification through final outcome assessment. The study was conducted at Foshan Women and Children Hospital between January 2022 and December 2023, utilizing the hospital’s comprehensive HIS database to identify eligible participants and extract relevant clinical variables. This 24-month study period was selected to ensure adequate sample size while maintaining consistency in clinical protocols and information system functionality.

Overall study design flowchart.

Inclusion criteria for study participants comprised women aged 18–40 years who underwent elective or scheduled urgent cesarean section during the study period, had singleton pregnancies with gestational age between 37 and 42 weeks, and possessed complete medical records within the HIS database including psychological assessment scores and outcome measurements. Elective cesarean sections were defined as planned procedures scheduled before the onset of labor, while scheduled urgent cesarean sections included planned procedures for maternal or fetal indications requiring delivery within 24–72 h but not immediately life-threatening. Additional inclusion requirements included absence of major psychiatric disorders prior to pregnancy requiring ongoing psychiatric medication, ability to communicate effectively in Mandarin Chinese, and availability of complete follow-up data for at least 6 weeks postpartum30.

Exclusion criteria encompassed emergency cesarean sections requiring immediate delivery for life-threatening maternal or fetal conditions, multiple pregnancies, significant maternal comorbidities including pregestational or gestational diabetes mellitus requiring insulin therapy or severe hypertensive disorders, previous history of major mental illness requiring psychiatric medication, and incomplete documentation of baseline psychological assessments or primary outcome measures within the HIS system. Patients with incomplete follow-up data due to transfer to other facilities before six weeks postpartum or those with incomplete psychological assessment data due to language barriers were also excluded from the analysis31.

The subject screening and selection process, depicted in Fig. 3, demonstrates the systematic approach employed to identify eligible participants from the hospital’s patient database. Sample size calculation was performed using the formula for comparing two proportions in cohort studies, with an expected reduction in primary outcome events of 15% in the intervention group compared to the control group, based on previous literature reporting similar intervention effects. With α = 0.05, β = 0.20 (power = 80%), and assuming a 10% loss to follow-up rate, the calculated minimum sample size was 284 participants per group, totaling 568 subjects for the entire study32.

CONSORT-style flow diagram of participant selection and follow-up.

The control group consisted of patients who received standard perioperative nursing care according to conventional protocols implemented before the introduction of the HIS-based psychological intervention model, specifically those treated between January 2022 and June 2022. The intervention group comprised patients who received care under the newly implemented HIS-based psychological nursing intervention model, treated between July 2022 and December 2023. This temporal separation ensures clear distinction between intervention and control conditions while maintaining similar baseline population characteristics.

Matching methodology employed propensity score matching to control for potential confounding variables between groups. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression with the following covariates: maternal age (continuous), education level (categorical: primary/secondary/higher education), parity (categorical: nulliparous/multiparous), gestational age at delivery (continuous), baseline anxiety level measured by STAI (continuous), pre-pregnancy body mass index (continuous), previous cesarean section history (binary), and socioeconomic status indexed by household income (categorical). The matching algorithm utilized 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.1 standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score to ensure optimal balance between groups while maintaining adequate sample size for statistical analysis. Balance assessment was performed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. After matching, all covariates achieved SMD < 0.1, ranging from 0.02 to 0.08, demonstrating successful balance between intervention and control groups (see Table 1; Fig. 4). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative caliper widths (0.05 and 0.2) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), with results remaining consistent across methods.

Data collection and variable definition

Data collection was conducted through comprehensive extraction from multiple integrated components of the Hospital Information System, ensuring systematic and standardized acquisition of all relevant clinical variables. The Electronic Health Record (EHR) system served as the primary repository for demographic characteristics, medical history, and clinical documentation, providing comprehensive longitudinal patient information from initial admission through postpartum discharge33. The Nursing Information System (NIS) contained detailed documentation of all nursing assessments, intervention protocols, and patient response evaluations, including standardized psychological assessment scores and intervention compliance records.

The Laboratory Information System (LIS) provided objective biochemical and hematological parameters, including stress-related biomarkers such as cortisol levels, inflammatory indicators, and routine perioperative laboratory values. Additional data sources included the Pharmacy Information System for medication administration records and the Picture Archiving and Communication System for relevant imaging studies when indicated34.

The HIS-based psychological nursing intervention model comprised a structured, multi-component protocol implemented through automated care pathways within the nursing information system. The intervention was delivered by psychiatric nurses with a minimum of 3 years of clinical experience in obstetric care and at least 40 h of specialized training in cognitive-behavioral techniques, relaxation training, and perioperative psychological assessment. The intervention protocol included the following standardized components: (1) systematic psychological risk assessment using validated screening tools (STAI and EPDS) at admission, postoperative day 1, and 6-week follow-up; (2) individualized one-on-one counseling sessions delivered 4–6 times throughout the perioperative period, with each session lasting 30–45 min, focusing on cognitive restructuring, anxiety management, and coping skill development; (3) structured educational programs about cesarean section procedures, pain management expectations, and postoperative self-care delivered through multimedia presentations and written materials; (4) relaxation training techniques including progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, and diaphragmatic breathing exercises taught during preoperative visits and reinforced postoperatively; and (5) continuous psychological support through the perioperative period with standardized follow-up assessments and booster sessions as needed based on individual risk stratification35.

Implementation of the intervention followed a standardized algorithm embedded within the HIS that automatically triggered specific intervention components based on individual patient risk profiles and assessment scores. High-risk patients (STAI score > 55 or EPDS score > 12) received intensified intervention with additional counseling sessions and daily brief supportive contacts. The HIS system generated automated reminders for nursing staff to conduct scheduled assessments, provided real-time access to standardized intervention protocols and decision-support algorithms, facilitated documentation of intervention delivery and patient responses through structured templates, and displayed visual dashboards showing patient psychological status trends. Quality assurance mechanisms included automated compliance monitoring that tracked completion rates for each intervention component, with supervisory review of intervention fidelity through electronic audit trails. During the intervention period (July 2022-December 2023), the overall intervention adherence rate was 89.4%, defined as completion of at least 75% of scheduled intervention components. Among the matched intervention group (n = 284), 254 patients (89.4%) completed all scheduled counseling sessions, 271 (95.4%) received all educational components, and 268 (94.4%) had complete psychological assessments at all time points.

Primary outcome measures encompassed maternal psychological well-being indicators, including anxiety and depression scores measured using validated instruments, and objective clinical parameters such as operative complications, blood loss, and recovery time. The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory was administered at multiple time points to assess intervention effectiveness, while the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale was used to evaluate postpartum mood disorders36. Secondary outcomes included neonatal health indicators such as birth weight, Apgar scores, and early feeding success, as well as maternal satisfaction scores and healthcare utilization patterns during the postpartum period.

Covariates included demographic characteristics, obstetric history, medical comorbidities, and baseline psychological status, as detailed in Table 2. All psychological assessment tools were administered by trained nursing staff according to standardized protocols embedded within the HIS, ensuring consistency in data collection and minimizing inter-rater variability. Data extraction was performed using automated queries from the HIS database, with manual verification of critical variables to ensure accuracy and completeness of the dataset.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to ensure comprehensive evaluation of intervention effects and rigorous control for potential confounding variables37. The selection of multiple statistical platforms was based on their complementary capabilities, with SPSS providing robust functionality for conventional parametric and non-parametric analyses, while R offered advanced packages for propensity score matching using the MatchIt package and specialized regression modeling techniques.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all baseline characteristics and outcome variables, with continuous variables presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test for sample sizes less than 50 and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for larger samples38. Given the large sample size (n > 500), normality assessment was complemented by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots to ensure appropriate selection of parametric versus non-parametric tests. Variables with substantial skewness or kurtosis despite large sample size were analyzed using non-parametric methods.

Univariate analyses were conducted to examine associations between intervention status and outcome variables, as detailed in Table 3. For continuous variables, independent samples t-tests were used when data met normality assumptions, while Mann-Whitney U tests were employed for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests when expected cell counts were less than 538. All comparisons report mean differences or risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals and two-tailed p-values.

Propensity score matching was implemented to reduce selection bias and create balanced groups for comparison. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression including the covariates specified above. Balance assessment after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values less than 0.1 indicating adequate balance39. A Love plot was generated to visualize covariate balance before and after matching (Fig. 4).

Multivariable analyses were conducted using appropriate regression models based on outcome variable characteristics. For continuous outcomes, multiple linear regression was employed adjusting for residual confounding from surgeon experience (categorized as < 5, 5–10, > 10 years), anesthesia type (general vs. neuraxial), and surgery timing (daytime vs. nighttime). For binary outcomes, logistic regression was utilized with the same covariate adjustments. To account for potential clustering of patients within surgical teams, we calculated cluster-robust standard errors using the sandwich estimator. Effect sizes for continuous outcomes were calculated using Cohen’s d, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of primary findings, including: (1) bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations to estimate 95% confidence intervals using the percentile method; (2) alternative matching specifications using different caliper widths and inverse probability of treatment weighting; (3) E-value calculations to quantify the strength of unmeasured confounding required to explain away observed associations; and (4) interrupted time-series analysis examining monthly trends in primary outcomes to assess potential secular trends. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p-values less than 0.05. Given the multiple outcome comparisons (10 primary and secondary outcomes), we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure to control for type I error inflation. Missing data analysis was conducted by comparing baseline characteristics between participants with complete versus incomplete follow-up data. The proportion of missing data ranged from 3.2% for operative outcomes to 8.8% for 6-week follow-up assessments. Complete case analysis was used for the primary analysis, with sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with 20 imputed datasets to assess the impact of missing data assumptions. Effect sizes were calculated and interpreted according to established conventions to assess clinical significance alongside statistical significance.

Results and analysis

Baseline characteristics analysis of study subjects

A total of 568 participants were included in the final analysis after application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, with 284 subjects in each group following propensity score matching. The demographic characteristics of the study population demonstrated a representative sample of women undergoing cesarean section at our institution, with mean maternal age of 29.9 ± 4.3 years in the intervention group and 30.1 ± 4.5 years in the control group, indicating no statistically significant difference between groups (mean difference: −0.2 years, 95% CI: −1.0 to 0.6, p = 0.287). The distribution of educational attainment showed similar patterns across both groups, with approximately 45% of participants having completed higher education, 38% having secondary education, and 17% having primary education only.

Clinical characteristics revealed comparable baseline obstetric profiles between the intervention and control groups. Gestational age at delivery averaged 39.0 ± 1.3 weeks in the intervention group compared to 39.1 ± 1.2 weeks in the control group (mean difference: −0.1 weeks, 95% CI: −0.3 to 0.1, p = 0.062), while nulliparous women comprised 50.0% and 48.9% of the intervention and control groups, respectively (p = 0.342). Pre-pregnancy body mass index showed no significant differences between groups, with mean values of 22.5 ± 3.0 kg/m² and 22.7 ± 2.9 kg/m² for intervention and control groups, respectively (mean difference: −0.2 kg/m², 95% CI: −0.7 to 0.3, p = 0.421).

Assessment of matching effectiveness using standardized mean differences confirmed successful balance achievement across all measured covariates, with all standardized mean differences falling below the 0.1 threshold recommended for adequate matching. The largest standardized mean difference observed was 0.09 for surgeon experience, while the smallest was 0.02 for parity and baseline STAI score. This level of balance indicates that propensity score matching successfully controlled for measured confounding variables and created comparable groups for subsequent outcome analyses.

Subgroup analysis revealed specific populations that may have differential response patterns to psychological interventions. First-time mothers demonstrated higher baseline anxiety scores compared to multiparous women (51.2 ± 9.3 vs. 45.8 ± 7.9, mean difference: 5.4, 95% CI: 3.8 to 7.0, p < 0.001), suggesting potential for greater intervention benefit in this population. Additionally, women with previous adverse pregnancy outcomes showed elevated baseline psychological distress, with 18.7% scoring above depression risk thresholds compared to 9.4% of women with uncomplicated obstetric histories (risk ratio: 1.99, 95% CI: 1.34 to 2.96, p = 0.001).

The comprehensive baseline characterization confirmed that both study groups represented typical populations of women undergoing cesarean section, with psychological status profiles consistent with published literature on perioperative anxiety and depression in obstetric populations. The successful matching procedure ensured that subsequent outcome analyses could reliably attribute differences to the intervention rather than baseline group imbalances, providing a robust foundation for evaluating the effectiveness of the HIS-based psychological nursing intervention model.

Sustainability analysis revealed that intervention effects persisted through the six-week postpartum follow-up period, with maintained improvements in all measured psychological domains. The average persistence coefficient across all psychological measures was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.78 to 0.88), indicating strong maintenance of intervention benefits beyond the immediate perioperative period.

The clinical significance of intervention effects was further assessed by examining the proportion of patients achieving clinically meaningful improvement, defined a priori as a ≥ 30% reduction in STAI anxiety scores from baseline. Among intervention group patients, 73.6% (209/284) achieved this threshold compared to 18.7% (53/284) in the control group (absolute risk difference: 54.9%, 95% CI: 47.8% to 62.0%, p < 0.001), yielding a number needed to treat (NNT) of 1.8 (95% CI: 1.6 to 2.1). These quantitative measures demonstrate substantial clinical value of the HIS-based psychological nursing intervention model, with effect sizes meeting or exceeding established thresholds for clinical significance across all measured psychological domains.

Factors influencing intervention effectiveness were evaluated through multivariate regression analysis. Maternal education level emerged as the strongest predictor of intervention response (β = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.21 to 0.47, p < 0.001), with higher education levels associated with greater intervention responsiveness. Baseline anxiety severity also significantly influenced treatment response, with moderate baseline anxiety levels (STAI scores 40–55) showing optimal intervention benefits compared to mild (STAI < 40) or severe (STAI > 55) anxiety presentations (interaction p = 0.008).

Discussion

Summary of principal findings

This quasi-experimental before-and-after study with propensity score matching evaluated the association between a HIS-based psychological nursing intervention model and maternal-neonatal outcomes among 568 cesarean section patients. The intervention was associated with substantial improvements in maternal psychological well-being, with anxiety scores decreasing by 27% and depression scores by 41% compared to standard care. These psychological improvements were accompanied by clinically meaningful reductions in operative duration (6.6 min), blood loss (57 mL), and hospital stay (0.9 days), along with decreased complication rates. Neonatal outcomes also showed improvements, with higher Apgar scores and reduced asphyxia rates in the intervention group. The effect sizes for psychological outcomes were large (Cohen’s d: 0.89–1.74), and the number needed to treat for achieving clinically significant anxiety reduction was 1.8, suggesting strong clinical impact.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating the beneficial effects of psychological interventions during the perioperative period. A systematic review by Dennis and Hodnett found that psychosocial and psychological interventions can reduce postpartum depression risk by approximately 40–50%, which aligns with our observed 68% reduction in postpartum depression rates. However, our study extends this evidence by demonstrating benefits within a technology-enhanced care delivery model integrated into routine hospital workflows. The magnitude of anxiety reduction observed in our study (STAI score decrease of 13.1 points) exceeds that reported in many previous trials of perioperative psychological interventions, which typically show reductions of 5–8 points. This enhanced effect may be attributable to the systematic, standardized, and continuous nature of HIS-facilitated care, which ensures consistent intervention delivery and enables real-time monitoring and adjustment based on patient needs.

The association between psychological interventions and improved surgical outcomes, including reduced operative time and blood loss, has biological plausibility. Maternal anxiety and stress activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to elevated cortisol and catecholamine levels that can increase muscular tension, blood pressure variability, and pain perception. By reducing preoperative anxiety, psychological interventions may facilitate improved patient cooperation during surgery, reduced muscular guarding, and enhanced physiological stability, thereby optimizing surgical conditions. Previous studies have reported similar findings, with preoperative anxiety reduction associated with 10–15% decreases in operative complications and improved recovery trajectories.

The observed improvements in neonatal outcomes, particularly Apgar scores and asphyxia rates, are consistent with the well-established effects of maternal stress hormones on fetal well-being. Elevated maternal cortisol can cross the placental barrier and influence fetal stress response systems, potentially affecting neonatal adaptation at birth. Our findings suggest that reducing maternal psychological distress may have downstream benefits for neonatal outcomes, although the mechanisms require further elucidation through studies incorporating biochemical markers such as cord blood cortisol levels and inflammatory markers.

Clinical and theoretical implications

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the integration of systematic psychological assessment and support protocols as standard components of perioperative care for cesarean section patients. The HIS-based delivery model offers several advantages over traditional approaches, including standardized risk assessment, automated care pathways that ensure consistent intervention delivery, real-time monitoring enabling early identification of deteriorating psychological status, and quality assurance mechanisms through electronic documentation and audit trails. Healthcare administrators should consider investing in information system enhancements to facilitate psychological care integration, as the demonstrated benefits (reduced complications, shorter hospital stays, improved patient satisfaction) may offset implementation costs.

From a theoretical standpoint, our findings support the application of stress-coping theory and self-efficacy theory to perioperative nursing care. The observed improvements in maternal self-efficacy, coping strategies, and birth satisfaction suggest that the intervention successfully enhanced patients’ psychological resources for managing the challenges of cesarean delivery and postpartum recovery. The temporal pattern of improvement, with rapid effects on anxiety followed by more gradual changes in depression and self-efficacy, aligns with cognitive-behavioral models suggesting that immediate anxiety reduction facilitates subsequent cognitive restructuring and skill development.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the relatively large sample size, rigorous propensity score matching to control for measured confounding, comprehensive assessment of both maternal psychological and clinical outcomes as well as neonatal outcomes, standardized intervention delivery through HIS integration ensuring fidelity, and detailed documentation of intervention components and implementation processes. The use of validated instruments (STAI, EPDS) for psychological assessment and standardized clinical outcome definitions enhances the reliability and comparability of our findings.

However, several important limitations must be acknowledged. First, the quasi-experimental before-and-after design with temporal separation between control and intervention groups limits causal inference due to potential secular trends. Changes in clinical practice, staffing, policies, or other unmeasured factors during the 18-month study period may have contributed to observed outcome differences independent of the psychological intervention. Although our sensitivity analyses, including interrupted time-series analysis examining monthly trends, did not reveal substantial temporal confounding, residual bias from secular trends cannot be completely excluded. Randomized controlled trials would provide stronger causal evidence.

Second, this single-center study may have limited generalizability to other healthcare settings with different patient populations, information system capabilities, or organizational cultures. The intervention required substantial investment in staff training and system development, which may not be feasible in resource-limited settings. Multi-center studies across diverse settings are needed to confirm external validity.

Third, despite comprehensive propensity score matching including 12 covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors remains possible. E-value analysis suggested that unmeasured confounding would need to be quite strong (risk ratio > 3.5) to fully explain away the observed associations, but factors such as patient motivation, social support quality, and healthcare provider communication skills were not fully captured. Additionally, while we adjusted for surgeon experience, anesthesia type, and surgery timing, other provider-level factors such as individual surgeon technique or team dynamics may have influenced outcomes.

Fourth, the measurement of some outcomes, particularly Apgar scores, involves subjective assessment that could be influenced by assessor awareness of group assignment, introducing detection bias. Although Apgar scoring was performed by neonatologists not directly involved in the intervention, we cannot confirm complete blinding. Future studies should incorporate objective biochemical measures such as cord blood pH and lactate levels alongside subjective assessments.

Fifth, attrition bias may have affected our 6-week follow-up results, with 8.8% loss to follow-up. Although participants lost to follow-up did not differ significantly from those completing the study in baseline characteristics, differential attrition based on unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Our sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation suggested minimal impact, but this assumption is untestable.

Sixth, the intervention was multi-component, making it difficult to isolate which specific elements (assessment, counseling, education, relaxation training) drove the observed benefits. Future dismantling studies could identify the most critical components to optimize resource allocation.

Finally, this study did not collect data on biochemical stress markers (cortisol, inflammatory cytokines) or long-term outcomes beyond 6 weeks postpartum. Incorporation of these measures in future research would strengthen mechanistic understanding and clarify the persistence of intervention benefits.

Implications for future research

Several research priorities emerge from this work. First, randomized controlled trials with concurrent control groups are needed to establish causal relationships while eliminating secular trend bias. Multi-center trials across diverse healthcare settings would enhance generalizability and identify contextual factors moderating intervention effectiveness.

Second, studies incorporating biochemical markers (cortisol, inflammatory cytokines, oxytocin) would elucidate the physiological pathways linking psychological interventions to clinical outcomes and identify biomarkers predicting intervention response.

Third, longer follow-up extending through the first postpartum year would assess the persistence of psychological benefits and potential impacts on subsequent pregnancies, parenting quality, and child development.

Fourth, cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to guide healthcare policy and resource allocation decisions. While our findings suggest that reduced complications and shortened hospital stays may offset intervention costs, formal economic evaluations incorporating implementation costs, training expenses, and long-term healthcare utilization are required.

Fifth, research into optimal intervention timing, intensity, and tailoring strategies could enhance efficiency. Machine learning approaches could identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from intensive versus standard interventions, enabling precision medicine approaches.

Sixth, implementation science studies examining barriers and facilitators to HIS-based psychological care adoption in real-world settings would support broader dissemination. Understanding organizational, technological, and cultural factors influencing implementation success would inform scale-up strategies.

Finally, comparative effectiveness research evaluating different psychological intervention modalities (cognitive-behavioral therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, supportive counseling) and delivery formats (individual vs. group, in-person vs. digital) would optimize intervention design.

Practical recommendations

Based on these findings, we offer several recommendations for clinical practice and healthcare policy. Healthcare institutions should consider implementing systematic psychological screening for cesarean section patients using validated instruments, with risk stratification to identify patients requiring intensified support. Hospital information systems should be enhanced to facilitate psychological assessment documentation, automated risk alerts, standardized intervention protocols, and outcome tracking. Nursing education programs should incorporate training in cognitive-behavioral techniques, anxiety management, and psychological assessment for obstetric populations. Interdisciplinary collaboration between obstetric nurses, psychiatric nurses, obstetricians, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists should be fostered to provide comprehensive perioperative psychological support. Quality improvement initiatives should be initiated to pilot test HIS-based psychological care models with iterative refinement based on implementation data and patient feedback. Healthcare policymakers should consider mandating psychological assessment and support protocols as quality indicators for obstetric care, with potential linkage to hospital accreditation and reimbursement structures. Finally, patient education materials should be developed to raise awareness about the importance of perioperative psychological well-being and available support resources.

Conclusion

This quasi-experimental study provides evidence that Hospital Information System-based psychological nursing intervention models are significantly associated with improvements in both maternal and neonatal outcomes during the cesarean section perioperative period. The comprehensive analysis of 568 participants demonstrated substantial reductions in maternal anxiety and depression levels, with corresponding improvements in clinical parameters including reduced operative duration, decreased blood loss, shorter hospital stays, and lower complication rates. The intervention was also associated with improvements in neonatal outcomes, including enhanced Apgar scores and reduced asphyxia rates.

The integration of psychological assessment protocols within routine hospital information systems enables systematic identification of high-risk patients and standardized implementation of evidence-based interventions, addressing a critical gap in current perioperative care practices. The HIS-based intervention model provides a scalable framework that can be adapted across different healthcare settings while maintaining standardization and quality assurance mechanisms. The quantified clinical benefits, including a number needed to treat of 1.8 for anxiety reduction and significant improvements in neonatal outcomes, provide healthcare administrators and policymakers with evidence supporting investment in comprehensive psychological support programs for cesarean section patients.

However, several important caveats must be considered when interpreting these findings. The quasi-experimental design with temporal separation between groups limits causal inference due to potential secular trends in clinical practice. The single-center setting may restrict generalizability to institutions with different patient populations or information system capabilities. Selection bias may have influenced outcomes, as the analysis included only patients with complete psychological assessment data. Additionally, unmeasured confounding from factors not captured in propensity score matching (such as provider communication quality and patient motivation) may partially explain observed associations.

Future research should prioritize prospective randomized controlled trials to confirm causal relationships between psychological interventions and clinical outcomes while controlling for temporal confounding. Multi-center studies across diverse healthcare settings would enhance external validity and support broader implementation recommendations. Investigation of biochemical pathways linking psychological status to clinical outcomes, optimal intervention timing and intensity, cost-effectiveness, and long-term follow-up examining persistence of benefits would further advance this field. Development of machine learning algorithms for precision targeting of interventions and implementation science studies examining real-world adoption barriers would facilitate translation of these findings into routine practice.

These findings suggest that systematic psychological nursing interventions may be beneficial as standard care for cesarean section patients. Healthcare policymakers should consider incorporating psychological assessment and support protocols within obstetric care standards, while nursing education programs should integrate training in technology-enhanced psychological intervention delivery. The demonstrated associations between HIS-based psychological care and improved maternal-neonatal outcomes justify continued investment in hospital information system enhancements that facilitate comprehensive, patient-centered perioperative care. However, definitive practice recommendations await confirmation through randomized controlled trials addressing the limitations of the current study.
