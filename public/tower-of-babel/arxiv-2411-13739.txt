Conditional t t -independent spectral gap for random quantum circuits and implications for t t -design depths
James Allen, Daniel Belkin, Bryan K. Clark

Abstract
A fundamental question is understanding the rate at which random quantum circuits converge to the Haar measure. One quantity which is important in establishing this rate is the spectral gap of a random quantum ensemble. In this work we establish a new bound on the spectral gap of the t t -th moment of a one-dimensional brickwork architecture on N N qudits. This bound is independent of both t t and N N , provided t t does not exceed the qudit dimension q q . We also show that the bound is nearly optimal. The improved spectral gaps gives large improvements to the constant factors in known results on the approximate t t -design depths of the 1D brickwork, of generic circuit architectures, and of specially-constructed architectures which scramble in depth O ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) O(\log N) . We moreover show that the spectral gap gives the dominant ϵ \epsilon -dependence of the t-design depth at small- ϵ \epsilon . Our spectral gap bound is obtained by bounding the N N -site 1D brickwork architecture by the spectra of 3-site operators. We then exploit a block-triangular hierarchy and a global symmetry in these operators in order to efficiently bound them. The technical methods used are a qualitatively different approach for bounding spectral gaps and and have little in common with previous techniques.

I.3
Proof overview

The bulk of this work is devoted to proving Theorem
1
. The first phase is a reduction from the brickwork architecture to a family of three-site operator. We begin by relating the brickwork architecture to the staircase architecture (Figure
3
). We then define an orthogonal decomposition of the vector space into subspaces. This corresponds to a specific block-matrix decomposition of the transfer matrix. Next, we show that the spectral gap of the architecture can be bounded in terms of the norms of each block in this matrix. We complete this phase by relating each block norms to the spectral gap of an effective three-site operator.

The second phase of our proof is concerned with bounds on these three-site spectral gaps. We show that it suffices to restrict these operators to a certain subspace, which we term the
complete derangement space
. Furthermore, the representation theory of the symmetric group allows us to decompose each of these subspaces into isotypic components.

At this point our bounds split into cases. The general strategy is to obtain analytic bounds when
q
or
t
is sufficiently large and numerically bound the spectral gap for smaller
t
. We use multiple numerical bounds depending on the size of
t
, with larger
t
values using looser bounds that are easier to compute, while smaller
t
values use tighter bounds that require more detailed analysis.

IV.1

Definition 27

A state

is a complete derangement is a basis for the isotypic component of
\vecspan\left(\mathfrak{D}_{3}^{(t)}\right)\cap\image(\Pi_{m})
corresponding to
\nu
.

Note that
\ket{e_{\nu}^{ij,\sigma}}\in\vecspan\left(\mathfrak{D}_{3}^{(t)}\right)
if and only if
\ket{I,I,\sigma}
is a complete derangement. The fact that
\ket{e_{\nu}^{ij,\sigma}}
lies in the isotypic component corresponding to
\nu
follows from the Schur orthogonality relations, while the fact that they span the space follows from the form of the canonical idempotent

\displaystyle P_{\nu}=\frac{\chi_{\nu}(1)}{t!}\sum_{\tau}\chi_{\nu}(\tau^{-1})\tau_{R}

(62)

A full proof may be found in Appendix
B.4
.

Using this basis, we can obtain a convenient factorization of the gate operator.

Lemma 36
.

In the basis defined by Lemma
35
,

\displaystyle K_{m}\ket{e_{\nu}^{ij,\sigma}}=\sum_{k,\xi\in\mathbb{D}_{t}}M_{m,\xi,\sigma}^{\nu,kj}\ket{e_{\nu}^{ik,\xi}}

(63)

Note that
\nu
,
i
, and
j
indices are not implicitly summed over. Furthermore,

\displaystyle M^{\nu}_{m}=D_{\nu}^{-1}W(Q_{1}Q_{2})D(Q_{2})C(Q_{1})D_{\nu}W(Q_{2}Q_{3})D(Q_{2})C(Q_{3})

(64)

where

\displaystyle Q_{1}=q^{m}

(65)

\displaystyle Q_{2}=Q_{3}=q

(66)

\displaystyle W(d)_{\sigma,\tau}^{ij}=\text{Wg}(\sigma^{-1}\tau,d)\delta_{ij}

(67)

\displaystyle D(d)_{\sigma,\tau}^{ij}=d^{-|\sigma|}\delta_{\sigma,\tau}\delta_{ij}

(68)

\displaystyle C(d)_{\sigma,\tau}^{ij}=d^{-|\sigma^{-1}\tau|}\delta_{ij}

(69)

\displaystyle D^{ij}_{\nu,\sigma,\tau}=V_{\nu}(\sigma)^{ij}\delta_{\sigma,\tau}

(70)

and the product above is matrix multiplication.

Proof.

Consider the action of
K_{m}=\Pi_{m}G_{m}\Pi_{m}
on one of the basis states. Since
\Pi_{m}
and
G_{m}
commute with right-action,

\displaystyle\Pi_{m}G_{m}\Pi_{m}\ket{e_{\nu}^{ij,\sigma}}=R_{\nu}^{ij}\Pi_{m}G_{m}\Pi_{m}\ket{I,\sigma}

(71)

We can compute

\displaystyle\Pi_{m}G_{m}\Pi_{m}\ket{I,I,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{m}G_{m}\ket{I,I,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{m}\sum_{\tau,\pi\in S_{d}st}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}\ket{I,\tau,\tau}

Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\Pi_{m}\ket{I,\tau,\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega\in S_{t}}\text{Wg}(\xi^{-1}\omega,Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega|}Q_{2}^{-|\tau^{-1}\omega|}\ket{\xi,\xi,\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega\in S_{t}}\text{Wg}(\xi^{-1}\omega,Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega|}Q_{2}^{-|\tau^{-1}\omega|}\xi_{R}\ket{I,I,\tau\xi^{-1}}

Combining these, we see

\displaystyle K_{m}\ket{I,I,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi}\text{Wg}(\xi^{-1}\omega,Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega|}Q_{2}^{-|\tau^{-1}\omega|}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}\xi_{R}\ket{I,I,\tau\xi^{-1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\xi\omega,Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega|}Q_{2}^{-|\tau^{-1}\omega|}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}(\xi^{-1}\tau)_{R}\ket{I,I,\xi}\quad(\xi\rightarrow\xi^{-1}\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi}\text{Wg}(\xi\omega^{-1},Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega^{-1}\tau|}Q_{2}^{-|\omega|}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}(\xi^{-1}\tau)_{R}\ket{I,I,\xi}\quad(\omega\rightarrow\omega^{-1}\tau)

(72)

In Lemma
57
, we will show

\displaystyle R_{\nu}^{ij}\sigma_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}R_{\nu}^{ik}V_{\nu}(\sigma)^{kj}

(73)

Applying this result,

\displaystyle K_{m}\ket{e_{\nu}^{ij,\sigma}}

\displaystyle=R_{\nu}^{ij}K_{m}\ket{I,I,\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi}\text{Wg}(\xi\omega^{-1},Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega^{-1}\tau|}Q_{2}^{-|\omega|}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}R_{\nu}^{ij}(\xi^{-1}\tau)_{R}\ket{I,I,\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi}\text{Wg}(\xi\omega^{-1},Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega^{-1}\tau|}Q_{2}^{-|\omega|}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}\sum_{k}V_{\nu}(\xi^{-1}\tau)^{kj}R_{\nu}^{ik}\ket{I,I,\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi}\text{Wg}(\xi\omega^{-1},Q_{1}Q_{2})Q_{1}^{-|\omega^{-1}\tau|}Q_{2}^{-|\omega|}\text{Wg}(\tau^{-1}\pi,Q_{2}Q_{3})Q_{2}^{-|\pi|}Q_{3}^{-|\sigma^{-1}\pi|}\sum_{k,\ell}V_{\nu}(\xi^{-1})^{k\ell}V_{\nu}(\tau)^{\ell j}R_{\nu}^{ik}\ket{I,I,\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi,\omega,\tau,\pi,k,\ell}\left[(D_{\nu}^{-1})^{k\ell}_{\xi\xi}W(Q_{1}Q_{2})^{\ell\ell}_{\xi\omega}D(Q_{2})^{\ell\ell}_{\omega\omega}C(Q_{1})^{\ell\ell}_{\omega\tau}(D_{\nu})^{\ell j}_{\tau\tau}W(Q_{2}Q_{3})^{jj}_{\tau\pi}D(Q_{2})^{jj}_{\pi\pi}C(Q_{3})^{jj}_{\pi\sigma}\right]R_{\nu}^{ik}\ket{I,I,\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}M^{\nu,kj}_{m,\xi\sigma}\ket{e_{\nu}^{ik,\xi}}

(74)

∎

We can reduce the isotypical subspace even further by considering global left-action symmetries as well as global right-action. In Appendix

K_{m}
in this subspace. For now, it is sufficient to prove Theorem

V.2

We will begin by computing the subleading eigenvalue of

Theorem 37
.

When

\lambda_{m}=\frac{q^{2}(q^{2m}-1)}{(q^{2}+1)(q^{2m+2}-1)}\leq\frac{1}{1+q^{2}}

Proof.

The only partitions of
2

(1,1)
and
2

transposition. These are referred to below as

and

In the trivial irrep, we have

W(d)=\frac{1}{1-d^{-2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1/d\\
-1/d&1\end{bmatrix}

D(d)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1/d\end{bmatrix}

C(d)=\begin{bmatrix}1&1/d\\
1/d&1\end{bmatrix}

D_{(2)}^{11}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle M^{(2)}

\displaystyle=D_{(2)}^{-1}W(q^{m+1})D(q)C(q^{m})D_{(2)}W(q^{2})D(q)C(q)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&\frac{q(q^{m}+1)(q^{m+2}-1)}{(q^{2}+1)(q^{2m+2}-1)}\\

Since this matrix is upper-triangular, its eigenvalues are the diagonal elements. The unit eigenvalue corresponds of course to the uniform
\ket{II}+\ket{SS}

In the alternating case,

D_{(1,1)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\\
\end{bmatrix}

but because the minus sign appears twice, it cancels out and we find

We may now compute

\displaystyle\sup_{m}\frac{q^{2}(q^{2m}-1)}{(q^{2}+1)(q^{2m+2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}}{q^{2}+1}\sup_{m}\frac{q^{2m}-1}{q^{2m+2}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}}{q^{2}+1}\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{q^{2m}-1}{q^{2m+2}-1}

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{2}+1}

(79)

where in the second line we use the fact that
q>1
.
∎

VI.1

We saw in Lemma

Note that this norm is not equivalent to the norm inherited from the inner product on the original Hilbert space.

The first term in the product is of the form

.

For

and

\displaystyle||W(Q_{1}Q_{2})||_{\text{basis}}\leq e

(86)

A proof may be found in Appendix
B.5

Lemma 43

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{t}^{(\mathfrak{D})}}\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\sigma^{-1}\tau|+|\tau|}

\displaystyle||D(Q_{2})C(Q_{1})||_{\mathfrak{D},\text{basis}}\leq\sqrt{d_{t}(q^{-2})}

The proof involves using the Frobenius norm to upper-bound the operator norm of

Lemma 44

When

\displaystyle d_{t}(q^{-2})\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}e^{\frac{1}{2t^{2}}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3}{t}\right)^{t}+\left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{t}}\right)^{t}\right]\left(\frac{t}{q}\right)^{t-2}\frac{1}{q^{2}}

The above bounds cover the case

With respect to the basis norm,

is a unitary operator.
It follows that

\displaystyle||M_{m}||_{\text{basis}}\leq||W(Q_{1}Q_{2})D(Q_{2})C(Q_{1})||_{\text{basis}}||W(Q_{2}Q_{3})D(Q_{2})C(Q_{3})||_{\text{basis}}=||H(Q_{1},Q_{2})||_{\text{basis}}||H(Q_{3},Q_{2})||_{\text{basis}}

where we define the half-operator

\displaystyle H(Q_{1},Q_{2})=W(Q_{1}Q_{2})D(Q_{2})C(Q_{1})

\displaystyle\text{Wg}\left(\sigma^{-1}\tau,d\right)=\frac{g_{\sigma^{-1}\tau}(d^{-1})}{f_{t}(d^{-1})}

Lemma 46

Let

\displaystyle\text{Wg}\left(\sigma^{-1}\tau,d\right)=\frac{g_{\sigma^{-1}\tau}(d^{-1})}{f_{t}(d^{-1})}

\displaystyle|H_{\sigma\tau}(Q_{1},Q_{2})|\leq\left(\frac{t}{q}\right)^{\left\lceil\frac{t}{2}\right\rceil}\overline{H}(t)_{\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle h^{2}(t)\leq\frac{t^{2\left\lceil\frac{t}{2}\right\rceil}}{1+t^{2}}

VII
Conclusion

We have established a nearly-optimal
t
-independent,
N
-independent lower bound on the spectral gap when
t\leq q
. This implies improvements to known bounds on approximate
t
-design depths of various architectures, including the brickwork
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, arbitrary well-connected architectures
[
14
]
, and specific rapidly-scrambling architectures
[
12
,
13
]
. Although the asymptotics of these results do not change, our improvements to the constant factors are often dramatic. The scaling with
t
is also improved for the regime
t\leq q<6t^{2}
in which the bound of ref.
17
did not apply. We also show that the resulting bound on the 1D brickwork
t
-design depth has nearly optimal small-
\epsilon
behavior.

Our first technical innovation is our strategy to relate the 1D staircase SEV to a sequence of 3-site operators. This differs from previously established methods, and we believe it has the potential to produce even stronger results with further work. As a primary example, we would like to extend our bound to the case
t>q
. From numerical evidence (see Appendix
C.2
), it appears that the eigenvalues of
K_{m}
remain bounded by
\frac{1}{q^{2}+1}
even when
t>q
, with the sole exception of the case
t=4
,
q=2
,
m=2
. There are, roughly speaking, two obstacles to extending our proof to the
t>q
regime. The first is the degeneracy of the permutation basis at
t>q
. This is partially resolved by Theorem
34
, but the intersection space
\mathcal{I}
is difficult to work with. Second, we bound
||K||
by factorization in terms of
||W||\cdot||DC||
. But for
t>q
both
||W||
and
||DC||
grow large even as
||WDC||
remains small, so bounds by factorization cannot succeed.

We would also like to extend our bounds to the regime of non-integer
q<t
. In this setting, the metric acquires negative eigenvalues, so additional techniques may be needed. Bounding these regions would allow us to extend the depth bounds on one-dimensional systems to all architectures through ref.
14
.

Recent works have shown that architectures very similar to the 1D brickwork form
\epsilon
-approximate
t
-designs in depth
O(\log N)
. However, a the best known scaling for the 1D brickwork itself remains
O(N)
. In this work we use our reduction to
3
-site operators only to bound the spectral gap of the 1D brickwork, but the implied block-triangular structure may be helpful for other analyses of the behavior of the moment operator. The deranged-subspace and irreducible-representation structures of both the original brickwork and of the three-site operators could provide additional insight to this behavior as well. If these decompositions can be used to study the entire eigenspectrum, it could lead to a better understanding of the true
N
-scaling of the 1D brickwork approximate
t
-design depth.

Appendix A
Relationship between spectral gap and
t
-design depth

This material is generally standard, but we include it here for completeness.

Definition 47
.

An ensemble
\varepsilon
of random quantum circuits is an additive-error
\epsilon
-approximate
t
-design if the diamond norm distance between its
t
-fold channel and the global Haar ensemble’s
t
-fold channel is less than
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle||\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}-\Phi_{\text{Haar}}^{(t)}||_{\diamond}\leq\epsilon

(99)

Definition 48
.

The frame potential
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}
of a random quantum circuit ensemble
\varepsilon
is a
4t
-th moment of its unitaries:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=\int_{\varepsilon^{\otimes 2}}|\text{tr}(U_{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}V_{\varepsilon})|^{2t}\text{d}U_{\varepsilon}\text{d}V_{\varepsilon}

(100)

Lemma 49
.

The diamond norm distance between two
t
-fold channels is bounded by the difference in their frame potentials:

\displaystyle||\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}-\Phi_{\text{Haar}}^{(t)}||_{\diamond}^{2}\leq q^{2Nt}\left(\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}-\mathcal{F}_{\text{Haar}}^{(t)}\right)

(101)

The frame potential is the Frobenius norm of the moment operator,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}=\text{tr}\left(\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{(t)\dagger}\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{(t)}\right)

(102)

Let
L_{O}
and
L_{E}
refer to the “even” and “odd” layers of the 1D brickwork architecture, respectively. The transfer matrix associated with a single period of the architecture is then
T_{1DB}=L_{O}L_{E}
.

Lemma 50
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\ell)
be the frame potential of the
\ell
-layer 1D brickwork architecture,
\ell\geq 2
. Then

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\ell)=\text{tr}\left((L_{E}L_{O}L_{E})^{\ell-1}\right)

Proof.

The first observation is that

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left((L_{E}L_{O}L_{E})^{\ell-1}\right)=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E}^{2})^{\ell-1}\right)=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\ell-1}\right)

(103)

Next, suppose
\ell
is even. Then the moment operator is
\Phi_{\epsilon}=(L_{O}L_{E})^{\ell/2}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\ell)=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\ell/2}(L_{O}L_{E})^{\dagger\ell/2}\right)

(104)

Now,
L_{O}
is a tensor product of
G
operators, each of which is an orthogonal projection operator. It follows that
L_{O}
is an orthogonal projection operator, which implies both
L_{O}^{\dagger}=L_{O}
and
L_{O}^{2}=L_{O}
. The same goes for
L_{E}
. Using these, we can see

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left(\Phi_{\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell}{2}}(L_{E}L_{O})^{\frac{\ell}{2}}\right)

(105)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell}{2}}L_{E}(L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell}{2}-1}L_{O}\right)

(106)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell}{2}}(L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell}{2}-1}\right)

(107)

where in the third line we have used the cylic property of the trace and the fact that
L_{O}L_{O}=L_{O}
.

Now suppose
\ell
is odd. Then
\Phi_{\epsilon}^{t}=T_{1DB}^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}L_{O}
, and we can compute

\displaystyle\text{tr}\left(\Phi_{\epsilon}\Phi_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}L_{O}L_{O}(L_{E}L_{O})^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}\right)

(108)

\displaystyle=\text{tr}\left((L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}L_{O}^{2}L_{E}(L_{O}L_{E})^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}-1}L_{O}\right)

(109)

B.1
Matrix bound from block decomposition (Theorem

)

Theorem 56
.

Let
P_{i}
be a complete orthogonal set of projection operators. Let
T
be a matrix. Define
A_{ij}=||P_{i}TP_{j}||_{\infty}
. The largest eigenvalue of
A
gives an upper bound on the largest eigenvalue of
T
.

Proof.

Let

be an eigenvector of
T
with eigenvalue
c
. Define

x_{i}=||P_{i}v||

y_{i}=||P_{i}Tv||

z_{i}=\sum_{j}A_{ij}x_{i}

and let

be the largest eigenvalue of
A
.
We must have
y_{i}=cx_{i}
. By assumption
y_{i}\leq z_{i}
, so

. In fact, since this holds independently for every

,

\displaystyle c\leq\min_{i}\frac{z_{i}}{x_{i}}

(139)

We will now show that

Define

We wish to show that
\max_{\vec{u}}f(\vec{u})\leq a
. We can compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f(\vec{x})}{\partial x_{i}}=\begin{cases}\min_{j:\frac{z_{j}}{x_{j}}=f(\vec{x})}A_{ij}&\frac{z_{i}}{x_{i}}>f(\vec{x})\\

(140)

(Note that this is only a subgradient, as
f
is not differentiable everywhere.) A maximum of

can occur only if every element of the subgradient is nonpositive. Since
A_{ij}\geq 0
, this happens only when
\frac{z_{i}}{x_{i}}=f(\vec{x})
for every
i
. In other words,
f
can be maximized only when

, which implies that
\vec{x}
is an eigenvector of
A
with eigenvalue
f(\vec{x})
. It follows that
f(\vec{x})\leq a
. We saw earlier that

, from which we obtain

.
∎

The derangement polynomial can be expressed as the following sum:

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=(-1)^{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t}(-1)^{k}\binom{t}{k}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{t}\big(1+(\ell-1)x^{2}\big)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\big(1+(\ell-1)x\big)^{2}

(216)

Proof.

We start with the original expression of the derangement polynomial:

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{t}^{(\mathfrak{D})}}\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\sigma^{-1}\tau|+|\tau|}

(217)

We consider the more general sum

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)\equiv\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\tau|}

(218)

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)^{2}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{t}}\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\sigma^{-1}\tau|+|\tau|}

(219)

Since the term in the sum is invariant upon simultaneous conjugation of
\sigma
and
\tau
,
\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\sigma^{-1}\tau|+|\tau|}
only depends on the conjugacy class of
\sigma
. Therefore, we can separate the sum over derangements
D_{k}
:

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{t}{k}\sum_{\sigma\in D_{k}}\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\sigma^{-1}\tau|+|\tau|}

For
\sigma\in D_{k}
,
\tau\in S_{t}
, we can use Lemma
58
to split
\tau=\rho\pi
,
\rho\in S_{k}
with
\pi
being a minimal representative, and get

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{t}{k}\sum_{\pi\in S_{t}:S_{k}}x^{2|\pi|}\sum_{\sigma\in D_{k}}\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}x^{|\sigma^{-1}\rho|+|\rho|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{t}{k}d_{k}(x)\sum_{\pi\in S_{t}:S_{k}}x^{2|\pi|}

(220)

Moreover, for any specific
k
,

\displaystyle c_{t}(x^{2})=\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{2|\tau|}=\sum_{\pi\in S_{t}:S_{k}}x^{2|\pi|}\sum_{\rho\in S_{k}}x^{2|\rho|}

(221)

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in S_{t}:S_{k}}x^{2|\pi|}=\frac{c_{t}(x^{2})}{c_{k}(x^{2})}

(222)

So

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\binom{t}{k}\frac{c_{t}(x^{2})}{c_{k}(x^{2})}d_{k}(x)

(223)

A system of linear equations from
d_{k}(x)
to
c_{k}(x)^{2}
which we can invert as

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{t}(-1)^{t-k}\binom{t}{k}\frac{c_{t}(x^{2})}{c_{k}(x^{2})}c_{k}(x)^{2}

(224)

From the Jucys-Murphy decomposition of permutations in
S_{k}
,

\displaystyle c_{k}(x)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}x^{|\sigma|}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{t}\big(1+(\ell-1)x\big)

(225)

So we can also write

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=(-1)^{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t}(-1)^{k}\binom{t}{k}\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{t}\big(1+(\ell-1)x^{2}\big)\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\big(1+(\ell-1)x\big)^{2}

(226)

∎

Lemma 61
.

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=c_{t}(x^{2})\hat{e}_{0}^{T}\left(\Delta+TG(x)\right)^{t}\vec{u}

(234)

where
\{\hat{e}_{i}\}
is the standard basis for
\mathbb{R}^{t+1}
and we have defined

\displaystyle\vec{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t+1}\hat{e}_{i}

(235)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\hat{e}_{i+1}\hat{e}_{i}^{T}

(236)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=T-I

(237)

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\frac{\big(1+jx\big)^{2}}{1+jx^{2}}-1\right)\hat{e}_{j}\hat{e}_{j}^{T}

(238)

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau\in S_{t}}x^{|\tau|}

(239)

Proof.

Define
g_{\ell}(x)=G_{\ell\ell}(x)
. We start with Lemma
60
, which we express as

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{t}(x^{2})(-1)^{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t}(-1)^{t-k}\binom{t}{k}\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(1+g_{\ell}(x))

(240)

Note that
g_{\ell}(0)=0
. Ignoring the prefactor
c_{t}(x^{2})
for now, this expression can be described as a weighted sum over paths. Each path occurs over
t
total time steps. At each time step we either move forward a space, acquiring weight
\frac{(1+\ell x)^{2}}{1+\ell x^{2}}=1+g_{\ell}(x)
if we are originally at position
\ell
, or we do not advance, acquiring a weight of
-1
. Any path that advances
k
steps in total therefore acquires a weight
(-1)^{t-k}\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}(1+g_{\ell}(x))
, and there are
\binom{t}{k}
such paths. At the end of this process, we sum over all weights of all possible paths, regardless of the final position. This weighted path sum can therefore be represented as a matrix element

\displaystyle u^{T}M^{t}\hat{e}_{0}

(241)

where
M
is the transfer matrix representing all possible actions at a single time step. The decision to advance and acquire a weight
(1+g_{\ell}(x))
corresponds to the transfer matrix
T+TG(x)
, while the decision to remain still and acquire a weight
(-1)
corresponds to
-I
. Therefore
M=T+TG-I
, so

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{t}(x^{2})u^{T}\big[T+TG(x)-I\big]^{t}\hat{e}_{0}

\displaystyle=c_{t}(x^{2})u^{T}\big[\Delta+TG\big]^{t}\hat{e}_{0}

-
∎

Lemma 62
.

Define an operator
\delta
which maps diagonal matrices to diagonal matrices by

\displaystyle\delta M=\sum_{i}(M_{i,i}-M_{i-1,i-1})e_{i}e_{i}^{T}

(242)

We have the following bound on the derangement polynomial:

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)\leq c_{t}(x^{2})\sum_{A}\binom{t}{A}A^{t-A}\max_{\lambda}\left|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{A}T^{\lambda_{i}+1}\delta^{\lambda_{i}}G(x)\hat{e}_{0}\right|.

(243)

Proof.

From Lemma
61
we have

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=c_{t}(x^{2})u^{T}\left(\Delta+TG(x)\right)^{t}\hat{e}_{0}

(244)

The matrix power
\big[\Delta+TG(x)\big]^{t}
can be expanded into
2^{t}
terms. Our general claim is that each of these terms decays exponentially with
t
. To demonstrate this claim, we start with the following observations:

•

\Delta u=0

•

\Delta
follows a pseudo product rule:
\Delta AB=[\Delta,A]B+A\Delta B

•

[\Delta,T^{k}D]=[T-I,T^{k}D]=T^{k+1}\delta D
, where
D_{ij}=\delta_{ij}d_{j}
is any diagonal matrix and
\delta D=\delta_{ij}(d_{j}-d_{j-1})
is its finite difference (note that this is also a diagonal matrix).

So

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\prod_{i}T^{k_{i}}D_{i}\right)\hat{e}_{0}=\sum_{j}\prod_{i}\left[(1-\delta_{ij})T^{k_{i}}D_{i}+\delta_{ij}T^{k_{i}+1}\delta D_{i}\right]\hat{e}_{0}

(245)

for any sequence of integers
k_{i}
and diagonal matrices
D_{i}
. That is, applying
\Delta
to a product of
\ell
factors
T^{k_{i}}D_{i}
gives us a sum of
\ell
products, where each term in the sum is the original product with one of the factors replaced by
T^{k_{i}+1}\delta D_{i}
. This lets us impose the inequality

\displaystyle\big|\hat{e}_{m}^{T}\Delta\left(\prod_{i=0}^{\ell}T^{k_{i}}D_{i}\right)\hat{e}_{0}\big|\leq\ell\max_{j}\big|\hat{e}_{m}^{T}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell}\left[(1-\delta_{ij})T^{k_{i}}D_{i}+\delta_{ij}T^{k_{i}+1}\delta D_{i}\right]\hat{e}_{0}\big|

(246)

Now we generalize the situation to a sequence of
t
operators which could either be
\Delta
or
TG
. Specifically, we have two vectors
a\in\{0...t\}^{m},b\in\{0...t\}^{m}
for some integer
m
such that
\sum_{i}a_{i}+b_{i}=t
. Let’s also say that
\sum_{i}a_{i}\equiv A
. We want to bound the product

\displaystyle\big|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Delta^{b_{i}}(TG)^{a_{i}}\hat{e}_{0}\big|

(247)

By imposing inequality (
246
) on each
\Delta
individually, we get

\displaystyle\big|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Delta^{b_{i}}(TG)^{a_{i}}\hat{e}_{0}\big|\leq\left(\prod_{k=1}^{m}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\big)^{b_{i}}\right)\max_{\lambda}\big|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{A}T^{\lambda_{i}+1}\delta^{\lambda_{i}}G(x)\hat{e}_{0}\big|

(248)

where
\lambda
is taken as any vector in
\{0...t-A\}^{A}
such that
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=t-A
. The prefactor
\prod_{k=1}^{m}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\big)^{b_{i}}
is counting the total number of terms in our sum after passing through each
\Delta
individually, and for a fixed
A
can be maximized if all the
\Delta
’s were applied at the end, i.e.
x_{1}=A,y_{1}=t-A
. This gives

\displaystyle\big|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\Delta^{b_{i}}(TG)^{a_{i}}\hat{e}_{0}\big|\leq A^{t-A}\max_{\lambda}\big|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{A}T^{\lambda_{i}+1}\delta^{\lambda_{i}}G(x)\hat{e}_{0}\big|

(249)

and therefore

\displaystyle d_{t}(x)=c_{t}(x^{2})u^{T}\left[\Delta+TG(x)\right]^{t}\hat{e}_{0}\leq c_{t}(x^{2})\sum_{A}\binom{t}{A}A^{t-A}\max_{\lambda}\big|u^{T}\prod_{i=1}^{A}T^{\lambda_{i}+1}\delta^{\lambda_{i}}G(x)\hat{e}_{0}\big|.

(250)

∎

Lemma 63
.

\displaystyle\left(\delta^{n}G(x)\right)_{ij}\leq 3x^{\frac{n+1}{2}}\delta_{ij}

(251)

where
G
is as defined in Lemma
61
and
\delta
is as defined in Lemma
62
.

Proof.

We again use the shorthand
g_{k}(x)=G_{kk}(x)
. We first write

\displaystyle\big[\delta^{n}G(x)\big]_{kk}=\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)

(252)

where for scalar-valued sequences we have defined the usual finite difference operator

\displaystyle\delta f_{k}(x)=f_{k}(x)-f_{k-1}(x)

(253)

Then

\displaystyle\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{i}g_{k-i}(x)

(254)

Let’s write

j_{x}(y)\equiv\frac{(1+y)^{2}}{1+xy}-1

\displaystyle g_{k}(x)=\frac{(1+kx)^{2}}{1+kx^{2}}-1=j_{x}(kx)

(255)

\displaystyle g_{k-i}(x)=j_{x}(kx-ix)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)\frac{(-ix)^{\ell}}{\ell!}

(256)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)\frac{(-x)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{i}i^{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)\frac{(-x)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{d^{\ell}}{dz^{\ell}}\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}(-1)^{i}e^{iz}\big|_{z=0}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)\frac{(-x)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{d^{\ell}}{dz^{\ell}}(e^{z}-1)^{n}\big|_{z=0}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{\ell=n}^{\infty}j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)\frac{(-x)^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{d^{\ell}}{dz^{\ell}}(e^{z}-1)^{n}\big|_{z=0}

In the last step we used the fact that
e^{z}-1
has no constant term in its Taylor series, so
\frac{d^{\ell}}{dz^{\ell}}(e^{z}-1)^{n}\big|_{z=0}=0
for all
\ell<n
. Also,
\frac{d^{\ell}}{dz^{\ell}}(e^{z}-1)^{n}\big|_{z=0}\leq\frac{d^{\ell}}{dz^{\ell}}(e^{z})^{n}\big|_{z=0}
(the latter has more terms in each Taylor series and all coefficients of the Taylor expansion are positive).
Therefore, in terms of magnitudes,

\displaystyle\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=n}^{\infty}|j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)|\frac{(nx)^{\ell}}{\ell!}

Next, we bound each
|j_{x}^{(\ell)}(kx)|
using the Cauchy integral theorem:

\displaystyle j_{x}^{(\ell)}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell!}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{j_{x}(z)}{(z-y)^{\ell+1}}dz

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell!}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{(1+z)^{2}}{(1+xz)(z-y)^{\ell+1}}dz

For
\ell\geq 2
, the contour integral vanishes at infinity. Of course, if we take the contour integral to infinity, we capture the extra pole at
z=-1/x
, so the residues of the two poles must sum up to zero. Therefore,

\displaystyle j_{x}^{(\ell)}(y)

\displaystyle=-\ell!\,\text{Res}\frac{(1+z)^{2}}{(1+xz)(z-y)^{\ell+1}}\big|_{z=-1/x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\ell!}{x}\frac{(1-1/x)^{2}}{(-y-1/x)^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell}\ell!x^{\ell-2}\frac{(1-x)^{2}}{(1+xy)^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle|j_{x}^{(\ell)}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\ell!\,x^{\ell-2}

For
\ell=0
,

\displaystyle j_{x}^{(0)}(y)=\frac{(1+y)^{2}}{(1+xy)}-1\leq 2y+y^{2}

(257)

and for
\ell=1
,

\displaystyle j_{x}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{2(1+y)(1+xy)-x(1+y)^{2}}{(1+xy)^{2}}\leq 2(1+y)

(258)

These inequalities hold in magnitude as well, provided
x<y<1
, which is consistent with our
x=Q^{-2}\leq t^{-2},y=kx\leq tx\leq t^{-1}
regime. Therefore, we have (where we define
\theta_{ij}=1
if
i\geq j
and 0 otherwise):

\displaystyle\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq(2kx+k^{2}x^{2})\theta_{0n}+2nx(1+kx)\theta_{1n}+x^{-2}\sum_{\ell=\max(2,n)}^{\infty}(nx^{2})^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq kx(2+kx)\theta_{0n}+2nx(1+kx)\theta_{1n}+\frac{(nx^{2})^{\max(2,n)}}{x^{2}(1-nx^{2})}

Given our initial condition
n\leq t\leq x^{-1/2}
, with
t\geq 2
, we have
\delta^{0}g_{k}(x)\leq 2.5x^{1/2}
,
\delta^{1}g_{k}(x)\leq 3x
,
\delta^{2}g_{k}(x)\leq 2/(1-1/8)x^{3/2}
, and for
n>2
,
\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)\leq 2x^{1.5n-2}\leq 2x^{(n+1)/2}
. Therefore, we always have

\displaystyle\delta^{n}g_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\leq 3x^{\frac{n+1}{2}}

∎

Appendix E
Irreps of generalized action

In the analytic part of our proof, we used the right-action symmetry of the effective 3-site operator to separate the basis into isotypical components, as described in Lemma
35
. However, the effective 3-site system also has symmetry under global left-action of any permutation, which is not an equivalent symmetry to right-action and can potentially split up the basis even further. In this section, we will generalize the right action isotypical basis of Lemma
35
to symmetries under both left and right action.
We will start with a state that lives in a right-action irrep indexed by a partition
\lambda_{R}
and a left-action irrep indexed by
\lambda_{L}
. For each conjugacy class
c
, we can select an arbitrary representative element
\sigma_{c}
, and note the centralizer
C(\sigma_{c})
with size
C_{c}
. Then, we define a basis for this isotypic component, indexed by left/right irrep indices
ij,k\ell
respectively, as well as a conjugacy class index
c
, as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}=\frac{1}{|C_{c}|}L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}|I,I,\sigma_{c}\rangle\bigg|i,j\in\{1...d_{\nu_{L}}\},k,l\in\{1...d_{\nu_{R}}\}\right\}

(267)

where as before we have used

\displaystyle R_{\nu}^{ij}=\sum_{\rho\in S_{t}}V_{\nu}(\rho^{-1})^{ij}\rho_{R}

(269)

and the analogous

\displaystyle L_{\nu}^{ij}=\sum_{\rho\in S_{t}}V_{\nu}(\rho^{-1})^{ij}\rho_{L}

(270)

Note that we are allowed to index our basis element only in terms of the conjugacy class
c
, instead of a full permutation
\sigma
, as attempting to replace
\sigma_{c}
with any other element
\tau_{c}
in the same conjugacy class is equivalent to applying the tensor

\displaystyle V_{\nu_{L}}(\pi)^{hi}V_{\nu_{R}}(\pi^{-1})^{\ell m}

(271)

where
\pi
is such that
\tau_{c}=\pi\sigma_{c}\pi^{-1}
. The above operation consists of left-action of
V_{\nu_{L}}(\pi)
to the left irrep indices, combined with right-action of
V_{\nu_{R}}(\pi^{-1})
to the right irrep indices. Therefore, it is actually a group homomorphism - we will call this operation
T(\pi)_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{hi,\ell m}
.

Each state
\ket{\gamma}=\sum_{ijk\ell c}\gamma^{ij,k\ell,c}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}
is then characterized by the tensor
\gamma^{ij,k\ell,c}
of size
d_{\lambda_{R}}^{2}d_{\lambda_{L}}^{2}N_{c}
, where
N_{c}
is the number of conjugacy classes in
S_{t}
- note that unlike the
\beta
tensor,
\gamma
is not indexed by permutations in
S_{t}
. Unlike the right-action ansatz, this is not surjective - i.e. there exists some nonzero
\gamma^{ij,k\ell,c}
tensors that yield an identically zero wavefunction (for example, if
\lambda_{L}=
Trv,
\lambda_{R}=
Alt,
\gamma^{c}=\delta_{c,I}
). This, combined with the fact that the single site metric in permutation space
g(\ket{\sigma},\ket{\tau})=q^{-|\sigma^{-1}\tau|}
becomes singular if
q<t
, means that there is a possibility of spurious
\gamma
tensors which are eigenstates of the gate but do not actually exist in the original space.

Like before, the left and right-action twirls commute with all gates, so applying the
G_{m}
gate yields

\displaystyle G_{m}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{c}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}G\ket{I,I,\sigma_{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{c}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}\sum_{\pi,\tau}\text{Wg}(\pi\tau^{-1},q^{2})q^{-|\tau|-|\tau\sigma_{c}^{-1}|}\ket{I,\pi,\pi}

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in S_{t}}f(\pi)=\sum_{a}\frac{1}{C_{a}}\sum_{\pi}f(\pi\sigma_{a}\pi^{-1}),

(272)

for conjugacy class index
a
, we can change this sum into

\displaystyle G_{m}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{c}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}\sum_{a,b}\frac{1}{C_{a}C_{b}}\sum_{\pi,\tau}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{a}\pi^{-1}\tau\sigma_{b}^{-1}\tau^{-1},q^{2})q^{-|\sigma_{b}|-|\tau\sigma_{b}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{c}^{-1}|}\pi_{L}\pi_{R}^{-1}\ket{I,\sigma_{a},\sigma_{a}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{c}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}\sum_{a,b}\frac{1}{C_{a}C_{b}}\sum_{\pi,\tau}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{a}\pi^{-1}\sigma_{b}^{-1},q^{2})q^{-|\sigma_{b}|-|\tau\sigma_{b}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{c}^{-1}|}(\tau\pi)_{L}(\tau\pi)^{-1}_{R}\ket{I,\sigma_{a},\sigma_{a}}\qquad(\pi\rightarrow\tau\pi)

We then use the commutation relations

\displaystyle L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}\sigma_{L}=V_{\nu_{L}}^{ih}(\sigma)L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}

(273)

\displaystyle R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}\sigma_{R}=R_{\nu_{L}}^{kn}V_{\nu_{R}}^{n\ell}(\sigma)

(274)

(using Einstein summation notation for all repeated irrep indices).
In fact,

\displaystyle L_{\nu_{L}}^{ij}R_{\nu_{R}}^{k\ell}\sigma_{L}\sigma_{R}^{-1}=T_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}(\sigma)^{ih,n\ell}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{L}}^{kn}

(275)

Therefore

\displaystyle G_{m}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{c}}\sum_{a,b}\frac{1}{C_{a}C_{b}}\sum_{\pi,\tau}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{a}\pi^{-1}\sigma_{b}^{-1},q^{2})q^{-|\sigma_{b}|-|\tau\sigma_{b}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{c}^{-1}|}T_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ih,n\ell}(\tau\pi)L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kn}\ket{I,\sigma_{a},\sigma_{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}^{ig,o\ell}_{ab}(q^{2})\mathcal{D}_{bb}(q)\mathcal{C}^{gh,no}_{bc}(q)\frac{1}{C_{a}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kn}\ket{I,\sigma_{a},\sigma_{a}}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{ig,o\ell}_{ab}(Q)=\frac{1}{C_{b}}\sum_{\pi}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{a}\pi^{-1}\sigma_{b}^{-1},Q)T(\pi)_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ig,o\ell}

(276)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{ab}(Q)=Q^{-|\sigma_{a}|}\delta_{ab}

(277)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{gh,no}_{bc}(Q)=\frac{1}{C_{c}}\sum_{\tau}Q^{-|\tau\sigma_{b}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{c}^{-1}|}T(\tau)_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{gh,no}

(278)

Applying
\Pi_{m}
afterwards gives

\displaystyle\Pi_{m}G_{m}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}^{ig,o\ell}_{ab}(q^{2})\mathcal{D}_{bb}(q)\mathcal{C}^{gh,no}_{bc}(q)\frac{1}{C_{a}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kn}\sum_{\pi,\tau}\text{Wg}(\pi\tau^{-1},q^{m})q^{-|\tau|-|\tau\sigma_{a}^{-1}|}\ket{\pi,\pi,\sigma_{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}^{ig,o\ell}_{ab}(q^{2})\mathcal{D}_{bb}(q)\mathcal{C}^{gh,no}_{bc}(q)\frac{1}{C_{a}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kn}\sum_{\pi,\tau}\text{Wg}(\pi\tau^{-1},q^{m})q^{-|\tau\sigma_{a}|-|\tau|}(\sigma_{a})_{R}\ket{\pi,\pi,I}

Where we took
(\pi\rightarrow\pi\sigma_{a},\tau\rightarrow\tau\sigma_{a})
in the final line. Dropping the
\mathcal{W},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{C}
matrices in front, and converting the sums over
\pi,\tau
into conjugacy class sums over
\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi,\tau\sigma_{d}^{-1}\tau
, this expression becomes

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{a}}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kn}\sum_{\pi,\tau,d,e}\frac{1}{C_{d}C_{e}}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1}\tau\sigma_{d}\tau^{-1},q^{m+1})q^{-m|\tau\sigma_{d}^{-1}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{a}|-|\sigma_{d}|}(\sigma_{a})_{R}\ket{\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1},\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1},I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{a}}\sum_{\pi,\tau,d,e}\frac{1}{C_{d}C_{e}}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1}\tau\sigma_{d}\tau^{-1},q^{m+1})q^{-m|\tau\sigma_{d}^{-1}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{a}|-|\sigma_{d}|}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kn}(\sigma_{a})_{R}\pi_{L}\pi_{R}^{-1}(\sigma_{e})_{R}^{-1}\ket{I,I,\sigma_{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{a}}\sum_{\pi,\tau,d,e}\frac{1}{C_{d}C_{e}}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1}\tau\sigma_{d}\tau^{-1},q^{m+1})q^{-m|\tau\sigma_{d}^{-1}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{a}|-|\sigma_{d}|}V_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{a})^{on}L_{\nu_{L}}^{hj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{ko}\pi_{L}\pi_{R}^{-1}(\sigma_{e})_{R}^{-1}\ket{I,I,\sigma_{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{a}}\sum_{\pi,\tau,d,e}\frac{1}{C_{d}C_{e}}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1}\tau\sigma_{d}\tau^{-1},q^{m+1})q^{-m|\tau\sigma_{d}^{-1}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{a}|-|\sigma_{d}|}T(\pi)^{hg,po}V_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{a})^{on}L_{\nu_{L}}^{gj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kp}(\sigma_{e})_{R}^{-1}\ket{I,I,\sigma_{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C_{a}}\sum_{\pi,\tau,d,e}\frac{1}{C_{d}C_{e}}\text{Wg}(\pi\sigma_{e}^{-1}\pi^{-1}\sigma_{d},q^{m+1})q^{-m|\tau\sigma_{d}^{-1}\tau^{-1}\sigma_{a}|-|\sigma_{d}|}V_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{e}^{-1})^{qp}T(\tau\pi)^{hg,po}V_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{a})^{on}L_{\nu_{L}}^{gj}R_{\nu_{R}}^{kq}\ket{I,I,\sigma_{e}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d,e}\mathcal{W}^{hf,pr}_{ed}(q^{m+1})\mathcal{D}_{dd}(q)\mathcal{C}^{fg,ro}_{da}(q^{m})V_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{e}^{-1})^{qp}V_{\nu_{R}}(\sigma_{a})^{on}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{gj,kq,e}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d,e}(\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{R},ee}^{qp})^{-1}\mathcal{W}^{hf,pr}_{ed}(q^{m+1})\mathcal{D}_{dd}(q)\mathcal{C}^{fg,ro}_{da}(q^{m})D_{\nu_{R},aa}^{on}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{gj,kq,e}}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\nu,ab}^{ij}=\delta_{ab}V_{\nu}(\sigma_{a})^{ij}

(279)

In total, we can therefore write the
\Pi_{m}G_{m}\Pi_{m}
operator as

\displaystyle\Pi_{m}G_{m}\Pi_{m}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ij,k\ell,c}}=\sum_{h,n}\mathcal{M}_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ih,n\ell,cb}\ket{e_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{hj,kn,b}}

(280)

where
\mathcal{M}
is a product of tensors, analogous to the right-action matrix product:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\nu_{L},\nu_{R}}^{ih,n\ell,cb}=\left[(\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{R}})^{-1}\right]^{o_{5}\ell}_{cc}\mathcal{W}(q^{m+1})^{ig_{3},o_{4}o_{5}}_{ca_{3}}\mathcal{D}(q)_{a_{3}a_{3}}\mathcal{C}(q^{m})^{g_{3}g_{2},o_{3}o_{4}}_{a_{3}a_{2}}\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{R},a_{2}a_{2}}^{o_{2}o_{3}}\mathcal{W}(q^{2})^{g_{2}g_{1},o_{1}o_{2}}_{a_{2}a_{1}}\mathcal{D}(q)_{a_{1}a_{1}}\mathcal{C}(q)^{g_{1}h,no_{1}}_{a_{1}b}

(281)

Note that the indices corresponding to the right-action irrep are applied as a right action, while the indices corresponding to the left-action irrep, and the conjugacy class indices, are applied as left action.

One advantage of working in this basis is that the size of each isotypical subspace is significantly reduced. For example, under only right-action symmetry, the largest isotypical subspace dimension
\max_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}(t!)^{2}
is 11520 at
t=6
. Under both left-action and right-action, the largest possible size of an isotypical subspace is
\max_{\lambda}d_{\lambda}^{2}N_{c}=2816
for
t=6
, corresponding to the partition
\lambda=(3,2,1)
. However, these matrices tend to be extremely sparse, which reduces this number even further - for example, there are only 50 nonzero singular values in
\mathcal{C}(2)
for the maximal dimension partition. The Gelfand-Tsetlin basis can be used to reduce the number of relevant vectors further. Since every leaf in the Bratteli diagram gives the same spectrum, only one path must be computed.

Appendix F
Special bound for
q=2

The bound

\displaystyle k_{m}\leq\frac{1}{q^{2}+1}

(282)

holds in every case we have checked except one. When
q=2
,
t=4
, and
m=1
, the eigenvalue is
\frac{1}{4}
instead of the expected
\frac{1}{5}
. We thus cannot prove Theorem
5
in this case by using Lemma
18
. We will now give an alternative to Lemma
18
that holds in this special case.

Lemma 66
.

Suppose that there exists
\lambda
such that the following conditions hold:

•

0.06963\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}

•

\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
i>1

•

\lambda\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}

Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{\text{stair}}\leq\max\left((1+\sqrt{1-\lambda})^{2}\lambda,\lambda_{1}+(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda})\sqrt{(1-\lambda_{1})\lambda\lambda_{1}}\right)

(283)

Proof.

From the assumptions in the proof, we see

P_{i}T_{N}P_{j}\leq A^{\prime}_{ij}

where

A^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda\mu_{1}&\lambda\mu\mu_{1}&\dots&\lambda\mu^{N-2}\mu_{1}&\lambda\mu^{N-1}\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{1}&\lambda&\lambda\mu&\dots&\lambda\mu^{N-2}&\lambda\mu^{N-1}\\
0&\mu&\lambda&\dots&\lambda\mu^{N-3}&\lambda\mu^{N-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&\mu&\lambda\\
\end{bmatrix}

(284)

If we apply the Gershgorin Circle Theorem to
M^{-1}A^{\prime}M
as in the proof of Lemma
18
, the first column sum is

\displaystyle C_{1}=\lambda_{1}+(\mu+\sqrt{\lambda})\mu_{1}=\lambda_{1}+(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda})\sqrt{(1-\lambda_{1})\lambda\lambda_{1}}

(285)

For
j>1
, the column sum is

\displaystyle C_{j}

\displaystyle=(\mu+\sqrt{\lambda})\mu\ +\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{k}+\lambda\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{j-1}\left(\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)

(286)

\displaystyle=(\mu+\sqrt{\lambda})\mu\ +\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{k}+\lambda\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{j}\frac{\mu^{\prime}}{\mu}

(287)

\displaystyle=(\mu+\sqrt{\lambda})\mu\ +\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{k}+\lambda\left[\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{j}\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu}-\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{k}\right]

(288)

\displaystyle=(\mu+\sqrt{\lambda})\mu\ +\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{k}+\lambda\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{j}\left[\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{k}\right]

(289)

\displaystyle=(\mu+\sqrt{\lambda})\mu\ +\lambda\frac{1}{1-\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}}+\lambda\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}\right)^{j}\left[\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu}-\frac{1}{1-\frac{\mu}{\mu+\sqrt{\lambda}}}\right]

(290)

\displaystyle=\left(\left(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\lambda}}}\right)^{j}\left[\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu}-\left(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\right)\right]\right)\lambda

(291)

We will now show that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{1}}{\mu}-\left(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\right)\leq 0

(293)

so that we may drop the last term of the bound. We know
\mu_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, so it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\leq\left(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda}\right)\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}

(294)

This holds whenever
0.6963\leq\lambda\leq 0.8474
.
We may then apply Theorem
56
to complete the proof.
∎

This argument can be extended to the case where the first few columns of
A
are exceptional, but we will not need it here. Note also that the
0.6963
condition is not tight when
\mu_{1}<\frac{1}{2}
. We check numerically that this bound holds for many cases.

Lemma
.

When
q=2
,
t\leq 6
, and

\displaystyle||K_{m}||\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}&m=1\\
\frac{1}{5}&m>1\end{cases}

Proof.

This is obtained by checking each case numerically using the algorithm described in Appendix

. Results are shown in Figure

.
∎

Substituting into Lemma

, we see that all three conditions are satisfied when we take
\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{4}
and
\lambda=\frac{1}{q^{2}+1}=\frac{1}{5}
. In this case

\displaystyle(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda})^{2}\lambda

\displaystyle\approx 0.7178

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}+(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda})\sqrt{(1-\lambda_{1})\lambda\lambda_{1}}

\displaystyle\approx 0.6169<0.7178
