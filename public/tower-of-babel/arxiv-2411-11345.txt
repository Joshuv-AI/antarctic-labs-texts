Real Gaussian exponential sums via a real moment map Thanks: Partially funded by DFG grant BE 2484/10-1
Léo Mathis

Abstract
. We study the expected number of solutions of a system of identically distributed exponential sums with centered Gaussian coefficient and arbitrary variance. We use the Adler and Taylor theory of Gaussian random fields to identify a moment map which allows to express the expected number of solution as an integral over the Newton polytope, in analogy with the Bernstein Khovanskii Kushnirenko Theorem. We apply this result to study the monotonicity of the expected number of solution with respect to the support of the exponential sum in an open set. We find that, when a point is added in the support in the interior of the Newton polytope there exists an open sets where the expected number of solutions decreases, answering negatively to a local version of a conjecture by Bürgisser. When the point added in the support is far enough away from the Newton polytope we show that there is an unbounded open set where the number of solution decreases. We also prove some new lower bounds for the Aronszajn multiplication of exponential sums.



1.
Introduction

From a finite subset
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, one can construct a family of (Laurent) polynomials in the variable
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})
:

(1.1)

Q(w):=\sum_{a\in A}c_{a}w^{a}

where
w^{a}:=w_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots w^{a_{m}}
. Such polynomials are often called
sparse
. Over the complex numbers, the main result in the study of roots of sparse polynomials is the Berstein Kushnirenko Khovanskii (BKK) Theorem that determines the number of solutions of a system of polynomials of the form
Eq.

1.1
. Before stating it, let us introduce the following notation. For every tuple of functions
f_{1}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}},\ldots,f_{d}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{k_{d}}
where
M
is a smooth manifold (in that case
\mathbb{C}^{m}
) and every open set
U\subset M
, we denote the set of solutions of the corresponding system by:

(1.2)

\mathcal{S}\left\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{d};U\right\}:=\left\{x\in U\ |\ f_{1}(x)=\cdots=f_{d}(x)=0\right\}.

In its most simple form, the BKK Theorem says the following, see for example
[
Ber75
]
.

Theorem

(BKK)
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
be a finite subset and for all
i=1,\cdots,m
, let
Q^{i}(w):=\sum_{a\in A}c^{i}_{a}w^{a}
with
c_{a}^{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then for a generic choice of
c_{a}^{i}
, we have

(1.3)

\#\mathcal{S}\left\{Q^{1},\ldots,Q^{m};(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{m}\right\}=m!\vol_{m}(P)

where
P:=\conv(A)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the convex hull of
A
, also called the
Newton polytope
.

Here the word
generic
means that in the space of coefficients
(\mathbb{C}^{A})^{m}
, Equation
Eq.

1.3
is true except on a set of measure zero. In that case, this set is the complex subvariety of complex codimension one where the system
Eq.

1.3
is degenerate, see
[
SS93
, Chapter I-4]
.

In this paper we want to study
real
solutions of systems of
real
polynomials. In that situation, there is no more
generic case
, indeed the subset of degenerate systems divide the space of coefficient in different connected components where the number of solution is different. It is then possible to take a probabilistic approach and study the
average
number of solutions of a
random
system. We establish in this context a result analogous to the BKK Theorem, but where the volume of the Newton polytope has to be taken with respect to a certain Riemannian metric, see
A
below.

1.1.
Main results

Before describing the result, we adopt the point of view of
exponential sums
. For every
w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{>0}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ w_{i}>0,\,\forall i=1,\ldots,m\right\}
in the positive orthant we write
x_{i}:=\log(w_{i})
in such a way that, for every
a\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, we have
w^{a}=e^{\langle a,x\rangle}
. This allows to take exponents that are reals and not only integers. Sums of monomials with real exponents are also called
signomials
and their optimization have application in physics and chemistry, see for example
[
DM22
]
and the references therein. It turns out to be equivalent and more convenient to work with exponential sum, see the discussion in
Section

4.1
.

For every finite set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and coefficients
(\alpha_{a})_{a\in A}
with
\alpha_{a}>0
, we define the
Gaussian exponential sum
:

(1.4)

\mathcal{E}(x):=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}\,\alpha_{a}\,e^{\langle a,x\rangle}

where
\xi_{a}\in\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian variables. We introduce another notation. If
\mathcal{X}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a random function on a smooth manifold
M
of dimension
m=kn
(in our case
M=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
k=1
) and
U\subset M
an open set, we write the (possibly infinite) expected number of solutions in
U
as:

(1.5)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};U\right\}:=\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n};U\right\}

where
\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n}
are iid, distributed as
\mathcal{X}
.

In this article, we will show how the relevant information for the expected zeros of
Eq.

1.4
is contained in the
potential

\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
given for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by:

(1.6)

\Phi(x):=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)^{2}\right]\right).

The function
\mathrm{K}(x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{E}(x)^{2}\right]=\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{2\langle a,x\rangle}
is called the
diagonal covariance
function of the Gaussian random field
\mathcal{E}
. We prove in
Lemma

4.5
that
\Phi
is strictly convex. We can then consider its
Legendre transform

\Phi^{*}
, see
Eq.

2.18
, which turns out to be a (smooth strictly convex) function on the Newton polytope
P
. We then obtain the following which is
Theorem

4.7
.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be given by
Eq.

1.4
. Then we have:

(1.7)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{\tfrac{m-2}{2}}s_{m}}\vol_{g}(P)

where
s_{m}
denotes the Euclidean volume of the unit sphere
S^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
and
\vol_{g}
is the Riemannian volume for the metric
g:=D^{2}_{p}\Phi^{*}
, i.e.,
g
is given by the Hessian of the Legendre transform
\Phi^{*}
of the potential
\Phi
.

Note that the total number of solutions
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}
is known to be finite even if
A
is not rational. For example, an upper bound in terms of the cardinality
\#A
was recently obtained by Ergür, Telek and Tonelli-Cueto in
[
ETTC23
]
.

A
is obtained by identifying a
moment map

\mu:=D_{x}\Phi
in this context. As the name suggest, it is similar to the moment map in toric geometry, see
[
Ful93
, Section 4.2]
or in the complex setting, see
[
Kla13
]
. We prove in
Lemma

4.6
that

(1.8)

\mu:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\interior(P)

is a diffeomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the interior of the Newton polytope.

By studying the potential
\Phi
and by some elementary properties of Legendre transform, we obtain, for example, in
Corollary

4.8
, a new lower bound for the expected number of solution:

(1.9)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}>\frac{1}{2^{m-1}s_{m}}\,\frac{\vol_{m}(P)}{\diam(P)^{m}}

where
\vol_{m}(P)
is the classical Euclidean volume of the Newton polytope and
\diam(P)
its diameter.

The main results we obtain are to study the
monotonicity
of Gaussian exponential sums. More precisely, we chose
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus A
and
\alpha_{0}>0
and we want to compare the zeros of the new exponential sum

(1.10)

\mathcal{E}_{0}(x):=\mathcal{E}(x)+\xi_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,e^{\langle a_{0},x\rangle}

where
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is a standard Gaussian variable independent of
\mathcal{E}
, with the zeros of
\mathcal{E}
. In the following we always assume that the Newton polytope
P
is full dimensional.

Peter Bürgisser conjectured (this conjecture only appears on the Bachelor thesis of Hendrik Schroeder
[
Sch24
]
) that, if
a_{0}
is in the interior of the Newton polytope
P
, then
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}\geq\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}
. Here we are able to prove that a local version of Bürgisser’s conjecture does not hold. The following is
Theorem

4.11
in the text.

Theorem B
.

Let
a_{0}\in\interior(P)
, then there is a non empty open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

(1.11)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};U\right\}<\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}.

In the case where the distance
d(a_{0},P)
from
a_{0}
to the Newton polytope is big enough, we obtain the following which, in the text, corresponds to
Theorem

4.13
.

Theorem C
.

Let
a_{0}
be such that
d(a_{0},P)>\diam(P)/m
. There exists a non empty unbounded open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

(1.12)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};U\right\}<\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}.

1.2.
Methods

To prove our results, we take a step back in abstraction and study Gaussian random fields (GRF) on manifolds. A GRF on a smooth manifold
M
is a random function
\mathcal{X}:M\to\mathbb{R}
, in our case smooth, such that the evaluation at tuples of points is a Gaussian vector.

Adler and Taylor developed a theory based on the Kac-Rice formula, see
Section

2.5
, to explain how the expected number of solutions
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};\cdot\right\}
is in fact the Riemannian volume for a certain Riemannian metric
g
, that we call the Adler-Taylor (AT) metric, see
Proposition

3.7
. The AT metric is described in terms of the GRF
\mathcal{X}
in
Proposition

3.10
.

We are interested in the case where the GRF is contained in a finite dimensional subspace of
C^{\infty}(M)
, that is, when it is of the form

(1.13)

\mathcal{X}=\xi_{1}\,f_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{d}\,f_{d}

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\in C^{\infty}(M)
are fixed smooth functions having no common zero, see
Definition

2.25
. The AT metric can then be expressed in terms of the functions
f_{i}
and the moment map
\mu
, see
Eq.

3.17
.

We then chose
f_{0}\in C^{\infty}(M)
linearly independent of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
and consider the GRF
\mathcal{X}_{0}:=\mathcal{X}+\xi_{0}\,f_{0}
where
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is a standard Gaussian variable independent of
\mathcal{X}
. We then introduce the function
\Psi:M\to\mathbb{R}
given for all
x\in M
by

(1.14)

\Psi(x):=\left(\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}(x)+f_{0}(x)^{2}}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{1+g^{x}(\tau(x))}.

where
\tau
is a one form on
M
defined in terms of
f_{0}
and the moment map
\mu
and
g^{x}
is the AT metric for
\mathcal{X}
on forms, see
Definition

3.13
. We prove that this function determines the local monotonicity, see
Theorem

3.14
in the text.

Theorem D
.

For every open set
U\subset M
we have

(1.15)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{0};U\right\}-\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};U\right\}=c_{m}\int_{U}(\Psi(x)-1)\,\mathrm{d}M(x)

where
c_{m}>0
is a dimensional constant and
\mathrm{d}M(x)
denotes integration with respect to the AT metric of
\mathcal{X}
.

It follows that, to determine the local monotonicity, one must determine the open sets
U_{-}:=\{\Psi<1\}
and
U_{+}:=\{\Psi>1\}
. In
Section

4.3
, we study this in the case of Gaussian exponential sum to prove
B
and
C
.

Interestingly enough, we show in
Proposition

3.19
that the one form
\tau
is integrable, that is that it is orthogonal to a family of hypersurfaces. We show in
Proposition

3.21
that, when restricted to these hypersurfaces, the number of solution always decreases. In the case of exponential sums, this is described in
Proposition

4.15
.

1.3.
Other results

We also study two operations on Gaussian exponential sums. Firstly a simple tensor product, see
Eq.

4.47
. The second operation, more subtle, was introduced by Malajovitch in
[
Mal23
]
and is called
Aronszajn’s multiplication
. Given two finite sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and choices of coefficients
\alpha_{a},\beta_{b}>0
, we consider the corresponding exponential sums
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
. Their Aronszajn multiplication, denoted
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\odot\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
is the exponential sum with support the Minkowski sum
C:=A+B
and coefficients
\gamma_{c}>0
given for all
c\in C
by:

(1.16)

\gamma_{c}:=\sqrt{\sum_{a+b=c}\alpha_{a}^{2}\beta_{b}^{2}}.

It turns out that the AT metric corresponding to the tensor product, respectively to the Aronszajn multiplication, is given by the direct sum, respectively the sum, of the previous AT metrics, see
Lemma

4.26
. We deduce how the number of solutions transform, see
Proposition

4.22
for the tensor product and
Lemma

4.26
for the powers of Aronszajn multiplication, which was already obtained in
[
Mal23
]
and reproved here in our slightly more general context of admissible fields. Finally, Malajovitch proved that the expected number of solutions is sub additive with respect to Aronszajn multiplication. We reprove this result with the understanding of the AT metrics involved and give a new lower bound, see
Theorem

4.29
for the more general statement.

Theorem E
.

Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be Gaussian exponential sum and let
\mathcal{E}\odot\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
be their Aronszajn multiplication. For any tuple of independent Gaussian exponential sum
\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}
, write
\mathrm{E}:=(\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{m-1}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-1}
. Then, for every open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have:

(1.17)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E},\mathrm{E};U\right\}+\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathrm{E};U\right\}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}\odot\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathrm{E};U\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E},\mathrm{E};U\right\}+\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathrm{E};U\right\}.

We illustrate these operations in an explicit example in
Section

4.6
where we also compute the open set
U_{-}
.

Finally, to complete the picture we show in
Section

5
how to prove the BKK theorem in our context. More precisely, we proved a generalization by Kazarnovskii in
[
Kaz18
]
. The proof turns out to be similar to the one given by Kazarnovskii. However it is given in more details and offer a perspective towards a generalization, for example to quaternions.

If
A\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a finite subset, we define the complex exponential sum

(1.18)

\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}(z):=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}e^{(a,z)}

with
\xi_{a}\in\mathbb{C}
complex Gaussian and
(\cdot,\cdot)
denoting the standard Hermitian form. Then we prove the following which is
Theorem

5.4
in the text.

Theorem F
.

For all
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have

(1.19)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};U\right\}=\frac{n!}{\pi^{n}}\int_{U}\det\left(\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z.

where
\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}(z):=\log\left(\sum_{a\in A}e^{2\Re{(a,z)}}\right)
.

To see how this result implies the BKK theorem, one needs to take
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and apply the change of variable given by the moment map. See more details in the proof of
Theorem

5.4
below.

2.
Preliminaries

We use the following convenient notation:

•

for every vector
x
in a vector space we write

(2.1)

\underline{x}:=\tfrac{1}{2}[-x,x]

•

if
\mathscr{S}
is a measurable set and
S
is a random element of
\mathscr{S}
, we write

(2.2)

S\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{S}.

In what follows,
V
denotes a real vector space of dimension
m<+\infty
and
V^{*}:=\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(V,\mathbb{R})
its dual.

2.1.
Convex geometry

We start with a few standard facts on convex geometry. For more details, we refer to
[
Sch14
]
. A
convex body

K
in
V
is a compact convex non empty subset of
V
. Its
support function
is the function
h_{K}:V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
given for all
u\in V^{*}
by:

(2.3)

h_{K}(u):=\sup\left\{\langle u,x\rangle\ |\ x\in K\right\}

The support function determines the convex body
K
, meaning that two convex bodies
K
and
K^{\prime}
are equal if and only if
h_{K}=h_{K^{\prime}}
, see
[
Sch14
, Section 1.7.1]
. Moreover, a function
h:V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
is the support function of a convex body in
V
if and only if it is
sublinear
, that is if
h(\lambda u)=\lambda h(u)
for all
u\in V^{*},\lambda\geq 0
and
h(u+v)\leq h(u)+h(v)
for all
u,v\in V^{*}
; see
[
Sch14
, Theorem 1.7.1]
.

If
K
and
K^{\prime}
are two convex bodies, their
Minkowski sum
is the convex body

(2.4)

K+K^{\prime}:=\left\{x+y\ |\ x\in K,\,y\in K^{\prime}\right\}.

If
V
is Euclidean, the
Hausdorff distance
between
K
and
K^{\prime}
is given by (see
[
Sch14
, Lemma 1.8.14]
)

(2.5)

d(K,K^{\prime})=\sup\left\{|h_{K}(u)-h_{K^{\prime}}(u)|\ |\ \|u\|=1\right\}.

The topology it induces on the space of convex bodies does not depend on the choice of euclidean structure.
Moreover, we note that the support function is
linear
with respect to Minkowski sum, that is
h_{K+K^{\prime}}=h_{K}+h_{K^{\prime}}
. Moreover, if
L:V\to W
is a linear map to a vector space
W
,
then
h_{L(K)}=h_{K}\circ L^{T}
where
L^{T}:W^{*}\to V^{*}
is the
transpose
of
L
, recall that it is given, fro all
u\in W^{*}
and
v\in V
by

(2.6)

\langle L^{T}u,v\rangle:=\langle u,Lv\rangle.

Another direct consequence of the definition is that for convex bodies
K,K^{\prime}
we have

(2.7)

K\subset K^{\prime}\quad\iff\quad h_{K}(u)\leq h_{K^{\prime}}(u)\,\forall u\in V^{*}.

The
convex hull
of a bounded set
A\subset V
is the smallest convex body containing
A
and is denoted
\conv(A)
. We note that, for its support function
h_{\conv(A)}
, the
\sup
in (
2.3
) can be taken over
A
instead of
\conv(A)
. The convex hull of a finite set is called a
polytope
.

For all
u\in V^{*}
and compact set
A\subset V
we denote by

(2.8)

A^{u}:=\left\{a\in A\ |\ \langle u,a\rangle=h_{\conv(A)}(u)\right\}=\left\{a\in A\ |\ \langle u,a\rangle=\sup\left\{\langle u,a^{\prime}\rangle\ |\ a^{\prime}\in A\right\}\right\}.

If
A=K
is a convex body,
K^{u}
is also called the
exposed face
of
K
in the direction
u
, we will simply call it a
face
.

A
vertex

v\in P
of a polytope
P
is a face of dimension zero, i.e.,
v
is a vertex if and only if there is
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
P^{x}=\{v\}
. Note that if
P=\conv(A)
then all vertices belong to
A
.

Remark 2.1
.

By a simple dimensional count, one can also see that the set
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ \dim(P^{x})=0\right\}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We now fix a Euclidean structure on
V
. The space of convex bodies, that we denote
\mathcal{K}(V)
, is endowed with the topology induced from the Hausdorff distance. This is equivalent to consider the supremum norm of support functions as functions on the sphere, see
[
Sch14
, Lemma1.8.14 ]
. Recall that the
mixed volume
is defined as follows, see
[
Sch14
, Theorem 5.1.7]
. We write
\mathcal{K}(V)
for the space of convex bodies of
V
(endowed with the Hausdorff distance).

Definition 2.2
.

The
mixed volume
is the unique continuous application
\MV:\mathcal{K}(V)^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is symmetric and Minkowski linear in each of its argument and that is such that for all
K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\in\mathcal{K}(V)
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\geq 0
we have

(2.9)

\vol(t_{1}K_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}K_{n})=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}=1}^{n}t_{i_{1}}\cdots t_{i_{m}}\MV(K_{i_{1}},\ldots,K_{i_{m}}).

In the following, we write

(2.10)

\|K\|:=\sup\left\{\|x\|\ |\ x\in K\right\}.

Note that
\|K\|
is the smallest number such that
K\subset\|K\|\,B_{V}
where
B_{V}
is the unit ball of
V
. Equivalently
h_{K}(u)\leq\|K\|\|u\|
for all
u\in V^{*}
.

We now describe how to obtain convex bodies with measures.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\left\{K_{s}\ |\ s\in S\right\}
be a measurable family of convex bodies in
V
indexed by a measurable space
S
. Let
\mu
be a non negative measure on
S
such that
\int_{S}\|K_{s}\|\,\mathrm{d}\mu(s)<\infty
for one (and thus any) Euclidean structure on
V
. Then, the
Minkowski integral
is the convex body
\int_{S}K_{s}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(s)
whose support function is given for all
u\in V^{*}
by:

(2.11)

h_{\int_{S}K_{s}\mathrm{d}\mu(s)}(u):=\int_{S}h_{K_{s}}(u)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(s).

In the particular case where
\mu
is a probability measure, we adopt the probabilistic notation. That is, if
K
is a random convex body of
V
with
\mathbb{E}\|K\|<\infty
, we write
\mathbb{E}K
for the convex body with support function given for all
u\in V^{*}
by:

(2.12)

h_{\mathbb{E}K}(u):=\mathbb{E}h_{K}(u).

In the particular case where the random convex bodies are segments we obtain a particular class of convex bodies called
zonoids
see
[
Vit91
, Theorem 3.1]
. Recall the notation for segments
Eq.

2.1
.

Definition 2.4
.

A convex body
K\subset V
is a
zonoid
if there is a random vector
X\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}V
with
\mathbb{E}\|X\|<+\infty
such that
K=\mathbb{E}\underline{X}
. We call
\mathbb{E}\underline{X}
the
Vitale zonoid
associated to
X
.

Remark 2.5
.

Remark 2.6

If

\ell:\mathcal{K}(V)\to\mathbb{R}

2.2.
Legendre transform

We gather here some properties of Legendre transformations, the reader can refer to
[
Roc70
, Section 12 and 26]
.
Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lower semi continuous convex function. The
domain
of
\Phi
is the closure of the set where it is finite:

(2.18)

\dom(\Phi):=\overline{\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ \Phi(x)<+\infty\right\}}

One easily check that the convexity of
\Phi
implies that
\dom()\Phi)
is convex.

The
Legendre transform
of
\Phi
is the function
\Phi^{*};\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
given for all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by:

(2.19)

\Phi^{*}(p):=\sup\left\{\langle p,x\rangle-\Phi(x)\ |\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}

It follows, see
[
Roc70
, Theorem 12.2]
, that
\Phi^{*}
is a lower semi continuous convex function. If
\Phi
is
C^{1}
, then the supremum in
Eq.

2.19
, if attained, is attained at a point
x^{*}
such that
D_{x^{*}}\Phi=p
where
D_{x^{*}}\Phi
is the differential (i.e., in that case the gradient) of
\Phi
in
x^{*}
. In other words, we have

(2.20)

\Phi^{*}(D_{x}\Phi)=\langle D_{x}\Phi,x\rangle-\Phi(x).

If this supremum is not attained then, we have
\Phi^{*}(p)=+\infty
.
We obtain the following.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{1}
convex function. Then the image of
D\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m};x\mapsto D_{x}\Phi
is the relative interior of
\dom(\Phi^{*})
. In particular the image of
D\Phi
is convex.

Proof.

The relative interior
U:=\mathrm{relint}(\dom(\Phi^{*}))
corresponds to the points where
\Phi^{*}
is finite, i.e. where the supremum
Eq.

2.19
is attained. By
Eq.

2.20
, it means that
p\in U
if and only if there is
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
D_{x}\Phi
, i.e., if and only if
p
is in the image of
D\Phi
.
∎

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
strictly convex function. Then
\Phi^{*}
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex on
\interior(\dom(\Phi^{*}))
. In that case, the inverse of
D\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\interior(\dom(\Phi^{*}))
is
D\Phi^{-1}=D\Phi^{*}
.

Proof.

The function
\Phi
is strictly convex if and only if, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the Hessian
D^{2}_{x}\Phi
is positive definite. It implies that
D\Phi
is a diffeomorphism onto its image and in particular that
\dom(\Phi^{*})
is of dimension
m
. This justifies the fact that we used the interior instead of relative interior. Let
p\in\interior(\dom(\Phi^{*}))
and let us write
\mu:=D\Phi
. By
Eq.

2.20
, replacing
x
by
(\mu)^{-1}p
, we have

(2.21)

\Phi^{*}(p)=\langle p,\mu^{-1}(p)\rangle-\Phi\left(\mu^{-1}(p)\right).

Differentiating in
p
, we obtain

(2.22)

D_{p}\Phi^{*}=\mu^{-1}(p)+\langle p,D_{p}(\mu^{-1})\cdot\rangle-D_{\mu^{-1}(p)}\Phi\circ D_{p}(\mu^{-1})

Now it is enough to note that
D_{\mu^{-1}(p)}\Phi=\mu(\mu^{-1}(p))=p
and we obtain
D_{p}\Phi^{*}=\mu^{-1}(p)
which is what we wanted.
∎

We deduce the following.

Corollary 2.10
.

Under the hypotheses of
Proposition

2.9
, the Hessian of
\Phi
and
\Phi^{*}
are inverse of each other. In particular
\det(D_{p}\Phi^{*})=\det(D^{2}_{x}\Phi)^{-1}
.

2.3.
Quadratic forms and Ellipsoids

In this section, we fix notation for quadratic forms and prove some elementary facts about volumes of corresponding ellipsoids. Since we will later apply this to manifolds, it is convenient to state results independently of the choice of a basis and Euclidean structure. Recall that
V
denotes a real vector space of dimension
m
.

A
volume form
on
V
is the choice of a non zero element
\omega\in\Lambda^{m}V^{*}
. A positive definite quadratic form
Q
on
V^{*}
induces a volume form
\omega:=\epsilon_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon_{m}
where
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}
is an orthonormal basis of
V^{*}
for
Q
. It induces a notion of volume, that we denote
\vol_{Q}
, given for any measurable subset
A\subset V
by

(2.23)

\vol_{Q}(A):=\int_{A}|\omega|

where
|\omega|
denotes the density induced by
\omega
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
Q
be a positive definite quadratic form on
V^{*}
and let
L\in Gl(V^{*})
, then
Q\circ L
is positive definite and we have:

(2.24)

\vol_{Q\circ L}=\frac{1}{|\det L|}\vol_{Q}.

Proof.

If
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}
is a
Q
-orthonormal basis, then
\epsilon_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}
with
\epsilon_{i}^{\prime}:=L^{-1}\epsilon_{i}
is a
(Q\circ L)
-orthonormal basis. Then we have
|\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}|=|\det L^{-1}|\,|\epsilon_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon_{m}|
. Then
\det L^{-1}=1/\det L
gives the result.
∎

Given a positive definite quadratic form
Q
on
V
, we denote by
Q^{\circ}
the dual quadratic form on
V^{*}
. Recall that it is given for every
u\in V^{*}
by

(2.25)

Q^{\circ}(u)=\sup\left\{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}\ |\ Q(v)\leq 1\right\}.

The dual quadratic form transforms under linear maps as follows.

Proposition 2.12
.

For all
L\in Gl(V)
, we have

(2.26)

(Q\circ L)^{\circ}=Q^{\circ}\circ L^{-T}

were
L^{-T}
is the inverse of the transpose
L^{T}
(recall its definition in (
2.6
)).

Proof.

Directly from the definition (
2.25
) we have that

(2.27)

(Q\circ L)^{\circ}(u)=\sup\left\{\langle u,v\rangle^{2}\ |\ Q(L(v))\leq 1\right\}=\sup\left\{\langle u,L^{-1}v\rangle^{2}\ |\ Q(v)\leq 1\right\}

and we conclude with the definition of the transpose (
2.6
).
∎

This duality satisfies
(Q^{\circ})^{\circ}=Q
and is order reversing in the following sense.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
Q,Q^{\prime}
be two positive definite quadratic forms on
V
such that for all
v\in V
, we have
Q(v)\leq Q^{\prime}(v)
then for all
u\in V^{*}
, we have
Q^{\circ}(u)\geq(Q^{\prime})^{\circ}(u)
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of the dual (
2.25
) and
Q^{\prime}(v)\leq 1\Rightarrow Q(v)\leq 1
.
∎

For every
u,u^{\prime}\in V^{*}
, we denote by
u^{2}
, respectively
u\cdot u^{\prime}
, the quadratic form on
V
given for all
v\in V
by:

(2.28)

u^{2}(v):=\langle u,v\rangle^{2},\quad\text{respectively }\,(u\cdot u^{\prime})(v):=\langle u,v\rangle\langle u^{\prime},v\rangle.

In more abstract terms, we are just identifying the space of quadratic forms on
V
with the symmetric
2
-power of
V^{*}
.

We note that, given
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\in V^{*}
, the quadratic form
\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}^{2}
is positive definite if and only if the family
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}
spans the whole space
V^{*}
.

Moreover, given a positive definite quadratic form
Q
on
V
, we denote the corresponding ellipsoid

(2.29)

B_{Q}:=\left\{v\in V\ |\ Q(v)\leq 1\right\}

which is the unit ball for the norm defined by
Q
.
We note that its support function is given for all
u\in V^{*}
by
h_{B_{Q}}(u)=\sqrt{Q^{\circ}(u)}.
Moreover, note that, for all
L\in Gl(V)
, we have

(2.30)

B_{Q\circ L}=L^{-1}(B_{Q}).

We also adopt the following notation:

(2.31)

B_{Q}^{\circ}:=B_{Q^{\circ}}.

Note that it coincides with the notion of
polar body
of a convex body, see
[
Sch14
, Chapter 1.6.1]
. From
Proposition

2.12
, we deduce that, for all linear map
L:V\to V
, the dual ball transforms as follows:

(2.32)

B_{Q\circ L}^{\circ}=L^{T}(B_{Q}^{\circ}).

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
Q
be a positive definite quadratic form on
V
. We have:

(2.33)

\vol_{Q}(B_{Q^{\circ}})=b_{m}

where
b_{m}=\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1)
is the Euclidean volume of the standard Euclidean unit ball.

Proof.

We choose a
Q
-orthonormal basis to identify
V
with
\mathbb{R}^{m}
then
Q
is, in this basis, the standard Euclidean quadratic form
\|\cdot\|^{2}
and thus
B_{Q}
and
B_{Q^{\circ}}
are identified with the standard Euclidean ball of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and this gives what we want.
∎

In our study, we will have to consider ellipsoids
B_{Q^{\circ}}
and
B_{(Q+u^{2})^{\circ}}
where
Q
is a positive definite quadratic form of
V
and
u\in V^{*}
. Next we show how their volume is related.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
Q
be a positive definite quadratic form on
V
and let
u\in V^{*}
. Then, for every
Q^{\prime}
positive definite quadratic form on
V
, we have

(2.34)

\vol_{Q^{\prime}}((B_{(Q+u^{2})})^{\circ})=\sqrt{1+Q^{\circ}(u)}\vol_{Q^{\prime}}(B_{Q}^{\circ}).

Proof.

Note that, because of the rule of transformation of volume (
2.24
), we only need to prove the equality for one quadratic form
Q^{\prime}
, and we will do it for
Q^{\prime}=Q
. In that case, we choose a
Q^{\circ}
-orthonormal basis
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}
of
V^{*}
in such a way that
u
is colinear with
\epsilon_{1}
, i.e
u=\sqrt{Q^{\circ}(u)}\epsilon_{1}
. Thus we have
Q+u^{2}=(1+Q^{\circ}(u))\epsilon_{1}^{2}+\epsilon_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\epsilon_{m}^{2}=Q\circ L
, where the linear map
L:V\to V
is given, in the basis dual to
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}
, by the diagonal matrix:

(2.35)

L:=\left(\begin{matrix}\sqrt{1+Q^{\circ}(u)}&&&\\
&1&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&1\end{matrix}\right).

It follows that,
\det(L)=\sqrt{1+Q^{\circ}(u)}
and thus we obtain, using (
2.33
) and (
2.24
) :

(2.36)

\vol_{Q}(B_{Q^{\circ}})=b_{m}=\vol_{Q+u^{2}}(B_{(Q+u^{2})^{\circ}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+Q^{\circ}(u)}}\vol_{Q}(B_{(Q+u^{2})^{\circ}})

which is what we wanted.
∎

The following will also be useful.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
Q
be a positive definite quadratic form on
V
and let
u\in V^{*}
. Let
L:V^{*}\to W^{*}
be a linear map such that
u\in\ker L
. Then we have
L(B_{(Q+u^{2})^{\circ}})=L(B_{Q^{\circ}})
.

Proof.

For this proof, the point of view of support functions is useful. First we note that
u\in\ker L
if and only if the image of
L^{T}
is contained in
u^{\perp}
. In particular,
(Q+u^{2})\circ L^{T}=Q\circ L^{T}
. We obtain

(2.37)

h_{L(B_{(Q+u^{2})^{\circ}})}=\sqrt{(Q+u^{2})\circ L^{T}}=\sqrt{Q\circ L^{T}}=h_{L(B_{Q^{\circ}})}

which is what we wanted.
∎

Let us now prove some bounds.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{n}
be positive definite quadratic forms on
V
, let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}>0
and write
Q:=t_{1}Q_{1}+\cdots+t_{n}Q_{n}
and
t:=t_{1}+\ldots+t_{n}
. Then we have

(2.38)

\frac{t_{1}}{\sqrt{t}}B_{Q_{1}}^{\circ}+\cdots+\frac{t_{n}}{\sqrt{t}}B_{Q_{n}}^{\circ}\subset B^{\circ}_{Q}\subset\sqrt{t_{1}}B_{Q_{1}}^{\circ}+\cdots+\sqrt{t_{n}}B_{Q_{n}}^{\circ}.

Proof.

We recall
Eq.

2.7
, which says that the inclusion of convex bodies is characterized by pointwise order of support functions. Let us write
h:=h_{B_{Q}^{\circ}}=\sqrt{Q}
and
h_{j}:=h_{B_{Q_{j}}^{\circ}}=\sqrt{Q_{j}}
. Then, for every
u\in V^{*}
, we have:

(2.39)

h(u)=\sqrt{Q(u)}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}h_{j}(u)^{2}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{t_{j}}h_{j}(u)

which gives the upper bound. For the other inclusion, we rewrite
h(u)=\sqrt{t}\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{t_{j}}{t}h_{j}(u)^{2}}.

Then, from the concavity of the square root, applying Jensen’s inequality, we get
h(u)\geq\sqrt{t}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{t_{j}}{t}h_{j}(u)
which is what we wanted.
∎

We now specialize to the case
V=\mathbb{R}^{m}
endowed with the standard scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
which we use to identify
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with its dual. If
Q
is a quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, there is a unique symmetric
m\times m
matrix
M_{Q}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have
Q(x)=\langle x,M_{Q}x\rangle
. In that case, we write

(2.40)

\det Q:=\det M_{Q}.

This determinant has the following geometric interpretation.

Proposition 2.18
.

If we denote
\vol
for the volume with respect to the Euclidean volume form, and if
Q
is a positive definite quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

(2.41)

\vol(B_{Q}^{\circ})=b_{m}\sqrt{\det Q}

Proof.

Let us denote
Q_{0}
the Euclidean quadratic form. Moreover, let
L:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be such that
M_{Q}=L^{T}L
. Then we have
Q=Q_{0}\circ L
. Using the rules of transformation
Eq.

2.30
and
Eq.

2.26
, we get that
B_{Q}^{\circ}=L(B)
where
B=B_{Q_{0}}
the standard Euclidean unit ball. The result then follows from the fact that
\det(L)=\sqrt{\det M_{Q}}=\sqrt{\det Q}
.
∎

Definition 2.19
.

If
Q
is a quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then we write

(2.42)

Q\leq\lambda\quad\iff\quad Q(x)\leq\lambda\|x\|^{2},\,\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}

and similarly for
>,\leq
and
<
. In particular
Q>0
means that
Q
is positive definite.

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
Q
be a quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose that
Q\leq\lambda
. Then
\det Q\leq\lambda^{m}
.

Proof.

Since we have
\sqrt{Q(x)}\leq\sqrt{\lambda}\|x\|
, it follows that
B_{Q}^{\circ}\subset\sqrt{\lambda}B
where
B=B^{\circ}
is the standard Euclidean ball. This implies that
\vol(B_{Q}^{\circ})\leq\lambda^{\frac{m}{2}}b_{m}
. The result then follows by
Proposition

2.18
.
∎

By simply applying Cauchy-Schwartz, we have the following.

Proposition 2.21
.

Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{2}
. Then we have
Q\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|u_{i}\|^{2}
.

Proposition 2.22
.

Let
Q>0
be a positive definite quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let
\lambda>0
and suppose that
Q\leq\lambda
. Then we have
Q^{\circ}\geq\lambda^{-1}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from the fact that the matrix of
Q^{\circ}
is given by
M_{Q^{\circ}}=M_{Q}^{-1}
. Thus the eigenvalues of
M_{Q}
are bounded from above by
\lambda
if and only if the eigenvalues of
M_{Q^{\circ}}
are bounded from below by
1/\lambda
.
∎

We conclude this section by proving a convenient relation between volumes of balls and spheres.

Lemma 2.23
.

Recall that we denote by
b_{m}
, respectively
s_{m}
the
m
-dimensional volume of the Euclidean unit ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, respectively unit sphere
S^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. We have:

(2.43)

\frac{m!b_{m}s_{m}}{(2\pi)^{m}}=2.

Proof.

We use the explicit formula for
b_{m}
and
s_{m}
in terms of the Gamma function, see
[
DLMF
,
5.19(iii)
]
:

(2.44)

b_{m}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{m}{2}}}{m\Gamma\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{m}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)}.

Legendre’s duplication formula, see
[
DLMF
,
5.5(iii)
]
states that
\Gamma\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{m+1}{2}\right)=2^{1-m}(m-1)!\sqrt{\pi}.
This yields:

(2.45)

b_{m}s_{m}=\frac{2^{2}\pi^{m+\frac{1}{2}}}{m}\frac{1}{2^{1-m}(m-1)!\sqrt{\pi}}=\frac{2(2\pi)^{m}}{m!}

which is what we wanted.
∎

2.4.
Gaussian vectors

A random vector
X\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}V
is called
Gaussian
if for every
u\in V^{*}
, the random variable
\langle u,X\rangle\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
is Gaussian. The law of a Gaussian random vector is characterized by its
mean

\mathbb{E}X\in V
and covariance
\Sigma
which is the Quadratic form on
V^{*}
given for all
u\in V^{*}
by
\Sigma(u):=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle u,X-\mathbb{E}X\rangle^{2}\right].

A Gaussian vector is said to be
non degenerate
if its covariance is non degenerate, that is if it is positive definite. In what follows, we will only consider
centered
Gaussian vectors, i.e. such that
\mathbb{E}X=0
. In that case, we note that the covariance is given for all
u\in V^{*}
by

(2.46)

\Sigma(u):=\mathbb{E}\left[\langle u,X\rangle^{2}\right].

If
V
is Euclidean, we call
standard Gaussian vector
the centered Gaussian vector whose covariance coincides with the quadratic form induced by the scalar product.

First we compute the Vitale zonoid of a Gaussian vector. Recall that the support function of the Vitale zonoid is given by
Eq.

2.13
.

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
X\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}V
be a non degenerate centered Gaussian vector with covariance
\Sigma
. Then we have
\mathbb{E}\underline{X}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}B_{\Sigma}^{\circ}.

Proof.

First we note that, if
L\in Gl(V)
then
L(X)
is a centered Gaussian vector with covariance
\Sigma\circ L^{T}
. Thus by
Eq.

2.30
and
Eq.

2.14
, it is enough to prove it for one (centered non degenerate) Gaussian vector, thus we will assume that
X\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a standard Gaussian vector. In that case,
\mathbb{E}\underline{X}
is invariant under the action of the orthogonal group
O(m)
and hence must be a (standard Euclidean) ball. To compute its radius, one note that, if
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then
\langle e_{1},X\rangle\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
is a standard Gaussian variable and thus
\mathbb{E}|\langle e_{1},X\rangle|=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}
. Dividing by
2
the gives the result.
∎

2.5.
The Kac-Rice formula with zonoids

The Kac-Rice formula is a tool developed to estimate the size of the zero set of certain random fields. The idea dates back to Kac
[
Kac43
]
who used it to study the average number of zeros of a random polynomial.
In this section, we partially introduce the framework developed by the author and Michele Stecconi in
[
MS24
]
which allows to interpret the Kac-Rive formula in terms of operations on certain zonoids.
Before doing so, let us give, informally, the idea behind the Kac-Rice formula for the reader that never encountered it before.

Suppose that we have a function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
for which
0
is a regular value, i.e., for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f(x)=0
,
f^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
. Then we can see that for every interval
(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}

(2.47)

\#\mathcal{S}\left\{f;(a,b)\right\}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{a}^{b}|f^{\prime}(x)|\,\mathds{1}_{|f(x)|<\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}x

where
\mathds{1}_{|f(x)|<\varepsilon}
is the characteristic function of the set
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\ |\ |f(x)|<\varepsilon\right\}
.
Now, for a
suitable
random function
\mathcal{X}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, one could exchange limits and integrals to obtain

(2.48)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};(a,b)\right\}=\int_{a}^{b}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{X}(x)|<\varepsilon\right)\,\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)|\,|\,|\mathcal{X}(x)|<\varepsilon\right]\,\mathrm{d}x.

Then, for a fixed
x\in(a,b)
, we have, again under suitable assumption on
\mathcal{X}
,
\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{X}(x)|<\varepsilon\right)\to\rho_{\mathcal{X}(x)}(0)
the density of the random variable
\mathcal{X}(x)\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
in
0
and the random function
(\mathcal{X}\,|\,|\mathcal{X}(x)|<\varepsilon)
should converge to a random field
(\mathcal{X}\,|\,\mathcal{X}(x)=0)
.
The whole subtlety of the Kac Rice formula is to determine for which class of random fields such statements are valid. Those are in general deep results. In that case, we obtain the most classic form of the Kac Rice formula:

(2.49)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};(a,b)\right\}=\int_{a}^{b}\rho_{\mathcal{X}(x)}(0)\,\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}(x)|\,|\,\mathcal{X}(x)=0\right]\,\mathrm{d}x.

One of the reason why this formula is so useful is that it belongs to this family of formulas that allows to compute a
global
quantity (in that case the number of zeros) as the integral of a
local quantity
. This formula can then be generalized to higher dimensions and to manifolds. The reader can refer to
[
AT07
,
AW09
]
.

In
[
MS24
]
, Stecconi and the author study appropriate random fields that they call ZKROK fields to which a Kac-Rice formula similar to
Eq.

2.49
in higher dimension and on manifolds apply. To see the precise definition of a ZKROK field, see
[
MS24
, Definition 4.1]
. Here, we choose to present the case of smooth random fields that are supported on a finite dimensional space of functions. This has the advantage of being less technical than the full ZKROK hypotheses but still give a grasp of the generality of the theory and being suitable for a lot of application, including ours. This is contained in
[
MS24
, Section 10.3]
.

Moreover, the work of
[
MS24
]
is to interpret the Kac Rice density (that is the integrand in
Eq.

2.49
) in terms of geometric quantities of certain
zonoids
. This might seem like an extra step in the computation of the density, however, we believe that the advantage is twofold. First it allows to compute a “density” even for fields of non zero codimension. It is the
zonoid section
below that plays this role. Moreover it gives a very geometrical picture of this density. This second point becomes particularly valid when studying monotonicity, see
Fig.

1
below. It is then a matter of comparing zonoids or, in our case, ellipsoids.

Definition 2.25
.

A random field
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}C^{\infty}(m,\mathbb{R}^{k})
is said to be
admissible
if there is a finite dimensional subspace
\mathscr{F}\subset C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}^{k})
endowed with a scalar product such that
\mathcal{X}\in\mathscr{F}
almost surely and such that the following holds:

(i)

for all
x\in M
, the linear map
ev_{x}:\mathscr{F}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
given for all
f\in\mathscr{F}
by
ev_{x}(f):=f(x)
is surjective;

(ii)

\mathcal{X}
admits a continuous density
\rho_{\mathcal{X}}:\mathscr{F}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\rho_{\mathcal{X}}(0)>0
and there exists
\alpha>\dim\mathscr{F}
such that
\rho_{\mathcal{X}}(f)=O(\|f\|^{-\alpha})
when
\|f\|\to\infty
.

When
k=1
, we say that the random field is
scalar
.

Note that being admissible implies that
\mathcal{X}^{-1}(0)
is a smooth submanifold of codimension
k
, see the proof in
[
MS24
, Proposition 10.7]
. Moreover, one can see that, because
\mathscr{F}
is finite dimensional, being admissible does not depend on the choice of the scalar product on
\mathscr{F}
. Moreover, as noted in
[
MS24
]
positivity in
0
condition is probably not needed.

If
\mathscr{F}\subset C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a finite dimensional subspace satisfying
Item

(i)
, then for all
x\in M
, we consider the subspace

(2.50)

\mathscr{F}_{x}:=\left\{f\in\mathscr{F}\ |\ f(x)=0\right\}\subset\mathscr{F}.

Equivalentely, one has
\mathscr{F}_{x}=\ker ev_{x}
. In particular, because of the surjectivity condition, this subspace has codimension
k
. Moreover, note that in the case
k=1
,
\mathscr{F}_{x}=ev_{x}^{\perp}
.

We then build, for every
x\in M
, a zonoid in the cotangent space
\Lambda^{k}T^{*}_{x}M
associated to admissible random fields. Recall the notation for segments
Eq.

2.1
and the definition of the Minkowski integral in
Definition

2.3
.

Definition 2.26
.

For every admissible random field
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}\subset C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R}^{k})
and every
x\in M
, we let:

(2.51)

\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x):=\frac{1}{\|ev_{x}^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge ev_{x}^{k}\|}\int_{\mathscr{F}_{x}}\quad\underline{D_{x}f^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge D_{x}f^{k}}\quad\rho_{\mathcal{X}}(f)\,\mathrm{d}f

where
ev_{x}:=(ev_{x}^{1},\ldots,ev_{x}^{k})\in(\mathscr{F}^{*})^{k}
is the evaluation at a point, i.e.
ev_{x}(f):=f(x)
for all
f\in\mathscr{F}
,
D_{x}f^{i}
denotes the differential of the function
f^{i}
at the point
x\in M
and where
\mathrm{d}f
denotes the integration with respect to the Lebesgue measure on the subspace
\mathscr{F}_{x}
.

One of the main results of
[
MS24
]
states that these zonoids compute geometric quantities of the zero set. We fix a Riemannian metric on
M
. The following is
[
MS24
, Corollary 7.2.]
.

Proposition 2.27
.

Let
\mathcal{X}_{1}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}C^{\infty}\left(M,\mathbb{R}^{k_{1}}\right),\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}C^{\infty}\left(M,\mathbb{R}^{k_{n}}\right)
be independent admissible random fields such that
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{n}=m
and let
U\subset M
be an open set, we have:

(2.52)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n};U\right\}=\int_{U}\ell(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}(x)\wedge\cdots\wedge\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{n}}(x))\mathrm{d}M(x).

In the case where
n=m
and
k_{1}=\cdots,k_{n}=1
, we get

(2.53)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n};U\right\}=m!\int_{U}\MV(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}(x),\ldots,\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{m}}(x))\mathrm{d}M(x),

and in particular if
\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{m}
are identically distributed:

(2.54)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{1};U\right\}=m!\int_{U}\vol(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{1}}(x))\mathrm{d}M(x)

where the volume and mixed volume are computed in the metric given by the Riemannian structure.

The zonoid section also satisfies a nice pull-back property. The following is
[
MS24
, Theorem B]
in the particular case of an embedding.

Proposition 2.28
.

Let
\iota:S\hookrightarrow M
be an embedded submanifold. Then
\mathcal{X}|_{S}:=\mathcal{X}\circ\iota
is admissible and we have for all
x\in S
:

(2.55)

\zeta_{\mathcal{X}|_{S}}(x)=\pi_{x}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))

where
\pi_{x}:=(D_{x}\iota)^{T}:T^{*}_{x}M\to T^{*}_{x}S
is the projection induced by the embedding.

Remark 2.29
.

The transversality assumption in
[
MS24
, Theorem B]
is not needed in the particular case of admissible fields. Indeed if
\mathcal{X}
is admissible, it is clear that
\mathcal{X}|_{S}
is also admissible. Therefore
0
is a regular value of
\mathcal{X}|_{S}
almost surely which is equivalent to saying that
\mathcal{X}^{-1}(0)
and
S
are transversal almost surely.

3.
GRFs and Adler-Taylor theory

We now specialize to the Gaussian case. We fix an admissible scalar (centered) Gaussian Random Field (GRF), that is, a non degenerate Gaussian vector
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}\subset C^{\infty}(M)
in a subspace
\mathscr{F}
of dimension
d<\infty
that satisfies the surjectivity condition
(i)
in
Definition

2.25
.

3.1.
General theory

We start with the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

The
covariance function
of
\mathcal{X}
is the function
\mathrm{K}:M\times M\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(3.1)

\mathrm{K}(x,y):=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(x)\mathcal{X}(y)\right].

We will also consider the
diagonal covariance function

\mathrm{K}(x):=\mathrm{K}(x,x)
. We will often abuse notation and call the latest also the covariance function.

Note that, if
\mathcal{X}
is admissible, the surjectivity condition in
Definition

2.25
is equivalent to the fact that for all
x\in M
, there is
f\in\mathscr{F}
such that
f(x)\neq 0
. It implies that
\mathrm{K}(x)
is strictly positive for all
x\in M
. The covariance
function

\mathrm{K}
is not to be confused with the covariance
\Sigma
of
\mathcal{X}
as a Gaussian vector of
\mathscr{F}
which is a quadratic form on
\mathscr{F}^{*}
. Although, one can check that the covariance function can be obtained by evaluating
\Sigma
at the linear form
ev_{x}\in\mathscr{F}^{*}
.

In the following we endow
\mathscr{F}
with the scalar product induced by the covariance
\Sigma
of
\mathcal{X}
.

Remark 3.2
.

If one uses this scalar product to identify
\mathscr{F}^{*}\cong\mathscr{F}
, then,
ev_{x}\in\mathscr{F}\subset C^{\infty}(M)
and one can then check that, as a function, it is given for all
y\in M
by
ev_{x}(y)=\mathrm{K}(x,y)
. Indeed, by definition of the scalar product, we have
ev_{x}(y)=\langle ev_{y},ev_{x}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(y)\mathcal{X}(x)\right]=\mathrm{K}(x,y)
. The covariance function
\mathrm{K}(x,y)
is alo called a
reproducing kernel
and
\mathscr{F}
a (real) Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). A whole theory of RKHS was developed by Aronszajn in the 40s and 50s, see
[
Aro50
]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
x\in M
, we have
\mathrm{K}(x)=\|ev_{x}\|^{2}
.

Proof.

It is just a matter of applying the definitions,
\|ev_{x}\|^{2}=\Sigma(ev_{x})=\mathbb{E}[\langle ev_{x},\mathcal{X}\rangle^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(x)^{2}]=\mathrm{K}(x).

∎

In the case where the random field is Gaussian, the zonoid section from
Definition

2.26
is an
ellipsoid
section that we can compute explicitly. To do that, we consider, for all
x\in M
, the linear map

(3.2)

D_{x}:\mathscr{F}\to T^{*}_{x}M

such that
D_{x}f
is the differential of the function
f
at the point
x
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}\subset C^{\infty}(M)
be an admissible centered Gaussian random field with covariance
\Sigma
. Endow
\mathscr{F}
with the scalar product induced by
\Sigma
. Then, for all
x\in M
, we have

(3.3)

\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\mathrm{K}(x)}}(D_{x}\circ\pi_{x})(B_{\Sigma}^{\circ})

where
\pi_{x}:\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}_{x}
is the orthogonal projection and where recall that
B_{\Sigma}^{\circ}
denotes the dual of the unit ball of the quadratic form
\Sigma
.

Proof.

First we note that, identifying
\mathscr{F}^{*}\cong\mathscr{F}
with the scalar product, the transpose of
\pi_{x}
is the inclusion
\pi_{x}^{T}:\mathscr{F}_{x}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{F}.
We have, by
Proposition

2.24
:

(3.4)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\pi_{x}(B_{\Sigma}^{\circ})=\mathbb{E}\underline{\pi_{x}(\mathcal{X})}=\int_{\mathscr{F}}\underline{\pi_{x}(f)}\quad\frac{e^{-\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\mathrm{d}f=\int_{\mathscr{F}_{x}}\underline{f}\quad\frac{e^{-\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}\mathrm{d}f

where in the last equality we integrated over the fibers of the orthogonal projection
\pi_{x}
. Note that we are integrating
segments
in the sense of
Definition

2.3
, this can be unsettling and the skeptical reader can always replace them with their support function evaluated at a point. However, the author finds it more elegant and direct this way and hopes to convince at least some of the readers.

It remains only to note that
\rho_{\mathcal{X}}(f)=\frac{e^{-\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\|f\|^{2}}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}.

Reintroducing in
Eq.

3.4
and applying the linear map
D_{x}
gives

(3.5)

\frac{1}{2\pi}(D_{x}\circ\pi_{x})(B_{\Sigma}^{\circ})=\int_{\mathscr{F}_{x}}\underline{D_{x}f}\quad\rho_{\mathcal{X}}(f)\mathrm{d}f.

The result follows by dividing both side by
\sqrt{\mathrm{K}(x)}
which, by
Lemma

3.3
, is equal to
\|ev_{x}\|
.
∎

In the spirit of Adler and Taylor
[
AT07
]
, we want to consider this ellipsoid as the unit ball of a certain Riemannian metric.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that the Gaussian random field
\mathcal{X}
is
non degenerate
if
\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
is full dimensional for all
x\in M
. In that case, we define the
Adler-Taylor
(AT) metric on
M
to be the Riemannian metric such that for all
x\in M
, its unit ball
B_{x}\subset T_{x}M
satisfy

(3.6)

\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}B_{x}^{\circ}.

Remark 3.6
.

Note that
\mathcal{X}
being a non degenerate random field is different than
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}
being non degenerate as a Gaussian vector in
\mathscr{F}
. In fact, as mentioned at the beginning of this section, we always assume it is non degenerate
as a Gaussian vector
which is no restriction since we can always restrict to the support of
\mathcal{X}
in
C^{\infty}(M)
. To see that this does not imply being non degenerate
as a GRF
one can consider the case where
\mathscr{F}
is one dimensional and spanned by the constant function in which case, everything is trivially mapped to zero.

The AT metric then compute the expected number of solution of the random field.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a non degenerate admissible scalar GRF and let
U\subset M
be an open set. If we endow
M
with the AT metric induced by
\mathcal{X}
, we obtain

(3.7)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};U\right\}=\frac{2}{s_{m}}\int_{U}\mathrm{d}M

where
\mathrm{d}M
denotes the integration with respect to the Riemannian volume form, i.e.,
\int_{U}\mathrm{d}M
is the Riemannian volume of
U
, and where recall that
s_{m}
is the volume of the Euclidean unit sphere
S^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
.

Proof.

By definition of the AT metric and by
Proposition

2.14
, we have for all
x\in M

\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))=\frac{b_{m}}{(2\pi)^{m}}.

Then, by
Proposition

2.27
, we have
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};U\right\}=\frac{m!b_{m}}{(2\pi)^{m}}\int_{U}\mathrm{d}M.

We conclude using the relation between volumes of spheres and balls in
Lemma

2.23
.
∎

In order to express the AT metric we will use the following notions that will become central in our later study of exponential sums.

Definition 3.8
.

We define the following
potential
function
\Phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
given for all
x\in M
by

(3.8)

\Phi(x):=\frac{1}{2}\log(\mathrm{K}(x)).

Moreover, the
moment map
is defined to be the one form
\mu:M\to T^{*}M
given for all
x\in M
by

(3.9)

\mu(x):=D_{x}\Phi=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(x)D_{x}\mathcal{X}\right].

The name
moment map
will be justified when we will study exponential sums where it plays the same role and share many similarities with the moment map of complex and toric geometry.

Remark 3.9
.

It is common, when studying GRF, to reduce to the case where
\mathrm{K}\equiv 1
which is always possible by substituting
\mathcal{X}
by
\mathcal{X}/\sqrt{\mathrm{K}}
. Since we are interested in the zero set and
\mathrm{K}
is strictly positive, it does not affect the result. This makes some formula like the quadratic form
g_{x}
in
Eq.

3.11
below simpler. However the price to pay is that the functions in
\mathscr{F}
are more complicated. Thus in explicit computations, the gain is not quite substantial. Moreover the moment map
\mu
becomes trivial and its role his completely hidden. Hence why we chose not to use this convention and adopt coordinates and metrics that seem more suited.

For computations, it can be convenient to fix an othonormal basis
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\in\mathscr{F}
. In this basis the random field
\mathcal{X}
is distributed as
\sum_{i}\xi_{i}f_{i}
where
\xi_{i}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian Variables. In that case, we get for all
x\in M
:

(3.10)

\mathrm{K}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}(x)^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{f_{i}(x)}{\mathrm{K}(x)}D_{x}f_{i}.

Proposition 3.10
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is non degenerate, the AT metric is given for every
x\in M
,
v\in T_{x}M
by

(3.11)

g_{x}(v)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(D_{x}\mathcal{X}(v)-\mathcal{X}(x)\langle\mu(x),v\rangle\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(D_{x}\mathcal{X}(v)\right)^{2}\right]-\langle\mu(x),v\rangle^{2}.

Proof.

First let us prove that the two quadratic forms on the right hand side are equal. We have

(3.12)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(D_{x}\mathcal{X}(v)-\mathcal{X}(x)\langle\mu(x),v\rangle\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[D_{x}\mathcal{X}(v)^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(x)^{2}\right]\langle\mu(x),v\rangle^{2}-2\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(x)D_{x}\mathcal{X}(v)\right]\langle\mu(x),v\rangle.

Then it is enough to note that
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(x)^{2}\right]=\mathrm{K}(x)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{X}(x)D_{x}\mathcal{X}(v)\right]=\mathrm{K}(x)\langle\mu(x),v\rangle
.

Now we let
B_{x}\subset T_{x}M
be the unit ball for
g_{x}
. By definition of the AT metric and by
Proposition

3.4
, we need to prove that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{K}(x)}}(D_{x}\circ\pi_{x})(B_{\Sigma}^{\circ})=B_{x}^{\circ}
. In order to do this, let us rewrite more explicitly the projection
\pi_{x}
. Since it is the projection onto the hyperplane orthogonal to
ev_{x}
and using the scalar product to consider
ev_{x}
as an element of
\mathscr{F}
, we get for all
f\in\mathscr{F}
:

(3.13)

\pi_{x}(f)=f-\langle ev_{x},f\rangle\frac{1}{\|ev_{x}\|^{2}}ev_{x}=f-f(x)\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}(x)}ev_{x}.

Recalling
Proposition

2.24
, we get the following.

(3.14)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(D_{x}\circ\pi_{x})(B_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{E}\underline{D_{x}\pi_{x}(\mathcal{X})}=\mathbb{E}\underline{D_{x}\mathcal{X}-(\mathcal{X}(x)/\mathrm{K}(x))D_{x}ev_{x}}.

Note that, for
x
fixed,
(\mathcal{X}(x)/\mathrm{K}(x))
is a constant and not a function and thus is not “differentiated” by
D_{x}
that we consider as a linear map
\mathscr{F}\to T^{*}_{x}M
, thus we indeed have
D_{x}((\mathcal{X}(x)/\mathrm{K}(x))ev_{x})=(\mathcal{X}(x)/\mathrm{K}(x))D_{x}ev_{x}
.

Finally, as an element of
\mathscr{F}
, we have (see
Remark

3.2
):

(3.15)

ev_{x}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(x)\mathcal{X}]=\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot).

Thus we have
D_{x}ev_{x}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(x)D_{x}\mathcal{X}]=\mathrm{K}(x)\mu(x).
Reintroducing in
Eq.

3.14
, we get

(3.16)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{K}(x)}}(D_{x}\circ\pi_{x})(B_{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{K}(x)}}\mathbb{E}\underline{D_{x}\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{X}(x)\mu(x)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}B_{x}

where in the last equality we used
Proposition

2.24
with the fact that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{K}(x)}}(D_{x}\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{X}(x)\mu(x))
is a Gaussian vector with covariance
g_{x}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

If
\mathcal{X}
is non degenerate, for all
x\in M
, we denote by
g^{x}
the quadratic form on
T^{*}_{x}M
that is dual to
g_{x}
, i.e. in the notation of
Section

2.3
,
g^{x}:=(g_{x})^{\circ}
.

Once again, it can be useful, for explicit computations, to express it in an orthonormal basis
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\in\mathscr{F}
. Recalling the notation (
2.28
) for quadratic forms, we obtain:

(3.17)

g_{x}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(D_{x}f_{i}-f_{i}(x)\mu(x)\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(D_{x}f_{i}\right)^{2}-\mu(x)^{2}

It is however not clear how to express the dual

in general. Finding upper and lower bound will be an important part of the study in the next sections.

We gather in the next proposition the equivalent formulation of the non degeneracy of
\mathcal{X}
. The proof is immediate and thus omitted.

Proposition 3.12
.

The Gaussian random field
\mathcal{X}
is non degenerate, if and only if, one of the following properties is satisfied for every

:

(i)

the quadratic form

defined by
Eq.

3.11
is positive definite;

(ii)

the ellipsoid
\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
is of dimension

;

(iii)

.

3.2.
Local monotonicity of GRFs

We now choose
f_{0}\in C^{\infty}(M)\setminus\mathscr{F}
and build the (admissible) random field

(3.18)

\mathcal{X}_{0}:=\mathcal{X}+\xi_{0}f_{0}

where
\xi_{0}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
is a standard Gaussian variable independent of
\mathcal{X}
.
We write

(3.19)

\mathscr{F}_{0}:=\mathscr{F}\oplus\mathbb{R}f_{0}

that we endow with the scalar product that makes this splitting orthogonal and such that
\|f_{0}\|=1
. We also write
\mathrm{K}_{0}
for the (diagonal) covariance function of
\mathcal{X}_{0}
which is given, for all
x\in M
by:

(3.20)

\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)=\mathrm{K}(x)+f_{0}(x)^{2}.

We want to compare the number of expected solutions of
\mathcal{X}_{0}
and of
\mathcal{X}
. In order to do so, we make the following definition.

Definition 3.13
.

We define the one form
\tau
on
M
given for all
x\in M
by

(3.21)

\tau(x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}}\left(f_{0}(x)\mu(x)-D_{x}f_{0}\right).

Moreover, we define
\Psi\in C^{\infty}(M)
given for all
x\in M
by:

(3.22)

\Psi(x):=\left(\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{1+g^{x}(\tau(x))}.

The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 3.14
.

For every open set
U\subset M
and any Riemannian metric on
M
, we have

(3.23)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{0};U\right\}-\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};U\right\}=\int_{U}(\Psi(x)-1)\,m!\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))\,\mathrm{d}M(x).

The proof is essentially based on the following lemma.

Lemma 3.15
.

The AT metric for
\mathcal{X}_{0}
is given, as a quadratic form, for all
x\in M
, by

(3.24)

(g_{0})_{x}=\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}\left(g_{x}+\tau(x)^{2}\right).

Proof.

Let us choose an orthonormal basis
f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
of
\mathscr{F}
. Then,
f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{F}_{0}
and by
Eq.

3.17
, we first express the new moment map
\mu_{0}
as follows:

(3.25)

\mu_{0}(x)=\mu(x)-\frac{f_{0}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}\left(f_{0}(x)\mu(x)-D_{x}f_{0}\right).

Note that we then have:

(3.26)

f_{0}(x)\mu_{0}(x)-D_{x}f_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}\left(f_{0}(x)\mu(x)-D_{x}f_{0}\right).

We can then compute the new metric
g_{0}
. We omit the dependence in
x
in the writing.

(3.27)

\displaystyle g_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{d}(f_{i}\mu_{0}-Df_{i})^{2}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(f_{i}\mu-Df_{i}-\frac{f_{i}f_{0}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}\left(f_{0}\mu-Df_{0}\right)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}(f_{0}\mu_{0}-Df_{0})^{2}

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}g-\frac{2f_{0}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}^{2}\mu-f_{i}Df_{i}\right)+\frac{f_{0}^{2}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}^{3}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}^{2}\right)\left(f_{0}\mu-Df_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{K}^{2}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}^{3}}(f_{0}\mu-Df_{0})^{2}

where in the second equality we used
Eq.

3.25
and in the third we expanded the square and used
Eq.

3.26
. Now we note that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}^{2}\mu-f_{i}Df_{i}=K\mu-K\mu=0
. This yields

(3.30)

g_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}\left(g+\frac{f_{0}^{2}+\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}^{2}}\left(f_{0}\mu-Df_{0}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}\left(g+\tau^{2}\right)

which is what we wanted.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

We note that the factor
\mathrm{K}/\mathrm{K}_{0}
is smaller than one (strictly where
f_{0}
is non zero) and thus contributes to make the metric
g_{0}

smaller
while the extra term
\tau^{2}
contributes to make it
bigger
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.14
.

We already know from
Proposition

2.27
that

(3.31)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};U\right\}=\int_{U}\,m!\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))\,\mathrm{d}M(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}_{0};U\right\}=\int_{U}\,m!\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(x))\,\mathrm{d}M(x).

We will prove that, for all
x\in M
, we have:

(3.32)

\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(x))=\Psi(x)\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))

which will be enough. Let
B_{0}
be the unit ball for the quadratic form
(g_{0})_{x}
, recall that, by definition of the AT metric,
\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(x)=(1/(2\pi))B_{0}^{\circ}
. Moreover, by
Lemma

3.15
, we have

(3.33)

B_{0}^{\circ}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}}\left(B_{g_{x}+\tau(x)^{2}}\right)^{\circ}.

Which yields,

(3.34)

\vol_{m}(B_{0}^{\circ})=\left(\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\vol_{m}(B_{g_{x}+\tau(x)^{2}}^{\circ})=\left(\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{1+g^{x}(\tau(x))}\,\vol_{m}(B_{x}^{\circ})

where in the second equality we used

2.15
.

The use of a basis in the proof of
Lemma

3.15
makes it rather simple and short but maybe some deeper understanding is lost. In fact, the decomposition of the quadratic form
(g_{0})_{x}
in
Lemma

3.15
, can be seen as coming from a splitting of the subspace
(\mathscr{F}_{0})_{x}
which we show in the next proposition.

Proposition 3.17
.

For all
x\in M
, the space
\mathscr{F}_{x}\subset\mathscr{F}\subset\mathscr{F}_{0}
is a subspace of
(\mathscr{F}_{0})_{x}\subset\mathscr{F}_{0}
and we have the orthogonal splitting

(3.35)

(\mathscr{F}_{0})_{x}=\mathscr{F}_{x}\oplus\mathbb{R}\varphi_{x}

where
\varphi_{x}\in\mathscr{F}_{0}
is the function given for all
y\in M
by

(3.36)

\varphi_{x}(y):=f_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{K}(x,y)-\mathrm{K}(x)f_{0}(y).

Moreover, we have
\|\varphi_{x}\|=\sqrt{\mathrm{K}(x)\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}.

Proof.

The fact that
\mathscr{F}_{x}\subset(\mathscr{F}_{0})_{x}
follows from the fact that they are both defined by the equation
f(x)=0
. First we note that, as a function
\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot)\in\mathscr{F}
and thus
f_{0}(x)\,\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot)-\mathrm{K}(x)f_{0}\in\mathscr{F}_{0}
. Moreover, since
\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot)
and
f_{0}
are linearly independent in
\mathscr{F}_{0}
and since
\mathrm{K}(x)>0
, we have that
\varphi_{x}\neq 0
(as an element of
\mathscr{F}_{0}
).

By substituting
y
by
x
in the definition of
\varphi_{x}
, we get
\varphi_{x}(x)=0
, i.e.
\varphi_{x}\in(\mathscr{F}_{0})_{x}
. We now show that it is orthogonal to
\mathscr{F}_{x}
. Indeed, for every
f\in\mathscr{F}_{x}
, we have

(3.37)

\langle\varphi_{x},f\rangle=f_{0}(x)\langle\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot),f\rangle-\mathrm{K}(x)\langle f_{0},f\rangle.

By definition of the scalar product on
\mathscr{F}_{0}
and since
f\in\mathscr{F}_{x}\subset\mathscr{F}
, we have
\langle f_{0},f\rangle=0
. Moreover, as previously observed in (
3.15
), identifying
\mathscr{F}\cong\mathscr{F}^{*}
, we have
\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot)=ev_{x}
. Thus we have
\langle\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot),f\rangle=f(x)=0
since
f\in\mathscr{F}_{x}
. We obtain
\langle\varphi_{x},f\rangle=0
and this proves the orthogonal splitting.

It remains to compute the norm of
\varphi_{x}
. By definition of the norm on
\mathscr{F}_{0}
, we get

(3.38)

\|\varphi_{x}\|^{2}=f_{0}(x)^{2}\|\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot)\|^{2}+\mathrm{K}(x)^{2}=f_{0}(x)^{2}\mathrm{K}(x)+\mathrm{K}(x)^{2}

where, in the second equality, we used the fact that that
\|\mathrm{K}(x,\cdot)\|^{2}=\|ev_{x}\|^{2}=\mathrm{K}(x)
. The result follows by factoring
\mathrm{K}(x)
.
∎

induced by the embedding. Now we have for all

\tau

\pi_{x}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}(x))=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}}\pi_{x}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x)).

vanishes identically on
U
, which means that
\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{X}|_{S},W;U\right\}=0
and this concludes the proof.
∎

As a last remark, we note that whenever
\Phi_{0}
is defined, the function
\Psi
can be completely written in terms of
\Phi_{0}
.

Proposition 3.22
.

Whenever
f_{0}(x)\neq 0
, we have

(3.49)

\Psi(x)=\left(1-\frac{e^{-2\Phi_{0}(x)}}{1+e^{-2\Phi_{0}(x)}}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{1+e^{2\Phi_{0}(x)}}g^{x}\left(D_{x}\Phi_{0}\right)}.

Proof.

The second term in the product on the right hand side should be clear using
Proposition

3.19
.To see the first term, first we recall that
e^{2\Phi_{0}}=\mathrm{K}/f_{0}^{2}
and then we write

(3.50)

\frac{\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}_{0}}=\frac{\mathrm{K}/f_{0}^{2}}{1+\mathrm{K}/f_{0}^{2}}=\frac{e^{2\Phi_{0}}}{1+e^{2\Phi_{0}}}=1-\frac{1}{1+e^{2\Phi_{0}}}=1-\frac{e^{-2\Phi_{0}}}{1+e^{-2\Phi_{0}}}

which is what we wanted.
∎

4.
Gaussian Exponential sums

We now come to our main setting. We fix a finite subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
of size
d:=\#A
. Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{a})_{a\in A}\in\mathbb{R}^{A}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
\alpha_{a}>0
for all
a\in A
. We build the following GRF on
M=\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.1)

\mathcal{E}(x):=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}\alpha_{a}e^{\langle a,x\rangle}

where
\xi_{a}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian variables. We are now in the setting of the previous section with
\mathscr{F}
being spanned by
f_{a}:=\alpha_{a}e^{\langle a,\cdot\rangle}
,
a\in A
. Note that
(f_{a})_{a\in A}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{F}
(for the scalar product induced by the covariance of
\mathcal{E}
).

Finally, we will denote

(4.2)

P:=\conv(A)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

and will call this the
Newton polytope.
We will always assume that
\dim(P)=m
. This is no loss of generality as in the lower dimensional cases the corresponding system of equations would have no solutions generically, see
Remark

4.2
below.

4.1.
Random polynomials

Before starting our study, let us detail a little bit more how it relates to the study of random polynomials. Suppose
A\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}
and write
w_{i}:=e^{x_{i}}
. Then, by considering the Gaussian exponential sum
Eq.

4.1
as a function of
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})
, one gets the Gaussian polynomial:

(4.3)

\mathcal{P}(w):=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}\alpha_{a}w^{a}

where
w^{a}=w_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots w_{m}^{a_{m}}
. The image of the map
x\mapsto w
is the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
. However, it is clear that
\mathcal{P}
is defined on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, or on
(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})^{m}
if we take
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{m}
instead.

Moreover, if we substitute
w_{i}
by
-w_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
in
Eq.

4.3
, it changes
\xi_{a}
to
(-1)^{a_{i}}\xi_{a}
which is still distributed as
\xi_{a}
. This means that the random polynomial
\mathcal{P}
is identically distributed in each orthant defined by some choice of sign of the coordinates. We can thus relate the expected number of zeros of random exponential sums and random polynomials. More precisely, if we write
\mathbb{R}^{*}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, then we have:

(4.4)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{P};\left(\mathbb{R}^{*}\right)^{m}\right\}=2^{m}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}.

Remark 4.1
.

If
L\in Gl_{m}(\mathbb{R})
and if we change
A
by
L(A)
, then the Gaussian exponential sum
Eq.

4.1
, changes to
\mathcal{E}\circ L^{T}
. Since
L
is invertible, this doesn’t change the total number of zeros:
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}\circ L^{T};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}
. Similarly for the corresponding system of random polynomials.

4.2.
Moment map and convex potential

We start by computing the covariance function and potential from
Section

3
. We have, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:

(4.5)

\mathrm{K}(x)=\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{2\langle a,x\rangle}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(x)=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}\,a

where

(4.6)

\lambda_{a}:=\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{2\langle a,x\rangle}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}.

Moreover, we will write

(4.7)

\overline{\mathrm{K}}(x):=e^{-2h_{P}(x)}\mathrm{K}(x)

where recall from
Section

2.1
the support function
h_{P}
of the Newton polytope. We recall also the notation
A^{x}
and
P^{x}
for the (exposed) face from
Section

2.1
. Note that we have

(4.8)

\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathrm{K}}(x)=\|\alpha^{x}\|^{2}+\sum_{a\in A\setminus A^{x}}\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{-2(h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle)}

where
\|\alpha^{x}\|^{2}:=\sum_{a\in A^{x}}\alpha_{a}^{2}.

We can also write down explicitly the AT metric
g_{x}
for
\mathcal{E}
using
Eq.

3.17
. We obtain for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.9)

g_{x}=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(a-\mu(x))^{2}=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}a^{2}-\mu(x)^{2}

Remark 4.2
.

Since
\mu
is a convex combinations of points in
A
, it belongs to the Newton polytope
P
. It follows that the quadratic form
g_{x}
given by
Eq.

4.9
is non degenerate if and only if
\dim(P)=m
. By
Proposition

3.12
, if this is not the case, i.e., if
\dim(P)<m
the GRF
\mathcal{E}
is degenerate and
\vol_{m}(\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))=0
which, in turn, implies that
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=0
. Hence we always assume that
\dim(P)=m
.

Asymptotically, we have the following.

Proposition 4.3
.

We have for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.10)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)=\|\alpha^{x}\|^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\Phi(tx)=h_{P}(x).

Moreover,

(4.11)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mu(tx)=\sum_{a\in A^{x}}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}}{\|\alpha^{x}\|^{2}}a=:\mu_{\infty}^{x}.

Proof.

Note that, by definition of the support function,
h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle>0
for all
a\in A\setminus A^{x}
and
x\neq 0
. Thus the first limit follows simply from
Eq.

4.8
. Moreover, the second limit follows from the first one and the fact that
\Phi(tx)=\tfrac{1}{2}\log(\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx))+th_{P}(x).

Finally, to prove the third one, write

(4.12)

\mu(tx)=\sum_{a\in A}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{-2t(h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle)}}{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)}a=\sum_{a\in A^{x}}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}}{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)}a+\sum_{a\in A\setminus A^{x}}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{-2t(h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle)}}{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)}a.

Using the first limit, we get that
\sum_{a\in A^{x}}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}}{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)}a\to\sum_{a\in A^{x}}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}}{\|\alpha^{x}\|^{2}}a
. Moreover, using again the fact that for all
a\in A\setminus A^{x}
,
h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle>0
, the second term goes to zero and this concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Note that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have
\mu_{\infty}^{x}\in P^{x}.

We will say more about what happens at infinity in
Section

4.5
.

An interesting phenomenon in this setting is the fact that the AT metric can be derived directly from the potential
\Phi
.

Lemma 4.5
.

The potential
\Phi
is strictly convex and we have for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.13)

g_{x}=\frac{1}{2}D^{2}_{x}\Phi

where
D^{2}_{x}\Phi
is the Hessian of the function
\Phi
considered here as a quadratic form.

Proof.

First of all, we note that it is enough to prove the equality of quadratic forms, since
g_{x}
is positive definite, it then follows that
\Phi
is strictly convex. We have

(4.14)

D^{2}_{x}\Phi=\frac{1}{2}\frac{D^{2}_{x}\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{D_{x}\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{D^{2}_{x}\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}-2\mu(x)^{2}.

Furthermore we have
\frac{D^{2}_{x}\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}=4\sum_{a\in A}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{2\langle a,x\rangle}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}a^{2}=4\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}a^{2}.

Reintroducing in the second expression for
g_{x}
in
Eq.

4.9
, it yields
g_{x}=\frac{1}{4}\frac{D^{2}_{x}\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}-\mu(x)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}D^{2}_{x}\Phi
which is what we wanted.
∎

The other remarkable property is the following, that is also one of the reasons for the name
moment map
. We denote by
\interior(P)
the interior of the Newton Polytope
P
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The moment map
\mu
is a diffeomorphism
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{m}\xrightarrow{\sim}\interior(P)
where
\interior(P)
.

Proof.

First of all, consider the expression
\mu(x)=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)a
. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
a\in A
,
\lambda_{a}(x)>0
and
\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)=1
. Therefore
\mu(x)\in\interior(P)
. Moreover,
D_{x}\mu=D^{2}_{x}\Phi
which we proved is always positive definite in
Lemma

4.5
. Thus we only need to prove surjectivity.

Since
\Phi
is convex, we have from
Lemma

2.8
that the image of
\mu
is convex. Thus it is enough to prove that the closure of the image of
\mu
contains every vertex of
P
. A vertex is a
0
dimensional face of
P
, i.e.,
v
is a vertex of
P
if and only if
v\in A
and there is
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\}
such that
A^{x}=\{v\}
. For such
x
, it follows from
Eq.

4.11
, that
\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mu(tx)=v
and thus
v
belongs to the closure of the image of
\mu
and this concludes the proof.

∎

We obtain the following which is one of the main results of this paper.

Theorem 4.7
.

For all open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

(4.15)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{\tfrac{m-2}{2}}s_{m}}\int_{\mu(U)}\sqrt{detD^{2}_{p}\Phi^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}p

where
\Phi^{*}:\interior(P)\to\mathbb{R}
is the Legendre transform of
\Phi
and where
s_{m}
denotes the
m
-dimensional volume of the unit sphere
S^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
. In particular, we have, for
U=\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.16)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{\tfrac{m-2}{2}}s_{m}}\vol_{g}(P)

where
\vol_{g}(P)
denotes the Riemannian volume of
P
for the metric given for all
p\in P
by
D^{2}_{p}\Phi^{*}
.

Proof.

From
Proposition

3.7
, we have

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s_{m}}\int_{U}\sqrt{\det g_{x}}\,dx

(4.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{2^{\frac{m}{2}}s_{m}}\int_{U}\sqrt{\det D^{2}_{x}\Phi}\,dx

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{m-2}{2}}s_{m}}\int_{\mu(U)}\frac{\sqrt{\det D^{2}_{\mu^{-1}(p)}\Phi}}{\det D^{2}_{\mu^{-1}(p)}\Phi}\,dp

(4.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{m-2}{2}}s_{m}}\int_{\mu(U)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det D^{2}_{\mu^{-1}(p)}\Phi}}\,dp

where in the second equality we used
Lemma

4.5
and for the third equality we applied the change of variable
p=\mu(x)
remembering that
D_{x}\mu=D^{2}_{x}\Phi
. We conclude using the property of Legendre transform, see
Corollary

2.10
.
∎

We deduce the following lower bound. We write
\diam(P)
for the diameter of
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, that is

(4.21)

\diam(P):=\max\left\{\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|\ |\ p_{1},p_{2}\in P\right\}.

Corollary 4.8
.

We have

(4.22)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}>\frac{1}{2^{m-1}s_{m}}\,\frac{\vol_{m}(P)}{\diam(P)^{m}}

where
\vol_{m}(P)
denotes the classical Euclidean volume of
P
.

Proof.

We use the explicit expression of the AT metric
Eq.

4.9
and Cauchy-Schwartz (more precisely
Proposition

2.21
) to obtain that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

(4.23)

g_{x}\leq\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)\,\|a-\mu(x)\|^{2}<\diam(P)^{2}.

where recall the meaning of the bound in
Definition

2.19
and where, for the second inequality we used the fact that
a\in P
and
\mu(x)\in\interior(P)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
a\in A
and that
\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)=1
.

Equivalently, using
Lemma

4.5
, we have
D^{2}_{x}\Phi<2\diam(P)^{2}
. Using the property of Legendre transform
Corollary

2.10
, we have
D^{2}_{\mu(x)}\Phi^{*}=(D^{2}_{x}\Phi)^{\circ}
. By
Proposition

2.22
, we obtain for all
p\in P
,
D^{2}_{p}\Phi^{*}>1/(2\diam(P)^{2})
and thus
\sqrt{\det D^{2}_{p}\Phi^{*}}>(2\diam(P))^{-\frac{m}{2}}.

Using
Eq.

4.16
, we obtain

(4.24)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{\tfrac{m-2}{2}}s_{m}}\int_{P}\sqrt{\det D^{2}_{p}\Phi^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}p>\frac{1}{2^{m-1}s_{m}\diam(P)^{m}}\int_{P}1\,\mathrm{d}p

which is what we wanted
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Note that the left hand side in
Eq.

4.22
is affine invariant, meaning that, changing
A
by an affine transformation does not change the expected total number of solutions (see
Remark

4.1
), but the right hand side is not. It would be interesting to understand the maximizers in an affine class. Maximizing the volume with a fixed diameter is known as the
isodiametric problem
. It is a surprising fact that, in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, among the polygons with
k
vertices, the regular
k
-gon is
not
optimal for
k>5
and even, see
[
Mos06
]
. It is a natural and interesting question to ask if the exponential sums corresponding to these maximizers (see for example
[
Mos06
, Figure 1]
) also maximize the expected total number of solutions among exponential sums with a support of size
k
. Although
Eq.

4.22
is not really a strong evidence in that direction since the use of Cauchy Schwartz in the proof is rather brutal and the inequality rather coarse.

4.3.
Monotonicity

We now choose
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus A
, and
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{a_{0}}>0
and let
f_{0}:=\alpha_{0}e^{\langle a_{0},\cdot\rangle}
. We define the exponential sum

(4.25)

\mathcal{E}_{0}:=\mathcal{E}+\xi_{0}f_{0}

where
\xi_{0}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
is a standard Gaussian variable independent of
\mathcal{E}
. The goal of this section is to use the general case of
Section

3.2
to compare the local expected number of solutions
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}
and
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};U\right\}
depending on the choice of
a_{0}
.

Remark 4.10
.

If we choose
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and shift
a\to a-b
then we have
f_{a-b}=e^{-\langle b,\cdot\rangle}f_{a}
for all
a\in A
and similarly for
a=a_{0}
. Thus
\mathcal{E}
changes to
e^{-\langle b,\cdot\rangle}\mathcal{E}
and similarly for
\mathcal{E}_{0}
. Since
e^{-\langle b,\cdot\rangle}
is a strictly positive function on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, this does not affect the zero set and thus without loss of generality we can shift
A
and
a_{0}
by the same vector
b
(in fact one can show that the zonoid section does not change when multiplying the random field by a positive function). Choosing
b=a_{0}
, this means that we can assume without loss of generality that
a_{0}=0
. We will sometimes do so, especially in proofs, as it makes computations easier. Nevertheless we will state our results for an arbitrary
a_{0}
. This has the advantage to be easier to interpret and easier to use in eventual explicit computations/applications.

Recall the definition of
\Phi_{0}
in
Eq.

3.40
. Note that
\log f_{0}(x)=\log(\alpha_{0})+\langle a_{0},x\rangle
is affine and thus
\Phi_{0}=\Phi-\log f_{0}
is also convex and we have

(4.26)

D_{x}\Phi_{0}=\mu(x)-a_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad D^{2}\Phi=D^{2}\Phi_{0}.

We also recall the function
\Psi
from
Section

3.2
that determines the local monotonicity of the expected number of solutions and in particular the expression obtained in
Proposition

3.22
.

We define the following open sets:

(4.27)

U_{-}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ \Psi(x)<1\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad U_{+}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ \Psi(x)>1\right\}.

It follows from
Theorem

3.14
, that for all open set
U\subset U_{-}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};U\right\}<\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}
and for all
V\subset U_{+}
,
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};V\right\}>\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};V\right\}
. The first result is the following.

Theorem 4.11
.

If
a_{0}\in\interior(P)
then
U_{-}
is non empty. Therefore, there exists
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

(4.28)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0};U\right\}<\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};U\right\}.

Proof.

The proof is surprisingly simple at this point. From
Lemma

4.6
, we know that
\mu
is an isomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\interior(P)
. Thus there exists
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\mu(x_{0})=a_{0}
. We then have
g^{x_{0}}(\mu(x_{0})-a_{0})=0
. Reintroducing in
Eq.

3.49
, we get

(4.29)

\Psi(x_{0})=\left(1-\frac{e^{-2\Phi_{0}(x_{0})}}{1+e^{-2\Phi_{0}(x_{0})}}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}<1,

i.e.,
x_{0}\in U_{-}
and this concludes the proof.
∎

Next we prove a convenient characterization of
U_{\pm}
.

Proposition 4.12
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x\in U_{-}
if and only if

(4.30)

g^{x}(\mu(x)-a_{0})<m+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\binom{m+1}{k+1}e^{-2k\Phi_{0}(x)}+e^{-2m\Phi_{0}(x)}.

Similarly
x\in U_{+}
if and only if the reverse inequality holds.

Proof.

First of all we note that
\Psi(x)<1
iff and only if
\Psi(x)^{2}<1
. From
Definition

3.13
, we get:

(4.31)

\displaystyle\Psi(x)^{2}<1

\displaystyle\iff g^{x}(\mu(x)-a_{0})<\left(\left(\frac{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\right)^{m}-1\right)\frac{\mathrm{K}_{0}(x)}{f_{0}(x)^{2}}

(4.32)

\displaystyle\iff g^{x}(\mu(x)-a_{0})<\left(\left(1+\frac{f_{0}(x)^{2}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\right)^{m}-1\right)\left(1+\frac{\mathrm{K}(x)}{f_{0}(x)^{2}}\right)

(4.33)

\displaystyle\iff g^{x}(\mu(x)-a_{0})<\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\left(\frac{f_{0}(x)^{2}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\right)^{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom{m}{k}\left(\frac{f_{0}(x)^{2}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}\right)^{k-1}.

To conclude, we substitute
\frac{f_{0}(x)^{2}}{\mathrm{K}(x)}=e^{-2\Phi_{0}(x)}
and use the identity
\binom{m}{k}+\binom{m}{k+1}=\binom{m+1}{k+1}
. Since all the quantities are non negative, the same proof works replacing “
<
” by “
>
”.
∎

We obtain the following.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
a_{0}
be such that
d(a_{0},P)>\frac{\diam(P)}{m}
. Then
U_{-}
is non empty and unbounded.

Proof.

We assume, without loss of generality, that
a_{0}=0
. Then, there is
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|\tilde{x}\|=1
such that
-h_{P}(\tilde{x})=d(0,P)
. By
Remark

2.1
, we can choose
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
\|x\|=1
such that
\dim(A^{x})=0
and
-h_{P}(x)>\diam(P)/m
. We fix such
x
, we write
\{a^{x}\}:=A^{x}
and we will show that, for all
t>0
big enough, we have
tx\in U_{-}
.

Since the support function
h_{P}
is negative in
x
, and since
\Phi
approximates the support function (see
Proposition

4.3
), it follows that, for all
t>0
big enough,
\Phi(tx)<0
. In that case, the right hand side in
Proposition

4.12
, satisfies:

(4.34)

m+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\binom{m+1}{k+1}e^{-2k\Phi_{0}(tx)}+e^{-2m\Phi_{0}(tx)}>c_{1}e^{-2mth_{P}(x)}

for some constant
c_{1}>0
.

We want to show that, asymptotically,
g^{tx}(\mu(tx))
is bounded from above by a term that grows slower than the right hand side in
Eq.

4.34
, then we could conclude using
Proposition

4.12
. For this, we need a non trivial lower bound on the quadratic form
g_{x}
. Let us denote
\eta(tx):=a^{x}-\mu(tx)
. It follows from
Proposition

4.3
that
\eta(tx)
goes to zero. Moreover, the AT metric (see
Eq.

4.9
) satisfies:

(4.35)

g_{tx}=\lambda_{a^{x}}(tx)\eta(tx)^{2}+\sum_{a\neq a^{x}}\lambda_{a}(tx)(a-a^{x}+\eta(tx))^{2}\geq\sum_{a\neq a^{x}}\lambda_{a}(tx)(a-a^{x}+\eta(tx))^{2}.

Now let
\bar{a}\in A\setminus a^{x}
be such that
\lambda_{\bar{a}}(tx)\leq\lambda_{a}(tx)
for all
a\in A\setminus a^{x}
. Since
\lambda_{a}(tx)=\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{t\langle a,x\rangle}/\mathrm{K}(tx)
, for
t
big enough,
\bar{a}
is a point in
A
that is the furthest in the direction
-x
, i.e.
\bar{a}
is any point in
A^{-x}
and in particular does not depend on
t
. We note that:

(4.36)

h_{P}(x)-\langle\bar{a},x\rangle=\langle a^{x}-\bar{a},x\rangle\leq d(a^{x},\bar{a})\leq\diam(P).

Moreover we get

(4.37)

g_{tx}\geq\lambda_{\bar{a}}(tx)\sum_{a\neq a_{x}}(a-a^{x}+\eta(tx))^{2}.

Since
P
is full dimensional,
\left\{a^{x}-a\ |\ a\in A\setminus a^{x}\right\}
spans the whole space and thus the quadratic form
\sum_{a\neq a_{x}}(a-a^{x})^{2}
is positive definite. Since
\eta(tx)
goes to zero, it follows that, there is
c_{2}>0
such that for all
t>0
big enough we have

(4.38)

g_{tx}\geq c_{2}\lambda_{\bar{a}}(tx).

It follows that, for
t>0
big enough,

(4.39)

g^{tx}(\mu(tx))\leq\frac{1}{c_{2}}\lambda_{\bar{a}}(tx)^{-1}\|\mu(tx)\|^{2}=\frac{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)\|\mu(tx)\|^{2}}{c_{2}}e^{2t(h_{P}(x)-\langle\bar{a},x\rangle)}.

The quantity
\frac{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)\|\mu(tx)\|^{2}}{c_{2}}
goes to a positive constant (again by
Proposition

4.3
). Thus, using
Eq.

4.36
, we get that there is
c_{3}>0
such that for all
t>0
big enough:

(4.40)

g^{tx}(\mu(tx))\leq c_{3}e^{2t\diam(P)}.

Since we chose
x
such that
-mh_{P}(x)>\diam(P)
, we get that, for
t>0
big enough

(4.41)

c_{3}e^{2t\diam(P)}\leq c_{1}e^{-2mth_{P}(x)}

and this concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

From this proof we can deduce a little bit more information on
U_{-}
. Indeed, we can see that for all
x
such that
\|x\|=1
and
-(m-1)h_{P}(x)>\diam(P)
, the ray
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}x
intersects
U_{-}
in an unbounded component. To know exactly the intersections, that is to determine the constants
c_{i}
seems harder. In particular, it is not clear to the author how to express
c_{2}
explicitly.

We conclude this section by applying
Proposition

3.21
in our context to find a convex hypersurface where the expected number of solution decreases. For all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the sublevel sets

(4.42)

\Lambda_{t}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\ |\ \Phi_{0}(x)\leq t\right\}.

By
Lemma

4.5
,
\Lambda_{t}
is convex. Moreover, from the limit
Proposition

4.3
, when
a_{0}\in\interior(P)
,
\frac{1}{t}\Lambda_{t}
approximates and is contained in
(P-a_{0})^{\circ}
. Writing
\partial\Lambda_{t}
for its boundary, which is a smooth convex hypersurface of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and write
t_{min}
for the minimal value attained by
\Phi_{0}
. Then we note that, by strict convexity, any
t>t_{min}
is a regular value of
\Phi_{0}
. We obtain the following, as a direct consequence of
Proposition

3.21
.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
m>1
and let
t>t_{min}
and let
S_{t}:=\partial\Lambda_{t}
. For any
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and for any admissible random fields
W\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}C^{\infty}(S_{t},\mathbb{R}^{m-2})
on
S_{t}
and all open set
U\subset S_{t}
, we have:

(4.43)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0}|_{S_{t}},W;U\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}|_{S_{t}},W;U\right\}.

In particular

(4.44)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{0}|_{S_{t}};U\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}|_{S_{t}};U\right\}.

In both cases, the inequality is an equality if and only if the right hand side is zero in which case every term is equal to zero.

4.4.
Tensors and Aronszajn multiplication

In this section, we want to keep track of the coefficients in the exponential sums. For every finite subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we write
\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}:=\left\{(\alpha_{a})_{a\in A}\ |\ \alpha_{a}>0\right\}
. As before, for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
, we define the exponential sum

(4.45)

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(\cdot):=\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_{a}\xi_{a}e^{\langle a,\cdot\rangle}

where
\xi_{a}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian variables. Similarly, we will indicate all the associated functions with a subscript:
\mathrm{K}_{\alpha},\Phi_{\alpha},\mu_{\alpha},
etc …

We want to define operations on the coefficients which will in turn give operations on the exponential sums. First let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be finite sets. Then
A\times B
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Note also that
\mathbb{R}^{A\times B}\cong\mathbb{R}^{A}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{B}
and that the tensor product preserves the positive cones. In other words, given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
we define
\alpha\otimes\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A\times B}
given for all
(a,b)\in A\times B
by

(4.46)

(\alpha\otimes\beta)_{(a,b)}:=\alpha_{a}\beta_{b}.

Then we define the Gaussian exponential sum
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
over
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
given for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
by:

(4.47)

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(x,y):=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\otimes\beta}(x,y)=\sum_{a\in A,\,b\in B}\xi_{ab}\alpha_{a}\beta_{b}e^{\langle b,x\rangle}e^{\langle a,y\rangle}

where
\xi_{ab}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian variables.

Remark 4.16
.

Note that technically we are not defining an operation on the random fields
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
but rather on their law. That is, the random field
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
is not a function of the random fields
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
but an entirely new field whose
law
depends on the law of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
. In this paper we are only interested in the laws since we are considering only expectations. We shall therefore continue to abuse notation this way and denote the operations we define as operations on random fields although they are operations on the probability measures.

Next we define another operation which originally appears in
[
Mal23
]
.

Definition 4.17

(Aronszajn’s multiplication)
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be finite subsets and consider their Minkowski sum
A+B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
, we define their
Aronszajn multiplication

\alpha\odot\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A+B}
to be given for all
c\in A+B
by

(4.48)

(\alpha\odot\beta)_{c}:=\sqrt{\sum_{a+b=c}\alpha_{a}^{2}\beta_{b}^{2}}

where the sum runs over all the
a\in A
and
b\in B
such that
a+b=c
.
We also consider the corresponding operation on exponential sums
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\odot\mathcal{E}_{\beta}:=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\odot\beta}
that we also call Aronszajn’s multiplication.

The tensor product and Aronszajn’s multiplication are commutative operations. We show next that they also commute with each other.

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
A,A^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B,B^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be finite sets. For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
,
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A^{\prime}}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
and
beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B^{\prime}}
, we have

(4.49)

(\alpha\otimes\beta)\odot(\alpha^{\prime}\otimes\beta^{\prime})=(\alpha\odot\alpha^{\prime})\otimes(\beta\odot\beta^{\prime}).

Proof.

This is just a straightforward computation. Indeed, let
(c_{1},c_{2})\in(A\times B)+(A^{\prime}\times B^{\prime})=(A+A^{\prime})\times(B+B^{\prime})
and let
\gamma:=(\alpha\otimes\beta)\odot(\alpha^{\prime}\otimes\beta^{\prime})
. Then we have:

(4.50)

\displaystyle\gamma_{(c_{1},c_{2})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{(a,b)+(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=(c_{1},c_{2})}(\alpha\otimes\beta)_{(a,b)}^{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\otimes\beta^{\prime})_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}^{2}}

(4.51)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{a+a^{\prime}=c_{1}}\sum_{b+b^{\prime}=c_{2}}\alpha_{a}^{2}\,\beta_{b}^{2}\,(\alpha^{\prime})_{a^{\prime}}^{2}\,(\beta^{\prime})_{b^{\prime}}^{2}}

(4.52)

\displaystyle=(\alpha\odot\alpha^{\prime})_{c_{1}}\,(\beta\odot\beta^{\prime})_{c_{2}}

which is what we wanted.
∎

We can give an interpretation of Aronszajn’s multiplication of exponential sums in term of tensor.

Proposition 4.19
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be finite sets and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
. Consider the diagonal embedding
\Delta:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, that is
\Delta(x):=(x,x)
. Then, in law, we have the equality

(4.53)

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\odot\mathcal{E}_{\beta}=\left(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\right)\circ\Delta.

Proof.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

(4.54)

\left(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}\right)(x,x)=\sum_{a\in A,\,b\in B}\alpha_{a}\beta_{b}\xi_{ab}e^{\langle a+b,x\rangle}=\sum_{c\in A+B}\left(\sum_{a+b=c}\alpha_{a}\beta_{b}\xi_{ab}\right)e^{\langle c,x\rangle}.

Then it remains only to see that
\sum_{a+b=c}\alpha_{a}\beta_{b}\xi_{ab}
is distributed as
(\alpha\odot\beta)_{c}\xi_{c}
where
\xi_{c}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
is a standard Gaussian variable.
∎

According to
Theorem

4.7
, the expected number of solutions of Gaussian exponential sums
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha};\cdot\right\}
can be understood from the convex potential
\Phi_{\alpha}
. We thus describe how this potential transforms under the operations we just defined.

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be finite subsets and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
. For all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

(4.55)

\Phi_{\alpha\otimes\beta}(x,y)=\Phi_{\alpha}(x)+\Phi_{\beta}(y).

Moreover, if
n=m
, then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

(4.56)

\Phi_{\alpha\odot\beta}(x)=\Phi_{\alpha}(x)+\Phi_{\beta}(x).

Proof.

We consider

(4.57)

\mathrm{K}_{\alpha\otimes\beta}(x,y)=\sum_{(a,b)\in A\times B}\alpha_{a}^{2}\beta_{b}^{2}e^{2\langle(a,b),(x,y)\rangle}=\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B}\alpha_{a}^{2}\beta_{b}^{2}e^{2\langle a,x\rangle}e^{2\langle b,y\rangle}=\mathrm{K}_{\alpha}(x)\mathrm{K}_{\beta}(y).

Taking the logarithm and dividing by
2
gives the first equality.
The second equality is done similarly.
∎

Remark 4.21
.

In Aronszajn’s theory of RKHS, this operation corresponds to a product of reproducing kernels, see
[
Aro50
, Section I-8]
.

We can then deduce properties of the expected number of solutions.

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be finite subsets and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
. For all
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(4.58)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta};U\times V\right\}=2\,\frac{s_{m}s_{n}}{s_{m+n}}\,\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha};U\right\}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\beta};V\right\}.

Proof.

The proof is a direct consequence of the previous proposition and the fact that the AT metric is the Hessian of the convex potential (
Lemma

4.5
). Indeed, let us denote by
g
, respectively
g_{\alpha}
and
g_{\beta}
the AT metric on
\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the GRF
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
, respectively of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
. Then for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
it follows from
Lemma

4.5
that

(4.59)

g_{(x,y)}=(g_{\alpha})_{x}\oplus(g_{\beta})_{y}.

Thus we have
\det g_{(x,y)}=\det(g_{\alpha})_{x}\det(g_{\beta})_{y}
. We then apply
Proposition

3.7
to get

(4.60)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta};U\times V\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s_{m+n}}\int_{U\times V}\sqrt{\det(g_{\alpha})_{x}}\sqrt{\det(g_{\beta})_{y}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

(4.61)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{s_{m+n}}\left(\int_{U}\sqrt{\det(g_{\alpha})_{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\int_{V}\sqrt{\det(g_{\beta})_{y}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right).

To conclude, we apply again
Proposition

3.7
to recognize
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha};U\right\}
and
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\beta};V\right\}
.
∎

Remark 4.23
.

If we consider
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\cdot\right\}
as a measure, this proposition says that
\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta};\cdot\right\}=c\,\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha};\cdot\right\}\otimes\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\beta};\cdot\right\}
where on the right hand side we have the tensor product of measures and
c=2\frac{s_{m}s_{n}}{s_{m+n}}
.

Remark 4.24
.

Although the proof is quite simple the result is not entirely trivial since the GRF
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(x,y)
is
not
distributed as
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(x)\mathcal{E}_{\beta}(y)
when taking
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
independent. Indeed the product of independent Gaussian
\xi_{a}\xi_{b}
is not a Gaussian variable and therefore not distributed as
\xi_{(a,b)}
. However,
Proposition

4.22
tells us that the density of expected number of zeros is the same (up to a constant).

A direct consequence is the following special case.

Corollary 4.25
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}
be finite sets, let
\alpha^{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
and let
\alpha:=\alpha^{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\alpha^{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
where
A=A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Then for every open sets
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}
, writing
U:=U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{m}
, we have:

(4.62)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha};U\right\}=\frac{m!b_{m}}{2^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha^{i}};U_{i}\right\}.

With the same proof as in
Proposition

4.22
, we can describe the transformation of the AT metric under the Aronszajn multiplication.

Lemma 4.26
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be finite subsets and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{B}
. Denote by
g
, respectively
g_{\alpha}
and
g_{\beta}
the AT metric on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
of the GRF
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\odot\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
, respectively of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\beta}
. Then for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

(4.63)

g_{x}=(g_{\alpha})_{x}+(g_{\beta})_{x}.

For every positive integer
d
, we denote by
\mathcal{E}^{\odot d}
the
d
-th power of
\mathcal{E}
for the Aronszajn multiplication. As a direct consequence of the previous proposition,
we obtain the following which is
[
Mal23
, Theorem 1.3]
(extended to admissible fields).

Proposition 4.27
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a finite set and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
. For every admissible random field
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m-1})
independent of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, every positive integer
d
and every
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

(4.64)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\right)^{\odot d},\mathcal{X};U\right\}=\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha},\mathcal{X};U\right\}

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.26
, the AT metric
g_{x}
associated to
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\odot d}
is

(4.65)

g_{x}=d\cdot(g_{\alpha})_{x}=(g_{\alpha})_{x}\circ\sqrt{d}

where on the last term we wrote
\sqrt{d}
for the linear operator that just multiplies by
\sqrt{d}
(recall that
(g_{\alpha})_{x}
is a quadratic form). Since the associated zonoid is (up to a multiplicative constant) the dual of the unit ball for this metric, by
Eq.

2.32
, the zonoids transform as

(4.66)

\zeta_{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\odot d}}(x)=\sqrt{d}\cdot\zeta_{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}(x).

It remains to apply the Kac-Rice formula, that is
Proposition

2.27
to get

(4.67)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}\right)^{\odot d},\mathcal{X};U\right\}=\int_{U}\ell(\zeta_{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\odot d}}(x)\wedge\zeta_{\mathcal{X}}(x))\mathrm{d}x.

We conclude by
Eq.

4.66
, by linearity of
\ell
and multilinearity of the wedge product.
∎

Example 4.28

(Kostlan polynomial)
.

Let
A:=\{0,e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\alpha\equiv 1
. Then the corresponding Gaussian Exponential sum is of the form

(4.68)

\mathcal{E}(x)=\xi_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}e^{x_{i}}

where
\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{m}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian. If we write
w_{i}:=e^{x_{i}}
, we obtain the random polynomial
\mathcal{P}(w):=\xi_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}w_{i}
, which in that case is just an affine function. It follows that

(4.69)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{P};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}.

Now we consider the
d
-th power of the Aronszajn multiplication. We have

(4.70)

A^{+d}=\left\{a\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\ |\ |a|:=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}\leq d\right\}

and
(\alpha^{\odot d})_{a}=\sqrt{\binom{d}{a}}
where
\binom{d}{a}:=\frac{d}{a_{1}!\cdots a_{m}!(d-|a|)!}
. If we consider again the Gaussian exponential sum
\mathcal{E}^{\odot d}
as a function of
w
, we obtain the
Kostlan polynomial
:

(4.71)

\mathcal{P}^{d}(w):=\sum_{|a|\leq d}\sqrt{\binom{d}{a}}\xi_{a}w^{a}

where
\xi_{a}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are iid standard Gaussian variables and where
w^{a}:=w_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots w_{m}^{a_{m}}
. We then obtain, from
Proposition

4.27
, Kostlan’s celebrated result, see
[
Kos93
]
:

(4.72)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{P}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}={2^{m}}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\odot d};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}={2^{m}}\frac{(\sqrt{d})^{m}}{2^{m}}=d^{\frac{m}{2}}.

We conclude with the following bounds. The upper bound was already obtained by Malajovitch in
[
Mal23
, Theorem 1.6]
but the lower bound is new.

Theorem 4.29
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be finite sets, let
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
, let
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
be positive integers and write
d:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}
. Then for every admissible random field
\mathcal{X}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{m-1})
independents of all the exponential sums, and every open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

(4.73)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{d_{j}}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{j}},\mathcal{X};U\right\}\leq\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\odot d_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{n}}^{\odot d_{n}},\mathcal{X};U\right\}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{d_{j}}\,\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{j}},\mathcal{X};U\right\}

Proof.

Let
g
be the AT metric for the GRF
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\odot d_{1}}\odot\cdots\odot\mathcal{E}_{\alpha_{n}}^{\odot d_{n}}
, then by
Lemma

4.26
, we have, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
g_{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}(g_{\alpha_{j}})_{x}
. The result follows by applying
Proposition

2.17
.
∎

4.5.
Behavior at infinity and Veronese map

In this section, we study the quantities that arise when studying the limit
tx\to\infty
for a fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and for
t
going to
+\infty
. From the point of view of polynomials, this limit is the
tropical limit
. Notice that we already have
Proposition

4.3
in that direction. We also introduce, as in
[
Mal23
]
, a real Veronese map that gives an alternative interpretation of the AT metric as a pull back of the round metric on the sphere.

This section is more of an extended remark and do not contain very deep result. However we think this direction deserves attention and, in particular, it would be interesting to investigate deeper connections to tropical geometry.

We fix a finite subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a list of coefficients
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
. For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let us denote by
\zeta(x):=\zeta_{\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}}(x)
the ellipsoid associated to the GRF
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
in
x
.
The first result is the following.

Proposition 4.30
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the limit
\zeta_{\infty}^{x}:=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\zeta(tx)
exists and is contained in a vector space parallel to the affine span of
P^{x}
.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward computation. Indeed, we have:

(4.74)

h_{\zeta(tx)}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}g_{x}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{a\in A}\alpha_{a}^{2}\frac{e^{-2t(h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle)}}{\overline{\mathrm{K}}(tx)}(a-\mu(tx))^{2}.

Now we apply
Proposition

4.3
to obtain, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

(4.75)

\lim_{t\to\infty}h_{\zeta(tx)}(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\sum_{a\in A}\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}}{\|\alpha^{x}\|^{2}}\langle a-\mu_{\infty}^{x},u\rangle^{2}}.

This implies that the support function converges
pointwise
to the support function of the ellipsoid defined by
Eq.

4.75
that we then denote
\zeta_{\infty}^{x}
. Pointwise convergence of support functions is actually enough to imply convergence in the Hausdorff distance, see
[
Sch14
, Theorem 1.8.15]
. Now to see the last part of the statement, recall that
\mu_{\infty}^{x}\in P^{x}
thus, if
a\in A^{x}
, for every
y
orthogonal to
P^{x}
, we have
\langle a^{x}-\mu^{x}_{\infty},y\rangle=0
. It implies that
\zeta_{\infty}^{x}
is contained in a vector space parallel to
P^{x}
which is what we wanted.
∎

We can actually see more precisely how the
AT
metric degenerates at infinity.

Proposition 4.31
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
y\in x^{\perp}
. Then we have:

(4.76)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}(g_{\alpha})_{y+tx}=(g_{\alpha^{x}})_{y}

where recall that
\alpha^{x}
is the restriction of
\alpha
to
A^{x}
and where the convergence of quadractic form is the uniform convergence as functions on the sphere.

Proof.

For every
a\in A
, we have

(4.77)

\lambda_{a}(y+tx)=\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{2\langle a,y\rangle}e^{-2t(h_{P}(x)-\langle a,x\rangle)}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}\alpha_{a^{\prime}}^{2}e^{2\langle a^{\prime},y\rangle}e^{-2t(h_{P}(x)-\langle a^{\prime},x\rangle)}}.

As
t\to\infty
we get

(4.78)

\lambda_{a}(y+tx)\to\begin{cases}\lambda^{x}_{a}(y):=\frac{\alpha_{a}^{2}e^{2\langle a,y\rangle}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in A}\alpha_{a^{\prime}}^{2}e^{2\langle a^{\prime},y\rangle}}&\text{if }a\in A^{x}\\
0&\text{if }a\in A\setminus A^{x}.\end{cases}

It follows that
\mu_{\alpha}(y+tx)\to\mu_{\alpha^{x}}(y)
. We obtain pointwise convergence, that is, for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we have

(4.79)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}(g_{\alpha})_{y+tx}(v)=(g_{\alpha^{x}})_{y}(v).

To deduce the uniform convergence (in
v
), we can consider these quadratic forms as (the square of) support functions of ellipsoid and use, as in the previous proof, the correspondence between pointwise and uniform convergence of support functions, that is
[
Sch14
, Theorem 1.8.15]
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.32
.

When changing coordinates with the moment map, the AT metric is a Riemannian metric on the interior of the Newton polytope
P
. The previous proposition tells us that, when we approach the boundary, it degenerates to a metric on the interior of the face
P^{x}
and that this metric is precisely the AT metric for the Gaussian exponential sum corresponding to the choice of coefficients
\alpha^{x}
.

We conclude this section by introducing a map that strengthens the parallel with toric geometry. It was also considered by Malajovitch in
[
Mal23
, Section 3]
. We write
S(\mathbb{R}^{A}):=\left\{(v_{a})_{a\in A}\ |\ \sum_{a\in A}v_{a}^{2}=1\right\}
the unit sphere of
\mathbb{R}^{A}
and
S(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}):=S(\mathbb{R}^{A})\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A}
.

Definition 4.33
.

The (real)
Veronese map
is the map
\nu_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to S(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A})
given for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
by:

(4.80)

\nu_{\alpha}(x):=\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{a}(x)}\right)_{a\in A}.

Remark 4.34
.

Note that we could as well consider the image in the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{A})
instead of the sphere. Since the hypersurface
S(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A})
is diffeomorphic (or isometric for the appropriate metric) to its image on the projective space, it makes no difference.

We have the following

Proposition 4.35
.

The Veronese map is an embedding and the AT metric on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the pull back of the standard spherical metric on
S(\mathbb{R}^{A})
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\pi:S(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{A})\to\interior(P)
given by
\pi(v):=\sum_{a\in A}v_{a}^{2}a
. Then the moment map is given by

(4.81)

\mu_{\alpha}=\pi\circ\nu_{\alpha}.

Since the moment map is a diffeomorphism, it follows that
\nu_{\alpha}
must be an embedding.

We now compute its differential. A straightforward computation gives us

(4.82)

D_{x}(\sqrt{\lambda_{a}})=\sqrt{\lambda_{a}(x)}(a-\mu(x)).

Now if we let
g_{0}
be the round metric on
S(\mathbb{R}^{A})
, and
e_{a}
,
a\in A
the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{A}
, we have for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(4.83)

(\nu_{\alpha}^{*}g_{0})(u)=g_{0}(D_{x}\nu_{\alpha}u)=\left\|\sum_{a\in A}\langle D_{x}(\sqrt{\lambda_{a}}),u\rangle e_{a}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)\langle a-\mu(x),u\rangle^{2}=(g_{\alpha})_{x}(u)

and this concludes the proof.
∎

The image of the Veronese map was called by Malajovitch the
real toric variety

[
Mal23
, Section 3]
. We see here that the metric properties of this submanifold are relevant to the study of Gaussian exponential sums. Moreover, from
Eq.

4.81
, we see that its closure can be identified with the Newton polytope
P
. This is, again, a striking similarity with the complex case and toric geometry.
In fact this point of view when
m=1
was adopted by Edelman and Kostlan in
[
EK95
]
where they show that the expected number of zeroes of a random polynomial is the length of the (spherical)
moment curve
, which corresponds exactly to the Veronese map just defined. This method was later applied by Jindal, Pandey, Shukla and Zisopoulos to study random univariate
sparse
polynomials in
[
JPSZ20
]
.

4.6.
An explicit example

In this section we illustrate the various results obtained in the previous sections in examples where explicit computations are possible.

Let us first consider the simplest setting with the set
A=\{0,1\}\subset\mathbb{R}
and the coefficients
o_{0}:=1=:o_{1}
. The corresponding exponential sum

(4.84)

\mathcal{E}_{o}(x)=\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}e^{x}

with
\xi_{0},\xi_{1}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
iid standard Gaussian variables, have a unique zero
x=-\log\left(\frac{\xi_{1}}{\xi_{0}}\right)
if and only if
\xi_{1}/\xi_{0}>0
which happens with probability
1/2
. Thus we have

(4.85)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{o};\mathbb{R}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}.

Equivalentely, considering the polynomial viewpoint (see
Section

4.1
), we have
\mathcal{P}_{o}(w):=\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}w
which is a random affine function and has almost surely one zero. This is coherent with
Eq.

4.4
.

We can compute the different quantities associated to this exponential sums, starting with the covariance function that is given for all

by

(4.86)

\mathrm{K}_{o}(x)=1+e^{2x}=2e^{x}\cosh(x)

where
\cosh(x):=\frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2}
. It follows that the potential is given by

(4.87)

\Phi_{o}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\cosh(x)\right)+\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log(2).

It yields:

(4.88)

where
\tanh:=\sinh/\cosh
with
\sinh(x):=(e^{x}-e^{-x})/2
.

We can use the operations of
Section

4.4
to have something more interesting. Consider the set:

(4.89)

C^{d}_{m}:=\{0,\ldots,d\}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Notice that we have

m

We let, for all

:

(4.90)

\mathcal{E}^{d}_{m}(x):=(\mathcal{E}_{o}^{\otimes m})^{\odot d}(x)=\sum_{c\in C^{d}_{m}}\binom{d}{c_{1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdots\binom{d}{c_{m}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\xi_{c}e^{\langle c,x\rangle}

where, as usual,
\xi_{c}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}

(4.91)

We can then, without doing any computation, obtain the expected number of solutions.

Proposition 4.36
.

We have for all integers
m,d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}>0
:

(4.92)

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{P}^{d_{1}}_{m},\ldots,\mathcal{P}^{d_{m}}_{m};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}=\frac{m!b_{m}}{2^{m}}\,\sqrt{d_{1}\cdots d_{m}}.

Proof.

We switch to the point of view of exponential sums to get

(4.93)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{P}^{d_{1}}_{m},\ldots,\mathcal{P}^{d_{m}}_{m};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}

\displaystyle=2^{m}\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{d_{1}}_{m},\ldots,\mathcal{E}^{d_{m}}_{m};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}

(4.94)

\displaystyle=2^{m}\sqrt{d_{1}\cdots d_{m}}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{1}_{m};\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}

(4.95)

\displaystyle=m!b_{m}\sqrt{d_{1}\cdots d_{m}}\left(\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{o};\mathbb{R}\right\}\right)^{m}

(4.96)

\displaystyle=\frac{m!b_{m}}{2^{m}}\sqrt{d_{1}\cdots d_{m}}

where in the second inequality we used
Proposition

4.27
, in the third
Corollary

4.25
, and in the last

4.85

5.
Complex Gaussian Exponential sums and the BKK Theorem

The aim of this section is to give a proof of the BKK Theorem and its generalization
F
, using Gaussain Random Fields and our framework. Let us start with some definitions.

Definition 5.1
.

A Gaussian vector
\xi\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}
is called a
standard complex Gaussian
if it is
U(1)
invariant and satisfy
\mathbb{E}\xi=0
and
\mathbb{E}[|\xi|^{2}]=1
. Equivalently,
\xi
is distributed as
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\xi^{R}+i\xi^{I})
where
\xi^{R},\xi^{I}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{R}
are standard (real) Gaussian variables. Moreover, we call
\xi\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}^{n}
a
standard complex Gaussian vector
if it is distributed as
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
where
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}
are iid standard complex Gaussians. Finally, a (centered)
complex Gaussian vector
is the image of a standard complex Gaussian vector by a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that, when identifying
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
a standard complex Gaussian is
not
a standard Gaussian vector of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
because of the coefficient
1/\sqrt{2}
. More precisely, a standard complex Gaussian vector in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
has density
z\mapsto\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}e^{|z|^{2}}.

We fix
A\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
finite and as before write
P:=\conv(A)
for the Newton polytope. We now want to study the complex Gaussian exponential sum:

(5.1)

\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}(z):=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)

where
\xi_{a}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}
are independent standard complex Gaussians and
f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z):=\alpha_{a}e^{(a,z)}
with
(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is the standard Hermitian form and
\alpha_{a}>0
.

Remark 5.3
.

Note that, if
\xi\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}
is a standard Gaussian and
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
c\xi
is distributed as
|c|\xi
thus the fact that we take real positve coefficients in the exponential sum is not a restriction.

As before, we define the following quantities for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
:

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}

(5.3)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}(z)

\displaystyle:=\log\left(\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\right)

\displaystyle\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\,a.

We want to prove the following.

Theorem 5.4
.

For all
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have

(5.4)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};U\right\}=\frac{n!}{\pi^{n}}\int_{U}\det\left(\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z.

In particular, if
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then for all
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(5.5)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};V\times i(-\pi,\pi)\right\}=n!\vol_{n}\left(\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(V)\right).

Which gives in the case
V=\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

(5.6)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};\mathbb{R}^{n}\times i(-\pi,\pi)\right\}=n!\vol_{n}(P).

We will of course apply the general theory introduced in
Section

2.5
. We let

(5.7)

\mathscr{F}:=\Span_{\mathbb{C}}\left\{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}\ |\ a\in A\right\}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}}\left\{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a},if^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}\ |\ a\in A\right\}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C}).

We endow this space with the Hermitian product such that
\{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}\}_{a\in A}
is a unitary basis that we denote
(\cdot,\cdot)
. As before, we write
\mathscr{F}_{z}:=\left\{f\in\mathscr{F}\ |\ f(z)=0\right\}
. Note that this is now a
complex
hyperplane and thus of real codimension
2
. However the situation remains similar as in the real case, in pareticular, we have the following.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
be fixed. For every
F:\mathscr{F}_{z}\to\mathbb{R}
measurable, we have

(5.8)

\int_{\mathscr{F}_{z}}F(f)\rho_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}}(f)\,\mathrm{d}f=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathbb{E}[F(\mathcal{G}_{z})]

where
\rho_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}}(f):=(1/\pi^{n})\exp(-|f|^{2})
is the density of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}
and where

(5.9)

\mathcal{G}_{z}:=\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}-\frac{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,\cdot)\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}_{z}

with

(5.10)

\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,z^{\prime}):=\sum_{a\in A}\overline{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)}\,f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z^{\prime}),\quad\forall z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Proof.

First of all, since
\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,z)
, one checks that
\mathcal{G}_{z}(z)=0
almost surely, i.e,
\mathcal{G}_{z}\in\mathscr{F}_{z}
almost surely. In fact we have

(5.11)

\mathcal{G}_{z}=\pi_{z}(\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}})

where
\pi_{z}:\mathscr{F}\to\mathscr{F}_{z}
is the orthogonal projection. Indeed, as in the real case, one sees that
\left(\mathrm{K}(z,\cdot),f\right)=f(z)
for any
f\in\mathscr{F}
, i.e.
\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,\cdot)
is the vector representing the complex linear form
ev^{\mathbb{C}}_{z}:f\mapsto f(z)
. Moreover one sees that
|ev^{\mathbb{C}}_{z}|^{2}=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)
and thus
\pi_{z}(f):=f-\frac{f(z)}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,\cdot)\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}_{z}

as claimed. The rest of the proof is straightforward:

(5.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathscr{F}_{z}}F(f)\frac{e^{|f|^{2}}}{\pi^{n-1}}\,\mathrm{d}f=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathscr{F}}F(\pi_{z}(f))\frac{e^{|f|^{2}}}{\pi^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}f=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[F\left(\pi_{z}(\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}})\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[F\left(\mathcal{G}_{z}\right)\right]

which is what we wanted.
∎

Considering
\mathscr{F}
as a
real
vector space, we have two
real
linear forms:

(5.13)

ev^{R}_{z}:f\mapsto\Re(f(z))\quad\text{and}\quad ev^{I}_{z}:f\mapsto\Im(f(z)).

We need to compute
\|ev^{R}_{z}\wedge ev^{I}_{z}\|
where
\wedge
denotes the
real
wedge product.

Proposition 5.6
.

For all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, we have
\|ev^{R}_{z}\wedge ev^{I}_{z}\|=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)
.

Proof.

Let us consider
\{\chi_{a},\bar{\chi}_{a}\}_{a\in A}\subset\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathscr{F},\mathbb{R})
the dual basis of the (real orthonormal) basis
\{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a},if^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}\}_{a\in A}
. Moreover, let us consider the endomorphism
J:\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathscr{F},\mathbb{R})\to\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathscr{F},\mathbb{R})
given for all
\chi\in\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathscr{F},\mathbb{R})
and
f\in\mathscr{F}
by

(5.14)

J\chi(f):=\chi(if).

Then, we have
\bar{\chi}_{a}=-J\chi_{a}
and
J^{2}=-1
. Evaluating on basis vector, one can see that

(5.15)

ev^{R}_{z}=\sum_{a\in A}\Re\left(f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\right)\chi_{a}-\Im\left(f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\right)\bar{\chi}_{a}

and that
ev^{I}_{z}=-Jev^{R}_{z}
. Since the complex structure
J
preserves the scalar product we get

(5.16)

\|ev^{R}_{z}\wedge ev_{z}^{I}\|=\|ev^{R}_{z}\wedge Jev_{z}^{R}\|=\|ev_{z}^{R}\|^{2}=\sum_{a\in A}\Re\left(f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\right)^{2}+\Im\left(f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\right)^{2}=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)

which is what we wanted.
∎

We will also need the following computation.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})
, and write
\alpha_{j}=u_{j}+iv_{j}
with
u_{j},v_{j}\in\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. We have

(5.17)

|u_{1}\wedge v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{n}\wedge v_{n}|=|\alpha_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\alpha_{n}|^{2}

Proof.

As in the previous proof, we consider the complex structure
J:\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})\to\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
given for all
u\in\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
by
Ju:=u\circ i
. Because of the Cauchy-Riemann relations, or equivalently from the equation
\alpha_{j}\circ i=i\alpha_{j}
we have that for all
j=1,\ldots,n
:

(5.18)

v_{j}=-Ju_{j}.

In fact, this turns
\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
into a
complex
vector space and the application
real part

\Re:\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})\to\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
is an isomorphism of complex vector space that preserves the Hermitian structure. Indeed, it is imediate from the
\mathbb{C}
-linearity that
\Re\circ i=J\circ\Re
and from the Cauchy Riemann relations
Eq.

5.18
, we get
\Re^{-1}(u)=u-iJu
. Thus we can work completely in the space
\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
and in particular we have

(5.19)

|\alpha_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\alpha_{n}|^{2}=|u_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}u_{n}|^{2}

where in the right hand side, we mean the complex determinant when
\Hom_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{R})
is considered as a complex vector space with the complex structure
J
. Now it is a classical result that the modulus square of a complex matrix, is the determinant of the underlying real matrix, see for example
[
BLLP16
, Lemma 5]
. In our case, this gives:

(5.20)

|u_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}u_{n}|^{2}=|u_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{n}\wedge Ju_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge Ju_{n}|

which, by
Eq.

5.18
, gives the result.
∎

One more computation.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
X\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a (centered) complex Gaussian vector with complex covariance
\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}\left[X\,\overline{X}^{T}\right].
Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be iid copies of
X
. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}X_{n}|^{2}\right]=n!\,\det(\Sigma)

where on the right hand side the determinant is taken over
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

First of all, note that the matrix
\Sigma
is Hermitian, thus
\det(\Sigma)
is real. Now if
X=M(\xi)
where
M:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a
\mathbb{C}
-linear map and
\xi\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then
\Sigma=M\overline{M}^{T}
and thus
\det(\Sigma)=|\det(M)|^{2}
. It follows that

(5.21)

\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}X_{n}|^{2}\right]=\alpha_{n}\,\det(\Sigma)

where

(5.22)

\alpha_{n}=\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\xi_{n}|^{2}\right]

with
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathbb{C}^{n}
are iid standard complex Gaussian vectors. To compute
\alpha_{n}
, we use the independence to write

(5.23)

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{1}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{2}\cdots\xi_{n}}\left[|\xi_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\xi_{n}|^{2}\right]\right]

(5.24)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{1}}\left[\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{2}\cdots\xi_{n}}\left[|e_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}N(\xi_{2})\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}N(\xi_{n})|^{2}\right]\right]

(5.25)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{1}}\left[\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{2}\cdots\xi_{n}}\left[|e_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\xi_{2}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\xi_{n}|^{2}\right]\right]

(5.26)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{1}}\left[\|\xi_{1}\|^{2}\right]\alpha_{n-1}=n\alpha_{n-1}

where
e_{1}
is the first vector of the standard basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
N:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a unitary matrix that is such that
N(\xi_{1})=\|\xi_{1}\|e_{1}
(note that inside the first expectation
\xi_{1}
is fixed) and where, in the third equality, we used the unitary invariance of the standard complex Gaussian vectors. Now it is enough to see that
\alpha_{1}=1
by definition of standard complex Gaussians and thus
\alpha_{n}=n!
. Reintroducing in
Eq.

5.21
gives the result.
∎

We are now ready to give a proof of the main result.

Proof of
Theorem

5.4
.

According to the general theory, that is to
Proposition

2.27
, we have

(5.27)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};U\right\}=\int_{U}\delta(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

where the Kac-Rice density
\delta
is given by

(5.28)

\delta(z):=\frac{1}{\|ev_{z}^{R}\wedge ev_{z}^{I}\|^{n}}\int_{(\mathscr{F}_{z})^{n}}|D_{z}f_{1}^{R}\wedge D_{z}f_{1}^{I}\wedge\cdots\wedge D_{z}f_{n}^{R}\wedge D_{z}f_{n}^{I}|\rho_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}}(f_{1})\cdots\rho_{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}}}(f_{n})\,\mathrm{d}f_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}f_{n}

where
f^{R}
, respectively
f^{I}
, denotes the real, respectively imaginary, part of
f
. The coefficient in front of the integral was computed in
Proposition

5.6
.
Now let us write
F:(\mathscr{F}_{z})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(5.29)

F(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):=|D_{z}f_{1}^{R}\wedge D_{z}f_{1}^{I}\wedge\cdots\wedge D_{z}f_{n}^{R}\wedge D_{z}f_{n}^{I}|.

Then, using
Proposition

5.5
, we can replace the integrals over
\mathscr{F}_{z}
by the expectation of the random field
\mathcal{G}_{z}
. More precisely, we have:

(5.30)

\delta(z)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)^{n}\pi^{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{G}_{z}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{z}^{n})\right]

where
\mathcal{G}_{z}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{z}^{n}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}_{z}
are iid copies of
\mathcal{G}_{z}
defined in
Eq.

5.9
.

Let us denote
\partial_{z}:\mathscr{F}\to\Hom_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^{n},\mathbb{C})
the complex differential, i.e,
\partial_{z}f=D_{z}f^{R}+iD_{z}f^{I}
. Note that we use here that the elements of
\mathscr{F}
consist of holomorphic functions. We can now use
Proposition

5.7
to switch from the real determinant, that is from
F
, to a complex determinant. Namely we have:

(5.31)

F(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=|\partial_{z}f_{1}\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\cdots\wedge_{\mathbb{C}}\partial_{z}f_{n}|^{2}.

Since
\partial_{z}
is a linear map and
\mathcal{G}_{z}\mathchoice{\raisebox{-1.50696pt}{$\displaystyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.5mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\displaystyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\mkern 1.0mu\raisebox{-1.1625pt}{$\textstyle{{}^{\subset}}$}\mkern-11.7mu\raisebox{1.93747pt}{$\textstyle{{}_{\subset}}$}}{\raisebox{1.50696pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-14.0mu\raisebox{-0.6458pt}{$\scriptstyle\subset$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}\mkern-13.5mu\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\subset$}}\mathscr{F}_{z}
is a complex Gaussian vector, it follows that
\partial_{z}G_{z}
is a Gaussian vector. Thus we can use
Proposition

5.8
to get

(5.32)

\mathbb{E}\left[F(\mathcal{G}_{z}^{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{z}^{n})\right]=n!\det(\Sigma_{z})

where
\Sigma_{z}
is the complex covariance of
\mathcal{G}_{z}
, i.e.,
\Sigma_{z}=\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{z}\mathcal{G}_{z}\overline{\partial_{z}\mathcal{G}_{z}}^{T}\right].

From its definition in
Eq.

5.9
,
\mathcal{G}_{z}
is given for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
by:

(5.33)

\mathcal{G}_{z}(z^{\prime})=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}\left(f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z^{\prime})-f_{a}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\frac{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,z^{\prime})}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}\right).

We need to apply
\partial_{z}
to this expression, that is, to differentiate in
z^{\prime}
. We first note that
\partial_{z}f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}=f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\,a.

where
a\in A
is identified with the
\mathbb{C}
-linear map
(a,\cdot)
. Then, we compute (recall that we only differentiate in
z^{\prime}
):

(5.34)

\partial_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z,\cdot)=\sum_{a\in A}\overline{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)}\,\partial_{z}f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}=\sum_{a\in A}\overline{f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)}\,f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\,a=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z).

It follows that

(5.35)

\partial_{z}\mathcal{G}_{z}=\sum_{a\in A}\xi_{a}f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)\left(a-\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\right)

which yields

(5.36)

\Sigma_{z}=\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}\left(a-\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\right)\overline{\left(a-\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\right)}^{T}.

Let us take a moment to gather what we obtained so far. Combining
Eq.

5.30
and
Eq.

5.32
, we obtain an expression for the Kac Rice density that we can reintroduce in the main equation
Eq.

5.27
. We get:

(5.37)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};U\right\}=\frac{n!}{\pi^{n}}\int_{U}\frac{\det(\Sigma_{z})}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)^{n}}\,\mathrm{d}z.

To obtain the claimed result
Eq.

5.4
, we need to compare
\Sigma_{z}
and
\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}
. Before computing
\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}
, let us note that

(5.38)

\partial_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{\partial}_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}

It yields:

(5.39)

\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}=\frac{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}-\partial_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}\bar{\partial}_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)^{2}}=\frac{\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}}{\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}-\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}.

Moreover, we have

(5.40)

\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}=\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}a\overline{a}^{T}.

We now expand
Eq.

5.36
to obtain:

(5.41)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}a\,\overline{a}^{T}+\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}-\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}\,\left(a\,\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}-\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\overline{a}^{T}\right)

(5.42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}a\,\overline{a}^{T}+\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}-2\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}

(5.43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in A}|f^{\mathbb{C}}_{a}(z)|^{2}a\,\overline{a}^{T}-\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\overline{\mu^{\mathbb{C}}(z)}^{T}

(5.44)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)\,\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}.

Reintrocucing in
Eq.

5.37
gives the desired
Eq.

5.4
.

To obtain the other statements in the Theorem, one needs to note that, when
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, comparing with
Eq.

4.9
, we have that
\partial_{z}\bar{\partial}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}=\tfrac{1}{2}D^{2}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}
. Moreover,
\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}
is invariant by translation in the imaginary directions, i.e., for every real number
r\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z+ir)=\mathrm{K}^{\mathbb{C}}(z)
. It follows that the Kac-Rice density has the same property and we obtain

(5.45)

\mathbb{E}\mathbb{S}\left\{\mathcal{E}^{\mathbb{C}};V\times i(-\pi,\pi)\right\}=\frac{n!}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{V\times i(-\pi,\pi)}\det\left(D^{2}_{z}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}z=n!\int_{V}\det\left(D^{2}_{x}\Phi^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Now we find ourselves in the real situation again and applying the change of variable
\mu^{\mathbb{C}}=\mu:V\mapsto\mu(V)\subset\interior(P)
gives the result.
∎
