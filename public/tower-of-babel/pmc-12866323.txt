Geographic inequalities in exclusive breastfeeding among infants under six months across 18 African countries
Authors: Dennis Bardoe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExclusive breastfeeding (EBF) is essential for infant survival, growth, and development. Despite known benefits, national averages often mask adherence to coverages and underlying geographic inequalities. While previous studies have examined national averages, less is known about how EBF rates vary within countries. This study assessed national and subnational disparities in EBF among infants under six months across 18 African countries using comparable 2019 estimates.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional ecological analysis used existing 2019 modelled EBF estimates from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), accessed through the World Health Organization (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT, 2024 update). EBF estimates follow the WHO 24-hour recall definition for infants aged 0–6 months. Geographic inequalities were quantified using difference (D), ratio (R), population attributable risk (PAR), and population attributable fraction (PAF) using WHO HEAT’s built-in analytical tools. Spatial patterns were visualised using QGIS.ResultsNational prevalence of EBF ranged from 14.4% in Comoros to 65.9% in São Tomé and Príncipe. While a small number of countries have achieved relatively high and uniform coverage, most continue to experience pronounced subnational gaps. Addressing these inequalities will require region-specific breastfeeding interventions, strengthened maternal support systems, and the systematic integration of equity considerations into national nutrition programmes. Background

Breastfeeding provides optimal nourishment for infant survival, growth, and development [1]. Beyond its benefits for the child, breastfeeding contributes to maternal well-being, supports postpartum recovery, and reduces the risk of several diseases in both infants and mothers [1, 2]. In addition to biological benefits, breastfeeding also contributes to social equity and poverty reduction [3]. This assertion is reinforced by James P. Grant, former UNICEF Executive Director, who noted that “Breastfeeding is a natural safety net against the worst effects of poverty. If a child survives the first month of life, exclusive breastfeeding goes a long way towards cancelling out the health difference between being born into poverty and being born into affluence.” From an economic perspective, breastfeeding is the least expensive and safest method of infant feeding [2]. Socially, it is regarded as a basic human right [3]. The evidence indicates that inadequate breastfeeding practices contribute substantially to preventable child deaths and disabilities [4]. As such, approximately 1.4 million children are estimated to die each year, and approximately 44 million suffer disabilities as a result of suboptimal breastfeeding [5]. The health advantages for both mothers and children depend on the frequency, duration, and exclusivity of breastfeeding [6]. Exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) is widely recognised as a cornerstone of infant nutrition and health promotion [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends EBF during this period because EBF provides essential nutrients for growth and development, protects against diseases, and reduces the risk of infant mortality [3].

The WHO and UNICEF have implemented several initiatives to promote, protect, and strengthen the advocated gains of EBF. Notably, the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative and the International Code of Marketing of Breast Milk Substitutes were launched [7]. These programs aimed to improve breastfeeding practices globally and address the persistent decline in EBF rates [7]. In 2018, the WHO and UNICEF, together with other partners, emphasised the need for family-friendly workplaces and social policies to support mothers in nurturing and bonding with their children [8]. In addition, the WHO designated the first week of August as World Breastfeeding Week to raise awareness and encourage collective global action [3]. Despite these extensive efforts, global adherence to EBF remains suboptimal [9]. Within Africa, for example, the pattern of progress is particularly uneven. While some countries have recorded improvements, national averages often mask substantial geographic disparities in EBF practices. For example, the Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (2022) reported that although the prevalence of breastfeeding was high (96.8%), the duration and exclusivity of breastfeeding were low, with only approximately 52% of children aged 0–5 months being exclusively breastfed [10]. This persistent gap makes inadequate adherence to EBF a significant and continuing public health concern in both developed and developing regions [6]. These disparities may be attributable to variations in access to maternal health services, sociocultural norms, levels of economic development, and the implementation of breastfeeding promotion policies [6, 11]. Therefore, understanding these spatial inequalities is essential for designing interventions that ensure equitable progress toward the WHO global nutrition target of 70% EBF by 2030 [12].

Although earlier studies have explored national trends and determinants of EBF, there is limited empirical evidence comparing subnational inequalities across multiple African countries using harmonised data. This knowledge gap is likely to constrain policymakers and public health stakeholders from addressing region-specific barriers and allocating resources effectively. Moreover, while country-specific data on EBF have been available for several decades, the most current harmonised subnational data suitable for cross-country comparisons are available only for the year 2019 in the WHO Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT). Therefore, this study examined the extent of geographic inequalities in EBF among infants under six months of age across 18 African countries via comparable 2019 data from the WHO HEAT. In global monitoring systems, including the WHO HEAT database used in this study, EBF is measured using a 24-hour recall of feeding practices among infants aged 0–6 months. Conceptually, this study contributes to the growing field of health equity and spatial epidemiology by illustrating how within-country inequalities are likely to persist even amid national improvements in maternal and child health indicators. This finding reinforces the importance of analysing breastfeeding data through an equity lens, which is consistent with the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health framework, which identifies “place” as a fundamental determinant of health outcomes. Practically, the findings provide evidence to guide regionally tailored breastfeeding promotion strategies within African countries. Identifying areas where inequalities are most pronounced or improving could help governments, NGOs, and international agencies intervene more effectively, thereby accelerating progress toward the global nutrition target of 70% EBF coverage by 2030 [12].

Methods

Study design and data source

This study employed a retrospective cross-sectional ecological design, using existing 2019 secondary data on exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among infants under six months of age. The data were drawn from the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), accessed through the World Health Organization (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT), 2024 update, which hosts retrospectively modelled registry-based estimates for the year 2019 [13]. The HEAT toolkit provides a structured platform for assessing health inequalities by integrating disaggregated health indicator data from the WHO Health Inequality Data Repository [13]. Moreover, the HEAT database provides harmonised, modelled estimates of key health indicators across countries and subnational regions, allowing for comparative analyses of health outcomes and inequalities over time [14]. Although there are earlier harmonised and comparable subnational disaggregation data across 18 countries, the 2019 dataset was the most recent.

Study setting and country selection

Eighteen African countries were included on the basis of the availability of subnational disaggregated EBF data in the HEAT 2024 dataset. These included West Africa (Benin, Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Guinea, Mauritania, Nigeria, Senegal), Central Africa (Cameroon, Democratic Republic of the Congo, São Tomé and Príncipe), East Africa (Comoros, Kenya, United Republic of Tanzania, Zambia), and Southern Africa (Eswatini, Lesotho, Zimbabwe, Angola) (Fig. 1). This grouping was based on the United Nations geoscheme for Africa. Country selection was based entirely on the availability of comparable 2019 subnational EBF estimates in the WHO HEAT database. As a result, northern and northeastern African countries are not represented in the dataset.

Measure

The outcome variable was the prevalence of EBF among infants aged 0 to 6 months. In accordance with WHO definitions, exclusive breastfeeding refers to feeding infants only breast milk (including expressed milk) and no other liquids or solids, except for oral rehydration solution, drops, or syrups of vitamins, minerals, or medicines, during the first six months of life [1]. It is worth noting that EBF estimates in the HEAT database are based on the WHO 24-hour recall definition for infants aged 0–6 months. This is a current-status indicator rather than a cumulative measure of feeding from birth. The equity stratifier used in this study was the geographic distribution of EBF within each country.

Measurement of geographic inequalities

To quantify within-country disparities in EBF, both absolute and relative measures of inequality were analysed via the HEAT 2024 toolkit. The selected summary measures were difference (D), ratio (R), population attributable risk (PAR), and population attributable fraction (PAF). These summary measures were selected in line with the World Health Organization’s recommended framework for health inequality monitoring, which advocates the combined use of absolute and relative measures to capture both the magnitude and proportional distribution of inequalities across population subgroups [13, 15]. D measures the absolute difference between the regions with the highest and lowest EBF prevalence within each country [16]. Similarly, R represents the relative difference, which is computed as the ratio of EBF prevalence in the highest-performing region to that in the lowest-performing region [16]. Moreover, PAR estimates the difference between the national average and the rate in the best-performing region, indicating potential improvement if all regions achieve the highest observed prevalence [16]. Finally, the PAF estimates the proportional equivalent of PAR, expressed as a percentage [16]. Together, these measures provide a comprehensive view of both the magnitude and proportional extent of geographic inequalities.

Data analysis and presentation

All analyses were conducted with the statistical tools embedded in the WHO HEAT online software (2024 update). The platform automatically computes summary inequality measures with corresponding 95% intervals. The extracted results were exported to Microsoft Excel for data management, tabulation, and graphical presentation. The findings are summarised in both tables and charts, highlighting the national prevalence of EBF and subnational (regional) variations in inequality indicators for the year 2019. Spatial visualisation was conducted with QGIS version 3.44.3. The resulting choropleth maps displayed the magnitude and spatial distribution of EBF prevalence with a graduated colour scale. The spatial patterns were interpreted in conjunction with descriptive statistics and regional averages to contextualise disparities.

Discussion

Exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among infants under six months across the 18 countries

This study examined the national and regional distribution of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among infants under six months of age across 18 African countries using the World Health Organization (WHO) Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) dataset. It revealed substantial variation in EBF coverage between and within countries. At the national level, the prevalence of EBF ranged from 14.4% in Comoros to 65.9% in São Tomé and Príncipe. Notably, Ghana, Lesotho, São Tomé and Príncipe, and Zambia were the only countries whose national prevalence surpassed the 2019 global average of 43.5% [17]. The majority of countries, particularly those in West and Central Africa, fell substantially below this benchmark. This uneven distribution highlights persistent challenges in achieving optimal EBF practices across much of the selected countries included in the study. The relatively higher prevalence of EBF observed in some of the selected southern and eastern African countries, such as Zambia (58.7%), Lesotho (57.9%), and Tanzania (52.3%), may indicate stronger policy implementation, broader community sensitisation, and more consistent enforcement of maternal and child health programmes [18, 19]. These regions have historically recorded higher antenatal care (ANC) attendance, greater integration of breastfeeding counselling into maternal health services, and better coverage of baby-friendly hospital initiatives [18–21]. In contrast, lower rates in countries such as Côte d’Ivoire (17.6%), Nigeria (19.8%), and Cameroon (26.6%) could stem from multiple intersecting factors, including weaker health system capacity, sociocultural norms that favour early introduction of complementary feeding, limited postnatal support, and urban lifestyle constraints [18, 21, 22]. Previous studies have also shown that maternal employment, inadequate maternity leave, and misconceptions about breast milk sufficiency remain key barriers to EBF in several West and Central African settings [18, 21–23].

It was also revealed that more than two-thirds of the countries included in the study remain below the global average. This highlights a significant implementation gap between international breastfeeding targets and national realities. While regional differences partly mirror socioeconomic and policy disparities, they also point to deeper structural inequalities in access to breastfeeding-friendly environments [11]. Health workforce shortages, inconsistent community-level follow-up, and limited enforcement of the International Code of Marketing of Breastmilk Substitutes continue to undermine progress in many of these low-performing countries [11, 24]. From a programmatic perspective, the variation observed between high- and low-performing countries suggests that national averages may obscure substantial intra-regional inequities [2, 6, 19]. This calls for renewed attention to subnational contexts, where progress could be accelerated by learning from local models of success. For example, countries such as Zambia and Lesotho demonstrate that higher EBF coverage is achievable when maternal health interventions are decentralised and sustained through community-based health promotion. Scaling up such context-specific approaches could help bridge the geographic inequalities identified in this study. These findings reaffirm that EBF practices across Africa are highly unequal both across and within countries. Therefore, addressing these disparities requires interventions that respond to local realities, sustained investment in maternal health systems, and stronger policy enforcement. Without such efforts, the continent risks stagnation in achieving global nutrition goals despite the well-documented benefits of EBF for child survival and development.

Magnitude and pattern of geographical inequalities in exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among infants under six months across 18 countries

In addition to national averages, this study revealed considerable national and regional disparities in EBF coverage across the 18 countries. Nigeria presented the widest subnational gap, with the prevalence of EBF ranging from 4.7% in Yobe to 42.9% in Osun. This was accompanied by a population attributable fraction (PAF) of 116.6%. Tanzania, Cameroon, and Ghana also demonstrated notable disparities, with PAFs exceeding 38%. In contrast, countries such as São Tomé and Príncipe, Lesotho, Eswatini, and Comoros presented minimal spatial inequality. In these countries, the differences between the highest- and lowest-performing regions were less than 6%, and the PAFs were less than 7%. The persistence of wide disparities in EBF in most of the countries points to structural and contextual limitations [25]. For example, health system factors, coverage of baby-friendly hospital initiatives, the availability of skilled lactation support, and the integration of breastfeeding counselling into antenatal and postnatal care may differ significantly between urban and rural regions [20]. Previous studies have noted that areas with greater health service penetration and stronger maternal and child health infrastructure tend to achieve greater implementation of EBF [24]. Conversely, limited health facility access, community outreach, and inconsistent counselling undermine early initiation and maintenance of EBF [1]. Moreover, socioeconomic and educational limitations may further compound these disparities. Numerous studies have shown that maternal education strongly predicts both knowledge and practice of EBF [26, 27]. Regions with higher literacy levels and greater access to media-based health information often exhibit stronger adherence to breastfeeding recommendations [26, 27]. In contrast, in low-literacy or resource-poor areas, misinformation, cultural beliefs, and competing childcare demands frequently lead to early introduction of water or other complementary foods [26, 27]. These behavioural deviations are not isolated decisions but reflections of systemic disadvantage [27].

Furthermore, employment and maternity protection policies could also influence the regional differences observed [26]. In contexts where formal employment is concentrated in urban areas with limited workplace support for breastfeeding, working mothers face structural barriers to maintaining exclusivity [27, 28]. Short maternity leave, a lack of lactation rooms, and inflexible work schedules may compel most of these women to introduce other feeds prematurely [28]. In contrast, regions with higher informal employment or extended family support structures may exhibit better EBF continuity, albeit moderated by economic hardship [29]. Such dynamics highlight how socioeconomic structure interacts with maternal autonomy and the policy environment to shape infant feeding practices. In addition to health system and socioeconomic factors, cultural practices and social norms remain critical determinants of regional disparities. In several African countries, early introduction of water or herbal mixtures is a culturally ingrained practice believed to protect infants from thirst or spiritual harm [30]. These beliefs persist despite public health campaigns promoting EBF. In settings where traditional authority structures strongly influence maternal behaviour, health promotion messages may be less effective unless they are integrated within local communication systems [31]. In contrast, in some southern African countries, such as Lesotho and Zambia, community health outreach and peer-support networks may have achieved better cultural alignment with breastfeeding promotion, potentially explaining their higher and more equitable coverage [22]. Community-based health volunteers, peer counsellors, and maternal support groups have proven effective in improving breastfeeding outcomes when implemented consistently [30, 32]. However, the intensity and continuity of such programmes vary widely between and within countries [32]. This variability may partly account for the large subnational inequalities observed in countries such as Ghana and Kenya, where community health programmes operate unevenly across districts [32]. Sustained investment in community-level capacity building, therefore, remains essential to bridging these gaps.

Broader implications for maternal and child health equity

Promoting equitable breastfeeding practices is integral to achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 2 and 3, whose emphasis includes ending hunger and ensuring health and well-being. The spatial inequalities observed in this study align with the broader pattern of health inequities across Africa, where geographic location continues to predict maternal and child health outcomes. This study demonstrates that inequalities in EBF move beyond an issue of access to an uneven distribution of health-promoting environments. To reduce subnational disparities, national governments must adopt regionally differentiated strategies that address both structural and behavioural determinants. This includes integrating equity indicators into national breastfeeding monitoring systems to enable real-time identification of lagging regions. Second, decentralised action plans should empower local health authorities to design culturally appropriate interventions that reflect regional realities. In low-performing areas, investments should prioritise the training of community health workers, the expansion of postnatal counselling, and the enforcement of maternity protection legislation. Equally important is the need to address the structural factors that shape breastfeeding choices. Policies should ensure that women, especially those in informal employment, are protected through social schemes that guarantee income security and flexibility for EBF. In addition, strengthening mass media and community communication strategies tailored to local languages and beliefs could help counter persistent misconceptions. Finally, sustained political commitment, consistent programme financing, and strong coordination between national and subnational health systems are crucial for achieving equitable outcomes.

Study strengths, limitations, and future directions

A key strength of this study is the inclusion of multi-country, harmonised data, which permits direct cross-country comparability of EBF estimates. The application of standardised inequality metrics enhances interpretability and provides quantifiable policy targets. Nonetheless, some limitations warrant consideration. The reliance on secondary data from 2019 means that recent changes in breastfeeding promotion efforts may not have been captured. These data, while harmonised for comparability, may differ slightly from country-specific survey estimates and are subject to inherent modelling uncertainties. Secondly, because the analysis was ecological and cross-sectional, associations observed between geographic location and EBF cannot be interpreted causally. Similarly, subnational aggregation may mask intra-regional heterogeneity, particularly in large countries with wide social and economic variation. Importantly, the analysis included only 18 African countries for which comparable subnational data were available in the HEAT 2024 update. Consequently, the findings may not fully represent the African continent as a whole, and selection bias is possible if countries with available data systematically differ from those without such data. The results should therefore be interpreted as illustrative of regional patterns rather than continent-wide estimates. Also, EBF estimates were based on 24-hour recall and therefore represent current-status feeding rather than exclusivity from birth. This may overestimate true adherence to exclusive breastfeeding, particularly in populations with fluctuating feeding practices. Despite these limitations, the findings provide a baseline for monitoring geographic progress in breastfeeding equity. Notably, the findings of this study reflect patterns only within the 18 included countries. As these countries are geographically clustered and do not evenly represent the entire continent, caution is needed when extrapolating the results to Africa as a whole. Future studies should extend this analysis via longitudinal or geospatial modelling to explore temporal trends and spatial clustering. Integrating contextual variables such as maternal education, healthcare access, and policy enforcement indices would help disentangle the structural drivers of inequality. Moreover, qualitative research could illuminate how cultural narratives and maternal decision-making processes interact with geographic context to shape breastfeeding behaviour.

Conclusion

This study provides a comparative assessment of geographic patterns and subnational inequalities in exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among infants under six months of age across 18 selected African countries. While some countries, notably São Tomé and Príncipe, Lesotho, and Zambia, demonstrated relatively high and equitable EBF coverage, substantial disparities persisted in many others, including Nigeria, Cameroon, and Côte d’Ivoire. The persistence of wide subnational gaps highlights the continued influence of geographic context on maternal and child health outcomes across the continent. Beyond individual behaviours and service-level factors, the observed patterns suggest that EBF practices are shaped by broader structural, socioeconomic, and policy environments that could either enable or constrain mothers’ ability to breastfeed exclusively. Although improvements in awareness and health promotion remain important, these efforts alone are insufficient to achieve equitable coverage. Therefore, strengthening health systems, enforcing maternity protection policies, and implementing culturally responsive, community-based support mechanisms are likely to be critical for reducing persistent spatial inequalities. Importantly, countries that have achieved more uniform coverage of EBF illustrate that equitable progress is attainable when political commitment, programme continuity, and local ownership are aligned. Closing the remaining geographic gaps in EBF coverage will therefore be essential for accelerating progress towards the World Health Organization (WHO) global nutrition target of 70% EBF by 2030 and for ensuring that all infants, regardless of place of birth, have an equal opportunity to benefit from the protective effects of EBF.
