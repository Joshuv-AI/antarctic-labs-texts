Rethinking yield stability through phenotypic plasticity and its link to modern statistical methods
Authors: Victor Sadras, Matthew Welsh, Beata Sznajder, Julie Hayes, Matthew Reynolds, Julian Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the context of advocacy for yield stability, trade-offs between yield and yield stability, the frequent lack of definitions, and the variation in methods when they are explicit, this review connects two perspectives: phenotypic plasticity and factor analytics. Phenotypic plasticity brings over a century of research in developmental biology, ecology, and evolution, and is gaining traction in crop science. Factor analytics is an advanced linear mixed model of multi-environment data with factor analytic variance structures for the genotype-by-environment interaction effect. We (i) review phenotypic plasticity, define agronomically adaptive plasticity where varieties (or practices) consistently return superior yield (or other traits) across environments with no trade-off, and describe percentile–plasticity plots to assess the agronomic value of plasticity; (ii) outline factor analytic models; and (iii) link phenotypic plasticity and factor analytic models mathematically and empirically. We show that phenotypic plasticity of cereal yield correlates positively with overall performance obtained from factor analytic models when plasticity is adaptive and negatively when it is maladaptive. We conclude that phenotypic plasticity contributes biological meaning to opaque analytical approaches, and that biologically grounded statistics are needed to challenge weak agronomic narratives, such as advocacy for stability that might reflect decision biases rather than critical consideration of its benefits.

Introduction

‘Balance’ and ‘stability’ are culturally embedded, value-laden concepts. In both Western cultures that draw deeply from ancient Greek philosophy and their Eastern counterparts informed by Taoism, Confucianism, and Buddhism, ‘balance’ and ‘stability’ are regarded as desirable (Hwang, 2012; Mou et al., 2012; Simberloff, 2014; Yulie, 2015; Mou, 2023; Head, 2025). Making explicit the cultural dimension of the concept, Head (2025) challenges the ‘balance-is-good’ proposition for Murray–Darling water allocation in the context of climate change. Russel Wallace, in around 1855, challenged the notion of a balance of nature as an undefined entity whose accuracy could not be tested, leading to the contemporary rejection of the concept and a focus on a dynamic, often chaotic nature buffeted by constant disturbances (Simberloff, 2014). In ecology, the notion of balance in nature is recognized as a ‘panchreston’: a term that means so many different things to different people that it has become useless (Simberloff, 2014). In evolutionary biology, a paradox of terminology is apparent whereby ‘a language of stasis and stability has long dominated the science of change’ (p. 8, West-Eberhard, 2003). Stabilizing selection (i.e. selection against extreme values of a trait) is assumed to be common (p. 234, Odling-Smee, 2024) despite weak empirical evidence (Haller and Hendry, 2014). In decision-making, a preference for stability is reflected in people’s widespread bias towards certainty (Box 1).

Stability and balance in agriculture from the viewpoint of prospect theory

Reasons for an unexamined preference for balance and stability in agriculture

As early as the 1700s, mathematicians observed that people’s economic decisions did not align with the calculated values of expected outcomes but instead showed a willingness to sacrifice value to avoid uncertainty (Bernoulli, 1738, as cited in Seidl, 2013). von Neumann and Morgenstern (1953) operationalized this idea as the ‘certainty equivalent’, the amount of money a person would accept in lieu of a risky gamble. As a simple (if extreme) example, imagine a gameshow where a contestant has a 50/50 chance of winning US$1 million or nothing, but the host offers a guaranteed US$400 000 as an alternative to playing. Most people will prefer the certain option despite it being less valuable than the US$500 000 expected value of the gamble. What an individual will choose depends on their own circumstances and how the problem is framed, but this bias towards certain outcomes is widely seen in behavioural economics and is a core aspect of prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979), which describes how people in general act when faced with risky choices—being risk averse and preferring lower value certain outcomes when faced with risky choices about potential gains but risk seeking when considering losses. The strength of these tendencies, however, is affected by cultural norms such as masculinity/femininity, individualist/collectivist, and uncertainty acceptance/avoidance—effects commonly considered in comparisons between countries (e.g. Rieger et al., 2015) but which can also differ between groups within a country. Some breeding efforts and value chains in agriculture more broadly attempt to account for some of these cultural aspects (Stoian et al., 2018; Badstue et al., 2022). Farmers often share a cultural milieu distinct from the broader population of a country and show a greater tendency towards collectivism due to their interdependence and reliance on cooperation (e.g. Uchida et al., 2019), and the levels of collectivism even differ between growers of particular commodities, for example wheat or rice (Talhelm and Dong, 2024; Sadras and Hayman, 2025). Given this, the role of known decision-making tendencies and biases goes some way towards explaining why an unexamined preference for balance or stability might exist in agriculture.

A parallel between agronomically adaptive plasticity and ‘heuristics and biases’ in decision making

We advanced a definition of agronomically adaptive plasticity, which is best seen as a null-hypothesis (see ‘Agronomically adaptive plasticity’). This parallels the ‘heuristics and biases’ approach to decision-making of Tversky and Kahneman (1974), which remains central to behavioural economics and psychology. Here, the purely rational behaviour predicted by economic theories of behaviour (e.g. utility theory) is used as optimal standard and compared with actual real-world behaviour. The systematic differences between optimal and actual decisions are the ‘biases’, while the proposed cognitive explanations of why they occur are the ‘heuristics’. As an example, the bias commonly called base rate neglect is a tendency for people to underweight base rate information (e.g. prior probabilities and long-term averages) when presented with both the base rate and new information (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974). Experiments observing conditions that strengthen and weaken this effect have identified constraints on human decision-making based on how our memories and other cognitive processes work and, in particular, how these interact with environmental constraints such as non-stationarity, namely variation in rates of occurrence across locations—to give rise to base rate neglect and limit how close to optimal human decision-making can be (Bar-Hillel, 1980; Welsh and Navarro, 2012).

In agriculture, yield stability is a growing focus of research (Fig. 1A), and ‘balance’ and ‘stability’ are regularly invoked as desirable goals. As a reflection of the ‘stability-is-good’ notion, advocacy for yield stability with no definition or quantification is not rare (Derbyshire et al., 2024; Jian et al., 2024; Ravi et al., 2024; Stadnik et al., 2024; Wakjira et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024; De Jonge et al., 2025). Sampled agronomic studies are diverse in scale and focus; they deal with single crops, rotations, farm, regional, and global scales, focus on not only productive but also environmental outcomes, such as dynamics of soil carbon, and use metrics such as coefficient of variation and ad-hoc indices of stability (Table 1). Breeding studies typically compare genotypes under a range of environments, with a focus on yield, and agronomic and quality traits (Table 1). Additive Main Effects×Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) models, slopes of reaction norms relating phenotype and environment, factor analytic (FA) models, and other methods to account for genotype-by-environment (G×E) interactions are more typical of studies aimed at ranking phenotypes in pre-breeding and breeding (Table 1).

In the context of advocacy for yield stability that might involve cultural and cognitive bias (Box 1), trade-offs between yield and stability of yield (Calderini and Slafer, 1999; Raj et al., 2023; Giordano et al., 2025), the frequent lack of definitions, and the variation in methods when they are presented, this review connects two perspectives: phenotypic plasticity and FA models embedded in linear mixed models (Cutello et al., 2005; Linnér et al., 2019). Our aim is to highlight the value of combining biological thinking and pragmatic statistics. Phenotypic plasticity draws on more than a century of research in developmental biology, ecology, and evolution, and is increasingly used to frame questions in crop science. FA is a contemporary method to analyse multi-environment data involving a linear mixed model with FA variance structures for the variety-by-environment interaction effect. We review biological insights from phenotypic plasticity, advance a definition of agronomically adaptive plasticity, describe percentile–plasticity plots to probe for the adaptive value of plasticity, outline FA approaches, and link phenotypic plasticity and FA models mathematically and empirically. Using published oat and wheat yield data, we show that phenotypic plasticity calculated as the slope of reaction norms correlates positively with overall performance from FA when plasticity is adaptive and negatively when it is maladaptive.

Phenotypic plasticity

Overview

Phenotypic plasticity—‘the amount by which the expressions of individual characteristics of a phenotype are changed by different environments’ (Bradshaw, 1965)—is central to developmental biology, ecology, and evolution (West-Eberhard, 2003; DeWitt and Scheiner, 2004; Nicotra et al., 2010; Day and McLeod, 2018; Diouf et al., 2020; Mitchell et al., 2024). Early in the 20th century, Woltereck (1909) measured morphological traits in water flea clones grown under contrasting environmental conditions, and drew curves, which he called reaction norms, relating traits and environmental factors; Sarkar (2004) traced the evolution of the concept since its formulation. The slope of reaction norms quantifies phenotypic plasticity, which can also be calculated with other measures of variation including range, variance ratio, relative change between treatments, such as drought versus well-watered, and random regression mixed models (Valladares et al., 2000; Dingemanse et al., 2010; Bhatia et al., 2014; Sadras and Richards, 2014; Berlin et al., 2017; Kadam et al., 2017; Arnold et al., 2019; Ruiz et al., 2019). Some methods return similar rankings of phenotypic plasticity, for example plasticity calculated as the variance ratio correlates with the reaction norm slope in some datasets (Sadras and Richards, 2014). Metrics based on differences or ratios return a single value of plasticity, whereas reaction norms can account for non-linearity (Woltereck, 1909). In controlled environments where a single source of environmental variation dominates, tight reaction norms can be fitted, for example between leaf expansion rate and vapour pressure deficit (Reymond et al., 2003). In the field, it is difficult to construct reaction norms where multiple environmental factors influence the phenotype (Chapin et al., 1987; Cade and Noon, 2003; Zandalinas et al., 2024); this can be overcome using the mean of the trait as the environmental variable (Calò et al., 1975; Trentacoste et al., 2011; Curin et al., 2021).

Phenotypic plasticity has been interpreted through the lens of adaptation to environmental heterogeneity, where four strategies have evolved (DeWitt and Langerhans, 2004): (i) specialization, whereby a single phenotype is produced that is well adapted to a particular environment even though it may experience a range of environments; (ii) generalization, whereby a ‘general purpose’ phenotype is produced, with moderate fitness in most environments; (iii) bet-hedging, whereby an organism produces either several phenotypes (e.g. among units in a modular plant) or a single phenotype probabilistically; and (iv) phenotypic plasticity, whereby environmental signals trigger the production of alternative phenotypes. Other strategies are conceptually similar and can be considered as forms of these four; for example, seed dormancy can be seen as a type of temporal bet-hedging. Modelled fitness, calculated as the number of progeny after four generations under the assumption of perfect plasticity and a simplified range of environments, returned a ratio of 1:1.6:1.5:25 for strategies (i) to (iv) (DeWitt and Langerhans, 2004). With the caveat that phenotypic plasticity is trait dependent, and varies with genotype and environmental factors (Bradshaw, 1965), the conclusion from this modelling exercise is that in the absence of constraints, plasticity is always superior in variable environments. The lack of ubiquity of plasticity thus highlights the influence of constraints, including the cost of plasticity, developmental constraints, and the unreliability of environmental cues that guide development (De Witt et al., 1998; Volis, 2009; Auld et al., 2010; Murren et al., 2015; Aphalo and Sadras, 2022; Odling-Smee, 2024). Phenotypic plasticity can therefore be adaptive or maladaptive (see ‘Definition: agronomically adaptive plasticity’).

Contemporary to the review of phenotypic plasticity by Bradshaw (1965), Finlay and Wilkinson (1963) and Eberhart and Russell (1966) looked at the genotype-dependent response to the environment in a crop breeding context; all three studies have been and remain influential (Fig. 1B). Finlay and Wilkinson (1963) were aware of research on phenotypic stability, heterosis, developmental homeostasis, and response to environmental change in developmental biology, population genetics, and evolutionary studies, but were concerned with the relevance of laboratory studies for field crops. Using a log–log model for statistical reasons, they made explicit that ‘means of yields on a logarithmic scale correspond to geometric means on the natural scale’ (Finlay and Wilkinson, 1963); geometric means have been used as a measure of fitness in evolution (Levins, 1968; Wimsatt, 2007). Both Finlay and Wilkinson (1963) and Eberhart and Russell (1966) fitted reaction norms where the slope is phenotypic plasticity, but the connection was not made explicit at the time. It would take another four decades for crop science to embrace phenotypic plasticity as a useful angle to look at the genotype-dependent phenotypic response to the environment (Sadras et al., 2009; Peltonen-Sainio et al., 2011; Sadras and Lawson, 2011; Sadras and Trentacoste, 2011; D’Andrea et al., 2013; Kadam et al., 2017; de Felipe and Alvarez Prado, 2021; Bicego et al., 2024; Giordano et al., 2024; Iqbal et al., 2024; Otwani et al., 2024; Prohaska et al., 2024; Adams et al., 2025; Elazab et al., 2025; Govta et al., 2025; Schell et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). With a focus on plant breeding for drought adaptation, Cooper and Messina (2023) illustrate a shift in mindset from the relationship between genotype and environment seen as a nuisance to one where it is a ‘subject of productive interest’ (West-Eberhard, 2003).

The early prediction that ‘the plasticity of a character is an independent property of that character and is under its own specific genetic control’ (Bradshaw, 1965) has been demonstrated experimentally (Reymond et al., 2003; Marguerit et al., 2012; Alvarez Prado et al., 2014; Bloomer et al., 2014; Berlin et al., 2017; Kadam et al., 2017; Giordano et al., 2026). This has three practical implications. First, probing for the genetics of trait response to environment pre-empts quantitative trait locus (QTL)-by-environment interactions (Reymond et al., 2003; Kadam et al., 2017). Second, phenotypic plasticity and the trait per se can be partially independent targets of breeding and selection. Third, if stability is desirable and plasticity is its reverse, breeding and selection for agronomic adaptation should favour more stable, less plastic phenotypes; but selection for yield and agronomic adaptation has favoured high phenotypic plasticity of yield in many breeding programmes: wheat in Australia, the USA, Argentina, the UK, and Canada (Giordano et al., 2026), oat in Australia (Hayes et al., 2026), soybean in Argentina (de Felipe and Alvarez Prado, 2021), and spring wheat in Finland (Peltonen-Sainio et al., 2011). Selection for yield potential often improves yield under stress (Foulkes and Reynolds, 2015; del Pozo et al., 2026), and capturing the upside of favourable seasons is important for long-term farm profitability. The apparent contradiction between high plasticity and stability reinforces the need for unambiguous definitions and rigorous biological interpretation of statistical models.

The relative rate of increase of average yield of maize, rice, and wheat is slowing down in major production countries, with consequences for food security (Grassini et al., 2013). In this context, Sadras (2024) has linked developmental (Amzallag, 2000) and ecological and evolutionary (Terentjev, 1931; Berg, 1960) perspectives on phenotypic integration leading to the prediction that breeding favours high plasticity of yield, where it is adaptive (see definition below), at the expense of heritability. [Following Terentjev (1931), Berg (1960) interpreted correlation pleiades, namely correlations between some traits and, simultaneously, lack of correlations between these and other traits, as the independence of certain developmental processes with respect to other processes within the organism and, as the outcome from the ‘discrepancy between the agencies participating in the formation of the character and the selective forces determining its function’. Hansen et al. (2007) used rigorous experimental systems to test Berg’s hypothesis, that evolved in frameworks of phenotypic integration with stronger focus on epistasis and epigenetics (Hansen, 2011).] Experimental evidence for this prediction has been reported for winter wheat in the USA, where broad-sense heritability of yield has declined from 0.23 to 0.15 between 1990 and 2020 in parallel with a linear increase in phenotypic plasticity of yield (Giordano et al., 2026). Phenotypic plasticity linked with phenotypic integration thus identified reduced heritability as an overlooked factor contributing to the reported slowing down of genetic gain in yield.

Agronomically adaptive plasticity

We need a definition to conclude objectively whether phenotypic plasticity is agronomically desirable. Brooker et al. (2022) defines fitness as the contribution of offspring to the next generation, and adaptive plasticity as changes in phenotype in response to changes in environmental conditions in a manner that enhances fitness, including stabilizing fitness across environments and limiting fitness declines. Here we identify agronomically adaptive plasticity (AAP) where genotypes (or practices) consistently return superior yield (or other traits) across environments with no trade-off. Our definition resonates with the definition by Maynard Smith (1982) of an evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) for the evolutionary game as a strategy ‘such that, if all members of a population adopt it, no mutant strategy can invade’. Both ESS and AAP are unrealistic targets in that phenotypes cannot be optimized in nature (Darwin, 1859) or agriculture (Sadras and Denison, 2016), but are useful null hypotheses, as departures from ESS and AAP predictions would reveal adaptive constraints (Kinraide and Denison, 2003; DeWitt and Langerhans, 2004; Sadras and Denison, 2016; Gómez-Llano et al., 2025). This parallels the ‘heuristics and biases’ perspective of Tversky and Kahneman (1974) in decision-making (Box 1).

Percentile–plasticity plots to test agronomically adaptive plasticity

Percentile–plasticity plots are useful to visualize and assess the agronomic value of phenotypic plasticity (Fig. 2). For each genotype, trait percentiles, say the 90th and 10th, are calculated to quantify the trait, for example yield, under favourable and unfavourable (e.g. severe drought), conditions; percentiles account for contrasting conditions while reducing the impact of outliers. Percentiles plotted against plasticity, calculated with any of the methods above, return patterns illustrated in Fig. 2. These patterns vary with trait and combination of genotypes and environments, can be tested against our definition of agronomically adaptive plasticity, and are supported with experimental data (Sadras et al., 2009; Peltonen-Sainio et al., 2011; Sadras and Trentacoste, 2011; Trentacoste et al., 2011; Alvarez Prado et al., 2014; de Felipe and Alvarez Prado, 2021; Giordano et al., 2024, 2026; Elazab et al., 2025; Hayes et al., 2026).

In pattern A, the more plastic pink genotype is superior in good conditions, with no trade-off under stress; high phenotypic plasticity is agronomically adaptive. As an example of pattern A, a collection of 20 commercial chickpea varieties grown in eight environments featured a yield range from 1.1 t ha−1 to 5.1 t ha−1 and phenotypic plasticity for yield from 0.5 for Almaz to 1.8 for CICA0857, representing the blue and pink genotypes, respectively (Sadras et al., 2016). Pattern B is the opposite: plasticity is agronomically maladaptive because the blue genotype, with the lowest plasticity, is superior under stress with no trade-off under favourable conditions. In the same chickpea dataset, harvest index conformed to pattern B (Sadras et al., 2016), highlighting the trait dependence of relationships. Pattern C involves a trade-off where the pink genotype is superior under favourable conditions at the expense of stress adaptation. To illustrate this divergent percentile–plasticity relationship, Finnish winter rye crops in a long-term experiment with a yield range from failed crop to 10 t ha−1 featured cultivars with plasticity from 0.72 (blue genotype in Fig. 2C), which were superior under drought and inferior in high-yielding conditions, to 1.36 (pink genotype in Fig. 2C), which were superior under favourable conditions at the expense of yield under stress (Peltonen-Sainio et al., 2011). Selection for the trait of interest should favour high phenotypic plasticity in A and low plasticity in B. Additional criteria are needed to solve the trade-off in C. ‘Linking phenotypic plasticity and factor analytic models’ presents further insights from percentile–plasticity plots.

Factor analytic models

Outline

FA models are the random-effects analogue of the AMMI model commonly used in plant breeding (Table 1; Malosetti et al., 2013). An FA model is a term used to explain a general linear mixed-model (LMM) that has an embedded FA model for the G×E interaction. In the following sections, we discuss the FA model for the G×E interaction in a statistical context; then we explain why G×E is a biologically inappropriate description of development.

Empirically

We tested the links between phenotypic plasticity and FA outputs using published data for yield and phenotypic plasticity of yield for Australian oat comprising 128 cultivars and advanced lines grown in 106 environments (Hayes et al., 2026) and for 169 CIMMYT wheat lines grown in six contrasting, irrigated and rainfed environments in Mexico (Sadras et al., 2009). Yield ranges were similarly broad in both datasets: from 0.2 t ha−1 to 7.8 t ha−1 in oat (Hayes et al., 2026) and from 0.4 t ha−1 to 8.1 t ha−1 in wheat (Sadras et al., 2009). Phenotypic plasticity of yield was calculated as the slope of linear reaction norms as explained in the original studies (Sadras et al., 2009; Hayes et al., 2026) and summarized in Supplementary Protocol S1; it ranged from 0.83 to 1.21 in oat and from 0.74 to 1.27 in wheat. These datasets were selected to illustrate agronomically adaptive (Fig. 3A) and maladaptive (Fig. 3B) plasticity of yield. Overall performance and stability were calculated with FA models presented in the previous section. A percentile–plasticity plot showed that yield of oat phenotypes with high yield plasticity was markedly superior under favourable conditions and slightly better under stress (Fig. 3A). High phenotypic plasticity was neutral for wheat yield under favourable conditions and was associated with low yield under stress in our chosen dataset (Fig. 3B). Where phenotypic plasticity of yield was agronomically adaptive (i.e. for oat in Australia), phenotypic plasticity correlated positively with overall performance from FA (Fig. 3E). Where phenotypic plasticity was maladaptive (i.e. for wheat in Mexico), plasticity correlated negatively with overall performance from FA (Fig. 3D). The SE of slopes from fitted reaction norms correlated with yield stability calculated from FA, more strongly in the oat (r=0.59, P<0.0001) than in the wheat dataset (r=0.21, P=0.005).

Advocating for biologically grounded statistics

A long list of scientific papers uncritically advocating for undefined yield stability is consistent with our proposition that preferences for balance and stability partially reflect cognitive tendencies and cultural influences (Introduction and Box 1). Awareness of how culturally embedded tendencies can skew our thinking is the first step towards acknowledging, accounting for, and potentially mitigating these biases.

In the 1920s, a framework was advanced that partitioned the phenotypic variance of a trait into genetic (G) and environmental components (E) with a trait-dependent G×E interaction and residuals (Fisher, 1919; Wright, 1920). The concept remains central to stability studies in both breeding and agronomic contexts, but is biased in at least three aspects. First, the G×E interaction is statistically justified but is a misleading term to describe development because ‘genes do not interact directly with the external environment during development. All interaction is indirect, via effects of both factors in a preexisting phenotype…’ (p. 15, West-Eberhard, 2003). Biologically, G×E is better described as genotype-dependent phenotypic response to the environment. Second, the view that the plant phenotype is a passive consequence of genetic and environmental factors overlooks that the phenotype influences the genome, and the environment in a process of niche construction (Sadras and Hayman, 2025); a cautious teleonomic view of the phenotype is useful (Schrödinger, 1958; Corning et al., 2023; Odling-Smee, 2024). Third, the term ‘interaction’ introduces a bias against additivity; that is, lack of interaction (Côté et al., 2016; Jimenez et al., 2024; Sadras et al., 2024, 2025; Ploschuk et al., 2025).

Our ability to critically determine the value of yield stability in agriculture is limited by the lack of definitions, trade-offs between yield and yield stability, sophisticated statistics with opaque biological meaning, and weak agronomic narratives such as the advocacy for stability that might reflect decision biases rather than critical consideration of its benefits. Trade-offs between yield and yield stability are important (Calderini and Slafer, 1999; Raj et al., 2023; Giordano et al., 2025). A factorial experiment that compared the yield of 20–55 cultivars under standard grower practice and intensive management in 21 environments in the southern US Great Plains demonstrated an agronomic trade-off where high yield stability was achieved at the expense of a high yield gap (Giordano et al., 2025). In a breeding context, Equation (8) makes explicit this trade-off captured in α between 1, solely selecting for performance, and 0, solely selecting for stability.

We advance a definition of agronomic adaptive plasticity and use percentile–plasticity plots to show that both adaptive and maladaptive plasticity are trait dependent and may emerge depending on the combination of genotypes and environments sampled (Fig. 2). To reach a conclusion of practical value, for instance to inform breeding and management decisions, large samples are needed that capture the diversity of genotypes and environments of interest (Wang et al., 2023; Hayes et al., 2026), as well as experimental protocols emphasizing agronomic standards (Sinclair and Rufty, 2022).

FA models can be insightful by linking with biological frameworks of phenotypic plasticity. The genotype scores from an FA model play the role of random regression slopes on latent environmental axes, and the environment loadings act as those axes. When factor-1 loadings align with environment quality, the first genotype score approximates the slope of a reaction norm. FA thereby decomposes genotype-dependent, phenotypic response to the environment into systematic and residual components. In a substantive sample of genotypes and environments where oat yield was measured in breeding plots, phenotypic plasticity was agronomically adaptive and correlated with overall performance calculated with FA (Fig. 3A, C). In a similarly large, agronomically relevant dataset of wheat yield, phenotypic plasticity was maladaptive and correlated negatively with overall performance calculated with FA (Fig. 3B, D).

Conclusion

Our biologically informed statistical synthesis shows that intricate statistical models, which are opaque to all except practitioners, return phenotypic traits that contain information comparable with transparent reaction norms relating yield and environmental mean yield, thus connecting the biologically rich concept of phenotypic plasticity with advanced statistics.
