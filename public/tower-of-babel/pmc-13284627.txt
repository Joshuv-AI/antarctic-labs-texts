Continued Nintedanib Treatment in Children and Adolescents With Fibrosing ILDs: Data From InPedILD‐ON
Authors: Robin Deterding, Lisa R Young, Emily M DeBoer, David Warburton, Steven Cunningham, Nicolaus Schwerk, Kevin K Brown, Václav Koucký, Jason P Weinman, Jill Simmons, Mihaela Dumistracel, Frauke Jantzen, Martina Gahlemann, Matthias Griese, the InPedILD‐ON trial investigators

Introduction

Childhood interstitial lung disease (chILD) comprises a large and heterogeneous group of rare respiratory disorders affecting infants, children, and adolescents [1, 2, 3]. Some patients with chILD develop pulmonary fibrosis, which can become progressive [2, 3, 4, 5]. The rarity of chILD and the challenges of measuring its progression, have resulted in a paucity of clinical trials and limited treatment options [4]. Clinical management of patients with chILD is focused on supportive care or empirical treatment with immunosuppressants [6, 7].

Nintedanib is an intracellular inhibitor of tyrosine kinases that inhibits processes involved in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis [8]. Nintedanib is approved for the treatment of adults with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), other progressive fibrosing ILDs, and fibrosing ILD associated with systemic sclerosis (SSc‐ILD), and has been shown to slow the progression of these ILDs in adults [9, 10, 11]. Nintedanib has also been approved in Europe for the treatment of children aged > 6 years with SSc‐ILD and children aged 6−17 years with clinically significant progressive fibrosing ILDs [12]. The randomized, placebo‐controlled InPedILD trial investigated the dose‐exposure and safety of nintedanib in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILDs. The results showed that using weight‐based dosing, exposure to nintedanib in pediatric patients was comparable to that in adults given a 150 mg twice daily dose and that nintedanib had an acceptable safety profile in pediatric patients [13]. Although the InPedILD trial was not powered to assess the efficacy of nintedanib, changes in forced vital capacity (FVC) % predicted over 24 weeks favored nintedanib over placebo [13, 14].

The safety and tolerability of nintedanib in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILDs continue to be assessed in the open‐label extension of the InPedILD trial, InPedILD‐ON. Exploratory data on lung function outcomes are also being collected. Here we present data from the first year of InPedILD‐ON.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The design of the parent InPedILD trial has been published [13]. Briefly, this trial enrolled patients aged 6–17 years with fibrosing ILD on high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT), FVC ≥25% predicted and clinically significant disease based on a Fan score ≥3 [15] or evidence of clinical progression over any time frame. Evidence of clinical progression was defined as a relative decline in FVC % predicted ≥10%; a relative decline in FVC % predicted of 5%–10% with worsened symptoms; increased fibrosis on HRCT; or other measures of clinical worsening attributed to progressive pulmonary fibrosis. Patients were randomized 2:1 to receive nintedanib or placebo for 24 weeks, followed by open‐label nintedanib. Dosing was based on weight‐dependent allometric scaling, with starting doses of 50, 75, 100, or 150 mg twice daily (bid) [13]. Reductions to the dose below were permitted to manage adverse events. Dose re‐escalation was allowed within 4 weeks of dose reduction for adverse events considered related to the trial drug and within 8 weeks of dose reduction for adverse events considered not related to the trial drug.

Median (minimum, maximum) exposure to nintedanib in the InPedILD trial was 45.6 (3.4, 80.3) weeks in patients randomized to nintedanib and 24.9 (6.7, 57.1) weeks in patients randomized to placebo. Patients who completed the InPedILD trial and new patients were eligible to participate in InPedILD‐ON. Patients who completed the InPedILD trial on treatment and had an off‐treatment transition period of ≤ 12 weeks before entering InPedILD‐ON were regarded as “rollover patients.” Patients who completed the InPedILD trial and had an off‐treatment transition period of > 12 weeks before entering InPedILD‐ON, and patients who had not participated in the InPedILD trial, entered InPedILD‐ON as “new patients.” New patients underwent a 4‐week screening period and had to meet the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as used in the InPedILD trial.

Trial Design

Rollover patients received nintedanib in InPedILD‐ON (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05285982; date of registration, 18 March 2022) at a dose corresponding to their assigned dose (nintedanib or placebo) at the end of the InPedILD trial. Patients taking a reduced dose due to an adverse event could stay on the reduced dose or increase to the dose corresponding to their weight. New patients received nintedanib in InPedILD‐ON at a dose corresponding to their weight, using the same thresholds as used in the InPedILD trial [13]. Treatment interruptions, dose reductions and dose re‐escalations were allowed as in the InPedILD trial. In InPedILD‐ON, visits occurred at baseline, at weeks 2, 12, 24, 36, 52, 64, 76, 88, 104, and then every 12 or 16 weeks during treatment. A follow‐up visit took place 4 weeks after the end of treatment.

The InPedILD trial was initiated at each site following approval by the respective institutional review board/independent ethics committee and competent authority according to national and international regulations. The trial was carried out in compliance with the protocol, the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Harmonized Tripartite Guideline for Good Clinical Practice from the International Conference on Harmonization, and other relevant guidelines. Written informed consent and/or assent (as applicable) were obtained prior to trial entry.

Endpoints

The primary safety endpoint was the incidence of adverse events. Adverse events were coded based on preferred terms in the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) v.26.0. Growth and bone and tooth development were monitored given the potential effects of nintedanib on epiphyseal growth plates of large bones and tooth development [16]. Endpoints included the incidence of pathological findings of the epiphyseal growth plate on imaging over 52 weeks; incidence of pathological findings on dental examination or imaging over 52 weeks; and changes from baseline in height, height‐for‐age z‐score, and body mass index (BMI)‐for‐age z‐score at Week 52. The z‐score gives the number of standard deviations that a value deviates from the mean value of the population. MRI and X‐ray assessments and dental panoramic X‐rays were centrally reviewed by experts in radiology and pediatric dentistry, respectively.

A pathological finding of the epiphyseal growth plate was defined as a pathological finding at the distal femur or the proximal tibia. Thickening of the epiphyseal growth plates and swelling of articular cartilage were always captured as pathological findings. Metaphyseal lines, narrowing of the lucent growth plate margin, and other findings were only captured as pathological findings if judged potentially pathological. No bone imaging was conducted in rollover patients who had closed physes at the end of the InPedILD trial.

For dental imaging, a pathological finding was defined as at least one tooth being reported with at least one of the following findings: change in root length; stunted growth (e.g., premature closing of the apex/apices with a blunted root appearance); accelerated growth (abnormally long roots exceeding two‐thirds of the length of the tooth); extra/supernumerary teeth; impacted permanent teeth; additional findings (cyst present, abscess present, solid lesion present, bone abnormality present); or other findings.

Efficacy endpoints included changes from baseline in FVC % predicted and oxygen saturation (SpO2) on room air at rest at Week 52. Spirometry was performed according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines [17] using spirometers supplied by the sponsor.

Analyses

Analyses were conducted in all patients and in two groups: (i) patients who received nintedanib in the InPedILD trial and had a transition between InPedILD and InPedILD‐ON ≤ 12 weeks (the “continued nintedanib” group), and (ii) patients who had a transition between InPedILD and InPedILD‐ON >12 weeks, patients who only received double‐blind placebo in the InPedILD trial, and patients who did not participate in the InPedILD trial (the “initiated nintedanib” group). Incidence rates of adverse events were calculated as the number of patients with the respective event per group divided by the time at risk and were expressed per 100 patient‐years. FVC % predicted values were calculated using equations published by the Global Lung Function Initiative [18]. Analyses were based on a data snapshot taken on 25 August 2023 (i.e., once all rollover patients had completed the 52‐week visit or withdrawn from the trial). Analyses were descriptive and based on observed data.

Results

Patients

Forty‐eight patients received open‐label nintedanib in InPedILD‐ON (28 in the continued nintedanib group, 20 in the initiated nintedanib group) (Figure 1). The most common diagnoses were surfactant protein deficiency (n = 12; 25.0%) and systemic sclerosis (n = 9; 18.8%). At baseline of InPedILD‐ON, mean (SD) age was 13.7 (3.2) years, FVC % predicted was 59.4 (21.6), and FVC z‐score was −3.4 (1.8) (Table 1). Mean (SD) BMI‐for‐age z‐score was −0.63 (1.84), and height‐for‐age z‐score was −0.85 (1.44).

Patient Disposition and Exposure

At the time of the data snapshot, 22 patients (78.6%) in the continued nintedanib group and 19 patients (95.0%) in the initiated nintedanib group were still taking nintedanib; six patients (21.4%) in the continued nintedanib group and one patient (5.0%) in the initiated nintedanib group had discontinued nintedanib (Figure 1). One patient discontinued nintedanib for each of the following reasons: adverse event, change of residence, burden of study procedures, pregnancy planning, clinical deterioration, other treatment option available. No reason was given by one patient. Median (minimum, maximum) exposure to nintedanib in InPedILD‐ON was 61.5 (1.4, 72.4) weeks in all patients, 70.2 (27.1, 72.4) weeks in the continued nintedanib group and 10.1 (1.4, 69.3) weeks in the initiated nintedanib group.

Dose Adjustments

Dose reductions were reported in 10 patients (20.8%) (five patients [17.9%] in the continued nintedanib group, five patients [25.0%] in the initiated nintedanib group). Treatment interruptions were reported in 15 patients (31.3%) (10 patients [35.7%] in the continued nintedanib group, five patients [25.0%] in the initiated nintedanib group). The mean (SD) duration of treatment interruption was 39.2 (50.9) days in all patients, 50.9 (58.9) days in the continued nintedanib group, and 15.9 (15.5) days in the initiated nintedanib group.

Adverse Events

Diarrhea was the most frequent adverse event, reported in 15 patients (31.3%) and at a rate of 50.4 per 100 patient–years (Table 2). No patients discontinued nintedanib due to diarrhea. Serious adverse events were reported in eight patients (16.7%) (five patients [17.9%] in the continued nintedanib group, three patients [15.0%] in the initiated nintedanib group). The most frequent serious adverse event was progression of ILD, which was reported in two patients (4.2%) (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). One patient (3.6%) in the continued nintedanib group had an adverse event that led to nintedanib discontinuation (weight decrease). One patient (5.0%) in the initiated nintedanib group had elevations in alanine transaminase and/or aspartate transaminase ≥3 times the upper limit of normal. Liver enzymes normalized within 1 week after nintedanib interruption. Nintedanib was resumed at a decreased dose without recurrence of the event.

Height and BMI

Mean (SE) change in height at Week 52 was 2.1 (0.6) cm in all patients (n = 27), 2.3 (0.6) cm in patients who continued nintedanib (n = 24), and 1.0 (2.1) cm in patients who initiated nintedanib (n = 3) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). Mean (SE) change in height‐for‐age z‐score at Week 52 was −0.04 (0.06) in all patients (n = 24), −0.05 (0.06) in patients who continued nintedanib (n = 21) and −0.01 (0.20) in patients who initiated nintedanib (n = 3) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Mean (SE) change in BMI‐for‐age z‐score at Week 52 was −0.26 (0.14) in all patients (n = 24), −0.17 (0.13) in patients who continued nintedanib (n = 21) and −0.90 (0.69) in patients who initiated nintedanib (n = 3) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3).

Lung Function

Mean (SE) change in FVC % predicted at Week 52 was −1.0 (1.4) in all patients (n = 26), −1.1 (1.5) in patients who continued nintedanib (n = 23), and −0.5 (2.5) in patients who initiated nintedanib (n = 3) (Figure 2). Discussion

The InPedILD‐ON trial is investigating the safety and tolerability of nintedanib in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILD. The safety profile of nintedanib over the first year of InPedILD‐ON was generally consistent with the safety profile seen in the InPedILD trial [13], supporting the tolerability of nintedanib in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILD over longer‐term use. As observed in trials of nintedanib in adults [9, 10, 11], and in the InPedILD trial [13], the most frequent adverse event in InPedILD‐ON was diarrhea, which was reported in 31% of patients but did not lead to discontinuation of nintedanib. Approximately 10% of patients had weight decrease reported as an adverse event in InPedILD‐ON, with one patient in the continued nintedanib group discontinuing nintedanib due to this adverse event. Weight loss is a known adverse event of nintedanib in adults with fibrosing ILDs [19, 20, 21, 22]. Monitoring children and adolescents treated with nintedanib for weight loss (or insufficient weight gain) is important to enable early and appropriate intervention. Importantly, in the InPedILD and InPedILD‐ON trials, growth plate imaging and changes in height‐for‐age z‐scores suggested normal growth. There were no pathological findings on bone imaging indicative of a causal relationship with nintedanib and no premature closure of epiphysis. In the InPedILD trial, six patients in the nintedanib group had stunted growth of the dental root on imaging over 24 weeks; however, these could not be confirmed by an expert pediatric dentist. No patients had accelerated or stunted growth of the dental root on dental imaging in InPedILD‐ON. Thus, based on regular dental examinations and dental imaging, the potential impact of nintedanib on tooth development was not confirmed.

Measuring changes in FVC in children with ILD can be challenging from a technical standpoint. As in the InPedILD trial and in the Phase II trial of hydroxychloroquine in children with fibrosing ILD [23], FVC measurements in InPedILD‐ON showed large variability. There was a small change in mean FVC % predicted over 52 weeks (−1.0%) that was numerically similar to the adjusted mean change observed over 24 weeks in patients taking nintedanib in the InPedILD trial (+0.3, compared with −0.9 in the placebo group) [13]. In a meta‐analysis of data from placebo‐controlled trials of nintedanib in adults with fibrosing ILDs, the treatment effect of nintedanib on change in FVC % predicted at Week 24 was 1.65 (in favor of nintedanib) [13]. Based on a Bayesian borrowing analysis that used data on the effect of nintedanib on FVC from trials in adults as well as from the InPedILD trial, the estimated treatment effect of nintedanib on FVC % predicted in children and adolescents over 24 weeks was 1.63 (in favor of nintedanib) [14]. Few data are available on the course of FVC in children with ILD, but among 44 children with ILD due to ABCA3 deficiency who survived beyond the age of 1 year, the decline in FVC % predicted was −1.1/year [24].

Our analysis has limitations, including the small number of patients, the lack of a comparator arm, and the potential for selection bias in that patients who received nintedanib in the InPedILD trial may have been more likely to continue into InPedILD‐ON if they experienced fewer adverse events or had less progressive disease. Some patients may have completed or nearly completed growth at the time of enrollment, impacting changes in height. InPedILD‐ON provides data on the longest period of treatment with nintedanib in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILD, but more data are needed to improve understanding of the course of ILD and the long‐term efficacy of nintedanib in these patients. Published data on the course of FVC in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILD who are not treated with nintedanib are limited. Patient registries will be key to improving our understanding of the natural history of chILD of various etiologies [2, 7, 25]. Diligently collected long‐term data are needed, including those that cover the transition of care from childhood to adulthood [26].

In conclusion, the adverse event profile of nintedanib in InPedILD‐ON was generally consistent with that reported in the InPedILD trial, supporting the tolerability of nintedanib in children and adolescents with fibrosing ILD. FVC measurements showed large variability. The InPedILD‐ON trial will continue to provide data on the clinical course of fibrosing ILD in pediatric patients and the safety of nintedanib in this patient population.
