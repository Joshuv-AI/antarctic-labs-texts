Nonlinear elliptic Dirichlet boundary value problems on time scales
Shalmali Bandyopadhyay, F. Ayça Çetinkaya, Tom Cuchta

Abstract
We establish existence and uniqueness results for nonlinear elliptic Dirichlet boundary value problems on n-dimensional time scale domains. Time scales provide a unified framework that encompasses continuous, discrete, and hybrid settings. Under a Lipschitz condition on the nonlinearity bounded by the first eigenvalue, we prove existence and uniqueness using the contraction mapping theorem. Under a weaker one-sided growth condition, we establish existence using the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem. To apply these functional analytic methods, we reformulate the problem as an operator equation, which requires developing the spectral theory for the Dirichlet Laplacian with mixed nabla-delta derivatives. We establish self-adjointness, positivity, and completeness of eigenfunctions, and the product eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal basis in the n-dimensional setting.



Keywords:
partial dynamic equations, elliptic problem, Dirichlet boundary conditions, eigenfunction expansion, time scale

34N05, 35J65, 35J61, 35J15.

2
Preliminaries

We establish the necessary background for our investigation. The delta derivative
f^{\Delta}
and nabla derivative
f^{\nabla}
are defined as in
[
5
]
. A point
t\in\mathbb{T}
is called right-scattered if
\sigma(t)>t
, right-dense if
\sigma(t)=t
, left-scattered if
\rho(t)<t
, and left-dense if
\rho(t)=t
. A point that is both right-scattered and left-scattered is called isolated.

We begin by stating the fundamental relationship between the delta and nabla derivatives. In
[
3
, Theorems 2.5, 2.6]
, it was shown that the
\Delta
-derivative and
\nabla
-derivative obey the following formulas: if
f\colon[a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and
\Delta
-differentiable on
[a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
with continuous
f^{\Delta}
, then
f
is
\nabla
-differentiable on
(a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
and

f^{\nabla}(t)=f^{\Delta}(\rho(t))\quad\text{for all }t\in(a,b]\cap\mathbb{T};

(2.1)

if
f
is
\nabla
-differentiable on
(a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
with continuous
f^{\nabla}
, then
f
is
\Delta
-differentiable on
[a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
and

f^{\Delta}(t)=f^{\nabla}(\sigma(t))\quad\text{for all }t\in[a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}.

(2.2)

The following integration by parts formulas
[
11
, Theorem 2.4]
are essential for establishing self-adjointness of our differential operators: for continuous
f
and
g
on
[a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
with
f^{\Delta}
and
g^{\nabla}
continuous and bounded,

\int_{a}^{b}f^{\Delta}(t)g(t)\Delta t=f(t)g(t)\Big|_{a}^{b}-\int_{a}^{b}f(t)g^{\nabla}(t)\nabla t,

(2.3)

and

\int_{a}^{b}f^{\nabla}(t)g(t)\nabla t=f(t)g(t)\Big|_{a}^{b}-\int_{a}^{b}f(t)g^{\Delta}(t)\Delta t.

(2.4)

Fubini’s theorem for time scales
[
6
, Theorem 3.10]
says for a function
f\colon\mathbb{T}_{1}\times\mathbb{T}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
that is bounded and
\Delta
-integrable over
R=[a,b)\times[c,d)\subset\mathbb{T}_{1}\times\mathbb{T}_{2}
, if the integrals
I(t)=\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}f(t,s)\Delta_{2}s
and
K(s)=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(t,s)\Delta_{1}t

exist for each
(t,s)\in[a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}_{1}\times[c,d)\cap\mathbb{T}_{2}\in[a,b)
, then the iterated integrals exist and satisfy

\iint_{R}f(t,s)\Delta_{1}t\Delta_{2}s=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}I(t)\Delta_{1}t=\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}K(s)\Delta_{2}s.

(2.5)

Finally, we recall the following standard fixed point results that are relevant for our purposes.

Theorem 2.1

(Hilbert–Schmidt theorem)
.

Let
(\mathscr{H},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a real Hilbert space and let
T\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
be a bounded, compact, self-adjoint operator. Then there exists a sequence of nonzero real eigenvalues
\mu_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
(where
N=\text{rank }T
), with
|\mu_{i}|
monotonically non-increasing, and an orthonormal set
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of corresponding eigenfunctions such that
Tx=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\langle\phi_{i},x\rangle\phi_{i}
for
x\in\mathscr{H}
. If
\ker(T)=\{0\}
, then the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathscr{H}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Contraction Mapping Theorem)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a nonempty complete metric space, and let
W\colon X\to X
be a contraction mapping; that is, there exists a constant
0<c<1
such that for all
x,y\in X
,
d(Wx,Wy)\leq c\,d(x,y)
.
Then
W
has a unique fixed point
x^{*}\in X
, and for any
x_{0}\in X
, the sequence defined by
x_{n+1}=W(x_{n})
converges to
x^{*}
.

Theorem 2.3

(Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem
[
17
, Ch. XII]
)
.

Let
X
be a real Banach space and
G\colon X\to X
a continuous, compact operator. Suppose that the set

\{x\in X\colon x=\tau Gx\text{ for some }\tau\in[0,1]\}

is bounded. Then
G
has at least one fixed point in
X
.

3
One-dimensional Dirichlet eigenvalue problem

We develop the eigenfunction expansion theory for the one-dimensional Dirichlet eigenvalue problem with the
\nabla\Delta
operator. In
[
11
]
, eigenfunction expansions for
-[p(t)y^{\Delta}(t)]^{\nabla}+q(t)y(t)=\lambda y(t)
were established using the
\Delta\nabla
operator with
\mu_{\nabla}
-measure. We develop the analogous theory for the Dirichlet problem with the
\nabla\Delta
operator and
\mu_{\Delta}
-measure.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a time scale and let
a,b\in\mathbb{T}
be fixed points with
a<b
such that
(a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
is nonempty. We consider the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}-y^{\nabla\Delta}(t)=\lambda y(t),&t\in(a,b)\cap\mathbb{T},\\
y(a)=y(b)=0.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Denote by
H
the real Hilbert space of all
\Delta
-measurable functions
y\colon[a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(i)

y(a)=0
whenever
a
is right-scattered, and

(ii)

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}y^{2}(t)\,\Delta t<\infty
.

The space
H
is equipped with the inner product
\langle y,z\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}y(t)z(t)\Delta t

and the induced norm
\|y\|=\sqrt{\langle y,y\rangle}
. This is the one-dimensional analogue of the space
\mathscr{H}
defined in Section
1
.

Remark 3.1
.

The space
H
is complete. To see this, recall that the Lebesgue
\Delta
-measure
\mu_{\Delta}
on time scales is constructed via the Carathéodory extension as described in
[
7
]
. The space
L^{2}([a,b),\mu_{\Delta})
of square-integrable functions with respect to this measure is a Hilbert space. The condition
y(a)=0
when
a
is right-scattered defines a closed subspace of
L^{2}([a,b),\mu_{\Delta})
, since evaluation at a right-scattered point is a continuous linear functional. Therefore
H
is complete as a closed subspace of a complete space.

Let
D
denote the set of all functions
y\in H
satisfying the following conditions (adapted as the one-dimensional analogue of the domain introduced in Section
1
)

(i)

y
is continuous on
[a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
,
y(a)=0
, there exists
y(b):=\displaystyle\lim_{t\to b^{-}}y(t)
and
y(b)=0
,

(ii)

y
is continuously
\nabla
-differentiable on
(a,b]
, and there exist finite limits
y^{\nabla}(a):=\displaystyle\lim_{t\to a^{+}}y^{\nabla}(t)
and
y^{\nabla}(b):=\displaystyle\lim_{t\to b^{-}}y^{\nabla}(t)
, and

(iii)

y^{\nabla}
is
\Delta
-differentiable on
[a,b)
with
y^{\nabla\Delta}\in H
.

We define the operator
A\colon D\subset H\to H
by
(Ay)(t)=-y^{\nabla\Delta}(t)
. The eigenvalue problem (
3.1
) is equivalent to the operator equation
Ay=\lambda y
for
y\in D
with
y\neq 0
.

Remark 3.2
.

The set
D
is a linear subspace dense in
H
. To see density, observe that in the discrete case when
(a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
consists of finitely many points,
H
is finite-dimensional and
D=H
. In the continuous case
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
, density follows from the standard result that
C_{0}^{\infty}(a,b)
is dense in
L^{2}(a,b)
. For general time scales, the condition
y(a)=0
when
a
is right-scattered (imposed in the definition of
H
) is essential for ensuring density; see
[
11
, Remark 3.4]
for the analogous observation in the
\Delta\nabla
setting.

We now explore self-adjointness and positivity of the operator
A
.

Theorem 3.3
.

The operator
A
is symmetric and positive. Specifically, for all
y,z\in D
,

\displaystyle\langle Ay,z\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle y,Az\rangle,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\langle Ay,y\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\left(y^{\nabla}(t)\right)^{2}\nabla t.

(3.3)

In particular,
\langle Ay,y\rangle>0
for all
y\in D
with
y\neq 0
.

Proof.

For
y,z\in D
,
\langle Ay,z\rangle=-\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}y^{\nabla\Delta}(t)z(t)\Delta t
. Apply (
2.3
) with
f=y^{\nabla}
and
g=z
to obtain

\displaystyle-\int_{a}^{b}y^{\nabla\Delta}(t)z(t)\Delta t

\displaystyle=-y^{\nabla}(t)z(t)\Big|_{a}^{b}+\int_{a}^{b}y^{\nabla}(t)z^{\nabla}(t)\nabla t=\int_{a}^{b}y^{\nabla}(t)z^{\nabla}(t)\nabla t,

where the boundary conditions
z(a)=z(b)=0
are used.
Now apply (
2.4
) with
f=y
and
g=z^{\nabla}
and use
y(a)=y(b)=0
to get

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}y^{\nabla}(t)z^{\nabla}(t)\nabla t

\displaystyle=y(t)z^{\nabla}(t)\Big|_{a}^{b}-\int_{a}^{b}y(t)z^{\nabla\Delta}(t)\Delta t=-\int_{a}^{b}y(t)z^{\nabla\Delta}(t)\Delta t=\langle y,Az\rangle,

(3.4)

proving (
3.2
).
Then (
3.3
) follows by setting
z=y
in (
3.4
).
Since the integrand in (
3.3
) is nonnegative, we have
\langle Ay,y\rangle\geq 0
. If
\langle Ay,y\rangle=0
, then
y^{\nabla}(t)=0
for almost every

, which implies
y
is constant. Boundary conditions in (
3.1
) then imply
y\equiv 0
. Therefore
\langle Ay,y\rangle>0
for all nonzero

. This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

All eigenvalues of (
3.1
) are real, positive and simple. Moreover, eigenfunctions corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue with eigenfunction
y\neq 0
. Since
A
is symmetric and positive on the real Hilbert space
H
, the eigenvalue
\lambda
satisfies

\lambda\|y\|^{2}=\langle Ay,y\rangle>0,

which implies
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

For orthogonality, let
Ay_{1}=\lambda_{1}y_{1}
and
Ay_{2}=\lambda_{2}y_{2}
with
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
. Then by (
3.2
)

(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle=\langle Ay_{1},y_{2}\rangle-\langle y_{1},Ay_{2}\rangle=0,

and since
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
, it follows that
\langle y_{1},y_{2}\rangle=0
.

Finally, we prove all the eigenvalues are simple. Let
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
be solutions of (
3.1
). Define the Wronskian

W(t):=y_{1}(t)y_{2}^{\nabla}(t)-y_{2}(t)y_{1}^{\nabla}(t).

We show that
W
is constant on
(a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
. Using the product rule for delta derivatives, we compute

\displaystyle W^{\Delta}=y_{1}^{\Delta}y_{2}^{\nabla}+y_{1}^{\sigma}y_{2}^{\nabla\Delta}-y_{2}^{\Delta}y_{1}^{\nabla}-y_{2}^{\sigma}y_{1}^{\nabla\Delta}.

(3.5)

By (
2.1
)–(
2.2
), we have
y_{i}^{\Delta}=y_{i}^{\nabla^{\sigma}}
. Then, substituting
y_{i}^{\Delta}=y_{i}^{\nabla^{\sigma}}
and
y_{i}^{\nabla\Delta}=-\lambda y_{i}
into (
3.5
), we see

W^{\Delta}=y_{1}^{\nabla^{\sigma}}y_{2}^{\nabla}-y_{2}^{\nabla^{\sigma}}y_{1}^{\nabla}-\lambda(y_{1}^{\sigma}y_{2}-y_{2}^{\sigma}y_{1}).

If
t
is right-dense, then
\sigma(t)=t
and thus
W^{\Delta}=0
.
If
t
is right-scattered then
y_{i}^{\sigma}=y_{i}+\mu y_{i}^{\Delta}
and upon simplifying, the terms again cancel, yielding
W^{\Delta}=0
.
Hence
W
is constant on
(a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
. Since
y_{1}(a)=y_{2}(a)=0
, we have
W(a)=0
, consequently
W\equiv 0
on
(a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
. Therefore
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
are linearly dependent.
∎

We now prove that the operator
A
has trivial kernel and construct its inverse using a Green’s function.

Lemma 3.5
.

\ker(A)=\{0\}
.

Proof.

If
y\in D
and
Ay=0
, then by (
3.3
),
0=\langle Ay,y\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left(y^{\nabla}(t)\right)^{2}\nabla t,

which implies
y^{\nabla}(t)=0
for all

. Hence the boundary condition

gives
y\equiv 0
on
[a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}
.
∎

Since
\ker(A)=\{0\}
, the inverse operator
A^{-1}
exists. To give an explicit representation, we introduce the Green’s function.

Definition 3.6
.

The Green’s function for problem (
3.1

G(t,s)=\frac{1}{b-a}\begin{cases}(t-a)(b-s)&\text{if }t\leq s,\\
(s-a)(b-t)&\text{if }t\geq s.\end{cases}

(3.6)

We now demonstrate how

.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
G
be defined as in (

). Then the inverse operator

is

\left(A^{-1}u\right)(t)=\int_{a}^{b}G(t,s)u(s)\Delta s.

(3.7)

Moreover,
A^{-1}
is symmetric and compact on

.

Proof.

Let
u\in H
and define
y(t):=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}G(t,s)u(s)\,\Delta s
. From (
3.6
),
G(a,s)=G(b,s)=0
for all
s
, hence
y(a)=y(b)=0
. For
t\in(a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
,

y(t)=\int_{a}^{t}G(t,s)u(s)\,\Delta s+\int_{t}^{b}G(t,s)u(s)\,\Delta s=\frac{b-t}{b-a}\int_{a}^{t}(s-a)u(s)\,\Delta s+\frac{t-a}{b-a}\int_{t}^{b}(b-s)u(s)\,\Delta s.

Define
I_{1}(t)=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{t}(s-a)u(s)\,\Delta s
and
I_{2}(t)=\displaystyle\int_{t}^{b}(b-s)u(s)\,\Delta s
, so that
y(t)=\dfrac{1}{b-a}\Big[(b-t)I_{1}(t)+(t-a)I_{2}(t)\Big]
. Then
I_{1}^{\Delta}(t)=(t-a)u(t)
and
I_{2}^{\Delta}(t)=-(b-t)u(t)
, hence after the product rule and simplifying,

(b-a)y^{\Delta}(t)=I_{2}(t)-I_{1}(t)-(b-a)\mu(t)u(t).

(3.8)

Applying (
2.1
), (
3.8
) becomes

(b-a)y^{\nabla}(t)=I_{2}(\rho(t))-I_{1}(\rho(t))-(b-a)\mu(\rho(t))u(\rho(t)).

To complete the verification, we compute
y^{\nabla\Delta}
directly. By (
2.1
),
I_{1}^{\nabla}(t)=I_{1}^{\Delta}(\rho(t))=(\rho(t)-a)u(\rho(t))
and
I_{2}^{\nabla}(t)=I_{2}^{\Delta}(\rho(t))=-(b-\rho(t))u(\rho(t))
. By the product rule,

\displaystyle[(b-t)I_{1}(t)]^{\nabla}

\displaystyle=-I_{1}(t)+(b-\rho(t))(\rho(t)-a)u(\rho(t)),

\displaystyle[(t-a)I_{2}(t)]^{\nabla}

\displaystyle=I_{2}(t)-(\rho(t)-a)(b-\rho(t))u(\rho(t)).

Adding these expressions reveals
(b-a)y^{\nabla}(t)=I_{2}(t)-I_{1}(t)
. Now we compute

y^{\nabla\Delta}(t)=\dfrac{I_{2}^{\Delta}(t)-I_{1}^{\Delta}(t)}{b-a}=\frac{-(b-t)u(t)-(t-a)u(t)}{b-a}=-u(t).

Hence
-y^{\nabla\Delta}(t)=u(t)
for
t\in(a,b)
, and thus
y=A^{-1}u
.

For compactness, let
M=\max\limits_{(t,s)\in\left([a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}\right)^{2}}|G(t,s)|
. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|(A^{-1}u)(t)|^{2}\leq\left(\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\,\Delta s\right)\|u\|^{2}\leq M^{2}(b-a)\|u\|^{2}.

Hence
\|A^{-1}u\|_{\infty}\leq M\sqrt{b-a}\,\|u\|
. Moreover, for
t_{1},t_{2}\in[a,b]
,

\Big|(A^{-1}u)(t_{1})-(A^{-1}u)(t_{2})\Big|\leq\sqrt{b-a}\,\|u\|\left(\int_{a}^{b}|G(t_{1},s)-G(t_{2},s)|^{2}\,\Delta s\right)^{1/2}.

Since
G
is uniformly continuous on
\left([a,b]\cap\mathbb{T}\right)^{2}
, the right-hand side tends to
0
uniformly as
t_{1}\to t_{2}
. Thus
A^{-1}
maps bounded sets of
H
into equicontinuous, uniformly bounded sets in
C([a,b]\cap\mathbb{T})
. By the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem,
A^{-1}
is compact on
H
. Since
G(t,s)=G(s,t)
by (
3.6
), we have
\langle A^{-1}u,v\rangle=\langle u,A^{-1}v\rangle
, so
A^{-1}
is symmetric.
∎

Next we employ the Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 3.8
.

For the eigenvalue problem (
3.1
), there exists an orthonormal system
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
of eigenfunctions corresponding to eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
, where
N=\dim H
. Each eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
is positive and simple. The eigenvalues can be arranged as

0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}<\cdots

The system
\left\{\phi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{N}
forms an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space
H
. Therefore, any function
f\in H
can be expanded as

f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\phi_{k}(t),

(3.9)

where the Fourier coefficients are given by

c_{k}=\langle f,\phi_{k}\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\phi_{k}(t)\,\Delta t.

When
N<\infty
, the sum (
3.9
) is finite and the equality is exact. When
N=\infty
, the series converges to
f
in the norm of
H
, i.e.

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\left\|f-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}\phi_{k}\right\|=0
. Moreover, Parseval’s equality
\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f^{2}(t)\Delta t=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}^{2}
holds.

Proof.

The eigenvalue problem
Ay=\lambda y
with
y\neq 0
is equivalent to
A^{-1}y=\widehat{\lambda}y
where
\widehat{\lambda}=1/\lambda
. By Theorem
3.7
,
A^{-1}
is compact and symmetric on
H
. By Lemma
3.5
,
\ker(A)=\{0\}
, and hence
\ker(A^{-1})=\{0\}
: if
A^{-1}y=0
for some
y\in H
, then applying
A
to both sides gives
y=A(A^{-1}y)=0
.

By Theorem
2.1
, there exists an orthonormal system
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
of eigenfunctions of
A^{-1}
corresponding to nonzero eigenvalues
\left\{\widehat{\lambda}_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{N}
, where
N=\dim H
. Since
\ker(A^{-1})=\{0\}
, this system forms an orthonormal basis for
H
.

Each
\phi_{k}
satisfies
A^{-1}\phi_{k}=\widehat{\lambda}_{k}\phi_{k}
with
\widehat{\lambda}_{k}\neq 0
. Applying
A
to both sides yields
\phi_{k}=\widehat{\lambda}_{k}A\phi_{k}
, hence
A\phi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\phi_{k}
where
\lambda_{k}=1/\widehat{\lambda}_{k}
. By Corollary
3.4
,
\lambda_{k}>0
for all
k
, and
\lambda_{j}\neq\lambda_{k}
for
j\neq k
. Since
|\widehat{\lambda}_{k}|
is monotonically non-increasing with
\widehat{\lambda}_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
when
N=\infty
, the eigenvalues satisfy
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\lambda_{3}<\cdots
. Since
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
is an orthonormal basis for
H
, any
f\in H
can be expanded as
f=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\phi_{k}
where
c_{k}=\langle f,\phi_{k}\rangle
, and Parseval’s equality
\|f\|^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}^{2}
holds. When
N=\infty
(countable infinity), convergence follows from orthonormality because
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}c_{k}^{2}=\|f\|^{2}<\infty
implies that

\left\|f-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}\phi_{k}\right\|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}-\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}^{2}\to 0

as
m\to\infty
.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

The number of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of problem (
3.1
) equals
\dim H
. When
(a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
contains finitely many points, say
m
points, then
H\cong\mathbb{R}^{m}
and problem (
3.1
) reduces to a matrix eigenvalue problem with exactly
m
eigenvalues. When
(a,b)\cap\mathbb{T}
is infinite, the space
H
is a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, so
\dim H=\aleph_{0}
, and problem (
3.1
) has countably many eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots
with
\lambda_{k}\to\infty
. This includes both the continuous case
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}
, where
H=L^{2}([a,b))
, and the discrete case
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{Z}
. The eigenfunction expansion (
3.9
) is then an infinite series converging in the norm of
H
.

The following estimate provides a lower bound for the first eigenvalue in terms of the interval length.

Theorem 3.10
.

The first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of problem (
3.1
) satisfies

\lambda_{1}\geq\dfrac{4}{(b-a)^{2}}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi_{1}
be the normalized eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{1}
, so that
\|\phi_{1}\|=1
and
A\phi_{1}=\lambda_{1}\phi_{1}
. From the integral equation
\phi_{1}=\lambda_{1}A^{-1}\phi_{1}
, by (
3.7
), we have
\phi_{1}(t)=\lambda_{1}\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}G(t,s)\phi_{1}(s)\Delta s
. Taking absolute values and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|\phi_{1}(t)|^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}\left(\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\Delta s\right)\left(\int_{a}^{b}|\phi_{1}(s)|^{2}\Delta s\right)=\lambda_{1}^{2}\left(\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\Delta s\right)\|\phi_{1}\|^{2}.

Since
\|\phi_{1}\|=1
, we have
|\phi_{1}(t)|^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\Delta s.

Integrating both sides with respect to
t
,

\int_{a}^{b}|\phi_{1}(t)|^{2}\Delta t\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\Delta s\,\Delta t.

The left-hand side is exactly
\|\phi_{1}\|^{2}=1
, so

1\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\Delta s\,\Delta t.

(3.10)

It remains to bound the double integral. From the (
3.6
), the Green’s function satisfies
0\leq G(t,s)\leq\dfrac{(b-a)}{4}
for all
t,s\in[a,b]
, since the maximum of
\dfrac{(t-a)(b-s)}{(b-a)}
and
\dfrac{(s-a)(b-t)}{(b-a)}
over the domain is achieved when
t=s=\dfrac{a+b}{2}
(see Figure
2
), giving
|G(t,s)|\leq\dfrac{b-a}{4}
for all
s,t\in[a,b]
. Therefore
|G(t,s)|^{2}\leq\dfrac{(b-a)^{2}}{16}
, and

\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}|G(t,s)|^{2}\Delta s\,\Delta t\leq\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{16}\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{b}\Delta s\,\Delta t=\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{16}\cdot(b-a)^{2}=\frac{(b-a)^{4}}{16}.

Combining with (
3.10
) we obtain
1\leq\lambda_{1}^{2}\cdot\dfrac{(b-a)^{4}}{16}
, and the proof is completed upon rearranging and taking a square root.
∎

4
n
-dimensional eigenvalue problem

In this section we extend the one-dimensional theory to
n
dimensions using separation of variables. Recall the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
and domain
\mathscr{D}
from Definitions
1.1
and
1.2
in Section
1
, with inner product

\langle u,v\rangle=\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}}\int_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}}\cdots\int_{a_{n}}^{b_{n}}u(x)v(x)\,\Delta_{n}x_{n}\cdots\Delta_{2}x_{2}\,\Delta_{1}x_{1},

(4.1)

and induced norm
\|u\|=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The space
\mathscr{H}
is a real Hilbert space.

Proof.

The proof follows from the fact that
\mathscr{H}
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu_{\Delta})
, where
\mu_{\Delta}
is the product
\Delta
-measure constructed as in
[
7
]
. The argument is analogous to the one-dimensional case discussed in Remark
3.1
.
∎

Define the linear operator
A\colon\mathscr{D}\subset\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
by

(Au)(x)=-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}}u(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x).

(4.2)

We now show that
A
is symmetric and positive.

Theorem 4.2
.

The operator
A
is symmetric and positive. Specifically, for all
u,v\in\mathscr{D}
,

\displaystyle\langle Au,v\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,Av\rangle,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\langle Au,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\left(u^{\nabla_{i}}(x)\right)^{2}\nabla_{i}x_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}\Delta_{j}x_{j}.

(4.4)

Also,
\langle Au,u\rangle>0
for all
u\in\mathscr{D}
with
u\neq 0
.

Proof.

We prove the result for the
i^{\text{th}}
term in the sum defining
A
. Consider
I_{i}=-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x)v(x)\Delta^{n}x
, where
\Delta^{n}x=\Delta_{n}x_{n}\cdots\Delta_{1}x_{1}
. By Fubini’s theorem, we integrate first with respect to
x_{i}
to get

I_{i}=-\int_{\Omega_{-i}}\left(\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x)v(x)\Delta_{i}x_{i}\right)\prod_{j\neq i}\Delta_{j}x_{j},

where
\Omega_{-i}
denotes integration over all coordinates except
x_{i}
.
For the inner integral, we apply the integration by parts formula (
2.3
) with
f=u^{\nabla_{i}}
and
g=v
, yielding

\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x)v(x)\Delta_{i}x_{i}=u^{\nabla_{i}}(x)v(x)\Big|_{x_{i}=a_{i}}^{x_{i}=b_{i}}-\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u^{\nabla_{i}}(x)v^{\nabla_{i}}(x)\nabla_{i}x_{i}.

Since
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
, the boundary term vanishes. Thus

I_{i}=\int_{\Omega_{\cancel{i}}}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u^{\nabla_{i}}(x)v^{\nabla_{i}}(x)\nabla_{i}x_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}\Delta_{j}x_{j}.

Now apply integration by parts formula (
2.4
) with
f=u
and
g=v^{\nabla_{i}}
to obtain

\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u^{\nabla_{i}}(x)v^{\nabla_{i}}(x)\nabla_{i}x_{i}=u(x)v^{\nabla_{i}}(x)\Big|_{x_{i}=a_{i}}^{x_{i}=b_{i}}-\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}u(x)v^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x)\Delta_{i}x_{i}.

Since
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
, the boundary term vanishes, giving
I_{i}=-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(x)v^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x)\Delta^{n}x
. Summing over
i
proves (
4.3
). Setting
v=u
in the intermediate step gives (
4.4
). The positivity assertion follows since if
\langle Au,u\rangle=0
, then
u^{\nabla_{i}}=0
almost everywhere for all
i
, which combined with the boundary conditions implies
u\equiv 0
.
∎

We now show that the
n
-dimensional eigenvalue problem can be solved by separation of variables. Consider the eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}Au=\lambda u&\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(4.5)

We shall solve (
4.5
) with separation of variables.

Theorem 4.3
.

The eigenvalue problem (
4.5
) admits solutions of the form

u(x)=u(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})=X_{1}(x_{1})X_{2}(x_{2})\cdots X_{n}(x_{n}),

(4.6)

where each
X_{i}
satisfies the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-X_{i}^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x_{i})=\mu_{i}X_{i}(x_{i}),\quad x_{i}\in(a_{i},b_{i}),\\
X_{i}(a_{i})=X_{i}(b_{i})=0,\end{array}\right.

(4.7)

with
\lambda=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}
.

Proof.

We seek a nontrivial solution of the form (
4.6
). Substituting into the eigenvalue equation
Au=\lambda u
, we must compute
u^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Since
u
is a product of functions, each depending on a single variable, and the operators
\nabla_{i}
and
\Delta_{i}
act only on the variable
x_{i}
, we have

u^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x)=\left(\displaystyle\prod_{j\neq i}X_{j}(x_{j})\right)X_{i}^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x_{i})
, thus
Au=-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}X_{j}(x_{j})\right)X_{i}^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x_{i})
. The eigenvalue equation
Au=\lambda u
becomes
-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\prod_{j\neq i}X_{j}(x_{j})\right)X_{i}^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x_{i})=\lambda\prod_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(x_{k})
. Since we seek a nontrivial solution, we divide both sides by
u(x)=\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(x_{k})
at points where
u(x)\neq 0
, obtaining

-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{X_{i}^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x_{i})}{X_{i}(x_{i})}=\lambda.

(4.8)

Now we invoke the fundamental principle of separation of variables. Define
\Phi_{i}(x_{i}):=-\dfrac{X_{i}^{\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}}(x_{i})}{X_{i}(x_{i})}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Equation (
4.8
) states that
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(x_{i})=\lambda
. The crucial observation is that each function
\Phi_{i}
depends only on the single variable
x_{i}
, while the sum equals the constant
\lambda
. We claim that each
\Phi_{i}
must itself be constant.

To see this, fix any index
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and consider two arbitrary points
t_{1},t_{2}\in(a_{k},b_{k})\cap\mathbb{T}_{k}
. Evaluating
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(x_{i})=\lambda
at
x_{k}=t_{1}
and
x_{k}=t_{2}
(with all other coordinates fixed) and subtracting, we obtain
\Phi_{k}(t_{1})-\Phi_{k}(t_{2})=0
, since the terms
\Phi_{i}(x_{i})
for
i\neq k
do not depend on
x_{k}
and therefore cancel. Since
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
were arbitrary,
\Phi_{k}
is constant. Denoting this constant by
\mu_{k}
, we have
-\dfrac{X_{k}^{\nabla_{k}\Delta_{k}}(x_{k})}{X_{k}(x_{k})}=\mu_{k}
for each
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, which can be rewritten as
-X_{k}^{\nabla_{k}\Delta_{k}}(x_{k})=\mu_{k}X_{k}(x_{k})
. Moreover, the constants satisfy
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}=\lambda
.

It remains to verify the boundary conditions. The condition
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
requires that
u
vanish whenever any coordinate
x_{j}
equals
a_{j}
or
b_{j}
. Consider the boundary face where
x_{j}=a_{j}
for some fixed
j
:

u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},a_{j},x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n})=X_{1}(x_{1})\cdots X_{j-1}(x_{j-1})\cdot X_{j}(a_{j})\cdot X_{j+1}(x_{j+1})\cdots X_{n}(x_{n})=0.

For this to hold for all choices of
x_{i}\in(a_{i},b_{i})
with
i\neq j
, and since each
X_{i}
is not identically zero, we must have
X_{j}(a_{j})=0
. Similarly,
X_{j}(b_{j})=0
. Since
j
was arbitrary, we conclude that
X_{i}(a_{i})=X_{i}(b_{i})=0
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Therefore, each factor
X_{i}
satisfies the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem (
4.7
), whose solutions are characterized by Theorem
3.8
, and the eigenvalue
\lambda
decomposes as the sum
\lambda=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}
of the one-dimensional eigenvalues.
∎

By Theorem
3.8
, each one-dimensional problem (
4.7
) has eigenvalues
0<\mu_{1}^{(i)}<\mu_{2}^{(i)}<\cdots
with corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions
\phi_{1}^{(i)},\phi_{2}^{(i)},\ldots
that form a basis for the one-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}_{i}=L^{2}([a_{i},b_{i}),\Delta_{i})
.

Theorem 4.4
.

The eigenvalues of the
n
-dimensional problem (
4.5
) are given by

\lambda_{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{p_{i}}^{(i)},\quad p_{i}\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\},

with corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions

u_{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}}(x)=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{p_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})
. The system
\{u_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}\}
forms a complete orthonormal basis for
\mathscr{H}
.

Proof.

We first establish orthonormality. By Fubini’s theorem (
2.5
), the
n
-dimensional integral over
\Omega=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}[a_{i},b_{i})
factors as a product of one-dimensional integrals:

\langle u_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}},u_{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{p_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\phi_{q_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\,\Delta^{n}x=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}\phi_{p_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\phi_{q_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\,\Delta_{i}x_{i}=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{p_{i}q_{i}},

where the last equality uses the orthonormality of the one-dimensional eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{k}^{(i)}\}
in each
\mathscr{H}_{i}
. The product
\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{p_{i}q_{i}}
equals
1
if and only if
p_{i}=q_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and equals
0
otherwise.

We now show completeness of the orthonormal system, meaning the set
\{u_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}\}
spans
\mathscr{H}
. Suppose
f\in\mathscr{H}
satisfies
\langle f,u_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}\rangle=0
for all indices
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
. We show
f=0
by induction on
n
. The case
n=1
is Theorem
3.8
. For the inductive step, assume the result holds for dimension
n-1
. Define

g_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}}(x_{n})=\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}}\cdots\int_{a_{n-1}}^{b_{n-1}}f(x)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\phi_{p_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\,\Delta_{n-1}x_{n-1}\cdots\Delta_{1}x_{1}.

Then
\langle f,u_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}}\rangle=\int_{a_{n}}^{b_{n}}g_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}}(x_{n})\phi_{p_{n}}^{(n)}(x_{n})\,\Delta_{n}x_{n}=0
for all
p_{n}
. Since
\{\phi_{p_{n}}^{(n)}\}
is a complete orthonormal system in
\mathscr{H}_{n}
, we conclude
g_{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1}}(x_{n})=0
for almost every
x_{n}
. That is,

\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}}\cdots\int_{a_{n-1}}^{b_{n-1}}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\phi_{p_{i}}^{(i)}(x_{i})\,\Delta_{n-1}x_{n-1}\cdots\Delta_{1}x_{1}=0

for all
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-1})
. By the inductive hypothesis,
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1},x_{n})=0
for almost every
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
. Therefore
f=0
almost everywhere on
\Omega
.
∎

Corollary 4.5
.

The smallest eigenvalue of the
n
-dimensional problem is

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1,1,\ldots,1}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{1}^{(i)}
, where
\mu_{1}^{(i)}
is the first eigenvalue of the
i^{\text{th}}
one-dimensional problem. Moreover,

\lambda_{1}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{4}{(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}.

(4.9)

Proof.

The first statement follows from the fact that the minimum of
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{p_{i}}^{(i)}
over all choices of positive integers
p_{i}
is achieved when each
p_{i}=1
. The bound (
4.9
) follows from Theorem
3.10
applied to each one-dimensional problem.
∎

Since the operator
A
is positive, it has trivial kernel, and its inverse
A^{-1}
exists. We characterize
A^{-1}
using the eigenfunction expansion.

Theorem 4.6
.

For any
u\in\mathscr{H}
, the eigenfunction expansion

u=\sum_{k}c_{k}u_{k},\quad c_{k}=\langle u,u_{k}\rangle,

(4.10)

converges in
\mathscr{H}
, where the sum is over all multi-indices
k=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
and
u_{k}
denotes the corresponding eigenfunction. The inverse operator
A^{-1}\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{D}
is given by

A^{-1}u=\sum_{k}\frac{c_{k}}{\lambda_{k}}u_{k},

(4.11)

and satisfies the bound

\|A^{-1}u\|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\|u\|\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathscr{H}.

(4.12)

Proof.

The expansion (
4.10
) follows from Theorem
4.4
. For the inverse, if
Au=f
with
u\in\mathscr{D}
and
f\in\mathscr{H}
, then expanding both sides in eigenfunctions gives

\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}u_{k}=\sum_{k}d_{k}u_{k},

where
c_{k}=\langle u,u_{k}\rangle
and
d_{k}=\langle f,u_{k}\rangle
. By uniqueness of the expansion,
c_{k}=d_{k}/\lambda_{k}
, which yields (
4.11
).

For the bound, we compute

\|A^{-1}u\|^{2}=\sum_{k}\frac{c_{k}^{2}}{\lambda_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}^{2}}\|u\|^{2},

using Parseval’s equality. Taking square roots gives (
4.12
), completing the proof.
∎

5
Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we now prove Theorem
1.3
by reformulating the boundary value problem as a fixed point equation and apply the contraction mapping theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Define the linear operator
A\colon\mathscr{D}\subset\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
by
(Au)(x)=-\Delta_{\mathbb{T}}u(x)
and the nonlinear operator
F\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
by
(Fu)(x)=f(x,u(x))
. The boundary value problem (
1.1
) is equivalent to the operator equation

Au=Fu\quad\text{for }u\in\mathscr{D}.

(5.1)

We must verify that
F
maps
\mathscr{H}
into
\mathscr{H}
. For any
u\in\mathscr{H}
, the Lipschitz condition (
1.2
) with
\gamma=0
gives

|f(x,u(x))|\leq|f(x,u(x))-f(x,0)|+|f(x,0)|\leq L|u(x)|+|f(x,0)|.

Since
f
is continuous on the bounded domain
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}
and
f(\cdot,0)\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous on the compact set
\overline{\Omega}
, the function
f(\cdot,0)
is bounded, say
|f(x,0)|\leq M
for all
x\in\Omega
. Therefore, by Young’s inequality, we obtain

|f(x,u(x))|^{2}\leq 2L^{2}|u(x)|^{2}+2M^{2},

and integrating over
\Omega
,

\|Fu\|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|f(x,u(x))|^{2}\Delta^{n}x\leq 2L^{2}\|u\|^{2}+2M^{2}|\Omega|<\infty,

where
|\Omega|=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})
is the volume of
\Omega
. Thus
Fu\in\mathscr{H}
, and
F\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is well-defined.

By Theorem
4.2

A
is positive with trivial kernel. Hence
A^{-1}
exists and (
5.1
) can be written as

u=A^{-1}Fu\quad\text{for }u\in\mathscr{H}.

(5.2)

Note that
A^{-1}
maps
\mathscr{H}
onto
\mathscr{D}
which ensures that any solution automatically satisfies the boundary conditions.
Define
G\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
by
G=A^{-1}F
. Then (
5.2
) becomes the fixed point equation
u=Gu
.

Next we show that
G
is a contraction on
\mathscr{H}
. For any
u,v\in\mathscr{H}
,

\|Gu-Gv\|=\|A^{-1}Fu-A^{-1}Fv\|=\|A^{-1}(Fu-Fv)\|\leq\|A^{-1}\|\cdot\|Fu-Fv\|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\|Fu-Fv\|,

where we used the bound (
4.12
).
Using the Lipschitz condition (
1.2
),

\|Fu-Fv\|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|f(x,u(x))-f(x,v(x))|^{2}\Delta^{n}x\leq\int_{\Omega}L^{2}|u(x)-v(x)|^{2}\Delta^{n}x=L^{2}\|u-v\|^{2}.

Therefore,
\|Fu-Fv\|\leq L\|u-v\|
, and combining with the previous inequality, we obtain the estimate

\|Gu-Gv\|\leq\dfrac{L}{\lambda_{1}}\|u-v\|
.

Finally, we have
\dfrac{L}{\lambda_{1}}<1
. Therefore,
G
is a contraction mapping on the complete metric space
\mathscr{H}
. By Theorem
2.2
,
G
has a unique fixed point
u^{*}\in\mathscr{H}
, which is the unique solution to the boundary value problem (
1.1
).
∎

Remark 5.1
.

By Corollary
4.5
, the condition
\dfrac{L}{\lambda_{1}}<1
is satisfied whenever

L<\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{4}{(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}.

This provides an explicit criterion in terms of the domain geometry.

6
Proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section we prove Theorem
1.4
. Throughout we work in the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
defined in Section
1
with inner product (
4.1
), and we use the linear operator
A\colon D\subset\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
given by (
4.2
). The first eigenvalue of the associated eigenvalue problem (
4.5
) is denoted by
\lambda_{1}>0
as in Corollary
4.5
.

We first justify that the nonlinear term in (
1.1
) gives a well-defined operator on
\mathscr{H}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Then,

(i)

for each fixed
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, the function

x\mapsto f(x,\eta)

is
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable on
\Omega
,

(ii)

if
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable, then the composition

x\mapsto f(x,u(x))

is
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Each time scale
\mathbb{T}_{i}
is a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}
with the subspace topology. Thus its closure
\overline{\Omega}:=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}[a_{i},b_{i}]\cap\mathbb{T}_{i}
is compact in the product topology. The
\Delta
-measure on
\Omega
is constructed as a Lebesgue-type measure via the Carathéodory extension from half-open intervals; in particular, every Borel subset of
\overline{\Omega}
is
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable
[
7
]
.

To prove (i), fix
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
f
is continuous on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, the map

x\mapsto f(x,\eta)

obtained by restricting
f
to
\Omega\times\{\eta\}
, is continuous with respect to the product topology on
\Omega
. Hence it is Borel measurable, and therefore
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable.

To prove (ii), let
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable. Define

U\colon\Omega\to\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
,
U(x)=(x,u(x)).

The first coordinate
x\mapsto x
is measurable by definition of the underlying measure space, and the second coordinate
x\mapsto u(x)
is measurable by hypothesis. Hence
U
is measurable with respect to the product
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
. Since
f
is continuous on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, it is Borel measurable. Therefore the composition

x\mapsto f(x,u(x))=f\bigl(U(x)\bigr)

is
\Delta_{1}\Delta_{2}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable on
\Omega
.
∎

We now define the nonlinear operator.

Lemma 6.2
.

Assume that
f
is continuous and satisfies the Lipschitz condition (
1.2
). Define

(Fu)(x):=f(x,u(x)),\qquad x\in\Omega,\quad u\in\mathscr{H}.

Then,

(i)

F\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is well-defined.

(ii)

F
is globally Lipschitz with constant
L
, i.e.

\|Fu-Fv\|\leq L\|u-v\|\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathscr{H}.

(6.1)

Proof.

To prove (i), let
u\in\mathscr{H}
. By definition of
\mathscr{H}
,
u
is
\Delta_{1}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable and square integrable over
\Omega
. By Lemma
6.1
(ii), the function
x\mapsto f(x,u(x))
is
\Delta_{1}\cdots\Delta_{n}
-measurable.

We now show
f(\cdot,u(\cdot))
is square integrable over
\Omega
. From (
1.2
) with
\gamma=0
we obtain

|f(x,u(x))|\leq|f(x,u(x))-f(x,0)|+|f(x,0)|\leq L|u(x)|+|f(x,0)|.

Since
f
is continuous and
\overline{\Omega}
is compact, there exists
M\geq 0
such that

|f(x,0)|\leq M\quad\text{for all }x\in\Omega.

Hence

|f(x,u(x))|^{2}\leq 2L^{2}|u(x)|^{2}+2M^{2}

for all
x\in\Omega
. Integrating over
\Omega
and using
u\in\mathscr{H}
and the finiteness of
\mu_{\Delta}(\Omega)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|Fu\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|f(x,u(x))|^{2}\,\Delta^{n}x

\displaystyle\leq 2L^{2}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{2}\,\Delta^{n}x+2M^{2}\int_{\Omega}1\,\Delta^{n}x

\displaystyle=2L^{2}\|u\|^{2}+2M^{2}|\Omega|<\infty.

Thus
Fu\in\mathscr{H}
, and
F\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is well-defined. To prove (ii), let
u,v\in\mathscr{H}
. By (
1.2
), for all
x\in\Omega
,

|f(x,u(x))-f(x,v(x))|\leq L|u(x)-v(x)|.

Squaring and integrating,

\displaystyle\|Fu-Fv\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|f(x,u(x))-f(x,v(x))|^{2}\,\Delta^{n}x\leq L^{2}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)-v(x)|^{2}\,\Delta^{n}x=L^{2}\|u-v\|^{2}.

Taking square roots yields (
6.1
).
∎

We next prove an inequality relating
A
and the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
A
and
\lambda_{1}
be as above. Then, for every
u\in\mathscr{D}
,

\langle Au,u\rangle\geq\lambda_{1}\|u\|^{2}.

(6.2)

Proof.

By Theorem
4.4
, there exists an orthonormal basis
\{u_{k}\}
of
\mathscr{H}
consisting of eigenfunctions of
A
with corresponding eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}
satisfying

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\cdots.

For
u\in\mathscr{D}
we write

u=\sum_{k}c_{k}u_{k},\qquad c_{k}=\langle u,u_{k}\rangle,

with convergence in
\mathscr{H}
. Then

\|u\|^{2}=\displaystyle\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}

by Parseval’s identity. On the other hand,

Au=\displaystyle\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}u_{k},
so

\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle=\left\langle\displaystyle\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}u_{k},\;\sum_{j}c_{j}u_{j}\right\rangle=\displaystyle\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}^{2}.

Using
\lambda_{k}\geq\lambda_{1}
for all
k
, we obtain

\langle Au,u\rangle=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}c_{k}^{2}\geq\lambda_{1}\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}=\lambda_{1}\|u\|^{2},

which is (
6.2
), completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

We now combine the properties of
A^{-1}
and
F
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
G\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
be defined by
Gu:=A^{-1}Fu
for
u\in\mathscr{H}
. Then
G
is continuous and compact.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.2
,
F\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is well-defined and continuous. By Theorem
4.6
, the operator
A^{-1}\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
is linear, bounded, and compact. The composition
G=A^{-1}\circ F
is therefore continuous and compact.
∎

We next obtain an a priori bound for solutions of the homotopy equation
u=\tau Gu
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Assume that
f
satisfies (
1.4
). Let
G
be as in Lemma
6.4
. Then the set

\mathcal{S}=\{u\in\mathscr{H}\colon u=\tau Gu\text{ for some }\tau\in[0,1]\}

is bounded in
\mathscr{H}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}
. Then there exists
\tau\in[0,1]
such that

u=\tau Gu=\tau A^{-1}Fu.

Applying
A
to both sides yields

Au=\tau Fu.

Since
A\colon\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{H}
and
A^{-1}\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{D}
, we have
u\in\mathscr{D}
. Taking the inner product with
u
and using Lemma
6.3
, we obtain

\lambda_{1}\|u\|^{2}\leq\langle Au,u\rangle=\tau\langle Fu,u\rangle.

(6.3)

Using the energy condition (
1.4
) with
\eta=u(x)
, we have

f(x,u(x))\,u(x)\leq\alpha u(x)^{2}+C\quad\text{for all }x\in\Omega.

Integrating over
\Omega
,

\langle Fu,u\rangle=\int_{\Omega}f(x,u(x))\,u(x)\,\Delta^{n}x\leq\alpha\|u\|^{2}+C|\Omega|.

(6.4)

Combining (
6.3
) and (
6.4
) and using
\tau\leq 1
, we obtain

\lambda_{1}\|u\|^{2}\leq\tau\langle Fu,u\rangle\leq\alpha\|u\|^{2}+C|\Omega|.

Rearranging,

(\lambda_{1}-\alpha)\|u\|^{2}\leq C|\Omega|.

Since
\alpha\in(0,\lambda_{1})
, the coefficient
(\lambda_{1}-\alpha)
is positive, and hence

\|u\|^{2}\leq\frac{C|\Omega|}{\lambda_{1}-\alpha}.

(6.5)

Thus every
u\in\mathcal{S}
satisfies (
6.5
), so
\mathcal{S}
is bounded in
\mathscr{H}
.
∎

We now prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
G\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
be defined by
Gu=A^{-1}Fu
as in Lemma
6.4
. By Lemma
6.4
,
G
is continuous and compact. By Lemma
6.5
, the Leray–Schauder set

\{u\in\mathscr{H}\colon u=\tau Gu\text{ for some }\tau\in[0,1]\}

is bounded in
\mathscr{H}
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.3
, there exists
u^{*}\in\mathscr{H}
such that

u^{*}=Gu^{*}=A^{-1}Fu^{*}.

Applying
A
to both sides, we obtain

Au^{*}=Fu^{*},

so
u^{*}
is a solution of the operator equation

Au=Fu.

Since
A^{-1}\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{D}
, we have
u^{*}\in\mathscr{D}
, and hence
u^{*}
satisfies the boundary conditions in (
1.1
). This shows that (
1.1
) has at least one solution in
\mathscr{D}
, which completes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.
∎

7
Examples

In this section we illustrate our main results with explicit examples. These examples demonstrate the scope of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. We first consider a two-dimensional example.

Example 7.1

(Two-dimensional domain)
.

Consider
n=2
with
\mathbb{T}_{1}=\mathbb{T}_{2}=\{0,1,2,3\}
. The domain is

\Omega=\{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}=\{(1,1),(1,2),(2,1),(2,2)\}.

The boundary
\partial\Omega
consists of all points
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{T}_{1}\times\mathbb{T}_{2}
where
x_{1}\in\{0,3\}
or
x_{2}\in\{0,3\}
.

For a function
u\colon\mathbb{T}_{1}\times\mathbb{T}_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, the time scale Laplacian at an interior point
(i,j)\in\Omega
is

\Delta_{\mathbb{T}}u(i,j)=u^{\nabla_{1}\Delta_{1}}(i,j)+u^{\nabla_{2}\Delta_{2}}(i,j)=u(i+1,j)+u(i-1,j)+u(i,j+1)+u(i,j-1)-4u(i,j).

Writing
u_{ij}=u(i,j)
and using boundary conditions
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
, the BVP (
1.1
) with
f(x,u)=C
becomes the system

\displaystyle 4u_{11}-u_{21}-u_{12}+C

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 4u_{12}-u_{22}-u_{11}+C

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 4u_{21}-u_{11}-u_{22}+C

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 4u_{22}-u_{12}-u_{21}+C

\displaystyle=0.

Adding all four equations gives
8(u_{11}+u_{12}+u_{21}+u_{22})+4C=0
. By symmetry of the system,
u_{11}=u_{12}=u_{21}=u_{22}
, and substituting yields

u_{11}=u_{12}=u_{21}=u_{22}=-\frac{C}{2}.

(7.1)

For this two-dimensional domain, the eigenvalues are given by Theorem
4.4
as

\lambda_{k,m}=\mu_{k}^{(1)}+\mu_{m}^{(2)}=4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi k}{6}\right)+4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi m}{6}\right),\quad k,m\in\{1,2\},

yielding
\lambda_{1,1}=1+1=2
,
\lambda_{1,2}=\lambda_{2,1}=1+3=4
, and
\lambda_{2,2}=3+3=6
. The first eigenvalue is
\lambda_{1}=2
, which equals
\mu_{1}^{(1)}+\mu_{1}^{(2)}
as stated in Corollary
4.5
.

Since
f(x,u)=C
satisfies (
1.2
) with
L=0<\lambda_{1}=2
, Theorem
1.3
guarantees the unique solution (
7.1
).

The following example explores the solutions for a one-dimensional hybrid discrete-continuous domain.

Example 7.2

(Hybrid continuous-discrete domain)
.

Consider the time scale
\mathbb{T}=[0,1]\cup\{2,3\}
, which combines a continuous interval with isolated points. Setting
a=0
and
b=3
, the interior is given by

u(0)=u(3)=0

-y^{\prime\prime}=\lambda y

y(t)=A\sin(\sqrt{\lambda}t)

\mathscr{D}
implies then that
y(1)=A\sin(\sqrt{\lambda})
. At the isolated point
t=2
, the equation
-y^{\nabla\Delta}(2)=\lambda y(2)
becomes

-\dfrac{y^{\nabla}(3)-y^{\nabla}(2)}{\mu(2)}=\lambda y(2)

2y(2)-y(1)=\lambda y(2).

(7.2)

At
t=1

-y^{\nabla\Delta}(1)=\lambda y(1)
gives

y(2)=(1-\lambda)y(1)+y^{\prime}(1^{-}),

(7.3)

where
y^{\prime}(1^{-})=A\sqrt{\lambda}\cos(\sqrt{\lambda})
is the left derivative at

from the continuous part.

Eliminating
y(2)
between (

) and (

) yields the transcendental equation

(\lambda^{2}-3\lambda+1)\sin(\sqrt{\lambda})+(2-\lambda)\sqrt{\lambda}\cos(\sqrt{\lambda})=0,

\lambda_{1}\approx 0.840,\lambda_{2}\approx 2.600,\lambda_{3}\approx 11.907
.

For the problem (
1.1
) with
f(x,u)=C

1.3
guarantees existence and uniqueness. We can solve explicitly: on the continuous part,

for some constant

\displaystyle u(1)-u(2)+u^{\prime}(1^{-})+2C

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 2u(2)-u(1)+C

\displaystyle=0.

Solving yields

This shows that the spectral bound

), unified by the time scale framework.

guarantees existence.

On the continuous part,
-u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda_{1}u=0

. At

(2-\lambda_{1})u(2)=u(1)

\lambda_{1}=4\sin^{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right)=1
and
\lambda_{2}=4\sin^{2}\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right)=3
. The corresponding eigenfunctions are
\phi_{1}(t)=\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{3}\right)
and
\phi_{2}(t)=\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi t}{3}\right)
for
t\in\mathbb{T}
, which satisfy the boundary conditions
\phi_{k}(0)=\phi_{k}(3)=0
. At the interior points, these take values
\phi_{1}(1)=\phi_{1}(2)=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
and
\phi_{2}(1)=-\phi_{2}(2)=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
.

Example 7.3

(Constant nonlinearity)
.

Let

. This function satisfies (

Now we consider the case where

Example 7.4

(Position-dependent nonlinearity)
.

Let

We now show an example for which Theorem
1.3
does not apply, but Theorem
1.4

Example 7.5

(Negative linear: existence and uniqueness)
.

Let
f(x,u)=-cu
with
c>\lambda_{1}=1
but
c\neq\lambda_{k}
for any
k
. For concreteness, take
c=2
. The Lipschitz constant is
L=2>\lambda_{1}
, so Theorem
1.3
does not apply. However, the one-sided condition (
1.4
) is satisfied since
f(x,\eta)\eta=-2\eta^{2}\leq 0<\alpha\eta^{2}+C
for any
\alpha\in(0,\lambda_{1})
and
C\geq 0
. Thus Theorem
1.4
guarantees existence.

The system (
7.4
) becomes

\displaystyle 2u_{1}-u_{2}-2u_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-u_{1}+2u_{2}-2u_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

which simplifies to
u_{2}=0
and
u_{1}=0
. The unique solution is
u\equiv 0
. Although Theorem
1.4
only guarantees existence, uniqueness holds here because
c=2
is not an eigenvalue of the linear problem.

We now show a similar example without uniqueness.

Example 7.6

(Resonance: existence without uniqueness)
.

Let
f(x,u)=-\lambda_{1}u=-u
. The Lipschitz constant is
L=\lambda_{1}=1
, so the strict inequality
L<\lambda_{1}
in Theorem
1.3
fails. However, as in Example
7.5
, the one-sided condition (
1.4

f(x,\eta)\eta=-\eta^{2}\leq 0
. Thus Theorem
1.4

The system (
7.4
) becomes

\displaystyle 2u_{1}-u_{2}-u_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-u_{1}+2u_{2}-u_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

which simplifies to
u_{1}=u_{2}
. Every function of the form
u_{1}=u_{2}=t
for

is a solution. This infinite family of solutions corresponds to multiples of the first eigenfunction
\phi_{1}
. This example shows that Theorem
1.4
provides existence but not uniqueness, and that the strict inequality
L<\lambda_{1}
in Theorem
1.3
is necessary for uniqueness.

The next example demonstrates that Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
provide sufficient but not necessary conditions for existence and uniqueness.

Example 7.7

(Positive linear: existence and uniqueness without theorems)
.

Let
f(x,u)=cu
with
c>\lambda_{1}=1
. For concreteness, take
c=2
. The Lipschitz constant is
L=2>\lambda_{1}
, so Theorem
1.3
does not apply. The one-sided condition requires
f(x,\eta)\eta=2\eta^{2}\leq\alpha\eta^{2}+C
for some
\alpha<\lambda_{1}=1
, which fails for large
|\eta|
. Thus Theorem
1.4
also does not apply.

Nevertheless, the system (
7.4
) becomes

\displaystyle 2u_{1}-u_{2}+2u_{1}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-u_{1}+2u_{2}+2u_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

which simplifies to

\displaystyle 4u_{1}-u_{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-u_{1}+4u_{2}

\displaystyle=0.

Substituting
u_{2}=4u_{1}
into the second equation gives
-u_{1}+16u_{1}=0
, so
u_{1}=0
and hence
u_{2}=0
. The unique solution is
u\equiv 0
.

Next, an example where no (real-valued) solution exists.

Example 7.8

(Superlinear growth: no solution exists)
.

Let
f(x,u)=1+u^{2}
. This function is not globally Lipschitz since
|f(x,u)-f(x,v)|=|u^{2}-v^{2}|=|u-v||u+v|

is unbounded as
|u|+|v|\to\infty
. Additionally, the one-sided condition (
1.4
) fails since
f(x,\eta)\eta=\eta+\eta^{3}
grows faster than
\alpha\eta^{2}+C
for any constants
\alpha,C
as
\eta\to+\infty
. Thus neither theorem applies.

The system (
7.4
) becomes

\displaystyle 2u_{1}-u_{2}+1+u_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-u_{1}+2u_{2}+1+u_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

From the first equation,
u_{2}=2u_{1}+1+u_{1}^{2}
. Substituting into the second equation yields

-u_{1}+2(2u_{1}+1+u_{1}^{2})+1+(2u_{1}+1+u_{1}^{2})^{2}=0.

Expanding and simplifying, this becomes

u_{1}^{4}+4u_{1}^{3}+8u_{1}^{2}+7u_{1}+4=0.

This quartic polynomial has no real roots, so no real-valued solution exists.

8
Conclusion

We proved existence and uniqueness results for nonlinear elliptic Dirichlet boundary value problems on
n
-dimensional time scale domains.
Under a Lipschitz condition bounded by the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
, Theorem
1.3
provides existence and uniqueness via the contraction mapping theorem. Under a weaker one-sided growth condition, Theorem
1.4
provides existence via the Leray–Schauder fixed point theorem. Both results require the spectral theory developed in Sections
3
–
4
, which extends the two-dimensional results of
[
14
]
to
n
dimensions and establishes completeness of the product eigenfunctions for the
\nabla\Delta
-Laplacian with
\mu_{\Delta}
-measure. The examples in Section
7
demonstrate the scope and sharpness of the hypotheses across discrete, hybrid, and higher-dimensional settings, and how the examples relate to the theorems appears in Table
2
.

A principal limitation of the present work is the restriction to rectangular domains
\Omega=\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}(a_{i},b_{i})\cap\mathbb{T}_{i}
, which arises from the use of separation of variables in the spectral theory. Extending to general domains would require constructing the
n
-dimensional Green’s function for the
\nabla\Delta
operator by variational methods. The natural approach via the Lax–Milgram theorem encounters a fundamental obstruction: integration by parts applied to the mixed operator
\nabla_{i}\Delta_{i}
produces the bilinear form

a(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}u^{\nabla_{i}}(y)\,v^{\nabla_{i}}(\sigma_{i}(y))\,\Delta^{n}y,

which evaluates
u^{\nabla_{i}}
and
v^{\nabla_{i}}
at different points whenever
y_{i}
is right-scattered. This measure mismatch prevents coercivity, since
a(v,v)
involves products of the form
v^{\nabla_{i}}(y)v^{\nabla_{i}}(\sigma_{i}(y))
rather than
|v^{\nabla_{i}}(y)|^{2}
, and the integrand need not be non-negative. This obstruction is absent in the continuous case
\mathbb{T}_{i}=\mathbb{R}
(where
\sigma_{i}
is the identity) and in the purely discrete case
\mathbb{T}_{i}=\mathbb{Z}
(where the delta and nabla measures coincide), but is present for general time scales with right-scattered points.

Several directions for future work present themselves. The Banach–Nečas–Babuška (inf-sup) framework, which replaces coercivity with the weaker inf-sup condition, may provide a path to well-posedness for the bilinear form
a(u,v)
on general time scales. Alternatively, following
[
23
,
19
]
, one could combine local existence via Picard–Lindelöf methods with maximum principles for a priori bounds, bypassing variational methods entirely; this approach has proved effective for lattice differential equations, which combine continuous time with discrete spatial structure. Within the spectral framework of the present paper, one may pursue multiplicity results when
L\geq\lambda_{1}
using topological methods, Sturm–Liouville or Robin boundary conditions, or singular nonlinearities treated via cutoff and approximation techniques.
