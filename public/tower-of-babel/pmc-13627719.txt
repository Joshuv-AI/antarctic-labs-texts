A dual-host regulatory hub integrating biofilm formation and innate immunity resistance
Authors: Alexandre Baillez, Amélie Dewitte, François Pierre, Mounia Kortebi, Virginia S. Lioy, Mélanie Hillion, Julie Hardouin, Sébastien Janel, Xavier Thuru, Charlotte Clarisse, Hassiba Bouafia, Catherine Robbe Masselot, Sébastien Bontemps-Gallo, Florent Sebbane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vector-borne pathogens must adapt to sharply distinct host environments, yet the regulatory logic that coordinates transitions between opposing host-specific programs remains poorly defined. Here, we identify a minimal regulatory module in the flea-borne pathogen Yersinia pestis that integrates vector transmission with resistance to mammalian innate immunity. Screening of flea-induced genes uncovered the regulator HdfR, which acts primarily through activation of maoP, encoding a nucleoid-associated protein. This HdfR-MaoP module promotes biofilm-dependent foregut blockage in the flea while coordinating baseline envelope adaptations that limit complement recognition and inducible responses that confer resistance to antimicrobial peptides under mammalian host-like conditions. Functional interchangeability of HdfR and MaoP homologs reveals evolutionary conservation of this regulatory logic, and pharmacological perturbation of the module sensitizes Y. pestis to antimicrobial peptides. Together, these findings define a parsimonious and evolutionarily conserved regulatory hub that orchestrates bacterial success across abrupt environmental transitions and exposes a tractable point of intervention.

Introduction

Organisms that alternate between disparate environments must continually interpret external cues and reconfigure their physiology to maintain fitness1. This principle is exemplified by vector-borne pathogens, which face abrupt transitions in temperature, nutrient availability and immune pressure as they cycle between arthropods and mammals2–4. Such ecological discontinuities impose conflicting selective constraints that require coordinated regulatory solutions.

Among vector-borne pathogens, Yersinia pestis, the agent of flea-borne plague, undergoes an exceptionally extensive physiological transition5,6. Within its flea vector, Y. pestis assembles a cohesive biofilm that obstructs the foregut, ensuring regurgitative transmission of contaminated blood7,8. Yet this same biofilm becomes a liability in mammals9, where the bacterium must evade innate immunity to establish the bacteremia required for transmission back to naive fleas10–12. Thus, successful transmission depends on the precise deployment of opposing physiological programs across hosts. Intriguingly, passage through each host enhances fitness in the next, indicating that Y. pestis adjusts its physiology in ways that benefit sequential stages of its life cycle8,13. These observations suggest that host-specific programs are not deployed in isolation, but are instead coordinated across transitions (Supplementary Fig. 1). Yet the mechanisms that coordinate the opposing demands of flea colonization and mammalian infection remain unclear.

Decades of research have dissected the regulatory networks underpinning the flea-rodent-flea cycle of Y. pestis14,15. Key regulators include the two-component systems PhoPQ and OmpR-EnvZ, transcriptional regulators such as RovA and Crp, and post-transcriptional regulators like Hfq. These systems respond to general stress cues, such as antimicrobial peptides, osmotic changes, nutrient limitation or temperature shifts and collectively support survival in both flea and mammalian environments16–21. However, their regulatory outputs remain largely compartmentalized. For instance, PhoPQ modifies lipopolysaccharides to resist antimicrobial peptides but does not prime resistance to serum complement16, while RovA’s temperature-dependent switch toggles between biofilm repression and virulence without preparing bacteria for mammalian immune attack18,22. Despite decades of study on Y. pestis, no regulatory hub has been shown to directly couple vector transmission to mammalian immune evasion, although such integration would be predicted to provide a major fitness advantage during the flea-mammal transmission cycle.

Here, we bridge this gap by characterizing the HdfR-MaoP axis as a minimal regulatory hub that directly couples biofilm-mediated flea blockage with resistance to mammalian innate immunity. Through a genetic screen targeting flea-upregulated genes, complemented by in vitro analyses and flea infection models, we show that the LysR-type transcriptional regulator HdfR activates maoP, encoding a nucleoid-associated protein-like factor, to orchestrate this dual adaptation. Strikingly, this regulatory logic is evolutionarily conserved, as heterologous expression of Escherichia coli HdfR and MaoP fully rescues all tested phenotypes in Y. pestis. Together, these findings define a parsimonious molecular hub that prepares bacteria for abrupt host transitions and provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how pathogens navigate complex life cycles.

Results

The HdfR-MaoP axis coordinates biofilm formation and innate immune resistance across host environments

Vector-borne pathogens must reconcile the conflicting demands of persistence in the arthropod vector and survival in the mammalian host. To identify genes capable of supporting adaptation in both environments, we screened a library of Y. pestis deletion mutants targeting genes previously reported as upregulated in the flea for susceptibility to human serum. This screen identified twelve candidates, including a mutant spanning the mlgA-araH10H11-hdfR locus, which exhibited marked sensitivity to native but not heat-inactivated serum, consistent with complement-mediated killing. Focusing on the transcriptional regulator HdfR, we found that deletion of hdfR was sufficient to reproduce this phenotype when bacteria were exposed to serum at 37 °C after being grown under either flea-like (21 °C) or mammalian host-like (37 °C), indicating that HdfR supports complement resistance both after flea-like temperature adaptation and under mammalian host-like conditions (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). By contrast, in cross-temperature antimicrobial peptide assays designed to distinguish prior temperature adaptation from exposure temperature, the ΔhdfR mutant displayed heightened susceptibility to antimicrobial peptides only after growth at 37 °C, regardless of the temperature used during peptide exposure (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Beyond these defects in innate immune resistance, the ΔhdfR mutant also exhibited reduced biofilm and exopolysaccharide production at 21 °C (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2c). Corroborating the pleiotropic role of HdfR and providing mechanistic insight, quantitative proteomic analyses performed at both 21 °C and 37 °C uncovered extensive HdfR-dependent changes in the protein landscape across temperatures, with 319 proteins differentially produced (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2d). The majority of these proteins were less abundant in the ΔhdfR mutant, notably those involved in central metabolism and membrane architecture, with stronger effects observed at 37 °C (Supplementary Figs. 2d and 3). Despite this broad impact, inspection of the dataset suggested that HdfR primarily acts through the activation of a single downstream factor, MaoP. Indeed, maoP expression was abolished in the ΔhdfR mutant at both temperatures (Fig. 1e, f), and deletion of maoP phenocopied all ΔhdfR defects, including serum sensitivity, antimicrobial peptide susceptibility, impaired biofilm formation, and the associated metabolic signature (Fig. 1a-c, g). Consistent with this hierarchical regulatory architecture, titrated expression of maoP from an arabinose-inducible promoter in the ΔhdfR background restored all phenotypes in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1h–j and Supplementary Fig. 4c–e), whereas overexpression of hdfR failed to rescue the ΔmaoP mutant (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). These data establish MaoP as the indispensable downstream effector of the HdfR regulon for the phenotypes tested here. In addition, HdfR positively autoregulated its own transcription, whereas MaoP negatively autoregulated maoP expression (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d), revealing feedback loops consistent with a self-stabilizing regulatory module capable of maintaining coordinated output across environmental conditions. Consistent with the condition-dependent phenotypes controlled by the HdfR-MaoP module, hdfR expression was higher at 37 °C than at 21 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5e), estimated MaoP abundance was approximately threefold higher at 37 °C than at 21 °C on a per-cell basis, and shifting bacteria from 21 °C to 37 °C increased MaoP protein abundance over the first hour, whereas MaoP levels remained stable in bacteria maintained at 21 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g).

Together, these results define the HdfR-MaoP module as a quantitative regulatory hub that integrates flea-stage biofilm capacity with mammalian-stage resistance to innate immune defenses, thereby contributing to bacterial adaptation across distinct host-associated conditions.

HdfR-MaoP promotes flea blockage through a hierarchical regulatory cascade targeting the hmsHFRS operon

Proteomic analyses revealed that HmsF, encoded within the hmsHFRS biofilm operon, was markedly less abundant in the absence of the HdfR-MaoP module (Fig. 1d), supporting a role for this regulatory axis in controlling the biofilm machinery required for efficient flea foregut obstruction7,8. Consistent with this hypothesis, deletion of either hdfR or maoP markedly reduced hmsHFRS promoter activity and HmsH protein accumulation under flea-like conditions (21 °C) (Fig. 2a–c). Ectopic expression of maoP in the ΔhdfR background fully restored HmsH production, establishing MaoP as the direct mediator of HdfR-dependent activation of biofilm genes (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 4f). At the biological level, these molecular defects translated into a reduced ability to produce a transmissible infection. Both ΔhdfR and ΔmaoP mutants blocked fewer fleas than the wild type in mono-infections (Fig. 2e), despite comparable bacterial loads in the flea gut (Fig. 2f), and were strongly outcompeted in 1:1 co-infection assays (Fig. 2g). Importantly, restoration of wild-type hmsHFRS expression in the ΔmaoP background rescued biofilm formation (Fig. 2h) and flea blockage to near wild-type levels (Fig. 2e). Further dissection of this regulatory cascade examined how MaoP controls hmsHFRS expression. Among genes downregulated in the ΔhdfR background, the zinc-uptake regulator zur emerged as a key intermediary. The transcription of zur was reduced in the absence of MaoP (Fig. 2b), and purified Zur bound directly to the hmsH promoter (Fig. 2j). Consistently, deletion of zur reduced hmsHFRS transcription to levels comparable to those observed in ΔmaoP (Fig. 2a). By contrast, the resulting defects in biofilm formation, exopolysaccharide production, and flea fitness were less severe in the Δzur mutant (Fig. 2g–i). This divergence became most apparent in the ΔmaoP Δzur double mutant, which exhibited the lowest levels of HmsH accumulation and biofilm production (Fig. 2c, h). Furthermore, overexpression of zur in the ΔmaoP background partially restored HmsH levels and biofilm formation (Fig. 2c, h). Together, these data reveal a hierarchical regulatory architecture in which MaoP activates hmsHFRS through parallel Zur-dependent and Zur-independent pathways, ensuring robust biofilm production and reliable flea blockage across fluctuating environmental conditions.

Small RNAs have been reported to activate translation of hmsHFRS and hmsT, which encodes a diguanylate cyclase that produces c-di-GMP required for HmsHFRS activity, providing a plausible molecular basis for the Zur-independent branch23–25. However, c-di-GMP levels were unchanged in the ΔhdfR mutant, and deletion of maoP did not reduce expression of the tested sRNA genes (Supplementary Fig. 6). The mechanism underlying the Zur-independent regulation of hmsHFRS therefore remains to be defined.

Distinct envelope-associated mechanisms underlie antimicrobial peptide and complement resistance

Proteomic analyses of the ΔhdfR mutant revealed reduced abundance of enzymes involved in core lipopolysaccharide (LOS) biosynthesis, namely GmhA and RfaD (Fig. 1d), suggesting a potential contribution of the HdfR-MaoP module to antimicrobial peptide resistance via envelope-associated mechanisms26,27. In agreement with these proteomic data, LOS analysis of the ΔhdfR mutant showed reduced LOS production at 37 °C but not at 21 °C (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Consistent with MaoP acting downstream of HdfR, deletion of maoP resulted in a reduction in gmhA and rfaD promoter activity at 37 °C (Fig. 3a), correlating with decreased LOS production (Fig. 3b) and increased outer membrane permeability (Fig. 3c). Strikingly, these defects were not detected at 21 °C, mirroring the temperature dependence of polymyxin sensitivity in the ΔmaoP mutant (Fig. 3a–c). In support of a role for HdfR-MaoP-dependent regulation of gmhA and rfaD in peptide resistance, ectopic expression of either gene in the ΔmaoP mutant restored polymyxin resistance, albeit partially (Fig. 3d, e). Consistent with the coordinated contribution of both enzymes to LOS core biosynthesis, combined expression of gmhA and rfaD further restored polymyxin resistance, most notably at higher polymyxin concentrations (Fig. 3f). Inspection of the remaining LOS and lipid A biosynthesis genes revealed no reduced expression in ΔmaoP (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). In addition, canonical lipid A modification pathways associated with polymyxin resistance were not transcriptionally reduced, including the pmrF operon and upstream genes involved in aminoarabinose modification, whereas eptA was increased (Supplementary Fig. 7b). By contrast, changes in the regulation of LOS biosynthesis pathways were unlikely to fully account for the complement sensitivity of HdfR-MaoP-deficient strains, because complement sensitivity was observed after growth at both 21 °C and 37 °C, whereas reduced gmhA/rfaD expression and detectable LOS defects were observed primarily at 37 °C. Mechanistic dissection of the complement activation system showed increased deposition of C3b, C4 fragments, and factor Bb on the surface of the ΔmaoP mutant in normal human serum (Fig. 3g, h), while recruitment of complement inhibitors, including vitronectin, factor H, and C4-binding protein, was comparable to wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Moreover, exposure to purified C3b or heat-inactivated serum did not increase C3b deposition, excluding enhanced docking of alternative pathway components as the primary cause of complement sensitivity (Fig. 3g). Instead, the ΔmaoP mutant exhibited increased binding of mannose-binding lectin and C1q (Fig. 3i), implicating heightened recognition by the lectin and classical pathways. To identify potential determinants of this increased recognition, we screened 27 candidate surface-associated genes selected from our omics datasets. None of the corresponding mutants individually recapitulated the complement sensitivity phenotype, supporting a distributed effect on surface ligand exposure rather than masking of one dominant determinant (Supplementary Fig. 7e).

Together, these findings indicate that the HdfR-MaoP module supports innate immune resistance through distinct envelope adaptation strategies. It reinforces LOS biosynthesis at mammalian host temperature to limit antimicrobial peptide susceptibility, while restricting complement activation through a distinct mechanism that limits recognition by lectin and classical pathway sensors. Although subtle or localized LOS alterations cannot be excluded, this dual control ensures continuity of vector-stage transmission while enabling survival in the mammalian host.

Plasmid-borne virulence-associated genes are integrated into the HdfR-MaoP regulatory network

Proteomic analyses of the ΔhdfR mutant revealed increased abundance of Caf1R and Caf1 (Fig. 1d), both encoded on the pMT1 plasmid. Caf1R activates the caf1M1A1 operon at mammalian host temperature, leading to production of the F1 capsule, a major Y. pestis virulence factor28,29. Consistent with the hierarchical organization of the HdfR-MaoP module, deletion of maoP increased Caf1 production under the conditions tested, suggesting that HdfR’s effect on Caf1 abundance is mediated, at least in part, by MaoP (Supplementary Fig. 8a). This effect did not extend to all plasmid-encoded virulence factors, as production of the pPla-encoded virulence factor Pla was unchanged in the ΔmaoP mutant (Supplementary Fig. 8b). The potential impact of HdfR-MaoP on the virulence pCD1-encoded functions could not be assessed because the strain used in this study lacks this plasmid. Altogether, these data indicate that HdfR-MaoP regulation can extend to specific plasmid-borne functions, such as Caf1 production on pMT1, suggesting that horizontally acquired elements can be integrated into host-associated regulatory networks governed by this module.

MaoP functions as a nucleoid-associated protein with broad DNA-binding activity

Previous studies in E. coli have proposed diverse functions for MaoP, including interactions with RNA polymerase, RNA binding, or organization of the Ori region through the MaoS site30–32. We therefore systematically evaluated these possibilities. Under the experimental conditions tested, MaoP did not interact with RNA polymerase in bacterial two-hybrid assays (RpoN, RpoB, RpoZ, RpoS, RpoE, RpoD) (Supplementary Fig. 9a), did not enrich for RNA above background in RIP experiments, and did not induce significant changes in local or global chromosome folding in vivo, as assessed by Hi-C contact maps and contact-probability decay as a function of genomic distance (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 9b). Deletion of the MaoS site, located in the intergenic region between hdfR and maoP, abolished endogenous maoP transcription and translation without affecting hdfR transcription (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b) and phenocopied the ΔmaoP mutant (Supplementary Fig. 10c–e). However, the MaoS site was not required for MaoP-dependent phenotypes once maoP expression was restored, as expression of maoP from an inducible promoter in a ΔMaoS site background fully restored resistance to antimicrobial peptides and serum, as well as biofilm formation (Supplementary Fig. 10f–k). Mutation of the ribosome-binding site phenocopied the ΔmaoP mutant, demonstrating that MaoP-dependent phenotypes are mediated by the protein product rather than by a non-coding RNA (Supplementary Fig. 11). MaoP is a small (14-kDa), non-highly basic DNA-binding protein that ranks among the top 150 most abundant proteins detected in our proteomic analysis, with an estimated abundance of approximately 1.4 × 104 and 4.4 × 104 molecules per cell at 21 °C and 37 °C respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5f and Supplementary Data 1). ChIP-seq revealed MaoP occupancy at discrete promoter regions, including its own locus, the zinc uptake regulator zur, the biofilm operon hmsHFRS and the pMT1-borne caf1R-caf1M1A1 region (Supplementary Data 2). However, microscale thermophoresis measurements demonstrated that MaoP binds DNA without detectable sequence specificity under the conditions tested (Fig. 4b). The HdfR-MaoP regulatory axis is functionally conserved across Enterobacterales

Comparative genomic analyses revealed that the hdfR-maoP locus is broadly conserved across Enterobacterales, being present in hundreds of sequenced genomes spanning pathogenic, commensal, and environmental species associated with terrestrial and aquatic habitats (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). Despite substantial sequence divergence outside conserved domains, alignment of HdfR homologs from Y. pestis and E.coli showed that 85% of identical residues cluster within the predicted DNA-binding domain (Supplementary Fig. 12a), consistent with conservation of regulatory function. To assess whether this conservation extends to functional interchangeability, E. coli hdfR and maoP were expressed in the corresponding Y. pestis deletion mutants. Despite only ~60% (HdfR) and ~90% (MaoP) overall amino acid identity, heterologous expression of either gene fully restored resistance to serum complement and antimicrobial peptides, as well as biofilm formation, to wild-type levels (Supplementary Fig. 12c–h). Conversely, deletion of hdfR or maoP in E. coli impaired biofilm formation, and expression of the corresponding Y. pestis homolog restored this defect, indicating reciprocal functional conservation for this shared biofilm-associated output (Supplementary Fig. 12i). These findings indicate that the HdfR-MaoP module constitutes an evolutionarily conserved regulatory unit, with conserved genomic organization, functional interchangeability, and shared biofilm-associated regulatory activity, while immune-resistance outputs may reflect species- and niche-specific deployment of this regulatory scaffold.

Pharmacological perturbation of the HdfR-MaoP regulatory module

To assess whether the HdfR-MaoP regulatory module is amenable to chemical perturbation, we monitored maoP promoter activity in wild-type Y. pestis in the presence of a library of FDA-approved compounds. This screen identified flunixin as a compound capable of reducing maoP promoter activity in a dose-dependent manner at 37 °C, with up to a twofold decrease under the conditions tested (Fig. 5a). At the highest concentration active at 37 °C, flunixin did not reduce maoP promoter activity at 21 °C (Fig. 5b). Consistent with this temperature-restricted transcriptional effect, flunixin exposure did not reproducibly impair biofilm formation under flea-like conditions (21 °C) (Fig. 5c), but increased susceptibility of Y. pestis to antimicrobial peptides under mammalian host-like conditions (37 °C) (Fig. 5d, e). This peptide-sensitizing effect required HdfR, as flunixin did not further increase ΔhdfR susceptibility, even at polymyxin concentrations where mutant survival remained above the lower detection plateau (Fig. 5d, e). In line with this genetic requirement, heterologous expression of E. coli hdfR restored flunixin-dependent peptide sensitization (Fig. 5d, e). Furthermore, at the biochemical level, microscale thermophoresis using purified HdfR showed direct binding of flunixin to HdfR, with an apparent Kd of approximately 1 µM (Fig. 5f). Discussion

Vector-borne pathogens face sharply contrasting selective pressures in arthropod vectors and mammalian hosts, requiring coordinated physiological reprogramming across abrupt environmental transitions1,5. How such opposing requirements are integrated at the regulatory level has remained incompletely understood. HdfR activates MaoP, forming a hierarchical regulatory module that controls biofilm-associated genes through both Zur-dependent and Zur-independent pathways and coordinates additional envelope adaptation programs. These coordinated molecular outputs support flea blockage and promote resistance to antimicrobial peptides and complement, thereby facilitating bacterial adaptation across vector and mammalian host environments. Temperature-dependent regulation highlights distinct roles of the HdfR-MaoP module under flea-like (21 °C) and mammalian (37 °C) conditions. At the core of this regulatory architecture lies MaoP, whose expression is controlled by the transcriptional regulator HdfR. In this hierarchy, HdfR links temperature-dependent host-associated cues to maoP expression, whereas MaoP acts downstream as a central effector, and its effects depend on both its expression level and the environmental condition. Through this arrangement, the HdfR-MaoP module couples biofilm formation required for flea-borne transmission with envelope-associated adaptations that enhance resistance to innate immune defenses in the mammalian host. These coordinated outputs support a role for HdfR-MaoP as a regulatory hub for integrating the opposing selective pressures inherent to vector-borne life cycles.

Our mechanistic analyses clarify MaoP’s molecular function within this framework. MaoP is a small, highly abundant protein with a basic central region and the capacity to autoregulate its own expression. It binds DNA without detectable sequence specificity and alters DNA conformation in vitro, as shown by MST and AFM. By contrast, Hi-C contact maps and contact-probability decay analyses did not reveal major changes in chromosome folding in vivo. This difference likely reflects the different scales probed by the two approaches, with AFM detecting local single-molecule DNA conformational changes, whereas Hi–C reports population-averaged chromosomal contacts at kilobase resolution, as well as potentially dynamic or heterogeneous MaoP-DNA interactions, as previously reported for H-NS33. Thus, transient, heterogeneous, or sub-kilobase MaoP-DNA interactions may not produce detectable changes in global Hi-C profiles. This scale-dependent behavior is consistent with MaoP acting as a local architectural DNA-binding factor rather than as a global nucleoid organizer, a role classically associated with abundant NAPs such as HU or IHF34,35. Together, these biochemical and functional properties place MaoP within the NAP functional spectrum. However, rather than acting as an autonomous global nucleoid organizer, MaoP appears to function as a quantitatively tuned architectural effector embedded in an HdfR-controlled, temperature-responsive regulatory module. How MaoP functionally interfaces with established nucleoid-associated proteins and DNA-binding regulators remains to be defined. These factors may act together with MaoP to modulate virulence-associated gene expression under specific conditions.

The difference between MaoP’s broad DNA-binding capacity in vitro and its promoter occupancy in vivo helps reconcile its sequence-agnostic DNA binding in vitro with its discrete promoter occupancy in vivo. Controlled maoP expression in the ΔhdfR background restores all tested phenotypes, indicating that HdfR is not required as a promoter-specific cofactor for MaoP activity. Instead, HdfR appears to coordinate the module by tuning MaoP abundance, thereby linking MaoP’s broad DNA-binding capacity to vector- and host-aligned molecular programs. Consistent with this model, MaoP binds DNA in vitro without detectable sequence specificity, its ChIP-seq peaks lack a clear consensus motif, and bacterial two-hybrid assays detected no interaction with the RNA polymerase subunits or sigma factors tested. These findings argue against strict sequence-specific recognition or protein-mediated recruitment as the main determinants of MaoP occupancy. Rather, MaoP occupancy at discrete promoters likely emerges from broad DNA binding constrained by local chromosomal accessibility, topology, and physiological state, as described for NAPs such as H-NS34,36. In this view, MaoP illustrates how architectural DNA-binding proteins can be integrated into environmentally responsive regulatory circuits to coordinate adaptive outputs across changing host-associated niches. In this context, HdfR-mediated tuning of MaoP abundance may ensure that MaoP-dependent phenotypes are expressed under appropriate temperature conditions. Autoregulatory loops at both the HdfR and MaoP levels may further reinforce this robustness.

Beyond immune evasion per se, the HdfR-MaoP module coordinates anticipatory adaptations across host transitions. Through this regulatory axis, envelope properties are preconfigured under flea-like conditions and selectively reinforced upon entry into the mammalian host, supporting resistance to distinct arms of innate immunity. By limiting exposure of immunogenic surface determinants while strengthening membrane robustness under host-associated conditions, HdfR-MaoP enables Y. pestis to withstand both complement-mediated attack and antimicrobial peptide stress. This temperature-gated coordination may allow the bacterium to anticipate, rather than merely react to, host-associated immune pressures during transmission and early infection.

The HdfR-MaoP module also illustrates how conserved regulators can incorporate horizontally acquired virulence genes. In S. enterica, HdfR directly regulates the horizontally acquired std fimbrial operon37, showing that conserved regulators can be wired to species-specific virulence programs. In Y. pestis, HdfR-MaoP-dependent regulation similarly extends beyond chromosomal loci to selected plasmid-borne virulence-associated genes, including the pMT1-encoded caf locus. Thus, horizontally acquired functions can be incorporated into host-responsive regulatory architectures governed by conserved modules.

The widespread conservation of the hdfR-maoP locus across Enterobacterales indicates that this regulatory architecture predates the specialized flea-mammal life cycle of Y. pestis. In diverse bacterial species, components of this module have been linked to distinct physiological outputs, including virulence, biofilm-associated traits, motility and mutagenic repair of DNA breaks38–43. Consistent with this functional diversification, HdfR has been shown to bind directly to promoters controlling adaptive traits in other Enterobacterales, including the flagellar master regulator flhDC in E. coli and the std fimbrial operon in S. enterica37,44. Our data connect these observations by showing that the organization and regulatory hierarchy of HdfR-MaoP have been retained despite substantial sequence divergence. The functional conservation of this scaffold is supported by prior evidence that MaoP expression is HdfR-dependent in E. coli45, and by our cross-species complementation experiments showing that HdfR and MaoP from E. coli restore all tested phenotypes in the corresponding Y. pestis mutants. Conversely, HdfR and MaoP from Y. pestis restore biofilm formation in the corresponding E. coli mutants, despite the absence of the HdfR-MaoP-regulated Y. pestis hmsHFRS biofilm genes in E. coli. Together, these findings suggest that HdfR-MaoP is an ancient regulatory module whose outputs have diversified across ecological contexts, with downstream outputs shaped by species-specific physiology and niche-associated pressures. In Y. pestis, this scaffold appears to have been integrated into regulatory programs that coordinate flea-borne transmission and mammalian host adaptation.

In conclusion, the HdfR-MaoP module functions as a compact regulatory architecture that resolves how opposing host-specific programs are integrated during vector-borne life cycles characterized by abrupt transitions between ectothermic and mammalian environments. Through this mechanism, Y. pestis can efficiently navigate transitions between its arthropod vector and mammalian host. By coupling biofilm-mediated transmission with anticipatory envelope-associated programs that limit complement recognition, while reinforcing resistance to antimicrobial peptides, this circuitry provides a mechanistic link between transmission-associated biofilm formation and host-associated envelope adaptation, allowing Y. pestis to meet the conflicting demands imposed by alternating vector and mammalian environments. The widespread conservation of this architecture across Gammaproteobacteria highlights an ancient and versatile strategy for physiological plasticity and positions HdfR-MaoP as a conserved regulatory hub coordinating adaptation across distinct ecological contexts. Consistent with this view, the identification of flunixin as a chemical probe provides a means to experimentally manipulate this conserved regulatory architecture, offering tractable entry points to modulate host- and environment-associated physiological states in Gram-negative bacteria.

Methods

Bacterial strains, mutant construction, complementation, and growth conditions

All bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 3. Y. pestis strains were grown in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) or Lysogeny Broth (LB) at 21 °C or 37 °C for 15 h without shaking unless otherwise specified. When required, media were supplemented with MgCl2 (4 mM), CaCl2 (4 mM), and carbenicillin (100 µg/mL), kanamycin (50 µg/mL), trimethoprim (25 µg/mL) or streptomycin (50 µg/mL). Mutations were generated using the λ Red recombinase system as previously described46. Briefly, the target sequence was replaced with an antibiotic resistance cassette amplified from the vectors listed in Supplementary Data 3 using the primer pairs shown in Supplementary Data 4. All constructs were verified by PCR. Complementation strategies were adapted to the experimental purpose. For in vitro assays, the gene of interest under its native promoter was cloned using the CloneJET PCR Cloning Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific), and the primer sets are listed in Supplementary Data 4. Alternatively, the coding sequence without its promoter was cloned into the pBAD30 expression vector for arabinose-inducible expression. For flea infection experiments, the gene under the control of its native promoter was integrated at the chromosomal attTn7 site. For this, PCR products were first cloned into the suicide vector pUC18R6KT-mini-Tn7T in E. coli DH5α λ-pir. Recombinant constructs were then co-electroporated with the helper plasmid pTNS2 into Y. pestis, enabling site-specific integration at the att Tn7 site47. Correct integration was confirmed by PCR.

Antimicrobial peptide and serum resistance assay

For both assays, 5 × 105 bioluminescent Y. pestis grown in LB at 21 °C or 37 °C were suspended in 10 µL of PBS (serum assay) or LB (antimicrobial peptide assay) and added to white 96-well plates (Fisher Scientific) containing 90 µL of the appropriate test solution, for a final volume of 100 µL per well. Plates were immediately sealed with a transparent film (ThinSeal™ ClearLine) and incubated at 21 °C or 37 °C without shaking. Luminescence was recorded at different incubation times using a Centro XS³ LB 960 luminometer (Berthold) and the Microwin 2010 5.24 software or a Victor™ X3 multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer) and the PerkinElmer 2030 Manager 4.0. For the antimicrobial peptide assay, the test solution consisted of LB supplemented with increasing concentrations of polymyxin B (0–84 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich). For the serum resistance assay, the test solution consisted of either normal human serum, heat-inactivated serum (56 °C, 30 min)48; serum was purchased from the Établissement Français du Sang (EFS).

Biofilm quantification and Congo red binding assays

Biofilm formation was quantified by crystal violet staining. A total of 2 × 106 Y. pestis from overnight cultures grown at 21 °C with shaking were inoculated into 24-well flat-bottom plates containing 1 mL of LB supplemented with 4 mM MgCl2 and 4 mM CaCl2 per well. After 24 h incubation at 21 °C with shaking, the medium was removed, and the wells were washed three times with water. Adherent biofilms were stained with 0.01% crystal violet for 15 min at room temperature, washed three times, and bound dye was solubilized with 1.5 mL ethanol-acetone (80:20). Aliquots were transferred to transparent 96-well round-bottom plates, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a Biotek ELx800 spectrophotometer. Exopolysaccharide production was quantified by Congo red binding as previously described49. Bacteria grown at 21 °C with shaking in LB supplemented with 4 mM MgCl2 and 4 mM CaCl2 were centrifuged and resuspended in the same medium containing 0.2% galactose and 3% Congo red to reach 109 CFU/mL. After 2.5 h of incubation at 21 °C without shaking, cultures were centrifuged, and the optical density of the supernatant was measured at 490 nm to quantify unbound dye.

Flea infection

The Xenopsylla cheopis fleas used in this study were originally obtained from the Rocky Mountain Laboratories, National Institutes of Health (RML/NIH, USA), and have been maintained in our laboratory. Flea infections were performed as previously described8. Starved X. cheopis were randomly selected from the same laboratory colony and allowed to feed for 1 h on heparinized male OF-1 mouse blood (Charles River Laboratories) containing 5 × 108 CFU/mL of Y. pestis grown overnight at 37 °C in BHI, using an artificial feeding system. Immediately after feeding, fully engorged fleas were randomly selected to generate cohorts of 100 fleas, either composed of equal numbers of males and females or of females only, and were selected and maintained in the dark at 21 °C and 75% relative humidity in a climatic chamber (Eratis). Fleas were fed twice weekly for 1 h on 2–5 day-old NMRI mouse neonates for up to 4 weeks. Neonates were obtained from an in-house breeding colony maintained under standard animal facility conditions: 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, 22 ± 2°C ambient temperature and 55 ± 10% relative humidity. Mouse sex was not determined because it could not be reliably assessed at this developmental stage. Mixed-sex cohorts were monitored twice weekly immediately after feeding to determine the blockage rate; blockage being defined as the presence of fresh red blood in the foregut but not in the midgut. Cohorts composed of females only were used to quantify the time course of gut colonization. At selected time points post-infection, fleas were surface-sterilized by sequential immersion in 1 mL of 3% hydrogen peroxide and 1 mL of absolute ethanol for 2 min each, rinsed in 10 mL of sterile PBS, and air-dried. Each flea was transferred into a Lysing Matrix H tube (MP Biomedicals) containing 1 mL of PBS and homogenized using a FastPrep-24 instrument (30 s, speed 6.0 m/s). Homogenates were mixed with LB agar supplemented with 1 µg/mL Irgasan and 10 µg/mL hemin and poured onto a base layer of the same medium. After drying, a second agar layer was added. Plates were incubated for 48 h at 28 °C before CFU enumeration. For co-infection experiments, fleas were infected with an equal mixture of two Y. pestis strains. Individual fleas were processed as described above, and homogenates were plated on non-selective supplemented LB agar to determine total bacterial loads and on selective supplemented LB agar to enumerate the antibiotic-resistant strain. The abundance of the competing strain was inferred by subtracting selective CFU counts from total CFU counts, and bacterial competition was quantified as the relative proportion of each strain within the total bacterial population recovered from each individual flea. All animal procedures were approved by the local Animal Care and Use Committee (Comité d’éthique en expérimentation animale n°75; reference: 2015102609372221 and 2022020316019750) and notified to the relevant French authorities, in accordance with current national legislation (Government Decree No. 2017-1411; Articles R.214-87 to R.214-126).

Proteomic analysis

Wild-type and ΔhdfR Y. pestis strains grown in LB at 21 °C or 37 °C were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C, washed twice with cold PBS, resuspended in PBS supplemented with an EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) and then lysed by sonication using a Branson Digital Sonifier (2 cycles, 45 s on / 5 s off, 70% power, 4 °C). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were ultracentrifuged at 100,000 g for 40 min at 4 °C (Beckman Colter Optima XE-90). Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Samples were dried in a SpeedVac™ concentrator (Thermo Scientific) and processed at the PISSARO proteomics platform (Université de Rouen Normandie, France) as already described50. Peptides were analyzed by nLC-MS/MS (RSLC Ultimate 3000 system coupled to an Eclipse mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific)). MS/MS spectra were exported for peptide identification against the Y. pestis KIM10+ proteome (4079 proteins, Uniprot UP000002490) and containing 4079 sequences) using Proteome Discoverer 1.4 and Mascot 2.6.0 (Matrix Science). Database searches were performed with the following parameters: 1 missed trypsin cleavage site allowed; variable modifications: carbamidomethylation of cysteine and oxidation of methionine. Mass tolerances for precursor and fragment ions were set at 5 ppm and 0.02 Da respectively. The FDR was set at 1%. Quantification was performed using Progenesis 4.1 LC-MS/MS software (Waters) under a label-free quantification workflow. Peptides and proteins with P-value and q-value < 0.05 and statistical power > 0.8 were retained. Only proteins identified with ≥2 peptides and showing a fold change >2 between conditions were considered significant. Five biological replicates per strain and condition were analyzed.

Bioinformatic analyses

Functional annotation and pathway classification of differentially regulated proteins were performed using the KEGG, NCBI, and UniProt databases.

Metabolomic study

Metabolite profiling of Y. pestis strains was performed as described previously51. Briefly, bacteria grown in LB at 21 °C or 37 °C were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C, and pellets were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Metabolites were extracted at −20 °C using methanol/acetonitrile/water (50:30:20). Extracts were vortexed for 5 min at 4 °C, centrifuged at 16,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were analyzed by LC-MS using a SeQuant ZIC-pHILIC column (Millipore). Chromatographic separation was performed using a linear gradient from 80% organic to 80% aqueous over 15 min at 200 µL/min, with the column maintained at 48 °C. The aqueous mobile phase contained 20 mM ammonium carbonate with 0.1% ammonium hydroxide, and the organic phase was acetonitrile. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Q-Exactive Plus instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating in electrospray ionization with polarity switching, at 35,000 resolution at m/z 200 over an m/z range of 75-1000. Lock masses ensured mass accuracy <5 ppm. Peak areas were extracted using TraceFinder (Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on the exact mass of the singly charged ion and known retention times on the HPLC column. Statistical analyses were performed using MetaboAnalyst 4.052. Hierarchical clustering was performed using a Pearson correlation-based distance metric and Ward linkage to group metabolites according to abundance patterns.

Promoter activity assays

Promoter activity was quantified using luciferase- or GFP-based transcriptional reporters. For the luciferase assay, a Y. pestis strain harboring at the att Tn7 site a promoterless luxCDABE operon downstream of a kanamycin-resistance cassette preceded by a single I-SceI restriction site was used53. The promoters of interest were amplified by PCR and inserted in place of the kanamycin cassette and the I-SceI restriction site using the λ Red recombinase system with I-SceI counter-selection8, employing the plasmids and primer pairs listed in Supplementary Data 3 and 4. All constructs were verified by PCR and sequencing. Cultures grown in LB at 21 °C or 37 °C were transferred into white 96-well plates (Fisher), and luminescence was recorded for 1s using a Centro XS³ LB 960 luminometer (Berthold). Luminescence values were normalized to OD600. For the GFP assay, the promoters amplified by PCR were cloned into the pPromX-GFP plasmid. Y. pestis harboring the recombinant plasmids were grown in LB at 21 °C or 37 °C, washed three times in PBS and transferred into black 96-well plates (Fisher) using excitation/emission settings of 488/535 nm. GFP fluorescence was immediately recorded for 1s using a Victor™ X3 multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer) and normalized to OD600 measured after the final wash.

Western-blotting

Y. pestis was directly resuspended in Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) containing β-mercaptoethanol after being grown in LB or after three washes with PBS when grown in human serum. Samples were boiled at 95 °C for 10 min and whole-cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham), which were then blocked in PBS containing 0.5% Tween-20 (PBS-T) and 5% skim milk. Membranes were incubated with the appropriate primary antibody (Supplementary Data 5), washed four times in PBS-0.5% Tween-20 and incubated for 45 min at room temperature with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Supplementary Data 5). After four additional washes, signals were detected using SuperSignal West Pico (Thermo Fisher). Images were acquired using a LAS-3000 imager (Fujifilm) or an ImageQuant 800 system (Cytiva) and analyzed with the Multigauge 2.1.1 and ImageQuant TL 10.2, respectively (Supplementary Data 5). Band intensities were normalized to the RNA polymerase alpha subunit, to Ponceau or Coomassie blue-stained gels. Specific antibody conditions are provided in Supplementary Data 5.

DOC-PAGE assay

DOC-PAGE was performed as previously described54. A total of 7.5 × 108 Y. pestis grown at 21 °C or 37 °C were harvested by centrifugation, washed once in PBS, and resuspended in DOC lysis buffer (2% sodium deoxycholate, 4% 2-mercaptoethanol, 10% glycerol, and 0.002% bromophenol blue in 1 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH 6.8). Suspensions were heated for 10 min at 100 °C, treated with Proteinase K (80 μg/mL) for 3 h at 55 °C, and the reaction was stopped by an additional 10 min incubation at 100 °C. Equivalent amounts of material equivalent to 2 × 107 bacteria per lane were loaded onto DOC-PAGE gels, and LOS was visualized by silver staining.

Outer membrane permeability assay

Outer membrane permeability was assessed using the 1-N-phenylnaphthylamine (NPN) uptake assay as previously described55. Y. pestis grown in LB at 21 °C or 37 °C was harvested, washed three times in PBS, and adjusted to 109 CFU/mL. NPN (10 µM final) was added to the suspension and fluorescence was recorded after 30 min incubation in the dark at 21 °C or 37 °C (excitation 350 nm, emission 420 nm) using a Victor™ X3 multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer). Background fluorescence (PBS + NPN) was subtracted from all readings, and values were normalized to the bioluminescence of the same suspensions, used as a proxy for bacterial load.

Detection of deposited serum components

Deposition of complement factors and serum proteins (C3b, C4, C4BP, factor H, factor B, vitronectin, C1q and MBL) on the bacterial surface was assessed by Western blot as previously described56, using the antibodies listed in Supplementary Data 5. Y. pestis was incubated with normal or heat-inactivated human serum at a final concentration of 90% (50% for assays involving factor H) using ~7.5 × 106 bacteria per reaction (~109 for factor H because higher bacterial input was required for detection). At the indicated time points, samples were chilled on ice and centrifuged at 4 °C, washed three times in cold PBS, resuspended in Laemmli sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, and analyzed by Western blot as described above.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted by resuspending Y. pestis pellets in TRI Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by the addition of chloroform (20% final; vortexed and incubated at room temperature for 10 min). After phase separation, the aqueous phase was mixed 1:1 with ethanol, and RNA was pelleted by centrifugation. Pellets were washed with 75% ethanol, centrifuged again, air-dried and resuspended in RNase-free water. Residual genomic DNA was removed by DNase I treatment (Merck). RNA concentration and purity were measured spectrophotometrically, and RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA synthesis was performed using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and random hexamers. Quantitative PCR was carried out using Takyon Rox SYBR MasterMix dTT Blue (Eurogentec) on a Stratagene Mx3005P instrument (Agilent Technologies), with fluorescence acquisition and Ct determination performed using MxPro 4.10 software. Relative transcript levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method with proC as the internal reference57. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 4.

High-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C)

Y. pestis (Δpla) cells crosslinking and Hi-C library preparation were performed as described in58. Hi-C sequencing data were processed starting with adapter trimming using Cutadapt 3.2. Paired-end reads were then aligned to the Y. pestis KIM10+ genome (accession number: PRJNA288) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.2). Hi-C data were further analyzed using the OCHA pipeline in R (https://github.com/js2264/OHCA), as described in59. For visualization, the contact maps were displayed at 1 kb bin resolution. To qualitatively compare normalized contact maps, we performed a ratio of matrices. This ratio is computed for each bin of the map by dividing the amount of contacts it presents in ΔmaoP by the amount of contacts in the WT, and applying a log2 transformation to the resulting matrix. The log2 of the ratio is plotted with a Gaussian filter (H = 1). The color code reflects a decrease or an increase in contacts in one condition compared to the other (blue or red signal, respectively). No change is represented by a white signal. To quantitatively compare chromosome organization between strains, contact probability was calculated as a function of genomic distance from normalized Hi–C contact matrices. Contact-probability decay curves were compared between WT Δpla and ΔmaoP Δpla strains to assess potential changes in short- and long-range chromosomal contacts.

ChIP-seq

ChIP-seq was performed as previously described with minor modifications60, using three independent biological replicate cultures per strain. Y. pestis lacking pCD1 and pla, while expressing a C-terminal FLAG-tagged MaoP protein (MaoP-FLAG), together with the corresponding untagged parental strains (negative controls) were grown in LB at 37 °C; MaoP-FLAG were being functional (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Cultures were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 30 min at 37 °C under gentle agitation, quenched with glycine, washed with TBS, pelleted and resuspended in IP buffer. Chromatin was sheared to 100–300 bp fragments using a Bioruptor sonicator (40 cycles, 30 s on / 30 s off, high power, 4 °C). Complete compositions of buffers are provided in Supplementary Data 6. Lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-FLAG magnetic beads (GenScript). Beads were washed, and bound DNA was eluted with alkaline elution buffer prior to reverse-crosslinking Protein-DNA complexes overnight at 65 °C. DNA was purified by phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, pH 4.0) extraction, precipitated in precipitation buffer (30:1, v/v) overnight at −20 °C, washed with ethanol 70% and resuspended in TE buffer. 50 ng of fragmented double-stranded DNA was used for library preparation using the MicroPlex Library Preparation kit v3 (Diagenode). Dual-indexed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 sequencer. Reads were processed using the Galaxy server61, mapped to the Y. pestis KIM genome with Bowtie2 2.5.5 + galaxy062, and peaks were called with MACS2 2.2.9.1 + galaxy063 using default settings.

Purification of recombinant proteins

The maoP and zur genes from Y. pestis were amplified by PCR and cloned into the pET24d or pET28a vectors to generate 6His-tag fusion constructs, using the primers and plasmids listed in Supplementary Data 3 and 4. Complete compositions of TNTI, TNI, and TN buffers, as well as all reagent formulations used for expression and purification, are provided in Supplementary Data 6. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli C41(DE3). Cultures were grown in LB at 37 °C to an OD600 of ~0.4, induced with 1 mM IPTG for 3 h at 37 °C, harvested, resuspended in PBS and lysed using a French press (Constant Systems). Clarified lysates were loaded onto HisPur™ affinity columns (Thermo Fisher) packed with complete His-Tag Purification Resin (Roche). Proteins were washed with TNTI and TNI buffers, and eluted with TNI containing 500 mM imidazole. Proteins were concentrated and exchanged into TN or TN AFM buffer using Amicon Ultra-15 devices (Merck). Purity was verified by SDS-PAGE.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

MST measurements were performed on a Monolith NT.115 Pico instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). Purified MaoP and Zur proteins were exchanged into TNG buffer (Supplementary Data 6) and quantified by BCA assay. Proteins were diluted to 200 nM in PBS-T (NanoTemper) and labeled with 100 nM RED-tris-NTA dye for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, then diluted to a final concentration of 10 nM. DNA ligands consisted of purified PCR products (amplified from sequences listed in Supplementary Data 4). PCR products were purified using a PCR cleanup kit (Macherey Nagel), quantified by spectrophotometry, and serially diluted in a 1:1 ratio across 16 concentrations to generate the ligand titration series. Equal volumes of labeled protein and diluted DNA were mixed and incubated for 5 min at room temperature before loading into standard capillaries. Thermophoresis was recorded using medium MST power and 20% LED. Data were acquired with MO. Control 1.6.1 and analyzed with MO.Affinity Analysis 2.3 (NanoTemper) (Supplementary Data 6).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP was performed on Y. pestis lacking pPla and expressing the E. coli MaoP-3×FLAG from the pAK004 plasmid. Cultures were grown overnight in LB at 37 °C supplemented with 10 mM arabinose, harvested, washed once in cold PBS and lysed by bead beating (Biospec) in ice-cold lysis buffer (Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% Triton X-100). Lysates were clarified by centrifugation and incubated with anti-FLAG magnetic beads (GenScript) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed four times with TBS, and MaoP-RNA complexes were eluted with RIP elution buffer using a thermomixer (70 °C, 800 rpm, 5 min). RNA was extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, pH 4.0), precipitated overnight at -20°C with precipitation buffer, washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, then resuspended in nuclease-free water. RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Merck) prior to being assessed for integrity by agarose gel electrophoresis and quantity with a Qubit RNA High Sensitivity kit (Thermo Fisher). Details on buffer compositions, washing conditions, and centrifugation parameters are provided in Supplementary Data 6.

c-di-GMP quantification

c-di-GMP was extracted and quantified by HPLC as described previously64, with minor modifications. Briefly, bacterial cultures were fixed with 0.18% formaldehyde, pelleted, washed, and resuspended in distilled water. An aliquot was used for protein normalization by BCA assay, while the remaining sample was heated at 99 °C and extracted sequentially with 95% and 70% ethanol. Dried extracts were resuspended in nanopure water and analyzed on a Dionex Chromeleon 7 HPLC system equipped with a reverse-phase C18 column. c-di-GMP was eluted using ammonium acetate-based water/methanol solvents, detected at 253 nm, quantified using a commercial c-di-GMP standard curve, and normalized to total protein content. Details on buffer compositions are provided in Supplementary Data 6.

Determination of MaoP copy number per bacteria

MaoP copy number per bacterium was estimated by quantitative immunoblotting. Clarified lysates were incubated with anti-FLAG magnetic beads (GenScript) for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. Beads were washed four times with Wash buffer, and MaoP-FLAG was eluted using the Alkaline elution buffer. After neutralization as recommended by the manufacturer, the purified proteins were quantified by BCA assay. Purified MaoP-FLAG was used as a calibration standard and loaded alongside whole-cell extracts prepared from 2.5 × 108 Y. pestis grown at 21 °C or 37 °C. Samples were resolved by electrophoresis, transferred, and probed with an anti-FLAG antibody. Band intensities were quantified and compared with the purified MaoP-FLAG standard curve to determine the amount of MaoP-FLAG in each extract. Molecule numbers were derived using the predicted molecular weight of MaoP-FLAG (14,065 kDa) and Avogadro’s constant, then normalized to bacterial cell counts. Details on magnetic beads and Antibody Supplementary Data 5, as well as buffer compositions, are provided in Supplementary Data 6.

Bacterial two-hybrid (BACTH) assay

Protein-protein interactions were assessed using the adenylate cyclase-based BACTH system (Euromedex) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Candidate genes were cloned into the pKT25 vector, and maoP was cloned into the pUT18 vector using the primers listed in Supplementary Data 4. The resulting plasmid pairs were co-transformed into E. coli DHM01 (ΔcyaA). Transformants were plated on MacConkey agar supplemented with maltose 1%, ampicillin and kanamycin, and incubated at 30 °C until colony color discrimination was evident. The pKT25-Zip and pUT18C-Zip plasmid pair was used as the positive control.

Atomic force microscopy

DNA and protein samples were prepared and imaged in liquid according to a previously published protocol65. DNA was obtained after PCR using primer sets in Supplementary Data 4, and subsequently was extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, pH 4.0), precipitated overnight at −20 °C, washed with ethanol 70% and resuspended in nuclease-free water. Purified MaoP in TN AFM buffer was obtained as described above. Samples were immobilized onto freshly cleaved mica (10 mm, Ted Pella, US) by incubating 4 µl of 10 ng/µl solution in a 70 µl drop of divalent cations buffer during 10 min for DNA or 30 min for DNA mixed with purified MaoP. Samples were then washed four times in the same buffer to remove unbound material and rinsed in Ni buffer to stabilize DNA-protein complexes during AFM scanning. Imaging was conducted on a FastScan Bio microscope (Bruker, US) operating NanoScope 9.4 software in Peak Force Tapping mode, using FastScan-D probes (Bruker, US). Engage and Peak Force imaging setpoints were minimized to [40–100] pN. Peak Force parameters were as follows: 2 kHz PF frequency, [10–20] nm PF amplitude, [150–1000] μm scan size, [256–512] pixels, [1–3] Hz scan rate, [5–10] nm lift height, [2–5] gain. Sync distances were adjusted at engagement and kept constant throughout imaging. Images were plane-fitted, cropped (if needed), and exported using NanoScope Analysis 1.9. Complete compositions of divalent cations buffer, Ni Buffer and TN AFM buffers, as well as all reagent formulations used, are provided in Supplementary Data 6. For DNA Bend angles analysis, only DNA molecules that were completely visible on the image and could be unambiguously traced were taken for analysis. DNA bend angles were measured using ImageJ 1.52, a software. The angles were defined by manually determining the path of the DNA segments. By convention, the DNA bend angle is defined as the deviation from straight.

Identification of compounds inhibiting the HdfR-MaoP module

Inhibitors of the HdfR-MaoP module were identified using Y. pestis carrying a chromosomal maoP-luxCDABE reporter integrated at the attTn7 site. Approximately 106 bacteria were dispensed into 384-well plates containing 50 µL of LB medium with or without individual compounds from an FDA-approved compound library (Tocris and Prestwick). Plates were incubated at 37 °C, and luminescence (RLU) and optical density (OD595) were measured at 0, 4, and 8 h using a Victor™ X3 plate reader (PerkinElmer). Luminescence values were normalized to OD595 to control for bacterial growth. Compounds that reduced maoP promoter activity by at least twofold relative to DMSO controls, without affecting OD595, were selected for dose-response analyses. Selected compounds were tested at concentrations ranging from 6 nM to 50 µM, performed in triplicate. The lead compound was validated by assessing Y. pestis survival after exposure to polymyxin B (1.25 µg/mL) and the lead compound (25 µM), alone or in combination (in the presence of the DMSO vehicle control), as described in the antimicrobial peptide susceptibility assays section.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). For experiments comparing multiple groups to a single control, an ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used. For antimicrobial peptide resistance assays, comparisons between strains across tested doses were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). n values correspond to distinct biological samples or independent experimental units, not repeated measurements of the same sample. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05.
