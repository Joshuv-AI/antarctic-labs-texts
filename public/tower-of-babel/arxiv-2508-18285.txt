An average Brun–Titchmarsh theorem and shifted primes with a large prime factor
Runbo Li

Abstract
. The author studies an average version of Brun–Titchmarsh theorem with large moduli. Using Maynard’s recent breakthrough on the Bombieri–Friedlander–Iwaniec type triple convolution estimates, we refine the previous result of Baker and Harman (1996). As an application, we improve a result of Baker and Harman (1998) on shifted primes with a large prime factor, showing that the largest prime factor of p − 1 p-1 is larger than p 0.679 p^{0.679} for infinitely many primes p p .

P^{+}(p+2)>P^{+}(p)=p.

2.
An outline of the proof

Let

\mathcal{A}^{q}=\{n:n\sim x,\ n\equiv a(\bmod q)\},\quad\mathcal{B}^{q}=\{n:n\sim x,\ (n,q)=1\},

\mathcal{C}_{d}=\{n:nd\in\mathcal{C}\},\quad P(z)=\prod_{p<z}p,\quad S(\mathcal{C},z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{C}\\
(n,P(z))=1\end{subarray}}1.

To prove our Theorem
1
, it suffices to show that

\sum_{x^{0.679}\leqslant p\leqslant x+a}\pi(x;p,-a)\log p>\varepsilon x.

(6)

Note that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leqslant x}\log(p+a)=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{p\leqslant x}\sum_{n|(p+a)}\Lambda(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{p_{1}\leqslant x}\sum_{p_{2}|(p_{1}+a)}\log p_{2}+o(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{p\leqslant x+a}\pi(x;p,-a)\log p+o(x)

(7)

and

\sum_{p\leqslant x}\log(p+a)\sim x.

(8)

By the Prime Number Theorem, we have

\sum_{p\leqslant x+a}\pi(x;p,-a)\log p>(1-\varepsilon)x.

(9)

From the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem and the Brun–Titchmarsh inequality, we have

\sum_{p\leqslant x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\pi(x;p,-a)\log p<\left(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon\right)x.

(10)

Thus, in order to show (6), we only need to establish that

\sum_{x^{\frac{1}{2}}<p\leqslant x^{0.679}}\pi(x;p,-a)\log p<\left(\frac{1}{2}-3\varepsilon\right)x.

(11)

We remark that one can also see
[
22
]
and previous articles on that topic to get the idea of using a continuous version of Harman’s alternative sieve to give an upper bound for a special sum over primes in a fixed range. By the definition of the sieve function and Prime Number Theorem, we have

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{A}^{q}}1=S\left(\mathcal{A}^{q},(2x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad S\left(\mathcal{B}^{q},(2x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=(1+o(1))\frac{x}{\log x}.

(12)

Thus, we need an estimate for
S\left(\mathcal{A}^{q},(2x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
that holds for almost all
q\sim Q
. Our aim is to show that the sparser set
\mathcal{A}^{q}
contains the expected proportion of primes compared to the larger set
\mathcal{B}^{q}
, which requires us to decompose
S\left(\mathcal{A}^{q},(2x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)
and prove asymptotic formulas of the form

S\left(\mathcal{A}^{q},z\right)=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}S\left(\mathcal{B}^{q},z\right)

(13)

for some parts of it, and drop the other parts. The dropped parts must be negative, since we want an upper bound. If we have, for almost all
q\sim Q
,

S\left(\mathcal{A}^{q},(2x)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)<C(\theta)\frac{x}{\varphi(q)\log x},

(14)

then trivially

\pi(x;q,a)<C(\theta)\frac{x}{\varphi(q)\log x}.

(15)

Note that
C(\theta)
is monotonic increasing ([
[
1
]
, Theorem 1(i)]). Combining (15) with the bound

\int_{0.5}^{0.679}C(\theta)d\theta<\frac{1}{2},

(16)

we can easily deduce (11) and prove Theorem
1
. Now we only need to prove (16) by giving upper bounds for the function
C(\theta)
. In Section 6 we shall consider it with
\theta
lies in different intervals. Note that each
\theta
corresponds to an independent sieve decomposition.

In the process of giving asymptotic formulas like (13) in Harman’s sieve, one needs the following types of arithmetical information (with all variables are dyadic constrained, and all coefficients are divisor-bounded):
