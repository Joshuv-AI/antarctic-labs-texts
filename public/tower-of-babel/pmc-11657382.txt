Association of altitude with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among patients with hypertension: a cohort study in Southwest China
Authors: Yu Xia, Linhong Pang, MingJing Tang, Zhiling Luo, Min Ma, Huadan Wang, Liping He, Lin Duo, Da Zhu, Xiangbin Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAvailable evidence falls short in assessing the risk of long-term outcomes among individuals with hypertension residing at various altitudes. We aimed to investigate the association between residential altitude and the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality among hypertensive patients.MethodsThis cohort study encompassed 67,275 hypertensive patients aged ≥35 years who participated in China’s Basic Public Health Service Program in 2018. Participants were categorized into four groups based on their residence altitude: <500 m, 500-1,499 m, 1,500-2,500 m, and > 2,500 m. The associations between residential altitude and the risks of all-cause and CVD mortality were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression models. The dose-response relationship was performed by the restricted cubic spline with multivariable adjusted models.ResultsAmong the 67,275 hypertensive patients included in the study (mean age of 63.9 years, with 45.3% male), 8,768 deaths were recorded, of which 5,666 were attributed to CVD. Following multivariate adjustment, when compared to the group residing at altitudes < 500 m, the groups living at altitudes of 500-1,499 m, 1500-2,500 m, and > 2,500 m exhibited significantly risks of all-cause mortality [HR = 1.45 (95% CI: 1.36–1.54), 1.35 (95% CI: 1.28–1.43), and 1.41 (95% CI: 1.28–1.54), respectively] and CVD mortality [HR = 1.47 (95% CI: 1.35–1.58), 1.42 (95% CI: 1.33–1.52), and 1.46 (95% CI: 1.31–1.62), respectively]. The restricted cubic spline curves revealed a nonlinear relationship between residential altitude and all-cause and CVD mortality. The risk of mortality was higher among participants with poorly controlled blood pressure, aged 65 years and above, and living in rural areas.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated a significant association between long-term residential high-altitude exposure and increased risks of all-cause and CVD mortality among hypertension patients. The implications of the findings call for a prioritization of public health resource allocation and early intervention efforts, especially for those living at high altitudes and in low-income areas where hypertension is prevalent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20891-1.

Background

Hypertension stands as a prominent driver of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and untimely mortality on a global scale [1], underscored by the “Global Burden of Disease Study 2019” revealing that hypertension was responsible for 19.2% (10.80 million) of deaths worldwide [2]. In 2018, approximately 245 million people in China suffered from hypertension, and 2.54 million people died as a result of the ailment [3]. Hypertension ranked as the foremost risk factor for mortality in China, contributing to 10.8% of all deaths [4]. Altitudinal variations have been observed in hypertension research, particularly in areas situated at altitudes exceeding 2,500 m, often referred to as plateau regions [5]. These regions, inhabited by approximately 140 million individuals worldwide, are characterized by hypoxia, low atmospheric pressure, and harsh temperatures [6]. Long-term high-altitude exposure can exert substantial impacts on the circulatory system [7, 8].

Recent studies explored the relationship between altitude and blood pressure, as well as its implications for CVD, have yielded inconsistent and sometimes contradictory findings [9, 10]. One prevailing theory posits that hypoxia, resulting from high-altitude living, may serve as a risk factor for hypertension [11]. Studies conducted in high-altitude regions such as Nepal [12], India [13], and Tibet above 3,000 m [14] have observed an elevated prevalence and risk of hypertension with increasing altitude. In contrast, certain regions at high altitudes have exhibited lower hypertension prevalence and risk, attributed to genetic adaptations that mitigate the effects of chronic hypoxia stress [15]. These findings suggest that the impact of elevation on hypertension might be modulated by the long-term adaptive capacity of the population [16]. Other investigations into altitude and blood pressure have revealed intriguing variations. Studies conducted among populations in the Andes [17], Tibetan communities in India [18], and Peru [19] have indicated that natives residing at higher altitudes tend to have lower blood pressure compared to those living at middle and lower elevations. The Swiss cohort study [20], encompassing a substantial sample of 1.64 million residents, showed that for every 1,000 m increase in altitude (ranging from 259 m to 1,960 m), there was a reduction in mortality from coronary heart disease and stroke by 22% and 12%, respectively. In China, elderly age and hypertension have been identified as risk factors for both CVD mortality and all-cause mortality [21]. Despite these significant findings, a critical gap remains in the literature. To our knowledge, there have been no prior investigations assessing the association between high-altitude exposure and the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality among hypertensive patients. Therefore, our study endeavor seeks to address this vital knowledge gap.

The study was conducted in Yunnan province, China, encompassing a population of 47 million with approximately 8.5 million individuals diagnosed with hypertension. Yunnan province is characterized by a diverse topography, featuring mountainous terrains that span from 76.4 m to 6,740 m in elevation. Altitudes falling within the range of 1,000 to 3,500 m account for 87.2% of the province’s total land area [22]. This study took a distinct and comprehensive approach, drawing its sample from all 1,410 townships spread across four distinct altitude groups within the entire province. This novel methodology allowed for a thorough exploration of the association between residential altitude and the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality, employing a population-based retrospective cohort study design.

Methods

Study design and participants

The research was conducted in Yunnan Province, situated in southwestern China, home to a population of approximately 47 million people. Yunnan Province is characterized by its mountainous plateau terrain, with altitudes ranging from as low as 76.4 m to as high as 6,740 m [23]. The recruitment of hypertensive patients took place from January to December 2018. This recruitment was carried out as hypertensive patients were registered into the China Basic Public Health Service (BPHS) program, and all participants were subsequently followed up until November 30, 2023. The BPHS program served as an electronic information system dedicated to the primary health management of hypertensive patients aged 35 years and above [24]. Within this program, each primary health provider conducted face-to-face follow-up services for all hypertensive patients on more than one occasion per year [25]. The BPHS system played a crucial role in recording both baseline information and follow-up data for each patient, ensuring comprehensive and consistent data collection and management throughout the study.

The study included all hypertensive patients aged 35 years and above who resided in townships below 500 m as well as those residing in townships above 2,500 m. Townships with altitudes between 500 and 2,500 m, which we randomly sampled using a multistage stratified random sampling strategy (Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material), represent the areas where the majority of residents and people with hypertension live. Specific township information is provided in the Supplementary Materials. The detailed sampling process is as follows: First, these townships are divided into two elevation groups, divided into 500-1,499 m, and 1,500-2,500 m. Within the two groups, four counties were randomly selected using the simple random sampling method, respectively. Within the eight counties selected, two townships were randomly selected. Finally, the study participants for the 500-2,500 m altitude group were determined based on the cluster sampling method. All hypertensive patients aged 35 years and above residing in these 16 selected townships were included in the study. This cluster sampling approach enabled the comprehensive representation of hypertensive patients within the defined altitude range.

To ensure the integrity of the study cohort, a rigorous process of participant exclusion was undertaken. The target population for BPHS hypertension management is residents of the district aged 35 years and over, so we excluded patients who did not meet this age [24]. Patients aged less than 35 years (n = 726), those with missing follow-up information (n = 391), individuals with incomplete residence data (n = 850), and those lacking essential blood pressure variables (n = 93) were systematically excluded from the analysis. The remaining 67,275 hypertensive patients were categorized into four distinct groups based on their residential altitudes, in alignment with prior research [26]: <500 m (n = 28,758), 500-1,499 m (n = 9,937), 1,500-2,500 m (n = 20,761), and > 2,500 m (n = 7,819). This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Yunnan Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, and all respondents provided written informed consent according to BPHS requirements in 2018.

Data collection and definition

Data were collected by trained primary care physicians who personally conducted face-to-face interviews with hypertensive patients. The process was meticulously structured to ensure the comprehensive acquisition of relevant information. Participants underwent structured interviews using a standardized questionnaire to gather demographic details, including gender, age, ethnicity, and education level. Ethnicity was categorized into two groups: Han Chinese, representing the majority ethnic group in China, and ethnic minorities. The ethnic minorities included primarily Tibetans and the Lisu ethnic group, the predominant hereditary ethnic communities residing at high altitudes above 2,500 m within Yunnan Province. Education level was categorized as illiterate, primary school, high school and above.

Current smokers were identified as individuals who engaged in daily smoking over the past year [27]. The presence of diabetes was ascertained through a combination of self-reported medical history and the use of insulin or antidiabetic drugs [28]. A History of CVD was determined based on the participant’s self-reported clinical experience of angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, or stroke. Participants were dressed in lightweight clothing and were asked to remove their shoes. Height and weight measurements were taken using precision-calibrated electronic scales that were accurate to the nearest 0.1 kg for weight and 0.1 cm for height. Body Mass Index (BMI) was subsequently calculated by dividing an individual’s weight (in kilograms) by the square of their height (in meters), with the following categorizations applied: overweight (BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m²) and obesity (BMI≥ 30 kg/m²), in accordance with established criteria [21].

After a period of sitting and resting for 5 min, systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were measured using an Omron electronic sphygmomanometer. Blood pressure control was defined by the 2018 Revised Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension in China [29]. Specifically, participants were considered to have blood pressure under control if their SBP was less than 140 mm Hg and their DBP was less than 90 mm Hg.

Outcomes

The key outcomes of this study were twofold: all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. All-cause mortality encompassed deaths that occurred during the follow-up period, commencing from the date of the initial baseline interview and extending until November 30, 2023. The process of ascertaining death-related information involved linking data to both the local coroner’s monitoring system and the healthcare system for rigorous verification. Each recorded death was examined to determine its specific cause. CVD mortality was defined in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases, tenth version (ICD-10) coding system, specifically those attributed to heart disease or CVD, regarding ICD-10 codes: I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69 [30].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted from December 2023 to May 2024. Categorical variables representing baseline characteristics were described by frequency and percentage (%), and comparisons between the four altitude groups were carried out using the Chi-square test. Continuous variables were characterized using mean and standard deviations, and ANOVA was applied to compare differences between groups. Survival time was defined as the duration in months, commencing from the baseline interview and extending either to the occurrence of death or until the end of the observation period, which was defined as November 30, 2023. In addition, the incidence of outcome events was calculated per 1000 person-years, offering a standardized measure that adjusted for the varying lengths of observation periods among participants. For a more comprehensive assessment of the mortality trends over time, unadjusted Kaplan-Meier curves were generated. These curves depicted the crude mortality probability across the study duration. To assess potential differences in survival probabilities among the four altitude groups, the log-rank test was employed, facilitating comparative analyses of the survival probability.

Cox proportional hazard regression models were employed to examine the association between altitude and the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality. The reference group for comparison was the altitude group with elevations < 500 m. Hazard ratios (HRs) and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using these regression models. The proportional hazards assumptions were tested by Schofield residuals, and no violations were found. Three models were constructed to investigate the association while accounting for relevant covariates: Model 1 represented the unadjusted analysis. Model 2 introduced adjustments for covariates, including sex, age, ethnicity, residence, and education level. Model 3 further incorporated baseline blood pressure, BMI, antihypertensive medication usage, history of CVD and diabetes mellitus (DM). These specific covariates were selected based on previous studies [21]. We assigned a median value to each elevation group to test for trends in HR values. Additionally, the estimated effects of elevation increment of both 100 and 500 m were examined to provide a more granular understanding of the altitude-outcome associations.

To construct visualized exposure-response associations between altitude and the rates of all-cause and CVD mortality, the “rms” package within the R software was used. This analysis performed the restricted cubic spline curves with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the altitude distribution. These curves provided a visual representation of both linear and non-linear relationships between altitude and the outcomes of interest, and the presence of nonlinearity was assessed using the likelihood ratio test.

Subgroup analyses were conducted using the “forestplot” package in R, focusing on key covariates that included age, sex, ethnicity, education level, smoking status, BMI, baseline blood pressure, antihypertensive medication use, the presence of CVD, and DM. These subgroup analyses also explored potential interaction effects between altitude and these covariates. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings. Participants who had been diagnosed with CVD before the baseline investigation or who had experienced death within 2 years of follow-up were excluded from this analysis. This step aimed to mitigate the potential bias associated with reverse causality [31].

Statistical analyses were performed utilizing R version 4.3.2. A two-sided P-value threshold of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. The study included a total of 67,275 hypertensive patients, with an average age of 63.9 years, and a standard deviation of 11.6 years. Among these participants, 30,527 (45.3%) were male, and 27,728 (41.2%) resided in rural areas. The baseline characteristics of the study subjects are detailed in Table 1, differentiating between four distinct altitude categories of residence: <500 m, 500-1,499 m, 1,500-2,500 m, and > 2,500 m. Participants residing at altitudes > 2,500 m were more likely to possess an illiterate education level, reside in rural areas, and belong to ethnic minority groups. Conversely, participants in the 1,500-2,500 m altitude group were more likely to be urban residents, of Han nationality, have attained high school education or higher, display lower baseline SBP and DBP readings, and exhibit higher rates of hypertension treatment and control.

Mortality analysis

Participants were observed over a median follow-up period of 5.0 years, with the interquartile range (IQR) spanning from 4.9 to 5.1 years. The cumulative person-years of follow-up for the entire cohort was corrected to 321,667 years. Throughout this follow-up period, a total of 8,768 deaths were documented, of which 5,666 were attributed to CVD. To visualize the trends in mortality, Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed, stratifying participants into four distinct altitude groups. These curves (Fig. 2) clearly illustrate that participants residing in the three higher altitude groups exhibited an elevated risk of both all-cause and CVD mortality when compared to those living at altitudes below 500 m. Notably, the group residing above 2,500 m displayed the most pronounced increase in mortality risk (Log-rank P < 0.0001).

Association of residential altitude with mortality

The Cox regression models after adjusted potential variables revealed that when compared to hypertensive patients residing at altitudes < 500 m, those in the 500-1,499 m, 1,500-2,500 m, and > 2,500 m groups exhibited significantly increased risks of both all-cause mortality [HR = 1.45 (95% CI: 1.36–1.54), 1.35 (95% CI: 1.28–1.43), and 1.41 (95% CI: 1.28–1.54), respectively] and CVD mortality [HR = 1.47 (95% CI: 1.35–1.58), 1.42 (95% CI: 1.33–1.52), and 1.46 (95% CI: 1.31–1.62), respectively]. Additionally, there was a significant increasing trend in the adjusted HRs for both all-cause and CVD mortality observed across altitude groups (Model 3, P trend < 0.001). Also relevant, a per 500-meter increase in elevation was associated with a 10% heightened risk of all-cause mortality and an 11% increased risk of CVD-related deaths (Table 2). Notably, the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality surged by 20% and 22%, respectively, with each standard deviation unit increase in altitude (see Table S1 in Supplementary materials).

As demonstrated in Fig. 3, the restricted cubic spline curve unveiled a non-linear association between residential altitude and both all-cause and CVD mortality( nonlinear P-value < 0.001 for both all-cause and CVD mortality). Analysis of the restricted cubic spline curve of the risk of all-cause mortality exhibited an initial increase, followed by a decrease, and then another increase with ascending altitude. Notably, inflection points were identified at approximately 1,214.6 m and 2,226.8 m, signifying critical thresholds in this complex relationship. A parallel trend was observed for CVD mortality, with inflection points identified at approximately 1,233.4 m and 2,320.5 m, mirroring the patterns seen in all-cause mortality.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Figures 4 and 5 provide visual representations in the form of forest plots, offering insights into how this relationship varied across different subgroups. Participants aged 65 years and older, with poorly controlled blood pressure, normal BMI, and living in rural areas showed a significant increase in the risk of all-cause mortality associated with altitude(P < 0.05 for interaction). Similar associations were observed in subgroup analyses of CVD mortality risk The results of these sensitivity analyses reaffirmed the association between altitude exposure and the increased risk of both all-cause and CVD mortality (see Supplementary file, Tables S2 and S3).

Discussion

The study revealed a significant association between residential altitude and all-cause and CVD mortality among patients with hypertension. Notably, the group residing at altitudes below 500 m exhibited the lowest mortality risk. Importantly, this association remained robust even after accounting for demographic factors, CVD risk elements, and baseline blood pressure levels. Subgroup analyses and sensitivity assessments further substantiated this correlation, considering variables such as ethnic diversity and initial blood pressure levels.

This finding represents a pioneering, population-based, and extensive epidemiological analysis of the correlation between residential altitude and mortality risk in hypertensive patients. The American Heart Association study suggest that individuals at risk of CVD or those who have previously experienced CVD events may face an elevated risk of adverse outcomes when residing in high-altitude mountainous regions [32]. Previous investigations, often characterized by limited sample sizes, have indicated that exposure to high altitudes can lead to an increased susceptibility to severe arrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, high-altitude pulmonary edema, and major adverse cardiac events [33–35]. Our study leveraged the geographical diversity within Yunnan province, encompassing a wide range of altitudes from 76.4 m to 6,740 m above sea level, this approach involved the systematic selection of samples from all 1,410 townships in the province.

Our study also highlights a 20% increase in all-cause mortality risk and a 22% increase in CVD mortality risk with each standard deviation unit increase in altitude (950.4 m). Interestingly, this contradicts findings from a Swedish study where mortality rates for coronary heart disease (22% per 1000 m) and stroke (12% per 1000 m) significantly decreased with rising altitude [20]. A Mendelian randomization study suggested a causal association between high altitude adaptation and reduced risk of CVD [36]. In contrast to our conclusion of increased mortality at high altitude due to inconsistencies in study design, participant inclusion criteria, altitude grouping, and outcome definitions. Our study is a real-world cohort study based on community participants with different altitudinal gradients, which provides strong support for fully exploring the effects of altitude on mortality in hypertensive patients. A study revealed that the prevalence of hypertension increases by 2% for every 100 m of altitude gain [37]. However, two other studies from Tibet and northern India reported opposing results [9, 38]. It’s essential to acknowledge that these inconsistencies may arise from limitations inherent in small sample sizes, the constraints of cross-sectional study designs, and the restricted geographic areas that limit the evaluation of altitude gradients and diverse subgroups. Our cohort study, conducted within a low-income province boasting a natural geographic elevation gradient, offers fresh insights into the impact of altitude on long-term outcomes among hypertensive patients.

In our investigation, we observed that for each 500-m elevation increment, there was a corresponding 10% increase in all-cause mortality risk and an 11% increase in the risk of CVD mortality. Several factors may contribute to this association. Firstly, the study participants residing at higher altitudes were predominantly composed of illiterate individuals from rural ethnic minority backgrounds. These individuals exhibited relatively low rates of antihypertensive medication usage and had lower rates of blood pressure control. These factors may contribute to the heightened mortality risk observed in this population. Secondly, elevated mortality rates at higher altitudes, particularly above 2,500 m, could be attributed to the influence of more extreme climatic conditions and environmental stimuli. Factors such as hypoxia (lower oxygen levels), cold temperatures, and increased exposure to ultraviolet radiation at these altitudes might impact the pathophysiology of the participants, further exacerbating mortality risk [39]. This study underscores the significance of future research endeavors and the development of guidelines that take altitude into account as a potential factor contributing to the reduction of the burden of CVD. China has witnessed the highest number of CVD deaths globally, a statistic that has surged by a staggering 89.1% over the past two decades [40]. This trend underscores the urgent need for enhanced quality management of hypertension and CVD, particularly in plateau regions and among vulnerable populations. Our study plays a pivotal role in deepening our understanding of premature all-cause and CVD mortality among hypertension patients living at different altitudes. It also serves as a valuable resource in shaping control plans and strategies, advocating for increased resource allocation for hypertension patients among highland minority populations.

The hypertension epidemic in China is contributing significantly to the burden of CVD [41]. In response to this growing health challenge, the State Council of China has issued a medium- to long-term plan (2017–2025) aimed at preventing non-communicable diseases (NCDs), with a specific goal of reducing premature deaths due to NCDs, including hypertension-related CVD, by 20% compared to 2015 levels by 2025 [42]. The regional disparities in China pose additional complexities in addressing the CVD burden [43]. The evidence presented in our study emphasizes the need for heightened attention and targeted interventions for potential premature death among hypertension patients residing at various altitudes, ultimately contributing to a reduction in premature deaths due to NCDs. It is recommended that healthcare providers conduct longitudinal examinations to assess the prognosis of hypertension in individuals living at high altitudes. Furthermore, proactive efforts in conducting lifestyle-related health education and awareness campaigns are crucial to increasing the population’s understanding of environmental factors that influence hypertension and CVD risk, especially in regions where these health issues are prevalent among high-altitude residents.

There are several limitations of this study that need to be considered. Firstly, there is a possibility that a small fraction of participants may have relocated between different altitude groups during the follow-up period, introducing the potential for bias. However, we mitigated this potential confounding by cross-validating the information using patients’ household registration and current address registration details. Secondly, certain crucial biological measures and behavioral lifestyle variables were not accessible for our participants at the baseline due to resource constraints in our cohort study, particularly in the context of this less developed province. Thirdly, our study only provides epidemiological clues to the association between altitude and outcome, but cannot demonstrate the mechanisms and the pathophysiological evidence for the association. Future research endeavors should aim to incorporate a broader range of confounding factors, and more extended prospective cohort studies are essential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the long-term outcomes associated with residential high-altitude exposure in hypertensive patients.

Conclusions

Prolonged residence at high altitude significantly increases the risk of subsequent all-cause mortality and CVD death in hypertensive patients. This study emphasizes that relevant guidelines and healthcare providers should consider altitude as a potential factor in reducing the burden of hypertension and CVD, especially in areas where hypertension and CVD risk factors are prevalent at high altitude.
