Distinct theta and alpha electrophysiological dynamics during audiovisual semantic processing in healthy aging and mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Jessica Ip, William C. Heindel, Brian R. Ott, Elena K. Festa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Semantic memory decline is increasingly recognized as an early feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI), yet the neural dynamics supporting automatic and controlled semantic retrieval in healthy aging remain poorly defined. This study examined task-evoked oscillatory activity during audiovisual object recognition in young adults (YA; N = 27), healthy older adults (OA; N = 33), and individuals with amnestic MCI (N = 21). Participants judged object orientation while viewing living and nonliving images paired with congruent or incongruent characteristic sounds, allowing semantic relationships to be manipulated under implicit retrieval demands. Accuracy was high across groups, although participants with MCI showed reduced performance under the more perceptually challenging inverted conditions. Reaction times were slower in OA than YA and further slowed in MCI, with group differences varying by object animacy and semantic congruency. Event-related spectral perturbation analyses revealed distinct group-related patterns. Healthy older adults showed reduced early and increased late theta activity in frontocentral and parieto-occipital regions, consistent with delayed recruitment of control-related and perceptual-attentional processes. In contrast, MCI participants showed elevated and less condition-sensitive posterior alpha power, together with task- and condition-specific differences in frontocentral theta. The principal pattern of delayed theta recruitment in healthy aging and elevated posterior alpha in MCI was also observed in a supplementary task requiring explicit audiovisual semantic judgments. These findings provide preliminary evidence that healthy aging and amnestic MCI are associated with partly distinct patterns of task-evoked oscillatory activity during audiovisual semantic processing.

Introduction

While impaired episodic memory is a well-established hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD), semantic memory impairments are also present in the earliest stages of the disease [1] and in amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a clinically heterogeneous condition associated with an increased risk of progression to AD [2], [3]. Deficits occur across a range of semantic tasks including object naming [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11] and category fluency [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], both of which have been linked to degradation of the semantic network [17], [18], [19]. Importantly, recent studies suggest that semantic memory deficits may even precede episodic memory loss in AD, raising the possibility that disruptions in semantic processing may be detectable early in the disease course [2], [20], [21].

Semantic cognition relies on distributed networks within frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital regions that support both the storage and retrieval of conceptual knowledge [22], [23], [24]. The semantic memory impairments exhibited by AD patients may arise from multiple disruptions within these networks. First, early pathological changes to the perirhinal and entorhinal cortices [25] may degrade object-level semantic representations. These regions support the integration of cross-modal features that uniquely define individual objects, and their integrity is critical for maintaining semantic distinctions [26], [27], [28], [29]. In healthy adults, the perirhinal cortex is implicated in object-specific semantic encoding [30], [31], and performance on object recognition tasks associated with perirhinal and entorhinal function has also been shown to predict conversion from MCI to AD [32].

Second, impaired semantic memory in AD likely arises from disruptions to large-scale corticocortical interactions that support the integration of semantic information across distributed cortical networks [33]. Neuropathological studies reveal systematic AD-related disruption of long-range projections linking functionally related cortical regions [25], [34], [35], [36], [37], and neuroimaging studies similarly demonstrate functional and structural connectivity alterations beginning in prodromal and even preclinical stages of the disease [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46]. These network-level disruptions affect multiple cortical regions implicated in semantic processing [47], [48], [49] and are related to disease progression [49], [50], [51]. Behavioral evidence further suggests that such network disruption may affect audiovisual integration differently in healthy aging and MCI. Using a McGurk paradigm involving both visual and auditory speech information, Festa et al. [52] found that healthy older adults showed enhanced visual influence when bottom-up audiovisual support was weak, whereas participants with amnestic MCI showed reduced audiovisual integration and greater reliance on top-down lexical information under the same conditions. This dissociation suggests that MCI-related audiovisual deficits are not simply an amplification of normal age-related changes, but emerge when cross-modal integration tasks place greater demands on effective interactions among distributed cortical regions.

Third, semantic deficits in AD may reflect impairments in controlled retrieval, which becomes particularly important when stimulus-driven activation is insufficient to access stored semantic information [24], [53]. Neuropsychological and neuroimaging studies have consistently implicated the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex in supporting the controlled retrieval of knowledge representations stored in posterior temporal regions [54], [55]. Structural changes within frontotemporal white matter pathways have been linked to semantic memory performance in prodromal AD [56] and shown to predict conversion to clinical AD [57]. Consistent with these findings, the semantic memory impairments observed in AD patients are also suggestive of disruptions to controlled retrieval [58], [59]. Specifically, AD patients perform poorly on semantic memory tests that place high demands on controlled retrieval, such as category fluency [60], yet show relatively intact performance on tasks with lower retrieval demands [61]. Deficits in attentional control further distinguish very mild AD from healthy aging [62] and predict conversion from healthy aging to AD [63]. Most notably, performance on a task combining attentional control and semantic retrieval has been associated with AD biomarker status in cognitively asymptomatic individuals [64].

Despite converging evidence for semantic processing deficits in MCI and early AD [33], [65], it remains unclear how the dynamic interaction between automatic and controlled semantic retrieval processes is altered in these populations. Distinguishing whether these difficulties reflect reduced bottom-up integration, impaired top-down controlled retrieval, or both requires methods with sufficient temporal resolution to separate early and later stages of processing.

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides millisecond-level temporal resolution, which makes it well suited for tracking the dynamics of semantic retrieval [66]. Event-related spectral perturbations (ERSPs) index changes in oscillatory power across time and frequency, with activity in different frequency bands associated with partially distinct neurocognitive functions [67], [68], [69]. Unlike traditional event-related potentials (ERPs), ERSPs incorporate both phase-locked and non-phase-locked neural activity, providing a complementary view of complex event-related brain dynamics across cortical regions [67], [70].

Neural oscillations are also considered a key mechanism for coordinating cross-talk between distributed cortical regions during cognitive processing [71]. Theta power (4–7 Hz) measured over frontocentral regions has been associated with cognitive control and conflict monitoring [67], whereas posterior theta has been linked to attentional selection and perceptual processing [72]. Age-related reductions in the efficiency of top-down control [73] may therefore be accompanied by altered timing or increased recruitment of posterior attentional mechanisms. In contrast, individuals with MCI may show a different pattern of posterior theta modulation, consistent with behavioral evidence of altered attentional selection and semantic processing in this group [74], [75].

Similarly, posterior alpha oscillations (8–12 Hz) have been associated with selective attention, perceptual processing, semantic integration, and inhibitory control [66], [76], [77], [78]. According to established accounts of alpha function, decreases in alpha power (alpha suppression/desynchronization) reflect reduced functional inhibition and increased engagement of task-relevant cortical processing, whereas increases in alpha power (alpha enhancement/synchronization) reflect greater inhibition of task-irrelevant processing or increased attentional gating [76]. In the context of semantic object processing, alpha suppression may accompany increased perceptual-semantic activation as evidence accumulates, whereas alpha synchronization may reflect greater regulation of competing or task-irrelevant information. Healthy aging may therefore be associated with increased posterior recruitment when top-down control is less efficient [79]. In contrast, reduced alpha suppression in MCI may reflect less efficient modulation of posterior processing during semantic retrieval. Together, these properties make ERSP measures well suited for examining age- and MCI-related differences in the temporal dynamics of semantic object processing.

A number of studies have demonstrated changes in resting-state EEG spectral activity in MCI and AD patients relative to healthy controls, most consistently showing increased theta and reduced alpha and beta power [80], [81], [82], [83], [84], [85], [86], [87]. By contrast, few studies have examined task-evoked spectral dynamics during semantic processing in these patient groups. In a semantic Go/NoGo task requiring inhibitory control, Nguyen et al. [78] reported reduced frontal and parietal theta power during NoGo trials in participants with MCI compared to healthy older adults, suggesting impaired semantic inhibitory control. Interestingly, they also found increased low-frequency parietal alpha power (decreased alpha suppression) during Go trials in participants with MCI, a condition placing relatively limited demands on response inhibition. Whereas resting-state studies typically report reduced alpha in MCI and AD, task-evoked increases may reveal subtle differences that emerge only under semantic retrieval demands. Using the same paradigm, Lydon et al. [66] observed that participants with late-stage MCI displayed greater central theta power than participants with early-stage MCI during Go trials despite similar behavioral performance. Moreover, participants with early-stage MCI, but not late-stage MCI, showed greater theta and lower alpha power (greater alpha suppression) for NoGo relative to Go trials, suggesting stage-related differences in recruitment of top-down and bottom-up mechanisms.

Successful object recognition depends on the quality of the perceptual input and on the balance between bottom-up stimulus-driven integration and top-down controlled retrieval processes needed to access the stored semantic representation [88]. When objects are presented in canonical orientations and accompanied by congruent semantic information, identification can rely more heavily on stimulus-driven processes. In contrast, noncanonical views or incongruent cross-modal information may increase demands on attentional selection and controlled retrieval processes to resolve semantic representations [79]. It remains unclear, however, how the dynamic interplay between these processes is altered in healthy aging and MCI. Clarifying how the two groups respond to perceptual atypicality and audiovisual semantic conflict is therefore an important next step.

To address this question, we recorded task-evoked oscillatory activity during audiovisual object recognition in young adults, healthy older adults, and participants with amnestic MCI. We examined theta and alpha power over frontocentral and posterior regions to characterize age- and MCI-related differences in the timing and modulation of processes associated with cognitive control, attention, and perceptual-semantic processing. Perceptual support was manipulated through object orientation, with canonical upright views contrasted with atypical inverted views, while audiovisual semantic relationships were manipulated through congruent and incongruent sound-object pairings. We also varied animacy (living vs. nonliving), given evidence that animate objects rely more on perceptual features than inanimate objects [33]. Because the primary orientation-discrimination task assessed semantic processing indirectly, we additionally examined an explicit version of the paradigm in which participants directly judged whether the auditory and visual stimuli were semantically congruent. This supplementary analysis tested whether the principal oscillatory patterns generalized across incidental and intentional semantic evaluation.

We predicted that healthy older adults would show reduced early frontocentral theta relative to young adults, consistent with age-related reductions in the efficiency of cognitive control [89]. We also predicted greater posterior alpha suppression in older than young adults, reflecting increased recruitment of posterior processing. Within groups, we expected stronger alpha suppression for upright than inverted objects and for semantically congruent than incongruent sound-object pairings, reflecting more efficient perceptual-semantic processing when stimulus support was strong. For participants with MCI, we predicted higher posterior alpha power (or reduced alpha suppression) compared to healthy older adults, consistent with less efficient modulation of posterior processing. Finally, we predicted increased posterior theta power in healthy older adults, reflecting greater late attentional recruitment, but reduced posterior theta activity in participants with MCI, consistent with altered attentional selection. The supplementary explicit task was expected to reproduce the principal age- and MCI-related theta and alpha patterns if they generalized beyond incidental semantic processing to explicit semantic retrieval.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirty-two young adults (YA), 41 cognitively healthy older adults (OA), and 33 older adults with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI) were enrolled. YA participants were recruited from the Brown University student community, while OA and MCI participants were recruited from the Rhode Island community and the Rhode Island Hospital Alzheimer's Disease and Memory Disorders Center (ADMDC) as part of a neurofeedback training study. The present data were collected at baseline, before any training. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Brown University and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. Participants provided written informed consent and received monetary compensation.

Inclusion criteria were normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing and no history of psychiatric or neurological illness, learning disability, or head injury. MCI participants underwent detailed neurological, neuropsychological, and medical evaluations at the ADMDC and met Petersen and colleagues' criteria for amnestic MCI [90], [91]. All participants completed a medical history questionnaire. Older participants also completed the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS; Long Form; [92]). Only OA and MCI participants with GDS scores below the severe range (<19) were included. Cognitive status in OA and MCI groups was assessed using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS; [93]) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; [94]). OA participants were required to have RBANS total scores above the 25th percentile (scaled score ≥ 90).

Participants were excluded if accuracy was below 75% in more than half of the experimental conditions or if fewer than eight artifact-free EEG epochs remained in any condition after preprocessing. A total of 25 participants were excluded: 1 participant with MCI for behavioral performance, 21 participants (5 YA, 7 OA, and 9 MCI) for insufficient artifact-free epochs, 2 participants with MCI for technical issues, and 1 OA participant for an administrative error. The final sample comprised 27 YA, 33 OA, and 21 MCI participants.

Stimuli and procedure

The experiment employed 64 color pictures and 64 corresponding audio clips depicting living and nonliving objects. Pictures were obtained through targeted web searches and selected to depict single objects (e.g., wolf, vacuum cleaner) in isolation on homogeneous backgrounds. Each exemplar was chosen because it was a prototypical representation of its object category, had a strongly associated and identifiable characteristic sound, and was perceptually distinct from the other exemplar while still being a strong member of the object category. Images were edited for consistency, resized to 250 × 250 pixels, and presented centrally on a black background for 2500 ms on a 19-in. ViewSonic PS-790 monitor (1024 × 768 pixels; 100 Hz refresh). Half of the images depicted living objects and half nonliving objects, with two exemplars of each object. All images were either in the public domain or used under fair use for research purposes.

Auditory stimuli consisted of 1-s recordings of characteristic object sounds (e.g., wolf howl, vacuum cleaner), obtained from a commercial sound library (https://www.sound-effects-library.com/). Each sound was selected because it was a prototypical, highly recognizable exemplar of its object and carried a strong and distinctive association with the corresponding visual exemplars. Sounds were drawn from a larger pool based on a pilot study (N = 50 young adults) in which only those identified with greater than 90% accuracy and with mean confidence ratings above 4 (on a 1–5 scale) were retained. Two audio exemplars per object were included, yielding 64 clips. All clips were normalized and edited with 100 ms linear fade-in and fade-out ramps to minimize onset and offset artifacts, then presented at approximately 60 dB through computer speakers. An additional set of eight images and sounds was created for practice, following the same selection criteria and format.

The task was programmed in E-Prime 2.0 (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA) and administered at a viewing distance of approximately 70 cm. On each trial, an audio clip began 150 ms before picture onset and continued during picture presentation. The picture and sound formed either a semantically congruent pairing (e.g., a wolf paired with a howl) or an incongruent pairing that crossed animacy categories (e.g., a living object paired with a nonliving object sound, such as a wolf paired with a fire engine; Fig. 1). Across trials, pictures were presented in either the upright or inverted orientation, and participants indicated whether the picture was upright or inverted within 2500 ms by pressing one of two labeled buttons on a Serial Response Box (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA). Participants were told that the audio sound was irrelevant to the task. Each trial was followed by a jittered interstimulus interval (ISI) in which a blank black screen was presented for 800, 1000, or 1200 ms (randomized across trials).Fig. 1Task schematic. After 16 practice trials (with feedback), participants completed four experimental blocks of 32 trials each (no feedback). Within each block, half of the pictures depicted living objects and half nonliving objects, and half were upright while half were inverted. Each image was shown both upright and inverted across the four blocks, and each audio clip was presented in both congruent and incongruent pairings. For example, one wolf image and one wolf sound appeared in each block; in two blocks they were paired congruently, while in the other two blocks they were paired with unrelated items. Stimulus order was pseudorandomized with the constraints that no more than four congruent trials occurred in succession, and the same exemplar was never repeated within a block. Across the experiment, each image was presented four times (twice upright, twice inverted) and each audio clip was heard four times (twice congruent, twice incongruent). Two counterbalanced versions of the task (A and B) were created. In Version A, a given picture was paired with a congruent audio clip in two blocks and an incongruent clip in the other two blocks, whereas in Version B the assignments were reversed. Participants were randomly assigned to either Version A or Version B, with group assignments approximately balanced. Reaction time and accuracy were recorded on each trial.

EEG data acquisition and processing

Continuous EEG was recorded from 64 Ag/AgCl electrodes mounted in an electrode cap (Quick-cap, Neuromedical Supplies, Inc.), arranged according to the International 10–20 system. Vertical and horizontal eye movements were monitored with electrooculography (EOG) using bipolar electrodes placed on the outer canthi (horizontal EOG) and above and below each eye (vertical EOG). Signals were acquired with a Pegasus Digital EEG system (EMS Inc., Korneuburg, Austria) at a sampling rate of 512 Hz, using a gain of 46 K and an online bandpass of 0.01–100 Hz, and were referenced online to the linked mastoids. Each electrode was filled with electro-gel (Electro-Cap International, Inc.) to maintain impedances below 10 kΩ throughout the recording session.

Offline preprocessing was performed in EEGLAB v2022.1 [70] running in MATLAB R2022a. Data were high-pass filtered at 0.1 Hz and low-pass filtered at 50 Hz using the firfilt plugin (v2.7.1; [95]). Line noise was removed using CleanLine [96]. Bad channels identified via visual inspection of the continuous raw EEG data were interpolated (Range = 0–10; YA: M = 2.07, SD = 2.73; OA: M = 5.79, SD = 2.36; MCI: M = 2.64, SD = 2.68). Signals were then re-referenced to the average reference. Independent component analysis (ICA) was performed using Picard [97] to remove ocular and muscle artifacts. Data were segmented into epochs from −700 ms to +1000 ms, relative to auditory onset and baseline corrected using the 300 ms interval immediately preceding auditory onset with binning functions from ERPLAB (v9.0) [98], and inspected manually. Trials with voltage exceeding ±150 μV were removed. Time-frequency analyses were computed using EEGLAB's ‘pop_newtimef’ function (wavelet cycles [3 0.8], padding ratio = 1, 100 output frequencies, 4–50 Hz). Spectral power was baseline-normalized by division and expressed in decibels (dB).

Data analysis

Behavioral performance was assessed using accuracy and reaction time (RT). Accuracy was calculated as the proportion of correct responses in each condition and compared between groups using Mann-Whitney U tests with Holm correction. RTs were averaged across correct trials for each orientation (upright, inverted), animacy (living, nonliving), and semantic congruency (congruent, incongruent) condition and analyzed with three-way mixed ANOVAs for healthy aging comparisons (YA vs. OA) and three-way mixed ANCOVAs for MCI-related comparisons (OA vs. MCI), with age and education included as covariates. For both healthy and MCI-related analyses, upright and inverted trials were modeled separately. Significant effects were examined with simple-effects analyses and pairwise comparisons using estimated marginal means from the ‘emmeans’ R package [99].

ERSP measures were extracted from theta (4–7 Hz) and alpha (8–12 Hz) frequency bands within two electrode clusters selected based on prior work linking frontocentral activity to cognitive control and parieto-occipital activity to attentional and perceptual processing: a frontocentral (FC) cluster comprising FC1, FCz, FC2, C1, Cz, and C2, and a parieto-occipital (PO) cluster comprising PO3, POz, PO4, O1, Oz, and O2. For each frequency band and cluster, mean spectral power was computed within early (100–300 ms) and late (300–500 ms) windows following image onset, based on scalp topographies of maximal band-specific activity (Figs. 3B–5B) and prior findings of age- and MCI-related differences [78]. Four-way mixed ANOVAs (YA vs. OA) and mixed ANCOVAs (OA vs. MCI with age and education as covariates) were conducted with Group, Animacy, Congruency, and Time window as factors. Upright and inverted trials were again analyzed in separate models. Significant interactions were followed with simple-effects analyses and pairwise comparisons using estimated marginal means.

Group differences in demographics were evaluated using Welch's t-tests for age and education and χ2 tests for sex with Cramer's V as the effect size. All analyses were conducted in RStudio (v2025.05.1) and JASP (v0.95; JASP [100]), and figures were generated with custom scripts using RStudio and MATLAB. An a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1; [101], [102]) indicated that a minimum of 16 participants were required to detect a medium effect size (Cohen's f = 0.25, [103]) with 80% power for the mixed factorial designs.

Behavioral reaction times

Effects of healthy aging (YA vs. OA)

Alpha and Theta dynamics during explicit Audiovisual semantic evaluation

The primary task examined audiovisual object processing under incidental semantic conditions, as participants judged whether pictures were upright or inverted while the semantic relationship between auditory and visual stimuli was task irrelevant. To determine whether the principal oscillatory patterns generalized to intentional semantic evaluation, we additionally analyzed a version of the task in which participants (Table S1) judged whether auditory and visual object pairings were semantically congruent or incongruent (see Supplementary Materials). As in the primary task, object orientation and audiovisual congruency were manipulated, but the explicit task required direct evaluation of the semantic relationship between the sound and image.

Behavioral results showed a graded slowing across groups, with younger adults responding fastest, followed by healthy older adults and participants with MCI (Fig. S1). This pattern was observed for both upright and inverted objects. Under inverted conditions, responses were slower for congruent than incongruent trials. Accuracy remained high for all groups and conditions, although participants with MCI exhibited relatively lower accuracy compared to OA for upright living-incongruent trials (Table S2). Compared with the implicit task, animacy effects on reaction time were less pronounced in the explicit task.

The principal frontocentral theta pattern was similar across tasks. Young adults exhibited greater early than late theta power, whereas healthy older adults showed relatively greater late theta recruitment, particularly under inverted conditions (Fig. S2). However, unlike in the implicit task, participants with MCI did not show congruency-related frontocentral theta modulation. Parieto-occipital theta also showed greater late-window activity in healthy older adults (Fig. S3). Posterior theta did not differ reliably between healthy older adults and participants with MCI, indicating broadly similar temporal modulation in the two older groups during explicit semantic evaluation. Posterior alpha provided the clearest convergence across tasks. Healthy older adults exhibited a reduction in alpha power from the early to late time window, whereas participants with MCI showed sustained elevation of posterior alpha and reduced early-to-late alpha suppression (Fig. S4).

Overall, the explicit task reproduced the principal group-related oscillatory patterns observed in the implicit task. Across both paradigms, healthy aging was associated with a temporal shift toward later theta activity and preserved posterior alpha suppression, whereas MCI was associated with elevated posterior alpha. The primary difference across tasks involved frontocentral theta, as congruency-related modulation in MCI was observed in the implicit task but not in the explicit task. Thus, the broad group differences generalized across incidental and intentional semantic evaluation although condition-specific behavioral and theta effects varied with task demands.

Discussion

The present study examined how healthy aging and amnestic MCI are associated with changes in the balance between top-down controlled retrieval and bottom-up perceptual-semantic integration during audiovisual object recognition. We measured frontocentral and parieto-occipital theta and alpha power, along with behavioral reaction times and accuracy, while manipulating object orientation, audiovisual semantic congruency, and animacy. We expected healthy aging to be characterized by slowed processing, reduced efficiency of cognitive control, and greater recruitment of posterior processing. For participants with MCI, we predicted reduced posterior alpha suppression and altered posterior theta activity, consistent with less efficient bottom-up modulation of perceptual-semantic processing and attentional selection.

Our findings indicate that healthy aging and MCI are associated with partly distinct behavioral and oscillatory patterns. Healthy older adults were as accurate as young adults across conditions but responded more slowly overall. They also displayed the expected sensitivity to stimulus conditions, including slower responses to nonliving than living objects and, in several conditions, slower responses to incongruent than congruent pairings. In contrast, the ERSP findings revealed marked age-related differences in the timing of frontocentral and parieto-occipital theta activity. Young adults showed greater early frontocentral theta, whereas healthy older adults exhibited reduced early theta followed by greater late frontocentral and parieto-occipital theta. This temporal shift may reflect slower or delayed engagement of control-related and perceptual-attentional processes in healthy aging. By contrast, the early-to-late reduction in parieto-occipital alpha was largely comparable in young and healthy older adults, suggesting that the modulation of posterior processing remained relatively preserved. Taken together, the combination of delayed theta recruitment and preserved posterior alpha suppression may represent an adaptive pattern that supports accurate performance despite age-related slowing.

Participants with amnestic MCI showed a different profile. Behaviorally, they were slower than healthy older adults and showed reduced accuracy in several inverted conditions. Their reaction time effects also varied across orientation, animacy, and congruency rather than reflecting only generalized slowing. Frontocentral theta showed condition-specific modulation with greater theta for incongruent than congruent objects during upright trials, but no comparable conflict-related increase during inverted trials. This pattern may indicate top-down control-related processes can still be recruited in MCI when perceptual information is relatively intact but are less flexibly engaged when perceptual demands increase. Parieto-occipital theta was broadly comparable to that of healthy older adults, suggesting relative preservation of the late posterior theta response. In contrast, parieto-occipital alpha was elevated and less selectively modulated in MCI, with broadly higher alpha under inverted conditions and a particularly pronounced group difference for congruent living objects presented in the canonical upright orientation. Because alpha suppression has been associated with reduced functional inhibition and increased engagement of task-relevant posterior processing, the elevated alpha observed in MCI may reflect less efficient release from inhibitory gating as semantic information becomes available.

The principal oscillatory patterns also appeared in the supplementary task requiring explicit judgments of audiovisual semantic congruency. Healthy older adults again showed a temporal shift toward later theta activity while retaining posterior alpha suppression, whereas participants with MCI showed sustained posterior alpha elevation. This convergence across tasks suggests the broad group differences were related to audiovisual semantic processing per se rather than solely to the perceptual or response demands of the orientation task. However, condition-specific frontocentral theta and behavioral effects differed across paradigms, indicating that retrieval mode influenced how these broad patterns were expressed.

Overall, healthy aging was characterized by delayed but potentially adaptive theta recruitment in the context of relatively preserved posterior alpha suppression. Amnestic MCI, in contrast, was characterized most consistently by elevated and less condition-sensitive posterior alpha, together with more context-dependent frontocentral theta modulation. One possible interpretation is that control-related processes remain available in MCI but become increasingly taxed when posterior mechanisms fail to efficiently regulate perceptual-semantic input. This interpretation remains tentative, however, because scalp-recorded oscillatory activity cannot uniquely identify the underlying neural mechanisms.

Alpha and Theta differences in semantic processing in healthy aging

Alpha suppression, reflected in lower alpha power, has been associated with reduced functional inhibition and greater engagement of task-relevant cognitive processing, whereas increased alpha power has been linked to greater inhibition of task-irrelevant activity or increased attentional gating [76], [104]. Within semantic and perceptual tasks, alpha suppression (desynchronization) has been interpreted as increased engagement of posterior processing during access to well-established representations, whereas alpha synchronization has been observed when retrieval places greater demands on internally-directed or top-down processing [76]. Consistent with this framework, divergent thinking tasks that require access to loosely integrated representations elicit alpha synchronization [76], [105], [106], while working memory and perceptual processing tasks commonly elicit alpha suppression as task-relevant cortical regions become engaged [76], [104], [107], [108].

In the present study, young and healthy older adults showed comparable reductions in parieto-occipital alpha power from the early to late time window across upright and inverted viewing conditions. This pattern was also observed in the supplementary task requiring explicit audiovisual semantic judgments. The convergence across incidental and intentional semantic evaluation suggests that posterior alpha modulation remained relatively preserved in healthy aging and was not limited to the response demands of the orientation-discrimination task. Preserved alpha suppression is consistent with continued engagement of bottom-up perceptual-semantic processing in healthy older adults. Thus, the age-related differences observed here appear to involve the timing and coordination of neural activity more than a generalized failure to modulate posterior alpha.

Age-related differences were more pronounced in frontocentral theta. Young adults showed greater early frontocentral, whereas healthy older adults showed reduced early followed by greater late theta. Frontocentral theta has been associated with cognitive control, conflict monitoring, and the coordination of task-relevant processing [67], [68], [69]. Young adults also showed greater condition-specific modulation under inversion, with greater theta for congruent than incongruent nonliving objects and for nonliving than living objects under congruent conditions. Healthy older adults showed no reliable condition-specific modulation, but exhibited greater late theta activity. This temporal shift is consistent with delayed or more reactive engagement of control-related processing in healthy aging, in line with evidence for reduced proactive control and greater reliance on later reactive control processes with age [109], as well as broader age-related slowing in the timing of cognitive operations [110], [111]. A similar shift toward later frontocentral theta was observed in the explicit semantic task, particularly under inverted viewing, suggesting that the altered timing of theta recruitment generalized across incidental and intentional retrieval demands.

Parieto-occipital theta showed a corresponding age-related shift toward later activity. Healthy older adults exhibited greater late than early posterior theta, whereas young adults showed greater early than late theta for upright objects and no reliable temporal modulation under inversion. Healthy older adults also showed greater posterior theta for nonliving than living objects under inverted viewing, suggesting increased category sensitivity when canonical perceptual information was disrupted [79]. Because posterior theta has been associated with attentional selection and perceptual processing, the greater late theta observed in older adults may reflect additional or prolonged recruitment of posterior attentional mechanisms as object recognition unfolds. This interpretation is supported by the explicit task, in which healthy older adults again showed increased late parieto-occipital theta. This later activity may reflect compensatory recruitment, although confirming that interpretation would require evidence that greater theta was associated with better performance.

Taken together, healthy aging was characterized by preserved posterior alpha suppression but a redistribution of frontocentral and parieto-occipital theta activity from earlier to later processing stages. One interpretation is that healthy older adults maintain effective perceptual-semantic processing while relying on slower or more prolonged recruitment of control-related and posterior attentional mechanisms. The recurrence of this temporal pattern across implicit and explicit tasks, alongside preserved accuracy, is consistent with an adaptive response to age-related slowing.

Alpha and Theta differences in semantic processing in MCI

In contrast to the preserved parieto-occipital alpha suppression observed in healthy aging, participants with MCI showed elevated and less condition-sensitive posterior alpha. During inverted viewing, alpha was broadly higher in participants with MCI than in healthy older adults, whereas under upright viewing the clearest group difference emerged for congruent living objects presented in their canonical orientation. A similar pattern was observed in the explicit semantic task, in which participants with MCI showed sustained posterior alpha elevation and reduced early-to-late alpha suppression. The convergence in findings across incidental and intentional semantic evaluation suggests that altered posterior alpha modulation in MCI was not specific to the perceptual or response demands of the orientation-discrimination task but rather was associated with dynamic changes in audiovisual semantic processing.

Increased alpha power has been associated with greater functional inhibition or attentional gating, whereas alpha suppression is generally linked to greater engagement of task-relevant posterior processing. Within this framework, the elevated alpha observed in MCI may reflect less efficient release from top-down inhibitory gating as semantic information becomes available. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies reporting reduced task-evoked alpha suppression in MCI during semantic Go/NoGo tasks [66], [78], including conditions placing relatively limited demands on response inhibition. The present findings extend this pattern to audiovisual object recognition and suggest that elevated posterior alpha may provide a useful measure of altered semantic processing dynamics in MCI.

Parieto-occipital theta showed a different pattern. Participants with MCI generally exhibited early-to-late increases in posterior theta that were comparable to those observed in healthy older adults, and no reliable group difference emerged in the explicit task. This relative preservation suggests that late posterior attentional or perceptual processing remained available in MCI, even when semantic relationships had to be evaluated directly. At the same time, condition-specific animacy effects differed between groups, indicating that the modulation of posterior theta was not entirely preserved across all stimulus conditions.

The combination of relatively preserved posterior theta and altered posterior alpha may also help explain earlier behavioral findings from our group. Festa et al. [52] found that participants with amnestic MCI integrated audiovisual speech normally when bottom-up support was strong but showed reduced integration and greater top-down lexical influence when bottom-up support was weak. The present findings raise the possibility that altered posterior inhibitory modulation becomes especially consequential when perceptual-semantic input is ambiguous and greater top-down support is required.

More pronounced group differences emerged in frontocentral theta. During upright trials in the implicit task, participants with MCI, but not healthy older adults, showed greater frontocentral theta for incongruent than congruent pairings. Under inverted viewing, however, healthy older adults showed a late increase in theta for incongruent objects, whereas participants with MCI showed no reliable modulation by time or congruency. This pattern may indicate that top-down control-related processes can still be recruited in MCI when perceptual information is relatively intact, but are less flexibly modulated when perceptual demands increase. The absence of congruency-related frontocentral theta modulation in the explicit task further suggests that this effect depended on task context rather than reflecting a stable response to semantic conflict.

Prior studies have also reported altered theta activity in MCI, although the direction and distribution of effects have varied across paradigms. Nguyen et al. [78] and Lydon et al. [66], for example, observed attenuated frontal or parietal theta during NoGo trials requiring response inhibition during semantic categorization. These differences may reflect distinct task demands. Go/NoGo paradigms emphasize response withholding and inhibition, whereas the present tasks manipulated audiovisual congruency, animacy, and object orientation under semantic evaluation. Together, the findings suggest that theta abnormalities in MCI may be context dependent, varying with the extent to which a task requires conflict detection, response inhibition, perceptual disambiguation, or sustained semantic evaluation.

Across both tasks, posterior alpha provided the most consistent distinction between participants with MCI and healthy older adults. In contrast, frontocentral theta effects were more variable and depended on orientation and retrieval mode, while posterior theta was broadly similar across the older groups. One possible interpretation is that control-related processes remain available but become less effective when posterior inhibitory regulation is inefficient. This interpretation is consistent with prior evidence of impaired semantic control in AD [1], [112], [113]. The convergence of the present findings across implicit and explicit tasks therefore suggests that altered posterior alpha modulation may be a more stable feature of semantic object processing in MCI than condition-specific theta responses. These results provide preliminary evidence that amnestic MCI is associated with altered oscillatory dynamics during semantic processing, extending beyond the episodic-memory deficits more commonly emphasized in early cognitive decline.

Potential contributions of cholinergic and inhibitory dysfunction to alpha dynamics in MCI

Young and healthy older adults showed the expected increase in alpha suppression from the early to the late time window during object recognition. In contrast, participants with MCI showed elevated and less condition-sensitive alpha in the implicit task and sustained elevation in the explicit task. Similar abnormalities in posterior alpha have been observed in individuals with MCI who later progressed to AD [86], suggesting that reduced task-related alpha suppression may reflect early disruption of neural systems involved in attentional and semantic processing in MCI.

One potential mechanism underlying elevated alpha in MCI is cholinergic dysfunction. Degeneration of basal forebrain cholinergic projections reduces acetylcholine availability and disrupts synaptic transmission, attentional modulation, and cortical inhibitory regulation [86], [114]. These changes may alter the balance of excitatory and inhibitory signaling within posterior cortical networks, contributing to less efficient modulation of alpha activity during semantic processing. Within this framework, reduced alpha suppression could reflect an impaired ability to disengage inhibitory gating and recruit task-relevant posterior regions as semantic information becomes available.

Broader alterations in excitatory-inhibitory balance may also contribute to this pattern. Cortical hyperexcitation has been documented in AD and linked to dysregulated GABAergic inhibitory interneuron function and impaired regulation of excitatory and inhibitory activity (E/I balance) [115], [116], [117]. Amyloid-beta and tau may also have partly distinct relationships with network physiology across disease stages. Some evidence suggests relatively greater amyloid-beta burden during MCI and increasing tau burden with progression to AD [118], while amyloid-beta and tau has been differentially associated with inhibitory and excitatory activity, respectively [119]. These findings raise the possibility that early inhibitory dysregulation may contribute to altered alpha synchronization in MCI and later evolve alongside broader network hyperexcitability as the disease progresses [116], [120]. This account remains speculative but provide a plausible framework linking disease-related neurochemical and proteinopathic changes to the altered alpha abnormities observed here.

Taken together, cholinergic disruption and altered excitatory-inhibitory regulation may contribute to elevated posterior alpha observed in MCI. The recurrence of this pattern across implicit and explicit tasks suggests that it may reflect a relatively stable alteration in the modulation of posterior processing during semantic evaluation rather than a task-specific response. Future studies combining task-evoked EEG with measures of cholinergic integrity, amyloid-beta, tau, and longitudinal clinical outcomes will be important for determining whether reduced alpha suppression tracks disease progression or predicts changes in semantic functioning.

Limitations

The present study provided the temporal resolution needed to characterize group-level differences in rapid oscillatory dynamics during semantic processing. Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. First, biomarker data, including measures of amyloid or tau pathology, were not available. The underlying etiology of MCI therefore could not be established, and the observed oscillatory patterns cannot be attributed specifically to AD-related pathology. MCI is etiologically heterogeneous and may arise in association with AD, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), vascular disease, or other neurological processes. Prior work suggests MCI associated with AD and MCI associated with DLB may have distinct electrophysiological profiles. DLB has been associated with reduced posterior cerebral blood flow [121] and slowing in dominant alpha-range frequency [122], [123]. Future studies directly comparing biomarker-characterized MCI subgroups will be needed to determine whether posterior alpha alterations differ across etiologies and whether they are specifically related to AD-associated semantic dysfunction.

Second, the modest sample size and cross-sectional design limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude conclusions about disease progression. In the implicit task, 12 of the 33 enrolled participants with MCI were excluded, primarily because too few artifact-free EEG epochs remained after preprocessing. The explicit task likewise involved a relatively small retained MCI sample and a substantial number of exclusions for insufficient artifact-free EEG data. In each task, only one participant with MCI was excluded because of behavioral accuracy, indicating that attrition was driven mainly by EEG data quality rather than task performance. Nevertheless, these exclusions may have introduced selection bias by favoring participants who were more able to provide usable EEG recordings, and the retained samples may not fully represent the broader clinical population. The small MCI samples also limit the precision and generalizability of the cross-task comparisons. Larger longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether altered alpha modulation or theta timing predict cognitive decline, changes in semantic functioning, or progression to AD-related dementia. In the absence of biomarker and longitudinal results, the present study should be interpreted as preliminary group-level differences rather than validated electrophysiological markers of AD-related semantic dysfunction.

Third, although participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing, sensory function was not assessed objectively. Unmeasured age- or disease-related differences in visual or auditory acuity could have contributed to behavioral or EEG effects. Future studies should include standardized sensory assessments and examine the influence of demographic and clinical factors in larger samples. Such work will be important for determining the robustness, specificity, and clinical relevance of task-evoked oscillatory differences across healthy aging and MCI.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct patterns of task-evoked theta and alpha activity in healthy aging and amnestic MCI during audiovisual object processing. Healthy aging was associated with reduced early and increased late theta activity alongside preserved posterior alpha suppression. Participants with amnestic MCI showed elevated and less condition-sensitive posterior alpha activity together with condition-specific differences in frontocentral theta. The principal pattern of delayed theta activity in healthy aging and elevated posterior alpha in MCI was also observed during explicit audiovisual semantic evaluation, suggesting that these broad group differences generalize across incidental and intentional semantic processing. These findings provide preliminary evidence of altered oscillatory dynamics during audiovisual semantic object processing. Studies incorporating etiological biomarkers, larger samples, and longitudinal follow-up are needed to determine whether these measures have disease-specific or clinical value.
