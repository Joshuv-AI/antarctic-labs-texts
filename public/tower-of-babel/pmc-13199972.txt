Mechanisms of Bifidobacterium animalis J12 in reshaping yogurt flavor homeostasis and alleviating constipation via the “metabolite-nerve-endocrine” framework
Authors: Yajie Zhang, Yu Deng, Xiaodong Song, Yuanhong Xie, Jingyu Gao, Hongxing Zhang, Junhua Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The application of bifidobacteria in fermented dairy products is often limited by insufficient storage stability and unclear mechanistic links between microbial metabolism and host benefits. Here, we investigated the technological performance, aroma homeostasis, and constipation-alleviating mechanism of Bifidobacterium animalis J12 co-fermented yogurt (J12-Y). J12 co-fermentation maintained excellent physicochemical stability during 28 days of refrigeration, with viable counts remaining above 7.0 log CFU/mL and preserving the classical buttery–creamy aroma backbone, as evidenced by stable 2,3-butanedione (OAV ≈ 41–42). In a loperamide-induced constipation mouse model, J12-Y improved bowel function by shortening the time to first black stool (approximately 110 vs. 131 min), increasing fecal water content (approximately 56% vs. 28%), and enhancing intestinal transport rate (approximately 67% vs. 45%) relative to the model group. Mechanistically, J12-Y was associated with a coordinated cascade of metabolic and neuroendocrine events. Fecal metabolomics revealed robust enrichment of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, alongside modulation of indole derivatives and bile acid-related metabolites. These metabolic shifts were accompanied by a pro-motility neuroendocrine profile, characterized by elevated excitatory neurotransmitters and hormones (5-HT, ACh, SP, GAS, MTL) and reduced inhibitory signaling (SS, VIP). Spearman correlation analysis further revealed critical functional links: butyric acid showed strong positive correlations with 5-HT (r = 0.62) and intestinal transport rate (r = 0.80); acetic acid was positively correlated with fecal water content (r = 0.67); and deoxycholic acid exhibited negative correlations with intestinal transport rate (r = −0.82) and 5-HT (r = −0.74). Collectively, these findings support a proposed “metabolite–nerve–endocrine” framework in which J12-Y mediates relief of constipation through integrated microbial metabolic and neuroendocrine modulation. Overall, B. animalis J12 represents a promising adjunct culture that couples aroma stability with constipation-relieving potential through integrated microbial metabolic and neuroendocrine modulation.

Introduction

With increasing consumer health awareness, functional fermented dairy products are favored for their probiotic functions, such as regulating gut flora and enhancing immunity (Gao et al., 2021). Bifidobacterium, a core probiotic in the human gut, is often added to yogurt to impart specific functional attributes (Ma et al., 2025). However, traditional commercial starters (e.g., Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus) usually have limited survival in the gut or their activity easily declines during fermentation and storage (Meng et al., 2024). For instance, Meng et al. (2024) reported that exogenously added probiotics frequently face challenges such as declined activity, flavor deterioration, and textural instability during fermentation and refrigeration (Meng et al., 2024). Therefore, screening new Bifidobacterium strains with excellent fermentation characteristics, storage stability, and the ability to improve host health significantly is of great significance for developing high-quality functional yogurt (Gao et al., 2021).

This study focuses on a strain of Bifidobacterium animalis J12 isolated from the infant gut. The species B. animalis was selected because it is widely used in the food industry with good safety and stability, and it has been shown to survive well in dairy products (Ma et al., 2025). Compared with other bifidobacterial species (e.g., B. longum or B. breve), B. animalis exhibits superior acid and oxygen tolerance, making it particularly suitable for yogurt fermentation and extended refrigerated storage. Previous studies found that J12 can significantly improve hyperglycemia during pregnancy and enhance the immune function of oral ulcers by intervening in the body's immunity (Yang et al., 2022). The central hypothesis of this study is that B. animalis J12 in yogurt may preserve product quality by reshaping the flavor metabolic network (e.g., stabilizing key buttery aroma compounds like 2,3-butanedione) while simultaneously regulating host gut neuroendocrine functions through its metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids, SCFAs), thereby alleviating constipation. This leads us to propose a “metabolite-nerve-endocrine” framework as an integrated working model. Simultaneously, using non-targeted metabolomics and GC-MS, the mechanism by which J12 reshapes the volatile flavor compounds of yogurt is analyzed (Li et al., 2023a). Furthermore, using a loperamide-induced constipation mouse model, the potential mechanism of J12 yogurt in relieving constipation is explored in depth, focusing on levels of key indicators, neuroendocrine regulation, and intestinal metabolites (Yu et al., 2023).

Notably, while some previous studies have suggested that bifidobacteria may improve constipation via gut microbiota modulation, the present work distinguishes itself by integrating product-level flavor stability with host-level neuroendocrine outcomes, and by proposing a unified “metabolite-nerve-endocrine” framework that links specific microbial metabolites (particularly SCFAs) to enteric nervous system signaling. This integrated approach provides a multi-dimensional understanding of how a single probiotic strain can simultaneously influence food quality and host physiology.

Materials and methods

Strain source and yogurt preparation process

Strain source

B. animalis J12 (CGMCC No. 25005) was isolated by our research group from the intestines of breastfed infants under 3 months old. The commercial starter (YoFlex-premium) was from Chr. Hansen, which is a mixed culture mainly containing Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus.

Preparation of yogurt

Pure milk with three different protein contents (2.8 g/100 mL, 3.0 g/100 mL, 3.2 g/100 mL) was heated to 50 °C, mixed with 6% (w/v) white granulated sugar, sterilized in a water bath at 95 °C for 5 min, and cooled to 42 °C for subsequent inoculation. B. animalis J12 was inoculated at 1 × 107 CFU/mL, and the Chr. The Hansen commercial starter was inoculated at 0.004% (v/v). The inoculation amount of J12 (1 × 107 CFU/mL) was based on preliminary optimization of its growth viability and acid-producing activity in milk, ensuring its initial dominance in the co-fermentation system while avoiding excessive acid production that could affect texture. The dosage of the commercial starter (0.004%) was added strictly according to the supplier's recommendation to ensure the stability of basic yogurt fermentation (Chr. Hansen YoFlex premium product manual). The experimental group (J12 group) was co-inoculated with B. animalis J12 (1 × 107 CFU/mL) and Chr. Hansen commercial starter (0.004%). The control group (YoF-P group) was inoculated only with Chr. Hansen commercial starter. After inoculation, they were placed at 42 °C for fermentation. The fermentation endpoint of 70 °T was selected based on the following considerations: this acidity is a common industrial standard for yogurt to achieve a good gel state, where all protein concentrations formed a stable curd at this acidity. Although the time to reach 70 °T varied slightly among different protein contents (2.8%: ∼4.5 h, 3.0%: ∼4.2 h, 3.2%: ∼4.0 h), the same endpoint was used to ensure a consistent baseline acidity for post-ripening and subsequent storage comparisons (Lee and Lucey, 2004). After fermenting for 4.5 h, when the acidity of the yogurt samples reached 70 °T, the yogurt samples were cooled, subsequently post-ripened at 4 °C for 16 h, and stored at 4 °C. Samples were taken at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days during the storage stage to determine indicators related to fermentation characteristics.

Determination of physicochemical indicators and textural properties

The pH values of yogurt samples were measured during different storage periods at 20 ± 3 °C. The acidity was determined according to the national standard GB 5009.239-2016 (Lee and Lucey, 2004). Weigh 5 g of yogurt sample (taken from a 4 °C refrigerator and mixed in advance) into a 250 mL conical flask, add 20 mL of water, mix well, and potentiometrically titrate with sodium hydroxide standard solution to pH 8.3. During titration, stir continuously with a magnetic stirrer and blow nitrogen into the conical flask to prevent the sample from absorbing carbon dioxide from the air. Determination of yogurt viable count

Add one tube of Lithium Mupirocin (5 mg/tube) per 100 mL of MRS solid medium. Take 1 mL of yogurt sample from each group and add it to 9 mL of 0.85% sterile physiological saline to obtain a 10−1 dilution. Prepare a 10-fold serial dilution series. At the appropriate dilution, use the plate count method for counting. After anaerobic culture at 37 °C for 48 h, count the number of colonies.

Determination of yogurt texture

Texture profile analysis (TPA) was performed on yogurt samples from each group at different storage periods. For measurement, 10 g of the sample was weighed and placed under the texture analyzer probe. Measurement parameters: Model TA10, pre-test and test speed 1.0 mm/s, post-test speed 10 mm/s, compression distance 10 mm, trigger force 5 g. The hardness, elasticity (springiness), cohesiveness, adhesiveness, gumminess, and chewiness indicators of each yogurt group were measured (Wang et al., 2019).

Determination of Water Holding Capacity

Determination of Water Holding Capacity (WHC) was performed on yogurt samples at different storage periods. Rheological properties

Rheological property analysis was performed on yogurt samples at different storage periods. The measurement method referred to the literature with slight modifications (Wang et al., 2022). Stir and mix the yogurt sample, and take a certain amount of the yogurt sample to the center of the stage. Measurement conditions: No. 4 rotor, temperature 25 °C, shear rate 0.1-100 rad/s. Measure the changes in shear stress and apparent viscosity of the yogurt with shear rate.

Sensory evaluation of yogurt

After the yogurt fermentation was completed and coagulated, it was placed in a 4 °C refrigerator for post-ripening. After post-ripening was completed, 20 experienced sensory evaluation personnel were organized to perform sensory evaluation on yogurt at different storage periods according to the evaluation criteria in Table S1.

Flavoromics analysis methods

Determination of volatile flavor compounds

Volatile flavor compounds were analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) following a previously described method (Zhao et al., 2023) with modifications.

Solution Preparation: Take 770 μL of n-hexane, put it into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and sequentially add appropriate amounts of C10-C25 commercial mix standard and C26, C27, C28, and C29 n-alkane standards, vortex to mix evenly, to obtain a C10-C29 n-alkane mixed stock solution (50 μg/mL). Take the above stock solution and dilute to 10 μg/mL, and analyze on the machine in the same batch as the samples. Take 1.0 g of sample, place it in a 20 mL headspace vial, add 2.5 μL of internal standard (naphthalene-d8 20 μg/mL) and 4 mL of saturated sodium chloride aqueous solution, and immediately seal the headspace vial. Static Headspace Technique/Solid-phase Microextraction (SPME) Conditions: SPME Arrow Fiber (120 μm, Thermo, divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane, DVB/CAR/PDMS). Samples were equilibrated at 60 °C for 10 min, extracted at 60 °C for 40 min, and desorbed in the injection port for 5 min. These parameters were optimized based on reference (Kang et al., 2024).

Chromatographic Conditions: Yogurt samples were injected into the Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) system in a split mode with a split ratio of 10:1 for analysis, followed by separation on a VF-WAXms capillary column (25 m × 0.25 mm × 0.2 μm, Agilent CP9204) before entering mass spectrometry detection. Inlet temperature 240 °C, carrier gas is high-purity helium, carrier gas flow rate 1.0 mL/min, septum purge flow rate 3 mL/min. Heating program: Initial temperature 40 °C, hold for 0 min, ramp at 8 °C/min to 120 °C, then ramp at 20 °C/min to 230 °C, and maintain for 4.5 min, total run time 20 min. Data Preprocessing: Data matrix obtained through database annotation, peak area normalization performed on data using an internal standard.

Quantification was performed using an internal standard semi-quantitative method based on peak area ratios relative to naphthalene-d8. Method validation results showed good linearity (R2 > 0.99) in the range of 0.1–200 μg/mL for representative compounds, limits of detection (LOD) of 0.01–0.10 μg/mL (S/N ≥ 3), limits of quantification (LOQ) of 0.05–0.30 μg/mL (S/N ≥ 10), intra-day precision RSD ≤9.5%, inter-day RSD ≤14.2%, and recoveries of 82.5%–115.8%. Odor activity values (OAV) were calculated as OAV = Ci/Ti, where Ci is the relative content of the compound (μg/g), and Ti is the odor threshold (μg/g) in water/milk matrix, primarily obtained from Czerny et al. (2008) (Czerny et al., 2008), and from van Gemert (2011) for dairy matrix-specific thresholds. Data processing included peak area normalization using the internal standard and compound identification by database matching. We acknowledge that matrix effects (protein, fat, pH) can influence OAV accuracy; therefore, thresholds determined in simple aqueous systems may not fully reflect the yogurt matrix. This limitation is discussed.

Metabolomics analysis of yogurt

Non-targeted metabolomics analysis was performed according to previously described methods (Kong et al., 2022; Ren et al., 2022). Yogurt samples stored at −80 °C were thawed at 4 °C, vortexed for 1 min to ensure homogeneity, and 100 μL of each sample was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Metabolite extraction and subsequent analysis were carried out using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Data processing and analysis were performed according to established protocols (Li et al., 2022a, 2022b; Zhang et al., 2024; Sah et al., 2015). Quality control (QC) samples were inserted every 5–10 injections to monitor instrument stability, and metabolites with RSD >30% in QC samples were excluded.

Construction of constipation mouse model and indicator detection

All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and have been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing University of Agriculture (approval no. GSCS-2025-006, date: April 10, 2025). The mice in this test were 5-week-old BALB/c (SPF grade) males (Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.). Animals were raised and managed strictly in accordance with the regulations of the Animal Ethics Committee of Beijing University of Agriculture.

Preparation of strains and yogurt

The preparation of yogurt was the same as above. Weigh 20 g of yogurt, add physiological saline to dilute it until the viable count of B. animalis in the yogurt is 5 × 109 CFU/mL. The viable count of J12 in the bacterial powder solution was 5 × 109 CFU/mL. The dose was selected based on the FDA's animal-to-human equivalent dose conversion formula, converting the adult daily recommended probiotic intake (1010 – 1011 CFU/day) to a commonly used effective dose for mouse body weight.

Establishment of constipation mouse model

In this study, 80 5-week-old male BALB/c mice were selected. After 1 week of adaptive feeding, they were randomly divided into 7 groups (Fig. 1): blank control group (n = 8), model group (n = 12), J12 yogurt group (J12-Y, n = 12), YoF-P yogurt group (YoF-P-Y, n = 12), J12 powder group (J12-P, n = 12), YoF-P powder group (YoF-P-P, n = 12), and milk group (n = 12). The sample size for the blank group (n = 8) served as a healthy baseline control, while the intervention groups (n = 12) were designed to account for multiple samplings (serum, feces, tissues) and potential individual differences. Sample size was calculated using G∗Power software (α = 0.05, power = 0.8), which indicated a minimum of 8 mice per group; intervention groups were set to 12 to account for potential attrition. No mice died or were excluded during the experiment. The entire animal experiment, including gavage, indicator measurement, and data analysis, was performed by different researchers who were blinded to group assignments.Fig. 1Animal experimental flow chart.

Animal experimental flow chart.

The experimental process is as follows: Intervention period (2 weeks): Each intervention group was gavaged with the corresponding sample (0.2 mL) daily, and the blank and model groups were gavaged with physiological saline. The bacterial powder concentration was 5 × 109 CFU/mL. Modeling period (1 week): Except for the blank group, each group was gavaged with loperamide hydrochloride (20 mg/kg·body weight, Sigma-Aldrich, CAS No. 34552-83-5) daily to induce constipation, followed by gavage of the corresponding sample 1 h later. The loperamide dose and modeling duration were based on a previously established protocol (Park et al., 2022) and confirmed by preliminary experiments to effectively inhibit intestinal motility and induce constipation-related phenotypes.

After the modeling and intervention phases, activated carbon was administered for transit-related testing. Fecal water content was determined on day 27, time to first black stool on day 28, and small-intestinal propulsion on day 29, with dissection performed 30 min after activated carbon administration on day 29.

Collection of mouse feces and determination of water content

On the morning of the 27th day of the experiment, before gavage, fresh feces were collected into weighed 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. The wet weight of the feces was recorded before gavage. The feces were vacuum freeze-dried, sealed, preserved, and weighed, recorded as fecal dry weight. Calculated by the following formula:(3)FecalWaterContent(%)=(FecalWetWeight−FecalDryWeight)/FecalWetWeight×100%

Determination of first black stool time

On the morning of the 28th day, after normal gavage, each mouse was placed individually in a cage lined with filter paper, and the start time was recorded. After the mouse excreted the first black stool, timing ended. The interval between the two times is the first black stool time. Mice were fasted for 16 h with free access to water before the experiment.

Determination of small intestinal propulsion rate

On the morning of the 29th day, 1 h after gavage of the sample, all groups were gavaged with activated carbon dilution solution (10% activated charcoal and 5% gum arabic in physiological saline, activated charcoal and gum arabic were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich). After accurately timing for 30 min, the mice were sacrificed. The abdominal cavity of the mouse was dissected, and the small intestine was cut from the upper end of the pylorus to the lower end of the cecum. The total length of the small intestine was measured. The length from the pylorus to the beginning of the activated carbon solution was recorded as the “activated carbon solution propulsion length”. Calculate the small intestinal propulsion rate by the following formula:

Small Intestinal Propulsion Rate (%) = Activated Carbon Solution Propulsion Length/Total Length of Small Intestine × 100% (4).

This method was based on the classic charcoal meal test (Williams et al., 2016).

Determination of serum gastrointestinal hormones and intestinal motility-related neurotransmitter levels

To minimize circadian rhythm effects, all mice were sacrificed and sampled between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. Blood samples were collected by ocular enucleation, allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Serum was separated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C until use. Concentrations of gastrin (Gas), motilin (MTL), somatostatin (SS), vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), substance P (SP), acetylcholine (Ach), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers' instructions (catalog numbers provided in Supplementary Table S2).

Determination of intestinal metabolites

Intestinal metabolites were analyzed using a non-targeted metabolomics approach. Fifty milligrams of mouse feces were weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, and metabolite extraction was performed as previously described (Cheng, 2010). Metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, amino acid derivatives, and indole compounds, were analyzed using LC-MS/MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Due to resource constraints, a random subset of samples (n = 6 per group) was used for these assays, as indicated in the figure legends.

Determination of fecal SCFAs

0.1 g of collected mouse feces was thawed at room temperature for 30 min. Using a tissue lyser, disperse at 70 Hz/min for 3 min. Add 15 μL of internal standard 2-ethylbutyric acid (2000 μg/mL), then add phosphoric acid (1 mL 0.5 %, v/v) to the suspension, and disperse with the tissue lyser at a speed of 70 Hz/min for 5 min. Vortex and mix the suspension for 1 min, and centrifuge at 12000 rpm for 10 min at 25 °C. Collect the supernatant, mix with ethyl acetate (1 mL), vortex and mix for 2 min, and centrifuge at 12000 rpm for 10 min. Collect the upper layer and filter using a 0.22 μm filter for analysis.

The GC method was used to determine the SCFAs content. The detection conditions and program configuration are as follows. Program: Gas chromatography system (7890A, Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and a fused silica capillary column (DB-WAX, 60 m, 0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 μm; Agilent). Use helium as the carrier gas with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Set the column temperature curve to 90 °C for 1 min, rise to 150 °C at a heating rate of 15 °C/min, hold at 150 °C for 1 min, rise to 170 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, then rise to 240 °C and hold for 5 min. The temperatures of the inlet and FID were set to 220 °C and 300 °C, respectively. The injection volume used was 1 μL. Use the internal standard (2-ethylbutyric acid) calibration curve to quantify acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, and isovaleric acid from peak areas.

Data processing and analysis

All assays were performed according to standard protocols, and data are expressed as mean ± SD. SPSS Statistics 27 was used for one-way analysis of variance. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. For non-parametric data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess associations between metabolites and neuroendocrine factors. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Origin 2021 was used for data visualization and graph preparation.

Results and discussion

Technological performance and storage stability of J12-fermented yogurt

As shown in Fig. 2, the incorporation of B. animalis J12 did not compromise yogurt stability or texture during 28 days of refrigerated storage (Sah et al., 2015). Both J12 yogurt and the YoF-P control exhibited typical post-acidification, with pH decreasing from approximately 4.6–4.7 at day 0 to around 4.2–4.3 at day 28 (Fig. 2A), accompanied by a gradual increase in titratable acidity from about 70 to 75 °T to 88–92 °T (Fig. 2C). Viable counts of J12 remained at a functional level, declining moderately from 8.0 to 8.2 log CFU/mL at day 1 to 7.0–7.2 log CFU/mL by day 28 (Fig. 2B), indicating good probiotic persistence. Textural parameters were largely stable, with hardness maintained around 0.38–0.48 (Fig. 2J), cohesiveness around 0.40–0.55 (Fig. 2D), elasticity around 0.75–0.90 (Fig. 2E), and apparent viscosity ranging from 15 to 25 Pa s (Fig. 2F), with higher protein formulations (3.0% and 3.2%) showing greater consistency. Overall, these results demonstrate that J12 is technologically compatible with yogurt fermentation, maintaining excellent physicochemical stability, probiotic viability, and desirable textural quality during refrigerated storage (Sah et al., 2015). The rheological characterization in this study focused primarily on shear-dependent flow behavior (apparent viscosity versus shear rate), which directly reflects the oral and swallowing properties of yogurt. All samples exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior throughout storage, and J12 yogurt showed comparable rheological profiles to the YoF-P control, supporting the conclusion that J12 does not adversely affect the desirable flow characteristics of yogurt.

Sensory evaluation of yogurt during storage

Fig. S1 shows the dynamic changes in sensory quality of yogurt groups during storage. Although color and tissue state scores decreased with no significant inter-group differences, indicating J12 did not negatively affect appearance, it had significant advantages in flavor and mouthfeel. Unlike the continuous decline in the YoF-P group, the flavor score of the J12 group initially rose and then fell, reaching a peak on the 14th day. This peak may be attributed to the continuous metabolism of J12 during early storage, which generates key flavor compounds such as 2,3-butanedione, while the post-ripening process enhances mouthfeel; in later storage, degradation of some flavor compounds or generation of off-flavors leads to a slight decline (Cheng, 2010). Especially for the high-protein (3.0 % and 3.2 %) J12 groups, the mouthfeel score on the 14th day was significantly higher than that of the other groups (p < 0.05), likely due to improved texture resulting from probiotic slime production and post-ripening (Yang et al., 2014). In summary, the introduction of J12 significantly improved the flavor delicacy and overall acceptance of yogurt.

Ketones represented the most abundant volatile class detected (17 compounds; Table S4), highlighting the dominant contribution of carbonyl metabolism. Notably, J12 yogurt effectively maintained the classical buttery-creamy aroma backbone through stable 2,3-butanedione, which remained consistent across storage (OAV ≈ 41–42, Table 1; relative abundance stable in Fig. 3E).

Although the pH decreased from approximately 4.6 to 4.2 during storage (Fig. 2A), which could theoretically destabilize 2,3-butanedione under acidic conditions, the J12 group maintained stable OAV values throughout storage. This apparent stability may be attributed to several compensatory mechanisms: continuous generation of 2,3-butanedione precursors (e.g., α-acetolactate) by J12 metabolism; binding with milk protein matrix components that enhance apparent stability; and protection by the complex yogurt matrix (Hassan et al., 2010). These observations suggest that J12 may possess a distinctive metabolic capacity to sustain buttery aroma under acidic storage conditions.

In contrast, conventional yogurt (YoF-P) relied more heavily on 2,3-pentanedione, which showed higher relative abundance in controls compared to J12 (Fig. 3F), suggesting divergent carbon flux partitioning between the strains. The molecular basis for this divergence may involve strain-specific enzymatic pathways. J12 likely harbors α-acetolactate synthase and diacetyl reductase activities that modulate carbon flux partitioning between 2,3-butanedione and 2,3-pentanedione. This is consistent with reports that different lactic acid bacteria exhibit distinct substrate specificities for butanediol dehydrogenase, which governs the balance between diacetyl and acetoin production (Li et al., 2023b).

The aroma-compound association network (Fig. 3B) further illustrates this distinction: 2,3-butanedione (diacetyl) formed the central hub strongly linked to buttery/creamy descriptors, confirming its dominant role in J12's aroma identity. While 2,3-pentanedione contributed similarly to creamy notes, its relative predominance in controls versus J12 indicates alternative metabolic strategies for achieving comparable sensory profiles.

Organic acids showed the most pronounced treatment-dependent differences. Acetic acid exhibited consistently higher relative abundance in J12 (Fig. 3D), consistent with bifidobacterial heterofermentative metabolism. However, its OAV remained below 1 (Table 1), indicating negligible direct sensory impact and confirming that J12's aroma stability derives primarily from ketone metabolism rather than acid accumulation.

Aromatic complexity was maintained through phenylacetaldehyde (floral/honey-like, Fig. 3G), geraniol (floral/fruity, Figs. 3H), and 2,4-decadienal (sweet/nutty, Fig. 3I), all showing comparable stability between J12 and controls, supporting that J12 reshapes aroma through specific carbonyl pathways without disrupting overall aromatic balance.

Beyond volatile outputs, differential metabolite profiling revealed upstream metabolic signatures modulated by J12. Bioactive peptide-related metabolites (Pro-Val, β-alanyl-L-lysine) (Fig. 3J and O), bile acid signatures (chenodeoxycholic acid, taurocholic acid) (Fig. 3K and L), lipid-associated substrates (hexadecanedioic acid; Fig. 3M), and sulfur-containing fingerprints (Fig. 3N) collectively highlight a distinctive metabolic landscape that likely reshapes precursor pools to support the specific volatile expression observed.

In summary, J12 fermentation uniquely preserves the dominant buttery-creamy backbone through stable 2,3-butanedione (distinct from controls relying on 2,3-pentanedione), introduces characteristic metabolic fingerprints in organic-acid and aromatic pathways, and maintains excellent aroma stability throughout refrigerated storage.

J12-fermented yogurt alleviates constipation by improving intestinal transit and stool hydration

Based on the excellent survival rate of J12 in high protein matrices confirmed in Section 3.1, we speculate that it can reach the intestine with a higher number of viable bacteria. Therefore, this section evaluates the in vivo efficacy of J12-fermented yogurt (J12-Y) in a loperamide-induced constipation mouse model.

As shown in Fig. 4, different intervention strategies exhibited markedly different efficacies in alleviating loperamide-induced constipation. Milk showed marginal improvement compared to the model group (first black stool approximately 125 min, fecal water content approximately 53%, intestinal transport rate approximately 62%), suggesting limited prokinetic effects beyond basic nutrient provision. Conventional yogurt (YoF-P-Y) and J12 yogurt (J12-Y) demonstrated moderate efficacy, significantly shortening defecation latency to approximately 110 min and restoring fecal water content to approximately 55–56% (Fig. 4A–B), likely via general probiotic metabolites and fermented dairy bioactives.Fig. 4Effects of J12-fermented yogurt on defecation parameters and intestinal transit in constipated mice. J12-Y produced robust improvements across constipation-related endpoints, shortening the time to first black stool to approximately 110 min (vs. 131 min in the model group), restoring fecal water content to approximately 55%, and enhancing intestinal transport rate to approximately 67% (Fig. 4A–C). The increase in 5-HT from approximately 280 pg/mL (model group) to 320 pg/mL (J12-Y) represents a modest (∼14%) elevation. However, such changes can be significantly amplified in the local intestinal microenvironment, where enterochromaffin cells release 5-HT directly onto nearby nerve endings, and are sufficient to affect intestinal peristalsis. Similar modest changes in circulating 5-HT have been shown to have physiological significance in other studies on gut microbiota-host interactions (Reigstad et al., 2014). Moreover, the functional relevance of this 5-HT increase in the present study is supported by concurrent improvements in intestinal transport rate (from ∼45% to ∼67%, Fig. 4C) and reduced time to first black stool (from ∼131 min to ∼110 min, Fig. 4A). These coordinated functional improvements provide internal validation that the observed neuroendocrine shifts are physiologically meaningful. Additionally, the coordinated upregulation of multiple excitatory neurotransmitters (5-HT, ACh, SP) alongside downregulation of inhibitory signals (SS, VIP) collectively reflects a functional shift toward pro-motility neuroendocrine regulation, which likely has a greater net impact than any single marker change alone.

Motility-related gastrointestinal hormones showed a clear recovery pattern: GAS increased from approximately 22 ng/L (model) to approximately 57 ng/L in J12-Y, while MTL increased from approximately 65 ng/L to approximately 100 ng/L in J12-Y (Fig. 5C and D). As a formulation comparison, the J12 powder group showed GAS and MTL values in the range of approximately 58–65 ng/L and approximately 105 ng/L, respectively (Fig. 5C and D), indicating broadly comparable endocrine recovery across J12 interventions. Both J12 formulations restored excitatory signaling, with J12-Y showing high 5-HT and ACh levels comparable to, or slightly exceeding, those observed in the powder group (Fig. 5A and B). Overall, across markers, J12-Y exhibited a coherent pro-motility neuroendocrine profile characterized by excitatory upregulation together with inhibitory modulation (Fig. 5).

J12-Y also attenuated inhibitory signaling relative to the model group. Somatostatin (SS) decreased from approximately 42 ng/L in the model group to approximately 35 ng/L in J12-Y, and VIP decreased from approximately 241 ng/L to approximately 178 ng/L (Fig. 5F and G). The excitatory neuropeptide substance P (SP) increased to approximately 65 ng/L across active interventions (Fig. 5E), whereas CGRP showed minimal variation among groups (approximately 18–20 pg/mL) (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these findings indicate that J12-Y is associated with a pro-motility neuroendocrine profile, characterized by increased excitatory markers and motility-related hormones together with reduced inhibitory SS/VIP signaling (Fig. 5).

Notably, while the J12 powder group showed a lower SS level (SS ∼29 ng/L), J12-Y exhibited higher excitatory 5-HT (approximately 320 vs. approximately 280–300 pg/mL) together with reduced VIP (approximately 178 ng/L) and a balanced SS reduction (approximately 35 ng/L) (Fig. 5A–F–G). This coordinated “excitatory upregulation with inhibitory modulation” pattern supports the mechanistic interpretation of J12-Y within the proposed metabolite–nerve–endocrine framework (Fig. 5).

Beyond SCFAs, lipid-associated metabolites showed moderate but detectable modulation. The relative abundance of linoleic acid, palmitic acid, and DL-2-hydroxy stearic acid remained broadly comparable among groups, indicating that J12 primarily influenced microbial fermentation outputs rather than inducing excessive lipid disruption (Fig. 6G–I). Importantly, several tryptophan/indole-derived microbial metabolites were significantly shifted. For example, 5-hydroxyindoleacetylglycine was elevated in intervention groups (Fig. 6J), while oxindole and indolepyruvate exhibited partial normalization trends (Fig. 6K–L), suggesting a potential link between aromatic amino acid metabolism that may contribute to serotonergic signaling (Yano et al., 2015). In addition, bile acid-associated metabolites such as deoxycholic acid and taurocholic acid were altered (Fig. 6N–O), supporting strain-dependent regulation of microbial bile acid transformation and gut signaling pathways.

Integrated mechanistic summary: the “metabolite-nerve-endocrine” framework linking yogurt metabolism to host motility

To consolidate the multi-layer evidence generated in this study, we propose an integrated three-tier “Metabolite-Nerve-Endocrine” framework through which B. animalis J12 coordinates product-level flavor homeostasis with in vivo constipation alleviation (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Schematic diagram of the “Metabolite-Nerve-Endocrine” regulatory mechanism by which Bifidobacterium animalis J12 yogurt alleviates constipation.

Schematic diagram of the “Metabolite-Nerve-Endocrine” regulatory mechanism by which Bifidobacterium animalis J12 yogurt alleviates constipation.

Tier 1 (Fig. 8) indicates that J12 is technologically compatible with yogurt manufacture and refrigerated storage, maintaining probiotic viability (>7.0 log CFU/mL) and stable rheological structure (Fig. 2), while preserving the classical buttery-creamy aroma backbone as key high-OAV carbonyls (e.g., 2,3-butanedione) remain stable over storage (Fig. 3A–B, Table 1); meanwhile, strain-dependent fingerprints (e.g., acetate enrichment and characteristic precursor pools) suggest that J12 subtly reshapes metabolic inputs without compromising sensory acceptance (Fig. 3C–O).

Building on these food-level inputs, Tier 2 (Fig. 8) captures the in vivo translation of the J12-Y metabolite signature, supported by fecal SCFAs and metabolomics readouts (Fig. 6). Specifically, J12-Y robustly restored major SCFAs depleted by loperamide, including acetate (approximately 100 μmol/g in J12-Y vs. approximately 75–80 μmol/g in the model), propionate (approximately 18–19 μmol/g vs. approximately 9 μmol/g), and butyrate (approximately 33 μmol/g vs. 13 μmol/g), with a particularly notable increase in butyrate (a key gut motility–relevant SCFA) in J12-Y (Fig. 6A–C). In parallel, J12-Y exhibited coordinated modulation of signaling-relevant metabolites, including indole derivatives and bile acid–related metabolites (Fig. 6J–O), forming a coherent “signaling-metabolite” profile beyond SCFA recovery alone. These metabolite features were accompanied by a pro-motility neuroendocrine profile, characterized by increased excitatory neurotransmitters and motility-related hormones (5-HT/ACh/SP and GAS/MTL) together with reduced inhibitory SS/VIP signaling (Fig. 5), collectively supporting a proposed metabolite–nerve–endocrine framework underlying constipation alleviation (Fig. 8).

To further substantiate the proposed “Metabolite-Nerve-Endocrine” axis, a Spearman correlation heatmap was constructed integrating fecal metabolites, serum neuroendocrine markers, and physiological phenotypes (Fig. 7). The analysis revealed a robust biological network. Notably, fecal butyric acid exhibited a strong positive correlation with the excitatory neurotransmitter 5-HT (r = 0.62) and a negative correlation with the inhibitory peptide VIP (r = −0.54). Furthermore, butyric acid was highly positively correlated with the intestinal transport rate (r = 0.80), while 5-HT itself also strongly correlated with the intestinal transport rate (r = 0.74). In parallel, acetic acid showed a significant positive association with fecal water content (r = 0.67). In contrast, bile acid-related metabolites such as deoxycholic acid exhibited strong negative correlations with both intestinal transport rate (r = −0.82) and 5-HT (r = −0.74), suggesting that J12-mediated modulation of bile acid metabolism may relieve inhibition on the enteric nervous system. Collectively, these strong statistical correlations provide critical supportive evidence that J12-driven enrichment of specific SCFAs (particularly butyrate and acetate) is statistically associated to the rebalancing of enteric neuroendocrine signaling, which consequently drives the restoration of intestinal motility and stool hydration.

Tier 3 (Fig. 8) manifests as integrated anti-constipation functional outputs supported by physiological endpoints (Fig. 4). Relative to the model group, J12-Y shortened the time to first black stool from approximately 130 min to approximately 110 min (Fig. 4B), increased fecal water content from approximately 28% to approximately 56% (Fig. 4A), and enhanced small-intestinal propulsion/transport rate from approximately 45% to approximately 67% (Fig. 4C).

Together, these Tier 3 outcomes align with the Tier 2 metabolite remodeling (Fig. 6) and the accompanying neuroendocrine shifts (Fig. 5) summarized in the proposed model (Fig. 8). Collectively, this Tier 1→Tier 2→Tier 3 cascade supports a model in which J12-fermented yogurt maintains stable product quality and aroma identity while delivering J12-associated metabolic remodeling and neuroendocrine modulation that contribute to constipation alleviation (Fig. 6).

Conclusions

Co-fermentation with B. animalis J12 produced a functional yogurt that maintained probiotic viability (>7.0 log CFU/mL) during 28 days of refrigerated storage and preserved the characteristic buttery–creamy aroma backbone, supported by stable 2,3-butanedione (OAV ≈ 41–42). In vivo, J12 yogurt (J12-Y) alleviated loperamide-induced constipation, as evidenced by shortened time to first black stool (approximately 110 vs. approximately 131 min), restoring fecal water content (approximately 56% vs. approximately 28%), and enhancing intestinal propulsion/transport (approximately 67% vs. approximately 45%).

Mechanistically, J12-Y was associated with a coordinated metabolite–neuroendocrine profile, including enrichment of fecal SCFAs (acetate approximately 100 vs. approximately 75–80 μmol/g; propionate approximately 18–19 vs. approximately 9 μmol/g; butyrate approximately 33 vs. approximately 13 μmol/g) alongside modulation of indole derivatives and bile acid–related metabolites. These metabolic shifts were accompanied by a pro-motility neuroendocrine profile characterized by elevated excitatory neurotransmitters and hormones (5-HT, ACh, SP, GAS, MTL) and reduced inhibitory signaling (SS, VIP). Spearman correlation analysis further revealed critical functional links within this network: butyric acid showed strong positive correlations with 5-HT and intestinal transport rate, while 5-HT itself was positively correlated with intestinal transport rate; acetic acid was positively correlated with fecal water content; and deoxycholic acid exhibited negative correlations with both intestinal transport rate and 5-HT. Collectively, these findings support the proposed metabolite–nerve–endocrine framework, in which J12-Y mediates constipation relief through integrated microbial metabolic and neuroendocrine modulation.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The loperamide-induced constipation model primarily inhibits intestinal motility pharmacologically, so the observed metabolic changes could be secondary to restored motility rather than direct microbial modulation. Consequently, the proposed “metabolite-nerve-endocrine” framework is based on correlational evidence, and direct causal validation—such as using GPR41/43 antagonists, germ-free mice, or time-course experiments—has not been established, nor has temporal precedence been determined. Inflammatory markers and intestinal permeability were not assessed, leaving alternative mechanisms such as anti-inflammatory effects or barrier repair unexplored. The volatile flavor analysis employed an internal standard semi-quantitative method without full external validation, limiting absolute quantification.

Regarding texture analysis, a compression distance of 10 mm (approximately 100% strain) was used in the TPA measurements. While this parameter allowed consistent comparison with our previous studies and did not obscure the observed group differences or storage trends, we acknowledge that such a high strain may partially disrupt the gel structure. Future studies should consider using a lower strain range (e.g., 30–50%) to obtain textural parameters closer to the native state of the sample.

In rheological characterization, the present study focused on shear-dependent flow behavior (apparent viscosity versus shear rate), which directly reflects oral and swallowing properties. However, oscillatory viscoelastic measurements (storage modulus G′ and loss modulus G″) were not performed. Consequently, the gel network structure remains incompletely described. Future studies should incorporate oscillatory rheology to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the gel network.

We also note the absence of quantitative data on J12 colonization in the mouse gut. The viable load of J12 in feces or intestinal tissues was not determined, which limits our ability to distinguish between direct colonization-dependent effects and metabolite-mediated actions. Future research should incorporate colonization assays (e.g., strain-specific qPCR or selective plating) to address this gap.
