The Relationship Between Left Atrial Epicardial Adipose Tissue Volume and Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction in Patients with Non-Obstructive Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: Liyan Fang, Lili Zhang, Shuangxiang Lin, Haipeng Liu, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate whether epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) volume correlates with coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) identified via invasive physiological evaluation, in comparison with EAT attenuation.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective study included 158 consecutive patients (mean age 65.40±9.76 years, 51.90% male) who underwent both coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and invasive coronary function testing. CMD was defined as coronary flow reserve (CFR) <2.0 and/or index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) ≥25. Total EAT volume, left atrial (LA)-EAT volume, and right atrial (RA)-EAT volume, EAT attenuation, and coronary fat attenuation index (FAI) were quantified from CCTA images. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to screen variables associated with CMD.ResultsCMD was diagnosed in 59 patients (37.34%). Patients with CMD had significantly higher total EAT volume, LA-EAT volume, and RA-EAT volume (all P <0.001) compared with patients without CMD. After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and cardiovascular risk factors, total EAT volume (OR=1.134, 95% CI 1.089–1.181, P <0.001), LA-EAT volume (OR=1.006, 95% CI 1.004–1.008, P <0.001) and RA-EAT volume (OR=1.003, 95% CI 1.002–1.005, P <0.001) were independently associated with CMD. The FAI of the three major coronary arteries showed no significant association with CMD (all P >0.05). LA-EAT volume demonstrated excellent discriminative ability for CMD (AUC=0.917), which was superior to total EAT volume (AUC=0.824) and RA-EAT volume (AUC=0.846). DeLong test analysis confirmed that the AUC of LA-EAT volume was significantly higher than that of total EAT volume (P=0.003) and of RA-EAT volume (P=0.029). No significant difference was observed between the AUCs of total EAT volume and RA-EAT volume (P =0.547).ConclusionTotal and atrial-specific EAT volumes, particularly LA-EAT volume, are strongly and independently associated with CMD, whereas FAI shows no association with coronary microvascular dysfunction. EAT volume measurements acquired from routine CCTA may act as a valuable imaging biomarker for CMD-related risk stratification. LA-EAT measurement obtained from routine CCTA is promising for convenient clinical screening of coronary microvascular dysfunction.

Introduction

Increasingly recognized as a key contributor to ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA), coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) affects up to 50% of patients with angina but no obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD).1,2 CMD is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including major adverse cardiac events and reduced quality of life.3,4 Nevertheless, because traditional diagnosis of CMD relies on invasive coronary function testing, its widespread clinical application remains limited.

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), the visceral fat depot surrounding the heart, has emerged as a metabolically active organ that may influence coronary artery disease pathogenesis through paracrine and systemic inflammatory mechanisms.5 Recent studies have suggested a potential association between EAT and CMD, with multiple reports indicating correlations between EAT volume or thickness and markers of microvascular dysfunction.6–11 Beyond cardiovascular-related illnesses, CT-quantified EAT volume also shows prognostic capacity in other clinical contexts. In one retrospective CT-based investigation, Marcucci et al12 identified a favourable cut-off value for EAT volume to predict severe disease burden among patients with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia. Even within an infectious-disease cohort, this work validates that CT-derived EAT quantification works as a robust imaging biomarker, which further inspires our exploration of its predictive effect on CMD. Nevertheless, findings from these studies have been inconsistent regarding which specific EAT characteristics—including volume, radiodensity, and atrial-specific distribution—are most strongly associated with CMD.

The fat attenuation index (FAI) is a novel imaging biomarker derived from coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) that quantifies inflammation in pericoronary adipose tissue. It has shown considerable potential in detecting cardiovascular events and assessing plaque vulnerability.13,14 Nevertheless, the relationship between FAI and CMD remains poorly understood, with existing evidence being limited and inconsistent.15

Furthermore, although atrial-specific EAT distribution has been investigated in the context of atrial fibrillation and cardiac systolic and diastolic function,16,17 its association with CMD remains to be systematically explored. We hypothesized that atrial-specific EAT depots, especially those adjacent to specific coronary territories, may exhibit stronger associations with microvascular dysfunction than total EAT measurements.

Therefore, the present study aimed to comprehensively investigate the associations of multiple EAT characteristics—including total EAT volume, atrial-specific EAT volume, EAT attenuation, and territory-specific FAI—with CMD diagnosed by invasive physiological evaluation in patients with suspected coronary artery disease.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the observational nature of the study. We consecutively enrolled patients who underwent both CCTA and invasive coronary function testing - including assessment of coronary flow reserve (CFR) and index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) - at our institution between January 2020 and February 2025.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) completion of both CCTA and invasive coronary function testing within 30 days; and (3) absence of obstructive coronary artery disease (defined as luminal stenosis <50% on invasive angiography). INOCA was defined in accordance with ESC and AHA consensus guidelines: patients presented with angina-related symptoms while no obstructive coronary stenosis (luminal stenosis <50%) was detected on coronary angiography.2,18 Exclusion criteria included: (1) previous coronary artery bypass grafting; (2) severe valvular heart disease; (3) left ventricular ejection fraction < 40%; (4) acute coronary syndrome within the preceding 3 months; (5) confirmed vasospastic angina; and (6) insufficient image quality for EAT or FAI.

Cardiac CT Acquisition

CCTA was performed using a SOMATOM Definition Flash dual-source CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). Scanning parameters were as follows: collimation 128 * 0.6 mm, gantry rotation time 280 ms, tube voltage 120 kVp with automatic tube current modulation (reference 320 mAs). Contrast agent (1.5 mL/kg body weight) was injected at 5 mL/s via an antecubital vein, followed by a 40 mL saline chaser. Images were acquired using ECG gating and were reconstructed at both end-systole (30–40% R-R interval) and end-diastole (70–80% R-R interval), with a slice thickness of 0.75 mm and a reconstruction interval of 0.5 mm.

Total Epicardial Adipose Tissue Volume Quantification

Total EAT volume was quantified using Syngo.via software (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), a dedicated semi-automated post-processing software, on contrast-enhanced CCTA images. EAT was defined as adipose tissue located within the pericardial sac, with tissue attenuation ranging from −190 to −30 Hounsfield units (HU).19,20 The pericardial boundary was manually and meticulously delineated slice by slice; areas containing calcified plaques, large epicardial vessels, and motion artifacts were carefully excluded to avoid interference. The software automatically calculated total EAT volume, which was recorded in cubic centimeters (cm3).

Atrial-Specific EAT Volume Quantification

Atrial-specific EAT volumes (LA-EAT and RA-EAT) were manually delineated around the left atrium and right atrium, respectively, using the same Syngo.via software and attenuation threshold. LA-EAT was defined as EAT surrounding the left atrium and atrioventricular groove, and RA-EAT as EAT adjacent to the right atrium and right atrioventricular groove. Volumes were automatically calculated and reported in mm3. Semi-automatic quantification of atrial-specific EAT was performed on CCTA images (Figure 1). Epicardial Adipose Tissue Attenuation Measurement

Mean EAT attenuation was measured in HU using Syngo.via software with the same attenuation threshold of −190 to −30 HU.19,20 Regions of interest were manually placed within the total EAT depot, carefully excluding calcified plaques, large epicardial vessels, and motion artifacts. The software automatically calculated the mean attenuation value. All measurements were independently performed by two senior radiologists (with 8 and 12 years of cardiovascular imaging experience, respectively) who were blinded to patients’ clinical and invasive physiological data, and the average value was used for statistical analysis to minimize measurement bias.

Fat Attenuation Index Quantification

FAI was quantified for the right coronary artery (RCA), left anterior descending artery (LAD), and circumflex artery (CX) using commercially available software (Shukun Technology, Beijing, China). FAI was defined as the mean attenuation of pericoronary adipose tissue within a radial distance equal to the vessel diameter at the same cross-section (ie, a total radial distance of one vessel diameter outward from the outer vessel wall), expressed in HU.13 The software provided fully automated quantification of FAI for each coronary artery, requiring no manual contouring or operator intervention.

Quality Control for CCTA Measurements

All CT measurements (total EAT volume, atrial-specific EAT volumes, and EAT attenuation) were independently performed by two senior radiologists who were blinded to patients’ clinical and invasive physiological data. All observers followed a unified delineation protocol and received pre-study training using standard reference images to ensure consistency.

Since the FAI was obtained by fully-automatic software computation without manual contour delineation, reproducibility testing was not conducted for FAI.

Thirty patients were randomly sampled from the study cohort for inter-observer reproducibility analysis using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The average of the two radiologists’ measurements was adopted for subsequent statistical analysis to reduce measurement bias.

Invasive Coronary Function Testing

Invasive coronary function testing was performed in accordance with standardized protocols.21 A pressure-temperature sensor-tipped guidewire was advanced to the distal segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery. Thermodilution measurements were acquired during maximal hyperemia induced by intravenous adenosine infusion (140 μg/kg/min). CFR was calculated as the ratio of hyperemic to resting mean transit time. The IMR was computed as the product of distal coronary pressure (Pd) and mean transit time during hyperemia. The measurement was repeated three times, and the average value was used. CMD was defined as CFR < 2.0 and/or IMR ≥ 25.2 Invasive intracoronary functional examinations including fractional flow reserve (FFR), CFR and IMR were conducted, as shown in Figure 2. Clinical Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, current smoking), and laboratory parameters (lipid profile, HbA1c, and renal function).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent samples t–test. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of CMD. Parameters that were insignificant in univariable analysis were excluded from the multivariable regression models. Models 1–3 were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, hypertension and diabetes. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships between EAT parameters, FAI, and invasive physiological indices (CFR and IMR), as appropriate. The diagnostic performance of EAT parameters in predicting CMD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The corresponding areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated and compared via DeLong’s test.

ICCs were calculated to assess inter-observer measurement reproducibility of EAT in a random subset of 30 patients.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Diagnostic Performance

ROC curve analysis revealed that total EAT volume yielded favorable diagnostic efficacy for predicting CMD (AUC = 0.824, 95% CI: 0.760–0.887, P < 0.0001). RA-EAT volume displayed marginally better diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.846, 95% CI: 0.781–0.910, P < 0.0001), whereas LA-EAT volume achieved the optimal diagnostic ability among all parameters (AUC = 0.917, 95% CI: 0.863–0.970, P < 0.0001).

The DeLong non-parametric test was employed to compare the AUC of the three indicators pairwise. The results indicated that LA-EAT volume exhibited the best diagnostic performance, significantly surpassing both total EAT volume (DeLong test, AUC difference = −0.093, P =0.003) and RA-EAT volume (AUC difference = −0.071, P =0.029). No significant difference was observed between total EAT and RA-EAT volume (DeLong test, AUC difference = −0.022, P =0.547). Among the three, LA-EAT volume demonstrated the strongest discriminative ability as a diagnostic indicator (Figure 5). Based on the maximum Youden-index, the optimal cut-off-values for total EAT, RA-EAT and LA-EAT volume were 62.16 cm3, 1705.00 mm3 and 2585.00 mm3, with corresponding sensitivity-specificity pairs of 0.983/0.535, 0.949/0.707 and 0.932/0.414. Discussion

The present study yields several key findings concerning the association between invasively diagnosed CMD and EAT phenotypes. First, after adjustment for age, gender, BMI, and conventional cardiovascular risk factors, elevated volumes of total EAT, LA-EAT, and RA-EAT were independently associated with CMD occurrence (all P < 0.001). Second, of note, LA-EAT volume exhibited the most robust association with CMD, along with superior diagnostic performance compared with RA-EAT and total EAT volume. Third, no significant correlation was observed between coronary FAI across the three major coronary territories and CMD, implying that FAI parameters may not be applicable for evaluating coronary microvascular dysfunction. Lastly, EAT attenuation remained comparable between CMD and non-CMD individuals, indicating that EAT volume rather than tissue density serves as a valuable biomarker for CMD risk stratification.

Our results regarding the association between EAT volume and CMD are in accordance with prior investigations while offering novel complementary insights. Numerous studies have previously demonstrated associations between EAT volume or thickness and indicators of CMD.6–11 In a cohort of 103 patients, Ito et al7 recently verified that the EAT volume index was independently associated with CMD (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06, P < 0.01), and showed moderate correlations with CFR (r = −0.39, P < 0.01) and IMR (r = 0.32, P < 0.01). Likewise, Abusnina et al6 reported from a prospective dual-center CMD registry study that the EAT volume index, rather than absolute EAT volume, served as a predictor of CMD (OR = 1.037, 95% CI: 1.014–1.063, P = 0.003). The present study reinforces these established associations in a relatively larger sample, and further identifies that absolute EAT volume, in addition to indexed EAT parameters, is a robust predictor of CMD, thereby expanding upon previous evidence centered solely on volume-indexed metrics.

Notably, the most novel finding of the present study is that LA-EAT volume possesses markedly superior predictive performance compared with total EAT volume, implying that the adverse impact of global EAT is predominantly mediated by localized left atrial adipose tissue.

Several anatomical and physiological reasons account for its stronger predictive capacity. The left atrial epicardial fat is anatomically adjacent to the circumflex coronary artery and left sided myocardial microvascular network. LA-EAT lies in direct proximity to the left atrial myocardium and secretes pro-inflammatory adipokines including TNF-α and IL-6, together with reactive oxygen species, via paracrine signaling. Through interstitial diffusion between adipose tissue and myocardium, these mediators impair coronary microvascular endothelial function, thereby contributing to microvascular vasomotor dysfunction, capillary rarefaction, and reduced myocardial blood-flow reserve. Compared with diffuse whole-heart epicardial fat and right atrial adipose tissue, left atrial fat derived inflammatory factors are spatially concentrated over the left sided coronary microcirculation and therefore exert more targeted microvascular injury.

Further evidence supporting the adverse cardiovascular prognostic value of elevated epicardial fat comes from a 10-year long-term cohort investigation.22 Arslan and Söner demonstrated that increased EAT volume served as an independent predictor for long-term all-cause mortality among systemic-sclerosis patients with cardiac microvascular impairment, reinforcing the theory that excess atrial-specific EAT derived inflammation damages myocardial microcirculation and worsens cardiac-related outcomes.

Prior research has predominantly centered on the associations between total EAT volume or EAT thickness and impaired CFR. Of significance, this is the first study to verify the predominant diagnostic value of LA-EAT in CMD detection, which provides novel mechanistic evidence supporting the proposed atrial-specific EAT–myocardium–microvascular axis hypothesis. With an AUC of 0.917 for LA-EAT in the present cohort, our results demonstrate that atrial-specific EAT quantification substantially improves diagnostic performance. Accordingly, localized left atrial adiposity should be prioritized in clinical risk assessment for CMD.

Another noteworthy unexpected finding of the present study is the identified association between RA-EAT volume and CMD. Despite such a correlation, RA-EAT volume exhibits relatively inferior diagnostic efficacy compared with LA-EAT volume, suggesting that its pathogenic contribution to CMD is weaker than that of LA-EAT. This disparity may be attributable to distinct hemodynamic pressure conditions and microcirculatory profiles inherent to the right heart system. Nevertheless, the anatomical adjacency of RA-EAT to the right coronary artery and coronary sinus remains capable of exerting paracrine influence on coronary microcirculation.

The European Society of Cardiology consensus statement further emphasizes that distinguishing between EAT and paracardial adipose tissue is critical for accurate phenotypic characterization. While the regional distribution of EAT has been previously linked to atrial fibrillation and coronary artery disease,16,17 its specific association with CMD remains unreported prior to the present study. Compared with total EAT and RA-EAT volumes, LA-EAT volume emerges as a more precise biomarker for systemic metabolic dysfunction. Future studies are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms governing this association and to determine whether LA-EAT quantification should be integrated into routine CMD risk stratification.

In contrast to EAT volume, no significant association was observed between FAI and CMD in the present study. This finding is consistent with the latest research by Smilowitz et al,15 who demonstrated in 31 patients with INOCA that FAI was not correlated with invasive indices of microvascular function. FAI was initially developed as a biomarker for coronary artery inflammation and has been shown to be useful in predicting cardiovascular events and plaque vulnerability.13,14 However, our results, in conjunction with those of Smilowitz et al, suggest that FAI may reflect inflammation of large coronary vessels rather than pathological changes in the coronary microcirculation. This distinction holds critical clinical implications, as FAI should not be employed as a surrogate marker for CMD.

In the present study, no significant association was identified between EAT attenuation and CMD. This finding is discrepant from that reported by Patel et al,23 who demonstrated reduced EAT attenuation among female patients with CMD relative to control individuals. Such inconsistency may be attributable to discrepancies in study population demographics (our cohort comprised both male and female subjects), sample size, and methodological approaches adopted for EAT attenuation quantification. Nevertheless, our results are in better accordance with cumulative existing evidence suggesting that EAT volume, rather than tissue density, represents the primary determinant of cardiovascular risk.7,8

The mechanistic pathways underlying the association between EAT and CMD are multifactorial. Epicardial adipose tissue secretes a panel of adipokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1, along with visfatin, whereas the expression of protective adiponectin is downregulated under pathological states.5,24 These mediators collectively precipitate endothelial dysfunction, exacerbate vascular inflammation, and impair coronary microvascular vasodilation. In addition, EAT may modulate coronary microvascular function via autonomic nervous system dysregulation and oxidative stress cascades.24 The relatively robust association observed for atrial-specific EAT further highlights the necessity of future investigations into the regional heterogeneity of EAT biological phenotypes and their paracrine influence on the coronary microcirculation.

The present study carries substantial clinical implications. Utilizing easily accessible post-processing software, quantitative assessment of EAT volume can be conveniently derived from routine CCTA. Given the close associations between EAT volume, especially LA-EAT, and CMD, such imaging parameters can act as reliable non-invasive biomarkers for CMD risk stratification.

Future large-scale, multicenter prospective trials are warranted to externally validate the present findings and establish the optimal cut-off value of EAT volume for CMD prediction. Longitudinal follow-up investigations are required to elucidate whether dynamic alterations in EAT volume over time correlate with the onset and progression of CMD. Moreover, integrated analyses combining EAT volume with advanced functional imaging metrics (eg, PET-derived myocardial flow reserve) may enable more comprehensive risk stratification for CMD. Ultimately, interventional studies evaluating the effects of weight reduction, statin treatment, and other therapeutic strategies on EAT regression and concomitant improvements in microvascular function are of considerable clinical value.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, the retrospective and single-center nature of our cohort may introduce selection bias and restrict the external generalizability of our findings. Second, owing to its cross-sectional design, causal relationships between EAT and CMD cannot be inferred. Third, although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, the presence of unmeasured residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Fourth, the study population was exclusively comprised of patients with INOCA, and thus our results may not be extrapolatable to other clinical cohorts. Fifth, while the sample size was adequate for primary outcome analyses, it may have reduced statistical power to detect weak-to-moderate associations involving FAI parameters. Finally, advanced imaging modalities such as MR spectroscopy were not implemented to characterize intrinsic EAT tissue composition, which would offer further mechanistic insights into the biological properties of epicardial adipose tissue.

Conclusions

In summary, total and atrial-specific EAT volumes, particularly LA-EAT, represent robust independent predictors of invasively diagnosed CMD. In contrast, no significant association was identified between FAI and CMD. Quantitative EAT volume measurement derived from routine CCTA may serve as a valuable non-invasive imaging biomarker for CMD risk stratification. Collectively, our findings support the incorporation of EAT evaluation into routine clinical workflows for patients undergoing CCTA, and warrant further exploration regarding whether EAT-modulating interventions can ameliorate microvascular function and long-term clinical outcomes.
