Dual versus single implant fixation for geriatric distal femur fractures: protocol for a randomised, controlled pilot study at five US level 1 trauma centres
Authors: Justin Haller, Chukwuebuka C Achebe, Garret Oman, Sanjit Konda, Matthew Garner, Brandon Yuan, Lucas S Marchand, Graham J DeKeyser

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeriatric distal femur fractures are associated with mortality rates exceeding 20%, comparable to hip fractures. Traditional single implant fixation often requires weight-bearing restrictions that delay recovery. Introduction

The incidence of distal femur fractures in elderly patients continues to rise, currently affecting 27.4 per 100 000 annually.1 This trend reflects both ageing demographics and increasing rates of knee arthroplasty.2 Despite mortality rates of 20–30% that rival geriatric hip fractures, distal femur fractures have not received equivalent research attention or evidence-based treatment protocols.3,6

Traditional operative fixation uses either lateral locked plates or retrograde intramedullary nails as single implant constructs. However, concerns about fixation stability in osteopenic bone often lead surgeons to impose weight-bearing restrictions, potentially delaying functional recovery and contributing to complications such as pneumonia, pressure ulcers and venous thromboembolism.7 8 Nonunion rates up to 14% have been reported with single implant fixation, prompting investigation of alternative strategies.9 10

Dual implant constructs—using either dual plates or nail-plate combinations—have shown promise in biomechanical studies demonstrating superior axial and torsional stiffness.11 12 Recent meta-analyses suggest lower complication rates with dual implants (5% nonunion) compared with single implants (0–25% nonunion).13 Enhanced mechanical stability may enable safer immediate weight-bearing, potentially improving mobility and reducing mortality.

A recent Cochrane review highlighted the lack of high-quality evidence for distal femur fracture management, while a survey of orthopaedic trauma surgeons found nearly 80% would have equipoise to participate in a randomised controlled trial (RCT) comparing treatment strategies.14 15 This pilot study aims to establish the feasibility of conducting a definitive multicentre RCT to determine optimal surgical management for this vulnerable population.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

This is a multicentre, prospective, randomised controlled pilot trial. Patients will be allocated 1:1 to single or dual implant fixation. The study will be conducted at five US level 1 trauma centres: University of Utah, Mayo Clinic, Oregon Health & Science University, New York University and University of Pittsburgh Medical Centre—Harrisburg.

Recruitment and allocation

Patients will be screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria on presentation to the emergency department and during preoperative evaluation through medical record review, physical examination and patient self-report. Our recruitment pool consists of all patients meeting inclusion criteria during the expected 12-month study duration from October 2024 to October 2025. Due to the emergent nature of these injuries, eligible patients or their legally authorised representatives will be approached in the emergency department, preoperative area or inpatient setting, with adequate time for questions before surgery. Spanish-speaking participants will be provided certified medical interpreters and translated consent forms. After informed consent, participants will be randomised using REDCap with variable block sizes (2 and 4) stratified by site. The allocation sequence will be concealed until randomisation. While surgeons cannot be blinded to treatment allocation, both interventions follow standardised protocols.

Study interventions

The study interventions consist of either single implant or dual implant fixation for displaced distal femur fractures. Single implant constructs use either a retrograde intramedullary nail with interlocking screws or a lateral locked plate, while dual implant constructs employ either an intramedullary nail with supplemental plate fixation or dual plating (eg, a lateral plate with a medial buttress plate, lateral-anterior configurations or other dual plate arrangements). The choice between specific implant options within each group is at the discretion of the treating surgeon based on fracture pattern and bone quality.

Patients will be randomised to receive either single or dual implant fixation according to a predetermined protocol coordinated by the University of Utah. All surgical procedures will be performed within 72 hours of hospital presentation by fellowship-trained orthopaedic trauma surgeons experienced in both techniques.

All patients will receive standardised perioperative care including prophylactic antibiotics (cephalosporin or clindamycin/vancomycin for cephalosporin allergy) administered within 60 min of incision and continued for 24 hours postoperatively. Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis will be initiated postoperatively according to institutional protocols. Critically, all patients in both groups will be permitted immediate weight-bearing as tolerated postoperatively, representing a departure from traditional restrictive protocols. This standardised approach to early mobilisation allows for direct comparison of implant stability and functional outcomes between groups.

Intraoperative data including surgical time, blood loss and complications will be recorded. Postoperative care including pain management, physical therapy protocols and discharge planning will be standardised across all sites to minimise treatment variation beyond the implant configuration.

All implants used in this study are commercially available, FDA-cleared devices routinely stocked at participating institutions. The cost of implants and surgical procedures is covered as standard of care; no additional implant costs are borne by patients as a result of randomisation. An exploratory cost analysis comparing total episode-of-care costs between treatment arms will be performed as a secondary analysis to inform the health economic evaluation of the definitive trial.

Randomisation

After informed consent, participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either single or dual implant fixation by the site research coordinator using a computer-generated randomisation sequence accessed through REDCap. The randomisation will use variable block sizes (2 and 4) with stratification by site to ensure balanced allocation across centres. The allocation sequence will be concealed from all clinical and research staff until the moment of randomisation.

To minimise performance bias, standardised surgical protocols have been developed for both interventions, and all surgeons have confirmed equipoise between treatments. Statistical analysis will be performed by analysts blinded to group allocation until the primary analysis is complete.

Follow-up

Standard clinical visits will occur at 2, 6, 12, 24 and 52 weeks with radiographs, functional assessments and patient-reported outcomes. Phone follow-up will be available for patients unable to attend visits.

Data management and monitoring

Data will be collected by site coordinators and entered into a secure REDCap database managed by the University of Utah coordinating centre. Regular monitoring will ensure safety and protocol compliance. An independent data safety monitoring board will review adverse events quarterly.

Statistical methods

This feasibility study is not powered to detect clinical differences between the treatments. The totality of the data collected will be used to assess the feasibility of a multicentre randomised clinical trial. Participant enrolment and flow throughout the study will be summarised using a Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram with descriptive statistics of means and percentages(figure 1).

Trial flow diagram. Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) flow diagram showing anticipated participant allocation, follow-up and analysis for the pDISIDE Femur pilot trial.

Baseline demographics (eg, age, sex) will be compared between groups to ensure appropriate balance has been achieved between the randomised study treatments. Participant demographic and clinical characteristics will be summarised using descriptive summary measures expressed as mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables and as number and percentage for categorical variables. The primary analysis will evaluate feasibility parameters including enrolment rates, protocol adherence and follow-up retention. The number of study-eligible patients, enrolments, follow-up visits completed and the success of randomisation will be collected and where appropriate rates and percentages calculated.

For secondary analyses, we have adopted a Phase II randomised screening design to facilitate non-definitive comparisons of the two surgical treatment strategies. Outcomes will be assessed on an intention-to-treat basis. Statistical comparison of means will be tabulated using Student’s two-tailed t-test (continuous variables) and Pearson’s χ2 test (categorical variables). Results will be expressed as mean differences or risk ratios with corresponding 95% CIs. No adjustment for multiple comparisons will be made as all investigations are considered exploratory in nature and used to provide information for planning of the larger clinical trial. All clinical pilot study data should be interpreted as exploratory.

Sample size determination

Target enrolment of 80 patients (40 per group) is determined by feasibility objectives: demonstrating adequate multicentre recruitment capacity based on retrospective case volumes showing approximately 203 eligible patients annually across the five participating sites. This sample size fulfils established recommendations for pilot studies and provides sufficiently precise estimates of variance for planning the definitive trial.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this pilot research. Feedback from future patient and public stakeholder involvement will help guide the design of the definitive trial.

Ethics and dissemination

This trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05292313). Ethics approval has been obtained from the University of Utah Institutional Review Board (IRB_00149119), the New York University Grossman School of Medicine Institutional Review Board, the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board, the Oregon Health & Science University Institutional Review Board and the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants or their legally authorised representatives prior to enrolment (online supplemental file 1). The findings will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at scientific conferences regardless of outcome. This protocol was developed following SPIRIT 2013 guidelines.16 Any significant amendments to this protocol will be submitted to the IRB for approval and updated in the clinical trial registry.
