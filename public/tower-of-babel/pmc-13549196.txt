Early Risk Stratification of Severe Trauma in the Emergency Department: Integrating Clinical Scoring Systems, Dynamic Biomarkers, and Artificial Intelligence—A Narrative Review
Authors: Xiabing Sun, Jie Han, Jianjian Ni, Yujie Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere trauma remains a major global cause of death and disability, particularly among individuals younger than 45 years. Emergency departments are the main gateway to trauma care, where rapid risk stratification guides resuscitation, surgical decision-making, intensive care admission, and resource allocation. Conventional tools, including the Revised Trauma Score, Glasgow Coma Scale, Injury Severity Score, and Trauma and Injury Severity Score, standardize early assessment but rely largely on static variables and may not capture dynamic physiological changes during resuscitation. Dynamic biomarkers offer additional biological information. Glial fibrillary acidic protein, S100B, neuron-specific enolase, serum lactate, and lactate clearance may reflect neuronal injury, tissue hypoperfusion, metabolic stress, and how effectively resuscitation is restoring perfusion. Artificial intelligence and machine-learning models can integrate vital signs, laboratory findings, imaging data, biomarkers, and demographic variables to generate individualized predictions of mortality, clinical deterioration, intensive care requirements, and complications. Recent studies suggest that some machine-learning models may outperform conventional scoring systems; however, limitations involving data heterogeneity, algorithmic bias, interpretability, external validation, privacy, and clinical workflow integration remain. This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on conventional trauma scoring systems, dynamic biomarkers, and artificial intelligence-based prediction models for early risk stratification of severe trauma in emergency departments. It also examines the potential of multimodal frameworks that combine physiological, anatomical, molecular, and computational information. Although integrated prediction systems may improve accuracy and clinical decision-making, prospective multicenter validation and evidence of real-world clinical benefit are required before routine implementation.

Introduction

Severe trauma is one of the most significant global public health issues and the main cause of death and disability, especially in people under the age of 45 years. In emergency department (ED) environments, the trauma burden is disproportionately experienced by clinicians, who are required to quickly determine the extent of injury, high-risk patients, and provide time-sensitive interventions. The initial risk stratification is consequently at the core of the trauma management and it informs the resuscitation decision, surgical intervention, intensive care admission, and the resource allocation. Several trauma scoring systems have been invented during the last few decades to standardize the assessment of injuries and to estimate patient outcomes.1 The Revised Trauma Score (RTS) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) physiological scoring systems and the Injury Severity Score (ISS) and Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) anatomical scoring systems continue to be popular in trauma triage. Composite models like the Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) and more modern models like the Score for Trauma Triage in the Geriatric and Middle-Aged (STTGMA) and the Geriatric Trauma Outcome Score (GTOS) have tried to combine physiological, anatomical, and demographic factors to enhance the prognostic predictivity. According to recent comparative studies, the scoring systems continue to be useful in early triage and systems like TRISS and GCS have shown good predictive validity in mortality in emergency department trauma populations.2

Although they are widely used, traditional trauma scoring systems have significant limitations. The majority of them are based on the static variables that are measured at one time and might not be sufficient to determine dynamic physiological alterations that take place during the first stages of trauma resuscitation. Moreover, they tend not to consider personal biological variability, comorbidities, and changing pathophysiological mechanisms like systemic inflammation, hypoperfusion and secondary brain damage.3 These constraints decrease predictive accuracy in nonhomogeneous trauma groups and emphasize the necessity to implement more responsive and personalized risk assessment measures. Recently, there have been encouraging signs of new methods of incorporating biomarkers and computational intelligence to improve early trauma prognostication. Dynamically changing biomarkers especially those that monitor tissue damage and metabolic stress have become more and more popular. As a case in point, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) has become a sensitive biomarker of astroglial damage and has been shown to be useful in early detection and severity measurement of traumatic brain injury (TBI). Likewise, serum lactate and serial lactate-clearance patterns provide clinically relevant information regarding tissue hypoxia, shock severity, and how effectively resuscitation is restoring systemic perfusion.4 At the same time, the ongoing swift progress in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) has made it possible to create predictive models that can process complex clinical data in real-time. Risk prediction tools based on machine learning utilize huge datasets that include physiological parameters, laboratory outcomes and demographic factors to produce personalized outcome forecasts.4 As an example, a machine learning model developed to predict risks in the emergency department, called RISKINDEX, incorporates laboratory parameters and patient characteristics to predict mortality in the short-term and has shown very good discriminatory ability with reported AUC values ranging between 0.88 and 0.98 in verification studies. Additional studies have also examined more recent studies on multimodal machine learning models that combine vital signs, blood gas measurements, and lab indicators measured soon after arrival to the ED to predict in-hospital mortality in trauma patients. These models have been shown to predict better than traditional scoring models, showing the promise of AI-based decision support in trauma care.4,5

Regardless of these developments, there are still a number of issues that have to be overcome before these technologies can be widely used in clinical practice. Issues regarding transparency in model, biasing in algorithms, performance across different healthcare systems and the ability to integrate with current clinical processes should be addressed. Moreover, little research has empirically assessed the potential of combining biomarker-based testing and AI-based prediction models with conventional trauma scoring methods to develop multimodal risk stratification models.6 This narrative review will, therefore, be aimed at synthesizing existing evidence on the new methods of early prediction of trauma risk in the emergency department. Gap Paragraph

Despite substantial advances in trauma assessment, the available literature remains fragmented across conventional scoring systems, biomarker-based research, and artificial intelligence-based prediction models. Most studies have evaluated these approaches separately, with limited consideration of how physiological, anatomical, molecular, imaging, and computational data could be combined for real-time emergency-department decision-making. In addition, relatively few reviews have focused specifically on risk stratification during the early phases of trauma resuscitation, when patient physiology changes rapidly and decisions regarding imaging, transfusion, surgery, intensive care, and resource allocation must be made within a limited period.

A narrative-review design was selected because the topic encompasses heterogeneous clinical tools, biomarkers, machine-learning methods, study designs, and outcome measures that cannot be adequately addressed through a single narrowly defined systematic-review question. This approach permits clinically oriented integration of evidence from original clinical studies, systematic reviews, comparative studies, and emerging artificial intelligence research. The present review therefore critically examines the individual strengths and limitations of trauma scoring systems, dynamic biomarkers, and artificial intelligence models and explores their potential integration into multimodal risk-prediction frameworks for emergency trauma care.

Methodology

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize current evidence on early risk stratification of severe trauma in emergency-department settings. PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Embase were searched for relevant English-language publications from January 2000 to March 2026. The search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings, including “severe trauma”, “emergency department”, “risk stratification”, “trauma scoring systems”, “Revised Trauma Score”, “Injury Severity Score”, “Trauma and Injury Severity Score”, “biomarkers”, “lactate”, “lactate clearance”, “glial fibrillary acidic protein”, “S100B”, “machine learning”, and “artificial intelligence”.

Clinical studies, comparative studies, systematic reviews, and recent clinically relevant publications addressing early trauma assessment, prognostic modelling, dynamic biomarkers, and emergency-care applications were considered. Priority was given to studies that compared trauma scoring systems, evaluated the prognostic value of biomarkers, developed or validated machine-learning models, or examined the integration of clinical and laboratory data. Studies conducted exclusively in pediatric populations, isolated case reports, and studies unrelated to acute trauma management were excluded unless they provided landmark or directly relevant evidence.

A narrative approach was used because the review covers heterogeneous interventions, data types, populations, prediction methods, and outcomes. The review was therefore not conducted as a formal systematic review or meta-analysis and did not follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses framework. Evidence was organized thematically into conventional trauma scoring systems, dynamic biomarkers, artificial intelligence-based prediction, and multimodal integration to permit a clinically relevant evaluation of current developments, limitations, implementation challenges, and future research priorities.

Severe Trauma Burden and Risk Stratification in the Early Prediction

A large percentage of the mortality and morbidity in the world is a result of trauma and millions of people die each year because of road traffic accidents, falls, violence and other injuries. According to the World Health Organization, traumatic injuries are among the most common causes of death in the world especially amongst the young adults. In addition to death, trauma is a major cause of long-term disability, medical expenses, and socioeconomic impact.7 The front line of trauma management is the emergency departments. Within the first golden hour, clinicians should be fast in assessing injury severity, detecting life threatening, and prioritizing treatment plans, including surgical repair, transfusion, or intensive care hospitalization. Risk stratification tools are needed in this time sensitive environment to aid clinical decision making.8 Conventional methods of trauma measurement are based on a mix of clinical judgment and structured scoring systems. Physiological scoring systems like the Revised Trauma Score have implemented variables like systolic blood pressure, respiratory rate and the Glasgow Coma Scale to estimate the severity of the injury. Injury Severity Score is an anatomical scoring system that uses the location and extent of injuries to classify trauma. Such hybrid models such as TRISS integrate physiological and anatomical parameters to enhance prognostic accuracy.8 Despite the contribution of these tools in standardized assessment of trauma, their prediction ability is still poor in heterogeneous patient groups. Numerous trauma scoring systems were built based on historical data and might not represent current trauma care practice or critical care innovations. In addition, they are often based on the static measurements at admission that might not reflect the dynamic physiological conditions of patients in the early resuscitation phases.9

Recent comparative research has revealed that although many scoring systems maintain moderate predictive validity, their effectiveness varies based on the demographics of the patients, injury patterns and the clinical environments. Indicatively, RTS and other physiological scores can still be helpful in the quick triage yet are not sensitive in complex trauma (has multiple organ injuries or comorbidities). Conventional Trauma Scoring Systems Used for Risk Stratification in Emergency Departments

Clinical scoring systems, dynamic biomarkers, and artificial intelligence can be integrated to greatly enhance the early trauma risk stratification within the emergency department. Traditional scoring methods offer quick first-line measurement using physiological and anatomical data; but in complex traumas, they may not be highly predictive. Additional information on neurological injury and systemic hypoperfusion is provided by biomarkers like GFAP and lactate, which reflect underlying pathophysiological processes that might not be reflected by clinical scores. Recent development in machine learning allows the incorporation of all these various datasets into predictive algorithms that can create individual risk profiles in real time. Early risk stratification in the emergency department is not only important for prognostic assessment but also plays a crucial role in operational decision-making. Accurate early prediction enables timely trauma team activation, prioritization of imaging such as whole-body computed tomography, initiation of massive transfusion protocols, and early surgical intervention when required. In addition, appropriate triage reduces both undertriage, which may delay life-saving treatment, and overtriage, which can lead to unnecessary resource utilization.11 Therefore, effective early prediction tools are essential for optimizing patient outcomes and improving the efficiency of emergency care systems.

These heterogeneous data streams are integrated using artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms to generate individualized risk predictions, including mortality, need for intensive care, and risk of clinical deterioration. The resulting outputs support real-time clinical decision-making, enabling optimized triage, resource allocation, and early intervention in emergency department settings.

Clinical Trauma Scoring Systems: Existing Evidence and Limitations

Clinical trauma scoring systems have always been very crucial in gauging the extent of injury and determining outcomes and guiding decisions regarding the triage of the injured in emergency departments. These scoring systems are designed to normalize measurements of physiologic derangement and anatomy damage in trauma patients (or both), using quantitative measures. They assist clinicians in treating patients by offering objective indicators of the severity of the injury, distributing resources of critical care and estimate the prognosis of patients.12

Physiological Scoring Systems

Physiological scoring systems: Physiological scoring systems are used to assess the immediate physiological condition of trauma patients based on vital signs and neurological assessment of the patient taken during the initial clinical evaluation. The Revised Trauma Score (RTS) that includes the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), systolic blood pressure, and the respiratory rate is one of the most popular physiological scoring systems. RTS was created with the primary focus on prehospital triage and emergency department assessment to enable the quick recognition of patients with critically injured injuries that need urgent intervention.12 A number of studies have proven that the RTS is still one of the systems that can be used to triage early trauma because it is simple and easy to use. Since the score is based on easily measurable physiological parameters, it can be computed within a short time in emergency departments without the need of highly diagnostic tests. Nevertheless, the RTS has significant shortcomings even though it proves useful. There is a possibility that during the initial years of trauma resuscitation, physiological parameters can vary, and neurological and hemodynamic measurements can be affected by intoxication, sedation, or underlying medical conditions. Therefore, the RTS can be inaccurate to identify the severity of injuries in some groups of patients, especially those who have occult internal injuries, or whose physiological degradation occurs slowly.13

Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is another popular method of neurological evaluation of patients with trauma, particularly when it comes to traumatic brain injury (TBI). The GCS measures eye opening, verbal response and motor response and gives a score that is used to measure the level of consciousness. The GCS is an important element of various systems of trauma scoring, but its predictive utility in these patients may be less than ideal, especially when intubated or sedated, or when experiencing intoxication. These aspects may make it difficult to assess the neurology and make the score less reliable as an independent predictor of outcomes.14

Anatomical Scoring Systems

Anatomical scoring systems are directed at the structural scope and spread of wounds, but not physiological reactions. Injury Severity Score (ISS) is one of the most popular anatomical scoring tools in trauma research and clinical practice. It is based on the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) that classifies injuries by the body region based on its severity. ISS computes the cumulative injury burden by adding the squares of the top three highest scores of the AIS of the three most injured body areas.15

The ISS offers a common way of measuring the severity of injury and has been extensively used in trauma registries and outcome studies. Greater ISS values are highly correlated with a higher mortality, longer length of stay, and risk of complications. Nevertheless, the ISS also has significant drawbacks. Since it is a system of merely categorizing anatomical injuries, it fails to consider physiological status, comorbidities, or patient-specific factors that could affect outcomes. Also, the ISS can be computed only with a very detailed injury coding, which is not always readily available in the early stages of trauma assessment in the emergency department.16 The big clinical trauma scoring systems vary in the methodology, parameters and clinical usage. Combined Trauma Scoring Systems

In order to address the shortcomings of entirely physiological or anatomical scoring systems, a number of hybrid scoring systems have been created. One of the most popular models that combine physiological parameters of RTS, anatomical injury severity of ISS, age of the patient, and the nature of the injury is the Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS). TRISS is a method that provides the likelihood of survival of trauma patients and has been widely tested in trauma registries throughout the world.17 Even though TRISS offers a better predictive accuracy than individual scoring systems, it also has practical implications in emergency cases. It involves several variables, such as detailed injury classification and statistical modeling, which might restrict its applicability in real time in the first stages of trauma management. Moreover, the model was initially trained on historical trauma data, and its predictive quality might be less effective in other populations and health care systems.18

Within more recent times, specialized scoring systems, eg, the Score for Trauma Triage in the Geriatric and Middle-Aged (STTGMA) and the Geriatric Trauma Outcome Score (GTOS), have been created to consider the specifics of older trauma patients. The factors used in these models include age, comorbidities, injuries pattern as well as physiological parameters to enhance better prediction of risk in aging population. Such models offer useful information on age-specific trauma outcomes as the percentage of elderly trauma patients keeps rising all over the world.19 Nevertheless, they might not be applicable in younger age groups or in the environment where detailed patient information is not easily accessible. A number of studies have compared predictive mortality and clinical outcome of trauma scoring systems. Comparative evidence indicates that the predictive performance of trauma scoring systems varies according to patient age, injury pattern, timing of assessment, physiological condition, and the availability of anatomical information. Strengths of Traditional Trauma Scoring System

Although conventional trauma scoring systems are widely used and valuable clinically, they have a number of limitations inherent to them. First, the majority of scoring systems are based on the use of static measurements at one point in time, usually at first patient examination. Trauma patients are known to change physiologically quickly during resuscitation, and fixed scoring systems might not be able to reflect the dynamic processes. Second, most scoring systems fail to sufficiently consider personal differences in trauma response.8 Preexisting medical conditions, medication use, genetic susceptibility and environmental influences are some of the factors that may have a significant impact on patient outcomes, but which are often not included in the conventional scoring models. Third, standard scoring methods might not capture the full spectrum of pathophysiological processes in case of severe trauma, such as systemic inflammation, metabolic abnormalities, and secondary organ damage. Consequently, they might not be very predictive in patients with multi-organ trauma or with dynamic clinical progressions. The constraints have led to growing attention to dynamical and data-driven methods of trauma risk prediction. Promising opportunities are in improving prognostic accuracy through utilizing biomarkers that capture underlying biological processes and machine learning models that can process large and complex datasets. Combining physiological information, molecular indicators, and computational analytics, these new methods can offer more detailed and individualized risk evaluation during trauma treatment.24

Critical Comparison Paragraph

Although trauma scoring systems have contributed significantly to standardized assessment, their clinical utility varies depending on timing, data availability, and patient characteristics. Physiological scores such as the Revised Trauma Score are advantageous in early triage due to their rapid bedside applicability, whereas anatomical scores such as the Injury Severity Score provide a more comprehensive assessment of injury burden but require detailed diagnostic information that may not be immediately available. Combined models like the Trauma and Injury Severity Score improve predictive accuracy but are often limited by computational complexity and reliance on retrospective datasets. Importantly, most scoring systems demonstrate acceptable discrimination but variable calibration across different populations, highlighting the need for more adaptive and individualized prediction models.

Dynamic Biomarkers in Trauma Risk Prediction

Traditional systems of evaluating trauma scores are mainly based on physiological variables and classification of injury by anatomy to predict patient outcomes. Although these tools are useful in giving valuable information during the initial evaluation of trauma patients, it might fail to give a complete picture of the underlying biological processes elicited by severe injury. Trauma triggers a cascade of pathophysiological effects, such as the systemic inflammation, metabolic imbalance, tissue hypoxia, and cell damage. Biomarkers of these processes have become promising to enhance early risk stratification and outcome prediction in trauma care.25 Dynamic biomarkers are especially beneficial as they can give real-time information on the severity and progression of injury. Contrary to static clinical variables, the level of biomarkers can change during the initial stages of trauma resuscitation and enable clinicians to track the success of treatment and identify complications at their initial stages. Some of these biomarkers have been explored in trauma populations such as neuronal injury, metabolic stress, and systemic inflammation.26

Neurological Injury Biomarkers

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in patients with trauma. Timely detection of the neurological injury is critical in directing the imaging, ascertaining the prognosis, as well as commencing relevant neurocritical care measures. Neuron and glial cell-derived biomarkers have become of significant interest in the detection of brain injury and prediction of outcome. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is one of the most widely researched biomarkers in this regard. GFAP is a middle-range filament protein that is mainly expressed in astrocytes of the central nervous system. In astrocytes after traumatic brain injury, the release of GFAP into the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid occurs, when the cells are damaged.26 High levels of GFAP have been found to be associated with the degree of brain injury and the presence of intracranial lesions in neuroimaging. There are several clinical studies that have shown that GFAP can be a sensitive biomarker in the early diagnosis of TBI, even mild cases that would not necessarily be immediately evident when relying solely on clinical examination. S100B is another calcium-binding protein that is a widely studied biomarker of neuronal injury, and is located in astrocytes and Schwann cells. Increased serum concentrations of S100B have been associated with blood–brain barrier disruption and neuronal damage. Due to its comparatively fast release after injury, S100B has been considered as a screening option to determine patients that might need neuroimaging. Nevertheless, S100B can also increase in extracranial traumas, which can restrict its specificity in polytrauma patients.27

In addition to glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase-L1 (UCH-L1) has emerged as a complementary biomarker for neuronal injury. UCH-L1 is a neuron-specific protein involved in ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation and is rapidly released into circulation following neuronal damage. Studies have demonstrated that the combination of GFAP and UCH-L1 improves diagnostic accuracy for detecting intracranial injury and predicting outcomes in traumatic brain injury. These biomarkers have also been incorporated into point-of-care diagnostic platforms, highlighting their potential clinical applicability in emergency settings.

Another neuron-specific enolase (NSE) is a neuron injury marker that has been studied in the trauma population. NSE is a neurons and neuroendocrine cell enzyme that gets released after neuronal damage. High NSE is linked to low neurological outcomes in severely traumatized brain injury patients. Despite this, NSE measurements can be affected by hemolysis and other systemic processes, which can make them difficult to interpret in a clinical environment, regardless of the promise.28 Trauma causes complex cascade pathophysiological reactions, such as systemic inflammation, metabolic imbalances, tissue hypoxia, and cell damage. Biomarkers of Tissue Hypoxia and Metabolic Stress

Besides the neurological damage, trauma often leads to systemic hypoperfusion and metabolic imbalances that lead to organ dysfunction and death. The biomarkers of tissue oxygenation and metabolic stress are thus useful in measuring the extent of shock and resuscitation outcome. Serum lactate has been identified as one of the most common biomarkers that are used to indicate tissue hypoxia. High levels of lactate indicate poor oxygen supply and anaerobic metabolism, typical of hemorrhagic shock or severe trauma. Several reports have indicated that high levels of lactate on admission to the hospital are linked to high mortality rates, prolonged hospitalization, and high chances of developing complications.9 In addition to single lactate values, lactate clearance rate at which the lactate levels fall during resuscitation has become a significant dynamic value of patient recovery. An increase in lactate clearance rates is normally linked to good outcomes, whereas the continued elevation in lactate level can be an indication of continuing tissue hypoperfusion or insufficient resuscitation. The trend of lactate with a period of time is thus a useful prognostic tool and can be used to make therapeutic decisions in the initial phases of trauma care. In trauma populations, other biomarkers of systemic inflammation and infection have been investigated. An example of this is procalcitonin (PCT), which is a calcitonin precursor peptide, which rises with bacterial infection and systemic inflammatory reactions. High levels of PCT after trauma have been linked to the risk of sepsis and other complications. Though it does not depend on the severity of trauma-related injury, PCT can be helpful in terms of secondary complications in critically injured patients.10

In addition to lactate, other physiological and metabolic indicators such as base deficit, shock index, arterial blood gas parameters, and coagulation markers (eg, international normalized ratio and platelet count) have been widely studied in trauma populations. Base deficit, in particular, is a sensitive indicator of metabolic acidosis and correlates strongly with hemorrhagic shock severity and mortality risk. Similarly, shock index, defined as the ratio of heart rate to systolic blood pressure, provides a simple bedside marker of hemodynamic instability. These parameters, when combined with lactate dynamics, may enhance early identification of high-risk trauma patients.

Biomarkers in Clinical Integration Trauma Care

Although there is an accumulating amount of evidence to support the use of biomarkers in trauma care, the use of biomarkers in clinical practice remains minimal. This challenge is caused by several factors such as inconsistency in the methods used to measure biomarkers, the differences in populations used to study them and the fact that there is no standardized threshold value to be used in clinical decision making. Moreover, single biomarkers might only give incomplete information about the multifaceted pathophysiological events related to trauma. To illustrate, neuronal injury biomarkers are mostly an indicator of brain damage, and metabolic biomarkers like lactate are a source of information on systemic perfusion.11 Therefore, it might not be sufficient to rely on one biomarker to reflect the complexity of severe trauma. Recent studies have thus concentrated on multimodal biomarker methodology, whereby several biomarkers are measured at the same time to enhance predictive power. While biomarkers provide important insights into the biological mechanisms underlying trauma, their predictive performance may be further enhanced through integration with advanced computational methods. Recent developments in artificial intelligence and machine learning have enabled the analysis of large and complex clinical datasets, offering new opportunities for improving early trauma risk prediction. Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Trauma Risk Prediction

The development of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) has brought about new possibilities to enhance the risk prediction in trauma care. Older trauma scoring systems are based on pre-established variables and statistical models which might not be able to represent the complex and nonlinear associations between clinical variables and patient outcomes. Machine learning algorithms on the other hand can examine large and heterogeneous data sets and thus be able to identify patterns that cannot be easily identified by traditional methods of analysis. These features have made AI a potential device to improve early risk stratification and decision-making in the management of emergency departments in trauma.30

Various clinical data, such as physiological measurements, laboratory biomarkers, imaging results, and demographic features, can be included in machine learning models. With the combination of these multidimensional datasets, ML models will be able to make personalized predictions regarding clinically relevant outcomes, including mortality, intensive-care requirement, complications, and length of stay. General AI-based trauma predicting systems are characterized by multiple steps, such as data acquisition, pre-processing and feature engineering, creating a model with the help of machine learning algorithms, model validation and producing clinically relevant predictions31 (Figure 3). Machine Learning Approaches Used for Trauma Outcome Prediction

The recent research has shown that machine learning models can be more effective in predicting patient outcomes than conventional trauma scoring systems. Indicatively, the predictive accuracy of mortality of ML-based models trained with trauma registry data has been found to be more accurate than traditional scoring methods like RTS and ISS. Likewise, clinical and laboratory data collected in the initial moments of emergency department admission have been shown to improve the performance of predictive models in identifying high-risk patients with trauma. A prominent illustration of an AI-based risk prediction tool is RISKINDEX, a machine learning model, which is created to estimate a patient mortality risk in the emergency department in the short term. The model combines regular lab parameters and clinical variables to produce custom risk scores. Validation studies have indicated high discriminative performance with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of about 0.90 or above in multiple cohorts. These results have demonstrated the possible benefits of ML-based models to improve early prognostic evaluation in acute care centers.33

Although these are encouraging factors, there are a number of issues that need to be resolved before trauma prediction models based on AI could become a routine in clinical care. A notable drawback is that of model interpretability. Lots of machine learning models can be viewed as black box, and clinicians can hardly find out how certain predictions are made. Such insufficiency in transparency can undermine trust in clinicians and slow down clinical implementation. Approaches to enhancing the explainability of models, including feature importance analysis and interpretable machine learning models, are thus becoming actively investigated. The other issue is associated with the quality of data and generalizability.34 The quality and representativeness of datasets used to train machine learning models is extremely critical. The models created based on the information about one institution or a particular group of patients can be ineffective in different healthcare contexts. To achieve reliability and robustness, external validation in varied populations and health care systems is therefore necessary. Another significant ethical and regulatory factor in the deployment of AI-based clinical decision-support systems is ethical and regulatory concerns. The problems of data privacy, algorithmic bias, and accountability should be thoroughly considered to make sure that AI technologies can be used safely and responsibly in trauma care.35 Nonetheless, artificial intelligence paired with conventional tools of clinical assessment and biomarkers has great potential to be applied in trauma risk prediction. Future multimodal prediction models can potentially help make better, prompt, and personal decisions during emergency trauma management by integrating structured clinical scoring systems, dynamic biomarker monitoring, and sophisticated computational analytics.

While artificial intelligence provides powerful analytical capabilities, the greatest potential may lie in integrating clinical scoring systems, biomarkers, and machine learning models into unified multimodal prediction frameworks. The following section discusses current challenges and future directions for implementing such integrated approaches in trauma care.36

Principles of Machine Learning Model Development and Validation

Many key steps are required in the development of machine learning models to predict trauma risk that include data preprocessing, feature selection, model training, and validation. The quality of the data and the lack of missingness as well as the adequate treatment of the class imbalance are the keys to the reliable performance of the model. The feature engineering is important in determining clinically relevant predictors, which include vital signs, laboratory values, and demographic variables. Model validation is also a very important aspect and involves internal validation methods like cross-validation and external validation using independent datasets. The model accuracy is usually assessed using performance measures, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration plots, sensitivity, and specificity. Moreover, recent research highlights the role of explainability in clinical machine learning models and explainability approaches. Additive exPlanations (SHAP) help to understand the importance of features and increase clinician trust. To translate AI-based models into clinical practice, strong validation and interpretability are necessary.

Integration of Clinical Scores, Biomarkers, and Artificial Intelligence for Multimodal Trauma Risk Prediction

Although all three traditional trauma scoring systems, dynamic biomarkers, and artificial intelligence models are valuable in understanding the prognosis of trauma, none of them can predict it effectively on their own. There has been an increase in studies aimed at incorporating these approaches into multimodal risk prediction models that consider both physiological, biological, and computational data to improve the precision of early trauma measurements.37 Clinical scoring systems like the Revised Trauma Score (RTS), Injury Severity Score (ISS) and Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) offer systematic approaches to measuring injury severity based on physiological and anatomical measures. The use of these tools is common in emergency rooms as they are easy to compute and offer quick estimates of patient risk. Their predictive accuracy, however, could be weak in heterogeneous traumatic populations because they use static measurements which were taken during the initial assessment of the patient.6

Dynamic biomarkers provide complementary data since they mirror the underlying biological processes activated by trauma. Biomarkers of neuronal injury, eg glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B) and neuron-specific enolase (NSE), can give information about the severity of traumatic brain injury. In the same manner, metabolic biomarkers like the levels of lactate and lactic clearance demonstrate systematic hypoperfusion and resuscitation success. Procalcitonin is an inflammatory marker that can be used to detect patients with the risk of infection and secondary complications. These biomarkers could be useful in combination with clinical scoring systems to identify high-risk trauma patients earlier and to offer further prognostic data.20

The machine learning models and artificial intelligence also increase the possibilities of risk prediction of trauma by allowing the incorporation of massive and multi-faceted data. Machine learning algorithms are able to detect nonlinear relationships and interactions between many variables in a single model, unlike traditional statistical models. This ability enables AI to integrate various types of data such as physiological measurements, laboratory biomarkers, imaging results, and patient demographics in predictive models.38

Some studies have found that multimodal strategies involving a combination of clinical variables and biomarker information can be useful in outcome prediction significantly than traditional scoring systems. Multimodal machine learning models that have been trained on multimodal datasets have demonstrated better performance in mortality, intensive care unit, and complications prediction of trauma patients. These models can create personalized risk predictions, potentially improving informed clinical decision-making, by combining structured clinical scores with molecular biomarkers and sophisticated computational techniques. The possibility to provide real-time clinical decision support systems in emergency departments is one of the main benefits of multimodal prediction frameworks. These systems might continuously analyze patient data as it is received during resuscitation of trauma patients, to update risk estimates and warn clinicians about possible complications.22 To illustrate, real-time vital signs monitoring combined with laboratory biomarkers and machine learning algorithms may aid in the detection of patients who are likely to deteriorate sooner than the conventional assessment protocols.

Although these developments are promising, a number of challenges have to be overcome to effectively apply multimodal trauma prediction systems in the clinical practice. The consolidation of information across various sources such as electronic health records, laboratory systems, and imaging platforms is one of the significant challenges. It is necessary to ensure that these systems can interact with each other to facilitate the smooth exchange of data and real-time analysis. The other critical point to be made is that there is a necessity to have strong validation of multimodal prediction models in a variety of patient groups and healthcare environments. The majority of the current research has been performed with the help of a single institution retrospective dataset, which can lead to the lack of generalizability to other populations. Prospective multicenter studies on a large scale are thus required to determine the effectiveness and reliability of integrated prediction frameworks.22

Current Evidence, Challenges, and Future Directions in Multimodal Trauma Risk Prediction

Despite the success in predicting trauma risks, there is a lack of clinical applications of emerging prediction tools to general practice. Conventional trauma scoring systems are fast at bedside but are not always sensitive enough to identify physiological deterioration of a complex nature. Likewise, individual biomarkers indicate certain pathophysiological mechanisms but might not reflect the multifactoriality of the responses to trauma. The predictive models developed with artificial intelligence provide better analytical power, but they demand the use of datasets of high quality and strong clinical validation prior to their widespread adoption.40 Research on multimodal predictive frameworks where clinical scoring systems, biological markers, and machine-learning algorithms are combined has gained greater attention recently. Likewise, research of biomarkers is also growing, particularly in relation to traumatic brain injury. The biomarkers glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and UCH-L1 have been shown to be effective in the diagnostic and prognostic values of detecting intracranial injury and neurological prognosis. Moreover, metabolic indicators of systemic hypoperfusion including lactate are useful markers of systemic hypoperfusion and are commonly used in emergency departments to measure the severity of trauma and mortality risk.46

There is a growing application of AI to trauma datasets with the aim of creating predictive models that can recognize high-risk patients soon after arriving at the emergency department. The most common variables that are used in these models include vital signs, laboratory values, and injury characteristics gathered in the initial minutes of patient assessment. Machine learning models that have incorporated such data have shown better results than conventional trauma scoring systems in making in-hospital mortality predictions.43,44,47–49 Although these advancements have been made, there are a number of challenges. Data heterogeneity is one of the key limitations because trauma data can differ greatly across institutions due to the differences in their patient population, injury mechanisms, and clinical variables available. This variability can limit the generalizability of predictive models developed using single-center datasets. Moreover, most machine learning systems are black-box systems, and clinicians cannot easily understand the logic behind predictions. Improving the interpretability of AI models by using explainable AI methods is thus a valuable area of research interest.45,50–53

The other important issue is associated with future clinical validation. Various predictive models are created on the basis of the retrospective databases and have not been tested in real-time clinical context yet. Multicenter trials should also be considered in the future to establish if integrated predictive models can have a beneficial impact on patient outcomes and aid clinical decision-making during emergency trauma care.49 Moreover, ethical issues such as algorithm bias, patient privacy, and transparency will have to be considered prior to the complete integration of AI-based clinical decision-support systems into trauma workflows. It will be important to ensure that predictive algorithms are trained on a wide range of data and that they are tested on various healthcare systems to reduce bias and enhance reliability.43

Future research should focus on the development of interpretable and clinically applicable multimodal prediction models that integrate physiological data, biomarkers, imaging findings, and machine learning algorithms. Large-scale multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these models across diverse populations and healthcare systems. Standardization of biomarker measurement protocols and timing will also be important to improve reproducibility. In addition, future models should incorporate dynamic prediction capabilities, allowing risk estimates to be updated continuously as new clinical data become available. The integration of wearable monitoring devices and real-time data analytics may further enhance early detection of clinical deterioration. Finally, impact studies evaluating whether these predictive tools improve clinical outcomes, resource utilization, and decision-making processes will be essential to justify their implementation in routine trauma care.

Conclusion

Severe trauma still is a significant global health burden and one of the primary causes of morbidity and mortality, especially among the younger population. Risk stratification in the early stages of emergency departments is thus critical in informing prompt clinical actions, the allocation of resources, and patient outcomes. Classical trauma scoring instruments like the Revised Trauma Score (RTS), Injury Severity Score (ISS), and Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) have been a time-tested instrument to measure injury severity and outcomes. These models offer systematic and standardized methods to trauma assessment, but as they are based on a set of parameters that are constant in clinical terms they are incapable of capturing the dynamic and multifaceted physiologic responses of severe trauma. The rise of dynamic biomarkers has provided novel possibilities to improve early trauma prognostication through underlying biological events, including neuronal damage, tissue hypoxia, and systemic inflammation. Biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), S100B, neuron-specific enolase (NSE), and lactate have shown to be promising clinical tools used to identify certain injury mechanisms as well as to monitor patient response to resuscitation. However, single biomarkers can only give partial information on the severity of the trauma and it is necessary to use integrated methods that use molecular indicators along with clinical assessment methods.

The recent developments in machine learning and artificial intelligence have only increased the possibilities of enhancing the process of trauma risk prediction. ML-based predictive models have the potential to combine extensive data on heterogeneous clinical data such as physiological parameters, laboratory biomarkers, imaging results, and demographic features to produce personalized risk forecasts. Other studies have shown that the models can be better than traditional trauma scoring systems in terms of prediction of mortality, intensive care unit admission, and complications. In spite of such encouraging trends, issues concerning interpretability of the models, heterogeneity of the data, external validation, and ethical concerns need to be resolved to enable the achievement of extensive clinical utilization.

The combination of clinical scoring system, dynamic biomarkers and artificial intelligence is a developing paradigm of improving the practice of trauma care. Multimodal predictive models integrating physiological measurements, molecular diagnostics and computational analytics could offer more precise, real-time and personalized risk stratification of trauma patients in the emergency department. The future studies should focus on design of interpretable and clinically applicable predictive models and prospective multicenter validation study to assess their usefulness in a variety of healthcare settings. With the ongoing development of digital health technologies and data-driven medicine, the incorporation of multimodal predictive tools in clinical workflow can considerably advance the early trauma assessment and decision-making. Finally, these technologies can enhance the accuracy of triage, efficiency of resource use, and decrease morbidity and mortality in patients with severe trauma. Ultimately, the transition from traditional static assessment models to dynamic, multimodal, and data-driven prediction systems represents a paradigm shift in trauma care. The successful integration of clinical expertise with advanced computational tools has the potential to transform emergency medicine by enabling earlier intervention, more precise risk stratification, and improved patient-centered outcomes.
