The effect of sleep quality and depressive symptoms on the relationship between burnout and health-related productivity loss in nurses working in intensive care: a cross-sectional study from the Chinese nurses’ health cohort study (TARGET)
Authors: Xueyan Liu, Juan Kong, Xiaodong Yuan, Qian Ma, Zhen Zhang, Patricia M Davidson, Yingjuan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout is highly prevalent among ICU nurses and compromises physical and psychological well-being, leading to productivity loss. However, limited evidence exists regarding the pathways through which burnout influences productivity, particularly the role of sleep disturbance and depression.AimFew studies have examined the mechanisms through which burnout affects health-related productivity in intensive care unit (ICU) nurses. This study addresses this gap by investigating the sequential mediating role of sleep quality and depressive symptoms in the relationship between burnout and health-related productivity loss.DesignA cross-sectional study.MethodsBaseline data from the Chinese Nurses’ Health Cohort Study, collected between December 2020 and February 2024, included 3,491 ICU nurses. Burnout, health-related productivity loss, sleep quality and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Human Services Survey, Stanford Presenteeism Scale, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Chain mediation analysis was conducted using the SPSS PROCESS macro, and subgroup analyses were performed.ResultsData from 3,491 ICU nurses were analyzed. Subgroup analyses showed female nurses aged 18–30 years, with a master’s degree or above, senior titles, contract positions, clinical roles, and 6–10 years of experience reported higher productivity loss. Productivity loss correlated with burnout, poor sleep quality, and depressive symptoms. Mediation analysis using Bootstrap resampling (5,000 iterations) revealed that burnout had a significant total effect on productivity loss (β = 0.1019, 95% CI [0.0963, 0.1076]). The direct effect remained significant after accounting for mediators (β = 0.0587, 95% CI [0.0527, 0.0647]). The sequential indirect effect through sleep quality and depressive symptoms was significant (β = 0.0422, 95% CI [0.0395, 0.0469]), indicating that burnout impaired sleep quality, which increased depressive symptoms and ultimately contributed to greater productivity loss.ConclusionThe relationship between burnout and health-related productivity is mediated by sleep quality and depressive symptoms. Interventions reducing burnout, improving sleep quality, and preventing depression may help mitigate productivity loss.RegistrationThe study protocol for TARGET was registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2100043202, February 8, 2021, https://trialsearch.who.int/Default.aspx).Keywords: Nurses, Intensive care unit, Burnout, Sleep quality, Depressive symptoms, Health-related productivity loss

Introduction

The intensive care unit (ICU) provides continuous monitoring and advanced treatment for critically ill patients with unstable conditions and a high risk of rapid deterioration [1]. ICU nurses play an indispensable role in ensuring patient survival through continuous assessment, timely clinical interventions, and the management of advanced life-support equipment. However, the global nursing workforce is currently facing escalating shortages and increasing levels of occupational burnout. Growing evidence indicates that nurses are experiencing substantial physical and emotional strain, reduced job satisfaction, and heightened turnover intention [2, 3]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in high-intensity environments such as the ICU, where rapid decision-making, sustained vigilance, and frequent exposure to life-threatening events accelerate occupational stress.

The Effort–Recovery Theory posits that sustained job demands consume psychological and physiological resources, and insufficient recovery leads to cumulative impairment [4]. Within this framework, chronic occupational stress may first manifest as burnout, reflecting emotional exhaustion and diminished professional efficacy. ICU nurses are especially vulnerable to burnout due to moral distress, heavy workloads, and the demanding nature of critical care practice [5]. A meta-analysis estimated that approximately 30% of nurses experience burnout [6]. Importantly, burnout is not merely an emotional outcome but a state of resource depletion that may initiate downstream physiological and psychological impairments [7].

Emerging evidence suggests that burnout is closely linked to sleep disturbance. Nurses frequently experience poor sleep quality, largely due to rotating shift schedules that disrupt circadian rhythm regulation and melatonin secretion [8]. One study reported that 75.5% of nurses experience poor sleep quality [9]. Healthcare professionals with higher burnout levels consistently report poorer sleep quality [10], while improved sleep has been identified as a protective factor against burnout [11]. From an effort–recovery perspective, sleep represents a primary physiological recovery mechanism. When burnout interferes with restorative sleep, physiological recovery becomes impaired, further exacerbating functional decline.

Sleep disturbance has important implications for both cognitive and emotional functioning. Poor sleep quality is associated with diminished occupational performance, impaired clinical decision-making, and increased risk of work-related injuries [12]. In ICU settings, where rapid and accurate judgment is critical, such cognitive impairment may directly compromise productivity. In addition to cognitive deficits, insufficient sleep disrupts emotional regulation and increases vulnerability to depressive symptoms. ICU nurses are frequently exposed to extended working hours, rotating shifts, and intensive patient contact, contributing to psychological stress [13]. The prevalence of depressive symptoms among ICU nurses has been reported to reach 21% [14]. Burnout has been positively associated with depressive symptoms among medical staff [15], and a meta-analysis confirmed a strong association between burnout and depression in nurses [16].

Depressive symptoms, characterized by persistent low mood, fatigue, and impaired concentration, may further undermine work performance. Stress levels and emotional well-being are recognized determinants of occupational productivity. Prior research indicates that depressive symptoms are associated with health-related productivity loss [17]. Health-related productivity loss, encompassing absenteeism and presenteeism, reflects reduced work performance due to health impairments [18, 19]. Nurses may be particularly vulnerable to productivity loss [20, 21]. In China, cultural expectations emphasizing dedication and perseverance may increase the likelihood of working while unwell, thereby exacerbating presenteeism and its consequences [22].

Although previous studies have separately examined associations between burnout and productivity loss, burnout and sleep disturbance, and burnout and depressive symptoms, few have integrated these relationships into a sequential explanatory pathway, particularly among ICU nurses. Given the high-stakes and cognitively demanding nature of ICU practice, resource depletion may progress more rapidly in this population, amplifying the impact of impaired recovery on performance outcomes. Guided by the Effort–Recovery Theory, this study therefore examined the association between burnout and health-related productivity loss among ICU nurses in China and investigated whether sleep quality and depressive symptoms sequentially mediate this relationship. By clarifying the physiological and psychological recovery mechanisms linking occupational stress to functional impairment, this study seeks to advance understanding of productivity loss in high-acuity clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study, with a cross-sectional design, was conducted and reported following the STROBE statement [23]. Figure 1 presents the research hypotheses of this study.

Hypothesized relationships among job burnout, sleep quality, depression symptoms, and health-related productivity loss

Participants and data collection

The Chinese Nurses’ Health Cohort Study (TARGET), initiated in 2020, is a prospective, nationally representative longitudinal study in China that collects data via structured online surveys. During the baseline phase (December 2020 to February 2024), 105 hospitals across 36 cities in 15 provinces participated. All registered nurses aged 18 years and older employed at participating hospitals were invited to participate. Details of the study design have been reported previously [20, 24, 25]. For the present study, inclusion criteria were: (1) registered ICU nurses, (2) aged ≥ 18 years, and (3) completion of all relevant study questionnaires. Exclusion criteria were: (1) administrative nurses not engaged in clinical ICU work, (2) nurses on extended leave during data collection, and (3) questionnaires with substantial missing data or logical inconsistencies. Among all eligible ICU nurses in the baseline cohort (n = 4107), a total of 3,491 completed valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 85%. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Variables and Instruments

Demographic characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, including age, gender, education, marital status, employment type, professional title, position, monthly income level, and work experience, were collected using a self-designed questionnaire.

Burnout

The Chinese version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) [26] was used to assess burnout. The scale consists of 22 items across three dimensions, emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment. Responses are rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 6 (“every day”), with higher scores indicating greater levels of burnout. This version has been previously validated among Chinese doctors and nurses [26]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.94.

Health-related productivity loss

The Chinese version of the Stanford Presenteeism Scale (SPS-6) [27] was used to assess health-related productivity loss. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). The total score ranges from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater levels of health-related productivity loss. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.95.

Sleep quality

The Chinese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (CPSQI) [28] was used to measure sleep quality. The scale comprises 19 items across seven dimensions, with responses rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3. Total scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores reflecting poorer sleep quality. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.93.

Depressive symptoms

We used the Chinese version of Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [29, 30] to assess depressive symptoms. Participants were asked to rate the frequency of each symptom experienced over the past two weeks using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). The total PHQ-9 score ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting greater severity of depressive symptoms. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.95.

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Given the large sample size (n = 3,491), the central limit theorem supports the assumption that the sampling distribution of the estimates approximates normality, even if the underlying variables deviate from perfect normality. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations among burnout, sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and health-related productivity loss. Chain mediation analysis was conducted using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro developed by Hayes [31]. The adequacy of the sample size for detecting indirect effects in mediation analysis was supported by the large sample (n > 3,000), which exceeds conventional recommendations for stable estimation of bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals in complex mediation models [32]. The significance of indirect effects was tested via bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. An indirect effect was deemed statistically significant if the CI did not include 0. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University in February 2021. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and risks, and were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without any consequences.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, several procedures were implemented. The online survey platform restricted multiple submissions from the same device or IP address. Questionnaires with substantial missing data (> 10%), inconsistent responses, or implausible completion times were excluded. Additionally, logical consistency checks were embedded within the survey to minimize response bias. These measures were implemented to enhance the reliability and validity of the collected data.

Analyses of chain mediating effects

The mediation model is illustrated in Fig. 2, and detailed results are presented in Table 3. The reported β coefficients represent unstandardized regression coefficients. After adjusting for age, gender, education, appointment type, professional title, position, and work experience, burnout was a significant positive predictor of both sleep quality (β = 0.062, P < 0.01) and depressive symptoms (β = 0.123, P < 0.01). The direct effect of burnout on health-related productivity loss was also statistically significant (β = 0.059, P < 0.01). Moreover, sleep quality was found to have a significant positive effect on depressive symptoms (β = 0.818, P < 0.01). Both sleep quality and depressive symptoms were significantly correlated with health-related productivity loss (β = 0.125, P < 0.01; β = 0.205, P < 0.01, respectively). Bootstrap analysis based on 5,000 resamples demonstrated that the total effect of burnout on health-related productivity loss was significant (β = 0.1019, 95% bias-corrected CI [0.0963, 0.1076]). After including the mediators, the direct effect remained significant (β = 0.0587, 95% CI [0.0527, 0.0647]), accounting for 57.62% of the total effect. The mediating effect was 0.0422 (95% CI [0.0395, 0.0469]), accounting for 41.41% of the total effect. More specifically, burnout could influence health-related productivity loss through three pathways: (a) Burnout→Sleep quality→Health-related productivity loss (indirect effect = 0.0077, 95% CI [0.0055, 0.0099]), accounting for 7.56% of the total effect; (b) Burnout→Depressive symptoms→Health-related productivity loss (indirect effect = 0.0252, 95% CI [0.0223, 0.0282]), accounting for 24.73% of the total effect; (c) Burnout→Sleep quality→Depressive symptoms→Health-related productivity loss (chain indirect effect = 0.0103, 95% CI [0.0088, 0.0119]), accounting for 10.11% of the total effect.

Common method bias test

Because all variables were assessed using self-report questionnaires, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to examine potential common method bias. Exploratory factor analysis without rotation showed that the first unrotated factor accounted for 30.47% of the total variance, which was below the recommended threshold of 40%, suggesting that common method bias was unlikely to substantially influence the results.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides novel evidence for the sequential mediating roles of sleep quality and depressive symptoms in the relationship between burnout and health-related productivity loss among ICU nurses. These findings have significant implications for strategies that can implement to mitigate health-related productivity loss.

Consistent with our expectations, the results demonstrate a significant positive effect of burnout on health-related productivity loss, indicating that as burnout increases, health-related productivity loss becomes more severe. This finding is in line with previous research. One study has shown that burnout increases health-related productivity loss [33]. Similarly, another study found that elevated emotional exhaustion contributed to increased absenteeism and presenteeism [34]. This relationship may be particularly pronounced in ICU settings due to the high cognitive and emotional demands of critical care nursing. ICU nurses must continuously monitor unstable patients, perform rapid clinical decision-making, and manage life-support equipment under time pressure. According to the Job Demand-Resource Model, sustained exposure to high workload without sufficient recovery leads to progressive depletion of psychological and cognitive resources, which may reduce attentional control, emotional regulation capacity, and clinical decision-making efficiency [35]. Such cognitive and emotional impairments may increase the likelihood of clinical errors and presenteeism, thereby contributing to productivity loss [36]. Therefore, rather than simply reducing burnout levels, organizational interventions should also aim to improve recovery opportunities and workload management in ICU environments.

This study found that sleep quality partially mediates the relationship between burnout and health-related productivity loss among ICU nurses. A meta-analysis demonstrated that elevated levels of burnout among nurses were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of sleep disturbances [11]. From a psychological perspective, this association may be explained by work-related rumination, defined as the inability to mentally disengage from work-related stressors during non-working hours [37]. Persistent cognitive preoccupation with clinical events, patient outcomes, or potential medical errors may maintain physiological and psychological arousal, thereby disrupting sleep initiation and sleep continuity. Physiologically, chronic occupational stress may activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and increase cortisol secretion, leading to circadian rhythm disruption and sleep disturbance [38]. Furthermore, poor sleep quality has been shown to be significantly related to health-related productivity loss [39, 40]. Sleep plays a critical role in neurocognitive restoration and emotional regulation. Sleep deprivation impairs memory consolidation, attention, and executive functioning [41], which are essential for maintaining high-quality clinical performance in ICU settings. In addition, sleep deprivation reduces stress tolerance and weakens physiological recovery capacity, increasing the likelihood that nurses will work while experiencing physical or psychological discomfort in order to avoid increasing the workload of colleagues. Such presenteeism behavior may further exacerbate productivity loss [38].

This study demonstrates that depressive symptoms as a mediator in the relationship between burnout and health-related productivity loss among ICU nurses. Previous studies have consistently identified burnout as a strong predictor of depressive symptoms [15, 42]. ICU nurses are particularly vulnerable to emotional dysregulation due to prolonged exposure to patient mortality, ethical dilemmas, and high-intensity clinical workloads [43]. Depressive symptoms, characterized by low mood, fatigue, impaired concentration, and reduced motivation, can directly impair occupational performance [44, 45]. From the perspective of the Effort–Recovery Theory, prolonged exposure to high job demands without adequate recovery may lead to cumulative psychological strain, emotional exhaustion, and depressive symptoms, which subsequently impair work performance. Sleep disturbance may further lower the threshold for depressive symptom development by impairing emotion regulation processes. Sleep is essential for regulating limbic system activity and maintaining prefrontal control over emotional responses [46]. Sleep deprivation has also been associated with increased inflammatory responses, including elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), which have been implicated in depressive symptom pathophysiology [47].

Furthermore, the present study identified a significant sequential chain mediating pathway involving sleep quality and depressive symptoms. Severe burnout may first impair sleep quality, which subsequently increases vulnerability to depressive symptoms and ultimately contributes to productivity loss. Burnout has been associated with fatigue, negative interpersonal attitudes, and decreased job satisfaction [48]. These psychological and physiological consequences may gradually exhaust nurses’ emotional and physical energy reserves, thereby impairing work performance and productivity [49]. Depressive symptoms among nurses have been associated with increased absenteeism, higher clinical error rates, and potential threats to patient safety [50]. Given the profound influence of burnout on ICU nurses’ sleep and mental health, it is imperative to implement targeted interventions aimed at enhancing sleep hygiene and offering comprehensive psychological support. Such efforts are essential not only for safeguarding nurse well-being but also for improving performance outcomes and maintaining patient care quality.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships between the observed variables cannot be established. Second, all variables in this study are assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias and affect the accuracy and reliability of the results. Third, although several confounding variables are statistically adjusted for in the analysis, their potential moderating influence on the mediation pathways is not further examined. Additionally, this study controlled for a limited number of confounding variables, but the potential for residual confounding factors cannot be ruled out. Fourth, data collection coincided with the COVID-19 outbreak, and pandemic-related stressors may have influenced participants’ psychological states and behaviors, potentially undermining the stability and generalizability of the findings. Finally, the depressive symptoms assessed in this study are not further validated through clinical diagnostic assessments. However, a substantial body of research has demonstrated a strong concordance between self-reported symptoms and objective measurement tools [51, 52].

Implications for practice

Burnout among ICU nurses not only undermines their health and work performance but also contributes to productivity loss, posing risks to both patient care quality and healthcare system sustainability. Given that this study identified a sequential mediating pathway from burnout to sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and productivity loss, intervention strategies may be more effective if designed using a stepped prevention framework targeting each stage of this pathway. Primary prevention should focus on reducing burnout at the source. Nursing administrators should prioritize organizational-level interventions that reduce excessive workload and improve recovery opportunities. Strategies may include optimizing nurse-to-patient ratios, improving shift scheduling to minimize circadian rhythm disruption, and establishing institutional policies that promote psychological detachment from work during off-duty hours. Creating a workplace culture that values rest and recovery may help prevent chronic resource depletion among ICU nurses. Secondary prevention should target sleep quality as an early physiological and psychological recovery mechanism. Considering the mediating role of sleep quality identified in this study, hospitals should implement sleep health promotion programs, including education on sleep hygiene, fatigue management, and shift-work adaptation strategies. Work-related rumination reduction interventions, such as mindfulness-based stress reduction training, may help nurses mentally disengage from clinical responsibilities and reduce cognitive arousal that interferes with sleep initiation and maintenance. Occupational health departments should also consider providing fatigue monitoring and recovery-support resources for shift-working nurses. Tertiary prevention should focus on early detection and management of depressive symptoms. Regular mental health screening programs should be integrated into occupational health services for ICU nurses. Accessible psychological counselling services, employee assistance programs, and peer support systems should be provided to facilitate early intervention for nurses experiencing emotional distress. Cognitive-behavioral therapy–based stress management training may also help improve emotional regulation and reduce depressive symptom severity. Furthermore, individual-level interventions such as resilience training, emotional self-regulation skill development, and professional recognition programs may help support nurses’ work motivation and psychological well-being. These strategies may be considered as complementary components of broader occupational health management systems. Integrating such tiered intervention approaches may contribute to improving workforce sustainability, supporting clinical performance, and maintaining high-quality patient care in critical care settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that burnout among ICU nurses is significantly associated with health-related productivity loss, and this association is partially mediated by sleep quality and depressive symptoms. These findings highlight the importance of addressing both psychological stress and physiological recovery processes in maintaining workforce productivity. Future studies using longitudinal or intervention designs are needed to further validate these relationships and explore effective organizational and policy-level strategies. Healthcare institutions should consider integrated occupational health approaches to support the physical and mental well-being of ICU nurses and improve patient care quality.
