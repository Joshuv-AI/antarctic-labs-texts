Disrupted anastomotic pseudoaneurysm in arteriovenous grafts: an etiology-driven case series with a proposed preliminary management framework
Authors: Aiqiang Zhou, Liangzhu Hu, Yafei Zi, Yong Lu, Chao Wang, Yedong He, Haihong Zhang, Wei Deng, Bailin Yu, Tao Huang, Luxiang Wen, Yangdong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisruption of an anastomotic pseudoaneurysm in an arteriovenous graft (AVG) is a rare but life-threatening complication in hemodialysis patients, distinct from the more common graft-body pseudoaneurysms caused by repeated needle puncture. A systematic management framework for this specific entity is currently lacking.ObjectiveTo propose a preliminary etiology-based diagnostic and therapeutic conceptual framework for AVG anastomotic rupture through the analysis of three cases with different underlying mechanisms, and to explore its clinical applicability.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical data, imaging findings, surgical findings, and outcomes of three patients with AVG anastomotic rupture caused by trauma, degenerative changes, and infection, respectively. Based on a review of the literature, we constructed an etiology-based diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making framework grounded in a three-dimensional “localization–characterization–condition” assessment.ResultsAll three patients presented with pulsatile masses in the anastomotic region, and imaging studies confirmed pseudoaneurysm formation with anastomotic disruption. Individualized surgical strategies were adopted based on intraoperative etiology: direct repair was performed for traumatic rupture; autologous vein interposition grafting was employed for mechanical rupture due to tissue edema and friability; and for infectious rupture, extensive debridement combined with direct repair and complete AVG excision was undertaken. All patients recovered uneventfully and were transitioned to temporary or long-term dialysis catheters.ConclusionThis preliminary framework suggests a stepwise approach to decision-making regarding treatment objectives, surgical modalities, and key technical details within a stepwise framework of emergent control versus elective planning. It highlights the potential importance of etiological prevention and correct intraoperative decision-making, and may offer a practical reference for clinical management. Further multicenter studies are required to validate its generalizability.

Introduction

Arteriovenous grafts (AVGs) are a critical vascular access option for hemodialysis patients when autologous vessels are exhausted. Anastomotic pseudoaneurysm disruption is a rare but life‑threatening complication of AVGs. Unlike the more common graft‑body pseudoaneurysms caused by repeated needle puncture, anastomotic pseudoaneurysms arise from disruption of the suture line‑arterial wall interface, with distinct etiologies including infection, mechanical stress, traumatic avulsion, and vascular degeneration. Despite its clinical importance, a systematic management framework based on underlying etiology is currently lacking. This case series analyzes three patients with AVG anastomotic rupture caused by trauma, degenerative changes, and infection, respectively. Based on these cases and a focused literature review, we propose a preliminary etiology‑driven framework to guide clinical decision‑making.

Methods

PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library were searched for articles published between January 2000 and March 2026. The search terms included, but were not limited to, the following keywords and their combinations: “arteriovenous graft”, “AVG”, “hemodialysis access”, “vascular access”, “prosthetic graft”, “PTFE graft”; “anastomotic pseudoaneurysm”, “false aneurysm”, “graft complication”. After initial deduplication, the search yielded 140 records. During the screening process, we additionally retained studies on anastomotic pseudoaneurysms in lower extremity prosthetic bypass grafts as a reference for underlying mechanisms. Reports on aortic or renal transplant anastomotic pseudoaneurysms were also excluded due to major differences in graft material and hemodynamics. After screening, 20 studies were considered relevant to the mechanisms or outcomes of AVG anastomotic pseudoaneurysms.

These three cases were treated at our institutions between December 2024 and January 2026. Inclusion criteria were all consecutive patients with AVG anastomotic pseudoaneurysm rupture treated at our institutions during the study period. No exclusion criteria were applied. Clinical data, imaging findings, surgical findings, and outcomes were retrospectively analyzed. This study was granted an ethics approval waiver by the Institutional Review Board of South China Hospital Affiliated to Shenzhen University, as it contains no identifiable patient information. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Case reports

Case 1

A 63‑year‑old woman with end‑stage renal disease on hemodialysis via a right neck catheter had undergone left upper extremity AVG creation (both anastomoses above the elbow, Fig. 1A) three weeks earlier. Her past medical history included epilepsy managed with carbamazepine and phenytoin. Four hours before admission, she fell from a bicycle during an epileptic seizure and developed progressive swelling and pain in the left arm. On admission, her elbow was held in forced semi‑flexion. Physical examination showed diffuse erythema and swelling of the entire left arm, most pronounced over the fistula, with increased skin tension at the elbow (Fig. 1B); a bruit was still audible. Preoperative Computed tomography angiography (CTA) and ultrasound revealed absence of flow in the AVG, thrombus and dissection within the graft, suspected disruption of the arterial anastomosis with pseudoaneurysm formation, and an extensive hematoma in the biceps and the AVG tunnel (Fig. 2).

The patient presented late at night and underwent emergency surgery early the following morning, within hours of admission. To precisely localize the bleeding site, minimize blind dissection, and avoid direct clamping in the hematoma, intraoperative angiography was performed with temporary balloon control. Angiography confirmed contrast extravasation from the brachial artery. After temporary balloon occlusion, the artery was exposed. Thrombus was evacuated. The extensive intimal damage in the mid‑graft indicated that the entire AVG was unsalvageable, reinforcing the decision to resect only the anastomotic segment. Because the graft was already non‑functional and the patient was planned for a new access on the contralateral arm, complete excision was avoided to limit surgical trauma. The arterial defect was then closed with sutures. Post‑repair, traumatic suture disruption was observed at the AVG stump (Fig. 1C).

Postoperatively, arm tension decreased and pain improved. The patient continued dialysis via the right neck catheter and was discharged uneventfully on day 2.

Case 2

A 64‑year‑old man had undergone left upper extremity AVG creation six months earlier. The AVG was a forearm loop graft (brachial artery to basilic vein) with a U‑shaped configuration. He had been using it for hemodialysis. One week before admission, he noticed a soft mass in the arterial anastomosis region that gradually enlarged. Physical examination revealed a palpable thrill and a pulsatile, non‑tender mass (~ 3 cm) with normal overlying skin (Fig. 3A). CTA and ultrasound suggested pseudoaneurysm formation from anastomotic disruption (Fig. 3B and D). CTA was performed to complement ultrasound by providing a global view of the upper extremity arterial anatomy, including distal perfusion, possible emboli, and any other occult lesions. Rheumatologic and immune‑related laboratory parameters were all negative.

Two separate incisions were made: one over the proximal brachial artery and another over the distal forearm to access the radial and ulnar arteries for placement of vascular clamps. A third incision was then made directly over the pseudoaneurysm, connecting the two, allowing complete exposure under a bloodless field. Given the enlarging mass, increasing tension, concern for rupture, and absence of contraindications to emergency surgery, the procedure was performed under brachial plexus anesthesia. The sac contained two adjacent circular defects in the brachial artery with active bleeding. No purulent discharge was seen. The arterial tissue was edematous and friable, making direct repair risky; therefore, the damaged segment was resected and replaced with an autologous perforating vein interposition graft (Fig. 4C). The AVG stump was ligated and excised (Fig. 4B). The patient’s parathyroid hormone level was more than nine times the upper limit of normal, and ultrasound also showed evidence of vascular calcification. Histopathology of the debrided arterial margin showed degenerative changes without evidence of infection, and microbiological culture was negative. No other plausible cause for the early anastomotic disruption was identified; therefore, it was attributed to degenerative changes of the vessel wall. The patient was transitioned to a long‑term right neck catheter and discharged on day 3. Long‑term surveillance with duplex ultrasound every 6 months was recommended to monitor for possible aneurysmal degeneration of the interposition graft.

Case 3

A 44‑year‑old man with diabetes, hypoalbuminemia, and diabetic foot gangrene presented with one week of left arm swelling and pain and three days of high fever. The AVG was a forearm loop graft (brachial artery to basilic vein). The mass progressively enlarged. On examination, a pulsatile 3 × 3 cm mass was noted over the AVG arterial anastomosis, with overlying erythema and warmth; no thrill was present. Laboratory studies showed markedly elevated white blood cell count and procalcitonin. Imaging suggested an infected pseudoaneurysm with perilesional fluid (Fig. 5D).

Emergency surgery revealed a pseudoaneurysm densely adherent to surrounding tissues, communicating with the original AVG. Purulent discharge was present within the graft tunnel and around previous puncture sites, and the graft was thrombosed. The pseudoaneurysm and the involved graft segment were completely excised, leaving a defect in the brachial artery (Fig. 6B). After extensive debridement to healthy tissue margins, the debrided arterial margins were sent for histopathology. The defect was lateral; longitudinal closure along the arterial axis was tension‑free. The basilic vein at the venous anastomosis was ligated, and the entire AVG was removed. The wound was irrigated and drained. Postoperatively, the patient received 4 weeks of sensitive antibiotics based on intraoperative culture (Escherichia coli). Histopathology showed no residual infection in the debrided margins. A 3‑month postoperative duplex ultrasound revealed no pseudoaneurysm recurrence or signs of infection. Continued surveillance with duplex ultrasound every 6 months was recommended to monitor for late infection or arterial degeneration. At discharge, the patient continued hemodialysis via the right neck temporary catheter, and a new AVG on the contralateral arm was planned. This temporary catheter was used to allow a 2‑to‑3‑week observation period to ensure resolution of infection before proceeding with a long‑term catheter or new AVG. The patient was discharged on day 14 with healed wound and normal circulation.

Follow-up outcomes

All three patients were followed for 3 months after the index surgery. During this period, their long‑term dialysis catheters remained functional without thrombosis or infection. The feasibility of creating a new AVG on the contralateral upper extremity was evaluated for each patient, and a surgical plan has been established. Creation of a new AVG is scheduled as soon as patient consent is obtained.

Discussion

The AVG is a lifeline for hemodialysis patients who have exhausted their autologous vessels. Pseudoaneurysm formation occurs in 2–10% of AVGs [1]. but most of these are graft‑body lesions caused by repeated needle puncture. Anastomotic pseudoaneurysms—the focus of this report—involve a distinct pathophysiology: suture‑line disruption due to infection, mechanical stress, traumatic avulsion, or vascular degeneration. Consequently, their management may require arterial wall reconstruction, infection control, and a tension‑free anastomotic design, rather than the simpler approaches used for graft‑body lesions. Although anastomotic pseudoaneurysms are well documented in lower extremity bypass grafts (10‑year incidence up to 34.55% [2]), they are rarely reported in AVGs. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically classified AVG anastomotic pseudoaneurysms by etiology or outlined a management framework. The three‑case series described below offers an initial step toward filling this gap by presenting a preliminary conceptual framework.

To provide context for this framework, we first review the few existing case reports of AVG anastomotic pseudoaneurysm. Yasim et al. reported an early mechanical detachment from the brachial artery two months after graft creation, treated by direct repair and ligation [3]. Yaşa et al. described a lower‑extremity case with a large femoral artery defect repaired by polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) interposition [4]. Triga presented an infected anastomotic pseudoaneurysm managed by interposition of a second PTFE graft [5]. However, none of these isolated reports proposed an etiological classification or a management approach. Our series, which includes three different etiologies (traumatic, degenerative, infectious), is the first to suggest such a classification, albeit based on a small number of cases.

Moving from literature to mechanisms, the etiological pathways in AVGs may be largely consistent with experience from lower extremity grafts [2]. although dialysis patients have unique metabolic and access‑related characteristics. As a conceptual proposal, we therefore classify etiologies into four types: infectious, biomechanical, vascular degenerative, and iatrogenic/traumatic (Table 1). It is important to note that these categories often overlap in clinical practice; the relative contribution of each factor should be assessed on a case‑by‑case basis, and management strategies may need to be individualized accordingly.

Regarding infectious mechanisms, these include overt infections (e.g., S. aureus) and insidious colonization (e.g., coagulase‑negative staphylococci forming biofilms [6]. Chronic inflammation can lead to anastomotic pseudoaneurysms with intraoperative findings of suture loosening and tissue edema without pus; symptoms may appear months to years later [7], and preoperative markers can be normal. In addition, foreign body reaction to the graft or suture material may mimic infection [8], requiring pathology and prolonged culture for differentiation.

For biomechanical mechanisms (mechanical rupture), in lower extremity grafts, 85–90% of pseudoaneurysms occur at either the proximal or distal arterial anastomosis [2, 9], suggesting cyclic joint stress as a key predisposing factor. Our cases suggest several possible contributing factors: (1) compliance mismatch between the graft and native artery; (2) stress concentration at the heel and toe of an end‑to‑side anastomosis; (3) sudden traction during joint extension when the graft is too short [7]; and (4) suture fatigue with tapered fracture ends [8]. A common intraoperative finding is intact sutures but an AVG avulsed from the calcified arterial wall—a feature that may help distinguish mechanical from infectious rupture. However, these observations are based on a small number of cases; larger studies are needed to confirm the role of biomechanical mechanisms in AVG anastomotic rupture.

Vascular degenerative mechanisms may accumulate over time: material fatigue, host artery degeneration (e.g., atherosclerosis, intimal fibrosis), and inflammatory diseases [8]. In lower extremity arterial bypass grafts, this etiology accounts for 66% of late‑onset pseudoaneurysms, with 12.6% of patients presenting for the first time 10 years after surgery [10]. notably, even autologous vein grafts can rupture as late as 30 years after implantation [11]. Case 2 in this series may represent a degenerative mechanism: the patient’s markedly elevated parathyroid hormone level and severe vascular calcification likely contributed to early anastomotic failure, with the graft avulsed from the calcified arterial wall despite intact sutures.

Iatrogenic and traumatic mechanisms are also significant: poor suture technique, excessive anastomotic tension, clamp injury [12], falls [13] or impacts. Intraoperative findings may include longitudinal traction marks on the anastomosis with intact sutures—different from the fracture pattern seen with suture fatigue. Additionally, puncture‑related pseudoaneurysms in the graft body can compromise anastomotic integrity if the puncture site is < 2 cm from the anastomosis [14] ; we suggest that this be recognized as an independent iatrogenic etiology.

When a mass appears at the AVG anastomosis, a rapid three‑dimensional assessment can be helpful, including localization of the mass origin (anastomosis, puncture site, or tunnel), characterization of the underlying etiology, and evaluation of local and systemic conditions (Table 2). For complex cases, three‑dimensional printing could assist in preoperative planning by providing a patient‑specific anatomical model [17].

Because pseudoaneurysms may enlarge over time and rupture risk might increase when the diameter exceeds 2 cm [8]. early intervention is generally advisable, although the optimal timing must be individualized. Emergency surgery is indicated for active bleeding, compartment syndrome, severe pain, or rapid enlargement [18]. However, in emergency settings, if preoperative assessment is insufficient, metabolic derangements could raise perioperative mortality [8]; therefore, when possible, necessary examinations should be completed before proceeding with blind surgery in unstable patients.

Reconstruction strategies may be stratified by etiology (Table 1). Open surgery is preferred for infectious, mechanical, and joint‑area ruptures because it allows thorough debridement and reconstruction with autologous vein. In contrast, endovascular repair has virtually no routine role in anastomotic pseudoaneurysms; if considered at all, it might be used only as a salvage or bridging measure —for example, to control life‑threatening hemorrhage in highly selected non‑infected patients who cannot undergo open surgery, followed by definitive surgical repair. Table 3 proposes a suggested algorithm for intraoperative decision‑making, keeping in mind that it is based on a small case series and should be adapted to individual patient circumstances.

Prevention could start at access planning: (1) consider avoiding designs that place both anastomoses above the elbow joint (transarticular design for both), because sudden elbow movement can then create opposing traction forces on the two anastomoses, increasing the risk of avulsion and pseudoaneurysm formation; (2) for patients with vascular calcification or a high risk of falls, it may be reasonable to explain the potential long‑term degenerative and trauma‑vulnerable risks of prosthetic grafts and to intensify surveillance, for example, with clinical examination every 6 months and duplex ultrasound every 12 months. Preserving contralateral arm vessels for future access is essential; temporary catheters should be used only as a bridge, and early planning for a new AVG is recommended once infection is cleared. Emerging technologies such as the Internet of Things could further enhance postoperative surveillance by enabling remote monitoring of vital signs and early detection of complications [19]. Such approaches hold promise for future clinical application, especially in high‑risk patients.

A suggested algorithm for decision-making in AVG anastomotic pseudoaneurysm and rupture

Emergency indications:

• CTA/US: extensive hematoma

• Exam: compartment syndrome, progressive mass/swelling, tension pseudoaneurysm

Elective indications:

• CTA/US: well‑demarcated pseudoaneurysm

• Exam: stable mass, no tension/ecchymosis expansion

Local: hematoma/skin/infection

Systemic: contralateral limb/central veins/comorbidities/Ca-P metabolism

A. Curative (Resection/Reconstruction)

B. Palliative (Salvage)

Curative: stable patient, long life expectancy → complete eradication, repair with autologous vein, preserve resources.

Palliative: poor condition, infection unresectable, life expectancy < 1 year → control bleeding/infection with minimal trauma; consider ligation if collaterals adequate.

A. Open Surgery

B. Endovascular (Covered Stent)

Open surgery preferred for: infection, massive hematoma, extensive graft damage, need for autologous vein, joint areas.

Endovascular (rare, salvage only): non-infectious, high open risk, extremely difficult anatomy; must combine with debridement & antibiotics; stent diameter 10–20% > target vessel.

A. Hemostasis Techniques

B. Materials for Artery Repair

C. Debridement Extent

Hemostasis: balloon occlusion (10–20% larger than artery), direct clamping, pneumatic tourniquet (30–50 mmHg above SBP, < 60 min), stent sealing (salvage only, caution in infection).

Materials: autologous vein (e.g., basilic vein, perforator vein, great saphenous vein) (gold standard; preferred for infection, joint areas, long life expectancy); prosthetic patch/AVG segment (only if non-infected, non-joint, no autologous vein); covered stent (see above).

Debridement: guided by imaging; evacuate hematoma, excise non-viable tissue. Infectious → to healthy bleeding margins; non-infectious → local excision of aneurysm wall, preserve healthy structures.

Limitations

This case series has several limitations. First, the sample size is small, reflecting the rarity of AVG anastomotic rupture. Second, the literature on this specific entity is sparse, which limits the strength of the evidence base. Third, although all three patients maintained functional long‑term dialysis catheters during the 3‑month follow‑up and the feasibility of creating a new AVG on the contralateral arm was evaluated, the follow‑up period is short. Longer‑term outcomes—such as patency of the repaired native arteries and the success of future contralateral AVG placement—remain to be determined. Additionally, the high variability in patient presentation and management, as well as potential surgeon/operator bias, may limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

This case series outlines a preliminary etiology‑based conceptual framework for AVG anastomotic rupture. Based on three cases with different etiologies, the framework offers a potential approach to guide individualized surgical decision‑making. Further validation with larger cohorts is needed.
