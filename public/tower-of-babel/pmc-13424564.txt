Ligands, orbitals, and applications across the f-block
Authors: Conrad A. P. Goodwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Communications Chemistry is pleased to present a Collection of research that explores the chemistry of the rare earths and the actinides. Here, Guest Editor Conrad Goodwin highlights key themes and looks towards the future of this field.

The rare earth and actinide elements, collectively “the f-block”, comprise thirty elements – roughly a quarter of those known to chemistry – and yet for much of the twentieth century these series were treated as a footnote in most inorganic textbooks. The familiar textbook descriptions persist: the 4f orbitals of the lanthanides are core-like and shielded, so the chemistry is dominated by hard Lewis acidity, sharp atom-like luminescence, and a tidy decrease in ionic radius across the row; meanwhile, valence 5f and 6d orbitals in the early actinides interact meaningfully with each other, opening multielectron redox chemistry and metal–ligand covalency that can rival that of the d-block. These descriptions hold truth, but belie nuance.

Communications Chemistry is pleased to present a Collection of articles that builds on this historic viewpoint and showcases contemporary work to unravel the complex bonding and electronic structure, photophysics, magnetism and quantum information, and reactivity of the f-elements. It also explores their separations and environmental chemistry. We highlight key themes below and close by pointing to future directions.

Bonding, covalency, and electronic structure across the f-series

The first thread concerns how 4f, 5d, 5f, and 6d orbitals are modulated by ligands, and how these interactions can be read spectroscopically. Conour et al. report an isostructural transuranic series of “clam-shell” metallocenes [AnIV(COTBIG)2] (An = Th, U, Np, Pu; COTBIG = {C8H6-1,4-(SiPh3)2}2–) in which the bent geometry and electron-withdrawing substituents bring 5f and ligand-π levels into closer register, with [PuIV(COTBIG)2] showing especially strong 5f–π mixing1. Ramanantoanina et al. combine core-to-core resonant inelastic X-ray scattering with high-energy-resolution XANES to map La(III) 4f and 5d bonding as a non-radioactive homologue for Ac(III), informing the development of actinium radiopharmaceuticals2. Kaumini et al. extend the conversation to Group 4 by benchmarking M–H covalency in [M(Cp*)2(ODipp)(H)] (M = Hf, Th, U; Cp* = {C5Me5}–, Dipp = {C6H3-2,6-iPr2}) against the Zr congener, using 1H NMR chemical shifts as a paramagnetism-tolerant readout3. These works demonstrate a recurrent theme in the Collection: experimental data and relativistic quantum chemistry in the f-block are closely linked, with synthesis offering real-world benchmarks and theory guiding spectroscopy in this challenging regime.

f-Elements in the world

The largest thread in the Collection concerns the f-elements as they appear outside the synthesis flask. Several papers address separations and environmental speciation. Understanding how actinide elements interact with elements across the periodic table in the natural environment, as well as in complex spent nuclear fuel mixtures, is key to remediation and recycling strategies. Ilgen et al. combine X-ray absorption fine-structure spectroscopy (XAFS) with operando flow microcalorimetry to show that adsorption of Ln(III) onto Al2O3 nanopores switches from outer- to inner-sphere as the cation’s hydration enthalpy becomes less exothermic, with neodymium showing the largest confinement effect4. Gao, Wilson and co-workers report a resin-anchored 18-membered macrocycle that selectively captures larger rare earths from a waste mixture, a vital first step towards new practical recycling methods with these critical elements5. Goncharov et al. introduce a hybrid calorimetry plus ab initio molecular-dynamics framework for determining thermodynamic parameters for LaIIICl3 in molten eutectic LiCl-KCl, offering a transferable route to the rational optimisation of pyrochemical separations of spent nuclear fuel6.

Three papers expand the toolkit of solid-state actinide host phases relevant to long-term waste management. van Hattem, Popa, Konings and co-workers describe BaO-deficient Ba3PuO6 perovskites, with X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) at the Pu L3 and M5 edges, confirming the Pu(VI) oxidation state and reporting the first extended low-temperature heat-capacity curve for a 5f2 Pu system7. Wang et al. report an actinide langbeinite compound – Cs2UIVLn(PO4)3 (Ln = Ce–Nd, Sm–Lu)8. These phosphate matrices can immobilise both tetravalent and trivalent f-elements simultaneously and exhibit remarkable thermal stability and resistance to leaching. Murphy et al. show that americium can be doped into zircaloy-derived monoclinic ZrIVO2 as co-existing Am(IV) and Am(III) at up to 5 mol%, with a lowered monoclinic-to-tetragonal phase-transition temperature9. This result has implications for understanding the composition and partitioning of nuclear fuel during operations and in spent nuclear fuel assemblies.

Two further papers address radiation and nuclear chemistry. Rajapaksha et al. report the first isostructural uranyl and neptunyl, {UVIO2}2+ and {NpVIO2}2+, peroxide phases and identify superoxide stabilisation within actinyl triperoxide complexes by EPR spectroscopy10. These species are relevant for understanding spent-fuel speciation under radiolytic conditions. Kobayashi et al. provide a synchrotron-based μ-XAFS forensic study of melt-fuel microparticles recovered from Fukushima Daiichi Unit 2, confirming that uranium and plutonium are predominantly in the An(IV) state, which can inform decommissioning strategies11. Spahr et al. push chemistry to geological extremes by synthesising UIII2(CO3)3 and UIV(CO3)2 in a laser-heated diamond-anvil cell at 20 GPa, and propose anhydrous uranium carbonates as candidate host phases for uranium in carbon-rich mantle lithologies12.

The Collection also reaches into biomedical, biological, and applied-materials chemistry. Zhang, Dai and Law et al. show that hydrophilic functionalisation of chiral DOTA scaffolds for Gd(III) raises longitudinal relaxivity (r1), the degree of enhancement of the water proton relaxation rate normalised to the concentration of the paramagnetic ion, into a clinically competitive regime13. This was achieved by engineering the second- and outer-sphere coordination environments, rather than the inner sphere. Woods, Abergel and Martinez-Gomez et al. report a striking generalisation of the lanthanide-alcohol-dehydrogenase paradigm to trivalent actinides, showing that trivalent actinium, americium, curium, berkelium, and californium support the growth of an evolved Methylobacterium extorquens AM1 strain, with an in vitro-reconstituted ethanol dehydrogenase confirming catalytic competence14. Finally, Zhang et al. demonstrate that dual La/Ce co-doping of the spinel LiMn2O4 delivers synergistic stabilisation alongside enhanced Li+ kinetics, a useful reminder that f-element redox flexibility continues to find applications in energy materials15.

Lanthanide luminescence, photophysics, and molecular thermometers

The third thread captures a community rewriting the photosensitiser rulebook. Kitagawa, Hasegawa and colleagues use a phosphine-oxide–phenanthrene scaffold whose triplet sits below the Tb(III) 5D4 level, contrary to Latva’s empirical rule, to access thermally-assisted photosensitised emission with 73% quantum yield16. A follow-up Tb–Nd dyad exploits the same bridge to deliver a record 4.4% K–1 thermal sensitivity in a lifetime-based lanthanide thermometer17. Maldonado et al. have extended molecular thermometry into the near-infrared, where mono-, di-, and hexanuclear Nd(III) complexes show anti-thermal quenching across the physiological range—the hexamer achieving Sᵣ = 2.4% K–1 at 293 K—and rationalised these observations quantitatively by CASSCF calculations on the Kramers doublets of the 4F3⁄2 level18. Min, Shi and Cheng et al. describe a dilanthanide Tb/Eu MOF that detects the UV-silent biomarker trimethylamine-N-oxide via outer-sphere recognition, using a smartphone read-out19, and Jiang, Wang and Ding et al. report a layer-by-layer NIR Nd/Yb-MOF core-shell platform for ratiometric sensing of the antihypertensive drug felodipine20. These results bring rare-earth-based luminescent sensing closer to real-world applications. Liu, Yang and Huang show that ligand-field engineering of three-coordinate Ce(III) phenoxides enables Laporte-allowed d→f emission in the visible range with photoluminescence quantum yields up to 62%21, which serves as a reminder that the 5d manifold of rare earths (and the 6d manifold in actinides) provides a complementary design space to tune physical properties alongside the 4f (and 5f) orbital sets which are often less sensitive to ligand properties.

Magnetism, organometallics, and quantum information

A fourth thread spans single-molecule magnetism through to molecular quantum information processing and the exploration of fundamental electronic structure with f-elements. Stevens, Ortu and co-workers report a family of iminophosphoranomethanide-stabilised alkaline-earth and rare-earth complexes, including divalent open-shell species, characterised by EPR, magnetometry, and ab initio calculations22. Sun, Roesky and colleagues study polymeric and monomeric Y/Er stannolediide sandwich complexes, finding the nuclearity of the Er complexes drives different magnetic relaxation barriers despite the similarity of their first coordination spheres23. Chen, Ding and Zheng et al. extend studies into the familiar {ErIII(COTR)} family to include comparisons between triple-halide-bridged species: [{ErIII(COTTMS2)}2(μ-X3)] (X = Cl, Br; COTTMS2 = {C8H6-(SiMe3)2}2–)24. They find that ferromagnetic axial dipolar coupling suppresses quantum tunnelling of the magnetisation and yields hard-magnet behaviour with open hysteresis loops up to 10 K. Morrillo, Mills, Chilton and colleagues address the molecular-qudit frontier with an ab initio fully coupled electronic and nuclear-spin treatment of [TmII{N(SiiPr3)2}2], identifying experimentally realistic conditions for coherent electric-field control of both 4f electronic and I=1/2I=1/2 nuclear-spin degrees of freedom25.

Reactivity and small-molecule activation

The final thread illustrates that actinide reactivity remains a productive frontier. Makarov, Fridman and Eisen show that Th(IV) and U(IV) complexes built around six-membered N-heterocyclic-iminato ligands catalyse ester hydroboration; manipulation of the spectator ligand removes an induction period, and the working catalyst is an actinide–alkoxide26. Prakash, Barnes and Windorff describe an ammonolysis route from [U(Cp*)(Bn)3] to generate a family of multinuclear uranium–nitrogen cluster cages bearing μ-N, μ-NH and μ-NH2 moieties with relevance to the synthesis of uranium nitride, a leading candidate fuel for Generation-IV reactors27.

Outlook

Taken together, the works in this Collection illustrate how f-element chemistry has matured into a field in which synthesis, advanced X-ray spectroscopy, relativistic computation, and a deliberate use of lanthanide congeners as actinide surrogates are deployed in concert. Several areas remain open and inviting. The molecular chemistry of the transuranium series beyond plutonium still has a small footprint and remains ripe territory. Quantum information processing and molecular magnetism using f-elements are covered in several papers here, but these are extremely active areas with new experimental opportunities highlighted in this Collection. And the rare-earth recycling challenge will sharpen rather than fade. Increasing activity in combining biology with f-element chemistry may open new avenues for recycling not yet developed in depth. We look forward to a continued expansion of what is considered possible, not least in the fundamental chemistry and reactivity of transuranium actinides, as facility capabilities and international collaborations make these elements more routinely accessible.
