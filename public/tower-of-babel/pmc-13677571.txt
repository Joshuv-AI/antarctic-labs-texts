Image-Guided Decubitus Craniospinal Irradiation Setup in a Fixed-Beam Proton Room: Accuracy and Efficiency
Authors: Qi Zeng, Zhiqiang Han, Arpit Chhabra, Huifang Zhai, Qing Chen, Andy Shim, Andrew Okhuereigbe, Anh Kha, Jewel Graham, Anya Burke, Meng Wei Ho, Francis Yu, Yao Yu, Yoshiya Yamada, Charles B. Simone, Haibo Lin, Dong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRecent developments have further demonstrated the feasibility of craniospinal irradiation (CSI) delivery using simplified beam arrangements, such as a single posterior brain beam. Extending these advances, CSI delivered in the lateral decubitus position within a fixed-beam proton room offers an opportunity to improve room utilization and treatment access but introduces unique challenges in patient setup and image guidance.MethodsThis study compares 2 image-guided setup workflows for decubitus CSI to evaluate whether meaningful differences exist. We retrospectively analyzed 10 CSI patients treated in the decubitus position between 2024 and 2025 using the fixed-beam ProBeam system with the 3-iso technique. Two setup workflows were evaluated: (1) Continuous Full-Field Image Guidance (CFF-IG), in which all isocenters were aligned prior to treatment delivery, and (2) Selective Field-Based Image Guidance (SF-IG), in which full-field imaging was applied only at the initial fraction, followed by field-specific verification thereafter. Setup accuracy was quantified using translational and rotational deviations derived from oblique kV imaging, and dosimetric robustness was assessed through target coverage and junction dose gradient analysis. Setup efficiency was evaluated using treatment room time stamps.ResultsAcross all 3 isocenters, mean translational deviations were below 4 mm and mean angular deviations were below 1.5°. Differences in target coverage (ΔD95) and junction dose gradients were small between the 2 workflows. No statistically significant differences in setup accuracy or dosimetric quality were observed. SF-IG was associated with shorter average setup time, with the majority of treatment sessions completed within 30 minutes.ConclusionsBoth CFF-IG and SF-IG achieved setup accuracy within institutional action levels and maintained dosimetric robustness for CSI delivered in the decubitus position using pencil beam scanning. The absence of significant differences suggests that either approach may be clinically feasible for decubitus CSI delivery. SF-IG offers a more time-efficient workflow without compromising treatment quality, supporting its use as the preferred default strategy, while CFF-IG may be reserved for select cases requiring enhanced global realignment. These findings support decubitus CSI as a practical extension of modern single-beam and simplified pencil beam scanning CSI techniques.

Introduction

Craniospinal irradiation (CSI) is a standard treatment modality for a range of central nervous system malignancies, particularly medulloblastoma.1, 2, 3, 4 The goal of radiation therapy is to achieve prescribed dose coverage across the entire craniospinal axis while sparing healthy tissue as much as possible. Proton therapy, with its zero exit dose and rapid distal fall-off, offers a significant advantage over photon-based CSI by reducing the integral dose to surrounding healthy organs and tissues,5, 6 which is particularly relevant in pediatric patients because late toxicities are a major concern.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

A major technical challenge in CSI delivery is the management of field junctions over the extended craniospinal target.13 Lin et al demonstrated that supine pencil beam scanning (PBS) CSI using volumetric gradient dose optimization can eliminate match-line feathering while maintaining dosimetric robustness against setup uncertainties, establishing a modern reference framework for multi-isocenter PBS CSI delivery.14 Building on this foundation, Hu et al further showed that CSI treatment efficiency can be improved by simplifying beam arrangements, specifically by using a single posterior brain beam without compromising target coverage or organ-at-risk sparing,15 thereby reinforcing the feasibility of streamlined PBS CSI workflows.

Treating CSI patients in the lateral decubitus position represents a logical extension of these advances. In fixed-beam proton rooms, decubitus positioning allows the use of a single posterior beam orientation for the entire craniospinal axis, analogous to single-beam PBS CSI concepts, while improving access to treatment capacity in centers with limited gantry availability.

However, decubitus positioning introduces distinct setup challenges, including altered anatomical reference axes, limited imaging flexibility, and patient-positioning complexity.10, 16 In fixed-beam rooms, patient alignment must rely on fixed oblique kV imaging systems oriented relative to the proton beam axis, as shown in Figure 1a. These imaging geometries differ fundamentally from the orthogonal kV imaging commonly used for supine CSI in gantry rooms and require therapists to perform patient alignment using oblique projections that are less intuitive for assessing translational and rotational errors, particularly roll. To address these challenges, 2 image-guided setup workflows—adapted from established supine CSI practices—were implemented for decubitus CSI delivery in the fixed-beam room. One approach emphasizes comprehensive pre-treatment alignment of all isocenters, while the other relies on selective, field-based verification after initial full alignment. Whether these 2 strategies yield clinically meaningful differences in geometric accuracy, dosimetric robustness, or workflow efficiency remains unclear.

Therefore, the purpose of this study is to systematically compare these 2 setup methods for CSI patients treated in the lateral decubitus position using PBS in a fixed-beam room. By explicitly evaluating setup accuracy, treatment efficiency, and dosimetric robustness under the constraints imposed by the fixed oblique kV imaging geometry shown in Figure 1a, we aim to determine whether either approach confers a significant advantage or whether they may be considered clinically comparable for routine decubitus CSI delivery.

Methods and materials

Patient cohorts

This retrospective study included ten adult patients treated with CSI in the lateral decubitus position at the New York Proton Center between 2024 and 2025. All patients were treated using proton PBS in a fixed-beam treatment room. Patients were excluded only if complete image-guidance records or treatment time stamps were unavailable. The majority of patients received CSI for palliative management of leptomeningeal disease. This study was conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for retrospective analysis.

Treatment system and imaging configuration

All treatments were delivered using a fixed horizontal proton beamline (ProBeam, Varian Medical Systems). The treatment room is equipped with 2 fixed oblique kilovoltage (kV) imaging systems positioned at approximately 45° and −45° relative to the proton beam axis. Unlike gantry-based systems, where imaging angles can be freely adjusted, the imaging geometry in the fixed-beam room is invariant with respect to patient orientation, requiring patient setup and verification to rely exclusively on these oblique projections.

Simulation and immobilization

Patients were simulated in the lateral decubitus position using a customized immobilization setup consisting of a thermoplastic head-and-neck mask and a Vac-Lok cushion supporting the torso and lower extremities. To enhance setup reproducibility, skin marks and mask reference marks were placed relative to room lasers during simulation, and additional tattoos were placed along the spinal region to assist with longitudinal alignment.

In the decubitus configuration, the patient coordinate system differs from that of the conventional supine position in the gantry room. Translational directions (longitudinal, vertical, and lateral) and rotational axes (pitch, yaw, and roll) were defined relative to the patient orientation as illustrated in Figure 2, and these definitions were used consistently for setup correction, robustness evaluation, and data analysis. Treatment planning and Robust optimization

All treatment plans were generated in RayStation version 11.0 using a 3-isocenter PBS technique, with separate isocenters for the brain, upper spine, and lower spine targets. A single posterior beam was used for each treatment field. Adjacent fields were designed with a minimum overlap of 10 cm to enable gradient-based dose matching at the junction regions.

The clinical target volume (CTV) included the whole brain and thecal sac. For plan optimization, auxiliary target structures were generated by expanding the brain CTV by 2 mm and the spinal CTV by 5 mm to guide dose shaping and dose fall-off. These auxiliary structures were not used as the primary structures for target coverage evaluation. Robust optimization was performed using ±3 mm translational setup uncertainty and ±3.5% range uncertainty, and target coverage metrics, including D95, were evaluated on the clinical CTV. Thus, the auxiliary optimization structures and robust optimization scenarios served distinct planning purposes and were not treated as additive setup margins.

In the robustness evaluation, cranio–caudal uncertainty was considered most relevant to junction perturbation because longitudinal shifts directly affect field overlap and gradient alignment. Vertical uncertainty was retained because of its potential influence on water-equivalent thickness for posterior beams and its coupling with roll in the lateral decubitus position. Lateral uncertainty was also retained because it may affect target-edge coverage, beam-path anatomy, or patient-specific asymmetric anatomy, even though it is less directly coupled to superior–inferior junction matching than cranio–caudal uncertainty.

Typical prescription doses were 3000 cGy delivered in 10 fractions, assuming a fixed relative biological effectiveness of 1.1.

Continuous Full-Field Image Guidance (CFF-IG): In this workflow, patient setup was performed by sequentially aligning all 3 isocenters (brain, upper spine, and lower spine) using oblique kV imaging prior to treatment delivery for each fraction. Field-specific verification imaging was subsequently performed before beam delivery.

Selective Field-Based Image Guidance (SF-IG): In this workflow, CFF-IG was applied only during the first treatment fraction. For subsequent fractions, patient alignment was verified individually for each field immediately prior to beam delivery using selective field-based kV imaging, without performing full multi-isocenter alignment at the beginning of each session.

Both workflows were implemented using the ARIA RV system (version 16).

Setup accuracy and dosimetric evaluation

Setup accuracy was assessed using oblique kV images acquired during treatment. Image registration was performed by matching bony landmarks between acquired kV images and digitally reconstructed radiographs. Translational deviations (longitudinal, vertical, and lateral) and rotational deviations (yaw, pitch, and roll) were recorded for each isocenter and each fraction. Institutional action levels for decubitus CSI setup were defined as residual translational deviations within 5 mm and residual rotational deviations within 2° before treatment delivery. Deviations exceeding these thresholds required therapist and/or physician/physicist review, additional couch correction when feasible, repositioning, or repeat imaging before beam delivery.

To assess the dosimetric impact of observed setup deviations, perturbed dose calculations were generated for each isocenter field. For each patient and workflow, the measured translational deviations recorded at the corresponding isocenter were applied to the associated treatment field, such that the brain, upper-spine, and lower-spine fields were perturbed independently according to their field-specific residual setup deviations. Rotational deviations were recorded and analyzed as geometric setup metrics but were not explicitly modeled in the dose recalculation. Therefore, the dosimetric perturbation analysis represented translational field-specific setup uncertainty rather than full 6D perturbation. Target coverage degradation was quantified using changes in CTV D95 (ΔD95) relative to the nominal plan. Dose gradient consistency at the junction regions between adjacent fields, including the brain–upper-spine and upper-spine–lower-spine junctions, was evaluated by extracting dose profiles through the junction regions and calculating the percent difference between nominal and perturbed junction gradients for both ascending and descending dose-gradient slopes.

Setup efficiency and statistical analysis

Setup efficiency was evaluated using treatment room timestamps extracted from the ARIA system. Total setup time was defined as the interval from patient entry into the treatment room to initiation of beam delivery.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize geometric accuracy, dosimetric metrics, and setup times. For each patient, setup accuracy, dosimetric, and time metrics were summarized separately for CFF-IG and SF-IG. Because both workflows were evaluated within the same patient cohort, paired comparisons between workflows were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P < .05.

Discussion

This study evaluated 2 image-guided setup workflows—CFF-IG and SF-IG—for CSI delivered in the lateral decubitus position using proton pencil beam scanning (PBS) in a fixed-beam treatment room. Although both workflows are well established for supine CSI in gantry-based rooms, their application to decubitus CSI under fixed oblique imaging constraints has not been systematically evaluated. The primary objective of this work was to determine whether these workflows could be safely and efficiently translated to the decubitus setting and whether meaningful differences exist in geometric accuracy, dosimetric robustness, or clinical efficiency.

The results demonstrate that both CFF-IG and SF-IG achieved setup accuracy within the predefined institutional action levels across all 3 isocenters. Mean translational and rotational deviations were below these thresholds, supporting the clinical feasibility of both image-guided workflows for decubitus CSI delivery. Importantly, no statistically or clinically significant differences in setup accuracy were observed between the 2 workflows, including at the upper and lower spine isocenters, where cumulative uncertainties are often of greatest concern. These findings indicate that selective field-based verification does not compromise geometric precision, even in the presence of fixed oblique imaging geometry and multi-isocenter delivery.

Although supine CSI remains the conventional approach in gantry-based rooms, the present study was not designed as a direct matched comparison between decubitus and supine positioning. Supine CSI typically benefits from a more familiar patient orientation and more flexible image-guidance geometry, whereas decubitus CSI in a fixed-beam room requires alignment using fixed oblique kV projections and introduces additional challenges in interpreting translational and rotational setup deviations, particularly roll. Despite these constraints, the observed decubitus setup deviations remained within institutional action levels, and no clinically meaningful degradation in target coverage or junction-gradient consistency was observed. These findings suggest that decubitus setup can be reproducible and stable within the evaluated workflow, although future studies with a matched supine CSI cohort would be needed to directly compare setup reproducibility, intrafraction stability, and workflow efficiency between the 2 positions.

Beyond geometric accuracy, clinical implementation of decubitus CSI also requires consideration of patient-specific workflow constraints. The present cohort consisted of adult patients, and the feasibility of decubitus CSI for pediatric patients requiring anesthesia was not directly evaluated. Although decubitus positioning may be technically feasible in selected pediatric patients, anesthesia introduces additional workflow and safety considerations, including airway access, monitoring-line management, emergency access, patient transfer, immobilization reproducibility, and coordination between anesthesia and radiation therapy teams. Therefore, implementation of decubitus CSI for anesthetized pediatric patients should be evaluated separately through institution-specific simulation, anesthesia review, and prospective workflow validation.

From a dosimetric perspective, both workflows maintained robust target coverage and stable junction dose gradients. Variations in D95 were minimal, junction-gradient perturbations were small overall, and no clinically relevant hot or cold spots were observed at the junctions. This is particularly relevant given the known sensitivity of CSI to cranio–caudal misalignment.13, 14 The use of extended overlap regions and gradient-based dose matching effectively mitigated the dosimetric impact of longitudinal setup uncertainties, which are recognized as the dominant contributor to junction dose perturbation in CSI. The absence of differences in junction robustness between CFF-IG and SF-IG further supports the comparability of the 2 workflows from a dosimetric standpoint.

A key distinction between the workflows was setup efficiency. SF-IG was associated with shorter setup time at the cohort level, with most SF-IG sessions completed within 30 minutes. However, this reduction was not uniform across all patients, and some cases showed similar or slightly longer setup times with SF-IG. This likely reflects patient-specific setup complexity, therapist workflow, and practical variability in image-guided treatment delivery. In contrast, CFF-IG frequently exceeded this duration and, in some cases, nearly doubled setup time without yielding measurable improvements in accuracy or dosimetric quality. When setup accuracy and efficiency were jointly evaluated, increased setup time associated with CFF-IG did not translate into systematic gains in geometric precision. These findings suggest that SF-IG provides a more favorable balance between efficiency and accuracy for routine decubitus CSI delivery.

From a clinical workflow perspective, these results have practical implications. CSI treatments in the decubitus position are inherently more time-consuming than conventional supine treatments due to patient positioning, immobilization complexity, and imaging limitations. Efficient workflows are therefore critical to maintaining patient comfort, minimizing intrafraction motion, and optimizing treatment room utilization. The demonstrated efficiency advantage of SF-IG, without loss of treatment quality, supports its use as the preferred default strategy for most decubitus CSI patients.

Nevertheless, CFF-IG retains an important role in selected scenarios. In our clinical experience, deformation of the Vac-Lok immobilization device over time can compromise reproducibility, particularly for long treatment courses. Additionally, roll correction—especially during setup of the lower spine after cranial and upper spine fields have been accepted—remains challenging in the decubitus position. In such cases, comprehensive multi-isocenter realignment using CFF-IG provides improved global control and may be necessary to restore accurate positioning. Accordingly, CFF-IG should be viewed not as inferior but as a complementary strategy reserved for situations where enhanced verification is clinically warranted.

This study has several limitations. First, setup uncertainties were assessed primarily using 2-dimensional oblique kV imaging, which provides indirect information on pitch and yaw but limited sensitivity to roll direction. In addition, although rotational deviations were recorded as part of the geometric setup analysis, rotational perturbations were not explicitly included in the dose recalculation. The dosimetric perturbation analysis was therefore limited to field-specific translational deviations, and the potential dosimetric impact of residual rotational uncertainty should be interpreted as a limitation of this retrospective analysis. Second, setup accuracy is influenced by non-imaging factors, including therapist experience, time pressure, and patient-specific anatomy. While these factors reflect real-world clinical practice, they introduce variability that cannot be completely controlled in a retrospective analysis. Respiratory motion and intrafraction stability were not explicitly quantified in this retrospective study, which limits our ability to fully characterize respiratory-related WET variation during decubitus CSI delivery. Fluoroscopy, repeated kV imaging, or volumetric imaging may provide useful tools for future prospective evaluation of intrafraction motion and setup stability in the decubitus position. Finally, the sample size was modest; however, the consistency of results across patients and isocenters supports the robustness of the conclusions.

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates the feasibility and practicality of treating CSI patients in the lateral decubitus position using PBS in a fixed-beam room. The findings support the broader concept that decubitus CSI represents a logical extension of modern simplified PBS CSI techniques, including single-beam and gradient-matched approaches, and can be safely implemented using efficient image-guided workflows.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that CSI can be safely and effectively delivered in the lateral decubitus position using proton pencil beam scanning in a fixed-beam treatment room. Both CFF-IG and SF-IG achieved setup accuracy within institutional action levels and maintained robust dosimetric quality, including stable junction dose gradients across all treatment fields.

No statistically or clinically meaningful differences were observed between the 2 setup workflows in geometric accuracy or dosimetric robustness. In contrast, SF-IG was associated with reduced setup time without compromising treatment quality. These findings support the use of SF-IG as the preferred default workflow for routine decubitus CSI delivery, while CFF-IG remains a valuable option for selected cases requiring comprehensive multi-isocenter realignment.

Overall, this work supports the clinical feasibility of decubitus CSI in fixed-beam proton therapy settings and establishes efficient, image-guided setup strategies that extend modern simplified PBS CSI techniques to environments with limited imaging and beam-angle flexibility.
