Machine-learning and simulation identify food-derived xanthine oxidase inhibitors with in-vitro activity
Authors: Bo Yu, Zhen Qin, Yuxi Wu, Chengsong He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gout is a common metabolic disorder caused by hyperuricaemia, with xanthine oxidase (XO) playing a key role in uric acid production. Food-derived XO inhibitors are therefore of interest as candidate chemotypes, although any nutraceutical application will require in-vivo validation. In this study, we developed an integrated in-silico workflow that combines machine learning, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics (MD) to prioritize potential inhibitors. From a library of 3,142 medicine–food homology compounds, we trained multiple fingerprint–algorithm classifiers; the topological-torsion Random Forest (TT-RF) model performed best, achieving an AUC of 0.992 and a precision of 0.98 on a held-out test set. Applying this model yielded 128 high-confidence hits, ten of which showed docking scores ≤ –9.0 kcal/mol. Subsequent 200-ns MD simulations indicated that luteolin-7-glucuronide, 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone, and uralenol form stable, compact complexes with XO. In-vitro assays further confirmed XO inhibition, with IC50 values of 26.15, 39.06, and 34.64 µM, respectively, and negligible cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells up to 100 µM. Together, these results identify food-derived compounds with reproducible in-vitro XO inhibition. Their significance for gout management remains to be established in in-vivo studies. More broadly, this study illustrates a scalable framework for natural-product–based inhibitor discovery that can guide future preclinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25624-x.

Introduction

Gout is a common inflammatory arthritis caused by the deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in joint tissues1. It typically manifests as episodes of severe pain and swelling in affected joints, and is strongly linked to chronic hyperuricaemia (elevated serum uric acid levels)1. In many countries, gout has become the most prevalent form of arthritis, affecting about 3–4% of adults in high-income regions2,3. Its global prevalence and burden have risen in recent decades, driven by aging populations and lifestyle factors such as purine-rich diets, obesity, and metabolic syndrome2,4,5. Uncontrolled gout not only impairs quality of life through recurrent flares and joint damage, but is also associated with comorbidities including cardiovascular and renal diseases, underscoring the need for effective long-term management2,6.

A key pathogenic feature of gout is the overproduction or under-excretion of uric acid, which leads to MSU crystal formation. Xanthine oxidase (XO)—a molybdopterin-containing enzyme in purine metabolism—catalyzes the oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to uric acid7,8. Because XO is responsible for the final step in uric acid synthesis, it has become a central therapeutic target in gout and hyperuricaemia management. In fact, clinically used urate-lowering therapies like allopurinol and febuxostat act as XO inhibitors (XOIs) to curb uric acid production9. Allopurinol (a purine analog) and febuxostat (a non-purine inhibitor) are effective at lowering serum urate and are considered first-line treatments to prevent gout flares. However, these drugs are not suitable for all patients—allopurinol can cause hypersensitivity reactions and other adverse effects, and febuxostat has been linked to cardiovascular risks in some reports10. Such safety concerns have prompted a search for safer, alternative XO inhibitors. In particular, there is growing interest in natural products and dietary substances as potential sources of XOIs that might be better tolerated11,12.

Recent research has identified a variety of food-derived compounds with significant xanthine oxidase inhibitory activity. A broad spectrum of phytochemicals—including flavonoids, phenolic acids, stilbenes, alkaloids, polysaccharides, and peptides—from edible plants and functional foods have shown the ability to reduce uric acid formation by inhibiting XO13,14. Polyphenols in particular are well-known for their XO inhibitory effects; these compounds, abundant in fruits, vegetables, tea, and other plant-based foods, can effectively lower uric acid levels in biological systems15,16. For example, many flavonoids (a class of polyphenols) exhibit potent XO inhibition in vitro, especially those with certain hydroxylation patterns, and have been correlated with reduced hyperuricaemia in vivo17. The efficacy of such natural XOIs has been demonstrated in various experimental models, and some food extracts (e.g. from traditional medicinal herbs, berries, or tea) have produced significant urate-lowering effects with minimal toxicity18. These findings highlight the potential of diet-derived bioactive compounds as adjunct or alternative therapies for gout. Leveraging natural XO inhibitors from food sources is attractive not only due to their abundance and structural diversity, but also because they may offer improved safety profiles compared to synthetic drugs.

Given the overlap between diet and medicine, researchers are increasingly focusing on edible medicinal species—a concept known as “medicine food homology” (MFH)—in the search for new gout remedies. In traditional Chinese medicine and other holistic health frameworks19, MFH refers to substances that serve both as foods and as medicines, with no strict boundary between nutritional and therapeutic use20. Such edible medicinal plants or functional foods are generally recognized as safe for long-term consumption, making them ideal candidates for managing chronic conditions like gout. Indeed, many MFH-listed botanicals (e.g. certain fruits, seeds, and culinary herbs) have documented urate-lowering or anti-gout effects in ethnomedicine. Focusing on XO inhibitors from MFH sources is therefore a logical strategy to discover treatments that patients could incorporate into their diet or use as nutraceutical supplements. Since these substances are inherently edible, the risk of severe side effects is expected to be low, which addresses one of the main drawbacks of current pharmacotherapy. For example, Lonicera japonica (Japanese honeysuckle) is an MFH plant whose extracts have been shown to significantly reduce hyperuricaemia in animal models. More broadly, functional foods enriched in anti-XO compounds have demonstrated efficacy in controlling serum urate with minimal adverse effects. The development of food-based therapeutics for gout—“edible medicines”—thus holds promise for safer long-term disease management21. This rationale underpins the emphasis on edible natural products in the present study.

While experimentally screening the myriad of natural compounds in foods for XO inhibition is resource-intensive, computational approaches offer a powerful means to accelerate this discovery process. In particular, machine learning (ML) techniques combined with chemical informatics have emerged as valuable tools for virtual screening of large compound libraries. By training on existing data of XOIs (both potent and weak binders), ML models can learn the key molecular features that distinguish effective inhibitors22. Chemical structures can be encoded as numerical descriptors or fingerprints—for instance, extended connectivity fingerprints (ECFPs)23 that capture substructural patterns—and used as input for algorithms to predict bioactivity. Recent studies have demonstrated that QSAR (quantitative structure–activity relationship) models24 employing ML can accurately predict the XO-inhibiting potential of diverse molecules, including natural products25. Crucially, these in silico models enable researchers to rapidly prioritize promising candidates from thousands of dietary phytochemicals, focusing experimental validation on the most likely inhibitors. For example, Zhou et al. constructed a machine-learning QSAR model from a database of known natural XOIs and successfully used it to identify new inhibitor leads, one of which—vanillic acid, a dietary phenolic compound—was confirmed to inhibit XO with sub-micromolar potency25. Likewise, Wu et al. developed a consensus ML model using ECFP fingerprints and pinpointed specific substructural motifs associated with high XOI activity, guiding the design of novel inhibitor analogues22. These examples illustrate the benefits of ML-driven virtual screening: it not only improves efficiency by filtering out unlikely candidates, but also yields insights into structure–activity relationships that can inform the rational design of more potent XO inhibitors. In the context of edible medicinal resources, such computational screening is especially valuable, as it can mine large phytochemical databases or compound libraries derived from foods to discover new anti-gout agents in silico before committing to laborious wet-lab assays.

After identifying candidate XO inhibitors through virtual screening and molecular docking, it is critical to validate their binding and efficacy using rigorous methods. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation26 has become an indispensable tool in this validation stage of drug discovery. By simulating the physical movements of atoms in the protein–ligand complex over time, MD provides a dynamic picture of how a candidate inhibitor interacts with the XO enzyme under near-physiological conditions. This approach can reveal whether the ligand remains stably bound in the active site (or at an allosteric site)27 and can elucidate key intermolecular interactions that contribute to its inhibitory effect. A promising inhibitor is expected to form a stable complex with XO, exhibiting limited structural drift (low RMSD fluctuations) and persistent hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic contacts over nanosecond to microsecond simulations. For instance, MD studies of polyphenolic inhibitors have shown that the ligand–XO complexes maintain consistent geometry and strong binding interactions throughout 100 ns simulations, supporting their potential as effective XOIs10. Additionally, MD simulations can capture conformational changes in the enzyme that static docking might miss—such as induced fit adjustments or flexibility in the active-site loop/tunnel region upon inhibitor binding. In one recent report, two peptides derived from food proteins were found to bind an allosteric site of XO; 200 ns MD runs confirmed that one peptide (LWM) engaged the enzyme more strongly than the other, causing a noticeable narrowing and twisting of the XO substrate channel, which corroborated its higher inhibitory potency10. Such MD analyses not only validate the stability and binding affinity of hits, but also enhance our mechanistic understanding (e.g., identifying crucial amino acid residues involved in inhibitor binding or allosteric regulation). In summary, incorporating MD simulation into the pipeline provides a robust check on virtually screened candidates, ensuring that only compounds with favorable dynamic binding profiles and interaction energies advance toward in vitro and in vivo testing.

By integrating these approaches, researchers can systematically discover and evaluate new XO inhibitors from edible sources. In this study, we pursued a strategy that combines medicine-food homology resources with modern computational techniques. First, a library of natural compounds derived from edible and medicinal foods was screened in silico using an machine-learning guided model to predict XO inhibitory activity. Top-scoring candidate compounds were then subjected to molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations to examine their binding modes and stability in the XO enzyme’s active site. Through this integrative workflow, we aim to identify potent XO inhibitors from food-source natural products that could serve as safer, efficacious agents for gout treatment. The results provide insight into the structure–activity relationships of edible XO inhibitors and lay the groundwork for developing diet-based interventions or novel nutraceutical drugs for managing hyperuricemia and gout. Materials and methods

Dataset and data preprocessing

The positive dataset utilized in this study comprises 2216 compounds, consisting of known XO inhibitors collected from the PubChem database28 and previously reported literature. To ensure effective model training, additional negative samples were randomly selected from PubChem to balance the dataset and mitigate class imbalance. Furthermore, food-derived molecules were identified based on the list of homologous food and medicine compounds published by the Chinese Ministry of Health, with molecular structures sourced from the TCMSP database29. Specifically, the dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 6:2:2 ratio.

SMILES strings30 were collected and standardized using RDKit31 to ensure molecular consistency and mitigate potential errors in structural representation. The standardization process involved converting SMILES strings to molecular objects, followed by normalization and removal of salts using the RDKit MolStandardize module. Invalid or unprocessable SMILES strings were identified and excluded from the dataset to maintain data integrity. Subsequently, the standardized molecular structures were encoded into multiple fingerprint representations, including MACCS32, Morgan, RDKit31, TopologicalTorsion33, and Atom pairs34. These fingerprints served as input features for the machine learning models, enabling comprehensive molecular characterization and facilitating predictive model development.

Clustering analysis

Molecular clustering was performed using extended fingerprints (2048-bit) generated from SMILES strings, utilizing the rcdk and rcdklibs libraries in R. Dimensionality reduction was applied using Rtsne, with a perplexity of 20, followed by hierarchical clustering (Ward’s method) using the stats package. Representative molecules were selected based on the highest average Tanimoto similarity35, calculated using the proxy package. Visualizations were generated using ggplot2 and plotly, with representative molecules distinctly labeled in the 2D t-SNE plot36.

Machine learning model development and hyperparameter tuning

The machine learning framework in this study was designed to predict xanthine oxidase (XO) inhibitors using multiple fingerprint representations and algorithms. The implemented models include Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP)37, Random Forest38, and XGBoost39. Each model was trained and evaluated using five molecular fingerprint types: MACCS, Morgan, RDKit, TopologicalTorsion, and Atom pairs.

For each fingerprint type, the dataset was split into training and testing sets using stratified sampling to preserve the class distribution. Data preprocessing involved handling missing values with mean imputation and scaling features using the StandardScaler module. Subsequently, hyperparameter tuning was conducted using GridSearchCV with fivefold cross-validation, optimizing for the area under the ROC curve (AUC).

The hyperparameter ranges for each model were defined as follows:

MLP: This model explored different network architectures with single-layer (50, 100 nodes) and two-layer (50, 50 nodes) configurations. The activation functions included tanh and relu, with solvers set to either stochastic gradient descent (sgd) or adam. Learning rates were tested at 0.001 and 0.01, with up to 10,000 iterations and optional early stopping to prevent overfitting.

Random Forest: The model varied the number of trees (100, 200, 300) and maximum depth (10, 20, 30, or unrestricted). Minimum samples required to split a node ranged from 2 to 10, and leaf size varied from 1 to 4 samples. Both bootstrap and non-bootstrap sampling were assessed.

XGBoost: Key parameters included the number of trees (100, 200, 300), maximum depth (3, 5, 7), learning rate (0.01, 0.1, 0.2), and subsampling ratios (0.8, 1.0). Column sampling ratios were also varied to assess model stability.

Model evaluation included calculating key metrics such as sensitivity (SE), specificity (SP), precision (P), F1-score, accuracy (ACC), balanced accuracy (BA), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and AUC. Additionally, ROC curves were plotted to visualize the discrimination capability of each model. Models achieving the highest AUC and MCC scores were further analyzed to assess their robustness in identifying active and inactive compounds, with emphasis on minimizing false positives and false negatives.

Virtual screening

We screened 3142 medicine–food homology compounds using our trained model and selected those with predicted scores > 0.9 for further molecular docking40–42. Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina, using a grid box of 20 Å × 35 Å × 20 Å centered at (x = − 22.8, y = 0.07, z = − 9.632). The 3D structure of the target protein was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3BDJ). To ensure the reliability of the molecular docking procedure, a re-docking study was performed using the co-crystallized ligand oxipurinol from the template protein structure of xanthine oxidoreductase (PDB ID: 3BDJ). The co-crystallized ligand was extracted from the crystal structure and docked back into the active site of the receptor under the same docking parameters as those applied in the virtual screening experiments. The resulting docked pose was then compared with the crystallographic conformation, and the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of heavy atoms was calculated. The obtained RMSD value of 0.092 Å indicates that the docking protocol was able to successfully reproduce the experimentally observed binding mode, thus validating the reliability of the docking setup (Figure S1).

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using the PMEMD.CUDA module within the Amber 22 software suite. A total of 200 ns simulations were performed for each system. The SHAKE algorithm was employed to constrain all bonds involving hydrogen atoms, while the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was applied to handle long-range electrostatic interactions, with a cutoff distance of 8 Å.

The MD workflow began with energy minimization to resolve steric clashes and optimize the initial structures. This process was divided into two stages: 500 steps of steepest descent, followed by 500 steps of the conjugate gradient method. After minimization, a gradual heating phase was implemented, increasing the system temperature from 0 to 300 K over 50 ps. Subsequently, a 50 ps density equilibration was conducted under NPT conditions at 300 K10,43,44.

For the final equilibration phase, constant pressure was maintained for 500 ps, ensuring system stability before proceeding with production runs. The production MD simulations were then carried out for 200 ns with a 2 fs time step, and the Langevin thermostat was applied with a collision frequency of 1 ps⁻1. Data was recorded every 100 ps, resulting in a total of 2000 frames for each system, providing the basis for subsequent structural and dynamic analysis.

XO inhibition assay and IC50 determination

The inhibitory activity of test compounds against XO was assessed using a spectrophotometric method based on the quantification of uric acid formation from xanthine. Uric acid production was monitored at 290 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. The reaction system comprised xanthine (0.5 mM), XO enzyme (0.02 U/mL), and a 1/15 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer, maintained at pH 7.5. Test compound including 5,4'-Dihydroxyflavone, Uralenoll, and Luteolin-7-glucuronide were prepared in a series of dilutions to generate dose–response curves. All reactions were conducted at 25 °C for 25 min.

Results

Dataset characteristics

The analysis of key molecular descriptors (Fig. 2) reveals clear physicochemical distinctions between XO‐active and inactive compounds in our dataset. Active molecules exhibit a right‐shifted distribution in molecular weight (MolWt) and molecular volume (MolVolume), with the majority of actives clustering between 350 and 600 Da and 80–160 Å3, respectively, compared to inactives, which predominantly lie below these ranges. Total polar surface area (TPSA) distributions are broadly similar for both sets but show a slight tendency for actives to occupy moderate TPSA values (50–120 Å2), suggesting an optimal balance between polarity and membrane permeability. Lipophilicity, as measured by computed LogP, is markedly higher in active compounds: the red (active) density curve peaks around LogP ≈ 4–6, whereas inactives center closer to 1–3, indicating that increased hydrophobicity may favor XO binding. Hydrogen‐bond donor (HBD) and acceptor (HBA) counts further differentiate the two groups: actives generally possess fewer HBDs (0–2) and moderate HBA counts (3–6), whereas inactives display broader, right‐skewed distributions, including a higher fraction with > 4 donors or > 7 acceptors. Collectively, these trends suggest that XO inhibitors in our library tend to be mid‐sized, moderately polar yet lipophilic molecules with restrained hydrogen‐bonding capacity—properties that can inform both feature selection for machine‐learning models and the prioritization of candidates for molecular docking and dynamics studies.

Machine learning

The machine learning models demonstrated significant predictive capability in identifying xanthine oxidase (XO) inhibitors based on training datasets comprised of known XO inhibitors. Five distinct fingerprint representations (MACCS, Morgan, RDKit, TopologicalTorsion, and Atom pairs) were evaluated across three machine learning algorithms: Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Random Forest, and XGBoost. Performance metrics, including Sensitivity (SE), Specificity (SP), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Accuracy (AC), Precision (P), F1 Score (F1), Balanced Accuracy (BA), and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), were comprehensively assessed.

Figure 4 presents the confusion matrix analysis for the model predictions across different fingerprint representations and machine learning algorithms. The confusion matrices illustrate the distribution of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives for each model. Notably, the XGBoost model utilizing Morgan fingerprints exhibited the highest classification accuracy, with the majority of misclassifications concentrated in the false negative quadrant. This observation indicates that the model effectively identifies active XO inhibitors but occasionally misclassifies borderline inactive compounds as active. Conversely, the Atom pairs-MLP model displayed higher false positives, indicating a tendency to overpredict active compounds.

Virtual screening

We applied our optimal TopologicalTorsion-RandomForest classifier to a library of 3,142 medicine–food homology compounds and selected 83 molecules with prediction scores above 0.9 for structure-based docking. Docking into the XO active site yielded ten ligands with predicted affinities ≤ –9.0 kcal/mol; the top three were 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone (− 9.88 kcal/mol), Luteolin-7-glucuronide (− 9.85 kcal/mol) and Uralenol (− 9.65 kcal/mol). Their 2D interaction maps (Fig. 7) reveal a conserved anchoring of the flavone core into the hydrophobic cleft adjacent to the molybdenum cofactor, consistently engaging in π–π stacking with Phe914. In comparison, the reference inhibitor allopurinol bound with a slightly weaker docking score (− 8.80 kcal/mol), engaging through multiple hydrogen bonds and polar contacts with residues including Arg804, Asp872, and Leu873.

Molecular dynamics simulation

In order to assess the dynamic stability and conformational behavior of xanthine oxidase (XO) upon binding different flavonoid‐type ligands, we carried out 200-ns all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on four systems: the unliganded enzyme (apo), XO–Luteolin-7-glucuronide, XO–5,4′-dihydroxyflavone, and XO–Uralenol. Allopurinol was included as a reference system. Backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) profiles (Fig. 8 A) show that all systems rapidly equilibrated within the first 20 ns of simulation. Thereafter, the apo enzyme maintained an average RMSD of 1.2 Å, indicating minimal structural drift. In contrast, ligand-bound systems exhibited higher deviations: XO–Luteolin-7-glucuronide plateaued at 2.6 Å, while XO–5,4′-dihydroxyflavone and XO–Uralenol averaged 3.4 Å and 3.8 Å, respectively. The XO–Allopurinol complex showed intermediate stabilization with an RMSD of 3.0 Å. RMSD density distributions (Fig. 8B) corroborate these values and illustrate the narrowest, most sharply defined peak for the Luteolin-7-glucuronide complex, reflecting its superior stabilization of the XO scaffold. Radius of gyration (Rg) analysis (Fig. 8C) further revealed that the compactness of the protein–ligand assemblies differed significantly: XO–Luteolin-7-glucuronide maintained the lowest average Rg of 30.2 Å, closely resembling the apo value (32.1 Å), whereas 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone and Uralenol complexes exhibited expanded conformations with Rg values of 35.0 Å and 33.0 Å, respectively. The Allopurinol complex displayed an Rg value closer to luteolin-7-glucuronide, reflecting moderate compactness. The corresponding Rg density plots (Fig. 8D) confirm these relative shifts in molecular compactness. Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) calculations (Fig. 8E) demonstrate that ligand binding increases surface exposure relative to the apo enzyme (50,500 Å2), with XO–Luteolin-7-glucuronide at 55,000 Å2, XO–Uralenol at 57,500 Å2, and XO–5,4′-dihydroxyflavone at 62,000 Å2; the XO–Allopurinol complex remained intermediate at 56,500 Å2. Finally, per-residue root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) profiles (Fig. 8G) reveal that binding of Luteolin-7-glucuronide confers the greatest rigidity to the catalytically important loop region (residues 350–370), with average fluctuations under 1.5 Å, whereas substantial flexibility (up to 8–12 Å) is observed in the same region for both 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone and Uralenol complexes, as well as in peripheral domains of the apo enzyme (Table S1). The Allopurinol complex showed reduced fluctuations compared with the aglycone flavonoids but did not match the stabilizing effect of luteolin-7-glucuronide. Taken together, these MD metrics consistently indicate that Luteolin-7-glucuronide forms the most stable and compact complex with XO, minimising both global and local conformational fluctuations, with Allopurinol providing a clinically validated benchmark.

IC50 inhibition rate and safety evaluation experiment

The three flavonoid derivatives all proved capable of inhibiting XO in a concentration‐dependent manner (Fig. 9A–C). Luteolin-7-glucuronide emerged as the most potent inhibitor, reducing XO activity from 23.4 ± 2.1% at 10 µM to 79.8 ± 3.2% at 80 µM and yielding an IC50 of 26.15 µM. Uralenoll exhibited intermediate activity, achieving 78.1 ± 3.0% inhibition at 80 µM with an IC50 of 34.64 µM, while 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone reached 73.5 ± 2.7% inhibition at the same concentration and displayed an IC50 of 39.06 µM. Allopurinol, serving as the positive control, showed an IC50 of 28.91 µM, consistent with its established pharmacological activity. These results indicate that glucuronidation of luteolin markedly enhances XO inhibitory potency, and highlight uralenoll as a more effective inhibitor than the aglycone 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone, while demonstrating that the natural glycosylated flavonoid can achieve inhibition comparable to allopurinol.

Discussion

The integrative pipeline that combines a topological-torsion Random Forest classifier with molecular docking and long-timescale molecular dynamics (MD) simulations proved effective for identifying food-derived xanthine oxidase inhibitors (XOIs). By capturing structural features that reflect both hydrophobic contacts and polar interactions, our machine-learning model reduced a library of more than 3,000 medicine–food homology compounds to a manageable set of high-confidence candidates. This strategy demonstrates how computational pipelines can accelerate the prioritization of natural products for further study, although it does not in itself establish clinical utility45,46.

Among the top-ranked ligands, luteolin-7-glucuronide consistently emerged as the most promising in-vitro hit. Its glucuronide moiety contributed multiple hydrogen-bond interactions within the catalytic pocket, while the flavone scaffold engaged in strong π–π stacking with Phe914 and surrounding aromatic residues. This dual binding mode stabilized the enzyme–ligand complex and accounted for the reduced backbone fluctuations and compact gyration radius observed during MD simulations. In comparison, nonglycosylated flavonoids relied more heavily on hydrophobic and π–π contacts and showed greater conformational flexibility. Importantly, luteolin-7-glucuronide inhibited XO with an IC50 of 26.15 µM, comparable to allopurinol (28.91 µM), supporting its relevance as a lead for further evaluation in appropriate in-vivo models.

Glycosylation appears to provide two complementary benefits: improved aqueous solubility and optimized binding geometry, both of which may contribute to greater stability and activity. From a medicinal-chemistry perspective, luteolin-7-glucuronide presents several favorable features, including moderate molecular weight, balanced polarity, and a scaffold amenable to structural modification. These attributes suggest potential for preclinical optimization, though any implications for nutraceutical or therapeutic development remain premature.

This study also has several limitations. All conclusions are based on computational predictions and in-vitro enzyme assays, which cannot fully reproduce the complexity of the physiological environment. Factors such as metabolic stability, intestinal absorption, tissue distribution, and clearance remain unknown. Furthermore, the concentrations required for enzyme inhibition in vitro may not be achievable in vivo because of limited bioavailability. Interactions with other dietary constituents or concomitant medications may further alter efficacy or safety profiles. Without addressing these pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic uncertainties, it would be premature to infer direct applicability to human therapy or functional food development. In addition, although retrospective performance of the TT-RF classifier was strong, prospective validation through large-scale biochemical screening and co-crystallography is essential to confirm predictive robustness.

Looking forward, several directions are promising. Structure-guided optimization—such as selective modification of glycosylation patterns or ring substitutions—could further improve affinity and pharmacokinetic properties. Expanding the computational pipeline to include graph neural networks or ensemble deep-learning models may better capture non-linear structure–activity relationships. Most importantly, integrating these computational and in-vitro results with systematic in-vivo pharmacokinetic, efficacy, and safety studies will be essential to determine whether food-derived XOIs can be advanced toward practical applications in hyperuricaemia and gout management.

Conclusion

We developed a computational workflow that integrates machine learning, molecular docking, and MD simulations for the discovery of food-derived XO inhibitors. The TT-RF model showed excellent predictive performance and identified 128 high-confidence candidates. Docking and MD consistently highlighted luteolin-7-glucuronide as the most stable binder, while 5,4′-dihydroxyflavone and uralenol also displayed favorable profiles. In-vitro assays confirmed XO inhibition with IC50 values in the 26–40 µM range, comparable to allopurinol, and negligible cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells up to 100 µM.

These results demonstrate reproducible in-vitro XO inhibition and establish a proof of concept for computationally guided natural-product discovery. At the same time, the findings do not imply immediate clinical benefit. Translation to gout management will require in-vivo validation of efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and safety. Overall, this work outlines a scalable pipeline for prioritizing natural products as enzyme inhibitors, while emphasizing that rigorous preclinical evaluation is essential before considering therapeutic application.
