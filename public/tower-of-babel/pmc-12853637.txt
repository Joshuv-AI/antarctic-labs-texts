The influence of mixed environmental air pollution on deliberate self-harm among university students: evidence from 20 cities in China
Authors: Tingzhong Yang, Sihui Peng, Shuhan Jiang, Randall R Cottrell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile people are exposed to various air pollutants, limited research exists on the combined effects of multiple pollutants on mental health, and no studies have explored the social and behavioral mechanisms of mental health issues in real-world settings. This study aimed to evaluate the association between mixed air pollutants and deliberate self-harm (DSH), as well as the potential social and behavioral mechanisms underlying these associations.MethodsThe study involved 5,025 university students, selected through a multistage survey sampling process from 22 universities in 20 cities. Participants provided information on socioeconomic characteristics and DSH via a standardized questionnaire. Regional socioeconomic status and air pollution data were obtained from a national database. Multilevel logistic regression models were used to assess the association between mixed air pollution and DSH, and a structural equation model was used to explore underlying social mechanisms.ResultsThe multilevel logistic regression model revealed that the mixed air pollution index, exposure to secondhand smoke (SHS), and the number of air pollution days were significantly associated with the likelihood of DSH, with odds ratios (OR) of 2.78 (95% CI: 1.22, 6.31) and 3.30 (95% CI: 1.67, 6.52), respectively. Significant quantitative correlations were found between the number of air pollution days and the likelihood of DSH (r = 0.5553, p < 0.0001). Structural equation modeling revealed that SHS exposure had a significant direct effect on DSH (β’ = 0.7458, p < 0 0.001). In contrast, air pollution days demonstrated both a direct effect (β’ = 0.3323, p < 0.001) and an indirect effect on DSH through a mediating pathway (β’ = 0.054, p < 0.001).ConclusionUsing nationally representative data from 20 cities in China, this study provides compelling evidence of the detrimental impact of environmental air pollution on DSH. It underscores the urgent need for stronger environmental protection policies and the development of effective mental health strategies to reduce DSH and alleviate environmental strain on mental health.Keywords: Mixed air pollution, Deliberate self-harm, Secondhand smoke, Environmental protection policy, University students

Introduction

Deliberate self-harm (DSH) is defined as the deliberate, direct destruction or alteration of body tissue without conscious suicidal intent and suicidal behavior [1, 2], and is also commonly referred to as nonsuicidal self-injury. Studies have shown that DSH is commonly observed not only in individuals with psychiatric diagnoses but also in nonclinical populations, particularly among adolescents and young adults [3, 4]. DSH in young people is receiving increasing attention worldwide and has become a key focus for both social policy and professional prevention efforts [5–7]. It is estimated that over 700,000 people worldwide die by suicide each year [7]. For each suicide, countless others engage in self-harm, whether or not they have suicidal intent, which is one of the most significant risk factors for suicide among young people [8, 9].

Air pollution, a major contributor to the global disease burden, is gaining recognition for its link to the development of mental health issues [10, 11]. The Stimulus (S), Mental Response (M), and Behavioral and Health Problems (P) theory suggests that different stimuli trigger mental responses, which subsequently lead to behavioral and health disorders [12]. The SMP can also be described as a social and behavioral model, as it provides a theoretical framework that applies to real-world social and behavioral situations. Environmental air pollutants act as harmful stimuli to the human body, provoking strong mental reactions that can lead to a range of behavioral and health issues. Numerous studies indicate that air pollution exposure is a significant environmental stressor associated with an increased risk of mental illness [13, 14]. Many studies have found that DSH is associated with an individual’s socioeconomic status [4, 15] and mental factors [16], as well as contextual variables [4, 15]. However, few studies have included environmental air pollution [17, 18].

While people are exposed to a mixture of air pollutants, previous studies on this issue often fail to assess the combined effects of multiple pollutants [19]. This disconnects research from real-world conditions and can result in inconsistent or contradictory findings, particularly in studies examining a broad range of specific air pollutants, which complicates the interpretation of the results [19]. Mixed environmental air pollution refers to exposure to a combination of various air pollutants, including particulate matter (PM), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), ozone (O₃), and potentially tobacco smoke. Some studies have found that certain air pollutants are significantly associated with DSH [18, 19], but no studies have included mixed air pollution. In the real world, both SHS and overall air pollution can be considered forms of mixed pollution. SHS refers to the smoke from burning tobacco products, generated by people smoking them. When tobacco smoke contaminates the air, especially in enclosed spaces, it is breathed by everyone. If SHS is present in the study area, it can be considered part of the mixed environmental pollution. However, if the study aims to distinguish SHS from other sources of pollution, such as traffic-related emissions or industrial pollutants, it may be more prudent to treat them as separate categories. From a preventive perspective, different sources of air pollution require different governance approaches, strategies and interventions. Social engineering governance is effective in reducing traffic-related emissions or industrial pollutants, while public education aimed at changing people’s behavior is more effective for addressing exposure to SHS.

The biological mechanisms underlying the health risks of environmental pollutants have been extensively studied [11, 20]. However, how social and behavioral mechanisms manifest in real-world contexts remains unclear in addressing this issue. Gaining a better understanding of how environmental pollutants influence DSH in real-world settings, alongside the social and behavioral structures at play, holds far greater significance for preventing these issues and shaping effective policies than merely understanding the biological mechanisms.

In this study, we use national data from 20 cities in mainland China to examine the impact of two indicators of mixed environmental air pollution—air pollution days per year (air pollution days) and SHS pollution on DSH, as well as the potential social and behavioral mechanisms underlying these associations. The specific research objectives are threefold: First, to examine the association between air pollution days, SHS pollution, and DSH. Second, to perform a quantitative analysis of the relationship between urban-level mixed environmental pollutant and DSH prevalence. Third, to explore the potential social and behavioral mechanisms underlying these associations within the SMP model. By focusing on the combination of air pollutants, which better captures real-world exposure, a more comprehensive evaluation of environmental air pollution may be provided. Exploring the mechanisms provides a better understanding of how environmental pollutants influence DSH in real-world settings. This study will strengthen the evidence supporting the harmful impact of air pollution on DSH and deepen the understanding of the social and behavioral mechanisms involved. It would also provide crucial insights for designing effective prevention strategies and interventions aimed at reducing the environmental burden on mental health.

Methods

This study utilized a multistage sampling design. In the initial stage, 22 universities were strategically selected across China to represent diverse regions. One university was chosen from each of the 20 cities, while certain large cities had two universities selected. In the second stage, classes enrolled in medical and health courses offered at the selected universities were chosen, and all students were invited to participate in the survey.

Data collection

Data were collected using the expanded version of GHPSS survey in China [21]. Once participants consented to join the study, they were given a structured self-administered questionnaire, which was distributed during regular lectures and took about 30 min to complete. All responses were anonymous. A standardized protocol was followed across all 22 universities to ensure consistency in questionnaire administration and data collection. The total of 5,182 participants responded to the investigation, and valid questionnaires were completed by 97.5% of potential participants, resulting in a sample of 5,052 students from 22 universities across 20 cities. Figure 1 showed the geographic distribution of the sample across 20 cities in mainland China. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Zhejiang University Medical Center, and verbal consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Measures

Dependent variable

DSH: Participants were asked “Have you ever intentionally harmed yourself but without suicidal intention in the past year?” If the answer is “Yes”, DSH types were assessed. These behaviors included cutting, scratching, carving, burning, hitting, banging, squeezing, pinching, pricking with needles, biting, and hair-pulling [22–24].

Individual-level independent variables

All respondents provided sociodemographic details, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, grade, major, parental occupation, annual household income, and family residence.

SHS exposure was assessed by a survey question about whether participants had been exposed to SHS in the past 30 days [25]. Responses were categorized as 1-no exposure, or 2-exposure. Elevated levels of individual SHS exposure indicate a higher intensity of environmental tobacco smoke pollution.

Mental stress. In the SMP model, mental stress was treated as an indirect variable. It was measured using the Chinese version of the Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS), which has been validated as reliable and valid for the Chinese population [26–28]. The scale contains 14 items assessing stress perception over the past month, with responses rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = very often). The total score is the sum of the item scores, with higher totals indicating greater perceived stress [26]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of CPSS was 0.78, suggesting acceptable reliability in measurement.

Regional-level independent variables

Differential levels implied variations in resources, opportunities, and learning environments. There were several independent variables that reflected potential regional variation. The first was the per capita Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in Yuan, including GDP per capita in the students’ province of origin (referred to as “regional GDP”) and GDP per capita in the city where the university was located (“university city GDP”). The second variable was the total population of the city in which the university was located. We extracted these data from the China Statistical Yearbook for Regional Economy (2014) [29] The third variable was the number of air pollution days, defined as days when pollution exceeded national standards [30]. It obtained from the China City Statistical Yearbook(2014) [31].

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (V.9.4). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sample characteristics and the prevalence of DSH. Both unadjusted and adjusted analyses were conducted. In this study, variables included in the adjusted analysis were selected based on their statistical significance in the unadjusted analysis, as these factors may serve as potential confounders. SAS survey logistic procedures were applied, accounting for intra-cluster correlation by specifying the university as the clustering unit, given the complex sampling design. Associations were validated using a multilevel logistic regression approach implemented via the SAS GlIMMIX procedure [32, 33]. Separate models were constructed for each key predictor, incorporating adjustments for potentially confounding variables. We began with a null model—a two-level structure encompassing individuals and cities—with random intercepts. This initial model (null model) included only a constant term and served to evaluate baseline variation in the probability of individuals experiencing DSH. Building on this foundation, we incorporated fundamental individual and regional variables as fixed effects to develop the baseline model. Subsequently, SHS exposure and the number of air pollution days were incorporated into the model to create SHS exposure model and air pollution days model, respectively. These additions enabled an evaluation of their impact on DSH. The final model, referred to as the integrated model, was constructed by simultaneously including SHS exposure and the number of air pollution days in a single equation. Its purpose was to explore the competitive effects of these two factors on the outcome. Associations between system-level variables and DSH were quantified using odds ratios, accompanied by 95% confidence intervals. Model fit was evaluated through changes in the − 2 log-likelihood values. The significance of random parameter variance estimates was tested using the Wald joint chi-square statistic [32].

This study examines the quantitative relationship between city-level air pollution days and DSH prevalence across 20 cities using correlation coefficients and scatter plots. Since the distribution of these variables is not normal, each variable was log-transformed before plotting.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was used to explore the social mechanisms of DSH with the SMP model [34]. The SAS CALIS procedure was used for SEM. The study examined both direct and indirect effects of SHS pollution and air pollution days on DSH, with mental stress as a potential mediator. The Maximum Likelihood method was used to estimate the model parameters, and model fit was assessed using GFI (>0.9), RMSEA (< 0.1), and NFI (>0.9) [33, 34].

All analyses were weighted, with a weighting factor applied to each student record to account for differences in selection probability across universities [35]. A non-response weight was not used due to the very low non-response rates, as nearly all students present during data collection completed the questionnaires. All analyses were conducted using the university as the clustering unit to account for within-cluster correlation due to the complex sampling design.

We identified 485 cases of DSH among 5,025 participants, resulting in a prevalence of 7.5% (95% CI: 4.1%, 10.9%). The unadjusted logistic analysis showed that age, ethnicity, mother’s occupation, SHS, university city-level population, and air pollution days were associated with DSH.

The multilevel logistic regression model indicated that both SHS and the number of air pollution days were significantly associated with the likelihood of DSH. The odds ratios (OR) were 2.78 (95% CI: 1.22, 6.31) for SHS exposure and 3.30 (95% CI: 1.67, 6.52) for air pollution days. Finally, both air pollution days and SHS exposure were included in the integrated model. Numerically, the association between air pollution days and DSH appeared stronger than that of SHS exposure; however, this difference was not statistically significant, with odds ratios of 3.78 (95% CI: 1.68, 8.49) and 2.68 (95% CI: 1.68, 6.19), respectively (See Table 2).

Discussion

This study found that air pollution days are associated with an increased likelihood of DSH. Based on running full analysis models, highest air pollution days have 3.30 times the likelihood of suffering from DSH, compared to the lowest group. Additionally, this relationship shows a quantitative correlation with r = 0.55523 and p < 0.0001. The study provides scientific evidence of the impact of environmental air pollution on DSH.

Unlike most previous studies, this research examined the combined impact of environmental pollution on mental disorders. Specifically, the study explored the influence of SHS pollution and air pollution days on DSH. Environmental air pollution includes traffic-related emissions, industrial and domestic pollutants, as well as SHS, a specific type of pollution caused by the smoking behavior of those nearby. This study provides a more accurate reflection of real-world exposure, offers a comprehensive evaluation of environmental air pollution. Air pollution is increasingly acknowledged as a major contributor to the global disease burden, with growing evidence linking it to the development of mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, and suicide [10, 11].

The biological mechanisms through which environmental pollutants affect health are well understood [11, 20]. This study’s findings can be explained by these mechanisms.

This study addresses a gap in the literature. The results indicate that the SMP model helps explain how environmental air pollution affects DSH, providing insight into the potential mechanisms by which high levels of air pollution may lead to health issues through mental stress [12]. Whether SHS pollution or other environmental air pollutants introduce harmful stimuli into the body, and regardless of their concentration, they have the potential to cause substantial damage to human health. Even low levels of exposure can lead to adverse effects, contributing to a range of physical and mental health issues over time. Environmental air pollution is a pressing issue that demands our full attention. We must remain alert to the significant and widespread harm it causes to public health, as its effects can be long-lasting and detrimental, impacting not only physical well-being but also mental health and overall quality of life. This information underscores the urgent need for the development, implementation, and enforcement of comprehensive and effective environmental protection laws, policies, and interventions. These measures are essential to mitigate the harmful effects of environmental pollution, safeguard public health, and ensure sustainable ecosystems for future generations.

This model also offers valuable insight, suggesting that mental stress serves as a mediator in the relationship between environmental pollution and DSH. In cases where it is not feasible to completely eliminate environmental pollution, implementing strategies to reduce or alleviate mental stress could play a key role in lessening the negative impact of pollution on DSH. Since DSH is primarily a mental and behavioral phenomenon, addressing mental issues caused by environmental air pollution may help mitigate its harmful effects and reduce the risk of developing severe mental health issues, including DSH. University students represent a particularly vulnerable group when it comes to mental health issues, as they often face high levels of academic pressure, social challenges, and the transition to independent adulthood. Mental health concerns such as anxiety, depression, and stress are prevalent among this population, and suicide has become the third leading cause of death among older adolescents and young adults. This underscores the urgent need for targeted mental health support and intervention programs that are specifically designed to address the unique psychological challenges faced by university students [7].

This study utilizes nationally representative data from 20 cities across mainland China to evaluate the impact of mixed environmental pollutants on DSH. By examining both types of environmental mixed air pollution—SHS pollution and air pollution days—the study aims to gain a deeper understanding of how these different environmental stressors contribute to the risk of DSH. The study not only explores the relationship between environmental air pollution and DSH, but also examines the quantitative nature of this association, assessing how variations in pollution levels correspond to changes in the likelihood of DSH. All results consistently support the proposed hypotheses, confirming the expected patterns of influence between environmental air pollution and mental health outcomes. The study developed a detailed social and behavioral mechanism to explain how environmental air pollutants influence DSH. The study strengthens the evidence base regarding the harmful influence of environmental air pollution on DSH, highlighting the urgent need for both air quality management and mental health services as essential components of DSH prevention efforts.

This study has several limitations that need to be addressed for greater clarity. First, the individual-level variables were collected through self-reported questionnaires, rather than through objective measurements. This reliance on self-reports may introduce reporting biases, which could potentially affect the accuracy and validity of the study’s findings. Second, SHS exposure in this study was assessed through self-reported questionnaires, which inevitably introduces subjectivity and potential recall bias. Such measurement limitations may affect the accuracy and reliability of the exposure assessment [36–38]. Consequently, when self-reported SHS data are considered alongside objectively measured air pollution indicators, the inconsistency in data quality constrains our ability to rigorously quantify mixture effects. This discrepancy also limits the extent to which robust inferences can be drawn regarding the health risks associated with combined exposures. Third, the study’s cross-sectional design restricts our ability to draw conclusions about causal relationships between variables. While we used a large sample size to reduce potential biases, and our results meet several criteria commonly used to support causal inference—such as the strength, consistency, and plausibility of observed associations, as well as the establishment of a social and behavioral mechanism model—these findings should still be interpreted with caution. To confirm and build upon these results, future research employing longitudinal designs is essential to better establish causality and validate the observed patterns. Fourth, the ‘air pollution days’ indicator is advantageous due to its convenience and comprehensiveness. It effectively reflects real-world conditions and helps overcome the inconsistent or contradictory findings often seen in studies that examine a wide range of specific air pollutants, thereby making the interpretation of results more straightforward. However, it serves as an aggregate indicator that does not capture the concentration or chemical composition of specific pollutants such as PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂, SO₂, or O₃. Consequently, this metric may obscure pollutant-specific associations with mental health outcomes. Future research would benefit from integrating more granular exposure data—such as daily pollutant concentrations obtained from ground-based monitoring stations or satellite-derived models—to more accurately identify potential causal pathways.
