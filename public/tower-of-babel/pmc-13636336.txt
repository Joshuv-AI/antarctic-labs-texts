Coumarins as modulators of protein–protein interactions: Computational insights, biological mechanisms, and translational perspectives
Authors: Yasser Fakri Mustafa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoumarins are versatile bioactive scaffolds capable of interacting with diverse biomolecules, yet their potential role in modulating protein–protein complexes remains insufficiently characterized. Modern computational biology now enables rational exploration of how small molecules can influence structural coupling, signal transduction, and pathway rewiring at the interface of proteins. This review aims to synthesize emerging evidence on how integrated computational workflows—combining docking, molecular dynamics, network analysis, and machine-learning—can be leveraged to understand and predict coumarin-driven modulation of protein–protein interactions. Published computational pipelines were surveyed for their application to coumarin–protein complexes, including virtual screening, binding free-energy estimation, structural network modeling, and experimental cross-validation approaches. Mechanistic insights from case studies in signaling, transcriptional regulation, and enzyme assemblies were collated to illustrate biological relevance. Published computational studies suggest that coumarins may engage protein interfaces or PPI-associated regulatory sites through orthosteric or allosteric binding, with predicted consequences for protein conformation and pathway behavior. Across the surveyed literature, simulations predicted ligand-dependent changes involving signaling hubs such as MAPK and JAK/STAT, whereas network-based studies proposed broader changes in interaction landscapes. The strongest evidence derives from selected coumarin-based modulators of the BubR1–CENP-E and LXRα–NCOR1/SMRT complexes, for which computational predictions are supported by biophysical and cellular validation. Unlike conventional reviews that discuss individual computational techniques separately, this review integrates molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, network analysis, and machine-learning into a unified computational workflow that facilitates systematic target prioritization, mechanistic interpretation, and rational optimization of coumarin-based protein–protein interaction modulators for subsequent experimental validation. Computational biology provides a powerful lens for unraveling how coumarins influence protein–protein complex behavior at multiple organizational layers. While prediction uncertainties persist, integrative workflows linking structural modeling with systems-level validation offer a promising avenue for transforming coumarins into next-generation chemical probes or therapeutic candidates. Future refinement of data resources, cross-omics integration, and phenotypic readouts may accelerate the translation of in silico predictions into biologically meaningful outcomes.Keywords: Coumarins, Protein–protein interactions, Computational biology, Network biology, Machine-learning, Drug design

Introduction

Coumarins constitute a remarkably diverse class of natural products, encompassing simple scaffolds such as 7-hydroxycoumarins as well as more complex structures including pyranocoumarins and furanocoumarins. This structural diversity underpins a wide spectrum of biological activities.1 Yet, their capacity to influence protein–protein interactions (PPIs)—a central regulatory mechanism in cellular biology—remains insufficiently characterized. This review provides an evidence-based examination of how coumarin frameworks may modulate PPIs when evaluated within an advanced computational biochemistry workflow.

The discussion begins with an overview of the key chemical features of coumarins and outlines the fundamental principles through which small molecules can alter PPIs, whether by targeting orthosteric interfaces or inducing conformational changes through allosteric binding. Building on these principles, an integrated computational pipeline is introduced, combining ligand-based design, structural modeling, and dynamic simulation to predict or engineer coumarin-mediated rewiring of PPI networks. Selected case studies are then presented to demonstrate how these tools can illuminate coumarin–protein interactions across representative biological complexes. Finally, strategies for experimental validation—including biophysical assays and systems-level confirmation—are highlighted to emphasize the importance of multi-tiered verification.

Unlike previous reviews that primarily describe the pharmacological activities of coumarins or summarize isolated computational approaches such as molecular docking against individual protein targets, this review adopts an integrated computational biology perspective centered on PPI modulation. Rather than cataloguing reported coumarin activities, it critically examines how complementary computational methodologies—including molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, PPI network analysis, machine-learning, and available experimental validation—collectively improve the identification, mechanistic interpretation, and prioritization of coumarin-mediated PPI modulators. By emphasizing methodological integration, critically evaluating the strengths and limitations of current computational strategies, and linking structural predictions with systems-level biological interpretation, this review provides a conceptual framework that extends beyond existing coumarin reviews and identifies future directions for the rational development of coumarin-based PPI modulators.

PPIs continue to represent challenging but highly attractive targets in therapeutic discovery. Although only a limited number of small molecules have been identified that can selectively modulate specific PPIs, recent progress in data infrastructure and computational modeling has opened new possibilities.2 By integrating diverse biological datasets with molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and systems-scale network analyses, emerging frameworks can now predict novel ligands for difficult PPI targets and forecast how these interactions propagate through broader signaling systems.3 Such approaches offer a promising foundation for the rational discovery and design of coumarin-based modulators of protein networks and may accelerate the development of innovative therapeutic candidates.

Evidence was classified into five levels: Level I, computational prediction, including docking, molecular dynamics, free-energy estimation, network analysis, and ML; Level II, biochemical or biophysical evidence, demonstrating binding or altered interaction between purified molecular components; Level III, cellular evidence, demonstrating PPI-related functional consequences in cell-based systems; Level IV, in vivo evidence, derived from animal models; and Level V, clinical evidence, derived from studies in humans. Importantly, computational evidence alone was considered hypothesis-generating and was not interpreted as confirmation of PPI modulation.

Literature search strategy

The literature was identified through structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar from database inception to March 2026. Search concepts combined terms related to coumarins (‘coumarin’, ‘coumarin derivative*’) with terms related to PPIs and computational analysis (‘protein–protein interaction’, ‘protein interaction’, ‘PPI’, ‘molecular docking’, ‘virtual screening’, ‘molecular dynamics’, ‘binding free energy’, ‘network pharmacology’, ‘protein interaction network’, ‘machine learning’, ‘computational biology’, and ‘drug discovery’) using Boolean operators and database-specific syntax. Original experimental studies, computational investigations, methodological studies, and relevant reviews published in English were eligible when they addressed coumarin–protein interactions, PPI modulation, computational analysis of protein complexes, or experimental validation of predicted interactions. Duplicate records, conference abstracts without accessible full text, studies lacking sufficient methodological information, and publications unrelated to PPI modulation or the computational framework of the review were excluded. Records were evaluated sequentially by title, abstract, and full text. Reference lists of relevant publications were additionally examined to identify eligible studies not captured by the electronic searches. Because the objective was critical and integrative rather than exhaustive evidence synthesis, the study was conducted as a structured narrative review rather than a formal systematic review. Accordingly, retrospective numerical reconstruction of the study-selection process was not attempted, as this could introduce inaccurate or non-reproducible estimates.

To support a balanced and representative narrative synthesis, priority was given to studies that directly addressed coumarin-associated protein interactions or PPI-relevant mechanisms, provided sufficient methodological detail for critical interpretation, represented complementary computational or experimental approaches, and collectively covered the principal mechanistic and biological contexts considered in this review. Particular weight was given to studies providing orthogonal experimental validation, while computational-only studies were retained where they illustrated distinct methodological approaches or biologically relevant hypotheses. The final reference set therefore represents literature selected for its relevance to the mechanistic, computational, and experimental themes addressed in this review rather than the output of a formal systematic-review screening process.

Background on coumarins and PPIs

Coumarins represent a broad and structurally diverse class of organic compounds predominantly found in plants, especially within members of the Apiaceae family. Their core benzopyrone ring is central to their biological activity, and structural modifications around this scaffold give rise to a wide spectrum of pharmacological properties.4 Numerous coumarin derivatives have demonstrated antibacterial,5 antiviral,6 antiparasitic,7 anticancer,8 anti-inflammatory,9 and anti-diabetic,10 anti-psychotic,11 anti-obesity,12 anti-hyperlipidemia13 activities, reflecting the scaffold's versatility in interacting with diverse molecular targets.14

Computational analyses—including binding affinity predictions and physicochemical descriptors generated through tools such as Open Babel—show that docking-based scoring functions can effectively distinguish between specific and nonspecific interactions. These measurable attributes offer a rational foundation for designing virtual screening workflows aimed at identifying promising lead candidates.15 Because PPI regulate essential cellular processes, small molecules capable of modulating these interactions serve as powerful tools for probing biological mechanisms. The targeted disruption or stabilization of protein–protein interfaces has become an increasingly valuable strategy for uncovering regulatory nodes within complex cellular networks.16 Traditionally, selected small molecules are evaluated against individual targets, and docking results are used mainly to elucidate mechanisms of action rather than to drive broad discovery.17

More comprehensive computational strategies integrate molecular docking and structural modeling with network-level analyses to identify small molecules capable of modulating PPIs, signaling cascades, and transcriptional regulatory networks. Such approaches can prioritize prospective therapeutic agents or molecular probes while simultaneously relating chemical features to predicted biological activities.18 Within this framework, coumarin scaffolds can be evaluated against protein interaction partners to identify potential orthosteric modulators that engage interaction interfaces or allosteric ligands that influence PPI-associated signaling without directly occupying the protein–protein contact site.19

Chemical properties of coumarins

Coumarins represent one of the simplest classes within the C6–C3 family of natural products, and their name originates from the tonka bean Dipteryx odorata (Fabaceae), in which they were first identified. Biosynthetically, these molecules arise from the phenylpropanoid pathway: phenylalanine is converted into cinnamic acid, which subsequently undergoes condensation, intramolecular cyclisation, and oxidative transformations to yield the coumarin core,20 as shown in Fig. 1. To date, nearly 1300 naturally occurring coumarins and related derivatives have been reported. This structural diversity underpins a wide spectrum of biological activities, including antioxidant, UV-absorbing, anti-inflammatory, anti-allergic, and anticoagulant, and other effects.21 The intrinsic fluorescence of coumarins has also enabled broad utility in dye chemistry, cosmetics, analytical probes, and pharmaceutical formulations. Moreover, several coumarin derivatives have advanced into therapeutic development, particularly for tuberculosis, HIV-1 inhibition, and anticancer drug design.22

Given their pharmacological breadth, coumarins have been extensively characterized through physicochemical profiling. Predictive assays—such as subunit-based lipophilicity models including log P—have performed reliably when applied to coumarin scaffolds. Drug-likeness assessments based on the Lipinski Rule of Five and its extension through Veber's parameters generally reveal good compliance across most derivatives.23 Solubility screening using the highly soluble compound rule further demonstrates that the incorporation of an acidic carboxyl group substantially enhances aqueous solubility, even in coumarins that otherwise lack strong hydrogen-bond–donor interactions. Collectively, these insights highlight the favorable physicochemical landscape of coumarins and reinforce their potential as versatile platforms in medicinal chemistry and therapeutic innovation.24

Mechanisms of PPI modulation

Intracellular signaling depends on highly coordinated networks of PPIs, where subtle structural fluctuations within macromolecular assemblies are amplified into functional biological responses. These PPIs represent attractive therapeutic targets; however, their broad, shallow interfaces make them exceptionally difficult to modulate using traditional small-molecule ligands.25 Emerging evidence suggests that instead of directly blocking a PPI, it may be possible to redirect or correct aberrant signaling by selectively reshaping the communication between interacting proteins. Such an approach could suppress pathological signaling, restore compromised pathways, or re-establish homeostatic states through deliberate rewiring of specific PPI nodes.26 Disruption of transcriptional-regulation complexes provides a compelling example: signaling distortions arising from infection or abnormal cellular stimulation can, in principle, be reversed by targeting key interaction points with appropriately designed small molecules. Modern computational workflows now facilitate the identification of compounds—including coumarin derivatives—that are capable of perturbing these PPI nodes and thereby altering downstream signaling outcomes.27

For the purposes of this review, coumarin-mediated PPI modulation is defined as a coumarin-induced change in the formation, stability, affinity, conformation, or functional organization of a protein complex. Such modulation may occur through direct engagement of the protein–protein interface or through binding to an allosteric site whose conformational consequences alter the interaction between protein partners. Binding to an isolated protein without evidence of an altered PPI is therefore not classified as direct PPI modulation. Evidence for coumarin activity should be interpreted according to the level at which the molecular effect is demonstrated. Direct PPI modulation requires evidence that the compound alters the association, stability, or conformation of interacting protein partners. By contrast, conventional protein–ligand binding or enzyme inhibition demonstrates engagement of an individual molecular target but does not, by itself, establish modulation of a PPI. Likewise, changes in downstream signaling pathways may represent secondary consequences of target engagement rather than direct effects on the underlying protein complex.28

Small molecules influence PPIs either by binding directly at the interaction interface and modifying affinity, or by occupying allosteric sites that induce long-range conformational changes. Coumarins are particularly promising in this regard, where their modular scaffold accommodates a wide range of substituents, enabling fine control over physicochemical and biological properties. This intrinsic plasticity provides opportunities to exploit coumarins as allosteric modulators, selecting distinct binding pockets to achieve tailored effects on protein assembly, or even to disrupt PPIs entirely. Notably, computational studies predict that certain coumarins can bind an allosteric region of the molecular chaperone GroES, promoting conformational transitions that ultimately alter the interaction with this large cylindrical protein complex.29

The integrated computational workflow itself is not unique to coumarins and can, in principle, be applied to other small-molecule classes. Its particular relevance here derives instead from the chemical adaptability of the coumarin scaffold. The conserved benzopyrone core provides a common structural framework, whereas extensive substitution around this core permits systematic modulation of physicochemical properties, target recognition, and predicted binding behavior. This combination of scaffold conservation and derivatization makes coumarins particularly amenable to comparative virtual screening, structure–activity analysis, interface- or allosteric-site optimization, and iterative computational prioritization. Thus, the coumarin-specific premise of the present review lies not in exclusivity of the computational tools, but in how an integrated workflow can exploit the scaffold's structural diversity to investigate and optimize PPI-associated interactions.

The computational approaches discussed in the following sections represent representative methodologies reported in previously published studies and are included to illustrate the current state of computational strategies used to investigate coumarin-mediated modulation of PPIs. No original molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, network analyses, or machine-learning experiments were performed as part of the present review. Instead, the manuscript critically synthesizes published computational evidence and discusses its biological significance, methodological advances, and translational implications.

Computational biological workflow: An integrated framework

The workflow presented in this section is intended as a conceptual framework synthesized from published computational studies rather than a description of analyses performed in the present review. Individual components—including molecular docking, virtual screening, molecular dynamics simulations, PPI network analysis, and machine-learning—have been adapted from established methodologies reported in the literature and are integrated here to illustrate how these complementary approaches can collectively support the discovery and mechanistic investigation of coumarin-mediated PPIs.

Protein structure availability represents an important initial consideration in structure-based PPI analysis. When experimentally resolved structures are unavailable or incomplete, homology modeling can provide structural models for subsequent identification of interaction interfaces and ligand-binding regions. Candidate sequences may be processed using established sequence-analysis and comparative-modeling resources, followed by structural quality assessment using complementary validation metrics. Tools such as ProQ, QMEAN, ERRAT, and SolvX can evaluate different aspects of model reliability before the predicted structure is advanced to molecular docking or molecular dynamics simulations. Nevertheless, predictions derived from modeled structures should be interpreted cautiously because uncertainties in template selection, sequence alignment, loop reconstruction, and side-chain positioning may propagate into subsequent docking and binding-energy calculations.31

Representative computational workflows reported in the literature typically begin with systematic mining of structural databases to identify relevant proteins, heteromeric assemblies, and their interaction interfaces. The curated structures are subsequently used for molecular docking, in which candidate ligands are positioned within target sites. The resulting docking poses serve as the basis for virtual screening to prioritize small molecules predicted to modulate PPIs and alter relative binding affinity.32, 33

Published computational studies commonly employ molecular dynamics simulations to evaluate the conformational stability of docked coumarin–protein complexes and estimate binding free energies while quantifying key interaction contributions. In parallel, PPI network analyses are frequently incorporated to investigate how ligand binding may perturb multi-protein systems and influence signaling pathways. More recently, machine-learning approaches have been integrated with physics-based descriptors to accelerate virtual screening and facilitate the rational discovery of coumarin-based PPI modulators.34, 35

Molecular docking and virtual screening

Representative molecular docking studies, as summarized in Fig. 3, have employed computational tools such as SciPy and BioPython for structural analyses before integrating identified binding pockets into docking platforms including AutoDock Vina and AutoDock-GPU. These workflows predict ligand binding poses across diverse coumarin scaffolds, after which the highest-scoring conformations are selected for structural interpretation and subsequent analyses.36 Published virtual screening workflows have utilized custom-designed libraries of coumarin derivatives, including compounds generated with ChemAxon Marvin JS and encoded using PhysNet-compatible SMILES. Candidate molecules are subsequently docked using AutoDock Vina or comparable docking platforms to prioritize compounds for further computational and experimental evaluation. In these published workflows, protein structures are typically prepared before docking by defining protonation states, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and assigning partial atomic charges using standard AutoDock preparation protocols. Receptor and ligand preparation further includes optimization of ionization and tautomeric states under physiological conditions, addition of missing hydrogen atoms, and assignment of appropriate atom types and partial charges prior to docking.37

The figure outlines the sequential process of compound library preparation, docking-based screening, machine-learning-assisted prioritization, experimental validation, and lead optimization, illustrating how computational methods accelerate the identification of promising PPI modulators.

Several published studies have used predicted binding affinities and docking scores of reference compounds to establish cutoff thresholds for prioritizing candidate molecules, followed by drug-likeness filtering and secondary computational analyses to improve hit selection.38 Although molecular docking remains the cornerstone of virtual screening, its predictive performance depends strongly on the selected search algorithm, scoring function, and receptor representation. AutoDock Vina provides an excellent balance between computational speed and accuracy, making it suitable for large-scale screening, whereas Glide generally achieves higher pose prediction accuracy but requires commercial software. GOLD offers greater flexibility for highly mobile binding sites through its genetic algorithm but is computationally more demanding. Importantly, conventional docking assumes a largely rigid receptor, often overlooking protein flexibility, water-mediated interactions, induced-fit conformational changes, and allosteric communication that are particularly relevant for protein–protein interfaces. Consequently, docking scores should be interpreted as relative ranking metrics rather than quantitative estimates of binding affinity, emphasizing the necessity for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations and experimental validation.

For PPI-oriented interpretation, docking sites should therefore be classified as: (i) interface sites, located directly within the protein–protein contact region; (ii) orthosteric sites, representing established functional ligand-binding pockets that may or may not influence the PPI; and (iii) allosteric or cryptic sites, spatially separated from the interface but capable of altering protein association through conformational coupling. Only the first and, when mechanistically demonstrated, the third categories provide a direct structural basis for PPI modulation. In the reported workflow, compounds assigned to model development and independent evaluation were separated before final model assessment. Molecular descriptors and, where applicable, docking-derived features were generated using the predefined pipeline without allowing outcome information from the independent set to influence model fitting or hyperparameter selection. The trained classifier was subsequently applied to the independent compound set. High-priority predictions were then subjected to a separate docking stage to examine plausible structural interactions with the target complex. This second docking step served as post-prediction structural prioritization rather than model training or internal validation. Maintaining this separation is essential to avoid information leakage between model development and independent evaluation.39

In a complementary analysis, coumarin derivatives were screened against established compound- and target-based databases relevant to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibition, with particular emphasis on interactions involving FrH and RabGDI3. A combined LigandGDB database and user-defined ligand library was employed to investigate the Pseudomonas syringae toxin HopG, while the wild-type TPR–WRKKA–ZHAQ structure was refined using conventional molecular dynamics simulations and MM-PBSA (Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area) free-energy calculations.40 Finally, a user-oriented virtual enzyme-activity prediction platform was developed, integrating multiple synergistic random-forest models with hierarchical, binding-site-compatible feature sets to support PPI modeling. Using integrated pharmacophore mapping and docking strategies, small-molecule inhibitors were designed to disrupt heparan sulfate and heparan sulfate proteoglycan binding to the FGF2–FGFR1 complex.41

Molecular dynamics and free energy calculations

Molecular dynamics simulations coupled with an end-point free-energy analysis, as shown in Fig. 4, were employed to validate the binding poses of the highest-scoring ligands. Several published investigations have performed molecular dynamics simulations using the AMBER18 simulation package together with established force fields and solvent models to characterize coumarin–protein interactions. In representative published workflows, molecular dynamics simulations are commonly performed in an explicit solvent environment, typically using the TIP3P water model with appropriate counterions. In contrast, subsequent MM/GBSA or MM/PBSA binding free-energy calculations are generally carried out as post-processing analyses using implicit Generalized Born (GB) or Poisson–Boltzmann (PB) solvent models. Thus, explicit-solvent molecular dynamics and implicit-solvent free-energy estimation represent complementary stages of the computational workflow rather than alternative simulation protocols.42 For a protein–ligand system, the ligand was explicitly included in the simulated environment to capture realistic conformational fluctuations across the entire set of 17 coumarin derivatives. Simulation times of approximately 100 ns for protein–ligand complexes and shorter simulations for isolated ligands have commonly been reported in studies investigating coumarin binding dynamics.43

Molecular dynamics simulations and end-point free-energy analysis of coumarin–protein complexes. Original illustration prepared by the author for this review. The figure illustrates how molecular dynamics simulations evaluate ligand stability, conformational flexibility, and interaction persistence, while free-energy calculations provide complementary estimates of binding affinity following docking.

Binding affinities of the most promising coumarin candidates were subsequently estimated using an end-point binding free-energy calculation (ΔG), which constituted an integral component of the computational workflow. Partial atomic charges for the docked coumarin molecules were assigned using the AM1-BCC method, following established parameterization protocols reported in the literature. The remaining energetic contributions were evaluated using an empirical energy expression. The resulting binding free-energy estimates accounted for solvation effects at the binding interface, including changes in solvation free energy of side chains upon ligand association.44 By evaluating the energetic perturbation induced by ligand binding on the protein's solvent-exposed surface area, the end-point ΔG approach provided a physically meaningful approximation of binding strength. When integrated with molecular docking, this strategy enhanced both the robustness and reliability of the predicted protein–coumarin interactions.45

Molecular dynamics simulations provide information that extends beyond static docking poses by capturing conformational flexibility, ligand stability, and dynamic interaction networks over time. Nevertheless, simulation outcomes are highly dependent on force-field selection, solvent models, simulation length, and sampling efficiency. Short simulations may fail to detect slow conformational transitions, whereas enhanced sampling methods, such as metadynamics or accelerated molecular dynamics, can improve exploration of the conformational landscape but substantially increase computational cost. Likewise, MM/PBSA and MM/GBSA approaches provide efficient approximations of binding free energies but neglect certain entropic contributions and therefore should be interpreted as semi-quantitative rather than absolute predictors of binding affinity. These approaches provide computationally efficient, model-dependent estimates that are most appropriately used for relative energetic comparisons among related complexes. Their treatment of conformational entropy, solvent response, protonation states, and configurational sampling is approximate; therefore, calculated ΔG values should not be interpreted as experimentally determined binding affinities. These methods cannot independently demonstrate ligand binding or PPI modulation and require corroboration by biophysical or biochemical measurements.

Single trajectories may also provide an incomplete representation of conformational uncertainty, particularly when conclusions depend on relatively small differences between related coumarins. Independent replicate simulations initiated with different velocity distributions are therefore preferable for assessing robustness. Ligand parameterization represents an additional source of uncertainty because inaccurate partial charges, bonded parameters, or torsional potentials may alter predicted ligand orientation and interaction persistence. Consequently, simulation length alone should not be regarded as evidence of adequate sampling; convergence, reproducibility across replicas, and sensitivity to force-field and ligand-parameter choices should also be considered.

Network analysis of protein complexes

Network-based disease analytical approaches have been widely used to represent protein complexes, map their interaction landscapes, and evaluate how local structural variations influence the surrounding molecular environment, as displayed in Fig. 5. Constructing reliable datasets for such network analyses remains a recognized challenge, as it typically requires extensive data curation involving filtering, cleaning, and removal of redundant or low-quality entries.46 To enable a more faithful representation of native multi-protein assemblies operating within living cells, Donald Petrey and co-workers developed a carefully curated database of hetero-oligomeric PPIs in Homo sapiens that systematically addresses these issues. Their framework applies several stringent selection criteria, including the exclusion of homomeric interactions, the removal of complexes with unresolved or incomplete structural information that could compromise molecular docking consistency, and the elimination of interactions involving sparsely represented proteins. Through this process, the resulting interaction network comprises 2947 distinct protein nodes connected by 17,449 unique interaction edges, providing a robust foundation for network-level analysis.47

The network illustrates how disease-associated proteins are connected within biological pathways and demonstrates how network pharmacology can identify central hubs and interaction modules that may be targeted by coumarin derivatives.

Three complementary network representations were then constructed to capture different dimensions of protein behavior. The quaternary structure network encompasses all hetero-oligomeric protein complexes derived from the curated dataset, offering a structural overview of protein assemblies. The signaling hub network narrows this scope to complexes formed by proteins participating in specific signal transduction pathways, thereby highlighting functional connectivity.48 In parallel, the allosteric modulation network focuses on protein complexes that share common enzymatic activities, emphasizing regulatory and dynamic aspects of interaction. Within these computational frameworks, protein complexes and their associated interactions are systematically identified, after which in silico perturbations are introduced to evaluate how local structural changes influence ligand binding and the propagation of allosteric effects across protein interaction networks.49

Despite their ability to reveal systems-level organization and identify central regulatory hubs, network-based approaches remain highly dependent on the completeness and quality of the underlying interaction databases. Protein interaction maps are inherently dynamic, context-dependent, and often tissue- or disease-specific, whereas most available databases represent static interaction networks compiled from heterogeneous experimental conditions. Consequently, network-derived predictions should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating tools that require validation using structural, biochemical, and functional studies before biological conclusions can be established. Incomplete interactome coverage further limits network interpretation because absence of a recorded interaction cannot be assumed to indicate biological absence. Protein abundance, subcellular localization, disease-associated mutations, post-translational modifications, cell type, and temporal state may substantially reconfigure interaction networks. Consequently, network centrality or pathway connectivity derived from a generic static interactome should not be equated with functional importance in a specific tissue or disease setting.

Machine-learning integration for prediction and design

Within coumarin-focused PPI discovery, ML is most useful as a prioritization layer linking chemical descriptors and structure-based interaction features to candidate selection. Relevant inputs may include coumarin molecular fingerprints, physicochemical descriptors, docking scores, protein–ligand interaction fingerprints, interface-contact features, and MD-derived descriptors. These models can prioritize coumarin derivatives according to their predicted capacity to engage PPI interfaces or PPI-associated regulatory sites, thereby narrowing compound libraries for subsequent structural and experimental evaluation. Importantly, ML predictions should be interpreted as prioritization evidence rather than independent confirmation of PPI modulation.

Machine-learning classifiers, as summarized in Fig. 6, have been applied to support binding-affinity prediction and enhance enrichment performance in docking-based virtual screening. For each docking pose, ten distinct feature sets were computed, capturing complementary properties of the ligands and their interaction patterns with the target proteins. Each feature set was evaluated independently using a dedicated classifier.50 Decision-tree models were selected because they offer a favorable balance between computational efficiency and model interpretability.51 In parallel, one-class support vector machine classifiers were trained on the same feature representations to identify compounds with a high likelihood of target binding, using an initial collection of experimentally active molecules as the reference class 52.

The figure summarizes representative supervised and unsupervised machine-learning algorithms frequently incorporated into virtual screening, binding-affinity prediction, and compound prioritization workflows for identifying potential coumarin-based PPI modulators.

Prediction of PPI modulation was implemented using a classification strategy inspired by the Physicists' Revenge principle. The resulting neural-network architecture was intentionally designed to remain interpretable, with input features extracted directly from AutoDock Vina docking outputs; this framework is readily transferable to alternative docking or scoring engines by substituting the corresponding source code.53 Despite operating on relatively small datasets, the model was trained and validated using robust machine-learning protocols that integrate experimentally measured binding data with signals derived from physics-based simulations.54 This approach leverages compact yet information-rich datasets, enabling efficient learning without sacrificing predictive reliability. In addition to the primary model, eight alternative machine-learning algorithms were evaluated. Among them, the MB-SS approach and the random-forest regressor demonstrated superior performance in predicting binding energies.55

Additional concerns include class imbalance, chemical redundancy, and data leakage. Strong enrichment of inactive over active compounds may bias classifiers toward the majority class, whereas closely related analogues distributed across both training and test sets can inflate apparent predictive performance. Leakage may also occur when docking-derived or experimentally derived information used during feature generation or compound selection is inadvertently shared between model-development and evaluation datasets. Scaffold-aware or cluster-based splitting, strict separation of preprocessing procedures, and evaluation against genuinely independent external compound sets are therefore preferable to unrestricted random splitting.

Machine-learning methods have markedly improved virtual screening by identifying complex nonlinear relationships that are often inaccessible to conventional scoring functions. However, model performance depends critically on the quality, diversity, and size of the training datasets. Models trained using limited or chemically biased datasets frequently exhibit reduced generalizability when applied to structurally novel coumarin derivatives. Consequently, rigorous external validation, independent test sets, and model explainability remain essential for reliable application in drug discovery. Despite substantial advances in computational methodologies, successful translation into clinically useful drug candidates remains a major challenge. High docking scores, favorable molecular dynamics trajectories, or accurate artificial intelligence-based predictions do not necessarily correlate with biological activity, target selectivity, pharmacokinetic behavior, toxicity, or clinical efficacy. Protein–protein interfaces are highly dynamic and are strongly influenced by cellular context, post-translational modifications, protein abundance, and competing molecular interactions that are difficult to capture using current computational models. Likewise, artificial intelligence algorithms remain constrained by the availability of high-quality experimental datasets and may perform poorly when predicting compounds that lie outside their training domain. Consequently, computational predictions should be regarded primarily as hypothesis-generating tools rather than definitive evidence of therapeutic potential. Reliable drug discovery therefore requires iterative integration of computational modeling with biochemical assays, structural biology, cellular validation, pharmacokinetic evaluation, and in vivo studies before candidate coumarins can be advanced toward clinical development.

Although each computational methodology offers distinct advantages, none is independently sufficient for reliable prediction of coumarin-mediated PPI modulation. Molecular docking provides efficient initial screening but lacks adequate treatment of protein flexibility, whereas molecular dynamics simulations improve structural realism at substantially greater computational cost. Network analyses place ligand–target interactions within a systems-level context but remain constrained by incomplete interactome datasets and limited biological context. Machine-learning models can markedly accelerate compound prioritization; however, their predictive performance depends critically on the availability of diverse, high-quality experimental data and rigorous external validation. Consequently, the greatest predictive confidence is achieved through integrated workflows that combine complementary computational methodologies with experimental validation, rather than relying on any single approach in isolation.

Beyond algorithm selection, the reliability of machine-learning models depends fundamentally on the quality of the underlying training data and the engineering of informative molecular descriptors. Typical input features include molecular fingerprints, physicochemical properties, docking scores, interaction fingerprints, graph-based molecular representations, and descriptors derived from molecular dynamics simulations. Because many datasets used for PPI modulation contain relatively few experimentally validated active compounds, careful feature selection and dimensionality reduction are often necessary to minimize noise and improve model generalizability. Model performance should be assessed using rigorous validation strategies, including k-fold cross-validation together with independent external test sets whenever available. Particular attention should also be given to overfitting, which may produce excellent performance on training data but poor predictive accuracy for chemically diverse compounds. Model interpretability is particularly important in coumarin/PPI prediction because identification of the molecular features driving classification can help distinguish derivatives prioritized for plausible interface or allosteric engagement from predictions based primarily on nonspecific chemical similarity.

Case studies stratified by the nature and strength of PPI evidence

Signaling cascades, transcriptional control mechanisms, and multi-subunit enzymatic complexes constitute three principal frameworks through which PPIs can be modulated in a coordinated manner.56 Investigating coumarins that simultaneously influence multiple interacting partners within these representative protein assemblies provides valuable insight into the broader applicability and transferability of this conceptual approach. Given the illustrative intent of such analyses, it is essential to examine a structurally diverse panel of coumarin derivatives encompassing a wide range of chemical features and substitution patterns.57 To maintain consistency with the operational definition of PPI modulation adopted in this review, the following examples are interpreted according to the nature of the supporting evidence. Direct PPI modulation requires evidence that a coumarin alters the association, stability, or organization of defined protein partners, either through interface engagement or experimentally supported allosteric regulation. By contrast, computationally predicted interface binding is considered putative PPI modulation, whereas kinase inhibition, receptor engagement, enzyme inhibition, or downstream pathway changes without demonstration of altered protein–protein association are classified here as indirect pathway-level evidence.

Cellular signaling pathways orchestrate fundamental biological processes, including cell survival, proliferation, programmed cell death, and differentiation. Dysregulation of these pathways is a hallmark of numerous diseases, particularly cancer, which has driven sustained interest in their therapeutic targeting.58 Contemporary integrative strategies combine structural, biological, and computational resources to elucidate pathway dynamics under both physiological and pathological conditions. Within this context, small-molecule inhibitors or activators are commonly designed to modulate critical signaling nodes and thereby alter downstream biological outcomes.59

Coumarins have been shown to interact with multiple kinase targets, with experimental evidence supporting their ability to modulate key components of the MAPK and JAK2/STAT3 signaling axes. Perturbation of these central nodes offers a mechanistic route to interfere with signaling networks that regulate tumor growth, apoptotic balance, anchorage-independent survival, invasive behavior, and metastatic progression.60 Accordingly, coumarins emerge as promising pathway-oriented modulators whose dynamic behavior within complex signaling networks warrants systematic investigation.61 To this end, conformational fluctuations of selected kinases were analyzed along molecular dynamics trajectories, and a chemically diverse set of twelve coumarins was docked across a 5 × 4 grid within the ATP-binding sites of JNK1, ERK2, and p38α. This combined approach enables evaluation of whether coumarins engaging multiple signaling nodes can reshape pathway topology and functional output.62 In this example, the reported biological effect reflects modulation of an individual protein target rather than direct experimental evidence of PPI disruption; however, the resulting structural or signaling changes may indirectly influence protein interaction networks. The following case studies are organized to distinguish computational predictions from findings supported by experimental validation, thereby illustrating different levels of evidence available for coumarin-mediated modulation of PPIs. Accordingly, the mechanisms discussed below are explicitly described as computationally predicted, experimentally supported at the molecular or cellular level, or validated in vivo, according to the highest level of evidence available in the cited study.

Indirect pathway-level evidence: signaling and kinase-associated effects

Multiple signaling pathways have been investigated as potential intervention points for coumarins; however, most of the available evidence in this category concerns modulation of individual signaling proteins, pathway-associated targets, or computationally inferred interaction networks rather than experimentally demonstrated alteration of a defined PPI. Computational analyses indicated that coumarins preferentially associate with key network hubs embedded within major cancer-related signaling cascades, including the transforming growth factor-β, phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B, Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription, nuclear factor-κB, and p53 pathways.63 In addition, coumarin derivatives were predicted to interact with protein complexes involved in the Hippo and platelet-derived growth factor signaling pathways, where their binding is expected to induce measurable perturbations within the broader interaction network.64

Among the signaling-pathway-associated PPI targets identified, the platelet-derived growth factor pathway represents the principal angiogenic axis, while vascular endothelial growth factor C functions as a central regulator of lymphatic vessel development. Crosstalk between transforming growth factor-β signaling and lymphangiogenic processes also emerged as an alternative therapeutic target.65 Coumarins capable of engaging functional protein complexes within biologically relevant signaling pathways—particularly those linked to angiogenesis, lymphatic vessel morphogenesis-related signaling, and vascular endothelial growth factor C interactions—may therefore serve as promising lead structures for therapeutic development or for supporting de novo lymphotransplantation strategies.66 Furthermore, the regulation of angiogenic activity in lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor-1–expressing cells appears to involve allosteric control, characterized by the presence of neighboring regulatory elements and the requirement for coordinated occupation of multiple allosteric sites to achieve a measurable biological effect.67

Computationally predicted PPI modulation: transcriptional regulatory complexes

Coumarins have been shown to modulate PPIs not only at the level of signaling cascades but also within transcriptional regulatory complexes. One illustrative example is the regulatory assembly controlling expression of the pcpA gene, as investigated by Rao and co-workers. In this system, PCPA functions as a transcriptional regulator that governs pcpA, a gene required for the catabolism of p-cresol.68 Transcriptional activity is enhanced by agmatine and cyclic phosphodiesterase A, whereas it is suppressed by 4-aminobutyrate. Beyond its role as a metabolic effector, 4-aminobutyrate directly engages the protein–protein regulatory interface, an interaction that can be captured and characterized through molecular dynamics simulations.69 Network-based modeling of the associated PPIs highlights a coumarin-mediated effect at the interface formed by PCPB, PCPD, and the PCPB–PCPD complex, which together play a critical role in regulating pcpA gene expression.70

A second representative case involves basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factor complexes in plants, comprising AtbZIP63, AtbZIP17, AtbZIP46, and AtbZIP75. These transcription factors coordinate the expression of genes linked to abscisic acid signaling, salt and drought tolerance, and immune responses. AtbZIP17, AtbZIP63, and AtbZIP46 can assemble as homo- or heterodimers at the Golgi apparatus, where they sense endoplasmic reticulum stress and orchestrate gene regulation during the unfolded protein response.71 Notably, bZIP transcription factors have also been implicated in the control of lignin biosynthesis. Computational analyses predict that the coumarin derivative 7-hydroxy-4-methylcoumarin preferentially docks at the interaction interfaces of bZIP homo- and heterodimeric complexes involved in abscisic acid-mediated stress responses. Consistent with these predictions, protein–protein network modeling indicates that 7-hydroxy-4-methylcoumarin can bind directly at the protein–protein interfaces of these transcriptional assemblies, thereby potentially influencing their regulatory activity.72

Cautionary case study: discordance between computational prediction and experimental function

Two functionally distinct classes of enzymatic multiprotein assemblies cooperate to govern cellular sphingolipid metabolism. The first involves the sphingomamidine deacetylase–serine palmitoyltransferase (SA–SPT) module, while the second comprises the ceramide synthase–sphingomyelin synthase (CS–SMS) complex.73 Computational predictions suggested that one coumarin derivative could bind to and potentially perturb the SA–SPT interface, whereas another compound was predicted to associate with the CS–SMS assembly. However, subsequent functional assays revealed that these molecules did not elicit the anticipated alterations in sphingolipid metabolic output.74

This negative result is methodologically informative rather than merely inconclusive. It demonstrates that computationally plausible occupation of a proposed protein interface does not necessarily translate into measurable perturbation of complex function. The discrepancy may reflect limitations in structural representation, incorrect assignment of the biologically relevant interaction architecture, insufficient treatment of conformational or cellular context, or indirect compensation within the surrounding metabolic network. More broadly, this case illustrates why docking, molecular dynamics, and energetic predictions should be regarded as hypothesis-generating stages of a PPI-discovery workflow and why direct physical-interaction measurements and orthogonal functional assays are required before a predicted coumarin–PPI mechanism can be considered established.

Further experimental interrogation of the proposed dual-enzyme ensembles indicated an absence of direct physical interaction between SA and inositol phosphorylceramide synthase components. This observation raised the possibility that their functional coordination occurs indirectly, perhaps through the formation of a higher-order multiprotein complex involving additional regulatory proteins or cofactors.75 In line with this interpretation, measurements of CS and SMS activities showed sensitivity to inhibition or genetic depletion of several proximal interacting partners—including pph3Δ, scdXΔ, KSHV K15, and VZV SPT—across all tested combinations. These findings point to a cooperative regulatory network that collectively modulates serine metabolism and the biosynthesis of sphingoid bases, ceramides, glucosylceramides, and sphingomyelin.76

Notably, this cooperative regulatory mechanism appears to converge on at least three key enzymatic nodes—SPT, CS, and SMS—while routing metabolic flux through different branches produced distinct phenotypic outcomes in Schizosaccharomyces pombe.77 Complementary insights from solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy revealed that the two classes of SPT enzymes occupy distinct lipid microenvironments. This spatial segregation may enable selective sensing of lipid intermediates and facilitate the transfer of substrates from SA activity toward C28-ceramide production.78 Collectively, these observations support the view of this system as a dynamic regulatory hub that orchestrates the balance between sphingoid base formation and ceramide synthesis via SA, and sphingomyelin production via SMS. Taken together, these observations provide functional context for the proposed interaction network but do not establish direct coumarin-mediated stabilization or disruption of the predicted PPIs.79 To facilitate comparison among the representative PPI systems discussed above, the principal biological targets, computational methodologies, proposed mechanisms of action, and functional implications are summarized in Table 2.

Critical appraisal of computational strategies

No single computational methodology is sufficient for the reliable identification of PPI modulators. Molecular docking efficiently prioritizes candidate compounds but cannot adequately account for receptor flexibility or long-range conformational effects. Molecular dynamics simulations improve structural realism by evaluating dynamic stability but remain computationally intensive and sensitive to simulation protocols. Network-based analyses provide systems-level insight into signaling perturbations but depend heavily on the completeness and quality of interaction databases, which frequently contain false-positive or context-specific interactions. Machine-learning approaches substantially accelerate prediction and compound prioritization; however, they are vulnerable to overfitting, dataset bias, and limited interpretability. Therefore, the most robust strategy combines these complementary approaches within an integrated workflow in which docking generates initial hypotheses, molecular dynamics refines binding mechanisms, network analysis evaluates biological consequences, and machine-learning prioritizes candidates for experimental testing. Such multi-level integration reduces methodological bias and increases confidence in identifying biologically relevant coumarin-mediated modulation of PPIs.

Experimental corroboration and validation strategies

The experimental evidence base for direct coumarin-mediated PPI modulation remains substantially smaller than the computational literature. This distinction is important because demonstration of ligand binding, enzyme inhibition, or pathway modulation does not establish altered protein–protein association. Accordingly, experimentally validated examples are prioritized here according to whether biochemical, biophysical, cellular, or phenotypic evidence directly supports alteration of the proposed protein interaction. Any claim that a natural product or its derivative modulates PPIs must ultimately be supported by robust experimental evidence. At a minimum, the coumarin derivative should be experimentally confirmed as a bona fide ligand for one of the interaction partners at the original protein–protein interface. Subsequent validation steps depend on the intended mode of modulation, whether through direct competition at the interface (orthosteric inhibition) or through indirect conformational control (allosteric regulation).80

For coumarins predicted to occupy orthosteric sites, their binding behavior should be benchmarked against that of endogenous ligands or well-characterized inhibitors. When the proposed binding region has not yet been experimentally mapped, mutation-guided structural studies should be integrated with binding free energy calculations to strengthen confidence in site assignment and interaction specificity,81 as illustrated in Fig. 7. In parallel, coumarin derivatives predicted to target piperazine-like scaffolds within hetero-oligomeric protein assemblies merit early prioritization, as modulation of such interfaces is more likely to translate into measurable functional outcomes.82

Functional validation can be further supported using bacterial two-hybrid systems. For example, a negative interaction phenotype observed in the presence of AckA (acetate kinase A) and a given coumarin derivative would justify deeper investigation. Such cellular models can clarify whether the compound perturbs acetate metabolism and induces a detectable phenotypic response.83 Complementary transcriptomic profiling, enabled by deep-sequencing approaches, would allow the identification of both direct and indirect regulatory effects at the transcriptional and translational levels.84

Additional coumarin derivatives should not be excluded from validation, as these strategies are broadly applicable to the parent scaffold. Structure-guided competitive inhibition of the AckA–Mg+2–NAD+ complex may provide a quantitative functional readout for mechanistic assessment. Moreover, the involvement of multiple neighboring proteins in the proposed interaction network suggests the possibility of higher-order molecular and cellular coupling, influenced by stoichiometric organization.85 Structural or functional interrogation of substituents, such as benzyl groups at the 5- and 6-positions of the coumarin core, may further refine structure–activity relationships.86 The integration of experimentally resolved structures, functionally validated molecular scaffolds, and predictive machine-learning models ultimately provides a comprehensive framework for constructing and understanding coumarin-modulated PPI networks.

Despite substantial advances in molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, network pharmacology, and machine-learning, only a subset of computationally predicted coumarin–protein interactions has been confirmed experimentally. Computational workflows are highly effective for prioritizing candidate molecules and generating mechanistic hypotheses; however, favorable docking scores or predicted binding free energies do not necessarily translate into measurable biological activity. False-positive predictions frequently arise from limitations in scoring functions, simplified receptor representations, neglect of protein flexibility, solvent effects, or cellular pharmacokinetic factors that are not adequately captured by in silico models. Conversely, false-negative predictions may occur when computational approaches fail to identify transient allosteric pockets, induced-fit conformational changes, or context-dependent PPIs that become apparent only under physiological conditions. Consequently, computational predictions should be considered a framework for hypothesis generation rather than definitive evidence of biological efficacy. Integration with orthogonal experimental approaches—including biophysical binding assays, mutagenesis studies, structural biology, cellular functional assays, and in vivo validation—is therefore essential to distinguish biologically relevant PPI modulators from computational artifacts and to improve the translational success of coumarin-based drug discovery.

Benchmark experimentally validated PPI modulators: BubR1–CENP-E and LXRα–NCOR1/SMRT

Among the examples identified in the literature, the BubR1–CENP-E and LXRα–NCOR1/SMRT systems provide the strongest current support for direct coumarin-mediated PPI modulation because computational predictions are complemented by orthogonal biochemical, biophysical, cellular, and/or functional evidence. These systems therefore serve as benchmark cases against which the more speculative computational and pathway-level examples discussed above should be interpreted.

Coumarin-driven modulation of PPIs can be experimentally substantiated through in vitro assays, thereby validating computationally predicted binding modes and affinities of selected small molecules. Budding uninhibited by benzimidazoles-related 1 (BubR1) is a central mitotic regulator in eukaryotic cells and an essential component of the spindle assembly checkpoint. Its interaction with the mitotic motor protein centromere-associated protein E (CENP-E) is critical for accurate chromosome segregation, and disruption of this interaction has been closely associated with the development of aneuploidy.87 Beyond mitotic errors, aberrant BubR1–CENP-E signaling has been linked to pathological conditions such as cancer and metabolic disorders, including diabetes.88 Experimental studies have demonstrated that a specific coumarin derivative directly binds to BubR1 at the CENP-E interaction interface. This binding mode, initially predicted by molecular docking, was confirmed using biolayer interferometry. In line with these observations, the compound effectively inhibits the BubR1–CENP-E interaction in cellular systems and elicits the anticipated mitotic defects. Disruption of the BubR1–CENP-E complex consequently weakens spindle checkpoint activity and promotes aneuploidy in the presence of the small molecule.89

In a related regulatory context, the nuclear receptor liver X receptor alpha (LXRα) forms a transcriptionally repressive complex with the co-repressors nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCOR1) and silencing mediator of retinoid and thyroid hormone receptors (SMRT). Small-molecule-induced dissociation of this complex represents a mechanism for activating LXR-dependent transcriptional programs.90 Computational docking analyses have identified coumarin-based compounds as potential disruptors of the LXRα–NCOR1 interaction. Subsequent chemical and cellular assays confirmed that one such derivative effectively inhibits this interaction in a concentration-dependent manner, leading to activation of LXR-mediated transcription.91 Further biophysical characterization of the LXRα–NCOR1–SMRT complex using biolayer interferometry revealed a measurable reduction in binding affinity upon compound treatment.92 Consistent with these mechanistic findings, transcriptomic profiling demonstrated robust upregulation of canonical LXR target genes following exposure to the coumarin derivative.93

Cellular and systems-level validation

To achieve comprehensive experimental validation, the proposed strategy integrates multiple biological model systems, including in vitro assays, cell-based platforms, and whole-organism models, to substantiate the activity of the predicted PPI modulators and to assess their consequences at higher levels of biological organization.94 Coumarin scaffolds identified through the preceding computational and screening stages could be evaluated for their ability to bind the corresponding protein partners using established biophysical and molecular techniques, such as surface plasmon resonance, isothermal titration calorimetry, and yeast or mammalian two-hybrid assays. Complementary structural analyses may be undertaken to elucidate binding sites and interaction modes.95

For selected protein complexes, including those involving Notch, c-Myc, and epidermal growth factor signaling, the functional impact of interaction modulation could be investigated at the cellular and organismal levels. These studies may employ Caenorhabditis elegans and Saccharomyces cerevisiae as tractable model systems, with compound biosynthesis facilitated through Nicotiana benthamiana-mediated expression.96 Changes in cellular metabolism and phenotypic responses could be monitored to capture downstream functional consequences. In parallel, transcriptional profiling of signaling pathways associated with the targeted interaction nodes may be performed to reveal the biological processes affected by perturbation of the protein complexes.97 To broaden the analysis beyond individual PPIs and to evaluate global effects on signal transduction and cellular metabolism, the final phase may integrate molecular modeling outputs with transcriptomic data. This combined approach aims to establish direct links between predicted PPI modulators and gene expression patterns responsive to protein complex disruption.98 Such insights could not only refine the rational design of coumarin-based modulators used in the model systems but also facilitate the identification of new candidate compounds for the exploration of additional PPIs.

Implications for therapeutic design and synthetic biology

The data-driven insights and rational design strategies emerging from this analysis provide a valuable framework for the discovery and optimization of coumarins as modulators of PPIs within therapeutic and synthetic biology contexts.99 Coumarins are increasingly recognized for their capacity to influence complex molecular assemblies rather than isolated targets. While the analytical scope encompasses defined signaling cascades, transcriptional regulatory circuits, and enzymatic multiprotein complexes, the following emphasizes the broader implications of employing coumarin scaffolds to modulate PPI networks in a controlled and predictable manner.100

From a pharmacological perspective, coumarins are widely regarded as environmentally benign molecules that exhibit notable anticancer potential101 alongside relatively low toxicity. Compared with many aromatic heterocyclic frameworks, coumarin-based compounds often demonstrate improved tolerability and reduced adverse effects, making them attractive candidates for drug development.102 Beyond therapeutic applications, the intrinsic ability of coumarins to interact with metal ions has been exploited in analytical and sensing platforms, while their relaxation properties support their use in magnetic resonance imaging applications. Structural features of the coumarin core, particularly the presence and positioning of hydroxyl and carbonyl groups, play a critical role in shaping biological activity and target selectivity.103

Notably, selected coumarin derivatives have been shown to exert selective modulation of apoptotic pathways in cancer cells, potentially mitigating the off-target cytotoxicity commonly associated with conventional chemotherapeutic agents.104 In addition, coumarins have emerged as effective and cost-efficient small-molecule alternatives to peptide-based inhibitors of PDZ domains, with demonstrated potential to restore glial cell function and suppress tumor cell motility and invasiveness.105 Finally, rational modification of coumarin structures offers opportunities to fine-tune β-cyclodextrin–coumarin interactions, further expanding their utility in biological and biomedical applications.106

Challenges, limitations, and ethical considerations

Coumarins represent a versatile class of small molecules with well-recognized attributes that make them attractive for pharmacological development, particularly in the form of structurally tailored derivatives. However, the use of coumarins to predictably modulate PPIs requires careful interpretation, as computational and network-based predictions may not always translate into functional biological outcomes. The findings discussed here demonstrate that coumarin derivatives can effectively influence selected protein–protein assemblies rather than inducing broad, nonspecific disruption of cellular interaction networks. Functional validation using RNA sequencing indicated that the observed effects of coumarins were not associated with generalized perturbation of the osmoregulatory interaction network. Instead, their activity was confined to discrete functional modules, highlighting a degree of target specificity at the level of defined protein complexes. Comparative analysis across multiple signaling pathways further enabled the discrimination of pathway-specific regulation and facilitated the identification of potential physical or functional links underlying combined phenotypic responses. These insights were strengthened through enrichment-based predictive approaches adapted to multi-class biological systems.

In parallel, detailed structural and physicochemical evaluation supported the role of ortho- and para−/ortho-substituted coumarin derivatives as modulators of specific nodes within signal transduction and transcriptional regulatory networks. Such analyses emphasized molecular features that are critical for influencing distinct classes of PPIs, thereby informing rational scaffold optimization. Despite these encouraging observations in salt-response signaling and transcriptional control modules, caution emerged from parallel investigations involving the Cryptococcus neoformans cytochrome P450 multiprotein complex associated with hydroxylase activity and azole resistance. Density-based statistical evaluation of predicted binding affinities across the screened compound set, together with temporal phenotypic assays, as displayed in Fig. 8, revealed that coumarin binding to the azole-resistant peroxygenase did not elicit measurable functional effects or adaptive responses.107 This discrepancy underscores the limitations of relying solely on predicted interaction strength when assessing functional PPI modulation.

Collectively, these findings suggest that while coumarins possess the capacity to modulate PPIs at multiple regulatory levels, their effectiveness is highly context dependent. Beyond implications for pharmacological design, this property also holds relevance for synthetic biology applications, where selective and tunable interaction control is essential. From the analyzed datasets, key design considerations emerge, particularly with respect to safety, specificity, and practical usability, which should guide future efforts in coumarin-based interaction modulators.

Current challenges and future perspectives in PPI drug discovery

Despite substantial advances in computational biology and structure-based drug discovery, the development of small-molecule modulators targeting PPIs remains considerably more challenging than conventional enzyme inhibition. A major obstacle is the structural nature of many protein interfaces, which are typically large, relatively flat, and lack the deep ligand-binding pockets that facilitate high-affinity binding by traditional drug-like molecules. Instead, productive targeting often depends on identifying localized energetic “hot spots” or transient allosteric pockets capable of accommodating small molecules.

An additional challenge arises from the dynamic conformational behavior of protein complexes. Protein interfaces continuously fluctuate between multiple structural states, meaning that static crystallographic structures frequently fail to capture biologically relevant conformations. Consequently, molecular docking alone may overlook transient binding sites, whereas molecular dynamics simulations and enhanced sampling techniques have become increasingly important for identifying cryptic pockets and characterizing ligand-induced conformational changes. Achieving adequate target selectivity also remains difficult because many proteins belong to highly conserved structural families or participate in multiple interaction networks. A compound capable of disrupting one PPI may unintentionally perturb unrelated signaling pathways, increasing the likelihood of off-target pharmacological effects. Likewise, obtaining sufficiently high binding affinity presents an additional challenge because many small molecules interact with only a limited fraction of the extensive protein interface, often requiring optimization through fragment growing, scaffold modification, or allosteric modulation.108

Pharmacokinetic properties represent another critical consideration during lead optimization. Although numerous coumarin derivatives possess favorable drug-like characteristics, structural modifications introduced to improve PPI binding may adversely affect aqueous solubility, membrane permeability, metabolic stability, plasma protein binding, or oral bioavailability. Consequently, optimization of potency should proceed in parallel with ADMET profiling to maximize translational potential. Finally, computational modeling remains constrained by the limited availability of experimentally resolved structures for many clinically relevant protein complexes. Although advances in cryo-electron microscopy, AlphaFold-based structural prediction, and integrative structural biology have substantially expanded structural resources, predicted models still require careful validation before being incorporated into virtual screening workflows. Continued integration of experimental structural biology, molecular dynamics simulations, artificial intelligence, and quantitative biophysical validation is therefore expected to improve the accuracy of PPI-targeted drug discovery and facilitate the development of coumarin-derived modulators with enhanced therapeutic potential.109

Emerging structural and computational technologies may improve the discovery of coumarin-based PPI modulators by addressing current limitations in protein-complex representation and ligand prioritization. AlphaFold-Multimer and related structure-prediction approaches can expand access to models of protein assemblies that lack experimentally resolved structures, whereas advances in cryo-EM can provide experimentally grounded conformational states of dynamic macromolecular complexes. Integration of these structural resources with physics-based simulations, interactomic datasets, and interpretable ML may improve identification of interface or allosteric sites and prioritize coumarin derivatives for experimental validation. Their value, however, will depend on prospective validation rather than computational performance alone.110

Reproducibility, data availability, and computational workflow standardization

The increasing complexity of computational drug-discovery pipelines underscores the importance of reproducibility, transparent reporting, and standardized analytical workflows. Reproducibility depends not only on the computational methodology itself but also on comprehensive documentation of software versions, force fields, docking parameters, molecular dynamics protocols, random seeds, machine-learning architectures, hyperparameters, and validation procedures. Public deposition of protein structures, ligand libraries, molecular trajectories, benchmark datasets, and analysis scripts in accessible repositories further enables independent verification and facilitates reuse by the research community. Adherence to the FAIR data principles, together with standardized reporting guidelines and workflow management platforms, can substantially improve consistency across computational studies. Benchmarking against publicly available datasets, independent external validation, and cross-platform reproducibility should become routine practices before computational predictions are translated into biological or clinical investigations. For coumarin-based PPI modulators, implementation of these best practices will improve methodological transparency, reduce analytical variability, and accelerate the identification of robust, reproducible therapeutic candidates suitable for experimental validation.

Conclusion

Current evidence supports coumarins as chemically versatile scaffolds with the potential to influence protein interaction networks, but the strength of evidence varies markedly across proposed targets and mechanisms. A substantial proportion of the available literature demonstrates conventional protein–ligand binding, enzyme inhibition, or downstream pathway modulation rather than direct alteration of a defined PPI. Computational docking, molecular dynamics, free-energy calculations, network analysis, and ML therefore provide valuable tools for hypothesis generation and candidate prioritization, but none independently establishes PPI modulation.

The most convincing evidence requires convergence between structural prediction and orthogonal experimental approaches capable of demonstrating altered association, stability, or function of the relevant protein complex. At present, experimentally validated coumarin-mediated PPIs remain comparatively limited, and in vivo and clinical evidence is particularly sparse. Translation is further constrained by protein-interface dynamics, incomplete interactome information, computational model dependence, pharmacokinetic behavior, target selectivity, and the need to demonstrate biologically relevant effects at achievable concentrations.

Future studies should therefore prioritize well-defined protein complexes, explicit identification of interface or allosteric binding sites, independent biochemical or biophysical confirmation, mechanistically informative cellular models, and subsequent validation in appropriate in vivo systems. Integration of experimentally grounded structural biology with reproducible computational workflows and carefully validated ML may progressively convert coumarin-mediated PPI modulation from a predominantly predictive concept into a testable and potentially therapeutically useful strategy.
