Associations of uric acid and lipids with depression, dimensions of depression and anxiety symptoms
Authors: Kyriakos Kintzoglanakis, Pavlos Kolias, Leonidas Pavlou-Skantzis, Panagiota Ntamka, Anastasios Malles, Ioannis Georgantzinos, Anastasia Vasiliki Tzina, Tatiana Themeli, Dimitrios S Karagiannakis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDiscovering biological correlates of depression (and symptom dimensions) and anxiety is a topic of ongoing research. We aimed to examine the associations of uric acid (UA) and lipids with depression (sum and emotional, neurovegetative, cognitive dimension) and anxiety symptoms.MethodsParticipants were screened for depression and anxiety symptoms using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) questionnaires, respectively, in primary care in Greece during January-June 2022. Serum UA and lipids were recorded and socio-demographic, behavioral, and personal history covariates were also collected. Poisson regression analyses were performed to determine associations between recorded parameters and the PHQ-9 (sum-score and emotional, neurovegetative, cognitive dimension) and GAD-7 scores.ResultsA total of 181 individuals answered the questionnaires. The mean age was 61.8 ± 10.8 years, and 68% were female. The prevalence of clinically significant depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10) and anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 10) symptoms was 20.4%, respectively. PHQ-9 score was negatively associated with UA, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and income, and positively with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and physical activity. The neurovegetative dimension of the PHQ-9 score was negatively associated with UA and HDL-C, while the cognitive was positively associated with LDL-C. GAD-7 score was negatively associated with HDL-C and income, and positively with LDL-C and physical activity, while it was higher in females and smokers.ConclusionsBoth UA and lipids were associated with depression and anxiety symptoms. The neurovegetative and cognitive symptoms of depression exhibited different biological correlates with low UA and HDL-C, and high LDL-C, respectively.Keywords: Depression, Anxiety, Biomarkers, Uric acid, High-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Neurovegetative symptoms, Lipids, Greece

Introduction

In the 2022 world mental health report, the World Health Organization acknowledges mental health as an integral part of global health and well-being and a basic human right [1]. It is estimated that 13% of the general population is living with mental disorders, with depressive and anxiety disorders being the most common, accounting for 28.9% and 31% of the total, respectively [1]. Depression is considered to be a leading cause of avoidable suffering, affecting individuals and their families and causing social dysfunction, productivity loss, and premature mortality due to suicide and physical illness [2]. Anxiety disorders (panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), social anxiety disorder, etc.) also contribute to considerable impairment, health-care utilization and economic burden, due to their high prevalence [3]. According to the latest 2021 Global Burden of Disease study, depressive and anxiety disorders are among the leading causes of disability the indices of which have substantially worsened between 2010 and 2021, due to the effect of the COVID pandemic on mental health [4]. Estimates suggest that depressive and anxiety disorders increased by more than 25% during the first year of the COVID pandemic [1].

Depressive and anxiety disorders are defined according to the current diagnostic systems accounting a number of key symptoms, but these symptoms are more or less pathognomonic and are common across psychiatric and physical illnesses [5]. For instance, depression and anxiety exhibit significant clinical overlap and their symptoms are frequently inseparable [6]. Moreover, they share much of the same treatment with antidepressants, the size effect of which is considered moderate and is further compromised by the co-existence of anxiety in depression [7].Variability in treatment responses can often be traced back in the heterogeneity of the biological underpinning of psychiatric symptoms [8]. It has been previously suggested that accounting symptoms within a particular diagnosis may not align with the underlying pathophysiologic mechanism and predictive of the therapeutic response [9]. To overcome the overlaps across diagnostic boundaries and heterogeneity within the same diagnosis, research is increasingly focused on a biomarker-guided approach to the underlying pathophysiology of psychiatric symptoms and disorders aiming to optimize diagnosis and treatment [8, 9].

Many biomarkers i.e., genetic, epigenetic, neuroimaging, cerebrospinal and serum, have been shown to represent different pathophysiologic mechanisms for vulnerability for depression [10], while evidence for anxiety is scarce [11]. For instance, oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory signaling have been proposed as potential contributors to the functional and structural disturbances of the brain observed in depression [12]. In line with this hypothesis, meta-analyses have depicted that anti-oxidant biomarkers, as for example serum uric acid (UA) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) are decreased, while oxidative stress biomarkers are increased in patients with depression compared to controls [13, 14]. Because of 20% of total oxygen consumption, higher lipid content and weaker anti-oxidant defense, the brain is more vulnerable to oxidative stress [12] so that oxidative imbalance and its biomarkers may contribute to the pathophysiology and heterogeneity of depression [13, 14].

In a broader context, immuno-metabolic dysregulations such as oxidative stress, low-grade inflammation and alterations in energy metabolism, as for example indicated by insulin resistance and low HDL-C, may play a role in the pathophysiology not only of cardiovascular disease, but also of depression [15]. These immuno-metabolic biologic dysregulations have been shown to correlate more with “atypical” behavioral symptoms of depression (hypersomnia, hyperphagia/weight gain, fatigue and leaden paralysis), related to the balance between energy intake and expenditure [15]. More specifically, recent evidence indicates that inflammation is associated with the physical or neurovegetative symptoms of depression such as fatigue, changes in appetite and sleep problems and not the emotional or cognitive symptoms [16, 17]. Furthermore, inflammation may moderate the effect of antidepressants, while anti-inflammatory medicines may more benefit patients with a cluster of “atypical” symptoms related to inflammation [15, 16]. Thus, within the highly heterogeneous phenotype of depression, analyzing individual symptoms and their biological correlates -instead of adding symptoms to a sum-score- would facilitate progress on identifying biomarkers and more efficient antidepressants [18, 19].

Despite extensive research for the discovery of biomarkers in Psychiatry, no single diagnosis can be validated by a laboratory or imaging biomarker until the current diagnostic criteria [18]. A reason for this failing, for example in depression, could lay in the different biological correlates of heterogeneous symptoms [18]. In light of the above, the present study aimed to examine cross-sectional associations of serum UA and lipids with depression (as a sum-score and as emotional, neurovegetative, cognitive dimension) and anxiety symptoms, in individuals attending primary care (PC) in Greece in Greece, after adjusting for potential confounders.

Methods

Study setting, participants and data collection

This cross-sectional study took place in four (one urban, three rural, three public, one private) primary care settings belonging to the same geographical department of central Greece. Participants were recruited between 1/1/2022 and 30/6/2022, when the COVID pandemic was stabilized, the majority of the population was already fully vaccinated, and any restriction measures were definitely raised.

A priori power analysis was conducted based on a previously reported correlation of r = − 0.179 between uric acid levels and depression scores [20]. Assuming a one-sided hypothesis (i.e., higher uric acid levels associated with fewer depressive symptoms), a sample size of 191 participants would be required to detect this effect at α = 0.05 with 80% power [21]. We initially recruited 220 participants to account for potential data loss. After excluding cases with missing data, the final sample consisted of 181 valid responses, which yielded an estimated power of 78.1%, slightly below the target but still adequate for detecting the hypothesized effect.

Healthy, ambulatory individuals presenting for interpretation of laboratory results within an appointed short consultation, were consecutively invited in the study. Inclusion criteria were: Greek decent, age range 18–80 years old, willingness to participate and ability to fill questionnaires. Exclusion criteria were factors that could potentially influence general or mental health [7], and included: presentation for an acute illness, belonging to an ethnic minority, abnormal laboratory results on hematocrit (< 40% in males and < 36% in females), thyroid function and vitamin B12, acute grieving period, personal history of dementia, psychosis, bipolar disorder and cancer with poor overall health (ESOG performance status 3–4).

Upon written informed consent, individuals eligible and willing to participate were delivered the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) instruments for the assessment of depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively. We followed the classification of depression symptoms into three dimensions: “psychological or emotional” symptoms (anhedonia, depressed mood, feelings of worthlessness or guilt and suicidal thoughts), “cognitive” symptoms (inability to concentrate) and “neurovegetative” symptoms (sleep problems, fatigue, change in appetite, psycho-motor retardation/agitation) [16, 17]. Accordingly, the results on PHQ-9 instrument were recorded as a sum-score and as scores on emotional dimension (answers in questions 1, 2, 6, 9), cognitive dimension (answer in question 7) and neurovegetative dimension (answers in questions 3, 4, 5, 8).

Laboratory results were recorded on serum UA, total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG). Data were also collected on covariables such as socio-demographic (age, gender, income, marital status, educational level and employment), behavioral (smoking and physical activity) and personal history characteristics (self-reported prior COVID disease, current use of antidepressant and number of chronic co-occurring chronic conditions in a given individual (multimorbidity)). These characteristics correspond to the covariates that should be taken into consideration when measuring inflammation in blood [16].

Evaluation instruments

Patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

PHQ-9 is a widely utilized self-administered diagnostic tool designed to assess the severity of depression. Consisting of nine questions, each corresponding to the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder outlined in the DSM-IV, the PHQ-9 allows for both screening and monitoring of the condition. Each item is scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), yielding a total score between 0 and 27, which categorizes depression severity from minimal to severe. The PHQ-9 has demonstrated robust psychometric properties, including high sensitivity and specificity, making it a reliable instrument in both clinical and research settings [22]. Additionally, its brevity and ease of administration contribute to its widespread adoption in primary care and psychiatric practice. [23] The Greek version of PHQ-9 is freely available [24], and the translation and cut off of 10 has been validated for assessing MDD in Greek rheumatologic patients [25].

Generalized anxiety disorder-7 (GAD-7)

GAD-7 scale is a concise, self-administered screening tool designed to evaluate the severity of generalized anxiety disorder. Comprising seven items that reflect the core diagnostic criteria for generalized anxiety disorder as outlined in the DSM-IV, the GAD-7 employs a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 21. Scores are classified into categories of mild, moderate, and severe anxiety. The GAD-7 has been validated extensively, demonstrating high reliability and validity in both clinical and general populations [26]. Its efficiency and simplicity facilitate widespread use in primary care and mental health settings, making it a valuable tool for initial screening and monitoring of treatment progress in patients with anxiety disorders [27]. A GAD-7 total score of 10 or above indicates a likely anxiety disorder [26]. The freely available Greek version of GAD-7 was used [28].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for the continuous predictive factors and depression and anxiety symptoms were presented with means, medians, standard deviations, minimum and maximum values, and for categorical predictors the absolute and relative frequencies were used. The instrument’s internal consistency was examined with the Cronbach’s alpha (a) and McDonald’s omega (ω) coefficients were calculated. In order to examine the association between UA, HDL-C, LDL-C and TG and the two outcomes (depression and anxiety levels), the zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP) regression model was used with an initial set of candidate covariates including demographic characteristics (gender, age, educational level, marital status, employment and income), lifestyle factors (physical activity and smoking), clinical characteristics (multimorbidity, prior COVID disease and antidepressant use. These variables were selected a priori based on prior evidence that they may confound or modify the relationship between lipid metabolism and mood symptoms—for example, smoking, physical activity, and certain medications (e.g., antidepressants) are known to affect both depressive symptoms and lipids [7].

The choice of the ZIP model was deemed appropriate as the dataset contained an increased number of zeros (12.8%), which correspond to patients without any depression or anxiety symptoms. Furthermore, the comparison of model fit metrics showed that the zero-inflated Poisson model yielded a lower AIC and BIC than the zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) alternative (ZIP AIC = 1005.20, ZINB AIC = 1007.13; ZIP BIC = 1103.40, ZINB BIC = 1104.24), suggesting that the added flexibility of the latter did not provide sufficient improvement in fit to justify the extra parameter. The ZIP model incorporates two components: the first component represents the underlying process that creates zeros, thus no observed symptoms, while the second component models the probability of the positive outcomes through a Poisson distribution. For the ZIP models, backward stepwise variable selection was performed in order to extract an optimal subset of predictors [29]. The threshold for inclusion was set to a = 0.1, therefore p-values lower than the threshold indicate that the variable would be included in the final model in order to adjust the estimated coefficients. The variable TC was excluded from the final model due to perfect multicollinearity, as it is an exact linear combination of LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides (TG), and thus redundant in the presence of these variables. The data analysis was performed in R programming language (version 4.3.3) through the library PSCL [30].

Ethical considerations

The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the 5th Regional Health Authority of Thessaly and Sterea, Central Greece. All individuals signed a written informed consent before their participation in the study.

The internal consistency of the two scales was assessed by estimating the Cronbach’s alpha and the McDonald’s omega coefficients. Both scales attained high internal consistency: PHQ-9 (Cronbach’s α = 0.81, McDonald’s ω = 0.82) and GAD-7 (Cronbach’s α = 0.88, McDonald’s ω = 0.88).

Discussion

In the present study, HDL-C was negatively associated with depression and anxiety symptoms, a well-defined relationship across the literature. For example, a meta-analysis showed higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicide attempts and violent behaviors in patients with low HDL-C [31]. This negative association was also evident in a meta-analysis of patients experiencing a first-episode of major depressive disorder (MDD) [32], but also in patients with long-standing depression symptoms [33]. An older prospective study showed a causal association between low HDL-C and a 5-year risk of depression [34], while recently, a large prospective study showed an association of low HDL-C with the risk of depression, anxiety and stress-related disorders during 6.7 years follow-up [35]. As oxidative stress and inflammation work interdependently and complementary in the brain disease proceeding [12], the anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory and immuno-modulatory properties of HDL-C could be neuroprotective [36]. Some of the pleiotropic effects of HDL-C implicated in depression and anxiety, could be the following: besides the efflux and reverse transport of excess cholesterol, HDL-C suppresses nuclear factor κB which is implicated in the expression of inflammatory cytokines, contains the anti-atherogenic apolipoprotein A-I and co-transports in plasma with the potent anti-oxidant enzyme paraoxonase 1 [36, 37]. Interestingly, inflammatory cytokines are increased in depression [15, 16] and GAD [11], apolipoprotein A-I is decreased in depression [38], whereas paraoxonase 1 is decreased in depression, GAD and other psychiatric diseases [39].

Similarly, our finding that UA was negatively associated with depression symptoms aligns with results from extensive cross-sectional studies [40–42] and meta-analyses [13, 14, 43]. Specifically, in a Scandinavian cohort higher levels of UA were associated with a lower risk of receiving a diagnosis of depression or an antidepressant [40]. A United States cohort showed a significant negative nonlinear association between UA and depressive symptoms [41]. In a Chinese cohort, patients with depression had lower serum UA than healthy controls, while higher serum UA differentiated an episode of bipolar depression from MDD and predicted shorter hospitalization, so that serum UA can be used as a depression biomarker [42]. Finally, a meta-analysis showed that patients with MDD had lower levels of UA that were increased after antidepressant treatment, so that UA may represent a state biomarker of MDD [43]. Although inside the cells UA acts as a pro-oxidant contributing to chronic diseases, in the extracellular space UA is a potent anti-oxidant [41]. Given the negative role of oxidative stress in depression, the anti-oxidant properties of UA can be protective, although other favorable actions of UA in inflammation, dopamine and glutamate’s neurotransmission, endothelial function and cerebral blood flow can be implicated [41].

Considering the importance of cholesterol for membrane structure and function and the abundance of the central nervous system in cholesterol (25%), the implication of LDL-C in brain function is crucial but literature results for depression are inconclusive [44]. A previous meta-analysis showed a negative cross-sectional association between LDL-C and depressive symptoms [44], while a recent meta-analysis observed no association between LDL-C and MDD diagnosed by a psychiatric interview but only a non-significant positive trend in the ≥ 40 years old group [45]. Interestingly, a recent cross-sectional study in more than 40,000 adults in PC identified a U-shaped relationship between LDL-C and depressive symptoms [46], while a very recent prospective study with more than 200,000 participants exhibited no association between LDL-C and a future risk of depression, anxiety and stress-related disorders [35]. Similarly, in a recent meta-analysis of metabolites in depression in more than 15,000 participants, no association between LDL-C and depression was observed [38]. Given the ever-decreasing values of LDL-C in cardiovascular medicine, it would be important to elucidate a clinically meaningful trend for LDL-C or threshold for low LDL-C within a psychiatric context [44]. More prospective work must be done on this topic [44] so the positive association of LDL-C with both depression and anxiety symptoms, and cognitive symptoms of depression, found in the present study, should not drive conclusions and could contribute to ongoing research.

Among the socio-demographic and behavioral covariates, income emerged as a strong protective factor against depression and anxiety, while females and smokers were found particularly affected by anxiety. These findings are in line with the well-documented negative role of income inequality in depression [2], but also the vulnerability of females in anxiety [3] and the bi-directional relationship between smoking and anxiety [47]. On the other hand, the finding that physical activity was positively associated with both depressive and anxiety symptoms, contradicts with the literature [48] and needs further validation. This perhaps is due to the cross-sectional design of the study and the relatively small number of participants. Furthermore, it is possible that a measurement error also exists because under the variable “physical activity” we measured “walking more than 30 min three times per week” and not intense exercise which is inversely related with incident depression [48]. So possibly, also reverse causality bias is present. The number of the participants did not allow for sensitivity analysis of this finding.

The strength of the present study could be the clustering of depression symptoms into emotional, neurovegetative and cognitive dimensions in order to identify symptom-specific associations with serum UA and lipids. Interestingly, the neurovegetative dimension of depression was associated with lower serum UA and HDL-C, while the cognitive was associated with higher LDL-C. This finding might imply that the heterogeneous symptoms of depression have a distinct underlying pathophysiology and, given the negative role of UA and HDL-C in inflammation, the neurovegetative dimension is inflammation-specific [16, 17]. This is in line with many recent studies examining symptom clusters of depression. For example, a study in 539 young adults showed that inflammation was associated with neurovegetative features of depression marked by eating, appetite and tiredness, rather than the overall score or severity of depression. [49] Another study in 1621 individuals from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA) found that a proteomic cluster associated with inflammation and metabolic dysregulation was present in MDD and was mainly associated with atypical/energy-related symptoms. [50] Similarly, a study from the NESDA cohort with more than 7,000 observations examined four dimensional profilers of depression: “atypical/energy-related symptom”, “melancholic symptom”, “anxious distress symptom” and “childhood trauma severity”. In this study the “melancholic symptom” profile was associated with relatively better metabolic health, while the “atypical/energy-related symptom” profile was associated with poorer immuno-inflammatory and metabolic health cross-sectionaly and 6-years longitudinally [51]. Current evidence in depression supports a robust association of physical/neurovegetative symptoms (sleep problems, changes in appetite, fatigue) with inflammation, while the association of emotional and cognitive symptoms with inflammation may not be independent of physical/neurovegetative symptoms [16, 17]. From a pathophysiology perspective, possibly the physiological process might be particularly vulnerable to inflammation and is primarily affected, while the emotional and cognitive processes might subsequently be affected via the neurofunctional impairments induced by inflammation [17].

Providing that low UA and HDL-C could serve as indicators of oxidative stress, inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, the association of low UA and HDL-C with neurovegetative symptoms could stratify patients with depression and more neurovegetative symptoms in a subgroup with underlying immuno-metabolic dysregulation. This might correspond to the concept of immuno-metabolic depression (IMD) which accounts for 30% of all cases and characterize a subgroup of patients with more atypical, energy-related symptoms (hypersomnia, increased appetite/weight, leaden paralysis, fatigue and anhedonia) and an underlying chronic overactivation of the immune system and metabolic dysregulation [52]. Identifying such a homogenous biotype of depression may correspond to the aims of precision psychiatry that, against “one-size-fits-all” practice, examines subgroups of patients that would more benefit from etiology-based treatment approaches facilitating personalized treatment [52].Taking metabolic dysregulation as an example, many immuno-metabolic modulatory interventions have been tested including intense exercise, Mediterranean diet, bupropion add-on, anti-inflammatory (e.g., infliximab, minocycline), lipid-lowering (e.g., statins, polyunsaturated fatty acids) and anti-diabetic (e.g., metformin, pioglitazone, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs)) medications [52, 53]. Despite promising results, these interventions are far from being introduced in clinical practice due to, for example, small-scale trials without pre-treatment stratification of patients and use of depression rating scales that cannot capture individual symptoms [18, 52, 53]. Thus, further randomized controlled trials are needed in more homogenous subgroups of patients with a similar neurobiological impairment and distinction between symptoms [18, 52, 53]. For GLP-1RAs in particular, they appear as an ideal treatment of the IMD subgroup for future large scale trials [52], since they were very recently shown to improve metabolic profile in patients with mental illness on psychotropics and mental health in patients with and without mental disorders in obesity and/or type 2 diabetes [54].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it originates from a single Greek region and involves a relatively small number of participants, which may affect the generalization of the findings. This study relied on self-report questionnaires, which can lead to overestimation or underestimation of the prevalence of depression and anxiety, as well as introduce recall bias. Self-report scales are perhaps inferior to clinician-administered and cannot substitute a structured clinical interview or objective measures (e.g., actigraphy for psycho-motor agitation/retardation). Since the research was conducted in PC units there is a potential prevalence-incidence bias; individuals with more severe depression or anxiety were likely to seek help from mental health rather than PC specialists, and were not included in this study. We wish the present study has avoided the non response bias since the response rate was > 80%. Finally, because this study has a cross-sectional design, it cannot establish a causal inference between UA or lipid levels and symptoms of depression or anxiety. Further longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted in order to explore whether modulating UA and lipid levels could alter symptom severity or UA and lipids are state biomarkers (simple bystanders).

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of individuals attending PC in Greece, low UA was associated with depression symptoms, while low HDL-C and high LDL-C were associated with both depression and anxiety symptoms. These associations might facilitate ongoing research for biomarkers in depression and anxiety. The symptoms of depression showed different biological associations: the neurovegetative with low UA and HDL-C and the cognitive with high LDL-C. These findings imply that the neurovegetative symptoms of depression might exhibit a biological correlate with oxidative stress, inflammation and metabolic dysregulation.
