Perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) adsorption on Fe-rich mineral assemblages and soils: experiments and surface complexation modeling
Authors: Devin Farrell, Zoi Dokou, Nefeli Bompoti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) is an emerging contaminant frequently detected in subsurface environments, raising significant concern due to its environmental persistence, mobility, and potential human health impacts. This study examines PFOS adsorption onto a range of solid substrates, including pure minerals, mineral assemblages, and natural soils. Specifically, the adsorption behavior of 2-line ferrihydrite, ferrihydrite-coated sand, and soil collected from a PFOS-impacted site in Killingworth, Connecticut was investigated to evaluate their capacity to retain PFOS under varying geochemical conditions. By integrating batch adsorption experiments with surface complexation modeling (SCM) and applying the component additivity approach, this study elucidates the reactive transport mechanisms governing PFOS behavior under a range of geochemical conditions. Our findings demonstrate that PFOS adsorption occurs significantly on both ferrihydrite and quartz surfaces, with the ferrihydrite-coated sand and soil exhibiting retention behavior attributable to contributions from both mineral phases. At lower pH values, sorption is predominantly governed by outer-sphere complexation driven by the surface charge characteristics of ferrihydrite. Specifically, under acidic conditions (pH < 5.5 for ferrihydrite-coated sand and pH < 6.0 for soil), PFOS retention is primarily facilitated through an outer-sphere hydrogen-bonded complex at ferrihydrite’s surface, while a secondary outer-sphere complex involving Na+ co-adsorption contributes to a lesser extent. At elevated pH levels, however, electrostatic interactions become less favorable, and non-electrostatic hydrophobic interactions with quartz surfaces become increasingly dominant, highlighting the transition in sorption mechanisms from charge-driven to hydrophobic partitioning under neutral to alkaline conditions. A comparison with traditional partitioning coefficients (Kd) revealed that their variability closely corresponds with changes in dominant surface complexes across different pH conditions. Given the critical role of solid-phase partitioning in governing PFAS transport in the subsurface, enhanced predictive capabilities are essential for advancing site-specific risk assessments and informing management strategies aimed at protecting both public and private water resources.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12932-025-00105-2.

Introduction

The persistence of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) has gathered significant scientific attention over the past few decades due to their widespread contamination of environmental media, including soils and water resources [1–6]. Due to their unique chemical structure, perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) were used in aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) to extinguish highly flammable liquids [7, 8]. Firefighting training exercises have often led to significant deposits of PFOS and PFOA in surrounding areas, contaminating nearby drinking water supplies, often at concentrations many orders of magnitude higher than background levels [9]. For individuals residing near contaminated sites, drinking water can serve as a primary source of PFAS exposure, with prolonged exposure associated with a range of adverse health effects [10]. Remediation efforts have proven to be both challenging and costly due to the unique chemical structure of PFAS, which feature carbon-fluorine bonds that render these substances highly resistant to biodegradation [6, 11–14]. Recently, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) designated PFOA and PFOS as hazardous waste substances under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) [15] and established legally enforceable maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for PFOS and PFOA in drinking water, setting the threshold at 4 parts per trillion (ppt) [16].

Mitigating the risks posed by PFAS contamination in soil and groundwater requires accurate prediction of PFAS fate and transport in the subsurface. Transport modeling in both laboratory setups and PFAS-impacted sites has shown promising results in predicting PFAS concentrations in freshwater [3, 17, 18]. When PFAS are released to the groundwater through leaching from soil, the interactions with solid materials in the aquifer are important components in determining the PFAS transport in the subsurface. Partitioning to the solid phase is one of the major mechanisms for PFAS retention in the saturated zone, [2, 2119−] while interfacial adsorption at the air-water and non-aqueous phase liquid (NAPL)-water interface are significant retention processes in the unsaturated zone [22, 23]. Additionally, interfacial tension at the organic immiscible liquid-water and air-organic immiscible liquid interfaces also plays a critical role in PFAS retention [24, 25]. Interactions with organic matrices such as natural organic matter and microbial communities also represent a significant factor influencing the uptake of PFAS and can substantially affect the transport dynamics of these contaminants [26–28].

For solid-PFAS interactions, soil composition and solution chemistry have been shown to strongly affect the adsorption of PFAS, particularly where electrostatic interactions are the major retention mechanisms [2, 29]. Most PFAS compounds, and particularly PFOS and PFOA, are fully deprotonated and negatively charged in most environmental conditions [20, 30]. Electrostatic attractions with anionic PFAS can contribute to PFAS retention at low pH values where adsorbents such as minerals, activated carbon, and polymer adsorbents become pronated and positively charged [31]. The presence of ions in the solution including Na+, Ca2+, and K+ can influence adsorption potential by reducing the surface charge [31]. However, these ions can reduce the electrostatic repulsion between PFAS molecules, potentially increasing adsorption [29, 31]. Ferrihydrite, as a poorly crystalline iron oxy(hydr)oxide commonly found in subsurface soils and river sediments, is one of the most reactive minerals due to its unique surface characteristics [32]. Campos-Pereira et al. [33] showed that PFAS sorption on ferrihydrite and other iron oxides may be substantial in environments with pH less than pH 5. Similar results have been found for other iron oxides, such as goethite, [20, 29, 34] hematite, [19, 35] boehmite, [36, 37] and alumina [38, 39]. Conversely, PFAS adsorption to silica is governed by non-electrostatic interactions [29, 40].

Reactive transport models can simulate and predict PFAS transport at contamination sites by accounting for factors including groundwater fluxes, advective transport, and physicochemical reactions [22, 41–44]. Typically, the solid-water partition coefficient (Kd) is commonly used to quantify the adsorption of PFAS in fate and transport models. However, the heterogeneity of subsurface conditions and the lack of mechanistic models has resulted in a wide range of Kd values; Rovero et al.,45 evaluated the Kd values of eight anionic PFAS reporting variability spanning three to five orders of magnitude. Furthermore, they observed that Kd values obtained in laboratory studies vary by approximately one order of magnitude compared to field studies, highlighting the variability of PFOS adsorption parameters [45].

Surface complexation models (SCMs) use a thermodynamic framework to describe the retention of ions on soil surfaces using various chemical reactions, including solution reactions and cation and anion adsorption on oxides and clays [46, 47]. Depending on the specific formulation, surface complexation models (SCMs) represent surface protonation processes using either the 1-pK or 2-pK approaches. Ionic charge at the mineral-water interface can be simulated by through either a single-site framework or more advanced models such as the Charge Distribution Multisite Complexation (CD-MUSIC) [48]. Additionally, SCMs incorporate various electrostatic models to account for surface potential and ion distribution, such as the Basic Stern model (BS) and the Triple Layer Model (TLM). Cogorno and Rolle (2024b) used a CD-MUSIC SCM for the adsorption of PFOS to goethite-coated sand. Their findings indicated that PFOS adsorption was governed predominantly by outer-sphere complexation with goethite particles. However, prior studies have demonstrated that interactions between PFOS and quartz surfaces can also be substantial [29]. Higgins and Luthy also employed the non-ideal competitive adsorption Donnan (NICA-Donnan) model to simulate hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions of PFOS with sediments [50]. These observations underscore the need for further refinement of mechanistic models that comprehensively represent PFOS-solid interactions in complex subsurface environments.

In this study, we investigated PFOS behavior across different matrixes, ranging from pure minerals to mineral assemblages and soils, and developed an additive SCM to simulate and predict PFAS adsorption in natural environments. Various materials including 2-line ferrihydrite, pure silica, iron-coated sand, and soil from a local PFOS-impacted site in Killingworth, CT were used to investigate PFOS adsorption. Additional data from the literature were included to calibrate and validate the SCM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first modeling approach that simultaneously accounts for the PFOS interactions of both iron-oxide and silica particles.

Materials and methods

Material synthesis and characterization

2-line ferrihydrite was synthesized based on Schwertmann and Cornell [51] method. Briefly, a solution of 0.2 M Fe(NO3)∙9 H2O was prepared and ferrihydrite was precipitated by adding approximately 150 mL of 1 M KOH until the pH was raised to 7.5. The ferrihydrite suspension was centrifuged in propylene centrifuge tubes at 2800 rpm and washed with DI water. Residual dissolved ions were removed by placing the remaining solid in dialysis tubing until conductivity was below 20 µS/cm. The formation of ferrihydrite particles using the Schwertmann and Cornell method has been verified in previous studies [52, 53]. The Brunauer, Emmett, and Teller (BET) surface area was measured at 348.194 m2/g and the average particle size of ferrihydrite particles was estimated at 3.4 nm based on a previous study [52].

Iron-coated sand was prepared following a modified procedure based on the method described by Mähler and Persson, [54] using the freshly synthesized ferrihydrite to coat quartz (Ottawa sand) at a ratio of 30 g sand to 100 mL ferrihydrite. The mixture was prepared in 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes, shaken for approximately 48 h, centrifuged, and subsequently air-dried for 48 h. This procedure was repeated twice. The iron-coated sand was sieved using a No. 200 standard sieve (75 μm) and rinsed with DI water to remove any loose ferrihydrite particles. The iron concentration in the coated sand was quantified at 4,600 mg/kg through analysis conducted using inductively couple plasma - mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS) according to the U.S. EPA Method 6020B. The BET surface area was measured to be 23.3 m2/g for quartz and 21.1 m2/g for the iron-coated sand. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed on iron-coated sand samples to analyze elemental composition and iron coating. SEM images and EDS spectra of the iron-coated sand are provided in Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 of the Supporting Information.

Soil sampling and characterization

Soil samples were collected nearby a former firefighter training site located in Killingworth, Connecticut, where contamination of soil and groundwater with PFAS has been attributed to historical use of AFFF. For the experiments described herein, soil was obtained from Irene Sheldon Park, also in Killingworth, CT, at an approximate depth of 2 feet. This area was selected as a background location to ensure that the soil used in experimental work was free from PFAS contamination and a sample was sent to a certified lab (Pace Analytical) to conduct confirmatory PFAS analysis. The concentrations in the Killingworth soil were 0.27 ng/g for PFOA and 0.66 ng/g for PFOS. The background concentrations were determined to be sufficiently low to be considered representative of non-PFOS-impacted soils. Previous studies on anthropogenic background concentrations in the New England region, and particularly in Massachusetts, have reported a median PFOS concentration of 1.01 ng/g. [55] The soil was sieved using a No. 40 standard sieve to remove debris, and hydraulic conductivity was assessed via a falling head permeability test (ASTM International, 2015). Batch adsorption experiments

Batch adsorption experiments were conducted to measure PFOS adsorption onto iron-coated sand, quartz and soil across a range of pH conditions. PFOS adsorption onto ferrihydrite was evaluated at pH 5.5. Several PFOS stock solutions were prepared and a target concentration of 1 ppm was achieved in the batch experiments. Briefly, samples were prepared in 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes with 125 g/L of solid for quartz, coated quartz, and soil, and 10 g/L for ferrihydrite. The ionic strength was controlled using a 0.01 M NaCl and the pH was adjusted with HNO3 and KOH. All samples were shaken for 24 h and then centrifuged and filtered with a 0.22 μm syringe filter. All stock solutions and aliquots of supernatants were analyzed at a certified laboratory (Pace Analytical) using EPA Method 573.1 by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).58 The pH was measured in the supernatants with a Metrohm Aquatrode Plus Pt1000 F/2 mm probe. The experiments were conducted under atmospheric conditions, which may have introduced additional dissolved carbonate species into the solutions, which we acknowledge as a factor of potential influence on the observed interactions. The adsorption of PFOS onto the solid materials was quantified based on mass-balance calculations, determined by subtracting the equilibrium concentration of PFOS in the supernatant from the initial concentration added to the batch.

Surface complexation model description

The SCM was developed utilizing the CD-MUSIC framework coupled with a Basic Stern electrostatic model [48]. The models simulations were performed based on custom-made Mathematica notebooks that have been previously used to simulate the rutile-water interface, [59–61], magnetite, [62] and iron oxides including goethite and ferrihydrite, [63–65] and Al-ferrihydrites [53]. The modeling approach was based on the principle of component additivity, where adsorption to soils and mineral assemblages are represented by their individual additive components [66]. The SCM was first calibrated using single-surface systems, where equilibrium constants for specific surface reactions were optimized to accurately represent adsorption behavior. These calibrated parameters were then applied to develop SCMs for the mineral assemblage and soil matrices, incorporating the validated surface reactions and parameters obtained from the reference materials. Our systems are comprised of ferrihydrite (pure iron oxide); quartz; iron-coated sand; and natural soil. For both the synthetic iron-coated sand and the natural soil, the surface was simulated by two surface components that primarily contributed to PFOS retention: that of ferrihydrite and quartz. In this study, a simplifying assumption was made that the soil’s low to moderate organic carbon content would not significantly contribute to PFAS retention. Given the low to moderate organic matter and elevated iron content, quartz and iron oxides were considered the dominant surface phases governing sorption behavior. We acknowledge that organic matter can significantly influence the uptake of PFOS. Recognizing the importance of this factor, future research will focus on investigating the mechanisms by which different types and concentrations of organic matter affect PFOS adsorption. Multiple iron oxides, such as goethite, hematite, and ferrihydrite, may be present in soil environments. For the purposes of the SCM, ferrihydrite was selected as a representative proxy for simulation.

The surface properties of ferrihydrite and quartz were adopted from previous studies [63, 67, 68]. Specifically, ferrihydrite surface is simulated by a 3-site model with one singly coordinated (SC) hydroxyl group and one triply coordinated (TC) group located on the (1–10) and (1–11) faces, and one TC group on the (001) and (00–1) faces. The quartz surface was simulated by a simpler 1-site model, with one singly coordinated group (SC). For both minerals, the doubly coordinated hydroxyl groups were considered inert and not included in the model. Table 1 shows the surface models for ferrihydrite and quartz.

The charging behavior of ferrihydrite and quartz is described by the surface reactions listed in Table 2. Surface charge development on ferrihydrite was modeled using the Basic Stern electrostatic model, with a capacitance value of 1.15 F/m2, adjusted to account for spherical particle geometry. In contrast, quartz has a significantly lower dielectric constant (ε = 4), which leads to a reduced capacitance. Accordingly, a capacitance value of 0.9 F/m² was used for quartz in this study. Electrolyte binding was treated as outer-sphere complexation, and surface reaction parameters and equilibrium constants were adopted from previous studies [63].

For PFOS interactions with iron-oxides, previous studies by Cogorno and Rolle [34, 49] have shown that two outer-sphere surface complexes are formed between deprotonated PFOS and goethite singly coordinated hydroxyls. The two surface complexes include one hydrogen-bonded complex and a weaker outer sphere complex with Na+ co-adsorption. Under acidic conditions and low ionic strength, the hydrogen-bonded complex between PFOS and goethite predominates, whereas at higher pH and ionic strength, the formation of the weaker outer-sphere PFOS-Na⁺-goethite complex is favored [34]. In addition, Campos-Pereira [33] confirmed the absence of inner-sphere complexes for several sulfur-containing PFAS on the ferrihydrite surface. In this study, we assume that PFOS complexes formed on the ferrihydrite surface are the same as goethite. In previous work, [64] we have shown that the type of surface complexes forming in iron oxides have similarities, for both goethite and ferrihydrite. However, the surface equilibrium parameters are expected to differ to account for variations in denticity, arising from differences in surface area and surface site density between the two minerals [64, 70]. The charge distribution (CD) factors for PFOS interactions were calculated based on the Pauling bond valence principle and the structural configuration of the adsorbed ligand, following the previous finding by Cogorno and Rolle [34]. The surface equilibrium log Ks were optimized utilizing the multi-start optimization algorithm for surface complexation equilibrium parameters (MUSE) that has been developed by the authors in a previous study [52]. Briefly, the algorithm provides an unbiased optimization of surface complexation parameters by combining a local optimization algorithm with a multi-start of a large number of starting points offering global optimization. The MUSE optimization algorithm is integrated into the original SCM model and can optimize multiple parameters using criteria such as the mean squared error (MSE) and Model Selection Criterion (MSC). The MUSE algorithm has been previously used for SCM applications integrating spectroscopic information on the type of surface complexes for iron oxides [52, 53, 64, 65].

For the PFOS interactions with quartz particles, we considered some electrostatic and non-electrostatic components. In the majority of reactive transport applications, the interactions between silica particles and negatively charged ligands are neglected since there are no electrostatic interactions. However, significant retention of PFOS on quartz has been demonstrated with a weak dependence on pH and ionic strength [20, 29]. Under most environmental conditions, quartz silica particles are predominantly negatively charged or neutral, suggesting that interactions with negatively charged ions cannot be attributed solely to electrostatic forces [68]. Based on Tang et al., [29] hydrophobic interactions between the perfluoroalkyl tails and the hydrophobic moieties on the silica surface are the main retention mechanism for PFOS on quartz. At a very low pH (< 3), the retention of PFOS on quartz becomes more significant, which indicates that some electrostatic forces may play a role at lower pH when the silica surface is protonated. Due to hydrophobic and charged moieties, the free energy change of sorption can account for both hydrophobic and Coulombic effects [50]. Higgins and Luthy [50] have used this approach previously to simulate the sorption of anionic surfactants in sediments in presence of organic matter. In this study, we aimed to model PFOS-quartz interactions by incorporating a partial electrostatic component to represent the interactions between PFOS and protonated quartz surfaces. We hypothesize that an outer-sphere complex is formed between the negatively charged sulfonate headgroup of PFOS and positively charged surface sites on quartz under acidic conditions.

To our knowledge, the only datasets that were referring to PFOS adsorption on pure ferrihydrite and silica particles were a pH edge and an isotherm for PFOS-ferrihydrite from the study of Campos-Pereira, [29] and a pH edge for PFOS-silica from Tang et al. Results and discussion

Adsorption experiments

Figure 1 shows a comparison of the adsorption edges across the various solid matrixes. The experiments were conducted at an ionic strength of 0.01 M NaCl with a PFOS concentration of 2 µM. Strong pH dependency was observed in the adsorption of PFOS on the iron-coated sand and soil. For the iron-coated sand, PFOS adsorption reached a maximum of 98% at pH 3.65, followed by a decline to a minimum of 57% at pH 9 (Fig. 1a). Similarly, in the soil matrix, PFOS adsorption peaked at 97% at pH 4.03 and decreased to a minimum of 61% at pH 7.13. Adsorption increased with decreasing pH for both the iron-coated sand and the soil, suggesting that electrostatic interactions governed by the surface charge of ferrihydrite play a significant role in PFOS retention. The pH-dependency observed in experimental observations agree with previous findings for PFOS adsorption on goethite [34] and ferrihydrite [33]. The main difference observed for coated-quartz and soil compared to pure minerals is that there is significant adsorption even at higher pH values. Given that the typical point of zero charge (PZC) for ferrihydrite is approximately 8.0 to 8.5, [46, 63] the surface of the mineral becomes increasingly negatively charged at pH values above this range. As a result, electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged ferrihydrite surface and the anionic PFOS molecules becomes more pronounced, leading to minimal or negligible adsorption in higher pH values. The observed retention of PFOS at higher pH values may be attributed to two potential mechanisms: (a) significant retention by quartz particles through hydrophobic interactions, which can become more prominent at higher pH; or (b) the increasing prevalence of carbonate species in solution at higher pH, which may facilitate the formation of ternary complexes involving PFOS. However, the formation and stability of such PFOS-carbonate-ferrihydrite ternary complexes is currently insufficiently understood, and further research is needed to elucidate these interactions.

For quartz, significant PFOS retention was observed, ranging from 63% to 72% across the pH range of 4 to 9. These results indicate a lack of pH dependency in PFOS adsorption onto quartz, which aligns with findings from previous studies [20, 29]. In the study of Johnson et al., [20] PFOS exhibited a higher affinity for quartz compared to goethite and kaolinite that was demonstrated in adsorption isotherms. This behavior was attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the perfluorinated tail of PFOS and the relatively large and accessible surface area of quartz. The hydroxylated surface of quartz has been shown to exhibit hydrophobic behavior [71, 72] and to engage in hydrophobic interactions with peptides [73] and other organic materials [74]. Similarly, Tang et al. [29] reported PFOS retention of approximately 88–92% on quartz particles, with consistently higher adsorption than on goethite. Their study showed minimal variation in adsorption across a broad pH range (pH 5–10), though an increase in adsorption was observed at pH 3, suggesting a weak pH dependence. This may indicate that electrostatic interactions play a minor role in PFOS adsorption under acidic conditions. Our findings similarly indicate substantial PFOS retention on quartz with minimal pH dependency, further supporting the role of hydrophobic interactions as the dominant adsorption mechanism with a minor electrostatic component.

PFOS adsorption was compared across various solid matrixes under identical conditions at pH 5.5, ionic strength (0.01 M), and initial PFOS concentration (2 µM) (Fig. 1b). Quartz exhibited lower PFOS adsorption, retaining approximately 64% of the initial PFOS concentration. Ferrihydrite adsorbed 81% of PFOS at pH 5.5, a level comparable to that observed for soil (83%) and iron-coated sand (80%). However, the soil and sand experiments were conducted at a substantially higher solid concentration (125 g/L) compared to ferrihydrite (10 g/L). Ferrihydrite’s elevated adsorption capacity is primarily attributed to its high specific surface area and favorable surface charge characteristics under acidic conditions, which enhance its interaction with anionic ligands including PFOS. The maximum adsorption of PFOS (~ 95–97% of an initial concentration of 2 µmol/L) was observed at pH 4 for both the iron-coated sand and the soil sample. Although both materials exhibited comparable SSAs, the soil contained approximately three times more total iron than the iron-coated sand. However, the iron-coated sand demonstrated ~ 2% greater PFOS retention. This observation suggests that the ferrihydrite in the iron-coated sand may be more accessible for adsorption, likely due to its distribution as a surface coating on quartz grains, which increases the exposure of reactive sites compared to the iron phases in the soil matrix. Additionally, the lower PFOS retention in the soil, despite its higher total iron content, may also indicate the presence of other iron oxide phases that are less reactive than ferrihydrite. While only a 2% difference was observed, it should be acknowledged that this value may be due to experimental variability.

We also acknowledge the potential for the formation of micelles, mixed micelles, or self-aggregated PFOS molecules, which may sorb to surfaces in the form of hemimicelles rather than as individual molecules. Previous research has identified the critical micelle concentrations (CMC) for PFOS at 7 mM [75] and 4573 mg/L, [76] which are significantly higher than the concentrations used in the current study. However, according to Johnson et al., [20] suggest that hemi-micelles may form at PFOS concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 1% of the CMC. This indicates that, although the concentrations in our study are below the established CMC values, there may still be the possibility of micellar or sub-micellar structures forming, which could influence PFOS behavior and sorption interactions.

The development of the SCM followed a stepwise approach, beginning with the calibration of models for ferrihydrite and quartz. These parameters were then combined through the component additivity approach to simulate PFOS adsorption on iron-coated sand and soil. The SCM for ferrihydrite was calibrated using experimental PFOS adsorption data reported by Campos-Pereira et al. [33] and the calibrated model was used to build the SCM for the mineral assemblage and soil. Specifically, the pH-edge and adsorption isotherm were used for model calibration. The resulting simulations are presented in Fig. 2. The optimized parameters included the surface complexation equilibrium constants (log Ks) for PFOS surface species including the hydrogen-bonded complex (Fh-OS-HB) and the outer-sphere complex with Na⁺ co-adsorption (Fh-OS-Na). The two simulated surface complexes were plotted as a function of pH and are presented in Fig. 2c and d. For both the pH-edge and adsorption isotherm simulations, the Fh-OS-HB complex was found to be the dominant species under all experimental conditions. This outcome is expected, given that the formation of the Fh-OS-HB complex is primarily driven by hydrogen bonding between the sulfonate group of PFOS and protonated singly coordinated surface hydroxyl groups on ferrihydrite [34]. This validates the strong pH-dependency. At higher ionic strengths (~ 100 mM), the Fh-OS-Na complex becomes more pronounced. Under the ionic strength conditions simulated in this study, co-adsorption of Na⁺ was less prominent, though it still contributed to the overall PFOS adsorption. Prior research has hypothesized that at elevated ionic strengths, Na+ co-adsorption may also involve hydrophobic interactions and potentially resulting in a flatter orientation of PFOS molecules on the mineral surface due to compression of the electrical double layer [34, 79]. While the influence of salinity on PFOS behavior has been examined in several studies, [29, 34, 80] particularly regarding its effects on both PFOS quantification and adsorption, further investigation is needed to elucidate the specific role of Na+ co-adsorption in saline environments.

Modeling simulations of PFOS retention on quartz particles are presented in Fig. 3. Across both datasets, substantial PFOS adsorption is observed with minimal dependence on pH, indicating that retention occurs over a broad pH range. As previously noted, the modeling simulations account for the electrostatic component governing PFOS-quartz surface interactions. Based on this context, we hypothesize that PFOS retention on quartz surfaces can be at least partially explained by a surface reaction mechanism involving the protonation of quartz functional groups and the subsequent formation of an outer-sphere complex between PFOS and the protonated, singly coordinated surface sites. Similar to the ferrihydrite-PFOS interactions, the MUSE algorithm was employed to optimize the surface equilibrium constant for the intrinsic interaction between PFOS and quartz (log Kin Q−PFOS), using the dataset provided by Tang et al., [29] The optimizations converged to a log Kin Q−PFOS of 6.8, with MSE of 8.57 × 10− 17 (Fig. 3a). When combined with the protonation constant for the quartz surface (log KQH+), the resulting total surface complexation constant can be expressed as log K Q−PFOS = log QH+ + log Kin Q−PFOS = 7.5 + 6.8 = 14.3. Given that the initial model underestimated PFOS adsorption at higher pH values, an additional simulation was conducted using a higher intrinsic surface complexation constant log Kin Q−PFOS of 7.5. This adjustment improved the model’s ability to capture the increased adsorption observed at higher pH; however, it resulted in a poorer overall fit to the data, with a higher MSE (1.25 × 10− 15), due to significant overestimation of adsorption at lower pH (Fig. 3a). The results suggest two possible interpretations: (a) hydrophobic interactions may become increasingly dominant at higher pH levels, whereas electrostatic interactions play a more significant role at lower pH; or (b) the adsorption process may be governed predominantly by hydrophobic interactions across the entire pH range. Particularly for organic ligands like PFOS, hydrophobic interactions between the fluorinated alkyl chain and hydrophobic mineral surfaces can become a dominant retention mechanism [29, 30]. In this context, models such as the NICA-Donnan framework, originally developed for binding to natural organic matter and other heterogeneous surfaces, may offer improved representation of these interactions.

The experimental data generated in this study were subsequently used for SCM predictions based on the optimized parameter sets (Fig. 3b). In both simulations, the model tended to overestimate PFOS adsorption at low pH. However, the simulation using log Kin Q−PFOS of 6.8 provided a more accurate representation of adsorption behavior at higher pH. Overall, we observe that the simulation for Tang’s dataset, which corresponds to a higher surface loading compared to the experimental conditions in this study, yields a better fit to the model predictions. This may also suggest that electrostatic interactions become more pronounced at higher surface loadings, due to increased surface charge and suppression of the hydrophobic contributions.

Hydrophobic interactions generally exhibit limited dependence on solution chemistry, including pH and ionic strength. However, Tang et al., [29] reported increased PFOS retention at lower pH values and higher ionic strength, which may be attributed to enhanced electrostatic interactions, including potential co-adsorption of Na⁺ ions that mitigate repulsive surface forces. Quartz surfaces, which have a low point of zero charge (PZC ≈ 2.0 ± 0.3), are typically negatively charged under environmentally relevant pH conditions [81]. As a result, PFOS molecules are subject to electrostatic repulsion both from the quartz surface and from each other. At low pH and elevated ionic strength, this repulsion is reduced due to increased protonation of surface sites and electrostatic shielding, which may further contribute to the enhanced PFOS retention observed [29]. Additional investigation is needed to fully elucidate the mechanistic contributions of hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions in PFOS sorption onto quartz under various environmental conditions.

PFOS retention on both iron-coated sand and soil exhibited a strong dependence on pH, with increased adsorption observed at lower pH values (Figs. 4 and 5). To simulate PFOS retention behavior on these solid matrices, three modeling approaches were employed: (a) an additive surface complexation model incorporating both ferrihydrite and quartz components, calibrated using experimental data from the pure minerals (Fh & Q-OS); (b) a simplified model considering only ferrihydrite, with its concentration estimated based on the total Fe content in the solid matrixes (Fh); and (c) an additive model including both ferrihydrite and quartz, in which the charge distribution of the outer-sphere surface complexation was adjusted specifically for the quartz component (Fh & Q-NE). Surface site densities for FeOH and SiOH surfaces, were adopted from the surface properties of the reference materials. These site densities were incorporated in the model based on mineral composition of the assemblage and soil, including the surface areas and solid concentrations for each sample as input parameters in the model simulations.

In the initial additive model scenario incorporating both ferrihydrite and quartz outer-sphere complexes (Fh & Q-OS), the model underestimated PFOS retention at higher pH values for the iron-coated sand (Fig. 4a), while it tended to overestimate retention in the pH range of 5–7 for both the soil (Fig. 5a) and the coated sand (Fig. 4a). In this simulation, we utilized the previous estimated surface complexation parameters for ferrihydrite, and for the quartz-PFOS interaction, the lower boundary value of the intrinsic stability constant Kin Q−PFOS of 5.7 was used. The MSE for the coated sand fit was 2.96 × 10− 13, and 7.72 × 10− 14 for the soil. As shown in the corresponding surface speciation profiles (Figs. 4b and 5b), the inclusion of the outer-sphere on quartz results in a marked suppression of PFOS-ferrihydrite complex formation and an overestimation of PFOS binding to quartz surface sites. From an electrostatic standpoint, this scenario does not accurately represent PFOS retention under environmentally relevant conditions, as quartz is typically negatively charged across a wide pH range, thereby exhibiting low affinity for the anionic PFOS, whereas ferrihydrite possesses more variable surface charge characteristics that can favor PFOS adsorption, particularly under acidic to neutral conditions. The observed overestimation arises from the model’s calibration using isolated mineral phases, which may not reflect the competitive interactions present in heterogeneous systems. Furthermore, the additive model framework assumes no interaction or synergistic effects between mineral phases. As a result, the model may artificially redistribute PFOS based on differences in surface area, charge density, and binding constants, leading to a disproportionate allocation of PFOS to quartz.

The second modeling scenario included only the ferrihydrite component (Fh), accounting for two outer-sphere complexes. This simulation was designed to evaluate whether ferrihydrite alone, given its high surface reactivity, could adequately simulate PFOS retention in iron-rich matrices. In both coated sand and soil systems, the model effectively captured PFOS retention under acidic conditions (pH < 6). However, at pH values above 6, the model significantly underestimated PFOS adsorption for both materials (Fig. 4a for coated sand; Fig. 5a for soil). The MSE for this simulation was 5.49 × 10− 13 for the coated sand and 2.78 × 10− 13 for soil, indicating a poorer fit compared to the additive modeling approach. The surface complexation speciation results are presented in Fig. 4c for coated sand and Fig. 5c for soil. For the soil, the hydrogen-bonded outer-sphere complex (Fh-OS-HB) dominated across most pH values. In contrast, for the coated sand, the outer-sphere complex with Na+ co-adsorption (Fh-OS-NA) became more prominent within the pH range of 5 to 7. The ferrihydrite modeling simulation reproduced the general trend of decreasing PFOS adsorption with increasing pH, consistent with the expected electrostatic behavior of ferrihydrite surfaces. The model predicted reduced PFOS retention at higher pH values, where the ferrihydrite surface becomes less positively charged or negatively charged, thereby diminishing its affinity for anionic species such as PFOS. However, experimental data demonstrated measurable PFOS retention even at higher pH levels, which the ferrihydrite model did not capture. This suggests that quartz plays a non-negligible role in PFOS adsorption under these conditions.

The third modeling scenario utilized a component additivity approach that incorporated both ferrihydrite and quartz surface complexes, with a modified charge allocation for the quartz surface (Fh & Q-NE). In this simulation, it was hypothesized that the charge distribution (CD factor) of the PFOS molecule does not govern its sorption behavior at the quartz interface. Instead, the surface complexation was modeled using a non-electrostatic (NE) representation, where charge allocation was assigned solely to the proton and no charge contribution from the PFOS molecule as [Δz0, Δz1]= [1,0]. This modeling approach provided a close alignment with the experimental sorption data for both the iron-coated sand and soil across the full pH range. The MSE for this scenario was 6.7 × 10− 15 for the coated sand and 5.4 × 10− 15 for the soil, indicating a significantly improved fit relative to the previous models (Fig. 4a for coated sand; Fig. 5a for soil).

In terms of surface speciation, the Fh-OS-HB complex, which primarily governed by surface charge and hydrogen bonding, shows increasing prevalence with decreasing pH and becomes negligible at pH values above 7 (Fig. 4d for coated sand; Fig. 5d for soil). The Fh-OS-Na complex contributes to a lesser extent but follows a similar pH-dependent trend, consistent with previously reported behavior for ferrihydrite. The non-electrostatic interaction between PFOS and quartz (Q-NE) becomes increasingly significant at pH values above 5. For instance, at pH 7, the Si-NE complex is the dominant species for both iron-coated sand and soil. The crossover point at which the Q-NE and Fh-OS-HB complexes contribute equally occurs at approximately pH 5.5 for the iron-coated sand and pH 6 for the soil. These crossover points align closely with the point of PZC for natural soils, typically in the range of pH 6.0–6.5.

Below the PZC, PFOS retention is primarily governed by electrostatic interactions, particularly via hydrogen-bonded outer-sphere complexes with ferrihydrite. Above the PZC, the increasing dominance of the Q-NE complex suggests that hydrophobic interactions become the primary retention mechanism, facilitating continued PFOS sorption even under conditions where electrostatic attraction is diminished. This is supported by the observation that PFOS adsorption remains substantial (~ 60%) at pH values above 7. This type of coordination requires further investigation to be fully understood; however, we hypothesize that it can serve as a useful proxy for simulating PFOS interactions with quartz in SCMs, offering a simpler alternative to more complex non-ideal approaches such as the NICA-Donnan model. These findings suggest that incorporating a non-electrostatic quartz-PFOS interaction into the additive modeling framework enhances its ability to reproduce PFOS retention behavior, particularly under conditions where electrostatic mechanisms alone cannot adequately explain the observed sorption.

Links with partition coefficients

Given that most fate and transport models still rely on partitioning coefficients, Kd (L/kg), to simulate sorption processes, we estimated Kd values for all samples as a function of pH (Fig. 13 of Supporting Information). Analysis of the Kd values revealed a consistent trend of increased PFOS sorption at lower pH values, particularly for iron-containing solids. This trend highlights the strong pH dependency of PFOS mobility, with lower pH conditions enhancing retention and thereby reducing transport potential. For the soil samples, Kd values ranged from approximately ~ 306 L/kg at pH > 6 to 5665 L/kg at pH ~ 4, indicating significant increase in sorption capacity with decreasing pH. A similar pattern was observed for the iron-coated sand. These results underscore the importance of accounting for pH-dependent sorption dynamics when modeling the environmental fate of PFOS, particularly in systems with variable geochemical conditions. The majority of PFAS-impacted groundwater environments are expected to typically exhibit near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH values, generally ranging from pH 6 to 8.5. However, in specific geochemical settings where the pH is significantly lower such as areas affected by acid mine drainage (AMD), legacy mining operations, or heavily polluted sites containing waste from acid-generating industrial processes or landfills, the environmental behavior of PFAS can differ significantly. In these low-pH systems, the Kd values are expected to be more than 15 times higher compared to the typical environmental conditions, which can significantly affect the uptake of PFAS on soils and sediments.

The Kd values obtained in this study are generally consistent with those reported in the literature. Rovero et al.45 synthesized data from 18 independent studies on soils or sediments and reported a mean Kd value of 368.6 L/kg for PFOS, which aligns well with the range of values observed in our experiments. In comparison to the distribution coefficients associated with bacterial uptake, the Kd values for iron-rich soil and coated sand under elevated pH conditions (pH > 6) are higher but still fall within the same order of magnitude (~ 100 L/kg), as reported by Butzen et al.27 Furthermore, Campos-Pereira et al.33 reported a markedly higher Kd value of 12,589.25 L/kg for PFOS adsorption onto ferrihydrite at pH 5. In comparison, our study yielded a similarly elevated Kd of 10,386.6 L/kg at a slightly higher pH, further corroborating the high sorptive capacity of ferrihydrite for PFOS.

The variability observed in estimated Kd values for PFOS highlights the complexity of predicting its mobility, which is highly dependent on environmental factors such as pH, organic matter content, mineral composition, and the surface charge properties of solid phases. As a result, subsurface contamination models that rely on a fixed or average Kd value may fail to capture the dynamic behavior of PFOS across varying environmental conditions. Consequently, accurate predictions of PFOS mobility, transport, and potential risks to groundwater and surface water resources require a more nuanced understanding of these interactions. The variability in Kd values also emphasizes the need for site-specific parameters and a more refined approach to modeling the reactive transport of persistent contaminants like PFOS in complex subsurface systems.

Conclusions

Accurate prediction of PFAS fate and transport in soil and groundwater is critical for mitigating the environmental and human health risks associated with PFAS contamination. While reactive transport modeling has demonstrated promise in both laboratory and field contexts, the reliability of these predictions fundamentally depends on a detailed understanding of geochemical processes and site-specific parameters, particularly sorption dynamics.

This study advances scientific knowledge of PFOS sorption by providing mechanistic, molecular-level descriptions grounded in SCM that capture thermodynamically consistent surface speciation under variable geochemical conditions. By integrating batch adsorption experiments with SCM, the adsorption behavior of PFOS on various surfaces including ferrihydrite, ferrihydrite-coated sand, and soil was elucidated. Our results demonstrate that PFOS sorption in soil and iron-coated sand is governed by a dual mechanism: electrostatic interactions, primarily associated with the ferrihydrite surface, dominate at low pH, while hydrophobic interactions with the quartz surface become increasingly significant at elevated pH values. An improved SCM model incorporating two outer-sphere complexes, one hydrogen-bonded and another involving Na+ co-adsorption, along with a primarily non-electrostatic interaction between PFOS and quartz surfaces, successfully captured the observed PFOS retention in both iron-coated sand and soil.

It is important to note that these observations closely align with the variability observed in Kd values, which correspond to shifts in surface complexes across different pH conditions. These mechanistic descriptions when coupled with groundwater models, can improve their capability to simulate the fate and transport of PFAS in the subsurface and provide robust groundwater plume predictions. A more accurate prediction of PFAS mobility in groundwater will in turn result in improved risk assessment and better-informed decision-making for the management of public and private water systems.
