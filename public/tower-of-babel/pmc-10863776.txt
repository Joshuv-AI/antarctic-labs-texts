Higher Cumulative Blood Pressure in Midlife Predicts an Increased Risk of Atrial Fibrillation: Evidence From the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study
Authors: Yun‐Jiu Cheng, Hai Deng, Yun Ling, Wei‐Dong Lin, Zhuomin Liang, Yili Chen, Xian‐Hong Fang, Hong‐Tao Liao, Yang Liu, Hui‐Qiang Wei, Fang‐Zhou Liu, Jun‐Rong Jiang, Shu‐Lin Wu, Yu‐Mei Xue, Zexuan Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElevated blood pressure (BP) is reportedly associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF). However, the association between cumulative BP exposure in midlife and incident AF in mid‐to‐late life remains unclear.Methods and ResultsParticipants enrolled in the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study with 4 consecutive BP measurements and no prevalent AF at baseline were included. Cumulative BP was calculated as the area under the curve from visit 1 to visit 4. Incident AF was identified by study visit ECGs, hospital discharge codes, or death certificates. A total of 9892 participants were included (44.6% men and mean age 62.9±5.7 years at visit 4) with 1550 (15.7%) individuals who developed new‐onset AF during an average follow‐up of 15.4 years. The incidence rates of AF per 1000 person‐years across the 4 quartiles of cumulative systolic BP were 7.9, 9.2, 12.5, and 16.9, respectively. After multivariable adjustment, the hazard ratios for incident AF among participants in the highest quartile of cumulative systolic BP, pulse pressure, and mean arterial pressure were 1.48 (95% CI, 1.27–1.72), 1.81 (95% CI, 1.53–2.13), and 1.22 (95% CI, 1.05–1.41), respectively, compared with those in the lowest quartile. The addition of cumulative systolic BP or pulse pressure slightly improved the ability to predict new‐onset AF.ConclusionsHigher exposure to cumulative systolic BP, pulse pressure, and mean arterial pressure was significantly associated with increased risk of incident AF.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, with its incidence and prevalence increasing globally over the past 5 decades. 1 , 2 Patients with AF are associated with a significantly increased risk of morbidity and mortality. 3 Elevated blood pressure (BP), is a modifiable risk factor for AF, 4 accounting for over one fifth of the new AF cases. 5 Each 20 mm Hg increase in systolic blood pressure (SBP) is associated with a 21% higher risk of AF, 6 whereas intensive BP reduction to a target of SBP <120 mm Hg is associated with a 26% lower risk of AF, 7 reinforcing the need for intensive management of elevated BP.

Cumulative BP exposure, which is derived from the curve (mm Hg×years) of multiple BP measurements over time, has emerged as a promising marker for assessing the long‐term severity and duration of BP exposure. 8 In prior studies, cumulative BP exposure has been proven to be a strong predictor of cardiovascular adverse events, 9 , 10 with incremental predictive values over a single measurement. Among the 5 components of metabolic syndrome, elevated BP had the greatest increase in AF risk, with an approximately 2‐fold increase in AF risk when elevated BP was detected 4 times repeatedly. 11 Individuals with hypertension burden of 1, 2, 3, and 4 across 4 annual health checkups were associated with an 8%, 18%, 26%, and 27% increased risk of incident AF, respectively, compared with those without hypertension, emphasizing the adverse impact of cumulative hypertension burden on new‐onset AF. 12 However, these studies adopted only a semi‐quantitative dose‐responsive analysis with a relatively short follow‐up (median 5.2 years), 12 and the association between cumulative BP exposure in midlife and incident AF in mid‐to‐late life remains unclear.

In general, left atrial (LA) remodeling, including atrial electrical, functional, and structural remodeling, is a critical mechanism mediating the development and perpetuation of AF. 13 A recent study demonstrated that higher cumulative BP from early adulthood to middle age was independently associated with adverse LA remodeling. 14 Nonetheless, it remains unknown whether LA metrics mediate the relationship between cumulative BP exposure and incident AF.

To address this question, we examined the longitudinal association between cumulative BP exposure in midlife and incident AF in mid‐to‐late life, as well as the incremental predictive value of cumulative BP exposure over traditional AF risk models. We also explored whether adverse LA metrics mediated the relationship between cumulative high BP exposure and incident AF. Data were collected from the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) cohort, in which 25‐year longitudinal follow‐up data are available. We hypothesized that exposure to higher BP in midlife would be associated with a higher AF risk in mid‐to‐late life, and that this association might be partially mediated by LA metrics.

Methods

ARIC Study Data

The data collected by the ARIC study are publicly available to qualified investigators with an approved article proposal and data use agreement. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

The ARIC study is a longitudinal cohort study conducted in 4 US communities (Washington County, Maryland; Forsyth County, North Carolina; Jackson, Mississippi; and suburban Minneapolis, Minnesota). 15 A total of 15 792 participants aged 45 to 64 years were recruited between 1987 and 1989 (visit 1). Participants were regularly followed up in 1990 to 1992 (visit 2), 1993 to 1995 (visit 3), 1996 to 1998 (visit 4), and 2011 to 2013 (visit 5). In addition, participants received annual follow‐up calls, with response rates exceeding 90% among survivors.

Participants were included in our analyses if they had undergone 4 BP measurements from visit 1 to visit 4. Participants with prevalent AF at baseline or missing follow‐up data were excluded.

Ethical Approval Statement

This study was approved by the local institutional review boards at each participating site, and all participants provided written informed consent. As the current study used only deidentified data with no additional patient contact, no additional ethical approval or patient consent was required.

Cumulative BP Exposure Calculation

The sitting BP was measured 3 times after 5 minutes of rest using a random‐zero sphygmomanometer. We evaluated SBP, diastolic BP (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and pulse pressure (PP) measures. PP is calculated as the difference between SBP and DBP. MAP is calculated as one‐third PP plus DBP. Cumulative BP exposure were estimated using area under the curve of the consecutive BP measurements from visit 1 to visit 4 according to previous studies, 16 and the formula was as follows: cumulative SBP = [(SBP1+SBP2)/2×time1‐2]+[(SBP2+SBP3)/2×time2‐3]+[(SBP3+SBP4)/2×time3‐4]. Among them, SBP1, SBP2, SBP3, and SBP4 were SBP levels measured during visit 1 to visit 4, and time1‐2, time2‐3, and time 3–4 were time intervals between 2 adjacent follow‐up BP measurements.

Assessment of AF on Follow‐Up

Participants were followed up, starting from visit 4, to evaluate the presence of AF. The diagnosis of AF was established through a 10‐second 12‐lead ECG examination during each visit, hospital discharge codes, or death certificates. 17 Participants were identified as AF if their discharge diagnosis codes were 427.31 (AF) or 427.32 (atrial flutter) according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM).AF associated with open cardiac surgery was excluded. In addition, AF was also ascertained if 427.3 (ICD‐9‐CM code) or I48 (ICD, Tenth Revision code) was reported as a cause of death.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported using mean±SD or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, and numbers and percentages for categorical variables. ANOVA and chi‐square test were used for comparison of baseline characteristics among different cumulative BP quartiles for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

The incidence rates of AF were calculated as the ratio of events to the accumulated person‐time of follow‐up for participants and reported as rates per 1000 person‐years within each cumulative BP quartile. Restricted cubic spline was used to assess the possible nonlinear relationship between cumulative BP and the risk of AF. The test for linear trend was performed using the median value of each cumulative BP quartile as a continuous variable. A 1‐SD difference in the cumulative BP values was used for trend estimate.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the hazard ratio (HRs) and 95% CIs of incident AF by quartiles of cumulative BP exposure with the lowest quartile as the reference. The models were adjusted for covariates assessed at visit 4, including age, race, sex, education level, income, marital status, smoking status, alcohol use status, diabetes, body mass index, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol level, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol level, triglycerides, and fasting blood glucose. Stratified analyses by race and sex were conducted with the same covariates.

Full information maximum likelihood was used to handle missing data under the missing‐at‐random assumption. Markov chain Monte Carlo multiple imputation method was used to handle missing baseline values before inclusion in the multivariable adjusted models. The results of 10 cycles of multiple imputation were combined to obtain the final estimates.

Time‐dependent receiver operator characteristic curve analysis was used to assess whether cumulative BP exposure improved the discrimination power of the basic AF risk prediction model. The area under the time‐dependent receiver operator characteristic curve, C‐statistic, net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement were calculated to quantify improvement in each model by adding cumulative BP exposure to the basic model. To quantify the extent to which significant associations between measures of cumulative BP exposure and incident AF were mediated by LA metrics, mediation analyses were performed by using the natural effect model. 18 All analyses were performed with Stata version 15.0 (Stata Corp) and R (version 3.4.4). P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

After excluding 5899 participants with prevalent AF at baseline and those with incomplete BP parameters or follow‐up data, a total of 9892 individuals were included in the study (44.6% male, 80.3% White, mean age: 62.9±5.7 years at visit 4). Table 1 displays the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants categorized by quartiles of cumulative SBP. In general, participants in the higher quartiles of cumulative SBP were more often older, had lower education levels, higher body mass index, higher blood pressure, fewer current smokers and alcohol drinkers, and higher levels of fasting blood glucose and triglycerides.

Cumulative BP Exposure and the Risk of AF

Over a median follow‐up period of 15.4 (interquartile range 11.9–16.7) years, 1550 (15.7%) participants developed new‐onset AF. The incidence rate increased across the quartiles of cumulative SBP, PP, and MAP (Figure 1). The incidence rates of AF per 1000 person‐years were 7.9 (95% CI, 7.0–8.9), 9.2 (95% CI, 8.2–10.3), 12.5 (95% CI, 11.3–13.7), and 16.9 (95% CI, 15.5–18.4) across the 4 quartiles of cumulative SBP (Table 2). Compared with individuals assigned to the lowest quartile, the HRs for the second, third, and highest quartile were 0.97 (95% CI, 0.83–1.14), 1.21 (95% CI, 1.04–1.42), and 1.48 (95% CI, 1.27–1.72), respectively. Similar associations were observed when analyzing cumulative SBP as a continuous various in the dose–response analysis. Figure 2 demonstrated a monotonic increase in the HRs of incident AF as the cumulative SBP increases. Similar trends were observed for cumulative PP and MAP quartiles. After adjusting for traditional cardiovascular disease risk factors, participants in the highest quartile of cumulative PP and MAP had HRs of 1.81 (95% CI, 1.53–2.13) and 1.22 (95% CI, 1.05–1.41), respectively, compared with those in the lowest quartile. However, no linear trend was observed for cumulative DBP quartiles and incident AF (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–1.02]).

Incremental Predictive Value of Cumulative BP on New‐Onset AF

Table S1 and Figure 3 illustrated the incremental predictive value of adding cumulative BP exposure to the basic model for AF. The addition of cumulative SBP slightly improved model discrimination, with an increase in the area under the time‐dependent receiver operator characteristic curve from 0.739 (95% CI, 0.724–0.754) to 0.745 (95% CI, 0.733–0.762). The C statistic was also increased from 0.685 (95% CI, 0.671–0.699) to 0.690 (95% CI, 0.676–0.704) (P=0.004). Additionally, there was a trend in the same direction for net reclassification improvement (17.1% [95% CI, 11.6%–22.6%]; P<0.001) and integrated discrimination improvement (0.4% [95% CI, 0.2%–0.6%]; P<0.001). Similar results were also obtained when cumulative PP or MAP were added to the basic model. However, no incremental value for AF was observed when cumulative DBP was added to the model.

Stratified Analyses by Sex and Race

In analyses stratified by sex and race, individuals in the higher quartiles of cumulative SBP or PP (quartile 3 or 4) were found to have increased risks of AF when compared with those in the lower quartiles (quartile 1 or 2) regardless of race or sex (Table S2). Notably, the AF association with cumulative SBP was the strongest among Black men (HR, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.21–1.82]), followed by Black women (HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.03–1.45]), White women (HR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.11–1.30]), and was not statistically significant in White men (HR, 1.09 [95% CI, 0.99–1.14]). With regard to cumulative PP, the risk of AF was the highest in Black men, followed by Black women, White women, and White men (HR, 1.39 [95% CI, 1.13–1.71] for Black men (1.33 [95% CI, 1.11–1.60] for Black women, 1.32 [95% CI, 1.21–1.44] for White women, and 1.13 [95% CI, 1.04–1.21] for White men, respectively).

Mediation Analyses

We also conducted mediation analyses to investigate if LA metrics mediated the association between cumulative BP exposure and new‐onset AF among a subgroup of 4821 participants who had echocardiographic data available during visit 5 (Table 3). The results showed that LA size, LA volume, and LA index all significantly mediated the associations between cumulative SBP and incident AF to a large extent. Specifically, the mediating effect was 58.1%, 70.2%, and 66.2% for LA size, LA volume, and LA index, respectively. However, the mediating effects of LA metrics on cumulative PP and AF were slightly weaker than those on cumulative SBP and AF. The partial mediation was 36.9%, 44.2%, and 56.2% for LA size, LA volume, and LA index, respectively.

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this large sample community‐based cohort study, we found that higher exposure to cumulative SBP and PP but not DBP was significantly associated with an increased risk of AF, regardless of race or sex. Adding cumulative SBP or PP to the basic risk factor models slightly improved the ability to predict new‐onset AF. Further mediation analyses indicated that LA metrics, including LA size, LA volume, or LA index, might mediate the effects of cumulative BP exposure on AF. To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate an independent association between cumulative BP exposure with AF.

Association Between Cumulative BP Exposure and Incident AF

Mounting evidence has ascertained a higher AF risk with hypertension, as they share common risk factors. 19 , 20 A recent study derived from the ARIC cohort indicated that elevated BP was a major contributor to 56.5% of AF cases. 5 Participants with hypertension were associated with a 50% higher risk of developing AF, 21 and further analysis suggested a causal relationship between BP levels and AF. 22 The severity or burden of hypertension during follow‐up was also linked to incident AF, with higher burdens of hypertension associated with a greater risk. Specifically, hypertension burdens of 1, 2, 3, and 4 estimated by a semiquantitative scoring system were associated with an increased risk of incident AF by 8%, 18%, 26%, and 27%, respectively. 12 Additionally, longitudinal BP variability, as opposed to a current single BP measurement, has been shown to be associated with a higher risk of subclinical AF or premature atrial contractions. 23 In our study, we found that cumulative SBP slightly outperformed a single baseline SBP in predicting incident AF (C‐statistic: 0.690 [0.676–0.704] versus 0.682 [0.668–0.696], P=0.008). Similar results were observed when comparing cumulative PP with baseline PP (C‐statistic: 0.695 [0.681–0.709] versus 0.691 [0.678–0.705], P=0.02). Our study contributes to this body of work by evaluating the longitudinal and cumulative effects of BP on incident AF using long‐term cumulative SBP, PP, and MAP exposure, which was found to be associated with an increased risk of new‐onset AF.

The exact mechanism linking cumulative BP exposure and AF is not yet fully understood. Animal models have demonstrated that elevated BP is associated with significant atrial electrical and structural remodeling, such as increased LA conduction heterogeneity and susceptibility to AF, as well as LA hypertrophy, fibrosis, and inflammation. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 In addition, in a spontaneously hypertensive rats model, older (11‐month‐old) spontaneously hypertensive rats were more likely to develop LA tachyarrhythmias induced by burst pacing than normotensive control rats or younger (3‐month‐old) spontaneously hypertensive rats, 26 suggesting that a longer duration of hypertension may be associated with LA remodeling and increased AF susceptibility. To explore the potential mediating role of LA remodeling parameters in the relationship between cumulative BP and AF, we conducted a mediation analysis, which confirmed partial mediated effects of LA metrics in this association. Further studies especially randomized controlled trials were warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Nevertheless, we did not observe a linear association between cumulative DBP and incident AF, and the underlying mechanisms for this lack of association remain unclear. Previous studies have established a robust association between higher SBP and an increased risk of incident AF. 21 , 29 , 30 However, the relationship between DBP and AF risk remains controversial. For instance, the Women's Health Study, which enrolled 34 221 women, demonstrated a highly significant increase in the risk of incident AF across increasing categories of SBP and DBP, although SBP outweighed DBP. 30 Another study using data from the ARIC cohort showed that DBP was associated with a lower risk of AF (HR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.84–0.97]). 21 However, the Framingham Heart Study did not observe a significant association between DBP and AF risk. 29 Further studies are needed to elucidate this controversy and shed light on the underlying mechanisms.

Racial Differences in Cumulative BP Exposure and Incident AF

The association between cumulative BP exposure and incident AF is influenced by both race and sex. Our study found that this association was the strongest among Black men, followed by Black and White women, and then White men. On the contrary, the mediating effect of LA metrics on the relationship between cumulative BP exposure and AF was the strongest for White men, whereas it was weaker for Black individuals.

Indeed, the lifetime risk and age‐adjusted incidence rate of AF can vary significantly by sex, race, and overall risk factor burden. 31 For instance, the incidence rate of AF in the ARIC cohort was 9.6 per 1000 person‐years with rates of 6.9 among Black participants, 10.5 among White participants, 8.1 among women, and 11.6 among men. 32 Despite being less susceptible to atrial tachycardia, Black participants in the ARIC cohort had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension (81% versus 63%), diabetes (34.8% versus 20%), and heart failure (12.7% versus 5.2%), compared with White individuals. 33 In addition, Black participants had greater atrial remodeling than White participants. 33 However, the effects of LA metrics on the relationship between cumulative BP exposure and AF were weak, indicating that other cardiovascular risk factors may play a more significant role in mediating this relationship. Further robust studies are warranted to unravel the racial paradox of AF.

Our study emphasizes the importance of long‐term intensive management of BP, as high cumulative BP exposure is a modifiable risk factor for AF. Previous study has shown that targeting a SBP of <120 mm Hg significantly reduces the risk of AF in patients with hypertension and a high risk of cardiovascular diseases. 7 Further studies are needed to investigate the effect of long‐term intensive BP management, particularly during early adulthood, on the occurrence of AF.

Strengths and Limitations

The community‐based cohort with a large sample and long‐term follow‐up, comprehensive adjustments for time‐dependent covariates, along with the mediation analyses made the study robust. However, there are still limitations worth mentioning. First, the study is observational in nature, which limits the ability to establish a cause‐and‐effect relationship between high cumulative BP exposure and the development of AF. Second, the absence of formal validation, such as cross‐validation or bootstrap validation, may restrict the extent to which our results can be generalized.

Third, the study population consists of middle‐aged adults, and the results may not be generalizable to younger individuals or older populations. Fourth, even though multiple sources were used to detect AF, subclinical cases may be still underrecognized. In addition, due to the limited data sources, we cannot distinguish paroxysmal AF from persistent AF, as well as AF from atrial flutter. Lastly, there have been advancements in BP management since the data collection period, which could potentially influence the incidence of AF. Nevertheless, our study can serve as a foundation for future studies to explore the association between updated BP therapeutic strategies and AF risk.

Conclusions

Exposure to higher SBP, PP, and MAP but not DBP in midlife were significantly associated with higher risk of incident AF in mid‐to‐late life, regardless of race or sex. The addition of cumulative SBP or PP to the basic risk prediction models slightly improved the ability to predict new‐onset AF. Our study also indicates that the effects of cumulative BP exposure on AF may be partially mediated by LA metrics.
