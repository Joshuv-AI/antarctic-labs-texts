Prior-free cosmological parameter estimation of Cosmicflows-4
C. Duangchan ,, A. Valade, N. I. Libeskind, M. Steinmetz

Abstract
Context. As tracers of the underlying mass distributions, galaxies’ peculiar velocities are valuable probes of the Universe, allowing us to measure the Hubble constant or to map the large scale structure and its dynamics. Yet, catalogs of peculiar velocities are noisy, scarce and prone to various interpretation biases. Aims. We aim to measure the bulk flow and the Hubble constant directly from the largest available sample of peculiar velocities, and without imposing a cosmological prior on the velocity field. Methods. To address these issues, we analyze the Cosmicflows-4 catalog, the most extensive catalog of galaxy peculiar velocities, reaching a redshift z = 0.1 z=0.1 . Specifically, we construct a forward modeling approach assuming only a flat Universe, which reconstructs the radial and bulk flows of the velocity field directly from measurements of peculiar velocities. Results. Our method accurately recovers cosmological parameters within a radius of 120 Mpc/h that can then be compared with the predictions from Λ \Lambda CDM . Apart from a 3 σ \sigma tension with Λ \Lambda CDM on the magnitude around 120 ​ Mpc / h 120\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h associated with a 4 σ \sigma tension on the supergalactic X X direction, we find a general agreement between the standard model and the observations. Lastly, our analysis suggests a Hubble constant value of approximately 75.8 ± 0.4 75.8\pm 0.4 km/s/Mpc, exacerbating (or independently confirming) the existing “Hubble tension”, however, for the first time accomplished with the largest set of peculiar velocities in existence.

The motion of galaxies has inspired cosmologists for at least a century when Hubble noted that the red-shifting of absorption lines was proportional to distance, thereby discovering the expansion of the Universe
(
Hubble 1929
)
. The Hubble law, predicted by Friedman some years prior is simple:
cz=H_{0}d
, where
H_{0}
is (the Hubble) constant,
z
is the redshift,
c
the speed of light and
d
the distance. Yet the redshift Hubble used is now known to be a combination of the velocity due to cosmic expansion and the line of sight component of a galaxy’s peculiar velocity – namely the velocity due to the net gravitational pull due to the full mass distribution of the Universe
(
Kaiser 1987
;
Dekel et al. 1993
)
. At the first order, the equation should read
cz=H_{0}d+v_{\rm r}
, with
(v_{r})
the radial component of the peculiar velocity. Thus radial peculiar velocity of a galaxy can be measured, provided a joint measurement of redshift and distance

v_{r}=cz-H_{0}d.

(1)

Such catalogs combining both redshifts and distances have demonstrated their relevance for cosmology. They enabled the discovery of the accelerated expansion of the Universe
(
Riess et al. 1998
;
Perlmutter et al. 1999
)
; yield a local value of
H_{0}

(see
Di Valentino et al. 2025
, for a review on the
H_{0}
tension)
; permit the constraining of cosmological parameters such as
f\sigma_{8}

(
Carreres et al. 2023
;
Rosselli et al. 2025
,
e.g.
)
or
\Omega_{M}

(
Brout et al. 2022
)
; test non-standard models such as
w_{0}w_{a}
CDM
(
Brout et al. 2022
)
; explore anisotropies in the expansion of the Universe
(
Giani et al. 2024
;
Boubel et al. 2025
;
Kalbouneh et al. 2025
)
and discuss the misalignment between local motion and CMB dipole
(
Sorrenti et al. 2023
,
e.g.
)
. Locally, peculiar velocities have been used to map the Large Scale Structure
(
Courtois et al. 2013
;
Valade et al. 2024
,
e.g.
)
and the discovery of local dynamical structures such as the Great Attractor
(
Dressler et al. 1987
)
, the Laniakea basin of attraction
(
Tully et al. 2014
)
or the dipole repeller
(
Hoffman et al. 2017
)
. Last but not least, measurements of peculiar velocities can capture the so-called “moments” of the velocity field
(
Regos & Szalay 1989
, namely the dipole, quadrupole, etc)
, which can be easily compared to theoretical predictions, providing a valuable test for cosmological models. This is the path we explore in this work.

While systematic redshift surveys with sufficient precision became available in the mid-eighties
(
de Lapparent et al. 1986
)
, measuring the physical distances to galaxies remains difficult. All distance measurements rely on essentially the same principle – the comparison between the observed magnitude or size with an assumed absolute magnitude or size. In the Local Universe, where individual stars can be resolved, this is typically more accurate as the magnitude of stars at the tip of the red giant branch
(
Lee et al. 1993
)
is a well-established standard candle. Beyond this region, absolute magnitudes need to be derived from scaling relations such as the Tully-Fisher
(
Tully & Fisher 1977
)
or the Fundamental Plane relations
(
Dressler et al. 1987
)
.

For observational reasons, the vast majority of distance measurements are provided in distance moduli
\mu\propto\log(d)
. Issues arise when errors on
\mu
(usually assumed to be normally distributed) are directly placed on the distance, since these then transform into log-normal distance errors, leading to biases
(
Strauss & Willick 1995
)
. This “breathing mode” motivated the development of various distance estimators
(
Watkins & Feldman 2015
;
Sorce et al. 2016
;
Hoffman et al. 2021
;
Watkins et al. 2023
;
Sorce et al. 2024
,
e.g.
)
, which need to be applied to such data prior to any analysis, such as the moments of the velocity field explored in this paper. While carefully derived, these methods remain very ad-hoc and result in the designing of heterogeneous analysis pipelines.

Methods designed to reconstruct bulk flow from corrected velocity data can be separated into three families. First, the maximum likelihood method proposed by
Kaiser 1988
and applied to various datasets by
e.g.

Ma et al. 2011
;
Ma & Pan 2014
;
Qin et al. 2018
;
Qin et al. 2021
;
Howlett et al. 2022
;
Whitford et al. 2023
. Second, the minimum-variance method developed by
Watkins et al. 2009
later applied by
e.g.

Feldman et al. 2010
;
Watkins & Feldman 2015
;
Watkins et al. 2023
;
Scrimgeour et al. 2016
;
Peery et al. 2018
;
Whitford et al. 2023
. Third, the application of the Wiener Filter / Constrained realization framework
(
Hoffman & Ribak 1992
;
Zaroubi et al. 1995
)
that have been notably applied to successive releases of the Cosmicflows catalogs
(
Tully & Courtois 2012
;
Tully et al. 2016
;
Tully et al. 2023
, CF2, CF3, CF4
resp.
)
published in (respectively)
Hoffman et al. 2015
;
Hoffman et al. 2021
;
Hoffman et al. 2024
. The ability to reconstruct the full three-dimensional velocity field of this last approach, assuming
\Lambda
CDM prior as well as a matter power spectrum, is among its strengths. Since it enforces a theoretical
\Lambda
CDM velocity-velocity correlation, this approach cannot truly probe the
\Lambda
CDM model, especially in regions beyond a few dozen Mpc, where the noise dominates the signal and data are scarce.

An alternative approach, known as forward modeling, consists of deriving the full posterior probability of the model’s parameters of interest (
e.g.
Bulk Flow,
H_{0}
) given the data using a Bayesian framework. The posterior probability is then explored with a Monte Carlo algorithm. Although computationally expensive, this approach is entirely self-consistent and offers greater flexibility than traditional distance estimators.

The application of forward modeling to reconstruct the velocity field was first proposed by
(
Lavaux 2016
)
and notably applied by
Graziani et al. 2019
to the Cosmicflows-3 catalog. The method has been further improved as described in
(
Boruah et al. 2021
;
Valade et al. 2022
)
and applied the Cosmicflows-4 data notably by
Courtois et al. 2023
;
Valade et al. 2024
. While the forward modeling approach has shown a greater ability to reconstruct small scales than the traditional Wiener Filter approach
(
Valade et al. 2023
)
, its ability to recover tension with
\Lambda
CDM on the large scales is even more limited than that of the Wiener Filter. Indeed, on top of assuming an underlying
\Lambda
CDM prior, the reconstructions are done in periodic boxes (
e.g.
1
\,\,{\rm Gpc}/h
in
Valade et al. 2024
) which is known to further dampen the recovered large-scale motion due to the missing large-scale tidal modes.

This work presents a forward modeling of the first spherical modes of the velocity field (radial flow, bulk flow) from peculiar velocity data which makes minimal
\Lambda
CDM assumptions. The
\Lambda
CDM model is used only in the equation relating distances and redshifts. More importantly, no velocity-velocity correlation function or power spectrum is assumed. Such a forward modeling reconstruction of the first spherical modes of the velocity field was proposed by
Boubel et al. 2024
;
Boubel et al. 2025
. Even though the approaches are similar, differences are found both in methodology and observational application:
Boubel et al. 2024
;
Boubel et al. 2025
are limited to the Cosmicflows-4 Tully-Fisher sample
(
Kourkchi et al. 2022
)
, while we apply our reconstruction method to the entire Cosmicflows-4 catalog of peculiar velocities. This enables a deeper result while raising issues of anisotropy, which has been shown to lead to overestimation of the bulk flow
(
Andersen et al. 2016
)
.

)

Method

3D Spatial distribution of the CF4 and mock catalog of each sub-type, in solid and dashed lines, respectively. The shade in a lighter color is the scatter among the mocks.

In this section, we detail the model and the method used to reconstruct the moments of the velocity field from measurements of peculiar velocities. The approach can be summarized as follows. The first two moments of the velocity field (
i.e.
a radial flow augmented by a flow in a constant direction) are chosen at random. Assuming that the errors in the redshift measurements are negligible, the distance to each galaxy is reconstructed from (a more accurate version of)
eq.

1
. The inferred distances, transformed into distance moduli, are then compared with the Cosmicflows-4 observations. Finally, the moments of the velocity field are updated with a Monte Carlo step. The result is a large collection of probable values of radial and bulk flows compatible with the Cosmicflows-4 data. This approach, dubbed forward modeling, is detailed in the following section.

3.1
Forward modeling

Forward modeling heavily relies on Bayes’s equation which encodes the
posterior
probability of the models parameters
\pi
given that the data
\mathcal{D}
have been obtained

P(\pi\mid\mathcal{D})\propto P(\mathcal{D}\mid\pi)P(\pi),

(2)

where the likelihood
P(\mathcal{D}\mid\pi)
and the prior
P(\pi)
respectively encode the observational and the underlying physical models.

Here, we aim at reconstructing the zeroth and first modes of the velocity field
(
Regos & Szalay 1989
)
. These are often called the monopole and the dipole, respectively. However, since the term “dipole” has come to be understood in common parlance as the motion of an observer relative to background, here we refer to the mean motion of galaxies flowing either radially (radial flow) or in some direction (bulk flow).

Throughout this paper, the radial flow is referred to as
V_{r}
. Note that although the radial flow has the dimensions of speed (km/s) and one of two directions (inward or outward), it is not a vector. The bulk flow is a three-dimensional vector in the traditional sense and is referred to here as
\bm{V}=(V_{X},V_{Y},V_{Z})
.

We consider
\mathcal{D}=\{\mu^{\rm obs}_{i}\}_{i<n}
, where
n
is the number of observations (namely the number of entries in the CosmicFlows-4 catalogue), is derived. Note that the redshift does not feature in
\mathcal{D}
since the error on it is neglected and therefore does not need to be inferred.

3.2
Likelihood

As mentioned above, the goal is to make a suggestion for each galaxy’s (or galaxy group’s) distance modulus, for the purpose of comparison with the measured value. Thus, the distance modulus for each entry is constructed as follows. First, the peculiar velocity of the constraint
i
is computed as

v^{i}_{r}=V_{r}+\bm{V}\cdot\hat{\bm{r}}^{i}

(3)

where
\hat{\bm{r}}
is a unit vector pointing toward the constraint. Assuming that the full redshift
z
is known with negligible uncertainty, the proposed cosmological redshift (namely the redshift just due to the Hubble expansion) is computed from the proposed peculiar velocity:

z_{\rm cos}=\frac{z-v_{r}/c}{1+v_{r}/c},

(4)

which is an accurate version of
eq.

1
. Then, the proposed physical distance
d
, is obtained by numerically integrating the following

d=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{z_{\rm cos}}\frac{\text{d}z}{\sqrt{(1+z)^{3}\Omega_{\rm M}+(1-\Omega_{\rm M})}}.

(5)

Lastly, the distance modulus is obtained:

\mu=5\log_{10}\left[(1+z_{\rm cos})d\right]+25.

(6)

It is noted that
eq.

5
assumes a flat Friedman-Lemaitre-Robertson-Walker cosmology. This is the minimal assumption we can make on the cosmological model since the relationship between redshift and distance necessarily depends on the density of the universe. The Hubble parameter
H_{0}
, as well as the density parameter
\Omega_{M}
are not explored by our forward model. Instead, their respective influences on the results are discussed in
section

4
and
appendix

A
.

Once these calculations have been carried out, the likelihood function can be constructed, namely the ensemble of proposed values of the distance moduli can be compared with the observed values and their errors. The distance moduli errors are assumed to be normally distributed around the mean value and independent of each other, which is the standard approach
(
Hoffman et al. 2021
;
Boruah et al. 2021
;
Valade et al. 2022
;
Tully et al. 2023
,
e.g.
)
. The likelihood over all the reconstructed distance moduli is expressed as the exponential product of the difference between the observed distance modulus and the proposed one, divided by the error on the distance modulus:

\displaystyle P\left(\{\mu^{\rm obs}_{i}\}\mid\pi\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i}P\left(\mu^{\rm obs}_{i}\mid\pi\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\mu^{\rm obs}_{i}-\mu_{i}(\pi)\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{\mu,i}^{2}}\right).

(8)

The observational model described above is similar to that of
Valade et al. 2022
. The core difference between the methods lies in the prior distribution: while the
Valade et al. 2022
assumed a
\Lambda
CDM prior and a matter power-spectrum, here, we set a flat prior on the velocity field, that is

P(\pi)=1

(9)

so as to avoid dampening possible tension between the data and
\Lambda
CDM ​. In contrast with field-level inference technics
(
Valade et al. 2022
;
Courtois et al. 2023
,
e.g.
)
and the Wiener-Filter approach
(
Hoffman et al. 2024
,
e.g.
)
, our method does not assume a
\Lambda
CDM ​-consistent velocity-velocity correlation function.

The posterior distribution is explored by means of a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) technique
(
Neal 2011
)
. An HMC (versus, e.g. a Markov Chain Monte Carlo) is more efficient at exploring the full permissible parameter space - here the parameter space consists of essentially just four numbers:
V_{r},\penalty\ B_{x},\penalty\ B_{y},\penalty\ B_{z}
. Each Monte Carlo chains comprises 1 000 states, preceded by a 100 step burn-in phase. Given the simplicity of our problem, any reasonable choice of hyper-parameters leads to an optimal convergence of the algorithm. The HMC implementation made use of
TensorFlow-Probability

(
Dillon et al. 2017
)
with a
Jax
backend
(
Bradbury et al. 2018
)
.

3.3
Inference of the velocity field in concentric shells

Since distances are measured via their observed distance moduli, the error on the peculiar velocity
(\sigma_{v})
for each entry of the catalog is proportional to both the measured error on the distance modulus (
\sigma_{\mu}
) and its (true) distance. The reader will note that for the vast majority of data points, the redshift distance is more precise than the physical distance inferred from the distance ladder:

\sigma_{v}\approx\frac{\log(10)}{5}\sigma_{\mu}\times cz.

(10)

Owing to distance-dependent errors, most of the constraining power of the data is found near the observer. This implies that running the inference method on the entire catalog at once would lead to a bulk flow estimated at an effective radius

r_{\rm eff}=\frac{c}{H_{0}}\frac{\sum_{i}z_{i}/\sigma_{v,i}}{\sum_{i}1/\sigma_{v,i}}\approx 100\,{\rm Mpc}\penalty\ \penalty\ (\approx 75\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h),

(11)

which is much shallower than that of the data (whose median distance is 155  Mpc/h).
This is due to (1) the decrease of density of constraints with distance and (2) the increase of individual error on the constraints. On top, it is to be noted that the CF4 catalog becomes very anisotropic beyond
165\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
(
z=0.055
).

We designed an approach to overcome this limitation. In words, the Monte Carlo fits are first run independently in concentric shells, then, the values in concentric
spheres
are reconstructed by averaging the contributions of the shells by their respective volume. More precisely, the catalog is split (according to each galaxy’s redshift distance
r_{i}\approx cz_{i}/H_{100}\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
) in concentric shells of thickness
\Delta r=20\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
, in each of which the Monte Carlo algorithm is run separately. The value of the sphere of radius
(R)
is then computed as:

\displaystyle\quad r_{j}

\displaystyle=j\times\Delta r

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{3}\pi\left(r_{j}^{3}-r_{j-1}^{3}\right),

\displaystyle Q^{(s)}(R)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j}^{r_{j}<R}Q_{j}^{(s)}\mathcal{V}_{j}}{\sum_{j}^{r_{j}<R}\mathcal{V}_{j}},

where
Q_{j}^{(s)}
is the
s
-th Monte Carlo step of the quantity
Q
in the
j
-th shell (
i.e.
Q_{j}
).
Equation

12
is applied independently to the radial flow
V_{r}
and the components of the bulk flow
V_{X/Y/Z}
.

Note that the
Q^{(s)}(R)
constitute only a pseudo-Monte Carlo chain and rather than an actual radius-dependent reconstruction. Namely, correlations between fitted values at different radii cannot be interpreted.

Before the method is applied to the CF4 data, we wish to determine its precision and accuracy, namely how well does it measure the two modes on the velocity field we are interested in (the radial infall and the bulk flow). It its thus applied to mock observations of the CF4 grouped catalog. In this way the estimated velocity field can be compared directly to the “ground truth”. Specifically, due to its complex footprint (anisotropy, sparse sampling, large errors), the construction of reliable mocks of the CosmicFlows catalog is non trivial and several methods have been developed
(
Doumler et al. 2013
;
Sorce et al. 2016
;
Qin et al. 2021
;

;

,
e.g.
)
. We note that applying the method to mock catalogs drawn from simulations necessitates choosing a cosmology. In this way the accuracy or inaccuracy of the method loses its “prior free’ nature. However, the reader is reminded that the assumption of a cosmology only affects the interpretation of the results: the method itself remains cosmology-free.

The
Big MultiDark

N
-body simulation
(
Riebe et al. 2013
)
, which covers a volume of

with
N=3840^{3}

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016
,
h=0.6777
,
\Omega_{M}=0.307
;)
. Halos with more than 20 particles are considered,

a lower mass resolution of
4.7\times 10^{11}\,M_{\odot}/h
.

The cosmological box is sliced into
4\times 4\times 4=64

(625\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h)^{3}

We acknowledge that this simplistic approach implies that the observers are statistically found in a cosmological underdense region, which affects results on the radial flow as discussed in section 4.1. The box coordinates are (arbitrarily) associated with Super-galactic coordinates (SGX, SGY, SGZ).

To grasp the complexity of the CF4 dataset, we separately construct three of its sub-catalogs: SDSS-PV, 6dFGRSv and “other”. This enables modeling the anisotropic footprints of the scaling-relation surveys contributing to CF4, since the catalogue’s anisotropy is dominated by the two wide angle sky surveys (SDSS-PV and 6dFGRSv). Radial flow

The reconstruction of the radial flow of the 64 mocks in spherical volumes out to

In the bottom panels of
fig.

\Delta=V_{r}-V^{\rm sim}_{r}
namely the estimated infall velocity at a given distance minus the true one. We also examine the ratio
R=|V_{r}|/|V^{\rm sim}_{r}|
. The method underestimates the slight outflow found below
50\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
seen in the simulations. The rising of the median
\langle V_{r}\rangle
within this region is reflects the observer’s under-dense position. The underestimation of this outflow is due to the fact that the simulations (poor) resolution (in the exact region where the observations can see the faintest objects) implies a disparity between the CF4 catalog and our mocks in the innermost region (also seen in
Figure

). In the absence of constraints, the mean tends to zero while the scatter increases, yet, due to the absence of
\Lambda

Hoffman et al. 2024

On larger scales, beyond
\sim 200\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
the method slightly under-estimates the true infall velocity by about
20\,\rm km/s
, which is small with respect to the expected cosmic variance (more than
100\,\rm km/s
at
200\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
) but comparable to the uncertainty yielded by the forward modeling technique itself. More prominently, observational limitations lead to an amplification of the signal starting
150\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
, reaching a factor of two at the
200\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
. The reconstructed velocity and true velocity begin to diverge at a distance of about
160\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h

Indeed all constraints beyond this distance are found exclusively in the SDSS-PV, whose coverage is limited to small fraction of the sky. In other words, it should not surprise the reader that a spherically averaged radial infall velocity is inaccurate if only a small fraction of the sky is observed.

Bulk flow

The three cartesian components of the bulk flow (

The salient points to be gleaned from

•

each individual component of the bulk flow is well estimated by this method within

•

Although the bulk flow is slightly underestimated within

the method does an acceptable job in this region. Beyond this distance there is an artificial overestimate of the bulk flow.

In conclusion, we note (without showing this explicitly) that there is no correlation between the radial flow and the bulk flow within
160\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h

4.3
Simulation based correction

The tests on mock catalogs conducted in the preceding section have not only given us confidence in which regime we expect the method to perform well, but also a hint of how to interpret (and thus correct) the regions where the method overestimates the true infall or bulk flow. In each shell of each component of the bulk flow and separately for the radial flow, we can use the value of
R
and
\Delta
to correct the measured value and thus recover the true velocity field moments. This is done by

1.

remove the mean over- or under-estimation, namely subtract the value of
\Delta
, (the
\Delta
-correction);

2.

divide by
R
the mean amplification (
R
-correction).

As shown in
figs.

5
and
6

R-
and
\Delta-
corrections behave differently depending on if the bulk flow or the in-flow is examined: the
\Delta
corrections are well suited to the infall measurements, while the
R
correction works ideally for the bulk flow correction.

5.1
Hubble parameter and radial flow

The mean radial flow is interesting not only because it is statistically predicted from the
\Lambda
CDM model but also because it is sensitive to the expansion rate
H_{0}
. In other words, the
\Lambda
CDM model predicts zero mean radial flow, beyond
\sim 120\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
(
i.e.
see the dashed black line in
fig.

3
). The assumed value of
H_{0}
has a direct effect on the estimate of this quantity. In
fig.

7
we show the effect of different values of
H_{0}
on the estimated radial flow, for the uncorrected and simulation-corrected curves. The impact of the matter density parameter is small, as demonstrated in
appendix

A
, so we assume
\Omega_{M}=0.3
in the CF4 analysis.

All curves linearly increase or decrease with distance out to
\sim 240\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
at a pace dictated by the value of
H_{0}
. In general, the lower the
H_{0}
, the greater the value of
\langle V_{r}\rangle
. This makes intuitive sense, considering
eq.

1
: for a fixed value of the redshift, a greater
H_{0}
implies a smaller
v_{r}

Note that the drop beyond
\sim 240\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
– the strong inflow the edge of the data – can be explained by the combination of (1) the limitation in the sky of coverage in the SDSS-PV volume and (2) the overwhelming influence of the Sloan Great Wall
(
Gott et al. 2005
;
Valade et al. 2024
)
in this region, whose distance coincides with the elbow in the radial flow. In essence, all entries in the CF4 catalog beyond
250\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
fall onto this massive object. Any estimate of the velocity field here is thus just probing the Sloan Great Wall. Note that it is not just on the far side of the SGW that the velocities are significantly affected – we presume that the velocities on the near side (
i.e.
between
160\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
-
250\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
) will also be strongly affected (albeit not entirely) by this object. Hence, the gradual increase of the radial flow on the near side and the steep decrease on the far side reflect the backside infall of the SGW.

In the assumption of a homogeneous Universe, the net radial inflow is expected to be zero on the scale of a few hundred Mpc, with fluctuations due to the local cosmography, expected and well understood
(
Wojtak et al. 2014
, see)
. This relatively minimal assumption motivates choosing a value of
H_{0}
that cancels the radial flow at (or within) this distance – the method employed by
Tully et al. 2023
.

While the value of
H_{0}=74.6\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
suggested by
Tully et al. 2023
suppresses the radial flow at the edge of the data, it also results in a strong outflow for all the inner spheres, reaching about a
300\,\rm km/s
at
200\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
(
3\sigma
from
\Lambda
CDM , according to
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016
). Disregarding the values beyond
240\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
, assumed polluted by the dynamics induced by the Sloan Great Wall, our method prefers a
H_{0}=75.8\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
. Visually, radial flow profiles for values of
H_{0}=75.8\pm 0.4\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
are slightly more than
1\sigma

\Lambda
CDM variance from the null signal at
240\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
, indicating a rough confidence interval at 68% of
0.8\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
.

Conclusion

In this work, we have explored a forward modeling approach to jointly reconstruct the two first modes of the velocity field from measurements of peculiar velocities: the radial inflow and the bulk flow. As it directly models the non-gaussian nature of the observational errors, this methodology avoids the recourse to velocity estimators
(
Watkins & Feldman 2015
;
Hoffman et al. 2021
,
e.g.
)
prior to the analysis
(
Whitford et al. 2023
;
Hoffman et al. 2024
,
e.g.
)
. The forward model is independently fitted on the data split in concentric shells before being reassembled in spherical volumes, weighing each shell by its volume. This enables us to reconstruct the radial and bulk flow as a function of the distance. We acknowledge that this approach bears resemblances with the reweighting of constraints found in the literature
(
Qin et al. 2021
,
e.g.
)
.

Compared to approaches such as the Wiener Filter
(
Hoffman et al. 2024
)
, or fully Bayesian field-level forward modeling
(
Graziani et al. 2019
;
Valade et al. 2022
;
Courtois et al. 2023
)
, our method does not assume the
\Lambda
CDM predicted velocity-velocity correlation function. It is furthermore not limited by the loss of large-scale power due to the setting of a box size, inherent to algorithms relying on the Fast Fourier Transform, such as Constrained Realizations
(
Hoffman & Ribak 1992
;
Hoffman et al. 2024
)
or fully Bayesian field-level forward modeling
(
Valade et al. 2022
;
Courtois et al. 2023
)
. These two effects may lead to the artificial dampening of tensions between the data and the underlying field model. In contrast, our method aims at relying minimally on
\Lambda
CDM , which only appears in the converting of cosmological redshifts to physical distances. This relationship depends on two cosmological parameters,
H_{0}
and
\Omega_{M}
, whose influence on our results are discussed.

Our methodology resembles that of
(
Boubel et al. 2024
)
, which is limited to the CF4 Tully Fisher sample of
10\,000
entries. The observational model presented in
Boubel et al. 2024
;
Boubel et al. 2025
is more accurate than the one developed here as it is targeted to the Tully-Fisher relationship
(
Tully & Fisher 1977
)
, but their method reports only a single value for the entire catalog, while our method allows us to compute the full radial inflow and bulk flow at all distances. Their reported value for
H_{0}=73.3\pm 2.1({\rm stat})\pm 3.5({\rm sys})\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
is consistent with ours.

The application of our method to a series of 64 realistic mock observations of the Cosmicflows-4 catalog yield two results. First, the method is reliable out to
120\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
, distance beyond which observational limits lead to an amplification of the underlying signal. Secondly, a simple simulation-based correction can be derived so as to enable us to exploit – with a grain of salt – radial and bulk flow values beyond
120\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
out to the edge of the data at
300\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
.

Following tests on the mock observations, we use our method to reconstruct the radial flow as well as the bulk flow from the CF4 dataset. Study of the radial flow shows that the Hubble value suggested by
(
Tully et al. 2023
,
H_{0}=74.6\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
;)
results in a steadily increasing outflow, reaching a
2\sigma
tension with
\Lambda
CDM at
240\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
. Beyond this distance, the radial flow appears to be dominated by the Sloan Great Wall, as predicted by
Valade et al. 2024
. In order to cancel any radial flow out to
240\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
, a value of
H_{0}=75.8\pm 0.4\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
has to be chosen. We acknowledge that the evaluation of systematic error on this value are not discussed and may be significant. In obvious tension with the CMB derived value of
Planck Collaboration et al. 2020
,
H_{0}=67.4\pm 0.5\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
, our result of
H_{0}
is on the upper end of values in the Local Universe
(
Brout et al. 2022
;
Riess et al. 2022
;
Boubel et al. 2024
,
H_{0}\approx 73.3\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
;)
. Nevertheless, our chosen value is very close to the latest survey using the fundamental plane distance estimator
(
Said et al. 2025
)
. Our result is only mildly sensitive to the choice of
\Omega_{M}
.

In summary, our findings display significant tension with
\Lambda
CDM at
120\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
on the
-
X direction (
>4\sigma
) which reports on the magnitude (
>3\sigma
). This bump in the bulk flow has been consistently described in the literature, with various amplitudes. Beyond this distance, we report no remarkable behavior as the tension with
\Lambda
CDM slowly decreases with distance.

We highlight that the bulk flow reconstructed by our algorithm is mildly sensitive to the value of
H_{0}
. Considering, for instance, Planck’s
H_{0}=67.77\,\rm km/s/\!\,{\rm Mpc}
value slightly decreases the magnitude of the bulk flow but simultaneously induces extreme outflows—on the order of thousands of km/s—at the edge of the data. This only marginally mitigates the tension mentioned above while creating a major – unphysical – radial outflow of matter.

It has to be noted that these values are based on a correction that assumes a
\Lambda
CDM cosmology. In other words, we are forcing, in so far as possible, the interpretation of the inflow and bulk flow to be as consistent as the data allow, with statistical predictions from the
\Lambda
CDM theory of stricture formation. Given that the observation still disagree to some extent (
\sim 3\sigma
outlier at
\sim 120\,\,{\rm Mpc}/h
), we are forced to conclude that (barring any unseen systematic in the data or our method), the local universe’s bulk flow is in tension with the
\Lambda
CDM . The radial flow on the other hand indicates a high value of
H_{0}
is necessary to be consistent with
\Lambda
CDM , in agreement with countless other local universe measurement of this quantity
(
Andersen et al. 2016
)
.

In a next work, we suggest to improve the model so as to include possible systematics in the observational data, notably issues of inter-calibration which have been proven to lead to significant spurious flows
(
Whitford et al. 2023
, fig. 9)
. We also look forward to more peculiar velocity data, specifically covering the heavens more spherically.
