A folate receptor–targeted NIR-II probe improves intraoperative imaging and surgery of metastatic ovarian cancer
Authors: Wanying Li, Zimu Yuan, Shiqi Bai, Yuewei Zhang, Tianyang Han, Yuhuan Li, Xiaosen Li, Shoujun Zhu, Qi Zhou, Songling Zhang, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intraoperative fluorescence imaging holds great promise for achieving complete cytoreduction in ovarian cancer, but its clinical utility is currently constrained by the limitations of existing imaging agents. In particular, the leading clinical probe, OTL38, operates in the near-infrared I (NIR-I; 700 to 1000 nanometers) window, which suffers from limited tissue penetration and suboptimal resolution. Here, a folate receptor α (FRα)–targeted NIR-II (1000 to 3000 nanometers) imaging probe was developed by incorporating a 4-(4-iodophenyl)butanoic acid moiety. This structural modification enables albumin binding, thereby prolonging systemic circulation and enhancing accumulation in FRα-positive tumors. In murine models of peritoneal metastasis, our probe enabled precise visualization of occult lesions undetectable by the naked eye. Compared to OTL38, this probe demonstrated superior tumor detection sensitivity (90.20% versus 78.75%) and allowed for the reliable identification of elusive lesions less than or equal to 3 millimeters in diameter. These findings highlight the potential of albumin-binding NIR-II imaging strategies to overcome the limitations of current clinical probes and enhance precision fluorescence–guided surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer ranks among the top three malignant tumors of the female reproductive system in both incidence and mortality, with over 300,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths annually (1–3). Moreover, the projected steady rise in ovarian cancer incidence over the next few decades poses a growing threat to women’s health and survival (4, 5). Because of the absence of typical symptoms during the early stages, over 70% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages. Surgical resection remains the foundation of primary ovarian cancer treatment, paving the way for subsequent chemotherapy and maintenance treatment (6). In ovarian cancer, the paramount surgical goal is to achieve complete cytoreduction (R0 resection), defined as the absence of visible residual disease (7–9). This procedure requires the removal of all grossly detectable tumors and is widely regarded as the most critical prognostic factor (10, 11). Achieving this optimal surgical outcome is crucial for the initial therapy of advanced disease, given that the degree of cytoreduction strongly correlates with patient prognosis (12). However, achieving R0 resection remains a major surgical challenge because surgeons often struggle to identify microscopic occult lesions in the setting of extensive intra-abdominal dissemination and diffuse implantation. This frequently results in macroscopic residual disease in 30 to 40% of cases (13, 14). Therefore, precise intraoperative tumor localization and real-time tracking represent a pressing clinical need that is pivotal for accomplishing R0 resection.

Near-infrared (NIR; 700 to 3000 nm) fluorescence imaging holds great promise for addressing the aforementioned challenges (15–18). This technique uses NIR fluorescent probes to specifically label tumor regions, while using highly sensitive optical detection instruments (such as charge-coupled device and InGaAs cameras) to capture the fluorescence signals, thereby enabling real-time intraoperative visualization of tumor size, location, and boundaries (16, 17, 19–24). Compared to other clinical imaging modalities such as ultrasound, x-ray radiography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging, NIR fluorescence imaging is noncontact and nondisruptive to the surgical procedure while offering high sensitivity and specificity, real-time signal acquisition, and the elimination of radiation exposure (15, 24–28). Recent reports have demonstrated that NIR fluorescence imaging has been successfully applied to the in vivo tracking and monitoring of various pathological lesions (21, 29–31). Consequently, NIR fluorescence imaging for tumor visualization offers a substantial advantage in facilitating a more complete cytoreduction of ovarian cancer.

Current perspectives suggest that NIR-II fluorescence imaging, due to reduced photon scattering and autofluorescence, offers greater tissue penetration depth, lower background, and higher resolution compared to NIR-I fluorescence imaging, making it more suitable for noninvasive in vivo tracking (15, 24, 32–37). For instance, NIR-IIb (1500 to 1700 nm) fluorescence imaging achieves high-quality imaging results, enabling even single-cell identification within a subcentimeter tissue thickness range (38). However, many inorganic probes used for NIR-II fluorescence imaging, including carbon nanotubes, quantum dots, and rare-earth nanocrystals, often contain toxic elements such as Pb and Cr, thereby limiting their clinical translation (20, 21, 39–41). In contrast, organic small molecules exhibit excretable metabolic behavior and have lower biotoxicity (42–44). Concurrently, some small-molecule probes, such as indocyanine green (ICG), methylene blue, IRDye 800CW conjugates, and OTL38, have already been approved for clinical use or clinical trials (45–48). It is particularly noteworthy that OTL38 has achieved targeted imaging of ovarian cancer and identified occult lesions (≤3 mm) missed by white-light inspection, thereby optimizing the outcomes of cytoreductive procedures in folate receptor α (FRα)–positive patients (49, 50). Recent studies also indicate that although the luminescence of these small molecules is primarily concentrated in the NIR-I region, the clinical probe ICG exhibits a long-wavelength emission tail in the NIR-II range (1000 to 1300 nm) (51). NIR-II fluorescence imaging using this emission has demonstrated superior tumor detection sensitivity (100% versus 90.6%) and signal-to-noise ratio (5.33 versus 1.45) compared to NIR-I fluorescence imaging (52). These findings demonstrate that rational small-molecule design is a viable strategy for achieving NIR-II fluorescence–guided surgery.

In this study, a folate-targeted, albumin-binding cyanine, FA-808-IP, was developed for NIR-II fluorescence imaging and surgery of metastatic ovarian cancer. By incorporating a 4-(4-iodophenyl)butanoic acid (IP) moiety for extended circulation and a folic acid (FA) headpiece to target FRα (overexpressed in 90 to 95% of epithelial ovarian cancers) (53, 54), this molecular design promotes extensive tumor accumulation to offset weak tail emissions. Compared to the clinical probe OTL38, FA-808-IP exhibited superior sensitivity during fluorescence-guided surgery, reliably delineating elusive sub–3-mm occult lesions missed by gross inspection. Collectively, this cooperative chemical synergy provides a NIR-II paradigm to elevate ovarian cancer cytoreduction from traditional macroscopic clearance toward a rigorous microscopic R0 standard.

RESULTS

Design and characterization of IR-808 derivatives

To equip the probes with tumor-targeting capability for NIR-II imaging, we designed the IR-808 derivatives with different terminal groups (Fig. 1A and fig. S1). FA-808 was synthesized by introducing FA at the meso-Cl position, and FA-808-IP was further prepared on the basis of FA-808 by conjugating an IP derivative to the carboxyl group. Moreover, the incorporation of a polyethylene glycol chain improved the water solubility of these molecules for in vivo research. The absorption spectra of the synthesized IR-808–derived molecules both fall within the NIR-I range of 600 to 900 nm, with only slight differences in their peak shapes. The absorption peaks of both dyes are around 800 nm, overlapping with the commonly used 808-nm excitation laser (Fig. 1B). In addition, we investigated the absorption behaviors of these two molecules in different solvents and found that their absorption peaks were strongest in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The absorption peak of FA-808 exhibited a noticeable blue shift in bovine serum albumin (BSA) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), while FA-808-IP showed a slight blue shift in PBS (fig. S2). The almost identical trailing NIR-II fluorescence of these dyes was recorded via emission spectroscopy under 808-nm NIR excitation (Fig. 1B). Moreover, NIR-II fluorescence imaging of the dyes in different buffers was performed and found that the fluorescence intensity of both molecules decreased sequentially in DMSO, serum, BSA, and PBS. Despite the variation in depletion, the NIR-II brightness of the IR-808 derivatives remains adequate for in vivo visualization (Fig. 1, C and D).

We further used the clinical fluorescent probe OTL38 as a control to evaluate the optical properties of the synthesized molecules. The NIR-II fluorescence (1000 to 1700 nm) quantum yields in DMSO and PBS were determined to be 9.3 and 2.4% for FA-808, 7.7 and 2.0% for FA-808-IP, and 10.4 and 1.5% for OTL38, respectively, using IR-26 as the reference (figs. S3 to S5 and table S1). Photostability tests of FA-808, FA-808-IP, and OTL38 under continuous 808-nm laser irradiation for 1 hour showed an apparent signal decline, highlighting the need to avoid prolonged irradiation during surgical procedures (fig. S6). In parallel, the NIR-II imaging capability of the IR-808 derivatives enabled penetration through a 7-mm thickness of 1% intralipid phantom (fig. S7).

To establish a rigorous foundation for biological evaluation, we systematically characterized the chemical structure, purity, and physiological stability of FA-808 and FA-808-IP. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry confirmed the expected molecular weight distribution (fig. S8), while high-performance liquid chromatography chromatograms verified a high chemical purity exceeding 98% (fig. S9). Furthermore, stability assays were conducted by incubating the dyes in both PBS and serum over a prolonged period. FA-808-IP demonstrated excellent storage stability (fig. S10), thereby confirming its robustness and suitability for subsequent in vivo imaging and fluorescence-guided surgery.

For normal ovarian cells IOSE-80 and ovarian cancer cells OVCAR3, no apparent cytotoxicity of FA-808 and FA-808-IP was observed at incubated concentrations up to 30 μΜ (fig. S11). Building upon this cell-level safety, multidimensional in vivo assessments encompassing hematological analysis, serum biochemistry, histopathology, and excretion dynamics were further conducted on BALB/c mice. Acute toxicity was evaluated 24 hours postinjection of FA-808 and FA-808-IP at graduated dosages (0 to 400 μΜ, 200 μl). Hematological profiles, serum biochemistry (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine), and reproductive indicators revealed no statistically significant deviations from the control group (figs. S12 and S13), confirming the absence of acute hepatic, renal, or reproductive dysfunction. Long-term systemic safety was verified 7 days postinjection of both FA-808 and FA-808-IP at same dosages (200 μΜ, 200 μl), with all blood parameters and organ function indices remaining within normal physiological ranges (fig. S14). Histopathologically evaluation was performed via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of major organs. Normal morphological structures exhibiting no inflammation, necrosis, or structural damage were observed in mice 24 hours postinjection of FA-808 (100 to 400 μΜ) and 7 days postinjection of both FA-808 and FA-808-IP (figs. S15 to S17), particularly within the liver, kidneys, and crucial reproductive and other abdominal and pelvic visceral organs. Furthermore, the excretion profiles of both probes were mapped via urine and feces collection over a 168-hour period. A favorable metabolic profile was demonstrated by the elimination kinetics, ensuring effective clearance from the reticuloendothelial system without accumulation-related toxicity (fig. S18). Together, these multifaceted in vitro and in vivo results confirm that both FA-808 and FA-808-IP are well tolerated and have an excellent biosafety profile for potential clinical translation.

The synthesized probes, FA-808 and FA-808-IP, along with the clinical OTL38 and structural control IR-808, were each administered via tail vein injection at a consistent dose of 200 μM. NIR-II whole-body imaging was then performed to track the pharmacokinetics of these dyes in vivo (Fig. 1E and fig. S19A). Quantitative analysis of the liver, kidney, and skin fluorescence intensities, along with their respective organ-to-skin ratios at specific time points, revealed marked differences in the pharmacokinetic profiles of the four dyes (Fig. 1F and fig. S19, B to E). Consistent with its clinical profile, OTL38 was primarily eliminated through the renal pathway and exhibited a relatively low liver-to-skin signal ratio. IR-808 exhibited an obvious accumulation in the skin, resulting in consistently low liver-to-skin and kidney-to-skin signal ratios over 72 hours, which is attributed to the covalent albumin binding of its meso-Cl group. In contrast, FA-808 and FA-808-IP exhibited lower absorption in the skin and a higher liver-to-skin signal ratio (Fig. 1F), as the introduction of FA at the meso-Cl site prevents covalent protein-dye binding. Similar to OTL38, FA-808 was found to be primarily eliminated renally, exhibiting a notably high renal-to-skin signal ratio (fig. S19E). Notably, FA-808-IP demonstrated a metabolic shift toward hepatic elimination and had a substantially prolonged in vivo circulation time compared to both FA-808 and OTL38 (Fig. 1E and fig. S19B). This enhanced systemic retention is attributed to the modified IP moiety, which regulates the imaging time window through noncovalent binding interactions. This is also supported by the imaging of serum samples collected at specific time points in 24 hours (Fig. 1, G and H).

Assessment of binding affinity and specificity

To test the binding affinity and specificity of the synthesized probes, we microarrayed BSA and FRα spots on the a plasmonic fluorescence–enhancing gold platform to capture the high-specificity probes (Fig. 2A). This platform represents a highly sensitive and reliable strategy for validating probe–target protein recognition, as extensively documented in previous studies to characterize the targeting performance of NIR fluorescent probes (55, 56). Using the high-throughput capability of this platform, five dyes—ICG, IR-808, OTL38, FA-808, and FA-808-IP—were incubated on the protein-microarrayed chips. After washing, bright fluorescent spots were observed, demonstrating high target specificity for FRα over BSA. The imaging results indicated that OTL38, FA-808, and FA-808-IP could selectively light up the FRα spots, whereas no signal was observed at the BSA sites. Notably, the fluorescence intensity of FA-808 and FA-808-IP after binding to FRα was significantly higher than that of OTL38(Fig. 2B). In this assay, ICG yielded negligible signals, whereas IR-808 exhibited intense positive signals, indicating that IR-808 has enhanced protein-binding capability due to the presence of the meso-Cl group substituent. While ICG is known to interact noncovalently with albumin in solution, its weak binding affinity (KD = 8.10 × 105 nM, as confirmed by biolayer interferometry) makes it susceptible to dissociation during the washing steps of the plasmonic gold platform. In contrast, the meso-Cl group of IR-808 facilitates a robust, covalent-like interaction (KD = 9.50 × 104 nM), allowing the probe to remain bound and generate a high-contrast signal (57). Collectively, quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and positive-to-negative (P/N) ratios demonstrated the superior binding affinity and specificity of the FA-targeted probes.

Subsequently, FRα was diluted with PBS and incubated with three targeting probes (OTL38, FA-808, and FA-808-IP) before being subjected to gel electrophoresis. The results revealed that all three probes exhibited a distinct fluorescent band corresponding to a molecular mass of ∼37 kDa (Fig. 2C), which indicated binding between the FA probes and the target protein. In addition, we conducted molecular docking and applied the results as the initial state for subsequent kinetic simulation to explore the binding differences in FA probes. The outcome of docking showed that FA-808 and FA-808-IP share similar protein-binding sites (Fig. 2D), which are distinct from those of OTL38 (fig. S20). The binding energy and docking score followed the order of FA-808-IP, FA-808, and then OTL38. The comparable values for FA-808-IP and FA-808 indicated a similar binding affinity between two probes (Fig. 2E and table S2). These findings provided preliminary evidence supporting the efficient in vitro binding of the target fluorescent probes to FRα for tumor cell labeling.

The structural modification between FA-808 and FA-808-IP lies specifically in the integrated IP moiety, which was designed to regulate the imaging time window via enhanced albumin binding. Consequently, molecular interactions between these two probes and various serum albumins (BSA, HSA, and MSA) were systematically investigated. Real-time binding kinetics were quantified via biolayer interferometry. As summarized in fig. S21 and table S3, compared to FA-808, FA-808-IP exhibits substantially lower KD values and larger association rate constants (kon) across all tested albumins. This demonstrates that incorporating the IP moiety substantially enhances binding affinity and velocity. The binding mode was further investigated via a native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) assay to evaluate noncovalent complex formation. Following incubation with the respective albumins, distinct NIR fluorescence signals were observed in the FA-808-IP groups, whereas no signal was detected in the FA-808 groups (fig. S22A), confirming the formation of stable noncovalent complexes. The specificity of this binding mediated by the IP moiety was further verified through a competitive displacement assay. Upon the introduction of free IP molecules under albumin-blocking conditions, the NIR fluorescence signal of the protein bands was effectively abolished (fig. S22B). Collectively, the structural design is validated by these biophysical and biochemical characterizations, confirming that rapid, high-affinity, and noncovalent binding to serum albumin is efficiently driven by the integrated IP moiety.

NIR-II imaging of subcutaneous tumor mice and fluorescence-guided tumor resection

To study the targeting ability in vivo, we established subcutaneous OVCAR3 tumor–bearing mice and administered five fluorescent dyes for subcutaneous tumor visualization and fluorescence-guided resection (Fig. 3A). The NIR-II imaging results revealed that FA-808, FA-808-IP, OTL38, and IR-808 enable clear visualization of the labeled tumors, whereas ICG failed to selectively trace the tumor tissue (Fig. 3, B to D). However, consistent with previous in vivo imaging results, IR-808 is not optimal for ovarian cancer imaging due to strong tissue absorption. Guided by the targeting moiety, FA-808 began to accumulate in the tumor within 15 min postinjection, with its fluorescence intensity peaking at 30 min before gradually declining. The maximum tumor–to–normal tissue (T/NT) fluorescence intensity ratio of 2.9 was achieved at 4 hours (Fig. 3D). Notably, FA-808 showed rapid tumor accumulation, which may be attributed to its fast in vivo metabolism. In contrast, FA-808-IP began to accumulate in the tumor at 30 min postinjection, reaching both peak fluorescence intensity and maximum T/NT fluorescence intensity ratio (2.8) between at 8 and 12 hours (Fig. 3D). These findings support our design strategy of incorporating the IP moiety to prolong the blood circulation time of the targeted dye, thereby enhancing tumor targeting. To ensure a rigorous comparison of this targeting efficiency, we injected FA-808 and FA-808-IP at an identical molar dose (200 μM, 200 μl) across all tumor imaging tests. Age-matched mice with synchronized tumor growth weighed 18 ± 0.5 g at the time of imaging, yielding a uniform normalized dose of ∼2.22 μmol/kg. This consistency restricted interanimal variation to less than 3%, successfully eliminating systemic dosing bias. For further validation, weight-adjusted doses (FA-808, 7 mg/kg; FA-808-IP, 8 mg/kg) were tested separately (figs. S23 and S24). The resulting tumor accumulation profiles and T/NT fluorescence intensity ratios were virtually identical to the original fixed–molar dose data, confirming that the superior performance of FA-808-IP genuinely originates from optimized molecular design rather than variations in systemic delivery.

Moreover, the clinic probe OTL38 exhibited minimal tumor accumulation within the first 12 hours. Its maximum T/NT fluorescence intensity ratio (1.7) was observed between 24 and 48 hours, which was markedly lower than that of both FA-808 and FA-808-IP (Fig. 3D). In addition, considering the NIR-II tail emission capability of OTL38, we compared its NIR-I versus NIR-II imaging performance in a subcutaneous tumor model. In these OVCAR3 xenografts, both modalities delineated the tumors 24 hours postinjection (fig. S25, A and B). However, NIR-II acquisition yielded consistently higher tumor fluorescence intensity (fig. S25C) and a significantly elevated T/NT signal ratio within the same mouse compared to its NIR-I counterpart (fig. S25D), validating the in vivo superiority of NIR-II tailing detection.

To further evaluate the tumor imaging capability of the targeted probes, we performed subcutaneous tumor resection at the optimal time points postinjection for each probe. Guided by the clear fluorescent margins, the tumors were completely excised under NIR-II imaging (Fig. 3E). When imaged at their respective optimal time points, FA-808-IP exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity in tumors than both FA-808 and OTL38 (Fig. 3F). Meanwhile, the T/NT fluorescence intensity ratios of FA-808 and FA-808-IP were comparable, and both were significantly higher than that of OTL38 (Fig. 3G). Among the three probes, FA-808-IP exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity and tumor-to-background ratio in excised tumors, followed by FA-808, while OTL38 showed the weakest (Fig. 3H and fig. S26).

The superior fluorescence and contrast of FA-808-IP stem from the modified IP moiety, which optimizes metabolic retention and tumor accumulation via albumin-binding interactions. Confocal imaging confirmed this mechanism at the cellular level, showing a substantially higher colocalization of endogenous albumin and NIR-II signals in FA-808-IP–treated tumors compared to FA-808 (fig. S27). This enrichment provides a higher local concentration of the probe within the tumor microenvironment, which further promotes folate receptor–mediated endocytosis and enhances intracellular signal intensity. Building upon this enhanced intracellular retention and IP-mediated albumin binding, the probe ensures exceptional lesion identification and an adequate surgical time window for optimal intraoperative navigation. In addition, major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were also harvested for ex vivo NIR-II imaging. The ex vivo imaging revealed that FA-808-IP accumulated substantially in the liver, while FA-808 and OTL38 showed high renal enrichment (fig. S28).

After resection, we prepared thin sections from the FA-808-IP–labeled tumor for H&E staining and concurrent NIR-II fluorescence imaging of identical regions along the T/NT interface (Fig. 3I). While minor nonspecific adsorption was observed in certain peritumoral tissues, the imaging contrast at the tumor boundaries remained highly distinct. Within the tumor mass, the uneven fluorescence distribution reflects the intrinsic heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment, where the spatial variation in signal intensity corresponds to the nonuniform distribution of molecular targets. Despite these variations, the overlay of H&E and NIR-II images revealed clear colocalization of FA-808-IP signals with histologically confirmed tumor areas, contrasting sharply with the near absence of signal in normal tissue regions. Furthermore, FRα expression of ovarian cancer tissues was examined by H&E and immunohistochemistry (IHC). The results in Fig. 3J demonstrated negative FRα expression in normal muscle tissues, whereas high expression was observed in ovarian carcinoma tissue. Collectively, our results demonstrate that FA-808-IP enables tissue-specific recognition by using the different FRα expression as its underlying mechanism.

To further validate the in vivo active targeting mechanism of FA-808 and FA-808-IP, we conducted a competitive inhibition (blocking) experiment. Free FA was preinjected into tumor-bearing mice to saturate the surface FRα before probe administration. NIR-II imaging revealed that the fluorescence signals in the tumors of the preblocked group were markedly attenuated compared to those in the nonblocked group (figs. S29 and S30). This substantial reduction in tumor uptake upon receptor saturation directly confirms that the targeting capability of both FA-808 and FA-808-IP is mediated by specific FRα binding rather than nonspecific accumulation. Furthermore, validation of targeting specificity was expanded through an in vivo comparative investigation of the FRα-negative A549 lung cancer xenograft model versus the high FRα–expressing OVCAR3 model. FRα-positive OVCAR3 tumors were characterized by intense, sustained fluorescence stemming from receptor-mediated internalization, whereas the FRα-negative A549 cohort exhibited a marked signal reduction to near-background levels of adjacent normal tissues (figs. S31 and S32). Through this distinct signal differentiation, tumor enrichment of FA-808-IP was confirmed to be driven mainly by FRα-mediated specific binding, rather than albumin binding or other nonspecific retention. Consequently, the FRα-targeted probes demonstrate exceptional targeting specificity toward ovarian tumor tissues, permitting the precise delineation of tumor boundaries.

NIR-II fluorescence–guided surgery for ovarian cancer

The synthetic NIR-II fluorophore FA-808-IP was applied for NIR-II fluorescence–guided surgery in a mouse model of peritoneal metastatic ovarian cancer due to its unique targeting specificity. A mouse model of peritoneal metastasis was established through intraperitoneal injection of the OVCAR3 cell line. The mice then received an intravenous injection of 200 μM FA-808-IP via the tail vein, followed by NIR-II imaging (Fig. 4A). Data from subcutaneous tumor imaging indicated that the accumulation of FA-808-IP peaked between 8 and 12 hours postinjection, defining this period as the optimal surgical window for peritoneal metastasis resection. We performed laparotomy to remove the metastatic tumors. Under NIR-II fluorescence guidance, widely metastatic lesions of varying sizes were resected from multiple anatomical sites, including the diaphragm, upper abdomen, intestinal and mesenteric surfaces, and pelvic floor (Fig. 4, B and C, and fig. S33). Notably, in mice undergoing FA-808-IP–guided surgery, punctate fluorescent areas were observed not only in macroscopically visible lesions but also in macroscopically occult lesions (Fig. 4, D and E), demonstrating the high accuracy of this probe in tumor identification. Pathological examination confirmed that these fluorescently identified regions were carcinoma foci that were invisible to the naked eye (Fig. 4F). Following surgical resection of abdominal lesions, the mean NIR-II fluorescence intensity within the region of interest decreased significantly (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4G). Moreover, the tumor–to–peritumoral fluorescence ratio (TPR), which quantifies the intensity gradient between tumor and peritumoral tissues in the metastatic ovarian cancer mouse model, was markedly reduced compared to preresection levels (P = 0.0001) (Fig. 4H). These findings demonstrate that FA-808-IP exhibits strong and precise tumor recognition capability and targeting specificity, enabling the resection of macroscopically undetectable lesions and thereby reducing surgical tumor residue.

For comparison, we performed similar fluorescence-guided surgery with OTL38 under the clinical NIR-I condition (850 to 1000 nm with 755-nm excitation) and evaluated it against two alternative settings: 850 to 1000 nm and above 1100 nm, both under 808-nm excitation. Tissue phantom assays demonstrated that the above 1100-nm window provided the sharpest signal boundaries because its narrower full width at half maximum minimized tissue scattering (fig. S34). During real-time navigation, both NIR-I cohorts showed comparable TPRs without statistical variance, whereas the above 1100-nm window yielded a significantly higher TPR for lesions (fig. S35). Diagnostic receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis revealed that the area under the ROC curve (AUC) values for the 755-nm NIR-I, 808-nm NIR-I, and above 1100-nm windows were 0.8403 [95% confidence interval (CI), 0.7443 to 0.9159], 0.8341 (95% CI, 0.7469 to 0.9181), and 0.8405 (95% CI, 0.7731 to 0.9296), respectively (fig. S36 and table S4). Pairwise comparisons of these AUC values showed no statistically significant differences among the three optical configurations (P > 0.05), indicating comparable macrolevel diagnostic efficacy across all three optical configurations. To evaluate intraoperative classification consistency, we performed kappa coefficient (κ) analysis. Pairwise κ evaluation demonstrated high diagnostic alignment among the optical cohorts (table S5). Notably, the above 1100-nm window achieved the highest concordance with the histological gold standard (κ = 0.598), outperforming both the 755- and 808-nm NIR-I settings (table S6). Benchmarking against this histological baseline confirmed that the above 1100-nm window minimized both false-positive and false-negative misclassifications, facilitating the detection of critical microlesions obscured by tissue scattering under clinical NIR-I conditions.

These results confirmed that introducing the standard clinical NIR-I conditions brought no imaging enhancement for OTL38 in this metastasis model, while the above 1100-nm window maintained the highest diagnostic accuracy. Consequently, the above 1100-nm window was prioritized for OTL38 surgical evaluation. This choice was justified by its enhanced signal sharpness, elevated TPR, superior diagnostic efficacy, and its capacity to facilitate a rigorous double-blind evaluation with FA-808-IP under the same 808-nm excitation. However, even within this optimal window, the detectable fluorescence signals from the abdominal lesions remained relatively weak, yielding a suboptimal TPR compared to those achieved with FA-808-IP (Fig. 4H and fig. S35C). Furthermore, all resection lesions were macroscopically visible, and no fluorescence-identified occult lesions were detected in any of the OTL38-treated mice.

To further demonstrate the superior detection capability of FA-808-IP and OTL38, we evaluated their imaging performance on mesenteric micrometastases, representing a clinically challenging region for tumor clearance (figs. S37 to S39). In this specific setting, FA-808-IP demonstrated a distinctly high TPR, enabling the facile and accurate detection of microscopic foci. Conversely, the low TPR of the clinical control, OTL38, rendered these minute lesions highly susceptible to being missed during surgical inspection. In addition, to investigate the imaging performance on deep-seated tumors, we conducted a tissue-simulating phantom experiment by covering resected tumor tissues with varying thicknesses of 1% intralipid. FA-808-IP exhibited markedly stronger penetration capabilities than OTL38, preserving fluorescent signals at depths where the OTL38 signal was severely degraded (fig. S40). Collectively, these results highlight the clinical potential of FA-808-IP to detect both microscopic and deeply embedded lesions.

Validation of clinical applicability was further extended through an ex vivo staining assessment using clinical samples of patient-derived peritoneal metastasis, which was conducted under institutional ethical approval. FA-808-IP consistently yielded intense and selective NIR-II fluorescence signals within the malignant nodules, whereas surrounding benign tissues remained largely nonfluorescent (fig. S41). This successful ex vivo tumor labeling directly confirms the high diagnostic accuracy of FA-808-IP in clinical specimens, thereby substantially strengthening its translational potential beyond the constraints of murine models.

Comparison of tumor identification and precise resection with FA-808-IP and OTL38

Tumors were resected under fluorescence guidance with FA-808-IP or OTL38, and supplementary biopsies were collected from representative abdominal sites. To ensure an unbiased assessment, we implemented a strict double-blind protocol across both surgical and pathological procedures. These tissues were divided into three groups based on diameter (>6 mm, 3 to 6 mm, and ≤3 mm) (Fig. 5A). Ex vivo imaging of these tissues demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity of resected lesions was significantly higher than that of supplementary biopsy samples (Fig. 5B and figs. S42 and S43). This substantial signal disparity is attributed to the exceptional target specificity of FA probes toward FRα-overexpressing tumor lesions in the peritoneal metastasis model. All resected lesions and supplementary biopsy samples were examined by H&E staining, which served as the gold standard for histopathological confirmation (Fig. 5C). In the FA-808-IP group, a total of 156 lesions from eight mice were resected under fluorescent surgical navigation. Subsequent H&E staining identified 138 as tumor-positive (malignant) and 18 as tumor-negative. Moreover, 113 lesions were obtained via supplementary biopsies, of which 15 were positive and 98 were negative in H&E examination. In the OTL38 group, 145 lesions were resected under fluorescence guidance. Histopathological examination confirmed 126 as tumor-positive and 19 as tumor-negative. In addition, there were 119 supplementary biopsied lesions, of which 34 were identified as positive and 85 as negative (Table 1).

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, Youden’s index, accuracy, and AUC along with their 95% CIs were calculated using cluster bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations, clustered at the mouse level) incorporating the bias-corrected and accelerated method to mitigate pseudoreplication bias caused by correlated lesions within individual mice.

To ensure the statistical validity of our diagnostic performance analysis and to account for the clustering effect of multiple lesions within the same mouse, we performed cluster bootstrap analyses (10,000 iterations, clustered by mouse) incorporating bias-corrected and accelerated correction to mitigate pseudoreplication bias and establish 95% CIs. Within this framework, ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of FA-808-IP compared to OTL38 (Fig. 5D and Table 1). As this was an exploratory preclinical study, the fluorescence threshold was optimized via post hoc analysis to maximize diagnostic sensitivity and specificity. The optimal cutoff values were determined by maximizing the Youden index, resulting in thresholds of 525 and 325 arbitrary units (a.u.) for FA-808-IP and OTL38, respectively. These values were derived under standardized imaging conditions (808-nm excitation at 65 mW/cm2, with 1000- and 1100-nm long-pass emission filters) to ensure consistency.

With these established thresholds applied, cluster-adjusted diagnostic performance analysis revealed that FA-808-IP demonstrated statistically superior diagnostic performance compared to OTL38 (Table 1). The sensitivity for FA-808-IP was 90.20% (95% CI, 86.67 to 93.59%) significantly higher than the 78.75% (95% CI, 71.88 to 83.93%) observed for OTL38 (P = 0.003). In addition, FA-808-IP achieved an accuracy of 87.73% (95% CI, 84.17 to 90.96%) and an AUC of 0.9492 (95% CI, 0.9368 to 0.9599), both of which were markedly greater than the corresponding metrics for OTL38 (P = 0.017 and P = 0.002, respectively). To corroborate these primary outcomes and ensure that the post hoc thresholds were free from overfitting, we further evaluated these diagnostic metrics in an independent validation cohort (table S7). Consistently, FA-808-IP maintained its superior performance across both cohorts, reinforcing the robustness and reliability of the established results.

All tumor lesions were further analyzed according to their maximum diameters, with cluster bootstrap correction subsequently applied to the statistical evaluation of diagnostic performance (table S8). In the group with a tumor diameter of >6 mm, both FA-808-IP and OTL38 detected all positive lesions, resulting in 100% sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV). For lesions measuring 3 to 6 mm, FA-808-IP maintained superior diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 97.44% (95% CI, 95.00 to 100.0%) and a specificity of 89.19% (95% CI, 80.00 to 97.56%), effectively outperforming OTL38 in the detection of mid-sized peritoneal implants. Last, lesions ≤3 mm in diameter represent a clinical challenge due to their limited visibility, but FA-808-IP demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy in this range. Fluorescence-guided surgery with FA-808-IP enabled the identification of these occult deposits with a sensitivity of 86.27% (95% CI, 77.78 to 92.54%) and a specificity of 76.19% (95% CI, 61.54 to 83.33%). In comparison, the OTL38 group exhibited a sensitivity of 61.40% (95% CI, 52.38 to 68.75%) and a specificity of 84.75% (95% CI, 78.12 to 91.67%) for these small lesions. These results demonstrate the superiority of FA-808-IP in identifying occult disease, ultimately facilitating more precise and complete tumor resection.

The calibration plots for both FA-808-IP and OTL38 detection models were generated using cluster-adjusted predictions to assess predictive accuracy and reliability (Fig. 5, E and F). In the training cohort (Fig. 5E), both models exhibited moderate deviations from the ideal reference line. FA-808-IP showed a minor overestimation within the very-low-risk range (<10% predicted probability), followed by a slight underestimation, while OTL38 demonstrated a more pronounced overestimation across risks below 30%. In the independent validation cohort (Fig. 5F), both probes demonstrated overall statistical comparability, with each displaying distinct performance profiles across risk zones. Graphically, in the moderate-risk range (∼30 to 70%), both probes exhibited remarkably similar calibration trajectories. However, FA-808-IP maintained superior calibration alignment in the extreme-low-risk (<30%) and high-risk (>75%) ranges, whereas OTL38 showed larger deviations, particularly overestimating lesion risk in the low-risk region. In both the training and validation cohorts (table S9), although point-estimate calibration errors (Emax, Eavg, and E90) did not reach statistical significance between probes (P > 0.05), FA-808-IP consistently demonstrated superior overall model fit. Specifically, FA-808-IP achieved a significantly lower Brier score (training: 0.097 versus 0.142, P < 0.001; validation: 0.086 versus 0.161, P = 0.019) and higher R2 values (training: 0.725 versus 0.503, P < 0.001; validation: 0.693 versus 0.378, P = 0.039), quantitatively confirming superior diagnostic reliability. These findings indicate that FA-808-IP provides more consistent and robust probability estimation across extreme risk spectrums, highlighting its potential for informed intraoperative decision-making.

DISCUSSION

From a design perspective, the novelty of FA-808-IP lies not in the mere combination of individual motifs but in a rational cooperative synergy aimed at fluorescence-guided surgery for ovarian cancer. Within this integrated framework, each functional component is strategically orchestrated, where the introduced IP moiety establishes a reversible albumin-binding dynamic to prolong blood circulation and facilitate background clearance, while the FA headpiece provides the molecular specificity to recognize FRα-positive malignant cells, and the cyanine core acts as the foundational optical scaffold (Fig. 1A).

Photophysically, FA-808-IP operates as an NIR-I cyanine fluorophore with an absorption maximum near 800 nm and achieves NIR-II imaging via its long-wavelength tail emission beyond 1000 nm. Although the intrinsic quantum yield within this window is restricted compared to narrowband NIR-II emitters, this physical limitation is overcome by the biological performance of the probe. Crucially, the exceptional contrast observed in vivo is predominantly driven by the FRα-targeting specificity and optimized pharmacokinetic profile of FA-808-IP. This localized enrichment at malignant loci yields a dense fluorescent signal that compensates for the weak tail emission, demonstrating that strategic biological targeting can empower conventional cyanine scaffolds for high-contrast tumor delineation.

According to ovarian cancer clinical guidelines, complete cytoreductive surgery substantially improves patient survival outcomes (58). To this end, FRα-targeted probes have been developed to enhance the accuracy of lesion detection during surgery, with the goals of minimizing residual tumor volumes, precisely delineating margins, and maximizing the preservation of healthy tissue. This approach offers a profound advantage over traditional surgery, which relies on visual inspection and palpation, and often falls short of achieving complete cytoreduction, particularly given the characteristic widespread intra-abdominal dissemination and diffuse implantation patterns of ovarian cancer (13). As a result, conventional methods frequently miss subtle or flat lesions, resulting in macroscopic residual disease. In contrast, fluorescence-guided surgery extends the surgeon’s visual field and achieves more thorough tumor debulking, presenting a superior strategy for the surgical management of ovarian cancer. However, realizing the full potential of this approach relies on the availability of high-performance fluorescent probes. The Food and Drug Administration–approved probe OTL38 was developed for targeted imaging of various cancers, including ovarian cancer, lung cancer, and endometrial cancers (49, 50, 59, 60). However, its clinical utility is limited by its NIR-I emission, which suffers from restricted tissue penetration and resolution, thereby compromising the accurate delineation of tumor margins (32, 52). Given the general scarcity of efficient dyes for NIR-II ovarian tumor imaging (19), we developed IR-808 derivatives and presented their efficient binding ability to FRα in vitro, establishing a critical foundation for in vivo tumor targeting. In a subcutaneous ovarian cancer model, the IR-808 derivatives provided more precise imaging and achieved a higher T/NT fluorescence ratio (>2) than that of OTL38. We further found two IR-808 derivatives with distinct pharmacokinetic profiles: FA-808, which was rapidly cleared, and FA-808-IP, which exhibits prolonged circulation and higher brightness (Fig. 3, B to D). It is worth noting that FA-808-IP and FA-808 at the same dose exhibited similar fluorescence intensities in in vitro serum (Fig. 1C), but they differed markedly in tumor accumulation in vivo (Fig. 3B). In vivo tumor imaging efficacy is determined by the probe structure, and comparison of the molecular structures of the two probes can clarify the enhanced imaging performance of FA-808-IP. These two molecules share the same heptamethine fluorescent core unit, which mainly governs their intrinsic optical properties. Meanwhile, their tumor-targeting ability and tumor enrichment are primarily achieved via the folate targeting moiety. The only structural difference between the two probes resides in the IP moiety, which facilitates serum protein binding, thereby enhancing tumor accumulation and yielding a superior T/NT fluorescence intensity ratio with prolonged metabolic retention. Combined with the subcutaneous tumor resection results guided by NIR-II imaging (Fig. 3, E to H), we conclude that the IP moiety mediates a time-dependent dosing effect that accounts for the observed improvements in detection sensitivity and specificity. Consequently, these combined features render FA-808-IP a promising candidate for fluorescence-guided surgery of ovarian cancer.

The extent of residual disease following cytoreductive surgery for ovarian cancer is critically associated with survival. Increasing survival benefits are achieved by progressively reducing the threshold of residual disease from <1 cm (associated with a 10-month benefit) to <0.5 cm (an additional 16 months) and ultimately to no gross residual (a further 14 months). Therefore, adopting more stringent surgical criteria is essential for improving patient prognosis. According to the phase II clinical trial report, OTL38 exhibited a sensitivity of 85.93% and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 88.14% for FRα-positive lesions (49). Using cluster-adjusted analysis to account for intra-animal correlations (Fig. 5D and Table 1), the diagnostic performance of OTL38 in this study was characterized by a sensitivity of 78.75% and an NPV of 71.43% in this mouse model of peritoneal ovarian cancer metastasis. This lower sensitivity may be attributable to the confined peritoneal cavity and the prevalence of small lesions characteristic of this model. In the same model, FA-808-IP demonstrated significantly superior diagnostic metrics, with a cluster-adjusted sensitivity of 90.20% and an NPV of 86.73%, consistently outperforming OTL38 (Table 1). When stratifying lesions by diameter, FA-808-IP exhibited markedly higher sensitivity than OTL38 for small lesions (<6 mm). For the most challenging sub–3-mm lesions, FA-808-IP reached a sensitivity of 86.27%, which was superior to 61.40% sensitivity obtained with OTL38. In this small-animal tumor model, ∼25% sensitivity improvement translated to the resection of 88 true-positive microlesions under FA-808-IP guidance, while OTL38 missed a larger proportion of these foci, resulting in 22 false-negative findings (table S8). By identifying macroscopically occult tumors, FA-808-IP provides a direct translational surgical benefit that facilitates more thorough disease clearance, serving as a fundamental cornerstone for achieving optimal surgical cytoreduction.

The primary translational significance of our study lies in the synergistic integration of the probe’s molecular targeting and the NIR-II window’s physical advantages, which directly manifest as improved surgical outcomes. Specifically, FA-808-IP serves as a more effective imaging agent than OTL38 for both the detection of peritoneal metastases and the guidance of subsequent cytoreductive surgery. The substantially elevated AUC reflects a robust enhancement in overall diagnostic accuracy and visual contrast, providing a superior foundation for intraoperative boundary judgment compared to OTL38. Driven by the reduced tissue scattering and suppressed autofluorescence inherent to the NIR-II window, this technology preserves high-contrast signals from subsurface lesions, effectively overcoming the depth barrier. Consequently, FA-808-IP–based intraoperative imaging identifies tumor lesions that remain occult to conventional scans, surpassing the resolution limits of traditional preoperative modalities such as ultrasonography, magnetic resonance imaging, and computed tomography. While phase II and phase III trials of OTL38 reported the resection of residual lesions that were only detectable by fluorescence (49, 50), these lesions were not identified in our OTL38-treated mice, likely owing to the limited intra-abdominal volume of mice, the inherently smaller size of diffuse metastatic lesions compared with those in humans, and the limited cohort size. In contrast, FA-808-IP successfully identified macroscopically occult tumor lesions in a single mouse, facilitating the achievement of the no gross residual resection goal (Fig. 4, D to F). Given the extensive nature of peritoneal metastases, the primary clinical end point in ovarian cancer is the attainment of no gross residual status (8, 9). Although R0 resection in this context is defined as the removal of visible residual disease, this standard is less stringent than the pathological requirement for the absence of microscopic tumor cells typically applied in gastrointestinal malignancies. By using FA-808-IP for fluorescence-guided surgery, the surgeon’s ability to differentiate true malignant lesions from suspicious tissues is enhanced, thereby preventing unnecessary overresection. Furthermore, this technique enables the detection of occult subperitoneal tumor deposits missed during conventional gross inspection. Consequently, the transition from no gross residual status toward the more rigorous benchmark of no microscopic residual disease is facilitated, thereby improving the oncological quality of surgery.

Notably, to fully realize these intraoperative diagnostic benefits, the photophysical boundaries of FA-808-IP must be managed to optimize surgical guidance. Under continuous 808-nm laser irradiation at a relevant power density of 65 mW/cm2, a substantial fluorescence decline occurs after 3 min of continuous exposure, which serves as the threshold for the onset of photobleaching. Consequently, to minimize signal attenuation, continuous illumination should ideally be restricted to within 3 min per session or managed via intermittent laser activation. Nevertheless, within a cumulative laser exposure time of up to 30 min, the probe retains sufficient, reliable signal contrast to achieve effective tumor delineation. This operational window complies with skin exposure safety limits and flexibly accommodates segmental surgical inspection across different anatomical sites without inducing detectable phototoxic tissue damage.

The clinical relevance of the murine peritoneal metastasis model used herein presents certain anatomical and translational limitations. Crucially, notable differences exist between the murine abdominal anatomy and the macro/microscale lesion distribution encountered during human ovarian cancer cytoreductive surgery. Consequently, the diagnostic results obtained from this murine model cannot be directly translated to predict the rates of complete cytoreduction in a clinical setting. Nevertheless, the murine model remains an indispensable platform for high-throughput pharmacokinetic screening and for establishing the fundamental detection thresholds for microlesions. This discrepancy is effectively addressed by the ex vivo validation using patient-derived tumor tissues. FA-808-IP demonstrated highly selective fluorescence targeting within clinical peritoneal metastasis specimens, providing direct evidence that supports its diagnostic utility. Therefore, despite the inherent anatomical limitations of rodent models, the precise labeling of human specimens solidifies the translational potential of this targeted probe for fluorescence-guided surgery.

In conclusion, our study introduces FA-808-IP, an NIR-II imaging probe that overcomes conventional photophysical constraints via strategic chemical synergy. By integrating FRα-targeting with albumin-binding pharmacokinetics, FA-808-IP enables high-contrast, real-time visualization of malignant foci undetectable by conventional intraoperative inspection. Our findings demonstrate that this design substantially outperforms OTL38, establishing a robust molecular imaging platform to advance ovarian cancer cytoreduction from macroscopic inspection toward rigorous, microscopic-level clearance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted according to the protocols approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of The First Hospital of Jilin University (approval no. JDYY20240365). All experiments involving human specimens were approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Jilin University (approval no. 2023-284-1).

Imaging penetration assay

FA-808 and FA-808-IP probes (200 μM in PBS) were added to capillary tubes for in vitro NIR-II imaging. Then a 1% intralipid phantom in a cylindrical dish was placed on the top of the tested sample, and the imaging depth was calculated from the dispensed volume and the dish’ area. NIR-II fluorescence imaging was performed using an 808-nm laser (power density, 65 mW/cm2) and recorded through a 1100-nm long-pass filter.

Binding assay on plasmonic gold slide

The plasmonic gold slide was prepared as previously reported (61). FRα (50 μg/ml) and BSA (50 μg/ml) were printed using a nanoplotter (version 2.1). After drying at room temperature for 2 hours, the printed slide was assembled into the frame for subsequent incubation and washing steps. An aliquot of 50 μl of ICG, IR-808, OTL38, FA-808, and FA-808-IP (50 μM) was added to each well and incubated for 2 hours. The reaction wells were washed five times with PBST, followed by three washes with PBS, and then dried for testing.

Gel electrophoresis analysis

A 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel was used for analysis. FRα dissolved in PBS at a concentration of 10 μg/ml and mixed with an equal volume of probe solution (10 μg/ml). After incubation at room temperature for 2 hours, the dye-labeled protein was combined with loading buffer and added to the sample loading wells. Electrophoresis was performed at 80 V for the stacking gel and 120 V for the separating gel. The electrophoresis was stopped when the bromophenol blue was ∼1 cm from the bottom of the gel.

Cell viability test

The in vitro cytotoxicity of FA-808 and FA-808-IP was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Briefly, OVCAR3 cells and IOSE-80 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells per well) and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The RPMI 1640 medium was then removed and replaced with fresh RPMI 1640 medium containing FA-808 or FA-808-IP at various concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, 25, or 30 μM). After an additional 24 hours of incubation, the liquid in each well was removed and replaced with 100 μl of 1640 medium containing 10 μl of CCK-8. The wells were then incubated for another 2 hours at 37°C. Subsequently, the absorbance value of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy LX, Bio-Tek, USA) to evaluate cell viability.

In vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging

NIR-II imaging was performed with a NIR camera (Princeton Instruments NIRvana-640 or Raptor) equipped with an 808-nm laser. Emission was collected using 1000- and 1100-nm long-pass filters. All mice were anesthetized with isoflurane before imaging and received the same intravenous dose of probes (200 μM). During imaging experiments, at least three mice were used as parallel controls. All fluorescence images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Animal and tumor model

Female BALB/c nude mice (4 to 6 weeks old) (catalog no. 211) and BALB/c mice (6 to 8 weeks old) (catalog no. 401) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen–free conditions in the barrier facility of the Experimental Animal Center at the First Hospital of Jilin University.

To establish the subcutaneous ovarian cancer model, OVCAR3 cells (2 × 106 cells in 100 μl PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the left axilla of the BALB/c nude mice. To establish the advanced ovarian cancer model, a suspension of 2 × 106 OVCAR3 cells was injected intraperitoneally into the mice.

H&E staining

Harvested tissues (including tumors, peritoneal lesions, biopsies, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight. These tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 3-μm-thick sections. H&E staining was then performed according to the protocol of the H&E kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, catalog no. C0105). H&E staining images were scanned using an Olympus VS200 whole-slide scanning system.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections (including tumor tissue and paraneoplastic muscle tissue) underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and microwave-assisted antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Following blocking, the slides were incubated with anti-FRα antibody (BioLegend, catalog no. 908304 AB_2629795; 1:200) overnight at 4°C. Signals were detected using a horseradish peroxidase–polymer/DAB system and counterstained with hematoxylin. IHC staining images of all tissues were scanned using an Olympus VS200 whole-slide scanning system.

Statistical analyses

All quantitative data were presented as means ± SD. Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent two-sample t tests. Statistical significance was defined at P < 0.05, with asterisks denoting significance levels: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Kappa analysis was conducted to assess interrater agreement, where higher κ coefficients indicated stronger consistency between paired measurements. Taking histopathological findings as the reference gold standard (positive = lesion-positive tissue and negative = lesion-negative tissue), ROC curves were constructed for the FA-808-IP and OTL38 probes using the full raw dataset.

The optimal fluorescence intensity cutoff value for each probe was determined by maximizing the Youden index (Youden index = sensitivity + specificity − 1). On the basis of these optimal thresholds, diagnostic metrics including sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, Youden index, overall accuracy, and AUC were calculated for each probe. To account for the statistical dependence of multiple lesions within individual mice, we performed 10,000 cluster bootstrap resampling iterations with mice defined as the independent cluster unit. The 95% CIs were derived from the resulting bootstrap distribution and further refined using the bias-corrected and accelerated method to effectively mitigate pseudoreplication bias. Calibration curves were plotted alongside Brier scores to quantitatively assess the consistency between predicted probabilities and actual pathological outcomes. zz-tests were used for pairwise comparisons of diagnostic indices; zz > 1.96 corresponded to P < 0.05. All statistical analyses and calibration curve plots were implemented in R software (v4.3.1). ROC curves were visualized using GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0), while full width at half maximum data processing and figure plotting were completed with Origin 2023.
