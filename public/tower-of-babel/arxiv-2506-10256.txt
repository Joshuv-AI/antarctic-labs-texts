Clustering of large deviations events in heavy-tailed moving average processes: the catastrophe principle in the short-memory case
Jiaqi Wang, Gennady Samorodnitsky

Abstract
How do large deviation events in a stationary process cluster? The answer depends not only on the type of large deviations, but also on the length of memory in the process. Somewhat unexpectedly, it may also depend on the tails of the process. In this paper we work in the context of large deviations for partial sums in moving average processes with short memory and regularly varying tails. We show that the structure of the large deviation cluster in this case markedly differs from the corresponding structure in the case of exponentially light tails, considered in Chakrabarty and Samorodnitsky 2024 . This is due to the difference between the “conspiracy” vs. the “catastrophe” principles underlying the large deviation events in the light tailed case and the heavy tailed case, correspondingly.

Introduction

Certain “extreme” events—such as hurricanes, earthquakes, power outages,
or financial crises—occur rarely but may have catastrophic
consequences. In certain systems, these rare events do not occur in
isolation; instead, they may cluster, with multiple rare events
happening close together in
time. When such clustering occurs, the impact of individual events is
amplified, and this can potentially lead to prolonged disruptions and
cause systemic risks. Understanding how such
clustering occurs is crucial for assessing risks and developing strategies to
mitigate their effects.

Probabilistic modeling of such phenomena is naturally based on large
deviation events, the events that occur with vanishingly small
probability as the size of the system grows.
Large deviation theory provides not only estimates for the
probabilities of rare events, but it also describes the
“circumstances”
that are likely to lead to such events. These “circumstances” differ
if the underlying stochastic model has exponentially light tails, and
if it has heavier, subexponential, tails. We will see
that this may lead to different clustering patterns of large
deviations events in the two cases.

A bit more specifically (but still very informally), rare events in a
stochastic model with exponentially light tails tend to exhibit the
“conspiracy principle”: these unlikely events are most likely to
happen because the random inputs driving the model all change their
behavior as if they are sampled from a “wrong” distribution - they
“conspire” to do so. While each individual change may be mild, the
overall effect of the “conspiracy” is major. In contrast, rare
events in a
stochastic model with subexponential tails tend to exhibit the
“catastrophe principle”: while the great majority of the random
inputs driving the model behave in their usual manner, a small number
of these inputs undergo a “catastrophic” change, sufficient to cause
the rare event. In many cases a single random input exhibits such
“catastrophic” change!
We refer the reader to

Dembo and
Zeitouni 1993
for an exposition of the large deviation
theory in the case of exponentially light tails and to

Denisov, Dieker and
Shneer 2008
for large deviations in the case of subexponential
tails.

The specific behavior of these random inputs depends on the stochastic model,
and the precise effect of the “conspiracy” and “catastrophe”
principles depends also on the memory in the model. The interplay
between the tails and the memory can be quite intricate.

One of the main contributions of this paper is to demonstrate that
clustering of large deviation events may be very different if the
underlying mechanism is the “catastrophe” phenomenon as opposed to
the “conspiracy” phenomenon. Intuitively, the conspiracy phenomenon
is often reflected in a temporary change of the drift, and the rare events
keep happening for as long as that temporary change of the drift
suffices. In contrast, under the catastrophe phenomenon, clustering
occurs when rare events share a common cause – often a single
extreme input– making the location of the extreme input crucial. One
consequence of this is that in the regime considered in this paper
(the short memory regime), the cluster of large deviations changes
from finite to infinite when the tails change from exponentially light
to power-like heavy.

The way rare events cluster can be expected to depend further on the
nature of the rare events.
In this work, we consider finite mean doubly infinite moving average processes
with regularly varying tails, with (properly defined) short
memory. Whenever well-defined (convergent), these processes are
stationary and
ergodic (even mixing) regardless of their tails. The large deviation
events we consider are the events on which the sample averages of the
consecutive observations are away from the true mean. This is also the setup of

Chakrabarty and
Samorodnitsky 2024
, except that the latter work
considered processes with exponentially light tails. Unlike that
paper that studied only univariate models, our processes are allowed
to take valued in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
.

Let

X_{k}=\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{i}Z_{k-i},\ \ k\in\mathbb{Z},

(1)

where
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
d
-dimensional
random vectors (the noise random vectors), and
(A_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
are
deterministic
d\times d
matrices (the coefficient
matrices). In the sequel we will use the notation
\|A\|=\|A\|_{2}

to denote the
\ell_{2}/\ell_{2}
operator norm of a matrix
A
, and all
norms of vectors are also the
\ell_{2}
norms.

We assume
that the noise vectors
(Z_{i})
have zero mean and a regularly
varying distribution with exponent
\alpha>1
. That is, there exists
a Radon measure
\nu
on
\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\frac{P(Z_{0}\in u\cdot)}{P(\|Z_{0}\|>u)}\xrightarrow{v}\nu(\cdot)

(2)

(vague convergence) as
u\to\infty
, and
\nu
(the tail measure of
the noise) satisfies
\nu(c\Phi)=c^{-\alpha}\nu(\Phi)
for all measurable sets
\Phi\subset\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
c>0
. The common notation is
Z_{0}\in RV(\nu,\alpha)
.

We assume that the coefficient matrices
(A_{i})
satisfy

\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\|A_{i}\|\coloneqq A_{\text{abs}}<\infty\ \ \text{and the matrix}\ \ \mathcal{A}=\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}A_{i}\ \ \text{is invertible.}

(3)

The first condition in (
3
) is the absolute
summability (of the norms of the coefficients) assumption. It is
clearly sufficient for the a. s. convergence of the series (
1
)
defining the infinite moving average process. Weaker summability
conditions on the coefficient norms are also sufficient, however; see

Hult and
Samorodnitsky 2008
. The absolute summability condition is
usually taken as producing a short memory moving average process. The
second condition in (
3
) avoids certain
cancellations corresponding to the null space of the matrix

\mathcal{A}
. Such cancellation, when present, can lead to certain negative
dependence phenomena that we wish to avoid in this paper.

Our rare events are defined by a failure set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a measurable
set bounded away from the origin, satisfying certain regularity
assumptions; see Assumption
2.1
below. Denote

B_{j}(n,\Gamma)=\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=j}^{n+j-1}X_{i}\in\Gamma\right\},\ j\in\mathbb{Z}.

(4)

By stationarity, for each fixed
n
, the events
(B_{j}(n,\Gamma))
have
the same probability and, when
n
is large, this probability is small
by the law of large numbers.

We are interested in the clustering of rare events: given that
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
happens, how many nearby rare events
B_{j}(n,\Gamma)
also
happen? In a marked difference from the case of exponentially light
tails considered in
Chakrabarty and
Samorodnitsky 2024
, given
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
, the conditional probability of
B_{j}(n,\Gamma)
converges to 1 as
n\to\infty
, for each fixed

j
. Therefore, we choose to investigate the size of the cluster of
large deviation events by studying the first time these events do not
happen. Specifically, let

J_{n}^{+}=\inf\{j>0:\,B_{j}(n,\Gamma)\,\text{ does not occur}\},\ J_{n}^{-}=\sup\{j<0:\,B_{j}(n,\Gamma)\,\text{ does not occur}\}.

(5)

We will investigate the joint limiting distribution of the properly normalized

(J_{n}^{+},J_{n}^{-})
.

Remark 1
.

When the
first condition in (
3
) fails, the moving average is
sometimes said to be long-range dependent. In that case the large
deviations are expected to cluster in a different manner. When the
tails are exponentially light, this was demonstrated in

Chakrabarty and
Samorodnitsky 2023
in the one-dimensional
case. When the
second condition in (
3
) fails, the moving average is
sometimes said to have negative long-range dependent. How the large
deviations cluster in this case is, to the best of our knowledge,
still unknown event when the tails are exponentially light. We plan to
address these questions for heavy tailed moving average processes in a
subsequent publication.

The following notation and terminology will be used
throughout the
paper. We denote the cardinality of a set
A
by
|A|
. We say that two
eventually nonvanishing sequences
(a_{n})
,
(b_{n})
are asymptotically
equivalent and write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
a_{n}/b_{n}\to 1
as

n\to\infty
. We write
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=\infty
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In

section 2
, we present our main results, which are more
general than the discussion so far. To prepare for the proof, we
establish preliminary results in

section 3
. The proofs of the propositions are given
in
section 4
, while
section 5
and

section 6
contain the proofs of our two main
theorems. Finally,
Appendix
contains the proofs of
the preliminary lemmas.

2
Main results

We will impose certain assumptions on the
failure set
\Gamma
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The failure set
\Gamma
is a measurable set bounded
away from the origin. That is, for some
\delta_{0}>0
,
\Gamma\subset B_{0}(\delta_{0})^{c}
, where
B_{x}(r)
denoted a radius
r
closed ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

centered at
x
. We assume that

(\nu\circ\mathcal{A}^{-1})(\Gamma)=\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma)>0,\ (\nu\circ\mathcal{A}^{-1})(\partial\Gamma)=\nu(\partial(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma))=0.

(6)

We will see in the sequel that the first assumption in (
6
) guarantees that a single
noise vector is involved into “the catastrophe” causing the rare
event
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
.
If this assumption fails, multiple noise
vectors may be needed. The second assumption in (
6
)
is a continuity assumption, that simplifies the statements of our
results.

Our first result can be viewed as precisely demonstrating the action
of the “catastrophe principle” in the situation considered in this
paper. The random inputs into the system are the noise random vectors

(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and Theorem
2.1
shows that, under
its assumptions, when
the rare event
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
occurs, exactly one noise
vector causes it.

Let
M
be the space of all Radon measures on

E=\mathbb{R}\times\bigl([-\infty,\infty]^{d}\setminus\{0\}\bigr)
, equipped with the topology of vague
convergence. We formulate our first result in the language of weak
convergence in
M
or, even more precisely, weak convergence in
M_{p}
,
the vaguely closed subset of
M
consisting of Radon point processes.
We refer the reader to
Resnick 1987

for more information on weak convergence in the vague topology.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that the set
\Gamma

satisfies Assumption
2.1
. Consider a sequence of point processes on
E
defined by

N_{n}=\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\epsilon_{(i/n,\,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}/n)},\ n=1,2,\ldots.

Then the conditional law of
N_{n}
given

B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
converges weakly, as
n\to\infty
, to the
law of a single point point process
\epsilon_{(U,X)}
where
U

is standard uniform, and

X
is an independent of
U
random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the law

P(X\in\cdot)=\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Gamma\cap\cdot))}{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma)}.

Remark 2
.

In addition to establishing that, for large
n
, a single noise vector is likely to drive the rare event
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
, Theorem
2.1
also demonstrates that the location of this vector is approximately uniformly distributed over the interval
(o(n),n+o(n))
. This behavior is characteristic of the short memory regime defined by (
3
). Intuitively, short memory ensures that no individual input
Z_{i}
has oversized overall
influence: the inputs in the above interval enter the partial sum

S_{n}
with, roughly, the same weight (equal to
\mathcal{A}
), so that they
have,
roughly, the same chance of causing large deviations

Our second main result answers the question of how large deviations
events
(B_{j}(n,\Gamma))
cluster given that the event
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)

occurs by describing the limiting distribution of the properly
normalized random vector
(J_{n}^{+},J_{n}^{-})
defined in
(
5
). We state the theorem is a somewhat greater
generality than what is required for this purpose. Denote

S_{j}^{n}=\sum_{k=j}^{n+j-1}X_{k},\ \ j\in\mathbb{Z},

so that
B_{j}(n,\Gamma)=\{S_{j}^{n}\in n\Gamma\}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that the sets
\Gamma
and

\Psi
both satisfy Assumption
2.1
. For each
n
, define

J_{n}^{\Psi,+}=\inf\{j\geq 0:S_{j}^{n}\in n\Psi^{c}\},\ \ J_{n}^{\Psi,-}=\sup\{j<0:S_{j}^{n}\in n\Psi^{c}\}.

Then, as
n\to\infty
, the conditional distribution of

(J_{n}^{\Psi,+}/n,\,-J_{n}^{\Psi,-}/n)
given
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)

converges weakly to the law of a random vector equal to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(U,1-U)&\ \text{with probability}\ \mu(\Psi),\\
(0,0)&\ \text{with probability}\ 1-\mu(\Psi),\end{array}\right.

where
U
is a standard uniform random variable and

\mu(\Psi)=\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Psi\cap\Gamma))}{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma)}.

In particular, the conditional distribution of

(J_{n}^{\Gamma,+}/n,\,-J_{n}^{\Gamma,-}/n)
given
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)

converges weakly to the law of a random vector
(U,1-U)
.

Remark 3
.

The last statement of Theorem
2.2
easily follows
from its general statement by taking
\Gamma=\Psi
, since

\mu(\Gamma)=1
.

The scenario described by the theorem is, once again, a reflection of
the “catastrophe principle”. The event
B_{0}(n,\Gamma)
occurs due
to a single extraordinary noise vector. This noise vector, nearly on
its own,
may place
S_{0}^{n}
into
n\Psi
. If it does, then
S_{j}^{n}
will stay in

n\Psi
for a roughly linear number of
j
, depending on the location
of the extraordinary noise vector. If it does not, no
S_{j}^{n}
is
likely to be in
n\Psi
.

We close this section with stating a key proposition making very
explicit how the “catastrophe principle” works, by exhibiting a
number of key equivalent quantities resulting from this
principle. The equivalence of these quantities will be used repeatedly
in our arguments. The proposition and its argument use the following
representation of the sums
S_{j}^{n}
, which follows directly from
(
1
):

S_{j}^{n}=\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\gamma_{i-j,n}Z_{i},\ \ \gamma_{in}=\sum_{k=-i}^{n-i-1}A_{k},\ i=\ldots,-1,0,1,2,\ldots.

(7)

Proposition 1
.

Let
M,\delta
be positive constants, and let
I_{n}\subset\{0,\dots,n-1\},\,n=1,2,\ldots
be such that
|I_{n}|\to\infty
.
Suppose that the set
\Gamma

satisfies Assumption
2.1
. Then, as
n\to\infty
, the following
quantities are asymptotically equivalent:

1.

(|I_{n}|/n)P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)

2.

|I_{n}|P(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)

3.

P(\text{there is}\ i\in I_{n}\ \text{with}\ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma)

4.

P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma\ \text{and there is}\ i\in I_{n}\ \text{with}\ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma)

5.

P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma,\ \text{there is}\ i\in I_{n}\ \text{with}\ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma\ \text{and for all}\ i\not=l\in[-n{M},n{M}],\|Z_{l}\|\leq n\delta).

In particular,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)}=1.

(8)

Note that the equivalence of 1., 3. and 4. above implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P\bigl(\{S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma\}\triangle\{\text{there is}\ i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}\ \text{with}\ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma\}\bigr)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)}=0.

(9)

We strengthen this conclusion in the following corollary.

Corollary 2.1
.

For any
0\leq\theta<1
,
\Gamma
satisfying Assumption

2.1
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P\bigl(\{S_{j}^{n}\in n\Gamma,\,0\leq j\leq n\theta\}\triangle\{\text{there is}\ i\in\{\lfloor n\theta\rfloor\ldots,n-1\}\ \text{with}\ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma\}\bigr)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)}=0.

(10)

Remark 4
.

The conclusion (
8
) is an immediate consequence of the
equivalence of 1. and 2. above. Furthermore, by the regular variation
(see (
2
)), it can restated as

\frac{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}{nP(\|\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\|\geq n)}\to\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma)}{\nu(y:\|\mathcal{A}y\|\geq 1)},

where
\nu
is the tail measure of the noise. If

\Gamma
is not a continuity set with respect to
\nu\circ\mathcal{A}^{-1}
, this statement should be reformulated as saying

\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}{\kern 0.0pt\Gamma}^{\mathrm{o}})}{\nu(y:\|\mathcal{A}y\|\geq 1)}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}{nP(\|\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\|\geq n)}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}{nP(\|\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\|\geq n)}\leq\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\bar{\Gamma})}{\nu(y:\|\mathcal{A}y\|\geq 1)}.

Preliminary calculations

In this section we present a number of preliminary results, stated as

Some of the results are stated in the one-dimensional case, and then

Recall that for a one-dimensional random variable
Z
, its distribution is said to be regularly varying with index
\alpha>0
and if there exists a slowly varying function
L:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a constant

\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{P(Z>z)}{z^{-\alpha}L(z)}=p,\ \ \lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{P(Z<-z)}{z^{-\alpha}L(z)}=q.

(11)

The following variance estimate for the truncated regularly varying
random variables will be used for in the sequel concentration
inequalities.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

be

regularly varying with index
\alpha>0
. For

let

Z^{*}_{x}=Z{\bf 1}(|Z|\leq x)
. Then, as a function of

is

E(Z^{2})
if
\alpha>2
and is regularly varying at infinity with

.

.

\displaystyle\frac{E\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\exp\left(-f(i/n,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}/n)\right)1_{B^{(i,n)}_{0}(\Gamma)}\right]}{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}-\frac{E\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\exp\left(-f(i/n,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}/n)\right)1_{\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma}\right]}{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}\to 0.

\displaystyle\left|\frac{E\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\exp\left(-f(i/n,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}/n)\right)1_{B^{(i,n)}_{0}(\Gamma)}\right]}{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}-\frac{E\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\exp\left(-f(i/n,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}/n)\right)1_{\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma}\right]}{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}\right|

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}P\bigl(\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma,\|Z_{l}\|>n\delta\ \text{for

Note that using inclusion exclusion formula, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}P\bigl(\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma,S_{0}^{n}\notin n\Gamma\bigr)}{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}\leq\frac{P(S_{0}^{n}\notin n\Gamma\ \text{and there is}\ i=0,\ldots,n-1\ \text{with}\ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma)}{P(S_{0}^{n}\in n\Gamma)}

\displaystyle P\left(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle P\left(\{T\notin n\Psi\}\cap\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right).

We start with the following claim.

P\left(\{T\notin n\Psi\}\cap\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)\to 0,\text{ as }n\to\infty

(25)

For continuity of the presentation we defer the proof of
(
25
) to the final section.

We now analyze the first probability in the right-hand side of
(
24
).
We write it as

\displaystyle P\left(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\bigl(T\in n\Psi\big|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)P\left(\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right).

We now apply the equivalence between 1., 3. and 4. in Proposition

1
to the moving average process for which
A_{0}=\mathcal{A}

and
A_{i}=0
for
i\not=0
to conclude that

\displaystyle P\bigl(T\in n\Psi\big|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)\sim P\bigl(\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Psi\ \ \text{for some}\ \ \lfloor n\theta_{1}\rfloor\leq i\leq\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rfloor\big|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\left(N_{n}\left(\bigl[\lfloor n\theta_{1}\rfloor/n,\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rfloor/n\bigr]\times\Psi\right)>0\big|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle P\bigl(\varepsilon_{(U,X)}\bigl([\theta_{1},1-\theta_{2}]\times\Psi\bigr)>0\bigr)=(1-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Psi\cap\Gamma))}{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma)}

by Theorem
2.1
and continuous mapping theorem. If

\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Psi\cap\Gamma))=0
, (
26
) converges
to 0, hence so does (
24
). The theorem holds in this
case. In the following, we assume, therefore, that
\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Psi\cap\Gamma))>0
. Under this assumption, if we show that

P\left(\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)\to 1,

then this, along with (
25
) and
(
24
) will give us

P\bigl(J_{n}^{\Psi,+}/n\geq\theta_{1},J_{n}^{\Psi,-}/n\leq-\theta_{2}\big|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)\to(1-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})\frac{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Psi\cap\Gamma))}{\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}\Gamma)}

for all
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\geq 0
such that
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}<1
,
which implies the statement of the theorem. We will prove that under
the assumption
\nu(\mathcal{A}^{-1}(\Psi\cap\Gamma))>0
,

P\left(\bigcup_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)^{c}\bigg|\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)\to 0,

for which it is enough to prove that

\frac{P\left(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap\bigcup_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)^{c}\right)}{P\bigl(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)}\to 0.

Since by

\displaystyle P\bigl(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\bigl(\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Psi\ \ \text{for
some}\ i=\lfloor n\theta_{1}\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rfloor

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt\text{and}\ \ \mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Gamma\ \ \text{for
some}\ i=0,\ldots,n-1\bigr)+o\bigl(nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\bigl(\Psi\cap\Gamma\bigr)\ \ \text{for some}\ i=\lfloor n\theta_{1}\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rfloor)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.45377pt+o\bigl(nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)\bigr)

we conclude that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{P\bigl(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\bigr)}{n(1-\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})P(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\bigl(\Psi\cap\Gamma\bigr))}\geq 1.

We will prove that

\frac{P\left(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap\bigcup_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)^{c}\right)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\bigl(\Psi\cap\Gamma\bigr))}\to 0.

(27)

Concentrating once again on the relevant individual noise variables

Z_{i}
, we write

\displaystyle P\left(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap\bigcup_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)^{c}\right)\leq P\left(\{T\in n\Psi\}\cap\bigcap_{i=\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil}^{\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rceil}\bigl\{\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\notin n\Psi\bigr\}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle P\left(\bigcup_{i=\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil}^{\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rceil}\bigl\{\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Psi\bigr\}\cap\bigcup_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)^{c}\right)=:I_{1}(n)+I_{2}(n).

The proof of the theorem will be completed once we prove
(
25
), as well as the claim that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{I_{i}(n)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\bigl(\Psi\cap\Gamma\bigr))}=0,\ i=1,2.

(29)

We begin by proving (
25
). We have

\displaystyle P\left(\{T\notin n\Psi\}\cap\bigcap_{j=-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}^{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}B_{j}(n,\Psi)\bigg|B_{0}(n,\Gamma)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{P\bigl(S^{n}_{-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}\in n\Psi,\,S^{n}_{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}\in n\Psi,\,T\notin n\Psi\bigr)}{P(B_{0}(n,\Gamma))},

so by the equivalence of 1. and 2. in in Proposition

1
, it is enough to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P\bigl(S^{n}_{-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}\in n\Psi,\,S^{n}_{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}\in n\Psi,\,T\notin n\Psi\bigr)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)}=0,

which will follow from the claims

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P\bigl(S^{n}_{-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}\in n\Psi,\,S^{n}_{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}\in n\Psi,\,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\notin n\Psi,\,i=\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1,\ldots,-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1+n\bigr)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{P\bigl(T\notin n\Psi,\,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\in n\Psi\ \ \text{for some}\ \ i=\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1,\ldots,-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1+n\bigr)}{nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)}=0.

(31)

In fact, the proof of (
30
) is very similar to the proof of
(
29
) for
i=1
, and the proof of (
31
) is very
similar to the proof of (

) for

.

Using (
9
),

\displaystyle P\bigl(S^{n}_{-\lfloor n\theta_{2}\rfloor+1}\in n\Psi,\,S^{n}_{\lceil n\theta_{1}\rceil-1}\in n\Psi,\,\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\notin n\Psi,\,i=\lfloor n\theta_{1}\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rfloor\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\bigl(A_{n,-}\cap A_{n,+}\cap\{\mathcal{A}Z_{i}\notin n\Psi,\,i=\lfloor n\theta_{1}\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor n(1-\theta_{2})\rfloor\}\bigr)+o\bigl(nP(\mathcal{A}Z_{0}\in n\Gamma)\bigr),

Proof of Lemma
3.2

Since
E[Z_{i}]=0
, and
(\beta_{in})
are uniformly bounded,
we have
|E[Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau_{n})]|\leq\delta/(4L)
for all
n
large enough and all
i
. Together with
\sum_{i}\beta_{in}\leq nL
, this implies that
for all
n
large enough,

\displaystyle\left|E\left[\sum_{i}\beta_{in}Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau_{n})\right]\right|\leq\sum_{i}|\beta_{in}||E[Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau_{n})]|\leq n\delta/2.

For such
n
,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{in}Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)\right|>n\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{in}\Bigl(Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)-E[Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)]\Bigr)\right|>n\delta/2\right).

We use a concentration inequality, due to
Prokhorov 1959
, which
we state here for the sake of completeness.

Lemma .1
.

Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}
be independent zero mean random variables
such that for some
c>0
we have

Pr(|Y_{i}|\leq c)=1
for all
i
. Then for any
t>0
,

P(Y_{1}+\cdots+Y_{m}>t)\leq\left(\frac{ct}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\rm Var}(Y_{i})}\right)^{-t/2c}.

If the series
\sum_{i}Y_{i}
converges, then the statement of Lemma

.1
extends to the case
m=\infty
. We use the
concentration inequality in this situation, with
Y_{i}=\beta_{in}\Bigl(Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)-E[Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)]\Bigr)
, so that

c=2n\tau
. Here
t=n\delta/2
. We estimate the
variances using
Lemma 3.1
. In the case
\alpha>2

we have

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{in}\Bigl(Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)-E[Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)]\Bigr)\right|>n\delta/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n^{2}\tau\delta}{EZ_{0}^{2}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{in}^{2}}\right)^{-\delta/(8\tau)}

for all
n
,
proving the statement of part (a) of the lemma in this case.

In the case
1<\alpha\leq 2
, let
g/2
be the regularly varying
function with index
2-\alpha
from Lemma
3.1
. Then
by the Potter bounds (see e.g. Proposition 0.8 in
Resnick 1987
),
for every
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
there is
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon,\tau/B)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(Y_{i})={\rm Var}\bigl(\beta_{in}Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)\bigr)=\beta_{in}^{2}g\bigl(\tau n/|\beta_{in}|\bigr)/2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta_{in}^{2}(1+\varepsilon)(B/|\beta_{in}|)^{2-\alpha+\varepsilon}g\bigl(\tau n/B\bigr)/2\leq B^{2-\alpha+\varepsilon}|\beta_{in}|^{\alpha-\varepsilon}g\bigl(\tau n/B\bigr),

and he statement of part (a) of the lemma in this case once again
follows from the concentration inequality.

For part (b) we write

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{in}Z_{i}\right|>n\delta\right)\leq

\displaystyle P\bigl(\max_{i}|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|>n\tau\bigr)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}\beta_{in}Z_{i}{\bf 1}(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|\leq\tau n)\right|>n\delta\right).

The required bound on the second term in the right-hand side above is
obtained in part (a), so the proof is completed by noticing that by
the Potter bounds, for every
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
there is
n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon,\tau/B)
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\displaystyle P(\max_{i}|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|>n\tau)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i}P(|\beta_{in}Z_{i}|>n\tau)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)B^{-\alpha+\varepsilon}P(|Z_{0}|>n\tau/B)\sum_{i}|\beta_{in}|^{\alpha-\varepsilon}.

∎
