Outer Retinal Volume on Macular OCT Predicts Two-Year Diabetic Retinopathy Progression
Authors: Janika Shah, Hanieh Niktinat, Shinji Kakihara, Amani A. Fawzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate whether baseline retinal volume from macular optical coherence tomography (OCT) is associated with diabetic retinopathy (DR) progression over two years.MethodsIn this longitudinal observational study, 190 eyes from 121 patients, across the DR severity spectrum without diabetic macular edema, completed two-year follow-up and were included in the analysis. Baseline 3 × 3-mm macular OCT scans, with manual correction of segmentation, were analyzed to derive inner and outer retinal volumes in the central subfield, 1–3 mm parafoveal area, and whole 3 × 3-mm scan. Disease progression was defined as having one or more of the following events: DR worsening on fluorescein angiography or vision loss ≥ 10 letters at the two-year timepoint, or development of vitreous hemorrhage or initiation of pan-retinal photocoagulation or anti-VEGF therapy at any point during follow-up. Generalized Estimating Equations were used for analysis.ResultsOf the 190 eyes (121 patients), 48 eyes (25.3%; 39 patients) demonstrated progression. Higher outer retinal volume (parafoveal and whole) at baseline was significantly associated with disease progression (P < 0.001), whereas inner retinal volume showed no significant association. After adjusting for baseline DR severity, hypertension, age, lens and Bonferroni correction, outer retinal volume remained independently associated with higher odds of progression (P < 0.001).ConclusionsIncreased outer retinal volume (parafoveal and entire 3 × 3-mm macular scan) predicts DR progression over two years. These findings highlight the prognostic value of OCT-derived outer retinal volume and support its potential role as a clinically accessible biomarker for identifying eyes at higher risk for DR complications.

Diabetic mellitus (DM) is a growing global epidemic, and diabetic retinopathy (DR) remains a leading cause of preventable blindness worldwide,1 where sight-threatening complications remain a critical burden.2,3 Although highly effective and cost-effective treatments exist for advanced stages such as proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and diabetic macular edema (DME), visual outcomes are not always optimal because of the presence of irreversible structural damage by the time treatment is initiated.4 Identifying diabetic eyes with the highest risk of progression remains a major clinical challenge. Thus, improving risk stratification is critical to mitigate sight-threatening complications and reduce the treatment burden through timely interventions.5

Advances in noninvasive imaging have revolutionized the ability to assess retinal pathology in diabetic eyes. Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is a non-invasive three-dimensional imaging method that enables visualization of vascular changes at the capillary level.6,7 Prior work by Ong et al. has demonstrated that non-perfusion in the deep capillary plexus (DCP) is a robust predictor of disease worsening in clinically referable non-proliferative DR eyes, with reported sensitivity up to 89% in identifying eyes at risk for complications at one year.8 In addition, recent data further support this association; specifically, greater macular ischemia in the DCP across the spectrum from diabetes without retinopathy (DM no DR) to PDR was found to increase the risk of complications at two years.9 Similarly, other studies have reported OCTA-derived biomarkers that predict diabetic eye complications.10–12 Although OCTA provides valuable perfusion metrics and has deepened our understanding of diabetic retinal pathology, it is not universally adopted in primary eye care settings because of its high cost and technical complexity.13

By comparison, structural OCT is routinely obtained in retina and comprehensive eye clinics.14 Current commercial OCT scans offer an axial resolution approaching approximately 5 µm, providing near-histologic visualization of individual retinal layers,15,16 and are currently the gold standard for diagnosing and monitoring DME.17 Prior cross-sectional OCT studies have demonstrated that structural abnormalities, including disorganization of the retinal inner layers and ellipsoid zone disruption, are associated with worse visual acuity in diabetic eyes.18,19 With respect to quantitative retinal measurements, these studies have primarily focused on thickness metrics, reporting associations between increased central macular thickness and DR severity.20,21 A previous longitudinal study of 276 eyes with type 2 diabetes, ranging from DM no DR to mild/moderate non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) and followed up over four years, found no significant difference in central macular thickness or ganglion cell–inner plexiform layer thickness between eyes that progressed and those that did not. However, the study was limited by exclusion of severe NPDR and PDR eyes due to limited sample size and did not assess the role of outer retinal thickness.22 Our study builds on these findings by evaluating a broader disease spectrum, from no DR to PDR, and by incorporating outer retinal structural metrics.

Given the relative DCP vulnerability in DR,23–26 we wondered whether the retinal volumetric parameters, integrating thickness across a defined macular area (and sublayer), could serve as a more robust predictor of clinical worsening in diabetic eyes. Therefore we sought to investigate whether retinal volume, derived from baseline macular OCT scans, can predict the development of complications in diabetic eyes over a two-year follow-up period. Given the accumulating evidence linking capillary non-perfusion to complications in diabetic eyes, we hypothesized that reduced outer retinal volume at baseline, as a consequence of DCP ischemia, would distinguish eyes that go on to develop complications at two years.

Methods

This prospective, two-year longitudinal observational study enrolled diabetic patients evaluated in the Department of Ophthalmology at Northwestern University, Chicago, Illinois, between October 2021 and September 2023. The study received Institutional Review Board approval and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before study procedures.

Study Participants

We recruited 232 participants with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, representing the full spectrum of DR severity, ranging from DM no DR to PDR, including eyes with stable, previously laser-treated PDR. Participants were excluded if they had clinical evidence of center-involving diabetic macular edema (central subfield thickness [CST] ≥ 300 µm on Heidelberg Spectralis OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). This threshold was selected because CST measurements exceeding 300 µm show excellent agreement with clinical examinations for detecting diabetic foveal edema.27 Additionally, in the Protocol V study, eyes with baseline CST >300 µm were more likely to receive anti-VEGF therapy. Therefore setting the threshold at 300 µm ensured that eyes with diabetic macular edema were excluded.28 Participants were also excluded if they had received intravitreal anti-VEGF or corticosteroid therapy within the prior 6 months, or ocular/systemic conditions that could confound retinopathy grading or visual acuity measurements. Additional exclusion criteria included major ocular surgery within the previous three months or an anticipated need for such surgery within six months of enrollment, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) >10.0%, or participation in a clinical trial involving investigational medicinal products. Both eyes were included when the eligibility criteria were met.

Patients underwent a comprehensive history and ophthalmic examination at baseline and at two-year follow-up. The study design was observational, and these patients were managed according to current standards of clinical care.29

Ultrawide Field Fundus Images

Ultrawide field (UWF) color fundus photographs and UWF fluorescein angiography (FA) images were obtained using a standardized imaging protocol with an UWF scanning laser ophthalmoscope (Optomap Panoramic 200; Optos PLC, Dunfermline, Scotland, UK). The DR severity was graded according to the International Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy, as outlined in a prior study.30 Eyes with diabetes but no DR showed no vascular abnormalities, whereas mild non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) was characterized by the presence of microaneurysms alone. Moderate non-proliferative DR included microaneurysms, intraretinal hemorrhages, or venous beading that did not meet the criteria for severe NPDR. Severe NPDR was graded based on the 4-2-1 rule, characterized by any of the following: more than 20 intraretinal hemorrhages in each of four quadrants, venous beading in at least two quadrants, or intraretinal microvascular abnormalities in at least one quadrant. Treatment-naïve PDR was identified by neovascularization of the disc or elsewhere, or by vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage without prior laser treatment.31 Quiescent PDR was defined as eyes with previously PRP-treated PDR that showed no active neovascularization for at least six months. The final DR severity at baseline and follow-up visit was determined using UWF-FA. Given that intraretinal hemorrhages are not visualized on FA, eyes with mild or moderate NPDR fall into a single category on FA grading.32

OCT Imaging and Analysis

Spectral-domain OCT imaging was performed using the RTVue-XR Avanti system (Optovue software version 2017.1.0.151; Optovue, Inc., Fremont, CA, USA). Volumetric 3 × 3 mm scans centered on the fovea were acquired at an A-scan rate of 70,000 scans per second with a central wavelength of 840 nm, providing axial and transverse resolutions of 5 µm and 15 µm, respectively. Images were obtained using a high-density raster scan protocol consisting of 304 horizontal B-scans, each composed of 304 A-scans. To minimize motion artifacts, the system's Motion Correction Technology was used to perform a three-dimensional orthogonal registration of horizontal and vertical raster volumes.33 Scans with a Q score (signal strength) ≥ 6 were included in our analysis. The software automatically segmented the retina layers into inner retina and outer retina (Fig. 1). The inner retina extended from the inner limiting membrane to the outer border of the inner plexiform layer while the outer retina spanned from the outer border of the inner plexiform layer to the outer border of RPE. All scans were manually reviewed to verify the accuracy of auto-segmentation. Any segmentation errors were corrected manually before analysis. Volumetric metrics for the inner and outer retinal layers were then exported. These metrics were quantified in the central 1-mm subfield, the 1–3 mm parafoveal region, and across the entire 3 × 3 mm scan.

Disease Progression

Disease progression was defined as occurrence of any one or more of the following events: (i) a ≥1-step worsening in DR severity according to International Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy levels, as graded on FA at the two-year follow-up; (ii) a loss of ≥10 letters in best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) at the two-year visit; (iii) development of new vitreous hemorrhage at any point during follow up; (iv) requirement of pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP) or intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy at any time during the study period. Eyes that did not experience any of these events were classified as having no progression.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Demographic and clinical data were compared with independent-sample t tests for normally distributed data, Mann-Whitney U tests for nonparametric data, and χ2 tests for categorical data.

Univariate and multivariate associations between retinal volumes and disease progression were assessed using logistic regression models with Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to account for inter-eye correlation. Retinal volume variables were analyzed in increments of 0.1 mm³, reflecting the typical magnitude of variation observed in OCT-derived retinal volume measurements; accordingly, reported odds ratios (ORs) represent the odds of progression per 0.1-mm³ increase in retinal volume. Our multivariable models were adjusted for baseline DR severity, hypertension, age and lens status. Baseline DR severity, hypertension, and age were included because these factors have been associated with complications in diabetic eyes.34–36 Lens status was included because eyes with disease progression had a significantly higher proportion of phakic eyes at baseline compared with non-progressing eyes. To address the potential heterogeneity of the composite progression endpoint, we performed an additional sub-analysis focusing on structural DR progression. FA-based DR worsening was defined as ≥1-step worsening in DR severity. However, eyes with baseline treatment-naïve PDR or quiescent PDR are already at the advanced end of the FA severity scale and may not demonstrate further stepwise worsening using this grading approach. Therefore, PRP therapy during the two-year follow-up period in eyes with baseline treatment-naïve or quiescent PDR was also included as a marker of structural worsening because it reflects the development or presence of active proliferative disease features such as neovascularization, preretinal hemorrhage, or vitreous hemorrhage. This sub-analysis was performed using GEE logistic regression models adjusted for baseline DR severity and hypertension, with retinal volume variables analyzed per 0.1-mm³ increase. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons, establishing a significance threshold of P < 0.0083. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were calculated for parafoveal and whole outer retinal volume and used to determine sensitivity and specificity.

Box plots with jittered points were used to visualize the distribution of inner and outer retinal volume metrics in the central subfield, parafoveal region, and entire 3 × 3 mm scan across study groups. Forest plots were generated to display ORs for inner and outer retinal volumes in predicting disease progression. For visual clarity and to accommodate the wide range of ORs, the x-axis of the forest plot was presented on a logarithmic scale. This transformation is used solely for visualization; the ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) represent the actual estimates from the multivariable logistic regression models.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Of the 320 eyes from 205 patients that were originally recruited, 123 patients (60% of 205 patients) or 192 eyes (60% of 320 eyes) completed the two-year follow-up. On reviewing automated segmentation, errors were identified in 29 eyes; 27 were successfully corrected manually, whereas two remained uncorrectable and were excluded. The final cohort with complete two-year follow-up included 190 eyes from 121 patients, of which 48 eyes (25.3%) from 39 patients demonstrated disease progression (Supplementary Fig. S1). Among the 82 patients (128 eyes) who did not complete the final two-year visit, 10 patients (14 eyes) formally withdrew, six patients (nine eyes) were lost to follow-up after enrolling, seven patients (10 eyes) had passed away, and 53 patients (86 eyes) completed all but the final two-year visit. Additionally, six patients (nine eyes) were excluded because of missing FA imaging at the final visit (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Table 1 shows that there were no differences in demographic or systemic factors between eyes that progressed and those that did not. Among ocular characteristics, the progression group had a higher proportion of phakic eyes compared to the non-progression group (81.2% vs. 58.5%, P = 0.007). Additionally, eyes that progressed had a significantly higher proportion of severe NPDR (25% vs. 14.1%) and PDR-naïve (22.92% vs. 9.2%) at baseline compared with eyes that did not progress (P < 0.001). BCVA, central macular thickness, and OCT scan quality did not differ between the two groups.

Disease Progression Patterns

At two years, 48 eyes (39 patients) demonstrated diabetic eye disease progression in one or more categories as defined earlier. Among these eyes, worsening of DR severity was the most common form of progression, occurring in 20 eyes (18 eyes with one-step worsening and two eyes with two-step worsening). This included one eye that also developed vitreous hemorrhage and three eyes that underwent PRP. In addition, 14 eyes with baseline PDR (9 PDR naïve and 5 PDR quiescent) received PRP during follow-up for high-risk PDR clinical characteristics as per the DRS, defined as neovascularization in retina with pre-retinal hemorrhage or neovascularization equal or greater than one third of disc area on or within 1 diameter of the disc.37 BCVA loss ≥ 10 letters occurred in 10 eyes, and initiation of anti-VEGF therapy for DME occurred in another four eyes (one eye with concurrent BCVA loss ≥10 letters and one-step DR worsening).

The clinical complication events contributing to disease progression are summarized in Table 2. Worsening DR severity on UWF-FA and the initiation of PRP were the primary drivers of progression, occurring in 43.8% and 35.4% of progressed eyes, respectively. Figure 2 illustrates the breakdown of progression events stratified by baseline FA-based DR severity in the progressed eyes. In the NPDR groups, progression was predominantly driven by worsening DR severity, whereas in the PDR categories, PRP therapy was the most common form of progression. Of the 48 progressing eyes, over half (n = 26, 54.2%) had NPDR (mild/moderate, or severe), whereas 39.6% (n = 19) had PDR (naive or quiescent), and three eyes (6.2%) had DM no DR at baseline.

However, these associations were no longer significant after Bonferroni correction.

Eyes that progressed had significantly larger baseline parafoveal outer retinal volumes (1.330 ± 0.091 vs. 1.277 ± 0.095 mm³; P < 0.001). For every 0.1-mm³ increase in parafoveal outer retina volume, the odds of disease progression increased nearly twofold (OR = 1.87; 95% CI, 1.33–2.62; P < 0.001). Similarly, whole outer retinal volume was significantly higher in progressors (1.923 ± 0.144 vs. 1.845 ± 0.121 mm³; P < 0.001) and was associated with increased odds of progression (OR = 1.66; 95% CI, 1.25–2.19; P < 0.001) (Table 3; Fig. 3). However, given the wide confidence interval, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

In multivariate GEE logistic regression analysis, accounting for inter-eye correlation, and adjusted for age, hypertension, baseline DR severity and lens status (Table 4), outer retinal volume metrics remained independently associated with DR progression. Larger parafoveal outer retinal volume was associated with increased odds of progression (adjusted OR = 1.94; 95% CI 1.30–2.88; P = 0.001), as was larger whole outer retinal volume (adjusted OR = 1.74; 95% CI 1.29–2.33; P < 0.001). Central outer retinal volume was also associated with progression (adjusted OR = 26.3; 95% CI, 2.36–294.13; P = 0.0079). However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the wide confidence interval. In contrast, none of the inner retinal volume measures (central, parafoveal, or whole) were significantly associated with progression after multivariate adjustment. These associations are summarized in the forest plot (Fig. 4), which displays the adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for inner and outer retinal volume measures.

In the sub-analysis, a total of 35 eyes met the endpoint of structural DR progression. In multivariable GEE logistic regression models adjusted for hypertension and baseline DR severity, outer retinal volume measures remained significantly associated with structural DR progression (Supplementary Table S2). Specifically, parafoveal outer retinal volume (adjusted OR = 1.80; 95% CI, 1.21–2.68; P = 0.004) and whole outer retinal volume (adjusted OR = 1.62; 95% CI, 1.22–2.15; P = 0.001) remained significant after Bonferroni correction. Inner retinal volume measures were not significantly associated with this endpoint.

ROC Analysis of Outer Retinal Volumes in Predicting Diabetic Eye Disease Progression

To assess the predictive value of outer retinal volumes for two-year diabetic eye disease progression, adjusted ROC analyses were performed, controlling baseline DR severity, age, and hypertension (Fig. 5). Both parafoveal and whole outer retinal volumes demonstrated fair discrimination. The adjusted model for parafoveal outer retinal volume demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.714, with a sensitivity of 67.3% and specificity of 68.5%. As well, whole outer retinal volume showed comparable predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.718, sensitivity of 65.3%, and specificity of 71.3%.

Discussion

In this prospective longitudinal study, we investigated the association between baseline OCT-derived retinal volume and diabetic eye disease progression over a two-year period. We observed that eyes that progressed had significantly higher outer retinal volumes at baseline compared with those that did not (Fig. 6). Specifically, higher outer retinal volumes in the parafoveal and whole 3 × 3-mm regions were associated with disease progression and remained significant after adjustment for age, hypertension, baseline DR severity, and lens status. Central outer retinal volume was also associated with progression in the fully adjusted model; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the wide confidence interval. These findings were further supported by a sub-analysis using a structural DR progression endpoint, in which parafoveal and whole outer retinal volumes remained significantly associated with progression (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, parafoveal and whole outer retinal volumes demonstrated fair predictive performance for identifying eyes at risk of developing complications, highlighting the potential prognostic utility of volumetric OCT imaging in DR.

Given the established link between capillary nonperfusion and disease progression,12,38–40 we originally hypothesized that lower retinal volume would be associated with progression. Instead, our findings indicate that eyes that progressed exhibited larger retinal volume at baseline. We reason that these findings are in line with histological studies that show more numerous microaneurysms, venular dilation, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates, cystoid macular edema, intraretinal microvascular abnormalities, and preretinal neovascularization in the setting of progressive capillary closure as DR severity increases.41 Collectively, these vascular and exudative changes may have contributed to increased retinal volume, likely masking the volumetric loss that would otherwise result from ischemia. This finding suggests that early structural changes preceding clinical worsening may extend beyond ischemic atrophy alone. Increased outer retinal volume may reflect subclinical tissue swelling related to VEGF release and proinflammatory activity, leading to altered vascular permeability, microaneurysm formation, microscopic hemorrhage, hard exudate deposition, and subtle fluid accumulation42,43 before DR progression becomes clinically apparent. Although our study cannot establish a causal mechanism, these findings suggest that OCT-derived outer retinal volumetric changes may serve as an early structural biomarker of disease activity. Because our study eyes were free of center-involving DME at baseline, pathological findings such as small intraretinal cystic alterations would have been located away from the fovea, contributing to extrafoveal retinal volume expansion. Thus relatively higher baseline retinal volume may signify a state of heightened disease activity, driven by these pathological features, likely predisposing these eyes to future progression as observed in our study.

Commensurate with the preponderance of data that emphasize the importance of the DCP ischemia in DR pathophysiology and progression, the relationship between retinal volume and disease progression in our study was driven by outer retinal thickening, in line with these models.25,26,44–46 In support of this, a recent study from our group using the same cohort demonstrated that OCTA-derived geometric perfusion deficits in the DCP provided excellent discrimination for referable DR and significantly improved risk stratification when added to standard clinical covariates.26 The outer retina derives its vascular supply from the DCP, a capillary network that is affected early and worsens progressively as DR advances.39,45,46 Consequently, the DCP is particularly vulnerable to microaneurysm formation, with associated vascular leakage contributing to expansion of the outer retinal compartment. Supporting this mechanism, OCT angiography studies have demonstrated that the majority of microaneurysms in diabetic eyes, especially those associated with edema, are located within the DCP rather than the superficial capillary plexus.47 Consistent with these findings, Horii et al.48 found that 80.3% of microaneurysms resided mainly in inner nuclear layer, a component of the outer retina layer. Furthermore, the presence of perfused microaneurysms detected on spectral-domain OCT has been shown to correlate with increased retinal thickness,49,50 providing a plausible explanation for the outer retinal volumetric expansion observed in our study. As DR progresses to moderate NPDR and beyond, dot-blot hemorrhages and hard exudates are frequently observed within the outer plexiform layer, further contributing to outer retinal thickening.51 Together, these microvascular and exudative changes predominate in the outer retina, providing a mechanistic explanation for outer retinal thickening, thereby linking higher outer retinal volume with subsequent disease progression.

In a recent study, our group studied whether incorporating another outer retinal feature, hyperreflective abnormal areas, into a clinical risk factor model improved the detection of referable DR. We found that incorporating these lesions significantly improved model performance, increasing the model AUC from 0.865 to 0.969 for referable DR and from 0.778 to 0.865 for vision-threatening DR.52 Qualitative overlay of these hyperreflective areas onto outer retinal thickness maps from the current study demonstrated a partial spatial correspondence between hyperreflective areas52 and regions of increased retina volume (orange and red areas) on en face OCT heatmaps. Morphologically, we believe these hyperreflective areas represent hard exudates, microaneurysms, and telangiectatic vascular structures, but not intraretinal cysts, given the latter lesions' hyporeflective appearance on OCT. This may explain the partial rather than complete spatial correlation between these hyperreflective features and outer retinal thickening. Taken together, our findings underscore the contribution of microvascular and exudative alterations in the outer retina to retinal volume expansion and highlight outer retinal markers on OCT as robust indicators of diabetic eyes at high risk of progression.

Individual eyes could experience multiple progression events, because the progression criteria were not mutually exclusive. We want to highlight that although baseline outer retinal volume was higher in progressors, the development of center-involving DME requiring anti–VEGF therapy in our cohort was the least frequent complication, occurring in only 4 of 48 progressed eyes (8.33%). Worsening DR severity on UWF-FA was the most common (21 eyes [43.8%]), followed by initiation of PRP in 17 eyes (35.4%) (Table 1). Thus higher outer retinal macular volume is not just a surrogate for current or future DME; rather it may reflect overall disease activity in diabetic eyes as seen in our study. One possible interpretation is that increased outer retinal volume may reflect an early subclinical or pre-cystoid stage of diabetic macular edema, characterized by diffuse retinal thickening before the development of overt cystoid changes. Intraretinal, hyporeflective, fluid spaces on OCT may be classified based on the location of fluid within the retinal layers, these changes commonly involve outer plexiform layer/henle's fiber layer, as well as the outer nuclear layer.53 With diabetic disease progression, there could be further dysfunction of inner and outer blood retinal barriers, leakage, and accumulation of intraretinal fluid that is detectable on OCT.54 In this context, transition from diffuse retinal thickening to cystoid changes may also indicate overall retina vascular instability, resulting in worsening of severity of DR. A prior OCT study demonstrated that visual acuity in eyes with macular edema correlates strongly with the volume of residual retinal tissue between the inner and outer plexiform layers in the central 1000 to 2000 µm rather than central macular thickness.55 The authors proposed that these residual neural columns, separated by large cystic or atrophic spaces, are composed of Müller cell fibers that provide the essential structural scaffolding for bipolar cell axons as known from electron microscopy studies. Visual function was therefore proposed to depend on the integrity and density of these residual neuronal pathways connecting foveal cones to ganglion cells.55 In this context, the outer retinal volume expansion observed in our study, particularly within the parafoveal annulus, may reflect these “atrophic” cystic changes, a consequence of outer retinal ischemia and neural tissue compromise. Thus we posit that in addition to exudative edema, these tissue-expanding changes may also reflect the ischemic burden and neural compromise in eyes that will subsequently experience diabetic disease progression.

Clinically, our findings highlight the potential utility of OCT-derived outer retinal volumes for risk stratification. ROC analyses demonstrate that outer retinal volume provides fair discrimination for future disease progression. Given the widespread availability of OCT in routine retinal practice, incorporating volumetric assessment of the outer retina could offer an objective, noninvasive approach to identifying high-risk eyes. Future studies that validate the utility of outer retinal thickening as shown in our study may confirm this as a signal for eyes that deserve closer surveillance, earlier referral, or intensified systemic risk factor management to potentially mitigate progression to vision-threatening disease.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strength of this study is its prospective longitudinal design, which allows for the establishment of a temporal relationship between baseline retinal volumes and subsequent disease progression. In addition, disease progression was defined in part by worsening DR severity on UWF-FA, a modality that is more sensitive than color fundus photography for identifying advanced stages of DR.56 Our statistical approach, which incorporated GEE models to account for inter-eye correlation and multivariable analyses to control for potential confounders such as age and hypertension, was further strengthened by the application of Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Collectively, these methodological features reduce the risk of false-positive findings and support retinal volume as an independent predictor of diabetic eye disease progression.

However, this study has several limitations. First, although the cohort was prospectively followed, the sample size and number of progression events were modest, which likely contributed to wide confidence intervals for some estimates, particularly central outer retinal volume. Further studies with larger cohorts are needed to examine outer retinal structural changes in greater detail, including layer-specific outer retinal measurements and qualitative features such as ellipsoid zone integrity. Second, volumetric analyses were derived from OCT scans obtained at a single baseline time point; longitudinal changes in retinal volumes or transition from diffuse thickening to detectable macular edema over time were not assessed. Future longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate the transition from subclinical thickening to intraretinal fluid and their relationship with DR progression. Third, automated OCT segmentation algorithms can occasionally be prone to errors, which occurred in 29 eyes requiring manual correction. However, this process failed in 2/29 eyes, which were excluded from our final analysis. Finally, this was a single-center study, and external validation in larger, multi-center cohorts would be necessary to confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusions

Baseline outer retinal volume measured on OCT was independently associated with two-year diabetic eye disease progression. Parafoveal and whole outer retinal volumes demonstrated robust associations and fair discrimination, after adjusting for baseline DR severity and other clinical risk factors. These findings suggest that outer retinal thickening may serve as a structural biomarker of vascular instability in diabetic eyes. Thus incorporating OCT-derived outer retinal volumetric assessment into routine clinical evaluation may enhance risk stratification and aid in identifying eyes at higher risk for clinically meaningful disease progression, who may benefit from tightened metabolic control and closer monitoring to avert vision-threatening complications.
