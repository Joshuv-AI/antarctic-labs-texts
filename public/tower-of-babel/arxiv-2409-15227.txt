Abstract
. There is a well-established dictionary between zonotopes, hyperplane arrangements, and their (oriented) matroids. Arguably one of the most famous examples is the class of graphical zonotopes, also called acyclotopes, which encode subzonotopes of the type-A root polytope, the permutahedron. Stanley (1991) gave a general interpretation of the coefficients of the Ehrhart polynomial (integer-point counting function for a polytope) of a zonotope via linearly independent subsets of its generators. Applying this to the graphical case shows that Ehrhart coefficients count induced forests of the graph of fixed sizes. Our first goal is to extend and popularize this story to other root systems, which on the combinatorial side is encoded by signed graphs analogously to the work by Greene and Zaslavsky (1983). We compute the Ehrhart polynomial of the acyclotope in the signed case, and we give a matroid-dual construction, giving rise to tocyclotopes, and compute their Ehrhart polynomials. Applying the same duality construction to a general integral matrix gives rise to a lattice Gale zonotope, whose face structure was studies by McMullen (1971) and whose duality nature is a special instance of D’Adderio–Moci’s arithmetic matroids. We describe its Ehrhart polynomials in terms of the given matrix.

2.
Tocyclotopes and the Flow Space

We start with an (unsigned, simple) graph
G=(V,E)
with incidence matrix
\mathbf{A}_{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

with a natural block form given by the connected components of
G

The matroid

can be given in terms of

We first modify
\mathbf{A}_{G}
(which has rank

) to a full-rank matrix that still represents

well-known construction. Namely, one uses elementary row operations to create a row of zeros and then
discards the latter. The result is a matrix of the form

of
G

Matroid duality yields a representation matrix

can be described purely in terms of
G

and the
independent sets are precisely the complements of spanning sets of
G
.
(A

of a connected graph is a subset of edges whose induced subgraph
contains a spanning tree.)

The

of

is the zonotope
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{G}^{\triangle})

We note that there were choices involved when constructing
\mathbf{A}_{G}^{\triangle}
; however,
different choices correspond to resulting matrices that are unimodularly equivalent (because
elementary row operations are unimodular), and so

is unique up to unimodular equivalence.

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}}(t)=\sum_{\mathbf{F}}g(\mathbf{F})\,t^{|\mathbf{F}|}

In Remark

These ingredients yield the companion result to Stanley’s
[

]

afore-mentioned Ehrhart polynomial structure of

Theorem 2.2
.

Let

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{G}^{\triangle})}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{|E|-|V|+1}d_{k}\,t^{k}

4.2.
The Ehrhart polynomial of the acyclotope of a signed graph

Our definition of
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})
implicitly depends on a choice of

(except loops and halfedges).
However, any property of the acyclotope discussed here is independent of these choices.
For example, the face structure of the zonotope is determined by the combinatorial structure of the corresponding hyperplane arrangement, i.e., the poset of intersections. This in turn does not depend on the choice of orientation of the hyperplane normals.

is a pseudo-forest.

\Sigma

Similarly, the number

of signed tree components

5.
Lattice Gale zonotopes

We now revisit the construction of the tocyclotope of a given graph
G
: starting
with the incidence matrix of
G
, we constructed a matrix representing the cographic
matroid, from which we generated a zonotope. This process is not confined to the incidence matrix of a graph, and so we now start with a general integral matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{Z}^{{n}\times m}
of rank

lattice Gale dual
of

as the matrix
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times(m-n)}

lattice basis vectors of

By construction,

This is reminiscent of the interplay of

and
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}^{\triangle})
(which is

and
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}^{T})

and the corresponding Gale diagrams

\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}^{\triangle})
. Our point is to extend this description to the arithmetic
structure (in the sense of integer points) of the derived zonotope
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}^{T})
; hence our construction of the lattice Gale dual.

Our goal is to describe the arithmetics of the
lattice Gale zonotope

\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}^{T})
in terms of
\mathbf{A}
. While this
zonotope depends on the construction of the lattice basis that yields

, the
following main result in this section implies that the arithmetic of the lattice

.
8

8

In the language of D’Adderio–Moci a lattice zonotope corresponds to representable, torsion-free arithmetic matroid with GCD property
[

]
and the Ehrhart polynomial is a specialization of the arithmetic Tutte polynomial

Our lattice Gale zonotopes then correspond to the dual representable torsion-free arithmetic matroids.

.

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}^{T})}(t)=\sum_{S}\frac{g(\mathbf{A}_{S})}{g(\mathbf{A})}\ t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}

and

is defined as in Theorem

Remark 5.2
.

As (the usual) Gale duality can be used to efficiently compute the face structure of a

.
This on the other hand induces a matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times(m-k)}
consisting of
the columns of
\mathbf{A}
indexed by
\rho\coloneqq[m]\setminus\overline{\rho}
; note that they contain a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Corollary 5.6
.

With the same conditions and notations as in
5.4
,

(23)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{D}_{\overline{\rho}})=\frac{g(\mathbf{A}_{\rho})}{g(\mathbf{A})}\,.

Proof.

First note that

(24)

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{D}_{\overline{\rho}})=\mathbb{R}^{k}\,,

since the rows of
\mathbf{D}_{\overline{\rho}}
are linearly independent and
k\leq m-n
.
By
Remark

5.5
and
5.4
,

(25)

g(\mathbf{D}_{\overline{\rho}})=\left|\Large\nicefrac{{\mathbb{Z}^{k}}}{{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{D}_{\overline{\rho}})}}\right|=\left|\Large\nicefrac{{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})}}{{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A}_{\rho})}}\right|=\frac{\left|\nicefrac{{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}}{{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A}_{\rho})}}\right|}{\left|\nicefrac{{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}}{{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A})}}\right|}=\frac{g(\mathbf{A}_{\rho})}{g(\mathbf{A})}\,.\qed

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

By Stanley’s
Theorem

2.1
,

(26)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}^{T})}(t)=\sum_{J}g\left((\mathbf{D}^{T})_{J}\right)t^{\lvert J\rvert}

where
J
indexes linearly independent subsets of columns of
\mathbf{D}^{T}
, i.e.,
the sum is over independent sets
J\subseteq[m]
in the dual matroid.
By matroid duality, these sets correspond to spanning sets
S=[m]\setminus J
in the primal matroid.
By
Remark

5.5
,
g((\mathbf{D}^{T})_{J})=g(\mathbf{D}_{J})
, and from
5.6

we know that
g(\mathbf{D}_{J})=\frac{g(\mathbf{A}_{S})}{g(\mathbf{A})}
,
and so

(27)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}^{T})}(t)=\sum_{S}\frac{g(\mathbf{A}_{S})}{g(\mathbf{A})}\ t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}

where the sum is now over spanning sets in the primal matroid.
∎

6.
Tocyclotopes for signed graphs

The goal of this section is to construct the signed tocyclotope and then compute its Ehrhart polynomial in terms of signed graph-theoretic data.
We could define the signed tocyclotope as a lattice Gale dual of the signed acyclotope as explained in the previous section.
However, we will take a slightly different route by first using a more combinatorial and concrete approach via bidirected network matrices.
This will turn out to fit into our more general framework.

As mentioned in
Section

4.1
, oriented signed graphs are equivalent to bidirected graphs.
Bidirected graphs were first defined by Edmonds and Johnson

[
15
,
16
]
.
Appa and Kotnyek
[
1
]
studied a bidirectional generalization of network matrices.
One of their central result, which we will use, are conditions on when the inverse of those matrices is integral.
In general, those inverses are half-integral, a result that first appeared in
[
8
]
.
For more information see also the references in
[
7
,
8
,
17
]
.

From now on we want to assume that our (signed) graph is connected.
If it is not connected, we can apply the following results to each of the connected
components and then take the appropriate product;
we will give more details in
Remark

6.6
below.
Additionally, we will assume that the incidence matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{{n}\times m}
has
full rank, i.e.,
\rank(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})=n
.
If this is not the case then the signed graph is balanced and can
hence be considered as an unsigned graph.

6.1.
Binet matrices and the tocyclotope

We will give the definition of a binet matrix
9
9

9

“The term binet is used here as a short form for
bi
directed
net
work,
but by coincidence it also matches the name of Jacques Binet (1786–1856) who worked on the foundations of matrix theory and gave the rule of matrix multiplication.”
[
17
, page 46]

as it was introduced by Appa and Kotnyek
[
1
]
in order to generalize network matrices to bidirected graphs.

Let
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
be the incidence matrix of the signed graph
\Sigma

and let
T\subseteq E
be a subset of the edges of
\Sigma
that forms a basis, as
discussed in
4.1
.
This implies that the submatrix
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
of
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
formed by choosing the columns indexed by
T
is invertible over
\mathbb{R}
.
After reordering columns, we can write the incidence matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
as
[\mathbf{R}\,|\,\mathbf{T}]
, where
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times(m-n)}
is the matrix formed from columns indexed by
R\coloneqq E\setminus T
.
Then we multiply
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}=[\mathbf{R}\,|\,\mathbf{T}]
with
\mathbf{T}^{-1}
from the left to obtain

(28)

\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}=[\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R}\,|\,\mathbf{I}]=[\mathbf{B}\,|\,\mathbf{I}]\,,

where
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
is the unit matrix and
\mathbf{B}\coloneqq\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(m-n)}
.
The matrix
\mathbf{B}
is called
binet matrix
. Appa and Kotnyek further present a graphical algorithm to compute binet matrices
[
1
]
.
The algorithm gives an easier and more direct way of computing binet matrices, relies on the intuition of flows on bidirected graphs, and is a useful perspective to prove properties of binet matrices. The algorithm was reformulated in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 6.1

(
[
1
, Lemma 17]
)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a signed graph and
T\subseteq E
be a subset that forms a maximal pseudo-forest.
The binet matrix
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R}
is integral if and only if
one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

every connected component in the maximal pseudo-forest spanned by
T
is a (signed) halfedge-tree, or

(2)

\Sigma
does not contain halfedges and
T
spans one connected component.

Since we assumed the signed graph
\Sigma
to be connected, we can always choose a pseudo-tree
T\subseteq E
that fulfills one of the conditions in
6.1
:
If the signed graph contains halfedges, choose a connected basis that contains one of the halfedges (case
1
in
6.1
),
otherwise choose any other connected basis (case
2
in
6.1
).
Then we know that the matrix
[\mathbf{B}\,|\,\mathbf{I}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
has integral coefficients.

It is immediate from the construction that the rows of
\mathbf{D}^{T}\coloneqq[\mathbf{I}\,|-\mathbf{B}^{T}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-n)\times m}
,
where here
\mathbf{I}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-n)\times(m-n)}
and
-\mathbf{B}^{T}=-(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R})^{T}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-n)\times n}
,
are contained in the kernel of
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
.
Since the matrix has full rank
m-n
, its rows span the kernel of
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
.

Note that
g([\mathbf{I}\,|-\mathbf{B}^{T}])=1
because the maximal minor given by the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}
equals one and hence the greatest common divisor of all minors as well.
From
Remark

5.5
it follows that the rows of
[\mathbf{I}\,|-\mathbf{B}^{T}]
form a lattice basis for
\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
So
\mathbf{D}^{T}=[\mathbf{I}\,|-\mathbf{B}^{T}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-n)\times m}
is the transpose of a lattice Gale dual of
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
.

Hence, we define the
tocyclotope for signed graphs
as the zonotope
{\mathcal{Z}([\mathbf{I}|-\mathbf{B}^{T}])}
.
As in the case of unsigned graphs, this zonotope depends on our choice of
\mathbf{T}
; however, its
face structure is independent of this choice (by
[
18
]
) and so is its
Ehrhart polynomial (by
Theorem

5.1
).

Parallel to the definitions for graphs, the
flow space
of the signed graph

\Sigma
is
\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})
, and the
signed cographic arrangement
is
the hyperplane arrangement induced by the coordinate hyperplanes of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
on

\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})
; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
9
,
10
]
.The proof of the following lemma is almost verbatim as that of
2.3
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a signed graph without coloops and
let
\mathbf{D}^{T}=[\mathbf{I}|-\mathbf{B}^{T}]
be as described above.
The linear surjection
\mathbf{D}^{T}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
maps the flow space

\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})
bijectively to
\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
. Thus the columns of the matrix
\mathbf{D}^{T}
are normal vectors for an isomorphic copy of the signed cographic arrangement living in
\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
.

Although this is not a main theme of this paper, we add a remark about the face structure
of the tocyclotope, as it follows directly from (oriented matroid) duality.
We recall that a cycle is a minimally dependent set of edges that is oriented in such a way
that it has neither a sink nor a source.
Recall that an orientation is totally cyclic if every (bioriented) edge is contained in a cycle.
The regions of the signed corgraphic arrangement, and therefore the vertices of the signed tocyclotope, correspond bijectively to totally cyclic orientations of the signed graph
\Sigma
, see
[
6
, proof of Theorem 4.5.(b)]
.
Higher dimensional faces of the signed tocyclotope can be understood via the flats of the signed corgraphic arrangement.

6.2.
The Ehrhart polynomial of the tocyclotope

The goal of this section is to prove the combinatorial description of the coefficients in the Ehrhart polynomial of signed tocyclotopes.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a connected signed graph whose incidence matrix has full rank.
Choose a connected basis
T\subseteq E
that contains a halfedge if
\Sigma
contains an halfedge.
Then the Ehrhart polynomial of the tocyclotope
\mathcal{Z}([\mathbf{I}\,|-(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R})^{T}])\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m-n}
is

(29)

\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}([\mathbf{I}\,|-\mathbf{B}^{T}])}(t)=\begin{cases}\sum_{S}2^{\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)}t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}&\text{if $\Sigma$ contains a halfedge,}\\
\sum_{S}2^{\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)-1}t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}&\text{if $\Sigma$ does not contain a halfedge,}\end{cases}

where the sums run over all sets
S\subseteq E
that contain a basis of
\Sigma
, i.e.,
\Sigma({S})
contains a maximal pseudo-forest of
\Sigma
, and
\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)
is the smallest number of pseudo-tree components plus loop-tree components that a maximal pseudo-forest contained in
S
can have.

We will again apply Stanley’s
Theorem

2.1
for zonotopes.
For that we need a combinatorial understanding of
g(\mathbf{J})
, where the columns in the submatrix

\mathbf{J}
of
\mathbf{D}^{T}=[\mathbf{I}\,|-(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R})^{T}]
are linearly independent, i.e.,
they correspond to independent sets
J
in the dual signed graphic matroid and,
therefore, correspond to subsets of edges
S=E\setminus J
in the signed graph
\Sigma
that contain a basis, i.e., a maximal pseudo-forest.

Corollary 6.4
.

A subset of columns
\mathbf{J}
of
\mathbf{D}^{T}=[\mathbf{I}\,|-(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{R})^{T}]
(as constructed above) is linearly independent if and only if the subset
\mathbf{S}
of columns in
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
indexed by
S=E\setminus J
is a spanning set, i.e.,
S
contains a maximal pseudo-tree in
\Sigma
.
In this case,

(30)

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{J})=\frac{g(\mathbf{S})}{g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})}\,.

Proof.

This follows from
5.6
.
∎

Therefore, it remains to understand the parameter
g(\mathbf{S})
for spanning sets in the signed graph.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
S\subseteq E
be a spanning set. Then there exists a maximal forest
F\subseteq S
such that

g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma({S})})=g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma({F})})
.
Moreover, this maximal forest
F
will be one with a minimal number of pseudo-tree components plus loop-tree components.

Proof.

From
Remark

5.5
we know that
g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma({S})})
is the greatest common divisor of all minors of size
n
in
\mathbf{S}
.
Since all minors are powers of
2
(by
4.3
), the greatest common divisor is the lowest power of
2
that appears.
The selection of columns in
\mathbf{S}
for which the minor attains its minimum corresponds to a forest
F\subseteq S
we are looking for. Then

(31)

g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma({S})})=g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma({F})})=2^{\operatorname{pc}(F)+\operatorname{lc}(F)}=2^{\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)}.

∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.3
.

By
Theorem

5.1

(32)

\operatorname{ehr}_{\operatorname{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)}(t)=\sum_{S}\frac{g(\mathbf{A}_{S})}{g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})}\ t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}

where the sum is over all spanning sets
S
in the matroid represented by
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
,
i.e., over all subsets
S\subseteq E
that contain a maximal pseudo-forest of
\Sigma
.

Note that for connected signed graphs
\Sigma
of full rank,
\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})=\mathbb{Z}^{n}

(and hence
g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})=1
by
Remark

5.5
) if and only if
\Sigma
contains a halfedge by
4.3
.
In the case of connected signed graphs without halfedges we can apply
5.6
, and we will get a correction factor of
2
since then
g(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})=2
again by
4.3
.
This explains the case distinction in (
29
) and the difference of
a factor of 2 between the cases.

The last missing piece now is to understand
g(\mathbf{A}_{S})=g(\mathbf{S})
. This is given in
6.5
: we need to find the minimal possible number
\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)
of loop-tree components plus pseudo-tree components in a maximal pseudo forest in the spanning set
S
.
Then we arrive at

(33)

\operatorname{ehr}_{\operatorname{\mathcal{T}}(\Sigma)}(t)=\begin{cases}\sum_{S}2^{\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)}t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}&\text{if $\Sigma$ contains a halfedge,}\\
\sum_{S}2^{\operatorname{pc}(S)+\operatorname{lc}(S)-1}t^{m-\lvert S\rvert}&\text{if
$\Sigma$ does not contain any halfedges.}\end{cases}

∎

We conclude this section with the extension of the results to signed graphs that are not connected.

Remark 6.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be an arbitrary signed graph with connected components
\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{c}
.
Then we can order nodes and edges so that the incidence matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}
has a block structure given by the connected components:

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{1}}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{2}}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\dots&\dots&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{c}}\end{bmatrix}.

This implies that the acyclotope of the signed graph
\Sigma
is simply the Cartesian product of the acyclotopes of the connected components:

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})=\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{1}})\times\dots\times\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{c}})\,.

Hence the Ehrhart polynomial of
\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})
is a product of Ehrhart polynomials

(36)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma})}=\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{1}})}\cdots\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{A}_{\Sigma_{c}})}\,.

A similar decomposition property can be found on the level of matroids.
Here the signed graphical matroid
M(\Sigma)
is the direct sum

(37)

\displaystyle M(\Sigma)=M(\Sigma_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus M(\Sigma_{c})\,.

This structure is preserved under taking matroid duals, hence

(38)

\displaystyle M^{\triangle}(\Sigma)=M^{\triangle}(\Sigma_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus M^{\triangle}(\Sigma_{c})\,.

So we can also apply our duality construction block by block to achieve a dual representation

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{\Sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{D}_{1}&\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\dots&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{D}_{c}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Then the signed tocyclotope is the Cartesian product

\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{\Sigma})=\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{1})\times\dots\times\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{c})
,
and hence its Ehrhart polynomial is again a product of Ehrhart polynomials

(40)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{\Sigma})}=\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{1})}\cdots\operatorname{ehr}_{\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{D}_{c})}\,.

7.
Open Problems

We conclude with two concrete open questions.
First, the lattice points in the acyclotope (for unsigned graphs) arise as indegree vectors from all orientations of the graph.
While this correspondence is bijective for acyclic orientations, it is not for general orientations.
For tocyclotopes we know that the vertices correspond to totally cyclic orientations.
Is there a similar interpretation for all lattice points in the tocyclotope?
One way to address this question might be via the algorithm in
[
1
]
.
Second, as we mentioned, the construction of the tocyclotope in the unsigned case depends
on certain choices, but the resulting zonotopes are unimodularly equivalent.
In the signed case, we again made certain choices when constructing the tocyclotope (and
more generally, lattice Gale dual zonotopes), and we showed that the resulting Ehrhart
polynomials are identical. Do we also have unimodular equivalence?
