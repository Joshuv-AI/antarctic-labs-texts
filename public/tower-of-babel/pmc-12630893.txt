A real time segmentation network for lithium battery surface defect detection
Authors: Jiaxing Xie, Peiwen Wu, Jiasi Chen, Yucheng Pan, Hongsheng Zhong, Zihao Deng, Daozong Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lithium Battery Surface defect detection plays a critical role in industrial applications. The current detection tasks face two major challenges: (1) significant variation in defect scales, especially the difficulty in capturing and identifying tiny defects; (2) the need for the detection process to simultaneously meet strict standards of high accuracy and real-time performance. To address these issues, this paper proposes a Dual Attention Pyramid Segmentation Network (DAPSeg), which ensures high precision while achieving real-time segmentation of surface defects. The Selective Kernel Module (SKM) assists the backbone network in adaptively extracting multi-scale features according to the importance of different scales, addressing the significant scale variation in defect scenarios. In the design part of the segmentation head, a lightweight architecture is adopted. The Blueprint Separable Layer (BSL) aims to capture more semantic information from different levels of the encoder while further improving the model’s inference efficiency; the Dual Attention Feature Fusion Module (DAFFM) is used for multi-scale feature fusion, obtaining finer segmentation regions from both spatial and channel dimensions. Additionally, we use the diffusion model to generate additional image data on the proposed lithium battery surface defect segmentation dataset (LB-SD) to alleviate the overfitting problem of model training caused by the imbalance of different defect samples. Experimental results show that DAPSeg achieves mIoU scores of 79.57%, 83.53%, and 89.10% on LB-SD, MT, and MSD, respectively, with a processing speed of 74.09 FPS. Compared with other state-of-the-art models, DAPSeg strikes a good balance between accuracy and inference speed, while also demonstrating strong generalization performance.

Introduction

Surface defect detection aims to identify abnormal areas on workpiece surfaces to ensure product qualification and reliable quality, while also meeting the urgent demands of industrial applications. Detection targets often face multiple complex defect challenges1, such as scratches, dents, foreign object coverage, and depressions, which impose high requirements on detection accuracy and real-time performance. However, traditional manual inspection methods are inefficient, costly, and susceptible to subjective factors2,3. Moreover, traditional texture analysis-based methods are not universally applicable to various specific tasks. The rise of deep learning technologies has provided new solutions to this problem. Existing studies have successfully applied convolutional neural network (CNN)-based methods4,5 to various industrial fields, such as magnetic tiles6, metals7, road cracks8–10, and batteries11. These methods demonstrate robustness across different surface areas and offer specific solutions for challenges like small objects. However, most of these methods are still based on classification and object detection, making it difficult to fully meet high precision requirements.

To achieve pixel-level detection of industrial surface defects, many general-purpose and real-time semantic segmentation networks12–14 have been applied to surface defect detection algorithms, accelerating the rapid development of industrial defect detection. However, existing network architectures often face a trade-off between accuracy and speed. On one hand, methods based on MaskFormer15 and Swin Transformer16,17 offer outstanding segmentation accuracy, but their high computational complexity makes it difficult to meet the stringent real-time requirements of industrial scenarios. On the other hand, lightweight networks such as MobileNet18,19 and ShuffleNet20–22 are fast but often fail to achieve satisfactory accuracy when dealing with defects of varying positions, scales, and shapes. Therefore, we focus on a high-precision real-time segmentation network, selecting the fully convolutional network architecture SegNeXt23 as our baseline. The backbone of SegNeXt incorporates an attention mechanism, and its multi-scale convolutional attention proves more efficient than standard convolution and self-attention. However, directly applying these methods to surface defect segmentation tasks does not yield optimal results. Surface defect segmentation differs from ordinary scene segmentation due to its unique challenges, such as low contrast, small targets, irregular shapes, and random position distribution24,25. These challenges necessitate the design of a lightweight, high-precision network. Consequently, our work focuses on enhancing small target detection, global context, and local feature representation.

In this study, to achieve a balance between accuracy and speed in lithium battery surface defect detection scenarios with significant size variations, we designed an encoder-decoder framework for lithium battery surface defect detection and proposed an efficient Dual Attention Pyramid Segmentation Network (DAPSeg). DAPSeg employs an improved MSCAN-S as the encoder, which activates spatial attention through simple element-wise multiplication and adapts to extract defect information at different scales by introducing the Selective Kernel Module (SKM). Simultaneously, we designed a novel segmentation head for DAPSeg to precisely locate and segment defects. The Blueprint Separable Layer (BSL) captures and enhances local details, while the Dual Attention Feature Fusion Module (DAFFM) fuses features at different scales to acquire long-range contextual information, resulting in more complete surface defect segmentation. Additionally, we compiled a Lithium Battery Surface Defect (LB-SD) dataset with pixel-level annotations, which poses significant detection challenges due to the varying sizes of defects. Extensive experiments conducted on the LB-SD, Magnetic Tile (MT), and Mobile Phone Screen Surface Defect (MSD) datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach. On the LB-SD dataset, DAPSeg achieved 79.57% mIoU and processed 512 × 512 images at a speed of 74.09 FPS on an RTX 3060 GPU, meeting the requirements for high accuracy and real-time performance. Related work

Defect detection

Defect detection involves locating defect regions in an image and classifying defect types. In early surface defect detection methods, defects were annotated using box-level object detection. Dang et al.26 proposed a sewer defect localization framework named DefectRT, based on the Detection Transformer (DETR). Su et al.11 introduced an object detection called BAF-Detector, which leverages a Bidirectional Attention Feature Pyramid Network (BAFPN) to locate multi-scale defects in photovoltaic cell EL images. However, these methods can only approximate defect regions using one or more closely matched bounding boxes, and they fail to accurately delineate the defect boundaries. The prevailing surface defect detection techniques employ pixel-level criteria for precise identification and segmentation of defect regions. This approach accurately provides information on the shape, size, and location of defects, thereby ensuring a reliable evaluation of surface quality27,28. L. Yang et al.29 developed an end-to-end nondestructive defect detection network, NDD-Net, which demonstrated excellent performance on two public datasets with severe class imbalance issues. L. Yang et al.30 proposed a pixel-level deep segmentation network based on the encoder-decoder architecture, achieving more efficient feature representation. However, semantic segmentation is a prediction task that necessitates high-resolution images and robust algorithms due to its extensive image detail requirements. The distribution of defect areas is typically sparse, constituting a very small proportion of the entire image, thereby complicating defect segmentation. Consequently, enhancing the resolution of images and the context information extraction capability of algorithms, as well as effectively managing sparse and minute defect areas, are pressing issues in the surface defect detection domain.

Light-weight network

Compared to traditional deep neural networks, lightweight networks possess fewer parameters and reduced computational complexity. This characteristic allows for efficient defect detection in resource-constrained environments such as embedded systems and mobile devices. This is typically achieved through the development of sophisticated network architectures, including MobileNet31,32 and EfficientNet33,34. Lu et al.4 used MobileNetV2 to extract multi-scale feature maps, and then employed MFEF and RA modules to fuse these features for prediction, achieving end-to-end real-time defect segmentation. J. Chen et al.8 designed a multi-scale mobile attention-based network for road defect detection. Despite significant improvements in inference speed, these networks often fall short in terms of detection accuracy in complex industrial scenarios, failing to meet industry standards. Given the substantial enhancements in performance observed in contemporary industrial equipment, one may consider employing more intricate semantic segmentation networks that deliver superior precision and robust generalization. These networks perform exceptionally well in high-performance computing environments, fully leveraging existing hardware resources to achieve efficient and high-precision segmentation tasks. This is typically accomplished by designing robust network frameworks and efficient modules, such as CNN-based HRNet35, OCRNet36, PIDNet37, SegNeXt, and Transformer-based SegFormer38, Swin Transformer39, among others. Wang, Chen et al.40 developed a bidirectional strip refinement attention (BSRA), which adaptively captures long-range spatial and channel dependencies with directional information, thereby enhancing the accuracy of defect detection in polycrystalline solar cells. Si et al.41 proposed an intelligent segmentation model for high-speed rail surface defects based on the Swin Transformer, achieving high segmentation accuracy. Advanced semantic segmentation networks facilitate rapid inference while maintaining high accuracy, making them suitable for complex industrial inspection tasks. This aligns with the need for efficient and precise segmentation in contemporary industrial settings.

Attention mechanisms

Attention mechanisms are prevalently employed in modern semantic segmentation networks to enhance their capacity to capture intricate scenes and details42,43. These mechanisms enable the model to assign varying weights to different sections of the input image, thereby extracting more critical information. This focus on specific areas within the image improves both the accuracy and efficiency of segmentation. The SE module44 obtains global information for each channel through global average pooling, then adjusts channel weights through a fully connected layer, thereby enhancing useful features and suppressing irrelevant ones. BAM45 and CBAM46 introduced spatial attention mechanisms in a similar manner, highlighting local significant areas and increasing attention to important spatial regions, thereby improving segmentation results. To address the issue of small defect targets, T. Liu & He47 designed a triple attention semantic segmentation network. Li et al.48 introduced SKConv, which aggregates information from multiple kernels to achieve adaptive receptive field sizes for neurons. Recently popular visual transformers, which introduce Self-Attention and Multi-Head Attention49, can establish long-distance dependencies, thereby expanding the model’s receptive field. However, they often overlook the adaptability of the channel dimension50. SegNeXt introduced an efficient attention mechanism that evokes spatial attention through multi-scale convolutional features, but neglected that targets of different scales have varying attention requirements for different kernel sizes. Therefore, we incorporate SK units into this multi-scale convolution to adaptively aggregate features extracted by multiple kernels, thereby more effectively capturing defect targets of different scales.

Methodology

Dual attention pyramid segmentation

Figure 1 illustrates the overall architecture of the Dual Attention Pyramid Segmentation (DAPSeg). DAPSeg is based on an encoder-decoder framework, with the encoder utilizing an enhanced MSCAN for deep feature extraction and intra-image correlation modeling. MSCAN’s multi-branch structure enables multi-scale feature extraction, and its multi-core strip convolution is particularly effective in identifying elongated defects, thereby allowing it to focus on multi-scale defects in complex environments. The decoder, designed based on FPN, captures multi-scale feature representations through a pyramid structure, gradually integrating feature maps and semantic information of varying resolutions.

Fig. 1Framework of the proposed DAPSeg for industrial surface defect image semantic segmentation.

Framework of the proposed DAPSeg for industrial surface defect image semantic segmentation.

In this study, we integrate a Selective Kernel Module (SKM) into the MSCA module of the encoder to facilitate adaptive fusion of attention. Specifically, feature maps and attention weights derived from attention branches with varying kernel sizes are weighted and fused to accommodate defects of diverse scales. While SegNeXt authors suggest that the initial layer output by the convolutional network is overly rich in low-level information and thus discards its feature data (as cited in literature), this approach may not be optimal for industrial defect detection. Industrial defect types predominantly consist of small-scale defects randomly generated at various locations and large-scale defects with irregular shapes. Consequently, defect segmentation necessitates the extraction of high-level semantic information and the low-level information from the first layer cannot be disregarded. Experimental results presented in Section “Experiments” further corroborate that discarding the feature map of the first layer leads to a marked decline in network performance and results in coarser segmentation. Therefore, this paper employs feature maps from four stages for defect segmentation.

The decoder incorporates a spatial feature fusion pyramid structure, introducing the DAPSegHead. Initially, a Blueprint Separable Layer (BSL) is employed to consolidate the channels of the four feature maps generated by the backbone network, thereby enhancing defect features. Subsequently, the Dual Attention Feature Fusion Module (DAFFM) is applied to capture and fuse high-level and low-level semantics. This approach effectively mitigates the issue of overlooking small targets in high-level features and eliminates complex and redundant information in low-level features.

MSCAN-SK

The encoder examined in this study is constructed based on the MSCAN of SegNeXt. MSCAN, a type of convolutional attention, proves to be more efficient and effective than the self-attention mechanism found in transformers. Its component, MSCA, comprises three elements: a depth-wise convolution for local information aggregation, multi-branch deep-wise strip convolutions for capturing multi-scale contextual information, and a 1 × 1 convolution for channel adjustment. The application of attention mechanisms with varying kernel sizes enables the extraction of features at different scales, thereby facilitating the detection of targets at each scale and enhancing the model’s detection accuracy. However, when integrating attention, the authors merely added the outputs of all branches without considering that the degree of defect detection at different scales varies according to the need for attention extraction from different kernels. Consequently, this paper redesigns this attention mechanism to enable the network to adaptively select and fuse feature map information from different receptive fields through learning. Specifically, we incorporate the working mechanism of attention fusion in SK (Selective Kernel Convolution), introducing an SK structure into MSCA to form MSCA-SK, as depicted in Fig. 2. By utilizing convolution kernels of multiple sizes within the network, it allows the network to adaptively choose the appropriate scale and enhance the segmentation efficiency and effect of defects at different scales in a soft-attention manner through adaptive kernel selection. Blueprint separable layer

To address the issue of information loss and ensure the accuracy of surface defect detection in lithium batteries, multi-scale information becomes crucial, particularly when dealing with defects of varying scales. To bridge the semantic gap between features at different scales, this study aggregates local detail information and global context through layer-by-layer connections. Since the four feature maps output by the backbone network (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{2}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{3}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{4}$$\end{document}) differ in terms of channel number, scale, and receptive field, their semantic information is misaligned and contains some noise. Therefore, before fusing these feature maps, it is generally necessary to further adjust and enhance them to better capture contextual information, thereby improving feature discrimination.

Specifically, we introduce a lightweight Blueprint Separable Layer (BSL) for unification and feature enhancement in the channel dimension of the decoder. To prevent the loss of small defect features during the training process of 3 × 3 convolution11, BSL decomposes a standard convolution into a point-wise 1 × 1 convolution followed by a depth-wise 3 × 3 convolution. The Conv1 × 1 aligns the semantics of different feature maps, while the subsequent DWConv3 × 3 further enhances the aligned defect information, resulting in more precise feature fusion.

Conversely, if the Depthwise Separable Convolution (DSConv) structure is used—enhancing features before semantic alignment—the performance will deteriorate. This can be explained as follows: First, in the initial Depthwise Convolution (DWConv), each kernel can only benefit from a single feature map, limiting the ability to fully exploit the complementarity and correlation among multiple feature maps, which restricts the model’s capacity to capture and represent complex features at the early stage. Second, channel unification typically involves some form of normalization or averaging of feature maps, which may weaken the strength of specific patterns learned by the DWConv local regions.

Blueprint Separable Convolution (BSConv), the inverse version of DSConv, has been demonstrated to achieve more efficient separation of standard convolutions in many cases compared to DSConv51. BSConv is commonly used in Backbones as a replacement for standard convolutions. In its original design, to ensure that the weights can be negative, no activation or normalization is applied after pointwise convolution, thereby preserving the original numerical range of the convolutional output. However, unlike the original literature, the BSL discussed in this paper is applied to channel unification and feature enhancement within the segmentation head. To ensure the effectiveness and robustness of feature representation after channel unification and to meet the task requirements of the segmentation head, activation or normalization must be applied. Dual attention feature fusion module

Previous studies52 have demonstrated that different layers contain distinct information. If simple element-wise addition or concatenation methods are used for fusion, the information from different layers cannot be effectively extracted. Such basic fusion approaches may lead to the loss or confusion of important defect features, particularly in cases involving small objects or significant variations in object scale.

To address this challenge, this paper designs the Dual Attention Feature Fusion Module (DAFFM). This module adopts a layer-wise aggregation approach, aiming to effectively fuse contextual information from different layers, thereby better addressing the issue of target scale variation in defect detection, especially for small targets. In DAFFM, two attention mechanisms are introduced: Channel Attention (CA) and Spatial Attention (SA). CA assists the model in identifying the importance of different feature channels, while SA helps the model focus on critical information in different locations of the image. By combining these two attention mechanisms, DAFFM achieves refined fusion of features across different layers, enabling the model to more accurately respond to target scale variations in defect segmentation tasks.

In this study, the high-level features undergo 1 × 1 Conv and bilinear interpolation upsampling to restore feature information, and are then concatenated with low-level features along the channel dimension, achieving the fusion of high-level and low-level semantics. Specifically, the high-level and low-level features are concatenated and then fused through the CBR to obtain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{m}$$\end{document}. Loss functions

During the training phase, a weighted combination of cross-entropy (CE) loss and Lovász-Softmax (Lovász) loss53 is employed as the loss function, facilitating more effective network construction and optimization. This combination not only retains the stability of CE loss in global classification but also leverages the strengths of Lovász loss in boundary handling, achieving a complementary balance between the two. Therefore, the overall loss is written as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}Loss=\beta\:{Loss}_{lovaz}+\left(1-\beta\:\right){Loss}_{ce}\:\end{array}$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} is a loss balancing factor. The CE loss function is widely used in classification tasks, particularly in multi-class classification problems. It measures the difference between the probability distribution of the model’s output and the true label distribution, optimizing the accuracy (Acc) metric. The CE loss function is written as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{Loss}_{ce}=-\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}\sum\:_{c=1}^{N}{y}_{i,c}\text{log}\left({p}_{i,c}\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} is the number of samples, C is the number of classes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i,c}$$\end{document} is a binary indicator (0 or 1) if class label c is the correct classification for sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{p}_{i,c}$$\end{document} is the predicted probability that sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document} belongs to class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:c$$\end{document}.

Lovász loss is a loss function specifically designed to optimize the Intersection over Union (IoU) metric, which is commonly used in tasks like image segmentation. In this defect detection task, Lovász loss addresses the limitation of CE loss in directly optimizing the IoU. Additionally, in industrial defect detection, class imbalance is common, and Lovász loss demonstrates excellent performance on imbalanced datasets. Compared to traditional pixel-level loss functions, Lovász loss results in more precise segmentation outcomes.

Experiments

Defect datasets

In this paper, the DAPSeg model is evaluated through the LB-SD dataset, and compared with the other two public data sets with similar defect characteristics to verify the generality and generalization of the model.

LB-SD

The LB-SD dataset was collected from the production line at the main factory of Contemporary Amperex Technology Co. Limited between October and December 2022, using a Hikvision MV- CA032-10GC camera with an effective resolution of 3.2 million pixels. The dataset comprises 325 sets of lithium batteries from different production batches, with each battery having four images taken of its anode and cathode, both front and back, totaling 1300 images. After being screened and annotated by professional technicians, 1272 images were selected, with 1018 images used as the training set and 254 images as the test set. As shown in Fig. 5, the dataset contains six types of defects—Tab damaged, Nonmetal impurity, Metal scrap, Electrode fold, Electrode damaged and Electrode crack—and one type of normal image. In previous studies, many methods only considered the positive samples of annotated defect classes in the dataset, neglecting the unannotated negative samples. This omission led to a high false positive rate for unannotated negative samples, resulting in poor visualization quality of the predicted results and unclear classification boundaries. Therefore, it is essential to include defect-free samples in the training dataset to train the model to recognize non-defective areas, thereby reducing false positives.

The number of images for the six types of defects in lithium batteries is shown in Table **. Due to the complexity of the industrial environment, the number of surface defect images collected from lithium batteries is limited. Deep learning-based defect detection models typically require a large amount of training data to achieve good performance. Additionally, there is a significant imbalance in the number of images across different defect categories in the dataset, especially the “Nonmetal impurity” category, which accounts for the majority of the images (92.37%). This imbalance can cause the model to be biased towards the categories with more samples during training, thereby affecting its generalization ability, particularly in defect detection tasks. To mitigate this issue, data expansion algorithms are used to expand and balance the training samples. Specifically, the study uses a generative model (Stable Diffusion) for data expansion.

Stable Diffusion models are highly regarded for their ability to generate high-quality, precise, and vivid images. In this study, we used Stable Diffusion 1.5 to synthesize images. This model generates realistic images through the U-Net architecture and latent diffusion process. To further enhance the image quality, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) technology was incorporated, which customizes the model by updating only the most critical weights, effectively reducing the required amount of training data while avoiding structural changes to the model. This approach has demonstrated significant performance improvements in industrial applications, with the generated images closely resembling real images in detail and accuracy, while high-quality synthetic images can be obtained from the base model without the need for complex text commands54–56. The comparison of synthetic defect images and real defect images generated using Stable Diffusion is shown in Table **, where the red solid boxes outline the corresponding defect areas. It can be intuitively seen that the synthetic defect images basically retain the physical properties and texture features of lithium battery defects. Additionally, Table 1 demonstrates that the data balancing strategy through data augmentation, using Stable Diffusion, is helpful for scenarios with insufficient and imbalanced training samples. The data augmentation methods include conventional techniques such as rotation, scaling, mirroring, Gaussian blur, etc. (Table 2).

MT

The MT dataset57 contains five types of defects and one defect-free class, including uneven, break, crack, blowhole, and fray, with a total of 1344 images and pixel-level annotation masks. Since MT does not provide a partitioning protocol, we randomly split the training and test sets in a 4:1 ratio.

MSD

The MSD dataset13 includes three different classes of mobile screen defects, including oil, scratch and stain, with a total of 1200 images at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Similarly, we randomly split the training and test sets in a 4:1 ratio.

Implementation details

The model training and evaluation were conducted on the Pycharm platform using the MMSegmentation in Pytorch. The experimental system was running Linux with 24GB of RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. Optimizers play a crucial role in training deep learning models, as a suitable optimizer ensures rapid model convergence. Experimental results show58 that the Lion optimizer has better generalization performance and stronger anti-noise capability, often performing on par with or even surpassing AdamW in most tasks. Therefore, we selected Lion as the optimizer for the DAPSeg model, with an initial learning rate set to 6e-6, betas set to 0.95 and 0.98, and a weight decay of 0.1. Since the Backbone is pre-trained to accelerate convergence, while the Decode head is not, the learning rate for the Decode head is set to 10 times that of the Backbone to speed up its convergence. The learning rate strategy employed a Warmup strategy, with the first 1500 iterations using a LinearLR strategy to rise from 1e-7, followed by a PolyLR strategy to gradually decrease it to 1e-8 by the last iteration.

Given the varying resolutions in the dataset images, it is necessary to format and enhance them. The model’s input size is set, with high-resolution images cropped to fit the input size, while images smaller than the input size are padded with zero pixels. Each batch of images undergoes random flipping and resizing, meaning that in each epoch, images are trained with different feature information. The input size for the LB-SD dataset is 512 × 512, with a batch size of 2, a maximum of 80,000 iterations, and evaluation intervals every 1000 iterations. The input size for the MT dataset is 256 × 256, with a batch size of 8, a maximum of 40,000 iterations, and evaluation intervals every 500 iterations. The input size for the MSD dataset is 512 × 512, with a batch size of 4, a maximum of 40,000 iterations, and evaluation intervals every 1000 iterations. To ensure the reliability of the experiments, all training runs had the random seed set to 0.

Metrics

This paper evaluates the performance of the DAPSeg using the following metrics: Accuracy (Acc), Intersection over Union (IoU), and F1 Score (F1). These three metrics are based on a confusion matrix containing True Positives (TP), False Positives (FP), True Negatives (TN), and False Negatives (FN). They are used to assess the predictions for each defect class, and the average of all metrics (mAcc, mIoU, and mF1) is used to evaluate overall model performance. For each class, Acc represents the proportion of correctly predicted samples out of all samples, IoU indicates the overlap between the predicted defect and the ground truth, and F1 is a harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. Comparison with state-of-the-art methods

To assess the effectiveness of DAPSeg, this paper conducts a comparative analysis with existing methods on the LB-SD dataset. The experiments were conducted under consistent conditions. For the model comparison, classic CNN-based semantic segmentation models were chosen, including FCN59, DeepLabV3+60, PSPNet61, PoolFormer62, VAN63, OCRNet36, PIDNet37, and SegNeXt23. Additionally, the comparison of experiments also includes Transformer-based network models, SegFormer38, SwinTransformer39, as well as lightweight models AFFormer64 and SeaFormer65. All comparative methods used the objective functions described in their original papers during training, and pre-trained models were loaded to expedite convergence. Furthermore, experiments were conducted on two other public datasets to verify the generality of the proposed DAPSeg. These experiments demonstrated that its performance is not significantly affected by the scale and difficulty of the datasets, and that it outperforms other models on small-sample and small-target datasets. Tables 3 and 4, and 5 list the accuracy and evaluation metrics for all methods across all classes (with the best accuracy in each row highlighted in bold) to provide a more detailed validation of the DAPSeg model’s performance.

The result of LB-SD

Table 3 presents the experimental results on the lithium battery dataset. DAPSeg achieved an overall mIoU of 79.57%, mAcc of 87.32%, and an mF1 score of 87.58%, outperforming all other models in these metrics. Not only did it achieve the best overall performance, but DAPSeg also demonstrated superior performance in most cases for each defect class. Particularly, its accuracy in segmenting small Nonmetal impurity and few-shot Metal scrap far exceeded that of other models, with the best-performing IoUs being surpassed by 5.28% and 6.36%, respectively. Even for the Electrode damaged, where the best performance was seen in Poolformer-S12, it only outperformed DAPSeg by 0.02%. These indicate that DAPSeg is effective in modeling lithium batteries. The model exhibits sufficient generalization across all defect types and is especially advantageous in detecting small targets and few-shot defects. Compared to the original SegNeXt, DAPSeg improved mIoU, mAcc, and F1 scores by 4.99%, 7.02%, and 3.92%, respectively, with significant gains in Nonmetal impurity, Metal scrap and Electrode crack, highlighting its enhanced detection rate for small targets and difficult-to-distinguish samples. When compared with SegFormer, mIoU increased by 4.63%, mAcc by 7.07%, and mF1 by 3.48%, demonstrating DAPSeg’s superiority over the currently popular Transformer models.

Figure 5 shows the inference results of the models on the LB-SD test set, which includes typical and complex defect cases. In the results of the first and fifth columns, all models were able to locate large defects like Tab damaged and Electrode fold. However, most failed to accurately predict the complete area, especially in boundary regions and protruding corners, while DAPSeg managed to extract more complete defect contours. Although DeepLabV3 and SeaFormer detected the defect, their detected areas were incomplete, still missing very small targets. While SegFormer and Swin Transformer could locate small defects, the identified areas were too large. DAPSeg not only detected Nonmetal impurity with higher precision, but its detected region also closely matched the ground truth. For instance, in the second column, only DAPSeg was able to fully predict the slender Nonmetal impurity. Regarding the Metal scrap, which is highly similar to the background, models like FCN, DeepLabV3, SegNeXt and SeaFormer struggled to accurately separate the defect from the background, and Transformer-based models even showed internal defect region loss. DAPSeg alleviated information loss by integrating defect features across scales through the BSL. In the third column, other models’ predicted defect boundaries were wavy and jagged, whereas DAPSeg, benefiting from the DAFFM’s aggregation of different contextual information, achieved a complete and sharply defined defect region almost perfectly aligned with the ground truth. The ablation experiments show that adding any network block improves segmentation performance. Table 7 demonstrates that when SKM is added to the baseline, the network’s multi-kernel aggregation attention mechanism enables adaptive selection, enhancing the model’s sensitivity to defects of different scales. Specifically, mIoU increased by 1.54%, mAcc by 1.41%, and mF1 by 1.18%. This indicates the effectiveness of applying multi-kernel adaptive aggregation within MSCAN, achieving a 1.54% performance improvement with minimal additional parameters, and reducing error compared to the simpler multi-path addition method.

When considering the inclusion of BSL in the baseline, mIoU for the LB-SD dataset increased by 1.05%, mAcc by 0.11%, and mF1 by 0.80%. This confirms the effectiveness of the feature enhancement module introduced in DAPSeg, which not only aligns feature channels but also enhances feature information while avoiding the high parameter and computational costs of standard convolution.

Adding DAFFM to the baseline significantly improved model performance, with mIoU increasing by 1.81%, mAcc by 1.24%, and mF1 by 1.30%. This demonstrates the efficiency of the DAFFM, and the introduced channel and spatial attention mechanisms effectively retain useful channel features and enhance the spatial features of defects, making the feature fusion more targeted, while also reducing parameter and computation costs compared to other complex fusion methods.

Additionally, this paper explores the combined effects of the modules. When both BSL and DAFFM are added, mIoU and F1 increased by 1.95% and 1.40%, respectively. When SKM and BSL are combined, mIoU increased by 1.77%, and F1 increased by 1.34%. When SKM and DAFFM are used together, mIoU increased by 2.10%, and mF1 by 1.52%. When all three modules (SKM, BSL, and DAFFM) are used simultaneously, mIoU increased significantly by 2.43%, F1 by 1.80%, and mAcc by 2.24%, resulting in the best performance of 79.57% mIoU while adding 1.95 FPS on inference. This indicates that the SKM, BSL, and DAFFM are compatible, complementary, and synergistic, contributing to the overall improvement of the model.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduce DAPSeg, a novel approach for capturing surface defect features in lithium battery to improve semantic segmentation. In particular, the SKM is specifically designed to enhance feature extraction across different scales through adaptive kernel selection. To facilitate the effective transfer of multi-scale information, we propose the BSL, which aligns semantic information and enhances semantic localization. Furthermore, this paper presents the DAFFM, integrating both channel attention and spatial attention mechanisms. By enabling the interaction between high-level and low-level features, the DAFFM establishes a direct connection between local and global information, thereby mitigating local information loss caused by semantic ambiguity and strengthening the model’s global representation capability. Finally, a combined loss function and the Lion optimizer are employed to optimize the DAPSeg network framework.

Comprehensive experiments conducted on three surface defect datasets demonstrate that the proposed DAPSeg outperforms current state-of-the-art defect detection methods in accuracy. A series of ablation studies further confirm the effectiveness of each module within DAPSeg. Additionally, visualization results reveal that DAPSeg can accurately locate defect regions and delineate clear defect boundaries, underscoring the reliability of the model’s final identification results.

In future research, we aim to achieve more balanced and superior segmentation performance across all classes while maintaining high detection accuracy. Furthermore, we will optimize the model framework to enable faster inference speeds for industrial applications.
