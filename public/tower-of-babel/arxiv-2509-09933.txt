Multi-Play Combinatorial Semi-Bandit Problem
Shintaro Nakamura, Yuko Kuroki, Wei Chen

Abstract
In the combinatorial semi-bandit (CSB) problem, a player selects an action from a combinatorial action set and observes feedback from the base arms included in the action. While CSB is widely applicable to combinatorial optimization problems, its restriction to binary decision spaces excludes important cases involving non-negative integer flows or allocations, such as the optimal transport and knapsack problems. To overcome this limitation, we propose the multi-play combinatorial semi-bandit (MP-CSB), where a player can select a non-negative integer action and observe multiple feedbacks from a single arm in each round. We propose two algorithms for the MP-CSB. One is a Thompson-sampling-based algorithm that is computationally feasible even when the action space is exponentially large with respect to the number of arms, and attains O ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) O(\log T) distribution-dependent regret in the stochastic regime, where T T is the time horizon. The other is a best-of-both-worlds algorithm, which achieves O ⁡ ( log ⁡ T ) O(\log T) variance-dependent regret in the stochastic regime and the worst-case 𝒪 ~ ​ ( T ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{T}\right) regret in the adversarial regime. Moreover, its regret in adversarial one is data-dependent, adapting to the cumulative loss of the optimal action, the total quadratic variation, and the path-length of the loss sequence. Finally, we numerically show that the proposed algorithms outperform existing methods in the CSB literature.

Introduction

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem is a fundamental framework to investigate online decision-making problems, where we study the tradeoff between
exploitation
and
exploration
problem
[
Auer et al. 2002
,
Audibert and Bubeck 2009
]
.
One of the most important subfields of MAB is the combinatorial bandit problem
[
Audibert et al. 2014
,
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi 2012
,
Combes et al. 2015
,
Wang and Chen 2018
,
Kveton et al. 2015
,
Chen et al. 2016b
,
Wang and Chen 2017
,
Chen et al. 2016a
,
Wen et al. 2014
]
, which has many practical applications such as the shortest path problem
[
Sniedovich 2006
]
, crowdsourcing
[
ul Hassan and Curry 2016
]
, matching
[
Gibbons 1985
]
, the spanning tree problem
[
Pettie and Ramachandran 2002
]
, recommender systems
[
Qin et al. 2014
]
, and learning spectrum allocations
[
Gai et al. 2012
]
.
In the combinatorial semi-bandit (CSB) problem, a player sequentially interacts with an unknown environment over
T
rounds. At each round, the player selects a combinatorial action to play and observes the losses for each selected component.
The goal is to minimize (expected) cumulative regret, defined as the difference between the loss of the player’s actions and the loss of the optimal action.

The CSB problem has been widely applied to modeling combinatorial optimization problems under uncertainty.
However, its restriction to
binary
decision spaces limits its applicability to problems involving integer flows or allocations, such as the knapsack problem
[
Dantzig and Mazur 2007
]
, the optimal transport (OT) problem
[
Villani 2008
]
, and numerous others.
These problems require a more general action space where decisions can take
non-negative integer values
. In these problems, each element of an action often represents some quantity. For example, in the OT problem, each element of an action can be seen as the number of trucks used to transport goods. In real-world applications, it is reasonable to assume that each truck is equipped with sensors and that feedback can be obtained from each truck.

In this paper, we propose a novel framework, multi-play CSB (MP-CSB), where the set of actions is a subset of the non-integer vector space, and multiple losses can be observed from a single arm. MP-CSB can be seen as a natural generalization of the ordinary CSB problem.

We introduce two algorithms for MP-CSB.
First, we introduce the Generalized Combinatorial Thompson Sampling (GenCTS) algorithm that is computationally feasible even when the action set is exponentially large with respect to the number of arms. Even though this algorithm is a naive expansion of the CTS algorithm proposed by
Wang and Chen 2018
, our regret analysis shows that the GenCTS algorithm achieves
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
distribution-dependent regret in the stochastic regime.
Then, we introduce a
best-of-both-worlds
(BOBW) algorithm named GenLBINFV (Generalized Logarithmic Barrier Implicit Normalized Forecaster considering Variances for semi-bandits), which achieves
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
variance-dependent regret in the stochastic regime and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\sqrt{T}\right)
regret in the adversarial regime.
Moreover, its regret in adversarial one is data-dependent, adapting to the cumulative loss of the optimal action, the total quadratic variation, and the path-length of the loss sequence.

Finally, we show that our algorithms outperform existing methods in the ordinary CSB literature by conducting numerical experiments with synthetic data.

Preliminaries

In this section, we formally define the MP-CSB problem and introduce the three regimes depending on how the loss is generated. Then, we show some typical applications of MP-CSB. Finally, we introduce existing works that are related to MP-CSB and discuss the optimality in MP-CSB.

2.1
Multi-Play Combinatorial Semi-Bandit (MP-CSB) Problem

Here, we formalize the MP-CSB problem. Suppose we have
d
base arms, numbered
1,\ldots,d
. In each round
t\in[T]
, the environment sets a set of losses
\left\{L_{i,j}(t)\right\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{i}}\subset[0,1]
for each base arm
i
, where
n_{i}
is the maximum number of samples the agent can obtain from base arm
i
in one round.
Then, the player chooses an action
\boldsymbol{a}(t)
from the action set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d}
, observes a set of losses,
\left\{L_{i,j}(t)\right\}_{j=1,\ldots,a_{i}(t)}
, for each
i
where
a_{i}(t)\geq 1
, and incurs a loss of
f\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t)\right)
, where
f:\mathcal{A}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{L}^{i}=\left(L_{i,1}(t),\ldots,L_{i,n_{i}}(t)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
.
The performance of the player is evaluated by regret
R_{T}
defined as the difference between the cumulative losses of the player and the single optimal action
\boldsymbol{a}^{*}
fixed in terms of the expected cumulative loss, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{a}^{*}=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}f\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t)\right)\right]

and

\displaystyle R_{T}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(f(\boldsymbol{a}(t),\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t))-f(\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t))\right)\right],

where the expectation is taken w.r.t. to the randomness of losses and the internal randomness of the algorithm.

We define
I_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\{i\in[d]\mid a_{i}\geq 1\}
, representing the set of indices of arms from which one or more samples are obtained. We define
J^{*}=[d]\setminus I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}
,
m=\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|I_{\boldsymbol{a}}\right|
, and
M=\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\left\|\boldsymbol{a}\right\|_{1}
. Note that
M\geq m
. Also, we define an action set dependent constant
\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}=\min\left\{M,W_{J^{*}}\right\}
, where
W_{J^{*}}=\sum_{i\in J^{*}}n_{i}
.
We assume that for all
i\in[d]
, there exists
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
a_{i}\geq 1
.

2.2
Considered Regimes

We consider three regimes as the assumptions for the losses.

In the
stochastic regime
, the losses are generated by unknown but fixed distributions.
Before the game starts, the environment chooses an arbitrary distribution
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for each base arm
i\in[d]
.
In each round
t
, for each
i\in[d]
, the environment samples a set of
n_{i}
random variables,
\{L_{i,j}(t)\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{i}}
, from
\mathcal{D}_{i}
.
We denote
\ell_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}_{i}}\left[\xi\right]
and
\sigma_{i}^{2}\in[0,1/4]
as the expected outcome and variance of base arm
i
, respectively.
Also, we assume that the expected loss of an action
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}
only depends on the mean outcomes of base arms in
I_{\boldsymbol{a}}
. That is, there exists a function
r
such that
\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t))]=r\left(\boldsymbol{a},\left\{\ell_{i}\right\}_{i\in[I_{\boldsymbol{a}}]}\right)
.

By contrast, in the
adversarial regime
, we do not assume any stochastic structure for the losses, and the losses can be chosen arbitrarily.
In this regime, for each
i\in[d]
and
j\in[n_{i}]
, the environment can choose
L_{i,j}(t)
depending on the past history until
(t-1)
-th round, i.e.,
\{(\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(s),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(s),\boldsymbol{a}(s))\}_{s=1}^{t-1}
.

We also consider the
stochastic regime with adversarial corruptions

[
Ito 2021
,
Zimmert and Seldin 2021
]
, which is an intermediate regime between the stochastic and adversarial regimes.
In this regime, for each
i\in[d]
, after a set of temporary loss
\left\{L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{i}}
is sampled from
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, the adversary corrupts
\{L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{i}}
to
\{L_{i,j}(t)\}_{j=1,\ldots,n_{i}}
.
We define the corruption level by
C=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\limits_{i\in[d]}\max\limits_{j\in[n_{i}]}\left|L_{i,j}(t)-L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right|\right]\geq 0
. If
C=0
, this regime coincides with the stochastic regime.

2.3
Typical Applications of MP-CSB

Here, we show typical applications where MP-CSB can be applied.

The Optimal Transport Problem.

The optimal transport (OT) problem
[
Villani 2008
]
models resource allocation from
N_{\mathrm{sup}}
suppliers to
N_{\mathrm{dem}}
demanders. It is defined on a complete bipartite graph, where each supplier
x\in S:=\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{sup}}\}
has
u_{x}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
trucks to deliver items, and each demander
y\in D:=\{1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{dem}}\}
requires
v_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
units of items. The goal is to find the most efficient transportation plan, minimizing the total transportation cost:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{min.}&\sum\limits_{x=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sup}}}\sum\limits_{y=1}^{N_{\mathrm{dem}}}a_{xy}c_{xy}&\\
\text{s.t.}&\boldsymbol{a}\in\{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{N_{\mathrm{sup}}\times N_{\mathrm{dem}}}\mid\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\boldsymbol{v}\},&\end{array}

where
a_{xy}
represents the number of trucks transported from supplier
x
to demander
y
, and
c_{xy}
is the transportation cost of edge
(x,y)
.

In some scenarios, the transportation cost
c_{xy}
is unknown and must be estimated. As a real-world application, each truck may have a sensor to measure the cost of edges it passes through, and feedback can be obtained from each truck. In such a case, we can apply the MP-CSB problem. Here, the number of arms
d
is the number of edges in the bipartite graph, i.e.,
d=N_{\mathrm{sup}}\times N_{\mathrm{dem}}
. Also,
n_{xy}
is the maximum number of trucks that can pass through edge
(x,y)
, i.e.,
n_{xy}=\min\left\{u_{x},v_{y}\right\}
.

The Knapsack Problem.

Next, we introduce an example of the knapsack problem
[
Dantzig and Mazur 2007
]
. In the knapsack problem, we have
d
items. Each item
i\in[d]
has a weight
w_{i}
and value
\mu_{i}
. Also, there is a knapsack whose capacity is
W
in which we put items. Our goal is to maximize the total value of the items in the knapsack, not letting the total weight of the items exceed the capacity of the knapsack. Formally, the optimization problem is given as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{maximize}_{\boldsymbol{a}}&\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}&\\
\text{subject to}&\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\leq W&\\
\text{and}&\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{d},&\end{array}

where
\boldsymbol{w}=\left(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}\right)
.

Then, let us consider online advertising. Suppose an advertiser considers placing different types of ads in a frame of size
W
on a website. The advertiser is allowed to place multiple ads of the same type. The size of each ad
i
is
w_{i}
, and it is assumed that the total size of all ads must not exceed
W
.
As feedback to advertisers, they observe the profits generated from each ad.
In this example, we can apply the MP-CSB problem. The number of arms is the number of types of ads, and
n_{i}
is the maximum number of ad
i
that can be put in a website, i.e.,
n_{i}=\left\lfloor\frac{W}{w_{i}}\right\rfloor
.

2.4
Related Works

In Table
1
, we show existing works that are related to MP-CSB.

The top three are state-of-the-art methods (SOTA) for the linear bandit (LB)
[
Ito and Takemura 2023a
,
Ito and Takemura 2023b
,
Lee et al. 2021
]
, which studies best-of-both-worlds algorithms. In MP-CSB, if the objective is linear, i.e.,
f\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}}L_{i,j}(t)
, we can apply these algorithms. However, there are several reasons why LB algorithms are not recommended for MP-CSB.
First, to apply LB algorithms, we need to enumerate all the actions in
\mathcal{A}
, which is unrealistic since the time complexity to enumerate them is exponential in
d
in general. Secondly, since LB algorithms assume full-bandit feedback, in which the agent observes only the sum of rewards
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L_{i,j}(t)
, they are not able to take advantage of the benefit of obtaining multiple samples from a single arm.

The middle two are existing works that show SOTA methods proposed for the ordinary CSB. One may apply existing CSB algorithms to MP-CSB by
duplicating
base arms so that the action space becomes binary. However, this duplicating technique has two major shortcomings. First, duplicating base arms greatly increases the number of base arms, making it computationally infeasible to maintain statistics for each base arm (e.g., sample mean) in some cases.
For example, in the OT problem, the total number of duplicated arms is
\left(\sum\limits_{x=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sup}}}u_{x}\right)\times N_{\mathrm{dem}}
. If
\sum\limits_{x=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sup}}}u_{x}\sim\mathcal{O}\left(10^{10}\right)
, the computational burden of handling such a large number of base arms would be impractical.
The second issue is the sample efficiency in the stochastic regime. Even with the duplicating technique, existing algorithms maintain separate statistics on each base arm
[
Wang and Chen 2018
,
Neu 2015
,
Chen et al. 2021
,
Tsuchiya et al. 2023
]
, even though all of the duplicated base arms follow the same distribution.
Such a lack of distinction between identical distributions leads to poor sample efficiency and may force the player to choose suboptimal actions frequently. See Appendix
A
for details.

3
Generalized CTS Algorithm

In this section, we introduce the generalized combinatorial Thompson sampling (GenCTS) algorithm, which is computationally feasible even when the action set
\mathcal{A}
is exponentially large in
d
. We show that the GenCTS algorithm achieves
O(\log T)
distribution-dependent regret in the stochastic regime.

Technical Assumptions.

To allow the GenCTS to handle not only linear loss functions but also a broader class of nonlinear loss functions, we assume that the function
r
is Lipschitz continuous. Specifically, there exists a constant
\kappa_{r}
, such that for every action
\boldsymbol{a}
and every pair of mean vectors
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}
,
\left|r\left(\boldsymbol{a},\left\{\mu_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right)-r\left(\boldsymbol{a},\left\{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right)\right|\leq\kappa_{r}\sum_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\left|\mu_{i}-\mu^{\prime}_{i}\right|
.
In the OT and knapsack problems,
r\left(\boldsymbol{a},\left\{\mu_{i}\right\}_{i\in[I_{\boldsymbol{a}}]}\right)=\sum_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}a_{i}\mu_{i}
, and we can easily confirm that
\kappa_{r}=\max\limits_{i\in[d]}n_{i}
.

Also, GenCTS assumes that we have an
oracle
that takes a vector
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\left(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}\right)
as input and output an action
\mathrm{Oracle}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}r\left(\boldsymbol{a},\left\{\rho_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right)
.
We assume that the time complexity of the oracle is polynomial or pseudo-polynomial

2

of the input (the largest integer present in the input)—but not necessarily in the length (dimension) of the input, which is the case for polynomial time algorithms.
in
d
.
For instance, since it is known that the OT problem can be solved in
O(d^{3}\log d)
time
[
Cuturi 2013
]
using a linear programming solver, it can be the oracle. For the knapsack problem, there is a dynamic programming-based algorithm that runs in pseudo-polynomial time
[

,
Fujimoto 2016
]
, and therefore it can be the oracle.

Algorithm.

GenCTS is shown in Algorithm
1
.
Initially, we set a prior distribution of all the base arms as the beta distribution
\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)
, which is the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. In each round
t
, we choose an action by drawing independent samples,
\{\theta_{i}(t)\}_{i\in[d]}
, from each base arm’s prior distribution, and use the output from the oracle,
\boldsymbol{a}(t)=\mathrm{Oracle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))
, as the action to play.
After we obtain losses, we update the prior distributions of each base arm
i
using the procedure
Update
(Algorithm
2
).
In the
Update
procedure, we update the prior beta distribution of each base arm as follows.
For each
i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}
and
j\in[a_{i}(t)]
, we generate a Bernoulli random variable
Y_{i,j}(t)
with mean
L_{i,j}(t)
, and update the prior beta distribution of base arm
i
using
Y_{i,j}(t)
as the new observation.
Let
p_{i}(t)
and
q_{i}(t)
denote the values of
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
at the beginning of round
t
, respectively. Here
p_{i}(t)-1
and
q_{i}(t)-1
represent the number of 1s and 0s in
\bigcup_{s=1}^{t-1}\bigcup_{j=1}^{a_{i}(s)}\{Y_{i,j}(s)\}
, respectively.
Then, following Bayes’ rule, the posterior distribution of arm
i
after round
t
is
\mathrm{Beta}\left(p_{i}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}Y_{i,j}(t),q_{i}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}(1-Y_{i,j}(t))\right)
, which is what the
Update
procedure does for
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
.

One key advantage of the GenCTS algorithm is that it does not require enumerating all the possible actions in
\mathcal{A}
in the beginning of the game. The total computation time of GenCTS is
O(\text{poly}(d)T)
or
O(\text{pseudo-poly}(d)T)
.

Regret Analysis.

Here, we show a regret upper bound of the GenCTS algorithm.

Theorem 3.1
.

The GenCTS algorithm achieves
R_{T}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\kappa_{r}^{2}\log m}{\Delta_{i}}\log T\right)
, where
\Delta_{i}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\}:a_{i}\geq 1}r\left(\boldsymbol{a},\left\{\ell_{i}\right\}_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}}}\right)-r\left(\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\{\ell_{i}\}_{i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}\right)

We can see that the upper bound of GenCTS is tighter than the asymptotic lower bound of consistent algorithms with the duplicating technique,
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}M}{\Delta}\log T\right)
, since
b_{i}\geq 1
and
M\geq m
. From the result in
Wang and Chen 2018
, if we use the ordinary CTS algorithm with the duplicating technique for MP-CSB, the regret upper bound is
\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\frac{\kappa_{r}^{2}\log M\log(T)}{\Delta_{\mathrm{i}}}\right)
. Therefore, we can see that the upper bound of GenCTS is tighter than that of the ordinary CTS algorithm with the duplicating technique.
Moreover, the time complexity of GenCTS in each round,
\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Poly}(d)\right)
, can be smaller than that of CTS with the duplicating technique, which is
\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{Poly}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\right)\right)
. For instance, in the example of the OT problem, the time complexity of the ordinary CTS in each round is
\mathcal{O}((\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i})^{3}\log(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}))
, which can be much larger than that of GenLBINFV, which is
\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3}\log d\right)
.

4
Generalized LBINFV Algorithm

In this section, we introduce the GenLBINFV algorithm, which is a BOBW algorithm for MP-CSB.

Technical Assumption for GenLBINFV.

For the GenLBINFV algorithm, we need an assumption that the loss function is linear, i.e.,
f\left(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{L}^{1}(t),\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}^{d}(t)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}}L_{i,j}(t)
.
This assumption holds for many combinatorial optimization problems with a linear objective, such as the OT and knapsack problems.

Algorithm.

We construct the algorithm based on the
optimistic-follow-the-regularized-leader
(OFTRL) framework, which has occasionally been used in the development of the BOBW algorithms
[
Wei and Luo 2018
,
Ito 2021
]
.
In each round
t
, we choose
\boldsymbol{a}(t)\in\mathcal{A}
so that
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{a}(t)\mid\boldsymbol{x}(t)\right]=\boldsymbol{x}(t)
, where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{\left\langle\boldsymbol{q}(t)+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(s),\boldsymbol{x}\right\rangle+\psi_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\right\}.

(3)

Here,
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
is a convex hull of the action set
\mathcal{A}
. Below, we define
\boldsymbol{q}(t)
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t)
, and
\psi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

q(t)
is called the optimistic prediction; intuitively, it estimates the loss of the arms in round
t
. For the choice of optimistic prediction
\boldsymbol{q}(t)
, we introduce two methods: the least squares (LS) and gradient descent (GD) methods. LS defines
\boldsymbol{q}(t)=\left(q_{1}(t),\ldots,q_{d}(t)\right)^{\top}\in\left[0,1\right]^{d}
by

\displaystyle q_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{N_{i}(t-1)}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(s)}L_{i,j}(s)\right),

(4)

and GD defines
\boldsymbol{q}(t)
by
q_{i}(1)=\frac{1}{2}
and

\displaystyle q_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\!\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\!(1-\eta)q_{i}(t)+\eta\frac{1}{a_{i}(t)}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L_{i,j}(t)&\text{if $a_{i}(t)\geq 1$},\\
\!q_{i}(t)&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

for all
i\in[d]
with a step size
\eta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
. The design of LS is to reduce the leading constant
\frac{1}{1-2\eta}
in the regret, and GD is to derive a path-length bound.

Next, we define
\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t)=\left(\hat{\ell}_{1}(t),\ldots,\hat{\ell}_{d}(t)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\hat{\ell}_{i}(t)=q_{i}(t)+\frac{a_{i}(t)}{x_{i}(t)}\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)
for
i\in[d]
, where

\boldsymbol{k}(t)=\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}(t)}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{1}(t)}L_{1,j}(t),\ldots,\frac{1}{a_{d}(t)}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{d}(t)}L_{d,j}(t)\right)
. From basic calculation, we can confirm that
\hat{\ell}_{i}(t)
is an unbiased estimator of
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L_{i,j}(t)\mid\boldsymbol{x}(t)\right]/x_{i}
, which can be seen as the average of the losses occurred by pulling arm
i
.
The optimistic prediction
\boldsymbol{q}(t)
plays a role in reducing the variance of
\hat{\ell}(t)
; the better
\boldsymbol{q}(t)
predicts
\boldsymbol{k}(t)
, the smaller the variance of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t)
becomes.

\psi_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex regularizer function given by
\psi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}(t)\varphi_{i}(x_{i})
, where
\varphi_{i}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is defined as

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(z)=n_{i}\left(\frac{z}{n_{i}}-1-\log\frac{z}{n_{i}}+\log T\left(\frac{z}{n_{i}}+\left(1-\frac{z}{n_{i}}\log\left(1-\frac{z}{n_{i}}\right)\right)\right)\right),

(7)

and regularization parameters
\left\{\beta_{i}(t)\right\}_{i=1,\ldots,d}
are defined as

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(t)=\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{\epsilon_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\log T}\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\left(\frac{a_{i}(s)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(s)-q_{i}(s)\right)^{2}\min\left\{1,\frac{2\left(1-\frac{x_{i}(s)}{n_{i}}\right)}{\left(\frac{x_{i}(s)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\log T}\right\}}.

(8)

Here,
\epsilon_{i}\in\left(0,\frac{n_{i}}{2}\right]
is a hyperparameter.
Our regularizer function
\varphi_{i}
consists of a logarithmic barrier term
-\log\frac{z}{n_{i}}
and an entropy term
\left(1-\frac{z}{n_{i}}\right)\log\left(1-\frac{z}{n_{i}}\right)
.
This type of regularizer is called a
hybrid
regularizer and was employed in existing studies for bounding a component of the regret
[
Zimmert et al. 2019
,
Ito et al. 2022a
,
Ito et al. 2022b
]
.
Our regularizer function can be seen as a generalization of that of LBINFV
[
Tsuchiya et al. 2023
]
since
n_{i}=1
for all
i\in[d]
in the ordinary CSB.
\beta_{i}(t)
determines the strength of the regularization.
When
a_{i}(s)\geq 1
,
\left(k_{i}(s)-q_{i}(s)\right)^{2}
in (
8
) can be seen as the squared error of the optimistic prediction, and the algorithm becomes more explorative when the loss is unpredictable or has a high variance.

Overall, intuitively,
\boldsymbol{q}(t)
and
\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t)
are values determined based on past information and are responsible for the
exploitation
. On the other hand, the regularizer
\psi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
prevents overfitting to past data and encourages moderate
exploration
. This is intended to minimize regret even in adversarial environments.

Computational complexity.

OFTRL in (
3
) can be solved in polynomial time in
d
as long as the convex hull
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{conv}(\mathcal{A})
is represented by a polynomial number of constraints or admits a polynomial-time separation oracle
[
Schrijver 1998
]
.
Given the solution
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{X}
, sampling a combinatorial action
\boldsymbol{a}(t)\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}(t)\mid\boldsymbol{x}(t)]=\boldsymbol{x}(t)
requires a convex decomposition of
\boldsymbol{x}(t)

[
Wei and Luo 2018
,
Ito 2021
]
.
By Carathéodory’s theorem, any point
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{X}
can be expressed as a convex combination
\boldsymbol{x}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}\boldsymbol{a}^{(k)}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}^{(k)}\in\mathcal{A}
,
\lambda_{k}\in[0,1]
,
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}=1
, and
m\leq d+1
.
When the linear optimization oracle over
\mathcal{A}
is efficient, the Frank-Wolfe (FW) algorithm can construct such a decomposition iteratively
[
Combettes and Pokutta 2021
]
. This holds, for instance, in OT problems, where the feasible set forms a transportation polytope and each FW step reduces to a tractable linear program.
In contrast, for NP-hard domains such as knapsack problems, solving OFTRL exactly is generally intractable. In practice, this can be addressed either by exploiting problem structure to keep the action set small enough to enumerate, or by using approximate optimization and sampling methods.

Regret Analysis.

Here, we show regret upper bounds of the GenLBINFV algorithm. First, we show regret upper bounds of the GenLBINFV algorithm for each optimistic prediction method under the stochastic regime.

Theorem 4.1
.

Regret upper bounds of GenLBINFV using LS and GD methods in the stochastic regime are
\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i\in J^{*}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\log T\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum_{i\in J^{*}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\log T\right)
, respectively, where
\Delta_{i}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}:a_{i}\geq 1}\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\ell}
.

We can see that both optimistic prediction methods achieve
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
variance-dependent regret bound. Variance dependency is a clear advantage since the variances of losses for each base arm are extremely small in many real-world applications
[
Tsuchiya et al. 2023
,
Komiyama et al. 2017
,
György et al. 2006
]
.
The upper bound of the ordinary LBINFV algorithm with the duplicating technique is
\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i\in J^{*}}b_{i}\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\log T\right)
, where
\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}=\min\{M,\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}-\left\|\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\right\|_{1}\}
.
In the example of OT, when
u_{x}
’s are large,
\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}-\|\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\|_{1}=\left(\sum_{x=1}^{N_{\mathrm{sup}}}u_{x}\right)\times N_{\mathrm{dem}}-\|\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\|_{1}
is much larger than
M
, and we have
\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}=M
. Therefore,
\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}=\min\{M,W_{J^{*}}\}\leq M=\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
, which implies that the upper bound of GenLBINFV is no looser than that of the LBINFV since
b_{i}\geq 1
.

Next, we show regret upper bounds of the GenLBINFV algorithm for each optimistic prediction method under the adversarial regime.
Let us denote the cumulative loss of the optimal action, total quadratic variation in loss sequence, and path-length of loss sequence by
L^{*}=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L_{i,j}(t)]
,
Q_{2}=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{k}(t)-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}(s)\|^{2}_{2}]
, and
V_{1}=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\|\boldsymbol{k}(t)-\boldsymbol{k}(t+1)\|_{1}]
, respectively, to introduce the data-dependent bound of the GenLBINFV algorithm.

Theorem 4.2
.

Regret upper bounds of GenLBINFV using the LS and GD methods in the adversarial regime are
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n^{2}_{i}\right)Z^{\mathrm{LS}}\log T}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n^{2}_{i}\right)Z^{\mathrm{GD}}\log T}\right)
, respectively, where
Z^{\mathrm{LS}}=\min\{L^{*},MT-L^{*},Q_{2}\}
and
Z^{\mathrm{GD}}=\min\{L^{*},MT-L^{*},Q_{2},\frac{V_{1}}{\eta}\}
.

Z^{\mathrm{LS}}
and
Z^{\mathrm{GS}}
can be seen as indicators of the problem. If the problem is relatively easy and can be assumed to be
\mathcal{O}\left(Z^{\mathrm{LS}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(Z^{\mathrm{GD}}\right)=o\left(T\right)
, the GenLBINFV can achieve a much smaller bound than the the worst case bound
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{M\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\right)T}\right)
.
The upper bound of the ordinary LBINFV using LS and GD methods are
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\right)Z^{\mathrm{LS}}\log T}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}\right)Z^{\mathrm{GD}}\log T}\right)
, respectively. In general, we do not know whether
\sum_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}
is smaller than
\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}
or not.

On the other hand, GenLBINFV is computationally friendlier than LBINFV with the duplicating technique, since when calling the oracle to compute (
3
), the time complexity of GenLBINFV in each round is
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Poly}(d))
, which can be much smaller than the time complexity of LBINFV with the duplicating technique,
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Poly}(\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}))
.

Regret Upper Bound of the Intermediate Regime

We have the following theorem for the intermediate regime.

Theorem 4.3
.

In the stochastic regime with adversarial corruptions, upper bounds of GenLBINFV using the LS and GD methods are
\mathcal{O}\left(R^{\mathrm{LS}}+\sqrt{CMR^{\mathrm{LS}}}\right)
and
\mathcal{O}\left(R^{\mathrm{GD}}+\sqrt{CMR^{\mathrm{GD}}}\right)
, respectively. Here,
R^{\mathrm{LS}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{i\in J^{*}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\log T\right)
and
R^{\mathrm{GD}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum_{i\in J^{*}}\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma^{2}_{i}}{\Delta_{i}}\log T\right)
.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we proposed the MP-CSB framework, where a player can select a non-negative integer action and observe multiple feedbacks from a single arm in each round. We proposed two algorithms for MP-CSB: GenCTS and GenLBINFV. GenCTS is computationally feasible even when the action set
\mathcal{A}
is exponentially large in
d
, and achieves a
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
distribution-dependent regret. GenLBINFV is a BOBW algorithm, which achieves
\mathcal{O}(\log T)
variance-dependent regret in the stochastic regime and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{T})
regret in the adversarial regime. We numerically showed that the proposed algorithms outperform existing methods in the CSB literature.

1\leq i\leq d
.

Proof.

Let
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots
be the time slots such that
i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}
and define
\tau_{0}=0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\{t\in[T]\mid i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)},|\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\ell_{i}|>\epsilon\right\}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}\left[i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)},|\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\ell_{i}|>\epsilon\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{w=0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=\tau_{w}}^{\tau_{w+1}-1}\mathbbm{1}\left[i\in I_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)},|\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\ell_{i}|>\epsilon\right]\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{w=0}^{n_{i}T}\Pr\left[|\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\ell_{i}|>\epsilon,N_{i}=w\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\sum\limits_{w=1}^{n_{i}T}\Pr\left[|\hat{\mu}_{i}(t)-\ell_{i}|>\epsilon,N_{i}=w\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\sum\limits_{w=1}^{T}\exp\left(-2w\epsilon^{2}\right)+\sum\limits_{w=1}^{T}\exp\left(-2w\epsilon^{2}\right)

(13)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+2\sum\limits_{w=1}^{\infty}\left(\exp\left(-2w\epsilon^{2}\right)\right)^{w}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+2\frac{\exp\left(-2\epsilon^{2}\right)}{1+\exp\left(-2\epsilon^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{2}{2\epsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}

where Eq (
13
) is because of the Chernoff-Hoeffding’s inequality (Fact
B.1
).
∎

By Lemma
B.1
, we know that the first term is upper bounded by
\left(\frac{dM^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}+d\right)\Delta_{\mathrm{max}}
, where
M=\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{1}
.

The Second Term of the RHS of (

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}}{\kappa_{r}}-({M^{*}}^{2}+1)\epsilon^{2}-\epsilon,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\left[\mathcal{S}(t)\land\mathcal{T}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)\right]\times\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}\right]+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\left[\mathcal{S}(t)\land\lnot\mathcal{T}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)\right]\times\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}\right].

Following the same discussion in

B.3.3
The Third Term of the RHS of (
12
)

Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}\right)
be a vector of parameters,
I\subseteq[d]
and
I\neq\emptyset
be some arm set, and
\boldsymbol{V}^{c}
be the complement of
\boldsymbol{V}
. Recall that
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{V}
is a vector whose
i
-th element is
\theta_{i}
if
i\in V
and 0 if
i\notin V
. Also, we use the notation
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{\boldsymbol{V}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{V}^{c}})
to denote replacing
\theta_{i}
’s for
i\in V
and keeping the values
\theta_{i}
for
i\in V^{c}
unchanged.

Given a subset
I\subseteq I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}
, we consider the following property for
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I^{c}}
. For any
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{Z}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{Z}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{Z}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, let
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}=\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{Z},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I^{c}}\right)
, then:

•

I\subseteq I_{\mathsf{Oracle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})}

•

Either
\mathsf{\mathsf{Oracle}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})=\boldsymbol{a}^{*}
or
\|\mathsf{Oracle}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)\|_{1}\geq\Delta_{\mathsf{Oracle}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})}-\left({M^{*}}^{2}+2\right)\epsilon

The first one is to make sure that if we have normal samples in
I
at time
t
(i.e., the samples value
\theta_{i}(t)
is within
\epsilon
neighborhood of
\ell_{i}
for all
i\in Z
), then all the arms in
I
will be played and observed. These observations would update the beta distributions of these base arms to be more accurate, such that the probability of the next time that the samples from these base arms are also within
\epsilon
neighborhood of their true mean value becomes larger. This fact would be used later in the quantitative regret analysis. The second one says that if the samples in
I
are normal, then
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)
can not happen. We use
\mathcal{E}_{Z,1}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\right)
to denote the event that the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I^{c}}
has such a property, and emphasize that this event only depends on the values in vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I^{c}}
.

What we want to do is to find some exact
I
such that
\mathcal{E}_{Z,1}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\right)
happens when
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)
happens. If such
I
exists, then for any
t
such that
\mathcal{E}_{Z,1}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\right)
happens, there are two possible cases:
\mathrm{i}
) the samples of all arms
i\in Z
are normal, which means
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)
cannot happen, and will update the posterior distributions of all the arms
i\in Z
to increase the probability that the samples of all the arms
i\in Z
are normal;
\mathrm{ii}
) the samples of some arms
i\in Z
are not normal, and
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)
may happen in this case. As time goes on, the probability that the samples in
I
are normal becomes larger and larger, and therefore the probability that
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)
happens becomes smaller and smaller. Thus,
\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}\left[\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)\right]\right]
has a constant upper bound.

The following lemma shows that such
I
must exist, and it is the key lemma in the analysis of the third term.

Lemma B.2
.

Suppose that
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)\land\mathcal{P}(t)
happens, then there exists
I\subseteq I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}
and
I\neq\emptyset
such that
\mathcal{E}_{Z,1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t))
holds.

Proof.

Firstly, consider the case that we choose
I=I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}
, i.e., we change
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}(t)
to some
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
and get a new vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}=\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}^{c}}(t)\right)
.
We claim that for any
\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
such that
I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}\land I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}=\emptyset
,
\mathsf{Oracle}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right)\neq\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}
.
This is because

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}\left(t\right)\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}\left(t\right),\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\rangle

(27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}(t),\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle+\left({\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}}-\left({M^{*}}^{2}+1\right)\epsilon\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle-\left({M^{*}}^{2}+1\right)\epsilon

(29)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle-M^{*}\epsilon

(30)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\rangle

(31)

Eq (
26
) is because
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)
only differs on arms in
I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}
but
I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}}\cap I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}\neq\emptyset
. Eq (
27
) is by the optimality of
\boldsymbol{a}(t)
on input
\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)
. Eq (
28
) is by the event
\lnot\mathcal{R}(t)
. Eq (
29
) is by the definition of
\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}(t)}
. Eq (
31
) again uses the Lipschitz continuity. Thus, the claim holds.

We have two possibilities for
\mathsf{Oracle}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right)
:

1a)

for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
,
I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}\subseteq I_{\mathsf{Oracle}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right)}

1b)

for some
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}-\boldsymbol{\ell}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
,
\mathsf{Oracle}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right)=\boldsymbol{a}^{1}
where
I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{1}}\cap I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}=I_{1}
and
I_{1}\neq I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}
,
I_{1}\neq\emptyset
.

In 1a), let
\boldsymbol{a}^{0}=\mathsf{Oracle}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\right)
. Then, we have
\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\rangle\geq\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\rangle\geq\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle-M^{*}\epsilon
.
If
\boldsymbol{a}^{0}\notin\mathsf{OPT}
, we have
\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{*},\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{0},\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle+\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}
.
Together, we have
\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{0},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}\rangle\geq\langle\boldsymbol{a}^{0},\boldsymbol{\ell}\rangle+\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}-M^{*}\epsilon
.
This implies that
\|\boldsymbol{a}^{0}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)\|_{1}\geq\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}-M^{*}\epsilon>\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}-\left({M^{*}}^{2}+1\right)\epsilon>\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}-\left({M^{*}}^{2}+1\right)\epsilon
.
That is, we conclude that either
\boldsymbol{a}^{0}\in\mathsf{OPT}
or
\|\boldsymbol{a}^{0}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)\|_{1}\geq\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}-M^{*}\epsilon>\Delta_{\boldsymbol{a}^{0}}-\left({M^{*}}^{2}+1\right)\epsilon
, which means that
\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*},1}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}(t)\right)=\mathcal{E}_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*},1}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}(t)\right)
holds.

Next, we consider 1b). Fix a
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}_{I_{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}}
with

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}f_{i}^{(2)}(y)

of

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}}f_{i}^{(1)}(y)=

\displaystyle f_{i}^{(1)}\left(y^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\left(x-y^{*}\right)+n_{i}\log\frac{y^{*}}{n_{i}}-n_{i}\log\frac{x}{n_{i}}+n_{i}\frac{x-y^{*}}{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n_{i}\log\frac{x}{y^{*}}+n_{i}\left(\frac{x-y^{*}}{y^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n_{i}\left(\log\left(1+a\frac{x}{n_{i}}\right)+a\frac{x}{n_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n_{i}g\left(a\frac{x}{n_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=0

(40)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle f_{i}^{(2)}\left(y^{*}\right)=

\displaystyle a\left(x-y^{*}\right)-n_{i}\left(1-\frac{y^{*}}{n_{i}}\right)\log\left(1-\frac{y^{*}}{n_{i}}\right)+n_{i}\left(1-\frac{x}{n_{i}}\right)\log\left(1-\frac{x}{n_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}}{2\beta_{i}(t)}+\frac{\delta_{i}\left|k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right|^{3}}{\beta_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{i}(t)}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\delta_{i}\right)\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{i}(t)}\frac{1-\frac{x_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}}{\gamma\left(\frac{x_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}}\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}.

We hence have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t)-\boldsymbol{q}(t),\boldsymbol{x}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}(t+1)\right\rangle-D_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{y}(t+1),\boldsymbol{x}(t)\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{2\beta_{i}(t)}+\frac{\delta_{i}}{\beta^{2}_{i}(t)}\right)\alpha_{i}(t).

(59)

We can show that a part of (
59
) is bounded as

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{2\beta_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)}-\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\beta_{i}\left(T+1\right)-\beta_{i}(1)\right).

(60)

The first inequality in (
60
) holds since

\displaystyle\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}(t)}-\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}(s)}+\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\gamma\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)}}

(61)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{2\gamma\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)}}

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{2\gamma\beta_{i}(t)},

(63)

where the inequality follows by
\alpha_{i}(t)\leq 1
. The second inequality in (
60
) follows since

\displaystyle\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)}-\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}}

(64)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)}-\beta_{i}(1)+\frac{1}{\gamma}

(65)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta_{i}(T+1)-\beta_{i}(1)+\frac{1}{\gamma},

(66)

where the first inequality follows from
\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{x-y}\leq\frac{y}{\sqrt{x}}
for
x\geq y\geq 0
and
\beta_{i}(1)\geq 1
.

Similarly, we can show

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta^{2}_{i}(t)}=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\beta^{2}_{i}(1)+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\right)

(67)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\gamma\log\frac{\beta_{i}(T+1)}{\beta_{i}(1)}+2.

(68)

The first inequality in (
68
) follows since

\displaystyle\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}(s)\right)-\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1}\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\log\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}(s)}\right)

(69)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\log\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)}\right)

(70)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}\alpha_{i}(s)},

(71)

where the first inequality follows from
\alpha_{i}(t)\leq 1
and the last inequality follows from
-\log(1-x)\geq x
for
x<1
. The second inequality in (
68
) follows from

\displaystyle\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\right)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\right)+\log\frac{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1}

(72)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\beta_{i}(T+1)}{\beta_{i}(1)}\right)+\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma\beta^{2}_{i}(1)-1}\right)

(73)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\log\frac{\beta_{i}(T+1)}{\beta_{i}(1)}+\frac{2}{\gamma}

(74)

where the last inequality follows from
\log(1+\frac{1}{x-1})\geq\frac{2}{x}
for
x\geq 3/2
. Bounding the RHS of (
58
) with (
60
) and (
68
) yields

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle\hat{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(t)-\boldsymbol{q}(t),\boldsymbol{x}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}(t+1)\right\rangle-D_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{y}(t+1),\boldsymbol{x}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\left(\beta_{i}(T+1)-\beta_{i}(1)+2\delta_{i}\log\frac{\beta_{i}(T+1)}{\beta_{1}}\right)+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\delta_{i}

(75)

Finally, by bounding the RHS of (
42
) and sequentially using (
44
), (
49
) and (
75
), we have

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[2\beta_{i}(T+1)-\beta_{i}(1)+2\delta_{i}\log\frac{\beta_{i}(T+1)}{\beta_{i}(1)}\right]+dW+2\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\delta_{i},

(76)

which completes the proof.
∎

C.2.2
A
Lower Bound

Below, we define

\displaystyle\Delta^{\prime}_{i,\mathrm{min}}=\min_{\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\}}\left\{\boldsymbol{a}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\ell}-{\boldsymbol{a}^{*}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\ell}:a_{i}=0\right\}.

(77)

To obtain the regret upper bound depending on
\Delta_{i}
in the stochastic regime and the stochastic regime with adversarial corruptions, we prove the following regret
lower bound
.

Lemma C.4
.

In the stochastic regime with adversarial corruptions, for any algorithm and any action set
\mathcal{A}
, the regret is bounded as

\displaystyle R_{T}\geq\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\Biggl(\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}\Delta^{\prime}_{i,\mathrm{min}}\left(a^{*}_{i}-a_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}}\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}\Delta_{i,\mathrm{min}}a_{i}(t)\Biggr)\Biggr]-2CM,

(78)

where
\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}=\min\left\{W_{I^{*}},W-M\right\}
.

Proof.

We can bound the regret as

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L_{i,j}(t)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a^{*}_{i}(t)}L_{i,j}(t)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a^{*}_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right)+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a^{*}_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right)\Biggr]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a^{*}_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right)\Biggr]-\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left|\max\limits_{i\in[d],j\in[n_{i}]}L_{i,j}(t)-L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right|\|\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a^{*}_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right)\Biggr]-2MC,

(79)

where the first inequality follows from the Hölder’s inequality, the second inequality follows since
\|\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\|_{1}\leq 2M
, and the last inequality follows from the definition of
C=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\max\limits_{i\in[d]}\max\limits_{j\in[n_{i}]}\left|L_{i,j}(t)-L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right|\right]\geq 0
. We then bound
\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a^{*}_{i}(t)}L^{\prime}_{i,j}(t)\right)\Biggr]
.

Below, we write
\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{a_{i}}L_{i,j}(t)
. We have
\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}\rangle-\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{a}^{\prime}\rangle
.

We consider the case of general action sets and recall that
I^{*}:=\{i\in[d]:\ {a}^{*}_{i}\geq 1\}
and
J^{*}=[d]\setminus I^{*}
. Since
\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}(a^{*}_{i}-a_{i}(t))\leq M^{*}
and
\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}a_{i}(t)\leq M
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\min\left\{W_{I^{*}},W-M\right\}}\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}\left(n_{i}-{a}_{i}(t)\right)\left\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\min\left\{W_{J^{*}},M\right\}}\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}a_{i}(t)\left\langle\boldsymbol{L},\boldsymbol{a}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}^{*}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\min\left\{W_{I^{*}},W-M\right\}}\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}\Delta^{\prime}_{i,\mathrm{min}}\left(n_{i}-{a}_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2\min\left\{W_{J^{*}},M\right\}}\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}\Delta_{i,\mathrm{min}}a_{i}(t).

Combining this inequality with (
79
)
completes the proof.
∎

Note that in the stochastic regime with adversarial corruptions, from Lemma
C.4
, it holds that

\displaystyle R_{T}\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Biggl[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\Biggl(\frac{1}{2M^{*}}\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}\Delta^{\prime}_{i,\mathrm{min}}\left(a^{*}_{i}-{a}_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}\Delta_{i,\mathrm{min}}a_{i}(t)\Biggr)\Biggr]-2CM

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2M^{*}}\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}\Delta^{\prime}_{i,\mathrm{min}}Q_{i}+\frac{1}{2M}\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}\Delta_{i,\mathrm{min}}P_{i}-2CM,

(80)

where equality follows from the law of iterated expectations.

C.3
Proof for the LS Method

In this section, we provide proof for the results of the LS Method.

C.3.1
Preliminaries

We use the following lemma to bound
\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)
for suboptimal arms
i\in J^{*}
.

Lemma C.5
.

It holds for any
i\in[d]
and
q_{i}^{*}\in[0,1]
that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-m^{*}\right)^{2}+\log\left(1+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{5}{4}

(81)

To prove this lemma, we use the following lemma.

Lemma C.6
.

Suppose
k_{i}(s)\in[0,1]
for any
s\in[t]
, and define
q_{i}(t)\in[0,1]
by

\displaystyle q_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{1+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)k_{i}(s)\right).

(82)

We then have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}-\left(k_{i}(t)-m^{*}\right)^{2}\right)\leq\frac{5}{4}+\log\left(1+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\right)

(83)

for any
m^{*}\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

From the definition of
q_{i}(t)
,
q_{i}(t)
is expressed as

\displaystyle q_{i}(t)\in\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{m\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(m-k_{i}(s)\right)^{2}\right\},

(84)

which implies

\displaystyle q_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(s)\right)=0.

(85)

We have

\displaystyle\left(m-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(m-k_{i}(s)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(m-q_{i}(t)+q_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(m-q_{i}(t)+q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(s)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(m-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+2\left(m-q_{i}(t)\right)\left(q_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(q_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(m-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+2\left(m-q_{i}(t)\right)\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(s)\right)+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(s)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)+1\right)\left(m-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(s)\right)^{2}

(86)

for any
m\in\mathbb{R}
. The third equality follows from (
85
).
Using this, for any
m^{*}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(m^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(k_{i}(t)-m^{*}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(T+1)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(m^{*}-q_{i}(T+1)\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(T+1)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(T+1)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+a_{i}(T)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(T)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)+q_{i}(T)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)+q_{i}(T)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+a_{i}(T)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(T)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)\right)^{2}+2\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)\right)\left(q_{i}(T)-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(q_{i}(T)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)\right)^{2}+2\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)\right)\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(t)-l_{ij}(t)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+a_{i}(T)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(T)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(T)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)+1\right)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-q_{i}(T)\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(T)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+a_{i}(T)\left(q_{i}(T+1)-k_{i}(T)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(q_{i}(1)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1)\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(1+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\right)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t)\right)

(87)

where the first and fifth inequalities follow from (
86
), and the last equality can be shown by repeating the same transformation
T
times.
Hence, for any
m^{*}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}-\left(k_{i}(t)-m^{*}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}-\left(k_{i}(t)-m^{*}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{n_{i}}\Biggl(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)+1\right)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}\Biggr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n_{i}}\left(m^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(t)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}-a_{i}(t)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+\left(\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)+1\right)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n_{i}}\left(m^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\Biggl(a_{i}(t)\left(2k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1)\right)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t)\right)-\left(\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)+1\right)\left(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t)\right)\Biggr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n_{i}}\left(m^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{a^{2}_{i}(t)}{4\left(1+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\right)}\left(2k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{i}}\left(m^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{a_{i}(t)}{\left(1+\sum\limits_{s=1}^{t-1}a_{i}(s)\right)}+\left(m^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log\left(1+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{5}{4}

(88)

where the second equality follows from
ax-tx^{2}=\frac{a^{2}}{4t}-\left(\frac{a}{2\sqrt{t}}-\sqrt{t}x\right)^{2}\leq\frac{a^{2}}{4t}
that holds for any
a,x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the forth inequality holds since
\left|2k_{i}(t)-m(t)-m(t+1)\right|\leq 2
, which follows from
k_{i}(t),m(t)\in[0,1]
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
C.5
.

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\Biggl(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\Biggr)^{2}\cdot\min\left\{1,\frac{2\left(1-\frac{x_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)}{\left(\frac{x_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\gamma}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\Biggl(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\Biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-m^{*}\right)^{2}+\log\left(1+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{5}{4}

(89)

where the second inequality follows from Lemma
C.6
.
∎

From Lemma
C.5
, in the stochastic regime, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\frac{a^{2}_{i}(t)}{n^{2}_{i}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{a_{i}(t)}+\log\left(1+\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\right)\right]+\frac{5}{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{n^{2}_{i}}P_{i}+\log\left(1+P_{i}\right)+\frac{5}{4},

(90)

where the first inequality follows from Lemma
C.5
with
m^{*}_{i}=\mu_{i}
and in the last inequality, we define

\displaystyle P_{i}=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}x_{i}(t)\right].

(91)

We give a bound on
\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)
using the following lemma.

Lemma C.7
.

It holds for any
i\in[d]

\displaystyle h_{i}(z)=\max\left\{4z+c_{i}\log\left(1+z\right),2n_{i}\beta_{i}(1)\right\}.

From this and (

), we obtain

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum\limits_{i\in J^{*}}\max\left\{4\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\Delta_{i,\mathrm{min}}}+c_{i}\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\Delta_{i,\mathrm{min}}}\right),2n_{i}\beta_{i}(1)\right\}+2\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}n_{i}\beta_{i}(1)\right)\log T

(120)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{i\in I^{*}}\frac{16(1+\delta_{i})^{2}\lambda_{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\Delta^{\prime}_{i,\mathrm{min}}}\sqrt{\log T}+o\left(\sqrt{\log T}\right)

(121)

which completes the proof of upper bound of the LS method under the stochastic regime for the stochastic regime.

From (

C.4

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\sum\limits_{i\in\{j\in[d]|a_{j}(t)\geq 1\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}{\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)-a^{*}_{i}}{n_{i}}\right)}k_{i}(t)+\frac{a^{*}_{i}}{n_{i}}k_{i}(t)\right]}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\left(R_{T}+L^{*}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

where the third inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality. By solving this equation in
R_{T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma L^{*}}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

(136)

which is the desired bound.

Finally, we prove
R_{T}\leq\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}(mT-L^{*})\log T}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)
. By setting
\boldsymbol{q}^{*}=\mathbf{1}
in (
135
) and repeating a similar argument as for proving
R_{T}\leq\sqrt{4\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}L^{*}\log T}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-1\right)^{2}\right]}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(1-k_{i}(t)\right)^{2}\right]}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(1-k_{i}(t)\right)\right]}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\Biggl(MT-\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a^{*}_{i}k_{i}(t)-\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}k_{i}(t)\left(a_{i}(t)-a^{*}_{i}\right)\Biggr)}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\left(MT-L^{*}-R_{T}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right)

where the third inequality follows since
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}(t)\leq M
and the forth inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality. By solving this inequation in
R_{T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq 2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}{n_{i}}^{2}\gamma\left(MT-L^{*}\right)}+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma+dW+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}n_{i}\delta_{i}\right),

(137)

which completes the proof.

C.4
Proof for the GD Method

Here, we provide proofs for the GD method. We can prove it by a similar discussion to that for the LS method. We first discuss the key lemma for this argument.

C.4.1
Preliminaries

Lemma C.8
.

Assume that
q_{i}(t)
is given by (
4
). Then, for any
i\in[d]
and
u_{i}(1),\ldots,u_{i}(T)\in[0,1]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\eta}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}a_{i}(t)\left(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t)\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta\left(1-2\eta\right)}\left(\frac{1}{4}+2\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}|u_{i}(t+1)-u_{i}(t)|\right)

(138)

Proof.

Take
i\in[d]
satisfying
a_{i}(t)=1
. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}-\left(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)\left(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t))+2(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\eta(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))^{2}+\frac{2}{\eta}(q_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t))(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta}(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))^{2}-(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1)^{2}),

where the inequalities follow from
y^{2}-x^{2}=2y(y-x)-(x-y)^{2}\leq 2y(y-x)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and the last inequality follows from the definition of
q_{i}(t)
in (
4
). Hence, we have

\displaystyle(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\left((k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t))^{2}+\frac{1}{\eta}\left((u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))^{2}-(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1))^{2}\right)\right).

(139)

From the definition of
\alpha_{i}(t)
and (
139
), we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\eta(1-2\eta)}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left((u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t))^{2}-(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\eta(1-2\eta)}\left(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left((u_{i}(t+1)-q_{i}(t+1))^{2}-(u_{i}(t)-q_{i}(t+1))^{2}\right)+(u_{i}(1)-q_{i}(1))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\eta(1-2\eta)}\left(\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(u_{i}(t+1)+u_{i}(t)-2q_{i}(t+1)\right)\left(u_{i}(t+1)-u_{i}(t)\right)+\frac{1}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t))^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\eta(1-2\eta)}\left(2\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left|u_{i}(t+1)-u_{i}(t)\right|+\frac{1}{4}\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

C.4.2
Proof for the Stochastic Regime

From Lemma
C.8
, setting
u_{i}(t)=k_{i}
for all
i\in[d]
and
t\in[T]

where
P_{i}
is defined in (
91
). By using this inequality instead of (
90
) and repeating the same argument as that in Appendix

(140)

C.4.3
Proof for the Stochastic Regime with Adversarial Corruption

Here, we show a regret upper bound of the GD method under the stochastic regime with adversarial corruptions given:

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq R^{\mathrm{GD}}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{CMR^{\mathrm{GD}}})

(141)

Letting
u_{i}(t)=\mu_{i}
for all
i\in[d]

t\in[T]
in Lemma
C.8

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)\right]\leq

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-l^{\prime}_{i}(t)+l^{\prime}_{i}(t)-\mu_{i}\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{4\eta(1-2\eta)}

C.4.4
Proof for the Adversarial Regime

From Lemma
C.8
, we immediately obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-2\eta}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{a_{i}(t)}{n_{i}}\right)^{2}\left(k_{i}(t)-u_{i}(t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\eta\left(1-2\eta\right)}\left(\frac{d}{4}+2\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{u}(t+1)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|_{1}\right)

(143)

for any
\boldsymbol{u}(t)=(u_{1}(t),\ldots,u_{d}(t))^{\top}\in[0,1]^{d}
.

First, we prove
R_{T}\leq\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{\eta(1-2\eta)}(d+8V_{1})}+\mathcal{O}(W\gamma)
.
From (
43
), letting
\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\boldsymbol{k}(t)
in (
143
), we can bound the regret as

\displaystyle R_{T}\leq

\displaystyle 2\gamma\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{{\beta_{i}(1)}^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_{i}(t)}\right]+\mathcal{O}\left(W\gamma\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n^{2}_{i}\right)\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}(t)}\right]+\mathcal{O}(W\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}^{2}}}{\sqrt{\eta(1-2\eta)}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\gamma\left(\frac{d}{4}+2\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T-1}\|\boldsymbol{k}(t+1)-\boldsymbol{k}(t)\|_{1}\right)}\right]+\mathcal{O}(W\gamma)

where the second inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the third inequality follows by setting

Next, we prove
