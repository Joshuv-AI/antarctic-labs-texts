Patient and Healthcare Provider Perspectives on Disease Burden of Seborrheic Dermatitis in the United States: Results from a National Survey
Authors: Meredith T Polaskey, Lakshi Aldredge, Candrice Heath, Moises Acevedo, David H Chu, Diane Hanna, Melissa S Seal, Matthew Zirwas, Raj Chovatiya

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSeborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a common, chronic inflammatory skin disease, but the physical and emotional burden of patients with SD experience has not been well characterized.MethodsThe Harris Poll conducted online surveys of US patients and healthcare providers (HCPs) from December 2021 to January 2022.ResultsAlmost half of patients reported that SD negatively impacts their emotional and physical well-being “a lot/a great deal”; HCPs underestimate the level of impact on patients. Key Summary Points

Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a common, chronic inflammatory skin disease, but the physical and emotional impact on patients with SD has not been well defined.Between December 2021 and January 2022, online surveys of US patients with SD and healthcare providers (HCPs) who treat SD were conducted.Almost half of patients reported that SD caused “a lot/a great deal” of negative impact on their emotional and physical well-being, while HCPs underestimated the level of impact on patients.Most patients felt they would be further along in their careers without SD, because symptoms reduced their confidence and willingness to interact with people at work, and nearly half reported having missed work because of symptoms.These findings highlight the necessity for a holistic approach to SD care that addresses physical symptoms as well as the psychological and social challenges faced by patients.

Introduction

Seborrheic dermatitis (SD) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by immune dysregulation, inflammation, and skin barrier disruption, exacerbated by metabolites from Malassezia yeast [1]. Despite its global prevalence, with studies reporting a worldwide presence of up to 5% [2–6], and a prevalence that is increasing over time [7], the ubiquity of SD contrasts sharply with the dearth of understanding regarding its physical and emotional impact on patients. Epidemiological understanding of SD remains limited [8].

SD is associated with significant global impact, markedly diminishing quality of life for those with the disease [9, 10]. This impact disproportionately affects specific demographics, such as women, younger individuals, those with higher education levels, and patients with scalp lesions, highlighting the disease’s uneven impact across population segments [11, 12]. Anxiety levels are notably elevated among patients with SD compared with healthy controls [13], aligning with findings indicating that individuals with chronic inflammatory skin conditions exhibit heightened levels of depression symptoms, general anxiety, social anxiety, and insomnia compared with those without such conditions [14]. The impact extends across all age groups, negatively affecting quality of life even in infants, toddlers, and preschoolers through pruritus and sleep disturbances [15]. In China, nearly half of individuals with SD reported experiencing significant emotional problems [16]. Importantly, individuals with SD experience a reduced quality of life compared with those with dandruff alone [12].

Despite increasing recognition of SD and its impact on quality of life, significant gaps persist in the literature, particularly regarding the potential disparity or harmony between patient experiences and healthcare provider (HCP) perceptions of the disease’s burden. Moreover, there is a lack of comprehensive data quantifying mental health impacts, including anxiety, depression, and effects on self-esteem and daily life, alongside an underexplored understanding of significant unmet needs and misconceptions within the SD treatment landscape from both patient and HCP perspectives. We conducted an online survey to explore patient and dermatology HCP perspectives on SD treatment preferences, experiences, and unmet needs, aiming to understand the physical and emotional burden of the condition.

Methods

The patient and HCP surveys were conducted by The Harris Poll, a reputable market research and analytics company specializing in public opinion research. The patient survey, lasting 10 min, ran from December 14, 2021 to January 19, 2022, whereas the HCP survey, lasting 12 min, took place from December 16, 2021 to January 19, 2022. Patient inclusion criteria included being at least 18 years of age, residing in the USA, and being diagnosed with SD by an HCP. Demographic data such as age, sex, education, race/ethnicity, region, income, household size, and marital status were weighted as needed to match population proportions. Black/African American patients were balanced back to natural fallout among qualified patients, and a propensity score variable was included to adjust for respondents’ online presence. Survey participants provided consent to participate in the survey and for their data to be reported in aggregate, not individually. No identifying information was included for any patient participant. Participants were aware that the results would be published in a journal.

HCP inclusion criteria comprised being at least 18 years of age, a dermatology specialist (physician, physician assistant [PA], or nurse practitioner [NP]) licensed to practice in the USA and seeing more than one patient per week and more than one patient with SD per year. Weighting for dermatologists included years in practice, sex, and region to align data with population proportions, whereas raw data for PAs/NPs were not weighted and represented only individuals who completed the survey. HCPs provided consent to participate in the survey, for the data to be reported in aggregate (not individual participants), and were aware that the results would be published in a journal. No identifying information was included for any HCP.

Each survey was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments and Good Clinical Practice guidelines of the International Council for Harmonisation. The survey was not reviewed by an ethics committee and because of the nature of the survey and information collected is exempt from this requirement on the basis of the Human Subject Regulations Decision Charts: 2018 Requirements: 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2) for Educational Tests, Surveys, Interviews, or Observation of Public Behavior.

Results

The survey comprised responses from 300 patients with SD, with an average age of 40 years and a male majority (55%). Patients identified their race and ethnicity as White (52%), Hispanic (31%), Black or African American (12%), Asian (3%), or more than one race (3%). On average, patients experienced symptoms for 3.6 years before seeking HCP treatment, with 20% waiting 6 years or more before seeking SD treatment. Among participating HCPs (N = 601), MD/DOs constituted over half (67%), followed by PAs (24%) and NPs (10%). The mean years in practice were 3.1, with an average of 157.7 patients seen per week for all skin conditions and 323.7 patients seen per year with SD. Impact of SD on Mental Health

Nearly half of patients reported significant negative impacts on their emotional (49%) and physical (42%) well-being due to SD (Fig. 2), whereas only 32% of HCPs assessing quality of life (85% of all HCPs surveyed) acknowledged such profound effects. Additionally, nearly all patients (90%) noted a detrimental effect on their self-esteem, with half of them experiencing a substantial impact. For 33% of patients, SD significantly negatively affected their ability to sleep (Fig. 2).

Seventy-six percent of patients reported agreement (ranging from somewhat to strongly) that their symptoms induce anxiety, whereas 73% reported the same for depression. In comparison, 78% of HCPs agreed that patients experience anxiety due to their SD symptoms, and 70% agreed regarding depression. Notably, a higher percentage of patients expressed strong agreement with these emotional impacts (Fig. 3).

Impact of SD on Social Life and Personal Relationships

Fifty-one percent of patients reported experiencing a significant impact (defined as “a lot” or “a great deal”) on their social life, whereas 44% noted a similar level of impact on their personal relationships (Fig. 2). Additionally, 48% reported significant negative impacts on their daily hygiene routine. Furthermore, 81% of patients and 96% of HCPs either somewhat or strongly agreed that SD negatively impacted their or their patients’ feelings of physical appearance and attractiveness (Fig. 3). Seventy-six percent of patients vs. 91% of HCPs acknowledged a significant influence on daily hygiene routines, although more patients strongly agreed with this statement (44% vs. 32%, respectively). Seventy-four percent of patients and 95% of HCPs expressed agreement, ranging from somewhat to strong, regarding the impact of SD on clothing choices. Challenges in concealing SD symptoms were reported by 81% of patients and recognized by 89% of HCPs, with a greater number of patients strongly agreeing (36% vs. 29%). Most patients (82%) admitting to feeling embarrassed when their symptoms are mentioned by others, with 78% reporting that their symptoms led others to assume they have poor hygiene. HCPs similarly recognized this embarrassment (98%) and the misconception surrounding hygiene (87%). Patients felt misunderstood as a result of their SD, with 73% somewhat to strongly agreeing they feel isolated and 76% reporting a lack of understanding by others of SD’s impact on their lives. The lack of understanding was echoed by HCPs with 71% recognizing the isolation patients feel and 87% somewhat to strongly agreeing that others fail to grasp the full extent of SD’s impact on patients’ lives.

Impact of SD on Work and Day-to-Day Life

Most patients (72%) reported that SD adversely affects their work life to some extent, with 29% experiencing a significant negative impact (categorized as “a lot” or “a great deal”) (Fig. 2). In addition, 46% noted a significant impact on their day-to-day life (Fig. 3). Many patients agreed that without SD, they might be further along in their career (61%), that it diminishes their confidence in the workplace (59%), makes them less likely to interact with people in the workplace (58%), and even influenced them to pursue a different career path (47%) (Fig. 4). Nearly half of patients (47%) admitted to having missed work as a result of SD symptoms.

Discussion

This study sheds light on the significant burden of SD, challenging the misconception of SD as a superficial skin issue and revealing areas of potential gaps between patient experiences and HCP perceptions. Despite HCPs treating an average of more than 300 patients with SD annually, they often underestimate the condition’s severity and broader implications as reported by patients. Although 84% of patients reported their SD symptoms as moderate-to-severe, only 60% were similarly classified by HCPs, echoing a pattern seen in other chronic dermatological conditions such as atopic dermatitis [17] and psoriasis [18]. In these diseases, approximately one-third of patients perceive their condition’s severity as more severe than their physicians, a phenomenon particularly notable in psoriasis, where the divergence often correlates with the presence of depression or anxiety [18]. Given substantial reports of anxiety and depression among our sample of patients with SD, this correlation underscores the critical need for a holistic treatment approach to bridge the gap in disease perception between patients and HCPs, fostering more effective and empathetic care strategies for those living with SD. The discrepancy in SD severity assessments between patients and HCPs furthermore highlights a critical need for standardized criteria in grading SD severity, as existing methods vary widely and lack comprehensiveness, notably missing patient-reported outcomes [19–23] and potentially contributing to the underestimation of the condition’s impact.

Our analysis further reveals that although HCPs often recognize the broad impact of SD, patients tend to feel these impacts more acutely, as shown by their higher rates of strong agreement to survey questions. This suggests that patients who experience significant impacts in areas such as physical appearance, daily hygiene routines, and social interactions feel these effects intensely. On the other hand, HCPs, in their role as observers and clinicians for a wide array of patients, may capture a more average perspective reflecting an aggregation of multiple patient experiences and thus rate these impacts to be lower. Nearly half of patients reported their SD had significant impact on their daily hygiene routine, which can be particularly burdensome for some patient populations whose hair type or culture necessitates less frequent shampooing [24]. In addition, hair care regimens using chemical relaxers and hair extensions may worsen symptoms further [25]. These insights underscore the importance of recognizing the depth and variability of SD’s effects on individual patients and highlights the need for a tailored approach in treatment and support, acknowledging that although some patients might not feel severely impacted, others might experience profound disruption to their quality of life. This variability necessitates a sensitive, patient-centered approach in clinical practice, where the individual experiences, needs, and perceptions of patients are given due consideration alongside clinical observations.

Furthermore, our results reveal a significant need for better understanding and addressing the stigma and social perceptions associated with SD. Patients expressed strong concerns, echoed by HCPs, about external misconceptions and the lack of understanding surrounding SD, especially regarding perceived hygiene issues. Results from a recent study involving more than 3000 patients across Europe showed that individuals with SD experienced the strongest connections between itch intensity and feelings of stigmatization, anxiety, and depression compared with various other itchy skin conditions, including psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and others [26]. In addition, our findings are consistent with another study that investigated patients with facial SD, revealing that social anxiety stemming from the appearance of their condition is common [27]. This calls for concerted efforts to dismantle myths related to SD, particularly those surrounding hygiene, and to promote a more accurate comprehension of the condition’s comprehensive impact.

The significant psychological and social challenges faced by patients with SD underscore a critical gap in patient–HCP communication. Nearly half of the patient respondents reported substantial negative impacts on their physical and emotional well-being, with a considerable number expressing feelings of isolation and embarrassment that potentially hinder professional advancement and social interactions. This context of dissatisfaction and isolation may drive patients toward alternative sources of information and support, notably social media. One study showed that most patients with SD express dissatisfaction with their current treatment plans, often turning to social media for advice on managing their condition [28]. This trend toward self-education through social media, where content about SD is predominantly created by non-HCPs and can be rife with inaccuracies [29], mirrors broader patterns in dermatology where patients seek out online resources to address their conditions [30]. Such reliance on potentially misleading information may deter patients from seeking professional medical care and lead to self-treatment strategies that have been associated with worsened outcomes [31]. This highlights the pressing need for HCPs to recognize the extensive impact of SD beyond its physical symptoms, fostering open, empathetic communication channels that encourage patients to seek accurate and reliable medical advice, thereby reducing reliance on unverified online resources.

Our research underlines the comprehensive impact of SD, extending beyond physical symptoms to encompass significant psychological challenges, thereby highlighting a gap in HCPs’ recognition of the mental health effects of SD. Although nearly half of our survey respondents reported considerable negative effects on their emotional well-being and self-esteem, the stark difference in the perception of anxiety and depression between patients and HCPs is noteworthy. Specifically, although 35% of patients strongly indicated experiencing anxiety and 29% reported depression due to SD, only a minority of HCPs strongly recognized these mental health issues (13% for anxiety and 8% for depression). This contrast becomes less pronounced when aggregating responses that either “strongly” or “somewhat agree,” suggesting an overall awareness among HCPs of the mental health challenges associated with SD, albeit with varying degrees of acknowledgment. These results corroborate existing studies that connect SD with heightened levels of depression [32, 33] and anxiety [13], with one recent study revealing that patients with SD exhibit the highest rate of depression among various common facial dermatoses [27]. Such findings are further supported by evidence that the severity of SD symptoms directly correlates with increased stress and depression [34], emphasizing the complex interplay between emotional stress and SD flares. The emotional toll of symptoms may perpetuate a cycle, as emotional stress commonly triggers SD flares [33, 35, 36]. These insights emphasize the critical need for comprehensive treatment approaches that address not only the clinical symptoms of SD but also its broader psychological and social ramifications, fostering strategies aimed at breaking the cycle of stress and symptom exacerbation to improve overall patient quality of life.

These data highlight the considerable influence of SD on patients’ professional lives, supporting previous research that suggests a more detrimental impact on employment compared with dandruff, albeit without specifying the exact work-related aspects affected [12]. Notably, our study reveals a substantial rate of work absenteeism among participants, with roughly half reporting missed work due to SD symptoms. This absenteeism mirrors the challenges seen in conditions like atopic dermatitis [37, 38] and implies significant indirect costs related to lost productivity and hindered personal economic growth in the USA [39]. Missed workdays, coupled with patients reporting feeling less confident and less inclined to engage in social interactions at work, could explain why many patients perceived their career progression as being hindered by their symptoms. Additionally, workplace environmental factors such as air conditioning, dry or damp conditions, and sun exposure can aggravate or alleviate symptoms for a considerable number of patients [40, 41]. Nearly half of patient participants reported that their SD prompted them to pursue a different career path, suggesting these factors may contribute to alterations or constraints in professional choices and advancement.

This study provides a comprehensive exploration of the complex challenges presented by SD, addressing both its physical symptoms and emotional burden among a diverse demographic, while providing valuable insights into the varying perspectives on the disease’s impact as perceived by both patients and HCPs. Limitations of this online study include reliance on self-reported data, potentially subject to recall and social desirability biases, and cross-sectional design, which may affect the generalizability of findings. However, the sampling method was designed and validated to provide an accurate estimation of the US population as a whole.

Conclusion

These findings collectively demonstrate the need for a holistic care approach to SD, integrating not only management of the physical signs and symptoms of disease but also addressing the psychological and social burdens faced by patients with SD. Additional prospective studies are needed to characterize SD risk factors, predictors of disease trajectory, and effective interventions further. In turn, this would allow for targeted interventions to ultimately enhance quality of life for those affected by SD.
