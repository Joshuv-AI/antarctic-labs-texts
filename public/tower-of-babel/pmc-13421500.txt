Differences in emergency ambulance use between older and younger individuals in Denmark: a nationwide register-based study
Authors: Stine Ibsen, Tim Lindskou, Erik Zakariassen, Allan Riis, Christian H Nickel, Morten Breinholt Søvsø
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs populations age, the demand for Emergency Medical Services (EMS) is rising, yet differences in ambulance use between age groups remain insufficiently understood. This nationwide register-based study examined all ambulance dispatches in Denmark from 2018 to 2024, comparing contacts from older (≥ 65 years) and younger (< 65 years) individuals. Contacts from older individuals accounted for over half of all dispatches and had ambulance use rates more than four times higher than those of younger individuals. Despite this, they were less often assigned to the highest urgency level and more frequently presented with symptoms such as decreased consciousness, paralysis, or dizziness. In contrast, chest pain was most common among younger individuals. Repeated ambulance use was significantly more prevalent among older individuals. These findings suggest that older individuals have distinct patterns of EMS use, underscoring the need for age-sensitive triage and planning to meet the evolving demands of an aging population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53513-4.Keywords: Emergency medical services, Aged, Ambulance dispatch patterns, Urgency levelsSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Globally, there is a demographic shift towards an older population1. In Denmark, the population of individuals over 65 years is expected to increase by 18% from 2025 to 2035, and the proportion of those over 80 years is expected to double2. Older age increases the prevalence of chronic medical conditions and frailty, thus increasing the need for EMS, including ambulance services3. Older patients, especially if frail, often present with conditions, such as altered mental status, functional decline, and generalized weakness4–6. These presentations underscore the importance of targeted examinations during the prehospital phase and transportation to appropriate health care facilities. However, little is known about whether the demographic shifts influence how older people access emergency care. In recent years, Denmark has experienced a growing demand for EMS7,8. This aligns with the demographic shift toward an ageing population and makes it essential to investigate how these trends are connected to understand their implications better. Nonetheless, studies investigating these time trends are limited. A regional Danish study reported 35% increase in emergency calls per 1000 residents from 2013 to 20229 and similar trends have been observed in Germany, where the rise in emergency calls outpaced population growth10. In the United States a study with 2014 data found that older adults, those aged 65 and older, accounted for a disproportionate share of EMS responses and presented with distinct clinical characteristics compared to younger adults11. Despite these findings little is known about how contact patterns with ambulance services have evolved, specifically for older individuals and whether these differ from those of younger individuals regarding utilization rates and urgency levels. This knowledge will provide valuable insights into the population’s evolving needs and help optimise health care resources. This study aimed to investigate how EMS contacts and patterns of emergency ambulance dispatch have evolved over seven years (2018–2024) among older (≥ 65 years) and younger (< 65 years) individuals in Denmark. Furthermore, the study examined differences in presenting symptoms and urgency levels between contacts involving older and younger individuals.

Methods

Study design

This was a nationwide historical population-based observational study covering January 1 st, 2018, to December 31 st, 2024, of Danish ambulance dispatches. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement was followed12.

Setting

Denmark has a total population 6 million inhabitants divided into five regions. Each region is responsible for healthcare services, including EMS and hospitals. As a public funded system, the Danish healthcare system, including EMS, is public funded and free for all citizens13. Each citizen in Denmark is registered with a unique civil registration number used for public contacts including healthcare contacts. Patients can request acute healthcare by calling the Danish emergency number 112, and ambulance dispatched based on these calls are referred to as emergency number dispatch. In instances where a patient has consulted a general practitioner or out-of-hours healthcare services with an acute condition, an ambulance can be dispatched if necessary. These ambulance dispatches are referred to as primary care dispatch. In Denmark, emergency calls relating to a medical emergency are forwarded to an Emergency Medical Coordination Centre (EMCC), where they are handled by regionally based healthcare professionals, including nurses and paramedics; depending on the region, a physician may be physically present or available for consultation by telephone. The healthcare professionals at the EMCC have the option to advise or refer the patient, as well as to dispatch an ambulance. Healthcare professionals use the decision support tool, Danish Index for Emergency Care (Danish Index), to assess the main reason for calling, among 37 criteria. The Danish Index also includes the criterion ‘assigned task’, which contains interhospital transfers along with tasks concerning transport from primary care services. Each criterion is subdivided into five urgency levels (A-E). Level A describes life-threatening conditions or potential life-threatening conditions, level B is urgent but not life-threatening conditions, level C is non-urgent conditions that require an ambulance, level D is non-urgent conditions requiring supine patient transport, and level E is conditions requiring medical advice only14. Due to regional differences in the use of the Danish Index for primary care dispatch; some contacts are not assigned a Danish Index. However, these contacts are still assigned an urgency level, which determine the appropriate vehicle response. The basic prehospital unit is an ambulance staffed by two prehospital professionals. Additional resources include rapid response vehicles and mobile emergency care units with prehospital anaesthesiologists; resource availability varies by region13.

Study population

The study comprised all contacts to an EMCC that resulted in the dispatch of an ambulance (urgency levels A, B and C) in the study period, including primary care dispatch and emergency number dispatch. The study period was selected based on the nationwide standardization of the electronic Danish Prehospital Medical Record usage across Danish regions from 2018 onwards, following its initial implementation in 2015.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

EMS contacts with repeated occurrences involving the same individual were included multiple times during the study period. Records lacking a valid civil registration number were excluded, as this prevents accurate age and sex determination and as data validity cannot be ensured in the prehospital dataset. Interhospital transfers were also excluded. Contacts from individuals of all ages were included. Urgency level D and E were excluded as they represent non-urgent conditions that do not require the initiation of a prehospital journal. We performed no imputation of missing values. Due to regional differences in how Danish Index is applied to primary care dispatches, these contacts were excluded from the analysis of Danish Index criteria.

Data source and variables

This study was based on data from the nationwide electronic Danish Prehospital Medical Record, comprising information recorded during emergency call-taking at the EMCC13. Variables included demographic data on the study population, age, and sex. Each call resulting in a dispatched ambulance was classified as either an emergency number dispatch or a primary care dispatch. For the distribution of Danish Index criteria, the top five most-used Danish index criteria for each group were reported. To assess repeated use, individuals were grouped according to the number of ambulance dispatches within each calendar year. Repeated use was categorized into three groups based on frequency: 2–3 uses, 4–5 uses, and 6 + uses. Data on the background population were retrieved from Statistics Denmark which provides continuously updated demographic data from the Danish Civil Registration System, ensuring complete national coverage15. Age was presented as a continuous variable and stratified into two groups: younger (0–64 years) and older (65–110 years) for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were carried out to summarise the characteristics of emergency ambulance dispatches stratified by the older and younger individuals. Age was analysed as a continuous variable and reported as medians with interquartile ranges reported with lower (Q1) and upper (Q3) quartiles. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages are reported. Analyses of repeated use was considered exploratory and assessed using descriptive statistics.

Incidence rates (IR) for ambulance dispatches were calculated for each year from 2018 to 2024, stratified by age groups. Data included annual case counts and population sizes for each group. IR were expressed per 1000 inhabitants, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using a Poisson distribution. To analyse temporal trends in EMS contacts a Poisson regression model was applied. The number of contacts was modelled as the dependent variable with the logarithm of populations size as offset. To evaluate differences in temporal trends between age groups an interaction term between calendar year and age groups was included. Robust standard errors were applied to account for potential overdispersion. A sensitivity analysis was performed in which older individuals were stratified into two age groups: 65–79 years and ≥ 80 years. Temporal trends were performed using the same Poisson regression model as in the main analyses. Stata/MP 19.5 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, United States of America) was used for all statistical analyses.

Ethical considerations

The need for informed consent was waived by the Danish Patient Safety Authority, in accordance with Danish law governing the use of registry data for research purposes. According to Danish legislation, registry-based studies that do not involve biological material do not require approval from the Committee on Health Research Ethics16. This study was registered in the North Denmark Region (F2024-137) and the handover of patient medical records for research was approved by the North Denmark Region (1–45-72–4526-24), following the Danish Health Act17. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including those outlined by the Danish Patient Safety Authority and national data protection laws.

Results

A total of 5 494 681 requests to EMCCs were made in Denmark during the study period (2018–2024). Of these, 2 155 923 contacts were excluded as they were urgency level D and E. 171 080 were excluded as they were identified as interhospital transports, and 121 776 were excluded due to missing civil registration number. Thus, 3 045 902 contacts were included, of which 1 449 171 (47.6%) involved younger individuals and 1 596 731 (52.4%) involved older individuals (see Fig. 1).

The proportion of repeated ambulance use was higher among older individuals, with 28% having multiple contacts, compared to 15.3% among younger individuals. A decline in repeated use was observed across all frequency categories (2–3, 4–5, and 6 + uses) for both younger and older individuals. Increasing levels of repeated use was observed with higher median age across both age groups and a higher proportion of females, see Supplementary Table 1.

Temporal trends and incidence rate ratios

In Poisson regression analyses the overall IR of ambulance dispatches showed a small but statistically significant decrease over time. The annual incidence rate ratio (IRR) was 0.992 (95% CI 0.985–0.999, p = 0.017), corresponding to an average annual decrease of approximately 0.8%. Older age was strongly associated with higher rates of ambulance dispatches. Older individuals had more than four times higher IRR compared with younger (IRR 4.47, 95% CI 4.28–4.68; p < 0.001). Male sex was associated with higher IRR compared with females (IRR 1.14, 95% CI 1.09–1.20; p < 0.001).

Interaction between year and age group

The temporal trend in ambulance dispatch rates differed marginal between age groups. (interaction IRR 0.99, 95% CI 0.97–1.00; p = 0.049), indicating that the decline in incidence over time was more pronounced among older individuals than among younger individuals.

Danish Index criteria and urgency levels

Danish Index criteria were missing in 105 367(5.2%) of the emergency number calls, 4.5% for the older group and 5.9% for the younger group. The top four Danish Index criteria were identical for both age groups, although they appeared in a different order (see Table 3). On average across the study period, these four criteria accounted for 58.9% of all dispatches among older individuals and 43.0% among younger individuals. The fifth most common criterion varied between the two groups; for the older group, it was Breathing difficulties 108 833 (11.6%), whereas for the younger group, it was Seizures 72 830 (6.9%). For the older group, the criterion Decreased consciousness – paralysis – dizziness was used the most with 19 295(15.8%) in 2018 and 31 676 (21.8%) in 2024. For the younger group, the most used criterion was Chest pain – heart disease, which increased from 17 824 (11.9%) in 2018 to 22 307 (14.9%) % in 2024. The use of the criteria Unclarified problem decreased for both groups during the period, from 19 014 (15.6%) to 12,260 (8.4%) for older group and 18 149 (12.1%) to 9 407 (6.3%) for the younger group. Additionally, the older group had a lower proportion of urgency level A ambulance dispatched, 626 567 (39.2%), compared to the younger group, 737 064 (50.9%). (see Table 4)

Sensitivity analysis

Among older individuals aged 65–79 years the incidence decreased with approximately 1.6% (IRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.98–0.99) and for the older individuals aged ≥ 80 years the incidence decreased approximately 2.5% (IRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99). Sex differences were observed in both groups. Among individuals aged 65–79 years, male individuals had a 29% higher incidence compared with female (IRR 1.29, 95% CI 1.22–1.37), among individuals aged ≥ 80 years this was 19% (IRR 1.19, 95% CI 1.12–1.27).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate temporal trends in EMS contacts resulting in ambulance dispatch and to describe differences in dispatch characteristics between contacts involving older (≥ 65) and younger (< 65) individuals from 2018 to 2024. The IRs of ambulance dispatches were more than four times higher for contact from older compared to contacts from younger individuals and decreased for both groups in the study period. The emergency call dispatch for older individuals changed from 2018 to 2024, with an increase in number dispatch and a decline in primary care dispatch. Furthermore, the study showed that younger individuals had a higher proportion of the highest urgency level A compared to older individuals. The first four of the top five used Danish Index criteria were the same for both groups but differed in order. Decreased consciousness, paralysis – dizziness was the most common Danish Index criteria for older individuals and Chest pain - heart disease for young individuals.

The increasing demand for ambulance services in Denmark aligns with trends previously reported across Danish regions7,8, and similar patterns have been observed in other European countries10,18. During the study period, COVID-19 led to extensive reorganizations within the Danish health care system to mitigate the effects of the disease, and fewer emergency call contacts19–21. The reduction in emergency contacts corresponds to these findings, where an overall decline was observed in 2020 for emergency number dispatches. In contrast, primary care dispatch increased from 2019 to 2020, which may indicate that patients changed their contact patterns due to COVID-19. The observed national decline in IRs contrasts with findings from Region Zealand, where a 35% increase was reported between 2013 and 20229. This discrepancy may reflect regional differences, as this study includes data from all five Danish regions. The high IRs among older individuals correspond to findings from Austria, where a comparable rate of 217 per 1000 inhabitants in 2019 was reported22. Interestingly, the most significant decline in IR was observed among older individuals. The reasons behind this trend remain unclear. It may reflect an overall improvement in the health status of the elderly population or perhaps the growing availability of alternative healthcare services tailored to their needs. One such example is the temporary stays, and so-called “acute-beds”, offered by Danish municipalities, where patients can be admitted not only after hospital discharge but also directly from their homes or care facilities23. Differences in dispatch patterns were observed with older individuals having the highest proportion of primary care dispatch (41.3%), a similar distribution has been reported from Norway24. However, the proportion of primary care dispatch decreased during the study period, demonstrating a change in older individuals’ contact patterns. A qualitative study from Ireland reported that older people face challenges when seeking out-of-hours medical care, especially due to transportation barriers and the discomfort of asking for help, particularly at night. As a result, many prefer contacting services by phone25. Additionally, a systematic review by Booker et al. highlighted that ambulance use for primary care-sensitive problems is influenced by a complex interplay of individual, social, and systemic factors, including perceived urgency, access barriers, and uncertainty about appropriate care pathways. These findings support the notion that changes in dispatch patterns may reflect broader shifts in healthcare-seeking behaviour rather than changes in clinical needs alone26.

In this study, older individuals received lower urgency levels compared to younger individuals. These findings correspond to what has previously been reported in a study from Sweden, where 37.3% of older adults (> 70 years) received the highest priority level compared to 46% of adults (18–69 years)27. In contrast, an Austrian study, which relied on prehospital clinical evaluation using NACA-score (0–7 severity scale, developed for grade the severity of medical and traumatic emergencies), found that older patients were assigned higher severity levels compared to younger patients22. This discrepancy may stem from the differences in prehospital assessment methods and might indicate the complexity of assessing emergency needs in older patients. Symptoms may be obscured by polypharmacy, cognitive decline, or natural age-related changes, which can blur the clinical picture and hinder the detection of serious conditions28. The lower assignments of highest urgency levels among older individuals may reflect both differences in clinical presentations and the triage process. Older patients frequently present with non-specific symptoms, which may complicate the recognition of serious underlying conditions and complicate early risk stratification4,5,28. Previous studies have shown that older patients are at increased risk of under triage in both the prehospital and emergency apartment settings, even when severity of illness is comparable to that of younger patients29–31. Furthermore, standardized triage and dispatch criteria may not fully account for age-related complexity and communication challenges inherent to telephone-based emergency assessment31,32. Among contacts from older individuals, the most frequently used Danish index dispatch criteria were Decreased consciousness, paralysis - dizziness, which increased by 38% from 2018 to 2024. This change may reflect both age-related differences in symptom presentation and external factors influencing symptom recognition and use of emergency medical services. A Swiss study found that presenting symptoms differ between frail and non-frail patients, with frailty being associated with generalized weakness after adjusting for age, sex, and disease severity6. The present study does not include information about frailty status; future research should further investigate whether frailty prevalence or comorbidities in older patients is associated with a change in presenting symptoms. In 2019, a nationwide stroke campaign using Danish national television as media was launched. It introduced a slogan to help recognize stroke symptoms and act quickly by calling 1-1-2. The period following this campaign coincided with an increased use of the Danish Index criteria Decreased consciousness, paralysis – dizziness among older individuals. Similar patterns have been observed in Norway, where a marked increase in ambulance dispatches for suspected stroke was not accompanied by an increase in confirmed stroke diagnoses33. In 2010, the Danish Health Authority released a consolidation act emphasizing that atypical chest pain could be a manifestation of acute coronary syndrome. This initiative temporally coincided with a high frequency of the Danish Index criterion Chest pain - heart disease among younger individuals calling 1-1-2. Given the observational design of the study, these temporal associations should not be interpreted as causal, and the relationship between national campaigns and the use of Danish Index criteria remains speculative. The proportion of individuals with Danish Index Unclarified problem declined over the study period. This trend has previously been observed and may be associated with targeted efforts within the EMCC to reduce its use34. This study is based on comprehensive national data from Denmark, which enhances the generalisability of the findings within similar healthcare systems. The Danish EMS system is publicly funded and universally accessible, which may differ from systems in countries with private or insurance-based healthcare models. Therefore, while the observed trends—such as the high incidence of EMS use among older individuals and the shift in dispatch patterns—are consistent with findings from other European countries, caution should be exercised when generalising the results to countries with different healthcare infrastructures. Nevertheless, the demographic shift towards an ageing population and the increasing demand for EMS are global phenomena, suggesting that several of the study’s findings may be relevant beyond the Danish context.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study was the large national study population. Danish citizens have free access to healthcare, including prehospital emergency medical services, which minimises selection bias based on financial resources. The results from this study may inform both clinical and organisational practice by providing population-level overview of ambulance utilization patterns, which can support resource allocation and targeted development of prehospital services, particular in relation to age-related differences in service use.

A limitation of this study is the exclusion of contacts without a registered civil registration number, which may introduce potential bias. Such cases may involve non‑resident individuals or critically ill patients who could not be identified at ambulance arrival. However, the direction and level of any resulting bias, including its impact on observed differences between older and younger individuals, cannot be determined. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the proportion of missing data was low, at only 2.2%. Another limitation was the missing Danish Index for primary care dispatch. Patients with certain symptoms may be more inclined to seek help from their general practitioners, potentially affecting the distribution of symptoms in this study. Furthermore, this study only included urgency level A, B and C, and it is possible that some level D contacts could be under triaged, especially for the older patients, as previously described29,30. Another limitation is the lack of data on potential confounding factors such as comorbidities, socioeconomic status, or frailty. These variables may influence both the likelihood of contacting EMS, and the urgency level assigned, and their absence limits the ability to adjust for underlying differences between age groups. Analyses of urgency level assignment were descriptive and limited to comparisons between age groups. Differences were not adjusted for factors such as sex or time of contact and may therefore be influenced by differences in clinical presentation or context rather than age alone. This should be taken into account when interpreting age‑related differences in urgency level assignment. This study was conducted at the system level, using emergency medical contacts as the unit of analysis rather than unique patients. Consequently, repeated contacts by the same patient were treated as independent events, limiting the ability to examine patterns among frequent users or symptom consistency across multiple contacts. The impact of the COVID-19 outbreak remains unclear; however, there was d a peak in 2021. This trend may not have been observed without the pandemic.

Conclusion

From 2018 to 2024, ambulance dispatches declined for both younger and older individuals, with the largest reduction found among older individuals - contrary to expectations and trends reported in other settings. Contacts from older individuals consistently accounted for over four times as many dispatches as younger individuals, while representing a smaller proportion of emergency number dispatch and being less frequently assessed as requiring the highest level of urgency. Among older individuals, stroke-related symptoms increased over time, while younger individuals more frequently presented with chest pain. This pattern may reflect increased public awareness driven by national health campaigns; however, no causal relationship can be established.

These findings highlight the need for age-sensitive EMS planning and resource allocation. The combination of high utilization and lower assigned urgency among older individuals suggests that current triage systems may not fully capture the complexity of this population, underscoring the importance of strengthened geriatric EMS assessment competencies in EMS.
