Plasma membrane invaginations and their diverse cellular functions
Authors: Hongxian Zhu, Carina Lyons, Andrew M Sydor, Donald W Hilgemann, John H Brumell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe plasma membrane (PM), a dynamic boundary between a cell and its environment, has a complex inner architecture that includes diverse subcellular structures. Among these, PM invaginations (membrane infolds) exhibit varied morphologies and functions. This review explores the structural diversity and physiological roles of PM invaginations, emphasizing their importance in regulating cellular processes in complex organisms. We describe their presence and significance in diverse cell types and organisms. Understanding these specialized structures offers deeper insight into cellular organization and function, highlighting their indispensable role in sustaining biological activity.Keywords: Plasma membrane, Subcellular structures, Fundamental structure

Excess membrane storage: a fundamental yet underrecognized feature of the PM

Historically, the plasma membrane (PM) has been regarded as a fundamental structure that serves as a barrier, separating the interior of the cell from the external environment [1, 2]. The early concept of the membrane was that it formed a simple, continuous boundary that was integral to essential functions such as signaling and ion transport [1–3]. As research methodologies advanced, however, a more complex view of the PM began to emerge. The membrane was no longer seen as a static, passive boundary but as a dynamic and multifunctional structure that plays an active role in cellular processes [4].

Over 100 years ago, scientists like Overton [1] and Gorter and Grendel [2] proposed that the PM was composed of lipid layers that form a bilayer structure. In 1961, Robertson further advanced this understanding with the “unit membrane concept,” indicating that all cellular membranes share a similar lipid-protein composition [3]. These foundational models laid the groundwork for the fluid mosaic model proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972, which depicted the PM as a dynamic structure with proteins embedded in a fluid lipid bilayer [4]. This model highlighted the PM’s role in various cellular functions, including signaling, transport, and interaction with the extracellular environment.

Further advances revealed that the PM does not merely serve as a simple and smooth barrier but contains various intricate and specialized structures (microdomains) that go beyond its functions at the surface [5]. The discovery of membrane protrusions, such as ruffles [6], filopodia [7, 8], and invadopodia [9], further extended the understanding of the PM’s complexity. These protrusions are dynamic structures that facilitate cellular movement, changes in shape, and interaction with the extracellular matrix, all of which contribute to essential processes like cell migration and tissue formation [6–9]. One of the most striking discoveries was that the PM often contains more surface area than is immediately apparent when considering the smooth outer layer of the cell [10]. Fibroblasts, for example, have 3 to 5 times more PM than would be needed to form a cell with a smooth surface [11]. This “excess” membrane area, which was found to exist in the form of invaginations, protrusions, and other specialized structures, challenged the earlier, oversimplified view of the membrane as merely a boundary. Given that lipid bilayers can be stretched by only a few percent before rupturing, it is essential that cells maintain a PM reservoir to allow for physical distortions during cell growth, mechanical perturbation, and movement [12].

Two of the most recognizable PM invagination systems are T-tubules and clathrin-coated pits. In 1881, it was proposed that muscle must contain a network of invaginations contiguous with the surface membrane to explain the rapid depolarization into striated muscle [13]. Just 16 years later, such invaginations were verified to exist in cardiac muscle via India ink staining [14] and the submicroscopic structure of cardiac T-tubules was described in 1957 [15]. In contrast, the discovery of clathrin-coated pits marked the inception of our understanding of intricate processes involved in cellular uptake of membrane, solutes, and membrane receptors. Electron microscopy studies in the early 1960 s revealed the presence of coated vesicles and pits on the cell surface of diverse cell types [16, 17]. Since then, an array of other invaginations [18, 19] including caveolae in mammalian cells [20–22] and eisosomes in yeast [23, 24], have been identified, each contributing to cellular architecture and diverse cellular functions.

The discovery of large PM reservoirs in cells naturally prompted speculation about how surplus membrane is sequestered, regulated, and utilized. How do cells maintain a balance between producing excess membrane and integrating it into cellular activities? Moreover, how is the abundance of this excess membrane adjusted in response to cellular needs? These questions form the core of ongoing research into membrane dynamics and function, highlighting the adaptive and multifaceted roles of the PM and PM microdomains in cellular homeostasis.

While protrusions and other membrane structures are integral to the overall function of the PM and are relatively well-characterized [25–29], PM invaginations are less explored and lack comprehensive categorizations. These specialized PM microdomains are not only critical for maintaining the cell’s mechanical and structural integrity but also play central roles in a wide variety of essential cellular processes. In this review, we will first describe the experimental and imaging techniques used to study plasma membrane invaginations, then discuss the theoretical frameworks for their formation. We will next examine their structures and functions in different cell types and how they are exploited in diverse cellular processes. Finally, we will survey PM invaginations across the tree of life, beginning with mammalian cells and extending to other eukaryotes and bacteria. Altogether, we will demonstrate that these ubiquitous structures are fundamental structures of the PM and central to a diverse array of cellular functions. A deeper understanding of the presence, formation, regulation, and functional significance of these invagination structures is crucial for unraveling the broader mechanisms through which cells utilize their excess membrane to support key physiological activities.

Techniques for exploring PM invaginations

The characterization of PM invaginations requires a diverse set of methodologies that span imaging, biochemical, biophysical, and computational approaches. Each technique offers unique insights into membrane structure, dynamics, and function across different biological scales.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy (EM) remains foundational for studying PM invaginations due to its unparalleled resolution. Indeed, the earliest PM invaginations were identified via EM in the early 1960’s [16, 17]. A wide range of EM modalities have since been employed, including scanning electron microscopy [30], transmission electron microscopy [31, 32], and cryo-electron microscopy [31, 33–36]. EM’s high resolution can be combined with fluorescence microscopy to target specific proteins, enabling correlative light-electron microscopy (CLEM). For example, recent CLEM studies have demonstrated that RAB10+ tubules can be contiguous with the PM [37].

A key challenge in using EM to visualize PM invaginations is achieving sufficient contrast of the PM. Tannic acid can be used during fixation to enhance PM straining [38] and improves staining by lead salts [39]. Other contrast-enhancing agents include Ruthenium Red [37, 40, 41] and Malachite Green, which stabilizes lipids and improves membrane visibility [37, 42].

Another challenge is avoiding membrane damage during chemical fixation. Techniques such as high pressure freezing (HPF) [43], in which samples are rapidly frozen under high pressure to prevent ice crystal formation, and freeze-substitution (FS) [44], a technique in which frozen water is replaced with an organic solvent at subzero temperatures, has been shown to yield superior results to chemical fixation in preserving the PM structure [45]. HPF-FS has been particularly valuable in yeast studies, revealing, for example, that deep invaginations form when a non-assembling mutant of Vps1, a dynamin homolog, complements a vps1 knockout strain [46].

Fluorescence microscopy

Fluorescence microscopy, especially confocal imaging, is widely used to study PM invaginations [21, 47–49]. It enables diverse measurements, including PM fluidity [50], lipid composition [51, 52], and live-cell imaging of invagination dynamics [37]. Furthermore, membrane dyes [53] such as Membright [54], CellMask (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and FM4-64 [55] help demonstrate continuity between invaginations and the PM.

Conventional fluorescence microscopy is limited by the diffraction barrier, restricting the resolution needed to visualize nanodomains, trafficking events, and PM-organelle contacts sites [56–58]. Super-resolution microscopy—including STED (Stimulated Emission Depletion), PALM (Photoactivated Localization Microscopy), and STORM (Stochastic Optical Reconstruction Microscopy)—overcome this limitation, achieving resolutions down to 5 nm [59]. These methods have been used to map spatial relationships between membrane sculpting proteins [21] and to study clathrin coat dynamics at endocytic sites [49].

Other techniques

Beyond fluorescence and electron microscopy, more niche techniques, such as optical tweezers, patch clamp, and computational modeling, have been utilized to study PM invaginations.

Optical tweezers use focused lasers to manipulate microscopic particles [60]. They have been used to study PM elasticity, stiffness, and membrane reservoir function [37, 61]. Notably, they revealed that PM flickering, which is the small-scale dynamics fluctuations in the shape and position of the PM, has both active and passive components [62] and has been used alongside EM and fluorescence microscopy to assess how lysophospholipid head group size affects PM invaginations [63].

Patch clamp electrophysiology records ionic currents across cell membranes, enabling detection of changes in membrane capacitance and conductance [64]. These changes reflect PM events such as invagination formation, fusion, and trafficking. Capacitance measurements are particularly useful for quantifying membrane surface area changes, providing indirect evidence of invagination dynamics and their roles as PM reservoirs [11, 65]. Recent innovations such as scanning ion conductance microscopy, and fluorescence patch clamp [66] promise enhanced spatial resolution and functional insights into PM invaginations.

Computational models simulate membrane curvature and protein interactions, offering mechanistic insights into PM dynamics [67]. These models can validate experimental data or predict invagination behavior. For example, computational modeling was used to support a cooperative curvature mechanism in clathrin coat formation observed via super-resolution microscopy [49].

Future outlook

This field is rapidly developing, and additional methods, such as expansion microscopy [68] and machine learning [69, 70], are poised to deepen our understanding of PM invaginations. Though their current application to PM invaginations remains limited, increasing accessibility and refinement will likely drive broader application. Ultimately, the characterization of the diverse array of PM invaginations will require an integrated approach, combining biochemical, imaging, biophysical, and computational tools to fully elucidate their structure and function.

Theory of PM invaginations

An underlying principle of nearly all forms of cellular membrane remodeling, including PM invaginations, is that membranes bend in response to forces or boundary conditions that alter their local curvature while preserving surface area [71]. This can be quantitatively described by the Helfrich elastic theory of lipid bilayers, which treats the membrane as a two-dimensional incompressible fluid sheet that deforms to minimize its bending free energy [71]. This framework was later extended by Reinhard Lipowsky in 1992 to describe domain budding driven by line tension [72]. When lateral heterogeneity (for instance, lipid phase separation or protein clustering) creates interfacial tension between membrane domains, the system can reduce its energy by bending to minimize the length of the domain boundary. This process captures the physical essence of membrane budding and tubulation and mirrors biological events in which protein or lipid domains nucleate curvature [72]. For example, BAR-domain proteins and clathrin coats impose a preferred curvature on the PM, stabilizing narrow necks and buds. The line tension-bending interplay also parallels the sorting of cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich microdomains that drive caveolae formation, where caveolin oligomers act as curvature generators and mechanical buffers against tension.

Extensions of the Helfrich model that include active stresses or monolayer asymmetry have been particularly useful in explaining dynamic processes such as clathrin-mediated endocytosis [73]. In this context, the bilayer itself is not a uniform elastic sheet but a strongly asymmetric composite: the outer leaflet is enriched in sphingomyelin and phosphatidylcholine, whereas the inner leaflet contains more phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine and phosphoinositides, together with an uneven distribution of cholesterol. Recent work shows that such phospholipid and cholesterol asymmetry generates leaflet-specific spontaneous curvature and bending rigidity, effectively pre-patterning the plasma membrane for coat assembly and fission at selected sites. Related biophysical modeling further suggests that inner-leaflet phosphatidylethanolamine can recruit cholesterol to the cytosolic leaflet to reduce bending free energy, directly coupling lipid asymmetry to membrane curvature [74]. Similarly, local phosphoinositide remodeling alters spontaneous curvature H0, facilitating the transition from flat lattices to deeply invaginated pits [73]. Besides lipid asymmetry, the polymerization of clathrin triskelions constrains the membrane into a spherical bud, while actin polymerization at the invagination rim provides the contractile force needed to overcome membrane tension and achieve scission [73].

At larger length scales, the same mechanical principles extend to tubular membrane instabilities involved in platelet biogenesis from megakaryocytes [75]. As demonstrated by Bächer et al. [76], the elongation and fragmentation of proplatelet shafts under hydrodynamic shear can be modeled as an elasto-hydrodynamic (Rayleigh-Plateau) instability, where the competition between membrane bending rigidity, surface tension, and viscous flow determines the wavelength and breakup pattern of tubules. This theoretical framework parallels the cytoskeleton-driven thinning and fission events observed during PM invagination and vesicle release [77].

Together, the Helfrich-Lipowsky formalism and its active extensions provide a unifying physical basis for understanding PM invaginations across scales, from nanometer-scale caveolae and clathrin-coated pits to micron-scale fragmentation of megakaryocyte protrusions during platelet formation and continue to guide the understanding of PM microdomain organization and the biophysical principles underlying PM invaginations formation despite their morphological heterogeneity.

Condensates and PM invaginations

There is a growing appreciation for the role of biomolecular condensates in the regulation of cellular processes and structures, including PM invaginations. In contrast to organelles enclosed by membranes, biomolecular condensates form through intermolecular forces. An important biophysical process contributing to biomolecular condensate formation is liquid–liquid phase separation, a thermodynamically favorable process by which a liquid mixture separates into distinct liquid phases [78, 79]. Phase separation is driven by a combination of low affinity for the solvent and higher affinity between the condensate components [78, 80]. These interactions are often weak and multivalent, which allows for dynamic break-and-reform interactions that concentrate biomolecules while maintaining liquid-like material properties [78]. These liquid-like properties allow biomolecular condensates to regulate various dynamic cellular processes, as reviewed elsewhere [81].

Biomolecular condensates can associate with the PM to carry out diverse functions. Some examples of function provided by biomolecular condensates at the PM include T cell signaling [82–84], receptor tyrosine kinase signaling clusters [85], zonula occludin phase separation required for tight junction formation [86], the organization of pre-synaptic vesicles to regulate neurotransmitter release [87–89], signaling from the post-synaptic density [90, 91], and ensuring proper bacterial chromosome segregation into daughter cells by PM-bound nucleoid occlusion protein condensates [92–94].

Interactions between condensates and biological membranes can be understood as “wetting”, a term describing liquid deformation through interactions with a solid surface [95]. The extent of wetting depends on the affinity of the condensate components for the surface: in the absence of interactions, the condensate remains “dewetted” and retains its spherical shape conferred by surface tension. Between the dewetted and wetted states, “pre-wetting” describes a state in which a single protein-rich layer coats a phase-separated membrane, effectively forming a two-dimensional condensate that preserves the compositional and dynamic features of bulk phase separation [96]. Such pre-wetting layers have been proposed to arise from coupled protein-lipid phase separation [96] and have been observed for signaling condensates at T cell and B cell interfaces [97, 98], suggesting that pre-wetted condensates could modulate local curvature and prime sites for PM invaginations. As wetting increases further, in partial wetting, the droplet partially deforms to adhere to the surface, whereas in complete wetting, the droplet spreads into a thin layer [99, 100]. Condensates can exert work on membranes through elastocapillarity, the deformation of soft substrates in response to wetting [89, 101]. The direction and extent of membrane deformation depend on factors such as the extent of wetting, interfacial tension, condensate and membrane radii, condensate elasticity, and membrane rigidity [89, 102]. For example, glycinin, a soybean vacuolar storage protein, undergoes phase separation in vitro [103, 104]. When giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) are added to glycinin condensates at low salt concentrations, the condensates remain dewetted [102]. However, at high salt concentrations, glycinin condensates contact the membranes to undergo wetting, ranging from partial to complete wetting in a salt concentration-dependent manner. The partially wetted glycinin condensates can induce curvature of the GUV membrane through the capillary forces generated by the high interfacial tension. These deformations are visualized as membrane ruffling at the condensate-membrane interface and depend on the membrane tension [102]. Similarly, artificially tethering condensates composed of FUS, a phase separation-prone RNA-binding protein, to the surface of a GUV using a 6xHis affinity tag causes inward invagination of the GUV membrane, whereas tethering a non-condensing 6xHis-GFP results in uniform coating of the GUV without altering membrane curvature [105].

These in vitro studies suggest that in addition to the other regulators of PM invaginations discussed in this review, the forces generated by condensate-membrane interactions may contribute to PM invagination. Indeed, recent work on endosomal sorting has added a phase separation perspective to the regulation of ESCRT-dependent membrane invagination and fission. The ESCRT-associated protein FREE1 undergoes liquid–liquid phase separation on endosomal membranes to form biomolecular condensates that, by themselves, can bend membranes inward and drive scission, generating intraluminal vesicles even in the absence of the canonical late-acting ESCRT machinery [106]. By wetting endosomal membranes, FREE1 condensates create line tension at the three-phase contact line, providing capillary forces sufficient for both budding and fission. These findings suggest that condensate-mediated mechanics can act in parallel with, or upstream of, ESCRT complexes and raise the possibility that similar phase-separation-driven, line-tension mechanisms contribute to ESCRT-dependent and -independent PM invaginations and fission events at the cell surface [106]. Together with recent three-dimensional reconstructions of ESCRT-III flat spirals on membranes, which link ESCRT-III polymer architecture to curvature generation and scission [35], these results underscore a broader role for phase-separated protein assemblies in sculpting and severing cellular membranes.

In addition, emerging evidence implicates condensates in endocytic pathways. In clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME), the extracellular RALF1 protein regulates receptor internalization in plants [107, 108]. RALF1 binds FER and LLG1 co-receptors at the PM [108–110], and its ability to induce their endocytosis requires the formation of RALF1-pectin condensates at the PM-cell wall interface [107]. On the cytosolic side, the plant TSET/TPLATE complex (TPC), which includes the phospholipid-binding AtEH1 protein [111, 112], forms condensates at the PM that recruit clathrin and accessory proteins to drive endocytosis [113]. Importantly, AtEH1 mutations that change the dynamics of the condensates cause defects in endocytic progression and in dynamin-mediated scission of invaginated structures [113]. PM bending in plants is energetically costly due to the high turgor pressure; thus, the capillary forces exerted by condensates may function to overcome this high membrane rigidity [105, 113, 114]. Although the TPC is not conserved in yeast and metazoans [114–116], analogous mechanisms of endocytosis initiation exist. In yeast, Ede1 phase separation induces recruitment of the endocytic machinery [117], while in metazoans, this role is performed by EPS15 and FCHO1 condensates [118]. Perturbations in these condensates’ formation or dynamics disrupt endocytosis [117, 118], pointing to an overarching role of finely tuned condensate properties for proper endocytosis across distinct evolutionary lineages. Beyond the initiation stage of CME, intersectin 1 (ITSN1) has recently been shown to organize and stabilize endocytic protein interaction networks by assembling EPS15/FCHO2-, AP2-, and dynamin2-containing clusters that support clathrin-coated pit maturation [119]. Complementary in vitro reconstitution and NMR studies further demonstrate that early endocytic condensates can nucleate clathrin triskelia and, reciprocally, that clathrin lattice assembly restricts condensate growth in a surfactant-like manner, thereby stabilizing protein–protein interactions while imposing the preferred curvature of the clathrin coat [120].

Furthermore, condensates also regulate a number of clathrin-independent endocytic pathways. In fast endophilin-mediated endocytosis (FEME), a type of dynamin-dependent endocytosis leading to the rapid internalization of specific receptors [121, 122], highly dynamic endophilin clusters form at the PM to prime receptor internalization upon ligand binding [121, 122]. In vitro, endophilin phase separates on GUVs and drives tubulation in the presence of lamellipodin [123]. Similarly, ultrafast endocytosis, a process 200 times faster than clathrin-mediated endocytosis, occurs in active zones of neuronal synapses and requires condensates composed of [124] a dynamin splice isoform (Dynamin 1xA) and syndapin-1, with inhibition reducing endocytic speed by 100-fold [125].

From a more global perspective, proteins involved in endocytic and vesicle trafficking pathways are enriched in intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) [79, 126], pointing to a potential broad role of condensates in regulating membrane dynamics [78]. IDRs can contribute to membrane deformation through steric forces due to their more expanded conformations [127] or through their ability to engage in weak multivalent interactions promoting condensate formation and membrane wetting [126]. Investigations of IDRs that drive PM invagination have revealed, for example, that deleting any of the IDRs in Cavin1, a protein involved in the formation of caveolae, causes defective phase separation in vitro and loss of caveolae in cultured cells [128]. In summary, these findings highlight the need to consider condensate-membrane dynamics as a key factor in PM invagination formation.

Functions of PM invaginations

PM invaginations serve as a nexus for a multitude of critical cellular processes (Fig. 1). Structurally, some PM invaginations allow for the expansion of the membrane surface area [33], accommodating fluctuations in cell volume in response to osmotic changes or pH variations [18, 129]. Nutrient exchange is another pivotal function of PM invaginations. In yeast, furrow-like PM invaginations termed eisosomes are known to play a role in the localization and regulation of specific nutrient transporters [23, 24]. For instance, in the presence of preferred nitrogen sources like ammonia, the transporters for alternative nitrogen sources are sequestered into eisosomes, effectively inactivating them [130]. This transporter sequestration is an energy-saving strategy that prevents unnecessary uptake of less preferred nutrients when more favorable ones are available [130]. Furthermore, PM invaginations form specialized domains for signal transduction that concentrate ligands and receptors, thereby enhancing the specificity and speed of cellular responses to external stimuli [20, 131].

Functions of plasma membrane invaginations. Plasma membrane (PM) invaginations have tight association with scaffold proteins (e.g., BAR domain proteins) and cytoskeletal structures and are essential for many functions such as (i) cell surface area control: PM invaginations allow expansion of the cell surface area to accommodate cell volume changes due to osmotic or pH variations, (ii) nutrient exchange: for example, in yeast, eisosomes regulate nutrient transporter localization and activity, conserving energy by sequestering transporters for less preferred nitrogen sources when ammonia is present, (iii) signal transduction: PM invaginations form specialized domains enhancing specificity and speed of cellular responses, (iv) endocytosis: PM invaginations facilitate internalization of molecules through clathrin-mediated and other endocytosis mechanisms, (v) organellar dynamics: PM invaginations establish functional interactions with mitochondria in yeast and facilitate muscle contraction in muscle cells by coordinating with the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), (vi) cell division: PM invaginations mark the site of cytokinesis, ensuring proper organelle and component distribution to daughter cells, and (vii) cell migration: PM invaginations maintain a pool of membrane that can enable extension of the cell surface in a directed manner. Endocytosis, the process by which cells internalize molecules, is facilitated by PM invaginations. Clathrin-mediated endocytosis, the best-studied example, involves the formation of clathrin-coated pits that invaginate and pinch off to internalize extracellular substances [132]. This mechanism is crucial for the regulated intake of nutrients and the recycling of membrane receptors [124]. Besides clathrin-mediated endocytosis, many types of PM invagination structures with different morphologies and stabilities have been found as intermediates of cargo uptake [133].

PM invaginations also play a role in organellar dynamics. For instance, the PM can establish functional interactions with mitochondria via PM invaginations in yeast [134, 135]. These contacts have been found to be essential for mitochondrial dynamics, including fission events in yeast [136]. Caveolae may form similar interactions with mitochondria in mammalian cells [135, 137]. Recent work further demonstrates that PM invaginations can serve as signaling platforms linking the PM, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and mitochondria [138]. Specifically, a non-clathrin endocytic (NCE) pathway of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) operates at tripartite PM-ER-mitochondria contact sites, where ER-derived Ca2⁺ oscillations stimulate mitochondrial ATP production to fuel local actin remodeling and vesicle fission. This multiorganelle platform directly couples PM curvature remodeling to mitochondrial metabolism, integrating growth factor signaling with cellular energy regulation and collective cell motility [138]. Another example of interplay between PM invaginations and organelles is the proximity of T-tubules to the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), a network of membrane-bound channels that stores and releases calcium ions, which facilitates muscle contraction by enabling efficient calcium release and binding to contractile proteins [18, 19].

The involvement of PM invaginations in cell orientation and division is exemplified by the cleavage furrow during cytokinesis, where invaginations mark the site of cell division [139]. This process is critical in ensuring that each daughter cell inherits the appropriate set of organelles and cellular components [140].

PM invaginations are not merely structural features but are integral to the cell’s interaction with its environment and its internal organization (Fig. 1). Their roles in ion homeostasis, nutrient exchange, signal transduction, endocytosis, and organellar dynamics underscore their importance in cellular physiology. Furthermore, their association with the cytoskeleton and scaffold proteins highlights the sophisticated coordination required to maintain cellular functionality. As such, PM invaginations are important for both simple and complex organisms, manifesting their necessity across the spectrum of biological complexity.

Characteristics and proposed functions of known PM invaginations in different species

Myocytes

Within the muscular system of mammals, one of the most significant PM invaginations is the T-tubule (Fig. 2A), which plays a pivotal role in orchestrating excitation–contraction coupling [18, 19]. Structurally, T-tubules are invaginations of the sarcolemma (specialized cell membrane), extending deep into the skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers. These tubular structures form an intricate network, ensuring synchronized depolarization and efficient signal propagation across the entire muscle [176, 177].

The primary function of T-tubules is to rapidly transmit action potentials into the interior of muscle fibers, ensuring the contraction signal reaches all regions simultaneously [180]. Their network ensures that every myofibril and sarcomere receive the signal, allowing for coordinated, synchronous muscle contraction. By extending the PM inward, T-tubules bring these impulses close to the SR and provide a larger platform for signals, enabling efficient calcium release [181]. The presence of T-tubules enhances the efficiency of excitation–contraction coupling by allowing for a rapid and synchronized release of calcium ions from the SR [182]. These calcium ions play a critical role in initiating muscle contraction by binding to specific proteins within the muscle fibers [183].

Although T-tubules are present in both cardiac and skeletal muscle cells, there are notable differences between them, as their structural and functional characteristics diverge in response to the distinct physiological demands of the heart and skeletal muscles [184]. In skeletal muscle, T-tubules are located at the junctions of the A and I bands and form a triad with two terminal cisternae of the SR. This highly organized structure facilitates the rapid propagation of action potentials and the efficient release of calcium from the SR, playing a key role in excitation–contraction coupling [181]. In contrast, cardiac muscle T-tubules are located at the Z-lines of the sarcomere and form a dyad with a single terminal cisterna of the SR. This less uniform distribution with more pronounced T-tubule branching may reflect the need to precisely synchronize and control calcium release via calcium influx through PM calcium channels [184]. The much larger diameter of cardiac T-tubules may be required in the heart to allow a larger calcium influx at each excitation as well as to allow a higher overall activity of transporters needed to support cardiac ion homeostasis.

The formation of T-tubules and their regulation involve an interplay of various proteins, including junctophilins, which tether the T-tubules to the SR, and dysferlin, implicated in T-tubule repair and maintenance [141]. Disruptions in these regulatory mechanisms can lead to impaired excitation–contraction coupling, contributing to muscle pathologies [185]. Additionally, the dynamic nature of T-tubules is influenced by membrane trafficking proteins, such as caveolins and amphiphysins, which regulate T-tubule biogenesis and stability [141].

In addition to their presence in cardiac and skeletal muscles, PM invaginations are also found in smooth muscle cells, although they differ significantly in structure and function. Smooth muscle cells lack the well-organized T-tubule system seen in striated muscle types. Instead, they contain small flask-shaped PM invaginations (termed caveolae) that are analogous to T-tubules and play an important role in signal transduction and mechanotransduction [151, 186]. Caveolae in smooth muscle cells serve as sites for the concentration of signaling molecules, facilitating rapid cellular responses to mechanical stress and changes in extracellular signals. These invaginations are particularly prominent in vascular smooth muscle, where they are involved in regulating blood pressure and vessel tone [151, 186]. Additionally, these caveolae play a crucial role in calcium signaling in smooth muscle cells, which is essential for processes such as contraction and relaxation [187]. However, unlike the organized T-tubule network of skeletal and cardiac muscles, the PM invaginations in smooth muscle are more dispersed and functionally integrated into a broad range of cellular processes, highlighting the adaptability of PM invaginations across different muscle types [187]. Further details on caveolae structures found in other cell types will be provided in the “ Caveolae” section below.

Neurons and related cells

Various forms of PM invaginations are found in the mammalian nervous system. Neurons are equipped with highly expanded PMs enclosing their projections and mediating action potential propagation, therefore requiring dynamic regulation of PM structures. As reviewed by Petralia and colleagues [142–144], several examples have emerged of invaginations present at synapses, which are thought to be important for synaptic plasticity [143]. Synapses can have a wide array of invaginating structures—with invaginations forming on either the pre- or post-synaptic neuron—while the adjacent neuron extends a protrusion into the invagination to form an interdigitated membrane structure.

Perforated synapses (Fig. 2B) obtain their name from electron micrograph observations of the post-synaptic density, an electron-rich structure underlying the post-synaptic PM. Perforated synapses occur when the post-synaptic dendritic spine invaginates into the pre-synaptic membrane and causes the postsynaptic density to appear fenestrated. This process is thought to allow morphological remodeling during development and during learning and memory [145]. While these discontinuous post-synaptic densities have been observed in the brains of rats and mice [143, 144, 146, 179], perforated synapses have also been characterized in the visual and motor cortices and superior colliculus of rabbit brain through observation of the localization pattern of synaptic vesicles [147, 148]. This pattern is termed the “pre-synaptic grid” and appears annulate or horseshoe-shaped at perforated synapses [147–149].

In support of the role of perforated synapses in synaptic plasticity, learning and memory, studies in rabbits have shown that animals that have undergone visual training show increased perforated synapses in the observed brain regions compared to untrained animals [147–149]. Similarly, rats reared in complex social environments had more perforated synapses in the occipital cortex than rats reared in isolation [146]. Furthermore, in vitro studies of organotypic slice cultures from the rat CA1 hippocampal region showed that induction of long-term potentiation via high frequency stimulation of neurons caused a threefold increase in the presence of perforated synapses in active synapses. These results suggest that PM invaginations are associated with persistent increases in synaptic transmission that occur during long-term potentiation [188].

A triple transgenic mouse Alzheimer’s disease (AD) mouse model has been used to examine synapse morphology [145] and these mice exhibit beta-amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. Interestingly, along with these hallmarks of AD, the animals show decreased hippocampal perforated synapses, reinforcing the connection between perforated synapses, synaptic plasticity, and memory [145]. Lending further support for the function of these invaginated synaptic structures, a prior study in mice identified the Kibra protein (encoded by Wwc1) as being important for the formation of perforated synapses [150]. Wwc1 KO mice showed decreased perforated synapses as well as deficiencies in learning during contextual fear and spatial learning tasks [150]. Deletion of the WWC1 gene in the human HeLa cell line also causes a decrease in PM invaginations, suggesting that loss of Kibra in the KO mice causes loss of invaginations at synapses [150].

Outside the central nervous system, mice also show a similar structure in rod photoreceptors, termed triad synapses [152]. A triad is formed by horizontal cells and rod bipolar cells invaginating into rod photoreceptors. The invaginations are lateral to the synaptic ribbon, which is an electron-dense structure surrounded by synaptic vesicles tethered to the PM. The synaptic ribbon serves as the site for glutamate release; therefore, mutations in genes that regulate its formation, such as ELFN1 and mGluR6, can disrupt these structures and lead to impaired glutamate signaling [152].

Another example of neuronal PM invaginations is the axonal PM invaginations observed in the neuron-like immortalized PC12 cell line from rats grown in vitro [153]. These structures require the Toca-1 protein (encoded by Fnbp1l), which was found to be expressed in rat hippocampal pyramidal and cortical neurons during development and to regulate different aspects of neuronal morphology through its binding partner N-WASP. Toca-1 has an F-BAR/EFC domain, which binds to phosphatidylserine and phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) and induces local membrane curvature, resulting in the formation of tubular invaginations in several cell lines [47, 153]. A FNBP1L mutation shown to cause loss of PM invaginations in HeLa cells caused a similar impairment of axonal elongation in the PC12 cells, indicating that Toca-1 regulates axonal elongation through regulation of PM invagination, although the axonal invaginations themselves were not visualized [153].

From a disease-related perspective, PM invaginations have been implicated in the early stages of prion-associated neuropathology, where pathogenic PrP(Sc) accumulates at membrane folds and cell contact sites. However, the precise function and relevance of these invaginations in prion conversion, propagation, or neurodegeneration remain poorly understood [189].

Platelets and related cells

PM dynamics are important for many blood cell types, allowing them to carry out rapid responses to different stimuli. For example, platelets must rapidly respond to vascular damage by secreting various factors and expanding in size to form a platelet plug [190]. Platelets in most mammals show networks of surface-connected and interconnected membrane channels that span the entire platelet, termed the open canalicular system (OCS) [30, 154] (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, horse, cow, and camel platelets do not possess this feature [155–157].

The OCS plays multifaceted functional roles that parallel those of many other PM invagination structures. It is involved in transport of substances into and out of the platelet, including the release of alpha granules, and serves as a membrane reservoir to facilitate the dramatic shape changes and surface area expansion required during platelet activation [154]. The OCS is also involved in the regulation of cell surface receptor levels and may be involved in calcium signaling [191], a feature shared with other membrane invagination systems such as caveolae and T-tubules. These roles are crucial for the rapid and reversible morphological transitions characteristic of platelet function. Beyond its physiological importance, alterations in OCS architecture and function have been implicated in human diseases, including bleeding disorders and platelet storage pool deficiencies. Structural defects or dysregulation of the OCS can impair platelet secretion and responsiveness, contributing to pathological conditions [192].

Platelets are derived from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow [193]. Megakaryocytes also have invaginated PM structures that form the demarcation membrane system (DMS) [154]. The DMS is continuous with the PM [158] and is required for the formation of pro-platelets. This PM network provides a large membrane reservoir that allows formation of long PM pro-platelet extensions that then fragment into platelets upon experiencing shear stress generated in the circulation [159]. One megakaryocyte can give rise to 4000 platelets through formation of PM invaginations. However, given the massive amount of membrane needed for the biogenesis and expansion of the DMS, rapid de novo membrane synthesis is also likely essential [158]. It is yet to be determined whether the DMS is formed intracellularly from the Golgi and then directed to the cell surface, or if newly synthesized membrane is first delivered to the PM and then quickly invaginates [158]. Additionally, the precise role of ER in DMS formation requires attention, given that ER is the largest intracellular membrane in most eukaryotic cells. Finally, it is notable that platelets themselves maintain extensive DMS structures [159] whose physiological function has not been well clarified.

Enterocytes

In the context of the mammalian digestive system, PM invaginations play a pivotal role in the efficient absorption and processing of nutrients [160]. A notable specialized structure known as the deep apical tubule (DAT) (Fig. 2D) is found within enterocytes, which are absorptive cells located primarily in the small intestine’s brush border region. DATs exhibit a unique morphology, penetrating up to 1 µm into the cytoplasm of endothelial cells. This depth allows the tubules to extend across the terminal web underneath the brush border and establish proximity to the apical recycling endosome, often referred to as the subapical compartment [160].

DATs exhibit distinct localization patterns for specific markers such as Caveolin-1, commonly used to identify lipid rafts and caveolae, as well as the glycolipid GM1 [160]. Manipulating membrane cholesterol levels through methyl-h-cyclodextrin—an agent that inhibits apical membrane trafficking—leads to the disappearance of these tubules from the apical surface, underscoring their composition as cholesterol-dependent lipid raft microdomains [194]. Annexin A2, a protein associated with lipid rafts and implicated in membrane trafficking processes [195], is also present in DATs. Intriguingly, DATs share a resemblance in composition with the subapical compartment and harbor the resident brush border enzyme aminopeptidase N [160]. This composition suggests that DATs act as a central hub for the biosynthetic membrane trafficking responsible for maintaining the brush border. Furthermore, their proximity to the endosomal compartment supports the notion that elements of the subapical compartment may directly fuse with the cell surface [160].

Endocytosis-associated PM invaginations

Along with the examples above, there are some well characterized examples of PM invaginations in mammalian cells that are nearly universally observed. These structures, such as caveolae and clathrin-coated pits, play a central role in endocytic uptake by concentrating cargo and initiating vesicle formation at defined sites on the PM.

Caveolae

Caveolae are small, 70–100 nm diameter flask-like PM invaginations [196]. Caveolae are enriched in proteins called caveolins [20–22, 196], which are integral membrane proteins that initiate caveolae formation by driving membrane curvature and are essential for stabilizing these structures through oligomerization and interaction with cholesterol-rich lipid domains [196].

Caveolae play various roles in mammalian cells, such as endocytosis, signaling, and lipid metabolism [20–22], and were recently shown to regulate mechanoadaptation [197]. Although there is disagreement in the field regarding the basal levels of caveolae mobility [161, 162], various stimuli, such as mechanical stress [198], oxidative stress [199], and ligand binding to caveolar-associated receptors [200], have been shown to drive a substantial increase in the internalization of caveolae. While caveolae are better characterized in mammalian cells, similar structures with some functional similarities have been observed in other organisms. For example, in some invertebrates like C. elegans, caveolae-like structures have been reported [201]. However, the molecular components and functions of these structures in non-mammalian organisms differ from those in mammalian cells [201]. Given the fundamental roles of caveolae proposed in mammalian cells, the generation of KO cells lacking the major caveolae-organizing protein, caveolin, was awaited with great anticipation. It could hardly have been more surprising, therefore, that animals lacking the three caveolin isoforms are both viable and fertile [202]. Although multiple striking phenotypes have indeed been described in the various available caveolin KO lines, the possibility is raised that membrane domains that do not involve caveolae per se can mediate most or all of the functions of caveolae.

Clathrin-coated pits

Another example of PM invagination that mediates endocytosis is clathrin-coated pits formed during clathrin-mediated endocytosis, a major endocytic pathway in mammalian cells [132]. During this stage [203, 204], clathrin triskelions assemble into a lattice-like structure around a region of the PM, which provides stability to the pit structure [203]. Once formed, clathrin-coated pits serve as sites for the internalization of specific molecules, such as ligand-bound receptors [203]. The clathrin coat is crucial for efficient cargo uptake by helping to maintain the integrity of the pit during the invagination process [203].

Dynamin, a fission GTPase, plays a pivotal role in the scission of deeply invaginated clathrin-coated pits, a prerequisite for vesicle internalization [205]. In addition to its established role in membrane fission, dynamin is also required during the late stages of pit invagination, and this function critically depends on its ability to bind and hydrolyze GTP to promote the efficient sequestration of cargo into nascent vesicles [205]. After cargo internalization, the coated pit transitions into a clathrin-coated vesicle. The coat then disassembles, and clathrin molecules are recycled for further use [203].

Clathrin-coated pits are regulated by various proteins and cellular signals. Adaptor proteins, like AP2, help select cargo molecules for internalization [204], while other accessory factors including epsin, intersectin, and actin cytoskeletal regulators contribute to membrane bending, coat assembly, pit maturation and the scission of clathrin-coated vesicles from the PM [132]. Additionally, these clathrin-coated pits are subject to modulation by lipid composition, phosphorylation events, and mechanical cues, ensuring spatial and temporal control of endocytic activity [203]. The diversity of cargo, ranging from nutrient transporters to signaling receptors, and the ability of cells to dynamically adjust CME in response to physiological conditions underscore the functional importance and versatility of this PM invagination mechanism [206]. Recent studies have uncovered cell-type-specific variations and non-canonical adaptors that further diversify the regulatory landscape of clathrin-coated pits and CME [207].

The stabilities of clathrin-coated pits and caveolae can vary depending on the specific context and cellular conditions. However, they tend to be relatively long-lived (up to 2 min) compared to some other types of PM invaginations involved in endocytosis, the turnover of which could happen in seconds [133]. Still, it is worth noting that the stability of PM invaginations during endocytosis can vary significantly based on the specific mechanism, cargo, and cellular context [203, 208, 209]. Notably, clathrin-coated pits are not limited to mammalian cells; they are found in a wide range of eukaryotic cells across various organisms, including those of non-mammalian organisms such as yeast, plants, and invertebrates [163–165].

Clathrin- and caveolae-independent endocytic sites

It is increasingly evident that endocytic mechanisms independent of clathrin-coated pits or caveolae play a significant role in most mammalian cells [210, 211]. In fact, clathrin/dynamin-independent endocytosis may be the predominant form of endocytosis in many cell types [212]. When activated, these pathways can be far more potent than conventional clathrin-mediated endocytosis [10], during which these endocytic mechanisms evidently rely on the formation of membrane domains that spontaneously undergo budding that minimizes line tension that occurs at domain interfaces [213].

In neurons and secretory cells, the primary form of clathrin-independent endocytosis at synapses is often referred to as bulk endocytosis [214]. This process forms vesicles that are notably larger than those produced through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, presumably because less energy is available to excise membrane than in endocytosis driven by adapters [215]. Bulk endocytosis is proposed to occur from omega-shaped pre-formed invaginations, however, the detailed mechanisms underlying the development of these micro-invaginations remain poorly understood [216].

In immune cells, professional phagocytes, such as macrophages and neutrophils, exhibit one of the most dramatic examples of PM remodeling through phagocytosis. Unlike clathrin- or caveolae-mediated uptake, phagocytosis is initiated by receptor engagement at the cell surface and driven by actin polymerization beneath the PM, forming expansive cup-shaped invaginations that engulf large extracellular particles, apoptotic cells, or pathogens [217–220]. The internalized vesicle, or phagosome, subsequently undergoes a maturation process, often referred to as postphagocytosis, that involves sequential fusion with endosomes and lysosomes, leading to cargo degradation and recycling of membrane components to the surface [219, 220]. These postphagocytic events depend on additional cycles of membrane invagination and tubulation, reflecting the cell’s capacity to dynamically restore its surface area following large-scale internalization. From a biophysical perspective, the formation and resolution of the phagocytic cup exemplify how actin-driven deformations of the PM operate within the same curvature and bending constraints described by Helfrich-type membrane mechanics. In this sense, phagocytic cells extend the principle of curvature-driven invagination to the micron scale, coupling active cytoskeletal forces with membrane elasticity to enable both immune surveillance and homeostatic membrane turnover [220].

A related but distinct form of large-scale endocytic remodeling has been observed in dendritic cells (DCs) during HIV-1 uptake, where viral particles exploit the CD169/Siglec-1 receptor to trigger massive PM invagination [221]. This process results in the formation of a sac-like compartment that can reach several microns in diameter and serves as a transient viral reservoir. The phenomenon resembles massive endocytosis (MEND), a clathrin-independent but cholesterol-dependent pathway characterized by rapid, extensive internalization of the PM without canonical coat proteins [221]. Live-cell imaging studies have revealed that sac-like compartment formation involves local cholesterol enrichment, transient actin depolymerization, and membrane depolarization. Pharmacological inhibition of protein palmitoylation or PI3K significantly attenuates compartment formation, supporting the view that lipid reorganization and signaling cascades cooperate to drive this large-scale endocytic event [221]. MEND thus represents an alternative curvature-driven mechanism of PM invagination, distinct from both classical endocytosis and phagocytosis, highlighting the remarkable versatility of immune cell membranes in reorganizing their surface architecture for pathogen capture and internalization.

In other non-excitable cells, particularly migratory cells, clathrin-independent endocytosis—often termed “CLIC” (clathrin-independent carrier) endocytosis—plays a major role [210, 212, 222]. Like bulk endocytosis mentioned earlier, this form of endocytosis is strongly dependent on the actin cytoskeleton and is tightly regulated by small GTPases. Notably, Rac1-dependent signaling, through effectors including PLC, cortactin, and ROCK1, can influence both the initiation and stability of PM-derived tubular invaginations, highlighting a critical mechanochemical control of CLIC pathway remodeling [223]. Electron tomographic analysis of the three-dimensional morphology of the earliest carriers reveals that these structures are multidomain tubular invaginations derived from the PM, forming a complex sorting station as they mature [222]. Upon excision from the PM, they give rise to tubular endosomes [212]. A critical question that remains to be addressed is the extent to which tubular structures like invaginations are fully formed before the final excision from the PM, and to what degree these PM-derived tubular structures might be stable, functional extensions of the PM.

Hidden PM invaginations: closed but contiguous with the outer PM

Recently, it has been proposed that a significant portion of the PM in certain cell types, including fibroblasts, T-cells, and various cell culture lines, exists as tubular invaginations that are isolated from the extracellular space by dynamin-mediated constrictions [11]. This hypothesis emerged from observations that BHK cells could nearly double their membrane area in response to a substantial increase in intracellular calcium despite containing few secretory vesicles near the PM [224, 225]. These membrane expansion responses, studied through patch-clamp capacitance recording, often displayed stepwise patterns corresponding to substantial membrane increases per expansion event. Such events, equivalent to tens of thousands of secretory vesicles, would require the opening of extensive membrane networks to the exterior. The opening of these tubules was found to be dependent on two key processes: first, the scrambling of anionic phospholipids by the TMEM16F scramblase [65], and second, the dissociation of dynamin-2 from the constriction sites of these PM invaginations [11]. Tubule opening events could be visualized using soluble fluorescent dyes, and the deletion of dynamin-2 led to PM expansion, suggesting the involvement of these invaginations in the overall membrane dynamics. The physiological roles of these PM invaginations remain largely unexplored, but they could potentially contribute to the uptake of extracellular signaling molecules into deep cellular compartments, facilitate inter-organelle communication, and mediate the extrusion of particles from internal cellular regions. Moreover, these invaginations appear to open in response to cell swelling, which activates mechanosensitive cation channels, triggering calcium influx sufficient to activate TMEM16F. These findings support the idea that these deep PM invaginations may assist in expanding the outer PM during cell spreading and migration, as well as in response to PM perturbations.

Regulation of PM invaginations in mammalian cells

Different cellular machinery is involved in producing PM invaginations, which vary in size and often serve as precursors to various cell signaling pathways. As mentioned above, there are many examples of PM invaginations that stably or dynamically exist in cells, with unique structures and formation mechanisms, to facilitate specialized functions. The intricate interplay between the cytoskeleton, membrane shaping/sculpting proteins and small GTPases ensures precise control over PM invagination, from initial membrane bending to final vesicle internalization. Additionally, many bacterial and viral pathogens can induce PM invaginations during their infection, favoring their entry or intracellular growth and spread.

Membrane-sculpting proteins

In most cases of pre-existing PM invaginations, membrane sculpting proteins are responsible for initiating tubulation and stabilizing invagination structures. BAR (Bin/Amphiphysin/Rvs) domain proteins stand out as one major family of proteins that play a vital role in these processes [226] as well as serving as platforms that recruit and organize the signaling complexes necessary for the diverse functions of PM invaginations [227, 228]. The generation of curvature and its propagation are coupled processes: local membrane distortions induced by a BAR domain dimer promote the attachment of additional dimers [226]. Membrane curvature begins with the electrostatic attachment of the BAR domain to the membrane, which sometimes involves the embedding of an N-terminal amphipathic helix (AH) into the membrane. This attachment relies on the interactions between the positively charged residues of the protein and the negatively charged lipids in the membrane, with a preference for certain BAR domains to bind phosphoinositides [229]. The introduction of AHs into the bilayer induces curvature by creating lipid composition asymmetry between the two leaflets [230]. Additionally, it has been found that in some BAR domain-containing proteins, AHs are crucial in segmenting smaller liposomes [231]. Nevertheless, the role of AHs in curvature creation remains inconclusive following experiments on BAR domains lacking AHs [232, 233]. Curvature propagation is contingent upon the collective action of numerous BAR domains, which create a structural support on the membrane surface called a scaffold. Scaffold formation is a property of all BAR domain proteins, which fundamentally influences their membrane-modulating outcomes [229]. The scaffold’s architecture is influenced by protein concentration and membrane tension. Coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations have indicated that at minimal concentrations, N-BAR domains (BAR domains with an N-terminal amphipathic helix) cluster on flat membranes and liposomes, forming filamentous structures and networks [234]. Once they reach about 20% surface density, these domains start to drive membrane protrusion [234]. The N-BAR domain protein endophilin, associated with endocytosis, can instigate tubule formation on giant liposomes at roughly 5% density and diminished surface tension [226]. Conversely, high protein densities are necessary for tubule development at increased surface tensions [226]. These observations indicate that lowering surface tension could facilitate the rapid endocytosis activation mechanism [235]. It is worth noting that not all BAR domains are active in shaping membrane tubules or invaginations [236–238].

Interestingly, besides BAR domain proteins, there are other significant players of membrane tubulation and reorganization. For example, the ATPase, Eps Homology Domain 1 (EHD1), is a membrane-binding protein that has also been shown to localize to T-tubules in skeletal muscle cells and plays a role in their remodeling [239, 240]. Proteins from the same family also display the abilities of membrane tubulation and reorganization of PM invaginations (by EHD2) and intracellular membrane compartments including tubular endosomes (by EHD3 and EHD4) [241, 242].

Several recent studies have revealed that proteins not classically associated with membrane remodeling also display curvature sensitivity or membrane-sculpting capabilities that influence the formation and regulation of PM invaginations. For example, the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 preferentially localizes to nanoscale membrane invaginations while being excluded from highly curved protrusions, revealing curvature-dependent spatial organization and conformational changes upon activation [243]. Similarly, the glycoprotein MUC1, a major component of the glycocalyx, avoids positively curved membranes and accumulates at negatively curved protrusions, a property that modulates its endocytosis and surface retention [244]. G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) have also been shown to redistribute according to shallow PM curvature, indicating that mechanical membrane geometry can directly modulate receptor signaling [245]. Beyond single proteins, extracellular matrix (ECM) fibers can impose topographic curvatures that promote the assembly of integrin αvβ5–mediated curved adhesions, linking PM curvature to cell anchorage and migration [246]. Furthermore, PM curvature itself serves as a spatial cue for organizing ER-PM contact sites via curvature-sensitive junctophilins [247] and defines hotspots for clathrin-mediated endocytosis [248]. Together, these findings highlight curvature not only as a passive outcome of cellular forces but as an active signal transductor that guides membrane-sculpting proteins’ activities in PM invaginations formation and regulation.

Lipids

In addition to protein-based mechanisms, the formation and regulation of PM invaginations are tightly controlled by specific lipid species and lipid signaling pathways, particularly those involving phosphoinositides (PIPs) [249]. PIPs serve as key regulators of membrane identity and curvature by modulating the recruitment and activity of membrane-sculpting proteins [250]. Among them, phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PI(4,5)P₂) is highly enriched at the PM and plays a central role in governing PM invagination dynamics [251]. For example, PI(4,5)P₂ directly binds to various BAR domain proteins, enhancing their membrane curvature-inducing activity and stabilizing invaginated structures [249]. Additionally, the composition of PIPs at the membrane can fine-tune the membrane curvature sensing and remodeling capacity of BAR domain proteins, thereby influencing invagination initiation and stability [252]. Besides PIPs, cholesterol depletion leads to the loss of caveolae and the flattening of clathrin-coated pits, disrupting normal endocytic architecture [253]. Meanwhile, an excess of anionic phospholipids, such as phosphatidylserine, is predicted to promote membrane curvature, thereby facilitating the formation of PM invaginations during endocytosis [254].

The dynamic turnover of PIP species through the coordinated actions of lipid kinases and phosphatases further regulates PM invagination processes [255, 256]. For example, the localized production of PI(3,4)P₂ at invadopodia, regulated by the kinase PI3K and the phosphatase SHIP2, drives PM invagination by recruiting BAR domain protein SNX9 and dynamin, which promote the formation of integrin-containing membrane buds and facilitate cell migration [256]. While during caveolae-mediated endocytosis, phosphoinositide kinases locally enrich PI(4,5)P₂ at sites of caveolae formation, contributing to their curvature and stability, while phosphatases modulate its levels to allow membrane scission and vesicle uncoating [257]. Collectively, these studies underscore the essential role of phosphoinositide metabolism and signaling in orchestrating the dynamic architecture of PM invaginations. The studies also underscore open questions about how and if lateral phospholipid diffusion can be restricted in cells.

Cytoskeleton

The cytoskeleton, particularly the actin network, plays a pivotal role in the formation and stabilization/destabilization of PM invaginations by regulating membrane curvature, tubulation, and scission, as reviewed by Kessels and Qualmann [77]. This dynamic process is orchestrated through the coordinated action of actin and BAR domain proteins, which drive membrane remodeling during invagination. BAR domain proteins, such as amphiphysin and endophilin, promote membrane curvature by binding to lipid bilayers and inducing tubular structures [77]. Notably, this tubulation is enhanced when the actin cytoskeleton is disrupted, suggesting that actin usually provides a stabilizing counterforce against membrane deformation [47].

As mentioned earlier, dynamin works in tandem with BAR and F-BAR proteins to promote membrane scission in cases of endocytosis. Previous studies have provided deeper insights into how actin and dynamin cooperate to drive endocytic membrane remodeling [47, 258]. Actin polymerization has been shown to transiently occur at sites of membrane invagination, peaking as clathrin-coated pits retreat from the PM into the cytosol [47, 258]. This actin recruitment coincides with a burst of dynamin activity, suggesting that dynamin triggers actin polymerization through intermediaries like syndapin and N-WASP [258].

These actin-mediated regulations on PM invaginations are also tightly controlled by the local phosphoinositide environment at the PM [259]. PIPs, particularly PI(4,5)P₂ and PI(3,4,5)P₃, act as spatial cues to recruit and activate a variety of actin regulatory proteins, including N-WASP, Arp2/3 complex, and ERM proteins, which in turn control filament nucleation, branching, and tethering beneath invaginating membranes [260]. The local enrichment or turnover of specific PIP species at sites of PM invagination dictates where and when actin filaments are assembled or disassembled [261]. In endocytosis, actin plays a significant role in regulating the dynamics of PM invaginations like clathrin-coated pits [262] and caveolae [263]. For example, at clathrin-coated pits, PI(4,5)P₂ accumulation promotes actin nucleation to support pit maturation, while subsequent dephosphorylation of PIPs facilitates pit scission and vesicle release [249]. Similarly, in caveolae, cortical actin interacts with local PI(4,5)P₂ to modulate the stability and dynamics of caveolar invaginations [257]. This phosphoinositide-actin interplay ensures that actin polymerization is precisely tuned to regulate the size, shape, and dynamics of PM invaginations, balancing membrane deformation with cytoskeletal support.

Ras superfamily small GTPases

Members of the Ras superfamily of GTPases serve as molecular switches for many cellular functions [264] and many contribute to the regulation of PM invaginations. For example, the accumulation of WASP at curved membrane along the cell body relies on the localized activity of the Ras superfamily small GTPase CDC42, which is typically enriched at the leading edge of migrating cells [265]. Both membrane curvature and front-enriched CDC42 activity are required to initiate WASP-dependent actin network and PM invagination formation during cell-substrate engagement in cell migration [265]. In migrating zebrafish primordial germ cells, CDC42-dependent PM invaginations enabled bleb-type protrusions for cell motility [266]. In mammalian cells, GRAF1 regulates the activity of CDC42 through its GTPase activating protein (GAP) domain and is targeted to PM invaginations through its BAR and PH domains [267, 268]. GRAF1 normally suppresses cellular blebbing, and cancer cells lacking GRAF1 are more invasive [269].

The GTPase RAB10 controls many aspects of membrane transport in mammalian cells [270] including the formation of tubular membrane structures [271–274]. Prior work suggested that these tubules may be connected to the PM [37]. Indeed, recent findings revealed that RAB10 generates tubular PM invaginations that serve as membrane reservoirs in its GTP-bound state through interaction with its multiple effectors and BAR domain proteins PACSIN2 and PACSIN3 [37]. Under basal conditions, these invaginations contribute to PM homeostasis and are preloaded with exocyst subunits, such as EXOC2 [37]. Like CDC42, RAB10 also contributes to cellular migration in zebrafish primordial germ cells [275].

Importantly, activation of another core member of the RAS superfamily, RhoA, has also been implicated to initiate endocytosis, specifically endocytosis that may be essential for amoeboidal cell migration [276]. This role was demonstrated by activating optogenetically RhoA locally at the edges of RAW 264.7 cells. Upon activation of RhoA in a patch of PM, endocytosis is initiated locally in that vicinity, and cells begin to migrate away from the area of RhoA activation that then becomes the trailing cell edge [276]. These results are consistent with the idea that PM flow occurs from front to back during amoeboidal migration, with exocytosis at the leading edge coupled functionally to endocytosis at the trailing edge.

Pathogen manipulation

During infection, PM invaginations can be induced by pathogens. There are various bacterial and viral proteins capable of inducing large tubular invaginations of liposomes in vitro and in the PM of cells [73]. For example, Shigella dysenteriae and some serogroups of Escherichia coli produce a dangerous bacterial toxin, known as Shiga toxin [277]. The Gb3 (glycolipid)-binding B-subunit of bacterial Shiga toxin (STxB) induces PM tubular invaginations in human cells [278, 279]. These authors suggested that this is a cargo-induced mechanism of membrane tubulation and uptake, independent of clathrin [279]. More recently, RAB12 was found to localize on Shiga toxin‐induced PM invaginations and to regulate retrograde toxin transport by promoting the delivery of STxB to the TGN [280]. Very recently, HIV invasion of cells has been shown to involve endocytosis that depends on PM domain formation, possibly depends on palmitoylations, and that occurs without dynamin or clathrin involvement [281].

Similar membrane remodeling phenomena are observed in other viral infections, where pathogens exploit lipid-protein interactions to trigger curvature and internalization of the host membrane. The non-enveloped DNA virus, simian virus 40 (SV40), uses pentameric VP1 capsid proteins to bind the carbohydrate moiety of GM1 gangliosides in the host cell PM to drive invagination and uptake [282]. Interestingly, SV40 (as well as isolated pentameric VP1) association with GM1 was sufficient to induce the formation of deep tubular invaginations of the PM [282]. The thin tubules identified in cells could reach several microns in length and were positive for the PM stain FM-1–43, demonstrating that they are connected to the PM. Other polyomaviruses and some bacterial toxins (Shiga and cholera toxins) also bind glycan moieties of glycosphingolipids using a very similar pentameric protein scaffold [283], suggesting that they induce membrane invaginations and uptake via a similar mechanism. Indeed, scientific modeling of Shiga toxin binding on membrane glycolipids has suggested that the molecular architecture of the STxB binding sites promotes invagination. Like SV40 capsids, STxB needs its glycosphingolipid receptor to have long acyl-chains to allow the generation of tubular membrane invaginations that drive toxin uptake into the cell [284]. However, STxB-induced membrane tubulation also requires the Gb3 acyl-chains to be unsaturated [284]. Ultimately, these toxins can interact with membranes in a manner that is suitable for membrane bending and invagination even in the absence of cellular machinery [285].

Additionally, the pore-forming protein perforin (PFN), secreted together with granule-associated proteases (granzymes) from cytotoxic T-cells and Natural killer cells during immune defence, is also capable of inducing PM invaginations, in the absence of other cellular machinery and external energy (ATP) [286]. It is worth noting that the formation of PM invaginations is not an active, regulated cellular defense mechanism in this in vitro setting; rather, it is a physical effect driven by membrane bending forces generated by perforin oligomerization and pore insertion. In this way, PFN-induced PM invagination formation, in addition to pore formation, resembles an endocytic-like event that facilitates the delivery of apoptosis-inducing granzymes into pathogen-infected or tumor cells [286].

Fungal pathogens such as microsporidia can also induce PM invaginations during host cell invasion [287]. Upon spore germination, the polar tube (PT), coated with polar tube proteins like PTP1 and PTP4, interacts with host surface receptors such as mannose-binding proteins and transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1). These interactions anchor the PT to the host membrane, forming an invasion synapse, a transient PM invagination that creates a protected microenvironment for sporoplasm entry. Subsequent signaling events, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis and actin rearrangement, facilitate internalization and formation of a parasitophorous vacuole, highlighting a complex interplay between fungal invasion machinery and host membrane dynamics [287].

Beyond the induction of new PM invaginations by pathogens, recent studies have highlighted how bacterial pathogens exploit pre-existing structures to facilitate invasion. For example, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium has evolved sophisticated mechanisms to manipulate host PM dynamics through its type 3 secretion system, which translocates virulence proteins (termed effectors) into host cells [288]. These effectors drive the formation of actin-rich membrane ruffles that promote bacterial uptake into Salmonella-containing vacuoles (SCVs) [289]. During Salmonella invasion, the bacterial effectors SipC, SopE2, and SopB recruit exocyst components from membrane reservoirs and other cellular compartments, enabling exocyst complex assembly and membrane mobilization essential for bacterial uptake [37]. Additionally, LRRK2 kinase plays a crucial role in this process by phosphorylating and activating RAB10 prior to infection, promoting the formation and stabilization of these PM invaginations and allowing their subsequent targeted delivery to invasion sites during infection [290]. Upon bacterial entry, dephosphorylation of RAB10, mediated by a TLR4/PIEZO1/TMEM16F-dependent pathway [290], is required for its inactivation by a bacterial GAP, SopD [291]. RAB10 inactivation by SopD facilitates PM scission and bacterial internalization. These insights underscore the intricate strategies employed by Salmonella to exploit host PM invaginations during invasion. While Salmonella has provided a paradigm for how pathogens exploit tubular PM invaginations, whether other bacterial or viral pathogens employ similar strategies remains an intriguing avenue for future research.

PM invaginations in invertebrates

The prevalence and diversity of PM invaginations in non-mammalian vertebrates remain less well characterized compared to mammalian systems, in part due to limited investigation in these species. However, invertebrates, with their vast diversity, offer a rich tapestry of examples such as the PM invaginations in iridocytes in cephalopods [166, 167] and PM invaginations within pericardial nephrocytes of Drosophila melanogaster (D. melanogaster) [168]. These examples not only underscore the versatility of PM invaginations but also set the stage for a comprehensive exploration of their roles and mechanisms in invertebrate biology, expanding our understanding beyond the well-studied mammalian context.

Cephalopods

Iridocytes are specialized cells known for their role in producing structural colouration that are found in cephalopods [167], but also in fish [292] and reptiles [293]. In cephalopods like squid and octopus, iridocytes stand out as captivating examples of cells with PM invaginations (Fig. 3A), which not only contribute to their astonishing ability for rapid and dynamic color change [166, 167] but also increase surface area for signaling and exchange of water [32].

In iridocytes of cephalopods, PM invaginations termed lamellae or iridosomal platelets contribute to colouration changes by creating a high refractive index change between the cytosol and the extracellular fluid contained within the invaginations [32, 166, 167]. In addition, these PM invaginations significantly increase the membrane surface area, which is thought to serve two important functions in iridophore cells [166, 167]: First, they enhance the accessibility of the entire photonic apparatus—comprising reflective platelets arranged in a periodic lattice—to extracellular signaling molecules such as acetylcholine (ACh). Second, they provide an extended membrane interface that facilitates rapid osmotic exchange of water between the extracellular space and the high refractive index platelets. Both mechanisms are believed to contribute to dynamic tuning of the photonic structure by enabling fast modulation of the lattice spacing and dielectric contrast between the platelets and the surrounding cytosol, ultimately leading to visible changes in the intensity and color of structural reflectance [166, 167]. Central to this process is the active control of the spacing between the reflective platelets within the iridocyte membranes, achieved through cellular mechanisms influenced by both intrinsic and environmental factors. This precise regulation allows cephalopods to instantaneously adapt their coloration to match diverse underwater environments or convey intricate behavioral signals [166, 167].

Drosophila melanogaster

PM invaginations within pericardial nephrocytes—specialized cells situated around the heart—are distinctive features observed in D. melanogaster. These invaginations, known as “nephrocyte slits” or “nephrocyte diaphragms,” form a complex network within the cell, significantly increasing the surface area [168] (Fig. 3B). Pericardial nephrocytes act as scavenger cells, filtering waste and maintaining hemolymph homeostasis, the insect’s analog to blood [168]. This selective filtration process contributes to maintaining hemolymph composition and eliminating harmful substances from the circulatory system. Additionally, the invaginations provide a platform for molecular processes, facilitating receptor-mediated endocytosis. Specialized proteins and receptors within the invaginations recognize and internalize specific substances [296].

Similarly, in the D. melanogaster tracheal system, PM invaginations play a central role in shaping the subcellular tubes of terminal cells during tubulogenesis [297]. These apical invaginations are dynamically regulated through endocytosis and late endosome-mediated trafficking, which balance membrane allocation between apical and basal domains. When endocytosis is inhibited, basal membrane expansion ceases while apical membranes overgrow, indicating that membrane is first delivered apically and then redistributed via continuous internalization [297]. A late endosomal-multivesicular body (MVB) compartment, dependent on endocytic activity, functions as a transit station for sorting and recycling these membranes, ensuring coordinated PM growth and polarity during development. This process exemplifies transcytosis, the directed transport of membrane and cargo from one PM domain to another through endocytic and exocytic trafficking pathways [297].

Besides these specialized invaginations in adult fruit flies, dynamic PM microdomains such as microvilli and membrane folds also function as critical surface reservoirs during D. melanogaster development [298]. In the syncytial embryo, prior to cellularization, the apical surface is extensively decorated with microvilli and PM invaginations that significantly increase the membrane surface area [298]. Live-cell imaging combined with fluorescent pulse-labeling has shown that these structures are rapidly flattened and repurposed during cleavage furrow ingression [299]. As the embryo transitions into a multicellular epithelium, labeled microvillar membranes are observed translocating into these invaginated furrows, providing direct evidence that PM invaginations unfold to meet the high membrane demand, without relying on endocytic recycling [299].

These PM invaginations are supported by dynamic F-actin and respond sensitively to changes in membrane tension. Increased tension restricts actin polymerization and promotes depolymerization of the filament core, triggering structural disassembly and unfolding of the invaginations [300]. This tension-mediated mechanism allows cells to rapidly mobilize membrane in a reversible and spatially controlled manner. In D. melanogaster, where precise and coordinated expansion of the cell surface is vital for morphogenesis, microvilli and PM invaginations serve as essential structural elements, enabling rapid morphological changes by acting as deployable membrane reservoirs [299].

Protozoa

Euglenozoa

The flagellar pocket is a distinctive PM invagination structure found in certain unicellular eukaryotes, notably in euglenozoa-like trypanosomes and euglenids [169]. This specialized structure represents a remarkable adaptation for these protists, where the PM invaginates to form a pocket-like structure that accommodates the insertion of the flagellum, a crucial organelle used for locomotion and other vital functions. In mammalian cells, an analogous membrane domain known as the ciliary pocket is present at the base of the primary cilium. This plasma membrane invagination shares striking morphological similarity with the flagellar pocket and is thought to play roles in endocytosis, vesicular trafficking, and ciliogenesis [301].

Morphologically, the flagellar pocket in Euglenozoa exhibits a unique configuration characterized by the PM folding inward to create a pocket-like structure, into which the flagellum is inserted. This configuration ensures a close association between the flagellum and the PM, allowing for efficient coordination of flagellar movements and other cellular activities [169, 294] (Fig. 3C).

The functions of the flagellar pocket are multifaceted and pivotal for the organism’s survival. Firstly, it serves as a site for the assembly, maintenance, and coordination of the flagellum’s beating motion, which is essential for motility and directional movement. Additionally, the flagellar pocket plays a crucial role in nutrient uptake and endocytosis. Nutrients and other materials from the extracellular environment are internalized through this invagination, facilitating nutrient acquisition and cellular metabolism [302].

The regulation of the flagellar pocket involves intricate cellular mechanisms. Its formation and maintenance are tightly controlled to ensure the proper function of the flagellum and efficient nutrient uptake. The coordination of flagellar movement and the endocytic processes within the pocket are also subject to precise cellular regulation to support the organism’s survival and adaptation to changing environmental conditions [302].

Ciliates

Another example of PM invaginations in Protozoa is the cytostome-cytopharynx complex found in ciliates, another major group of protists. This complex has a unique morphology and plays essential roles in feeding and phagocytosis [170, 303]. The cytostome-cytopharynx complex consists of two main components. The cytostome is the oral groove, which is a depression or mouth-like structure on the cell surface. It is here that the PM invaginates to form the cytopharynx, a narrow, tubular structure that extends from the cytostome into the cell’s interior (Fig. 3D) [295]. This tubular PM invagination acts as a channel through which food particles are ingested. The complex is typically highly structured and can vary in length depending on the ciliate species [303].

The primary function of the cytostome-cytopharynx complex is to facilitate feeding and phagocytosis. Ciliates are heterotrophic organisms that feed on bacteria, smaller protists, or organic particles in their aquatic environments. The cytostome-cytopharynx complex allows the ciliate to capture and engulf food particles. As food particles are swept into the cytostome by the coordinated beating of the cilia, they enter the cytopharynx and are surrounded by a portion of the PM, forming a temporary food vacuole. Digestive enzymes are then secreted into the food vacuole to break down the ingested particles, and nutrients are absorbed [170].

The regulation of the cytostome-cytopharynx complex is influenced by environmental factors and the ciliate’s nutritional needs. When food is scarce, ciliates may reabsorb the cytostome-cytopharynx complex to conserve energy. Conversely, in the presence of abundant food, the complex can be expanded and adapted for efficient feeding [170]. The cytostome-cytopharynx complex represents a remarkable adaptation in ciliates, where PM invagination plays a central role in feeding and digestion. Its specialized morphology and functions underscore its importance in the biology of these protists, enabling them to thrive in diverse aquatic environments by efficiently acquiring nutrients through phagocytosis [170].

Apicomplexa

Members of the phylum Apicomplexa are obligate intracellular parasites that depend on host-derived nutrients to sustain growth and replication [304]. Parasite proliferation occurs within host cells, where nutrient acquisition is essential yet mechanistically incompletely understood. Ultrastructural observations have consistently revealed a unique PM invagination with a dense neck, termed the micropore, on the surface of intracellular apicomplexans, including Toxoplasma gondii [305]. Despite being a conserved feature across life-cycle stages, the physiological function of this structure remained obscure for decades.

Recent findings have validated the micropore as an essential endocytic organelle responsible for nutrient internalization from the host cytosol and Golgi-derived vesicles in T. gondii. The protein Kelch13 localizes to the neck region of the micropore, where it functions as a molecular hub for recruiting endocytic machinery. Intriguingly, efficient micropore activity requires the parasite’s de novo ceramide synthesis pathway, linking lipid metabolism to endocytic competence [305].

PM invaginations in plants

Symbiotic relationships with Rhizobia bacteria in legume plants allow for nitrogen fixation to occur in the roots. This symbiosis is facilitated by the formation of PM invaginations called infection threads [171, 306]. To begin infection, the cell wall is locally degraded by Rhizobia for its contact with the PM. Vesicle delivery from within the cell then allows deep invaginations to form. Bacteria are then endocytosed into a peribacteroid membrane to form a symbiosome in which they carry out nitrogen fixation [172]. The biochemical signaling underlying this process is triggered by the bacterial Nod factor, which is recognized by the LysM domain receptor kinases expressed on the surface of root epidermal cells. Downstream signaling leads to increased trafficking of exocytic vesicles to the PM, allowing the formation of deep tubular invaginations [173].

Another example of plant PM invaginations elicited by microorganisms are those induced by AK toxin, a host-specific toxin secreted from Alternaria fungi, the causative agents of black spot disease in Japanese pears (Pyrus pyrifolia, also known as Pyrus serotina) [307, 308]. Treatment of leaves with AK toxin results in rapid leakage of electrolytes within minutes [309], and subsequently drives the formation of PM invaginations [307]. Deepening of AK toxin-induced PM invaginations appears to be promoted by delivery of exocytic vesicles from the Golgi, as evidenced by the increase in Golgi-derived polysaccharides in the invaginations over time [307]. The invaginations induced by AK toxin appear to be associated with deformations of plasmodesmata [307], PM-enclosed intercellular bridges that connect the cytoplasm of neighboring plant cells across the cell wall [310]. Along with polysaccharides and membrane debris, the invaginations contain fragments of desmotubules [307], which are tubular ERs that span plasmodesmata [310]. The accumulation of these contents in the PM invaginations may drive further deepening into the cell. Whether these PM invaginations are a cause or a consequence of AK toxin-induced cytotoxicity remains unclear.

The question of whether plant cells carry out endocytosis has only been elucidated relatively recently. Initial evidence for PM-derived vesicles emerged in the early 1970 s [311, 312], following findings that plant cells could take up extracellular proteins [313]. These studies of the PM ultrastructure in stamen hair and filament cells of Tradescantia virginian [311] and meristematic tissues from shoot apices of Vinca rosea [312] using transmission electron microscopy demonstrate different stages of PM invagination as well as vesicles that seem to have pinched off from the PM. These invaginations can have diverse morphologies, ranging from wide openings to more constricted and oval-shaped invaginations. Interestingly, the elongating invaginations were found to project into the cell’s central vacuole, with the vacuole membrane appearing tightly constricted around the invaginated PM. Several structures inside the vacuole were no longer continuous with the PM but were rather vesicular with a double membrane, presumably composed of an inner PM-derived membrane and an outer vacuolar membrane [312].

The mechanistic characterization of clathrin-mediated endocytosis in plants came nearly 40 years later when clathrin-coated pits were observed by transmission electron microscopy in root epidermis cells of Arabidopsis thaliana [314]. These invaginations are smaller than their mammalian counterparts, with diameters of approximately 30 nm compared to 100 nm in mammalian cells. Different stages of this endocytic process were visualized, including uncoating of the pinched off vesicles. Overexpression of a C-terminal fragment of the clathrin heavy chain (CHC) acts as a dominant-negative mutant by sequestering the endogenous full-length CHC in the cytoplasm. This dominant-negative CHC mutant was shown to significantly reduce uptake of the FM4-64 dye, which marks endolysosomes, as well as reduced uptake of the PIN auxin efflux carrier, an integral PM protein that relies on the endomembrane system for its recycling to the PM [314].

Additionally, a clathrin-independent endocytosis pathway that relies on a PM protein called flotillin (Flot1) was identified in Arabidopsis thaliana in 2012 [315]. Flot1 is associated with detergent-resistant membrane microdomains and was previously shown to drive PM invaginations in animal cells [316]. Flot1-positive PM invaginations give rise to much larger endocytic vesicles than clathrin-coated pits (approximately 100 nm). These structures can be visualized in root cells overexpressing GFP-tagged Flot1, where they localize as puncta at the PM and partially overlap with endocytic markers [315]. Flot1 overexpression leads to an increase in endocytic vesicles, while its depletion results in a significant reduction in vesicle numbers, indicating a role in promoting endocytosis [315]. These results also highlight the role of membrane microdomains in PM invaginations.

PM invaginations in yeast

A well-characterized example of PM invaginations is found in fission yeast, termed elongated eisosomes [317]. These are filamentous structures of approximately 50 nm in depth, which form on the non-growing lateral cortex of the cell [317]. Eisosomes comprise a series of immobile PM patches named the Membrane Compartment occupied by Can1 (MCC) due to the punctate localization of the Can1 arginine permease [318]. MCC domains are associated with peripheral membrane proteins, and together, form eisosome structures [318]. Eisosomes have been found to play roles in cell signaling, osmotic stress responses, sphingolipid homeostasis, PI(4,5)P2 regulation, and patterning of tubular cortical ER and ER-PM contact plasticity [24, 319–321].

Several molecules have been found to be involved in eisosomes formation, including BAR domain proteins like Pil1, Lsp1, Slm1, and Slm2. Another class of eisosomes, termed punctate eisosomes, are found in other fungal species such as budding yeast, Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida albicans, and an unidentified bread mold [23]. Earlier studies reported similar membrane-associated punctate structures, likely corresponding to eisosomes, which were proposed to function in glycogen storage in budding yeast [45]. It is worth noting that, structurally, BAR domain proteins are important for the formation of both elongated and punctate eisosomes [174].

PM invaginations in bacteria

Although bacteria are classically seen as lacking membrane-bound organelles, there are examples of bacterial cells displaying organization and regulation of PM structures and dynamics to carry out various specialized functions. For example, L-form bacteria—cell wall-deficient variants of classical bacteria—can generate an abundance of membrane-derived structures, including buds, tubules, and vesicles [322]. This membrane remodeling appears to be primarily driven by the synthesis of lipids with highly complex biophysical properties that can profoundly affect the structure of lipid bilayers [322].

Planctomycetes

An endocytosis-like pathway has been found to occur in the planctomycete Gemmata obscurioglobus to mediate receptor-mediated uptake of proteins into vesicle-like structures [323]. PM invaginations and vesicles containing GFP can be observed by electron micrograph, demonstrating the first evidence of uptake of extracellular proteins into intracellular vesicles in bacteria [323]. This process is restricted to the uptake of proteins but does not show protein specificity [323]. Interestingly, planctomycetes express proteins structurally related to eukaryotic clathrin and coat proteins [324]. One such protein, the Gemmata obscurioglobus protein gp4978, was found to localize to the PM in its initial stages of invagination, providing insights into the mechanism by which this process occurs [323].

Escherichia coli

Bacterial membranes tend to maintain a constant lipid-to-protein ratio [325]. Therefore, increased membrane protein synthesis, for example, by overexpression of ATP synthase leads to membrane production and the formation of invaginated structures as found in Escherichia coli [326]. Whether the formation and organization of these structures is active or due to passive folding due to decreased tension remains unclear (Fig. 4).

Phototrophic bacteria

Chloroplasts and mitochondria are membrane-bound organelles that provide energy to eukaryotic cells [327]. Some bacteria possess similar energy-yielding membrane structures. These invaginating structures provide increased surface area for more efficient electron and proton transfers. Purple phototrophic bacteria such as Rhodobacter sphaeroides build complex networks of intracytoplasmic membranes (ICM) to allow increased surface area for solar energy absorption for photosynthesis [33]. These structures rely on the RC-LH1 complex, which forms a dimer with an angled conformation, causing local cytoplasmic membrane curvature and formation of spherical structures of approximately 70 nm in diameter. The ICMs can be continuous with the membrane, while some bud off to form vesicular structures. Secondary buds can also form on existing budding membranes [33].

Magnetotactic bacteria

In magnetotactic bacteria, PM invaginations serve as magnetosomes, which allow the bacteria to orient themselves in geomagnetic fields to reach their favored microaerophilic environments [175]. Magnetosomes are chains of 15–20 magnetite crystals of approximately 50 nm in diameter, each surrounded by invaginating PM. These chains run along the length of the cell and are supported by parallel networks of long cytoskeletal filaments made up of MamK, a prokaryotic actin-like protein [31].

Concluding remarks and perspectives

In this review, we have discussed the broad spectrum of PM invaginations that exist in diverse cell types; multi-faceted domains of the PM that for a long time were unrecognized and thus not appreciated. They represent dynamic interfaces between the cell’s interior and the external environment, showcasing the remarkable adaptability of cellular membranes. Notably, the concept of “excess membrane” is crucial to understanding how cells utilize these structures. This excess membrane, which exists in the form of invaginations, protrusions, and other specialized microdomains, challenges earlier views of the PM as a smooth and passive boundary and highlights its complex and active role in maintaining many fundamental cellular functions.

Across various species, PM invaginations manifest in a variety of forms. Prokaryotic species, including bacteria, exhibit infoldings that play roles in essential cellular processes such as energy generation, geomagnetic fields navigation, and nutrient transport. Eukaryotic cells, on the other hand, feature a broad array of invaginations, each tailored to meet the specific needs of the organism. These invaginations range from membrane microdomains to more complex and organized assemblies. Structurally, they may be shallow or deep, organized or random, open or closed, reflecting the cell’s adaptability to its niche and metabolic demands. These structural properties underscore the membrane’s ability to undergo dynamic changes and support a multitude of cellular functions. They facilitate processes such as endocytosis, exocytosis, and the regulation of membrane lipid composition. These invaginations also contribute to the organization and clustering of membrane proteins, which in turn mediate cell signaling and communication with the extracellular environment.

Looking forward, several key questions and research directions remain to be explored. First, while the structural diversity of PM invaginations is well documented, the common molecular principles that govern their formation across species remain incompletely understood. Are there universal protein-lipid modules that shape these structures, or have different lineages evolved unique solutions? Second, there is an emerging need to dissect the functional crosstalk between PM invaginations and intracellular organelles, particularly in processes like mechanotransduction, calcium signaling, and metabolic sensing that involve extensive organellar contacts and crosstalk. Additionally, the relevance of PM invagination dysfunction in human diseases—ranging from myopathies to thrombocytopenia and possibly neurodegeneration—warrants deeper investigation. Future studies should also aim to address the dynamic regulation of these PM invaginations in response to physiological and pathological cues. Advanced imaging, high-resolution structural biology, and lipidomics will be essential in deciphering how these domains are remodeled in real time. Furthermore, comparative studies across model organisms may reveal evolutionarily conserved principles that unify the formation and function of PM invaginations.

In conclusion, PM invaginations exemplify the intricate interplay between structure and function in cellular biology. Their diverse forms and versatile functions highlight the adaptability of cells to different ecological niches and physiological demands. This review has provided the first comprehensive overview of PM invaginations, offering insights into their structural diversity and their vital roles in supporting the myriad processes that govern cellular life, and their emerging disease links which make them a rich and exciting area for future research.
