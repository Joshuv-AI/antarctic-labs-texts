The consumption of onion extract tablet modulates expression of human plasma microRNAs: a randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial
Authors: Mao Yamamoto, Yoshinori Fujimura, Ryotaro Chiba, Modena Shimizu, Motofumi Kumazoe, Akane Haseda, Jun Nishihira, Mari Maeda-Yamamoto, Hirofumi Tachibana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
MicroRNAs (miRNAs), which are non-coding RNAs of approximately 20 nucleotides in length, have attracted attention for their involvement in various biological processes and the regulation of diseases. However, the effects of foods and supplements on miRNA expression in vivo remain unclear. Onion extract tablets (OET) alleviate male menopausal symptoms and stress; however, their effects on miRNA expression in vivo are unknown. Herein, we investigated the effects of OET on miRNA expression in vivo. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study was conducted on 19 healthy Japanese participants (men and women) aged 30–65 years. Participants consumed either a placebo tablet (PT) or OET which included 30 mg of sulfur-containing amino acids daily for 2 weeks. MiRNAs prepared from plasma samples before and after intake were comprehensively analyzed using next-generation sequencing. A comparison of the variation before and after intake showed that the expression levels of three miRNAs, miR-106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p, were significantly increased in the OET group than in the PT group. Receiver operating characteristic analysis of these miRNAs also showed that miR-106b-5p had the highest discriminatory power. To our knowledge, this study is the first to show that consumption of onion extract can modulate miRNA expression in humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23332-0.

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), single-stranded non-coding RNAs consisting of approximately 20 bases, have attracted attention as molecular species responsible for negatively regulating the transcription and translation of mRNA by complementarily binding to target mRNAs1,2. It is known that miRNAs regulate approximately 30% of mammalian genes and are involved in homeostasis and disease development3. miRNAs have been reported to be associated with cancer, cardiac diseases, and lifestyle-related diseases, and may be potential targets for liquid biopsy for disease assessment, diagnosis, and prediction of disease states4. For example, miR-21, which is upregulated in various cancers, negatively regulates the expression of many tumor suppressors, such as phosphatase/tensin homolog (PTEN), and programmed cell death protein 4 (PDCD4)5, and the inhibition of miR-21 has been shown to suppress tumor growth6. A set of ten miRNAs, including miR-101-3p, miR-127-3p, and miR-210-3p, has been reported to show specific expression changes in patients with acute kidney injury, suggesting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers7. In addition to the roles of mammalian miRNAs, the bioregulatory effects of plant-derived miRNAs have recently been reported. Osa-miR-172d-5p, one of the rice-derived miRNAs, suppressed lung fibrosis by targeting the transforming growth factor-b activated kinase 1 (Tabl)8. Broccoli-derived miR-159 targets transcription factor 7 (TCF7) in the Wnt signaling pathway in human breast cancer cells, and its activity has been reported to suppress tumor growth9. Thus, the regulation of miRNA expression may be a promising therapeutic strategy for various diseases.

Dietary intake influences miRNA expression. For example, human studies have shown that plasma miRNA expression is altered by the consumption of a Mediterranean diet10. Several food components exert diverse physiological effects through the regulation of miRNA expression. Epigallocatehin-3-O-gallate (EGCG), the major bioactive catechin in green tea, exerts antimelanoma effects via let-7b, a type of miRNA, in mice11. Sesame lignans show antioxidant effects via miR-669c-3p when administered to mice12. In humans, four weeks of orange juice consumption is known to modulate inflammatory pathways by regulating the expression of multiple miRNAs13. However, the effect of food components on human miRNA expression remains unclear.

Onion extract tablets (OET) with a high sulfur-containing amino acid content have been reported to alleviate menopausal symptoms and suppress stress in men14. Sixty healthy men aged 50–80 years with subjective symptoms of male menopause were given a supplement (OET) containing 30 mg/day of sulfur-containing amino acids from onions for 4 weeks, and improvements were observed in the total Hinemann’s Aging Males Symptoms (AMS) score, a questionnaire used to diagnose male menopausal symptoms, and psychological factors14. In addition, the OET group showed significantly higher values than the placebo tablet (PT) group for mental health items of the SF-36, a health-related quality of life scale14. Moreover, the OET have been reported to improve sleep quality and alleviate stress15. Thirty healthy men and women aged 30–50 years who were dissatisified with their sleep were given OET for 5 days, and the delta power during non-rapid eye movement sleep and the delta power per minute in the first sleep cycle increased significantly compared with the intake of PT15. Additionally, Salivary α-amylase level, a stress indicator, decreased significantly with the intake of OET15. Quercetin, the major bioactive compound in onions, is known to regulate miRNA expression16, but the regulatory effects of sulfur-containing amino acids in OET on miRNA expression remain unknown. On the other hand, the regulatory effects of allicin, the representative sulfur compound in onion, chinese chives, and garlic, on miRNA expression have been reported17. However, it is still unclear whether OET, containing sulfur-containing amino acids and quercetin, is effective in regulating miRNA expression. Therefore, the objective of this study was to clarify the effects of OET on miRNA expression in human plasma.

Results

Effect of consumption of OET on compresensive miRNA levels

Sixty healthy Japanese participants aged 30–65 years were initially evaluated for eligibility. Twenty participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either placebo tablets (PT) or onion extract tablets (OET); 19 (PT: n = 9; OET: n = 10) completed the study. One participaint in the PT group was excluded from the analysis because of absence due to moving. No adverse or unexpected effects were observed in both groups. The clinical trial protocol and flow diagram of the study are shown in Fig. 1. All 19 test participants met the inclusion criteria and none were excluded. The characteristics of the 19 participants are shown in Table 1, and no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups. For each participants group, plasma samples before and after 2 weeks of PT or OET intake were subjected to next-generation sequencing (NGS) for the comprehensive analysis of miRNAs (Fig. 1). The miRNAs detected in more than half of the participants in the PT and OET groups were included in the analysis. In the PT group, 173 types of miRNAs were detected before intake, 162 types were detected after intake, and 154 types were common before and after intake (Fig. 2a). In the OET group, 162 types of miRNAs were detected before intake, 186 miRNAs were detected after intake, and 156 miRNAs were common before and after intake (Fig. 2b). Additionally, 143 miRNAs were common to both the PT and OET groups before and after intake. These 143 miRNAs were analyzed in subsequent studies (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 1).

For NGS data analysis, we used the Q values from the DESeq method, which is the most commonly used method for analyzing changes in miRNA expression. The results are shown using a volcano plot analysis. We compared the expression levels of each miRNA before and after PT and OET intake. The results showed that three miRNAs (miR-146a-5p, miR-501-3p, and miR-151a-5p) were upregulated and one miRNA (miR-148a-3p) was downregulated in the PT group (Fig. 2d). Seven miRNAs (miR-99b-5p, miR-126-3p, miR-27b-3p, miR-142-5p, miR-126-5p, miR-191-5p, and miR-151a-5p) were upregulated and three miRNAs (miR-320a, miR-92a-3p, and miR486-3p) were downregulated in the OET intake group (Fig. 2e). Of the 10 miRNAs in the OET group, 9 miRNAs, except for miR-151a-5p, showed expression changes only in the OET group.

Next, the expression levels of the nine miRNAs that showed changes only in the OET group were compared between the PT and OET groups at each time point after intake. No miRNAs showed significant differences between the groups at the time point after intake (Fig. 2f). Comparisons with Q values tend to have difficulty in capturing minor variations because of the false discovery rate (FDR) correction required to suppress false positives in multiple testing. Variations in miRNA expression due to food or tablets are expected to be minor compared to dynamic variations caused by diseases. Therefore, even if miRNAs that were significantly altered in the OET group but not altered in the PT group were selected, baseline changes could obscure the original food component-induced changes. Considering this, the following study focused on the variation in miRNA expression before and after ingestion of food components.

Analysis based on the variation values of miRNA expression before and after intake of OET

The variation in the expression of each miRNA before and after PT or OET intake was calculated, and the differences between the two groups were compared. Three miRNAs (miR106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p) were upregulated by OET, whereas none showed decreased expression (Fig. 3a). Figure 3b-d showed the results of the comparison of the variation in the expression of each miRNA between the PT and OET. No miRNAs were observed among the nine miRNAs, as shown in Fig. 2.

The ability of these three miRNAs to discriminate between the PT and OET intake groups was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis. The results showed that miR-106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p had Area Under Curve (AUC) values of 0.944, 0.778, and 0.789, respectively, indicating medium-to-high discrimination ability (Fig. 3e). miR-106b-5p showed the highest discrimination accuracy.

Enrichment analysis by genes predicted to be targets of each miRNA

To explore the biological significance of each miRNA, the target genes of each miRNA were predicted using two public miRNA databases, miRDB and TargetScan 8.0, and the pathways involved were predicted using the genes commonly predicted by both databases. miR-106b-5p predicted 1,384 target genes using miRDB and 1,385 target genes were predicted using TargetScan 8.0. A total of 482 genes were predicted using both databases (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table S2). Next, we searched for pathways involving the 482 target genes of miR-106b-5p using ShinyGO 0.82. Circadian rhythm, bladder cancer, and glioma were predicted to be pathways involving miR-106b-5p that were altered by OET consumption (Fig. 4a). In contrast, miR-339-3p predicted 46 target genes using miRDB and 729 using TargetScan 8.0 (Fig. 4b). In addition, 40 genes were predicted using both the databases (Supplementary Table S3). We searched for pathways involving 40 types of miR-339-3p target genes using ShinyGO 0.82, and found no pathways with a significantly predicted involvement. This may be because only a few genes were predicted to be commonly targeted by miRDB and TargetScan. In contrast, miR-181b-5p predicted 1408 target genes using miRDB and 1,371 target genes using TargetScan 8.0 (Fig. 4c). A total of 831 genes were predicted using both databases (Supplementary Table S4). Therefore, we used ShinyGO 0.82 to search for pathways involving the 831 target genes of miR-181b-5p. Pathways predicted to be involved in miR-181b-5p alteration following OET consumption included long-term potentiation, cellular senescence, and colorectal cancer (Fig. 4c).

A search for genes commonly targeted by the three miRNAs (miR-106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p) revealed that only one gene was common, targeting the pleckstrin homology domain-interacting protein (PHIP) gene (Fig. 4d). Next, ShinyGO 0.82 was used to search for pathways involving genes predicted to be targeted by miR-106b-5p and miR-181b-5p (110 (109 + 1) genes) and genes predicted to be targeted by at least two of the three types of miRNAs (116 (109 + 1 + 6) genes); however, no significant pathways were found. This may be because of the small number of genes predicted to be targeted. Genes (1,236 genes) predicted to be targeted by any of the three types of miRNAs identified in this study were analyzed using ShinyGO 0.82 to search for pathways involving the predicted target genes. Long-term potentiation, endocrine resistance, and microRNAs in cancer were predicted to be the pathways involving miRNAs altered by OET consumption (Fig. 4e). Although the miRNAs identified in this study may be involved in the bioregulatory effects of OET through these pathways, further clarification of these mechanisms in cellular and animal experiments is indispensable for elucidating detailed causal relationships.

Discussion

Although miRNAs are involved in various biological phenomena and health promotion, the effects of food and supplements on miRNA expression in vivo remain poorly understood. Onions have been reported to have various physiological effects, such as anti-inflammatory18, antioxidant19, and immunomodulatory20. The onion extract tablet (OET) used in this study is known to alleviate male menopausal symptoms and relieve stress; however, its effect on miRNA expression in vivo is unknown. In contrast, this study is the first to identify three types of miRNAs whose expression varied with the consumption of OET; among them, miR-106b-5p was the most capable of discriminating between the placebo tablet (PT) and OET intake groups. These findings indicate that OET can alter plasma miRNA levels in vivo. Further analysis is needed to determine how this ability is related to the bioregulatory function of these tablets.

In this study, the DESeq method, which is the most common method for analyzing changes in miRNA expression, was used. Nine miRNAs whose expression varied only in the OET group did not differ significantly between the PT and OET groups at any time point after intake. Even if miRNAs that changed significantly in the OET group but not in the PT group were selected, baseline changes may have made it difficult to capture the original changes caused by the tablet. Dietary studies have focused on the changes before and after intake. A study demonstrating that the Mediterranean diet is effective for weight loss via miRNAs21 showed changes in miRNA expression before and after the intake of food components. Therefore, we focused on the changes in miRNA expression before and after tablet intake to compare the differences between the two groups.

In this study focusing on the variation values of miRNA expression before and after ingestion of tablet, three types of miRNAs, miR106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p, were upregulated after ingestion of OET. Several studies have reported the relationship between the three miRNAs identified in this study and various diseases. miR-106b-5p has been reported to suppress hyperglycemia22 and inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis23, whereas miR-339-3p inhibits colorectal cancer growth and metastasis24. miR-181b-5p improves type 2 diabetes25 and cardiac function in patients with heart failure26. In the present study, PHIP was found to be a common target of miR106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p. PHIP encodes a protein that regulates the translocation of glucose transport proteins in skeletal muscle cells. However, there are no reports of its association with onion consumption. Although onion consumption has been reported to be involved in the control of these diseases27, it is unclear whether the miRNAs described above are involved in their actions. Onions (Allium cepa) have been reported to contain several plant miRNAs, such as ace-miR162 and ace-172c-3p28; however, these miRNAs were not detected in the present NGS analysis. To date, there have been no reports examining changes in the expression of human miRNAs due to onion consumption. Our study presents the first basic data on changes in miRNA expression in human plasma due to onion consumption. Further studies on the involvement of miRNAs in the bioactive effects of onions are required in the future.

Quercetin, a flavonoid found in onions, has been reported to reduce branch tracheobronchial lung dysplasia by suppressing the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway29. Among the three miRNAs identified, miR-106b-5p was predicted to target MAPK4, whereas miR-181b-5p was predicted to target MAPK1IP1L, MAPK1, and MAPK8. Enrichment analysis of all genes predicted to be targeted by each miRNA suggested that the three miRNAs identified in this study may be involved in the MAPK signaling pathway. Quercetin has also been reported to inhibit tumorigenesis in prostate cancer by suppressing the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt signaling pathway30. The results of the enrichment analysis suggest that miR-181b-5p may be involved in the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether these miRNAs are involved in the mechanisms of action of quercetin. Furthermore, it has been reported that onion extract intake may improve sleep quality15. Herein, we explored the relevant pathways of the target gene of miR-106b-5p and suggest that it is involved in the circadian rhythm, which is deeply involved in the improvement of sleep quality31. Although the results of this study indicate that onion components can alter plasma miRNA levels, it is not possible to conclude whether onion components exert their action to improve sleep quality via miRNAs. Further elucidation of this mechanism through cellular and animal experiments is essential to resolve this point.

Recently, we performed miRNA microarray analysis to compare plasma miRNA levels in mice orally administered three flavonoids: daidzein, delphinidin, and quercetin16. Daidzein treatment alters the expression of 74 miRNAs in mouse plasma. Similarly, quercetin and delphinidin treatments altered the expression of 53 and 198 miRNAs, respectively. Among them, miR-25-5p, miR-146b-5p, and miR-501-3p increased in the three flavonoid groups, whereas miR-148b-3p, miR-669e-5p, and miR-3962 decreased. Interestingly, many of fluctuating miRNAs differed for each flavonoid. These results suggest that miRNAs may be potential mediators of flavonoid-specific bioactive effects as well as biomarkers that reflect flavonoid intake. However, there are no reports on the effects of onion-derived sulfur-containing amino acids on miRNA expression in vivo. Elucidation of this effect is extremely important for clarifying the function of onion extracts, which contain sulfur-containing amino acids, quercetin, and various other components.

Dietary pattern plays a critical role in maintaining health and preventing the onset of disease. However, the current methods for assessing dietary patterns rely predominantly on subjective self-reported questionnaires, which lack objectivity and precision. Recently, biomarker-based approaches have emerged as promising tools for evaluating the intake of specific food items, such as coffee, tea, and sugar-sweetened beverages, based on their constituent compounds or metabolites32. Furthermore, urinary metabolites have been identified as reliable indicators of green tea intake33, and similar approaches have been used to assess onion consumption through urinary metabolite profiling34. In addition to metabolites, miRNAs have attracted attention as potential biomarkers of dietary intake that could replace traditional questionnaire-based subjective assessments. A recent study showed that 108 miRNAs, including miR-19 and miR-22, are altered by energy and fat intake35. miR-23a-3p has been reported to be positively correlated with sodium intake36. MiRNAs have also been proposed as candidate biomarkers for assessing nutritional interventions aimed at preventing type 2 diabetes37. Each of the three miRNAs identified in this study has been reported as biomarkers for various diseases. miR-106b-5p has been associated with vascular calcification in chronic kidney disease38, miR-339-3p with aortic dilatation39 and heart failure40, and miR-181b-5p with gastric cancer-associated malignant ascites41. These three miRNAs have been reported as biomarkers in separate diseases, but at this time, none of the three have been reported as biomarkers simultaneously in the same disease. The simultaneous expression changes of these three miRNAs may be a phenomenon specific to OET intake, suggesting that they could serve as specific biomarkers of OET ingestion. The individual AUC value for these three miRNAs were calculated by ROC analysis in Fig. 3, but the AUC value for a combination of the three miRNAs were not provided. Therefore, we calculated the AUC value for the combination and found a value of 0.989. These circumstances support the possibility that a combination of the three miRNAs could be a specific biomarker for OET ingestion.

Herein, we identified three miRNAs (miR-106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p) associated with OET intake. These findings highlight the potential utility of miRNAs as objective biomarkers to assess the intake of onion components rich in sulfur-containing amino acids. Although the small sample size of this study was accompanied by limitations regarding the interpretation of the results such as statistical power and generalizability, future work, including trials with larger sample sizes and functional validation, is needed to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of these miRNAs and to analyze the variation in these miRNAs in diverse populations and dietary intake situations. The participants who met the inclusion criteria in this study were considered to be healthy, although they experienced mild stress. Although a healthy population, it is difficult to generalize the results of this study to all healthy populations. Future studies involving healthy participants not experiencing mild stress would be needed to test the generalizability of these results.

Conclusions

Herein, we investigated the effects of a 2-week consumption of OET on plasma miRNA expression in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study. Although the small sample size limited the statistical power and generalizability of the results, miR-106b-5p, miR-339-3p, and miR-181b-5p levels were significantly higher in the OET group than in the PT group. ROC analysis of these miRNAs showed that miR-106b-5p was the most discriminative miRNA between the two groups. Furthermore, we obtained information on the biological pathways associated with the target genes of these miRNAs; however, functional validation was not conducted in this study. Therefore, we cannot conclude whether the physiological effects of OET are regulated through these putative pathways. Further research is necessary to determine whether the proposed pathways are involved. This is the first study to show that onion extracts modulate miRNA expression in humans. Our findings also suggest that these miRNAs are potential biomarkers for assessing the intake of onion components.

Materials and methods

Participants

Sixty healthy Japanese participants aged 30–65 years were initially assessed for eligibility. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) The participants felt stressed. (2) participants who showed relatively low score of Brief Job Stress Questionnaire (BJSQ). (3) participants who showed relatively low scores on the visual analog scale (VAS) questionnaire were assessed for stress. (4) Participants who showed a relatively high HRV score of heart rate variability (Ratio of Low-Frequency (LF) to High-Frequency (HF)). The participants who met the inclusion criteria in this study were considered to be healthy, although they experienced mild stress14. Twenty participants met the inclusion criteria and were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either placebo tablets (PT) or onion extract tablets (OET). Of these, 19 participants (PT, n = 9; OET, n = 10) completed the trial. The clinical trial protocol and flow diagram of the study are shown in Fig. 1.

This study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects (Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology and Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare). The Ethics Committee for Human Health of Hokkaido Information University and the Ethics Committee of Kyushu University reviewed and approved the feasibility and ethical and scientific validity of this clinical trial. Before we started this clinical trial, we registered the summary of our experiment in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) “Effect of Daily Intake of Active Test Food on stress-reducing: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled, Parallel Group Comparison Study”(registration number: UMIN000038874; registration date: 13/12/2019).

Study design and clinical test

This was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study (Duration of the trial: 1/2/2020-15/2/2020). The trial goal is to analyze miRNAs in blood samples collected from human intervention trials, and clarify the health maintenance and promotion effects of agricultural, forestry, and fishery products and foods based on their expression levels. The primary outcome measure of this study is to examine the underlying relationship between blood miRNA expression and OET intake; secondary outcome measures are not defined in detail because this is an exploratory study. Because this was an exploratory study, it was conducted in a manner that ensured the minimum number of 18 participants necessary to identify significant differences or trends in effects between groups or before and after intake. Using the stratified permuted block method, we randomly categorized the participaints according to gender, age, LF and HF balance into two groups as follows: PT group and OET group. Randomization will be conducted by the person in charge of food allocation and an allocation table will be prepared. The allocation list will be stored as an electronic medium with a password in a locked place by the person in charge of food allocation. The allocation was disclosed after the study was completed, and the cases, data, and analysis methods were fixed. The test tablets consisted of two types: test tablets (OET) and placebo tablets (PT), which were indistinguishable in appearance. The sponsor assigned an identification code to the test and placebo tablets, printed the corresponding identification code, manufacturing date, and expiration date on the packaging bag of the test tablets, and notified the test tablet allocation personnel at the test tablet allocation organization of the identification code and allocation information. These tablet codes were kept in strict confidence by the test tablet allocator. The onion extract tablet (OET) was composed of main components (dextrin, onion extract, citric acid), reduced maltose syrup, crystalline cellulose, calcium stearate, silicon dioxide, shellac, yeast cell walls, and glycerin. This tablet contained 10 mg of sulfur-containing amino acids per tablet, and the participants took it thrice a day, consuming 30 mg of sulfur-containing amino acids daily. In contrast, placebo tablets (PT) are composed of reduced maltose syrup, crystalline cellulose, calcium stearate, silicon dioxide, shellac, yeast cell wall, and glycerin. Participants took 0.3 g tablet three times a day for a total daily intake of 0.9 g.

Blood sample collection and preparation of plasma specimens

The blood samples have been collected in vacutainer tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate buffer (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA) at Hokkaido Information University. After the blood collection, the samples were transferred to Kyushu University. The Vacutainer tubes were centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 15 min. at 4℃; each plasma top layer was transferred to the new 1.5 mL tubes and stored at -80℃.

Total RNA isolation and miRNA sequencing

Total RNA, including miRNA, was extracted from plasma samples using the miRNeasy serum/plusma kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). RNA samples (5 µL) were reverse-transcribed using a Truseq small RNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) in a thermal cycler (Real-Time PCR System; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). miRNA-sized libraries were extracted using a 5% Mini-PROTEAN TBE Precast Gel (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The final concentrations of the libraries were determined using a Qubit4 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) using a QbitTM 1×dsDNA HS Assay kit (Invitrogen). Libraries were pooled and denatured according to the standard Illumina miniSeq library pooling guide. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed using an Illumina MiniSeq High Output Kit (Illumina). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the raw read counts of each sample obtained by NGS were analyzed using the DESeq method, the total read counts of each sample were corrected to one million. The obtained dataset was subjected to data analysis. Variations in miRNA expression were defined based on differences in read counts before and after intake. Specifically, the change in expression (ΔRead Counts) for each miRNA was calculated by subtracting the read counts obtained before intake from those obtained after intake, as follows: ΔRead Counts = Read Counts (post-intake) − Read Counts (pre-intake).

Statistical analysis

Comparison of the variation values in miRNA expression before and after intake between PT and OET was performed using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 was considered significant. The discriminatory power of the OET group from the PT group was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using the read counts of the three selected miRNAs as the input data. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 5.0; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and SPSS (version 13.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Target gene prediction and functional enrichment analysis

Putative target genes of the three selected miRNAs were predicted using TargetScan Human 8.0 (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/). To evaluate the biological functions of target genes, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using ShinyGO 0.82 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) with a false discovery rate (FDR) cut-off of 0.05.
