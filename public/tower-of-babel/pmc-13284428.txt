“Migratory grief” and structural barriers to care: a community-engaged qualitative study of immigration-related stress among immigrants living with or at risk of HIV
Authors: Marc Shi, Jordy Rojas Antigua, Lesly Diaz Chacha, Nat Orihuela, Chanelle M Diaz, Viraj V Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Structural barriers caused stress for immigrants living with and at risk of HIV.•Services specific to people living with HIV can help overcome structural barriers.•Accompaniment is a key community-based model in supporting immigrants with HIV.Keywords: Immigration, Social determinants of health, HIV service delivery, Community-based participatory research, Qualitative research

Background

In the United States, immigrants are disproportionately affected by HIV with increased prevalence of disease, higher rates of advanced illness, and increased risk of worse outcomes compared to U.S.-born counterparts (Ross et al., 2018). Immigration is argued to be a social determinant of health as it affects known determinants such as housing, employment, and income (Castañeda et al., 2015). Social, political, and financial challenges related to migration can broadly be termed immigration-related stressors. These obstacles can make it difficult for immigrants in the US to access medical care (Hacker et al., 2015) and have been identified as factors affecting various stages of the HIV care continuum, including diagnosis, engagement and retention (Sheehan et al., 2015, Ross et al., 2019, Ross et al., 2017). Data suggests that these stressors are associated with poorer HIV outcomes for immigrants living with HIV (ILWH) (Ross et al., 2018, Arora et al., 2021), but the specific mechanisms by which immigration-related stressors affect outcomes and access to care have not been well elucidated.

Similarly, immigration-related stressors negatively affect mental health of immigrants (Lee, 2019, Noh et al., 2012). The experience of exile due to conflict, persecution, or economic reasons can lead to a sense of loss (Ekerin et al., 2024). Many migrants endure perilous journeys often because of the circumstances under which individuals are forced to migrate or restrictive policies regulating migration. These traumatic experiences increase risk of developing PTSD and other anxiety-related disorders (Coughlin, 2012). Mental health is further impacted by rising anti-immigration rhetoric and restrictive laws, as noted by increased policing leading to decreased mobility for migrants (Pinedo and Valdez, 2020, von Werthern et al., 2018, Alegria et al., 2003). Poor mental health is a well-known negative predictor of HIV outcomes (Remien et al., 2019, O’Grady et al., 2024, Sumari-de Boer et al., 2012), and thus addressing these stressors in ILWH is an important element of HIV care. While the impact of immigration-related stressors on mental health is well documented, how these stressors affects outcomes such as access to and engagement with mental health care by ILWH is less well known.

Given that many immigrants have intersecting minority identities that are priorities groups in the strategy to End the HIV Epidemic (The White House), it is important to understand how immigration-related stressors play a role in HIV risk and access to care for these groups. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing targeted interventions that address the unique needs of ILWH. Community-engaged methods that center the perspectives of historically marginalized individuals help correct institutional power imbalances in who conducts and benefits from research, and to capture the nuances of how marginalized communities experience care (Ubri et al., 2024, Oetzel et al., 2018, Vaughn et al., 2017). The aim of this study was to use a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach to elucidate how immigration-related stressors impact engagement with mental health and HIV care, and potential facilitators to address such barriers.

Methods

Setting

We conducted this study in New York City (NYC), which has a population of over 3 million immigrants (Adams and Castro, 2023). NYC also has a relatively robust infrastructure of HIV services, including the HIV and AIDS Services Administration (HASA), which provides case management and social services for people living with HIV (PLWH) and funds scatter-site affordable housing specifically for PLWH (HIV/AIDS Services Administration (HASA) - Overview - Benefits Plus 2024). During the time in which interviews were conducted, NYC was experiencing an increase in newly arrived immigrants, primarily from Latin America. This led to both an increase in temporary emergency shelters established for new arrivals and an increase in anti-immigrant rhetoric (Sundaram, 2024).

Community advisory board

We employed a CBPR approach (Vaughn et al., 2017) given the multiple structural barriers faced by the population of interest, and our aim to address the historical exclusion of this population from research. Central to that approach was establishing a Community Advisory Board (CAB) of individuals with immigrant and LGBTQ+ experience who lived in the NYC area. We assembled this CAB from local community-based organizations (CBOs) serving immigrants, such as the Queer Detainee Empowerment Project (QDEP) (Queer Detainee Empowerment Project (QDEP) :Office of the New York City Comptroller Brad Lander 2025), as well as from individuals recommended by community groups. There were 8 active CAB members throughout the duration of the study, and CAB member characteristics are defined in Table 1. 2-hour meetings between the research team and the CAB team took place every other month, and a chat group was maintained with the CAB for ongoing communication between meetings. CAB members were compensated $50 per hour for each 2-hour meeting and live interpretation was available at each for members who were monolingual Spanish speakers.

The first two CAB meetings were used to introduce members, build rapport and conduct an initial needs assessment. During meeting three the CAB developed the initial conceptual framework to guide priorities for the study (Fig. 1). Subsequent CAB meetings were used to identify the CAB’s priority areas for interviews using a nominal rank voting process, which involves a process of facilitated group discussion followed by individual members individually ranking priority areas. Group consensus arrived on the following priority areas: 1) housing 2) legal services 3) mental health. Subsequent group meetings were used to review iterative versions of the interview guide, in which CAB members proposed and revised questions for inclusion in the guide via facilitated group discussion. The CAB met after the completion of interviews for a member-checking process, which involved facilitated group discussion on interview transcripts and their relation to the initial conceptual framework developed by the CAB. Engagement in the CAB was measured using validated participatory measures (Oetzel et al., 2018) to assess adherence to core values and participation.

Research team

The research team included a variety of perspectives that intersect with the current study. All of our team members have personal or family history of immigration. Two team members had previously conducted community-engaged research with immigrant populations. The PI (MS) on this project has extensive advocacy and community organizing experience addressing social needs such as housing, and another study team member (CD) has extensive advocacy experience on immigration rights and immigrant justice issues. Multiple team members were bilingual (English/Spanish) and had personal connections to various Latinx and LGBTQ+ communities in New York City, as well as personal immigration experience from Latin American countries. The interviews were conducted by three members of the research team; data analysis and interpretation were done by all members of the research team.

Participants and recruitment

We recruited participants with support from the CAB and CBOs using purposive sampling that included intra-categorical comparisons based on HIV status and history of immigration to maximize participation of individuals with multiple intersectional identities. Staff members from partner CBOs and CAB members informed individuals in their personal networks of the study. If they agreed to be contacted, their phone numbers were provided to the research team, and they were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥18, (2) residing in the NYC-metro area, (3) self-identified as immigrant, (4) living with HIV or vulnerable to acquiring HIV, (5) spoke English or Spanish, and (6) able to provide informed consent. Experience living with HIV was determined by endorsement of HIV infection or treatment and vulnerability to HIV was determined if the participant had at least one characteristic considered high priority for HIV prevention (including but not limited to: identifying as MSM, transgender, queer or gender non-conforming, engaging in injection drug use, experiencing housing instability). Due to the vulnerable nature of prospective participants, many of whom were undocumented immigrants, we collected only the minimum necessary information to enroll into the study, including a single identifier (including pseudonyms if necessary) and a point of contact. Participants provided verbal consent for participation in interviews. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the lead author’s institution (IRB# 2022-14078).

Interviews

The interview guide was developed by the research team in consultation with the CAB and based on the CAB’s priority areas. In brief, the interview asked participants to describe their immigration journey, how they accessed resources related to HIV care and mental health as well as key social resources such as housing and legal assistance, and asked participants to reflect on how their immigration experience impacted their ability to keep themselves healthy in relation to their HIV and mental health. The full interview guide is available on request. The interview guide was piloted with one study participant who was a CAB member and repeatedly revised for clarity and to include novel topics that emerged during interviews. The research team met regularly to debrief and solve problems encountered during interviews.

We conducted semi-structured one-on-one interviews in English or Spanish depending on participant preference, either in person or via videoconference. Interviews were conducted by members of the study team (JA, LDC, NO) who were bilingual in English and Spanish, obviating the need for live interpretation during interviews. All study team members received training on interview techniques with the study interview guide. Interviews were audio recorded and then professionally transcribed for analysis. Interviews conducted in Spanish were transcribed in Spanish and then translated to English via a professional transcribing service. To maintain anonymity, particularly for participants with tenuous immigration status, personal identifying information was not recorded or transcribed, and recordings were stored in a secure database. Participants were compensated $75 for the interview via physical or electronic gift card depending on participant preference. Participants who completed interviews were invited to maintain ongoing contact for future dissemination of findings and distribution of community resources. We also developed a protocol to address re-traumatization or mental health crises that may emerge during interviews. Finally, a list of resources to address social needs was developed and distributed to CAB members and participants.

Conceptual framework and data analysis

The CAB developed a conceptual framework (Fig. 1) which proposes specific mechanisms by which immigration-related stressors impact downstream HIV and mental health outcomes. Drawing on this framework priorities identified by the CAB, we focused on experiences accessing legal and housing-related resources, as well as healthcare and mental health services. In addition to identifying barriers, we employed strengths-based framing to illuminate the facilitators to overcome structural barriers.

An iterative, grounded theory approach (Chun Tie et al., 2019) to analysis was used to detect patterns that emerged in the interviews. Our analysis approach was as follows: an initial deductive codebook was developed based on our interview guide and applied to initial interview transcripts. All transcripts were independently coded by three team members (MS, NO, JA) using Dedoose Version 9.2.005. At bi-weekly coding team meetings, the coding team (MS, NO, JA) reviewed each coded transcript, and resolved any disagreements in coding by reviewing coder rationale and arriving at full consensus. During these meetings, reflexive memos and coder and interviewer reflections were used to generate emergent codes, leading to subsequent versions of the codebook that were then applied to future transcripts. Once coding was complete, individual team members reviewed clusters of codes in parallel to identify emergent themes using a thematic analysis approach, then biweekly team meetings were used to discuss preliminary themes and further refine the codebook until prominent patterns across and within transcripts were identified and agreed upon. The team reviewed codes and new themes iteratively throughout data collection, and saturation was reached once no new themes were identified with successive interviews. During early team meetings, the team participated in reflexivity and positionality exercises (Jacobson and Mustafa, 2019). Reflexivity was maintained through data collection, analysis and writing by recording memos after review of each interview, and through regular team meetings which were used to discuss interpretations individual team members brought to the data. These discussions and memos were used to determine applicability of new codes and ultimately in deciding the core themes reported on below. The member-checking process completed with the CAB also informed final thematic selection by focusing themes on key priority areas determined by the CAB, reinforcing the initial conceptual framework developed by the CAB.

Results

Of the 39 individuals eligible for the study, a total of 19 individuals completed full one-on-one interviews in participants’ language of choice. Participants were enrolled on a rolling basis until saturation was reached and no further interviews were considered additive to core themes (Fig. 2). Contact information was retained for additional eligible participants for possible future studies, and all prospective participants who completed the recruitment received resource materials at completion of the study. Interviews ranged from 23-75 minutes in length, with 18/19 interviews conducted in person and 1/19 conducted via videoconference. 17 interviews were conducted in Spanish with post-hoc translation. As interviewers were fluent in English and Spanish, interpreters were not needed for any interviews. Participants identified with a range of gender and sexual identities, with 12/19 (63%) of participants identifying as trans- or genderqueer person. Most participants were Spanish-speaking and from Latin America (17/19). Over half (10/19) of the participants were living with HIV, and 9/19 participants had experiences with immigration detention. Given the community-based partnerships that were involved in recruitment, the HIV risk category for all participants who were at risk of acquiring HIV was identifying as MSM, trans, queer or gender non-conforming. Limited access to social resources was a source of stress, and was partially ameliorated by resource pathways specific to people living with HIV

Limited paths to legal resources for all participants

Almost all participants highlighted legal support as a critical need, and the difficulty of accessing such resources. Many participants identified the lack of available resources, and lack of financial accessibility of resources that did exist: “I stayed up nights countless times searching on the net trying to find a pro-bono lawyer. [...] Some told me that they're not taking any more cases, because they're overwhelmed.” (genderqueer, living with HIV). Lack of legal resources was found to be a significant barrier to stability in the US and a source of stress for the majority of participants, with some explicitly linking it to negative mental health impacts: “It affected my health in the way that it made me feel more stressed because [...] we were getting close to the date to submit the asylum application, I felt even more stressed. I was thinking about what we would [do] if we missed the deadline. I kept thinking and thinking about it” (ciswoman, not living with HIV).

Limited legal sources of income

Many participants also highlighted the need for legal pathways to employment, specifically work authorization and employment opportunities. For participants who discussed work, it was often connected to notions of engagement in US society or being able to support oneself as a productive member of society: “Well, right now [...] I want to start working. I have my permit and then I can generate taxes and return to New York everything it has given me, honestly. Yes, because if they already helped you, now return the favor” (genderqueer, living with HIV). Others identified the need for formal authorization for work to avoid police interactions: “now I do have [a work permit], but the permit to sing in the street is different, so sometimes the police stop me, but I keep doing it because I need to eat.”(genderqueer, living with HIV). Legal pathways to income were seen as a need both to sustain a livelihood but also due to societal and legal pressures to integrate formally into US society.

Access to housing resources shaped by HIV status

A major priority for participants was having stable housing. Regardless of their HIV status, many participants specifically named access to housing as a key source of stability and as linked to health: “You're not worried anymore about losing your medication because they leave it in your mailbox or it gets directly to your house. And there's no problem of having other people seeing it and having to give explanations of what your medication is for, which is what happened at the shelter”(genderqueer, not living with HIV). However, in contrast to other social needs, experiences of housing access were mediated by participants’ HIV status. Participants living with HIV had access to resources for housing that expedited access, such as HIV/AIDS Services Administration (HASA): “they sent us to HASA […] That was when they told us that we could look for an apartment and that they'd take care of everything else.” (genderqueer, not living with HIV). Some participants commented on how HIV status was associated with more efficient access to resources: “Well, from what I've seen, I feel that they help people a little bit more when they have HIV. If they don't, they almost don't help them.” (transwoman, not living with HIV). Participants without an HIV diagnosis generally described a longer time until they were able to access stable housing or described going through the city’s shelter system: “we had enough money to pay about two months' rent, but they suggested that through shelters they could help us get in the applications faster and so on, we had to go through shelters to get help”. (transman, not living with HIV).

Entry into medical care was facilitated by community support and services specific to people living with HIV

Structural barriers to entering medical care

Structural barriers also existed for those newly entering medical care. Availability of insurance and concerns about healthcare costs, were commonly noted barriers: “Once, we went to a hospital in Brooklyn, and we provided the insurance number, but two days later, they called to charge me more than $1000 and my wife had to pay around $600”(cisman, not living with HIV). Others noted challenges that arose as a result of their experiences as immigrants or their interactions with the healthcare system: “Yes, it was kind of hard, because at first I had problems getting my medication, because I didn't have […] identification when I left immigration, they took my passport” (genderqueer, living with HIV). In particular, engagement with gender-affirming care resulted in variable experiences, with one participant noting frustration at having to ‘start from scratch’, including having to get full re-assessments: “Because you arrive here being new to the process, and they ask you, 'What are you here for?' And you say, 'Well, I'm a transgender patient. I used to be on hormonal treatment back in Colombia, and I had...' And they say, 'Forget about all that!' They say it like that” (transwoman, not living with HIV).

Community-based connections and targeted services for people living with HIV facilitated entry into medical care

Some participants were able to overcome these barriers and have positive experiences with medical care, often due to key facilitating factors. First, CBOs with relationships to clinical services often served as a gateway into clinical care. Many participants specifically highlighted CBOs that provide direct connection into care as a key facilitator - “I've pretty much had everything through the organization, they refer you to somebody. [They] made the referral to the clinic for me” (cisman, not living with HIV). Second, most participants living with HIV identified efficient access to HIV providers and treatment, and rapid entry into care: “They tested me, and they gave me an appointment for eight days later. And that's when they started to give me medication” (cisman, living with HIV). Infrastructure specific for people living with HIV helped to facilitate this access: “I didn't have insurance or an address at that time, so they activated my emergency ADAP [AIDS Drug Assistance Program] [...] I applied for it at HASA on Wednesday, and on Thursday, I went to get my card even though not all the benefits were active. ADAP covered my medicine for a month” (genderqueer, living with HIV). Rapid engagement in medical care was cited as a source of stability and reduced stress for many participants: “I got my medication back, and I got checked... I couldn't believe it. My experience started to change towards something positive” (cisman, living with HIV).

Structural barriers limited engagement in mental health services

Participants experienced persistent trauma derived from their immigration experiences and ongoing barriers once in NYC

In contrast to medical care, engagement in mental health services was limited by barriers not effectively addressed by the facilitators described above. This is despite many participants experiencing psychosocial stress starting with their decision to immigrate through to their new life in NYC. Some participants described persistent emotional distress related to lingering trauma from their migration experience: “It's as if a relative just died, and I'm in the middle of this grieving process. You've buried them, but you know that the memory of that person will always be there. […] living with that memory of the process of your migratory grief” (genderqueer, not living with HIV). Many participants identified ongoing mental health sequelae that persisted after arrival: “from all the episodes that I've lived through, I've been suffering from depression, anxiety, due to the stress of not having a lawyer, of being alone in this country, of not being able to speak the language” (transwoman, living with HIV).

In addition, social and political factors shaping the response to immigration once arrived in the US also created ongoing stress. Many participants described a climate of ongoing xenophobia and anti-immigrant sentiment once arrived in the US: “Yeah, the school. Something like "villa". That school, every day there were protests, every day they saw you coming in and they started swearing, right?” (transman, not living with HIV). Other participants identified ongoing psychosocial stress from fear of deportation or interaction with immigration enforcement: ““It's frightening. It's scary. It's terrifying. All the bad words because it's like you're literally on your toes because you don't know if something is going to happen, if they're going to pick you up, if [ICE] and all these different people are going to take you away” (genderqueer, living with HIV). Ongoing trauma both from the experience of immigrating to the US and a perceived hostility towards immigrants once in the US created a clear need for increased mental health support in study participants.

Structural barriers affected the ability to access mental health care and ongoing engagement with mental health services

Despite this ongoing trauma, there were varied experiences with engagement in mental health care. Some participants described the need for significant self-advocacy to engage with mental health care: “They sent me a voicemail asking me if I still need a mental health service, [...] I called them back but they put me on hold and then they didn't call me back. [...] they did offer it to me, but you have to be very aware and if you aren't, you'll lose it.” (genderqueer, living with HIV). In one case, a participant reflected on how care payment models affected their experience with care: “So that bothered me more, because that person was so interested in his payment of 30 minutes, because if he works one more minute, the insurance company is not going to pay him. He was more concerned about his pay than he was about my mental health.” (genderqueer, living with HIV). Other participants identified language barriers and necessity of care being mediated by a translator as a reason they did not further engage in mental health services. A minority of participants did indicate positive experiences with mental health providers and appreciated being proactively referred to mental health services, often as a part of other care such as HIV care or gender-affirming care: “I was lucky, thank God, that when they were going to do the operation on my testicles, they provided me with a therapist and a psychologist, and the psychologist stayed on with me.” (transwoman, not living with HIV).

Intensive community-based connections were instrumental in overcoming structural barriers

Participants all highlighted community-based connections as an important pathway to resources. These took various forms, from individual relationships to participation in CBOs. These connections provided material support and assistance with navigating resources: “they've helped me get clothing and shoes, and what they call a 'pantry'. They've also helped me get shampoo, toothpaste, a toothbrush, and some appointments related to jurisdiction issues.” (transwoman, not living with HIV). They were also a source of emotional support and opportunity to reflect on traumatic experiences: “by going to these types of organizations, you kind of see the light. […] You feel that you're among people who've perhaps suffered the same or even worse things than you.” (cisman, living with HIV). Being taken through an unfamiliar process was seen as particularly valuable for many participants, especially on arrival to NYC: “[The CBO staff member came] back [...] for us at the shelter. He took us to the hospital. That's where we got connected to the [hospital]. We then began getting meds. He navigate[d] us to go to school to get our GED and also he did a lot of stuff for us” (genderqueer, not living with HIV). Many continued to stay connected to CBOs, and some described playing ongoing roles in those organizations: “When I came here, I didn't [have many] friends. I met one gay person and that one gay person, he introduced me to [another] gay person. They introduced me to this group and the group is just like a regular conference where they talk about this and I go there” (genderqueer, living with HIV).

Notably, a minority of participants did identify tensions or feelings of exclusion from certain community spaces. Often this stemmed either from interpersonal conflict, or else discrimination along lines of specific minoritized identities, specifically along racial lines: “It was really, really tough because it's like they look down on me being black and I'm like, but if you guys are trying to serve the community, why would it be so difficult to assist me in getting certain services?” (genderqueer, not living with HIV).

Additional findings

Some additional findings emerged that were not contained in our core themes but that appeared across numerous interviews. In particular, these were related to impact of immigration detention on mental and physical health, as well as the role of cultural narratives in shaping attitudes towards engagement in care once arrived in the US. Though not the particular focus of this study, additional exploration of these findings in future studies will help elucidate the pervasiveness and scope of these ideas, and how they affect immigrants’ engagement with mental health and HIV care. Discussion

This study is among the first to use CBPR to explore the impact of immigration-related stressors on engagement with HIV and mental health care, and the facilitators used to address such stressors. While participants did not always connect their limited access to social resources to downstream health risks, we see the impact of these barriers on health through the ‘structural vulnerabilities’ framework (Quesada et al., 2011, Carruth et al., 2021), which identifies how one's location within multiple social structures constrain choices and affect health outcomes. This term captures the ways in which exclusionary and inequitable policy decisions shape risk of poor health outcomes. For immigrants, structural vulnerabilities such as limited availability of social and healthcare resources, traumatic experiences related to detention, challenges navigating immigration policy, amongst others, create barriers to access of services including those that address the social determinants of health and impact health outcomes. A structural vulnerability framework allows us to frame stressors and resultant effects on health behavior within the context of structural decisions that create stressors and risk for immigrants. In our study, this framing helps us understand how policy decisions shaping access to resources such as legal services and housing services constrain the options available to PLWH and people at risk for HIV, thus increasing risk of poor health outcomes.

In using strengths-based framing, our study also demonstrates key areas where participants used strategies to face structural barriers. Participants highlighted the role of CBOs in accessing material resources and as a source of emotional support. CBOs have been previously identified as playing a pivotal role in providing support to ILWH, whether by directly offering health services or by providing social interventions that impact health (Ubri et al., 2024). Our results further clarify the role that CBOs can play particularly in providing consistent navigation and support in addition to material support. In our sample, the CBOs that seemed to be most impactful for participants used what has been previously described as an ‘accompaniment’ model (Harlan, 2022, Wilkinson and D’Angelo, 2019, Phillips, 2023, Farmer, 2013, Farmer and Gustierrez, 2013). In this model, CBOs provide access to material resources but also serve as a space for individuals to develop community and build trust with staff that can connect to them to health services (Ubri et al., 2024). The strong positive role that accompaniment models play in supporting ILWH facing structural barriers suggests that interventions based in this model may have a robust positive impact on downstream health behaviors.

Another strength was the way in which services specific to people with HIV facilitated access to resources, at times allowing for even greater access than those living without HIV. It is well understood how social determinants such as housing impact HIV outcomes, and our study emphasized the positive role that services specific for individuals living with HIV can play to address these determinants. However, our study also shows how immigrants living with and without HIV often face similar circumstances regarding access to resources, stemming from policy decisions that shape the resource landscape. Given these common conditions, our results highlight how ultimately addressing these structural-level factors that are now well-recognized in driving acquisition of HIV and worsening HIV outcomes require status-neutral approaches that can meet the needs of those both living with HIV and vulnerable to acquiring HIV. Structural-level vulnerabilities require structural-level interventions that do not map cleanly into disease categories and require policy level change that improves broad availability of social resources. Current interventions and resource pathways for individuals living with HIV can serve as a model of how to address structural barriers for those living with and at risk of HIV. For example, the Housing Opportunities for People with AIDS (HOPWA) funding program (Housing Opportunities for Persons With AIDS 2025) may expand to include those at vulnerable to HIV, and other service programs specific to PLWH may consider status-neutral approaches that use HIV risk, not just HIV status, to determine resource provision. The lived reality of scarcity for our participants, in a city like NYC that is a global financial capital, also highlights the need for broader advocacy around economic equity as a component of any strategy to end the HIV epidemic.

Engagement in medical care highlights the ways in which these two facilitating pathways interact with each other to improve access to a much-needed resource. Some participants did identify barriers to accessing healthcare, related both to features of the US healthcare system and specific immigration-related stressors, but others that engaged the facilitating strategies noted above often experienced relatively smooth access to and positive experiences with healthcare. Many participants described being connected to healthcare via CBOs. This highlights the role that CBOs play in vetting trustworthy providers and linking individuals into care, as well as the importance of strong institutional-CBO connections to facilitate such linkage. In addition, the pathways made available for PLWH to access HIV-specific care further exemplifies how targeted investment and resources can create a service infrastructure that addresses population level barriers. Once in care participants largely had positive experiences. This identifies the value of creating clear, rapid, entry-points into care, and highlights the potential role of healthcare settings as a linkage point to additional social services and resources.

Participants’ engagement with mental health services illustrates both the high need and the specific obstacles ILWH experience. Existing literature describes specific psychosocial stressors faced by immigrants, including an association between anti-immigration policies and negative mental health outcomes (Martinez et al., 2015). Experience in detention, fear of deportation and harassment from immigration authorities are sources of stress and lead to avoidance of health services (Pinedo and Valdez, 2020, Chavez, 2012). Our data reinforce these findings by depicting how traumatic experiences during migration, punitive immigration policy, and scarcity of resources create psychosocial stress, highlighting the unique traumas experienced by immigrants and thus the need for contextualized support services, such as those developed through trauma-informed models of care. At times participants identified how these psychosocial stressors affected their ability to prioritize health, directly linking these mental health impacts to health behaviors. In our data the facilitators that assisted in access to other resources did not ease access to mental health resources to the same degree. Notably, in discussion with our team members and CAB, cultural perceptions of care and stigma related to mental health may have further impacted engagement in this domain, providing an alternate explanation for the relative lack of engagement in mental health services. Further exploration is required to understand how cultural perceptions of mental health may play a role. In addition, future studies should develop and test implementation strategies that might meet the specific needs of this population, such as culturally sensitive outreach, language concordance, and direct connection to mental health providers via CBOs, a strategy that our study shows has been effective regarding medical care.

This study’s sample is unique for a few reasons, which while providing insight into the specific population in question, does also present some limitations to the generalizability of these findings. New York State, and NYC in particular, has a robust infrastructure to support immigrants. However, since 2022 NYC has seen an increase in newly arrived migrants (Office of New York City Comptroller 2025) and has struggled to adapt local infrastructure, leading to both public anti-immigrant rhetoric and new restrictions on access to services such as shelter for new arrivals (Report on the Investigation of the Implementation of the “60-Day Rule” for Asylum-Seeker Families 2025). Importantly, our sampling structure may have shaped the perspectives of our participants. Many of our participants were referred from our partner CBOs or connected via CBO members; as such, our sample may not capture participants who are not connected to CBOs or have had prior negative experiences with CBOs. Finally, while we did not explicitly capture self-identified race data, the population in our sample were primarily from Latin American countries and so did not capture the experience of immigrants with other identities. This is generally consistent with the population distribution of immigrants in NYC (Mayor’s Office of Immigrant Affairs. State of Our Immigrant City 2018), however additional perspectives on how other intersecting identities such as racialized status impact immigration-related barriers may not be represented in our sample, as suggested by some of our data reflecting racialized experiences within CBOs. These features of our sample mean that while we capture the unique experience of this population, future studies that include individuals not connected by CBOs, are from other countries of origin or have other racialized identities may shed greater light on perspectives not represented in our sample.

This study provides a unique view on how barriers related to immigration can affect access and engagement in mental and physical health care for PLWH and at risk of acquiring HIV. Our findings highlight that structural policy decisions limiting availability of social resources can increase psychosocial stress and create downstream effects on health behaviors. In particular, our study shows how the experience of immigration and of being an immigrant in the US, shaped by current immigration policy, both itself creates mental and physical health risks and constrains access to necessary health and social resources. At the same time, the participants highlight the instrumental role that community accompaniment and dedicated infrastructure to target vulnerable and high-risk individuals can play in addressing such barriers. Ending the HIV epidemic means developing tools to address not only individual-level risk but also population-level risk, which means understanding the structural landscape that shapes access to social and healthcare resources. Our findings make an argument for immigration as a key component of that structural landscape, and one that must be considered when developing interventions and policy that not only serve PLWH but also provides care for people vulnerable to HIV. Future studies are needed to explore how specific components of the immigration policy landscape, such as detention and deportation practices, further impact the health and engagement in care of this vulnerable population.
