A Thermodynamically Consistent Free Boundary Model for Two-Phase Flows in an Evolving Domain with Moving Contact Lines, Variable Contact Angles and Bulk-Surface Interaction
Patrik Knopf, Yadong Liu

Abstract




Abstract

We derive a thermodynamically consistent model, which describes the time evolution of a two-phase flow in an evolving domain. The movement of the free boundary of the domain is driven by the velocity field of the mixture in the bulk, which is determined by a Navier–Stokes equation. In order to take interactions between bulk and boundary into account, we further consider two materials on the boundary, which may be the same or different materials as those in the bulk. The bulk and the surface materials are represented by respective phase-fields, whose time evolution is described by a bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard equation. This approach allows for a transfer of material between bulk and surface as well as variable contact angles between the diffuse interface in the bulk and the boundary of the domain. To provide a more accurate description of the corresponding contact line motion, we include a generalized Navier slip boundary condition on the velocity field. We derive our model from scratch by considering local mass balance and energy dissipation laws. Finally, the derivation is completed via the Langrange multiplier approach. We further show that our model generalizes previous models from the literature, which can be recovered from our system by either dropping the dynamic boundary conditions or assuming a static boundary of the domain.

Keywords:

two-phase flow,
free boundary problem,
phase-field model,
moving contact line,
variable contact angle,
Navier–Stokes equations,
Cahn–Hilliard equations,
bulk-surface interaction.

1  Introduction

The description of two-phase flows is an important topic in modern continuum fluid dynamics, which has widespread applications in biology, chemistry, and engineering.
To represent the interface separating the different components, two fundamental approaches have been developed, namely
sharp-interface methods
and
diffuse interface methods
. We refer to
[
32
,
3
,
46
,
72
]
for a comparison of the two approaches.

In sharp-interface models, the interface is represented by an evolving hypersurface contained in the surrounding domain. The time evolution of the interface is then described by a free boundary problem.

In diffuse interface models, the interface between the two materials is represented by a thin layer whose thickness is proportional to a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
. The location of the two components is usually represented by an order parameter, the so-called
phase-field
, which describes the difference of the local concentrations (or volume fractions) of the materials.
This means that the phase-field attains values close to
-1
or
1
in the regions where the single fluids are present. At the the diffuse interface between the fluids, the phase-field is expected to exhibit a continuous transition between the values representing the pure phases. In normal direction across the diffuse interface, a certain transition profile can usually be observed. The main advantage of this approach is that the time evolution of the phase-field can be described via an Eulerian formulation as a PDE on the whole domain. This avoids directly tracking the interface between the phases. If a proper scaling with respect to the interface parameter
\varepsilon
is used, the corresponding sharp-interface model can be recovered (in most cases at least formally) as the
sharp-interface limit
(see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).

One of the most fundamental models for describing the motion of two viscous and incompressible fluids with equal densities is the
Model H
.
It was first proposed in
[
56
]
and later rigorously derived in
[
53
]
. The system consists of an incompressible Navier–Stokes equation that is coupled to a convective Cahn–Hilliard equation.
The fundamental assumption in the derivation of the Model H is that the individual constant densities of the fluids are equal. They are therefore referred to as
matched densities
. This directly entails that the density of the whole mixture is constant.

In the past two decades, various works have proposed generalizations of the Model H
describing incompressible mixtures with
unmatched
densities and/or compressible two-phase flows.
We refer to

[
5
,
65
,
19
,
31
,
75
,
76
,
46
,
55
,
37
]

to at least mention some of them.

One of the most popular models for viscous incompressible two-phase flows with unmatched densities is the one derived by Abels, Garcke and Grün in
[
5
]
. In view of the initials of the authors, it is often referred to as the
AGG Model
. It was shown in
[
5
]
that by including an additional flux term in the Navier–Stokes equation, the model can be made thermodynamically consistent even if the constant individual densities of the fluids are
unmatched
. Analytical results for the AGG model can be found, e.g., in
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
4
,
6
,
48
,
49
]
. An alternative derivation of the AGG model via mixture theory was recently developed in
[
77
]
.

However, as the classical AGG model is considered subject to a no-slip boundary condition for the velocity field as well as homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions for the Cahn–Hilliard quantities (i.e., the phase-field and the chemical potential), it still has some drawbacks if a precise description of the behavior of the fluids close to the boundary is required.
In the Cahn–Hilliard equation, the homogeneous Neumann condition on the phase-field enforces the diffuse interface to always intersect the boundary at a ninety degree contact angle, which is very unrealistic in many applications. The homogeneous Neumann boundary condition on the chemical potential entails the conservation of mass within the considered domain, which makes a description of transfer of material between bulk an boundary (e.g., absorption processes) impossible.
These issues have also been noticed by physicists
(see, e.g.,
[
18
,
35
,
36
,
57
]
),
who proposed the inclusion of an additional surface free energy to describe short-range interactions between bulk and boundary more precisely.

Due to these observations, several types of dynamic boundary conditions for the Cahn–Hilliard equation have been proposed and analyzed in the literature.
Especially in recent times, dynamic boundary conditions that also exhibit a Cahn–Hilliard type structure have become very popular because they allow for a quantification of the mass transfer between bulk and boundary. For brevity, we refer to such models as
bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard
models.
We refer to
[
43
,
44
,
27
,
26
,
25
,
38
,
71
]
for analytical results and to
[
68
,
69
,
54
,
67
,
14
,
15
,
21
,
20
]
for a numerical investigation of the bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard system. A nonlocal bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard system was analyzed in
[
60
,
66
]
.
A convective variant of the bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard system with a prescribed velocity field was analyzed, for example, in
[
28
,
29
,
47
,
51
,
61
,
62
]
.
The role of dynamic boundary conditions in Cahn–Hilliard type models will be discussed in more detail in Subsection
2.3
.
For more information on the Cahn–Hilliard equation with classical homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions or with dynamic boundary conditions, we refer to the recent review paper
[
78
]
as well as the book
[
70
]
.

Moreover, as shown for example in
[
73
,
74
]
, a no-slip boundary condition on the velocity field is not very well suited for the description of moving contact line phenomena. This is because convective effects occurring at the boundary (or close to the boundary) are neglected if the velocity field is assumed to be identically zero at the surface. Therefore, if a no-slip boundary condition is imposed, the motion of the contact line is primarily driven by diffusion or convection processes away from the boundary. To overcome this issue, a generalized Navier slip boundary condition, which provides a better description of moving contact lines, was derived in
[
73
]
.

Based on these considerations, Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard models with dynamic boundary conditions on the Cahn–Hilliard subsystem as well as a generalized Navier slip boundary condition for the Navier–Stokes equation have been proposed and investigated in the literature.
We refer to
[
40
,
41
]
, where a second-order (Allen-Cahn type) dynamic boundary condition was used for the Cahn–Hilliard subsystem, and to
[
50
]
, where a fourth-order (Cahn–Hilliard type) dynamic boundary condition is considered.
The model derived in
[
50
]
was further analyzed in
[
42
]
. In these models, the Cahn–Hilliard quantities at the boundary enter the generalized Navier slip boundary condition via a forcing term. We also refer to
[
34
,
24
]
, where
compressible
two-phase flows were considered. In
[
34
]
a compressible Navier–Stokes–Allen–Cahn model was analyzed and in
[
24
]
, a compressible Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard model with dynamic boundary conditions was investigated.
Moreover, models for two-phase flows with
phase-transition
were studied in
[
10
,
8
]
.

We point out that all the models discussed so far are each considered in a fixed bounded domain that does not change during the time evolution. In some applications, however, the domain in which a two-phase flow is considered will evolve over the course of time. A simple example is a droplet (surrounded by a further substance such as air) that is placed on a deformable substrate, for example, an elastic sheet (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1:
Droplet on a moving surface.

(Only the relevant part of the evolving domain
\Omega(\cdot)
and its boundary is displayed.)

A more complex example is the motion of a single biological cell, for instance, a bacterium (cf. Figure
2
). In this case, the bacterium is represented by an evolving domain and its cell wall, represented by the boundary of the domain, is an evolving surface. Different substances inside the bacteria (e.g., cell organelles) can be described as a two-phase (or even multi-phase) flow. Also the materials on the cell wall (such as proteins or polysaccharides) can be interpreted as a mixture of different substances whose time evolution is of interest.

Figure 2:
Evolving domain (e.g., describing a bacterium) with

two phases in the bulk and two phases on the evolving boundary.

In this paper, for the aforementioned reasons, we derive a new model, which generalizes the two-phase flow model derived in
[
50
]
to the framework of an evolving domain with a free boundary.
Here, a key assumption is that the evolution of our domain is driven by the velocity field of the mixture contained in the domain. This means that the normal velocity of the free boundary is equal to the normal component of the velocity field at the boundary.

1.1  Formulation of our model

To formulate our system of equations, we consider an evolving domain
\{\Omega(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
T>0
). We set
\Gamma(t)\coloneqq\partial\Omega(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. This means that
\{\Gamma(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a closed, oriented evolving hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For brevity, we use the notation

Q_{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{0<t<T}\{t\}\times\Omega(t)\quad\text{and}\quad S_{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{0<t<T}\{t\}\times\Gamma(t)

We further assume that the evolving domain
Q_{T}
is transported by a vector field
{\mathbf{u}}\in C^{2}(\overline{Q_{T}})
, which is supposed to be the
volume-averaged velocity field
of the two materials that are contained inside the domain.
This entails that
S_{T}
is transported by the trace vector field
{\mathbf{u}}|_{S_{T}}\in C^{2}(S_{T})
. Since
S_{T}
is orientable, there exists an evolving unit normal vector field
{\mathbf{n}}\in C^{2}(S_{T})
and the normal velocity
\mathcal{V}
of
S_{T}
is thus given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=\mathcal{V}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$}.

For a mathematically precise formulation of the notion of evolving domains and surfaces that are transported by a velocity field, we refer to
[
13
,
17
]
.

In order to describe the time evolution of the velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}
, we derive the following thermodynamically consistent model:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\rho(\phi){\mathbf{u}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\big({\mathbf{u}}\otimes(\rho(\phi){\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{J})\big)=\textnormal{div}\,(\mathbf{T}),\quad\textnormal{div}\,{\mathbf{u}}=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }Q_{T},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\phi=\textnormal{div}\,(m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu)

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }Q_{T},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta\phi+\varepsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(\phi)

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }Q_{T},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta)-\beta m_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }S_{T},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\theta=-\delta\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi+\delta^{-1}G^{\prime}(\psi)+\alpha\varepsilon\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }S_{T},

(1.1e)

\displaystyle K\varepsilon\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=\alpha\psi-\phi,\quad Lm_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=\beta\theta-\mu,\quad K,L\in[0,\infty],

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }S_{T},

(1.1f)

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}+\nabla_{\Gamma}q+\gamma_{\tau}{\mathbf{u}}\big]_{\tau}=\Big[\frac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})\,{\mathbf{u}}-\delta\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\Big]_{\tau}

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }S_{T},

(1.1g)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}+\gamma_{\mathbf{n}}\mathcal{V}=\frac{1}{2}\,(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})\mathcal{V}-qH

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }S_{T},

(1.1h)

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}=0,\quad{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\quad\text{on }S_{T}.

(1.1i)

This system can be understood as a generalized version of the model introduced in
[
50
]
, adapted to the situation of an evolving domain with free boundary.
An equivalent reformulation of this model will be discussed in Subsection
2.1
.

In the above equations and throughout this paper,
\nabla
denotes the gradient,
\nabla_{\Gamma}
denotes the tangential gradient on the surface,
div
denotes the divergence, and
\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}
denotes the surface divergence. For (sufficently regular) functions
\mathbf{w}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbf{M}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we use the conventions

[\nabla\mathbf{w}]_{ij}=\partial_{j}\mathbf{w}_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad[\textnormal{div}\,\mathbf{M}]_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\partial_{k}\mathbf{M}_{ik}

for all
i,j\in\{1,...,d\}
. This means that for any vector-valued function,
\nabla
provides its Jacobian matrix.
For functions on the surface
S_{T}
, an analogous convention is employed.
Moreover, for any (column) vectors
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, their tensor product is given by
\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{w}^{T}
.

In system (
1.1
), the volume-averaged velocity field of the two fluids is represented by the vector-valued function
{\mathbf{u}}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The scalar functions
\phi:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mu:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\psi:S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\theta:S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the
bulk phase-field
, the
bulk chemical potential
, the
surface phase-field
, and the
surface chemical potential
, respectively.
It is a key feature of the model (
1.1
) that the evolving domain
Q_{T}
is transported by the velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}
. This is reflected in the boundary condition
\eqref{eqs:NSCH:9}_{2}
and, therefore, system (
1.1
) is a
free boundary problem
.

The time evolution of
{\mathbf{u}}
is described by the
incompressible Navier–Stokes equations
(
1.1a
). Here,
\rho(\phi)
stands for the phase-field dependent
density
,
\mathbf{J}
represents the
mass flux
related to interfacial motion, and
\mathbf{T}
is the
stress tensor
. They are given by the formulas

\displaystyle\rho(\phi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}_{2}(1+\phi)+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\rho}_{1}(1-\phi),

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1})m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbf{S}-p\mathbf{I}-\varepsilon\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{S}\coloneqq 2\nu(\phi)\,\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}},

(1.4)

where the constants
\tilde{\rho}_{1},\tilde{\rho}_{2}>0
are the individual densities of the fluids,
\nu(\phi)
is the phase-field dependent
viscosity
, and

\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big((\nabla{\mathbf{u}})+(\nabla{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\big)

denotes the
symmetric gradient
of
{\mathbf{u}}
.
The matrix
\mathbf{S}
is referred to as the
viscous stress tensor
.

The evolution of the phase-fields
\phi
and
\psi
is described by the
bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard system
(
1.1b
)–(
1.1f
).
Here, the
bulk Cahn–Hilliard equation
(
1.1b
)–(
1.1c
) is coupled to the
surface Cahn–Hilliard equation
(
1.1d
)–(
1.1e
), which can be interpreted as a dynamic boundary condition. To state the Cahn–Hilliard equations, we are using the following notation. For scalar functions
f:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and
g:S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\partial_{t}^{\bullet}f\coloneqq\partial_{t}f+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla f,\qquad\partial_{t}^{\circ}g\coloneqq\partial_{t}\tilde{g}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla\tilde{g}=\partial_{t}\tilde{g}+{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\tilde{g}+\mathcal{V}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\nabla\tilde{g}

(1.5)

to denote the
material derivatives
of
f
and
g
, respectively. Here,
\tilde{g}
denotes an arbitrary (sufficiently regular) extension of
g
onto a neighborhood of
S_{T}
. In fact, the expression
\partial_{t}^{\circ}g
does actually not depend on the choice of this extension (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Remark 2.7.2]
).
The phase-field dependent functions
m_{\Omega}(\phi)
and
m_{\Gamma}(\psi)
represent the
mobilities
of the bulk- and the surface materials, respectively. Moreover,
F^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime}
are the derivatives of
double-well potentials

F
and
G
, respectively.
The bulk and surface quantities of the Cahn–Hilliard equations are further coupled through the boundary conditions in (
1.1f
), which are to be understood as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\phi=\alpha\psi&\text{if $K=0$},\\
K\varepsilon\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=\alpha\psi-\phi&\text{if $K\in(0,\infty)$},\\
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=0&\text{if $K=\infty$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{on }S_{T},

(1.6a)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mu=\beta\theta&\text{if $L=0$},\\
Lm_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=\beta\theta-\mu&\text{if $L\in(0,\infty)$},\\
m_{\Omega}(\phi)\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0&\text{if $L=\infty$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\text{on }S_{T}.

(1.6b)

Here,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are prescribed parameters.
The parameters
K,L\in[0,\infty]
are used to distinguish different cases, each resulting in a certain solution behaviour related to a corresponding physical phenomenon (see Subsection
2.3
for more details).
If
K=0
, we additionally assume that
\alpha\neq 0
.

Furthermore, on the surface
S_{T}
, the tangential component of the velocity field is governed by the
generalized Navier slip boundary condition
(
1.1g
) with a slip parameter
\gamma_{\tau}
that may depend on the materials involved (i.e., the phase-fields).
Here, for any vector field
\mathbf{w}
, the notation
\mathbf{w}_{\tau}
indicates that only the tangential component of
\mathbf{w}
is considered.
The normal component of
{\mathbf{u}}
is determined by the relation (
1.1h
), which involves
the
mean curvature

H=-\frac{1}{d}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{n}}
.
Moreover,
\gamma_{\mathbf{n}}
is a prescribed scalar function that may depend on the phase-fields.

The energy functional associated with system (
1.1
) is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega(t)}\frac{\rho(\phi)}{2}\left|{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\Omega(t)}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\phi)\Big)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}

(1.7)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma(t)}\Big(\frac{\delta}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}G(\psi)+\frac{1}{2}\mathds{h}(K)\left|\alpha\psi-\phi\right|^{2}\Big)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1},

where the function

\mathds{h}:[0,\infty]\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathds{h}(r)\coloneqq\begin{cases}r^{-1}&\text{if $r\in(0,\infty)$},\\
0&\text{if $r\in\{0,\infty\}$}\end{cases}

(1.8)

is used to account for the different choices of the parameter
K
. In (
1.7
), the first summand on the right-hand side represents the
kinetic energy
. The second summand is the
bulk free energy
and the third summand is the
surface free energy
. They are both of
Ginzburg–Landau type
and the free energy densities crucially depend on the potentials
F
and
G
, respectively, which are assumed to be double-well shaped. This means that they are coercive with two local minimum points close to
-1
and
1
(as these values represent the pure phases of the materials) and a local maximum point in between (usually at zero).
A typical choice for
F
and
G
is the so-called
Flory–Huggins potential

\displaystyle W_{\textup{log}}(s)\coloneqq\frac{\Theta}{2}\big[(1+s)\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\ln(1-s)\big]-\frac{\Theta_{c}}{2}s^{2},\quad s\in(-1,1)

(1.9)

for
0<\Theta<\Theta_{c}
, which can be derived from Boltzmann kinetics. It is sometimes simply referred to as the
logarithmic potential
.
Since
W_{\textup{log}}^{\prime}(s)\to\pm\infty
as
s\to\pm\infty
, the potential
W_{\textup{log}}
is a so-called
singular potential
. This property ensures that for any solution of system (
1.1
) with
F=G=W_{\textup{log}}
, the phase-fields
\phi
and
\psi
attain their values within the physically relevant range
[-1,1]
. An important consequence is that due to
\left|\phi\right|\leq 1
, we have

0<\min\{\tilde{\rho}_{1},\tilde{\rho}_{2}\}\leq\rho(\phi)\leq\max\{\tilde{\rho}_{1},\tilde{\rho}_{2}\}<\infty,

which means that the density
\rho(\phi)
remains uniformly positive and finite.
Furthermore, our model has the following physically relevant properties:

•

Energy dissipation.

Sufficiently regular solutions of system (
1.1
) satisfy the
energy-dissipation law

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\psi)+\int_{\Omega(t)}2\nu(\phi)\left|\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\Gamma(t)}\gamma_{\tau}\left|{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}

(1.10)

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma(t)}\gamma_{{\mathbf{n}}}\left|{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+\int_{\Omega(t)}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Gamma(t)}m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+\int_{\Gamma(t)}\mathds{h}(L)\left|\beta\theta-\mu\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=0

for all
t\in[0,T]
. In this sense, our model can be considered
thermodynamically consistent
.

•

Conservation of mass.

Sufficiently regular solutions of system (
1.1
) fulfill the
mass conservation law

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\Omega(t)}\phi(t)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\Gamma(t)}\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=\beta\int_{\Omega(0)}\phi(0)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\Gamma(0)}\psi(0)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}

(1.11)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. In the case
L=\infty
, it even holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega(t)}\phi(t)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=\int_{\Omega(0)}\phi(0)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\Gamma(t)}\psi(t)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=\int_{\Gamma(0)}\psi(0)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}

(1.12)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, so the bulk mass and the surface mass are conserved separately.

•

Conservation of bulk volume and surface area.

For any sufficiently regular solution of system (
1.1
), the volume of the evolving domain
\Omega(\cdot)
and the area of the evolving boundary
\Gamma(\cdot)
are preserved over the course of time.
Namely, as the bulk fluid is assumed to be incompressible (i.e.,
\textnormal{div}\,{\mathbf{u}}=0
in
Q_{T}
) and there is no extra source, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{Vol}_{d}\big(\Omega(t)\big)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega(t)}1\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=\int_{\Omega(t)}\textnormal{div}\,({\mathbf{u}})\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=0\quad\text{for all $t\in[0,T]$}

(1.13)

by means of the Reynolds transport theorem (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Theorem 2.11.1]
).
Similarly, as we also demand
\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}=0
on
S_{T}
, the transport theorem for evolving hypersurfaces (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Theorem 2.10.1]
) yields

1.3  The outline of this paper

The plan of this paper is as follows.
In Section
2
, we present an equivalent reformulation of system (

). Moreover, we compare two simplified variants of our model, one without dynamic boundary conditions and one in a domain with static boundary, with previous models proposed in the literature. We further discuss the benefits of dynamic boundary conditions in our model as well as in Cahn–Hilliard type models in general.
Section

2.3  The role of dynamic boundary conditions in our model

As already discussed in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
50
]
), the homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions in (
2.3d
) are quite restrictive. On the one hand, the condition
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi=0
on
S_{T}
means that the contact angle between the diffuse interface separating the fluids and the boundary
\Gamma(\cdot)
is always exactly ninety degrees. Of course, this is unrealistic in many applications, where the contact angle is expected to change dynamically over the course of time. On the other hand, the condition
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0
on
S_{T}
implies that no transfer of material between bulk and surface can occur (cf. (
1.12
)). Therefore, absorption or adsorption processes cannot be described.

These issues are overcome in model (
1.1
) by means of the bulk-surface convective Cahn–Hilliard subsystem (
1.1b
)–(
1.1f
).
At least for the non-convective bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard system (i.e.,
{\mathbf{u}}\equiv 0
), it has been observed in several numerical simulations that a transfer of material between bulk and surface occurs if
L\in[0,\infty)
and that the contact angle between the diffuse interface and the boundary changes dynamically over the course of time (see, e.g.,
[
59
,
15
,
69
,
21
,
20
]
).
Unfortunately, the sharp-interface limit of such bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard models, which would provide an explicit law for the behavior of the contact angle, is still not very well understood.

In a fixed domain, the bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard system subject to the coupling conditions (
1.6
) with
K,L\in[0,\infty]
has already been studied extensively in the literature. In particular, the coupling conditions (
1.6
) with
K,L\in[0,\infty]
were introduced and investigated in the following papers:

•

For
K=L=0
, the non-convective bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard equation was proposed in
[
39
,
52
]
. Since
L=0
, the chemical potentials are assumed to always remain in a chemical equilibrium, and a rapid transfer of material between bulk and surface is expected to occur.

•

For
K=0
and
L=\infty
, the non-convective bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard equation was derived in
[
63
]
by an energetic variational approach. In this case, the mass flux between bulk and surface is zero. Consequently, no transfer of material between bulk and surface will occur.

•

The case
K\in(0,\infty)
and
L=\infty
was first proposed an analyzed in
[
58
]
. It was shown that solutions of this model converge to solutions of the system with
K=0
and
L=\infty
as the parameter
K
is sent to zero.

•

For
K=0
and
L\in(0,\infty)
, the non-convective bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard equation was proposed in
[
59
]
to interpolate between the models associated with
K=0
,
L=0
and
{K=0}
,
{L=\infty}
. In this case, a transfer of material between bulk and surface will occur, and the number
L^{-1}
is related to the rate of absorption (or adsorption) of bulk material by the boundary (cf.
[
59
]
).

•

In
[
61
,
62
,
51
]
, the convective bulk-surface Cahn–Hilliard equation with a given velocity field was studied for all cases
K,L\in[0,\infty]
in a unified framework.
Of course, these results also include the non-convective case by simply choosing
{\mathbf{u}}\equiv 0
for the prescribed velocity field.

3  Model derivation

In this section we will derive the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system with dynamic boundary conditions (
1.1
).

We consider an evolving domain
\{\Omega(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of class
C^{2}
. For every
t\in[0,T]
, let
\Gamma(t)\coloneqq\partial\Omega(t)
. This means that
\{\Gamma(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a closed, oriented evolving hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of class
C^{2}
. For brevity, we use the notation

Q_{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{0<t<T}\{t\}\times\Omega(t)\quad\text{and}\quad S_{T}\coloneqq\bigcup_{0<t<T}\{t\}\times\Gamma(t).

We further assume that the evolving domain
Q_{T}
is transported by a vector field
{\mathbf{u}}\in C^{2}(\overline{Q_{T}})
, which is supposed to be the
volume-averaged velocity field
of the two fluids that are considered.
Therefore,
S_{T}
is transported by the vector field
{\mathbf{u}}|_{S_{T}}\in C^{2}(S_{T})
.
This means that

\displaystyle\Omega(s)=\eta_{s}\big(t,\Omega(t)\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma(s)=\eta_{s}\big(t,\Gamma(t)\big)\quad\text{for all $s,t\in[0,T]$,}

where for any
(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in\overline{Q_{T}}
, the flow map
[0,T]\ni s\mapsto\eta_{s}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\in\overline{\Omega(s)}
is the unique solution of the
ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\eta_{s}(t,{\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{u}}\big(s,\eta_{s}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\big),\quad s\in[0,T],

which satisfies the initial condition

\eta_{s}(t,{\mathbf{x}})|_{s=t}={\mathbf{x}}
.
Since
S_{T}
is orientable, there exists an evolving unit normal vector field
{\mathbf{n}}\in C(S_{T})
and the normal velocity
\mathcal{V}
of
S_{T}
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=\mathcal{V}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$}.

(3.1)

For more details about the theory of evolving domains and hypersurfaces as well as corresponding calculus and transport theorems, we refer to
[
13
,
17
]
.

In the following, we write
{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{\mathbf{n}}
to denote the tangential component and the normal component of
{\mathbf{u}}
, respectively.
We further assume that each bulk material has a constant individual density
\tilde{\rho}_{i}
,
i=1,2

and each boundary material has a constant individual density
\tilde{\sigma}_{i}
,
i=1,2
.

3.1  Considerations based on local mass balance laws

The mass densities
\rho_{i}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
, of the two fluids in the bulk are supposed to satisfy the following mass balance law:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}\,\,\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}=0,\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.2)

Here,
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}
are the mass fluxes corresponding to the motion of the two materials in the bulk.
In our model, we also want to account for a transfer of material between bulk and surface. This is, for instance, needed in order to describe absorption processes.
Therefore, we also consider functions
\sigma_{i}:S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
, which represent the mass densities of the substrates on the free boundary. In general, the materials on the surface might differ from those in the bulk. This is the case, for example, if the materials are transformed by chemical reactions occurring at the boundary. Therefore, we interpret the densities
\sigma_{i}
as independent functions that are not necessarily the traces of the densities
\rho_{i}
, respectively.
We assume that the the following mass balance law holds on the free boundary:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}\sigma_{i}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}=\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,i}\cdot{\mathbf{n}},\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.3)

In this relation,
\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}
are (not necessarily tangential) vector fields representing the mass fluxes on the boundary.
Moreover,
\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,i}
are (not necessarily tangential) vector fields describing the transfer of material between bulk and boundary.
Here and in what follows, the expression

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}h=\partial_{t}^{\circ}h-{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}h=\partial_{t}\tilde{h}+\mathcal{V}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\nabla\tilde{h}

(3.4)

denotes the
normal time derivative
of any (sufficiently regular) function
h:S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Here,
\tilde{h}
denotes an arbitrary (sufficiently regular) extension of
h
onto a neighborhood of
S_{T}
.
In (
3.3
), the normal time derivative needs to be used instead of
\partial_{t}\sigma_{i}
to account for the normal velocity of the evolving hypersurface
S_{T}
.
In order to ensure the local preservation of surface area (cf. (
3.27
) below), we additionally postulate that

\displaystyle\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,1}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}+\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0\quad\text{on $S_{T}$}.

(3.5)

We next assume that the motion of our materials is described by the individual velocity fields
{\mathbf{u}}_{i}:Q_{T}\cup S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,2
.
In view of the mass fluxes and the mass densities, we have the following relations:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}_{i}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}}{\rho_{i}}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$},\qquad{\mathbf{u}}_{i}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$},\qquad i=1,2.

Consequently, the mass balances (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}\,(\rho_{i}{\mathbf{u}}_{i})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}\sigma_{i}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\sigma_{i}{\mathbf{u}}_{i})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,i}\cdot{\mathbf{n}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.7)

We now introduce
\phi_{i}:Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi_{i}:S_{T}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,2
, to denote the volume fractions of the two fluids in the bulk and the two substrates on the boundary, respectively.
Recalling that each component has a constant density
\tilde{\rho}_{i}
,
\tilde{\sigma}
,
i=1,2
, the volume fractions can be identified as

\displaystyle\phi_{i}=\frac{\rho_{i}}{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}\qquad\psi_{i}=\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{i}}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$,}\qquad i=1,2.

(3.8)

Under the assumption that the excess volume is zero, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}=1\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}\qquad\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}=1\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.9)

We further define the order parameters

\displaystyle\phi=\phi_{2}-\phi_{1}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}\qquad\psi=\psi_{2}-\psi_{1}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$,}

(3.10)

which are referred to as the
phase-fields
.
Using the phase-fields
\phi
and
\psi
, the velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\phi_{2}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.11)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\psi_{2}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.12)

We now introduce

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{i}-\rho_{i}{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}-\sigma_{i}{\mathbf{u}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{on $S_{T}$,}

(3.14)

which represent the mass fluxes relative to the volume averaged velocity field. The mass balance equations (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) can thus be rewritten as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\textnormal{div}\,(\rho_{i}{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{i}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}\sigma_{i}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\sigma_{i}{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{i}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,i}\cdot{\mathbf{n}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.16)

In this context,
\mathbf{J}_{i}
and
\mathbf{K}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, can be regarded as diffusive flow rates. We now define the total mass densities as

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\quad\text{in $S_{T}$.}

(3.18)

This leads to the relations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\textnormal{div}\,(\rho{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,(\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{2})

\displaystyle=0

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\circ}\sigma+\sigma\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{K}_{1}+\mathbf{K}_{2})

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,1}+\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,2})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}

on
S_{T}
.

(3.20)

Moreover, in view of the balance of linear momentum with respect to the velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,(\rho{\mathbf{u}}\otimes{\mathbf{u}})=\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{T}}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.21)

Here,
\tilde{\mathbf{T}}
denotes the stress tensor, which needs to be specified by constitutive assumptions. In the following, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}_{1}+\mathbf{J}_{2}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}\qquad\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}_{1}+\mathbf{K}_{2}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.22)

Using (
3.19
) to reformulate (
3.21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \rho(\partial_{t}{\mathbf{u}}+{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\nabla{\mathbf{u}})&=\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+(\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}){\mathbf{u}}=\textnormal{div}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+{\mathbf{u}}\otimes\mathbf{J})-\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\;\mathbf{J}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}\end{aligned}

(3.23)

We now define the objective stress tensor

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+{\mathbf{u}}\otimes\mathbf{J}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.24)

which is frame indifferent. Hence, (
3.23
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\rho\partial_{t}{\mathbf{u}}+\nabla{\mathbf{u}}\;(\rho{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{J})=\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{T}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.25)

We now multiply the equations (
3.6
) each by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, and we add the resulting equations. Recalling (
3.8
),(
3.9
) and (
3.11
), we deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{div}\,{\mathbf{u}}=\textnormal{div}\,\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\frac{\rho_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\right)=-\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\rho_{1}}{\tilde{\rho}_{1}}+\frac{\rho_{2}}{\tilde{\rho}_{2}}\right)=-\partial_{t}1=0&&\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}\end{aligned}

(3.26)

which means that the volume averaged velocity field is divergence-free.
Similarly, multiplying (
3.7
) by
{1}/{\tilde{\sigma}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, adding the resulting equations, and recalling (
3.5
), (
3.8
), (
3.9
) and (
3.12
), we infer

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,{\mathbf{u}}=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}}{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\right)\\
&\quad=-\partial_{t}^{\circ}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}}\right)+{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\left(\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}}\right)+\left(\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,1}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}+\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\\
&\quad=\left(\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,1}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}+\frac{\widehat{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,2}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}}\right)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0&&\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}\end{aligned}

(3.27)

We now define

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{J}_{i}}{\tilde{\rho}_{i}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.28)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,i}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{i}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{J}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,2}-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,1}

on
S_{T}
,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{K}_{i}}{\tilde{\sigma}_{i}},

\displaystyle\quad i=1,2,

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{K}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{2}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{1}

on
S_{T}
.

(3.30)

Multiplying the equations (
3.15
) each by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, and subtracting the resulting equations, we deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}\,(\phi{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}=0\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.31)

Moreover, multiplying the equations (
3.15
) each by
{1}/{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}
, adding the resulting equations, and recalling (
3.9
) and (
3.26
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2})=-\partial_{t}(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})-\textnormal{div}\,\big((\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}){\mathbf{u}}\big)=0\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.32)

Proceeding similarly with (
3.16
), and recalling (
3.9
) and (
3.27
), we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}=\partial_{t}^{\scalebox{0.5}{$\square$}}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\psi{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}=\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$,}

(3.33)

and

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{2})

\displaystyle=-\partial_{t}^{\circ}(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big((\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}){\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\big)+\big(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,1}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{\Gamma,2}\big)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0

(3.34)

on
S_{T}
.
Using (
3.28
) and (
3.32
), which entails
\textnormal{div}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1})=-\textnormal{div}\,(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2})
, we infer that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}&=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tilde{\rho}_{2}\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}\bigg]+\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\tilde{\rho}_{2}\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}\bigg]\\[4.30554pt]
&=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{2}-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}\,\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_{1}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}&&\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}\end{aligned}

(3.35)

This means that the fluxes
\mathbf{J}
and
\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1})\mathbf{J}_{\phi}
can merely differ by an additive divergence-free function. As in
[
5
,
50
]
, we thus assume that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.36)

In the same way, we derive the identity

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}=\frac{\tilde{\sigma}_{2}-\tilde{\sigma}_{1}}{2}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.37)

Assuming that
\phi
and
\psi
only attain values in the interval
[-1,1]
, we use (
3.9
), (
3.10
) and (
3.17
) to conclude that the density
\rho=\rho(\phi)
is given by

\displaystyle\rho(\phi)=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\phi+\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}+\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.38)

3.2  Local energy dissipation laws

As in
[
5
]
and
[
50
]
, we consider the following energy density in the bulk:

\displaystyle e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)=\frac{\rho(\phi)}{2}\left|{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}+f(\phi,\nabla\phi)\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.39)

Here, the first summand on the right-hand side represents the kinetic energy density. The second summand denotes the free energy density in the bulk. It is assumed to be of Ginzburg–Landau type, that is

\displaystyle f(\phi,\nabla\phi)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(\phi)\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.40)

As in
[
50
]
, we additionally introduce the surface energy density

\displaystyle e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)=g(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.41)

Here,
g
stands for the free energy density on the surface. It is also assumed to be of Ginzburg–Landau type with an additional term accounting for the relation between
\phi
and
\psi
, namely

\displaystyle g(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)=\frac{\delta}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}G(\psi)+\mathds{h}(K)(\alpha\psi-\phi)^{2},\quad\text{$K\in[0,\infty]$}

(3.42)

on
S_{T}
, where
\mathds{h}
is the function introduced in (
1.8
).
Here,
\delta>0
is related to the thickness of the diffuse interface on the boundary,
and
G
is a potential that usually exhibits a double-well structure.

To derive local energy dissipation laws, we now consider an arbitrary (sufficiently smooth) test volume
V(t)\subset\Omega(t)
,

t\in[0,T]
, that is transported by the velocity field
{\mathbf{u}}
.
In an isothermal situation, the second law of thermodynamics leads to the dissipation inequality

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\int_{V(t)}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\right]\\
&\qquad+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Omega(t)}\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial V(t)}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}+\int_{\partial(\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t))}\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial(\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t))}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-2}.\end{aligned}

(3.43)

In this inequality, the vector fields
\mathbf{J}_{e}
and
\mathbf{K}_{e}
are energy fluxes that will be specified later,
{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial V(t)}
is the outer unit normal vector field on
\partial V(t)
, and
{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial(\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t))}
is the unit conormal vector field on the relative boundary
\partial\big(\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)\big)
within the submanifold
\Gamma(t)
. As we consider a thermodynamically closed system, there is no exchange of energy over the boundary
\Gamma(t)
and thus, the domain of the first integral in the second line is just
\partial V(t)\cap\Omega(t)
instead of
\partial V(t)
. Applying Gauß’s divergence theorem for not necessarily tangential vector fields (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Proposition 2.5.1]
) on both integrals in the second line, we reformulate (
3.43
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left[\int_{V(t)}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\right]\\
&\qquad+\int_{V(t)}\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{e}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\,.\end{aligned}

(3.44)

Here,
H=-\frac{1}{d}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{n}}
is the mean curvature of
\Gamma(t)
, and we point out that
{\mathbf{n}}_{\partial V(t)}={\mathbf{n}}(t)
on
\Gamma(t)
.
Applying the Reynolds transport theorem for evolving domains (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Theorem 2.11.1]
), we deduce the identity

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{V(t)}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}=\int_{V(t)}\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\,\big(e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\mathbf{u}}\big)\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}.

(3.45)

Similarly, recalling that
\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}=0
(see (
3.27
)), we use the transport theorem for orientable evolving hypersurfaces (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Theorem 2.10.1]
) to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}=\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}\partial_{t}^{\circ}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}.\end{split}

(3.46)

Now, by means of (
3.45
) and (
3.46
), (
3.44
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\int_{V(t)}\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\,\big(e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\mathbf{u}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{e}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}\\
&\quad+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}\partial_{t}^{\circ}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}\\
&\quad+\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}.\end{aligned}

(3.47)

As (
3.47
) holds in particular for all test volumes
V(t)\subset\Omega(t)
with
\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)=\emptyset
, we infer the following local energy dissipation law in the bulk:

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}\coloneqq\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\,\big(e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\mathbf{u}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{e}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.48)

Let now
c>0
be arbitrary and let us consider a generic test volume
V(t)\subset\Omega(t)
with
\left|V(t)\right|
being sufficiently small such that

\displaystyle\int_{V(t)}\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}\;\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{x}}<c.

Invoking (
3.48
), we infer from (
3.47
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial V(t)\cap\Gamma(t)}\partial_{t}^{\circ}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}<c.

As
c>0
and the test volume
V(t)
were arbitrary (except for the above smallness assumption), we conclude the following local energy dissipation law on the boundary:

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}\coloneqq\partial_{t}^{\circ}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

(3.49)

3.3  Completion of the model derivation by the Lagrange multiplier approach

To complete the model derivation, we employ the
Lagrange multiplier approach
(see
[
64
]
). Therefore, we introduce the Lagrange multipliers
\mu
,
\theta
and
q
that need to be appropriately adjusted in the following approach.
In the final model,
\mu
will represent the
bulk chemical potential
,
\theta
will represent the
surface chemical potential
and
q
will represent the
surface pressure
.
Invoking the identities (
3.31
) and (
3.33
), the local energy dissipation laws (
3.48
) and (
3.49
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi)+\textnormal{div}\,\big(e_{\Omega}({\mathbf{u}},\phi,\nabla\phi){\mathbf{u}}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{e}

\displaystyle\quad-\mu\big(\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}\,(\phi{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\big)-p\,\textnormal{div}\,{\mathbf{u}}

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.50)

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{\circ}e_{\Gamma}(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle\quad-\theta\big(\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}-\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\big)-q\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}

on
S_{T}
.

(3.51)

In the following, we will simply write
\rho
,
f
and
g
instead of
\rho(\phi)
,
f(\phi,\nabla\phi)
and
g(\phi,\psi,\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)
to provide a cleaner presentation.
By the definition of the energy densities
e_{\Omega}
and
e_{\Gamma}
(see (
3.39
) and (
3.41
)), we reformulate (
3.3
) and (
3.3
) as

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}\big)+\textnormal{div}\,\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\left|{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}{\mathbf{u}}\big)+\partial_{t}f+\textnormal{div}\,(f{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{e}

\displaystyle\quad-\mu\big(\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}\,(\phi{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,\,\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\big)+p\,\textnormal{div}\,{\mathbf{u}}

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.52)

\displaystyle 0\geq-\mathcal{D}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{\circ}g+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle\quad-\theta\big(\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}-\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\big)+q\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}

on
S_{T}
.

(3.53)

We now assume that
\psi
is suitably extended onto a neighborhood of
S_{T}
.
By means of the chain rule, the derivatives

\partial_{t}f
and
\partial_{t}^{\circ}g

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}f\,\partial_{t}\phi+\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\,\partial_{t}\nabla\phi

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.54)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\circ}g

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}g\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\phi+\partial_{\psi}g\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\partial_{t}^{\circ}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi

on
S_{T}
.

(3.55)

3.3.1
Computations in the bulk

In the bulk, we proceed exactly as in
[
5
]
and
[
50
]
to reformulate inequality (
3.3
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\textnormal{div}\,\big[\mathbf{J}_{e}-\tfrac{1}{2}\left|{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{T}^{T}{\mathbf{u}}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}+\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\phi\big]\\
&\quad+\big[\partial_{\phi}f-\textnormal{div}\,(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f)-\mu\big]\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\phi+\nabla\mu\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\\
&\quad-\big[\mathbf{T}+p\mathbf{I}+\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\big]:\nabla{\mathbf{u}}&&\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}\end{aligned}

(3.56)

To ensure (
3.56
), we now choose the chemical potential
\mu
and the energy flux
\mathbf{J}_{e}
as

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\partial_{\phi}f-\textnormal{div}\,\big(\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\big)

in
Q_{T}
,

(3.57)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{T}^{T}{\mathbf{u}}+\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}-\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\partial_{t}^{\bullet}\phi

in
Q_{T}
.

(3.58)

Due to these choices, the first two lines of the right-hand side of (
3.56
) vanish.
We further assume the mass flux
\mathbf{J}_{\phi}
to follow Fick’s law. This means that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\phi}=-m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.59)

where
m_{\Omega}=m_{\Omega}(\phi)
is a nonnegative function representing the mobility in the bulk.
Hence, (
3.56
) reduces to

\displaystyle 0\geq-\big[\mathbf{T}+p\mathbf{I}+\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\big]:\nabla{\mathbf{u}}-m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.60)

We next assume that the stress tensor
\mathbf{T}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{S}-p\mathbf{I}-\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

where the scalar variable
p
denotes the pressure,
\mathbf{I}
stands for the identity matrix, and
\mathbf{S}
is the
viscous stress tensor
that corresponds to irreversible changes of the energy due to friction. The term
\nabla\phi\otimes\partial_{\nabla\phi}f
can be interpreted as a Korteweg force. For Newtonian fluids,
\mathbf{S}
is usually assumed to be given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=2\nu(\phi)\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}},

(3.61)

where the nonnegative function
\nu=\nu(\phi)
represents the viscosity of the fluids, and
\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}
denotes the symmetric gradient of
{\mathbf{u}}
. This choice along with (
3.57
), (
3.58
) and the identity
p\mathbf{I}:\nabla{\mathbf{u}}=p\,\textnormal{div}\,\,{\mathbf{u}}=0
in
Q_{T}
ensures that (
3.56
) is fulfilled as it reduces to

\displaystyle 0\geq-\nu(\phi)\left|\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}\right|^{2}-m_{\Omega}(\phi)\left|\nabla\mu\right|^{2}\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.62)

In particular, recalling (
3.40
), the chemical potential
\mu
given by (
3.57
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta\phi+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F^{\prime}(\phi)\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.63)

Moreover, the total stress tensor
\mathbf{T}
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{T}&=\mathbf{S}-p\mathbf{I}-\varepsilon\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\\
&=2\nu(\phi)\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}-p\mathbf{I}-\varepsilon\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}\end{aligned}

(3.64)

Plugging (
3.59
) into (
3.31
), we further obtain the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\textnormal{div}\,(\phi{\mathbf{u}})=\textnormal{div}\,\big(m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\big)\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.65)

Eventually, recalling (
3.24
) and (
3.64
), we use (
3.21
) to derive the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho{\mathbf{u}})+\textnormal{div}\,\big({\mathbf{u}}\otimes(\rho(\phi){\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{J})\big)-\textnormal{div}\,\big(2\nu(\phi)\mathbf{D}{\mathbf{u}}\big)+\nabla p=-\varepsilon\textnormal{div}\,(\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla\phi)\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$,}

(3.66)

where, according to (
3.36
) and (
3.59
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{2}-\tilde{\rho}_{1}}{2}m_{\Omega}(\phi)\nabla\mu\quad\text{in $Q_{T}$.}

(3.67)

3.3.2
Computations on the boundary

We now consider the local energy dissipation law (
3.3
) on the surface.
Recalling the formulas for
f
(see (
3.40
)) and
\mathbf{J}_{e}
(see (
3.58
)), we deduce from (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\partial_{\phi}g\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\phi+\partial_{\psi}g\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\partial_{t}^{\circ}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi+\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\,\mathbf{K}_{e}+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\\
&\quad-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}){\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}-\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}+\varepsilon\,\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\phi\\
&\quad-\theta\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{K}_{\psi}\theta)+\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}+\theta\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\\
&\quad-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(q{\mathbf{u}})+\nabla_{\Gamma}q\cdot{\mathbf{u}}\end{aligned}

on
S_{T}
.
Now, the most technical step is to reformulate the term
\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\partial_{t}^{\circ}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi
.
According to
[
17
, Lemma 38]
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}^{\circ}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi-\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi&=\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi-\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}\big[\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\big]\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\\
&=\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi-\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}\big[\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\big]\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\end{split}

(3.69)

on
S_{T}
. Here, the symmetric matrix
\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}
represents the orthogonal projection onto the tangent space of
\Gamma=\Gamma(t)
.
Invoking the definition of
g
(see (
3.42
)), we further deduce

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\Big\{\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi-\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}\big[\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\big]\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\Big\}\\
&\quad=-\delta\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot(\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi=-\delta\big(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\big):\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}\end{aligned}

(3.70)

on
S_{T}
. From these two identities, we thus infer

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\partial_{t}^{\circ}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\\
&\quad=\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\,\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\cdot\Big\{\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi-\mathbf{P}_{\Gamma}\big[\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}+(\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}})^{T}\big]\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\Big\}\\
&\quad=\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\,\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi-\delta\big(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\big):\nabla_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{u}}\\[4.30554pt]
&\quad=\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big[\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi-\delta(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\mathbf{u}}\big]-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\big)\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\delta\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\mathbf{u}}\end{aligned}

(3.71)

on
S_{T}
. We further notice that

\displaystyle\delta(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=\nabla_{\Gamma}\Big(\frac{\delta}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\right|^{2}\Big)\cdot{\mathbf{n}}+\Delta_{\Gamma}\psi\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0

(3.72)

on
S_{T}
.
Using these equations, inequality (
3.68
) can alternatively be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big[\mathbf{K}_{e}-\theta\mathbf{K}_{\psi}+\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi-\delta(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\mathbf{u}}-q{\mathbf{u}}\big]\\
&\quad+\big[\partial_{\phi}g+\varepsilon\nabla\phi\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\big]\partial_{t}^{\circ}\phi+\big[\partial_{\psi}g-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\big)-\theta\big]\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi\\
&\quad+\big[\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}+\nabla_{\Gamma}q-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}){\mathbf{u}}+\delta\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\big]_{\tau}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\\
&\quad+\big[\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})\big]({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})\\
&\quad+H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}+\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\,\mathbf{K}_{\psi}+\theta\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\end{aligned}

(3.73)

on
S_{T}
.
In order to ensure that the inequality (
3.73
) is fulfilled, we choose the mass flux
\mathbf{K}_{\psi}
, and the energy flux
\mathbf{K}_{e}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=-m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta

on
S_{T}
,

(3.74)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{e}

\displaystyle=\theta\mathbf{K}_{\psi}-\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\,\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi+\delta(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi){\mathbf{u}}+q{\mathbf{u}}

on
S_{T}
.

(3.75)

In (
3.74
),
m_{\Gamma}=m_{\Gamma}(\psi)
is a nonnegative function representing the mobility of the materials on the surface.
The choice of
\mathbf{K}_{e}
entails that the first line of the right-hand side in (
3.73
) vanishes. Except for

, all terms on the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle H\mathbf{K}_{e}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=Hq({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})\quad\text{on $S_{T}$.}

Due to (
3.74
)–(
3.76

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq\big[\partial_{\phi}g+\varepsilon\nabla\phi\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\big]\partial_{t}^{\circ}\phi+\big[\partial_{\psi}g-\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi}g\big)-\theta\big]\partial_{t}^{\circ}\psi\\
&\quad+\big[\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}+\nabla_{\Gamma}q-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}){\mathbf{u}}+\delta\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\big]_{\tau}\cdot{\mathbf{u}}_{\tau}\\
&\quad+\big[\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})+Hq\big]({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})\\
&\quad-m_{\Gamma}(\psi)\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta\right|^{2}+\theta\mathbf{J}_{\psi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\phi}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\end{aligned}

(3.77)

on
S_{T}
. We now assume that the flux terms
\mathbf{J}_{\phi}
and
\mathbf{J}_{\psi}

(3.78)

Here, the second equality follows from (

We further make the constitutive assumptions

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}+\nabla_{\Gamma}q-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}){\mathbf{u}}+\delta\,\textnormal{div}_{\Gamma}(\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\otimes\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi)\big]_{\tau}

(3.79)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}{\mathbf{n}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})({\mathbf{u}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}})+Hq
