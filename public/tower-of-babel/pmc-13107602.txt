Analysis of risk factors and development of a predictive model for new vertebral fractures subsequent to percutaneous kyphoplasty in patients with single-segment osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures
Authors: Fumai Zhang, Jiayi Chen, Guang Li, Bin Wu, Wentao Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the risk factors for new vertebral compression fractures (NVCFs) after percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) and to construct a postoperative risk stratification nomogram for predicting refracture probability and identifying high-risk patients who require intensive postoperative monitoring and aggressive preventive interventions.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study enrolled 257 patients with single-segment OVCFs treated at Hainan General Hospital from January 2021 to December 2023. Participants were stratified into refracture (n = 56) and non-refracture (n = 201) groups based on new vertebral fracture occurrence within 1-year post-PKP. Data were randomly partitioned into training (n = 180) and validation (n = 77) sets at a 7:3 ratio. Independent risk factors were identified through univariate screening followed by multivariate logistic regression. A refracture risk nomogram was constructed using significant multivariate predictors, with comprehensive validation of predictive utility through tripartite assessment: receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsUnivariate analysis revealed significant between-group differences in sex, bone mineral density (BMD), vertebral height recovery rate, fracture severity, intradiscal cement leakage, anti-osteoporosis treatment, early postoperative mobilization, and history of postoperative falls (all P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified moderate fractures [OR = 7.08, 95%CI (1.39-54.00), P = 0.029], severe fractures [OR = 8.60, 95%CI (2.03–60.20), P = 0.009], intradiscal cement leakage [OR = 10.40, 95%CI (2.55–51.30), P = 0.002], and postoperative falls [OR = 4.99, 95%CI (1.75–15.30), P = 0.003] as independent risk factors positively associated with refracture. Conversely, higher BMD [OR = 0.61, 95%CI (0.40–0.91), P = 0.016], anti-osteoporosis treatment [OR = 0.24, 95%CI (0.08–0.63), P = 0.005], and early mobilization [OR = 0.28, 95%CI (0.09–0.77), P = 0.017] demonstrated protective effects. The nomogram maintained robust discrimination across cohorts: training set AUC = 0.892 (95%CI:0.832–0.952) with 78.60% sensitivity and 89.90% specificity at 0.355 cut-off; testing set AUC = 0.836 (95%CI:0.691–0.982) with 78.60% sensitivity and 85.70% specificity at 0.269 cut-off. Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated clinical utility with positive net benefits at 0%-76% (training) and 0%-82% (testing) risk thresholds.ConclusionsLow BMD, moderate-severe fracture severity, bone cement intradiscal leakage, inadequate anti-osteoporosis treatment, delayed postoperative mobilization, and falls are predominant risk factors for NVCFs after PKP. A validated nomogram prediction model was developed based on these six established risk factors.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.

Introduction

The prevalence of osteoporosis is rising in tandem with the global demographic shift toward an aging population. Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) typically present with persistent low back pain, vertebral kyphosis, and a significant decline in quality of life [1]. While conservative treatment may alleviate symptoms in early-stage patients, complications from prolonged immobilization often limit non-surgical management. In 1984, Galibert et al. first performed percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) for vertebral hemangiomas [2]. Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP), developed by Mark Reiley in 1999, corrects kyphotic deformities and promotes rapid recovery with minimal invasiveness [3]. However, with the widespread clinical adoption of this procedure, complications such as cement leakage and NVCFs in adjacent segments have gradually emerged [4, 5]. Research indicates that the incidence of NVCFs following PKP ranges from 5% to 50%, which not only exacerbates the necessity for reoperation but also imposes sustained impairment on patients’ quality of life, representing a critical challenge to long-term therapeutic efficacy [6–8]. The development of NVCFs following PKP is a complex process driven by the synergistic interplay of multidimensional factors, with established risks encompassing advanced age, diminished bone mineral density, and cement leakage [9–11]. Nomograms have emerged as robust graphical tools for quantifying clinical risk by integrating multiple predictors, thereby providing a valuable reference for individualized risk assessment and clinical decision-making [11, 12]. Consequently, this study aimed to identify comprehensive risk factors—encompassing preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative variables—to construct a postoperative risk stratification nomogram for predicting the probability of NVCFs.

Materials and methods

A total of 257 OVCFs patients treated with PKP at Hainan General Hospital from January 2021 to December 2023 were enrolled. All patients completed 1-year follow-up with documented informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Complete perioperative clinical and imaging data; (2) Preoperative laboratory results; (3) Standardized postoperative follow-up records; (4) MRI-confirmed acute single-segment OVCFs; (5) Severe mobility impairment due to pain; (6) Spontaneous or low-energy traumatic fracture; (7) No active infection within 15 cm of puncture site; (8) No severe cardiopulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction, nor coagulation abnormalities.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Multiple-segment fractures (≥ 2 vertebrae); (2) Pathological fractures (e.g., malignancy, metastasis, or hemangioma-related); (3) Severe spinal degeneration; (4) Malignancy history; (5) Severe cognitive/psychiatric disorders; (6) Neurological deficits (spinal cord compression/progressive symptoms); (7) Systemic infection or surgical contraindications.

Surgical technique

A consistent operative team conducted all procedures. Patients were placed in the prone position and received local anesthesia. Target vertebrae were radiographically localized, and 4-mm incisions created at designated entry points. Transpedicular needles were advanced under fluoroscopy at sagittal angles of 15–20° toward the vertebral anterior third of the vertebral body. Contrast-inflated balloons restored height, followed by viscosity-controlled Polymethylmethacrylate(PMMA)bone cement injection under real-time imaging to verify filling and detect leakage. The bilateral approach required contralateral replication [13].

Clinical follow-up

Postoperatively, patients began early mobilization while wearing a thoracolumbar brace and performed guided lower limb strengthening exercises during bed rest to prevent deep vein thrombosis. Anteroposterior and lateral spinal radiographs were obtained 24 h after surgery, with most patients discharged within 2–3 days. Unless contraindicated, standardized anti-osteoporosis therapy was administered, consisting of oral calcium carbonate-D3 (600 mg once daily) and oral calcitriol (0.25 µg daily) for 3 months, supplemented by an annual intravenous infusion of zoledronic acid (5 mg) for 2–3 years. Patients attended scheduled follow-ups at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively where clinical symptoms and signs were documented; structured telephone follow-ups were conducted for appointment non-attendees. Any report of acute back pain prompted immediate spinal MRI to detect new vertebral fractures. Confirmation of new vertebral compression fractures required either: (1) In-house MRI demonstrating fresh vertebral compression changes, or (2) External MRI reports definitively diagnosing new vertebral fractures, with validation through structured telephone verification documenting the treating hospital, examination date, and medical record number. Adherence to anti-osteoporosis treatment was verified through prescription records and medication reconciliation at each visit.

Observation indicators

The following information was recorded preoperatively. (1) Patient-related factors: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), bone mineral density (BMD), smoking history, drinking history, prior fracture history, diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, HBV. (2) Intraoperative factors: Operation approach, fractured vertebral segment, preoperative Cobb angle, preoperative vertebral height, fracture type, fracture severity, bone cement morphology, cement volume, cement distribution pattern, cement-endplate contact, injury-to-surgery interval, surgical time. (3) Postoperative factors: blood calcium, blood phosphorus, postoperative Cobb angle, vertebral height alteration, height recovery rate, cement leakage, anti-osteoporosis treatment, postoperative physical inactivity, early postoperative mobilization, history of postoperative falls.

Variable definitions

History of fractures: all clinically documented or radiographically verified fractures occurring between follow-up initiation and new index vertebral fracture diagnosis - including non-index vertebral fractures (symptomatic or asymptomatic), non-vertebral fractures, and MRI-confirmed chronic vertebral fractures.

Cement distribution criteria: on anteroposterior radiographs, bone cement confined to one side of the vertebral sagittal midline defined unilateral distribution; cement crossing the midline defined bilateral distribution.

Normal vertebral height H0= (previous normal vertebral height H1+next normal vertebral height H2)/2.

Vertebral height recovery rate: (postoperative anterior height - preoperative anterior height)/H0* 100%.

Cobb angle: angle formed between the superior endplate and inferior endplate of the fractured vertebra.

Cement leakage: Based on anatomical pathways, Yeom categorized bone cement leakage into Type B (basivertebral vein), Type S (segmental vein), and Type C (cortical defect), with the latter further subdivided into intradiscal and paravertebral types [14]. In the present study, leakage was classified as absent, intradiscal, or paravertebral using standardized postoperative anteroposterior and lateral digital radiography (DR). To ensure rigor, all images were independently assessed by two blinded senior spinal surgeons. Disagreements were arbitrated by a third senior specialist to maintain high inter-observer reliability and data consistency.

Anti-osteoporosis treatment: It was defined as an overall adherence of ≥ 80% to the prescribed multimodal combination therapy protocol. The standardized intervention required both an annual intravenous infusion of zoledronic acid and a 3-month foundational course of oral calcium carbonate-D3 combined with calcitriol. Patients were classified as not having received anti-osteoporosis treatment if they experienced cumulative medication interruptions or omissions ≥ 20% of the total duration (e.g., ≥ 18 days of interruption during the 3-month oral course) or if they received the intravenous infusion without maintaining the oral therapy. Adherence to zoledronic acid infusions was objectively verified through physician orders and administration records within the Electronic Medical Record (EMR) system. For outpatient oral medications, the research team utilized the EMR to extract prescription refill records to calculate the Medication Possession Ratio (MPR). Furthermore, structured questionnaires were administered during scheduled follow-ups at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively to meticulously trace actual medication days, discontinuation milestones, and reasons for interruption.

Early postoperative mobilization: It was defined as a graduated functional rehabilitation program initiated under medical supervision within 24–48 h postoperatively. The protocol encompassed a progression from in-bed limb stretching to bedside sitting for 5–10 min, followed by walking exercises of 5–15 min performed 2–3 times daily under the protection of a thoracolumbar brace and physical assistance, ultimately achieving a stepwise restoration from standing reconstruction to independence in activities of daily living.

Postoperative falls : Such incidents were defined as sudden events occurring within the one-year follow-up period, in which an accidental loss of balance resulted in the patient coming to rest on the ground, floor, or a lower level. To ensure the integrity of data collection, a dual-source verification mechanism was implemented. Primary data were extracted from standardized medical records during scheduled outpatient follow-ups at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, where fall history was routinely screened and documented by physicians. For cases with missing outpatient data, trained researchers conducted structured telephone interviews with patients or their co-residing family members to retrospectively trace key clinical details, including the timing and circumstances of the fall, as well as any associated acute back pain or emergency medical consultations.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses incorporated univariate regression methods. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared via independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests; categorical variables were expressed as ratios and analyzed with chi-square tests, applying Fisher’s exact test when expected frequencies were < 5 or total sample size was < 40. Variables achieving statistical significance (P < 0.05) in multivariate regression analysis were incorporated into a nomogram prediction model constructed using the “rms” package in R-Studio software. Calibration curves were generated through 1000-bootstrap resampling to assess prediction-to-observation concordance. Model performance was validated via tenfold cross-validation. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, with area under the curve (AUC) quantifying discriminative capability. Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated net benefit rates across clinical decision thresholds. Identical validation protocols were implemented in the testing set to systematically evaluate external validity and clinical applicability.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, Chicago, USA) and R-Studio (version 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), employing R packages including rms, regplot, ggDCA, ggplot2, and pROC for nomogram development, calibration plotting, decision curve generation, and ROC analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Basic patient information

The study included clinical data from 257 patients, comprising 31 males and 226 females. Among them, 56 patients developed new fractures postoperatively while 201 patients had no new fractures during follow-up.

Univariate and multivariate analyses

Univariate analysis of patient-related factors revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in gender and BMD (Table 1). Analysis of intraoperative factors demonstrated a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) in fracture severity between the two groups (Table 2). For postoperative factors, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in vertebral height recovery rate, cement leakage, anti-osteoporosis treatment, early postoperative mobilization, and history of postoperative falls (Table 3). Multivariate analysis of NVCFs after PKP

Establishment and validation of the nomogram prediction model

Following the identification of statistically significant predictors via univariate logistic regression (P < 0.05) in the training cohort, a multivariate model was developed. The multivariate analysis identified BMD, fracture severity (moderate/severe), intradiscal cement leakage, anti-osteoporosis treatment, early postoperative mobilization, and a history of postoperative falls as independent risk factors for NVCFs (all P < 0.05), which were subsequently utilized to construct the nomogram (Fig. 1). The model exhibited robust discriminative performance: in the training cohort, the AUC reached 0.892 (95% CI: 0.832–0.952), with 78.60% sensitivity and 89.90% specificity at an optimal threshold of 0.355 (Fig. 2a). Correspondingly, the testing cohort yielded an AUC of 0.836 (95% CI: 0.691–0.982), with a sensitivity of 78.60% and a specificity of 85.70% at a cutoff of 0.269 (Fig. 3a). Calibration was rigorously assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (training P = 0.40; testing P = 0.25) and bootstrap-corrected calibration curves (n = 1000), which demonstrated excellent goodness-of-fit and high concordance between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Figs. 2b and 3b). DCA further established clinical utility, showing positive net benefit within risk thresholds of 0%-76% (training) and 0%-82% (testing), outperforming extreme-scenario curves across clinically relevant ranges (Figs. 2c and 3c).

Discussion

PKP has been increasingly utilized in clinical practice as a minimally invasive technique. Previous studies indicate that the incidence of NVCFs following PKP ranges from 5% to 50% [5, 6]. NVCFs remain one of the most common complications following surgery for OVCFs, posing significant clinical challenges [9]. The nomogram, a visual predictive tool based on statistical regression models, quantifies the impact of multiple variables on event probability and has been widely adopted across various medical disciplines. Multivariate analysis identified BMD, moderate/severe fracture severity, bone cement intradiscal leakage, anti-osteoporosis treatment, early postoperative mobilization, and postoperative falls as independent risk factors. Consequently, a nomogram integrating these six clinically accessible independent predictors was established as a postoperative risk stratification tool, enabling the precise and individualized quantification of refracture risk. Internal validation demonstrated favorable discriminative ability (AUC: training set 0.892; testing set 0.836) and calibration accuracy, supporting its clinical applicability.

BMD measurement, the gold standard for diagnosing osteoporosis, quantitatively reflects the state of bone microstructure through mineral content assessment [10]. Significant BMD reduction leads to cortical thinning and trabecular discontinuity, resulting in the systemic deterioration of skeletal biomechanical properties. This structural degradation not only compromises vertebral load-bearing capacity but also creates postoperative biomechanical stress concentrations, potentially triggering a cascade of adjacent vertebral fractures [11]. Current research consensus confirms a positive correlation between reduced BMD and postoperative refracture risk, mediated via three core mechanisms: First, altered spinal stress distribution following cement injection concentrates mechanical loading onto osteoporotic vertebrae. Second, progressive bone loss chronically impairs bone remodeling capacity, accelerating microdamage accumulation within vertebral endplates. Third, disrupted bone metabolic homeostasis increases susceptibility to biomechanical failure at the disc-vertebral interface [11, 12].Consequently, for patients with significantly reduced BMD, a multidimensional approach extending beyond conventional pharmacotherapy is essential. This includes: (1) implementing personalized surveillance protocols with close monitoring of bone turnover markers; (2) prescribing mechanoadaptive exercise regimens to enhance bone structural competence; and (3) optimizing cement injection parameters to balance vertebral reinforcement with physiological stress distribution [15, 16]. Such interventions targeting the pathological bone microstructure represent critical strategies for interrupting the chain reaction of refractures.

The progression of osteoporosis has been established as a significant contributor to the risk of NVCFs following surgery, rendering anti-osteoporosis therapy imperative [17]. This intervention primarily functions to decelerate or halt the pathological trajectory of osteoporosis, thereby reducing bone fragility and fundamentally mitigating the incidence of subsequent fractures [18]. Bawa et al. [19] conducted a 3-year prospective study demonstrating that systematic anti-osteoporotic therapy significantly reduces the incidence of recurrent vertebral fractures in patients following vertebral augmentation procedures. Multivariate regression analysis in the present study further confirms that postoperative failure to receive efficacious anti-osteoporosis therapy constitutes a significant and independent risk factor for NVCFs following PKP. Collectively, current evidence supports the integration of standardized anti-osteoporosis therapy into postoperative management protocols for patients with OVCFs undergoing PKP [20]. Such therapeutic adherence aims to enhance skeletal integrity and reduce long-term fracture recurrence risks, thereby establishing this approach as an integral component of comprehensive care paradigms.

Genant’s semi-quantitative grading system classifies vertebral compression fractures as mild (20%-25% height loss), moderate (26%-40%), or severe (> 40%), providing an objective framework for risk stratification [21]. Moderate and severe fractures critically disrupt spinal biomechanical equilibrium, inducing aberrant focal stress concentrations. Such structural compromise not only increases the load on adjacent segments but also accelerates bone loss, establishing a cyclical pattern of escalating fracture risk [20]. Severe fractures pose particularly significant hazards. Primarily, extensive cortical disruption creates structural weaknesses through which bone cement readily extravasates into paravertebral or disc spaces. This aberrant cement distribution provokes abnormal stress transmission, subjecting adjacent vertebrae to excessive loading that predisposes to new fractures [22]. Secondarily, severe vertebral collapse often generates intraosseous fissures, resulting in inhomogeneous cement dispersion with aggregate formation. Poor integration between cement clusters and surrounding bone generates interfacial stress risers that may precipitate surgical-level vertebral refracture even under routine physiological loading [23]. Our analysis substantiates this correlation: patients with moderate-to-severe fractures demonstrated significantly elevated postoperative refracture incidence compared to those with mild fractures. Consequently, clinical management necessitates a tripartite approach integrating precise preoperative assessment, intraoperative technical modifications, and postoperative comprehensive management to mitigate long-term refracture rates by interrupting this biomechanical deterioration cascade.

Intradiscal cement leakage constitutes a significant risk factor for NVCFs following vertebral augmentation. Such leakage primarily occurs under two circumstances: (1) when severe vertebral compression compromises the integrity of the superior or inferior endplates, and (2) due to suboptimal surgical needle placement during the procedure [24, 25]. Cement migration into the disc space substantially alters load distribution within adjacent spinal segments [26]. Normally, intact discs buffer and dissipate mechanical stresses transmitted from augmented vertebrae. However, intradiscal cement simultaneously disrupts disc functionality: primarily by inducing localized stress concentration at the adjacent vertebral endplates, and alternatively by eliminating the segment’s inherent stress-dampening capacity [27–29]. This dual mechanism generates pathologically amplified focal stresses that predispose to new compression fractures at contiguous levels [29]. Multivariate analysis in the current investigation confirms that intradiscal cement leakage independently and significantly elevates postoperative fracture risk. Consequently, preventive measures against such leakage are paramount in clinical practice. This necessitates a meticulous preoperative assessment of endplate integrity in severely compressed vertebrae to guide potential technique modifications, alongside strict adherence to operative protocols that ensure a precise needle trajectory to minimize iatrogenic leakage.

Implementation of early postoperative mobilization protocols is a critical strategy for reducing the incidence of new fractures in patients with OVCFs following vertebral augmentation [30]. Prolonged bed rest must be rigorously avoided due to its multifaceted detrimental consequences: the absence of mechanical loading triggers osteoclastic activation, accelerating bone resorption while suppressing formation, thereby exacerbating bone mineral density loss and compromising spinal load-bearing capacity [31]; concomitant paravertebral muscular atrophy aggravates spinal imbalance and concentrates stress on adjacent vertebrae, potentiating sequential fracture cascades [32]; additionally, cardiorespiratory deconditioning and impaired balance regulation ultimately predispose patients to fall-related injuries [33]. Clinical evidence suggests that the initiation of progressive weight-bearing activities and the implementation of standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocols within 24–48 h post-surgery not only significantly mitigate refracture incidence in elderly patients with multiple comorbidities but also demonstrate a profound synergistic effect when integrated with pharmacological anti-osteoporosis management [30, 33]. The protective mechanisms of early mobilization derive from multidimensional synergies: biomechanically, controlled loading avoids excessive stress on uncured cement while facilitating bone-cement interfacial integration; metabolically, mechanical stimuli promote osteoblastic activation while suppressing bone resorption, attenuating bone mass deterioration; structurally, core muscle activation protocols enhance dynamic spinal stabilization and redistribute aberrant intervertebral forces; and functionally, gait and balance training directly mitigate fall propensity [31, 34]. Crucially, while exercise interventions alone yield modest improvements in severe osteoporosis cases, their integration with standardized pharmacological regimens synergistically modulates bone metabolic homeostasis and biomechanical competence [35].

Postoperative falls serve as a pivotal predictor of NVCFs following vertebroplasty, possessing dual clinical significance as both a proximal mechanical trigger for fracture and a systemic hallmark of frailty [36, 37]. Large-scale cohort studies have confirmed that the predictive value of a fall history for refracture remains significant independently of BMD, suggesting that falls reflect an underlying state of frailty characterized by sarcopenia, impaired neuromuscular coordination, or diminished balance [38]. Rather than being isolated injurious events, the frequency and severity of falls are intrinsically linked to the patient’s systemic frailty, reflecting a highly synergistic pathogenic mechanism [39]. The instantaneous dynamic overload generated during a fall frequently exceeds the physiological load thresholds of the vertebrae, which not only compromises the stability of the bone-cement interface but also precipitates microstructural damage in adjacent vertebrae [36]. Clinical evidence indicates that the risk of refracture is substantially elevated during the immediate post-fall period, primarily due to direct mechanical insult and the suppression of bone formation induced by traumatic stress [40, 41]. For high-risk populations—including the elderly, those with limited mobility, or those using neurological medications—a stratified management approach is essential: (1) early postoperative anti-resorptive therapy to mitigate fracture susceptibility; (2) customized balance rehabilitation programs to enhance muscular competence and neuromuscular coordination; and (3) home environmental modifications to eliminate hazards [42]. All patients should be integrated into a systematic fall prevention framework, encompassing regular postoperative risk assessments, bone turnover monitoring, restriction of psychotropic medications associated with increased fall risk, protein supplementation to maintain musculoskeletal integrity, and continuous optimization of environmental safety [43]. This integrated strategy is fundamental to disrupting the pathological cascade of frailty, falls, and subsequent fractures, thereby optimizing long-term patient outcomes.

The predictive model developed in this study possesses substantial clinical utility, characterized by a balanced integration of methodological rigor and practical feasibility. By utilizing comprehensive clinical datasets and visualizing risk assessments through an intuitive nomogram, the model allows clinicians to seamlessly integrate routine parameters for personalized analysis. Given its reliance on key dynamic variables captured during the perioperative and postoperative periods, this instrument should be explicitly defined as a postoperative risk stratification tool rather than a strictly preoperative decision-making model. Its primary value lies in assisting medical teams in the precise identification of high-risk individuals following discharge, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation for implementing tiered postoperative management strategies.

Despite its clinical significance, several inherent limitations of this risk stratification model must be acknowledged. First, because variables such as intradiscal cement leakage, anti-osteoporosis treatment, postoperative falls, and early mobilization are inherently postoperative occurrences, their inclusion precludes the model’s application in purely preoperative settings and direct surgical planning. Second, while the use of routine radiography to evaluate cement leakage aligns with the cost-effectiveness and real-world scenarios of primary healthcare, its spatial resolution is inferior to thin-slice CT. This may lead to the underdiagnosis of minor cement extrusions, potentially underestimating the predictive weight of the leakage variable. Furthermore, the retrospective collection of fall data is susceptible to recall bias, as patients may overlook minor incidents that did not cause significant pain or prompt medical consultation, potentially distorting the recorded incidence of falls. Similarly, although medication adherence was assessed using objective pharmacy refill records cross-validated with structured interviews, social desirability bias may lead patients to overestimate their compliance, affecting the statistical weight of this factor. Finally, because the model was validated using data from a single medical center, its external validity and predictive stability across diverse healthcare systems and geographic regions require further verification.

To address these limitations, future research will focus on three strategic directions. We aim to develop purely preoperative predictive models based solely on baseline data to serve as a necessary supplement for optimizing initial surgical and treatment plans. Additionally, we plan to explore dynamic risk assessment strategies that integrate real-time perioperative data with longitudinal follow-up, establishing a comprehensive monitoring system from baseline through long-term management. Lastly, multicenter prospective cohorts will be established to systematically validate the model’s predictive stability in varied clinical environments. By refining the model’s applicability across both temporal and spatial dimensions, we aspire to construct a precision clinical decision support system that encompasses the entire continuum of orthopedic care and provides a robust, evidence-based framework for the risk management of vertebral fractures.

Conclusion

Low BMD, moderate-severe fracture severity, bone cement intradiscal leakage, inadequate anti-osteoporosis treatment, delayed postoperative mobilization, and postoperative falls are predominant risk factors for NVCFs after kyphoplasty. A validated nomogram prediction model was developed based on these six established risk factors.
