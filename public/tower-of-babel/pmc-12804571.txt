Tobacco smoking is associated with solid growth pattern in primary lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Alissa Blessing, Agnes Csanadi, Claudia Kayser, Felicitas Oberndorfer, Gian Kayser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral studies have explored whether tobacco consumption is associated with the histologic growth patterns of pulmonary adenocarcinomas, and whether these patterns influence prognosis. However, findings have remained ambiguous. This study aimed at further exploring these associations in a larger cohort, enhancing precision compared to previous studies by assessing growth patterns on digitalized slides.MethodsResection specimens from 196 patients with pulmonary adenocarcinoma were retrospectively assessed and categorized. Where available, up to three tumour bearing H&E stained slides were digitized. Growth patterns were quantitatively assessed and categorized based on these digital images. Clinical and demographic data, including smoking history, were obtained from patient admission records.ResultsSmoking, particularly heavy smoking (> 30 pack-years), was significantly associated with solid growth, while non- or light smokers were more likely to display an acinar predominance. These two patterns were by far the most prevalent, together accounting for 84.3% of all cases. Median overall survival (OS) for the entire cohort was 47 months. Solid predominant tumours were associated with shorter OS (25 versus 57 months compared to all other predominant patterns, p = 0.042) while lepidic tumours were associated with longer OS (112 versus 46 months compared to all other predominant patterns, p = 0.025). No statistically significant correlation with OS was observed for either acinar, cribriform, or micropapillary tumours.ConclusionThese findings support an association between smoking behaviour and histologic growth patterns, as well as between growth patterns and OS. The associations appear particularly robust for solid predominant tumour, highlighting their prognostic relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04187-3.Keywords: Lung cancer, Adenocarcinoma lung, Growth pattern, Smoking

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 1.81 million deaths in 2022, marking an upward tendency compared to 1.76 million lung cancer deaths in 2018 [1, 2]. This increase highlights the critical importance of continued research in this field. Although the incidence of lung carcinoma in never smokers is rising, and other risk factors such as air pollution are gaining significance, tobacco smoking continues to represent the preeminent risk factor [1].

A substantial body of research has focused on identifying morphological and genetic predictors of disease progression and survival. Several studies have reported significant correlations between the histological composition of pulmonary adenocarcinomas and overall survival (OS). Specifically, solid and micropapillary predominant tumours appear to be associated with shorter OS, whereas lepidic predominance is considered to be a positive prognostic factor [3]. The 5th edition of the WHO classification of Lung Tumours (2021) introduced cribriform growth as a subtype of the acinar pattern, although it is generally considered to be associated with an adverse prognosis [4]. Nonetheless, the prognostic implications of cribriform morphology, particularly in relation to OS and disease-free survival, remain a subject of ongoing debate.

Further uncertainty persists regarding the extent to which histomorphological features influence prognosis and the most accurate methods for their quantification. Recent studies suggest that not only the predominant growth pattern but also minor components of prognostically relevant patterns may significantly impact survival outcomes [5–7]. Previous studies often relied on estimations of proportional growth pattern distribution. In contrast, the present study aims to improve precision by quantitively analysing growth patterns using digitized slides.

The retrospective analysis of 196 lung cancer patients investigates the association between tobacco consumption and dedifferentiated growth patterns. Moreover, it examines whether smoking status independently predicts poor prognosis, irrespective of the predominant histological growth pattern.

Patients and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included 196 patients who underwent complete resection (R0) for invasive lung adenocarcinoma between 1998 and 2009 at the University Medical Centre Freiburg. Surgical procedures comprised lobectomy, (multi-)segmentectomy, and pneumonectomy, including extended resections were necessary. All resection specimens meeting inclusion criteria during the defined 10-year period were included, which was deemed a sufficient sample size. Patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy, as well as those diagnosed with metachronous or synchronous malignancies, were excluded. Additionally, cases of adenocarcinoma in situ or minimally invasive adenocarcinoma were not included. Clinical information was retrieved from the patient admission records. To distinguish between persons who smoke heavily and persons who smoke lightly (PWSH and PWSL, respectively), a cut-off of 30 pack-years was applied, corresponding to the median value for within the cohort. This threshold has also been proposed by Wong et al. [8]. Ethical approval for the use of human tissue was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the university of Freiburg. All procedures were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Histologic classification

We analysed the five commonly recognized histologic growth patterns-lepidic, papillary, micropapillary, solid (Fig. 1), and acinar-and additionally assessed whether cribriform growth had an independent impact on survival.

A predominantly acinar carcinoma (a) and predominantly solid carcinoma (b), 200x magnification. Glandular structures of highly atypical carcinoma cells are the typical appearance of acinar adenocarcinoma, whereas solid carcinomas merely form small to bigger conglomerates of tumour cells without forming a lumen

Histological assessment of tumour patterns was carried out in accordance with the 2021 World Health Organization Classification of Lung Tumours. Staging was performed using the 9th edition of the TNM classification for lung cancer [9].

All specimens were inflated and fixed in formalin, paraffin-embedded, and routinely stained with hematoxylin-eosin. To ensure precise assessment of tumour composition, up to three tumour-bearing slides per case were selected and retrospectively evaluated by a doctoral candidate under the direct supervision of a trained pathologist specialising in thoracic malignancies. Slides were subsequently digitized using a high-resolution digital slide scanner and analysed with Panoramic Viewer software. To ensure precise quantification, each slide was subdivided into smaller units, and the relative proportions of each growth pattern were assessed in relation to the total tumour area. Even minimal components were recorded, as no lower threshold was defined. Particular care was taken in differentiating between acinar and lepidic patterns, which can be morphologically similar. Architectural distortion of the alveoli, e.g., variations in size and shape, as well as marked fibrotic expansion of alveolar septa were used as distinguishing criteria.

To improve prognostic stratification of predominant growth patterns, we applied a three-tiered classification. Group 1 included lepidic predominant carcinomas, which are associated with favourable prognosis. Group 2 included the prognostically neutral papillary, acinar, and cribriform patterns, and group 3 comprised solid and micropapillary carcinomas, which are associated with the least favourable prognosis. Although cribriform growth has been associated with poorer outcomes in previous studies, it was not significantly linked to worse outcome in our cohort. It was therefore assigned to the intermediate-risk rather than high-risk group.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, the distribution of numerical data was tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Clinicopathological correlations were evaluated using Mann-Whitney U-test and chi-squared test. Differences in predominant growth pattern distribution by smoking status were evaluated using a chi-squared test. OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared with the log-rank test. For multivariance analysis, a Cox proportional hazards regression was performed, adjusting for pT/pN-status, nodule size, sex, and age. All Cox models were checked for fulfilment of the proportional hazards assumption using log-minus-log survival plots, and no significant violations were detected. To test for mediation of effects, two sequential Cox proportional hazard models were constructed. Model 1 included clinicopathological covariates and the grouped predominant growth pattern to estimate its independent association with OS. Model 2 added the interaction term between growth pattern groups and smoking status to assess whether the prognostic impact of morphology was moderated by smoking behaviour. An additional Cox regression analysis was performed including cumulative tobacco exposure (pack-years) and its interaction with growth pattern, to examine whether the extent of the morphological effect varied with increasing smoking intensity.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 21 (IBM Corp.). Results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The alpha level was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Patients and clinical characteristics

The median age of the 196 patients included in this cohort was 65 years (range: 31–83 years). Of these, 35.9% were female and 64.1% male. A total of 77.6% of patients were persons who smoke (PWS), with a higher proportion of male PWS compared to female PWS. Among the PWS, PWSH and PWSL were almost equally represented. The average number of pack-years was 30, with male patients showing a higher cumulative tobacco exposure than female patients. The distribution of the patients according to pT-stage is summarised in Table 1. Notably, PWS developed lung cancer at a significantly younger age than persons who don’t smoke (PWDS) (62 versus 66.9 years, p = 0.005, Mann-Whitney U Test). Furthermore, PWSH were diagnosed on average 3.5 years earlier than PWSL (61.5 vs. 64.8 years, p = 0.010, Mann-Whitney U Test). Table 2 shows relevant clinical and pathological data of the cohort.

Histopathological and clinicopathological characteristics

In terms of growth pattern predominance, acinar was by far the most frequent subtype, accounting for 50.5% of all tumours, followed by solid predominance in 34.2% of all cases. The third most common patterns were lepidic and cribriform, each occurring in 4.6% of cases, while micropapillary and papillary predominance were observed in 3.6% and 2.6% of tumours, respectively.

Subgroup analyses investigating associations between predominant growth patterns and sex, pT status, pN status, or age yielded no statistically significant results. Neither did specific analyses for solid or lepidic predominant carcinomas alone. However, lepidic predominant carcinomas were significantly less likely to exhibit lymph node metastasis (p = 0.007, chi-squared test).

PWS demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of solid predominant tumours compared to PWDS (p = 0.001, chi-squared test), whereas acinar predominance was more common in PWDS (p = 0.002, chi-squared test, Table 3). No significant associations were observed for the remaining patterns in relation to smoking status, although a non-significant trend suggested that PWS were less likely to present with lepidic predominance (p = 0.104, chi-squared test).

Survival

The median OS for the entire cohort across all pT-stages was 47 months, with a 5-year-survival probability of 43%. Women had a numerically longer median OS than men (59 vs. 46 months), though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.31, Kaplan-Meier, Suppl. Figure 2). Similarly, Conventional clinicopathological parameters, including pT status, pN status, and the number of metastatic lymph nodes, were significantly associated with reduced OS. For instance, patients with negative nodal status had a median survival of 77 months, compared to 19 months in those with nodal involvement (p < 0.001, Kaplan-Meier, Suppl. Figure 4).

Total effect of smoking on survival

PWDS showed a longer median survival compared to PWS (68 vs. 46 months), without reaching statistical significance (p = 0.19, 95 CI 0.86–1.96, Cox-Regression, Suppl. Figure 3). The absolute number of pack-years was not significantly associated with OS (p = 0.212, 95% CI 0.99–1.01, Cox-Regression). Neither was a stratification into PWSH and PWSL (p = 0.244, 95 CI 0.87–1.70, Cox-Regression).

Direct effects of smoking on survival

In the multivariate Cox regression model including smoking status, predominant growth pattern classified into three prognostic groups, and clinicopathological covariates, smoking was not associated with OS (p = 0.39, 95% CI 0.77–1.90).

Indirect effect of smoking on survival

In an additional Cox-regression model, both the grouped growth pattern classification (p = 0.001, HR = 1.783, CI 1.19–2.19) and its interaction with smoking status (p = 0.019, HR = 2.258, 95% CI 1.45–67.14) were significantly associated with OS. These findings indicate that the prognostic impact of smoking is modulated by histological growth pattern: the adverse effect of smoking was most pronounced in tumours with unfavourable morphology, whereas it was less evident in tumours with favourable growth patterns.

Moderated mediation effect of smoking on survival

To test whether the mediation effect of tumour growth pattern on the association of smoking status and OS was dependent on smoking intensity, a Cox regression model including the interaction term between grouped growth pattern and cumulative tobacco exposure (pack-years) was fitted. The interaction term was statistically significant (p = 0.023, HR = 1.017, 95% CI 1.00–1.03.00.03), indicating that the adverse prognostic impact of unfavourable growth patterns increased with higher smoking intensity. In other words, the strength of the mediation effect via tumour morphology was moderated by the cumulative exposure to tobacco smoke.

Mediation effects of smoking on survival

Taken together, these results suggest a partial mediation, in which the association between smoking and OS is at least partly explained by its influence on tumour growth pattern. Although the total effect of smoking on survival is not significant, the presence of significant indirect and interaction effects supports the existence of a morphologically mediated pathway.

With respect to histological growth patterns, solid predominant tumours were associated with significantly shorter OS compared to all other predominant patterns (25 versus 57 months, p = 0.042, Kaplan-Meier, Fig. 2). When stratifying by smoking behaviour, PWS with predominantly solid carcinomas had a significantly shorter median OS of 24 months compared to 53 months in PWS with any other predominant pattern (p = 0.02, Kaplan-Meier, Fig. 3). Lepidic predominant adenocarcinomas were associated with markedly better OS (112 vs. 46 months compared to all other predominant patterns, p = 0.025, Kaplan-Meier, Fig. 4). However, lepidic predominance was observed in only a small subset of cases. Micropapillary predominant tumours demonstrated a trend towards worse outcome, although this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.12, Kaplan-Meier, Suppl. Figure 5). Papillary predominant tumours showed no observable effect on OS. Due to low case numbers, subgroup analyses by smoking behaviour were not feasible for micropapillary or papillary carcinomas. No definitive conclusions could be drawn regarding the prognostic relevance of predominantly acinar or cribriform carcinomas. Consequently, cribriform tumours were grouped with acinar and papillary tumours as a prognostically neutral group.

Discussion

Tobacco smoking is a well-established contributor to the development of lung carcinoma through the upregulation of oncogenes and tumour-promoting transcription factors. For instance, phosphorylation of GSK-3β leads to stabilization of β-catenin, thereby promoting dysregulation of the cell cycle. Moreover, inactivation of tumour suppressor genes such as p16 and p21 further promotes uncontrolled cell proliferation [10]. Numerous additional carcinogenic mechanisms of tobacco smoke have been identified, establishing its detrimental impact on human health.

In this retrospective study we investigated the correlation between smoking behaviour and histologic growth patterns in adenocarcinomas of the lung, as well as the prognostic relevance of specific patterns in terms of OS. To assess the generalizability of our results, we first compared the distribution of predominant patterns in our cohort with those reported in previous studies (Table 6). Compared to Hwang et al. [11] as well as Warth et al. [12], our results showed a largely similar distribution. While other major studies [13–15] reported comparable frequencies for acinar and micropapillary patterns, solid predominance appeared to be overrepresented in our dataset. Reported frequencies of lepidic and papillary patterns varied widely across published cohorts. It is important to highlight that our study was the only one to quantitively assess tumour composition using digitalized whole-slide imaging. The methodological difference may have contributed to the observed discrepancies, as it enables more precise and objective evaluation of growth patterns (Table 7).

Regarding smoking habits and their association with specific growth patterns, we observed clear correlations for the two most frequent patterns, namely solid and acinar. Solid predominant tumours occurred significantly more often in PWS, while acinar predominance was more frequently observed in PWDS. These findings align with several previous studies reporting an increased frequency of solid adenocarcinomas in PWS [16–18].

PWS in our cohort showed fewer acinar predominant tumours, as well as a lower overall proportion of acinar growth. This trend was even more pronounced in PWSL compared to PWSH. To our knowledge, no prior studies have explicitly addressed the relationship between smoking status and acinar growth in pulmonary adenocarcinomas. However, our data suggest that acinar predominance may be positively correlated with tobacco abstinence.

A non-significant trend was noted indicating that micropapillary predominant carcinomas may be more common in PWSL than in PWSH. This observation appears somewhat counterintuitive, as micropapillary growth has been associated with poorer prognosis by several authors [11, 12, 19]. Recent literature specifically addressing this subtype remains sparse. While some studies report a higher frequency of micropapillary components in PWDS [6, 20], Pyo et al. [21] on the opposite proposed a positive association with smoking.

We also observed a trend towards lepidic predominance, and a higher proportion of lepidic growth, in PWDS compared to PWS, and in PWSL compared to PWSH. This is in line with previous reports suggesting tobacco abstinence correlates with lepidic growth [3, 22, 23]. Due to the small number of lepidic predominant tumours in our cohort-likely because such tumours are often rather diagnosed as minimally invasive carcinoma- we cannot conclusively confirm this association with statistical certainty.

With regard to papillary growth, PWSH showed a trend towards higher proportions of papillary components and were significantly more likely to exhibit papillary predominance. As no relevant literature on this specific topic could be identified, further studies are warranted.

For cribriform growth, statistical analyses yielded no conclusive results. The available data on this topic remains inconsistent; while Warth et al. [24] also reported no correlation with smoking status, Kadota et al. [25] found that cribriform growth occurred less frequently in PWDS.

Compared to other studies exploring the relationship between histological growth patterns and survival in pulmonary adenocarcinomas, our findings provide further support.

Most notably, solid predominant tumours were significantly associated with reduced OS, a finding consistent with previous reports [16, 17].

Several studies have shown that lepidic growth-typically considered a marker of well-differentiated carcinomas- is linked to better survival, both when predominant [15], and when present in non-predominant proportions [5]. Our results corroborate these observations, as lepidic predominant tumours in our cohort were significantly associated with better OS.

Given the rarity of micropapillary predominant tumours in general, and in this cohort specifically, it is not surprising that only a modest, non-significant trend towards worse OS was observed in this subgroup. Notably, we identified no studies focusing on the prognostic significance of micropapillary predominance. This may be due to the low frequency of this pattern, which has prompted some authors to define tumours as micropapillary-positive once a specified threshold proportion of micropapillary components is exceeded. These studies, in turn, have demonstrated that the presence of micropapillary elements in any proportion is associated with unfavourable survival outcomes [6, 20, 26, 27].

According to the 2021 edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours of the Lung, Pleura, Thymus and Heart [4], acinar and papillary predominant patterns are considered no definitive prognostic impact. Our findings are consistent with this classification, as we likewise observed no significant influence of these patterns on OS. While some authors have reported a negative prognostic significance of papillary adenocarcinomas [11], others described a favourable association [28], and yet others found no prognostic relevance at all [11].

The cribriform pattern, which is currently classified as a subtype of acinar adenocarcinomas rather than as a distinct pattern, has been less extensively studied. Some reports have suggested that cribriform growth may be associated with shorter survival [24, 25]. Interestingly, cribriform predominant carcinomas appear to behave more aggressively from a clinical standpoint. However, data from a large, comprehensive study on lung adenocarcinoma conducted in Japan indicated that OS in this subgroup falls between that of papillary and acinar carcinomas [29]. The authors attributed this observation to a better response to chemotherapy and a higher prevalence of targetable genetic alterations. Similarly, in our study, we did not observe a significant impact of cribriform predominance on OS. Nevertheless, we recommend that pathologists continue to report the presence of cribriform growth due to its potential clinical implications.

Recent studies have proposed that the total duration of tobacco exposure may be more prognostically relevant than a combined measure of duration and intensity, such as pack-years [30]. While calculating exposure based solely on the number of years of tobacco use could potentially offer improved prognostic accuracy, pack-years remain the most commonly used and reported metric in both clinical practice and retrospective research. In the present study, only pack-year data were available. Future studies may wish to distinguish more clearly between the duration and the intensity of tobacco consumption. Notably, no significant association was observed between pack-years and OS. These findings support the use of categorial definitions of smoking behaviour and raise questions about the adequacy pack-years as a proxy for the prognostic impact of tobacco exposure.

In conclusion, our analysis of 196 resected pulmonary adenocarcinomas supports the hypothesis that tobacco use and smoking behaviour are associated with the histological composition of tumour growth patterns. Although a causal relationship cannot be established based on our data, the observed associations may serve as a foundation for future studies aiming to confirm and further elucidate these associations. Our findings emphasise the importance of accurately identifying the predominant growth pattern, which emerged as an independent prognostic factor for OS. Given the considerable variation in the distribution of growth patterns across major study cohorts, the present study adds valuable data to the current body of evidence. Interobserver variability may pose a challenge, and enhanced training could help improve consistency in pathological reports. Moreover, the potential prognostic relevance of cribriform predominance warrants further investigation and critical discussion.

From a clinical perspective, our results may assist in more accurate prognostic stratification; for instance, the identification of a solid predominant growth pattern, particularly in patients with a history of smoking, should be considered when estimating survival expectations. Conversely, lepidic predominant carcinomas were associated with a markedly more favourable prognosis. Stratification into the three prognostic subgroups proposed above provides a readily assessable yet independent tool for estimating patient outcomes. While clinicians are not expected to have an in-depth understanding of every detail in a histological report, a basic knowledge of predominant growth patterns in lung adenocarcinoma and their prognostic implications may enhance communication with patients and inform therapeutic decision-making.

Limitations of this study include its single-centre design, which may limit the representativeness of larger populations, as reflected by the differing pattern frequencies across previously published cohorts. Our cohort included 196 patients and was exceeded in size by only two major investigations on this topic. Importantly, our primary focus was placed on the quality of histological assessment rather than on cohort size alone. Nonetheless, sample size may influence statistical power and external validity. Additionally, clinical data were derived from patient admission forms, and self-reported smoking histories may be subject to recall bias or underreporting. As this was a retrospective study, causal relationships cannot be established. Also, the time frame of included resections predates the routine use of PET-CT imaging, most recent follow-up protocols, and molecular testing for targetable genetic alterations. However, this time frame was chosen to ensure a sufficiently long follow-up duration for survival analysis.
