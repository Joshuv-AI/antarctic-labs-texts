Prüfer Transformation and Spectral Analysis for a Sturm–Liouville-Type Equation
Shalmali Bandyopadhyay, F. Ayça Çetinkaya, Tom Cuchta

Abstract
. We study a second-order differential equation involving a quasi-derivative of the form y ′ + s ⁡ ( x ) ​ y y^{\prime}+s(x)y , leading to a non-self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville-type problem with four coefficient functions. To analyze this equation, we develop a generalized Prüfer transformation that expresses solutions in terms of amplitude and phase variables. Using this framework, we establish comparison theorems, prove the monotonicity of eigenfunction zeros with respect to the spectral parameter, and derive upper and lower bounds for the eigenvalues.

1.
Introduction

This paper studies the second-order differential equation

(1.1)

-\left[p(x)(y^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y(x))\right]^{\prime}+s(x)y(x)(y^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y(x))+q(x)y(x)=0,

which represents a non-self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville-type problem with four coefficient functions involving a quasi-derivative of the form
D_{s}y=y^{\prime}+s(x)y
. To analyze this equation, we develop a generalized Prüfer transformation that expresses solutions in terms of amplitude and phase variables. The resulting system is nonlinear, reflecting the structure of the original operator. Using this framework, we establish comparison theorems, prove the monotonicity of eigenfunction zeros with respect to the spectral parameter, and derive upper and lower bounds for the eigenvalues.

Sturm–Liouville theory constitutes one of the fundamental pillars of mathematical analysis, with applications spanning from quantum mechanics and vibration analysis to signal processing and mathematical physics. The classical Sturm–Liouville problem, involving the self-adjoint differential equation

(1.2)

-[p(x)y^{\prime}]^{\prime}+q(x)y=\lambda w(x)y

with various boundary conditions, has been extensively studied since its introduction by Jacques Charles François Sturm and Joseph Liouville in their seminal works of 1836–1837
[
16
,
17
]
. The well-established theory for such problems provides a complete characterization of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions, including orthogonality properties, completeness of eigenfunction expansions, and oscillation theorems
[
5
,
18
]
.

The classical approach to analyzing Sturm–Liouville problems relies heavily on the self-adjoint nature of the differential operator, which guarantees real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions in an appropriate weighted inner product space
[
19
]
. However, many practical applications give rise to problems that do not fit the standard self-adjoint framework. These include problems with complex coefficients, eigenparameter-dependent boundary conditions, or more general quasi-derivative operators. The Prüfer transformation, originally developed by Heinz Prüfer in 1926
[
15
]
as a generalization of Poincaré phase-plane analysis has proven to be a powerful tool for studying oscillatory properties of solutions to second-order differential equations. Recent research has extensively utilized Prüfer transformation techniques to establish comparison theorems, analyze oscillation behavior, and develop numerical methods for eigenvalue problems
[
1
,
4
,
11
]
. The effectiveness of the Prüfer approach lies in its ability to convert the original second-order problem into a system of first-order equations involving amplitude and phase variables, thereby facilitating both theoretical analysis and numerical computation.

Recent developments in Prüfer theory have focused on extending classical results to more general settings. Kong et al.
[
10
,
12
]
have investigated eigenvalue problems where the leading coefficient function changes sign, while Binding and Browne
[
2
]
developed Prüfer-based numerical methods for two-parameter Sturm–Liouville problems. The application of Prüfer transformations to periodic and semi-periodic boundary conditions has been studied by several authors
[
13
,
9
]
, demonstrating the versatility of this approach across different boundary value problems.

The study of non-self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville problems has gained significant attention in recent years due to their relevance in modeling dissipative systems and problems with complex potentials
[
7
,
3
]
. Unlike their self-adjoint counterparts, these problems may exhibit complex eigenvalues, non-orthogonal eigenfunctions, and more intricate spectral properties. Non-self-adjoint operators arise naturally in the discussion of processes that proceed without conservation of energy, including problems with friction, the theory of open resonators, and problems of inelastic scattering. The spectral theory of such operators presents unique challenges since traditional variational methods, which rely on the minimization of energy functionals and guarantee real eigenvalues with orthogonal eigenfunctions, are not directly applicable. Consequently, researchers have developed specialized techniques including resolvent analysis, asymptotic expansions, and modified variational approaches
[
7
,
14
]
. Recent developments have focused on establishing completeness theorems for generalized eigenfunctions, characterizing spectral singularities, and developing trace formulas for non-self-adjoint differential operators, while the application of Prüfer transformations to such problems remains an active area of research
[
14
,
4
]
.

Despite the extensive literature on Sturm–Liouville theory, several important gaps remain in our understanding and computational capabilities. While significant progress has been made in studying either non-self-adjoint problems or quasi-derivative operators separately, there is a notable lack of comprehensive analysis for problems that combine both features. The equation we study in (
1.1
) represents a non-self-adjoint operator with a quasi-derivative structure involving four coefficient functions, which has not yet been systematically analyzed using Prüfer transformation techniques. Existing Prüfer transformations are primarily designed for self-adjoint problems or specific classes of non-self-adjoint equations. The development of a generalized Prüfer transformation that can handle the nonlinear structure arising from our quasi-derivative operator represents a significant theoretical contribution. Most existing work treats analytical and numerical aspects separately. There is a need for unified frameworks that seamlessly connect qualitative spectral theory with practical eigenvalue computation, particularly for non-self-adjoint problems where standard variational approaches fail. While shooting methods for self-adjoint problems have well-established convergence properties, the theoretical foundation for non-self-adjoint cases remains less developed. This is particularly important given the potential for complex eigenvalues and the associated computational challenges.

In this paper we introduce a novel Prüfer transformation specifically designed for the quasi-derivative operator
D_{s}y=y^{\prime}+s(x)y
, resulting in a nonlinear system that captures the essential spectral properties of the original problem. We establish existence and uniqueness results for the generalized Prüfer system, prove comparison theorems analogous to classical Sturm theory, and derive monotonicity properties for eigenfunction zeros with respect to spectral parameters. We obtain sharp upper and lower bounds for eigenvalues using variational techniques adapted to our non-self-adjoint setting.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we develop the generalized Prüfer coordinates and establish the fundamental equivalence between the original differential equation (
1.1
) and its associated Prüfer system, proving existence and uniqueness properties through Lipschitz continuity analysis. Section
3
focuses on the spectral properties and applications of the Prüfer phase equation, beginning with the decoupling of amplitude and phase variables and demonstrating how the phase function completely determines solution zeros. We then establish Sturm-type oscillation theorems and comparison results, followed by analysis of eigenvalue problems including the crucial monotonicity property of eigenfunction zeros with respect to the spectral parameter. The section concludes with variational techniques that yield sharp upper and lower bounds for eigenvalues, and presents an elegant eigenvalue criterion expressed entirely in terms of the phase function.

2.3.
Existence and uniqueness properties

To ensure the well-posedness of the Prüfer system, we establish the Lipschitz continuity of the phase equation, which guarantees unique solutions.

Lemma 2.2

(Lipschitz Continuity)
.

Let

f(x,\theta)=-\dfrac{\cos^{2}(\theta)}{p(x)}-s(x)\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)+q(x)\sin^{2}(\theta)-\dfrac{s(x)r(x)}{p(x)}\sin^{2}(\theta)\cos(\theta),

Proof.

Compute the partial derivative of

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\sin(2\theta)}{p(x)}-s(x)\cos(2\theta)+q(x)\sin(2\theta)-\frac{s(x)r(x)}{p(x)}\left(2\sin\theta\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{3}\theta\right).

Using the triangle inequality, we obtain the stated Lipschitz constant, completing the proof.

3.
Spectral Properties and Applications of the Prüfer Phase Equation

The power of the Prüfer transformation becomes most apparent when analyzing the oscillatory and spectral properties of differential equations. In this section, we demonstrate how the phase function
\theta
completely determines the zeros of solutions, leading to profound insights into eigenvalue problems and comparison theorems.

3.1.
Decoupling and the role of the phase function

Once the phase function
\theta(x)
is determined by solving equation (
2.11
), the amplitude equation (
2.10
) becomes a first-order nonlinear differential equation that can be written in Bernoulli form

r^{\prime}(x)=r(x)G(x)+r^{2}(x)H(x),

where

G(x)=-\left(\frac{1}{p(x)}+q(x)\right)\sin(\theta(x))\cos(\theta(x))-s(x)\sin^{2}(\theta(x)),\quad H(x)=\frac{s(x)}{p(x)}\sin(\theta(x))\cos^{2}(\theta(x)).

This decoupling reveals a fundamental property: the zeros of solutions are completely determined by the phase function, independent of the amplitude. Since
y(x)=r(x)\sin(\theta(x))
and
r>0
, we have
y(x)=0
precisely when
\sin(\theta(x))=0
, which occurs when
\theta(x)\in\pi\mathbb{Z}
.

This observation has profound implications for oscillation theory. The frequency and distribution of zeros in any nontrivial solution depend solely on how rapidly
\theta
increases, allowing us to study oscillatory behavior by focusing exclusively on the phase equation (
2.11
). Moreover, this enables direct comparison of oscillatory behavior between different equations by comparing their respective phase equations.

3.2.
Sturm-type oscillation theory

The Prüfer approach provides an elegant framework for establishing oscillation and comparison theorems. The key insight is that larger potential functions lead to faster growth of the phase function, resulting in more frequent zeros.

Theorem 3.1

(Sturm oscillation theorem)
.

Let
p,s,q_{1},q_{2}\in C([a,b])
with
p>0
on
[a,b]
, and suppose
q_{1}(x)\leq q_{2}(x)
for all
x\in[a,b]
. Consider the generalized Sturm–Liouville equations

-\left[p(x)\left(y_{i}^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y_{i}(x)\right)\right]^{\prime}+s(x)y_{i}(x)\left(y_{i}^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y_{i}(x)\right)+q_{i}(x)y_{i}(x)=0,\quad i=1,2,

on
[a,b]
. If
y_{1}
(corresponding to
q_{1}
) has two consecutive zeros at
x_{1}<x_{2}
, then
y_{2}
(corresponding to
q_{2}
) has at least one zero in the open interval
(x_{1},x_{2})
.

Proof.

Applying the Prüfer substitution to both equations, the phase functions satisfy for
i=1,2
,

\theta_{i}^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{\cos^{2}(\theta_{i}(x))}{p(x)}-s(x)\sin(\theta_{i}(x))\cos(\theta_{i}(x))+q_{i}(x)\sin^{2}(\theta_{i}(x))-\frac{s(x)r_{i}(x)}{p(x)}\sin^{2}(\theta_{i}(x))\cos(\theta_{i}(x)).

The crucial observation here is that the potential functions appear in the terms
-q_{i}(x)\sin^{2}(\theta_{i}(x))
. Since
q_{1}(x)\leq q_{2}(x)
, we have

\theta_{1}^{\prime}(x)\geq\theta_{2}^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{whenever }\theta_{1}(x)=\theta_{2}(x).

Since
y_{1}
has consecutive zeros at
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, we have
\theta_{1}(x_{2})-\theta_{1}(x_{1})=\pi
. By the comparison principle for first-order ODEs, this implies

\theta_{2}(x_{2})-\theta_{2}(x_{1})\geq\pi.

Therefore,
\theta_{2}
must cross at least one multiple of
\pi
in
(x_{1},x_{2})
, ensuring that
y_{2}
has at least one zero in this interval.
∎

3.3.
Eigenvalue Problems and Spectral Analysis

We replace

(3.1)

-\left(p(x)\left(y^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y(x)\right)\right)^{\prime}+s(x)p(x)\left(y^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y(x)\right)+q(x)y(x)=\lambda\omega(x)y(x).

The spectral parameter

The theorem below establishes that zeros of solutions to the eigenvalue problem move leftward as the eigenvalue parameter increases, a fundamental monotonicity property in Sturm-Liouville theory. The proof uses the Prüfer transformation to convert the second-order eigenvalue problem into a first-order phase equation, where the dependence on the eigenvalue parameter

Theorem 3.3

(Monotonicity of zeros with respect to

3.4.
Eigenvalue Bounds

The Prüfer framework enables precise bounds on eigenvalues through variational techniques. In this section, we present the results related to the lower bound followed by the upper bound of the
n^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
. The first theorem establishes a lower bound for eigenvalues of a general second-order differential operator by employing a substitution technique followed by comparison methods. The key idea is to transform the original problem using
u(x)=y^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y(x)
to eliminate first derivative terms, yielding a simplified form involving the associated coefficient function
Q
. We then construct a comparison eigenvalue problem with explicit solutions using an auxiliary function
f
that bounds
p
from below and apply variational principles to relate the eigenvalues of both problems. The resulting lower bound combines a scaled version of the comparison eigenvalues with the minimum value of
\frac{Q}{\omega}
. Similarly, the second theorem provides an upper bound by constructing a dual comparison problem with an auxiliary function
h
that bounds
p
from above, yielding an upper bound that involves the maximum value of
\frac{Q}{\omega}
. Both bounds provide flexibility in choosing the comparison functions while capturing the essential spectral behavior.

To analyze the eigenvalues of the boundary value problem with boundary conditions
y(a)=y(b)=0
, we first rewrite it in a self-adjoint form. To this end, we introduce a new dependent variable
v(x):=e^{\int_{a}^{x}s(t)\mathrm{d}t}y(x)

[a,b]

y(x)=e^{-\int_{a}^{x}s(t)\mathrm{d}t}v(x)
and
y^{\prime}(x)+s(x)y(x)=e^{-\int_{a}^{x}s(t)\mathrm{d}t}v^{\prime}(x)

satisfies the differential equation

(3.2)

-\left(p(x)v^{\prime}(x)\right)^{\prime}+Q(x)v(x)=\lambda\omega(x)v(x),\quad v(a)=v(b)=0,

where the
associated coefficient function
is defined by

(3.3)

Q(x):=q(x)-\left(p(x)s(x)\right)^{\prime}+p(x)s^{2}(x).

This self-adjoint formulation allows us to apply variational methods to derive lower and upper bounds for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
by constructing appropriate simpler auxiliary problems with auxiliary functions
f
and
h
that bound the coefficient

from below and above, respectively.

6
]
] is a fundamental result in spectral theory that provides a variational characterization of eigenvalues for self-adjoint operators. For a self-adjoint boundary value problem with Rayleigh quotient
\mathcal{R}[v]
, the
n^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
takes form

\lambda_{n}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset H_{0}^{1}(a,b)\\
\dim(S)=n\end{subarray}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}[v]=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}T\subset H_{0}^{1}(a,b)\\
\dim(T)=n-1\end{subarray}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in T^{\perp}\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}[v],

where
T^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal complement of the subspace
T
in
H_{0}^{1}(a,b)
. The first expression (minimax) states that
\lambda_{n}
is the minimum over all
n
-dimensional subspaces
S
of the maximum value of the Rayleigh quotient on
S
. The second expression (maximin) provides the dual characterization. This principle is powerful because it reduces the infinite-dimensional eigenvalue problem to finite-dimensional optimization problems over subspaces. For obtaining eigenvalue bounds, the strategy is to choose specific subspaces where the Rayleigh quotient can be estimated: for lower bounds, we select a particular
n
-dimensional subspace and bound the maximum of
\mathcal{R}[v]
from below; for upper bounds, we choose trial functions and bound
\mathcal{R}[v]
from above. We first derive the lower bound.

Theorem 3.5

(Lower bound on the
n^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue)
.

Let
f\in C([a,b])
satisfy
0<f\leq p
, and suppose there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\omega f<c
on
[a,b]
. Then, with
m:=\displaystyle\min_{x\in[a,b]}\frac{Q(x)}{\omega(x)}
, where
Q
is given by (
3.3
), the
n^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of (
3.1
) satisfies

\lambda_{n}\geq\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{c\left(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{f(x)}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}}+m,\quad n=1,2,3,\dots.

Proof.

As shown in the self-adjoint formulation, the original problem (
1.1
) is equivalent to (
3.2
). The associated Rayleigh quotient is
\mathcal{R}[v]=\frac{\int_{a}^{b}p(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{b}Q(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x}.
By the minimax principle,
\lambda_{n}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset H_{0}^{1}(a,b)\\
\dim(S)=n\end{subarray}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in S\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}[v]
. Now consider the comparison problem

-f(x)\phi^{\prime\prime}(x)=\tilde{\lambda}\phi(x),\quad\phi(a)=\phi(b)=0,

whose eigenvalues are
\tilde{\lambda}_{n}=\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{\left(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{f(x)}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}}
. Let
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}
be the first
n
eigenfunctions of this problem and define
S=\text{span}\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\}
. Then for any
v\in S\setminus\{0\}
, we have

\int_{a}^{b}p(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{a}^{b}f(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x,\quad\int_{a}^{b}Q(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x\geq m\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x<c\int_{a}^{b}\frac{v^{2}}{f(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore,

\mathcal{R}[v]\geq\frac{\int_{a}^{b}f(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x}+m\geq\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{n}}{c}+m.

Taking the infimum over all such subspaces yields the stated lower bound, completing the proof.
∎

Now we derive the upper bound.

Theorem 3.6

(Upper bound on the
n^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue)
.

Let
h\in C([a,b])
satisfy
h\geq p
, and define
D:=\displaystyle\min_{x\in[a,b]}\omega(x)h(x)>0
. Then, with
M:=\displaystyle\max_{x\in[a,b]}\frac{Q(x)}{\omega(x)},
where
Q
is defined by (
3.3
), the
n^{\text{th}}
eigenvalue of (
3.1
) satisfies

\lambda_{n}\leq\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{D\left(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{h(x)}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}}+M,\quad n=1,2,3,\dots

Proof.

From the self-adjoint form, the Rayleigh quotient is
\mathcal{R}[v]=\frac{\int_{a}^{b}p(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{a}^{b}Q(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x}
. Consider the comparison problem

-(h(x)\varphi^{\prime})^{\prime}=\mu\cdot\frac{1}{h(x)}\varphi(x),\quad\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)=0,

with eigenvalues
\mu_{n}=\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{\left(\int_{a}^{b}\frac{1}{h(x)}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{2}}
. Let
V=\text{span}\{\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\}
. For any
v\in V\setminus\{0\}
, we estimate

\int_{a}^{b}p(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{a}^{b}h(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x,\quad\int_{a}^{b}Q(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x\leq M\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x,

\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x\geq D\int_{a}^{b}\frac{v^{2}}{h(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Hence,

\mathcal{R}[v]\leq\frac{\int_{a}^{b}h(x)(v^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{a}^{b}\omega(x)v^{2}\mathrm{d}x}+M\leq\frac{\mu_{n}}{D}+M.

Taking the infimum over all
n
-dimensional subspaces of
H_{0}^{1}(a,b)
proves the result.
∎

3.5.
The phase function criterion

Perhaps the most elegant application of the Prüfer method is the complete characterization of eigenvalues through the phase function. At this point, it is appropriate to explain why the function space
H_{0}^{1}(a,b)
is used in the application of the minimax principle. This Sobolev space consists of all functions
v\in H^{1}(a,b)
that vanish at the endpoints, i.e.
v(a)=v(b)=0
. This is the natural setting for the variational formulation of the eigenvalue problem (
3.2
) since it ensures the square-integrability of both
v
and
v^{\prime}
, making the Rayleigh quotient well-defined. Moreover,
H_{0}^{1}(a,b)
reflects the boundary conditions and carries a Hilbert space structure under which the self-adjoint differential operator associated with (
3.2
) admits a discrete spectrum. The minimax principle characterizes the
n^{\text{th}}
-th eigenvalue as the smallest maximum of the Rayleigh quotient over all
n
-dimensional subspaces of
H_{0}^{1}(a,b)
, justifying its central role in the proof.

Theorem 3.7
.

[Eigenvalue criterion via the phase function]
Let
\theta(x;\lambda)
be the solution to the initial value problem (
2.11
) with initial condition
\theta(a;\lambda)=0
, where
p,s,q,r\in C([a,b])
and
p>0
on
[a,b]
. Then a real number
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of the boundary value problem (
3.1
) with
y(a)=y(b)=0
if
\theta(b;\lambda)=n\pi
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We recall that
\theta(x;\lambda)
describes the angle corresponding to the solution
y
and its quasi derivative. In particular, the condition
\theta(x;\lambda)=n\pi
implies
\sin(\theta(x;\lambda))=0
, and hence
y(x)=0
. The initial condition
\theta(a;\lambda)=0
corresponds to
y(a)=0
. Thus, the boundary condition
y(b)=0
is satisfied if and only if
\theta(b;\lambda)=n\pi
for some integer
n
. Therefore, the real numbers
\lambda
for which
\theta(b;\lambda)=n\pi
are precisely the eigenvalues of the boundary value problem (
3.1
) subject to the Dirichlet conditions
y(a)=y(b)=0
.
∎

This criterion transforms the eigenvalue problem into a root-finding problem: eigenvalues are precisely those values of
\lambda
for which
\theta(b;\lambda)
equals an integer multiple of
\pi
. This provides both theoretical insight and computational advantages for numerical eigenvalue algorithms.

The elegance of this approach lies in its reduction of a two-point boundary value problem to the solution of an initial value problem, making it particularly suitable for numerical implementation and theoretical analysis.

4.
Future Directions

The theoretical framework established in this paper opens several promising avenues for future research. The authors are currently developing a companion paper focusing on the numerical implementation of the generalized Prüfer transformation developed here. This forthcoming work will feature the development of robust shooting methods specifically adapted to the nonlinear Prüfer system derived in Section
2
, taking advantage of the phase function criterion established in Theorem
3.7
. The numerical approach will leverage the decoupling property discussed in Section
3.1
, where the phase equation can be solved independently before determining the amplitude, leading to more stable computational schemes compared to traditional methods. Additionally, this work will include comprehensive numerical analysis of convergence properties for eigenvalue computation in non-self-adjoint settings and extensive computational validation of the eigenvalue bounds derived in Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
across various parameter regimes.

A natural and significant extension of this work involves adapting the generalized Prüfer transformation to dynamic equations on time scales. The authors are initiating a research project to develop time scale analogues of the quasi-derivative operator, generalized Prüfer coordinates for second-order dynamic equations on time scales with non-self-adjoint structure, and time scale versions of the comparison theorems and oscillation results established in Section
3.2
. This extension is particularly compelling because time scales provide a unified framework that encompasses both continuous and discrete settings, potentially revealing new connections between differential and difference equations in spectral theory. The time scale approach may also yield computational advantages in certain applications where the underlying physical system naturally evolves on a non-uniform time domain.
