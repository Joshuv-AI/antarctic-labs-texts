Complexity Analysis of Normalizing Constant Estimation: from Jarzynski Equality to Annealed Importance Sampling and beyond
Wei Guo, Molei Tao, Yongxin Chen

Abstract
Given an unnormalized probability density π ∝ e − V \pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-V} , estimating its normalizing constant Z = ∫ ℝ d e − V ⁡ ( x ) ​ 𝑑 x Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}\mathrm{d}x or free energy F = − log ⁡ Z F=-\log Z is a crucial problem in Bayesian statistics, statistical mechanics, and machine learning. It is challenging especially in high dimensions or when π \pi is multimodal. To mitigate the high variance of conventional importance sampling estimators, annealing-based methods such as Jarzynski equality and annealed importance sampling are commonly adopted, yet their quantitative complexity guarantees remain largely unexplored. We take a first step toward a non-asymptotic analysis of annealed importance sampling. In particular, we derive an oracle complexity of O ~ ​ ( d ​ β 2 ​ 𝒜 2 ε 4 ) \widetilde{O}\left(\frac{d\beta^{2}{\mathcal{A}}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\right) for estimating Z Z within ε \varepsilon relative error with high probability, where β \beta is the smoothness of V V and 𝒜 \mathcal{A} denotes the action of a curve of probability measures interpolating π \pi and a tractable reference distribution. Our analysis, leveraging Girsanov’s theorem and optimal transport, does not explicitly require isoperimetric assumptions on the target distribution. Finally, to tackle the large action of the widely used geometric interpolation, we propose a new algorithm based on reverse diffusion samplers, establish a framework for analyzing its complexity, and empirically demonstrate its efficiency in tackling multimodality.

Introduction

We study the problem of estimating the normalizing constant
Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\pi}(x)\mathrm{d}x

\pi\propto\widehat{\pi}:=\mathrm{e}^{-V}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
\pi(x)=\frac{\widehat{\pi}(x)}{Z}
. The normalizing constant appears in various fields: in Bayesian statistics, when
\widehat{\pi}
is the product of likelihood and prior,
Z
is also referred to as the marginal likelihood or evidence
(
Gelman et al., 2013
)
; in statistical mechanics, when
V
is the Hamiltonian,

. The task of normalizing constant estimation has numerous applications, including computing log-likelihoods in probabilistic models

, estimating free energy differences

Estimating normalizing constants is challenging in high dimensions or when
\pi
is multimodal (i.e.,
V
has a complex landscape). Conventional approaches based on importance sampling
(
Meng and Wong, 1996
)
are widely adopted to tackle this problem, but they suffer from high variance due to the mismatch between the proposal and the target when
\pi
is complicated

In particular, JE points out the connection between the free energy difference between two states and the work done over a series of trajectories linking these two states, while AIS constructs a sequence of intermediate distributions and estimates the normalizing constant by importance sampling over these distributions. These two methods are our primary focus in this paper.

Despite the empirical success of annealing-based methods

;

)
, while works on JE usually simplify the problem by assuming the work follows simple distributions (e.g., Gaussian or gamma)

Arrar et al., 2019
)
. Moreover, only analyses asymptotic in the number of particles derived from central limit theorem

Contributions.

Our key technical contributions are summarized as follows.

1.
We discover a novel strategy for analyzing the complexity of normalizing constant estimation, applicable to a wide range of target distributions (

2.

3.

Related Works.

Below, we summarize the related works in four aspects.

I. Methods for normalizing constant estimation.
We mainly discuss two classes of methods here. First, the
equilibrium
methods, such as TI
(
Kirkwood, 1935
)
and its variants
(
Brosse et al., 2018
;
Ge et al., 2020
;
Chehab et al., 2023
;
Kook and Vempala, 2025
)
, which involve sampling sequentially from a series of equilibrium Markov transition kernels. Second, the
non-equilibrium
methods, such as AIS
(
Neal, 2001
)
, which samples from a non-equilibrium stochastic process that gradually evolves from a prior distribution to the target distributions. In
Sec.

H.1
, we show that TI is a special case of AIS using the “perfect” transition kernels. Recent years have also witnessed the emergence of
learning-based
non-equilibrium methods, which are typically byproducts of neural samplers
(
Nüsken and Richter, 2021
;
Zhang and Chen, 2022
;
Máté and Fleuret, 2023
;
Richter and Berner, 2024
;
Sun et al., 2024
;
Vargas et al., 2024
;
Máté et al., 2024
;
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2025
;
Blessing et al., 2025
;
Chen et al., 2025
;
Havens et al., 2025
;
Du et al., 2025
)
. Finally, there are also methods based on particle filtering
(
Kostov and Whiteley, 2017
;
Jasra et al., 2018
;
Ruzayqat et al., 2022
)
.

II. Variance reduction in JE and AIS.
Our proof methodology focuses on the discrepancy between the sampling path measure and the reference path measure, which is related to the variance reduction technique in applying JE and AIS. For example,
Vaikuntanathan and Jarzynski (2008)
introduced the idea of escorted simulation,
Hartmann et al. (2017)
proposed a method for learning the optimal control protocol in JE through the variational characterization of free energy, and
Doucet et al. (2022)
leveraged score-based generative model to learn the optimal backward kernel. Quantifying the discrepancy between path measures is the core of our analysis.

III. Complexity analysis for normalizing constant estimation.

Chehab et al. (2023)
studied the asymptotic statistical efficiency of the curve for TI measured by the asymptotic mean-squared error, and highlighted the advantage of the geometric interpolation. In terms of non-asymptotic analysis, existing works mainly rely on the isoperimetry of the target distribution. For instance,
Andrieu et al. (2016)
derived bounds of bias and variance for TI under Poincaré inequality (PI),
Brosse et al. (2018)
provided complexity guarantees for TI under both strong and weak log-concavity conditions, while
Ge et al. (2020)
improved the complexity under strong log-concavity using multilevel Monte Carlo.

IV. Complexity analysis of sampling beyond isoperimetry.

Our analysis of estimating normalizing constants of non-log-concave distributions is also closely related to the study of sampling beyond log-concavity. In general, such problems are NP hard
(
Ge et al., 2018
;
He and Zhang, 2025
)
. Existing works providing convergence guarantees have leveraged more general isoperimetric inequalities such as weak PI
(
Mousavi-Hosseini et al., 2023
)
, tried to establish convergence in weaker notions
(
Balasubramanian et al., 2022
;
Cheng et al., 2023
)
, or utilized denoising diffusion models
(
Huang et al., 2024a
;
He et al., 2024
)
. We highlight
Guo et al. (2025)
that this paper mainly draws inspiration from, which introduced the action of a curve in quantifying the convergence of annealed sampling. While they focused on sampling and presented cases where annealing works, we extend the analysis to a conceptually different task, and further establish lower bounds on the action of the commonly used geometric interpolation, motivating a new algorithm based on reverse diffusion samplers.

Preliminaries and Problem Setting

To motivate the study of normalizing constant estimation, we first present several examples.

Example 1.
[
Free energy difference.
] In many statistical physics problems
(
Lelièvre et al., 2010
)
, given two energy functions
U_{0},U_{1}
(possibly linked through some thermodynamic process), one is often interested in estimating the
free energy difference

\Delta F:=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log(\int\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U_{1}}\mathrm{d}x/\int\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U_{0}}\mathrm{d}x)
, which is related with the normalizing constant of the distributions
\pi_{i}\propto\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U_{i}}
.

Example 2.
[
Likelihood in latent variable models.
] In latent variable models such as variational autoencoders
(
Kingma and Welling, 2013
)
, a common evaluation metric is the
marginal likelihood
of a data point
x
,
p_{\theta}(x)=\int p_{\theta}(x|z)p(z)\mathrm{d}z
. This is nothing but the normalizing constant of the posterior distribution of the latent variable
z
given data
x
,
p_{\theta}(z|x)\propto_{z}p_{\theta}(x|z)p(z)
.

Example 3.
[
Volume of convex bodies.
] In theoretical computer science, a classical problem is to estimate the
volume
of a convex body
\mathcal{K}

(
Dyer et al., 1991
;
Cousins and Vempala, 2018
;
Kook et al., 2024
)
, which is equivalent to the normalizing constant of the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{K}
,
\pi\propto 1_{\mathcal{K}}
.

Building on prior theoretical results
(
Brosse et al., 2018
;
Ge et al., 2020
)
, we study the oracle complexity of estimating the normalizing constant of a density under the following criterion:

Aim:
Given a density
\pi\propto\widehat{\pi}:=\mathrm{e}^{-V}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, bound the complexity
of obtaining an estimator
\widehat{Z}
of
Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\pi}(x)\mathrm{d}x
such that with constant probability, the relative error is within
\varepsilon(\ll 1)
:

\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}.

(1)

.

We make two remarks regarding
1
.
First, similar to how taking the mean of i.i.d. estimates reduces variance, we show in

1

Second,
1
also allows us to quantify the complexity of estimating the free energy
F=-\log Z
, which is often of greater interest in statistical mechanics than the partition function

with

A straightforward method for estimating
Z
is through
importance sampling
, i.e.,
Z=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{0}}\frac{\widehat{\pi}}{\pi_{0}}
for some tractable proposal distribution
\pi_{0}
, yet its variance can be large due to the mismatch between
\pi_{0}
and
\pi
. The rationale behind

involves a gradual transition from
\pi_{0}
to
\pi_{1}=\pi
. Throughout this paper, we consider a curve of probability measures denoted as

\left(\pi_{\theta}=\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}}\mathrm{e}^{-V_{\theta}}\right)_{\theta\in[0,1]},

where
V_{1}=V
is the potential of
\pi
, and
Z_{1}=Z
is what we need to estimate. We do not specify the exact form of this curve now, but only introduce the following mild regularity assumption on the curve, as assumed in classical textbooks such as
Ambrosio et al. (2008)
;
Ambrosio et al. (2021)
;
Santambrogio (2015)
:

Assumption 1
.

The potential
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni(\theta,x)\mapsto V_{\theta}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
is jointly
C^{1}
, and the curve
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}
is
absolute continuous
with finite
action

\mathcal{A}:=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\dot{\pi}\right|_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta
.

Here,
|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}:=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}(\pi_{\theta+\delta},\pi_{\theta})}{|\delta|}
is the Wasserstein-2 (
\text{W}_{\text{2}}
)
metric derivative
of the curve
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}
at
\theta
, which measures the “speed” of the curve in the space of probability distributions, and
absolute continuity
means the above limit exists and is finite for all
\theta\in[0,1]
.
A curve having a finite action is a weaker condition than requiring each
\pi_{\theta}
to satisfy isoperimetric inequalities (e.g., Poincaré or log-Sobolev). We refer readers to
Sec.

A.2
for details of optimal transport (OT), and in particular, we highlight the connection between the metric derivative and the continuity equation (
Lem.

4
), which will serve as a key tool in our analysis.

For the purpose of non-asymptotic analysis, we further introduce the following mild assumption:

Assumption 2
.

V
is
\beta
-smooth,
\nabla V(0)=0
, and
m:=\sqrt{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\|\cdot\|^{2}}<{+\infty}
.

Remark 2
.

One can always find a stationary point
x_{*}
of (possibly non-convex)
V
using optimization methods within negligible cost compared with the complexity for estimating
Z
. By considering the translated distribution
\pi(\cdot-x_{*})
, we assume
0
is a stationary point without loss of generality.

Equipped with this fundamental setup, we now proceed to introduce the JE and AIS, and establish an analysis for their complexity.

3
Analysis of the Jarzynski Equality

To elucidate how annealing works in the task of normalizing constant estimation, we first consider
annealed Langevin diffusion (ALD)
, which runs
Langevin diffusion (LD)
with a dynamically changing target distribution.
Recall that the LD with target distribution is the stochastic differential equation (SDE)
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\nabla\log\pi(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, which converges to
\pi
as
t\to\infty
.
To define ALD, we introduce a reparameterized curve
(\widetilde{\pi}_{t}=\pi_{t/T})_{t\in[0,T]}
for some large time duration
T
to be determined later, and consider the following SDE:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},~t\in[0,T];~X_{0}\sim\widetilde{\pi}_{0}.

(2)

The following Jarzynski equality provides a connection between the work functional and the free energy difference, which naturally yields an estimator of normalizing constant.

Theorem 1

(Jarzynski equality
(
Jarzynski, 1997
)
)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}^{\to}
be the path measure of
2
. Then the work functional
W
and the free energy difference
\Delta F
have the following relation:

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\mathrm{e}^{-W}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta F},\quad\text{where}~~W(X):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta}|_{\theta=\frac{t}{T}}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t~~\text{and}~~\Delta F:=-\log\frac{Z_{1}}{Z_{0}}.

Below, we sketch the proof from
Vargas et al. (2024, Prop. 3.3)
, which offers a crucial aspect for our analysis: the forward and backward SDEs. See
Sec.

A.1
for a detailed introduction.

Sketch of Proof

Let
\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}
be the path measure of the following backward SDE with time-reversed Brownian motion (BM)
(B^{\leftarrow}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
(i.e.,
(t\mapsto B^{\leftarrow}_{T-t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard BM, see
Def.

1
):

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{t},~t\in[0,T];~X_{T}\sim\widetilde{\pi}_{T}.

(3)

Intuitively, this is running the ALD backward in time from
T
to
0
, targeting distribution
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
at time
t
.
Leveraging the Girsanov’s theorem (
Lem.

1
) and Itô’s formula, one can establish the following identity of the Radon-Nikodým (RN) derivative between the forward and backward path measures, known as the
Crooks fluctuation theorem

(
Crooks, 1998
;
Crooks, 1999
)
:

\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}}(X)=-\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t=W(X)-\Delta F,\quad\text{a.s.}~X\sim\mathbb{P}^{\to},

(4)

which implies JE by the identity
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}}=1
. Complete proof can be found in
Sec.

C.1
.

\square

Under the
ideal
setting where (i)
Z_{0}
is known, (ii) the ALD in
2
can be simulated exactly, and (iii) the work functional
W(X)
can be computed precisely,
Thm.

1
provides an unbiased estimator
\widehat{Z}:=Z_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)}
for
Z=Z_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta F}
. Despite its dominant use
(
Chipot and Pohorille, 2007
;
Lelièvre et al., 2010
)
, the statistical efficiency of this estimator is not well understood. While it is known that the variance of
\widehat{Z}
can be large,
non-asymptotic
analyses quantifying its efficiency is lacking. We address this gap by establishing an upper bound on the time
T
required for the ALD to satisfy the accuracy criterion
1
in the following theorem, whose proof is detailed in
Sec.

C.2
.

Theorem 2
.

Under
Assump.

1
, it suffices to choose
T=\frac{32\mathcal{A}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
to obtain
\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}
.

We first observe that our bound aligns with the decay rate of the variance of the work in
Mazonka and Jarzynski (1999)
(see also
Lelièvre et al. (2010, Chap. 4.1.4)
), which considered a special case
\pi_{\theta}=\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(\theta L,\frac{1}{K}\right)
. They showed that
W\sim\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(B_{T},2B_{T}\right)
with
B_{T}=\frac{L^{2}}{T}\left(1-\frac{(1-\mathrm{e}^{-KT})}{KT}\right)
, and hence the
normalized variance

\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}=\mathrm{e}^{2B_{T}}-1
is
asymptotically

O\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
as
T\to\infty
. Our bound, under a different criterion
1
, is
O\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)
for
all

T>0
.

To illustrate the proof idea of
Thm.

2
, note that while the ALD
2
targets the distribution
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
at time
t
, there is always a lag between
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
and the actual law of
X_{t}
. Similarly, the same lag exists in the backward ALD
3
. This lag turns out to be the source of the error in the estimator
\widehat{Z}
.

In practice, to alleviate the issue of high variance in estimating free energy differences,
Vaikuntanathan and Jarzynski (2008)
proposed adding a compensatory drift term
v_{t}(X_{t})
to the ALD
2
. Ideally, the optimal choice would eliminate the lag entirely, ensuring
X_{t}\sim\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Inspired by this, we compare the path measure of ALD
\mathbb{P}^{\to}
to the SDE having the perfect compensatory drift term, whose path measure
\mathbb{P}
has marginal distribution
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
at time
t
. To make possible the perfect match, it turns out that
v_{t}
must satisfy the Fokker-Planck equation with
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
. The Girsanov’s theorem (
Lem.

1
) enables the computation of
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\to})
and
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow})
, which are related to
\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\pi}_{t})}^{2}
. Finally, among all admissible drift terms
v_{t}
,
Lem.

4
suggests an optimal choice of
v^{*}_{t}
to minimize this norm, thereby leading to the metric derivative
|\dot{\widetilde{\pi}}|_{t}
and the action
\mathcal{A}
. This way avoids the explicit dependence of isoperimetric assumptions in our bound.

A similar connection between free energy and action integral was discovered in stochastic thermodynamics
(
Sekimoto, 2010
;
Seifert, 2012
)
, one paradigm for non-equilibrium thermodynamics. By the second law of thermodynamics, the averaged dissipated work, defined as the averaged work minus the free energy difference, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{diss}}:=\mathcal{W}-\Delta F:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}W-\Delta F
, is non-negative. When the underlying process is modeled by an overdamped LD,
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{diss}}
can be quantified by an action integral divided by the time duration
(
Aurell et al., 2011
;
Chen et al., 2020
)
. This follows from the observation that
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{diss}}=\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\to}\|\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow})
and then a similar argument to that above. This connection provides a finer description of the second law of thermodynamics
(
Aurell et al., 2012
)
over a finite time horizon.

Finally, we place
Thm.

2
within the broader theme of
sampling v.s. normalizing constant estimation
by comparing
Thm.

2
with the complexity of non-log-concave sampling.
Guo et al. (2025)
proved that under the same assumptions, the ALD
2
can draw a sample within
\varepsilon^{2}
-error in
\operatorname{KL}(\pi\|\cdot)
with the same order of time
T\asymp\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\varepsilon^{2}}
. While the classical work
Jerrum et al. (1986)
proved the existence of a polynomial-time algorithm for sampling and a polynomial-time algorithm for estimating normalizing constant imply each other in the
discrete
settings, we establish a similar quantitative connection between the complexities of these two tasks in the
continuous

Analysis of the Annealed Importance Sampling

In practice, it is not feasible to simulate the ALD precisely, nor is it possible to evaluate the exact value of the work
W(X)

Theorem 3

(Annealed importance sampling equality
(
Neal, 2001
)
)
.

Suppose we have probability distributions
\pi_{\ell}=\frac{f_{\ell}}{Z_{\ell}}
,
\ell\in\left[\!\left[0,M\right]\!\right]
and transition kernels

,
\ell\in\left[\!\left[1,M\right]\!\right]
, and assume that each
\pi_{\ell}
is an invariant distribution of
F_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\left[\!\left[1,M\right]\!\right]
. Define the path measure

\mathbb{P}^{\to}(x_{0:M})=\pi_{0}(x_{0})\prod_{\ell=1}^{M}F_{\ell}(x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}).

(5)

Then the same relation between the work function

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\mathrm{e}^{-W}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta F},\quad\text{where}~~W(x_{0:M}):=\log\prod_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\frac{f_{\ell}(x_{\ell})}{f_{\ell+1}(x_{\ell})}~~\text{and}~~\Delta F:=-\log\frac{Z_{M}}{Z_{0}}.

Proof.

Since
\pi_{\ell}

B_{\ell}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{\pi_{\ell}(x^{\prime})}{\pi_{\ell}(x)}F_{\ell}(x^{\prime},x),~\ell\in\left[\!\left[1,M\right]\!\right].

By applying these backward transition kernels sequentially, we define the backward path measure

\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}(x_{0:M})=\pi_{M}(x_{M})\prod_{\ell=1}^{M}B_{\ell}(x_{\ell},x_{\ell-1}).

(6)

4

implies the desired equality.

Normalizing Constant Estimation via Denoising Diffusion

Disadvantage of Geometric Interpolation.

From the analysis of JE and AIS (
Thms.

2
and
4
), the choice of the interpolation curve
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}
is crucial for the complexity. The geometric interpolation
7
is widely adopted due to the availability of closed-form scores of the intermediate distributions
\pi_{\theta}
, and for certain structured non-log-concave distributions, the associated action is polynomially large, enabling efficient AIS. For instance,
Guo et al. (2025, Ex. 2)
analyzed a Gaussian mixture target distribution with identical covariance, means having the
same
norm, and arbitrary weights. However, for general target distributions, the action of the related curve can grow prohibitively large. We now establish an exponential lower bound on the action of a curve starting from a Gaussian mixture, highlighting the potential inefficiency of AIS under geometric interpolation.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the Gaussian mixture target distribution
\pi=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(m,1\right)

\mathbb{R}
for some sufficiently large

, whose potential is
\frac{m^{2}}{2}
-smooth. Under the setting in AIS (
Thm.

4

\pi_{\theta}(x)\propto\pi(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda(\theta)x^{2}}{2}}

\theta\in[0,1]

\lambda(\theta)=m^{2}(1-\theta)^{r}
for some

(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}

\mathcal{A}_{r}

\mathcal{A}_{r}\gtrsim m^{4}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{m^{2}}{40}}

The full proof is in
Sec.

E.1
. The key technical tool is a closed-form expression of the
\text{W}_{\text{2}}

\mathbb{R}
expressed by the inverse cumulative distribution functions (c.d.f.s) of the involved distributions, and we lower bound the metric derivative near the target distribution, where the curve changes the most drastically. This observation provides a novel perspective on the quantitative description of the

or

(
Woodard et al., 2009

Syed et al., 2021

Intuitively, during the annealing process, the probability mass needs to be transported from one mode of the distribution to another well-separated mode in a short period of time (e.g., through the change of weights in both modes), which leads to torpid mixing for many samplers.

As discussed, RDS can be viewed as an

This highlights a fundamental trade-off between the complexity of the drift term estimation and the property of the interpolation curve.

Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Directions

This paper investigates the complexity of normalizing constant estimation using JE, AIS, and RDS, and takes a first step in establishing non-asymptotic convergence guarantees based on insights from continuous-time analysis. Our analysis of JE (
Thm.

2
) applies to general interpolations without explicit dependence of isoperimetry, thereby substantially extending prior work limited to log-concave distributions. Several limitations remain: the tightness of our upper bounds (
Thms.

2
and
4
) are unknown; the lower bound on the action in
Prop.

1
does not directly imply that JE needs exponentially long time to converge; though the action provides a clean analysis of the statistical efficiency of annealing–which isoperimetric inequalities cannot deal with–its practical interpretability is not well understood. Finally, we conjecture that our proof techniques can extend to samplers beyond overdamped LD (e.g., Hamiltonian or underdamped LD
(
Sohl-Dickstein and Culpepper, 2012
)
, parallel samplers
(
Anari et al., 2024

A.1
Stochastic Analysis: Forward-backward SDEs and Girsanov’s Theorem

For a stochastic differential equation (SDE)
X=(X_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
defined on
\Omega=C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the distribution of
X
over
\Omega
is called the
path measure
of
X
, defined by
\mathbb{P}^{X}
: measurable
A\subset\Omega\mapsto\operatorname{Pr}(X\in A)
.
The following lemma, as a corollary of the
Girsanov’s theorem

(
Üstünel and Zakai, 2013
, Prop. 2.3.1 & Cor. 2.3.1)
,
provides a method for computing the Radon-Nikodým (RN) derivative and KL divergence between two path measures, which serves as a key technical tool in our proof.

Lemma 1
.

Assume we have the following two SDEs with
t\in[0,T]
:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=a_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~X_{0}\sim\mu;\quad\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=b_{t}(Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~Y_{0}\sim\nu.

Denote the path measures of
X
and
Y
as
\mathbb{P}^{X}
and
\mathbb{P}^{Y}
, respectively. Then for any trajectory
\xi\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}(\xi_{0})+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle a_{t}(\xi_{t})-b_{t}(\xi_{t}),\mathrm{d}\xi_{t}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}(\|a_{t}(\xi_{t})\|^{2}-\|b_{t}(\xi_{t})\|^{2})\mathrm{d}t.

In particular, plugging in
\xi\leftarrow X\sim\mathbb{P}^{X}
, we can compute the KL divergence:

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\|\mathbb{P}^{Y})=\operatorname{KL}(\mu\|\nu)+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{X}}\|a_{t}(X_{t})-b_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t.

Remark 3
.

The Girsanov’s theorem requires a technical condition ensuring that a local martingale is a true martingale, typically verified via the Novikov condition
(
Karatzas and Shreve, 1991
, Chap. 3, Cor. 5.13)
, which can be challenging to establish. However, when only an upper bound of the KL divergence is needed, the approximation argument from
Chen et al. (2023, App. B.2)
circumvents the verification of the Novikov condition. For additional context, see
Chewi (2022, Sec. 3.2)
. In this paper, we omit these technical details and
always
assume that the Novikov condition holds.

We now present the theory of backward stochastic integral and the Girsanov’s theorem, which are adapted from
Vargas et al. (2024)
. Here, we include relevant results and proofs to ensure a self-contained presentation.

The backward SDE can be perceived as the time-reversal of a forward SDE:

Definition 1

(Backward SDE)
.

Given a BM
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, let its time-reversal be
(B^{\leftarrow}_{t}:=B_{T-t})_{t\in[0,T]}
. We say that a process
(X^{\leftarrow}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the
backward SDE

\mathrm{d}X^{\leftarrow}_{t}=a_{t}(X^{\leftarrow}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{t},~t\in[0,T];~X^{\leftarrow}_{T}\sim\nu

if its time-reversal
(X_{t}=X^{\leftarrow}_{T-t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the following forward SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-a_{T-t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~t\in[0,T];~X_{0}\sim\nu.

Remark 4
.

Intuitively, one can understand the backward SDE through the following Euler-Maruyama discretization: with
\Delta t>0
:

\displaystyle X^{\leftarrow}_{t-\Delta t}

\displaystyle\approx X^{\leftarrow}_{t}+a_{t}(X^{\leftarrow}_{t})(-\Delta t)+\sigma(B^{\leftarrow}_{t-\Delta t}-B^{\leftarrow}_{t})

\displaystyle\iff X_{T-t+\Delta t}

\displaystyle\approx X_{T-t}-a_{t}(X_{T-t})\Delta t+\sigma(B_{T-t+\Delta t}-B_{T-t}).

where
B^{\leftarrow}_{t-\Delta t}-B^{\leftarrow}_{t}\sim\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,\Delta tI\right)
and is independent of
(X^{\leftarrow}_{s})_{s\in[t,T]}
.

The forward and backward SDEs are related through the following Nelson’s relation:

Lemma 2

(Nelson’s relation
(
Nelson, 1967
;
Anderson, 1982
)
)
.

Given a BM
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and its time-reversal
(B^{\leftarrow}_{t}=B_{T-t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, the following two SDEs

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=a_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~X_{0}\sim p_{0};\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=b_{t}(Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{t},~Y_{T}\sim q

have the same path measure if and only if

q=p_{T},\quad\text{and}\quad b_{t}=a_{t}-\sigma^{2}\nabla\log p_{t},~\forall t\in[0,T],

where
p_{t}
is the p.d.f. of
X_{t}
.

Proof.

The proof is by verifying the Fokker-Planck equation. For
X
, we have

\partial_{t}p_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(a_{t}p_{t})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta p_{t}.

Let
\star^{\leftarrow}_{t}:=\star_{T-t}
. Then
p^{\leftarrow}_{t}
satisfies

\partial_{t}p^{\leftarrow}_{t}=\nabla\cdot(a^{\leftarrow}_{t}p^{\leftarrow}_{t})-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta p^{\leftarrow}_{t}=-\nabla\cdot((-a^{\leftarrow}_{t}+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log p^{\leftarrow}_{t})p^{\leftarrow}_{t})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta p^{\leftarrow}_{t},

which means
(X^{\leftarrow}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
has the same path measure as the following SDE:

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-(a^{\leftarrow}_{t}-\sigma^{2}\nabla\log p^{\leftarrow}_{t})(Z_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~Z_{t}\sim p^{\leftarrow}_{t}.

On the other hand, by definition,
(Y^{\leftarrow}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfies the forward SDE

\mathrm{d}Y^{\leftarrow}_{t}=-b^{\leftarrow}_{t}(Y^{\leftarrow}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},~Y_{0}\sim q,

and thus the claim is evident.
∎

We now introduce the concept of backward stochastic integral, which allows us to represent the RN derivative between path measures of forward and backward SDEs.

Definition 2

(Backward stochastic integral)
.

For two continuous stochastic processes

, and the convergence is in the probability sense. When both
X
and

are continuous semi-martingales, one can equivalently define

\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle Y_{t},*\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle:=\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle Y_{t},\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle+[X,Y]_{T},

(15)

where
[X,Y]_{\cdot}
is the cross quadratic variation process
6
6

6

of the local martingale parts of

Remark 5
.

Although rarely used in practice, the backward stochastic integral is sometimes referred to as the Hänggi-Klimontovich integral in the literature. Recall that the Itô integral is defined as the limit of Riemann sums when the leftmost point of each interval is used, while the Stratonovich integral is based on the midpoint and the backward integral uses the rightmost point. The equivalence in

A.2
Optimal Transport: Wasserstein Geometry and Metric Derivative

We provide a concise overview of essential concepts in optimal transport (OT) that will be used in the paper. See standard textbooks
(
Villani, 2003
;
Villani, 2008
;
Ambrosio et al., 2008
;
Ambrosio et al., 2021
)
for details.

For two probability measures
\mu,\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite second-order moments (i.e.,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\|\cdot\|^{2},\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}\|\cdot\|^{2}<\infty
), the
Wasserstein-2 (
\textbf{W}_{\textbf{2}}
) distance
between
\mu
and
\nu
is defined as
\mathrm{W}_{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int\|x-y\|^{2}\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)\right)^{1/2}
, where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all couplings of
(\mu,\nu)
. The Brenier’s theorem states that when
\mu
has a Lebesgue density, then there exists a unique coupling
\left(\operatorname{id}\times T_{\mu\to\nu}\right)_{\sharp}\mu
that reaches the infimum. Here,
\sharp
stands for the push-forward of a measure (defined by
T_{\sharp}\mu(\cdot)=\mu(\{\omega:T(\omega)\in\cdot\})
), and
T_{\mu\to\nu}
is known as the
OT map
from
\mu
to
\nu
, which can be written as the gradient of a convex function.

Given a vector field
v=(v_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
and a curve of probability measures
\rho=(\rho_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
with finite second-order moment on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say that
v

generates

\rho
if the continuity equation
\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}v_{t})=0
,
t\in[a,b]
holds in the weak sense. The
metric derivative
of
\rho
at
t\in[a,b]
is defined as

|\dot{\rho}|_{t}:=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}(\rho_{t+\delta},\rho_{t})}{|\delta|},

which can be interpreted as the speed of this curve. We say
\rho
is
absolutely continuous
if
|\dot{\rho}|_{t}
exists and is finite for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[a,b]
. The metric derivative and the continuity equation are related through the following fact
(
Ambrosio et al., 2008
, Thm. 8.3.1 & Prop. 8.4.5)
:

Lemma 4
.

For an absolutely continuous curve of probability measures
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
, any vector field
(v_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
that generates
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
satisfies
|\dot{\rho}|_{t}\leq\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}
for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[a,b]
. Moreover, there exists an a.s. unique vector field
(v^{*}_{t}\in L^{2}(\rho_{t}))_{t\in[a,b]}
that generates
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
and satisfies
|\dot{\rho}|_{t}=\|v^{*}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}
for Lebesgue-a.e.
t\in[a,b]
, which is
v^{*}_{t}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{T_{\rho_{t}\to\rho_{t+\delta}}-\operatorname{id}}{\delta}
.

Finally, we define the
action
of an absolutely continuous curve of probability measures
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[a,b]}
as
\int_{a}^{b}|\dot{\rho}|_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}t
, which plays a key role in characterizing the efficiency of a curve for normalizing constant estimation. For basic properties of the action and its relation to isoperimetric inequalities such as log-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities (see definitions below), we refer the reader to
Guo et al. (2025, Lem. 3 & Ex. 1)
.

Definition 3

(Isoperimetric inequalities)
.

A probability measure
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a
Poincaré inequality (PI)
with constant
C
, or
C
-PI, if for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}f\leq C\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\|\nabla f\|^{2}.

It satisfies a
log-Sobolev inequality (LSI)
with constant
C
, or
C
-LSI, if for all
0\not\equiv f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}f^{2}\log\frac{f^{2}}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}f^{2}}\leq 2C\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\|\nabla f\|^{2}.

Furthermore,
\alpha
-strong-log-concavity implies
\frac{1}{\alpha}
-LSI, and
C
-LSI implies
C
-PI
(
Bakry et al., 2014
)
.

Proof.

By Girsanov’s theorem (

We first prove the following result

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=a_{t}(x_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle a_{t}(x_{t}),*\mathrm{d}x_{t}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}\left\langle a_{t}(x_{t}),\mathrm{d}x_{t}\right\rangle+2\int_{0}^{T}\operatorname{tr}\nabla a_{t}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t.

Proof.

Therefore, for

The proof builds on the techniques developed in

We define

4

Employing the change-of-variable formula leads to

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\frac{T_{\ell}}{\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1}}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}\left\|\nabla\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{\theta})}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}.

(31)

Since

(32)

to make the l.h.s. of

O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Notably,
T
is the summation over all
T_{\ell}
’s, which has the same order as the total time
T

2
) in the continuous scenario, in
Thm.

. Plugging this

into the second summation, and noticing that by

\left\|\nabla\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\theta^{\prime}}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\pi_{\theta})}^{2}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{x\sim\pi_{\theta}}{\|(\lambda(\theta)-\lambda(\theta^{\prime}))x\|^{2}}\leq(\lambda(\theta)-\lambda(\theta^{\prime}))^{2}m^{2},

we conclude that

.

Step 2.

Now consider the other constraint
26
. By chain rule and data-processing inequality,

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\overline{\mathbb{P}}^{\to})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\operatorname{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\ell-1}}(x_{\ell-1})F^{*}_{\ell}(x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell})\|\pi_{\theta_{\ell-1}}(x_{\ell-1})\widehat{F}_{\ell}(x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}))\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\operatorname{KL}(\mathbf{P}^{\ell}\|{\mathbf{\widehat{Q}}}^{\ell}),

where
\mathbf{P}^{\ell}
is the previously defined path measure of the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{t}+u^{\ell}_{t})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(-\nabla V(X_{t})-\lambda\left(\theta_{\ell-1}+\frac{t}{T_{\ell}}(\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1})\right)X_{t}+u^{\ell}_{t}(X_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},~t\in[0,T_{\ell}];~X_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\ell-1}},

and
{\mathbf{\widehat{Q}}}^{\ell}
is the path measure of
10
initialized at
X_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\ell-1}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(-\nabla V(X_{0})-\lambda\left(\theta_{\ell-1}+\frac{t}{T_{\ell}}(\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1})\right)X_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},~t\in[0,T_{\ell}];~X_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\ell-1}}.

By
Lem.

1
, triangle inequality, and the smoothness of
V
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbf{P}^{\ell}\|{\mathbf{\widehat{Q}}}^{\ell})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}{\|\nabla V(X_{t})-\nabla V(X_{0})-u^{\ell}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}\left[\|\nabla V(X_{t})-\nabla V(X_{0})\|^{2}+\|u^{\ell}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\right]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}{\|X_{t}-X_{0}\|^{2}}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\|u^{\ell}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{t})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t

To bound the first part, note that under
\mathbf{P}^{\ell}
, we have

X_{t}-X_{0}=\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{\tau}+u^{\ell}_{\tau})(X_{\tau})\mathrm{d}\tau+\sqrt{2}B_{t}.

Thanks to the fact that
X_{t}\sim\mu^{\ell}_{t}
under
\mathbf{P}^{\ell}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}{\|X_{t}-X_{0}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}{\left\|\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{\tau}+u^{\ell}_{\tau})(X_{\tau})\mathrm{d}\tau\right\|^{2}}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{\|\sqrt{2}B_{t}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim t\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}{\|(\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{\tau}+u^{\ell}_{\tau})(X_{\tau})\|^{2}}\mathrm{d}\tau+dt

\displaystyle\lesssim t\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{\tau}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{\tau})}+\|u^{\ell}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{\tau})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\tau+dt

\displaystyle\lesssim T_{\ell}\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\left(\|\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{\tau}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{\tau})}+\|u^{\ell}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{\tau})}^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\tau+dT_{\ell},~\forall t\in[0,T_{\ell}],

where the second inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality
(
Cheng et al., 2018
, Sec. 4)
:

\left\|\int_{0}^{t}f_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\right\|^{2}=t^{2}\|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,t)}{f_{\tau}}\|^{2}\leq t^{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,t)}{\|f_{\tau}\|^{2}}=t\int_{0}^{t}\|f_{\tau}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbf{P}^{\ell}\|{\mathbf{\widehat{Q}}}^{\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{P}^{\ell}}{\|X_{t}-X_{0}\|^{2}}\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\|u^{\ell}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{t})}^{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\|\nabla\log\mu^{\ell}_{\tau}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{\tau})}\mathrm{d}\tau+(\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}+1)\int_{0}^{T_{\ell}}\|u^{\ell}_{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\mu^{\ell}_{\tau})}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tau+d\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}\frac{T_{\ell}}{\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1}}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}\|\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi_{\theta})}\mathrm{d}\theta+(\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}+1)\frac{\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1}}{T_{\ell}}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta+d\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}.

Recall that the potential of
\pi_{\theta}
is
(\beta+\lambda(\theta))
-smooth. By
Lem.

13
and the monotonicity of
\lambda(\cdot)
,

\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}\|\nabla\log\pi_{\theta}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi_{\theta})}\mathrm{d}\theta\leq\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}d(\beta+\lambda(\theta))\mathrm{d}\theta\leq d(\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1})(\beta+\lambda(\theta_{\ell-1})).

Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\overline{\mathbb{P}}^{\to})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\left(\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{3}d(\beta+\lambda(\theta_{\ell-1}))+(\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}+1)\frac{\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1}}{T_{\ell}}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta+d\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\left(\beta^{2}dT_{\ell}^{2}\left(T_{\ell}(\beta+\lambda(\theta_{\ell-1}))+1\right)+(\beta^{2}T_{\ell}^{2}+1)\frac{\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1}}{T_{\ell}}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta\right)

Assume
\max_{\ell\in\left[\!\left[1,M\right]\!\right]}T_{\ell}\lesssim\frac{1}{\beta}
, i.e.,
29
. so
\max_{\ell\in\left[\!\left[1,M\right]\!\right]}T_{\ell}(\beta+\lambda(\theta_{\ell-1}))\lesssim 1
, due to
\lambda(\cdot)\leq 2\beta
. We can further simplify the above expression to

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}\|\overline{\mathbb{P}}^{\to})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\left(\beta^{2}dT_{\ell}^{2}+\frac{\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1}}{T_{\ell}}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\lesssim\beta^{2}d\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}T_{\ell}^{2}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}dT^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\lesssim\beta^{2}d\frac{\mathcal{A}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}.

So
29
implies that the r.h.s. of the above equation is
O(1)
.

∎

Finally, we have arrived at the last step of proving
Thm.

4
, that is to decide the schedule of
\theta_{\ell}
’s.

Define
\vartheta_{\ell}:=1-\theta_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\left[\!\left[0,M\right]\!\right]
. We consider the annealing schedule
\lambda(\theta)=2\beta(1-\theta)^{r}
for some
1\leq r\lesssim 1
, and to emphasize the dependence on
r
, we use
\mathcal{A}_{r}
to represent the action of
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}
. The l.h.s. of
27
is

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\int_{\theta_{\ell-1}}^{\theta_{\ell}}(\lambda(\theta)-\lambda(\theta_{\ell}))^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\theta_{\ell}-\theta_{\ell-1})(2\beta(1-\theta_{\ell-1})^{r}-2\beta(1-\theta_{\ell})^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})(2\beta\vartheta_{\ell-1}^{r}-2\beta\vartheta_{\ell}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})(\vartheta_{\ell-1}^{r}-\vartheta_{\ell}^{r})^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})^{2}=\beta^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})^{3},

where the last inequality comes from
Lem.

9
. So to satisfy
27
, it suffices to ensure

\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})^{3}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{m^{2}\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}},

while
28
and
29
are equivalent to

\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})^{2}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{d\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}^{2}},\qquad\max_{\ell\in\left[\!\left[1,M\right]\!\right]}(\vartheta_{\ell-1}-\vartheta_{\ell})\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\beta\mathcal{A}_{r}}.

Since we are minimizing the total number of oracle calls
M
, the Hölder’s inequality implies that the optimal schedule of
\vartheta_{\ell}
’s is an arithmetic sequence, i.e.,
\vartheta_{\ell}=1-\frac{\ell}{M}
. We need to ensure

\frac{1}{M^{2}}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{m^{2}\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}},\qquad\frac{1}{M}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{d\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}^{2}},\qquad\frac{1}{M}\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\beta\mathcal{A}_{r}}.

So it suffices to choose
\frac{1}{M}\asymp\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{m\beta\mathcal{A}_{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\wedge\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{d\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}^{2}}
, which implies the oracle complexity

M\asymp\frac{m\beta\mathcal{A}_{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\vee\frac{d\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{4}},

and the hyperparameter
T_{\ell}
is thus
T_{\ell}\asymp\frac{\mathcal{A}_{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{m\beta}\wedge\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{d\beta^{2}\mathcal{A}_{r}}
according to
32
.

\square

E.1
Proof of
Prop.

1

Proof.

The claim of smoothness follows from
Guo et al. (2025, Lem. 7)
. A similar approach for proving the lower bound of metric derivative was used independently in
Chemseddine et al. (2025, App. B)
.

Throughout this proof, let
\phi
and
\Phi
denote the p.d.f. and c.d.f. of the standard normal distribution
\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,1\right)
, respectively. Unless otherwise specified, the integration ranges are assumed to be
(-\infty,\infty)
.

Note that

\displaystyle\pi(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle\propto\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(x-m)^{2}}{2}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}x^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda+1}{2}x^{2}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{2(\lambda+1)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda+1}{2}\left(x-\frac{m}{\lambda+1}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{2(\lambda+1)}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(x\left|0,\frac{1}{\lambda+1}\right.\right)+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{2(\lambda+1)}}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda m^{2}}{2(\lambda+1)}}}\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(x\left|\frac{m}{\lambda+1},\frac{1}{\lambda+1}\right.\right).

Define
S(\theta):=\frac{1}{1+m^{2}(1-\theta)^{r}}
, and let

\displaystyle\underline{\pi}_{s}(x):\propto\pi(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1/s-1}{2}x^{2}}=w(s)\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(x|0,s\right)+(1-w(s))\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(x|sm,s\right),

where

w(s)=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)m^{2}/2}},\quad w^{\prime}(s)=-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)m^{2}/2}m^{2}/2}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)m^{2}/2})^{2}}.

By definition,
\pi_{\theta}=\underline{\pi}_{S(\theta)}
. The p.d.f. of
\underline{\pi}_{s}
is

f_{s}(x)=\frac{w(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)+\frac{1-w(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right),

and the c.d.f. of
\underline{\pi}_{s}
is

F_{s}(x)=w(s)\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)+(1-w(s))\Phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right).

We now derive a formula for calculating the metric derivative. From
Villani (2003, Thm. 2.18)
,
\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}(F_{\mu}^{-1}(y)-F_{\nu}^{-1}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y
, where
F_{\mu},F_{\nu}
stand for the c.d.f.s of
\mu,\nu
. Assuming regularity conditions hold, we have

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}(\underline{\pi}_{s},\underline{\pi}_{s+\delta})}{\delta^{2}}=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{F_{s+\delta}^{-1}(y)-F_{s}^{-1}(y)}{\delta}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{s}F^{-1}_{s}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

Consider change of variable
y=F_{s}(x)
, then
\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}=f_{s}(x)
. As
x=F_{s}^{-1}(y)
,
(F_{s}^{-1})^{\prime}(y)=\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}y}=\frac{1}{f_{s}(x)}
. Taking the derivative of
s
on both sides of the equation
x=F_{s}^{-1}(F_{s}(x))
yields

0=\partial_{s}F_{s}^{-1}(F_{s}(x))+(F_{s}^{-1})^{\prime}(F_{s}(x))\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)=\partial_{s}F_{s}^{-1}(y)+\frac{1}{f_{s}(x)}\partial_{s}F_{s}(x).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}(\partial_{s}F^{-1}_{s}(y))^{2}\mathrm{d}y=\int\left(\frac{\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)}{f_{s}(x)}\right)^{2}f_{s}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int\frac{(\partial_{s}F_{s}(x))^{2}}{f_{s}(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Consider the interval
x\in\left[\frac{m}{2}-0.1,\frac{m}{2}+0.1\right]
, and fix the range of
s
to be
[0.9,0.99]
. We have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-w(s)=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}\asymp\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}},&\forall m\gtrsim 1\\
-w^{\prime}(s)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}m^{2}/2}{(1+\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2})^{2}}\asymp\frac{m^{2}}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}},&\forall m\gtrsim 1\end{array}\right.

First consider upper bounding
f_{s}(x)
. We have the following two bounds:

\frac{w(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2s}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(m/2-0.1)^{2}}{2\times 0.99}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{m^{2}}{8}},~\forall m\gtrsim 1,

\frac{1-w(s)}{\sqrt{s}}\phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(sm-x)^{2}}{2s}}=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{(sm-x)^{2}}{s}+(1-s)m^{2}\right]\right).

The term in the square brackets above is

\displaystyle\frac{(sm-x)^{2}}{s}+(1-s)m^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{s}\left(sm-\frac{m}{2}-0.1\right)^{2}+(1-s)m^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{2}}{4s}-0.2\left(1-\frac{1}{2s}\right)m+\frac{0.01}{s}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{m^{2}}{4\times 0.99}-0.1m+0.1\geq\frac{m^{2}}{4},~\forall m\gtrsim 1.

Hence, we conclude that
f_{s}(x)\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{m^{2}}{8}}
.

Next, we consider lower bounding the term
(\partial_{s}F_{s}(x))^{2}
. Note that

\displaystyle-\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=-w^{\prime}(s)\left(\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+w(s)\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\frac{x}{2s^{\frac{3}{2}}}+(1-w(s))\phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\left(\frac{x}{2s^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{m}{2s^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right).

As
x\in\left[\frac{m}{2}-0.1,\frac{m}{2}+0.1\right]
and
s\in[0.9,0.99]
, all these three terms are positive. We only focus on the first term. Note the following two bounds:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\geq\Phi\left(\frac{m}{2}-0.1\right)\geq\frac{3}{4},&\forall m\gtrsim 1,\\
\Phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\leq\Phi\left(\frac{m/2+0.1-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\leq\Phi(-0.4m+0.1)\leq\frac{1}{4},&\forall m\gtrsim 1.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, we have

-\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)\gtrsim\frac{m^{2}}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}.

To summarize, we derive the following lower bound on the metric derivative:

\displaystyle|\dot{}\underline{\pi}|_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(\partial_{s}F_{s}(x))^{2}}{f_{s}(x)}\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\frac{m}{2}-0.1}^{\frac{m}{2}+0.1}\frac{(\partial_{s}F_{s}(x))^{2}}{f_{s}(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\frac{m}{2}-0.1}^{\frac{m}{2}+0.1}\frac{m^{4}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-s)m^{2}}}{\mathrm{e}^{-m^{2}/8}}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\gtrsim m^{4}\mathrm{e}^{\left(s-\frac{7}{8}\right)m^{2}}\geq m^{4}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{m^{2}}{40}},~\forall s\in[0.9,0.99].

Finally, recall that
S(\theta):=\frac{1}{1+m^{2}(1-\theta)^{r}}
, and
\pi_{\theta}=\underline{\pi}_{S(\theta)}
. Hence, by chain rule of derivative,
|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}=|\dot{}\underline{\pi}|_{S(\theta)}|S^{\prime}(\theta)|
. Let

\Theta:=\{\theta\in[0,1]:~S(\theta)\in[0.9,0.99]\}=\left[1-\left(\frac{1/0.9-1}{m^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}},1-\left(\frac{1/0.99-1}{m^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{r}}\right].

We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{r}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\pi}|_{\theta}^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{}\underline{\pi}|_{S(\theta)}^{2}|S^{\prime}(\theta)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta\geq\int_{\Theta}|\dot{}\underline{\pi}|_{S(\theta)}^{2}|S^{\prime}(\theta)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\theta\in\Theta}|S^{\prime}(\theta)|\cdot\int_{\Theta}|\dot{}\underline{\pi}|_{S(\theta)}^{2}|S^{\prime}(\theta)|\mathrm{d}\theta=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}|S^{\prime}(\theta)|\cdot\int_{0.9}^{0.99}|\dot{}\underline{\pi}|_{s}^{2}\mathrm{d}s.

For any
\theta\in\Theta
,

|S^{\prime}(\theta)|=\frac{m^{2}r(1-\theta)^{r-1}}{(1+m^{2}(1-\theta)^{r})^{2}}\geq\frac{m^{2}r\left(\frac{1/0.99-1}{m^{2}}\right)^{1-1/r}}{\left(1+m^{2}\left(\frac{1/0.9-1}{m^{2}}\right)\right)^{2}}=\frac{m^{2/r}r(1/99)^{1-1/r}}{(1/0.9)^{2}}\gtrsim m^{2/r}\gtrsim 1,

where in the first “
\gtrsim
” we used the inequality
r\left(\frac{1}{99}\right)^{1-\frac{1}{r}}\geq\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{4}}
that holds for all
r\geq 1
. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 9
.

In the above theorem, we established an exponential lower bound on the metric derivative of the
\text{W}_{\text{2}}
distance, given by
\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{W}_{2}(\underline{\pi}_{s},\underline{\pi}_{s+\delta})}{|\delta|}
. In OT, another useful distance, the
Wasserstein-1 (
\text{W}_{\text{1}}
) distance
, defined as
\mathrm{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|\gamma(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y)
, is a lower bound of the
\text{W}_{\text{2}}
distance. Below, we present a surprising result regarding the metric derivative of
\text{W}_{\text{1}}
distance on the same curve of probability distributions. This result reveals an exponentially large gap between the
\text{W}_{\text{1}}
and
\text{W}_{\text{2}}
metric derivatives on the same curve, which is of independent interest.

Theorem 6
.

Define the probability distributions
\underline{\pi}_{s}
as in the proof of
Prop.

1
, for some large enough
m\gtrsim 1
. Then, for all
s\in[0.9,0.99]
, we have

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{W}_{1}(\underline{\pi}_{s},\underline{\pi}_{s+\delta})}{|\delta|}\lesssim 1.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\int|F_{\mu}(x)-F_{\nu}(x)|\mathrm{d}x

(
Villani, 2003
, Thm. 2.18)
, by assuming regularity conditions, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{W}_{1}(\underline{\pi}_{s},\underline{\pi}_{s+\delta})}{|\delta|}

\displaystyle=\int|\partial_{s}F_{s}(x)|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int\left|w^{\prime}(s)\left(\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\right)\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int\left|w(s)\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\frac{x}{2s^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\int\left|(1-w(s))\phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\left(\frac{x}{2s^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{m}{2s^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)\right|\mathrm{d}x.

To bound the first term, notice that for any
\lambda>0
,

\Phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)\lesssim\begin{cases}\sqrt{s}m\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(x-sm)^{2}}{2s}},&\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\geq\lambda;\\
\sqrt{s}m\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2s}},&\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\leq-\lambda;\\
1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore, using Gaussian tail bound
1-\Phi(\lambda)\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}}
, the first term is bounded by

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{m^{2}}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}\left[2\sqrt{s}\lambda+sm+sm(1-\Phi(\lambda))+sm\Phi(-\lambda)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{m^{2}}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}[\lambda+m+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda\leftarrow\Theta(m)}}{{\lesssim}}\frac{m^{3}}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}=o(1).

The second term is bounded by

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\phi\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{s}}\right)|x|\mathrm{d}x=s\int\phi(u)|u|\mathrm{d}u\lesssim 1.

Finally, the third term is bounded by

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}\int\phi\left(\frac{x-sm}{\sqrt{s}}\right)(|x|+m)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}\int\phi(u)(|u|+m)\mathrm{d}u\lesssim\frac{m}{\mathrm{e}^{(1-s)m^{2}/2}}=o(1).

∎

E.2
Proof of
Prop.

2

Proof.

We first prove a more general result with
\phi
being
any
distribution with weak regularity condition, and then focus on the special case where
\phi=\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,I\right)
.

Note that the LD with target distribution
\phi

\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\nabla\log\phi(Y_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},~Y_{t}\sim\overline{\pi}_{t},

can be perceived as the Wasserstein gradient flow of
\operatorname{KL}(\cdot\|\phi)
.
\overline{\pi}_{t}
satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation
\partial_{t}\overline{\pi}_{t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\overline{\pi}_{t}\nabla\log\frac{\overline{\pi}_{t}}{\phi}\right)

\left(v_{t}:=-\nabla\log\frac{\overline{\pi}_{t}}{\phi}\right)_{t\in[0,\infty)}

(\overline{\pi}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
, and each
v_{t}
can be written as a gradient field of a potential function. Thus, by the uniqueness result in
Lem.

4
, we conclude that

|\dot{}\overline{\pi}|^{2}_{t}=\left\|\nabla\log\frac{\overline{\pi}_{t}}{\phi}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\overline{\pi}_{t})}=\operatorname{FI}(\overline{\pi}_{t}\|\phi)=-\partial_{t}\operatorname{KL}(\overline{\pi}_{t}\|\phi)\implies\int_{0}^{\infty}|\dot{}\overline{\pi}|^{2}_{t}\mathrm{d}t=\operatorname{KL}(\pi\|\phi),

\operatorname{FI}

For the special case where
\phi=\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,I\right)

Def.

3
), the smoothness of
V
, and
Lem.

13
, we can further bound the KL divergence as follows:

\operatorname{KL}(\pi\|\phi)\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{FI}(\pi\|\phi)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi(x)}\|-\nabla V(x)+x\|^{2}\leq\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\|\nabla V\|^{2}+\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\|\cdot\|^{2}\leq d\beta+m^{2}.

E.3
Proof of
Thm.

5

Proof.

By Nelson’s relation (
Lem.

2
),
\mathbb{Q}
is equivalent to the path measure of the following SDE:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=X_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{t},~t\in[0,T-\delta];~X_{T-\delta}\sim\overline{\pi}_{\delta}.

Leveraging Girsanov’s theorem (
Lem.

3
), we know that for a.s.
X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}
:

\displaystyle\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}(X)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\phi(X_{0})}{\overline{\pi}_{\delta}(X_{T-\delta})}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\left(\left\langle X_{t}+2s_{T-t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}}),\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle-\left\langle X_{t},*\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\left(\|X_{t}+2s_{T-t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})\|^{2}-\|X_{t}\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}t.

Note that for
X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}
,
\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\left\langle X_{t},*\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\left\langle X_{t},\mathrm{d}X_{t}\right\rangle+[X,X]_{T-\delta}
and
[X,X]_{T-\delta}=[\sqrt{2}B,\sqrt{2}B]_{T-\delta}=2(T-\delta)d
. Some simple calculations yield

\displaystyle\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}(X)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\phi(X_{0})}{\overline{\pi}_{\delta}(X_{T-\delta})}-(T-\delta)d+\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\left(\|s_{T-t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\left\langle s_{T-t_{-}}(X_{t_{-}}),\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\log Z+W(X)+\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}(X_{T-\delta}).

Thus, the equation
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}=1
implies

Z=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}(X)}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(X_{T-\delta})\approx\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}(X)}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{Z}.

Since
\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}(X)\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}(X_{T-\delta})
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Pr}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}(X)\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}(X_{T-\delta})-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Pr}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}(X)-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right)+\operatorname{Pr}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}(X_{T-\delta})-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right).

The inequality is due to the fact that
|ab-1|\geq\varepsilon
implies
|a-1|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
or
|b-1|\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
for
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. It suffices to make both terms above
O(1)
. To bound the first term, we use the similar approach as in the proof of
18
in
Thm.

2
:

\operatorname{Pr}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}(X)-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right)=\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right)\lesssim\frac{\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})}{\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})}}{\varepsilon}.

Hence, it suffices to let
\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})^{2}\lesssim\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
. To bound the second term, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pr}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger}}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}(X_{T-\delta})-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Pr}_{X\sim\mathbb{Q}}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}(X_{T-\delta})-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right)+\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\pi}_{\delta}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}\overline{\pi}_{\delta}}-1\right|\gtrsim\varepsilon\right)+\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\operatorname{TV}(\overline{\pi}_{\delta},\pi)}{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon.

Therefore, it suffices to make
\operatorname{TV}(\overline{\pi}_{\delta},\pi)\lesssim\varepsilon
.
∎

E.4
An Upper Bound of the TV Distance along the OU Process

Lemma 7
.

Assume that the target distribution
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-V}
satisfies
Assump.

2
. Let
\overline{\pi}_{\delta}
be the distribution of
Y_{\delta}
in the OU process
12
initialized at
Y_{0}\sim\pi
, for some
\delta\lesssim 1
. Then,

\operatorname{TV}(\pi,\overline{\pi}_{\delta})\lesssim\delta(\beta m^{2}+d+\beta d)+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}}\beta m.

Remark 10
.

Consider a simplified case where
\beta\gtrsim 1
and
m^{2}\asymp d
. Then it suffices to choose
\delta\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\beta^{2}d^{2}}
in order to guarantee
\operatorname{TV}(\pi,\overline{\pi}_{\delta})\lesssim\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Our proof is inspired by
Lee et al. (2023, Lem. 6.4)
, which addresses the case where
V
is Lipschitz.

Without loss of generality, suppose
\pi=\mathrm{e}^{-V}
. Let
\phi
be the p.d.f. of
\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,I\right)
, and define
\sigma^{2}:=1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta}\asymp\delta
. We will use the following inequality:
|\mathrm{e}^{a}-\mathrm{e}^{b}|\leq(\mathrm{e}^{a}+\mathrm{e}^{b})|a-b|
, which is due to the convexity of the exponential function. By the smoothness of
V
,
\|\nabla V(x)\|=\|\nabla V(x)-\nabla V(0)\|\leq\beta\|x\|
.

Define
\pi^{\prime}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{d\delta}\pi(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)
, and thus
\overline{\pi}_{\delta}(x)=\int\pi^{\prime}(x+\sigma u)\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u
. Using triangle inequality, we bound
\operatorname{TV}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
and
\operatorname{TV}(\pi^{\prime},\overline{\pi}_{\delta})
separately. First,

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int|\mathrm{e}^{-V(x)}-\mathrm{e}^{-V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)+d\delta}|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\int(\pi(x)+\pi^{\prime}(x))(|V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)-V(x)|+d\delta)\mathrm{d}x.

By the smoothness,

\displaystyle|V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)-V(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla V(x)\|(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}-1)\|x\|+\frac{\beta}{2}(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}-1)^{2}\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta\|x\|\delta\|x\|+\beta\delta^{2}\|x\|^{2}\lesssim\beta\delta\|x\|^{2}.

\displaystyle\implies\operatorname{TV}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\int(\pi(x)+\pi^{\prime}(x))(\beta\|x\|^{2}+d)\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
\int\pi(x)(\beta\|x\|^{2}+d)\mathrm{d}x=\beta m^{2}+d
. Since
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\varphi=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\varphi(\mathrm{e}^{-\delta}\cdot)
, we also have

\int\pi^{\prime}(x)(\beta\|x\|^{2}+d)\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{e}^{-2\delta}\beta m^{2}+d\leq\beta m^{2}+d.

We thus conclude that

\operatorname{TV}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\lesssim\delta(\beta m^{2}+d).

Next,

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(\pi^{\prime},\overline{\pi}_{\delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\left|\int(\pi^{\prime}(x+\sigma u)-\pi^{\prime}(x))\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\right|\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint|\pi^{\prime}(x+\sigma u)-\pi^{\prime}(x)|\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint(\pi^{\prime}(x+\sigma u)+\pi^{\prime}(x))|V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}(x+\sigma u))-V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)|\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x.

Again, by smoothness,

\displaystyle V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}(x+\sigma u))-V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla V(\mathrm{e}^{\delta}x)\|\mathrm{e}^{\delta}\sigma\|u\|+\frac{\beta}{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\delta}\sigma^{2}\|u\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta\mathrm{e}^{\delta}\|x\|\mathrm{e}^{\delta}\sigma\|u\|+\beta\mathrm{e}^{2\delta}\sigma^{2}\|u\|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta\|x\|\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|+\beta\delta\|u\|^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(\pi^{\prime},\overline{\pi}_{\delta})

\displaystyle\lesssim\beta\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\iint(\pi^{\prime}(x+\sigma u)+\pi^{\prime}(x))(\|u\|\|x\|+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|^{2})\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x.

Note that, first,

\displaystyle\iint\pi^{\prime}(x)(\|u\|\|x\|+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|^{2})\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi^{\prime}}\|\cdot\|d^{\frac{1}{2}}+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}d\leq md^{\frac{1}{2}}+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}d;

second,

\displaystyle\iint\pi^{\prime}(x+\sigma u)(\|u\|\|x\|+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|^{2})\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\iint\pi^{\prime}(y)(\|u\|\|y-\sigma u\|+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|^{2})\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint\pi^{\prime}(y)(\|u\|\|y\|+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|^{2})\phi(u)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}y\lesssim md^{\frac{1}{2}}+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}d.

Therefore,
\operatorname{TV}(\pi^{\prime},\overline{\pi}_{\delta})\lesssim\beta\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}}(m+\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}d^{\frac{1}{2}})
. The proof is complete.
∎

E.5
Discussion on the Overall Complexity of RDS

In RDS, an accurate score estimate
s_{\cdot}\approx\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{\cdot}
is critical for the algorithmic efficiency. Existing methods estimate scores through different ways. Here, we review the existing methods and their complexity guarantees for sampling, and leverage
Thm.

5
to derive the complexity of normalizing constant estimation. Throughout this section, we always assume that the target distribution
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-V}
satisfies
m^{2}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi}\|\cdot\|^{2}<\infty
and that
V
is
\beta
-smooth.

(I) Reverse diffusion Monte Carlo.

The seminal work directly leveraged the following Tweedie’s formula
(
Robbins, 1992
)
to estimate the score:

\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}(x)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(x_{0}|x)}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-t}x_{0}-x}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}},

(33)

where

\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(x_{0}|x)\propto_{x_{0}}\exp\left(-V(x_{0})-\frac{\|x_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{t}x\|^{2}}{2(\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1)}\right)

(34)

is the posterior distribution of
Y_{0}
conditional on
Y_{t}=x
in the OU process
12
. The paper proposed to sample from
\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(\cdot|x)
by LMC and estimate the score via empirical mean, which has a provably better LSI constant than the target distribution
\pi
(see
Huang et al. (2024a, Lem. 2)
). They show that if the scores
\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}
are uniformly
\beta
-Lipschitz, and assume that there exists some
c>0
and
n>0
such that for any
r>0
,
V+r\|\cdot\|^{2}
is convex for
\|x\|\geq\frac{c}{r^{n}}
, then w.p.
\geq 1-\zeta
, the overall complexity for guaranteeing
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
is

O\left(\operatorname{poly}\left(d,\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{O(n)}\right),

which is also the complexity of obtaining a
\widehat{Z}
satisfying
1
.

(II) Recursive score diffusion-based Monte Carlo.

A second work
Huang et al. (2024b)
proposed to estimate the scores in a recursive scheme. Assuming the scores
\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}
are uniformly
\beta
-Lipschitz, they established a complexity

\exp\left(\beta^{3}\log^{3}\operatorname{poly}\left(\beta,d,m^{2},\frac{1}{\zeta}\right)\right)

in order to guarantee
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
w.p.
\geq 1-\zeta
.

(III) Zeroth-order diffusion Monte Carlo.

The following work
He et al. (2024)
proposed a zeroth-order method that leverages rejection sampling to sample from
\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(\cdot|x)
. When
V
is
\beta
-smooth, they showed that with a small early stopping time
\delta
, the overall complexity for guaranteeing
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
is

\exp\left(\widetilde{O}(d)\log\beta\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

(IV) Self-normalized estimator.

Finally, a recent work
Vacher et al. (2025)
proposed to estimate the scores in a different approach:

\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}(x)=-\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t}}\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\xi\mathrm{e}^{-V(\mathrm{e}^{t}(x-\xi))}]}{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{e}^{-V(\mathrm{e}^{t}(x-\xi))}]},\quad\text{where}~\xi\sim\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,(1-\mathrm{e}^{-2t})I\right).

Assume that
V
is
\beta
-smooth, and the distributions along the OU process starting from
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-V}
and
\pi^{\prime}\propto\mathrm{e}^{-2V}
have potentials whose Hessians are uniformly
\succeq-\beta I
, then the complexity for guaranteeing
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{Q}\|\mathbb{Q}^{\dagger})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
is

O\left(\left(\frac{\beta(m^{2}\vee d)}{\varepsilon}\right)^{O(d)}\right).

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-t}(-X_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}X_{t})=\mathrm{e}^{-t}(2s_{T-t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t})

\displaystyle\implies\mathrm{e}^{-t_{k+1}}X_{t_{k+1}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{k}}X_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle=2(\mathrm{e}^{-t_{k}}-\mathrm{e}^{-t_{k+1}})s_{T-t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}})+\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}B_{t}.

The covariance between two zero-mean Gaussian random variables

and

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(\Xi_{k},B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_{k}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Xi_{k}(B_{t_{k+1}}-B_{t_{k}})^{\mathrm{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-t_{k+1})}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right)\left(\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{e}^{-(t-t_{k+1})}\mathrm{d}t\cdot I=\sqrt{2}(\mathrm{e}^{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}-1)I.

Appendix G
Review and Discussion on the Error Guarantee

G.1
Literature Review of Existing Bounds

Estimation of
Z
.

Traditionally, the statistical properties of an estimator are typically analyzed through its bias and variance. However, deriving closed-form expressions of the variance of
\widehat{Z}
and
\widehat{F}
in JE remains challenging. Recall that the estimator
\widehat{Z}=Z_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)}
,
X\sim\mathbb{P}^{\to}
for
Z=Z_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta F}
, and that JE implies
\operatorname{Bias}\widehat{Z}=0
. For general (sub-optimally) controlled SDEs,
Hartmann and Richter (2024)
established both upper and lower bounds of the relative error of the importance sampling estimator, yet bounds tailored for JE are not well-studied. Inspired by this, we establish an upper bound on the
normalized variance

\operatorname{Var}\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}
in
Prop.

3
at the end of this section using techniques in Rényi divergence. However, we remark that connecting this upper bound to the properties of the curve (e.g., action) is non-trivial, which we leave for future work.

Estimation of
F
.

Turning to the estimator
\widehat{F}=-\log\widehat{Z}
for
F=-\log Z
, we have

\operatorname{Bias}\widehat{F}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}W-\Delta F=\mathcal{W}-\Delta F=\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{diss}}.

Bounding the average dissipated work
\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{diss}}=\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\to}\|\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow})=-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t
remains challenging as well, as the law of
X_{t}
under
\mathbb{P}^{\to}
is unknown, thus complicating the bounding of the expectation. To the best of our knowledge,
Chen et al. (2020)
established a lower bound in terms of
\mathrm{W}_{2}(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})
via the Wasserstein gradient flow, but an upper bound remains elusive. Furthermore,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{F}^{2}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}(X)}\left(\log Z_{0}-W(X)\right)^{2}
is similarly intractable to analyze.

For multiple estimators, i.e.,
\widehat{F}_{K}:=-\log\left(Z_{0}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X^{(k)})}\right)
where
X^{(1)},...,X^{(K)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}^{\to}
,
Zuckerman and Woolf (2002)
;
Zuckerman and Woolf (2004)
(see also
Lelièvre et al. (2010, Sec. 4.1.5)
) derived approximate asymptotic bounds on
\operatorname{Bias}\widehat{F}_{K}
and
\operatorname{Var}\widehat{F}_{K}
via the delta method (or equivalently, the central limit theorem and Taylor expansions). Precise and non-asymptotic bounds remain elusive to date.

G.2
Equivalence in Complexities for Estimating

and

We prove the claim in

that estimating

with
O(\varepsilon)

\mbox{\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{eq:acc_whp}}\implies\operatorname{Pr}\left(|\widehat{F}-F|\leq 2\varepsilon\right)\geq\frac{3}{4},\quad\text{and}\quad\mbox{\lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{eq:acc_whp}}\impliedby\operatorname{Pr}\left(|\widehat{F}-F|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}.

G.3
1
is Weaker than Bias and Variance

We demonstrate that
1
is a weaker criterion than controlling bias and variance, which is an immediate result from the Chebyshev inequality:

\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right)^{2}=\frac{\operatorname{Bias}^{2}\widehat{Z}+\operatorname{Var}\widehat{Z}}{\varepsilon^{2}Z^{2}},

\operatorname{Pr}\left(|\widehat{F}-F|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\widehat{F}-F)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}=\frac{\operatorname{Bias}^{2}\widehat{F}+\operatorname{Var}\widehat{F}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

On the other hand, suppose one has established a bound in the following form:

\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq p(\varepsilon),\quad\text{for some}~p:[0,\infty)\to[0,1],

and assume that
\widehat{Z}
is unbiased. Then this implies

\operatorname{Var}\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right)^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left(\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right)^{2}\geq\varepsilon\right)\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}p(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\mathrm{d}\varepsilon.

G.4
An Upper Bound on the Normalized Variance of
\widehat{Z}
in Jarzynski Equality

Proposition 3
.

Under the setting of JE (
Thm.

1
), let
(v_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be any vector field that generates
(\widetilde{\pi}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
, and define
\mathbb{P}
as the path measure of
17
. Then,

\operatorname{Var}\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}\leq\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left(14\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}-1.

Proof.

The proof is inspired by
Chewi et al. (2022)
. Note that

\operatorname{Var}\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}\right)^{2}-1=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)+\Delta F}\right)^{2}-1=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}\right)^{2}-1,

which is the
\chi^{2}
divergence from
\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}
to
\mathbb{P}^{\to}
. Recall the
q(>1)
-Rényi divergence defined as
\operatorname{R}_{q}(\mu\|\nu)=\frac{1}{q-1}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\nu}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}\nu}\right)^{q}
, and that
\chi^{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}\|\mathbb{P}^{\to})=\mathrm{e}^{\operatorname{R}_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}\|\mathbb{P}^{\to})}-1
. By the weak triangle inequality of Rényi divergence
(
Chewi, 2022
, Lem. 6.2.5)
:

\operatorname{R}_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}\|\mathbb{P}^{\to})\leq\frac{3}{2}\operatorname{R}_{4}(\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}\|\mathbb{P})+\operatorname{R}_{3}(\mathbb{P}\|\mathbb{P}^{\to}).

We now bound
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{q}
for any
q\in\mathbb{R}
. By Girsanov’s theorem (
Lem.

1
),

\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(X)=\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\langle v_{t}(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right),~\text{a.s.}~X\sim\mathbb{P}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\frac{q}{\sqrt{2}}\left\langle v_{t}(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle-\frac{q}{4}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\frac{q}{\sqrt{2}}\left\langle v_{t}(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle-\frac{q^{2}}{2}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{q^{2}}{2}-\frac{q}{4}\right)\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\sqrt{2}q\left\langle v_{t}(X_{t}),\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle-q^{2}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left[\left(q^{2}-\frac{q}{2}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where the last line is by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Let
M_{t}:=-\sqrt{2}q\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle v_{r}(X_{r}),\mathrm{d}B_{r}\right\rangle
,
X\sim\mathbb{P}
be a continuous martingale with quadratic variation
[M]_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}2q^{2}\|v_{r}(X_{r})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}r
. By
Karatzas and Shreve (1991, Chap. 3.5.D)
, the process
t\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{M_{t}-\frac{1}{2}[M]_{t}}
is a super martingale, and hence
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{M_{T}-\frac{1}{2}[M]_{T}}\leq 1
. Thus, we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{q}\leq\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left[\left(q^{2}-\frac{q}{2}\right)\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right]\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

From Girsanov’s theorem (
Lem.

3
), we can similarly obtain the following RN derivative:

\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}(X)=\int_{0}^{T}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left\langle v_{t}(X_{t}),*\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{t}\right\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right),~\text{a.s.}~X\sim\mathbb{P}.

and use the same argument to show that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{q}
has exactly the same upper bound as
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}}\right)^{q}
. In particular, we can use the same martingale argument, whereas now the
backward
continuous martingale is defined as
M^{\prime}_{t}:=-\sqrt{2}q\int_{t}^{T}\left\langle v_{r}(X_{r}),*\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{r}\right\rangle
,
X\sim\mathbb{P}
, with quadratic variation
[M^{\prime}]_{t}=\int_{t}^{T}2q^{2}\|v_{r}(X_{r})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}r
. Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}\|\mathbb{P}^{\to})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left(14\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)+\frac{1}{4}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left(5\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}}\exp\left(14\int_{0}^{T}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\right).

Thermodynamic Integration

(I) Review of TI.

We first briefly review the thermodynamic integration (TI) algorithm. Its essence is to write the free-energy difference as an integral of the derivative of free energy. Consider the general curve of probability measures
(\pi_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}
defined in
7

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\log Z_{\theta}=-\frac{1}{Z_{\theta}}\int\mathrm{e}^{-V_{\theta}(x)}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta}(x)\mathrm{d}x=-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{\theta}}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta}\implies\log\frac{Z}{Z_{0}}=-\int_{0}^{1}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{\theta}}\partial_{\theta}V_{\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta.

(35)

One may choose time points
0=\theta_{0}<...<\theta_{M}=1
and approximate
35
by a Riemann sum:

\log\frac{Z}{Z_{0}}\approx-\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}(\theta_{\ell+1}-\theta_{\ell})\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}}\partial_{\theta}|_{\theta=\theta_{\ell}}V_{\theta},

(36)

where the expectation under each
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
can be estimated by sampling from
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
. Nevertheless, there is a way of writing the exact equality instead of the approximation in
36
: since

\displaystyle\log\frac{Z_{\theta_{\ell+1}}}{Z_{\theta_{\ell}}}

\displaystyle=\log\int\frac{1}{Z_{\theta_{\ell}}}\mathrm{e}^{-V_{\theta_{\ell}}(x)}\mathrm{e}^{-(V_{\theta_{\ell+1}}(x)-V_{\theta_{\ell}}(x))}\mathrm{d}x=\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(V_{\theta_{\ell+1}}-V_{\theta_{\ell}})},

by summing over
\ell=0,...,M-1
, we have

\log\frac{Z}{Z_{0}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\log\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(V_{\theta_{\ell+1}}-V_{\theta_{\ell}})},

(37)

which constitutes the estimation framework used in
Brosse et al. (2018)
;
Ge et al. (2020)
;
Chehab et al. (2023)
;
Kook and Vempala (2025)
. Hence, we also use TI to name this algorithm.

(II) TI as a special case of AIS.

We follow the notations used in
Thm.

3
to demonstrate the following claim:
TI
37
is a special case of AIS with every transition kernel
F_{\ell}(x,\cdot)
chosen as the perfect proposal
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
.

Proof.

In AIS, with
F_{\ell}(x,\cdot)=\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
in the forward path
\mathbb{P}^{\to}
, we have
\mathbb{P}^{\to}(x_{0:M})=\prod_{\ell=0}^{M}\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}(x_{\ell})
. In this special case,

and hence the AIS equality becomes the following identity, exactly the same as
35

\frac{Z}{Z_{0}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\mathcal{F}}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{e}^{-W}}=\prod_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}}\mathrm{e}^{-(V_{\theta_{\ell+1}}-V_{\theta_{\ell}})}.

(38)

∎

(III) The distinction between
equilibrium
and
non-equilibrium
methods.

In our AIS framework, the distinction lies in the choice of the transition kernels
F_{\ell}(x,\cdot)
within the AIS framework.

In equilibrium methods, the transition kernels are ideally set to the perfect proposal
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
. However, in practice, exact sampling from
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
is generally infeasible. Instead, one can apply multiple MCMC iterations targeting
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
, leveraging the mixing properties of MCMC algorithms to gradually approach the desired distribution
\pi_{\theta_{\ell}}
. Nonetheless, unless using exact sampling methods such as rejection sampling – which is exponentially expensive in high dimensions – the resulting sample distribution inevitably remains biased with a finite number of MCMC iterations.

In contrast, non-equilibrium methods employ transition kernels specifically designed to transport
\pi_{\ell-1}
toward
\pi_{\ell}
, often following a curve of probability measures. This distinguishes them as inherently non-equilibrium. A key advantage of this approach over the equilibrium one is its ability to provide unbiased estimates, as demonstrated in JE and AIS.

(IV) Complexity bounds for TI.

For the TI algorithm in
Algorithm

1
used to estimate

indicates that it suffices to choose
K=\widetilde{\Theta}(\sqrt{d})
intermediate distributions and

particles with multilevel estimation, which leads to a total complexity of

to achieve the requirement in

(note that the condition number of the potential

Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusion (CMCD,
Vargas et al. (2024)
).

We borrow the notations from
Sec.

3
due to its similarity with JE.

Given
(\widetilde{\pi}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
and the ALD
2
, we know from the proof of
Thm.

1
that to make
X_{t}\sim\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
for all
t
, the compensatory drift term
(v_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
must generate
(\widetilde{\pi}_{t})_{t\in[0,T}
. Now, consider the task of learning such a vector field
(u_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
by matching the following forward and backward SDEs:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\to}:~~

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(\nabla\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t}+u_{t})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},~X_{0}\sim\widetilde{\pi}_{0},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow}:~~

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(-\nabla\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t}+u_{t})(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B^{\leftarrow}_{t},~X_{T}\sim\widetilde{\pi}_{T},

where the loss is
\operatorname{KL}(\mathcal{P}^{\to}\|\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow})
, discretized in training. Obviously, when trained to optimality, both
\mathcal{P}^{\to}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow}
share the marginal distribution
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
at every time
t
. By Girsanov’s theorem (
Lem.

3
), one can prove the following identity for a.s.
X\sim\mathcal{P}^{\to}
:
\log\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{\to}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow}}(X)=W(X)+C^{u}(X)-\Delta F
, where
\Delta F
and
W(X)
are defined as in
Thm.

1
, and

C^{u}(X):=-\int_{0}^{T}(\left\langle u_{t}(X_{t}),\nabla\log\widetilde{\pi}_{t}(X_{t})\right\rangle+\nabla\cdot u_{t}(X_{t}))\mathrm{d}t.

We refer readers to
Vargas et al. (2024, Prop. 3.3)
for the detailed derivation. By
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}^{\to}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{\to}}=1
, we know that
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{P}^{\to}}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)-C^{u}(X)}=\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta F}
. As the paper has not established inference-time performance guarantee given the training loss, we prove the following result characterizing the relationship between the training loss and the accuracy of the sampled distribution as well as the estimated normalizing constant.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\widehat{Z}=Z_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-W(X)-C^{u}(X)}
,
X\sim\mathcal{P}^{\to}
be an unbiased estimator of
Z=Z_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta F}
. Then, to achieve both
\operatorname{KL}(\mathcal{P}^{\to}_{T}\|\pi)\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
(where
\mathcal{P}^{\to}_{T}
is the law of
X_{T}
in the sampled trajectory
X\sim\mathcal{P}^{\to}
) and
\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq\frac{3}{4}
, it suffices to choose
u
that satisfies
\operatorname{KL}(\mathcal{P}^{\to}\|\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow})\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
.

Proof.

The proof of this theorem follows the same reasoning as that of
Prop.

4
. For normalizing constant estimation,

\operatorname{Pr}\left(\left|\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=\mathcal{P}^{\to}\left(\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow}}{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{P}^{\to}}-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\lesssim\frac{\operatorname{TV}(\mathcal{P}^{\to},\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow})}{\varepsilon}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(\mathcal{P}^{\to}\|\mathcal{P}^{\leftarrow})}}{\varepsilon}\lesssim 1.

For sampling, the result is an immediate corollary of the data-processing inequality.

I.1
Modified Müller Brown distribution

The Müller Brown potential energy surface is a canonical example of a potential surface used in molecular dynamics. Here, we consider a modified version of this distribution as defined in
He et al. (2024, App. D.5)
. For
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the target distribution is
\pi(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp(-0.1(V_{q}(x)+V_{m}(x)))
, where

\displaystyle V_{q}(x)

\displaystyle=35.0136(\overline{x}_{1}+0.033923)^{2}+59.8399(\overline{x}_{2}-0.465694)^{2},

\displaystyle V_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{4}A_{i}\exp(a_{i}(\overline{x}_{1}-X_{i})^{2}+b_{i}(\overline{x}_{1}-X_{i})(\overline{x}_{2}-Y_{i})+c_{i}(\overline{x}_{2}-Y_{i})^{2}).

In the above equations,
\overline{x}_{1}=0.2(x_{1}-3.5)
,
\overline{x}_{2}=0.2(x_{2}+6.5)
,
A=(-200,-100,-170,15)
,
a=(-1,-1,-6.5,0.7)
,
b=(0,0,11,0.6)
,
c=(-10,-10,-6.5,0.7)
,
X=(1,0,-0.5,-1)
,
Y=(0,0.5,1.5,1)
. The ground truth value of the normalizing constant computed by numerical integral (
scipy.integrate.dblquad
) is
Z=22340.9983
with estimated absolute error
0.0001
.

We run each method with approximately the same oracle complexity. Aside from the quantitative results in
Tab.

1
, we also visualize the samples drawn from each method against the level curves of the potential in
Fig.

2
. It is clear from the table and figure that TI and AIS fail to provide accurate estimates of the normalizing constant or sample from the target distribution due to the deficiency of the exploration of different modes. All four RDS-based methods provide accurate estimates of the normalizing constant, with SNDMC and ZODMC being the two best methods.

I.3
Implementation Details

General implementation details.

For both experiments, we run each method for
1024
rounds and output the mean and standard deviation of all
1024
estimates of
\frac{\widehat{Z}}{Z}
. In each round, we parallelly run
1024
i.i.d. trajectories, which produces
1024
i.i.d. samples from
\pi
and
1024
i.i.d. estimates of the normalizing constant, and we treat the average of the estimates as the final estimate of that round. We record the oracle complexity of each algorithm and tune the hyperparameters to make sure that the oracle complexity for producing
each sample
from
\pi
is between
50000
and
60000
for a fair comparison. For TI, we choose
\lambda_{0}=100
,
\lambda_{i+1}=\frac{1.45}{1+1/\sqrt{d}}\lambda_{i}
until
\lambda_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}
, and
N=32
i.i.d. samples. For AIS, we choose
\lambda_{0}=100
,
M=60000
steps, and ALMC step size
T_{\ell}=0.01
. For all RDS-based methods, we choose the total time duration
T=5
, early stopping time
\delta=0.005
, and
N=50
uniformly spaced time points
t_{n}=\frac{n}{N}(T-\delta)
. Specifically, for RDMC, we use
64
samples from
\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(\cdot|x)
to estimate the score
\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}(x)
, and run LMC for
16
steps with step size
0.01
, initialized by importance sampling from
\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(\cdot|x)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-V(\cdot)}\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(\cdot|\mathrm{e}^{t}x,(\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1)I\right)
with proposal
\operatorname{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{t}x,(\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1)I\right)
; for RSDMC, we choose the number of recursive steps as
2
, use
16
samples from
\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(\cdot|x)
to estimate the score
\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}(x)
, and run LMC for
10
steps with step size
0.01
using the same initialization based on importance sampling; finally, for both ZODMC and SNDMC, we use
1024
samples from
\overline{\pi}_{0|t}(\cdot|x)
to estimate the score
\nabla\log\overline{\pi}_{t}(x)
.

Evaluation metrics for sampling.

In the experiment of Gaussian mixture distribution, in each round, we draw
1024
samples from both the algorithm and the target distribution, and compute the following two metrics to evaluate the quality of the samples. For two sets of samples
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, the MMD is defined as

\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,i^{\prime}\leq n}k(x_{i},x_{i^{\prime}})-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n}k(x_{i},y_{j})+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{1\leq j,j^{\prime}\leq m}k(y_{j},y_{j^{\prime}})},

where
k(x,y)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)
is a multiscale radial basis function (RBF) kernel. Following the implementation in
He et al. (2024)
, we set
K=10
and
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{10}=\{-4,-2,0,...,12,14\}
. Second, the
\text{W}_{\text{2}}
distance is computed by
ot.emd2(a, b, M) ** 0.5
using the Python Optimal Transport (POT) package
(
Flamary et al., 2021
)
, where
\mathtt{a}=\frac{1}{n}1_{n}
,
\mathtt{b}=\frac{1}{m}1_{m}
, and
\mathtt{M}=(\|x_{i}-y_{j}\|^{2})_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m}
.
1_{n}
represents the vector of all ones with length
n
.
