Social support and its related factors among elderly people: a cross-sectional analysis within the PERSIAN Guilan Cohort Study (PGCS)
Authors: Zahra Ahmadnia, Saman Maroufizadeh, Fateme Sheida, Masoud Faghih Akhlaghi, Fariborz Mansour-Ghanaei, Farahnaz Joukar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe worldwide rise in life expectancy has resulted in an expanding elderly population, particularly in developing nations, amplifying the demand for social support to manage the challenges associated with aging. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate social support and its associated factors among older adults in the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) Guilan Cohort study (PGCS) population.MethodsThe present cross-sectional study was conducted on 389 participants of the PGCS population. The social support was measured using 10-item Duke Social Support Index (DSSI-10). Data analysis was performed in SPSS v16 with a significance level of < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of the elderly people was 69.88 (SD = 2.62) and 51.4% were female. The mean total DSSI-10 score was 20.05 (SD = 4.61). Based on the multiple linear regression analysis, lower age (P = 0.001), residency in rural area (P = 0.002) and being in the third tertile of wealth score index (P = 0.011) was statistically associated with lower total DSSI-10 scores. The coefficient of determination (R2) was 9.2%, indicating that 9.2% of the variation in the total DSSI-10 scores was explained by the demographic characteristics.ConclusionBased on the results, it can be concluded that younger elderly individuals, those living in rural areas, and those with lower socioeconomic status experience significantly lower levels of overall social support. These findings highlight the need for focused strategies and interventions to improve social support among these vulnerable subgroups to promote better health and well-being in aging populations.

Introduction

The global increase in life expectancy coupled with declining fertility rates has led to a significant rise in the elderly population worldwide, making aging a critical public health and social issue. According to the World Health Organization, the number of individuals aged 65 and above is rapidly growing, with projections estimating nearly 2 billion older adults by 2050, predominantly in developing countries [1–3]. In Iran, the elderly population is also expanding rapidly, mirroring trends observed in the broader West Asia and Middle East region [4–6].

As individuals age, their need for social support intensifies due to the increasing prevalence of physical, psychological, and social challenges associated with this life stage [7]. Social support, encompassing emotional, instrumental, and informational dimensions, plays a pivotal role in enhancing the well-being and quality of life of older adults by fostering resilience, reducing stress, and promoting healthier behaviors [8–10]. Socioeconomic status and health conditions significantly influence the availability and perception of social support among the elderly [11–13]. However, the specific factors associated with social support in Iranian elderly populations remain underexplored.

Social support in older adults is influenced by marital status, gender, socioeconomic status, and health. Married individuals often report greater support due to emotional and practical assistance, while women generally have broader social networks and men rely more on spouses [9, 11, 14, 15]. Financial stability enhances social participation, whereas lower income and education increase the risk of mental health challenges due to stress and limited healthcare [11, 12, 16–22]. Health status also affects social engagement, with poor health causing isolation and good health supporting better well-being [23]. A systematic review highlights the role of family support in Asia and its positive impact on reducing depression in older adults [24].

Understanding the current status of social support among older adults and identifying its associated determinants is essential for designing effective interventions and policies aimed at promoting successful aging and mitigating the adverse effects of social isolation. This study aims to investigate the social support status of the elderly and explore the factors influencing it, thereby contributing to the growing body of knowledge necessary for enhancing the health and social care systems for aging populations.

We hypothesize that higher socioeconomic status, assessed by wealth index tertiles, is associated with greater social support among elderly individuals. Additionally, we hypothesize that better physical and mental health status are positively correlated with higher perceived social support. Furthermore, elderly individuals living in urban areas are expected to have higher social support than those residing in rural areas. We also hypothesize that smoking is negatively associated with social support levels. Finally, age and gender are hypothesized to influence the degree of social support received by older adults.

Methods

Participants and study design

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted on 389 elderly participants (aged >65 years) who were selected by random sampling from the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies of Iranian Adults (PERSIAN) Guilan Cohort Study (PGCS) population [25], residing in both urban and rural areas of Some’e Sara County, Guilan, Iran. The cohort members had initially been enrolled in the PGCS between October 2014 and January 2017 [26]. In the present study, social support was assessed for all available and eligible participants using the 10-item Duke Social Support Index (DSSI-10) during a single data collection phase through face-to-face interviews. The face-to-face interviews were conducted in a dedicated room at the Guilan Cohort Center. Data collection was carried out by healthcare professionals affiliated with Guilan University of Medical Sciences during the period from December 2024 to April 2025.

All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1403.399).

The inclusion criteria were elderly individuals aged over 65 years who were participants in the PERSIAN Guilan Cohort Study (PGCS) and were available and willing to participate during the study period. The exclusion criteria included those who were unable or unwilling to participate in face-to-face interviews, as well as individuals with a documented diagnosis of severe mental health disorders such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or severe personality disorders noted in their clinical files or a very limited social network that would hinder the meaningful assessment of social support.

Sample size and its calculation

Given that the main aim of this study was to explore factors linked to social support scores in older adults, multiple linear regression was employed for data analysis. As a general rule of thumb, a minimum of 20 subjects per independent (predictor) variable is recommended for multiple linear regression [27]. Therefore, with 17 individual-social predictors in the model, a sample size of at least 340 elderly participants was required.

A total of 2,288 participants aged over 65 took part in the PGCS. Among them, participants were assessed for eligibility according to inclusion criteria. Those unable or unwilling to participate were excluded with reasons documented. Ultimately, 389 participants meeting all criteria were included in the final analysis. Questionnaires

Demographic questionnaire

The demographic variables of participants included age, sex, marital status, number of children, education, place of residency, wealth score index (WSI), insurance, living status, number of chronic diseases, smoking, opium, and alcohol consumption.

The Wealth Index Score (WSI) used in our study was developed based on participants’ socioeconomic indicators such as income, assets, educational level, and living conditions. These variables were combined and categorized into three tertiles to stratify the population by socioeconomic status, with tertile 1 representing the lowest and tertile 3 the highest wealth levels. This approach follows established methods for socioeconomic status assessment in cohort studies to improve comparability and interpretability [12].

10-item Duke Social Support Index (DSSI-10)

The Duke Social Support Questionnaire was developed by Duke in 1984. It consists of 10 items: item 1 uses a 3-point scale, items 2 to 4 use a 7-point scale, items 5 to 10 are rated on a 5-point frequency scale (never, often, rarely, sometimes, and always), and item 10 is rated on a 5-point satisfaction scale ranging from completely dissatisfied to completely satisfied. Two subscales can be identified from this questionnaire: social interaction (items 1 to 4) and satisfaction with social support (items 5 to 10). The reliability coefficient of this questionnaire, estimated by Cronbach’s alpha, was calculated as 0.78 by Hosseini Badanjani (2008), indicating acceptable internal consistency for this instrument [28].

In our study, to ensure validity, the study employed the content validity index (CVI) and content validity ratio (CVR) for assessing content validity. Regarding the CVI, all questions across three sections demonstrated simplicity, clarity, and relevance scores exceeding 0.7, while the CVR for all questions was also above 0.7. To evaluate the scientific reliability of the questionnaire, it was divided into sections, and Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for the total scale as well as the social interaction and social satisfaction subscales, yielding values of 0.894, 0.731, and 0.961, respectively.

Statistical analysis

In this study, categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) and continuous variables as mean (standard deviation (SD)) and median) interquartile range (IQR)(. In univariable analysis, the relationship of DSSI-10 scores with demographic characteristics were examined using Pearson correlation coefficient, independent t-test and one-way ANOVA, as appropriate. In multivariable analysis, a multiple linear regression analysis was used to investigate the association of DSSI-10 scores with demographic characteristics. Also, multiple linear regression was used to control for potential confounding variables. The covariates included in the regression model were selected based on evidence from previous literature regarding factors influencing social support among the elderly, as well as their theoretical relevance. These covariates included age, sex, place of residency (urban/rural), socioeconomic status measured by wealth score index tertiles, smoking status, and other demographic variables considered important in the context of social support. This approach was intended to adjust for confounding effects and isolate the independent associations between these factors and social support outcomes.

All data analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and level of significance was set at 0.05.

Relationship between demographic characteristics and DSSI-10 scores

Univariable analysis

There was significant positive correlation between age and total DSSI-10 scores (r = 0.160, P = 0.002). Similar result was observed for social interaction subscale (r = 0.260, P < 0.001). Both correlations were small in magnitude. The mean of total DSSI-10 scores in elderly people who resident in rural area was significantly lower than those who resident in urban area (P = 0.006). Similar results were observed for social interaction and social satisfaction subscales (P = 0.020 and P = 0.019, respectively). The mean of social interaction scores in smokers was significantly lower than non-smokers (P = 0.033). The mean of total DSSI-10 scores in elderly people who were in third tertile of WSI was significantly lower than those who were in first and second tertile of WSI (P = 0.005). Similar results were observed for social satisfaction subscale (P = 0.010) (Table 3).

Multivariable analysis

In multivariable analysis, according to the regression coefficients, being older (P < 0.001) and residency in urban area (P = 0.008) were statistically associated with higher social interaction scores. The coefficient of determination (R2) was 10.7%, indicating that 10.7% of the variance in the social interaction scores was explained by the demographic characteristics.

For social satisfaction, rural residency (P = 0.011) and being in the third tertile of WSI (P = 0.009) were statistically associated with lower social interaction scores. The model R2 was 6.5%.

For total DSSI-10 scores, lower age (P = 0.001), residency in rural area (P = 0.002) and being in the third tertile of WSI (P = 0.011) were statistically associated with lower total DSSI-10 scores. The model R2 was 9.2% (Table 4).

Discussion

Social support has been identified as a key contributor to the health and well-being of older adults [1, 16, 29]. This factor not only improves their quality of life but also helps decrease mental health problems like depression and alleviates physical difficulties linked to aging, making it a significant topic of research interest [8, 29–31].

Results from the present study revealed that older age and urban residency were significantly associated with higher levels of social interaction, as measured by the DSSI-10. Consistent with previous studies in worldwide, a study in China found a strong and positive correlation between social interaction and self-reported health among elderly people living in urban areas. More frequent interactions within neighborhoods were associated with significant improvements in how these individuals perceived their own health. This underscores the importance of urban environments in fostering social connections for older adults, particularly in light of initiatives aimed at enhancing neighborhood cohesion and social capital in cities [32]. Additionally, age differences play a role in social participation and self-assessed health, as older adults are more vulnerable to social isolation, making social engagement especially important for reducing this risk [33, 34]. Early old age is a period when physical functions are good enough to feel that one is still young enough to work, and efforts are made to maintain social relationships and roles [34]. As a result, it seems that these people were more independent and received less support from their families.

Moreover, with increasing age, individuals often experience a decline in their socioeconomic status such as decreased income, fewer assets, and the loss of a spouse which is typically linked to lower levels of life satisfaction [35, 36].

The results of this study regarding that individuals residing in rural areas and those in the lowest socioeconomic tertile experience significantly lower overall social support and social satisfaction. this finding is consistent with results from various other studies. These studies demonstrated that people living in rural areas frequently encounter difficulties like restricted access to social networks, limited community resources, and physical isolation, which collectively lead to decreased social support [32, 34, 37, 38]. Additionally, the association between lower socioeconomic status and diminished social support has been well-documented. People with fewer financial resources typically have reduced opportunities for social participation due to economic constraints, limited transport, or living in deprived neighborhoods, which can weaken social ties and reduce overall satisfaction with their social environment. Studies from low- and middle-income countries also reflect similar patterns, where poverty is linked to both poorer mental health and lower perceived social support [17, 31, 34, 39].

However, some studies suggest that rural communities may have strong informal networks and a sense of close-knit social cohesion, which can mitigate social isolation for some individuals despite economic hardships. This indicates that the quality and nature of social support in rural settings can vary, depending on cultural, social, and infrastructural factors [40, 41].

In this study, participants were selected via random sampling from the well-established PERSIAN Guilan Cohort, which helps to minimize selection bias. Data collection was performed by healthcare professionals through face-to-face interviews, reducing the chance of reporting bias. Although these procedures reduce the risk of bias, potential sources of selection and reporting bias cannot be fully excluded. However, given the rigorous cohort design, standardized data collection methods, and the training of data collectors, the impact of such biases on the study findings is likely limited.

While our study provides valuable insights into demographic factors associated with social support among the elderly, future research could further explore longitudinal designs to establish causal relationships. Additionally, qualitative studies might deepen understanding of the mechanisms by which demographic and socioeconomic factors influence social support. Research focusing on intervention strategies to enhance social support in vulnerable subgroups, such as rural residents or those with lower socioeconomic status, would be beneficial. Moreover, examining the role of psychological, physical health, and social network size variables as potential mediators or moderators could provide a more comprehensive understanding of social support dynamics in aging populations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between demographic factors and social support, highlighting the need for longitudinal research to assess changes over time. The reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce reporting bias or inaccuracies. Additionally, since the study population was drawn from a specific geographic region, the findings may not be generalizable to elderly populations in different cultural or socioeconomic contexts. Important potential confounding factors, such as mental health status, physical disabilities, or the size of social networks, were not measured, which could affect the observed associations. Furthermore, the statistical models explained only a small proportion of the variance in social support scores (R² ranging from 6.5% to 10.7%), indicating that other unmeasured variables may play significant roles in influencing social support among the elderly.

Conclusion

This study highlights important associations between demographic factors and social support among elderly participants. Older age and urban residency were positively associated with higher levels of social interaction and overall social support, indicating that elderly individuals residing in urban areas tend to experience greater social engagement. Conversely, living in rural areas and belonging to the lower wealth tertile were linked to lower social satisfaction and diminished overall social support scores. Additionally, smoking was associated with reduced social interaction. These findings suggest that social support among the elderly is influenced by socioeconomic status, place of residence, and lifestyle factors. Targeted interventions to enhance social support networks, particularly for rural residents and those with lower socioeconomic status, could help improve wellbeing in this population.
