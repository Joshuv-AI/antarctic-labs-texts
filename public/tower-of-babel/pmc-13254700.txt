A Narrative Review of Candidate Biomarkers of Total Fruit and Vegetable Intake
Authors: Matthew P Madore, Seoeun Ahn, Kijoon Kim, Kunsun Eom, Myoung Hoon Jung, HyunSeok Moon, Jin‐Young Park, Ock K Chun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile an abundance of cohort studies has suggested that greater consumption of fruits and vegetables (FVs) is associated with reduced risk of chronic diseases, current self‐report based dietary assessment tools are susceptible to biases that can reduce their accuracy in estimating dietary intakes, and consequently, the risk estimates obtained from this research. As a result, considerable attention has been directed towards the identification and validation of dietary biomarkers, which represent a more objective method for estimating FV intake and have the potential to improve the validity of future epidemiological studies concerning FV intake and its relationship with health outcomes. Several biomarkers, including urinary potassium, flavonoids, hippuric acid, serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate, serum/plasma vitamin C, and blood and skin carotenoids, appear to offer promise as FV intake biomarkers. However, many possess important limitations that have not been thoroughly explored and addressed, and continued efforts are being made to discover others that may offer further advantages in relation to the cost, ease, and invasiveness of measurement methods. Therefore, this review seeks to discuss the characteristics of a reliable FV intake biomarker, describe and detail the benefits and limitations of existing candidates, and highlight areas where further research may be required.Keywords: carotenoids, folate, fruits, intake biomarkers, vegetables, vitamin C

Introduction

Greater intake of fruits and vegetables (FVs) has consistently been associated with lower rates of morbidity and mortality from numerous diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), diabetes, site‐specific cancers, asthma, and cataracts (Angelino et al. 2019; Aune et al. 2017; Hosseini et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2015; Yip et al. 2019). While these findings have been largely consistent across a number of diverse populations and have subsequently motivated the incorporation of FV recommendations into national dietary guidelines (Services 2020), they have predominantly arisen from studies that use dietary assessment methods prone to both random and systematic measurement errors (Bennett et al. 2017).

Dietary assessment tools frequently used in nutritional epidemiology include food frequency questionnaires (FFQs), 24‐h dietary recalls (24‐h DRs), and food records. These tools serve to provide estimates of an individual's intake of nutrients, foods, or food groups over different time periods. Each of these methods has distinct advantages and disadvantages. Food records and 24‐h DRs can yield more accurate intake estimates but can be time‐consuming, costly, require participant and administrator education, whilst being limited by only reporting recent dietary intakes. In contrast, FFQs estimate dietary intake over longer reference periods and are less cost‐intensive, but are less accurate, can be difficult to understand, and rely on longer‐term memory (Bailey 2021).

These characteristics grant the assessment tools differing levels of utility dependent upon the research question of interest. For example, acute responses of certain biomarkers to specific nutrients or foods are best assessed using the more accurate, short‐term assessment tools such as food records or 24‐h DRs, whereas differences in long‐term health outcomes related to habitual dietary intakes are best assessed using FFQs or multiple 24‐h DRs collected over a greater span of time (Bailey 2021).

Despite improvements in the accuracy of these tools resulting from extensive efforts to quantify and correct measurement errors associated with their use (Freedman et al. 2014, 2017, 2018; Park et al. 2018), inherent biases resulting from their subjective nature, such as social desirability and recall bias, can still attenuate the strength of risk estimates obtained from studies on diet and disease relationships (Freedman et al. 2014; Hebert et al. 1995; Naska et al. 2017; Schatzkin et al. 2003).

Due to these limitations, there has been a growing interest in developing alternative or complementary dietary assessment methods for estimating food and nutrient intakes. Recently, dietary biomarkers have garnered attention for their potential ability to serve this purpose (Heinzmann et al. 2015; McGrath et al. 2016; Poppitt et al. 2005). Described as measurable and quantifiable biological indicators of dietary intake or nutritional status, dietary biomarkers are not dependent on self‐report and may therefore offer a more objective method for assessing food and nutrient intake (Potischman and Freudenheim 2003). Accordingly, the identification and adoption of dietary intake biomarkers have the potential to help improve the accuracy of both food and nutrient intake estimates, as well as strengthen the validity of epidemiological research into their relationships with health outcomes (Potischman and Freudenheim 2003).

Recent advances in metabolomics have allowed for improved identification of putative biomarkers (Praticò et al. 2018). Given the consistent associations between greater FV intake and improved health outcomes, as well as the fact that recent studies reveal the majority of adults in the US are not consuming minimum FV intake recommendations (Lee et al. 2022; Moore et al. 2017), a considerable amount of research has been focused on biomarkers of FVs. Multiple reviews have identified several candidate biomarkers and evaluated the strength of their associations with total or individual FV intake (Baldrick et al. 2011; Goerdten et al. 2026; Pennant et al. 2015; Radtke et al. 2020; Souverein et al. 2015; Woodside et al. 2017; Yuan et al. 2022). However, similar to intake biomarkers for other foods and food groups, validation of these biomarkers has fallen behind the rate of their identification (Landberg et al. 2024). Until recently, this was largely a result of the absence of a standardised validation process. To address this, Dragsted et al. used a consensus‐based procedure to develop a set of eight criteria for the systematic validation of biomarkers of food intake. These include plausibility, dose response, time response, robustness, reliability, stability, analytical performance, and interlaboratory reproducibility (Dragsted et al. 2018). Along with these criteria, they also created a validation assessment system with 8 main questions and sub criteria, which can be used to establish the level of validation of a given biomarker. While the publication of these criteria has allowed for an evaluation of the validity of intake biomarkers for various foods and food groups (Cuparencu et al. 2024; Landberg et al. 2019, 2024), there has yet to be such an evaluation for potential biomarkers of FVs.

In addition to their level of validation, potential differences in the cost, timeliness, and convenience associated with the measurement methods for potential biomarkers of FV intake, such as those between blood and skin carotenoids (Mayne et al. 2010), are important to consider in facilitating their application, similar to current dietary assessment tools. Therefore, the aim of this review is to list and describe candidate biomarkers of FV intake, discuss the evidence supporting their utility, assess their overall validity according to the criteria proposed by Dragsted et al. (2018), highlight their strengths and weaknesses, and identify areas where further research may be needed. This review will focus specifically on markers of total FV intake due to their greater relevance to dietary recommendations for the prevention of chronic disease.

Methods

The PubMed database was searched for literature on biomarkers of total FV intake. The following search terms were utilised: fruits, vegetables, intake, consumption, marker, and biomarker. Candidates that were directly studied for their ability to serve as a biomarker of total FV intake on more than a single occasion were selected for inclusion in this review, which included urinary potassium, flavonoids, and hippuric acid, serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate, serum/plasma vitamin C, blood and skin carotenoids (Baldrick et al. 2011; Radtke et al. 2020; Souverein et al. 2015; Yuan et al. 2022). Additional articles pertinent to the validation of these markers were identified through hand searching and reference tracking. No publication time or design constraints were implemented.

Validation Criteria for FV Intake Biomarkers

As outlined in the position paper authored by Dragsted et al. (2018), there are many important characteristics that must be adequately satisfied for a potential candidate to be deemed a valid intake biomarker. Through an extensive literature review and a consensus‐based procedure, they determined eight criteria important for systematic validation of food intake biomarkers. These criteria are listed and described briefly below.

Plausibility

An effective biomarker of food intake must be demonstrated to be specific to the target food or food group. Food chemistry analysis or experimental results should provide an explanation as to why intake of the food or food group affects the levels of the marker. The content of parent compounds should not be too variable such that it elicits substantial variation in biomarker responses. While variation in the content of such compounds or the absence of strict specificity of a biomarker to a food or food group does not necessarily preclude its validity, the marker must be able to reasonably distinguish the food or food group from others.

Dose–Response

A thorough investigation of dose–response relationships between foods or food groups and their candidate biomarkers is paramount, as it helps establish how responsive the biomarker is to shifts in intake and whether this responsiveness changes across various intake ranges. Controlled intervention studies are the most robust form of evidence to establish dose–response relationships; however, cross‐sectional or longitudinal data with 24‐h food records can also be utilised. In establishing a dose–response, it is important to consider habitual (baseline) intakes of target foods or food groups, the bioavailability of the biomarkers or their precursors, and saturation effects.

Time‐Response

Researchers must investigate the temporal relationship between target foods or food groups and their candidate biomarkers, with an emphasis on determining the half‐life of the biomarker, its kinetics, and the ability of repeated measures to assess intake, all of which are crucial to determining the time period in which the biomarker signifies exposure.

Robustness

The ability of candidate biomarkers to estimate food or food group intakes should be investigated in controlled intervention studies and cross‐sectional studies conducted on diverse, free‐living study populations in multiple settings to assess their applicability to specific demographics and investigate potential interactions with other foods and their constituents.

Reliability

The performance of a candidate biomarker in assessing dietary intake must be compared against a gold standard or an existing reference method known to effectively assess intake to establish its reliability.

Stability

Appropriate methods for collecting, storing, and processing biomarker samples that maintain their quality must be established via careful study of the decomposition of the analytes.

Analytical Performance

The precision, accuracy, and detection limits of the analytical method used for the candidate biomarker should be thoroughly studied to confirm its adequacy.

Inter‐Laboratory Reproducibility

Standardised analytical methods should be developed such that results obtained using candidate biomarker samples are reproducible across different laboratories.

For each of these criteria, Dragsted et al. established a validation assessment system comprised of a set of questions and sub‐criteria to be used to ascertain their degree of validation, which are outlined in Table 1. In this review, determination of whether a criterion has been satisfied is evaluated for the included FV intake biomarkers based on how well the sub‐criteria for these validation questions have been fulfilled. Following this evaluation, each criterion is then classified as one of the following: S (Satisfied, meaning there is sufficient evidence to support the full satisfaction of the criteria), P (Partially satisfied, meaning there is some evidence to support the satisfaction of the criteria but some sub‐criteria may not be satisfied or it is only satisfied under certain circumstances), and N (Not satisfied, meaning the criteria has either not been satisfied by the available evidence or has yet to be studied).

Urinary Markers

Urinary Potassium

Due to the fact potassium is ubiquitous in FVs (National Academies of Sciences 2019), research has revealed FV consumption contributes significantly to total dietary potassium intake in children and adults (Woodruff et al. 2020). Since potassium homeostasis is predominantly maintained by altered renal excretion of dietary potassium (Palmer and Clegg 2016), urinary potassium levels have been considered as a potential biomarker of FV intake (Baldrick et al. 2011). Given that potassium is also found in serum/plasma and that blood samples are more frequently collected in cohort and intervention studies, concentrations of potassium in the blood may appear to be a preferable biomarker. However, randomised controlled trials have found that the relationship between dietary potassium intake and serum/plasma potassium among healthy adults is tenuous and does not reflect a clear dose–response (Cappuccio et al. 2016). This finding is likely due to the fact that, like urinary potassium, plasma potassium increases acutely following ingestion of potassium‐rich meals or supplements; it returns to baseline in around 6 h. Therefore, single blood or urine collections are not well‐suited to capture patterns in regular intake (Rabelink et al. 1990). In contrast, both the area under the curve (AUC) of plasma/serum potassium and 24‐h urinary concentrations increase in conjunction with increased dietary intake and are maintained with continued ingestion of greater quantities of potassium (Macdonald‐Clarke et al. 2016; Rabelink et al. 1990). However, due to the considerable increases in invasiveness, cost and time that accompany the collection and analysis of serial blood draws required to calculate plasma/serum AUCs, as well as the poor correlation between single plasma/serum potassium measurements and potassium intake, 24‐h urinary potassium concentrations are the preferred choice for estimating dietary potassium intake and, subsequently, as a preferable candidate biomarker for FV intake.

Despite the promise urinary potassium seems to possess based on these premises, the few interventional and cross‐sectional studies investigating its potential to serve as a biomarker of intake have largely indicated that although 24‐h urinary potassium concentration may correlate significantly and dose‐dependently with changes in vegetable intake, it does not consistently correlate with changes in fruit intake, and its overall performance in distinguishing differences in total FV intake is poor (Krogholm et al. 2004; Steptoe et al. 2003; Suzuki et al. 2024; Takashashi et al. 2003). While the collection and analysis of multiple 24‐h samples may improve its ability to estimate vegetable intake, such improvements were not observed for fruit intake (Suzuki et al. 2024). Upon further consideration, these findings are not entirely unexpected given some important limitations of urinary potassium in regard to its ability to reflect differences in total FV intake.

The most salient of these limitations is the fact that potassium is found in a variety of foods in addition to FVs, such as rice, legumes, whole grains, dairy products, and some animal proteins (McLean and Wang 2021). While FVs may indeed represent an appreciable proportion of dietary potassium intake among adults in the US, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) have shown their overall contribution is merely a fraction of total intake, with milk, coffee, savoury snacks, and meat‐based dishes also providing meaningful contributions (O'Neil et al. 2012; Woodruff et al. 2020). Alongside these notable contributions from other foods, intake of potassium from supplements also represents an important source of non‐FV intake and may further reduce the specificity of urinary potassium for FVs. Although there is a paucity of data on the quantities of potassium supplied by supplements, research analysing trends in their use among NHANES participants has indicated that around 20%–30% of adults report using potassium supplements, not including multivitamin/multimineral supplements (Kantor et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020).

Additionally, aside from changes in dietary potassium intake, there are a multitude of factors that can influence renal potassium excretion and thus may reduce the ability of urinary potassium to function as a biomarker of FV intake. These include the use of medications such as diuretics, beta‐adrenergic agents, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and renin‐angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors, concentrations of hormones such as insulin, epinephrine, aldosterone, and glucocorticoids, or health conditions such as hypokalemia, hyperkalemia, chronic kidney disease, neurodegenerative diseases, and genetics (Udensi and Tchounwou 2017).

Finally, in addition to the fact urinary potassium concentrations are not very effective at distinguishing differences in total FV intake, the collection of one or multiple 24‐h urinary samples is time‐consuming and may be inconvenient for many individuals, which may ultimately introduce a selection bias in studies using it as a biomarker of FV intake.

Currently, three main methods exist for the determination of potassium concentrations in the urine: the flame photometry, ion‐selective electrode, and colorimetric enzymatic methods. While research comparing these methods has shown they each perform similarly well, with strong positive correlations between the measurements obtained from all three (Albert et al. 2011; Garcia et al. 2018), most of the studies concerning the relationship between urinary potassium and FV intake have utilised the flame photometry method due to its long‐standing history and recognition as a reference method. Regarding stability, the concentration of urinary potassium is able to be maintained for over a decade under proper storage conditions, even without the use of preservatives (Remer et al. 2014).

In sum, while some studies have demonstrated that 24‐h urinary potassium correlates significantly with vegetable intake in select populations, current evidence suggests it correlates poorly with total FV intake and does not represent a particularly attractive or effective biomarker of FV intake.

Urinary Flavonoids

Flavonoids, a large group of bioactive polyphenolic compounds abundant in fruits, vegetables, cereals, nuts, seeds, and flowers, have long been dietary compounds of interest due to their antioxidant, anti‐cancer, and anti‐inflammatory properties, among other biological effects (Ullah et al. 2020). Since certain flavonoids and their metabolites are prevalent in a broad variety of FV and are distributed in the blood and urine, concentrations of single and multiple flavonoids and their metabolites in these biological samples have been considered as potential biomarkers of specific and total FV intake (Brevik et al. 2004; Cassidy and Minihane 2017). However, due to the short half‐life of many flavonoids, their concentrations in fasting morning blood samples are likely to be low and may not reflect habitual dietary intake well (Manach et al. 2005). Therefore, concentrations of flavonoids and their metabolites in 24‐h urine samples, which increase in response to dietary intake over the day of measurement (Hollman et al. 1997; Manach et al. 2003), have garnered the most interest regarding their potential as short‐term FV intake biomarkers. While no specific subset of urinary flavonoid metabolites has been consistently evaluated as a potential biomarker of total FV intake, controlled intervention studies have investigated the performance of similar subsets of metabolites using a high‐performance liquid chromatographic mass spectrometric (HPLC‐MS) quantification method developed and validated by Nielson et al. (Nielsen et al. 2000). These studies found that the sum of either 24‐h or morning urinary flavonoid metabolites, which included phloretin, naringenin, hesperidin, quercetin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin, and tamarixetin, was significantly correlated with the intake of FV, with the correlation of the 24‐h concentrations former being stronger and dose‐dependent (Brevik et al. 2004; Krogholm et al. 2004; Nielsen et al. 2002). Likewise, a number of cross‐sectional studies have also found the sum of urinary flavonoids was dose‐dependently associated with self‐reported FV intake assessed using a variety of assessment tools (Brantsæter et al. 2007; Carlsen et al. 2011; Hill et al. 2025; Krogholm et al. 2012). Notably, in three of these studies, the sum of urinary quercetin, isorhamnetin, tamarixetin, kaempferol, hesperetin, naringenin, eriodictyol, phloretin and apigenin were examined, whereas Hill et al. examined the sum of only quercetin, phloretin, naringenin, hesperetin, kaempferol, and isorhamnetin. Also, while concentrations of total urinary flavonoids were significantly correlated with total FV intake assessed using 1 or 3‐day diet records, their correlation with FV intake assessed via a 30‐day FFQ was not statistically significant, underscoring their ability to specifically reflect recent dietary FV intake (Hill et al. 2025).

At present, the existing literature examining the ability of the sum of certain urinary flavonoid metabolites to serve as a biomarker of total FV intake has suggested it may be a good candidate biomarker; however, there remain some limitations and concerns pertaining to their use that must be considered. Similar to urinary potassium, one limitation is that some metabolites commonly measured as part of the sum of flavonoid metabolites, including quercetin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin, tamarixetin, and apigenin, are not specific to just FVs. They are metabolised following the ingestion of tea, chocolate, coffee, spices, and wine, and therefore differences may be a reflection of the consumption of these foods and beverages rather than FVs (Panche et al. 2016).

Furthermore, while there is substantial overlap in the selection of urinary flavonoid metabolites which comprise the sum of urinary metabolites measured in studies examining their potential as biomarkers of FV intake, there does not exist a universally agreed upon subset. If the sum of urinary flavonoids is to be utilised as a biomarker of intake, there needs to be a concerted effort to standardise the individual metabolites that should be measured.

Also, while the correlation between the concentration of flavonoid metabolites in 24‐h urine samples and FV intake was the strongest, the collection of such samples may be infeasible for larger scale prospective epidemiological studies. A few studies have observed that concentrations of most flavonoid metabolites in 24‐h and spot urine samples are significantly correlated (Mennen et al. 2008), as well as between self‐reported FV intake and metabolite concentrations in spot urine samples (Krogholm et al. 2012; Mennen et al. 2006). While spot samples are less time‐consuming and simpler to collect, the weaker strength and lack of a linear dose–response indicate this may come at the cost of reduced precision in risk estimates for health outcomes (Fraser et al. 2016). In addition, the cost of equipment and expertise required for implementation of HPLC‐MS techniques to quantify concentrations of flavonoid metabolites in urine may be prohibitive (Vogeser and Seger 2008).

Finally, it is important to recognize that flavonoid absorption, metabolism, and excretion can be influenced by several factors, which can result in significant inter‐individual heterogeneity in responses to similar intakes. Thus, such factors must be identified and accounted for if the sum of urinary flavonoid metabolite concentrations is to be used as a biomarker of FV intake. Currently, those suspected to exert an influence on flavonoid responses include age, sex, genetics, prescription medication use, dietary fat, fibre, and alcohol intake, the food matrix, and the gut microbiome (Cassidy and Minihane 2017). Notably, the evidence concerning the influence of these factors is generally weak and inconsistent, stemming mainly from animal or in vitro studies (Fu et al. 2012; Ortega et al. 2009; Tamura et al. 2007). Additionally there may be other factors that affect flavonoid metabolism that have not been studied yet, such as smoking and health status, underscoring a need for well‐designed clinical studies to facilitate a better understanding of factors affecting flavonoid responses.

Although there is a paucity of data on the longer‐term stability of the specific combinations of flavonoids in urinary samples under the storage and handling conditions employed for the HPLC‐MS quantification method outlined by Nielson et al., the concentrations of many of the included compounds were stable for at least 2 years under those used for a Rapid Folin–Ciocalteu method for the quantification of total urinary phenolic compounds described by Medina‐Remón et al. (2009).

Overall, the limited evidence from well‐controlled interventions and cross‐sectional studies suggests that the sum of selected flavonoid metabolites in 24‐h urine samples have good potential as a biomarker of total FV intake, though further research is needed to establish factors which influence flavonoid responses and the selection of metabolites that best reflects overall intakes of FV. Furthermore, despite the presence of a correlation between flavonoid concentrations in 24‐h samples and spot samples, and between concentrations in spot samples and self‐reported FV intake, the lack of a linear dose–response and the appreciably weaker strength of these correlations indicate the use of concentrations from 24‐h samples is preferable.

Urinary Hippuric Acid

In addition to flavonoids, the urinary concentration of one of their main metabolic end products, hippuric acid (HA; as well as its hydroxyl derivatives), which increases in the 12 h following ingestion of polyphenol‐rich meals, has been considered as a potential biomarker of FV intake (de Ferrars et al. 2014; Rechner et al. 2002). Two studies on children and adolescents have yielded promising results regarding the performance of this metric, showing that HA concentrations in 24‐h urine samples were significantly and dose‐dependently correlated with FV intake (or flavonoid intake from FVs), assessed via 3‐day weighed dietary records in both groups, and that cross‐classification into the same or adjacent quartiles was, at minimum, 75% (Krupp et al. 2012; Penczynski et al. 2017). Furthermore, among children, the correlation coefficient between urinary HA and self‐reported FV intake was similar to those for established biomarkers used to estimate dietary protein and potassium intake (Krupp et al. 2012). Likewise, another study on 1185 kidney stone formers aged 10 to 82 observed that 24‐h urinary HA was significantly and dose‐dependently associated with self‐reported FV intake according to a simple food frequency questionnaire, and this association was not meaningfully altered by variables such as age, sex, dietary acid load, and drug use (Guerra et al. 2019). In contrast, a 10‐week pre‐post study on adults aged 18 to 45 looking to lose weight who were given advice to increase FV intake found that, despite increasing intake over the course of the study, the concentration of HA from spot urine samples was only significantly associated with self‐reported vegetable intake at baseline. These results demonstrate that, in contrast with 24‐h collections, the concentrations of HA in spot urine collections are not effective as a biomarker of intake beyond the meal closest to the time of collection (Clarke et al. 2020).

Despite findings that urinary 24‐h HA concentrations are significantly positively associated with self‐reported FV intake using different dietary assessment methods, research on this candidate biomarker is limited, with the majority of the data coming from studies comprised of children/adolescents and kidney stone formers. Further, there are a few potential limitations to this metric that deserve further consideration. One of these is that there are several foods and beverages such as coffee, tea, wine, cocoa products, and whole grains that are rich in polyphenols (including flavonoids) which are metabolized to HA and therefore may increase urinary concentrations independent of FV intake (Mulder et al. 2005; Ogawa et al. 2011; Urpi‐Sarda et al. 2010). In their study on healthy adolescents, Penczynski et al. found that adjustment for coffee, tea, and cocoa consumption did not significantly alter the observed association between 24‐h urinary HA and self‐reported flavonoid intake from FVs; however, they hypothesised this may have been due to the low intake level and proportion of consumers in their cohort, highlighting a need for additional research on the subject (Penczynski et al. 2017). Furthermore, as noted in a few of the aforementioned studies, the common food preservative benzoic acid is also metabolized to HA and may influence the strength of the correlation between FV intake and urinary HA concentrations (Krupp et al. 2012; Penczynski et al. 2017). In one of these studies, data from another publication on benzoic acid intake in children was used to determine that it may correspond to around 20% of the observed HA excretion (Krupp et al. 2012). Considering that no adjustments or controls were implemented for benzoic acid intake in any of the existing research, there is a need to consider its potential influence on the relationship between FV intake and HA excretion in future studies.

Moreover, a number of additional dietary, physiological, genetic, and environmental factors may exert influences on HA metabolism and excretion and must be appropriately considered in investigations of the utility of HA as a biomarker of FV intake. Currently, research suggests that age, sex, body weight, gut microbiome composition, medication use, renal function, milk intake, and certain health conditions such as type 2 diabetes, Crohn's disease, schizophrenia, obesity, and some cancers may be associated with differences in urinary HA concentrations (Calvani et al. 2010; Crozier et al. 2010; Guerra et al. 2019; Penczynski et al. 2017; Urpi‐Sarda et al. 2010). Notably, most of these associations have not been confirmed as independent of possible differences in dietary polyphenol intake that may exhibit collinearity with the factor(s) in question, indicating a need for further study to confirm whether they independently affect urinary HA concentrations.

Lastly, even in the small number of studies investigating the potential of urinary HA to serve as a biomarker of FV intake, the methods used to quantify HA concentrations in urine samples have been inconsistent, with some using a chromatographic method (Clarke et al. 2020; Guerra et al. 2019) and others using a colorimetric method (Krupp et al. 2012; Penczynski et al. 2017). Although both of these methods have been validated for their ability to measure urinary HA (Bhattacharyya et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2011), the colorimetric method tends to yield greater HA values, a finding that has been attributed to the fact it encompasses additional HA derivatives (Ogata et al. 1980). Since some of these derivatives, particularly hydroxyhippuric acids, have been found to be responsive to ingestion of FVs, and the presence of dietary precursors for the other derivatives is typically low, it has been argued that the use of the colorimetric method should be preferred (Krupp et al. 2012; Penczynski et al. 2017). Regardless of the choice made, future studies should try to unify with respect to the measurement method being used to improve overall comparability and interpretation of their findings.

With respect to stability, HA has been shown to be stable at room temperature when stored using filter paper for around 2 weeks, and for a month when stored at 0°C (Ogata and Taguchi 1988), however, data on its stability when stored at cooler temperatures and over longer periods of time is lacking.

Although a few studies have found that 24‐h urinary HA is significantly positively associated with FV intake, there are a number of significant limitations and knowledge gaps that must be addressed before it can be reasonably considered an effective FV intake biomarker.

Blood Markers

Serum/Plasma and Erythrocyte Folate

Another metric that has received attention for its potential to serve as a biomarker of FV intake is the serum/plasma and erythrocyte concentrations of the b‐vitamin folate. FVs are rich sources of dietary folate, and plasma/serum and erythrocyte levels have been found to be sensitive to changes in folate intake within ranges commonly consumed among diverse populations (Duffy et al. 2014; Institute of Medicine Standing Committee on the Scientific Evaluation of Dietary Reference et al. 1998; Novaković et al. 2018). With regards to the time frame of intake reflected by these metrics, plasma/serum folate is considered an early indicator of changes in folate exposure, and changes in correspondence with dietary folate intake in the previous few days, whereas erythrocyte folate responds more slowly to changes in folate intake, typically reflecting intakes over the previous 3–4 months (Duffy et al. 2014).

Existing data from intervention studies and observational research has suggested that serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate levels correlate significantly with self‐reported FV consumption using various assessment methods, albeit weakly (Aparicio Vizuete et al. 2010; Appel et al. 2000; Ashfield‐Watt et al. 2003; Bermejo et al. 2007; Brevik et al. 2005; Brouwer et al. 1999; Coyne et al. 2005; Li et al. 2012; Mikkelsen et al. 2007; Silaste et al. 2003; Souverein et al. 2015; Zappacosta et al. 2013). Of note, associations between serum/plasma folate concentrations and self‐reported FV intake tend to reflect a consistent dose–response relationship, whereas the same is not as evident for erythrocyte folate, despite the existence of a clear dose–response in previous controlled folate feeding studies. This is perhaps a result of the fact that erythrocyte folate tends to reflect longer term differences in intakes and some of the assessment methods utilized in studies examining its association with FV intake focus on more recent, shorter‐term intake. Additionally, there is not nearly as much evidence supporting the ability of erythrocyte folate to serve as a biomarker of FV intake. Furthermore, there are a few important factors that reduce the ability of serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate to serve as a biomarker of FV intake in select populations that have served to deter their widespread adoption.

Most notably, as with potassium, folate is not unique to FV, and is found and consumed in appreciable quantities from a variety of other foods, as well as supplements. Especially problematic is the widespread fortification of breads and other grain products with synthetic folate (folic acid), which became mandatory in the US on January 1st 1998 in order to help reduce the incidence of neural tube defects resulting from insufficient folate intake (Institute of Medicine Standing Committee on the Scientific Evaluation of Dietary Reference et al. 1998). The issue presented by this change is twofold; the proportion of folate intake from foods other than FV increased drastically, and the form contained in these foods is more bioavailable than that naturally found in non‐fortified sources, including FV (Winkels et al. 2007; Zhou et al. 2023). A recent analysis of data from NHANES underscored the pertinence of these points, revealing that from 2007 to 2018, folate intake among US adults was split almost evenly between fortified and natural sources, and that around one third of adults utilized supplements containing folic acid (Zhou et al. 2023). Therefore, even though serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate have been demonstrated to be sensitive to changes in FV intake in previous studies, their utility as biomarkers of FV intake, at least in countries with fortification and/or widespread supplement use is questionable. In these countries, differences in serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate among these populations are likely to be reflective of differences in supplementation and fortified food consumption behaviours, which is likely a partial explanation for why their correlation with reported intakes of FV is weak in many studies.

Another potential reason for the weak nature of this correlation is the existence of factors that affect the absorption, metabolism, and serum/plasma and erythrocyte responses to dietary folate. Previous cross‐sectional and interventional trials have observed that a number of factors, including sex, age, energy intake, smoking, alcohol intake, race/ethnicity, and certain genetic polymorphisms, may influence serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate concentrations independent of dietary folate intake (Baart et al. 2021; Chiuve et al. 2005; Gabriel et al. 2006; Guinotte et al. 2003; Perry et al. 2004), which may subsequently diminish the strength of their correlation. Though the presence of such factors does not preclude the potential utility of serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate as biomarkers of FV intake, it does indicate that an effort to account for these factors is crucial if they are to be used for this purpose.

Furthermore, an additional concern regarding the use of serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate as biomarkers of FV intake is that analytical methods used to determine folate concentrations across studies have been inconsistent, with some studies employing immunoassay and others radioassay techniques (Souverein et al. 2015). Although these methods have both been well validated, these inconsistencies are expressly concerning, as a previous interlaboratory comparison study evaluating the performance of multiple analytical methods for assessing blood folate concentrations revealed that while each method had similar results regardless of the lab they were analysed in, there was notable inter‐method variation in the obtained values (Gunter et al. 1996). At present, due to its inexpensiveness, relatively simple instrumentation, and responsiveness to multiple forms of folate species, the World Health Organization recommends that the microbiological assay should be used in the assessment of folate status (Pfeiffer et al. 2018), and therefore it seems most sensible that this method should be standard for any future research. Additionally, regardless of the method used, the need to collect and analyse blood samples can be time‐consuming, costly, and introduce a selection bias since many prospective participants may decline to have their blood drawn. As for their analytical stability, it has been demonstrated that with proper handling and storage, the concentrations of both plasma/serum and erythrocyte folate can remain stable for well over a year (Fazili et al. 2021; Jansen and Beekhof 2018).

Overall, while research indicates serum/plasma and erythrocyte folate are responsive to changes in short and long‐term FV intake, respectively, they possess limitations that can prevent them from serving as an effective biomarker of intake.

Serum/Plasma Vitamin C

Similar to urinary potassium and plasma folate, the serum/plasma concentration of the antioxidant vitamin C, or ascorbic acid, has been considered as a potential FV intake biomarker since fruits and some vegetables commonly represent the primary dietary sources of vitamin C, and pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated it is responsive to typical intake ranges among healthy individuals (Dehghan et al. 2007; Levine et al. 1996; Ness et al. 1999). Accordingly, it is unsurprising that the majority of human intervention studies have observed that concentrations of serum/plasma vitamin C are positively and significantly correlated with FV intake, although not very strongly (Baldrick et al. 2011; Macdonald et al. 2009; Pennant et al. 2015; Souverein et al. 2015). Importantly, within common daily intake ranges, vitamin C is directly absorbed from the small intestine, and plasma levels peak within 3 h of its ingestion (Olson and Hodges 1987; Padayatty et al. 2004; Sánchez‐Moreno et al. 2003). Thus, while it may represent an effective biomarker of recent, short‐term dietary intake, it may not represent an accurate marker of longer‐term, usual FV intakes. Notwithstanding its promise as a biomarker of FV intake in this context, there exist a few caveats that can complicate its implementation, particularly in modern food environments.

While FVs essentially represent the only natural dietary source of vitamin C, the previous few decades have seen an increased prevalence and consumption of vitamin C enriched/fortified foods, mainly cereal, as well as increased supplementation. In fact, multiple analyses of data from NHANES have found that fortified/enriched products and supplements contribute substantially to total vitamin C intake among US adults and adolescents, and that average intakes from both surpass that from natural foods (Fulgoni and Buckley 2015; Fulgoni 3rd et al. 2011; Newman et al. 2019). The notable contribution of these foods and supplements to overall vitamin C intake would reduce the specificity serum/plasma vitamin C would otherwise have for dietary FVs and offer a potential explanation as to why their correlation can appear weak, particularly in more recent studies. Ultimately, these changes diminish the capability of serum/plasma vitamin C to serve as an effective biomarker of FV intake.

Like serum/plasma folate, another possible reason that the correlation between serum/plasma vitamin C and FV intake is frequently considered weak is that there are multiple factors other than dietary vitamin C intake that can influence the plasma concentrations of vitamin C. Previous investigations have revealed that factors most commonly found to associate with differences in serum/plasma vitamin C concentrations are age, body weight, race/ethnicity, physical activity, pregnancy/lactation, genetic polymorphisms, smoking, alcohol intake, and health status (Carr and Rowe 2020). Consequently, information pertinent to these factors should be collected and they must be accounted for if researchers choose to use plasma vitamin C as a biomarker of FV intake.

Another factor that adversely influences the performance of serum/plasma vitamin C as a FV intake biomarker is the nature of its dose–response curve. Previous studies have determined that the relationship between plasma concentrations and dietary intake of vitamin C is sigmoidal, and that saturation of the blood occurs following around 200–400 mg of dietary vitamin C in healthy individuals (Levine et al. 1996; Lykkesfeldt and Tveden‐Nyborg 2019). Although it has been proposed that concentrations may be able to increase further with very large doses of oral vitamin C ingested multiple times per day, it has not been thoroughly investigated (Lykkesfeldt and Tveden‐Nyborg 2019). The decrease in responsiveness at or beyond these intakes, especially in the presence of the increased total intakes from dietary supplements and fortified/enriched foods that have been seen in the previous decades, can impair the sensitivity of serum/plasma vitamin C to differences in FV intake among those with greater habitual intakes. This exact phenomenon was observed in many of the human intervention studies. In their 2011 systematic review, Baldrick et al. noted that the 8 studies where serum/plasma vitamin C did not increase significantly in response to increased FV intake were those where participants had greater baseline status, approaching levels indicative of saturation (Baldrick et al. 2011). Similarly, while Macdonald et al. observed a significant association between vitamin C and FV intake in groups not provided fruit and vegetables at baseline and both follow‐ups (1 and 2 years, respectively), the association between the two was only significant at baseline for the group provided fruit and vegetables, which they suggest is likely due to the fact their status was already high prior to consumption of additional FVs (Macdonald et al. 2009).

Also, while HPLC with ultraviolet or electrochemical detection has been well‐validated for the assessment of plasma vitamin C (Chung et al. 2001), collection and storage of samples must be very carefully managed, as ascorbic acid is highly unstable and reactive, especially during collection (Leonard et al. 2018; Yin et al. 2022). Research indicates it can be stored for up to 80 days at −70°C (Karlsen et al. 2007), and although there is evidence demonstrating that longer term storage is possible, it requires extremely low temperature cryopreservation, which may not be feasible in many studies (Jenab et al. 2005). Furthermore, not all studies investigating the use of vitamin C as a biomarker of FV intake have detailed standardisation procedures, leading to concerns regarding potential inter‐laboratory variability in results (Baldrick et al. 2011). Indeed, an interlaboratory comparison including nine European laboratories that analysed plasma samples spiked with vitamin C found that good agreement between methods and laboratories was difficult to obtain during the first round of testing. However, after correction of some discordant results due to malfunctioning equipment, there was a fairly low (13%–20%) coefficient of variation for samples within the normal biochemical range of vitamin C, indicating that achieving interlaboratory reproducibility is possible (Bates 1994). Alongside the potential selection bias introduced by the need for blood draws, these special considerations can increase the time and cost of collecting and analysing samples for vitamin C, and represent significant downsides to this method, especially when used on a larger scale.

Based on these considerations, plasma/serum vitamin C would serve best as a biomarker of recent, short‐term FV intake among populations where supplementation and consumption of vitamin C fortified or enriched food is limited, average intakes are not at or beyond those at which blood saturation occurs, and in situations where prolonged storage of samples is not required.

Blood Carotenoids

Due to their presence in a variety of common FVs and the inability of humans to synthesise them endogenously, blood concentrations of certain absorbable carotenoids, tetraterpene pigments synthesised by photosynthetic bacteria, fungi, algae, and plants, have received considerable attention as potential biomarkers of FV intake (Maoka 2020). While around 20 types of carotenoids are found in commonly consumed foods and beverages, just six of them comprise over 90% of circulating blood carotenoid concentrations, including the pro‐vitamin A carotenoids beta‐carotene, alpha‐carotene, and beta‐cryptoxanthin, as well as lycopene, lutein, and zeaxanthin (Maoka 2020). Concentrations of these carotenoids in plasma after a single dose peak within around 24–48 h (Institute of Medicine Panel on Dietary and Related 2000). The total sum of carotenoids in the plasma increase significantly in response to increased consumption of dietary carotenoids from FVs within around 2 weeks and return to baseline after around 3 weeks of previous intake levels (Ahn et al. 2024; Jahns et al. 2014). Thus, in addition to being sensitive to recent shifts in FV intake, they can reflect differences in habitual intakes over the previous 3 weeks. Although serum/plasma concentrations of most carotenoids appear to plateau after prolonged intakes of large doses, most of these doses are well beyond those obtained naturally via the diet (Böhm et al. 2021). Also, although carotenoids are used as dietary additives and can be found in foods other than FVs such as eggs and certain seafood products, these are only minor contributors to the intake of specific carotenoids (mainly beta carotene, lutein, and zeaxanthin), with FVs comprising the majority of dietary intake (Kim et al. 2023; Meléndez‐Martínez et al. 2022). Furthermore, while carotenoid supplements are fairly accessible, research indicates they are used by a much smaller proportion of the US population than vitamin C, folate, and potassium, and are subsequently less of an immediate concern with regard to their potential to diminish the specificity of blood carotenoids to FVs (Kantor et al. 2016; Li et al. 2020). As a result, the ability to measure blood concentrations of these carotenoids, both independently and summed together, have been extensively studied for their capacity to serve as biomarkers for habitual intakes of specific and total FVs. An abundance of cross‐sectional, case–control, prospective, and interventional trials have found that self‐reported dietary intakes of total and individual carotenoids from FVs correlate significantly and dose‐dependently with their concentration in the blood across a broad range of intakes and demographics, as does self‐reported FV intake with the concentration of some single carotenoids and the sum of carotenoids (Baldrick et al. 2011; Burrows et al. 2015; Souverein et al. 2015). Due to their clear advantages over previously studied biomarkers, as well as the consistency of these findings, the Panel on Dietary Antioxidants and Related Compounds of the Institute of Medicine currently views blood concentrations of carotenoids as the best biological markers for the consumption of FVs (Institute of Medicine Panel on Dietary and Related 2000).

Nonetheless, the use of blood carotenoids as a biomarker of FV intake requires consideration. Although the sum of blood carotenoids has been found to be positively associated with FV intake across diverse populations, the overall strength of the correlation, even in diet‐controlled interventions, is weak (Souverein et al. 2015). There are two likely primary explanations as to why this is the case. The first is that there exist multiple dietary and non‐dietary factors which impact carotenoid metabolism, and subsequently blood responses to carotenoid ingestion independent of dietary intake from FV. Previous research has identified that these include sex, body mass index, smoking status, carotenoid supplement intake, alcohol intake, medication use, genetics, plant stanol/sterol, dietary fat, and fibre intake, and may also include pregnancy/lactation status, genetics, menstrual cycle phase, and health status, though further studies are required to confirm the influence of the latter factors (Allore et al. 2019; Bohn et al. 2017; Moran et al. 2018). Therefore, identifying and accounting for these factors is critical for effective implementation of blood carotenoids as a biomarker of FV intake. Second, despite the fact carotenoids are essentially specific to a broad selection of FVs, several commonly consumed FVs including apples, bananas, some berries, grapes, parsnips, potatoes, turnips, and strawberries contain little to no carotenoids (O'Neill et al. 2001), In populations where these items represent a significant proportion of total intake, the strength of the correlation between the sum of blood carotenoids and total FV intake may be attenuated.

Current validated methods for the measurement of blood carotenoids include high‐performance liquid chromatography photo diode array detection (HPLC/PDA) and high‐performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC/MS/MS), with the latter being the preferred method mainly due to its greater sensitivity for most carotenoid analytes (Kopec et al. 2013). Similar to measurement methods for other biomarkers, these can be time‐consuming, expensive, and invasive. The need for blood collection in particular can introduce selection bias due to exclusion of participants that prefer not to have their blood drawn (Mayne et al. 2010). Although these limitations are not much of an issue in smaller studies, they can make the implementation of blood carotenoids as a biomarker of FV intake in larger studies challenging.

When it comes to stability, the structure of carotenoids makes them particularly sensitive to damage or oxidation if exposed to oxygen, light, or heat. However, carotenoid concentrations in blood samples appear to remain stable for up to 10 years as long as they are handled properly and stored below −70°C (Tworoger and Hankinson 2006).

All in all, evidence from human clinical trials suggests that the sum of blood carotenoids is a reliable standalone biomarker of FV intake, reflecting differences in intake over the previous 2–3 weeks.

Alternative Markers

Skin Carotenoids

Due to the cost, invasiveness, inconvenience, and time‐consuming nature of many methods discussed, there has been a recent push towards the identification and validation of biomarkers of FV intake that can be measured using more cost‐effective, quick, non‐invasive, and convenient methods. At present, the main marker that has received a considerable amount of attention in this regard has been skin carotenoids, which can be measured using various optical methods.

In addition to their accumulation in the bloodstream, carotenoids are also stored in subcutaneous fatty tissue, the liver, and the skin (Darvin et al. 2022). In the skin, carotenoids accumulate in lipid lamellae found within the stratum corneum, or the horny outermost layer of the epidermis (Darvin et al. 2022). Similar to blood carotenoid concentrations, skin carotenoid concentrations rise in response to increased dietary intake within as little as 2 weeks, and continue to increase. However, they take 4 weeks or greater to return to baseline following a return to habitual diets as compared to the 2–3 weeks for blood carotenoid concentrations. Therefore, as well as being sensitive to recent shifts in FV intake, they can be used to classify individuals according to their habitual intake over the previous month (Ahn et al. 2024; Jahns et al. 2014).

Due to the presence of over 10 π‐π‐conjugated C=C double bonds, carotenoids are natural filters of high‐energy visible light, and can effectively absorb light in the violet‐green spectral range, allowing their concentration in the skin to be quantified using optical methods such as resonance Raman spectroscopy (RRS) and reflectance spectroscopy (RS) (Ermakov et al. 2018). RRS serves to measure skin carotenoids by evaluating the magnitude of the Raman response from the vibrating carbon backbone of carotenoids following excitation using a low‐power laser or spectrally narrowed LED light, while RS measures the reflection signals of carotenoids in response to a low‐power white light source while topical pressure is applied to the area of measurement (Ermakov et al. 2018). Both of these devices have been thoroughly validated and represent effective methods to objectively and non‐invasively measure skin carotenoid concentrations; however, RS devices may be preferable in certain research settings due to their greater portability and lower cost (Ermakov et al. 2018; Hasnin et al. 2023). Another positive aspect of skin carotenoids as FV intake biomarkers is that due to the absence of a need for sample collection when using RRS and RS devices to assess skin carotenoids, no added care must be taken to ensure long term sample stability.

In recent years, many studies have demonstrated that skin carotenoid concentrations measured using both RS and RRS correlate significantly with both plasma/serum carotenoid concentrations and FV intake estimated using multiple dietary assessment methods, both in cross‐sectional and controlled intervention studies, suggesting they may serve as convenient, non‐invasive, and cost‐effective methods for the objective assessment of FV intake in both adults and children (Hasnin et al. 2023; Hwang et al. 2023; Jahns et al. 2019; Jilcott Pitts et al. 2023, 2025; Radtke et al. 2020).

Although these findings are quite promising, and results have been consistent across various populations, there remains a need for further validation studies including samples diverse in age, race/ethnicity, and health status to confirm the efficacy of these devices across a broad range of individuals before they can be deemed appropriate for widespread use. Likewise, while methods used to measure skin carotenoids may be advantageous over those used to assess blood carotenoids in regards to cost, time, and convenience, they still possess some of the same limitations as blood carotenoids, such as the existence of dietary and non‐dietary factors that can directly influence their concentrations or responsiveness to dietary carotenoid intake and the inability to capture intakes of certain FVs due to their naturally low carotenoid concentrations. Since carotenoids must be transported in circulation to the skin prior to storage, it has been hypothesised that most of the factors affecting blood carotenoid concentrations independent of FV intake also exert some degree of influence on skin carotenoid concentrations. Indeed, existing validation studies for RRS and RS devices have found that factors such as sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol intake, and medication use have been associated with significant differences in skin carotenoid levels, though most of these studies were cross‐sectional, adjustments for dietary sources of carotenoids were infrequently included, and there exists substantial heterogeneity in findings pertaining to many of these (Madore et al. 2023). Additionally, some validation studies have found that recent UV exposure, vitamin C intake, and race/ethnicity were associated with differences in skin carotenoid levels, and therefore may warrant consideration if skin carotenoids are to be used as a biomarker of FV intake (Madore et al. 2023).

Overall, the lower cost, convenience, and reduced invasiveness of the spectroscopy methods used to measure skin carotenoids, as well as the time period of dietary intakes of carotenoids that they reflect, make them an attractive alternative to blood carotenoids as a biomarker of FV intake, particularly in longer‐term observational studies concerning relationships between diet and chronic disease. However, there remains a need for well‐designed clinical studies to better characterise factors that may influence their correlation with FV intake and validate their performance among racially and physiologically diverse populations.

Combined Biomarkers

Since the concentrations of many of the candidate FV intake biomarkers that have been considered in previous research tend to be altered following consumption of compounds present mainly in specific subsets of FVs, it is not unexpected that one of the most common limitations is that they correlate weakly with overall FV intake. In the hopes of addressing this limitation, there has been a growing interest in examining potential combinations of previously studied markers, which should exhibit responsiveness to a broader variety of FVs that may be statistically combined to represent a more effective biomarker of their intake. Though there are only a limited number of studies investigating the relationship between combined biomarker concentrations and FV intake, and their chosen biomarkers and approaches have differed, they have largely yielded favourable results.

Using data from an 18‐week randomised controlled intervention study (FLAVURS) involving sequential increases in FV intake, Yannan et al. carried out an analysis to determine if an integrated biomarker calculated using plasma vitamin C, cholesterol‐adjusted plasma carotenoids, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP; a measure of the antioxidant capacity of plasma, which may be enhanced by FV intake) could better predict FV intake compared to each of the individual markers (Jin et al. 2014). They found that while the integrated biomarker had a greater correlation with the intake of FV compared to the individual markers, the magnitude of the correlation was not significantly different than that of plasma total carotenoid, and that the addition of urinary potassium to the integrated marker did not significantly alter any of the results. Furthermore, they observed that the ability of both markers to predict an individual participant's intake was poor, but suggested that they could be used to rank moderate‐ and high‐FV consumers according to their intake.

In another analysis, McGrath et al. sought to investigate if combining potential FV intake biomarkers would help improve the prediction of FV intake using data from a 4‐week highly controlled randomized dietary intervention study carried out on healthy young adults (BIOFAV), as well as three intervention studies on older participants and/or those at high risk for CVD with less strict dietary control that lasted between 8 and 16 weeks (FAVRIT, FIRST, and ADIT) (McGrath et al. 2016). Their results indicated that in all of the included trials, a combined biomarker model including plasma vitamin C and carotenoids performed better than models containing only vitamin C or lutein, as evidenced by a greater percentage agreement between actual categories of intakes and predicted categories, weighted kappa values, and C‐statistics. Importantly, the improvements in performance were most pronounced in the tightly controlled trial, which the authors hypothesised may be due to the reduction in control over diets, but could also be a result of differences in the health status of participant populations and the FVs selected for consumption. Regardless of the cause, further research is needed to better establish models that can account for other factors that affect biomarker concentrations independent of FV intake. Likewise, the ability of combined biomarkers to estimate FV should be investigated in larger, longer‐term cohort studies to confirm their validity.

Finally, in addition to investigating the dose–response curves between FV consumption and plasma carotenoid, folate, and vitamin C concentrations using individual participant data from 12 diet‐controlled intervention studies, Souverein et al. analysed the performance of a prediction model including combinations of these biomarkers and participant characteristics in estimating FV intake (Souverein et al. 2015). They found that a multivariable fractional polynomial (MFP) model including plasma carotenoids, folate, vitamin C, sex, age, BMI, smoking status, and interaction terms for BMI and plasma lycopene and age and alpha‐carotene performed best in estimating total FV intake, evidenced by the fact the model had the strongest correlation with actual intakes, the lowest root mean squared error, lowest mean bias, and smallest limits of agreement. Notably, the correlation coefficient of this model was far greater than any of those pertaining to correlations between plasma carotenoids, their sum, plasma folate, and plasma vitamin C and observed intakes of FVs, underscoring both the potential for combining multiple metrics and accounting for factors influencing their relationship with intakes of FVs to improve their ability to serve as biomarkers of FV intake. The authors suggested that due to the strong correlations between predictions and actual intake, as well as the low mean bias, the prediction models they created could be used to rank individuals based on their FV intake when validating questionnaires used to measure intake and to estimate average FV intake on a group level. However, as the authors note, even the highest‐performing models showed wide limits of agreement (LoA). Therefore, although these biomarkers appear capable of estimating mean population intakes, they lack the precision necessary to accurately determine an individual's FV consumption.

Altogether, though few in number, these analyses suggest that the statistical combination of multiple individual biomarkers of FV intake, including plasma carotenoids, folate, and vitamin C, in addition to participant characteristics that can affect plasma responses or concentrations, can offer a way to meaningfully improve estimates of average FV intakes among groups. However, currently, they still fall short with regard to estimating individual intakes. Given the positive findings from these analyses, investigation of the effects of including other biomarkers, such as urinary flavonoids and HA, as well as factors that may influence biomarker concentrations, on the ability of predictive models to estimate FV intake should be encouraged. Alas, it is critical to acknowledge that despite the clear advantages that using combined biomarkers may offer, there are also significant disadvantages to this approach. The use of more than one biomarker may necessitate additional sample collection and analysis, which can be time‐consuming, create additional participant burden, and increase project costs, which are particularly prohibitive when it comes to larger scale prospective studies where repeated measures are desirable. Furthermore, careful selection of analytical methods for biomarkers is crucial to ensure comparability of results when combined biomarker approaches are used to estimate FV intake. Encouragingly, previous studies have demonstrated that identification and validation of analytical methods capable of quantifying multiple food intake biomarkers is possible, allowing for standardisation of combined biomarker panels and more robust dietary intake assessment (Beckmann et al. 2020; Hassenberg et al. 2026; Landberg et al. 2024).

Conclusion

In recent years, extensive efforts have been made to discover and validate a variety of FV intake biomarkers that are less prone to biases commonly associated with estimates obtained from current dietary assessment tools. Multiple candidate biomarkers have been identified and studied, including plasma/serum folate, blood and skin carotenoids, plasma/serum vitamin C, and urinary flavonoids, HA, and potassium.

Due to their responsiveness to very recent shifts in FV intake, serum/plasma vitamin C, serum/plasma folate, and urinary HA, potassium, and flavonoids are suited to serve as biomarkers of adherence to shorter‐term dietary interventions involving alterations in FV consumption. In contrast, blood and skin carotenoids and erythrocyte folate, which exhibit responsiveness to changes in FV intake over the previous 2–4 weeks, are better equipped to serve as biomarkers of habitual FV intake in cohort studies or longer‐term intervention studies. Of the candidates that correspond to shorter term FV intake, vitamin C and serum/plasma folate are the most extensively studied and satisfy the most validation criteria. However, widespread food fortification and supplement use can impair the ability of these metrics to function as biomarkers of FV intake. Additionally, plasma/serum vitamin C is highly sensitive and requires careful handling and storage to prevent degradation. Although urinary flavonoids and HA appear to be potentially promising biomarkers, more research is necessary to establish their reliability, robustness, and plausibility.

As for the candidates that correspond to differences in longer‐term FV intake, skin and blood carotenoids satisfy the most validation criteria, though the inter‐laboratory reproducibility of blood carotenoid analyses has not yet been established, and additional research is needed to further confirm the robustness of skin carotenoids. Despite satisfying most validation criteria, erythrocyte folate suffers the same limitations as plasma/serum folate with regards to supplement use and fortified food consumption, and its performance hasn't been studied as comprehensively as that of blood and skin carotenoids. Between blood and skin carotenoids, blood carotenoids have the most robust evidence supporting their efficacy in estimating habitual FV intake among diverse populations, and unsurprisingly, are currently considered the gold standard of habitual FV intake biomarkers by the Institute of Medicine (Institute of Medicine Panel on Dietary and Related 2000). While skin carotenoid measurement offers a potentially viable alternative to blood carotenoids and offers several advantages, including reduced time, cost, and invasiveness, it requires additional validation via controlled clinical trials on diverse populations before it can be established as an effective biomarker of intake.

Another important consideration when examining candidate biomarkers is that their concentrations can be affected by dietary, genetic, environmental, and physiological factors, which are crucial to account for if they are to be used to obtain estimates of FV intake. Apart from a few biomarkers, this is an area of research that is particularly lacking and should be a focus of future studies seeking to further validate FV intake biomarkers.

Furthermore, various FVs contain limited quantities of carotenoids, vitamin C, folate, and flavonoids, which may impair the ability of any of these individual markers to capture total FV intake. It has been proposed that a combined biomarker obtained from a statistical combination of these individual markers might serve as a better estimator of total FV intake. Indeed, analyses of strictly controlled dietary intervention studies have provided convincing evidence that using a combination of plasma carotenoids, vitamin C, and folate significantly improves FV intake estimations compared to individual biomarkers. However, the use of a combined biomarker does not negate and may even compound the limitations of the individual biomarkers it contains.

Lastly, while some of the biomarkers discussed can provide reliable estimates of total FV intake, they may not capture the differential health effects that have been observed for specific subgroups of FV in previous research (Zhang et al. 2024; Zurbau et al. 2020). However, many nutrients whose concentration in various tissues have been proposed as biomarkers of FV intake are also consumed in greater quantities among those who have greater intake of FVs considered “high metabolic quality” based on their association with improved cardiometabolic biomarkers, including potassium, carotenoids, and flavonoids (Zhang et al. 2024). Therefore, in some cases, the limited quantities of nutrients in specific types of FV may enhance rather than impair their ability to capture intakes of those with greater health‐promoting qualities.

In summary, current research suggests a few biomarkers may effectively estimate habitual or recent, shorter‐term FV intake, and that combinations of these biomarkers may present an even more accurate estimate of mean population intakes, but that they are not yet capable of reasonably estimating intake at the individual level. Further, in spite of their apparent improvements on previous assessment tools, they are not without limitations, and there remain important gaps in knowledge pertaining to additional factors that may affect concentrations of some of these markers, the most appropriate analytical procedures, inter‐laboratory reproducibility of results obtained from these procedures, and their efficacy in diverse populations. Thus, moving forward, in addition to continuing efforts to identify novel markers, it is critical to conduct well‐controlled clinical trials designed to address these gaps.
