Influence of Hydrothermal Temperature on Structure and Optical Response of Ammonium Tungsten Bronze toward Advanced NIR Shielding
Authors: Tanisara Noinonmueng, Thanaphon Kansaard, Wanichaya Mekprasart, Kanokthip Boonyarattanakalin, Chalicheemalapalli Kulala Jayasankar, Wisanu Pecharapa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ammonium tungsten bronze (AWO) was prepared through a two-step hydrothermal route. In the first stage, tungsten trioxide (WO3) was obtained hydrothermally from sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4). In the second stage, the obtained WO3 powder was reacted with thiourea (CH4N2S) under hydrothermal conditions at 100–150 °C for 24 h. Structural features, crystallinity, and elemental composition were examined using X-ray diffractometer (XRD) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), while the morphology was evaluated by scanning electron microscope (SEM). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) verified the mixed-valence tungsten states in AWO, and the optical behavior was assessed by UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–vis DRS). At 100–120 °C, the diffraction profiles were mainly associated with WO3. When the synthesis temperature was raised to 130–150 °C, the hexagonal NH4WO3 phase became evident, its crystallinity improved, and WO3 reflection progressively diminished. The resulting AWO showed strong infrared attenuation, supporting its potential use as a broad-rang NIR-shielding material.

Introduction

Under increasing pressure to lower energy demand, smart-window technology provides a practical route for improving building energy efficiency. By regulating solar heat input, such glazing systems can reduce annual energy consumption in buildings. Control of near-infrared radiation (NIR, 780–2500 nm) has therefore become increasingly important for environmentally responsible energy management. , When conventional glass permits excessive NIR transmission, heat accumulates indoors, which increases air-conditioning load and power use. , Accordingly, sustainable window materials that suppress NIR radiation while preserving high visible-light transparency (VLT) are of considerable interest for energy-efficient building applications. −

Among candidate functional oxides for this application, tungsten trioxide (WO3) is particularly attractive because its optical and electronic characteristics can be tuned. , As a transition-metal oxide, WO3 displays electrochromic, photochromic, and thermochromic responses that are associated with reversible incorporation and removal of small cations, including H+, Li+, and NH4 +. , Cation intercalation partially converts W6+ into W5+ and produces localized electronic carriers, thereby modifying light absorption and electrical transport. These properties have led to a broad investigation of WO3-based materials for smart windows, electrochromic components, gas sensors, and photocatalytic systems. ,

Ammonium tungsten bronze ((NH4) x WO3), a reduced derivative of WO3, is a promising material for NIR shielding because it combines metallic appearance, high electrical conductivity, strong NIR absorption, and relatively high transmission in the visible range. Incorporation of NH4 + into the WO3 framework promotes partial tungsten reduction and helps stabilize the hexagonal tungsten-bronze lattice, enabling greater electron delocalization and improved optical response. The coexistence of W6+/W5+ states, together with the open bronze-type crystal structure, is therefore central to its strong NIR-blocking behavior and energy-saving value.

Hydrothermal processing has become an effective strategy for obtaining tungsten-bronze nanostructures with adjustable morphology and crystallinity. Parameters such as reaction temperature and duration strongly influence phase development and the optical properties of (NH4) x WO3. Clarifying how synthesis temperature relates to crystal structure and optical response is therefore necessary for performance optimization.

Here, (NH4) x WO3 nanostructures were produced by hydrothermal synthesis to clarify how reaction temperature affects structural evolution and NIR-shielding behavior. Phase composition, morphology, surface chemistry, elemental distribution, and optical properties were examined systematically, providing insights into AWO formation and its applicability as an advanced NIR-shielding component for energy-saving smart windows.

Results and Discussion

Figure b presents the diffuse reflectance spectra of WO3 and AWO prepared at hydrothermal temperatures from 100 to 150 °C. Pristine WO3 shows the greatest visible-region reflectance, approximately 65%, consistent with its yellow color. Raising the synthesis temperature markedly lower AWO reflectance to below 20%, reflecting stronger light absorption and a color transition from yellow to deep blue. This change is associated with partial of W6+ to W5+ reduction and free-electrons generation following NH4 + insertion. In the NIR region, WO3 remains highly reflective and therefore absorbs weakly, whereas AWO, especially the 150 °C sample, shows pronounced NIR absorption and strong shielding performance. These observations demonstrate that higher hydrothermal temperature drives the WO3-to-hexagonal AWO transformation and improves NIR-blocking behavior for energy-saving window use.

Figure c shows Raman spectra for WO3 and AWO powders synthesized at 100–150 °C. For WO3, bands at 646.0, 809.0, and 949.0 cm–1 are assigned to O–W–O bending, W–O–W stretching and terminal WO stretching vibrations, respectively. After NH4 + incorporation at higher temperatures, these bands broaden and their intensities decline, suggesting lattice distortion together with and partial conversion of W6+ to W5+. A new feature near 715 cm–1 appears in the AWO specimens and is linked to oxygen-vacancy formation and development of the tungsten-bronze framework. The broader spectral profiles further support increased structural disorder during AWO formation.

The surface chemistry of WO3 and AWO was examined by X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS), as shown in Figure . Survey spectra confirmed the elemental composition of the samples (Figure a). The deconvoluted W 4f spectra in Figure b–e reveal tungsten oxidation states associated with W5+ and W6+. Each W 4f envelope can be resolved into spin–orbit doublets corresponding to two different tungsten valence states. The dominant W6+ components appear at approximately 38 eV for W 4f5/2 and 36 eV for W 4f7/2, as shown in Figure b,c. At a hydrothermal temperature of 150 °C, the AWO sample exhibits a clear increase in the W5+ contribution. The W5+ 4f7/2 component at 34.7 eV remains less intense than the W6+ 4f7/2 peak at 36.20 eV, as summarized in Figure e and Table . During hydrothermal synthesis, NH4 +/NH3 species and a mildly reducing environment promote partial W6+ reduction to W5+, with oxygen vacancies generated to maintain charge balance. These changes support the formation of NH4WO x with a hexagonal tungsten-bronze structure, in which delocalized electrons and small polaron hopping between W6+ and W5+ centers contribute to the optical response. The valence-state evolution is consistent with the UV–vis-NIR results and reflects coupled changes in charge compensation, phase development, and optical properties.

Thermal-insulation behavior of the WO3 and AWO film was evaluated using a custom-built setup at room temperature (25 °C). As illustrated in Figure a, a sealed box was exposed to an infrared lamp, and the internal temperature was monitored as a function of irradiation time. The measured surface temperature for the WO3 and AWO films were 40.4, 38.0, 36.6, and 36.4 °C, respectively (Figure b), whereas the blank glass sample heated more rapidly. These results indicate that the AWO film synthesized at 150 °C provides superior thermal insulation, NIR absorption, and shielding performance.

Figure a shows that the tungsten oxide film is yellow before UV exposure and gradually turns blue after 5 min of UV irradiation (Figure e), confirming a photochromic response. This change arises from partial, reversible W6+ reduction to W5+ together with oxygen-vacancy generation in the WO3 lattice. The UV-induced modification of the electronic structure creates localized electrons and facilitates small-polaron hopping between the W6+ and W5+ sites. Consequently, near-infrared free-carrier absorption increases, enhancing NIR attenuation. The photochromic color shift therefore reflects not only visible color modulation but also electronic structure changes that contribute to the NIR-shielding behavior of WO3 and AWO films.

Conclusions

WO3 and AWO powders were successfully obtained by hydrothermal synthesis. The influence of hydrothermal temperature on phase formation, morphology, and optical behavior was evaluated systematically. XRD analysis showed a gradual transition from WO3 to hexagonal AWO with increasing reaction temperature, with complete phase development at 150 °C. XPS confirmed the presence of both W6+ and W5+ demonstrating partial tungsten reduction after NH4 + insertion. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and TEM observations indicated that higher synthesis temperature favored well-crystallized rod-like nanostructures. Optical measurements showed that the 150 °C sample delivered the strongest NIR absorption and excellent shielding performance while retaining good visible-light transparency. Overall, controlling hydrothermal temperature is an effective way to obtain AWO with enhanced optical properties for energy-efficient smart-window applications.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of WO3 and AWO Nanoparticles

Analytical-grade reagents were used as received. For the first hydrothermal step, sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O, 4.6 g) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 3 mL) were added to 20 mL of deionized water and stirred vigorously for 30 min. The mixture was placed in a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, and deionized water was added until the vessel reached 80% of its capacity. The sealed autoclave was maintained at 100 °C for 24 h and then allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The yellow WO3 powders was recovered by centrifugation, washed several times with deionized water and absolute ethanol, and dried in air at 100 °C for 24 h. In the second step, 1.0 g of the prepared WO3 and 0.98 g of thiourea (CH4N2S) were dispersed in 20 mL of deionized water, followed by vigorous stirred for 30 min. The suspension was transferred to a 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, and deionized water was added to 80% of the total volume. Hydrothermal treatment was performed at 100–150 °C for 24 h, after which the autoclave was cooled naturally. The resulting green-to-blue AWO powders were separated by centrifugation, rinsed repeatedly with deionized water and absolute ethanol, and dried in air at 100 °C for 24 h.

Characterizations

Sample phase purity was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Rigaku SmartLab, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). Reflectance spectra were collected with a UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer (UV–vis, HITACHI, UH-1450, Japan), and the data were used to estimate band gap energy. Morphology and microstructure were characterized by a field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-7001F) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL, JEM-2100 plus). Surface chemical composition was analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ULVAC-PHI, VersaProbe-4). Transmittance of WO3 and AWO-coated glass films was measured by a UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer (UV–vis, HITACHI, UH-1450, Japan) over the 250–2600 nm wavelength range.

Preparation of WO3 and AWO/PVA Coating on the Glass Substrate

Film preparation was carried out after the glass substrates had been ultrasonically cleaned with DI water and ethanol and dried at room temperature. A 10 wt % PVA solution was prepared separately. The coating precursor was made by dispersing 0.05 g of WO3 or AWO in 5 mL of DI water and then mixing it with 3 mL of PVA solution and 1 mL of glycerol. The resulting precursor was deposited on the glass substrate, and the coated glass was annealed at 80 °C for 24 h.
