Response of soil nematode communities in Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe to mowing frequencies
Authors: Renheng Wu, Zhendan Wu, Yaling Zhang, He Ye, Dianlin Yang, Mei Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soil nematodes are essential indicator of organisms for assessing the health of grassland ecosystems, and their community structure is sensitive to changes in grassland utilization practices. However, there is still a lack of research on how long different mowing frequencies affect the characteristics of soil nematode communities. Therefore, this study aimed to address this gap by studying the impacts of different mowing frequencies M1, M2, and M3 on the nematode community structure and metabolic footprints. The results showed that a total of 45,320 soil nematodes were captured in the experimental area. Among them, the most dominant genera were Cervidellus and Acrobeloides (23.84%), and the dominant genera were Nygolaimus, Chiloplacus and Cephalobus (20.57%). Compared with no mowing treatment, the total abundance of soil nematodes and the abundance of each trophic group showed inflection points in the M2 treatment. The life history of nematode community tended to be k-strategist, and the Wasilewska index was > 1, indicating that soil microbivores were sensitive to the environment and the soil mineralization pathway did not change. NCR value was > 0.5, indicating that the decomposition of grassland organic matter is still dominated by bacterial and fungal channels, which is not affected by mowing frequency. In addition, Soil pH and ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N) are environmental factors that affect nematode communities. In summary, M2 treatment can increase nematode diversity and metabolic activity, improve anti-interference and soil health, and promote soil food web maturation. It is the best cutting frequency for grassland soil health, and has potential benefits for grassland restoration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02444-1.

Introduction

Grasslands are important to terrestrial ecosystems [2] Grassland utilization methods mainly include mowing, grazing, and fencing. As one of the grassland utilization methods, scientific and rational mowing can promote forage regeneration, increase forage yield, and promote material circulation, energy flow, and information transfer ensure grassland stability. Reasonable mowing is the key to maintaining long-term and efficient use of grasslands [30]. At present, research on mowing mainly focuses on different grassland types [38], comparisons between grassland utilization methods, and mowing frequencies with relatively short cycles [19, 20]; [23].These studies mainly combine mowing with the above-ground plant community part and draw conclusions. Some studies have shown that [37], to a certain extent, the response of grassland ecosystems to mowing depends on underground ecological processes. As an essential connection point between above-ground and below-ground ecological processes, soil animals are important drivers of energy flow and material cycling [3].

Soil animals are involved in almost all underground ecological processes and play a crucial role in ecological processes. Among them, soil nematodes are the most abundant metazoans in soil animals, accounting for four-fifths of multicellular organisms on land [3]. They occupy a critical position in the soil food web and can respond rapidly to changes in the external environment, and are regarded as sensitive indicators of changes in grassland ecosystems [22]. Some studies have shown significant differences in the quantity, community structure and ecological indicators of soil nematodes under different grassland types and grassland utilization methods [23, 28]. The research results from [18] showed that the nematode abundance and diversity in typical grasslands are higher than those in desert grasslands [32]. studied soil nematode communities in meadows with different degrees of degradation on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, and pointed out that the nematode community diversity indices in wet meadows and moderately degraded meadows are higher than those in severely degraded meadows. Grassland utilization methods (grazing, fencing, mowing) are the key factors affecting the community structure and diversity characteristics of soil nematodes in grasslands. In the semi-arid grassland ecosystem in Inner Mongolia [26], found that soil nematode abundance increased with the increase of grazing intensity, among which the richness indices of omnivorous-predatory nematodes and bacterivorous nematodes changed significantly [15]. pointed out on alpine meadow grasslands that both nematode abundance and the number of bacterivorous nematodes increased with the increase in grazing intensity, while the number of omnivorous-predatory nematodes showed the opposite trend. In addition, there are studies on soil nematodes in typical temperate grasslands under different grassland utilization methods [23]. Those studies showed that mowing significantly reduced the soil nematode community’s Shannon index and evenness index and was conducive to soil nutrient enrichment [38]. showed that higher mowing frequency (mowing once a year) significantly reduced the abundance, diversity and community structure of soil nematodes, while moderate mowing frequency (mowing once every two years) enhanced these indices to varying degrees. It further shows that the change pattern of nematode index with mowing frequency conforms to the moderate interference theory. The results of [23] showed that mowing frequency had a significant effect on the abundance and diversity of nematodes in meadow steppe, and soil pH was an environmental factor affecting soil nematode communities, further indicating that the consistency of nematode index changes followed moderate mowing frequency. In summary, many research results have enriched the characteristics of grassland soil nematode diversity changes, and confirmed that the grassland ecosystem status indicated by soil nematodes changes with the shift in utilization methods. Although the effects of short-term or very long-term mowing frequency on soil nematodes are involved in the existing research, they are all relatively discrete long-term mowing treatments. Therefore, this study revealed the quantitative relationship between mowing frequency and nematode community changes more accurately through more intensive gradient settings (mowing once a year, mowing once every two years, and mowing once every three years), which can evaluate the effect of grassland management measures and understand the function and stability of soil ecosystems.

Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe is one of the most typical grassland ecological types in Eurasia, and it has important ecological implication. It is an important ecological barrier in North China, and is representative of ecosystem functions such as primary productivity maintenance, species richness support, and organic carbon sequestration. It is a typical representative of grassland ecosystem health and stability [16]. At the same time, it is also the most essential natural pasture and mowing ground in China, which provides critical support for developing animal husbandry [13]. To solve the problem of insufficient livestock feed in autumn and winter, most herdsmen in the Stipa baicalensis grassland area carry out mowing every August. Exploring the response of soil nematodes to different mowing frequencies in Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe can provide a theoretical basis for the scientific assessment of the health status of grassland ecosystems. Therefore, this study took the Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe as the research background to explore the response of soil nematodes to cutting frequency. We hypothesized that compared with mowing once a year (M1) or mowing once every three years (M3): (1) Moderate mowing frequency M2 enhances nematode diversity and ecological indicators; (2) Moderate mowing frequency M2 will enhance the metabolic footprint of each trophic group of nematodes; (3) The main environmental factors driving the change of soil nematode community structure under different mowing frequencies were pH and NH4+-N.

Materials and methods

Study site description

The research area is located in the Stipa baicalensis meadow grassland in Yiminhe Town, Hulunbuir City, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region (48°27’N ~ 48°35’N, 119°35’E ~ 119°41’E), with an altitude of 765–770 m and flat terrain. The climate type in this area is a continental temperate climate, with an annual average temperature of −2.1℃, an annual average precipitation of 344.6 mm, an annual evaporation of 299.5 mm, and a frost-free period of about 102 days. The soil type is dark chestnut soil, and the vegetation type is Stipa baicalensis and Leymus chinensis.

Experimental design

In the herdsmen’s pastures in Yiminhe Town, Ewenki Autonomous Banner, Hulunbuir City, representative areas with open and flat terrain and evenly distributed vegetation were selected for enclosure experiments. In June 2017, the experimental plots began to enclose the grazing-prohibited regions, and a long-term periodic mowing-frequency experiment was set up. The mowing-frequency treatments included a control with no mowing (CK), mowing once a year (M1), mowing once every two years (M2), and mowing once every three years (M3), in which the soil nematode communities were investigated. Four replicates were set for each treatment. The area of each plot was 20 m×8 m = 160 m2, and a 2-m isolation belt was set between each plot and the replicate area. Since 2017, mechanical mowing has been conducted once a year in late August, with no other anthropogenic disturbance in any of the experimental sample plots. As of sampling time 2023, the control has been fenced for 6 years, the M1 treatment has been mowed in 6 rounds, the M2 treatment has been mowed in 3 rounds, and the M3 treatment has been mowed in 2 rounds.

Soil sampling methods

In mid-August 2023 (before mowing), soil samples were collected. Undisturbed points were randomly selected within the experimental plots. A 2-cm soil drill was used to collect soil at soil depths of 0–10 cm and 10–20 cm. Twenty subsamples were randomly collected in each plot, and the collected soil samples were thoroughly mixed into one sample. Impurities such as visible plant roots, weeds, and gravel were removed. Four replicates were taken for each treatment, and the collected soil samples were divided into three parts. One part was placed in a self-sealing bag and stored in a refrigerator at 4℃ for soil nematode extraction; one part was used for the determination of soil physical and chemical properties; and the other part was placed in a self-sealing bag and stored in a −20℃ freezer for the determination of ammonium-nitrogen and nitrate-nitrogen. Soil samples were collected from the team’s long-term positioning experiments and no permission was required.

Measurement methods

Soil nematode collection and identification

Take 100 g from the collected fresh soil samples. Using the modified shallow dish method, place the samples in a room-temperature experimental environment for 48 h, and then isolate and extract soil nematodes. After isolation, kill the nematodes by the gentle heat method and add a fixing solution (8% formalin, 2% glycerin, and two drops of glacial acetic acid). Then label and store them in specimen bottles. Count the nematodes under a stereomicroscope, convert the number to the nematode count per 100 g of dry soil. Refer to [14]《De Nematoden van Nederland》to classify and identify the nematodes. Calculate the relative abundance of each genus of nematodes in the community. According to the morphological characteristics of the nematode head and feeding habitats, divide them into different trophic groups : bacterivorous (Ba), fungivorous (Fu), plant-parasites (Pp), omnivores-predators (Op) and life history c-p values (divided into five groups from extreme r-strategists (c-p value of 1) to k-strategists (c-p value of 5) [5].

Determination of soil physical and chemical properties

The following soil physical and chemical properties were determined according to to Bao [1]. Briefly, soil moisture content by soil drying method; soil bulk weight by ring knife method; soil pH was determined potentiometrically (soil: water = 1:5); soil organic carbon (SOC) by potassium dichromate volumetric method; soil total nitrogen (TN) by Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K9840 Kjeldahl)); soil total phosphorus (TP) by sodium hydroxide melting-molybdenum antimony anti-colorimetric method; soil total potassium (TK) by sodium hydroxide melting-flame photometric method; soil available phosphorus (AP) by sodium bicarbonate extraction-molybdenum antimony anti-colorimetric method; soil available potassium (AK) by ammonium acetate extraction-flame photometric method; soil nitrate-nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen by calcium chloride extraction and flow analyzer determination.

Data analysis

According to the abundance of groups, the dominance of different groups was divided into extremely dominant group, dominant group, sub-dominant group, common group and rare group if the number of individuals accounts for more than 10%, 5–10%, 2–5%, 1–2%, and less than 1% of the total number of individuals, respectively [6].

The basal index (BI) reflects the sensitivity of microbivorous nematodes to the environment; the enrichment index (EI) and the structural index (SI) are for nematode faunal analysis, which are used to assess soil nutrient enrichment and food web structure. The faunal is divided into four quadrants, and the ranges of EI and SI are from 0 to 100. In the formula: e represents the enrichment component in the food web, mainly referring to bacterivorous and fungivorous nematodes with c-p values of 1 and 2; b represents the basal component in the food web, primarily referring to bacterivorous and fungivorous nematodes with c-p values of 2; s represents the structural component in the food web, including bacterivorous, fungivorous, plant-parasitic, and predatory-omnivorous nematodes with c-p values of 3–5.

Metabolic footprint index: Is used to evaluate the contribution of soil nematodes to the grassland ecosystem function [10]. The total nematode metabolic footprint in soil, including the plant-parasites metabolic footprint (PPMF), the bacterial feeders metabolic footprint (BFMF), the fungal feeders metabolic footprint (FFMF) and the omnivores-predators metabolic footprint (OPMF). The calculation formula for nematode metabolic footprint is:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{NMF}}=\Sigma ({\text{Nt}} \times (0.1 \times ({\text{Wt/mt}})+0.273 \times ({\text{W}}{{\text{t}}^{{\text{0}}{\text{.75}}}}))$$\end{document}

where Nt represents the number of nematode individuals in class t; mt represents the c-p value of nematodes in class t; Wt represents nematode biomass. The biomass of various nematodes (i.e., fresh weight) can be directly cited from the values listed by Ferris at http://plpnemweb.ucdavis.edu/nemaplex. The Functional Metabolic Footprint is the total area of the structure and enrichment footprints, as shown as: (SI − 0.5×sf/k, EI); (SI + 0.5×sf/k, EI); (SI, EI − 0.5×ef/k); (SI, EI + 0.5×ef/k), where k is the adjustment coefficient. Data collation and statistical analysis were carried out using Microsoft Excel 2016 and IBM SPSS Statistics 22. One-way ANOVA was used to test the significant differences in nematode community structure and soil environmental factors under different mowing frequencies (P < 0.05). Origin 2021 was used for mapping. The mean ± standard error in the charts was based on untransformed data. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to analyze the correlations between the trophic structure of soil nematodes and environmental factors under different mowing frequencies. Before conducting the ordination analysis, environmental variables with high autocorrelation (> 10) were removed through variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. The data of explanatory variables and response variables were log(x་1)-transformed to make the overall sample tend to a normal distribution. The significance of environmental variable explanations was analyzed through Monte Carlo tests. Redundancy analysis was carried out using CANOCO 5.0.

Results

Soil nematode community composition

A total of 45,320 soil nematodes were isolated under different mowing frequencies, with an average density of 1,416 nematodes per 100 g of dry soil. Sixty-three genera were identified, including 13 genera of bacterivorous nematodes, nine genera of fungivorous nematodes, 28 genera of herbivorous nematodes, and 13 genera of omnivorous-predatory nematodes (Fig. 1).

In the 0–10 cm soil layer, 27,268 soil nematodes belonging to 53 genera were isolated. The dominant genus in the community was Cervidellus, accounting for 13.7% of the total nematode capture in the 0–10 cm soil layer. The subdominant genera included Cephalobus, Acrobeloides, and two others, constituting 31.22% of the total capture. There were 12 secondary dominant genera (36.22%), six common genera (8.05%), and 30 rare genera (10.66%).

A total of 18,052 soil nematodes of 55 genera were isolated from the 10–20 cm soil layer, and the extremely dominant genera of the community were Cervidellus and Acrobeloides, which accounted for 24.11% of the total nematode catch in the 10–20 cm soil layer; the dominant genera were Cephalobus, Chiloplacus, and six other genera, which accounted for 37.11% of the total catch; the subdominant genera had six genera (17.50%); 5 genera (7.87%) were common; and 36 genera (13.00%) were rare. Compared with the 0–10 cm soil layer, the dominance of Acrobeloides increased to a highly dominant genus, the dominance of Eucephalobus decreased to a common genus, and the dominance of Tylenchusincreased to a dominant genus.

Soil nematode trophic groups

The abundances of nematodes in various trophic groups showed a trend similar to the total abundance of nematodes under different mowing frequencies (Fig. 3). Compared with CK, the abundance of microbivores nematodes (Ba, Fu) reached the peak in M2 treatment in different soil layers; the lowest abundance of herbivorous nematodes in the 0–10 cm soil layer occurred in M2, and the highest abundance in the 10–20 cm soil layer occurred in M2; the highest abundance of omnivorous-predatory nematodes in the 0–10 cm soil layer occurred in M1, and the highest abundance in the 10–20 cm soil layer occurred in M2; in summary, the significant changes in each trophic group of nematodes were mainly concentrated in the M2 treatment.Fig. 3Percentages of soil nematode trophic groups under different mowing frequencies

The relative abundances of c-p values of various nematode groups under different mowing frequencies are shown in (Fig. 4). In the 0–10 cm soil layer, compared with the CK treatment, the M2 treatment increased the relative abundances of nematode groups with c-p values of 3, 4, and 5, and decreased the relative abundances of nematode groups with c-p values of 1 and 2. In the 10–20 cm soil layer, compared with the CK treatment, with the decrease in mowing frequencies, the relative abundances of nematode groups with c-p values of 4 and 5 were increased, while the relative abundances of nematode groups with c-p values of 1 and 2 were decreased at the same time. Discussion

Effects of mowing frequency on the composition of soil nematode communities

Soil nematodes play essential role in maintaining the stability of the soil ecosystem, promoting nutrient recycling and energy flow, and are also ideal environmental indicator organisms. In this study, a total of 63 genera of soil nematodes were identified, including 13 genera of bacterivorous nematodes, 9 genera of fungivorous nematodes, 28 genera of herbivorous nematodes and 13 genera of omnivorous-predatory nematodes, with an average individual density of 1,416 nematodes per 100 g dry soil. We found that the total abundance of soil nematodes varies under different mowing frequencies, and the surface aggregation phenomenon is evident, which is consistent with [9]. The 0–10 cm soil is relatively loose and rich in nutrients, which is more suitable for nematodes to survive. As the soil depth increases, the food sources for nematodes are scarce, consequently decreasing the number of nematodes. The relative abundance of microbivores nematodes in the M2 treatment of this study increased significantly (Fig. 3), which may be related to the most suitable increase in surface organic matter input caused by mowing. The amount of litter and root exudates produced by moderate mowing M2 can provide a better supply. This ‘moderate input’ accurately matches the growth needs of microorganisms, and the increase in microbial biomass directly offers sufficient food for microphagous nematodes, forming a short-chain energy flow efficient cycle of ‘plant residue-microorganism-microphagous nematodes’. This is consistent with the findings of [15]. Under moderate disturbance, micro-feeding nematodes act as a ‘bridge’ between microorganisms and higher trophic levels, and their abundance directly corresponds to the highest point of ecosystem material circulation efficiency. The results of this study showed that in M2 treatment, the nematodes with c-p value of 1 were missing, while the proportion of nematodes with c-p values of 3,4, and 5 increased (Fig. 4), because the nematodes with c-p value of 1 were r-strategist with strong adaptability to environmental interference and often appeared in highly disturbed systems [5]. This is a direct reflection of the moderate interference of M2. The absence of the c-p1 group proved that the interference did not break through the ecological threshold, and the enrichment of the c-p3-5 group reflected resource stability and environmental stability. This balance not only drives the efficiency of the interaction between nematodes and microorganisms, but also improves ecosystem productivity through the ‘continuous supply’ model of nutrient cycle, indicating that the Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe is not disturbed by external disturbances. The overall life history of nematodes is biased towards k-strategist, which provides a basis for disturbance regulation with the response of biological communities as the core for the sustainable management of meadow steppe [6].

Effects of mowing frequency on soil nematode diversity and ecological functioning.

The nematode diversity index can effectively indicate soil biodiversity and resource diversity, and it is an important index that reflects the number of species in the community and the complexity and stability of the food web [17]. In this study, the soil nematode diversity index, evenness index and richness index under different mowing frequencies are all higher than those of the control group (CK), and the dominance index decreases accordingly. This indicates that mowing increases the stability of the soil nematode community, which is consistent with the previous research [15, 29]. The WI index is used to evaluate the relative importance of plant and microbial resources in the soil food web and to indicate soil health [34]. In this study, the WI index was higher than one under different mowing frequencies, indicating that the overall soil health of Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe was good. Still, the WI index of M2 treatment (mowing once every two years) was higher than that of M1 and M3 treatments. The proportion of phytophagous nematodes was low, which further confirmed the effect of M2 treatment on the soil ecosystem [4]. The core is that M2 makes the balance between grassland disturbance and restoration more accurate through moderate mowing frequency, improves the interaction efficiency between micro-feeding nematodes and microorganisms, and inhibits the excessive reproduction of phytophagous nematodes, thus achieving the synergistic improvement of soil health and ecological function. The MI index is used to evaluate the degree of soil environment being disturbed and stressed, and indicates the succession and recovery process of the soil ecosystem after being disturbed; The high-frequency mowing of M1 treatment led to frequent disturbance of soil environment, forcing the nematode Life cycle to be dominated by nematodes with c-p values of 1–2. Although its rapid reproduction could adapt to the disturbance, the MI index of the community was low. Although the low-frequency mowing of M3 reduced the disturbance frequency, the excessive growth of plants led to intensified resource competition, and the proportion of nematodes with a c-p value of 3–5 was limited due to unstable food source [38]. The two-year interval of M2 treatment exactly matches the generation cycle of nematodes with c-p value of 3–5, so that such nematodes have sufficient time to complete reproduction and community construction after mowing. Finally, the nematode community develops to a more mature stage, so that the grassland ecosystem is in a relatively stable state [7].

Effects of mowing frequency on the metabolic footprint of soil nematodes.

The metabolic footprint characteristics of soil nematodes can quantitatively assess the adequate resources and energy transfer status in the soil food web by analyzing the carbon-metabolic functions of different nematode groups and energy channels in the soil food web [15]. The results of this study show that the combined metabolic footprints of nematodes among different treatments in the same soil layer all show a trend of increasing first and then decreasing as mowing frequency decreasing from once every year to once every 3 years. The inflection point appears in the M2 treatment (Fig. 5), which is consistent with the observations of [23]. The results showed that M2 treatment was the optimal state between the disturbance frequency and the energy recovery cycle of the soil micro-food network, so that the carbon metabolic activity of the nematode community changed from ‘disturbance limitation’ (low metabolism of M1) to ‘resource saturation’ (metabolic stagnation of M3). This balance not only ensures continuous litter input (resource base) through biennial mowing, but also sets aside sufficient time (1.5-2years) for microbial-nematode metabolic coupling, and ultimately drives the improvement of energy transfer efficiency of the entire food network by maximizing the metabolic footprint of micro-nematodes [27]. Therefore, the peak metabolic footprint of M2 treatment is not only a direct reflection of moderate disturbance at the level of energy flow, but also a basis for the management of grassland ecosystem precise regulation based on carbon metabolic efficiency [8].

The structure and enrichment footprints can respectively reflect the metabolic footprints of nematodes at high and low trophic levels. And the total area they enclosed in the nematode fauna diagram represents the functional metabolic footprint, which measures the metabolic balance between prey and predators in the soil food web [36]. The functional metabolic footprint of nematodes in the mowing treatment is significantly higher than that in the CK, which indicates that mowing can increase the resources in the soil food web [12], thereby enhancing the metabolic activities of nematodes at higher trophic levels (omnivorous-predatory), and making the system in a relatively stable metabolic balance state [10]. However, the functional metabolic footprint of nematodes in the M3 treatment is significantly lower than that in the CK, indicating that the soil-grassland ecosystem is in an unbalanced state.

Relationship between nematode community structure and soil environmental factors

Mowing, one of the most common utilization methods in grasslands, when carried out for a long time, can affect the energy flow and circulation processes in the underground ecosystem by changing the vegetation structure and soil physical and chemical properties [25],and then affect the habitat environment of soil nematodes. Through the non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis of the dominant genera of soil nematodes, it has been found that the mowing frequency has a significant impact on the nematode community composition, and there is an evident grouping phenomenon in the nematode community composition as the soil depth increases. The M2 and M3 treatments do not overlap with the CK treatment in different soil layers. This indicates that the dominant genera in the community are affected by the mowing frequency at the surface layer, resulting in the grouping phenomenon. Mowing can affect the accumulation of above-ground biomass, litter, and root resources, thereby affecting the food sources of nematodes, and the slow above - and below-ground energy flow can lead to the limitation of soil nutrient status, having a negative impact on the composition of the soil nematode community. The results of redundancy analysis (RDA) show a significant correlation between the soil nematode community and soil environmental factors. Among them, soil pH and soil NH4+-N are shown to be the main environmental factors affecting the soil nematode community in the Baikal needle-grass meadow steppe in different soil layers.

Mowing will affect the growth and development of nematodes. Some studies have shown that nematodes are very sensitive to changes in soil pH and prefer to live in a neutral soil environment [21]. In this study, the soil pH gradually decreased with the decrease in mowing frequency, while the soil NH4+-N gradually increased. The possible reason is that after the grassland is mowed, it directly affects the physiological activities of plant roots, which causes the plant roots to secrete substances such as (H+) and organic compounds into the soil. After being mowed, to adapt to environmental changes, plants increase the competitive ability of roots to absorb soil nutrients. They activate nutrients in the soil by secreting more hydrogen ions and organic acids, thus leading to a decrease in soil pH (Table 1). Organic compounds secreted by roots will provide a carbon source for microorganisms in the soil and promote the growth and reproduction of microorganisms. Microorganisms participate in the transformation process of N in the soil, converting organic N into ammonium-N, increasing the amount of ammonium-N (Table 1). Some studies have shown that an increasing ammonium-N will reduce the number of plant-parasitic nematodes [39]. The possible reason is that an increase in ammonium-N improves plants’ health and defense ability, thereby increasing plants’ resistance [24], thus increasing the difficulty for herbivorous nematodes to obtain food. Current studies have also found that soil nematodes will release a large amount of nutrients such as ammonium-N after preying on microorganisms [11, 35]. This indicates that in addition to directly affecting the soil nematode community through predation, the soil microbial community may also indirectly affect the soil nematode community by changing soil nutrient input.

Conclusion

The results showed that cutting frequencies affected soil nematode community structure in Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe differently. Among them, M2 treatment could increase the abundance of nematodes, metabolic footprint. The abundance of groups with strong anti-interference ability, improve soil health, and promote the maturation of the soil food web. The decomposition of organic matter in meadow steppe was mainly dominated by micro-feeding nematodes; the diversity index of soil nematodes changed with the cutting frequency. The results were concentrated in the M2 treatment with moderate cutting frequency. Soil pH and NH4+-N were the main environmental factors affecting the development of nematode communities. This study suggests that moderate mowing M2 can promote the overall beneficial function of the soil nematode community, and is the proper mowing frequency to maintain the stability of the Stipa baicalensis meadow steppe ecosystem. However, this study is limited to a single sampling year and lacks microbial data, and thus, multi-year continuous sampling and microbial nematode interactions will be studied in future studies.
