No Female Germline Stem Cells Were Identified by Advanced Dual Recombinase‐Based Lineage Tracing System
Authors: Yang Gao, Zhiming Shen, Xiuhong Cui, Min Chen, Mengyue Wang, Kui Liu, Fei Gao, Hongmei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhether female germline stem cells (fGSCs) exist in postnatal mammalian ovaries has been debated for decades. Since the fGSCs were first reported in 2004, a large number of studies have been conducted using different approaches. However, the persistent controversy surrounding the results has caused confusion among many researchers and clinicians. To clearly clarify this question, we developed advanced tracing system to label any possible fGSCs in adult female mice. Our results showed that no Stra8‐expressing germ cells were detected in the ovaries of adult female mice using a long‐term uninterrupted lineage tracing system under both physiological and pathological conditions. Furthermore, we adopted the interleaved reporter tracing system that allows mutually exclusive labelling of germ cells and non‐germ cell populations while preventing potential secondary recombination. This approach revealed no evidence of de novo germ cell formation during postnatal life. Together, our findings provide the evidence that fGSCs and postnatal oogenesis do not exist under both physiological and pathological conditions in mouse models, therefore reinforcing the notion that the primordial follicle pool established perinatally serves as the primary source of oocytes through the entire reproductive lifespan.

Introduction

In mammals, the ovaries are responsible for producing fertilizable oocytes and secreting the hormones and factors that maintain female reproductive characteristics [1, 2]. Ovarian follicles are the basic functional units which are formed perinatally via cyst breakdown [3, 4]. The primordial follicles are composed of one oocyte surrounded by a single layer of squamous pre‐granulosa cells [5]. It has been believed that the number of primordial follicles is determined once they are formed and will not be replenished throughout the entire reproductive lifespan [6, 7]. However, the debate over the existence of female germline stem cells (fGSCs) was started in 2004, and more than 80 studies have been published by different research groups on this topic. The existence of fGSCs has been characterised in multiple species, including mice [8, 9, 10], rats [11], pigs [12], cows [13], non‐human primates [14] and humans [8, 15]. Although the debate over the existence of fGSCs has lasted for decades, the evidence remains controversial. In 2004, Jonathan Tilly's group first reported mitotically active germ cells in the ovarian surface epithelium, together with the statistical analysis, they proposed that fGSCs exist in postnatal mouse ovaries [16]. Furthermore, they proposed that putative fGSCs reside in bone marrow and sustain oocyte production after birth under both physiological and pathological conditions [17]. Both Wu's lab and Jonathan Tilly's group isolated fGSCs using anti‐DDX4 antibody and demonstrated their ability to develop into oocytes in vivo [10, 15]. Wu's group further reported that these cells are capable of generating offspring [10]. The following study by Zheng's lab identified a group of Oct4‐positive putative fGSCs in the ovarian cortex of adult mice and reported that these cells underwent meiosis and formed primordial follicles [18]. In 2017, Stra8‐expressing germ cells were detected in mouse ovaries after birth using lineage tracing mouse model and these cells were reported to form functional oocytes [19].

However, studies from other research groups have reached completely opposite conclusions. The studies using parabiosis models refuted the bone marrow origin hypothesis and found no evidence that the putative fGSCs contribute to new oocytes [20], while statistical analyses demonstrated that the primordial follicle pool established perinatally is sufficient to sustain the oocyte population required for fertility throughout the reproductive lifespan [21]. The experimental approach of isolating putative fGSCs was subsequently challenged by Zhang et al., who raised concerns regarding the reliability of DDX4 antibody‐based isolation, as DDX4 was reported to be localised in the cytoplasm of ovarian germ cells [22]. The studies using cell lineage tracing with the Cre‐loxP recombination system by Zhang et al. and Lei et al. also reported that no mitotically active germ cells exist in adult ovaries and no postnatal folliculogenesis was detected [23, 24, 25]. A recent study using high‐throughput single‐cell sequencing did not identify fGSCs in the human ovarian cortex [26]. However, both conventional lineage‐tracing and single‐cell sequencing approaches have limitations in detecting rare fGSCs; therefore further investigation is needed to clarify this question.

To further clarify and hopefully resolve this long‐term debate, we introduced the dual recombinase‐based lineage tracing system, which is based on the specific recombination of Cre‐loxP [27] and Dre‐rox [28]. As a more powerful tool, the dual recombinase‐based lineage tracing system allows a minimum interference of unintended labelling while ensuring accurate activation in the appropriate cell type at designated time points [29], and has enabled researchers to advance our understanding in several critical biological processes, including beta‐cell development [30], hepatocyte generation [31] and the origin of alveolar stem cells [32]. Stra8, the gatekeeper of meiosis initiation, is exclusively expressed in germ cells during meiosis initiation. In mouse model, Stra8 expression begins in female germ cells at E12.5 and disappears around E15.5. As a gatekeeper of meiosis initiation, inactivation of Stra8 causes the detect of mitosis‐to‐meiosis transition in germ cells [33, 34, 35].

Considering the essential role of Stra8 in meiosis initiation, we hypothesised that if fGSCs existed and generated the functional oocytes, they should undergo meiosis initiation accompanied with Stra8 expression. Therefore, we developed the dual recombinase‐mediated lineage tracing system to continuously and seamlessly record Stra8‐expressed meiosis initiation events in adult ovaries. With this dual lineage tracing strategy, no evidence of meiosis initiation was detected during long‐term tracing of 3 and 6 months in postnatal ovaries under both physiological and pathological conditions. Meanwhile, to further investigate whether other cell types can develop into oocytes regardless of whether they underwent meiosis, we used the interleaved reporter mouse model (R26‐IR1) [30, 36] to separately label germ cells and non‐germ cell populations with different markers. Our findings indicate that non‐germ cells do not develop into oocytes in postnatal ovaries under both physiological and pathological conditions. Thus, these findings provide supporting evidence that no fGSCs contribute to the generation of functional oocytes in adult mice.

Results

No Stra8‐Expressing Germ Cells Were Detected in Postnatal Ovaries

To investigate whether fGSCs exist in ovaries and initiate meiosis after birth, a Stra8‐CreER T2 mouse model was generated and the knock‐in strategy was illustrated as in Figure S1A. Genotyping of Stra8‐CreER T2 was shown in Figure S1B. In Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice, the meiotic germ cells would be labelled with GFP after Tamoxifen treatment. To validate the mouse model, the expression of GFP in Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice was analysed at different developmental stages with Tamoxifen treatment (Figure S2A,C,E). As shown in Figure S2B, GFP signal was detected in germ cells of female Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice with Tamoxifen treatment at E12.5. By contrast, no GFP signal was detected in germ cells of female Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice at P7 with or without Tamoxifen treatment at E15.5 (Figure S2C,D, arrowheads). To further verify this mouse model, 4‐week‐old male and female Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice were injected with Tamoxifen and the expression of GFP was analysed 2 days later. GFP signal was detected in germ cells of Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG testis after Tamoxifen treatment, while no GFP signal was detected in ovaries of Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice (Figure S2E,F). These results indicated that the Stra8‐CreER T2 mouse model was successfully generated and Stra8‐CreER T2 could be specifically induced by Tamoxifen in pre‐meiotic germ cells.

To test whether fGSCs exist and initiate meiosis in adult ovaries, 4 weeks old Stra8‐CreERT2; mT/mG females were treated with Tamoxifen for six consecutive times and the ovaries were collected 1 week after treatment (Figure 1A). The results of immunostaining for GFP and MVH (also known as DDX4, a marker of germ cells) showed that all MVH positive germ cells were GFP negative (Figure 1B,C), indicating that no germ cells initiated meiosis in the ovaries of adult females. To further test whether fGSCs could initiate meiosis under pathological condition, we generated a POI (premature ovarian insufficiency) mouse model by treating mice with busulfan and cyclophosphamide as reported previously (Figure 1D) [17]. After chemotherapy treatment, the female Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT/mG mice were treated with Tamoxifen and the expression of GFP in ovaries was examined 2 weeks later (Figure 1D–F). The results of immunostaining showed that all MVH positive germ cells were GFP negative and no GFP positive oocyte was detected (Figure 1E,F, arrows).

Generation of an Uninterrupted Lineage Tracing System to Label Meiotic Germ Cells in the Ovaries of Adult Mice

To further continuously trace the Stra8‐expressing germ cells in the ovaries of adult females, we first generated a Stra8‐CrexER mouse model. The knock‐in strategy and genotyping are illustrated in Figure S3A,B. As shown in Figure 2A, the ERT2 element in Stra8‐CrexER T2 mice is flanked with two rox sites. Under normal physiological conditions, the activity of Stra8‐Cre is dependent on Tamoxifen administration in the Stra8‐CreER T2 mouse model. By contrast, the ERT2 element will be removed when Dre is activated, and Stra8‐Cre will be activated independent of Tamoxifen induction afterwards in the Stra8‐CrexER T2 mouse model. Additionally, we also generated an H11‐TLR reporter mouse model, and the genotyping is illustrated in Figure S4B. As shown in Figure S4, two stop cassettes were inserted between two loxP and rox sites, respectively. Upon Cre‐loxP and Dre‐rox mediated recombination, the expression of ZsGreen or tdTomato is activated. Meanwhile, the Cre‐loxP and Dre‐rox recombination are mutually independent and do not influence each other.

ZsGreen‐positive germ cells were observed in the testes, but not in the ovaries of Stra8‐CrexER; R26‐DreER T2 ; H11‐TLR mice (Figure 2E). Additionally, pregnant females were treated with Tamoxifen or sesame oil at E8.5 and the testes and ovaries were analysed at E17.5. ZsGreen and MVH double positive germ cells were only detected in the ovaries with Tamoxifen administration (Figure S3C,D). These results indicated that the activity of Stra8‐Cre could be induced uninterruptedly with Tamoxifen treatment in Stra8‐CrexER; R26‐DreER T2 ; H11‐TLR mice and the system ensures the precise activation at appropriate time.

No Stra8‐Expressing Germ Cells Were Detected in Ovaries After Birth With Uninterrupted Lineage Tracing

To further determine whether new oocytes are generated through meiosis in postnatal ovaries, female Stra8‐CrexER; R26‐DreER T2 ; H11‐TLR mice were treated with Tamoxifen at 4 weeks. The ovaries were collected at 3 and 6 months post‐treatment for further analysis (Figure 3A). Serial sections of ovaries were prepared and the expression of ZsGreen and MVH in all sections was examined by immunostaining. The results of immunostaining showed that all the germ cells were labelled with MVH, but no ZsGreen signal was detected at both 3 and 6 months after Tamoxifen treatment(Figure 3B,D). The proportion of ZsGreen and MVH double positive germ cells was quantitatively analysed across serial sections. The results showed that all the MVH‐positive germ cells were ZsGreen negative and no ZsGreen and MVH double positive germ cells were detected at both 3 and 6 months after Tamoxifen treatment (Figure 3C,E). These results indicated that no germ cells initiate meiosis in adult mouse ovaries under physiological conditions.

No Other Somatic Cells Contributed to the Pool of Oocyte in Postnatal Female Mice

The results of the above study were obtained by tracing putative fGSCs in adult female mice based on Stra8 expression during the initiation of meiosis. To further determine whether other cell types contribute to the pool of oocytes in female mice after birth, a dual recombinase‐based lineage tracing system was used. As shown in Figure 4A, R26‐IR1 reporter mouse model features interleaved loxP sites and rox sites. Specifically, when Cre‐loxP recombination occurs, one rox site is removed, thereby preventing potential Dre‐rox recombination. Thus, this cell population permits only one type of recombination, ensuring exclusive labelling of a specific cell population and preventing further labelling by another recombination system.

To employ the dual recombinase‐based lineage tracing system, we first generated a Stra8‐Dre knock‐in mouse model. The strategy for construction was shown in Figure S6A. Dre was inserted at the upstream of the stop codon in the last exon of the Stra8 gene. The genotype for Stra8‐Dre mice was verified with primers as illustrated in Figure S6B. Stra8‐Dre mouse model was verified by crossing with R26‐IR1 reporter mice. All germ cells in both male and female Stra8‐Dre; R26‐IR1 testes and ovaries were tdTomato positive at P8, whereas no tdTomato signal was detected in control mice (Figure S6C,D). tdTomato signal was also undetectable in other tissues (such as heart, liver, lung, kidney and brain) of Stra8‐Dre; R26‐IR1 mice (Figure S6E). These results indicated that Stra8‐Dre could be specifically activated in germ cells undergoing meiosis.

To test whether other cells could contribute to the oocyte pool in adult mice, 4‐week‐old Stra8‐Dre; CreER T2 ; R26‐IR1 female mice were treated with Tamoxifen and ovaries were collected at 3 or 6 months after induction (Figure 4B). In this system, pre‐existing oocytes could be labelled with tdTomato mediated by Stra8‐Dre and all the rest cells will be labelled with ZsGreen by CreER T2 after Tamoxifen induction. Serial sections of ovaries were prepared and all sections were analysed by immunostaining. With Tamoxifen treatment, strong ZsGreen+ signal was detected throughout the entire ovaries coupled with the tdTomato signal of MVH‐positive germ cells. All the MVH‐positive germ cells were labelled with tdTomato, but no ZsGreen and MVH double positive germ cells were detected at both 3 and 6 months after Tamoxifen induction(Figure 4C,E). The results of quantitative analysis also showed that all germ cells were tdTomato positive and no ZsGreen positive germ cells were detected in Stra8‐Dre; CreER T2 ; R26‐IR1 female mice at both 3 and 6 months after Tamoxifen induction (Figure 4D,F).

To test whether new oocytes were formed under pathological conditions, Stra8‐Dre; CreER T2 ; R26‐IR1 female mice were treated with busulfan and cyclophosphamide after Tamoxifen administration. The ovaries were collected at 3 and 6 months after chemotherapy treatment (Figure 5C). We found that the number of germ cells was dramatically reduced after chemotherapy treatment, indicating that exposure to chemotherapy drugs leads to the depletion of oocytes. The results of immunostaining for ZsGreen, tdTomato and MVH revealed that no ZsGreen positive germ cells were observed at both 3 and 6 months after chemotherapy drug administration (Figure 5D). All these results indicated that no other cell type contributes to the oocyte pool in postnatal female mice under both physiological and pathological conditions.

Discussion and Conclusion

In mammals, the pool of primordial follicles is formed perinatally through the process of cyst breakdown. The primordial follicles possess three distinct developmental fates: (1) remaining quiescent; (2) undergoing atresia in quiescence; (3) being activated through initial recruitment [5]. Traditionally, it is believed that the number of primordial follicles is fixed once formed perinatally and it will not be replenished thereafter. However, in recent years, the existence of female germline stem cells (fGSCs) in postnatal ovaries has become a topic of intense debate.

Besides the evidence demonstrating that fGSCs probably reside in bone marrow and peripheral blood [17], opposite results are obtained by other research groups [20, 21, 23, 24, 25]. Especially, Eggan et al.'s study using a parabiosis model refutes the bone marrow origin hypothesis [20]. Wu's group successfully isolated MVH‐positive fGSCs and demonstrated that these cells could be amplified under in vitro condition. Most importantly, the offspring were obtained after fGSCs transplantation [10]. However, these results could not be repeated by other research groups [22]. Although the fGSCs notion has also been tested by other groups, the results remain controversial. Based on these results, it is still hard to draw a conclusion on whether fGSCs exist in adult females.

Cell lineage tracing experiment is the most reliable approach to map cell fate during development. This strategy has been widely used in numerous studies [30, 31, 36, 37, 38]; however, no direct evidence has been obtained due to the lack of a specific marker and the limitations of the labelling system. Conventional lineage tracing systems could not directly label the cells which potentially develop into oocytes in adult females. In mammals, female germ cells are originated from primordial germ cells (PGCs) and initiate meiosis right after sex determination. As a gatekeeper of meiosis initiation, the expression of Stra8 is directly stimulated by retinoic acid (RA) and first detected in germ cells of female mice at E12.5 [33, 34, 35]. Its expression is absent in female germ cells after E15.5 [35]. In male mice, Stra8 starts to express in germ cells approximately 5 days after birth and is continuously expressed in the premeiotic germ cells of puberty and adult testes [39]. Theoretically, Stra8 will not be expressed in female germ cells after birth. If fGSCs exist and new oocytes are formed in adult females, they must undergo meiosis and the expression of Stra8 could be detected in adult ovaries.

In 2017, Wang et al. used the Stra8‐rtTA; tetO‐Cre; Rosa26‐YFP mouse model to label Stra8‐expressing germ cells in adult female mice by treating the mice with doxycycline for 21 days. Interestingly, YFP+ germ cells were observed in the ovaries of Stra8‐rtTA; tetO‐Cre; Rosa26‐YFP mice, and YFP+ offspring were obtained by crossing with wild‐type males [19]. In this study, we used Stra8‐CreER T2 and mT‐mG reporter mouse models to trace germ cells which were undergoing meiosis in adult females. In this model, the Stra8‐Cre was activated upon Tamoxifen induction. To test whether Stra8‐expressing germ cells exist in adult ovaries, Stra8‐CreER T2 ; mT‐mG mice were injected with Tamoxifen at 4 weeks old. To improve the labelling efficiency, the mice were injected with Tamoxifen for six consecutive times. However, no GFP positive germ cells were detected in the ovaries 1 week after Tamoxifen induction, which was inconsistent with Wang et al.'s study [19]. The limitation of this model is that Cre activity is temporarily activated upon Tamoxifen treatment. To continuously trace the Stra8‐expressing germ cells in the ovaries after a certain developmental stage, we generated a Stra8‐CrexER mouse model, and the Stra8‐Cre can be permanently activated upon Tamoxifen‐induced Dre activity. This strategy has previously been used to trace hepatocyte proliferation during liver homeostasis and regeneration [31]. We traced the Stra8‐expressing germ cells up to 6 months, and more than 8000 germ cells from three individual mice were examined. However, no ZsGreen positive germ cells were detected. Theoretically, compared to previous study [18, 19, 23, 24, 25], the tracing system used in our study was more reliable.

The advantage of dual recombinase‐based interleaved reporter lineage tracing system(R26‐IR1) is simultaneously labelling the target cells and all the rest of cells with different fluorescence. This system minimises the interference of unintended labelling while ensuring accurate activation of the appropriate cell types at the designated time point. In this system, the pre‐existing oocytes were labelled with Stra8‐Dre during embryonic stage and all the rest of non‐germ cells were labelled with CreER T2 at 4 weeks by Tamoxifen induction. The efficiency of CreER T2 is dependent on the dosage of Tamoxifen. In this study, more than 90% of non‐germ cells were labelled with ZsGreen and no oocyte was ZsGreen positive, indicating that non‐germ cells were efficiently labelled with CreER T2 and the loxP sites in all the pre‐existing oocytes were specifically blocked by Stra8‐Dre. More than 8000 oocytes from 3 individual mice were examined and the cells were traced up to 6 months. Moreover, we examined this lineage‐tracing system at both physiological and pathological conditions. Theoretically, if fGSCs exist and contribute to the pool of oocyte after birth, it must be detected by our tracing system. In this model, the germ cells were labelled with MVH, which is a general germ cell marker.

The Stra8‐based lineage‐tracing strategy allowed us to trace whether fGSCs exist and contribute to oocytes through meiosis. However, this strategy alone may not be sufficient to rule out the possibility that somatic cells could give rise to germ cells without undergoing meiosis. To further investigate this possibility, the mutually exclusive lineage‐tracing system (Stra8‐Dre; CreER T2 ; R26‐IR1) was used to label germ cells and non‐germ cells independently. With long‐term tracing, this system enabled us to detect the de novo oogenesis whether meiosis was initiated or not in postnatal mice. In summary, no fGSCs were detected in adult female mice under both physiological and pathological conditions using advanced dual recombinase‐based lineage tracing systems. Our findings provide direct evidence against the existence of fGSCs in female mice, and suggest caution against the premature proposal of clinical applications. In this study, we investigated the existence of fGSCs using genetically modified mouse models. Whether other mammalian species contain fGSCs needs further investigation.

Methods

Mice

All mouse experiments were performed in accordance of Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IOZ‐IACUC‐2024‐184). The mice used in this study were of mixed background consisting of C57BL/6J and ICR. The generation of mT/mG, Cre‐ER T2 , R26‐DreER T2 and R26‐IR1 was as described previously [40, 41, 42]. Stra8‐CreER T2 , Stra8‐CrexER, Stra8‐Dre and H11‐TLR mouse models were generated using CRISPR‐Cas9 technology. Stra8‐CreER T2 , Stra8‐CrexER and Stra8‐Dre mouse models were generated by the Animal Experiment Center, Institute of Zoology. H11‐TLR mouse model was generated using CRISPR‐Cas9 technology by Shanghai Biomodel Organism. The primers used for genotyping are listed in Table S1.

For induction of CreER‐loxP or DreER‐rox recombination, tamoxifen (Sigma, T5648) or 4‐hydroxytamoxifen (Sigma, H6278) was dissolved in sesame oil and administered by oral gavage at the indicated time points at doses of 0.1–0.25 mg/g body weight for tamoxifen and 60 μg/g body weight for 4‐hydroxytamoxifen, as described previously [31, 43]. To establish the chemotherapy‐induced premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) model, mice were administered 120 mg/kg cyclophosphamide (CTX, Sigma, C0768) and 12 mg/kg busulfan (BUS, Sigma, B2635) at the indicated time points via intraperitoneal injection.

Immunostaining

Immunostaining was performed according to previous protocols [31, 40]. Briefly, ovaries and other organs were collected and washed in PBS to remove excessive blood, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C. The fixed tissues were dehydrated in both 10% and 25% sucrose solutions in PBS at 4°C until tissues were fully penetrated. The tissues were then embedded in optimum cutting tissue (O.C.T., Sakura) and stored at −80°C. Cryosections with a thickness of 20 μm were collected with slides, washed in PBS, and incubated with blocking solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.3% Triton X‐100 for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature or overnight at 4°C. After washing three times in PBS, tissue sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI for 1 h at room temperature. After that, slides were washed with PBS and mounted with fluorescence aqueous mounting medium (Sigma, F4680). For immunofluorescence (IF) analysis of paraffin sections, 5 μm paraffin sections were deparaffinised and rehydrated. Following antigen retrieval, the staining and incubation procedures for paraffin sections were similar to those for cryosections. The antibodies employed were as follows: anti‐GFP (1:1000, Abcam, ab13970), anti‐MVH (1:200, Abcam, ab13840) and anti‐STRA8 (1:200, Abcam, ab308124).

For paraffin sections, ovaries were serially sectioned into 5‐μm thickness, and one out of every five consecutive sections was examined throughout the ovary. For frozen sections, all sections obtained by serially sectioning were analysed. To avoid repeated counting, oocytes were counted only when a clearly identified nucleus was present. Multiple sections from the same mouse were analysed, but individual sections were not considered independent biological replicates.

Statistical Analysis

All mice of each genotype were randomly assigned to different groups, and all experiments were performed using at least three individual animals. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1.
