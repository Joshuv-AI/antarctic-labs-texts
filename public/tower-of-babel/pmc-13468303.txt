Sepsis in Autoimmune Rheumatic Diseases: Risk Factors, Pathophysiology, and Outcomes
Authors: Meiyi Tian, Zhuo Sun, Yang Zhang, Jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs), with distinct time-dependent outcomes that differ across disease subtypes, sepsis definitions, immune phenotypes, and follow-up time horizons. Chronic immune dysregulation, disease-specific cytokine signatures, cumulative organ damage, and dose- and duration-dependent immunosuppressive therapies render this population particularly vulnerable to sepsis. Evidence indicates that ARDs and sepsis share overlapping immunopathological mechanisms, including cytokine dysregulation, disease-dependent neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) abnormalities, Treg/Th17 imbalance, endotoxin-related immune tolerance, and phenotype-specific immune suppression. However, the functional consequences of these shared pathways diverge substantially by ARD subtype and disease activity. Whether ARDs independently influence sepsis susceptibility and clinical outcomes remains controversial, largely due to heterogeneity in sepsis definitions, endpoint variability, and incomplete confounder control. In this systematic review with narrative synthesis, conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidance (87 studies included), we synthesize current epidemiological, genetic, and mechanistic evidence and discuss risk-management and healthcare-policy implications. We summarize disease-related, immune-related, treatment-related, demographic, and healthcare-system risk factors, stratified by evidence strength and specificity to ARDs, and critically evaluate the time-dependent mortality paradox: preserved or improved short-term survival in certain ARDs versus substantially elevated long-term mortality. A deeper understanding of these complex interactions, including pathogen spectra, vaccination status, and modifiable risk factors, may facilitate the development of clinically actionable risk stratification tools, inform personalized therapeutic decision-making, and improve both short- and long-term outcomes in this high-risk population.

Introduction

Autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by immune-mediated tissue injury. The spectrum includes autoinflammatory diseases (e.g, familial Mediterranean fever), classic autoimmune diseases (e.g, systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE], rheumatoid arthritis [RA]), and mixed-pattern connective tissue diseases (e.g, undifferentiated connective tissue disease).1 Core pathobiological features common to most ARDs include loss of self-tolerance, chronic inflammation driven by autoreactive lymphocytes and autoantibodies, and progressive end-organ damage.2

Sepsis, as defined by the Third International Consensus (Sepsis-3), is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection;3,4 septic shock represents a critical subset characterized by persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg and serum lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation, with hospital mortality exceeding 40%. This definition superseded earlier Sepsis-2 criteria based on systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), making comparison across older and newer studies difficult. The resulting heterogeneity in sepsis definitions across published studies, ranging from Sepsis-2/severe sepsis to Sepsis-3 and ICU-specific administrative codes, introduces substantial bias in incidence and mortality estimates, a limitation we systematically address in this review through definition-stratified synthesis. Sepsis remains a major global health burden, accounting for approximately 19.7% of all deaths worldwide in 2017, with an estimated 48.9 million incident cases annually. Although age-standardized incidence and mortality rates have declined, the absolute disease burden remains substantial, particularly in low- and middle-income countries and among immunocompromised populations.5 Patients with ARDs represent a prototypical immunocompromised population at disproportionately high risk for sepsis, driven by the convergence of intrinsic immune dysregulation, disease activity, and immunosuppressive therapy exposure.6 Among patients with ARDs admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), sepsis is the leading cause of death, accounting for 35–50% of ICU mortality in this population.7 When sepsis occurs in patients with ARDs, reported short-term mortality ranges from 35% to 42% in hospitalized cohorts, substantially higher than the general sepsis population.8–10

Given this high disease burden, improving risk stratification, prevention, and management of sepsis in patients with ARDs is an urgent clinical, research, and healthcare-policy priority. This review is structured around a unifying conceptual framework that integrates: (i) epidemiological and genetic evidence for sepsis susceptibility, (ii) shared and distinct immunopathological mechanisms, (iii) a hierarchical evaluation of risk factors, (iv) the time-dependent paradox of short- versus long-term mortality outcomes, and (v) risk-management implications for clinical pathways and health systems. We hypothesize that sepsis susceptibility and outcomes in ARDs are not determined uniformly by autoimmune diagnosis alone, but by the interaction among disease-specific immune endotypes, disease activity, organ damage, treatment exposure, sepsis phenotype, and healthcare-system factors. By critically appraising the evidence and proposing testable hypotheses, this review aims to inform clinical decision-making, risk-management policy, and future research.

Methodology

This systematic review was conducted following a predefined protocol to synthesize and critically appraise the current evidence regarding the interplay between ARDs and sepsis. The review adheres to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

A systematic literature search was performed in PubMed (including MEDLINE), Embase (Ovid), and Web of Science Core Collection for articles published from database inception to October 2025. The last search was executed on October 31, 2025. The full search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Search terms combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords for ARDs and sepsis, with septic shock included as a prespecified sepsis-related outcome. Nonspecific terms such as severe infection and bacteremia were not used as stand-alone eligibility concepts; studies retrieved through broader infection terminology were included only when sepsis, septic shock, organ dysfunction, ICU-treated sepsis, or validated sepsis codes were clearly reported. Reference lists of included systematic reviews and key articles were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) peer-reviewed original research or high-quality systematic reviews used for contextual synthesis; (2) adult study populations (≥18 years) with a diagnosed ARD; (3) sepsis or septic shock defined by Sepsis-2, Sepsis-3, ICU-treated sepsis, or validated administrative codes; (4) English language or English abstract available. Exclusion criteria: (1) case reports, case series (n<10), editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts; (2) pediatric populations; (3) non-human studies; (4) studies focusing solely on localized infection, nonspecific severe infection, or bacteremia without systemic involvement, organ dysfunction, or a sepsis-related endpoint.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

After removal of duplicates, two independent reviewers (initials) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review of potentially eligible articles. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s κ (κ = 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.89). Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. The following data were extracted into a standardized form: study characteristics (design, location, years), population (ARD type, sample size, demographics), sepsis definition, risk factor estimates (OR/HR with 95% CI), mortality outcomes, and confounder adjustment sets. A PRISMA-style flow diagram (Figure 1) summarizes the screening process. In total, 3426 database records were identified (PubMed, n=942; Embase, n=1356; Web of Science, n=1128). After removal of 1579 deduplicates, 1847 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 1631 records were excluded, and 216 reports were sought for retrieval. Eight reports could not be retrieved; therefore, 208 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Evidence Quality Assessment

Evidence quality was systematically assessed using a predefined tiered grading system based on study design, sample size, and confounder control (not effect size magnitude). High-quality evidence: (i) large prospective cohort studies (n>500) with multivariable adjustment for key confounders (age, comorbidities, immunosuppression) OR (ii) Mendelian randomization analyses with F-statistic >10 for instrument strength and sensitivity analyses addressing horizontal pleiotropy. Moderate-quality evidence: (i) retrospective cohort studies with adequate confounder adjustment (n=100-500) OR (ii) smaller prospective studies OR (iii) MR studies with moderate instrument strength (F-statistic 5–10). Low-quality evidence: (i) unadjusted analyses, (ii) small sample sizes (n<100), (iii) high risk of bias per ROBINS-I assessment. Disagreements in grading were resolved by consensus discussion between two authors (initials).

Handling of Sepsis Definition Heterogeneity

To address sepsis definition heterogeneity, studies were stratified by diagnostic criteria during data synthesis: (1) Sepsis-3, defined as infection with organ dysfunction, (2) septic shock, when vasopressor requirement and lactate-based criteria were reported, (3) Sepsis-2/severe sepsis, defined by infection, SIRS criteria, and organ dysfunction, (4) ICU-treated sepsis, and (5) administrative-code-based sepsis. Where meta-analysis was not feasible due to definition variation, findings are presented narratively with explicit notation of the sepsis definition used in each cited study.

Results of the Literature Synthesis

Overview of Included Evidence

The final synthesis included 87 studies spanning observational cohorts, registry analyses, Mendelian randomization studies, translational mechanistic studies, and systematic reviews. The included evidence addressed RA, SLE, ANCA-associated vasculitis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, systemic sclerosis, spondyloarthritis, and mixed connective tissue diseases, although the evidence base was strongest for RA and SLE.11 Across studies, the most consistent determinants of sepsis risk were high disease activity, glucocorticoid exposure, organ involvement, comorbidity burden, older age, and treatment intensity.12,13 Evidence for a uniform genetic or causal relationship between autoimmune disease status and sepsis was inconsistent.

Shared and Disease-Specific Pathophysiological Mechanisms

Sepsis is characterized by profound dysregulation of the host immune response; however, a uniform cytokine storm is not present in all patients, and clinical phenotypes differ in inflammatory, cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, and immune-suppression profiles.14,15 The Sepsis-3 framework also distinguishes septic shock as a high-risk subset involving vasopressor-dependent hypotension and elevated lactate despite adequate fluid resuscitation.16 In ARDs, baseline immune abnormalities may interact with these sepsis phenotypes in disease-specific ways. In RA, chronic TNF-α and IL-6 elevation may prime the innate immune system, potentially conferring a degree of endotoxin tolerance; however, RA is heterogeneous, and different Th17/Treg profiles may modify both acute inflammation and mortality (Figure 2). In SLE, a type I interferon signature, impaired NET clearance, and treatment-related immune suppression may predispose to secondary infections or prolonged immune dysfunction.14 In ANCA-associated vasculitis, inflammasome activation, neutrophil activation, renal involvement, and induction immunosuppression may jointly increase infection risk.17 In idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, interstitial lung disease and intensive combination therapy are particularly relevant. NETs are web-like chromatin structures released by activated neutrophils via NETosis. While NET formation contributes to pathogen containment, dysregulated or excessive NETosis contributes to autoimmune pathogenesis, microvascular thrombosis, endothelial injury, and sepsis-induced organ injury. Disease-specific NET abnormalities include enhanced NET formation in RA, impaired NET degradation in SLE, and ANCA-triggered NETosis in ANCA-associated vasculitis.18 These abnormalities may prime ARD patients for organ injury during sepsis, although direct prospective evidence remains limited.19,20 Treg and Th17 pathways are also relevant but should not be interpreted uniformly across ARDs.21,22 In RA, Th17 dominance may coexist with endotoxin tolerance or immune preconditioning, depending on disease stage, inflammatory burden, treatment exposure, and immune phenotype.23 In SLE, Treg dysfunction and type I interferon activity may contribute to immune imbalance and infection susceptibility.24 In sepsis, dynamic shifts from hyperinflammation to immunosuppression may interact with these baseline immune abnormalities.25 Therefore, ARD-sepsis mechanisms should be investigated using serial immunophenotyping rather than static disease labels alone.

Disease-Related and Immune-Related Risk Factors

A substantial body of observational evidence has demonstrated an association between several ARDs and increased incidence sepsis risk26 (Table 1). However, the association is confounded by disease activity, immunosuppressive therapy, glucocorticoid exposure, organ dysfunction, advanced age, and comorbidity burden.6,10,27 Mendelian randomization studies have yielded partially inconsistent results, reflecting differences in study design, populations, genetic instruments, and endpoints.28 Collectively, current evidence does not support a uniform disease-independent genetic causal relationship between autoimmune disease and sepsis.29 Instead, evidence supports a model in which risk is driven by modifiable and measurable factors, particularly high disease activity, glucocorticoid exposure, and organ dysfunction.30,31 High disease activity is one of the most consistently replicated risk factors for sepsis across ARDs.32,33 In RA and SLE, higher disease activity is associated with increased sepsis-related infection risk independent of baseline treatment exposure in several adjusted analyses.34,35 In ANCA-associated vasculitis, high disease activity and renal or pulmonary involvement are strongly linked to sepsis-related infection risk.36 In idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, interstitial lung disease and intensive induction regimens are major contributors. Treatment-Related Risk Factors

Patients with ARDs have a higher incidence of sepsis than the general population, and treatment-related factors are major modifiable contributors38 (Table 2). Glucocorticoid exposure is consistently associated with infection and sepsis risk in a dose- and duration-dependent manner across ARDs. The risk is particularly high with doses exceeding 10 mg/day prednisone-equivalent, prolonged exposure, combination immunosuppression, and active disease.33 Because confounding by indication is common, infection risk should be interpreted in relation to disease activity and treatment phase.39,40 Among conventional immunosuppressive agents, cyclophosphamide is an independent predictor of sepsis-related infection in SLE and vasculitis, especially during induction therapy.36,41 In contrast, hydroxychloroquine appears to be associated with lower infection risk in SLE, possibly through immunomodulatory effects on Toll-like receptor signaling and endotoxin-related pathways rather than broad immunosuppression.35,42 This observation is clinically intriguing but should be interpreted cautiously because direct evidence linking hydroxychloroquine, endotoxin modulation, and sepsis outcomes in SLE remains limited. Biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs require dedicated discussion because of their increasing use in rheumatology.43,44 TNF inhibitors are associated with increased overall and opportunistic infection risk, especially early after treatment initiation.45,46 IL-6 receptor blockade may complicate infection recognition by suppressing C-reactive protein responses and fever.47 Rituximab may increase infection susceptibility through hypogammaglobulinemia and impaired humoral immunity.48 Janus kinase inhibitors are associated with viral and opportunistic infection risks and may be relevant in high-risk ARD subgroups.36 At the same time, some biologic agents may alter post-infection inflammatory trajectories, and observational studies have suggested that infection incidence and sepsis mortality may not move in the same direction. This distinction is important for risk-benefit assessment and clinical policy. Other Clinical and Healthcare-System Risk Factors

Beyond disease activity and treatment exposure, advanced age, male sex, chronic lung disease, renal dysfunction, diabetes, malnutrition, smoking, prior infections, vaccination status, and socioeconomic factors influence infection and sepsis risk.49 Pulmonary and renal involvement are especially important in systemic ARDs because they increase both infection susceptibility and organ vulnerability during sepsis.50 Data on pathogen spectrum, infection focus, vaccination response, prophylactic antimicrobial strategies, and healthcare access remain inconsistently captured in existing studies, limiting individualized risk prediction.51,52

Mortality and Time-Dependent Outcomes

Patients with ARDs complicated by sepsis often have higher baseline Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores, higher comorbidity burden, and higher rates of pre-admission glucocorticoid exposure than non-ARD sepsis patients.53,54 However, multiple adjusted analyses suggest that ARDs are not uniformly associated with increased short-term sepsis mortality. In RA, several studies even report preserved or lower short-term mortality, possibly related to immune preconditioning, endotoxin tolerance, earlier healthcare contact, treatment intensity bias, or residual confounding.55,56 These findings should be interpreted cautiously because sepsis phenotypes, ICU admission thresholds, limitation-of-care decisions, and severity adjustment may differ across populations.57 Long-term outcomes appear less favorable. Patients with RA and other ARDs may face elevated infection-related mortality and rehospitalization risk over months to years after sepsis.58,59 This time-dependent mortality paradox suggests that acute-phase outcomes and post-acute outcomes are shaped by different factors. Short-term outcomes may reflect acute immune adaptation, clinical vigilance, and treatment intensity, whereas long-term outcomes are more strongly shaped by chronic disease burden, cumulative organ damage, secondary infections, persistent immune dysfunction, and ongoing immunosuppression.60,61

Discussion

This review indicates that ARD-sepsis interactions are best understood as disease-specific, phenotype-dependent, and treatment-modified rather than uniform across autoimmune diagnoses. RA, SLE, vasculitis, and myositis differ in dominant immune pathways, organ involvement, therapeutic exposures, and infection spectra. Sepsis itself is heterogeneous, with distinct clinical phenotypes and septic shock representing a particularly high-risk subset. Therefore, broad claims about ARDs as a single group should be avoided unless supported by disease-stratified evidence. The evidence also suggests that immune alterations in ARDs may have biomarker and therapeutic-target potential. Lymphocyte count, Th17/Treg balance, NET markers, cytokine profiles, immunoglobulin levels, endotoxin-related measures, and multi-omics signatures may help identify clinically meaningful endotypes. Bedside endotoxin measurement is particularly relevant because endotoxin-related immune tolerance and TLR-mediated pathways may influence both RA- and SLE-associated sepsis phenotypes. However, these approaches remain investigational, and prospective validation is needed before they can guide anti-endotoxin or immune-modulating therapies in routine care.

From a risk-management and healthcare-policy perspective, the findings support standardized sepsis definitions, disease-specific risk stratification, glucocorticoid stewardship, vaccination optimization, infection surveillance, and structured post-sepsis follow-up. For clinical systems, ARD patients should not be treated as a homogeneous risk group. Instead, risk tools should incorporate disease subtype, disease activity, organ involvement, glucocorticoid dose and duration, biologic or targeted synthetic DMARD exposure, prior infection history, vaccination status, and sepsis phenotype. Integrating multi-omics data, endotoxin-related bedside biomarkers, and detailed clinical phenotyping may enable more precise risk stratification and personalized management strategies for this vulnerable population. These findings have several practical implications (Figure 3). First, rheumatology and acute-care services should implement shared protocols for early sepsis recognition in high-risk ARD patients, especially those receiving high-dose glucocorticoids, induction immunosuppression, rituximab, JAK inhibitors, or combination regimens. Second, infection-prevention policies should prioritize vaccination review, steroid minimization when clinically feasible, pneumocystis prophylaxis in appropriate high-risk groups, and monitoring for hypogammaglobulinemia after B-cell depletion. Third, post-sepsis care pathways should address secondary infection risk, immune reconstitution, rehabilitation, medication de-escalation, and long-term mortality risk. Limitations and Future Perspectives

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity in sepsis definitions and outcomes limits direct comparability across studies. Second, most clinical evidence remains observational and is vulnerable to residual confounding by disease activity, glucocorticoid exposure, comorbidity, and treatment intensity. Third, disease-specific evidence is strongest for RA and SLE, while data for vasculitis, myositis, systemic sclerosis, and other ARDs remain limited. Fourth, biomarker and therapeutic-target hypotheses, including endotoxin measurement, Th17/Treg balance, NET markers, and multi-omics endotypes, remain investigational. Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter cohorts with serial immunophenotyping, endotoxin measurement, standardized sepsis and septic shock definitions, and harmonized short- and long-term outcomes. Studies should distinguish sepsis from nonspecific severe infection or bacteremia, report septic shock separately when possible, and stratify analyses by ARD subtype, disease activity, organ involvement, and treatment exposure. Randomized or pragmatic trials of prevention strategies, such as vaccination optimization, glucocorticoid-sparing protocols, antimicrobial prophylaxis in high-risk groups, and post-sepsis follow-up pathways, are also needed.

Conclusion

Patients with ARDs face a disproportionately high risk of sepsis owing to disease-specific immune dysregulation, persistent systemic inflammation, cumulative organ damage, and dose- and duration-dependent immunosuppressive therapies. Although ARDs and sepsis share several immunopathological features, the relationship is disease-specific, phenotype-dependent, and strongly influenced by treatment exposure and healthcare context. Current evidence does not support a uniform genetic or causal relationship between autoimmune diseases per se and sepsis. Instead, modifiable and measurable factors, particularly high disease activity, glucocorticoid exposure, organ dysfunction, infection-prevention gaps, and post-sepsis vulnerability, appear to be dominant determinants of risk and outcome. Future ARD-sepsis research should integrate standardized sepsis and septic shock definitions, disease-specific clinical phenotyping, endotoxin-related biomarkers, multi-omics data, and long-term follow-up. Such efforts are essential to transform the observed time-dependent survival paradox into risk-management pathways and policy-relevant prevention strategies that improve outcomes in patients with ARDs.
