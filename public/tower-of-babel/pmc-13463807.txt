Exploring the rationale for upfront corticosteroid–JAK inhibitor combination therapy in acute severe ulcerative colitis
Authors: Arshdeep Singh, Arshia Bhardwaj, Vandana Midha, Laurent Peyrin-Biroulet, Ajit Sood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC) is a rapidly evolving, cytokine-driven immune emergency characterized by dynamic transitions from innate to adaptive inflammation. Although intravenous corticosteroids remain first-line therapy, 30–40% of patients fail to respond, and many require rescue therapy or subsequent colectomy. Sequential escalation strategies may be temporally misaligned with the accelerated immunobiology of ASUC, as delayed intervention permits immune entrenchment, cytokine redundancy, and diminished treatment responsiveness. Upfront combination therapy, initiated at hospitalization rather than after steroid failure, offers a strategy for early immune interception. Emerging randomized and cohort data suggest that early corticosteroid–JAK inhibitor combinations achieve faster clinical response and lower short-term colectomy rates compared with conventional step-up approaches. This viewpoint examines the immunopathogenic basis underpinning this strategy and synthesizes the mechanistic and clinical rationale supporting early multi-pathway intervention. By integrating evolving biological insights with emerging clinical evidence, it provides a conceptual framework to inform the use of upfront combination therapy in ASUC.FundingNone.Keywords: Acute severe colitis, Colectomy, Janus kinase inhibitors, Corticosteroids, Combination therapy

Introduction

Acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC) represents the most extreme inflammatory manifestation within the spectrum of ulcerative colitis (UC) and remains one of the few true medical emergencies in gastroenterology.1 The current management follows a sequential escalation model in which intravenous corticosteroids (IVCS) are administered first, with rescue therapy reserved for patients who subsequently demonstrate treatment failure. While this approach is rooted in historical practice, it permits ongoing inflammatory activity during the critical early phase of hospitalization. Advances in mucosal immunology have revealed ASUC to be a rapidly evolving inflammatory network driven by multiple interconnected pathways. This accelerated tempo has critical therapeutic implications, as the dominant inflammatory drivers shift dynamically during this short time frame. This evolving understanding raises an important question: should therapeutic intervention target these pathways earlier, before inflammation becomes firmly established? In this viewpoint, we examine the biological rationale, emerging clinical evidence, and potential role of upfront combination therapy with corticosteroids and Janus Kinase (JAK) inhibitors in the management of ASUC.

Biological rationale for early multi-pathway intervention

The pathogenesis of ASUC involves a complex interplay between epithelial barrier dysfunction and dysregulated immune activation. Across human disease and experimental models, the initiating event in ASUC is believed to be catastrophic failure of the intestinal epithelial barrier, rather than primary immune dysregulation.2,3 Disruption of the mucosal barrier permits increased exposure to luminal antigens, leading to activation of innate immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells.4,5 This, in turn, results in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including tumour necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-1β, and IL-6, largely mediated through nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signalling. Neutrophilic infiltration is a hallmark of ASUC and contributes directly to tissue destruction through the release of reactive oxygen species, proteolytic enzymes, and neutrophil extracellular traps.6, 7, 8 Concurrently, antigen presentation promotes expansion of adaptive immune responses, particularly Th1 and Th17 pathways, which further amplify inflammation through cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ), IL-17, and IL-22. Many of these cytokines signal through the JAK–STAT pathway, leading to sustained transcription of pro-inflammatory genes and perpetuation of tissue injury.9

Among the cytokine pathways implicated in intestinal inflammation, IL-6–STAT3 signalling has emerged as a potentially important candidate. Experimental studies suggest that IL-6 may promote T-cell survival, influence Th17 differentiation, and contribute to epithelial dysfunction. Associations between STAT3 activation and disease severity have also been reported in IBD.10 Similarly, signalling through interferons and other cytokines that utilize JAK–STAT pathways may contribute. The cumulative effect is widespread ulceration, crypt destruction, vascular congestion, and severe impairment of epithelial regeneration.11, 12, 13 (Fig. 1).

(B) Barrier disruption and innate immune amplification. Epithelial injury, loss of tight junctions and mucus depletion permit translocation of luminal microbes and microbial products, triggering rapid recruitment and activation of neutrophils, monocytes, and CD14+ inflammatory macrophages, together with activation of dendritic cells. This phase is characterized by high local concentrations of innate cytokines and chemokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, GM-CSF, and CXCL8 (IL-8), driving neutrophil influx, endothelial activation, and self-reinforcing inflammatory amplification. During this early period, immune responses remain relatively plastic and transcription-dependent. (C) Transition to adaptive immune dominance and immune entrenchment. Persistent innate activation promotes antigen presentation and differentiation of pathogenic CD4+ T-cell subsets, particularly Th1 and Th17 lymphocytes. Although neutrophils and TNF-α remain detectable within the inflamed mucosa, they no longer constitute the dominant inflammatory drivers. Adaptive cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-17, IL-22, IL-2, IL-12/23) increasingly dominate the inflammatory milieu and converge on shared intracellular signalling pathways, notably the JAK–STAT axis, sustaining inflammation despite fluctuations in individual cytokines. This stage is marked by progressive cytokine redundancy, reduced immune plasticity, and diminished responsiveness to therapies acting primarily at the level of cytokine transcription. The upper gradient illustrates the progressive contraction of the therapeutic window for immune interception and the parallel development of cytokine redundancy and immune entrenchment over time.

In addition to local mucosal inflammation, ASUC is associated with systemic inflammatory responses manifested by elevated C-reactive protein, hypoalbuminaemia, anaemia, thrombocytosis, and increased intestinal permeability. Hypoalbuminaemia not only reflects severe inflammation but also alters the pharmacokinetics of monoclonal antibodies such as infliximab through increased drug clearance, potentially contributing to primary non-response. Similarly, high inflammatory burden may accelerate faecal loss of biologic agents, reducing therapeutic exposure during the critical induction phase.

Although the precise temporal sequence of these events in humans remains incompletely understood, the available evidence supports a dynamic evolution from an initially treatment-responsive inflammatory process to a more self-sustaining immune state. As inflammatory pathways become increasingly interconnected and mutually reinforcing, a compelling biological rationale emerges for therapeutic approaches that simultaneously target multiple components of the immune cascade.

Current rescue therapy: successes and unmet needs

For patients with steroid-refractory disease, infliximab and calcineurin inhibitors have become the standard rescue therapies. Randomized trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated broadly comparable efficacy between infliximab and cyclosporine, with no clear superiority regarding short-term colectomy rates.14,15 Tacrolimus has emerged as an alternative calcineurin inhibitor in centres with relevant expertise and has shown favourable short-term outcomes in selected patients.16, 17, 18

Despite these advances, important therapeutic gaps remain. Approximately 30–40% of patients fail first-line rescue therapy, while others experience only partial clinical improvement or early relapse.19,20 Increasing numbers of patients now present with prior exposure to anti-TNF agents, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, or JAK inhibitors, complicating subsequent therapeutic decisions. Furthermore, the optimal sequencing of advanced therapies after rescue failure remains poorly defined. Historically, sequential use of infliximab after cyclosporine or vice versa was discouraged because of concerns regarding excessive immunosuppression and infectious complications. These evolving clinical challenges have stimulated growing interest in combination therapy as a strategy to overcome treatment resistance, reduce colectomy rates, and expand therapeutic options for patients with refractory ASUC.

Upfront combination therapy in ASUC

Upfront combination therapy seeks to intervene during the period of maximal immune plasticity, before the pathogenic networks become entrenched, thereby increasing the likelihood of rapid disease control and sustained remission. Importantly, combination strategies differ in the depth and breadth of immune interception they achieve (Table 1). Corticosteroids combined with infliximab provide a form of vertical blockade within a predominantly TNF-centric inflammatory framework. JAK inhibitors offer a mechanistically distinct approach by attenuating signalling downstream of multiple cytokine receptors simultaneously. Unlike therapies directed at individual cytokines, JAK inhibition may provide broader immunomodulation across several interconnected inflammatory pathways. In addition, JAK inhibitors possess several practical advantages in ASUC, including rapid onset of action, oral administration, short half-life permitting dose flexibility, absence of luminal drug loss, and relatively predictable pharmacokinetics despite severe inflammation.

The therapeutic synergy between IVCS and JAK inhibitors lies in their complementary targeting of distinct but interconnected components of the inflammatory cascade.21 Corticosteroids primarily reduce cytokine production at the transcriptional level, while JAK inhibitors limit downstream signal propagation. This dual approach may provide a more comprehensive suppression of inflammatory activity and help reduce the “inflammatory momentum” that characterizes ASUC.22

The concept of early multi-pathway intervention is increasingly supported by emerging clinical data. In a retrospective study, upfront infliximab combined with corticosteroids was associated with superior endoscopic outcomes and durable remission compared with conventional sequential rescue therapy.23 More recently, observational studies evaluating early JAK inhibition have demonstrated encouraging results. Initial proof-of-concept was provided by Berinstein et al., who reported rapid clinical and biochemical improvement with upfront high-dose tofacitinib plus IVCS in a small case series of patients at high risk of corticosteroid failure.24 Similarly, multicenter observational cohorts evaluating upadacitinib have reported low short-term colectomy rates and high rates of steroid-free remission.25 Most notably, the TACOS randomized controlled trial demonstrated significantly higher day-7 response rates and reduced need for rescue therapy among patients receiving tofacitinib in addition to corticosteroids.26 Although cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, these findings collectively support further evaluation of upfront combination approaches (Table 2).

Beyond the short term response

Therapeutic success in ASUC has traditionally been defined by short-term endpoints, particularly avoidance of in-hospital colectomy and early clinical response. While clinically meaningful, these endpoints fail to capture the broader disease trajectory, as long-term outcomes, including recurrent hospitalization, surgery, quality of life, and survival, are equally critical.19,28

It has been proposed that inadequate suppression of inflammation during acute phase permits immune entrenchment, characterized by persistence of tissue-resident memory T cells, epigenetically reprogrammed macrophages, and cytokine-redundant signalling networks.11 Once established, these circuits sustain chronic inflammation, predispose to recurrent severe flares, and increase the likelihood of surgery. Emerging data suggest that early introduction of targeted therapies may partially mitigate this trajectory. The TACOS study, in addition to reducing short-term colectomy, demonstrated sustained clinical benefits beyond the index hospitalization, with lower rates of colectomy and favourable clinical outcomes during follow-up.26 Maintenance infliximab following corticosteroid response and early use of tofacitinib have both been associated with reduced treatment failure, fewer rehospitalizations, and improved event-free survival compared with conventional strategies.29,30 An additional potential advantage of early combination therapy is the reduction in cumulative corticosteroid exposure by enabling earlier disease control and steroid tapering.

From a health economic perspective also, this therapeutic strategy may have important implications for healthcare utilization. ASUC is associated with significant direct and indirect costs, driven by prolonged hospitalization, the need for rescue medical therapies, and surgical interventions such as colectomy. Although upfront combination therapy entails higher initial expenditure, more effective early disease control may translate into reduced downstream resource utilization, including shorter hospital stays, fewer treatment escalations, and lower surgical rates. Nevertheless, robust cost-effectiveness analyses are required to more precisely delineate the balance between upfront costs and potential long-term economic benefits.

Safety considerations

Safety remains a key consideration when combining advanced therapy with IVCS during ASUC. Concerns primarily relate to excessive immunosuppression, with the potential for serious infections, venous thromboembolism, cardiovascular events, and other treatment-related adverse effects. Current evidence from retrospective studies, the TACOS trial, and observational upadacitinib cohorts has not identified major unexpected safety signals; however, available data remain limited by small sample sizes, selected populations, and short follow-up. Consequently, the risks of opportunistic infections, venous thromboembolism, cardiovascular events, and malignancy with intensified immunosuppression remain incompletely characterized. Careful patient selection, exclusion of active infections, appropriate thromboprophylaxis, and close clinical monitoring remain essential when adopting this strategy. Importantly, these risks must be also balanced against those of uncontrolled ASUC, including prolonged hospitalization, sepsis, urgent colectomy, postoperative complications, and mortality. Long-term safety remains uncertain, particularly regarding prolonged dual immunosuppression. Whether upfront combination therapy increases cumulative infectious or malignancy risk beyond that associated with conventional treatment has not yet been established. Large prospective studies with extended follow-up are therefore required to better define the long-term safety profile.

Unanswered questions and future directions

Despite these encouraging observations, several important questions remain. The optimal patient population, timing of initiation, choice of therapeutic agents, and duration of combination treatment have not been clearly established. Whether treatment should be guided by clinical predictors, inflammatory biomarkers, pharmacokinetic monitoring, or molecular signatures also requires further investigation. Most available evidence is derived from retrospective studies and small prospective cohorts, with only limited randomized controlled trial data. Well-designed multicenter trials are needed to compare different combination strategies, and to define the optimal sequencing and de-escalation approaches following induction of remission. Among currently available therapeutic strategies, upfront combination therapy with IVCS and JAK inhibitors represents a biologically plausible combination for achieving rapid control of inflammation in ASUC. Nevertheless, it is not universally effective, and treatment failure and colectomy continue to occur in a subset of patients. This likely reflects the complex and heterogeneous nature of ASUC, in which disease progression is not driven exclusively by cytokine signalling through the JAK–STAT pathway. Alternative immune mechanisms, including innate immune sensing through toll-like receptors, autoantibody-mediated responses, and intracellular signalling via MAPK/ERK, JNK, and PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathways, may also contribute to inflammation and may vary in importance between patients and over time.31 Consequently, JAK–STAT signalling should be viewed as one important component of a broader inflammatory network rather than a singular pathogenic driver. Ongoing randomized trials, including ACESO, ACUTE, and SMART, are evaluating upfront corticosteroid–JAK inhibitor strategies versus conventional step-up therapy and will provide essential evidence on these aspects.

Advances in precision medicine, including biomarker-driven patient selection and therapeutic drug monitoring, may enable a more individualized approach to upfront combination therapy. Future studies should also identify patients most likely to benefit from early intensive treatment while minimizing unnecessary exposure in those expected to respond to conventional rescue therapy (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

ASUC remains a medical emergency in which delayed disease control can lead to colectomy, morbidity and mortality. Emerging evidence suggests that upfront combination therapy with IVCS and JAK inhibitors may achieve more rapid and effective suppression of inflammation, improving short-term outcomes in selected high-risk patients. However, current data are largely derived from observational studies, and key questions regarding patient selection, optimal regimens, safety, and cost-effectiveness remain unresolved. Until further randomized controlled trials are available, upfront combination therapy should be considered a promising but investigational strategy. As the evidence base continues to evolve, this approach has the potential to redefine the management paradigm of ASUC by shifting treatment from sequential rescue to early, targeted disease control in carefully selected patients.
