Implant selection and fixation in radial head arthroplasty: A qualitative analysis of surgeon decision-making
Authors: Harry Beale, Alex Abouharb, Peter Reilly, Angela E Kedgley, Sanjeeve Sabharwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadial head arthroplasty (RHA) requires surgeons to choose an implant and a fixation strategy where comparative trial evidence is sparse. How training, intraoperative judgement and tactile feedback shape these choices remains poorly characterised.MethodsTen UK consultant orthopaedic surgeons sub-specialised in upper-limb arthroplasty and elbow surgery were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling and interviewed using a semi-structured guide. Transcripts underwent inductive thematic analysis until no new conceptual categories emerged.ResultsFive themes and 13 sub-themes were identified: implant selection drivers; fixation philosophy; intraoperative cues; patient profiles and outcomes. Surgeons applied pragmatic, reproducible heuristics where comparative trial evidence is absent. Convergence centred on bone quality as the pivotal determinant of fixation, on modularity for managing anatomical variability and on functional rather than radiographic definitions of success. Surgeons diverged on the threshold for the ‘unfixable’ fracture, on primary stem fixation philosophy and on whether excision remains an acceptable definitive option in low-demand elderly patients.DiscussionSurgeons apply systematic, shared heuristics under uncertainty. Several prioritised determinants, including tolerance of controlled stem toggle, intraoperative bone quality assessment and avoidance of overstuffing, are not captured by current quantitative frameworks. These findings are hypothesis-generating and identify candidates for future comparative work.

Introduction

Radial head arthroplasty (RHA) is the established treatment for unreconstructible Mason type III and IV fractures,1,2 adopted when open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) cannot achieve a stable, congruent articulation. 3 The procedure is underpinned by a developed biomechanical literature defining the principles of radiocapitellar load transfer, lateral column stability and the kinematic consequences of altered radial length,4–6 together with comparative cohort and registry data on revision and survival.7–11 Surgeons have access to a validated range of fixation strategies, including cemented, press-fit and intentionally loose-fit options, alongside monoblock or modular implant designs, each with a coherent biomechanical and historical rationale.12–15

The difficulty in RHA does not, therefore, arise solely from an absence of evidence, but also from the requirement to operationalise general biomechanical principles within highly variable trauma scenarios. Substantive gaps do remain, and the outcome of radial head excision is among the clearest of them, since biomechanical work has described the kinematic and stability consequences of resection 4 without establishing which patients and which elbows tolerate it well in practice. Comparative trial data are likewise sparse or underpowered, and contemporary systematic reviews continue to report equivocal between-implant comparisons.16–18 Registry signals are similarly limited: national elbow arthroplasty datasets have documented substantial completeness and case-ascertainment issues for radial head procedures, 19 no Orthopaedic Data Evaluation Panel (ODEP) rating is available for radial head implants, and the outcome domains routinely captured in registry-based work do not yet align with those that surgeons and patients prioritise. 20 Surgeons must therefore integrate biomechanical reasoning, surrogate registry signals and intraoperative findings to reach a defensible choice in real time, and the resulting variation in practice is well documented.21–23

Qualitative methodology is designed for precisely this task. Rather than measuring established outcomes, it develops concepts: it surfaces the variables, heuristics, and decision thresholds that surgeons use in practice, generates hypotheses for subsequent quantitative testing, and exposes determinants of practice that registries cannot see. 24 Recent qualitative studies in arthroplasty have demonstrated the value of this approach for shoulder 25 and hip26,27 implant selection, identifying constructs such as physiological age, revision burden, and surgeon-specific tolerance for anatomical variation. Surgeon decision-making in elbow surgery has also been identified as a priority for research by the James Lind Alliance. 28 Equivalent work in RHA, where indications are narrower and intraoperative judgement is arguably more pivotal, has not been undertaken.

This study explores the cognitive, experiential, and intraoperative factors that shape surgeon decision-making in RHA.

Methods

Design

This was a qualitative study using reflexive thematic analysis,24,29 reported in accordance with the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research (COREQ) guidelines. 30

Participants and recruitment

Purposive and snowball sampling were used to recruit consultant orthopaedic surgeons practising in the United Kingdom and sub-specialised in upper-limb arthroplasty and elbow surgery. Surgeons below consultant grade were ineligible. Ten surgeons were recruited, drawn from major trauma centres, tertiary upper-limb units and district general hospitals.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview guide was developed from the existing literature and refined through two pilot interviews with non-participant surgeons. Interviews were conducted virtually via Microsoft Teams (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) by the lead investigator, a trauma and orthopaedic registrar and doctoral research candidate with clinical experience in upper-limb trauma. The shared professional background facilitated rapport and accurate interpretation of technical terminology; to limit the influence of the interviewer's own clinical preferences on the line of questioning, a position of reflexive bracketing was adopted. Interviews lasted a mean of 40 min (range 30–50). Recordings were transcribed using the built-in Microsoft Teams transcription service, then manually corrected against the original audio recording for accuracy. Repeat interviews were not conducted.

Data analysis

Transcripts were analysed in NVivo v14 (Lumivero, Denver, CO, USA) using an inductive thematic approach, beginning with line-by-line open coding and progressing through axial grouping to the development of themes. Coding was led by the lead investigator, with the resulting framework discussed iteratively with a second researcher (a trauma and orthopaedic foundation doctor with previous qualitative research experience) to surface alternative interpretations and challenge shared clinical assumptions. Coding proceeded after each interview, with reflexive and analytical memos recorded in NVivo throughout. After the eighth interview, no new codes or conceptual insights were identified, and the two subsequent interviews served to confirm and elaborate existing themes rather than generate new ones.

Ethics and data protection

The study was conducted as an evaluation of professional practice involving healthcare staff recruited solely in their professional capacity, with no patient involvement and no access to patient-identifiable data. Formal Research Ethics Committee review was therefore not required under the UK Health Research Authority decision tool. All participants were fully anonymised. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to interview for the use of their anonymised data in publication.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Results

All 10 participants were consultant orthopaedic surgeons practising in the United Kingdom and specialising in upper-limb trauma and elbow surgery, representing major trauma centres, tertiary upper-limb units and district general hospitals. Nine of the 10 were fellowship-trained, and consultant experience ranged from 4 to over 21 years. Reported annual primary RHA volume ranged from one to more than 15 cases, with four of the 10 performing 11 or more each year (Table 1).

Five main themes and 13 sub-themes were identified (Figure 1). Decision-making was shaped by implant selection drivers, fixation philosophy, intraoperative cues, patient profiles and outcomes, broadly consistent with the distribution of approaches observed in national surveys of radial head fracture management.21,22 Taken together, the themes pointed to a shared pattern of pragmatic clinical reasoning under uncertainty. Surgeons substituted the absence of high-level comparative trial data with reproducible heuristics, drew on biomechanical reasoning and registry data as surrogate evidence, and resolved residual uncertainty through structured intraoperative verification. Theme 1: Implant selection drivers

Three sub-themes emerged: training and habit, the evidence gap and modularity. Running through all three was a search for predictability and intraoperative control.

The development of habitual practice, anchored in fellowship exposure and early consultant experience, was the primary determinant of implant choice for most surgeons. Habit was framed not as inertia but as an active risk-reduction strategy that minimised cognitive load and technical error under the time pressure of trauma surgery.I think we are all creatures of habit. I use [Implant X] because that's what I was trained to use and I've had good results. When you are in a trauma case, you don’t want to be figuring out a new jig or a new reamer. You want muscle memory to take over (Surgeon 1).Surgeons described a high evidentiary threshold for changing systems: a registry alert, a recall, or ‘catastrophic failure’ of their current implant, none of which were considered to be present.Unless there is a registry alert or a massive recall, I am not going to change what works in my hands just because a rep shows me a shiny new brochure (Surgeon 4).The perceived absence of high-level comparative evidence was described not as paralysing but as redirecting decision-making towards surrogate indicators of quality: ODEP ratings (where available), registry survivorship, biomechanical literature and personal audit.There isn’t a randomised trial telling me that a bipolar is better than a monopolar. With other implants I also look at the ODEP rating: if it's a 10A, I feel justified using it, but this is not available for RHA (Surgeon 7).Preference for modular over monoblock designs was near-universal, framed in terms of intraoperative utility rather than biomechanical superiority. Modularity allowed surgeons to trial head sizes, fine-tune joint volume and insert components in tight elbows without disturbing the stem or risking cartilage damage.I use a modular system; it gives you that flexibility. If you are struggling for exposure, getting a monoblock past the capitellum is a nightmare. Modularity saves you from damaging the cartilage (Surgeon 5).Only one surgeon preferred monoblock designs, citing the elimination of dissociation risk despite acknowledging greater technical demand.

Theme 2: Fixation Philosophy

Three sub-themes emerged: biological press-fit, the loose-stem concept and the cementing threshold. The first two sub-themes reflected divergent but internally consistent responses to uncertainty about long-term implant behaviour. The third described a universal salvage pathway, pivoting on bone quality.

Most surgeons preferred uncemented press-fit fixation in patients with adequate bone stock, motivated less by survivorship data than by the anticipated revision burden in younger patients. The ‘nightmare’ of cement extraction from the canal at revision was cited as a primary driver.My default is uncemented press-fit, purely for biological fixation and revision options later. If you cement a radial head in a 40-year-old, and they need a revision in 15 years, getting that cement out of the canal is a nightmare. Press-fit allows for biological fixation and makes the revision scenario much more manageable (Surgeon 8).A second group described an intentionally loose-fitting smooth-stemmed approach, designed to toggle within the canal and accommodate the non-isometric kinematics of forearm rotation. Within this philosophy, stem motion was a design feature rather than a sign of failure.I use a loose-fitting stem, it toggles. It's designed to be loose. The philosophy is that it allows the radial head to find its own centre of rotation, accommodating for the non-isometric rotation of the radius. If you put a rigidly fixed stem in, you are fighting those mechanics (Surgeon 3).Despite this philosophical divergence, all participants identified the same intraoperative bail-out condition. The wide, cylindrical, osteoporotic ‘stove-pipe’ or ‘chimney pot’ canal was unanimously described as a contraindication to press-fit fixation, with cement adopted as a necessary salvage to achieve immediate stability.If you encounter a ‘stove-pipe’ canal in an osteoporotic lady, you simply cannot get an interference fit. You put the broach in and it just spins. In that scenario, you have to change tack. Cement becomes the necessary grout to ensure stability (Surgeon 10).

Theme 3: Intraoperative decision-making

Two sub-themes emerged: the avoidance of overstuffing, and the influence of associated ligamentous injury. The thread running through both was risk asymmetry, with an explicit preference for reversible technical errors over irreversible ones.

The fear of overstuffing the radiocapitellar joint was the most prominent technical theme, raised by every participant. Lengthening the radius relative to the lesser sigmoid notch was framed as a definitive cause of stiffness and capitellar erosion, and was consistently weighted as a worse error than mild under-sizing.Overstuffing is the big enemy. If you put in a head that is too thick, or a stem that sits too high, you lengthen the column. The patient will get stiffness and rapid capitellar erosion. I would rather be slightly under-sized than over-stuffed. If I am between sizes, I always size down (Surgeon 9).This anxiety drove a standardised sequence of intraoperative verification steps: assessing the lesser sigmoid notch alignment, checking the proximal radioulnar joint, and performing a ‘pull test’ for distractability.I spend a lot of time checking the height. I look at the lesser sigmoid notch contact, I check the proximal radioulnar joint, and I do the pull test. If you can’t distract the joint by a millimetre or two, you are too tight. You have to back off (Surgeon 5).Associated ligamentous injury was framed as a determinant that removed excision from the option set. In complex patterns such as the terrible triad, the radial head was characterised not merely as an articular surface but as a critical secondary stabiliser protecting ligament repair.If the ligaments are torn, particularly the MCL, the radial head becomes a more important secondary stabiliser. That dictates your strategy, it mandates replacement over excision. You need that column to protect the ligament repair (Surgeon 7).

Theme 4: Patient profile and indications

Two sub-themes emerged: physiological age and functional demand, and the threshold for the ‘unfixable’ fracture. Surgeons used patient context to compensate for residual uncertainty about implant behaviour.

Chronological age was consistently described as less relevant than physiological age and functional demand. For young, active, manually working patients, surgeons prioritised anatomical restoration and long-term biomechanical stability; for older, lower-demand patients with poor bone, several explicitly endorsed excision as a definitive option, accepting potential instability to avoid the morbidity of implantation and revision. This position was not universal, and the acceptability of excision as a definitive treatment in low-demand elderly patients was among the clearest points of divergence in the dataset.If they are 80, low demand, and have poor bone, I might just excise the fragments. The recovery is quicker, they don’t get implant complications, and they are unlikely to develop symptomatic instability for the tasks they need to do (Surgeon 2).There was unanimous agreement that the primary indication for replacement is the unreconstructible Mason type III or IV fracture, with ORIF as the gold standard and replacement as a salvage option. However, several surgeons described a downward drift in their personal threshold for declaring a fracture unfixable, driven by the perceived predictability of modern arthroplasty relative to ORIF in highly comminuted patterns.I used to try and fix everything. Now, if it looks like a bag of gravel, I move straight to replacement. The results are more predictable than a plate covered in adhesions (Surgeon 6).

Theme 5: Outcomes

Two sub-themes emerged: a functional definition of success, and a hierarchy of failure modes. Surgeons defined success in functional rather than radiographic terms.

When asked to define a successful outcome, the majority described functional milestones (hand-to-mouth feeding and hand-to-perineum hygiene) rather than range-of-motion degrees or radiographic alignment.Success isn’t a perfect X-ray; success is getting their hand to their mouth to eat and getting their hand to the perineum for hygiene. If they have that functional arc and no pain, that is a win, even if they lack 20 degrees of extension (Surgeon 6).Radiographic anomalies, including radiolucent lines around smooth stems, were tolerated where the patient remained asymptomatic.

Success isn’t a perfect X-ray; success is getting their hand to their mouth to eat and getting their hand to the perineum for hygiene. If they have that functional arc and no pain, that is a win, even if they lack 20 degrees of extension (Surgeon 6).

Discussion of failure was dominated by two complications: symptomatic aseptic loosening (distinguished from the asymptomatic toggle of loose-fit stems) and capitellar erosion. Capitellar wear was framed as the most feared long-term complication and was explicitly linked back to the overstuffing theme.We worry about the metal head wearing out the capitellum, that’s the long-term worry. Once you grind that cartilage away, you have converted a unicompartmental problem into a whole joint problem. That is why we are so obsessed with not overstuffing (Surgeon 1).

Discussion

This study provides the first qualitative account of surgeon decision-making in RHA. Read in aggregate, the five themes describe not an absence of evidence-based reasoning but a coherent collective behaviour: in a clinical area where randomised comparative data are absent, registry signals are weak and ODEP ratings are unavailable, surgeons fill that gap with stable, shared heuristics that function as a systematic mode of expertise-driven reasoning. These heuristics are not offered as a substitute for higher-level evidence. Three threads run through the data.

Adaptive decision-making under evidentiary uncertainty

Reliance on training-derived habits, the use of registry survivorship and ODEP ratings as surrogate quality markers, and a high evidentiary threshold for changing systems are not departures from evidence-based practice but coordinated responses to its limits. The constructs surgeons described, including habitual practice as risk mitigation, modularity as a tool for managing anatomical variability, fixation philosophy aligned to anticipated revision burden and functional definitions of success, were highly consistent across diverse practice settings. Similar reasoning has been reported in shoulder arthroplasty, where surgeons resolved uncertainty about reverse versus anatomical implants by anchoring decisions in physiological age, anatomical thresholds and revision profiles, 25 and in revision hip arthroplasty for infection, where surgeons balanced evidence, infrastructure and personal expertise. 26 A similar pattern in hip arthroplasty implant selection has been described around bone quality assessment. 27 The convergence across procedures suggests that what is often framed as ‘non-evidence-based’ practice may instead reflect a reproducible form of clinical reasoning that current outcome frameworks do not capture. Read together, these findings also account for the persistence of variation. Implant choice is anchored in training and then actively maintained as a risk-reduction strategy. The threshold participants set for abandoning a system, namely a registry alert, a recall or catastrophic failure, is one that the present data infrastructure cannot trigger, since no ODEP rating exists for radial head implants and registry ascertainment for the procedure is incomplete. 19 Success is judged functionally rather than radiographically, so a range of implants and fixation philosophies each appear to perform acceptably in the hands of the surgeons who use them. Variation therefore reflects the absence of any feedback signal capable of discriminating between the available strategies, rather than a disregard for evidence, and is consistent with wider accounts of the barriers to changing established surgical practice. 31

Surgeon-prioritised variables absent from outcome models

Several of the determinants that surgeons identified as causally linked to pain, loosening and capitellar erosion are not currently measurable in national registries or recorded in published comparative cohorts. Registry-linked data have been able to demonstrate associations between surgeon-level factors and outcome elsewhere in upper-limb arthroplasty, 32 but the intraoperative determinants described here are recorded in no current registry dataset. These include the tactile ‘scratch-fit’ of the broach engaging cortical bone, the deliberate tolerance of controlled stem toggle in loose-fit philosophies,13,14 the intraoperative classification of canal morphology (notably the ‘stove-pipe’ canal), and the surgeon's functional definition of acceptable loosening. Capitellar wear, identified by surgeons as the most feared long-term complication, is similarly described in the biomechanical and clinical literature as a key failure pathway,33–35 yet is poorly captured by registry datasets. 19 The continued comparison of cemented versus uncemented fixation in registry studies,12,16–18 without reference to these intraoperative determinants, may therefore obscure rather than identify the drivers of implant performance. This study offers candidate variables for incorporation into future quantitative work, particularly studies seeking to refine indications for fixation strategy.

Risk asymmetry as a structuring principle

The universal preference for slight under-sizing over over-sizing, the standardised verification of radial length, and the elevation of capitellar erosion above mechanical loosening in the hierarchy of feared complications all express the same logic: reversible technical errors are preferred to irreversible ones. The biomechanical literature has independently identified overstuffing as a definitive cause of stiffness and capitellar wear,5,6 and our participants treated this evidence base as actionable rather than aspirational. The same framework parallels findings in shoulder arthroplasty, where surgeons favour a ‘one operation’ solution in older patients to minimise the consequences of subsequent revision, 25 and is consistent with the broader behaviour, documented across qualitative studies of arthroplasty decision-making, of weighting downside consequences more heavily than point estimates of risk when comparative evidence is incomplete.26,27,36

The findings also identify a clear set of next steps for the field. Across themes, participants implicitly defined the information they would want to support their decisions: a validated intraoperative assessment of bone quality and canal morphology, comparative data that distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic loosening, and outcome frameworks that capture surgeon-prioritised functional endpoints. Each of these is a tractable target for subsequent quantitative or biomechanical investigation, and each addresses a determinant of practice that is currently inferred rather than measured. These findings are hypothesis-generating, identifying constructs that comparative studies, registry datasets, and formal consensus exercises could now be designed to test.

Strengths and limitations

The study's strengths include the novel focus on RHA, achievement of conceptual saturation by the eighth interview with two confirmatory interviews thereafter, the diversity of UK practice settings represented, and reporting in accordance with COREQ. The depth of clinical engagement enabled by an insider interviewer was preserved by reflexive bracketing and iterative discussion of coding with a second researcher.

Limitations are inherent to qualitative work. All participants practise in the United Kingdom, so the findings cannot speak to decision-making in healthcare systems with different training pathways, implant procurement structures or referral patterns. The sample is also academically and sub-specialty weighted. Nine of the 10 held a higher degree, and all were recruited through a specialist upper-limb network, which is not representative of the orthopaedic consultant body as a whole. This follows from the purposive design, which sought surgeons with substantial RHA experience rather than a cross-section of the profession, and the sample is therefore closer to the group who perform most of this surgery than to the average consultant. Transferability to occasional-volume surgeons managing radial head fractures in general trauma practice is nonetheless limited, and their reasoning may differ. Self-reported reasoning may diverge from observed intraoperative behaviour; video-reflexive ethnography would be a natural complement. The single-interviewer model, while facilitating technical depth, was mitigated but not eliminated by reflexive practice.

Conclusion

This study describes how surgeons manage uncertainty where high-level comparative evidence is absent and identifies why variation in RHA practice is likely to persist. Choices are anchored in training and then maintained deliberately; the threshold participants set for changing practice is one that current registry and rating infrastructure cannot trigger and success is judged functionally, so competing strategies, each, continue to appear successful in routine use. Decision-making is predominantly experiential and intraoperative, pivoting on bone quality and on the avoidance of overstuffing as an irreversible failure mode, with modularity and physiological patient stratification enabling pragmatic adaptation. The determinants that surgeons prioritise (including tactile assessments of bone quality, tolerance of controlled stem toggle and functional rather than radiographic definitions of success) are not currently captured by registry-based outcome frameworks, suggesting that future evaluation of RHA implants and fixation strategies will be most informative where it is structured around these surgeon-prioritised variables. The findings are intended to direct that work rather than replace it.
