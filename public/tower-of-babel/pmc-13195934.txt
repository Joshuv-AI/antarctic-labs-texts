Breast milk-associated mushroom poisoning in a toddler: a case report
Authors: XueJiao Miao, ShaoCong Zheng, Ya Sun, Shan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMushroom poisoning represents a significant global public health concern, particularly in endemic regions with abundant wild mushroom resources. While the pathophysiological mechanisms of acute poisoning through direct ingestion are well-characterized, the potential for secondary poisoning via breast milk remains poorly understood, with only isolated case reports documented in medical literature. This knowledge gap presents considerable challenges for the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of mushroom toxicity in breastfed toddlers.Case presentationWe present a suspected case of a 2-year-8-month-old girl who elevated liver enzymes (AST 64 U/L, ALT 46 U/L), and gastrointestinal symptoms within 20 hours after breast milk from her mother who had consumed wild mushrooms containing amatoxins. The child achieved complete recovery following standard treatments including activated charcoal administration, gastric lavage, and supportive care.DiscussionThis report provides compelling evidence for toxin transmission through breast milk and highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by nonspecific presentations and limited detection methods in pediatric cases. Clinicians evaluating toddlers with unexplained hepatogastrointestinal symptoms, particularly in mushroom-endemic regions, should maintain high suspicion for secondary poisoning and immediately discontinue breastfeeding following maternal toxin exposure. Our findings emphasize the need for evidence-based guidelines for managing breastfeeding mothers with potential mushroom toxin exposure and call for further research on breast milk transmission mechanisms and optimal breastfeeding cessation duration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06779-6.

Introduction

Mushrooms, as macrofungi, can cause acute poisoning when ingested mistakenly. Acute mushroom poisoning exhibits distinct regional, seasonal, and familial clustering patterns [1]. The annual mortality rate ranges from 5 to 10 per 100,000 individuals, primarily concentrated in regions such as Europe, the United States, Japan, China, and Iran [2, 3].

The most common toxic mushroom species belong to the genera Amanita, Lepiota, Galerina, Boletaceae, Russula, Chlorophyllum, Omphalotus, Entoloma, Psilocybe, and Gyromitra. Notably, over 95% of fatal cases are attributed to amatoxin-containing mushrooms, particularly those from the Amanita genus [4, 5].

Clinical manifestations of mushroom poisoning vary depending on the species involved. More than 90% of cases initially present with nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms, such as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea, which do not directly correlate with prognosis. Subsequently, depending on the toxin type, multiorgan involvement may occur, leading to the following clinical subtypes: Acute liver injury, Acute renal failure, Hemolysis, Rhabdomyolysis, Gastroenteritis, Neuropsychiatric symptoms, Photosensitive dermatitis, Other organ damage [6, 7].

Mushroom poisoning represents the most lethal category of foodborne illnesses. While extensive research has been conducted on acute poisoning caused by direct ingestion of toxic mushrooms, reports on secondary poisoning via breastfeeding remain exceedingly rare. This case report describes a toddler who developed secondary poisoning after consuming breast milk from a mother who had ingested toxic wild mushrooms. We aim to enhance clinicians’ awareness for early recognition and management of such cases.

Case presentation

A 2-year-and-8-month-old previously healthy, breastfed female toddler was exposed to wild mushrooms indirectly through breast milk after her mother consumed 100 g of wild mushrooms while continuing intermittent breastfeeding. The child did not directly ingest any mushrooms. Seven hours after ingestion, the mother developed vomiting and diarrhea and presented to a local hospital. At 21 hours post-ingestion, significant liver enzyme elevation was noted, prompting treatment with gastric lavage, activated charcoal adsorption, catharsis, and fluid replacement before transfer to a tertiary hospital. Laboratory tests at 52 hours revealed severe hepatotoxicity (AST 1418 U/L [reference: 9–40], ALT 1501 U/L [reference: 5–40]) with coagulopathy (APTT 44.9 s [reference: 24.5–44.5], PT 18.9 s [reference: 9.5–15.5], PT activity 51% [reference: >70%], INR 1.59). Photographic identification by family members confirmed consumption of Amanita subjungens. All seven individuals who shared the meal developed similar toxic manifestations. Although no blood toxin analysis was performed, mushroom poisoning was diagnosed based on history and clinical presentation (Poisoning Severity Score 3: nausea/vomiting with hepatic failure and coagulation abnormalities following Amanita ingestion). The mother received treatment with silibinin antidote, glutathione hepatoprotection, bile drainage, and supportive care, achieving hepatic recovery by day 9 (ALT 198 U/L, AST 61 U/L) prior to discharge.

The breastfed toddler developed mild transaminitis (AST 64 U/L [reference: 0–40], ALT 46 U/L [reference: 5–40], ALP 243 U/L [reference: 40–160]) with normal GGT (9 U/L [reference: 1–50]), albumin (49.8 g/L [reference: 35–50]), and globulin (19.70 g/L [reference: 20–30]), along with strongly positive stool occult blood (4+) 20 h post-exposure through breast milk. Electrolytes, coagulation profile, renal function, and complete blood count remained normal. Given the confirmed toxin exposure history accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms and mild hepatic/enteric injury (PSS score 2), the toddler was strongly suspected of mushroom poisoning. Management included gastric lavage, activated charcoal (1 g/kg) for toxin adsorption, reduced glutathione (10 mg/kg IV) for hepatoprotection, and intravenous fluid therapy. Discussion

Toddlers and young children are a vulnerable population with the following characteristics: lower toxin tolerance thresholds, limited ability to express symptoms, and nonspecific clinical manifestations. Combined with the lack of reliable toxin detection methods for this age group, these factors often lead to delayed diagnosis and treatment, increasing the risk of severe outcomes. The mechanism of amatoxin poisoning via breast milk remains poorly understood, and reported cases are scarce. Previous studies have documented cases where mothers continued breastfeeding after mushroom poisoning without any symptoms observed in their toddlers and toddlers [8].

Shively et al. (2019) reported a case in which a mother breastfed her 4-month-old daughter 4 hours after consuming poisonous mushrooms (containing Amanita phalloides). At 29 hours post-ingestion, the mother’s AST level was 105 U/L, peaking at 1535 U/L, without creatinine or INR elevation, while her daughter showed no symptoms and had an AST level of 44 U/L. Breast milk collected on the first day of exposure was analyzed by LC-MS/MS, and no amatoxins were detected [9]. Similarly, J. Galland et al. (2021) described a 33-year-old mother who breastfed her 5-month-old daughter three times daily within 36 h after ingesting poisonous mushrooms. At 40 h post-ingestion, the mother’s AST and ALT levels were 612 U/L and 615 U/L respectively, progressing to 805 U/L (AST) and 999 U/L (ALT) by 52 h post-ingestion. LC-MS/MS analysis of breast milk collected at 41 h post-ingestion similarly detected no amatoxins [10].

In this case, the mother developed vomiting and diarrhea 7 hours after ingesting wild mushrooms, exhibited markedly elevated liver enzymes at 21 h, and progressed to severe liver injury (AST 1418 U/L, ALT 1501 U/L) with coagulopathy (INR 1.59) by 52 h. The toddler developed mildly elevated liver enzymes and occult blood-positive stools 20 h after maternal ingestion of the toxic mushrooms. The toddler had no direct exposure to the toxic mushrooms and had been receiving intermittent breastfeeding until symptom onset. The toddler was highly suspected of mushroom poisoning and recovered after treatment with gastric lavage, toxin adsorption, and hepatoprotective therapy. Although no toxin analysis was performed, the clinical presentation and therapeutic response strongly suggested mushroom poisoning-related hepatic and gastrointestinal involvement. To objectively assess poisoning severity, we employed the Poisoning Severity Score (PSS) [4, 11]. According to this system, the mother was classified as PSS grade 3 (severe) due to manifested liver failure and coagulopathy, while the toddler was assigned PSS grade 2 (moderate) based on the observed milder hepatointestinal injury. Compared to the mother who developed symptoms within 7 h of direct ingestion, the toddler only manifested symptoms 20 h after exposure via breast milk. This delay can be attributed to two main mechanisms: First, the difference in exposure routes—the toddler received toxins secondarily through breast milk, requiring a process of toxin transfer from maternal blood to milk and accumulation to a pathogenic threshold [12]. Second, the maternal liver acted as a “primary filter,” metabolizing some toxins and reducing the toxic load entering the milk, resulting in a lower and slower cumulative dose for the toddler. Consequently, the toddler’s symptoms were not only delayed but also milder, consistent with the characteristics of secondary poisoning.

Compared with previous reports, this case demonstrated earlier and more pronounced elevation of both AST and ALT along with increased INR in the mother, indicating that toxins might transfer through breast milk when exceeding a certain threshold. Furthermore, given the diversity of mushroom toxins and the fact that prior cases only tested for amatoxins, other toxins may also be transmitted via breastfeeding.

Therefore, when breastfed toddlers and infants of mothers with mushroom poisoning present with liver dysfunction or gastrointestinal symptoms, secondary poisoning should be suspected. However, the exact mechanism of toxin transfer to breast milk remains unclear and requires further investigation. Based on existing research, potential transfer pathways may include:

Passive diffusion: Amatoxins (molecular weight ~ 900 Da) may overcome the 500 Da molecular weight threshold via concentration gradients. Lactating mothers should avoid exposure to potentially toxic substances, as these compounds may be transferred to toddlers and infants via breast milk. If exposure to suspected toxins occurs, breastfeeding should be discontinued, and the toddler and infant should be closely monitored if the mother shows signs of poisoning. Although J. Galland et al. reported no detectable amatoxins in breast milk at 41 h post-ingestion, the possibility of other toxins and individual variability cannot be ruled out. Due to the lack of large-scale studies, further research is needed to determine the optimal duration of breastfeeding cessation.

In this case, the mother’s diagnosis of Amanita subjunquillea poisoning was based on medical history, clinical presentation, and family identification. No toxin analysis was performed on the mother’s or toddler’s blood, nor on the mother’s breast milk after poisoning. Although the toddler was highly suspected of secondary mushroom poisoning and recovered after conventional treatment (gastric lavage, activated charcoal adsorption, fluid replacement, and hepatoprotective therapy), the lack of direct objective evidence highlights the need for more comprehensive clinical studies to assess the risks of breastfeeding after mushroom poisoning and to determine the appropriate duration of breastfeeding cessation.
