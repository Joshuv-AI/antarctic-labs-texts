Sono-triggered endoplasmic reticulum-targeted ROS burst silencing CD300ld to alleviate polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells for breast cancer treatment
Authors: Xuejun Chen, Shaoyue Li, Qing Lu, Yitong Li, Mingrui Zhu, Shen Zhang, Yan Fang, Congjian Wen, Daohui Yang, Peili Fan, Huixiong Xu, Haohao Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Immunogenic cell death (ICD) significantly boosts anti-tumor immunotherapy effectiveness; however, the recruitment of polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs), driven by factors such as adenosine accumulation and oxygen depletion during ICD, impairs the overall therapeutic outcome as well as facilitates tumor development and metastasis. Recent studies identify CD300ld as a key regulator of PMN-MDSCs recruitment, making it a promising immunotherapeutic target. Here, an innovative strategy is developed to eminently amplify ICD while alleviating PMN-MDSC infiltration upon ultrasound (US) stimulation. A modified generation 5 (G5) poly(amidoamine) dendrimer (G5PBA) was employed to encapsulate hematoporphyrin (GH)—a widely used organic sonosensitizer—and modified the complex with pardaxin peptides (Par) to achieve precise endoplasmic reticulum (ER) targeting, and adsorbed the negatively charged siCD300ld (PGH@siRNA). Under US irradiation, PGH@siRNA precisely accumulates at ER to induce localized reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, and effectively silence CD300ld, thereby amplifying endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS)-mediated ICD and reducing PMN-MDSCs infiltration to reshape the tumor microenvironment. This systematic preclinical evaluations demonstrated enhanced immune activation, suppressed metastasis, and improved therapeutic outcomes. This study introduces a sono-responsive synergistic strategy integrating ER-targeted sonodynamic therapy with gene silencing, offering a novel paradigm for targeting PMN-MDSCs and enhancing triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) immunotherapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03852-7.

Background

Anti-tumor immunotherapy has achieved considerable success in cancer treatment. However, despite its promise, several challenges persist, including suboptimal responses in certain patients, the frequent emergence of resistance, and the suppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TME) [1]. These barriers are pronounced in “cold tumors” such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which exhibits minimal T-cell infiltration and consequently demonstrates poor responsiveness to immunotherapy. The induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) represents an emerging paradigm in cancer immunotherapy, primarily mediated through its dual capacity to eliminate malignant cells while eliciting systemic immune responses. This specific type of regulated cell death is characterized by the release of essential damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including calreticulin (CRT), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP). These DAMPs signal to mature dendritic cells (DCs) and prime T-cells, enhancing tumor antigen recognition and triggering a robust systemic anti-tumor immune response [2, 3].

Recent advancements in ICD-inducing therapies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, photodynamic therapy, and nanoparticle-based delivery systems, have shown great potential in overcoming immune resistance and improving therapeutic outcomes. One of the key mechanisms that trigger robust ICD is endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Severe endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) disrupts cellular homeostasis, triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR) and drives DAMP exposure. Recent studies have demonstrated that directing photodynamic or photothermal therapies specifically to the ER can significantly amplify ICD, thereby enhancing antitumor immune activation [4]. Ultrasound (US) offers noninvasive, deep-tissue, and spatiotemporally precise activation of sonosensitizer-derived reactive oxygen species(ROS) from sonosensitizers; however, most conventional agents lack ER tropism, limiting ER-confined oxidative bursts [4–6]. Consequently, US-responsive platforms that target the ER represent a rational strategy to strengthen ICD while retaining on-demand control. Compared with light-based modalities, ER-targeted, US-responsive activation uniquely combines noninvasive, deep-tissue, on-demand control with ER-localized ROS generation, a feature set expected to strengthen ICD while minimizing off-target exposure—an advantage not achievable with conventional non-targeted ICD inducers. Consistent with this rationale, Pardaxin-derived peptides (Par) has been reported to perturb ER Ca²⁺ homeostasis and activate UPR/ER stress [7, 8], and ER-addressed peptide/nanoplatforms can further amplify ER stress in tumors [4, 9]. Accordingly, ER-targeted US is expected to generate ER-confined ROS that engage ER stress and elicit ER-linked ICD—mechanistically distinct from conventional, nonlocalized ROS-induced ICD characterized by diffuse oxidative injury with variable ER engagement.

However, ICD-based treatments may lead to the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) into tumors. These MDSCs, driven by adenosine accumulation and tumor hypoxia, facilitate tumor immune evasion [10, 11]. Among MDSCs, the polymorphonuclear subset (PMN-MDSCs) is widely implicated as the dominant suppressive compartment in solid tumors [12]. Functionally, PMN-MDSCs primarily suppress T cells through antigen-restricted mechanisms—elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), arginase-1 (ARG1), and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)—whereas monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) mediate antigen-independent suppression via nitric oxide (NO), TGF-β, and induction of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [12–14]. Notably, PMN-MDSCs are significantly enriched in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and poor clinical outcomes [15]. Although various nano-delivery strategies are being explored to target MDSCs, challenges such as drug leakage during circulation and off-target effects due to the lack of specific molecular markers remain significant barriers to their clinical application [16, 17].

Recent studies have identified CD300ld as a crucial regulator of PMN-MDSCs recruitment and immunosuppressive function [18]. CD300ld is highly expressed in various tumors, and is associated with poor prognosis [18, 19]. Mechanistically, CD300ld has been linked to a tumor-derived CD300ld-STAT3/S100A8/A9 axis that promotes infiltration of PMN-MDSCs, fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment and contributing to resistance to immune-checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapies [18]. Targeting CD300ld therefore represents a rational approach to restrict PMN-MDSC trafficking and function, offering a potential complement to ICD-based therapeutic strategies.

Building on these advantages, we developed a US-responsive, ER-targeted nanoplatform that couples amplified ER-ICD with on-target silencing of CD300ld. Specifically, we harnessed the unique structural and functional properties of fifth-generation polyamidoamine (G5 PAMAM) dendrimers—characterized by their well-defined nanoscale spherical architecture, abundant cationic amine groups, and highly tunable surface chemistry—to serve as a versatile nanocarrier. These dendrimers were meticulously engineered to co-encapsulate hematoporphyrin (HMME), a potent sonosensitizer, and siCD300ld, with Par (pardaxin-derived) peptide ensuring precise ER-specific targeting. Upon US irradiation, the system generates ER-confined ROS to intensify ER stress and ICD, while promoting efficient intracellular delivery and lysosomal escape of siCD300ld to attenuate the STAT3/S100A9-linked PMN-MDSC axis. Taken together, this modular design integrates ICD amplification with myeloid-axis modulation within a single platform (Fig. 1).

Results

Synthesis and characterization of PGH@siRNA for efficient siRNA delivery

Initially, we performed a genomic enrichment analysis utilizing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) to assess variations in gene expression patterns between primary breast cancer samples and surrounding non-cancerous tissues. The results revealed a significant upregulation of CD300ld expression in breast cancer tissues of certain patients (Fig. S1). Furthermore, patients with high CD300ld expression exhibited lower five-year survival rates and poorer prognoses than those with low CD300ld expression (Fig. S2). These findings suggest that targeting CD300ld could potentially impede tumor progression. To construct efficient siRNA transfer nanoassemblies, G5PBA polymers were initially presynthesized by modifying PBA to a G5 PAMAM dendrimer under heating conditions (Fig. S3a). The nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR) analysis verified the successful attachment of PBA moieties to the G5 PAMAM dendrimer surface, with an average of 101 PBA groups modified per dendrimer, as determined from the integrated areas (Fig. S4). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measured a hydrodynamic diameter of 130.2 ± 3.3 nm for blank G5PBA. GH was then obtained by co-assembling G5PBA with HMME via hydrophobic and π–π stacking interactions. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of HMME was 67.5%, and the loading content (LC) was 6.7%. The negatively charged siCD300ld interacts with the positively charged GH through electrostatic interactions to obtain GH@siRNA. To develop an ER-targeting nanosystem, the Cat-PEG polymers were covalently modified with pardaxin (Par) to obtain Par-PEG (Fig. S3b). Then, Par-PEG polymers were added to the mixture to form Par-PEG shielded nanoassemblies via reversible boronate esters, ultimately yielding PGH@siRNA (Fig. 2a). The molar ratio of Par to Cat-PEG in each Par-PEG polymer was about 1:1.4 (Fig. S5). The LC of Par and siRNA were calculated to be 6.3% and 7.8%, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 2b-c, through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) examination, the nanoparticles of GH, GH@siRNA and PGH@siRNA were found to exhibit a consistent and regular microstructure. Further DLS analysis revealed a slight increase in the particle size of GH@siRNA and PGH@siRNA compared to GH, which were 126.6 ± 2.4 nm, 137.4 ± 2.1 nm and 157.6 ± 3.7 nm for GH, GH@siRNA and PGH@siRNA, respectively (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the PGH@siRNA displayed considerable stability in PBS at pH 7.4 up to 7 days at 4 ℃, and in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium with 10% fetal bovine serum up to 120 h at 4 ℃, without obvious aggregation (Fig. S6). These results indicate that the polyplexes possess monodisperse nanoscale dimensions, which are favorable for cellular uptake. In addition, the zeta potential of GH was 42.43 ± 1.39 mV, and when siRNA and Par-PEG loading succeeded, the average zeta potential of GH@siRNA and PGH@siRNA decreased to 34.06 ± 0.73 mV and 25.23 ± 0.96 mV (Fig. 2e). The distinct UV–vis absorption features confirm the successful loading of siRNA and the encapsulation of Par-PEG within the nanosystem (Fig. 2f). The capacity to retain siRNA molecules in GH at various w/w ratios was assessed using a gel mobility shift assay. The result indicated that siRNA showed significantly reduced migration at a w/w ratio of 8 or above (Fig. 2g). The data confirmed the fully complex and stable containment of siRNA molecules within GH. Importantly, PGH@siRNA could effectively protect siRNA from RNase A digestion (Fig. 2h), whereas naked siRNA was rapidly degraded (Fig. 2i), underscoring the RNase resistance ability of PGH@siRNA.

ROS generation plays a pivotal role in promoting ICD [20, 21], thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses. US stimulation has been demonstrated as an effective strategy to trigger intracellular ROS production [22, 23]. To evaluate the ROS generation capability of PGH@siRNA under US exposure, singlet oxygen (1O2) production was first assessed utilizing 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) as a molecular probe. Upon US stimulation, a time-dependent decline in the characteristic absorbance of DPBF at 418 nm was observed in the presence of PGH@siRNA, confirming efficient 1O2 generation (Fig. 2j). In contrast, negligible changes were observed in HMME-free PG@siRNA without and with US (Fig. S7), demonstrating that ROS arises primarily from HMME-mediated sonodynamic effects. Furthermore, electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis validated these results, showing the strongest peaks of 1O2 species in PGH@siRNA + US group (Fig. 2k). In addition, ROS generation was further examined in GH@siRNA + US and PGH + US groups (Fig. S8). Comparable DPBF oxidation rates were observed under US irradiation across all formulations (Fig. S8), indicating that ROS production derives from the embedded HMME sonosensitizer rather than from other components of the nanoplatform.

Excellent cellular uptake and ER targeting capabilities of PGH@siRNA

Effective cellular uptake is critical for efficient gene transfection and antitumor effect [24]. To explore the cellular internalization of PGH@siRNA into tumor cells, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry were employed to evaluate uptake efficiency in 4T1 cells. The results revealed a clear time-dependent increase in intracellular fluorescence intensity across all groups, with the 8 h incubation showing the strongest signals. Notably, US treatment significantly enhanced siRNA uptake in both GH@siRNA and PGH@siRNA formulations, indicating that US exposure plays a key role in facilitating intracellular delivery (Fig. S9, 10). Subsequently, subcellular localization studies using CLSM following lysosomal and ER staining revealed that both GH@siRNA + US and PGH@siRNA (without US) predominantly colocalized with lysosomes, whereas PGH@siRNA + US exhibited reduced lysosomal colocalization and preferential accumulation in the ER, underscoring the synergistic effect of Par modification and US in enhancing uptake and precise ER delivery (Fig. 3a-d). As an orthogonal control, mitochondria colocalization was minimal in the PGH@siRNA + US group and similarly low in the other conditions, supporting ER-selective rather than generalized organelle association (Fig. S11).

We then explored the cytocompatibility in 4T1 cells using standard cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) viability test. The findings indicated that PGH@siRNA nanosystems showed little cytotoxicity at concentrations under 1000 nM, indicating a high biocompatibility threshold within this range (Fig. S12a). To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of the PGH@siRNA nanosystem, six experimental groups were established: Control, US only, GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US, PGH@siRNA, and PGH@siRNA + US. CCK-8 assays showed that PGH + US and PGH@siRNA + US significantly reduced 4T1 viability compared with the other groups, whereas US only did not differ from Control (Fig. S12b). Under matched conditions (Fig. S12c), US alone or naked siCD300ld had negligible effects on cell viability, while Par alone induced only a limited reduction in viability. In contrast, the ROS-generating group (HMME + US) caused the most pronounced cytotoxicity (54.5%), supporting that ROS-mediated sonodynamic activation serves as the dominant driver of tumor cell killing and ICD induction. A non-targeting siRNA control (siNC) did not reduce viability (Fig. S12d). Collectively, these data support a framework in which US-triggered ROS mediates tumor-cell killing/ICD, Par-directed ER addressing amplifies this effect, and siCD300ld contributes primarily via immunomodulation in vivo. Apoptosis in 4T1 cells subjected to different treatments using Propidium iodide (PI) staining further confirmed the excellent therapeutic outcome of PGH and PGH@siRNA-mediated US-triggered ERS (Fig. 3e). Upon US irradiation, PGH + US and PGH@siRNA + US groups revealed intense red fluorescence signals. On the contrary, 4T1 cells were stained with strong green fluorescence in Control and US only group, implying that the 4T1 cells were strongly destructed under ER-targeted sonodynamic therapy (SDT). To detect intracellular ROS generation, cells were incubated with ROS indicator DCFH-DA. As displayed in Fig. 3f, PGH and PGH@siRNA under US irradiation revealed stronger intracellular green fluorescence in 4T1 cells, compared to other groups, indicating the efficient US-triggered ROS generation by PGH@siRNA. Collectively, these data indicated that the toxicity of PGH and PGH@siRNA under US irradiation results from the efficient ER accumulation of nanosystem and intracellular in situ ROS bursts.

ER-targeting SDT mediated by PGH@siRNA induced ICD in vitro

DCs efficiently capture antigens released by tumor cells, presenting them to immune cells and migrating to nearby lymph nodes to initiate and regulate adaptive immune responses [25]. To explore the bone marrow dendritic cells (BMDCs) maturation, we evaluated the changes in expression levels of CD80/CD86 by flow cytometry. To rigorously delineate the contribution of each component, six experimental groups were established: Control, US only, GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US (without siRNA), PGH@siRNA, and PGH@siRNA + US, thereby minimizing potential confounding factors. We built up a transwell system to co-culture 4T1 cells after different treatments with BMDCs separated from BALB/c mice (Fig. 4a). After co-stimulation for 24 h, the maturity of DCs (CD80+CD86+ cells) increased in the PGH + US (49.5%) and PGH@siRNA + US groups (51.3%), compared with Control and US only groups (19.3% and 19.7% respectively) (Fig. 4b;Fig. S13). Furthermore, quantitative enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) revealed that cytokine secretion by DCs, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interleukin-12p70 (IL-12p70), interleukin-6 (IL-6), was elevated in the GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US and PGH@siRNA + US groups. This elevation was particularly pronounced in the PGH + US and PGH@siRNA + US groups (Fig. 4c–f). Conversely, these groups had lower IL-10 levels, compared with Control and US only groups (Fig. S14). Collectively, these results confirm 4T1 cells treated with sonodynamic therapy can trigger ICD. The US-activated ER-targeted strategy effectively induces significant ERS, enhancing ICD effects and BMDC maturation, thereby triggering a more robust antitumor immune response than the non-targeted approach.

Given the significance of ERS in ICD induction, we assessed the expression of ERS-related proteins in 4T1 cells following various treatments. CHOP, an ERS-induced apoptosis marker, showed significantly higher mRNA expression in the ER-targeted groups (PGH + US and PGH@siRNA + US) compared with the non-targeted groups (Control, US only and GH@siRNA + US) (Fig. 4g). Consistent with these results, Caspase-3 levels were also increased in the ER-targeted groups, indicating enhanced apoptosis. Moreover, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) demonstrated significant downregulation of CD300ld and STAT3 in the siRNA-loaded groups, confirming target silencing. US further increased the knockdown magnitude, yielding comparable reductions in GH@siRNA + US and PGH@siRNA + US, consistent with US-enhanced internalization for both carriers (Fig. S9,10). Immunofluorescence revealed increased surface CRT in the PGH + US and PGH@siRNA + US groups, and quantitative image analysis confirmed significantly higher levels relative to Control, US only, and GH@siRNA + US (Fig. 4h;Fig. S15). Time-course profiling under PGH@siRNA + US placed ROS at 1 h, CHOP at 8 h, and CRT at 24 h (Fig. S16), indicating that ROS precedes ER stress, which precedes ICD. Collectively, these results confirm that US-triggered ER-targeted therapy induces pronounced ERS, promotes ERS-related apoptosis, and drives efficient ICD.

Transcriptomic analysis and pathway enrichment of PGH@siRNA + US-treated 4T1 cells

To further clarify the mechanism related to cell regulation by PGH@siRNA + US treatment, we conducted RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on 4T1 cells treated with Control and PGH@siRNA + US for 24 h.

The volcano chart analysis identified 1,184 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the PGH@siRNA + US group, with 718 genes up-regulated and 466 down-regulated (Fig. 5a,b). These results suggest that the PGH@siRNA+ US treatment was the main factor driving gene differential expression. KEGG enrichment analysis identified that PGH@siRNA + US treatment activates multiple pathways associated with apoptosis and anti-tumor immunity, with the p53 signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway and apoptosis pathway being the most prominent (Fig. 5c). The p53 signaling pathway is essential in regulating cell cycle progression, apoptosis, DNA repair, and cellular senescence, establishing it as a key tumor suppressor mechanism in cancers such as breast cancer [26–29]. Mitochondrial dysfunction initiates the intrinsic pathway by releasing cytochrome c into the cytoplasm, which activates Caspase-9 and forms the apoptosome, culminating in cell death through Caspase-3 [30]. Bcl-2 family proteins are essential in modulating apoptosis, with pro-apoptotic members like Bax and Bak promoting cell death by increasing mitochondrial membrane permeability [31]. Extrinsic apoptosis initiates when TNF family ligands (e.g., FasL, TRAIL) engage their cognate death receptors [32]. This interaction recruits and activates Caspase-8 [33], which triggers downstream cascades and amplifies apoptotic signals through cross-talk with the mitochondrial pathway, culminating in cell death [31].

In addition, Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that upregulated gene expression profiles exhibited significant association with the activation of immune response and ER stress pathways (Fig. 5d,; Fig. S17a). Consistent with these findings, Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated the significant enrichment of DEGs in immune response-associated pathways and protein transport with ER stress-related pathways, including the positive regulation of apoptotic process pathway, regulation of immune system process pathway, cellular response to reactive oxygen species pathway and response to unfolded protein pathway (Fig. S17b). These pathways enhance anti-tumor immunity by promoting dendritic cell maturation, T cell activation, and cytokine regulation [5, 34, 35]. Furthermore, the DEGs protein interaction network emphasized apoptosis and immune response activation as key contributors to the observed anti-tumor effects (Fig. 5h). In conclusion, these results confirm that PGH@siRNA + US strategy effectively enhances the therapeutic outcomes of ICD therapy by sono-activated severe ERS.

Biodistribution and anti-tumor effects of PGH@siRNA + US treatment in 4T1 tumor models

To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of the US-trigger spatiotemporally controlled PGH@siRNA nanocomplexes, it is crucial to assess the safety and tumor-targeting efficacy of the PGH@siRNA nanocomplexes. Initially, the biosafety of the PGH@siRNA nanosystem was evaluated in healthy BALB/c mice. After tail vein injections of PGH@siRNA nanocomplexes at 5 mg/kg and 10 mg/kg, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested on days 7 and 30 post-injection for histopathological analysis using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. No significant necrosis or inflammation was observed in any of the major organs compared to the Control group (Fig. S18,19). Additionally, biochemical assays evaluating liver function markers (alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)) and renal function markers (uric acid, creatinine and blood urea nitrogen) demonstrated no significant differences between the treatment groups and the Control group (Fig. S20,21). These findings confirm the favorable biosafety and biocompatibility of the PGH@siRNA nanosystem. To further assess its in vivo behavior, whole-blood fluorescence was quantified at predefined time points, and the elimination half-life (t₁/₂) was estimated to be approximately 7.2 h (Fig. S22).

Subsequently, the tumor-targeting capability of the PGH@siRNA nanocomplexes was assessed in BALB/c mice with 4T1 tumors. The biodistribution of Cy5.5-NHS-labeled PGH@siRNA within nanocomplexes was assessed in vivo using fluorescence imaging. A fluorescence signal at the tumor site became detectable as early as 2 h post-injection, indicating the onset of tumor accumulation and targeted delivery (Fig. S23). The fluorescence intensity increased over time, reaching its maximum at 24 h, likely due to the prolonged circulation and enhanced stability of the nanocomplexes due to the enhanced permeability and retention effect (EPR) (Fig. S23; Fig. 6a, b).

Building on this excellent tumor-targeting behavior, we further evaluated the in vivo US-immunotherapy efficacy of PGH@siRNA in a primary 4T1 tumor mouse model. On the seventh day of tumor inoculation, mice were randomly divided into six groups: Control, US only, GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US, PGH@siRNA, and PGH@siRNA + US, with treatments administered from days 7 to 14 (Fig. 6c). Tumor growth was monitored every 48 h until day 19. US only group showed negligible suppression of tumor growth. In contrast, GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US, and PGH@siRNA + US treatments could delay tumor growth. Notably, compared with PGH + US (ER-targeted group) and GH@siRNA + US (gene silencing group) groups, the PGH@siRNA + US group exhibited the slowest tumor growth rate (Fig. 6d, e). These results confirmed that the US-triggered ER-targeted strategy combined with effective silencing of CD300ld exerted a pronounced anti-tumor effect in vivo. Moreover, the treated mice exhibited negligible changes in body weight (Fig. S25), further demonstrating the low systemic toxicity of the PGH@siRNA nanocomplex. The changes in tumor weight closely mirrored the tumor volume dynamics (Fig. 6f). Histological examinations of tumor sections from various groups corroborated these findings. Ki-67 staining showed that the PGH@siRNA + US treatment most effectively suppressed tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 6g). In addition, immunofluorescence analysis using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) revealed maximal tumor necrosis and apoptosis in the PGH@siRNA + US group (Fig. 6g), as evidenced by the most intense magenta fluorescence in TUNEL staining. In contrast, minimal necrosis was observed in the control group. In contrast, the tumor tissues of the GH@siRNA + US and PGH + US groups were partially damaged. These findings further substantiate that the superior antitumor immune response elicited by US-activated PGH@siRNA arises from the synergistic effect of spatiotemporally controlled, ER-targeted ICD amplification and the concurrent alleviation of PMN-MDSC infiltration. Taken together, relative to ER-SDT alone (PGH + US) or siRNA delivery alone (GH@siRNA + US), the PGH@siRNA + US arm achieved the greatest tumor control, consistent with a synergistic action of ER-confined ICD induction and US-enhanced CD300ld silencing that attenuates the PMN-MDSC/STAT3 axis.

In vivo anti-tumor immune activation of PGH@siRNA + US treatment

To explore the mechanisms underlying the potent antitumor immune response elicited by the US-activated PGH@siRNA system, we assessed DCs maturation, lymphocyte infiltration, and cytokine release. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of mature DCs (CD11c+CD80+CD86+) in the PGH@siRNA + US group (69.7%), compared to the Control (46.9%), US only (47.6%), GH@siRNA + US (53.1%), PGH + US (65.5%), and PGH@siRNA (51.9%) groups (Fig. S26, 27). These results demonstrate the combination of ER-targeted ICD amplification and precise CD300ld silencing via synergistically promotes DC maturation in vivo. Notably, the highest DC maturation rate observed in the PGH@siRNA + US group underscores the dual contribution of spatiotemporally controlled ER stress-induced ICD by US and PMN-MDSC infiltration alleviation in optimizing antigen presentation and immune activation.

Assessment of CD8+ T cell infiltration in primary tumors via flow cytometry verified systemic adaptive immunity activation. The Control group exhibited a lower presence of CD8+ T cells, indicative of the “cold” tumors. The GH@siRNA + US and PGH + US groups exhibited limited CD8+ T cell infiltration, likely due to inadequate induction of ICD. The PGH@siRNA + US group exhibited a 3.6-fold increase in CD8+ T cell proportion relative to the Control group (Fig. 7a; Fig. S28). Additionally, significant downregulation of PMN-MDSCs (CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+) was observed in the PGH@siRNA + US group (Fig. 7b; Fig. S29). To directly interrogate the contribution of S100A9 to ER-SDT–associated recruitment, we added an in vivo S100A9-neutralization arm and quantified intratumoral PMN-MDSCs across four groups. The results were as follows: Control + isotype antibody (19.0%), PGH + US + isotype antibody (ER-SDT alone, 22.3%), PGH + US + anti-S100A9 antibody (13.1%), and PGH@siRNA + US + isotype antibody (CD300ld knockdown, 12.9%) (Fig. S29, 30). Neutralization decreased PMN-MDSCs infiltration relative to ER-SDT only group, and CD300ld silencing yielded a similar reduction,consistent with CD300ld functioning upstream of the STAT3/S100A9 axis in mediating PMN-MDSC recruitment. The immunofluorescence stain of tumor sections corroborated the results from the flow cytometry evaluation, revealing an elevation in CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) infiltration and a decrease in PMN-MDSC infiltration in tumors treated with PGH@siRNA + US (Fig. 7c; Fig. S31). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis confirmed reductions in exhaustion-associated CD8+ T-cell subsets and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in PGH@siRNA + US group, with PD-1+CD8+ T cells (11.1%), TIM-3+CD8+ T cells (11.8%), CD4+FoxP3+ Tregs (10.0%) compared to Control (26.2%, 44.4%, 27.6%, respectively, Fig. S32,S33). Collectively, these findings indicate that the US-triggered, ER-targeted strategy effectively induces potent ICD and activates adaptive anti-tumor immunity, which is further amplified by CD300ld silencing through alleviation of PMN-MDSCs-mediated immunosuppression. Cytokine analysis by ELISA revealed that in PGH@siRNA + US group, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-12p70, and IL-6 were markedly increased, whereas IL-10 was decreased in serum, with similar cytokine patterns observed in tumor lysates (Fig. 7d–h; Fig. S34).Taken together, these findings are consistent with ER-targeted SDT–amplified ICD and relief of myeloid suppression upon siCD300ld, supporting a more permissive intratumoral T-cell response.

We utilized western blot analysis to explore the immunoregulatory mechanisms associated with the immunosuppressive TME remodeling resulting from CD300ld downregulation [13, 36]. Tumors from GH@siRNA + US and PGH@siRNA + US showed a significant decrease in CD300ld and S100A9 protein expression, whereas Control, US only, and PGH + US showed no significant change (Fig. 7i). Given the pivotal role of STAT3 in MDSC differentiation and in driving S100A9 transcription [22, 31, 37], we further assessed STAT3 signaling. Western blot demonstrated that both total and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) were decreased in siRNA + US arms, including GH@siRNA + US and PGH@siRNA + US groups (Fig. 7i), indicating suppression of the STAT3 axis despite elevated IL-6. Functionally, PGH + US increased CHOP consistent with ER-confined SDT, but did not lower p-STAT3 or S100A9, nor reduce intratumoral PMN-MDSCs (Fig. 7c, i–j). In contrast, PGH@siRNA + US decreased total/p-STAT3 and S100A9, reduced PMN-MDSCs infiltration, and coincided with higher CD8⁺ T-cell activity (Fig. 7c, i–j). Together with increased CHOP and cleaved caspase-3 in PGH@siRNA + US group, these data indicate that while ER-SDT elicits ER-stress/ICD signaling, CD300ld silencing is required to attenuate the STAT3/S100A9 myeloid axis and sustain T-cell responses.

Anti-distant tumor effects of PGH@siRNA + US

We assessed the effects of PGH@siRNA + US treatment on distant tumors via a bilateral murine model bearing 4T1 tumors. Mice were randomly assigned to either the Control group or the PGH@siRNA + US group. For bilateral tumor modeling, 4T1 cells were first implanted in the right mammary fat pad (primary site), then subsequently inoculated to the left abdominal region (day 6) to establish distant tumors. The protocol for local tumor treatment was applied exclusively to the primary tumor, leaving the distant tumor untreated (Fig. 8a). Throughout the treatment period, the growth of both primary and distant tumors was monitored, with data collected and analyzed after 28 days. The distant tumors in the control cohort exhibited significantly accelerated growth rates, as evidenced by the tumor progression curves (Fig. 8b). In contrast, PGH@siRNA + US treatment effectively suppressed the growth of distant tumors. In addition, treated mice exhibited no significant abnormal weight changes (Fig. S35), indicating minimal systemic toxicity. Upon completion of the treatment, bilateral tumor tissues were harvested and their masses quantified, showing changes in tumor weight that mirrored the trend observed in tumor volume (Fig. 8c), confirming the substantial therapeutic efficacy and effective control of distant tumors.

This distant tumor suppression may originate from the systemic antitumor immunity, mediated by PGH@siRNA + US treatment. As shown in Fig. 8d, e and Fig. S36, flow cytometry analysis revealed a notable increase in CD8+ T cells and a reduction in PMN-MDSCs within distant tumor tissues.In support, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the increased CD8+ T cell infiltration and downregulation of PMN-MDSCs in the slices of distant tumors (Fig. 8f, g; Fig. S37). These results suggest that effective control of distant tumors is achieved through the activation of anti-tumor immunity while simultaneously reducing immune suppression, thereby enhancing the synergistic effect of ER-targeted acoustic immunotherapy.

Lung metastasis is a common cause of death in cancer patients [38]. An effective cancer treatment should target both the primary tumor and lung metastases. Therefore, a lung metastasis model was established (Fig. 8h). To prolong survival, the primary tumors were resected on day 21. To evaluate the anti-metastatic effects of the treatments, we collected lung tissues from surviving mice on day 42 and assessed lung metastasis usingthrough fluorescence imaging system and H&E staining. The PGH@siRNA + US group demonstrated a marked reduction in lung metastases compared to the Control group, indicating a prominent anti-metastatic effect(Fig. 8i–k; Fig. S38). These results demonstrate that the US-activated PGH@siRNA-based combinatorial immunotherapy effectively inhibits lung metastasis in treated mice.

To explore the potential mechanisms by which this tumor immunotherapy strategy elicits robust immune memory, we analyzed changes in memory T cells in mouse spleens using flow cytometry. The findings revealed a notable rise in effector memory T (TEM, CD62L⁻CD44⁺) cells and a reduction in central memory T (TCM, CD62L⁺CD44⁺) cells among CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in long-term surviving mice after PGH@siRNA + US treatment, suggesting a significant transition from TCM to TEM phenotypes (Fig. 8l–m; Fig. S39). TCM cells, primarily located in lymph nodes, lymphoid tissues, and the spleen, are responsible for long-term memory of tumor antigens [39]. Upon antigen stimulation, TCM cells continuously generate a large number of TEM cells, which further differentiate into numerous effector T cells to exert antitumor effects [39–41]. Therefore, these findings confirm that PGH@siRNA + US treatment promotes the formation of memory T cells, thereby establishing a robust anti-tumor immune response to prevent lung metastasis.

Discussion

TNBC constitutes 15–20% of all breast cancer subtypes and is characterized by pronounced tumor heterogeneity, invasiveness, early onset, and a high risk of recurrence and metastasis [42–44]. Owing to the absence of specific targets, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment for TNBC but provides limited benefit. ICD-based antitumor immunotherapy has exhibited promise for improving the treatment and prognosis of TNBC [45]. However, ICD efficacy is often hampered by the recruitment of PMN-MDSCs, which reinforce an immunosuppressive TME. To address this, we rationally designed a dendritic macromolecular nanosystem—PGH@siRNA that integrates ER–targeted acoustic sensitizers with siCD300ld delivery. Upon US activation, ER-confined ROS bursts intensify ERS and ICD, while CD300ld silencing mitigates PMN-MDSC recruitment. Together, these effects enhanced DC activation, CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and suppression of primary tumors, distant tumor growths, and lung metastases in TNBC.

Previous studies have shown that ER-targeted SDT can amplify ER stress and promote ICD [8, 46, 48]. In our study, PGH@siRNA nanosystem specifically accumulated in the ER, unlike non-targeted formulations that localized to lysosomes. Upon US activation, the confined ROS burst upregulated CHOP and triggered CRT exposure in a time-dependent manner. Compared with light-based phototherapies, US provided deeper tissue penetration and precise spatiotemporal control over ROS generation and siRNA release [46–48]. Importantly, the dendritic scaffold protected siRNA from degradation, promoted endosomal escape, and ensured efficient gene silencing within the ER microdomain. This integrated design establishes PGH@siRNA as both an amplified ICD inducer and an effective gene-regulation vehicle within the same subcellular niche. Distinct from prior US-triggered, ER-targeted platforms that primarily reported tumor-cell ICD endpoints, PGH@siRNA couples ER-specific SDT with on-target siCD300ld to concurrently modulate the myeloid/STAT3 axis.

Functionally, DC activation in our setting was primarily driven by ICD-associated danger signals, with cytokine signaling playing a supportive role. Importantly, inflammatory responses remained well-controlled without systemic toxicity. This DAMP-cytokine dual pathway mechanism supports both the immunostimulatory efficacy and systemic safety of the nanoplatform. Furthermore, multicolor flow cytometry revealed a reduction in exhausted PD-1⁺CD8⁺ and TIM-3⁺CD8⁺ T cells, as well as decreased Treg populations in the PGH@siRNA + US group, consistent with enhanced effector T cell activity and a more favorable immune microenvironment. Tumor cytokine profiles mirrored the serum trends, indicating restrained intratumoral inflammation and being consistent with ICD-mediated DC maturation.

Beyond tumor-cell ICD, the myeloid compartment—particularly PMN-MDSCs—critically shapes antitumor immunity across solid tumors. These cells are the predominant suppressive subset across solid tumors [12] and contribute significantly to immune evasion in TNBC. Therapeutic approaches such as CXCR2 inhibition or blocking tumor-derived NAAG signaling with MK801 can restrict PMN-MDSC trafficking but often cause systemic neutropenia [49–51]. In our study, CD300ld silencing significantly reduced intratumoral PMN-MDSC infiltration and enhanced CD8⁺ T cell accumulation, indicating a reshaped immune landscape. Notably, despite a modest increase in IL-6, phosphorylated STAT3 levels decreased upon CD300ld knockdown, as confirmed by Western blot, suggesting suppression of the STAT3/S100A9 axis. These findings highlight that CD300ld functions as a key driver of myeloid-mediated suppression in TNBC, and its inhibition may provide a more targeted immunomodulatory approach than existing strategies.

A literature-based qualitative comparison of representative US-triggered, ER-targeted systems [8, 46, 48] suggests that most prior designs emphasize tumor-cell ER-ICD readouts (e.g., CRT, HMGB1/ATP) without engaging myeloid/STAT3 regulation. In contrast, PGH@siRNA integrates Par-directed ER-SDT with siCD300ld delivery, sustaining ER-linked ICD with well-defined temporal kinetics—ROS generation at 1 h, CHOP induction at 8 h, and CRT exposure at 24 h—while simultaneously attenuating the STAT3/S100A9-associated PMN-MDSC axis. This dual mode of “ER stress amplification and immune reprogramming” differentiates PGH@siRNA from previously reported US/ER nanoplatforms.

Conclusion

In summary, our study presents an innovative, US-responsive nanoplatform that synergistically combines ER-targeted ICD amplification with precise CD300ld silencing, offering spatiotemporally controlled and tumor-specific immunotherapy for TNBC. This is, to our knowledge, the first demonstration of using a US-triggered ER-targeted delivery system to regulate PMN-MDSCs via CD300ld silencing, providing a safe and effective approach with minimal toxicity. The strategy overcomes major limitations of ICD-based therapies and holds significant translational promise for treating TNBC and other solid tumors with immunosuppressive microenvironments.

Methods

Material

All chemicals in our study were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise specified. Amine-terminated G5 PAMAM dendrimer (Mw: 28,826 Da) was sourced from Rayson Biotech Co., Ltd. (Xi’an, China). 4-(Bromomethyl) phenylboronic acid (Mw: 214.85 Da) was obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Catechol-modified polyethylene glycol maleimide (Cat-PEG, Mw: 2000 Da) was synthesized by Ponsure Biological (Shanghai, China). The Pardaxin (Par) peptide (H-GFFALIPKIISSPLFKTLLSAVGSALSSSGGQE-OH) was synthesized by Qiang Yao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Hematoporphyrin monomethyl ether (HMME) and cy5.5-NHS was obtained from Xi’an Ruixi Biological Technology Co. Ltd. RNase A was supplied by J&K Scientific (Shanghai, China). Antibody against CHOP (30 kDa, Catalog No. 66741-1-IG) was sourced from Sanying Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The CD300ld antibody (40–43 kDa, Catalog No. 30798-1-AP) was obtained from Thermo Fisher. Antibodies against S100A9 (13 kDa, Catalog No. GB111149) and STAT3 (88 kDa, Catalog No. GB12176) were obtained from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Phospho-STAT3 (88 kDa, Catalog No. AP0705) were obtained from ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cleaved caspase-3 antibody (17 kDa, Catalog No. AF7022) was obtained from Affinity Biosciences Co., Ltd. All siRNAs, including Cy3-labeled siRNA (conjugated at the 5′ end), were synthesized by Qing Ke Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The sequences for siCD300ld were as follows:

Sense: GAUGGUCUUUGUGGAGUUA (dT);

Antisense: UAACUCCACAAAGACCAUC (dT).

Modification of amine-terminated G5 dendritic polymer and encapsulation of HMME

G5 PAMAM dendrimer (100 mg) was reacted with 4-(Bromomethyl) phenylboronic acid in anhydrous DMSO at a molar ratio of 1:256 for 48 h at 80 °C. The product was purified by dialysis (MWCO: 8000-14,000 Da) for 72 h in distilled water (9 times, 2 L), followed by lyophilization to yield PBA-modified G5 dendritic polymer (G5PBA). To encapsulate HMME, 16 mg G5PBA and 2 mg HMME were dissolved in 2 mL DMF, followed by the slow addition of 5 mL double-distilled water under ultrasonic conditions for 10 min. The solution underwent dialysis (MWCO: 8000-14,000 Da) for 4 h, followed by lyophilization, and designated as GH.

Synthesis of Par-modified PEG polymer

Par-modified PEG (Par-PEG) polymer was synthesized by reacting Cat-PEG with Pardaxin (Par) in PBS. Specifically, the amino group of the Par peptide was protected using (Boc)₂O (1:5.2 molar ratio) in DMF under ice-cold, dark conditions for 12 h. EDC/NHS activation (Par: EDC: NHS = 1:5:10) was performed for 2 h before the addition of Cat-PEG (1:1 molar ratio). The reaction proceeded for 24 h, followed by deprotection using 12 M HCl, neutralization with 3 M NaOH, dialysis for 48 h, and lyophilization.

Synthesis of GH@siRNA and PGH@siRNA

The GH@siRNA nanocomplex was prepared by incubating GH with siRNA (10:1 w/w) at 37 °C for 20 min. Par-PEG was mixed with GH@siRNA (1:5 mass ratio) and stirred for 24 h, followed by dialysis and lyophilization to obtain PGH@siRNA.

Characterization of PGH@siRNA

1H NMR spectra were obtained in D2O using Bruker DRX-400 and DRX-500 instruments (Switzerland). TEM (FEI Tecnai G2 F20) and AFM (Burker Dimension FastScan) were used to analyze nanostructure morphology. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were determined utilizing a Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern, UK), based on triplicate measurements to ensure reproducibility. Optical absorption measurements were conducted with a PerkinElmer Lambda 950 spectrophotometer operating across the UV-visible-NIR spectral ranges.

Encapsulation efficiency and drug loading measurement

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of HMME, as well as the loading amounts of Par and siRNA, in the PGH@siRNA nanosystem were determined using UV–visible spectrophotometry or HPLC–UV analysis. HMME and siRNA were quantified by UV–visible spectrophotometry at 398 nm and 260 nm, respectively, while Par (a peptide) was analyzed by HPLC–UV at 214 nm. The concentrations of free and total components were determined from corresponding calibration curves. EE and DL for HMME were calculated from the incorporated and total amounts, whereas the DL values of Par and siRNA were obtained by comparing their incorporated amounts with the total nanoparticle mass.

Gel electrophoresis and RNase a protection assays

GH@siRNA complexes at varying weight ratios (0–10) underwent 2% agarose gel electrophoresis with ethidium bromide staining, conducted at 80 V for 40 min at 37 °C. Bands were documented under UV light utilizing a Bio-Rad Gel Doc system.

RNase A protection assay: naked siRNA and PGH@siRNA were each incubated with 0.01 mg/mL RNase A at 37 °C for different durations. Subsequent siRNA release was achieved using 2% SDS, followed by electrophoresis. siRNA bands were visualized using a Gel Doc system (Bio-Rad) under UV transillumination. Naked siRNA was used as the control.

Intracellular ROS generation and localization DPBF

A 5 µL DPBF solution in DMSO was mixed with PGH@siRNA (100 µg/mL in PBS). The mixture underwent US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W cm− 2, 50% duty cycle) for predetermined times (0, 1, 3, 5 min). 1O2 generation was evaluated by monitoring absorbance changes at 418 nm with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. In ESR measurements, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) served as a 1O2 trapping agent. PGH@siRNA was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4), followed by the addition of TEMP. The mixture underwent the same ultrasound treatment. 1O2 production was detected using an ESR spectrometer. For comparison, PG@siRNA + TEMP and PG@siRNA+ US + TEMP were also evaluated.

Cell culture

Both parental 4T1 cells (American Type Culture Collection) and Luc+4T1 cells (OBiO Technology) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and with media refreshed every 48 h.

CCK assay

Cell viability was assessed via CCK-8 assay to assess the biosafety and synergistic therapeutic efficacy of the nanosystem. 4T1 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were treated with increasing doses of PGH@siRNA (0, 50, 100, 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000 nM) for 12 h. In addition, cells were subjected to various treatment conditions according to the experimental design under ultrasound exposure (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W cm⁻², 50% duty cycle, 5 min).

Cellular uptake and subcellular co-localization study

4T1 cells were incubated with Cy3-conjugated GH@siRNA or PGH@siRNA for 2, 4 and 8 h, with or without ultrasound exposure. The US parameters employed were 1.0 MHz frequency, 1.0 W cm⁻² intensity, 50% duty cycle, and 5 min duration. After treatment, cells were rinsed three times with PBS. Following fixation, cellular nuclei were counterstained for visualization. Quantitative cellular uptake was further analyzed by flow cytometry under the same experimental conditions to evaluate internalization efficiency. For further investigation of subcellular localization, 4T1 cells were incubated with PGH@siRNA or GH@siRNA nanocomplexes, followed by staining with ER-Tracker, lysosomal dye, or Mito-Tracker for 30 min to visualize the endoplasmic reticulum, lysosomes, and mitochondria, respectively. Following PBS washing, nuclei were counterstained, and CLSM was used to assess the co-localization of nanocomplexes with specific organelles. Manders’ colocalization coefficients were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Apoptosis detection

4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells) were seeded and incubated to allow adherence. Following the designated treatments, cells were rinsed with PBS, incubated with calcein-AM and PI, and examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Intracellular ROS generation and time-resolved ICD analysis

4T1 cells were analyzed at 0, 1, 8, and 24 h after ultrasound (US) exposure to determine the temporal relationship between ROS generation, ER stress, and immunogenic cell death (ICD). US irradiation was performed at 1.0 MHz frequency, 1.0 W cm⁻² intensity, 50% duty cycle, for 5 min. Intracellular ROS was quantified by flow cytometry using DCFH-DA staining, ER stress was evaluated by Western blot of CHOP, and ICD onset was determined by flow cytometric detection of cell-surface calreticulin (CRT).

Intracellular ROS generation detection

Intracellular 1O2 production was evaluated using ROS indicator DCFH-DA. 4T1 cells were allocated into six groups: Control, US only, GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US, PGH@siRNA, and PGH@siRNA + US. Cells underwent a 6-hour incubation at 37 °C with their designated treatments. The medium was refreshed with 100 µL of serum-free medium containing 10% DCFH-DA. Cells were exposured to US irradiation (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W cm− 2, 50% duty cycle, 5 min). After an additional 30-minute incubation, cells were rinsed twice with PBS, and intracellular ROS production was visualized utilizing CLSM.

In vitro DC maturation assessment

DCs were extracted from the bone marrow of 6-week-old female BALB/c mice. 4T1 cells were pretreated under various conditions for 12 h. After treatment, 4T1 monolayers were washed three times with PBS and replenished with fresh medium. Co-culture was performed in Transwell inserts with treated 4T1 cells in the upper chamber and immature BMDCs in the lower chamber. BMDCs were not directly exposed to ultrasound or nanoparticles at any point. Flow cytometry assessed DC maturation using anti-CD11c-FITC, anti-CD80-PE, and anti-CD86-APC antibodies (BioLegend; 1:100 dilution in PBS). Supernatants were collected for ELISA-based quantification of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-12p70, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ.

qPCR

4T1 cells were cultured at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/well, with 24 h allowed for complete attachment prior to further experiments. After the designated treatments and a 6-hour incubation period, total RNA was isolated, followed by isopropanol precipitation and a 75% ethanol wash. The RNA pellet was subsequently dissolved in DEPC-treated water. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the reverse transcription kit from Roche, and quantitative PCR was performed. Gene expression levels were normalized to Gapdh, and each qPCR assay was conducted with three technical replicates (n = 3). Primer sequences were as follows:

Mouse CHOP: Forward: CCAGGAAACGAAGAGGAAGAAT;

Reverse: CACTGACCACTCTGTTTCCGTTT.

Mouse Caspase-3: Forward: AGCACCTGGTTACTATTCCTGGAG;

Reverse: GGATCTGTTTCTTTGCGTGGA.

Mouse CD300ld: Forward: GCTGATGGTCTTTGTGGAGTTAC;

Reverse: GCAACAGGACTATGGGTCTTCA.

Mouse STAT3: Forward: AACAGCCGCCGTAGTGACAGA;

Reverse: GTCTCCTTGGCTCTTGAGGGTTT.

CRT detection

4T1 cells (1 × 104 cells) were cultured in 35- m confocal dishes (Φ = 15 mm) and divided into six groups for the specified treatments. Cells were incubated overnight, rinsed and subsequently fixed. Cells were incubated with rabbit anti-CRT primary antibody for 1 h, followed by labeling with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H&L). Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (20 min) prior to CLSM imaging.

Transcriptomics analysis

4T1 cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. After receiving the specified treatments, cells were incubated for an additional 6 h, then carefully collected for transcriptomic analysis.

In vivo safety evaluation

BALB/c mice (6 weeks old, n = 15) were assigned to three treatment groups, receiving treatments of Control, 5 mg/kg PGH@siRNA, or 10 mg/kg PGH@siRNA. Mice were sacrificed on day 7 and 30 after treatment. Biochemical analysis of blood samples was conducted to evaluate liver and kidney function. Vital organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested on day 7 and 30 after treatment for H&E-based histopathological analysis.

In vivo fluorescence imaging

Female 6-week-old BALB/c mice were purchased from Life River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. 1× 106 4T1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right second mammary gland of the mice to establish the unilateral 4T1 primary tumor model.

Mice with tumors measuring 150–200 mm³ were intravenously injected with 150 µL of cy5.5-NHS-labeled PGH@siRNA at a dosage of 5 mg/kg. Prior to in vivo fluorescence imaging using the VISQUE imaging system at different time points post-injection, anesthesia was administered. Mice were euthanized through cervical dislocation at 24 and 48 h, and vital organs were collected for fluorescence signal analysis. In parallel, tumors and major organs were harvested for siRNA quantification via qPCR to validate tissue-specific distribution beyond fluorescence imaging.

Pharmacokinetic analysis of PGH@siRNA

To evaluate the in vivo circulation behavior of PGH@siRNA, Cy5.5-NHS-labeled nanocomplexes were intravenously injected into BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors. Whole blood samples (25 µL) were harvested at predetermined time points post-injection, then diluted with an equal volume of PBS. The fluorescence intensity of the diluted blood samples was quantified with a microplate reader and normalized to initial values. Pharmacokinetic parameters were determined by fitting the fluorescence–time data to a two-compartment model. To estimate the terminal elimination half-life, fluorescence data from 8 to 48 h were log-transformed, and the natural logarithm of fluorescence intensity was plotted against time. Linear regression was applied to calculate the elimination rate constant (k), using the equation t₁/₂ = ln(2)/k.

In vivo antitumor efficacy evaluation

A unilateral 4T1 tumor model was established by subcutaneously injecting 1 × 106 Luc+4T1cells into the right second mammary gland of female BALB/c mice. Mice were randomly assigned to six groups: Control, US only, GH@siRNA + US, PGH + US, PGH@siRNA, and PGH@siRNA + US, upon ultrasound exposure (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W cm− 2, 50% duty cycle, 5 min). On the seventh day of tumor inoculation, the specified treatments (with nanoparticles at a dose of 5 mg/kg) were intravenously injected via tail vein injection every 48 h, totaling four injections. US irradiation was applied on the second day post-injection and every 2 days, for a total of four times. Every other day, both body weight and tumor dimensions were monitored. Tumor volume was determined using the standard formula: (length × width²)/2. In compliance with ethical guidelines, mice were humanely euthanized upon reaching moribund status or when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3. Mice were sacrificed after 14 days of treatment, and both blood samples and tumors were collected. Tumor proliferation (Ki-67) and apoptosis (TUNEL) were quantified in histological sections.

In vivo antitumor immune mechanisms

For flow cytometry, single-cell suspensions were prepared from excised 4T1 tumors and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD8, CD11c, CD80, CD86, Ly6G, and CD11b (BioLegend, 1:100 in PBS). Cell populations, including CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells, CD11c⁺CD80⁺CD86⁺ dendritic cells (DCs), and PMN-MDSCs (CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺), were quantified using a BD FACSCanto II cytometer. Single-cell suspensions were prepared from excised 4T1 tumors and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD8, CD11c, CD80, CD86, Ly6G, CD11b, PD-1, TIM-3, and FoxP3 (BioLegend, 1:100 in PBS). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a BD FACSCanto II cytometer.

For immunofluorescence, tumors were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (5 μm), and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against CD8 and Ly6G overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI counterstaining. Images were acquired using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

Serum concentrations of IL-6, IL-12 p70, IL-10, TNF-α, and IFN-γ were determined were quantified via ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (n = 3 per group).

Western blot

Tumors from each treatment group were collected and lysed using RIPA buffer, followed by incubation on ice for 30 min with gentle agitation. Cell lysates were further sheared using a 25 G syringe. Following centrifugation at 13,680 × g, supernatants were collected and total protein concentration was measured. Equal amounts of total protein from each sample were separated and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. Membranes were first blocked, and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies targeting CD300ld, CHOP, Cleaved caspase-3, STAT3, pSTAT3, and S100A9. After rinsing, PVDF membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected with Pierce™ ECL.

Immune activation in distant tumors

A bilateral 4T1 mammary tumor model was generated through sequential subcutaneous implantation of 1 × 10⁶ cells into the second mammary fat pads of female mice, with the right flank inoculated on Day 0 and the left flank on Day 6. On the seventh day of tumor inoculation, mice were randomly divided into two groups: Control and PGH@siRNA + US. The primary tumors received the specified treatments, while distant tumors remained untreated (n = 3 per group). Tumor growth kinetics were assessed every 48 h until day 19, followed by excision of both primary and distant tumors. Tumor tissues underwent CD8+ T cells and PMN-MDSCs immunofluorescence staining and immune response analysis via flow cytometry (n = 3 per group).

Anti-metastasis assay

A lung metastasis model was established by subcutaneously injecting 1 × 106 Luc+4T1 cells into the right second mammary gland. The mice were then randomly divided into two groups: Control and PGH@siRNA + US. On Day 21, the primary tumors were surgically excised. On Day 42, lungs were harvested for fluorescence signal detection, followed by fixation in Bouin’s solution, and pulmonary micrometastases were quantified microscopically (n = 3 per group).

Spleen tissues from each group were collected to evaluate memory T-cell populations and stained with the following antibodies: APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD8a (BioLegend, Catalog No. 100712), FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD3 (BioLegend, Catalog No. 100203), and PE-conjugated anti-mouse/human CD44 (BioLegend, Catalog No. 103007), and APC/Cyanine 7-conjugated anti-mouse CD62L (BioLegend, Catalog No.104428). Antibodies were diluted 1:100 in PBS. Flow cytometry was performed to isolate and analyze these subsets.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between groups were conducted using unpaired Student’s t test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Significance levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
