RNAi-based therapeutics targeting the F gene in human metapneumovirus: An in-silico approach
Authors: Proshanto Ghosh, Md Nur Islam, Shahriar Hossain, Ayesha Akter, Naima Akter Munni, Md Abunasar Miah, Muhammad Tawhid, Md Easin Mia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Human metapneumovirus (HMPV) is a major cause of acute respiratory tract infections, particularly in young children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals. Despite its significant clinical burden, no approved antiviral therapies or vaccines are currently available. This study aimed to identify potential RNA interference (RNAi)-based therapeutic candidates targeting the highly conserved fusion (F) gene of HMPV.Methods: An in silico pipeline was employed to design and evaluate small interfering RNA (siRNA) candidates targeting the HMPV F gene. A total of 869 siRNA sequences were initially generated using siDirect 2.1 software, which were sequentially filtered to 34 and then to 10 candidates based on sequence characteristics and predicted silencing efficiency. Molecular docking was performed to assess interactions between selected siRNAs and the human Argonaute-2 (AGO2) protein, followed by 100-ns molecular dynamics simulations to evaluate structural stability. Principal component analysis and free-energy landscape analyses were also performed.Results: Three siRNA candidates (F11, F19, and F26) demonstrated favorable RNAi characteristics, including high predicted silencing efficiency and strong target binding. Among them, F11 exhibited the highest sequence conservation across global HMPV strains, the highest melting temperature, favorable AGO2 interaction, and stable dynamic behavior. Principal component analysis indicated that F11 sampled the most constrained conformational subspace, which had a well-defined low-energy basin, thereby identifying it as the most promising candidate.Conclusions: This study identified F11 as the most promising siRNA candidate targeting the highly conserved HMPV fusion (F) gene, demonstrating strong potential as an RNAi-based therapeutic agent according to computational analyses. However, further in vitro and in vivo validation is required to confirm its efficacy and safety.Keywords: Human metapneumovirus (HMPV), RNA interference (RNAi), Small interfering RNA (siRNA), Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, Argonaute 2 (AGO2)

Introduction

Several novel respiratory pathogens causing human disease were identified in the early twenty-first century, including human metapneumovirus (HMPV) and the coronavirus responsible for severe acute respiratory syndrome. HMPV is a prevalent etiological agent of respiratory tract infections.1 The initial discovery of HMPV occurred in the Netherlands in 2001, when viral pathogens were isolated from respiratory samples of 28 epidemiologically unrelated infants using a monkey kidney cell line.2 Metapneumoviruses belong to the order Mononegavirales and the family Pneumoviridae, which also includes the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV).3 Similar to RSV, HMPV is transmitted via respiratory secretions and contact with contaminated surfaces. Symptoms of HMPV infection range from asymptomatic to moderate, such as fever and cough. In some cases, wheezing may progress to severe manifestations, including bronchiolitis, pneumonia, and exacerbation of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).4

HMPV is a spherical, enveloped virus with a diameter of approximately 209 nm. Its genome consists of a single-stranded, non-segmented, negative-sense RNA of about 13.3 kilobases. The genome encodes several proteins, including the main polymerase subunit (L), small hydrophobic protein (SH), attachment protein (G), transcription elongation factor (M2–1), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), fusion protein (F), nucleoprotein (N), and RNA synthesis regulatory factor (M2–2). The two principal genetic lineages, A and B, are further divided into sublineages A1, A2, B1, and B2.5, 6

The fusion (F) protein is the primary immunodominant protein of HMPV. With a molecular weight of 58.4 kDa, the F protein is essential for viral attachment and entry into host cells by mediating fusion of the viral envelope with the cell membrane.7, 8 The Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequence within the F protein facilitates interaction with RGD-binding integrins, which serve as cellular receptors for the fusion process in a pH-independent manner, likely within the endosomal compartment.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14

Although several HMPV genes, including N, P, G, SH, and F, have been considered potential RNAi targets, they differ in their conservation and functional importance. The N and P genes are relatively conserved and have been successfully targeted by siRNAs, but they primarily function during viral replication after host cell entry. In contrast, the G and SH genes exhibit substantial genetic variability among HMPV strains, limiting their suitability for broad-spectrum RNAi therapy. The F gene was prioritized because it is highly conserved, showing approximately 95% amino acid sequence identity between HMPV groups A and B, and plays an essential role in viral entry, making it an ideal target for developing durable and broadly effective siRNA therapeutics.7, 15 Therefore, the fusion protein has also been used as a computational target for other emerging respiratory and zoonotic viruses, including the structure-guided design of a multi-epitope construct against the Langya henipavirus fusion glycoprotein using molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations.16 These considerations illustrated the tractability of the fusion protein as a target for structure-based computational antiviral approaches.

HMPV infects humans of all ages, with a particular concentration on small infants and immunocompromised individuals.2, 17 Nonetheless, serological research indicates that almost all children are infected by the time they are 5 to 10 years old, and that HMPV has been present in humans for at least 50 years.18, 19 HMPV has been documented globally, including North America, South America, Europe, Asia, Africa, and Oceania. According to prospective studies conducted on young children with Acute Respiratory Tract Infections (ARTIs), HMPV is linked to 3.9–9% of respiratory infections.20 A dramatic rise in HMPV cases has been reported since late 2024; these cases were initially mostly in China but are now also found in the USA, India, the UK, Malaysia, Kazakhstan, and Pakistan, among other countries.21 In recent years, a 30-year-old lady in Bangladesh was the victim of the first HMPV- related fatality in history on January 15, 2025.22

Currently, treatment for HMPV is limited to supportive care, as no approved or licensed antivirals are available, despite ongoing development efforts.23 Ribavirin and immunoglobulin have been explored, with anecdotal evidence of human use, but no controlled trials or official guidelines support their use.24 Consequently, the development of safe and effective prophylactic and therapeutic strategies against HMPV, including vaccines and direct-acting antivirals such as RNA interference (RNAi)-based approaches, remains a primary objective. To address the persistent threat of HMPV to public health and the lack of effective treatments, this study aimed to design potential siRNA-based therapeutics targeting the HMPV fusion protein. Accordingly, the HMPV genome was computationally screened to identify the most potent siRNA candidates for therapeutic intervention.

As a potential component of the RNAi pathway, small interfering RNA (siRNA) treatment may be a promising strategy to immunize against HMPV-mediated infection.25, 26 siRNA is a 19–25 base pair RNA duplex with two nucleotide overhangs on the 3′ end. It causes post-transcriptional gene silencing by binding to and enzymatically degrading the complementary target mRNA.27, 28 A successful antiviral response via RNAi-mediated gene silencing requires effective interaction between the siRNA duplex, especially the guide strand, and the RISC complex, primarily the human argonaute (AGO) protein.29 The argonaute protein is a catalytic component of RISC, guides the complex to the complementary mRNA, and promotes cleavage of the target strand following the removal of the sense strand of the target mRNA.30 For correct RISC assembly and maximum siRNA activity, siRNA must successfully bind with the AGO protein. There are eight members of the AGO protein family in humans. Only argonaute-2 (AGO2) has endonuclease activity, whereas AGO1 to AGO4 can bind both miRNAs and siRNAs. The target mRNA sequence is cleaved by AGO2 once the siRNA binds to its corresponding mRNA target.31 In this scenario, siRNA is advantageous for current therapeutic applications because it can be identified from genome sequences, engineered, chemically synthesized, and delivered to cells to exert its effect. According to recent reports, siRNAs reduce mRNA translation and ultimately cleave target RNAs in specific target cells, playing a vital role in gene expression.32

This development process established a virtual framework to identify highly effective siRNAs targeting the fusion protein for potential therapeutic application. Notably, this study reports, for the first time, the identification of a potent siRNA targeting the fusion (F) protein, highlighting its significance as a foundational step for future therapeutic innovation. However, these results should be validated through more comprehensive studies before their application.

Materials and methods

Protein sequence retrieval and evolutionary study

Coding sequences of the fusion (F) protein of human metapneumovirus (HMPV) were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database in FASTA format. The accession numbers of all selected HMPV strains were recorded and are provided in Supplementary Table 1. The retrieved coding sequences were subjected to evolutionary analysis using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis version 12 (MEGA12).33 A total of 45 HMPV strains were included to infer the evolutionary relationship among the selected F gene targets. Multiple sequence alignment was performed prior to tree construction, and the alignment was manually inspected to confirm that homologous coding regions were correctly aligned. The phylogenetic tree was generated using the neighbor-joining method, a distance-based approach for reconstructing phylogenetic relationships.34 Evolutionary distances were estimated using the Tamura–Nei substitution model because it accounts for unequal nucleotide frequencies and differences between transition and transversion rates.35 The robustness of the tree topology was assessed using 1000 bootstrap replications. The final unrooted tree was used to evaluate the genetic relatedness of the selected HMPV strains and to support the selection of conserved F gene regions for siRNA design.36, 37

Recognition of target sequences and siRNA prediction

The coding sequences (CDS) of the HMPV F gene were submitted to the siDirect 2.1 web server to design functional and site-specific siRNA molecules. siDirect was selected because it integrates empirically validated siRNA design rules and off-target reduction parameters.38, 39 Candidate siRNAs were generated against conserved regions of the F gene so that the selected molecules would have broader applicability across the analyzed HMPV strains. In this case, multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was conducted by global (45) HMPV strains with the help of the Jalview web tool, and result analysis was processed through the Mview tool (https://www.jalview.org). The selection criteria followed the built-in parameters of siDirect 2.1, ensuring a seed-target duplex melting temperature (Tm) below 21.5 °C to minimize off-target effects. Beyond this default threshold, a more stringent seed-duplex melting temperature criterion of below 15 °C was subsequently imposed during downstream screening. Because the likelihood of seed-dependent off-target silencing decreases as the thermodynamic stability of the seed-target duplex decreases, only candidates whose seed-duplex Tm fell below 15 °C were retained for further analysis. Additionally, the algorithm integrates the Ui-Tei, Amarzguioui, and Reynolds empirical rules for efficient siRNA design, which were also followed in the screening datasets.38, 39 Candidate siRNAs containing ambiguous bases, unsuitable GC composition, or sequence features associated with poor silencing performance were excluded. The guide strand of each selected siRNA was then used for further downstream analyses.

Nucleotide sequence similarity for minimizing off-targets

Although siDirect predicts siRNAs based on design rules, there is always a possibility that computationally predicted siRNAs could show unintended complementarity with human transcripts. Therefore, off-target analysis is the next important step to ensure specificity and avoid unintended gene silencing. Each candidate siRNA sequence was searched against the human genomic plus transcript databases using the BLAST algorithm.40 The BLASTN search was performed using the “somewhat similar sequences” option, with an E-value threshold of 0.05. The maximum target sequence parameter was set to 5000 to inspect a broad range of possible human sequence similarities.

Candidates showing high similarity to human genes, especially within the seed region or across a large portion of the guide strand, were excluded. Off-target potential was therefore controlled at two complementary levels. First, seed-dependent (microRNA-like) off-target silencing, which arises from partial complementarity between the siRNA seed region and non-target transcripts, was minimized at the design stage: siDirect ranks candidates by the thermodynamic stability of the seed-target duplex and was developed specifically to reduce seed-dependent off-target effects,39 and only candidates with a seed-duplex Tm below 15 °C were retained, a threshold more stringent than the 21.5 °C default. Second, extended complementarity to host genomic and transcript sequences was screened genome- and transcriptome-wide using BLASTN, as described above. Because seed-mediated and extended-complementarity off-target mechanisms are governed by different sequence features, addressing both provides broader specificity coverage than either approach alone.

GC content computation & prediction of secondary structures of the siRNAs

Once the sequence specificity was determined, the structural and thermodynamic characteristics of the siRNA candidates were investigated. These characteristics are very important for the stability of siRNA and the efficiency of gene silencing. The GC content, a major factor influencing siRNA efficiency, was investigated for the siRNA candidates.41To date, the target sequences have been analyzed using the OligoCalc web server to calculate their GC percentage.42 GC content below 30% is considered insignificant and thus excluded from the study, resulting in poor and nonspecific binding.43, 44 The secondary structure of each guide strand was predicted using the RNAstructure web server.45 Minimum free energy folding was used to assess whether the guide strand formed stable self-folded structures. Candidates with highly stable intramolecular structures were considered less favorable because strong self-folding may reduce accessibility for target mRNA binding. The folding energy values were recorded and compared among candidates to select siRNAs with suitable structural accessibility and thermodynamic behavior.

Thermodynamic evaluation of siRNA and target mRNA binding affinity

To evaluate whether the guide strand could bind efficiently to the target HMPV F gene mRNA, thermodynamic interaction analysis was performed using the DuplexFold module of the RNAstructure web server.45 For each candidate, the guide strand and the corresponding target mRNA sequence were submitted as a pair of RNA sequences. DuplexFold predicted the most favorable siRNA–mRNA hybrid structure and calculated the minimum free energy of duplex formation. The binding free energy value was used as an indicator of interaction stability. More negative ΔG values indicate stronger and more thermodynamically favorable binding between the siRNA guide strand and the target mRNA. However, candidates were not selected based on ΔG alone; ΔG values were interpreted alongside GC content, off-target screening, secondary structure, melting temperature, and predicted silencing efficacy. This combined approach helped avoid selecting candidates that were thermodynamically stable but biologically less suitable.

siRNA-mRNA complex melting temperature calculation

Apart from binding affinity, the thermal stability of siRNA-mRNA complexes also plays a crucial role in the efficacy of gene silencing assays under physiological conditions. To further assess thermal stability and confirm the accuracy of the siRNA-mRNA complex prediction, a melting-temperature assay was performed. The melting temperature was determined using the DINAMelt web server, which employs a thermodynamic approach to evaluating nucleic acid duplex stability.46 A temperature-dependent evaluation of the heat capacity (Cp) is conducted. The peak (local maximum) of the heat capacity curve is the melting temperature, Tm (Cp), determined from the heat capacity curve. Furthermore, the concentration-based melting temperature, T(Conc.), is the temperature at which the concentration of the double-stranded molecule drops to half its initial maximum value. Consequently, the concentration plot was used to display the Tm (Conc.) values of the siRNA-mRNA complexes.

Evaluation of the efficacy of siRNA molecules

As much as thermodynamic stability is crucial, it is not fully predictive of the biological efficacy of siRNA. The efficacy of the expected siRNA molecule was verified using the siRNAPred tool.47 This tool estimates the real efficacy of a query siRNA sequence (both 21- and 19-mer) using the support vector machine method (SVM) with high accuracy. The study compared the effectiveness of consensus target 1 siRNA to the main 21-mer dataset, which included 2182 siRNA varieties, using a binary pattern. A siRNAPred score greater than 1 suggests “very high” effectiveness, while a score in the 0.8–0.9 range suggests “high effectiveness”. Again, a score of 0.7–0.8 indicates “moderate effectiveness”.48 Moreover, the inhibitory efficacy is predicted by the siPRED web server.49 The siPRED predicts siRNA inhibitory effectiveness using a two-layer structure in which different sequence and thermodynamic parameters are examined separately and then combined using machine learning models such as SVM or Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). A siPRED inhibition efficacy score greater than 80% is considered optimal.48 Both of these efficacies rationally affirm the suitability of siRNAs and are expected to achieve optimal silencing throughput.

Molecular docking of guide siRNA and argonaute-2 protein with validation

Before docking, the human AGO2 crystal structure was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4Z4D), which captures AGO2 in complex with a t1-G target RNA and thus represents the biologically relevant, RNA-loaded conformation of the protein.31, 48 Receptor preparation, including removal of the co-crystallized RNA ligand, addition of missing hydrogen atoms, assignment of partial charges, and correction of incomplete residues, was performed in UCSF Chimera (version 1.16). Meanwhile, three-dimensional structures of the siRNA guide strands were generated using RNAComposer, and each guide strand was subsequently energy-minimized in UCSF Chimera with the steepest-descent algorithm to relieve steric clashes and obtain a low-energy starting conformation prior to docking.50 Next, the HDOCK server was used to perform molecular docking between the siRNA (guide strand) and the AGO2 protein.45, 50 HDOCK employs a hybrid docking strategy combining template-based modeling with ab initio free docking. Rotational sampling was performed at an angular interval of 15°, and translational sampling was conducted at a grid spacing of 1.2 Å. For each sampled rotation, the top 10 translations with the highest shape complementarity were further refined using an iterative knowledge-based scoring function derived from statistical mechanics principles.51

Docking was conducted in global (blind) mode, wherein the HDOCK algorithm exhaustively sampled all possible binding orientations across the entire molecular surface of AGO2 without prior specification of a binding pocket. This blind docking approach is appropriate for the siRNA-AGO2 system because AGO2's functional RNA-binding channel spans multiple structural domains, including PAZ, MID, and PIWI. For each siRNA candidate, HDOCK generated 10 ranked binding models. Later, these models were studied manually and accepted as biologically valid only if the guide strand was positioned within the canonical RNA-binding cavity of AGO2, spanning the PAZ, MID, and PIWI domains, in a manner consistent with the architecture observed in the reference PDB 4Z4D structure.51

Following docking, the PDBsum web server was used to enumerate all non-covalent interactions between AGO2 and each docked guide strand, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic contacts, and hydrophobic interactions.52 Interaction profiles for all siRNA candidates were compared with those of prioritized molecules exhibiting stable, biologically plausible interactions.29, 31

Analysis of molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were used to monitor the behavior of protein–ligand complexes over time, enabling assessment of their structural compatibility and stability at successive time steps. In this study, simulations of human AGO2 and siRNA complexes were conducted to gain clearer insight into their overall compactness and dynamic behavior. In this case, GROMACS 2024.4 was run on a Lenovo IdeaPad Gaming 3 15ARH7 (AMD Ryzen™ 57535HS with Radeon™ Graphics × 12, NVIDIA GeForce RTX™ 2050, 2 × 8 GB DDR5 RAM, 512 GB SSD, Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS). The system was set up using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field and the TIP3P water model, followed by solvation and neutralization with appropriate amounts of Na + and Cl- ions, and the inclusion of Mg2+ ions to help stabilize the nucleic acid phosphate backbone. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 50,000 steps, with a maximum force cutoff of 10 kJ/mol. This was followed by Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature (NVT) and Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature (NPT) equilibration phases of 200 Picoseconds (ps) each, using a 2 Femtoseconds (fs) time step; the temperature was kept at 300 K during NVT and the pressure at 1.0 bar during NPT. Finally, a 100 ns production MD run was carried out with a 2 Femtosecond (fs) time step, saving coordinates every 10 ps to closely monitor structural changes over time. These trajectories were then examined further to evaluate dynamics and structural conformations throughout the simulation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and free energy landscape (FEL)

To calculate the conformational dynamics of AGO2-siRNA complexes, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was carried out on the MD simulations. PCA simplifies complex motion by generating all-atom coordinates, followed by diagonalization to yield new vectors that describe the significant motion of the trajectory.53 PC1 ae Energy Landscape (FEL) calculation using gmx_sham, based on applying a Boltzmann distribution to PC1- and PC2-sampled conformations, was computed.54 The FEL plot displays basins and totes of low and higher energy, respectively, indicates thermodynamic characteristics of complex stability and conformational variety over the 100 ns run.

Statistical analysis of trajectory descriptors

The structural descriptors monitored during the molecular dynamics simulations, namely the backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), the per-atom root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), the radius of gyration (Rg), the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and the number of hydrogen bonds, were extracted for the F11, F19, and F26 siRNA-AGO2 complexes and summarized statistically. For each descriptor, the mean and population standard deviation were calculated across all frames of the 100-ns production trajectory, and RMSF was computed per atom over the trajectory.

The accession IDs for different HMPV strains were obtained from the NCBI nucleotide database. Starting from the HMPV fusion (F) gene coding sequence, siDirect 2.1 predicted 869 candidate siRNAs. Off-target reduction through nucleotide sequence similarity and evolutionary relationship

To ensure the specificity of the predicted siRNA candidates and prevent unintended target gene silencing, off-target analysis was performed for all 10 siRNAs based on their similarity in nucleotide sequences using the human genomic and transcript datasets, as well as using the E-value score for transcript sequences, aligning from 0.31 to 299, and those for genomic sequences ranging from 0.079 to 299. However, there were no cases in either the transcript or genomic data where an E-value was less than or equal to the threshold of 0.05; all 10 siRNAs had E-values greater than the threshold. Therefore, as the E-value score increases, there is weaker evolutionary homology between siRNA molecules and human genomic or transcript sequences, reflecting greater nucleotide sequence dissimilarity.55 Meanwhile, a nucleotide BLAST was performed to identify 45 HMPV genomes with high genomic coverage. MSA analysis revealed an overall conserved pattern within the F gene across the 45 global HMPV strains, which is promising for avoiding sequence mismatches. More than 50% conservation of the sequences was detected successfully by the F11 siRNA (Fig. 3B). The evolutionary relationships among them were emphasized by the close relationships, with more than 90% of bootstrap replicates observed across several subclusters. However, the majority of the internal nodes showed high bootstrap support (>70%), indicating a robust phylogenetic inference (Fig. 3A).

GC content calculation & prediction of secondary structures of the siRNAs

The selected siRNAs contain GC content between 30% and 39%. Among them, F27 contains 39%, the highest, and F18 contains the lowest (19%) GC content. We considered the optimal GC content; however, those targets (F4, F18, F22, F25, F29) had GC content below 30% and were therefore excluded from the study (Table 4). Moreover, the computed folding energy ranged from −1.3 to 0.4. Because of the negative free energy of folding, the siRNA forms a bound conformation that creates secondary structures that require energy to unfold, and this additional energy could impede efficient siRNA-mRNA binding.56 Thus, F28 was excluded at this stage (Fig. 4).

Thermodynamic evaluation of siRNA and target mRNA binding affinity

After the sequence specificity and the appropriateness of the structure have been determined, the binding specificity of the chosen siRNA guide strand with the targeted mRNA sequence was analyzed using thermodynamic measurements. The binding energy of an siRNA-mRNA complex is an important factor that significantly contributes to gene silencing activity through its thermodynamic stability.57 Hence, Tm (cp) and Tm (Conc.) were computed to assess the thermodynamic stability of the selected siRNA molecules (Table 5). In addition, the binding energy for all 10 siRNAs is within the range of −30.0 to −39.2 kcal/mol. Evaluation and verification of the efficacy of predicted siRNAs

Following thermodynamic and structural validation, the predicted siRNA candidates underwent a prediction analysis to assess their gene-silencing efficacy. Using the seed efficacy prediction tool siRNAPred, the seed efficiency of each predicted siRNA candidate ranged from 0.784 to 0.995, as shown in Table 4. Of the four candidates assessed at this stage, F27 fell below the threshold with a siRNAPred score of 0.797 and was therefore excluded, resulting in a selection of three effective siRNAs: F11, F19, and F26, as compared in Table 5. Although a high seed score near 1 is often expected to indicate a highly effective siRNA, the inhibition efficiency of the three evaluated candidates ranged from 92.63% to 93.73%. Hence, all selected siRNA candidates have inhibition efficiency >90%; therefore, they are expected to be effective gene silencers (Table 5).

Assessment of the siRNA-mRNA complex melting temperature

In addition, Melting Temperature (Tm) calculations were performed to assess the thermal stability of siRNA guide strand–target mRNA duplexes. In this regard, a heat capacity plot describes the contribution of each molecular species within those ensembles, whereas a concentration-based plot defines Tm as the concentration at which half of the maximum concentration is reached. Therefore, melting temperature calculations in the form of heat-capacity Tm (Cp) and concentration-based Tm (Conc) plots were performed individually for the three selected siRNA compounds. All three siRNA compounds had melting temperatures >80 °C; out of these three siRNA compounds, F11 had a higher thermal stability with 85.5 °C and 84.5 °C obtained in Tm (Cp) and Tm (Conc), respectively, whereas F19 had a lower Tm in comparison to others with 81.9 °C and 80.5 °C in Tm (Cp) and Tm (Conc), respectively (Table 5). The determination of melting temperatures above 80 °C clearly indicates that all of these siRNAs are more stable and, therefore, would have higher efficacy; all three siRNAs will be taken for further study.

Molecular docking and post-docking analysis of guide siRNA and argonaute-2 protein

Molecular docking is a widely used method to assess the binding affinity and interactions between receptor and ligand molecules. Docking was performed using the HDOCK server. The best docked complex, determined by the lowest energy, was selected for further interaction analysis with Chimera and the PDBsum web server. Docking scores for F26 and F19 were 420.66 and 321.2, respectively, representing the highest and lowest values among the three complexes. F11 exhibited a docking score of 379.76, intermediate between F19 and F26. According to the HDOCK algorithm, a more negative docking score indicates a more favorable interaction.51 All three siRNA molecules demonstrated favorable docking scores and were therefore selected for molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. Exploring molecular dynamics from simulation trajectory and statistics

Molecular dynamics simulation is a computational approach used to investigate the dynamic behavior and stability of molecular complexes over time. Several parameters are commonly analyzed to evaluate these properties, including Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), Radius of Gyration (Rg), Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA), and the number of hydrogen bonds (H-bonds).

Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis of the F11, F19, and F26 complexes revealed substantial backbone deviations over the 100-ns simulation (Fig. 7A). The F11 complex underwent an initial equilibration phase with minor changes during the early trajectory (approximately 5–20 ns), followed by a stable RMSD plateau (≈20–100 ns) with consistent deviations (approximately 0.45–0.55 nm). F26 displayed greater variability and deviations in the later stages (≈60–90 ns), while F19 achieved stability after 30 ns. Although F19 fluctuated initially, it did not reach the overall consistency observed in F11 after equilibration.

(B) Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) plots. (C) Radius of Gyration (Rg) plots. (D) Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) plots. (E) Number of Hydrogen Bonds (H-bonds) plots. AGO-2: Argonaut- 2, Ns: Nanosecond.

Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analysis provided atom-wise flexibility profiles for F11, F19, and F26, revealing differences in local mobility across the structures (Fig. 7B). The F11 complex exhibited limited variation, with only isolated increases and a moderate terminal rise at approximately 14,000 atoms. In contrast, F26 demonstrated the highest flexibility, including a pronounced terminal spike (approaching ∼1.6–1.7 nm), while F19 showed greater peaks than F11 in certain terminal regions.

The radius of gyration (Rg) was used to evaluate the compactness of the F11, F19, and F26 complexes, revealing distinct differences in folding over the 100-ns simulation (Fig. 7C). The F11 complex initially expanded (≈0–15 ns; ∼3.16–3.20 nm) before gradually becoming more compact, maintaining a consistently low Rg range of roughly ∼3.00–3.06 nm from about 40 ns to 100 ns. F19 also became more compact, with a significant decrease around ∼55–60 ns, followed by slight increases, whereas F26 remained more expanded and fluctuated throughout the simulation (∼3.10–3.20 nm), indicating lower compactness than F11. Both F11 and F19 were more compact than F26, with F11 achieving compactness earlier (around 30 ns) and F19 after 55 ns; both exhibited minor initial fluctuations. Solvent exposure was assessed using Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) analysis for the three complexes (Fig. 7D). The F11 complex generally exhibited the highest SASA values, with regular variations mostly within the 395–410 nm2 range and a gradual decline toward ∼390–395 nm2 as the simulation progressed. The F19 complex showed a general decline from ∼405–410 nm2 to lower values later in the simulation, with sudden, short drops (notably around ∼10–15 ns and ∼ 55–60 ns) and a lower range (∼370–385 nm2) near ∼80–100 ns. The F26 complex remained intermediate between F11 and F19, fluctuating around 380–395 nm2, lacking the marked decrease observed for F19. Overall, F19 and F26 displayed marginally smaller solvent-accessible areas, oscillating mainly within the ∼380–395 nm2 range, suggesting relatively limited solvent exposure. In contrast, F11 maintained higher SASA values, initially around 395–410 nm2, without significant destabilizing peaks, and showed a reduced area toward the end of the simulation, indicating a consistent structure despite a slightly larger exposed surface. Fig. 7E shows the number of hydrogen bonds formed, with F11 yielding a lower average (635) than F19 (665) and F26 (660). Based on these dynamic descriptors, all three complexes were comparably stable, with F11 showing the lowest residue fluctuations, indicating the highest structural rigidity among the three.

To supplement the qualitative trajectory analysis and enable quantitative comparison, the mean and standard deviation of each descriptor were calculated over the 0–100 ns trajectory (Table 7). All three complexes exhibited broadly similar stability profiles, with low backbone RMSD values (0.476–0.522 nm). The per-atom RMSF was lowest for F11 (0.224 ± 0.119 nm), indicating slightly greater rigidity, while the radius of gyration, solvent-accessible surface area, and hydrogen-bond counts were comparable across the complexes. Since the mean RMSD, RMSF, and hydrogen-bond values were within one standard deviation of each other, and the radius of gyration and solvent-accessible surface area showed only modest differences, the three complexes can be interpreted as comparably stable throughout the simulation.

Principal component analysis and free energy landscape

Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to identify the dominant collective motions in the protein-ligand complexes, thereby reducing the high-dimensional trajectory data to a limited set of eigenvectors representing the principal dynamics of each complex. The first two eigenvectors (PC1 and PC2) were projected into a two-dimensional space to evaluate the dynamic behavior of the three complexes: F11, F19, and F26. Following matrix diagonalization, the maximum motion distributions were determined to be 143 nm2 for F26, 104.4 nm2 for F19, and 98.4 nm2 for F11, as depicted in Fig. 8A. These results indicate that F11 samples a more restricted conformational space compared to F19 and F26. This trend is summarized in Fig. 9, which compares the combined PC1 and PC2 variance across the three complexes and confirms that F11 sampled the most confined essential subspace, followed by F19 and then F26.

Simultaneously, the Gibbs free energy landscape (FEL) was calculated to characterize the thermodynamic properties of the complexes along PC1 and PC2. The minimum predicted free energy values were 13.7 kJ/mol for F11, 14.6 kJ/mol for F19, and 15.7 kJ/mol for F26, demonstrating that F11 possesses the deepest and most thermodynamically favorable free-energy basin among the three complexes. These results provide insight into the thermodynamic potentials associated with directional movement, as illustrated in Figs. 8B-8D.

Discussion

Human metapneumovirus (HMPV) is a widespread respiratory pathogen that primarily affects children under 5 years of age58, 59 but also poses significant risks to older adults and immunocompromised individuals. Surveillance data from multiple regions indicate that detection rates are highest among infants and young children aged 6 months to 4 years.60, 61, 62 Adults aged 65 years and older tend to experience more severe outcomes from HMPV, especially when comorbidities are present, despite lower overall detection rates. In hospitalized patients aged 65 years and older with acute respiratory tract infections, pooled HMPV positivity was 2.01% in low- and middle-income countries and 2.46% in high-income countries, corresponding to an estimated 473,000 global hospitalizations in 2019.63 Hospitalization and incidence rates vary according to age, geographic location, and healthcare setting. However, comprehensive mortality data remain scarce.

In temperate regions, HMPV peaks worldwide from late winter to early spring.60, 64, 65 Pre-pandemic, it circulated mainly from January to April, following RSV patterns. During COVID-19 (2020−2021), incidence dropped globally due to interventions,66 with post-pandemic shifts including delayed peaks in China and Korea, though most areas have returned to pre-pandemic norms.67, 68 Additionally, the ongoing HMPV outbreak in China underscores its growing significance in public health, with HMPV detection accounting for 6.2% of respiratory cases and 5.4% of hospitalizations among children under 14 years from December 16th to 22nd, 2024, surpassing even COVID-19 cases in the country.69 High incidence occurs in children <5 years across North/South America, Europe, and Asia, with outbreaks in the US, China, Mexico, and Argentina.70, 71, 72 In tropical/subtropical areas such as India and Southeast Asia, peaks align with monsoons. Post-pandemic, later pediatric age groups and household clustering indicate evolving dynamics.61

Beyond its epidemiological footprint, HMPV imposes a clinically distinct burden on vulnerable groups: in young children, it is a leading cause of bronchiolitis and pneumonia requiring hospitalization, whereas in older adults and immunocompromised patients, particularly transplant recipients, infection can progress to respiratory failure and death. For these patients, the absence of any approved antiviral or vaccine confines clinical management to supportive care, with off-label ribavirin and immunoglobulin used only anecdotally.24 Against this unmet need, the RNAi technique has matured from the laboratory into a clinically validated modality: since the 2018 approval of patisiran, several siRNA therapeutics, including givosiran, lumasiran, inclisiran, vutrisiran, and nedosiran, have reached the clinic, with more than a hundred programs now in trials.73 The approach has also shown direct utility against respiratory pathogens; an inhalable siRNA targeting a conserved SARS-CoV-2 region inhibited a broad range of variants, including Delta, at picomolar concentrations and prevented pulmonary pathology without drug-related toxicity.74 Earlier studies have shown that siRNAs targeting the HMPV nucleoprotein and phosphoprotein genes directly suppress viral replication.75 These precedents indicate that the F-gene-directed candidates prioritized here, F11 in particular, act through a modality whose clinical safety and antiviral efficacy are no longer hypothetical, positioning the present computational predictions as a rational basis for experimental antiviral development against an emerging respiratory threat.

RNA interference (RNAi) is a post-transcriptional gene silencing process present in nearly all eukaryotes, and it is mediated by the Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or endogenous small non-coding microRNAs (miRNAs).76 Synthetic siRNA, which silences targeted gene expression, was developed based on this natural biological mechanism. RNAi can be used to analyze gene function or be developed as therapeutic agents to target genes linked to disease.27, 77 Additionally, the use of siRNA in the development of medications and treatments has demonstrated promising utility through the introduction of siRNA nanoparticles against diseases such as cancer, viral infections, and genetic disorders.69, 73, 78, 79

The selection of an optimal gene target is a decisive step in designing effective RNAi-based therapeutics, and the HMPV genome offers several candidate genes with varying degrees of suitability. Earlier work showed that siRNAs targeting the nucleoprotein (N) and phosphoprotein (P) genes can effectively suppress HMPV replication,75 confirming that the virus is susceptible to RNA interference. In contrast, siRNAs targeting the attachment glycoprotein (G) gene were ineffective at reducing viral growth,80 consistent with the high sequence variability of the G gene across strains, which undermines the broad-spectrum specificity required for a durable siRNA. The small hydrophobic (SH) and G proteins are among the most divergent HMPV proteins and are therefore poorly suited to a conserved, strain-independent design.15 The fusion (F) gene, by comparison, presents the most favorable target profile. It is functionally indispensable, mediating fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane and thereby enabling viral entry, so its silencing solely disrupts the infection process.7, 8 Remarkably, the current study showed that F11 siRNA directly targeted more than 50% of HMPV strains; thus, the selection of F11 siRNA may be sufficient to immunize against global HMPV strains (Table 5 and Fig. 3B). Moreover, the F protein is additionally the principal immunodominant antigen of HMPV, underscoring its central role in the viral life cycle.1 For these combined reasons, namely essentiality for entry, high cross-lineage conservation, and a demonstrated susceptibility of HMPV to RNAi in this study, which was already mentioned earlier.

In the process of screening potential siRNAs, all available algorithms provided by the siDirect server and i-Score Designer were utilized (Table 1). The findings of the present study are consistent with and extend previous computational and experimental mechanistic studies of RNAi. The overall design strategy adopted here, combining siDirect-based prediction under the Ui-Tei, Reynolds, and Amarzguioui rules with off-target screening and molecular dynamics validation of the siRNA-AGO2 complex, closely mirrors the pipeline successfully applied to the research works.48 Target sequences were selected according to specific criteria, including GC content, nucleotide length, and seed duplex instability (Tm). The most suitable targets were then chosen for further analysis, as presented in Table 4, Table 5.

A previous study suggests that RNA molecules should have the highest free energy of folding.81 Because siRNA molecules have a lower folding free energy, the development of secondary structure may hinder the RISC complex from cleaving the target. Calculating the probable secondary structure and folding free energy is crucial.43 siRNA molecules that do not have a negative free energy of folding the guide strands may be able to reach the target more easily and interact with it more frequently, leading to effective gene silencing.57 A crucial feature of potential siRNA is its GC content, which should range from 30% to 64%.41 In this study, seed duplex instability (Tm) was thoroughly examined, with an emphasis on maintaining lower Tm values to increase silencing efficiency. However, five siRNAs (F4, F18, F22, F25, F29) had GC content below the 30% threshold, compared with the recommended limit (Table 4). Furthermore, the folding energy of the F28 molecule was negative. Additionally, F27 had an siRNApred score of 0.797, indicating lower efficiency. Consequently, these candidates received no further consideration. Given these factors, three siRNAs (F11, F19, and F26) were finally selected for additional analyses because prospective features are reserved, as shown in Fig. 4 and Table 5.

The free energy of binding to the target is another important parameter, as RNAi efficacy is closely linked to the binding energies of siRNAs to their individual target mRNAs.76 A stronger connection between siRNA and target mRNA indicates a higher likelihood of blocking the target, and, hence, a lower binding energy is calculated. Following analysis, the binding free energy of the last three siRNAs was more negative in expression (−38.9 for F11, −35.8 for F19, and − 35.8 for F26) (Fig. 5). Moreover, the links between GC content and thermodynamic stability must be established, as GC content substantially affects the thermodynamic stability of siRNA molecules and plays a pivotal role in subsequent silencing.82 Additionally, the siPred server was used to assess inhibition efficiency, with F11, F19, and F26 showing>92% inhibition (Table 5), making them the most effective siRNA options. 80% inhibition is the ideal level.49 F11, F19, and F26 were therefore logically selected for docking with the AGO2 protein for additional investigation.

A docking study was conducted to assess the interaction between the siRNA molecules and the human AGO2 protein. F11, F19, and F26 had docking scores of 379.76, 321.2, and 420.66, respectively (Table 6). Although all candidates had promising docking scores, F11 and F26 did not achieve substantially higher scores than F19. Furthermore, the GC content of both the F19 and F26 molecules was at baseline (30%), compared with that of other siRNA molecules, such as F11 (38%).

After careful screening, F11, F19, and F26 underwent molecular dynamics simulation. To assess their structural stability and compactness, important parameters such as RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and hydrogen bonds were calculated using a 100 ns trajectory. As shown in Fig. 7, among these stable candidates, F11 consistently emerged as the most favorable. It showed the lowest residue fluctuation, indicating the greatest internal rigidity, and in the principal component analysis, it sampled the most constrained essential subspace of the three, with the lowest combined variance along the first two principal components (Fig. 9), reflecting the most restricted large-scale collective motion. The Gibbs free-energy landscapes (FEL) indicated that all three complexes populated well-defined low-energy basins, and F11 occupied a compact, clearly defined basin consistent with a single stable conformational ensemble (Fig. 8A-8D). Considering these features, convergent results were established for all three siRNA-AGO2 complexes; however, F11 was identified as the most stable and best-defined candidate and was therefore prioritized as the lead. The combined use of molecular docking, followed by molecular dynamics simulation, to estimate the structural stability of receptor-ligand complexes using descriptors is a well-established validation strategy in recent structure-based drug discovery.83 During initial thermodynamic assessments, F11 had a higher melting temperature (85.5) than the other candidates. This suggests that higher Tm (Cp) may complementarily affect the dynamic stability and efficiency of the complexes.84

This behavior of MD simulation over a 100 ns output was found to be stable and has been reported in many studies, including the Nipah virus siRNA-AGO2 complex,48 confirming that paramyxovirus-targeted siRNAs form stable associations with AGO2. A further study targeting the RdRp gene of SARS-CoV-2 using siDirect and HDOCK-based AGO2 docking reported a favorable docking score for its lead candidate,85 reinforcing the suitability of this docking approach for predicting siRNA-AGO2 recognition. Importantly, some of the studies experimentally demonstrated that siRNAs targeting the HMPV nucleoprotein and phosphoprotein genes inhibit viral replication in vitro at sub-nanomolar concentrations.75 Beyond viral systems, the broader value of integrated computer-aided pipelines that couple molecular docking with molecular dynamics simulations is well established in structure-based drug discovery, where such approaches have been used to prioritize candidate inhibitors and guide their experimental development.86, 87, 88, 89 Collectively, these comparisons define a clear path toward the in vitro and in vivo validation required for therapeutic development. Similarly, a recent computational study targeting Helicobacter pylori-associated gastric cancer employed an integrated in silico pipeline involving hub-gene identification, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM/GBSA binding free-energy analysis to identify potential inhibitors of the MUC4 and BIRC3 proteins. The study demonstrated that compounds with favorable docking scores, stable RMSD profiles, and negative MM/GBSA binding energies were prioritized as promising therapeutic candidates, underscoring the utility of combining multiple computational approaches to improve candidate selection prior to experimental validation.90 Although the biological targets and therapeutic strategies differ from the present RNAi-based approach against HMPV, both studies illustrate how comprehensive computational screening and molecular dynamics analyses can efficiently prioritize the most promising therapeutic candidates while reducing the need for extensive preliminary laboratory screening.

To verify the methodical execution of drug discovery, researchers are seeking to determine the appropriate approach to delivering therapies as the field grows. To achieve effective silencing via RNAi, various siRNA delivery methods have been documented. Among them, polyethyleneimine (PEI) is one of the most widely used due to its potential to protect siRNAs from degradation and deliver them successfully to target sites of action, although in vivo validation is still needed.91, 92, 93 Additionally, delivery strategies include using nano-vesicle delivery and lyophilization to increase siRNA stability and enhance therapeutic potential.69 Lipid nanoparticles are widely used and safe delivery systems with proven clinical efficacy for siRNA therapeutics, enabling efficient circulation and targeted delivery to various tissues.94 Additionally, promoter-controlled and plasmid-based vectors facilitate targeted gene delivery and enable evaluation of the efficacy of siRNA-mediated silencing in specific cell types.95, 96

Synthetic siRNAs can be recognized as foreign nucleic acids by several pattern-recognition receptors, including the endosomal Toll-like receptors TLR3 (which senses double-stranded RNA), TLR7 and TLR8 (which sense single-stranded GU- and U-rich RNA), as well as the cytoplasmic sensors RIG-I and MDA5, leading to type I interferon and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.97, 98 Therefore, Activation of the innate immune system is a central consideration in the development of siRNA-mediated therapeutics. This immunostimulant is largely sequence- and structure-dependent: TLR7/8 activation is associated with specific motifs such as 5’-GUCCUUCAA-3′, 5’-UGUGU-3′, and 5’-UGGC-3′ and with U-rich strands, whereas RIG-I preferentially recognizes blunt-ended duplexes bearing a 5′-triphosphate.99 To assess this risk for the identified siRNAs, the guide and passenger strands of F11, F19, and F26 were screened for these canonical immunostimulatory motifs. None of the three candidates contained the strong TLR7/8-activating motifs (5’-GUCCUUCAA-3′, 5’-UGUGU-3′, or 5’-UGGC-3′), and only short, low-potency sequences that occur ubiquitously in cellular transcripts were observed, suggesting a low predicted propensity for sequence-driven innate immune activation. Importantly, where residual immunogenicity is a concern, it can be effectively minimized during downstream development through well-established chemical modifications. For example, 2’-O-methyl and 2′-fluoro substitutions abrogate TLR and RIG-I recognition while preserving silencing activity, and through the use of symmetric 3′ overhangs rather than blunt ends to avoid RIG-I engagement.97, 99 However, these considerations should be addressed experimentally during preclinical validation to assess non-specific immune stimulation and the antiviral effect of the selected siRNAs.

In a broader therapeutic context, RNAi has already been pursued against several respiratory viruses, including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza, and SARS-CoV-2, in which both computationally and experimentally validated siRNAs have shown potent, broad inhibition. For HMPV itself, earlier work established susceptibility to RNAi by targeting the internal nucleoprotein and phosphoprotein genes.75 By targeting the entry machinery, such as the F protein, rather than the internal replication complex, and by using a target conserved across the A and B lineages, this work expands the repertoire of candidate RNAi targets for HMPV. In the future direction of this study, molecular intervention could reciprocally enhance the mood of verification of selected siRNAs. Specifically, a logical validation pathway would begin with luciferase reporter assays, in which the F-gene target region is cloned downstream of a reporter to quantify siRNA-mediated knockdown, followed by quantitative RT-PCR to measure reductions in viral mRNA and by plaque reduction assays to assess the inhibition of infectious virus, before progression to in vitro HMPV infection models in permissive cell lines and, ultimately, to suitable animal models. This staged experimental scheme mirrors the approach used to validate siRNAs targeting the HMPV nucleoprotein and phosphoprotein genes: initial reporter screening was confirmed by quantitative RT-PCR and plaque immunoassays, yielding sub-nanomolar inhibitory concentrations.

Conclusion

RNA interference (RNAi) is a natural cellular mechanism in which small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) direct the degradation of complementary messenger RNAs, thereby downregulating specific gene expression. In this in silico study, three siRNA candidates (F11, F19, and F26) were designed to target the conserved fusion (F) protein of human metapneumovirus. Among these, F11 demonstrated strong thermodynamic properties, high predicted silencing efficiency, favorable interaction with AGO2, and a notably stable dynamic profile throughout simulations. While the potential of siRNAs targeting the highly conserved F protein for antiviral therapy against human metapneumovirus (HMPV) was confirmed, these conclusions are based solely on computational models. Therefore, further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to validate these virotherapeutic agents. This work should be regarded as an initial step, with subsequent in vitro experiments, studies in relevant animal models, and rigorously designed clinical trials necessary to ensure safe delivery, reliable activity in biological systems, and translation into practical therapies.

Limitation

Despite these findings, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The analyses are based entirely on computational predictions, and although candidate siRNAs were prioritized through a multi-stage pipeline and their complexes with AGO2 were assessed using an extensive set of in silico methods, these approaches cannot fully capture the cellular determinants of RNAi efficacy. Aspects such as the kinetics of gene silencing, intracellular delivery, target-site accessibility within folded mRNA, and off-target or innate immune responses require further investigation. While seed-dependent and extended-complementarity off-target risks were addressed computationally through thermodynamic filtering and genome-wide BLASTN screening, these predictions may not fully represent the cellular off-target landscape. Consequently, the identified candidates should be considered prioritized leads that require experimental validation. Further validation through both in vitro and in vivo experiments is required to confirm the efficacy of the selected siRNAs. In addition, the lack of binding free-energy decomposition analyses (MM-GBSA or MM-PBSA) and independent replicate simulations, resulting from computational resource limitations, represents a significant constraint. Developing an effective delivery system for the selected siRNAs is also critical to achieving optimal gene silencing. Despite these limitations, the current predictions provide a basis for future antiviral drug design and development, potentially accelerating advances in academic and pharmaceutical research.
