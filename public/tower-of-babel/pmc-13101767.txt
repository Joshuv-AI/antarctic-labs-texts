Linking Climate Features to Human Life Expectancy in the United States: Implications for Integrated Climate and Health Policies
Authors: George Sun, Longling Sun, Yi-Hui Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionClimate change is increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of human health, yet its integration into health policy remains limited. Understanding the link between climate variables and human life expectancy is essential for developing targeted interventions that mitigate health risks. This study examines the relationship between key climate features and life expectancy, providing evidence to inform integrated climate and health policies.MethodsWe applied a multi-method modeling framework, including Random Forest, Geographically Weighted Regression, and Generalized Additive Models, to analyze the impact of climate variables on human life expectancy in the United States. Key environmental factors, such as vapor pressure, temperature, actual evapotranspiration, and solar radiation, were assessed for their influence on life expectancy across diverse geographic regions. Model performance and variable importance were evaluated to ensure robustness and consistency.ResultsOur findings indicate that higher vapor pressure, actual evapotranspiration, and temperature are associated with reduced life expectancy, whereas increased solar radiation demonstrates a protective effect. These associations remained consistent across all models, highlighting the reliability of our approach. The Random Forest model exhibited strong predictive performance, reinforcing the validity of our findings.ConclusionsThis study underscores the significant impact of climate on human life expectancy and highlights the need for integrated climate and health policies. Addressing harmful climate variables through targeted interventions—such as urban planning and emergency management—can enhance public health resilience. Our findings provide a scientific foundation for policymakers to develop sustainable strategies that safeguard human well-being in the face of climate change.

INTRODUCTION

Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges of the 21st century, with far-reaching implications for human health and well-being. Alterations in atmospheric conditions, thermal stress patterns, and hydrological cycles are exacerbating health risks worldwide, from heat-related illnesses to changes in disease transmission patterns [1,2]. Despite growing evidence of the interconnectedness of climate and health, policy responses often remain siloed, with climate and health sectors operating independently [3]. In many national and local contexts, climate policy frameworks focus primarily on emission reduction and infrastructure resilience, while health strategies emphasize disease prevention and healthcare capacity with limited cross-sector coordination. For instance, the WHO and Lancet Countdown reports note that climate adaptation and health planning are frequently developed under separate agencies or funding streams, resulting in fragmented implementation and reduced policy efficiency [1,4]. Strengthening evidence that quantifies the shared drivers of climate and health outcomes can help bridge these institutional divides.

Human life expectancy, a key indicator of population health, is influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, behavioral, and environmental factors [5]. In this study, we use life expectancy at birth as a standardized, population-level measure of health and lifespan. While “longevity” often refers to individual lifespan, life expectancy represents an aggregated demographic metric that captures the average expected lifespan within a population, providing a consistent and comparable indicator for climate–health analysis. Among these, climate variables such as atmospheric moisture (vapor pressure), thermal extremes (maximum temperature), ecosystem water balance (actual evapotranspiration), and solar radiation have been shown to play a significant role in shaping health outcomes [6]. For example, high atmospheric moisture combined with extreme heat intensifies physiological stress and is associated with increased mortality rates [7], particularly among vulnerable populations. Conversely, adequate solar radiation exposure is linked to improved mental health, vitamin D synthesis, and reduced risk of chronic diseases [8]. However, the quantitative contribution of specific climate variables to population-level life expectancy patterns across diverse geographic contexts remains underexplored, and there is a critical need for spatially explicit, data-driven research to inform integrated climate and health policies. Previous research has focused primarily on short-term temperature extremes or regional mortality studies, which often lack the spatial resolution and multi-variable framework necessary to capture complex, long-term interactions among climatic, hydrological, and health factors [[9], [10], [11]].

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly Random Forest models, have enabled researchers to analyze complex interactions between environmental variables and health outcomes [12,13]. These models provide a powerful tool for identifying key predictors of life expectancy and quantifying their contributions, offering insights that can guide policy interventions. Compared with traditional regression models that assume predefined relationships or require temporal structures, Random Forest offers a flexible, data-driven framework capable of capturing nonlinear and interaction effects among multiple climatic determinants of health. This capacity allows identification of critical thresholds and dominant factors that can inform region-specific climate–health adaptation strategies. By leveraging these techniques, this study aims to bridge the gap between climate science and public health, providing empirical evidence on the relationship between climate features and human life expectancy.

The integration of climate and health policies has the potential to yield mutual benefits, enhancing both environmental sustainability and public health outcomes [14]. For instance, urban planning strategies that reduce heat islands and improve air quality can simultaneously mitigate climate change and reduce the burden of respiratory diseases [15]. Similarly, policies promoting renewable energy and sustainable transportation can lower greenhouse gas emissions while improving physical activity levels and reducing obesity rates [16]. However, achieving these synergies requires a robust understanding of the specific climate variables that impact health, as well as the development of targeted interventions that address these factors.

This study contributes to this growing body of knowledge by integrating multiple analytical frameworks, including Random Forest, Geographically Weighted Regression, and Generalized Additive Models to identify and quantify the impact of key climate features on life expectancy across the United States. By highlighting the most influential variables, such as vapor pressure, solar radiation, actual evapotranspiration and temperature, this research provides actionable, data-driven insights that can inform region-specific adaption and resilience strategies. This study underscore the importance of integrating climate and health policies, offering a pathway to protect human health in the face of a changing climate while advancing environmental sustainability.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Data Collection and Preprocessing

We utilized a comprehensive dataset comprising climate variables and life expectancy metrics from diverse geographic regions across the contiguous United States. Climate data included mean values for vapor pressure, solar radiation, actual evapotranspiration, and other relevant features, obtained from publicly available climate databases https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/cdo-web/. The outcome variable, life expectancy at birth was obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) U.S. Small-Area Life Expectancy Estimates Project (USALEEP) (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-visualization/life-expectancy/index.html, accessed October 1, 2024). These estimates provide census-tract-level life expectancy for the period 2010-2015, derived from aggregated mortality and population counts using official vital statistics. The data represent cross-sectional, population-based estimates that cover nearly all U.S. census tracts, thereby offering a nationally representative spatial dataset suitable for examining geographic variations in climate-health relationships. To ensure consistency, all datasets were merged by census tract number. The total observations contain 73,120 census tracts, with 31,994 complete cases. Model Development: Random Forest

We employed a random forest model (RF) [17], implemented using the caret package in R, to analyze the relationship between climate features and life expectancy. RF is an ensemble learning method that excels at handling non-linear relationships, high-dimensional data, and complex interactions among predictors, making it particularly suitable for this study [18]. The dataset was partitioned into a training set (70%) and a validation set (30%) to ensure robust model evaluation.

Hyperparameters, including the number of trees (ntree) and the maximum depth of each tree (mtry), were optimized using a systematic 10-fold cross-validated grid search to maximize predictive accuracy. The number of variables randomly sampled at each split (mtry) was optimized across the grid 17 with the final value (mtry=4, selected by RMSE minimization) automatically chosen by caret. The number of trees (ntree) was fixed at 100 after confirming convergence in preliminary tests.

Feature Importance and Interpretation

To identify the most influential climate features affecting human life expectancy, we employed a dual approach to assess feature importance using two complementary metrics. Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) was utilized to quantify the contribution of each feature to the model's predictive accuracy by measuring the total reduction in node impurity (e.g., Gini index) attributable to the feature across all trees in the random forest ensemble. This metric provides insight into how effectively each feature contributes to the model's decision-making process at the tree level. Additionally, we implemented permutation importance. This method measures the increase in prediction error when a feature's values are randomly shuffled. It provides a robust measure of feature relevance that is independent of the model's internal structure and less susceptible to bias from correlated features [19].

To interpret individual climate-variable contributions, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), a model-agnostic method based on cooperative game theory. SHAP values quantify each feature’s marginal contribution to model output, providing both global and local interpretability. Specifically, we calculated the mean absolute SHAP value for each variable to assess its average influence on life expectancy predictions across all samples. This approach enables a transparent understanding of how different climate features affect model predictions, even in non-linear and interaction-rich models such as random forests.

In summary, variables selection was guided by criteria: (1) established relevance to human health based on epidemiological literature, (2) representation of distinct climatic dimensions to avoid redundancy (Supplementary Table 3).

Rationale for Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR)

The random forest model identified census tract number as a significant predictor of life expectancy, highlighting the importance of geographic variability in climate impacts. This finding motivated the use of geographically weighted regression (GWR) [20], a spatial modeling technique that accounts for spatial heterogeneity by allowing regression coefficients to vary across geographic locations. GWR is particularly suited for capturing localized relationships between climate variables and life expectancy, providing insights into region-specific effects [21]. Bandwidth selection was automated using the gwr.sel() function, which minimizes the cross-validation score. Unlike global regression models, GWR estimates spatially varying coefficients, meaning that the relationship between each climate variable and life expectancy can change across geographic locations. This approach captures regional heterogeneity in effect strength and direction, providing location-specific parameter estimates rather than a single global coefficient.

Generalized Additive Model (GAM)

To further explore the nonlinear effects of climate variables and control for spatial dependence, we employed a generalized additive model (GAM). Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) extend linear models by allowing smooth, nonlinear relationships between predictors and the outcome variable. This makes them ideal for capturing complex climate–life expectancy interactions [22]. The model included smooth terms for latitude and longitude to account for spatial autocorrelation, ensuring that geographic patterns were adequately addressed. GAM used REML-based smoothness selection, with no manual tuning. Smooth terms were automatically optimized by the algorithm. In this study, the GAM model was formulated as follows for the ith census tract number:lifeexpectancyi=β0+s(longitude,latitude)+s(tmaxi)+s(snowwaterequivalenti)+s(Runoffi)+s(actualevapotranspirationi)+s(soilmoisturei)+s(Palmerdroughtseverityindexi)+s(solarradiationii)+s(vaporpressurei)+s(windspeedi)+errori where s represents the spline function. The significance of smooth terms was assessed using p-values, and the model's explanatory power was evaluated using the proportion of deviance explained and adjusted R².

In contrast to GWR, the GAM framework does not model spatially varying coefficients directly. Instead, it uses nonlinear smooth terms to capture curved, potentially nonmonotonic relationships between predictors and the outcome. GAM smooths across the data to describe nonlinear functional forms, while GWR allows regression parameters to vary across space. Together, these approaches provide complementary insights: GWR emphasizes geographic variability, and GAM highlights nonlinear effects.

Model Validation and Comparison

To ensure robustness and reproducibility, we validated all models using both 10-fold cross-validation and independent test sets. For the RF model, performance was assessed using root mean square error. The GWR model was evaluated via cross-validation scores, while the GAM employed restricted maximum likelihood for model fit estimation. The RF model served as the baseline to identify key predictors and evaluate overall predictive accuracy. The GWR model was applied to examine spatial heterogeneity in predictor-outcome relationships, whereas the GAM captured potential nonlinear effects. By integrating these complementary modeling approaches, we aimed to provide a comprehensive and interpretable analysis of the complex associations between climate variables and human life expectancy. Software information is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Confounding Control and Coefficient Stability Analyses

To address potential confounding, we conducted a coefficient-stability analysis using temporally aligned socioeconomic and environmental data. The outcome variable was life expectancy at birth from the U.S. Small-Area Life Expectancy Estimates Project (USALEEP, 2010–2015), representing 31,994 U.S. census tracts with complete information. The nine primary climate predictors averaged over 2010–2015 included maximum temperature, snow water equivalent, runoff, actual evapotranspiration, soil moisture, Palmer Drought Severity Index, solar radiation, vapor pressure, and wind speed.

Socioeconomic variables were obtained from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-Year Estimates (2011–2015) (U.S. Census Bureau, 2016). These included median household income (B19013_001E), percentage of adults with a bachelor’s degree or higher (B15003_022E), and percentage of White population (B02001_002E). These data provide a four-year temporal overlap with the climate and life-expectancy datasets, ensuring close alignment of measurement periods.

All datasets were harmonized at the county level using Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) codes, with census-tract–level climate and life-expectancy data aggregated to counties. Nested models were constructed to compare (1) climate-only, (2) climate + socioeconomic factors. This framework allowed evaluation of the stability of climate coefficients under progressive covariate adjustment.

RESULTS

Climate Predictors of Life Expectancy: Variable Importance and Model Performance

SHAP analysis revealed distinct patterns in the contribution of climate variables to predicted human life expectancy. As illustrated in Figure 2, vapor pressure exhibited the highest mean SHAP value (0.437), indicating it was the most influential variable in the random forest model. Maximum temperature (0.330), actual evapotranspiration (0.307), and solar radiation (0.257) also showed substantial contributions, suggesting a strong association between heat- and moisture-related climate factors and life expectancy outcomes. In contrast, variables such as soil moisture (0.109), runoff (0.121), and wind speed (0.131) demonstrated lower SHAP values, reflecting a relatively minor impact on model predictions. These results underscore the central role of atmospheric moisture and temperature dynamics in shaping health-related vulnerabilities. The integration of Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) in the Supplementary File Figures S1 and SHAP value analyses offers a robust framework for disentangling global and local feature effects within the model. PDPs quantify the average marginal response of the predicted outcome to changes in each variable, effectively summarizing global trends and the strength of nonlinear associations. In the present results, high PDP ranks for variables such as solar radiation (#1), actual evapotranspiration (#2), and vapor pressure (#3) indicate strong and often nonlinear global effects on the response surface, whereas lower PDP ranks for features like soil moisture (#9) and snow water equivalent (#8) suggest weaker marginal sensitivity. SHAP values, on the other hand, capture the localized contribution of each predictor to individual predictions, thereby revealing how feature influence varies across the input space. Variables such as vapor pressure and maximum temperature exhibit the highest SHAP importance, implying that they contribute strongly to prediction accuracy even in contexts where PDP indicates complex or region-specific effects. This divergence underscores a key methodological insight: PDP measures average marginal influence, which may be attenuated by feature interactions or redundancy, while SHAP measures contextual contribution, highlighting features whose effects are locally dominant. The combination of these approaches thus enhances interpretability by linking global effect structures (from PDP) with local attribution patterns (from SHAP), enabling the distinction between features that exert consistent, system-wide control (e.g., solar radiation, actual evapotranspiration) and those whose influence is highly context-dependent yet impactful (e.g., vapor pressure, temperature). This dual-perspective interpretation (Supplementary Table 5) advances model explainability beyond single-metric assessments, revealing both the breadth and specificity of predictor influence within complex environmental systems.

Among the climate variables, several displayed pronounced nonlinear effects according to their effective degrees of freedom (EDF). Ranked by EDF, the strongest nonlinear relationships were observed for vapor pressure (EDF = 1.996), actual evapotranspiration (EDF = 1.986), soil moisture (EDF = 1.984), snow water equivalent (EDF = 1.975), solar radiation (EDF = 1.959), runoff (EDF = 1.955), and wind speed (EDF = 1.927). These results suggest that most hydrological and moisture-related variables influence life expectancy through complex, threshold-like relationships, whereas temperature shows an approximately linear association across its range. Specifically, moderate vapor pressure, soil moisture, and evapotranspiration levels were associated with higher life expectancy, while extreme humidity or aridity corresponded to lower values. Discussion

Interpretation of Main Findings and Policy Implications

Building upon the modeling results, this study demonstrates the significant impact of multiple climatic factors on human life expectancy. Across all models, temperature emerged as a consistent and statistically significant predictor, showing a near-linear negative relationship—life expectancy declined steadily as temperature increased. In parallel, vapor pressure, actual evapotranspiration, and soil moisture exhibited strong nonlinear associations, indicating that excess humidity or dryness can intensify health risks. Solar radiation showed a moderate positive effect within an optimal range, aligning with evidence on the benefits of balanced sunlight exposure. Together, these results suggest that both thermal and moisture dimensions of the climate system contribute meaningfully to life expectancy variation across U.S. regions.

The findings provide quantitative support for the hypothesis that climate variability—particularly factors influencing heat stress and hydrological balance—may exert meaningful influence on population-level life expectancy [9,10]. The moderate nonlinearity of drought severity indicates varying effects across drought conditions [23,24], with stronger negative impacts at severe drought levels. In contrast, the near-linear negative slope for temperature implies a consistent, incremental decline in life expectancy with rising temperature, without clear thresholds. The negative associations observed with high vapor pressure and evapotranspiration may reflect the physiological burden of thermal discomfort and the potential for increased transmission of vector-borne diseases in humid environments. In contrast, the positive role of solar radiation is consistent with studies linking moderate UV exposure to vitamin D production, lower depression rates, and decreased cardiovascular risk [25,26].

With respect to public health implications, first, the observed negative associations of high vapor pressure and actual evapotranspiration with life expectancy suggest that environments characterized by excess humidity and limited evaporative cooling may elevate heat-related stress and disease transmission risks [27,28]. Accordingly, public health and urban adaptation strategies should prioritize humidity and heat management, such as expanding cooling infrastructure, increasing urban ventilation, and enhancing early-warning systems during periods of extreme moisture and heat exposure[29,30].

Second, the positive association of moderate solar radiation and the nonlinear benefits of balanced soil moisture highlight that both insufficient and excessive environmental moisture can have health implications. This finding underscores the importance of regional water management and drought-mitigation policies, particularly in arid or flood-prone regions where extremes of soil moisture or precipitation influence health outcomes.

Third, the spatial heterogeneity revealed by the GWR and GAM analyses, with lower life expectancy observed in areas experiencing extreme heat or drought[10,31], emphasizes the need for place-based adaptation planning. Regions exhibiting climate–health vulnerabilities could benefit from integrated climate–health monitoring systems and localized resilience measures.

The relatively modest R² values observed in our models (0.14–0.35) are comparable to those reported in other U.S.-based ecological analyses of life expectancy that incorporate environmental and socioeconomic variables [11,32]. Given the multifactorial nature of life expectancy, models restricted to climatic predictors are expected to explain a limited but informative proportion of the variance. The consistency of our results across multiple modeling frameworks indicates that, while climate is not the sole determinant of life expectancy, it exerts a measurable and policy-relevant influence that persists even amid broader social and behavioral drivers.

Our findings align with previous studies linking temperature and humidity to mortality and life expectancy. For instance, Anderson and Bell (2009) [9] and Gasparrini et al. (2015) [10] reported strong heat-related mortality effects, consistent with the nearly linear negative association between temperature and life expectancy observed here. However, our analysis extends these findings by identifying moisture-related variables (vapor pressure, evapotranspiration, soil moisture) as equally important determinants—a dimension rarely captured in prior nationwide studies. The positive association of moderate solar radiation with life expectancy also agrees with evidence on the health benefits of balanced UV exposure [25]. The associations observed here are consistent with previous studies linking climatic and hydrological factors to health outcomes. High vapor pressure and humidity have been shown to exacerbate heat stress and cardiovascular strain [6], while excessive evapotranspiration and low soil moisture are markers of arid, drought-prone conditions that increase mortality risk through dehydration and reduced agricultural productivity [24]. Similarly, moderate solar radiation has been linked to beneficial physiological effects such as vitamin D synthesis [25,26] and improved mental health [25]. The persistence of these relationships across modeling frameworks reinforces the biological plausibility of the observed climate–health linkages.

These results suggest that multiple climatic pathways—thermal stress, hydrological imbalance, and sunlight exposure—interact to affect long-term health outcomes. Consequently, public health adaptation strategies should consider heat management, humidity control, and water resource balance as integrated elements of climate–health policy.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of a multi-method machine learning approach integrating Random Forest, GWR, and GAM to analyze complex interactions between climate features and human life expectancy across a nationally representative dataset. The application of SHAP values further enhances interpretability by providing transparent, variable-level attribution of climate contributions to life expectancy predictions. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the study relies on aggregated data, which may mask local variations in climate and health outcomes. Second, while the model identifies associations, it does not establish causality. Future research could incorporate longitudinal data and causal inference methods to address these limitations. The robustness of our findings was further supported by sensitivity analyses in which key socioeconomic covariates were added to the Random Forest model. The climate variables retained strong predictive importance, indicating that the observed associations are not substantially confounded by socioeconomic factors (Supplementary Table 10).

Future Directions

This study lays the groundwork for further research on the integration of climate and health policies (Figure 5). Future work could explore the mechanisms underlying the observed relationships, such as the role of heat stress or air quality in mediating the effects of climate on life expectancy. Overall Contribution and Conclusion

This work contributes new evidence to the growing field of climate–health research by integrating high-resolution spatial data and machine-learning methods to identify the climatic determinants of life expectancy in the United States. By highlighting the comparable importance of temperature, humidity, and hydrological balance variables, this study extends prior research that has focused predominantly on heat exposure. These findings provide a scientific foundation for region-specific adaptation and climate–health policy, emphasizing that mitigation efforts should address both heat and moisture extremes.

The analyses demonstrate that temperature, vapor pressure, actual evapotranspiration, soil moisture, and solar radiation are significant determinants of life expectancy, consistent with earlier studies showing that heat, humidity, and radiation jointly influence mortality and wellbeing [9,10,25]. Excess atmospheric moisture and extreme thermal exposure were associated with reduced life expectancy, likely mediated through heat stress and environmental disease transmission, whereas moderate solar radiation demonstrated a protective role. Geographic variability, particularly the amplified negative temperature effects in warmer regions, underscores the importance of context-specific public health strategies.

Rather than inferring causality, this study identifies robust climatic associations that inform future hypothesis-driven and longitudinal analyses. As climate change intensifies, understanding how multiple climatic pathways—including temperature, moisture balance, and solar exposure—shape human life expectancy will be critical to developing integrated, evidence-based health and adaptation policies. The results call for a multidisciplinary approach linking environmental science, epidemiology, and policy innovation to protect population health in a changing climate.
