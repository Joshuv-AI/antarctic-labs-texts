Comparison of GBLUP and WGBLUP in genomic selection for beef cattle under different LD patterns and mixed multi-population scenarios: a simulation study
Authors: Le Zhou, Fengying Ma, Lin Zhu, Chencheng Chang, Mingjuan Gu, Risu Na, Wenguang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenomic selection (GS) is a key tool to enhance genetic progress in livestock and poultry breeding, but the effects of differences in linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns between populations and the way reference populations are constructed on the accuracy of genomic prediction (GP) still need to be thoroughly investigated. Therefore, in this study, three beef cattle populations with different genetic backgrounds and LD patterns were simulated to compare the performance of GBLUP, WGBLUP, and their mixed reference population derivation methods (GBLUPmix and WGBLUPmix) in predicting genomic breeding values, with a focus on analyzing the effects of different LD patterns and reference population sizes on prediction performance.ResultsThe results showed that increasing the reference population size significantly improved the accuracy of GP, especially when the reference population was increased from 4000 to 5000, and the WGBLUPmix model showed the highest prediction accuracy in multi-population integration scenarios. In addition, the performance of the WGBLUPmix model was significantly different in different LD modes. The superiority may be due to the realization of capturing different LD patterns in the genome, adaptation to different genetic contexts of population structure, and better capture of large effect SNP contributions through weight adjustment or assignment.ConclusionThese results highlight the importance of using the WGBLUPmix model to flexibly adjust weights to accommodate different reference population sizes and LD patterns in multi-population genome prediction, demonstrate the superiority of WGBLUP in low-LD populations and its synergistic effect with mixed reference populations, and provide an effective strategy to improve prediction accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12224-2.Keywords: Genomic selection, GBLUP, WGBLUP, Linkage disequilibrium, Mixed reference population, Beef cattle

Background

With the continuous development of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarray and resequencing technologies, GS has rapidly emerged as a superior research methodology in the field of plant and animal breeding, and GP has been widely used in breeding programs for major commercial livestock breeds, significantly improving annual genetic gain [1, 2]. Traditional breeding methods may be limited by lower accuracy and slower genetic progress in some traits, and these limitations are overcome by the GS approach, which allows candidate selection of superior individuals by relying on prediction equations derived from reference populations with available phenotypes and genotypes [3]. Based on experimental evidence and theoretical foundations, GS has been shown to be more accurate and faster than traditional breeding methods, resulting in better breeding results [4–6]. However, genomic evaluations have been primarily implemented in large breeds. However, small breeds face unique challenges. These breeds often have a limited number of progeny test bulls available. Additionally, they struggle to establish large reference populations consisting of animals with precise phenotypes. Achieving accurate GP typically requires large populations of individuals with both phenotypes and genotypes. This requirement is not always feasible for all traits or all varietiess. To overcome these limitations, there are several strategies that can be employed. One remedy is multi-breed prediction. This approach involves adding data from other breeds to improve the accuracy of SNP effect estimation. Another strategy is a cross-breed prediction. This method utilizes SNP effects from another breed that has a large reference population [7]. It has been shown that combining multiple closely related populations into a common reference population improves the accuracy of GP in dairy cattle [8]. Integrating multiple populations into a shared reference population has the potential to enhance the accuracy of GP, but many challenges remain in merging and evaluating genomic breeding values.

In small breeding populations, it is often impractical to construct a sufficiently large reference population for GP, and in cross-breed GP, the value of the information provided by other breeds is often limited, especially when these breeds are genetically distant [9]. The size of the reference population is an important factor affecting the reliability of GP, and for breeds with small population sizes, there are many challenges in carrying out GP, among which the lack of a sufficiently large reference population has become a key obstacle to the accuracy of their GP [10]. In multi-population or cross-population scenarios, the reference population of a single population may not be able to adequately capture the genetic variation of the target population, leading to a reduction in prediction accuracy. In addition, the presence of large effect SNPs may also significantly affect prediction accuracy, especially when multiple trait prediction is involved and when populations are closely related. However, it has also been shown that for multi-breed prediction, a multi-breed reference population dominated by one breed reduces the prediction accuracy of smaller breeds. In Australian Red cattle, prediction accuracy increased significantly when a small number of Holstein bulls were included in the reference population, and while increasing the size of the reference population usually improved prediction accuracy, further increasing the number of Holstein bulls reduced accuracy, sometimes below that achieved within the breed [11, 12]. Thus, for breeds with small population sizes, it may be more effective to select a reference population that is more closely related to the target breed than to use a large reference population, so care needs to be taken in designing multi-breed reference populations rather than using all available data.

In addition, accuracy is dependent on the proportion of genetic variance explained by genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and the accuracy of the estimation of the effects of these SNPs, both of which are closely related to LD between SNPs and quantitative trait loci (QTLs) [13]. When multiple breeds are combined to form a reference population, prediction accuracy depends on SNP-QTL LD across breeds; however, LD may differ between breeds due to selection and genetic drift, or the phases of SNP and QTL alleles may be reversed between breeds [14]. Integrating QTL or SNPs closely linked with QTL into GP marker panels can be beneficial. This practice is applicable both in multi-breed reference populations and for cross-breed prediction. It may help to mitigate the issue of varying SNP-QTL LD between breeds. This assumption may not hold true if there are interactions between QTL and genetic background. Therefore, it may be more appropriate to assume that QTL (and therefore SNP effects) are different but correlated, rather than identical, between breeds. Thus, differences in LD between certain beef cattle breeds or populations and the limited size of their reference populations hinder the accuracy of genomic estimated breeding values (GEBV).

Genomic Best Linear Unbiased Prediction (GBLUP), as a genome-based prediction method, is widely used in practical genetic assessment work. It assesses the genetic similarity between individuals by calculating the genetic relationship matrix (G) between individuals. This genetic relationship is constructed based on the deviation of allele doses of marker genotypes from the population mean [15]. However, in structured populations such as multi-species or mixed populations, there may be significant differences in marker allele frequencies between subpopulations. In this case, if the average allele frequency of the whole population is still used to construct the genetic relationship matrix, it may reduce the accuracy of the estimation of genomic breeding values, especially in the presence of large effect SNPs or more complex patterns of LD. Although GBLUP typically assumes that all genetic variants contribute relatively little to traits, by using a weighted genomic relationship matrix [16, 17], which could allow certain preselected sequence variants to have a greater effect on traits. In recent years, the weighted GBLUP (WGBLUP) method has been proposed, which reflects the degree of contribution of each SNP to the trait more accurately by assigning weights to different SNPs, and thus improves the accuracy of GP [18, 19]. In this study, the prediction models are mainly divided into two categories: one is the GBLUP model based on inter-individual genomic relationships, which can reflect the genetic similarity between individuals and is calculated by the deviation of allele doses of marker genotypes from the population mean; the other is the WGBLUP model that introduces a weighted genomic relationship matrix, which can more accurately reflect the contribution of each SNP to the trait.

Therefore, this study focused on assessing the effects of different LD patterns, reference population size on prediction performance by simulating the performance differences in genomic breeding value prediction of three beef cattle populations with different genetic backgrounds and LD patterns, GBLUP, WGBLUP, and their hybrid reference population derived methods (GBLUPmix and WGBLUPmix). The study, emphasize the importance of integrating reference population size, LD differences and genetic background complexity in multi-population GP. In addition, we pay special attention to the performance of the WGBLUP model in the case of multi-population merging when the reference population size is increased from 4000 to 5000, as well as its adaptability to different LD modes.

Methods

Data simulation

Firstly, the pedigree and genomic data were simulated using QMSim V1.10 [20]. To investigate the impact of different LD patterns on the accuracy of genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) in beef cattle populations, the initial historical population (HP) was simulated into three distinct beef cattle populations (PopA, PopB, and PopC) based on three different LD patterns. Secondly, the final breeding animals from these populations were used as candidates for alternative selection schemes. QMSim was used to simulate five replicates, i.e., five sets of final breeding animals. Within each replicate, the breeding schemes studied selected the first batch of breeding animals from the same population, although different schemes could use different groups of breeding animals. To save data storage space and without compromising computational speed, only the genotypic and phenotypic data of animals in the 9th and 10th generations were simulated. The phenotypes were sampled from a normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1, with the overall mean being the only fixed effect. Population structure

In QMSim, the HP was generated based on equal sex ratios, non-overlapping generations, random mating, no selection, and no migration. These conditions were used to simulate initial values of LD, mutations, and genetic drift, thereby mimicking the structure and extent of LD present in beef cattle populations [21]. The population structures of the three distinct beef cattle populations (including historical, base, and current population structures) were simulated in detail as described in Fig. 1.

First, the HP of PopA was simulated, consisting of an initial 200 individuals that remained at a constant size of 200 individuals over 1,000 generations. In the subsequent 95 generations, the population size gradually expanded to 1,000 individuals (100 males and 900 females) to establish initial LD and mutation-drift equilibrium. Next, 250 males and 3,000 females were randomly selected from the last generation of the HP to expand the population size. All males and females were randomly mated, with each female producing one offspring per generation for 10 generations. These selected individuals were simulated over the most recent 100 generations, with each female producing one offspring per generation. Genome

The bovine genome assembly version ARS-UCD1.2 (< https://jul2023.archive.ensembl.org/Bos_taurus/Info/Index>) was used to simulate the 29 pairs of autosomes in beef cattle, with a total length of 2715.85 centimorgans. The aim was to create a more realistic scenario by considering the true distances between markers and quantitative trait loci (QTL) sites. SNP markers were uniformly distributed and randomly generated, with a density of 50k markers, representing diallelic loci with segregation. The number of SNPs on each chromosome was proportional to its size. The effects of markers on traits were assumed to be neutral. The whole genome consisted of 725 quantitative trait loci (QTL). The number of recombinations per Morgan was sampled from a Poisson distribution with a mean of 1, and crossovers were randomly placed along the chromosomes. In the final generation of the HP, a TLR effect for a production trait was introduced. This trait had a heritability of 0.42, similar to the heritability of birth weight in beef cattle [22]. In QMSim, the genetic effects for this trait were sampled from a gamma distribution with a shape parameter of 0.4. Over the 1095 generations of the HP, the mutation rate for both QTL and SNPs was 2.5 × 10⁻⁵, with the SNP mutation pattern set to “recurrent,” meaning mutations only switched between alleles without generating new mutation types. Genotype quality control

The PLINK v1.9 software [24], a widely used toolset for whole-genome association and population-based linkage analyses, was employed to further filter SNPs based on the following criteria: minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05, genotype call rate < 0.10, individual call rate < 0.10, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium < 1e-5. After genotype quality control, PopA, PopB, and PopC retained 52,676, 53,340, and 52,081 segregating SNPs with MAF >0.05, respectively, for subsequent analyses. The simulation assumed that QTL allele effects were identical across all breeds, but the frequencies of QTL varied among breeds, resulting in differences in the variance explained by each breed.

Admixed-reference population implementation (GBLUPmix/WGBLUPmix)

In this study, the GBLUPmix and WGBLUPmix methods extend the standard GBLUP and WGBLUP approaches by constructing strategically mixed reference populations. For the purposes of this research, we created mixed reference populations by combining individuals from PopA, PopB, and PopC. Specifically, we randomly selected 1,000, 2,000, 3,000, 4,000, and 5,000 individuals from each population. This sampling scheme generated balanced mixed reference populations with total sizes of 3,000, 6,000, 9,000, 12,000, and 15,000 individuals, respectively, while maintaining consistent population proportions across different sizes. Subsequently, these mixed populations were utilized for the evaluation of genomic prediction models, which were constructed in the same manner as the standard forms: the model applications of GBLUP and WGBLUP methods have been described above.

Two-way analysis of variance (2-way ANOVA)

To statistically assess the differences in predictive accuracy across various populations and conditions, we utilized a two-way analysis of variance (2-way ANOVA) with Prism software. This analytical method enables us to examine the main effects of two categorical independent variables, as well as their potential interactive effects, on a continuous dependent variable—in our case, genomic prediction accuracy.The two-way ANOVA model incorporated two factors: (1) the impact of different populations (e.g., PopA, PopB, PopC) on genomic prediction accuracy; (2) the influence of condition factors (e.g., reference population size, prediction model type) on genomic prediction accuracy. The two-way ANOVA provided estimates of the main effects for each factor, as well as the interaction effect between the two factors. The significance of these effects was determined by the calculated F-statistics and the corresponding p-values. P-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Population genetic characterization

To explore genetic population structure, we performed PCA using genotyping datasets from three different populations, popA, popB, and popC (Fig. 2). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) were chosen in this study because together they explained most of the variation in the data. As shown in Fig. 1, the distributions of popA, popB, and popC on PC1 and PC2 were significantly different, suggesting that these three groups are genetically distinct at the genomic level. the differences between popA and popB may reflect their differences in some key genetic traits, whereas the independent distributions of popC may be indicative of a unique genetic background.

Figure 3 illustrates the pattern of LD decay in three different populations (PopA, PopB, PopC). LD is a non-random association between allele frequencies at two or more loci in a genome. In this study the strength of LD was quantified using the r2 statistic, where r2 values close to 1 indicate strong LD, while close to 0 indicates weak LD. As shown in Fig. 3, the LD values of all the three populations declined with the increase in intergenic distances, which is in line with the trend of LD decay reported in the existing literature. Specifically, PopA showed the highest r2 values at all distances examined, indicating the slowest rate of LD decay. In contrast, PopB and PopC had lower r2 values and PopC had the fastest rate of LD decay. The difference in r2 values between the three populations was more significant at smaller genetic distances (0 to 500 kb). As the distance increases, this difference gradually decreases, and by 1000 kb, the r2 values of the three populations tend to be close to each other.

The impact of different LD patterns on the accuracy of GEBV prediction

Firstly this study compared the accuracy of genetic evaluation using GBLUP and WGBLUP methods in three beef cattle populations under different LD patterns and different reference population sizes, and the results are shown in Table 3. The results showed that PopC consistently achieved the highest GP accuracies when the reference set comprised 1,000, 2,000, 3,000 or 5,000 individuals, outperforming both PopA and PopB. Further inspection of the LD-decay plot (Fig. 3) revealed that PopA displayed the slowest LD decay, retaining elevated r² over longer genomic distances. This extended LD enables a larger proportion of SNPs to remain in effective linkage with causal QTL, thereby allowing GBLUP/WGBLUP to capture genetic effects more precisely and yielding high accuracy. In contrast, PopB and PopC exhibited rapid LD decay, causing marker–QTL associations to break down quickly and reducing the models’ ability to stably link genotypic information to phenotypes, which translated into lower accuracies. PopB showed an intermediate LD-decay profile, and accordingly its prediction accuracies ranked between those of PopA and PopC across all reference-population sizes.

Notably, the prediction accuracy of the WGBLUP method was significantly higher than that of the GBLUP method regardless of the reference population size and LD pattern. In particular, the prediction accuracy of WGBLUP in PopC was better than GBLUP at reference population sizes of 1,000, 2,000, 3,000, and 5,000, and exhibited stronger LD decay between the 50–200-kb region, suggesting that it was able to capture associations between markers and QTL more effectively. In order to further optimize the accuracy of genomic genetic assessment, hybrid versions of GBLUP and WGBLUP (GBLUPmix and WGBLUPmix) were also introduced, that is, samples with different reference population sizes were extracted from three populations (PopA, PopB and PopC) in equal proportions, and a hybrid reference population was constructed for evaluation, and the results are shown in Table 4. Discussion

Accuracy of genomic genetic evaluation reveals high and low heritability [25–27], the size of reference population [Predicting the effect of reference population on the accuracy of within, across population on the accuracy of within, across, and multibreed GP; Increased accuracy of artificial selection by using the realized relationship matrix. Accounting for population structure in selective cow genotyping], marker densities [28, 29], magnitude and number of QTL effect [30], and the linkage between marker and QTL disequilibrium [31, 32] and other factors on GP performance. In GP of multi-breed beef cattle, the reasons for the limited improvement of prediction accuracy are multiple, and one of the key factors is the differences in LD between SNPs and QTLs among different beef cattle breeds [33]. Most previous studies have focused on GP of single breed populations or hybrid breed populations against purebred breed populations, and have neglected the potential impact of differences between different interlocking disequilibrium patterns on prediction accuracy when constructing mixed reference populations in multibreed populations. The objective of this study was to investigate multi-breed reference populations of beef cattle under different LD patterns by comparing four evaluation methods, GBLUP, WGBLUP and their hybrid versions (GBLUPmix and WGBLUPmix), and attempting to construct reference populations from multi-breed populations by extracting different reference population sizes and mixing them equitably, in order to determine whether multi-breed mixed reference populations to determine whether multi-species mixed reference populations could improve the likelihood of prediction accuracy.

In studies of GP in multi-breed beef cattle, the impact of LD differences between SNPs and QTL among different breeds on prediction accuracy cannot be ignored. As pointed out by Schmid et al. [34], differences in LD between SNPs and QTLs among different breeds may lead to multi-breed GP with limitations, such as decreased prediction accuracy, genetic background differences among breeds, underrepresentation of reference populations, differences in QTL-SNP associations, and inconsistent LD phases. In small breed populations, these challenges are particularly evident due to the limited size of reference populations. Consequently, these populations require joint genetic evaluation across breeds to overcome difficulties in genetic assessment. In this study, we found that when different breeds of beef cattle were simply combined into a single reference population and GPs were made, prediction accuracy was not significantly improved in populations where the LD patterns were all different, which is consistent with previous findings [14, 35–37]. This suggests that prediction accuracy may not be effectively improved by increasing the size of the reference population alone without taking into account the LD differences between SNPs and QTLs across varieties. Even when there are partially overlapping QTL between breeds, these differences may lead to a decrease in prediction accuracy. To overcome the problem that prediction accuracy was not improved after simply combining different breeds of beef cattle into a single reference population, in this study, we adjusted the weights based on the weighted GBLUP method by introducing SNP weights to reflect the differences in the contribution of different SNPs to the phenotype, using the effect estimates of neighboring SNPs, and at the same time, we took into account the linkage imbalance among SNPs in the mixed reference population of multiple breeds (LD), an approach that assumes that effect sizes of neighboring SNPs tend to be similar in model fitting while temporarily setting aside other factors to improve prediction accuracy. The results show that the significant improvement in the effect of our method in terms of prediction accuracy improvement compared to training alone and simply merging the reference populations can be explained by the fact that when merging the reference populations of two study subjects, if a common substitution effect is estimated for a SNP in an additive model, then certain SNPs with a chained imbalance with the QTL in one of the study subjects, the If the phase of the chain disequilibrium with the QTL is different in the other study subject, this opposite phase of chain disequilibrium may generate false signals in estimating the effects of these SNPs. However, in this study, we used the weighted GBLUP method, in which we not only considered the effect of allele frequencies of SNPs on the weights, but also adjusted the weights of the current SNPs by a window-weighting scheme using the effect estimation of the neighboring SNPs, to efficiently filter out SNPs with similar chain disequilibrium phases in multiple study subjects and estimate their effects individually. By the above method, we are able to effectively screen out SNPs with similar chained imbalance phases in multiple research objects and estimate their effects individually. The subset of SNPs selected based on the weighted GBLUP was regarded as more informative SNPs, thus significantly improving the prediction accuracy by this scheme. This approach not only preserves population-specific estimates of QTL effects but also leverages cross-population information, resulting in a significant overall gain in prediction accuracy compared with either single-population or simply merged reference sets.

In this study, it was found that an increase in the size of the reference population significantly improved the accuracy of GP, which is in line with the findings of Brito, Nwogwugwu, and Perez et al. [21, 38, 39] are consistent with the findings that the size of the reference population is an important factor in GP. However, we also note that simply increasing the reference population size does not completely solve the problem of prediction accuracy. As mentioned in the study of Yin et al. [40], LD reflects the degree of non-random association among genomic loci, which is affected by population structure, genetic background and historical selection. In multi-population GP, the closer the kinship between populations, the more similar the LD patterns are, and the higher the applicability of the prediction model; on the contrary, if the kinship is far away, the LD differences are large, and the applicability is reduced. The limited sample size of small populations and the high uncertainty of LD estimation also affect the prediction accuracy. Therefore, an increase in reference population size can only maximize its potential if LD differences between SNPs and QTLs across breeds are taken into account and acted upon accordingly. Some studies have also focused on the effect of LD differences among different breeds of beef cattle on the combined reference population, both from the perspective of selecting more genetically diverse animals as GWAS discovery sets [41], or from the perspective of Bayesian assigning higher weights to SNPs close to causal polymorphisms [8, 42], the former focuses more on improving the resolution and accuracy of GWAS, whereas the latter focuses more on standardizing genotypes through the use of varietal-specific allele frequencies to better reflect genetic differences among varieties. Our comprehensive simulation study indicates that the relative performance of GBLUP and WGBLUP methods is significantly influenced by the LD patterns and marker density in cattle populations. Under low LD conditions, GBLUP demonstrates stable predictive accuracy; however, when LD is high or when marker weights align with genetic structure, such as regions carrying growth-related QTLs, WGBLUP shows greater advantage. This difference may stem from WGBLUP’s ability to prioritize markers associated with functional variation. Nevertheless, the biological mechanisms underlying these differences, such as pleiotropy or epistasis interactions, still require further investigation through integrated transcriptomic or GWAS data analysis.Thus, this study emphasizes that in multi-population GP, the increase in reference population size, although important, must be considered in conjunction with differences in LD between populations and the complexity of the genetic background.

Although some positive results were obtained in this study, we also recognize that there are some limitations of the model. For example, this study was based on simulated data for analysis, and while simulated data allow for flexibility in controlling parameters such as population and genetic background, there are still differences between real data. Despite the precision of QMSim, its default parameter settings have several limitations: (1) environmental effect modeling only considers additive variance components and does not incorporate genotype-environment interactions (G×E); (2) simplified assumptions regarding population structure (e.g., fixed migration rates, dispersed generations); (3) limited capability to simulate epigenetic effects and non-additive genetic effects.In addition, only GBLUP, WGBLUP and their hybrid versions of the assessment methods were examined in this study, while other GP models such as Bayesian methods were not used, which may not fully reflect the performance of all methods in different populations and reference population sizes. Finally, the accuracy of GP is affected by a variety of genetic parameters, such as the heritability of a trait and the number of QTL [9, 43, 44]. The present study did not explore in depth the variation of these genetic parameters across populations and evaluation methods and their impact on prediction accuracy, which somewhat limits in-depth interpretation of the results. These limitations suggest that in subsequent studies, we need to combine real data and a wider variety of assessment methods to explore in depth the mechanism of the role of genetic parameters and to expand the range of reference population sizes in order to further optimize the strategy of multi-population GP.

In our study, we observed that the increase in reference population size significantly improved the accuracy of GP, especially when the reference population size was increased from 4000 to 5000, and the WGBLUPmix model showed the highest prediction accuracy when multiple populations were combined. In addition, there were significant differences in the performance of the WGBLUPmix model under different LD patterns.The superiority of the WGBLUPmix model in these several scenarios could be attributed to several potential impacts by adjusting or assigning different weights, including capturing different LD patterns in the genomes, adapting to the genetic backgrounds in the different population structures, and being able to better capture large effect SNP contributions [19, 45, 46]. These results emphasize the importance of flexibly adjusting or assigning different weights to accommodate different reference population sizes and chain imbalance patterns in multi-population GP through WGBLUPmix models, providing an effective strategy for improving prediction accuracy.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study delved into the effects of reference population size and LD on the accuracy of GP by modeling beef cattle populations with different genetic backgrounds. Based on the findings, the importance of integrating reference population size, chain imbalance differences and genetic background complexity in multi-population GP was emphasized.The WGBLUPmix model showed significant advantages in handling multi-population data, especially when there were differences in chain imbalance patterns. These findings provide an important theoretical basis for beef cattle breeding and provide directions for future research, especially in optimizing reference population construction and evaluation methods.
