Prophylactic laxative use for postoperative constipation in urological cancer: a real-world cohort study
Authors: Wasilijiang Wahafu, Feiya Yang, Tiejun Yang, Zhaohui Tan, Jianhua Lan, Ting Chen, Zhihong Cao, Minjun Jiang, Guochang Bao, Qiong Pei, Xianming Zhou, Xiao Guo, Yifei Liu, Tong Lu, Yueqing Tang, Chunlin Liu, Yingmin Xu, Hengtai Zhao, Lizhi Huo, Guimin Zhang, Nianzeng Xing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess the effectiveness of prophylactic laxative use compared to symptom-based management for preventing constipation in patients undergoing surgery for urological cancers.MethodsOf 2,024 screened patients (aged 18–80) with urological tumors, 1,724 eligible (no severe gastrointestinal disease, non-pregnant, scheduled for surgery) were non-randomly assigned to prevention (prophylactic laxatives, n = 965) or non-prevention groups (symptom-based, n = 748). Propensity score matching with a 1:1 optimal matching strategy was used to minimize baseline differences between the two groups. The primary outcome was the incidence of constipation within 7 days post-surgery. Secondary outcomes included complete spontaneous bowel movements (CSBM), patient assessment of constipation quality of life (PAC-QOL) scores, and patient assessment of constipation symptoms (PAC-SYM) scores at 7 days post-surgery. A nomogram was developed for predicting postoperative constipation.ResultsCompared to non-prevention, the incidence of postoperative constipation was significantly lower in the prevention group (12.3% vs. 42.7%; HR 0.24, 95% CI: 0.19–0.30, P < 0.001). A higher proportion of patients in the prevention group achieved ≥ 3 CSBMs per week ( 96.0% vs. 88.2%, P < 0.001), and PAC-SYM mean difference (MD = − 0.56, 95% CI: −0.79 to − 0.33) and PAC-QOL (MD = − 1.22, 95% CI: −2.03 to − 0.41) scores were improved. Age, constipation diagnosis, prophylactic laxative use, and baseline CSBM were identified as independent risk factors for postoperative constipation.ConclusionProphylactic laxative use was associated with a significantly lower incidence of postoperative constipation in patients with urological tumors. Our findings offer preliminary evidence for clinicians in identifying patients at risk of developing postoperative constipation.Trial registrationChiCTR2100042876 (www.chictr.org.cn).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00345-025-06050-0.Keywords: Postoperative constipation, Urological tumor, Prophylactic, Laxatives, Real-world study

Introduction

Urological cancers are one of the most common cancers, accounting for approximately 12.6% of all cancer cases [1]. Constipation, a prevalent and distressing symptom in patients with cancer, is characterized by difficulty in defecation, hard stools, an incomplete sense of evacuation, and reduced bowel movement frequency [2]. Between 40% and 90% of patients with cancer experience constipation [3].

Surgery is a primary treatment for early-stage cancer; however, surgery significantly increases the risk of postoperative constipation [3–5]. Patients may experience anxiety and difficulty with bowel movements, directly impacting postoperative recovery [6]. Therefore, preventing postoperative gastrointestinal dysfunction is a key aspect of cancer patient management [7]. Studies estimate that 50% to 70% of patients develop constipation after surgery, typically within 3 days [4, 8]. If left unmanaged, postoperative constipation can prolong hospitalization, reduce quality of life, increase medical expenses, and elevate postoperative mortality risk [9–11].

Optimal management strategies for postoperative constipation in patients with cancer remain uncertain. Although some guidelines recommend pharmacological interventions, others emphasize non-pharmacological approaches [3, 12, 13]. Current pharmacological treatments include gastrointestinal stimulants, laxatives, prokinetic agents, probiotics, and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) [14]. Commonly used laxatives include bulk-forming, osmotic, softening, and stimulant laxatives [12]. For TCM, some of the bioactive agents promoting bowel movements are produced only after being metabolized by gut microbiota [15–17]. However, some studies indicate that once constipation develops, patients may not respond well to laxatives [18, 19], whereas prophylactic laxative use can prevent constipation symptoms and improve quality of life [20, 21].

Despite these insights, limited research exists on the effectiveness of prophylactic laxatives in reducing postoperative constipation risk in patients with urological cancer. In this study, we hypothesized that the administration of prophylactic laxatives to patients undergoing surgery for urological tumors was associated with a reduced incidence of constipation compared to a symptom-based treatment.

Subjects and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, multicenter, non-interventional study was conducted across 38 hospitals in 34 cities across China. Patients aged 18 to 80 years with urological tumors scheduled for surgical resection were consecutively enrolled. They were assigned to one of two groups: the prevention group, which received prophylactic laxatives postoperatively, and the non-prevention group, which did not receive prophylactic laxatives. Group allocation was not randomized. The study was registered at www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2100042876) and approved by the Ethics Committee of Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (20/353–2549). All participants signed written informed consent prior to participation in the study.

Study procedures

The study consisted of four visits: screening, baseline (day 0), postoperative day 3, and postoperative day 7. All patients received guidance on high-fiber intake and adequate hydration after the procedure. Prevention was defined as the use of laxatives before symptom onset, while symptom-based treatment referred to laxative use after symptoms developed. The selection of laxatives and the duration of treatment were determined by the physician’s clinical assessment. Laxative dosages adhered to the prescribing information. Constipation was diagnosed based on the Rome IV criteria [22] and the American College of Gastroenterology clinical practice guidelines [23] (Supplementary 1). Data were collected at screening, baseline, and on postoperative days 3 and 7.

Outcomes and measurements

The primary outcome was the incidence of constipation within 7 days post-surgery. Secondary outcomes included changes in CSBM frequency, PAC-SYM scores, and PAC-QOL scores at day 7, as well as factors influencing postoperative constipation incidence. Exploratory outcomes examined the impact of different laxative medications on bowel movements.

Bowel movement diaries were used to record bowel movement frequency, accompanying symptoms, and laxative use adherence. The PAC-SYM questionnaire assessed constipation-related symptoms [24]. The scale comprised three sections: abdominal symptoms (items 1–4), rectal symptoms (items 5–7), and stool symptoms (items 8–12). A higher score indicated more severe symptoms. A negative change in the score from baseline signified improvement, while a positive change indicated worsening. The PAC-QOL questionnaire evaluated quality of life [25]. The scale comprised four sections: physical discomfort (items 1–4), psychosocial discomfort (items 5–12), worries/concerns (items 13–23), and satisfaction (items 24–28). A decrease in PAC-QOL scores from baseline indicated improved quality of life.

Statistical analysis

To improve baseline comparability between the prevention and the non-prevention groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:1 optimal matching strategy. Matched variables included age, gender, severity of constipation, tumor site, CSBM frequency in the past 7 days, and PAC-SYM scores. To ensure data integrity and accuracy, missing data in matched variables were imputed using the random forest algorithm. For the primary outcome, comparisons between groups were performed using Cox proportional hazards models. We adjusted for key confounding factors, including group allocation, age, gender, history of constipation, tumor site, baseline CSBM frequency, baseline PAC-SYM score, and baseline PAC-QOL score, to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 statistical software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and the R packages “Mice” and “MatchIt”.

Results

Study participants

A total of 2,024 hospitalized patients were screened for eligibility, and 1,724 patients were deemed eligible for participation. The prevention group included 965 patients who received prophylactic laxatives, while the non-prevention group included 748 patients who did not receive prophylactic laxatives (Fig. 1).

Primary end points

Postoperative constipation was observed in 92 patients (12.3%) using prophylactic laxatives, while 319 patients (42.7%) not using prophylactic laxatives experienced constipation. Compared to the non-prevention group, prophylactic laxative use was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative constipation (HR 0.24, 95% CI: 0.19 to 0.30), consistent with analyses before PSM (Table 2).

In the subgroup analysis, compared to the non-prevention group, prophylactic laxative use was associated with a lower incidence of constipation both in patients without a prior diagnosis (HR 0.20, 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.28, P < 0.001 in patients without a constipation diagnosis; HR 0.32, 95% CI: 0.22 to 0.47, P < 0.001 in patients with a constipation diagnosis). This association appeared consistent across subgroups defined by tumor site, age, and baseline CSBM frequency (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Secondary end points

Incidence of constipation symptoms and severity

Compared to the non-prevention group, a lower proportion of patients in the prevention group reported constipation-related symptoms and varying degrees of constipation (Supplementary Table 1).

Changes in bowel-related indicators at 7 days post-surgery

Compared to the non-prevention group, a significantly higher proportion of patients using prophylactic laxatives achieved CSBM at least three times within 7 days post-surgery (96.0% vs. 88.2%, P < 0.001). Similarly, more patients in the prevention group had CSBM within 3 days post-surgery (91.4% vs. 81.3%, P < 0.001). Compared to the non-prevention group, patients using prophylactic laxatives showed greater improvements in PYC-SYM scores (-0.56, 95% CI: -0.79 to -0.33, P < 0.001) and PAC-QOL scores (-1.22, 95% CI: -2.03 to -0.41, P = 0.003) at 7 days post-surgery. However, there were no significant differences in the length of hospital stay (Supplementary Table 2).

Specifically, in the three PYC-SYM scales, prophylactic laxative use was associated with greater improvements in rectal symptoms (-0.37, 95% CI: -0.53 to -0.22, P < 0.001) and abdominal symptoms (-0.13, 95% CI: -0.19 to -0.06, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 3). Among the four PAC-QOL subscales, prophylactic laxatives reported statistically significant differences in the physical domain (-0.18, 95% CI: -0.29 to -0.07, P = 0.001) and satisfaction (-0.38, 95% CI: -0.66 to -0.09, P = 0.01) compared to the non-prevention group (Supplementary Table 3). Subgroup analyses by tumor site suggested that prophylactic laxative use was associated with favorable changes in bowel-related indicators, compared to the non-prevention group (Supplementary Table 4).

Analyses of risk factors for postoperative constipation

Multivariable logistic regression identified age, prior constipation diagnosis, and lower baseline CSBM as risk factors, while prophylactic laxative use was associated with a reduced risk of postoperative constipation. Compared to the non-prevention group, prophylactic laxative use was associated with a lower odds of postoperative constipation (OR, 0.15, 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.21, P < 0.001) (Table 3). A predictive model incorporating these factors was developed as a nomogram (Fig. 2A), which exhibited moderate predictive power, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.768 to 0.817) (Fig. 2B).

Effects of different laxative types on bowel-related indicators

We categorized laxative medications into three groups: TCM, pharmaceutical agents, and others, and combination therapy. Specifically, TCM included Shouhui Tongbian capsules, Biantong capsules, and others. Pharmaceutical agents and others included lactulose, polyethylene glycol, and enemas. Combination therapy entailed administering two or more medications with distinct mechanisms of action, with at least one being a TCM. In the prevention group, the proportion of TCM laxative use was significantly higher than that of other medications (Supplementary Table 5). Compared to pharmaceutical agents and others, a higher proportion of patients using TCM maintained a ≥ 3 CSBM per week (OR: 20.08, 95% CI: 7.70 to 52.38, P < 0.001) and improved CSBM post-surgery (coefficients 1.35, 95% CI: 0.85 to 1.84, P < 0.001). Similar results were observed in PAC-SYM scores and PAC-QOL scores. There were no significant differences between the groups (Supplementary Table 6).

Adverse events

A total of 40 patients (5.3%) in the prevention group and 26 patients (3.4%) in the non-prevention group experienced adverse events; however, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

Constipation impacts postoperative recovery in patients with cancer and has been associated with prolonged hospital stays and adverse outcomes. Managing bowel health in this population presents significant challenges, as chemotherapy, surgery, and opioids are recognized risk factors for constipation [19, 26]. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale observational study to estimate the incidence of postoperative constipation and explore potential management approaches in patients undergoing surgery for urological tumors. Our findings revealed that the prevalence of postoperative constipation in this population was 42.7%. Prophylactic laxative use was associated with approximately 76% lower observed incidence of postoperative constipation in the prevention group compared to the non-prevention group. Most patients in the prevention group used prophylactic laxatives for 3 to 7 days, suggesting that short-term prophylaxis may be a simple and practical approach to support postoperative bowel function. These findings align with results from prior research [11, 20].

Subgroup analyses across diverse population characteristics further support these findings. Notably, this preventive strategy significantly lowered the incidence of postoperative constipation, independent of age, prior constipation diagnosis, tumor site, or baseline CSBM. Although the incidence of constipation increased with age [27], particularly among patients over 60 years, preventive treatment benefited individuals across all age groups.

To comprehensively evaluate stool consistency and account for the complexity and variability of constipation presentations, we employed the PAC-SYM and PAC-QOL assessment tools [25, 28]. Patients receiving prophylactic laxatives showed a 1-point reduction in PAC-SYM scores post-surgery from baseline, indicating a significant improvement in symptoms [28]. The robustness of this finding is further strengthened by the detailed analysis of its subscales.

We conducted a comprehensive review of prior studies on laxatives and summarized their efficacy (Supplementary 1). Piche & Dapoigny demonstrated that treatment with either lactulose plus paraffin or polyethylene glycol resulted in a 1-point reduction in PAC-SYM scores at 14 days in patients with functional constipation [29]. Additionally, a single-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial demonstrated that L. rhamnosus JYLR-127 significantly increased defecation frequency (3.04 ± 1.54) in patients with fractures and substantially improved PAC-SYM scores, particularly reducing abdominal symptom severity compared to placebo [30]. A double-blind, randomized, and controlled trial found that lubiprostone and senna resulted in a mean reduction of -0.28 in PAC-SYM scores in postoperative orthopedic patients treated with opioids [31]. Interestingly, our study yielded similar real-world results.

Our study revealed that over 90% of patients had a prior diagnosis of constipation, which is a risk factor for developing postoperative constipation [3, 32]. Yet, preventive strategies are rarely implemented, as clinicians predominantly rely on symptomatic management [3, 33]. By identifying independent risk factors and developing a predictive nomogram, we provide a tool to facilitate early risk stratification and guide preventive interventions during the perioperative phase.

Exploratory analysis further indicated that patients using TCM experienced greater symptom relief and better clinical outcomes compared to those treated with pharmaceutical agents or combination therapy. In our study, a considerable number of patients selected TCM as their preferred preventive strategy, further demonstrating efficacy comparable to that of other medications [29, 34–36]. Constipation may be caused by increased resistance to elimination, reduced rectal motility, and insufficient intestinal fluid secretion [37]. TCM holds that conduction issues in the large intestine are exacerbated by imbalances in qi and blood, along with disruptions in yin-yang harmony [38, 39]. Patients with cancer often experience prolonged illness and physical weakness, and surgical trauma can further deplete qi and blood, as well as reduce intestinal moisture, which can lead to constipation. TCMs, such as Shouhui Tongbian capsule, which nourishes yin, boosts qi, and promotes bowel movements, help replenish vital energy and blood, effectively alleviating constipation symptoms and aiding in the recovery of postoperative intestinal function [40]. From a modern pharmacological perspective, these TCMs regulate gastrointestinal hormones and gut microbiota, repair the intestinal barrier, promote intestinal fluid secretion, and enhance motility [40–44]. Our findings suggest that both TCM alone and combination therapy may potentially improve CSBM, alleviate abdominal bloating and pain, and ease defecation difficulty [40, 45–47]. Combination therapy may be a viable strategy for patients who do not respond well to laxatives alone [41].

This study has several limitations. First, as a non-randomized observational cohort study, causal relationships between the interventions and outcomes cannot be definitively established. Second, the baseline clinical data collected were limited, potentially omitting variables that could influence the accuracy of the risk assessment. For instance, variations in surgical approaches for the same cancer type may affect patient outcomes [48]. Although the majority of patients in our cohort underwent minimally invasive procedures, detailed information regarding specific surgical techniques was not recorded, precluding assessment of potential differences in functional parameters and outcomes between open and minimally invasive surgeries. Nevertheless, these findings may provide a foundation for the design of future studies. Lastly, although the proposed nomogram has undergone internal validation, external validation in independent cohorts is required to confirm its generalizability, reliability, and robustness.

Conclusion

Our study is among the first to suggest that prophylactic laxative use is associated with a reduced risk of postoperative constipation in patients undergoing surgery for urological tumors. These findings offer preliminary evidence for clinicians to identify individuals at high risk and highlight the potential value of preventive strategies in postoperative constipation management. Further randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.
