Tactics for Improving Least Squares Estimation
Qiang Heng, Hua Zhou, Kenneth Lange

Abstract
This paper deals with tactics for fast computation in least squares regression in high dimensions. These tactics include: (a) the majorization-minimization (MM) principle, (b) smoothing by Moreau envelopes, and (c) the proximal distance principle for constrained estimation. In iteratively reweighted least squares, the MM principle can create a surrogate function that trades case weights for adjusted responses. Reduction to ordinary least squares then permits the reuse of the Gram matrix and its Cholesky decomposition across iterations. This tactic is pertinent to estimation in L2E regression and generalized linear models. For problems such as quantile regression, non-smooth terms of an objective function can be replaced by their Moreau envelope approximations and majorized by spherical quadratics. Finally, penalized regression with distance-to-set penalties also benefits from this perspective. Our numerical experiments validate the speed and utility of deweighting and Moreau envelope approximations. Julia software implementing these experiments is available on our web page.

Introduction

It is fair to say that most estimation problems in classical statistics reduce to either least squares or maximum likelihood and that much of maximum likelihood estimation reduces to iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS). Quantile regression and generalized linear model (GLM) regression are just two of the many examples of IRLS. The modern era of frequentist statistics has added to the classical inference soup sparsity, robustness, and rank restrictions. These additions complicate estimation by introducing nonsmooth terms into the objective function. In practice, smooth approximations can be substituted for nonsmooth terms without substantially impacting estimates or inference. The present paper documents the beneficial effects on model selection and solution speed of several tactics: (a) systematic application of the majorization-minimization (MM) principle, (b) substitution of ordinary least squares for weighted least squares, (c) substitution of Moreau envelopes for nonsmooth losses and penalties, and (d) application of the proximal distance principle in constrained estimation.

The speed of an optimization algorithm is a trade-off between the cost per iteration and the number of iterations until convergence. In our view, this trade-off has not been adequately explored in IRLS. Recall that in IRLS, we minimize the objective

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{mi}(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2},

(1)

at iteration
m
. Here
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{i})
is the response vector,
\boldsymbol{X}=(x_{ij})
is the design matrix, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{j})
is the vector of regression coefficients. The dominant cost in high-dimensional IRLS is the solution of the normal equations
(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{m}\boldsymbol{X})\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{m}\boldsymbol{y}
, where
\boldsymbol{W}_{m}
is the diagonal matrix of case weights. For dense problems, solution of the normal equations can be achieved via the Cholesky decomposition of the weighted Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{m}\boldsymbol{X}
or the QR decomposition of the weighted design matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{m}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}
.

Unfortunately, both decompositions must be recalculated from scratch each time the weight matrix
\boldsymbol{W}_{m}
changes. Given
p\leq n
regression coefficients, the complexity of computing the Gram matrix, weighted or unweighted, is
O(np^{2})
. Fortunately, matrix multiplication is super fast on modern computers that have access to highly optimized BLAS routines. On large-scale problems with
p
comparable to
n
, Cholesky and QR decompositions execute more slowly than matrix multiplications even though the former has computational complexity
O(p^{3})
, and the latter has computational complexity
O(np^{2})
. If it takes
m
iterations until convergence, these costs must be multiplied by
m
. In repeated unweighted regressions, a single QR decomposition or a single paired Gram matrix and Cholesky decomposition suffices, and the computational burden drops to
O(p^{3})
. On the other hand, the process of deweighting may well cause
m
to increase. The downside of the Cholesky decomposition approach is that the condition number of
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}
is the square of the condition number of
\boldsymbol{X}
. In our view, this danger is overrated. Adding a small ridge penalty overcomes it and is still consistent with rapid extraction of the Cholesky decomposition. In practice, code based on Cholesky decomposition is faster than code based on QR decomposition, and our numerical experiments exploit this fact.

The majorization-minimization (MM) principle
[
25
,
37
,
33
]
is our engine for leveraging Moreau envelopes and converting weighted to unweighted least squares. In minimization, MM iteratively substitutes a surrogate function
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
that majorizes a loss
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
around the current iterate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
. Majorization is defined by the tangency condition
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
and the domination condition
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\geq f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. The surrogate balances two goals: hugging the objective tightly and simplifying minimization. Minimizing the surrogate produces the next iterate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}
and drives the objective downhill owing to the conditions

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\leq g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}).

In maximization, the surrogate minorizes the objective and must be maximized instead. The tangency condition remains intact, but the domination condition
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\leq f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is now reversed. The celebrated EM (expectation-maximization) principle for maximum likelihood estimation with missing data
[
40
]
is a special case of minorization-maximization. In the EM setting, Jensen’s inequality supplies the surrogate as the expectation of the complete data log-likelihood conditional on the observed data.

In practice, many surrogate functions are strictly convex quadratics. When this is the case, minimizing the surrogate is achieved by the Newton update

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})^{-1}\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}).

The second form of the update reflects the tangency condition
\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
. In this version of gradient descent, the step length is exactly
1
. The local rate of convergence is determined by how well the surrogate curvature matrix
d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
approximates the actual curvature matrix
d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
. In the current paper, we focus on quadratic surrogates of fixed curvature, denoted by
\boldsymbol{H}
. This approach has precedent in the early statistical literature. Examples include multinomial logistic regression
[
8
]
, weighted least squares
[
28
]
, and support vector machines
[
19
]
. These applications emphasize the virtue of inverting the curvature matrix just once. The present work builds on this tradition in several ways. We underscore the computational value of recycling matrix decompositions across iterations, offer new theoretical insights into the convergence of the quadratic MM algorithm, and demonstrate its utility across a diverse set of new examples.

The Moreau envelope of a function
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
from
\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined by

\displaystyle M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\Big[f(\boldsymbol{\nu})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\Big].

When
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is convex, the infimum is attained, and its Moreau envelope
M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is convex and continuously differentiable
[
42
]
. However,
M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
also exists for many nonconvex functions
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

[
43
]
. In the absence of convexity, the proximal operator

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits}\Big[f(\boldsymbol{\nu})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

(3)

can map to a set rather than a single point. For a proxable function
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
, the definition of the Moreau envelope implies the quadratic majorization

\displaystyle M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{m})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\nu}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}

(4)

at
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{m}
for any
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{m}\in\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
. Generally,
M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and
\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
under mild conditions. In particular, if
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is Lipschitz with constant L, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-M_{\mu f}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq\frac{L^{2}\mu}{2}.

Despite the elementary nature of the Moreau majorization (
4
), its value in regression has largely gone unnoticed.

In Section
2.5
we rederive the majorization of
[

]
, replacing the weighted least squares criterion (
1
) with the unweighted surrogate

g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[\tilde{y}_{mi}-\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\bigr]^{2}+c_{m},

(5)

where
\tilde{y}_{mi}=w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\mu_{mi}
is the shifted response and
c_{m}
is irrelevant in the subsequent minimization. In our examples, the regression functions

depend linearly on the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. The majorization (

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}|.

The nonsmooth absolute value function has Moreau envelope equal to Huber’s function

\displaystyle M_{\mu|\cdot|}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2\mu}r^{2},&|r|\leq\mu\\
|r|-\frac{\mu}{2},&|r|>\mu\end{cases}

with proximal map

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu|\cdot|}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(1-\frac{\mu}{\max\{|r|,\mu\}}\Big)r.

(6)

The loose majorization (
4
) generates the least squares surrogate

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{i}-z_{mi})^{2},

(7)

where
r_{i}=y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
r_{mi}=y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
, and
z_{mi}=\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu|\cdot|}(r_{mi})
. Minimizing the surrogate reuses the same Cholesky decomposition at every iteration.

Alternatively, one can employ the best quadratic majorization
[
13
]

\displaystyle\quad M_{\mu|\cdot|}(r)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2|r_{m}|}(r-r_{m})^{2}-r-\frac{1}{2}\mu&r_{m}\leq-\mu\\
\frac{1}{2\mu}r^{2}&|r_{m}|<\mu\\
\frac{1}{2|r_{m}|}(r-r_{m})^{2}+r-\frac{1}{2}\mu&r_{m}\geq\mu\end{cases}.

(8)

This majorization translates into a weighted sum of squares plus an irrelevant constant. The resulting weighted sum of squares can be further deweighted by appealing to the surrogate (
5

These advances are ultimately as important as faster hardware. Together the best hardware and the best algorithms will make future statistical analysis even more magical.

2.2
M Estimation

In M estimation
[
14
,
15
,
24
]
based on residuals, one minimizes a function

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(r_{i})

of the residuals
r_{i}=y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
with
\rho(r)
bounded below. We have already dealt with the choice
\rho(r)=|r|
in LAD regression. In general, one can replace
\rho(r)
by its Moreau envelope, leverage the Moreau majorization, and immediately invoke ordinary least squares. Many authors advocate estimating
\boldsymbol{\beta}
by minimizing a function

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(r_{i}^{2})

of the squared residuals. Conveniently, this functional form is consistent with log-likelihoods under an elliptically symmetric distribution
[
34
]
. If
\rho(s)
is increasing, differentiable, and concave, then

\displaystyle\rho(s)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho(s_{m})+\rho^{\prime}(s_{m})(s-s_{m})

for all
s
. This plays out as the majorization

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{mi}(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})^{2}+c_{m},

with weights
w_{mi}=\rho^{\prime}[(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})^{2}]
and an irrelevant constant
c_{m}
that depends on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
but not on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. This surrogate can be minimized
by deweighting and invoking ordinary least squares. The Geman-McClure choice
\rho(s)=\frac{s}{1+s}
and the Cauchy choice
\rho(s)=\log(1+s)
for
s\geq 0
show that the intersection of the imposed conditions is non-empty.

A prime example
[
11
,
22
]
of robust M estimation revolves around the
\text{L}_{2}\text{E}
objective
[
46
,
38
]

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta},\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2\sqrt{\pi}}-\frac{\tau}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}r_{i}^{2}}{2}}.

This loss is particularly attractive because it incorporates a precision parameter
\tau
as well as the regression coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. Because
-e^{-s}
is concave, one can construct the MM surrogate

\displaystyle-e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}r_{i}^{2}}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}r_{mi}^{2}}{2}}+\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}e^{-\frac{\tau^{2}r_{mi}^{2}}{2}}\left(r_{i}^{2}-r_{mi}^{2}\right)

at iteration
m
with
\tau
fixed. The resulting weighted least squares surrogate can be further deweighted as explained in Section
2.5
. Under deweighting it morphs into the surrogate

g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=\frac{\tau^{3}}{2n}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{y}_{mi}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)^{2}+c_{m},

where
\tilde{y}_{mi}=w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
is the shifted response and
w_{mi}=e^{-\tau^{2}r_{mi}^{2}/2}
is the case weight. This ordinary least squares surrogate allows one to recycle a single Cholesky decomposition across all iterations. For
\boldsymbol{\beta}
fixed, one can update the precision parameter
\tau
by gradient descent
[
22
]
or an approximate Newton’s method
[
38
]
. Both methods rely on backtracking.

2.3
Quantile Regression

Quantile regression
[
29
]
is a special case of M estimation with the objective
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{q}(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta})
, where

\displaystyle\rho_{q}(r)=\Big(q-\frac{1}{2}\Big)r+\frac{1}{2}|r|

is called the check function. The argument
r
here suggests a residual. When
q=\frac{1}{2}
, quantile regression reduces to LAD regression. The check function is not differentiable at
r=0
. To smooth the objective (
2.3
), one can take either its Moreau envelope or the Moreau envelope of just the nonsmooth part
\frac{1}{2}|r|
. In the former case, elementary calculus shows that the minimum is attained at

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\rho_{q}}(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}r-q\mu,&r\geq q\mu\\
0,&-(1-q)\mu<r<q\mu\\
r+(1-q)\mu,&r\leq-(1-q)\mu\,.\end{cases}

It follows that

M_{\mu\rho_{q}}(r)=\begin{cases}qr-\tfrac{\mu}{2}q^{2},&r\geq q\mu,\\
-(1-q)r-\tfrac{\mu}{2}(1-q)^{2},&r\leq-(1-q)\mu,\\
\tfrac{1}{2\mu}r^{2},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A benefit of replacing
\rho_{q}(r)
by
M_{\mu\rho_{q}}(r)
is that, by definition,
M_{\mu\rho_{q}}(r)
satisfies the Moreau majorization (
4
) at the anchor point
r_{m}
. This majorization can be improved, but it is convenient because it induces the uniform majorization

\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{\mu\rho_{q}}(r_{i})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\rho_{q}(z_{mi})+\frac{1}{2\mu}(r_{i}-z_{mi})^{2}\right],

(9)

where
z_{mi}=\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\rho_{q}}(r_{mi})
, which is an unweighted sum of squares in the residuals
r_{i}=y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
.

Convolution approximation offers yet another avenue for majorization

[
17
,
20
,
26
,
48
,
52
]
. If we select a well-behaved kernel
k(x)\geq 0
with total mass
\int k(x)dx=1
, then the convolution

\displaystyle C_{\mu g}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int g(x)k\Big(\frac{y-x}{\mu}\Big)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu}\int g(y-x)k\Big(\frac{x}{\mu}\Big)dx

approximates
g(y)
as the bandwidth
\mu\downarrow 0
. Given
g(x)=|x|
and the symmetric uniform kernel
k(x)=\frac{1}{2}1_{\{|x|\leq 1\}}
, the necessary integral

\displaystyle C_{\mu|\cdot|}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{-\mu}^{\mu}|y-x|dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\Big[1+\Big(\frac{y}{\mu}\Big)^{2}\Big]&|y|\leq\mu\\
\frac{|y|}{\mu}&|y|>\mu\end{cases}

is straightforward to calculate. This is just the Moreau envelope
M_{\mu|\cdot|}(y)
shifted upward by
\frac{\mu}{2}
. This approximate loss is now optimized via the Moreau majorization

\displaystyle\Big(q-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big)r+\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\mu|\cdot|}(r)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\mu}\big[r-z_{m}+(2q-1)\mu\big]^{2}+c_{m},

(10)

where
c_{m}
is an irrelevant constant and
z_{m}=\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu|\cdot|}(r_{m})
. Although our numerical experiments in quantile estimation feature the convolution-smoothed quantile loss (
10
), the choice (
9
) works equally well. What is more important is the transformation of smoothed quantile regression into a sequence of ordinary least squares problems with shifted responses.

2.4
Sparsity and Low Matrix Rank

The
\ell_{0}
-norm, the rank function, and their corresponding constraint sets are helpful in inducing sparsity and low matrix rank. Directly imposing these nonconvex penalties or constraint sets can lead to intractable optimization problems. A common middle ground is to use convex surrogates, such as the
\ell_{1}
-norm and the nuclear norm, but these induce shrinkage bias and a surplus of false positives in support recovery. The Moreau envelopes of these nonconvex penalties offer an attractive alternative that leverages differentiability and majorization.

The
\ell_{0}
norm has Moreau envelope
[
6
]

\displaystyle M_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\min}\Big[\|\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{0}+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\min_{\nu_{j}}\Big[1_{\{\nu_{j}\neq 0\}}+\frac{1}{2\mu}(\beta_{j}-\nu_{j})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\begin{cases}1,&\frac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\geq\mu\\
\frac{1}{2\mu}\beta_{j}^{2},&\frac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}<\mu\end{cases}.

The corresponding proximal map sends
\beta_{j}
to itself when
\frac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}\geq\mu
and to
0
when
\frac{1}{2}\beta_{j}^{2}<\mu
. The Moreau envelope of the
0/\infty
indicator of the sparsity set
S_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{\beta}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq k\}
is determined by projection onto
S_{k}
. Elementary arguments show that

\displaystyle M_{\mu\delta_{S_{k}}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\Big[\delta_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})+\frac{1}{2\mu}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{j=1}^{p-k}\beta_{(j)}^{2},

where
\beta_{(1)},\ldots,\beta_{(p-k)}
denote the
p-k
smallest
\beta_{j}
in magnitude
[
6
]
. The Moreau majorization defined by equation (
4
) is available in both examples. In general, the Moreau envelope of the
0/\infty
indicator of a set
S
is
\frac{1}{2\mu}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\beta},S)^{2}
, where
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\beta},S)
is the Euclidean distance from
\boldsymbol{\beta}
to
S
. Other nonconvex sparsity inducing penalties
p(\beta)
, such as the SCAD penalty
[
16
]
and the MCP penalty
[
56
]
, also have straightforward Moreau envelopes
[
43
]
.

For matrices, low-rank is often preferred to sparsity in estimation and inference. Let
R_{k}
denote the set of
p\times c
matrices of rank
k
or less. The Moreau envelope of the
0/\infty
indicator of
R_{k}
turns out to be
\frac{1}{2\mu}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{B},R_{k})^{2}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{j>k}\sigma_{j}^{2}
, where the
\sigma_{j}
are the singular values of
\boldsymbol{B}
in descending order. If
\boldsymbol{B}
has singular value decomposition
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
, then the corresponding proximal map takes
\boldsymbol{B}
to
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
retains the

k
largest singular values and sets the rest to zero. This result is just the content of the Eckart-Young theorem. The function
\mathrm{rank}(\boldsymbol{B})
has Moreau envelope
[
23
]

\displaystyle M_{\mu\,\mathrm{rank}}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\sum_{i}\begin{cases}1,&\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq\mu\\
\frac{1}{2\mu}\sigma_{i}^{2},&\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}<\mu\end{cases}

The map
\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\,\mathrm{rank}}(\boldsymbol{B})=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mu}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
retains
\sigma_{j}
when
\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}\geq\mu
and sets it to
0
otherwise. In other words, the proximal operator hard thresholds the singular values. In both cases the Moreau majorization (
4
) is available.

The nuclear norm
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{*}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}

[
45
]
is a widely adopted convex penalty in low-rank estimation. Its proximal map soft thresholds all
\sigma_{i}
by subtracting
\mu
from each, then replacing negative values by
0
, and finally setting
\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\boldsymbol{B})=\boldsymbol{U}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
contains the thresholded values. The Moreau envelope
M_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\boldsymbol{B})
serves as a smooth-approximation to
\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{*}
and benefits from the Moreau majorization (
4
).

2.5
Deweighting Weighted Least Squares

To remove the weights and shift the responses in least squares,
[
21
]
and
[
28
]
suggest a crucial deweighting majorization. At iteration
m
, assume that the weights satisfy
0\leq w_{mi}\leq 1
and that
\mu_{i}
abbreviates the mean
\mu_{i}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
of case
i
. Then

\displaystyle w_{mi}(y_{i}-\mu_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{mi}\bigl[(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})+(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})\bigr]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{mi}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})^{2}+w_{mi}(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle+\;2w_{mi}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{mi}^{2}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})^{2}+w_{mi}(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle+\;2w_{mi}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})+d_{mi}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle w_{mi}^{2}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})^{2}+(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle+\;2w_{mi}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})+d_{mi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[w_{mi}(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})+(\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i})\bigr]^{2}+d_{mi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigl[w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\mu_{mi}-\mu_{i}\bigr]^{2}+d_{mi},

where
d_{mi}=w_{mi}(1-w_{mi})(y_{i}-\mu_{mi})^{2}\geq 0

and
d_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{mi}
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Equality holds, as it should, when
\mu_{i}=\mu_{mi}
. Derivation of the surrogate requires the assumption
0\leq w_{mi}\leq 1
. Because rescaling does not alter the minimum point, one can divide
w_{mi}
by
w_{\max}=\max_{j}w_{mj}
at iteration
m
to ensure
0\leq w_{mi}\leq 1
.

2.6
Quadratic Upper and Lower Bound Principles

The quadratic upper bound principle applies to functions
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
with bounded curvature
[
7
]
. Given that
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is twice differentiable, we seek a matrix
\boldsymbol{H}
satisfying
\boldsymbol{H}\succeq d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
and
\boldsymbol{H}\succ{\bf 0}
in the sense that
\boldsymbol{H}-d^{2}f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is positive semidefinite for all
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
is positive definite. The quadratic bound principle then amounts to the majorization

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})+df(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})+\,(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})^{\top}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}d^{2}f[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}+t(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})](1-t)\,dt\,(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})+df(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}).

The quadratic surrogate is exactly minimized by the update (
2
) with
d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
replaced by
\boldsymbol{H}
. The quadratic lower bound principle involves minorization and subsequent maximization.

The quadratic upper bound principle applies to logistic regression and its generalization to multinomial regression
[
8
,
30
]
. In multinomial regression, items are drawn from
c
categories numbered
1
through
c
. Logistic regression is the special case
c=2
. If
y_{i}
denotes the response of sample
i
, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
denotes a corresponding predictor vector, then the probability
w_{ij}
that
y_{i}=j
is

\displaystyle w_{ij}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{e^{r_{ij}}}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{ik}}},&1\leq j<c\\
\frac{1}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{ik}}},&j=c\end{cases}

where
r_{ij}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
. To estimate the regression coefficient vectors
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
over
n
independent trials, we must find a quadratic lower bound for the log-likelihood

\displaystyle\quad\quad\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\begin{cases}r_{iy_{i}}-\ln(1+\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{ij}}),&1\leq y_{i}<c\\
-\ln(1+\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{ij}}),&y_{i}=c\end{cases}

(11)

Here
\boldsymbol{B}
is the matrix with
j
th column
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}
. It suffices to find a quadratic lower bound for each sample, so we temporarily drop the subscript
i
to simplify notation.

The log-likelihood has directional derivative

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{V}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1_{\{y<c\}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{y}-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{j}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{j}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w})\otimes\boldsymbol{x}]^{\top}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{V})

for the perturbation
\boldsymbol{V}
of
\boldsymbol{B}
with
j
th column
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
. In the Kronecker product representation of
d_{\boldsymbol{V}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})
,
\boldsymbol{y}
is now an indicator vector of length
c-1
with a
1
in entry
j
if the sample belongs to category
j
, and
\boldsymbol{w}
is the weight vector with value
w_{j}=\frac{e^{r_{j}}}{1+\sum_{k=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{k}}}
in entry
j<c
. The quadratic form associated with the second differential
d^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})
is

\displaystyle d^{2}_{\boldsymbol{V}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{j})^{2}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{j}}}+\frac{(\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{j}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{j})^{2}}{(1+\sum_{j=1}^{c-1}e^{r_{j}})^{2}}.

Across all cases
[
8
]
derives the lower bound

\displaystyle d^{2}_{\boldsymbol{V}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{V})^{\top}\left[\boldsymbol{E}\otimes\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\right]\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{V}),

where the
(c-1)\times(c-1)
matrix
\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{1}{c}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top})

has explicit inverse
\boldsymbol{E}^{-1}=2(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top})
.
The full-sample multinomial log-likelihood is then minorized by the quadratic

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}[(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{mi})\otimes\boldsymbol{x}_{i}]^{\top}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{\top}\left[\boldsymbol{E}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\right]\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta}),

where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
. Maximizing the surrogate yields the update

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{m+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{E}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\right]^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{mi})\otimes\boldsymbol{x}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{E}^{-1}\otimes(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\right]\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{w}_{mi})\otimes\boldsymbol{x}_{i},

or in matrix form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{W}_{m})\boldsymbol{E}^{-1},

(12)

so that
\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}=\boldsymbol{B}_{m}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{m+1}
. Here
\boldsymbol{W}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(c-1)}

conveys the weights for the first
c-1
categories and the rows of

\boldsymbol{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(c-1)}
are indicator vectors conveying category membership.
In the special case of logistic regression, the solution reduces to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}+4(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X})^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w}_{m}).

We also consider low-rank multinomial regression driven by the penalized loss
-\frac{1}{n}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{B})+\lambda M_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\boldsymbol{B})
. Applying the minorization (
2.6
) and the Moreau majorization yields the MM surrogate

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits[\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{W}_{m}-\boldsymbol{Y})]^{\top}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2n}\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta})^{\top}\bigl[\boldsymbol{E}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\bigr]\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\bigl\|\boldsymbol{B}-\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\bigr\|_{F}^{2}+c_{m},

whose stationary condition is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{C}_{m})-\left(\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{E}\otimes\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\boldsymbol{I}\right)\mathop{\rm vec}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\Delta}),

with
\boldsymbol{C}_{m}=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{W}_{m})+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}(\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})-\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
. This condition is the same as the two equivalent matrix equations

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{E}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\boldsymbol{\Delta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{m}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\Delta}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{E}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{m}\boldsymbol{E}^{-1}.

The second of these is a Sylvester equation
[
3
]
for the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
. It can be solved by extracting the spectral decompositions
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{E}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
, left multiplying the equation by
\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
, and right multiplying the equation by
\boldsymbol{V}
. If in the result

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{V}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{m}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}

we treat
\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{V}
as a new variable
\boldsymbol{Z}=(z_{ij})
, then this simpler Sylvester equation has the entry-by-entry solution

\displaystyle z_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{C}_{m}\boldsymbol{V})_{ij}\sigma_{2j}}{\frac{1}{n}\sigma_{1i}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\sigma_{2j}},

where
\sigma_{1i}
and
\sigma_{2j}
are the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}
.
Overall, this highly efficient way of updating
\boldsymbol{B}
requires just two spectral decompositions, which can be recycled across all iterations and all values of
\lambda
and
\mu
. Computation of the proximal map
\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu\|\cdot\|_{*}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
must still be performed at each iteration.

2.7
Integration with the Proximal Distance Principle

The proximal distance principle is designed to minimize a loss function
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
subject to
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in C
, where
C
is a nonempty closed set
[
27
,
31
,
32
]
. For instance,
C
could be the sparsity set
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq k\}
. The general idea of the proximal distance method is to approximate the solution by minimizing the penalized loss
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{\beta},C)^{2}
for
\lambda>0
large. The squared Euclidean distance is majorized by the spherical quadratic
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-P_{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|_{2}^{2}
, where
P_{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
denotes the Euclidean projection of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
onto
C
. If
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is an ordinary sum of squares, then the surrogate
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-P_{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|^{2}
remains within this realm. Otherwise, it may be that
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is majorized by an ordinary sum of squares
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
. The overall surrogate
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-P_{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|^{2}
is then also an ordinary sum of squares.

Algorithm 2

Proximal Distance Algorithm

0:

loss function
f
, constraint set
C
, initial point
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
,
annealing rate
\rho>1
, tolerances
\epsilon_{\mathrm{in}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{out}}>0

0:

approximate constrained minimizer
\boldsymbol{\beta}

1:

\boldsymbol{\beta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
,
\lambda\leftarrow\lambda_{0}

2:

while

\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-P_{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|_{2}\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{out}}

do

3:

repeat

4:

\boldsymbol{\beta}\leftarrow\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}}\;g(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}-P_{C}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|_{2}^{2}

until

\text{convergence criterion}<\epsilon_{\mathrm{in}}

\boldsymbol{\beta}

In the proximal distance algorithm, simply setting
\lambda
equal to a large
constant can cause the procedure to converge prematurely and prevent adequate
exploration of parameter space. Although the converged value of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
will
be close to the constraint set
C
, the influence of the loss will be slight.
The remedy is to start with a small value of
\lambda
and gradually increase
it at a slow geometric rate to a final desired level. For a given

\lambda
, we leverage distance majorization to optimize the penalized loss.
Once the convergence criterion is met, we increment
\lambda
and start anew. Algorithm
2
summarizes the process. Our
previous papers
[
27
,
31
]
provide
essential algorithmic details such as annealing schedules and stopping
criteria. In this paper, we take a slightly different approach in dealing
with the Moreau envelope parameter
\mu
governing the smoothed

\ell_{0}
and nuclear norm penalties. Rather than adopting the inner/outer
iteration structure of Algorithm
2
, we either
hold
\mu
fixed or anneal it simultaneously with the iterative
updates.

Sparse quantile regression provides a concrete example of the proximal distance
principle in action. After distance penalization, the smoothed quantile loss
(
10
) is majorized by the surrogate function

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4n\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[y_{i}-z_{mi}+(2q-1)\mu-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}]^{2}

(13)

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-P_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|_{2}^{2}+c_{m}.

The stationary condition is then

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{2n\mu}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\Big)\boldsymbol{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n\mu}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}+\lambda P_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}),

where
\tilde{y}_{i}=y_{i}-z_{mi}+(2q-1)\mu
are the shifted responses.
Let
M=\frac{1}{2n\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{m}=M\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}+\lambda P_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
for brevity. Given the pre-computed spectral
decomposition
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}
of the
p\times p
Gram
matrix, the normal equations have the explicit solution

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}\big(M\boldsymbol{\Lambda}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}_{m}.

Although a spectral decomposition is more expensive to compute than a Cholesky
decomposition, a single decomposition suffices across all values of
\lambda
.
In contrast, the Cholesky decomposition must be recomputed each time
\lambda

changes. When
p\gg n
, we instead work with the spectral decomposition

\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}
of the
n\times n
matrix
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}
.
The Woodbury identity gives

\displaystyle\big(M\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-\frac{M}{\lambda^{2}}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}\Big(\boldsymbol{I}_{n}+\frac{M}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\Big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}

and the update becomes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{b}_{m}-\frac{M}{\lambda^{2}}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Q}\Big(\boldsymbol{I}_{n}+\frac{M}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\Big)^{-1}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{b}_{m}.

Since
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}
is
n\times n
, the spectral decomposition costs
O(n^{3})

rather than
O(p^{3})
, and the matrix–vector products drop from
O(p^{2})
to
O(np)
, yielding substantial savings
in high-dimensional problems.

Sparse quantile regression can also be achieved by adding the Moreau envelope
penalty
\lambda M_{\alpha\|\cdot\|_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
to the loss. (Observe that

\mu
and
\alpha
are distinct smoothing parameters; nothing prevents us from
equating them.) The surrogate function (
13
) remains the same,
provided we substitute the proximal map
\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\alpha\|\cdot\|_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

for the projection
P_{S_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
and the constant
\frac{\lambda}{2\alpha}

for the constant
\frac{\lambda}{2}
. If
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\lambda}
denotes the minimum
point of the penalized loss, then the curve
\lambda\mapsto M_{\alpha\|\cdot\|_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\lambda})
now becomes an object of interest.
This curve should smoothly approximate a jump function with downward jumps
of
1
as
\lambda
increases.

2.8
Acceleration Strategies

Conversion of weighted to ordinary least squares comes at the expense of an increased number of iterations until convergence. This adverse consequence can be mitigated by various acceleration strategies.

Nesterov acceleration

Nesterov acceleration
[
41
]
is an important acceleration technique that is easier to implement than to understand. In practice, one maintains both the current iterate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
and the previous iterate
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m-1}
. The shifted point
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}+\frac{m-1}{m+2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m-1})
is then taken as the base point in majorization-minimization. The next point
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}
is no longer guaranteed to reduce the objective. If the descent property fails, we take the standard precaution of restarting the Nesterov acceleration at
m=1
.

Step-doubling

Step-doubling
[
12
,
50
]
is another
simple yet effective acceleration device.
Let
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
denote the minimizer of the surrogate

g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
. Rather than taking
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
as the next
iterate, step doubling proposes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}+2(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\;=\;2\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}.

It is worth noting that, if the surrogate function is quadratic, then step-doubling never increases the objective. The key distinction between step-doubling and Nesterov is that Nesterov extrapolates before the MM step (
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}=T(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}+\tfrac{m-1}{m+2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m-1}))
), whereas step-doubling extrapolates after the MM step (
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}=2T(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
). Here
T(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is the quadratic MM mapping.

Smoothing Constant Annealing

Replacing nonsmooth loss functions and penalties with their Moreau envelopes produces a smoother optimization problem. Larger values of
\mu
yield smoother approximations at the cost of greater deviation from the original objective. Sending
\mu
to
0
recovers the original function at the cost of increasing computational difficulty. To balance statistical accuracy with computational efficiency, we initialize
\mu
at a large value
\mu_{\max}
to capitalize on rapid early progress and then gradually decrease
\mu
toward zero. When
\mu
is exceedingly small, the curvature of the Moreau envelope near the origin becomes extremely steep, causing the algorithm to make little or no progress. Consequently, we impose a lower bound
\mu_{\min}>0
, below which
\mu
is not allowed to go. The value
\mu_{\min}
governs the statistical properties of our smoothed estimators.

Other popular acceleration strategies include Anderson acceleration
[
1
]
and SQUAREM
[
51
]
. In our experience, both are more complex to implement and less effective on the problems considered here.

2.9
Convergence Theory

As a prelude to establishing the convergence of our MM algorithms, one needs to prove that an optimal point actually exists. This issue is usually tackled in minimization by showing that the objective
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
has compact sublevel sets
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}:f(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq c\}
. This notion is implied by the coerciveness condition
\lim_{\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|\to\infty}f(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\infty
, which in turn is implied by strong convexity. In the absence of strong convexity, one can check that a finite convex function
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is coercive by checking that it is coercive along all nontrivial rays
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}+t\boldsymbol{v},\,t\geq 0\}
emanating from some arbitrarily chosen base point
\boldsymbol{\alpha}

[
37
]
. For instance in quantile regression, the objective
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is convex and

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}+t\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{q}(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}-t\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}).

If
\boldsymbol{X}
has full rank and
\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
, then at least one inner product
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}
does not vanish. The corresponding term
\rho_{q}(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}-t\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})
then tends to
\infty
as
t
tends to
\infty
. Given that all other terms are nonnegative,
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is coercive.

As pointed out earlier, our algorithm maps assume the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}-t_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}),

where the matrix
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})
is positive definite and
t_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in(0,1)
is a step-size multiplier chosen by backtracking. For our MM algorithms, the choice
t_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=1
always decreases the objective.
[
36
]
and
[
54
]
prove the following proposition pertinent to our problems.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose the objective
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
has compact sublevel sets and
\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is continuous and positive definite. If the step size
t_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is selected by Armijo backtracking, then the limit points of the sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}=\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
are stationary points of
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
. Moreover, the set of limit points is compact and connected. If all stationary points are isolated, then the sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
converges to one of them.

Our examples omit backtracking and involve surrogates satisfying the uniform Lipschitz gradient condition

\displaystyle\|\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\delta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{\delta}\|

(14)

for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
,
\boldsymbol{\delta}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
. Proposition 8 of
[
35
]
then offers the following simpler but more precise result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
be a coercive differentiable function majorized by a surrogate satisfying condition (
14
). If
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\infty}
denotes a minimum point of
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
, then the iterates
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
delivered by the corresponding MM algorithm satisfy the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{k})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2L[f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})-f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\infty})].

It follows that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|=0
. Furthermore, when
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is continuously differentiable, any limit point of the sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}
is a stationary point of
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Proposition 12 of
[
35
]
dispenses with the isolated stationary point assumption of Proposition
2.1
and proves convergence when
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is coercive, continuous, and subanalytic and all
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
are continuous,
\mu
-strongly convex, and satisfy condition (
14
) on the compact sublevel set
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}:f(\boldsymbol{\beta})\leq f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})\}
. Virtually all well-behaved functions are subanalytic. For instance, the Moreau envelope of a convex lower-semicontinuous subanalytic function is subanalytic
[
9
]
; the function
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}
is semialgebraic and therefore subanalytic
[
31
]
.

For the convex problems, one can also attack global convergence by invoking monotone operator theory
[
4
]

Proposition 2.3

For a convex problem with
\boldsymbol{H}=d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})
a fixed positive definite matrix, suppose that the set of stationary points is nonempty. Then the MM iterates

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

converge to a minimum point.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let

be

satisfies the Lipschitz condition (

satisfy

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[1-\left(\frac{\mu}{2L}\right)^{2}\right]^{m}[f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})],

\boldsymbol{\alpha}

In many of our examples, the Hessian
d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})=c\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}
for some
c>0
. The constants of Proposition
2.4
satisfy
L=c\sigma_{1}^{2}
and
\mu=c\sigma_{p}^{2}
, where the
\sigma_{i}
are the ordered singular values of
\boldsymbol{X}
. The ratio
\frac{L}{\mu}
is the condition number of
\boldsymbol{X}
relevant to the rate of convergence. Note that
c
disappears in the condition number. In the proximal distance method, we encounter the Hessian
d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})=c\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}+\lambda\boldsymbol{I}
, where the constant
c
no longer cancels in the condition number. Although addition of
\lambda\boldsymbol{I}
improves the condition number pertinent to inner iterations, annealing of
\lambda
towards
\infty
in outer iterations slows overall convergence to the constrained minimum. Multinomial regression replaces the Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}
by the Kronecker product
\left(\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{1}{c}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\right)\otimes\left(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\right)
, whose singular values are products of the eigenvalues of the matrices
\boldsymbol{I}-\frac{1}{c}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}
. Because these are
\frac{1}{c}
and
1
and the
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, respectively, the revised condition number turns out to equal
\frac{cL}{\mu}
.

3
Numerical Experiments

The numerical examples in this section demonstrate the versatility of the MM principle in deriving fast estimation algorithms. We consider quantile regression (ordinary and sparse),
\text{L}_{2}\text{E}
regression (ordinary and isotonic), and multinomial regression (ordinary and reduced-rank). For sparse and low-rank regression, the penalties ignore intercept parameters. All computations were performed on a Mac with an M4 chip
and 32GB of RAM. We primarily employ restarted Nesterov acceleration to speed up convergence of our MM algorithms. For problems involving a smoothing parameter
\mu

\mu
described in Section
3.5
.

3.1
Unpenalized Models

We now compare the performance of our MM algorithms and popular competing algorithms for smoothed quantile regression,
\text{L}_{2}\text{E}

\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(p-1)}

\boldsymbol{X}

(n=10000,\;p=100)
, sparse quantile regression under the smoothed
\ell_{0}
penalty
(n=500,\;p=1000)
, and low-rank multinomial regression
(n=5000,\;p=250,\;c=20)
. The data generation protocol is the same as previously described. We terminate the MM algorithms when
\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|<10^{-3}
.

For multinomial regression, we compare three variants: no acceleration, step-doubling, and Nesterov acceleration with restart. In step-doubling, we skip the first 20 iterations to avoid being stuck at large function values. For the latter two problems, we also consider annealing
\mu
from a large initial value. Specifically,
\mu
decays exponentially from
\mu_{\max}=10.0
to
\mu_{\min}=0.01
over 100 iterations. We also consider the combination of annealing with Nesterov acceleration. Figure
6
aids visualization of two types of trajectories: the objective value (evaluated at
\mu=\mu_{\min}
), and the distance to the final converged solution (
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
or
\boldsymbol{B}^{*}
), obtained by running the algorithm for 10,000 iterations with Nesterov acceleration.

In general, restarted Nesterov acceleration yields a more substantial speedup than step-doubling or annealing. The annealing strategy encourages large steps in early iterations, quickly bringing the iterates into the vicinity of the optimal solution. A combination of annealing and Nesterov acceleration appears to be the most effective strategy when a small smoothing parameter is desired. This synergy leverages both the rapid initial progress of annealing and the sustained impact of restarted Nesterov acceleration.

3.6
Effect of the Smoothing Parameter on Estimation Error

The Moreau envelope provides a smooth approximation to the original loss and penalty functions. A larger
\mu
yields a coarser approximation but reduces computational difficulty. We now investigate how the smoothing parameter
\mu
affects the estimation error of our estimators.

We consider four settings: Huber regression with varying
\mu

(n=10000,\;p=100)
; sparse quantile regression under the smoothed
\ell_{0}
penalty
(n=250,\;p=500,\;q=0.7)
with either varying
\mu
or varying
\alpha
; and low-rank multinomial regression
(n=2000,\;p=50,\;c=5)
with varying
\mu
. The smoothing parameter ranges over 30 exponentially spaced points between
10^{2}
and
10^{-3}
. When we vary
\mu
for sparse quantile regression, we fix
\alpha
at
0.01
. Likewise, when we vary
\alpha
for sparse quantile regression, we fix
\mu
at
0.25
. The data generation protocol remains as previously described. For sparse quantile regression and low-rank multinomial regression, we generate an independent validation set and select the optimal
\lambda
from a grid of 30 points decreasing exponentially from
10
to
10^{-4}
, using quantile loss or negative log-likelihood on the validation set. We report
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{true}}\|
or
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}-\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{true}}\|
as the final estimation error, where
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\text{true}}
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\text{true}}
are the true coefficients used to generate the data.

Fig 7:
Estimation error and computation time for several problems under varying smoothing parameters. Data points are averaged over 50 random replicates, and the error bar denotes
\pm 1
standard deviation.

Figure
7
shows that an overly large
\mu
or
\alpha
leads to gross estimation errors. In general, estimation error decreases as
\mu
and
\alpha
decrease. Interestingly, however, the smallest smoothing constant does not always yield the most accurate estimates. For Huber regression, the lowest estimation error occurs at a small but nonnegligible value of
\mu
(near 1), suggesting the existence of an optimal smoothing level. This phenomenon has been highlighted in recent papers on adaptive Huber regression
[
47
]
and smoothed quantile regression
[
17
]
and can be understood as an
example of the bias–variance tradeoff. Although smoothing the check loss introduces bias in estimating regression coefficients, it reduces variance and improves statistical efficiency, so the resulting estimators may achieve lower mean squared error in finite samples.

For sparse quantile regression, estimation error (under the best
\lambda
) appears to plateau once the smoothing constant becomes sufficiently small. For low-rank multinomial regression, there also appears to be an optimal smoothing level. As the second row of Figure
7
shows, the computation time of our MM algorithms generally decreases as the smoothing constant grows. These observations suggest a simple practical guideline. Rather than driving
\mu
and
\alpha
to near zero, choosing a small but nonnegligible smoothing constant simplifies optimization and yields estimation accuracy competitive with its nonsmooth counterparts.

4
Discussion

The current paper stresses the MM principle, smoothing by Moreau envelopes, and the recycling of matrix decompositions. The MM principle simplifies optimization by: (a) separating the variables of a problem, (b) avoiding large matrix inversions, (c) linearizing a problem, (d) restoring symmetry, (e) dealing with equality and inequality constraints gracefully, and (f) turning a nondifferentiable problem into a smooth problem. Our examples feature quadratic surrogates, by far the most common surrogates and the easiest to implement in practice. Quadratic surrogates are not necessarily parameter separated, and their stationary conditions require solving linear equations. This is where recycling of matrix decompositions comes into play. Cholesky, QR, and spectral decompositions are expensive to extract in high dimensions, so there is strong motivation to employ quadratic surrogates with the same curvature across all iterations. Deweighting and Moreau majorization are two tactics achieving this goal. Although Cholesky decompositions are more likely to suffer from ill conditioning than QR decompositions, they are faster to compute and feature exclusively in our numerical examples.

Many loss and penalty functions are nondifferentiable. Smoothing by Moreau envelopes and other approximation methods removes the kinks and allows the standard rules of calculus to apply in optimization. A Moreau envelope can be majorized by a spherical quadratic with parameters separated. This advantage extends to squared distance-to-set penalties in constrained optimization. These majorizations are not particularly tight, so it often takes many iterations to converge. Fortunately, Nesterov acceleration mitigates most of the drawbacks. Our
numerical examples illustrate the virtues of these tactics. Computational speed is often an order of magnitude better than that delivered by popular competing methods. More subtly, statistical inference is improved by the parsimony imposed by regularization and by outlier flagging.

In any event, it is the combination of tactics that produces the fastest and most reliable algorithms. Techniques such as alternating minimization (block descent and ascent), profile log-likelihoods, penalty constant annealing
[
58
]
, and quasi-Newton methods
[
57
]
have also proved valuable in many settings. Readers should keep in mind that the MM principle can be invoked on the subproblems of alternating minimization. Progress, not perfection, is usually adequate in solving the subproblems. Optimization requires both art and science. Although there is no panacea, overarching themes can guide the construction of good algorithms. Our examples vividly demonstrate the speed improvements achievable through thoughtful algorithm design. There are doubtless many advances yet to be made in accelerating the optimization algorithms so vital to the progress of statistics. Readers who want to replicate our experiments and extend our Julia code can visit the website
https://github.com/qhengncsu/MMDeweighting.jl
.

Deweighting the Sharpest LAD Majorization

In LAD regression, deweighting the sharpest quadratic majorization (
8
)
leads to the surrogate (
7
) derived from the Moreau majorization
(
4
). To prove this assertion, recall that the Moreau surrogate
(
7
) is

g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{i}-z_{mi})^{2},

where
r_{i}=y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
z_{mi}=\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu|\cdot|}(r_{mi})
.
On the other hand, the best quadratic majorization (
8
) in LAD is

g_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{mi}r_{i}^{2}+c_{m},

where
w_{mi}=1
if
|r_{mi}|<\mu
and
w_{mi}=\mu/|r_{mi}|
if
|r_{mi}|\geq\mu
.
In both cases the weights satisfy
0\leq w_{mi}\leq 1
. Deweighting

g_{1}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
yields the new surrogate

g_{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=\frac{1}{2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl[w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}\bigr]^{2}.

When
|r_{mi}|\leq\mu
,

w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\;=\;y_{i}\;=\;y_{i}-\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu|\cdot|}(r_{mi}),

and when
|r_{mi}|>\mu
,

\displaystyle w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}

\displaystyle=y_{i}-(1-w_{mi})(y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle=y_{i}-(1-w_{mi})\,r_{mi}

\displaystyle=y_{i}-(r_{mi}-\mu)

\displaystyle=y_{i}-\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\mu|\cdot|}(r_{mi}).

In both cases,
w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}=y_{i}-z_{mi}
.
It follows that
g_{2}(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})=g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
,
completing the proof.

\text{L}_{2}\text{E}

penalized loss

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta},\tau)

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-1)\times p}
is a matrix that generates the differences of adjacent components of

\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and
\mathbb{R}^{p-1}_{+}
is the
(p-1)
-dimensional nonnegative orthant.
Our double majorization of the
\text{L}_{2}\text{E}

of the set penalty together produces the MM surrogate

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\lambda}{2}\bigl\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\beta}-P_{\mathbb{R}^{p-1}_{+}}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\bigr\|_{2}^{2}+c_{m}.

(17)

\boldsymbol{\beta}
is

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\tau^{3}}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\lambda\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}\right)\boldsymbol{\beta}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\tau^{3}}{n}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}+\lambda\,\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}P_{\mathbb{R}^{p-1}_{+}}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}),

(18)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
is the vector of shifted responses with components

\tilde{y}_{i}=w_{mi}y_{i}+(1-w_{mi})\beta_{mi}
. The spectral decomposition of
\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{D}
is computed once and recycled across all iterations and all values of
\tau
and
\lambda
.
Block descent alternates
the updates of
\tau
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
. The precision parameter
\tau
can be updated
by gradient descent
[
22
]
or an approximate Newton’s method

[
38
]

D.1
Proof of Proposition
2.3

Our attack exploits the inner product
\langle\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle_{\boldsymbol{H}}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\beta}
and corresponding norm
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\beta}}
associated with the positive definite second
differential
\boldsymbol{H}=d^{2}g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta})
. Taking

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
in the majorization

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{\beta})+\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})

(19)

leads to the conclusion

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\,f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\frac{1}{2}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}).

(20)

Now consider the function
g_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
It is convex, achieves its minimum at the stationary point
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
, and
satisfies the analogues of inequalities (
19
) and (
20
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\quad=g_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-g_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\quad\geq\frac{1}{2}\nabla g_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla g_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})].

By symmetry,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\quad\geq\frac{1}{2}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})],

and adding the last two inequalities gives

\displaystyle[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]

\displaystyle\quad\leq[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}).

We are now in a position to prove that the operator

S(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}-2\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
is non-expansive in the
norm
\|\cdot\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|S(\boldsymbol{\beta})-S(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\|_{\boldsymbol{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{H}}-4(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{\top}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]

\displaystyle\qquad+4[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]^{\top}\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|^{2}_{\boldsymbol{H}}.

The related operator

\displaystyle T(\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}S(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})

is
\frac{1}{2}
-averaged with fixed points equal to the stationary points of

f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
. To complete the proof, we note that the sequence

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}=T(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
defined by an averaged operator is known to converge
to a fixed point; see, for example, Proposition 7.5.2 of
[
37
]
or
Corollary 22.20 of
[
5
]
.
∎

D.2
Proof of Proposition
2.4

Existence and uniqueness of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
follow from the strong convexity of
f(\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Because
\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\mathbf{0}
, the

L
-smoothness of
g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha})
gives the quadratic upper bound

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha})-g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha})+\frac{L}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{2}^{2},

which incidentally implies
\mu\leq L
. In view of the strong convexity assumption,
we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|_{2}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})

\displaystyle\geq f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta})

(22)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}.

Therefore,
\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|_{2}\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}
.
Combining (
21
) and (
22
) yields the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) bound

\displaystyle\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu^{2}}{2L}\bigl[f(\boldsymbol{\beta})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\bigr].

We now turn to the MM iterates and take

\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})
.
The PL inequality implies

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1})-f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq g(\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-g(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{L}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})^{\top}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2L^{2}}\cdot L\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4L^{2}}\bigl[f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\bigr],

Subtracting
f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
from both sides and rearranging gives

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m+1})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4L^{2}}\right)\bigl[f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m})-f(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\bigr].

Iteration of this inequality yields the claimed linear convergence.
∎
