The effect of preload on Doppler systolic time in a large emergency department cohort with suspected sepsis
Authors: Sarah Atwi, Jared Nunnally, Kristen Ugale, So Mi Ko, Jaclyn Bond, Kimberly Boozer, Raymond J Bautista, Catalina Torres, Matthew Sedillo, Daniel Lachner-Piza, Tracy Savery, Melissa Hebscher, Joseph K Eibl, Jon-Emile S Kenny, Korbin Haycock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChange in the corrected flow time of the common carotid artery (ccFTΔ) during a preload challenge (PC) is a surrogate for stroke volume change (SVΔ) to infer preload responsiveness (PR).MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study comprising patients with suspected sepsis in a large, publicly funded, academic emergency department; no patient health information is linked to the Doppler assessments. Carotid Doppler spectrograms from a wireless, wearable critical care Doppler ultrasound system are analyzed for ccFTΔ during a PC (either infusion of 300 mL of crystalloid over 3 min or a Trendelenburg maneuver). Comparisons of related outcomes were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm procedure, baseline ccFT discrimination was quantified using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and TTP was estimated using patient-level bootstrap resampling. Background

The duration of mechanical systole is measured as the time from the onset of systole to its offset [1]; this can be obtained from either the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) or from common carotid artery Doppler pulses as they are interchangeable [2]. The duration of mechanical systole is the time that the aortic valve is open and ejecting blood and is a long-established measure of left ventricular function combining elements of stroke volume (SV), afterload and contractility [1]. When afterload and contractility are relatively constant, changing systolic time and stroke volume (SVΔ) are directly proportional. This is true primarily during preload change, for example, during a passive leg raise, Trendelenburg or rapid fluid challenge [3–5]. With this, change in systolic duration in response to preload augmentation helps detect preload responsiveness (PR) – or a significant SVΔ – a resuscitation paradigm that significantly reduces mortality in sepsis [6].

There are many tests and measurement approaches to PR that can be tailored based upon patient phenotypes [7]. In our emergency department (ED), rapid response team (RRT) and sepsis emergency response team (SERT), we employ a recently developed wireless, wearable critical care Doppler ultrasound system that continuously measures the time of systole from the carotid artery corrected for heart rate (i.e., the carotid corrected flow time, ccFT) during preload augmentation as a test for PR [8–15]. Here we present a descriptive analysis of this growing dataset with the following objectives: (1) describe the median ccFTΔ as a measure of PR for over 4100 septic ED patients; (2) describe the trajectory of ccFTΔ classified by responsiveness, cardiac cycle regularity, and unsupervised cohorts; and (3) describe the interaction between baseline absolute ccFT and PR based upon both 4 and 7 ms ccFTΔ thresholds. We hypothesize that a patient’s baseline, static, absolute ccFT will not predict change in ccFT (ccFTΔ) during preload augmentation.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective cohort study comprising over 4367 patients in whom severe sepsis was identified in a large, publicly funded, academic ED in southern California. This dataset includes patients evaluated between January 2023 and May 2026; though clinical data was captured for all patients, for this analysis, no patient health information is linked to the Doppler assessments.

Ethical approval

The study protocol (Pro00085445) was approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board on May 24th, 2026, with a determination of exemption from Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) regulations and a waiver of patient informed consent to analyze de-identified data.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

At our institution, all patients in the ED or general medical floor in whom changes in vital signs or laboratory values trigger a Best Practice Alert (BPA) in the electronic medical record and are identified to have severe sepsis (i.e., the presence of at least two SIRS criteria, an identified or presumed infectious source and end-organ dysfunction) are seen by a dedicated nursing-led rapid response team or sepsis emergency response team (SERT) [14]. As described previously [14, 16, 17], our SERT is composed of RNs with dedicated point of care ultrasound (POCUS) and sepsis resuscitation training. This training begins with POCUS examinations accompanied by an experienced SERT preceptor followed by physician review of at least 10 examinations. The trainee must demonstrate aptitude to pass training and certify competency. Afterwards, SERT RNs maintain competency by exhibiting the POCUS skills with the lead physician (K.H.) instructor yearly. To illustrate the effectiveness of our training program, we conducted a prospective observational study revealing that ED physicians agreed with SERT RN POCUS assessments in virtually all cases [16, 17]. The SERT registered nurse (RN) employs bedside ultrasound and a device to assess PR and/or fluid tolerance [12, 18, 19] including use of the wireless, wearable 4 MHz, continuous wave, F.D.A. cleared critical care ultrasound system (Flosonics Medical, Sudbury, Canada).

Clinical exclusion criteria for using the wearable Doppler ultrasound are known, severe (> 60%), bilateral carotid artery stenosis, inability to cooperate with a hemodynamic intervention (e.g., hyperactive delirium), known severe aortic or mitral valve disease, age less than 18 years or contraindication for augmenting preload.

Doppler system and measures

The wireless wearable Doppler ultrasound is a U.S. F.D.A. cleared critical care ultrasound (CCUS) system that auto-calculates the duration of Doppler systole and its change during preload augmentation. The device is shown in Fig. 1A along with its graphical user interface and features of the spectrogram used to calculate the ccFT. Individual cardiac cycles were excluded if features were not detectable due to signal drop, artifact interference, low signal-to-noise ratio, irregular pulse morphology, or poor usability of derived waveform features. The ccFT is calculated for each cardiac cycle and displayed as shown in Fig. 1 by the equation of Wodey (Eq. 1) [3, 4]; though a recent evaluation also used the equation of Bazett (Eq. 2) [5, 20]. In all subjects the common carotid Doppler pulse was detected by the displayed spectrogram and audible Doppler shift consistent with the common carotid artery. Once obtained, the wearable ultrasound device is adhered in place.

Preload challenge

All patients receive a preload challenge (PC) as either a rapid fluid challenge of 100 mL/min over 2–3 min or Trendelenburg positioning from 30 degrees head up to 10–20 degrees head below the horizontal for at least 90 s; PC type is decided by the treating clinician. The rate and volume of the rapid fluid challenge was derived from a systematic review and meta-analysis [21] and a crystalloid pharmacokinetic study using real-time Doppler ultrasonography as the response measure. In the pharmacokinetic study [22], the authors administered normal saline in 50 mL aliquots over 30 s and found that the optimal delivered volume to detect PR was 250 mL. At our institution, Trendelenburg position is contraindicated at the discretion of the treating clinician. Conditions which preclude this maneuver are when it is not clinically safe to transiently increase intracerebral pressure, if there is concern for venous thrombosis or pulmonary embolus, when there is a significant aspiration risk and/or if there is intra-abdominal hypertension (including a gravid uterus), which can lead to false negative PR results because of impaired abdominal venous return [23].

To determine PR, carotid Doppler spectrograms are analyzed for ccFTΔ, calculated as the difference between cardiac cycles averaged during ‘assessment’ (i.e., RFC or head-down) and before assessment (i.e., ‘baseline’). Baseline windows are selected based on a minimum of 15 cardiac cycles, low coefficient of variation, and high density of cardiac cycles. Assessment windows are selected based on a minimum of 20 cardiac cycles, low coefficient of variation, high density of cardiac cycles, and the largest change with baseline. Maximal quality-qualified windows rather than average windows at arbitrary timepoints were chosen in the assessment period to capture all instances of potential physiological response. Sessions that do not meet the criteria for adequate assessment windows were excluded. We chose 4 and 7 ms ccFTΔ thresholds to detect PR based upon 3 previous studies that used bioreactance [3], left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral from trans-esophageal echocardiography [4] and minimally invasive, auto-calibrated pulse contour analysis [5] as reference standards. These studies reported optimal thresholds between 4 and 7 ms ccFTΔ.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were calculated for patient-level ccFTΔ values for each correction method, including the proportion of patients who exceeded the predefined response thresholds of ≥ 4 ms and ≥ 7 ms. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed and as median [interquartile range (IQR)] when non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Distributional assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspection of the observed distributions; baseline vs. assessment stages were compared using Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Proportions of PR were compared between paired Wodey- and Bazett-corrected values using McNemar’s exact test, with Holm correction applied across the two threshold comparisons.

The relationship between mean baseline ccFT (determined from an adequate assessment window described above) and intervention-associated ccFTΔ was assessed at the patient level using Pearson and Spearman correlations calculated separately for Wodey- and Bazett-corrected ccFT. Individual cardiac cycles were not treated as independent observations. Mean baseline ccFT was calculated from the cardiac cycles within each patient’s baseline assessment window, such that each patient contributed a single baseline ccFT value and a single PR/PUR classification to each threshold/formula ROC analysis. To determine the ability of baseline ccFT to directly discriminate between PR from PUR, we quantified the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) with sensitivity, specificity, and the corresponding baseline ccFT cutoff identified using Youden’s J statistic.

The cohort was divided into regular and irregular. Irregular cardiac cycle rhythm patients were determined based on an automated irregularity detection algorithm. A patient was determined to exhibit irregular beats when more than 25% of beats have a rolling peak-to-peak coefficient of variation above 20%. CcFT measures were compared between patients with regular and irregular cardiac rhythms using two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests. To account for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Holm procedure within the prespecified family of irregular-versus-regular comparisons, with a family-wise α of 0.05. Proportions of PR and PUR between irregular and regular patients were compared using a two-sided two-proportion z-tests, with Holm correction applied across the four formula/threshold comparisons.

To illustrate and characterize population-level physiological response temporal trajectory, the beat-level ccFTΔ trajectories (defined as a cardiac cycle ccFT at that point in PC less the mean baseline window ccFT) across responsive and non-responsive patients (determined for each threshold and correction method) were visualized on a common temporal grid referenced to the beginning of assessment and assigned to 5-second bins up to 120 s. Across-session means and 95% confidence intervals were computed for each bin. Additionally, between-patient variability underlying the population-level trajectories was explored in a supplementary analysis in which cardiac cycles were first averaged within each patient and 5-second time bin. The median and interquartile range of these patient-level values were then calculated across patients within each time bin and displayed.

The first sustained time-to-peak (TTP) in ccFT following PC was estimated by patient-level bootstrap resampling. For each iteration (n = 1,000), patients were resampled with replacement to the original group size, all contributing beats were pooled, binned into 5-second time-elapsed windows referenced to the start of PC, and averaged to produce a bootstrapped mean ccFTΔ trajectory. TTP was defined as the bin center of the first local maximum occurring at least 10 s after intervention onset, where the local peak and the subsequent two bins all remained at ≥ 90% of the local peak value (a sustained plateau of 15 s). A local-peak definition was used to identify the first true response and avoid sensitivity to late-window drift in the trajectory. Median and 95% confidence intervals are reported.

To explore the threshold agnostic trajectories of ccFT response to PC, a k-means clustering approach was used. K-means clustering requires complete, equal-length feature vectors, residual missing values occurring at the leading or trailing edges of otherwise eligible trajectories were filled using the nearest observed value (backward or forward filling, respectively). Therefore, for each patient, beat-level ccFTΔ was linearly interpolated onto a common 0–120 s time grid in 5-second steps, referenced to the start of PC. To limit the influence of the interpolation procedure, sessions flagged as irregular, with fewer than 10 valid beats, less than 50% grid coverage (< 60 s PC duration), or with more than 30% missing values after interpolation were excluded. Remaining missing values were forward and backward filled, and trajectories were smoothed using a Savitzky-Golay filter (15 s smoothing window). K-means clustering was then applied (k = 2 to 8) and evaluated both within-cluster sum-of-squares (elbow) and average silhouette score; the optimal k was selected by elbow inflection and supported by interpretability of physiology. Post-hoc, phenotypes were assigned to each cluster based on their average trajectory shapes. Phenotype peak ccFTΔ and TTP (as previously defined) were estimated by session-level resampling (1,000 iterations) within each cluster.

Results

Study population

A PC with the wearable critical care Doppler ultrasound was attempted in 4367 patients. Of these, an adequate ccFTΔ was completed in 4128 patients (i.e., 95%) (Fig. 2). Across all eligible patients, 1,020,238 cardiac cycles were considered. Of these, 37,301 were excluded, leaving 982,937 cardiac cycles for analysis. Mean length of the entire assessment was 131.6 ± 54 s (baseline: 40.3 ± 31 s, PC: 91.5 ± 41 s). Baseline median (IQR) heart rate was 101.4 bpm (87.8-113.5) bpm and ccFT was 281.7 ms (265.9-297.1) using Wodey and 291.9 ms (270.6-312.4) using Bazett. During PC, heart rate dropped to 100.1 bpm (87.1-112.3), however ccFT increased to 286.9 ms (270.7–303.0, Wodey, p < 0.01) and 298.7 ms (277.6-319.7, Bazett, p < 0.01).

Distribution of ccFTΔ and preload responsiveness

The median ccFTΔ was 7.3 ms (2.5–12.5) with Wodey, significantly lower than Bazett’s ccFTΔ at 9.64 ms (3.6–16.2, p < 0.001). Using Wodey, 51.9% of patients met the responsive threshold of ccFTΔ ≥ 7 ms and 68.6% met the ≥ 4 ms threshold; using Bazett, the corresponding rates were significantly higher at 61.2% and 73.5% (p < 0.0001). The distributions of patient-level ccFTΔ are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Time course of ccFTΔ during preload challenge

For patients identified as PR for both ccFT correction methods and thresholds, the first TTP following PC median (95% CI) was at 27.5 s (17.5–32.5 s) into PC. No sustained rise was observed in unresponsive patients at both thresholds and ccFT correction methods (Fig. 3). While population-level trajectories appear stable, between-patient variability was observed across time bins as illustrated by patient-level median and IQR (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Diagnostic performance of baseline ccFT

Although significant, baseline ccFT was weakly inversely associated with intervention-associated ccFTΔ using both Wodey (Pearson r = − 0.181, Spearman ρ = −0.128, R² = 0.033; both p < 0.0001) and Bazett (Pearson r = − 0.203, Spearman ρ = −0.160, R² = 0.041; both p < 0.0001).

Baseline absolute ccFT discriminated PR from PUR patients only modestly under both correction formulae (AUROC 0.56–0.57 across all four threshold-formula combinations). At the Youden-optimal cutoff, sensitivity ranged 0.59–0.68 and specificity 0.42–0.52 (Wodey ≥ 4 ms: optimal cutoff 283.4 ms, AUROC 0.56; Wodey ≥ 7 ms: 282.9 ms, AUROC 0.56; Bazett ≥ 4 ms: 295.2 ms, AUROC 0.57; Bazett ≥ 7 ms: 300.5 ms, AUROC 0.56).

Irregular cardiac rhythm subgroup analysis

Of the cohort, 247 patients (6.0%) were classified as having irregular cardiac cycles. As expected, the within-session CV was 2.5-fold higher than in regular patients under both correction formulae (p < 0.001), confirming that the two populations are distinct. Irregular patients had similar heart rate at baseline and into PC, while regular patients demonstrated a statistically significant drop that was not clinically significant (i.e., diminished by 1.5 beats per minute). Although absolute ccFT values were lower in irregular patients at both baseline and into PC under both correction formulae, the ccFTΔ did not differ significantly between irregular and regular patients. Among PR patients, the median (95% CI) TTP was 32.5 s (12.5–97.5) using Wodey at both the ≥ 4 ms and ≥ 7 ms thresholds. Using Bazett, the TTP was 27.5 s (12.5–107.5 s) at the ≥ 4 ms threshold and 52.5 s (17.5–97.5 s) at the ≥ 7 ms threshold (Table 1).

Unsupervised trajectory phenotypes

Unsupervised k-means clustering on ccFTΔ trajectories agnostic to thresholding revealed four response phenotypes separately for both methods of ccFT correction in 2,415 eligible patients. The phenotypes consisted of the following: (1) Negative-responders, who exhibited a paradoxical decline into PC (Wodey 8.5%, median peak ccFTΔ = − 8.8 ms; Bazett 12.3%, peak ccFTΔ = − 8.6 ms) (2) Non-responders, who showed a slow rise in ccFT that predominantly did not reach a sustained increase (Wodey 47.7% of the cohort, peak ccFTΔ = 0.53 ms, median TTP = 72.5; Bazett 50.6%, peak ccFTΔ = 2.5 ms, TTP = 70.0 s) (3) Sustained responders, with a steady rise in ccFT (Wodey 39.6%, peak ccFTΔ = 6.4 ms, TTP = 30.0 s; Bazett 33.6%, peak ccFTΔ = 9.1 ms, TTP = 20.0 s) (4) and a small but pronounced High-responder phenotype (Wodey 4.2%, peak ccFTΔ = 27.3 ms, TTP = 50.0 s; Bazett 3.6%, peak ccFTΔ = 36.3 ms, TTP = 50.0 s) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest dataset of ED patients with suspected sepsis receiving continuous assessment of Doppler systolic time during a preload challenge. This analysis includes 4,367 patients encompassing nearly 1 million carotid Doppler cardiac cycles. The use of an innovative wireless, wearable critical care Doppler ultrasound system made this possible, enabling us to characterize the median ccFTΔ, time to first sustained peak ccFTΔ, preload response trajectories, and the interaction between absolute, baseline ccFT and PR. We considered this analysis using two of the most common clinical equations for correcting the duration of systole for cycle length: Wodey and Bazett. Our observations are clinically relevant because we describe the fraction of PR versus PUR in ED patients with suspected sepsis based upon ccFTΔ, gauge how long a PC should be performed, and describe the behaviour and evolution of preload response in the early phases of resuscitation.

The median ccFTΔ for all 4,128 septic ED patients was 7.29 and 9.64 ms by the equations of Wodey and Bazett, respectively. These values are clinically important when we consider what is the best threshold to identify PR early in sepsis care. Barjaktarevic and colleagues evaluated a number of ccFT change thresholds to best detect a 10% change in SV using the equation of Wodey [3]. Notably, a 7 ms ccFTΔ had excellent specificity (i.e., 96%), with adequate sensitivity (i.e., 69%), while a 4 ms ccFTΔ had very good sensitivity and specificity (i.e., 82%, 83%, respectively). Clinically this means that early in ED care, adopting a 7 ms ccFTΔ risks missing some truly responsive patients given the higher false negative rate, while a 4 ms ccFTΔ minimizes false negatives at a slightly higher false positive rate. Because of this, we have previously suggested that a 4 ms ccFTΔ is a clinically appropriate threshold early in care [9], so long as it is safe to administer IV fluid based upon a fluid tolerance assessment [14, 15]. Of additional interest, we found that when 20 or more consecutive cardiac cycles are averaged at baseline and during preload augmentation with trans-esophageal echocardiography as the reference standard [4], a 4 ms ccFTΔ was optimal, with a sensitivity and specificity very close to that reported by Barjaktarevic et al. [3]. Similarly, using Bazett’s equation and invasive arterial line pulse contour analysis as a reference standard, Yang and colleagues recently found that 7 ms ccFTΔ was the optimal threshold to detect a 10% SV augmentation. In our dataset of ED patients with suspected sepsis, a 4 versus 7 ms ccFTΔ would identify 69% and 52% of patients as PR by Wodey and 74% and 61% as PR by Bazett. Early in septic shock, following initial fluid loading, at least two-thirds of patients are expected to be PR [24–26]. Given that our patients are largely assessed whilst fluid naïve and that we generally only assess PR if a patient is fluid tolerant, the 4 ms ccFTΔ by Wodey’s equation is more in-line with clinical expectations.

Whether by Wodey or Bazett equation or based upon 4 or 7 ms ccFTΔ, the time to a sustained peak effect for PR patients was at around 27.5 s into PC, although estimates were later and less precise in irregular patients. This finding has practical relevance for clinicians using Doppler ultrasound to predict PR as it identifies the optimal physiological response time during preload augmentation in most patients. Notably, this temporal window is consistent with previous data where PR was detected during passive leg raising and descending aortic blood flow measured by esophageal Doppler was the reference standard [27]. This concordance strengthens the plausibility that ccFTΔ reflects change in left ventricular output during preload augmentation [3, 4, 28]. Furthermore, given that PUR patients and the non-responder phenotypes did not see a sustained rise in ccFT within the first 60 s, the PC may safely be truncated earlier. However, for irregular rhythm patients, despite similar ccFTΔ proportions, confidence intervals are much larger, implying longer PC observation periods may be required in this group. Collectively, this may streamline clinical workflow and improve dynamic assessment efficiency in busy environments like the ED.

We found a significant yet very weak relationship between baseline, absolute ccFT and PR based on both thresholds and equations. In other words, a static, baseline ccFT alone is insufficient to identify ccFTΔ with clinically actionable accuracy. This finding is counter to other literature, primarily in the elective, anesthesia space, where low absolute ccFT predicts PR [29–33]. We suspect that in septic patients there is a higher percentage of patients with a depressed Starling curve because of acute systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction [34, 35]. Such patients can have low absolute ccFT and/or low absolute SV [36] but remain PUR as previously described [11, 12, 37]. These findings emphasize the importance of performing a dynamic maneuver to elaborate the slope of the Starling curve, prior to administering preload. In other words, measuring ccFT at baseline and then again during preload modification to calculate the change in ccFT is critical to infer PR, as reported by others [3, 5, 38–41].We believe this to be especially important in acutely septic patients because in this context, targeted volume management by dynamic assessments significantly reduced mortality in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized studies [6].

Finally, in an exploratory, hypothesis-generating, sub-analysis using an unsupervised and a threshold agnostic- approach, we describe preload response that may be hidden through a binary classification snapshot based on ccFTΔ thresholds. Both Wodey and Bazett equations independently recovered the same four phenotypes, which likely indicates underlying physiology rather than artifacts of heart rate correction. Indeed, for both equations, the recovered trajectories clustered away from the 4 and 7 ms thresholds and reveal a similar TTP around 20–30 s into PC for the responder phenotypes. Notably the negative-responder phenotype, defined by a paradoxical decline in beat-level ccFTΔ during PC, encompassed a clinically relevant pattern consistent with a fluid-intolerant plateau as previously described [11]. This approach provided a data-driven characterization of early ccFTΔ response patterns in responsive patients; whether these phenotypes are associated with patient-centered outcomes warrants further study. We think that these findings are notable given that the time course and evolution of PR remain under studied [42].

Our study has several limitations of generalizability. First, this is a single center evaluation which introduces selection bias. The patients who screen for sepsis in our hospital may be different in some way (e.g., cardiovascular comorbidities, socioeconomic status, etc.) such that these findings may not generalize. Because this dataset is not linked to demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, illness severity, SOFA score, mortality, etc.), it is difficult to evaluate how well this cohort represents early sepsis resuscitation and the clinical applicability of these findings. However, some of our findings imply that this population does represent a cohort of early severe sepsis and septic shock. For instance, we observed known and reported proportions of PR versus PUR patients [21], time to peak preload effect [27], and rates of arrhythmia/irregular rhythm [43] in these patients. Second, part of our sepsis resuscitation protocol involves nursing-led point of care ultrasound for fluid intolerance [14, 18, 19]. If it appears unsafe to administer IV fluid (i.e., the patient is fluid intolerant) by either physical examination or radiographic or ultrasonographic findings, preload responsiveness testing is often not done because an alternative strategy to augment perfusion is chosen (e.g., vasoactives). Accordingly, this analysis concentrates patients who appear euvolemic or hypovolemic in the ED and may not represent the entire spectrum of early septic pathophysiology. Third, patients with significant neck anatomical abnormalities or those who are delirious and cannot cooperate with a fluid responsiveness assessment are also excluded from carotid Doppler assessments. Fourth, patients received preload augmentation at the discretion of the treating clinician, which at our institution is Trendelenburg maneuver or a rapid fluid challenge, as described above. These are physiologically distinct maneuvers and analyzing them together limits the interpretation of time to peak estimates and trajectories. Fifth, although exploratory, the trajectory clustering is limited by edge-filling for sessions with incomplete coverage of the observation window, potentially introducing artificial flatness in late-window cluster means. Furthermore, this trajectory analysis is inherently descriptive rather than an independent validation of physiological responsiveness because no reference standard for SVΔ was employed. Finally, inclusion in our sepsis protocol begins with a screening alert based upon systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and organ dysfunction. We do not screen patients based upon Sepsis-3 criteria because, in the United States, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services grounds its extraction criteria for bundle completion based upon the older sepsis definition. Screening for sepsis based upon SIRS has diminished specificity (i.e., increased false positive rates) in the ED with the goal of keeping sensitivity high (i.e., mitigating false negatives) because sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction [14]. Thus, a clinically significant fraction of the patients in this analysis likely do not go on to have sepsis based upon either the older or newer Sepsis-3 definitions; because this data is de-identified, we cannot specifically comment on this proportion which is a limitation. Despite these limitations, our investigation has many strengths. To our knowledge this is the largest reported dataset of ED patients with suspected sepsis receiving PR assessments by Doppler ultrasound of any kind; this analysis clarifies ccFTΔ thresholds and ccFT evolution over the course of a PC. These data are important for determining length of PC which streamlines care but also forms hypotheses for forthcoming analyses linking this Doppler data to patient outcome. For instance, does the pattern of ccFTΔ during a PC in the ED associate with mortality or another life-threatening organ dysfunction?

In conclusion, this study comprises nearly 1 million carotid Doppler cardiac cycles obtained from over 4,100 ED patients with suspected sepsis. In these patients, 52% and 69% are judged to be PR on their first assessment based upon 7 ms and 4 ms ccFTΔ thresholds, respectively. The time-to-peak ccFTΔ is in the first 30 s of intervention for most responsive patients in the overall cohort, consistent with passive leg raise data and esophageal Doppler data. Four distinct and heretofore undescribed ccFTΔ trajectories during a PC are reported and may represent distinct preload response patterns during early suspected sepsis resuscitation.
