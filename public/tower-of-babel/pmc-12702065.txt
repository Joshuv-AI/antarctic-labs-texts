Paediatric procedural sedation and or analgesia in the emergency unit; A descriptive study of practice at a Ugandan Referral Hospital
Authors: Anna Meridah Kaguna, John Mark Mayanja Kasumba, Mary Ellen Lyon, Jonathan Byasi, Celine Jacobs, Rachael Parke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPaediatric Procedural Sedation and/ or Analgesia (PPSA) is the recommended standard of practice for children undergoing painful and distressing procedures outside the operating room; however, global practice may vary. In Sub-Saharan Africa, data on the practice of PPSA is lacking. This study aimed to determine the current practice and outcomes of PPSA in the Emergency unit of Mulago National Referral Hospital to inform future standardisation of care.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive study from 1st October to 31st December 2023 in the surgical section of the emergency unit of Mulago National Referral Hospital. Data was collected using specifically designed and pretested observational checklists, documenting demographics, pre-procedure assessment and preparation, intra-procedural and post-procedural care, average sedation scores attained, pain control and adverse events observed. Data was coded using EPI-Data and analysed to describe PPSA practice and outcomes.ResultsWe recruited 183 children undergoing procedures at the surgical section of the emergency unit. Majority were male, 104(56.8 %), and the median age (IQR) was 8 (4–12)years. Most procedures performed were orthopaedic, 149(81.4 %). Only 15(8.2 %) children got both sedation and analgesia, 4(2.2 %) got sedation only while 164(89.6 %) got analgesia only. Propofol was the most used sedative, while lignocaine with paracetamol was the most used analgesia. Most children, 92(50.3 %), had pain scores ≥8, and adverse events occurred in 6(3.3 %), all of whom belonged to the sedation and analgesia group. Anaesthetic officers were the primary providers in 75 % of the sedation-only cases and 46.7 % of the sedation and analgesia group.ConclusionPPSA practice in this Emergency setting deviates from recommended standards with limited monitoring and inadequate pain control. The clustering of adverse events among children receiving both sedation and analgesia underscores safety risks. Safer sedative choices, clear provider roles, local guidelines, and targeted training are needed to improve the safety and quality of PPSA.

Introduction

Paediatric Procedural Sedation and/ or Analgesia (PPSA) entails the provision of medications to induce a state where a patient will tolerate noxious stimuli while maintaining his/her cardio-respiratory function without invasive support and monitoring [1]. Globally, it is the recommended standard of care for all children undergoing painful or distressing procedures outside the operating room. It is an important technique to practice because it helps lessen children’s physical pain, anxiety, psychological trauma and maximizes comfort for the child, which improves the positions of manipulation achieved, gives better results of healing and lessens the need for intra-operative intervention [2].

Multiple international professional organisations have published guidelines to ensure safe practice of PPSA, including the American Academy of Paediatrics (AAP), American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP), American Association of Anaesthesiologists (ASA) and, in Sub-Saharan Africa, the South African Association of Anaesthesiologists (SASA) guidelines [[2], [3], [4]]. These guidelines provide recommendations on drugs of choice, fasting protocols, monitoring, pre-procedural risk assessment, and post-procedure care. Globally, PPSA is often performed by non-anaesthesiologists, including providers in intensive care medicine, emergency medicine, radiology, oncology, gastroenterology, and nursing [5]. However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), PPSA practice outside of the operating theatre remains inconsistent, especially in facilities without established guidelines, dedicated sedation services, or formal provider training [6]. In Uganda, most emergency units across regional referral hospitals are run by medical officers and intern doctors who occasionally give analgesia or sedation during procedures; however, data on practice across the country at the moment is lacking.

At Mulago National Referral Hospital (MNRH), procedural sedation outside the operating room occurs in the emergency, radiology, paediatric acute care, and paediatric intensive care units. The emergency unit serves a diverse patient population, with procedures performed by a range of healthcare workers, including consultants, senior house officers, anaesthetic officers, medical officers, intern doctors, and nurses. Anaesthetic officers in Uganda are mid-level providers trained in anaesthesia, typically through a two- to three-year higher diploma program, who play a key role in delivering perioperative and procedural care, particularly in settings where physician anaesthesiologists are scarce [7].

Given the absence of formal PPSA guidelines, varying levels of provider training, and multiple cadres involved in care, this study aimed to assess current practice patterns and immediate PPSA outcomes among children undergoing procedures in the emergency unit of MNRH.

Methods

We conducted a descriptive study at the surgical side of the emergency unit of MNRHwith prospective data collection from 1st October to 31st December 2023. MNRH is located in Kampala, Uganda and serves as a national referral and teaching hospital, offering specialized treatment to a population of over 44 million [8,9]. It has an official bed capacity of 1790, but often admits over 3,000 patients [9]. The emergency unit comprises surgical and medical wings and receives approximately 48,000 trauma-related cases annually, of which 17 % are children aged 6–17 years and 9 % are under 5 years [10]. This study was restricted to the surgical section of the emergency unit, which primarily handles trauma and surgical emergencies. At the time of the study, there were no existing records documenting the number or frequency of PPSA procedures performed in this setting. Additionally, there were no formally trained PPSA providers, so practice depended solely on the availability and skill of the provider, although aneasthetic officers were available in some cases to provide mid-level anaesthesia assistance.

We included all children aged 0–17 years who underwent procedural sedation and/or analgesia for painful or distressing procedures in the surgical section of the emergency unit of MNRH during the study period. Children who received sedation for acute delirium or psychosis were excluded.

The sample size was determined using the Leslie Kish formula, assuming a 5 % margin of error at a 95 % confidence interval. After accounting for a 10 % non-response rate, the final sample size was calculated at 183 participants. We used consecutive sampling, recruiting all eligible participants who met the inclusion criteria during the study period until the target sample size was reached. Recruitment was halted once the required sample size was achieved.

We used specifically designed and pre-tested checklists that were developed from existing guidelines (AAP, ACEP, SASA) to observe each procedure performed under PPSA from preparation to completion. These checklists captured patient demographics, PPSA indications, pre-procedure assessment and preparation, intra- and post-procedural care, pain and sedation scores, adverse events, and final disposition. This data was collected by trained research assistants who were full-time nurses in the emergency unit. They received training in the use of checklists and the interpretation of pain and sedation scales by the principal investigator. To minimise observer bias, we blinded clinicians to the data collection process, and research assistants did not interfere with clinical care. Participants were recruited consecutively, and data were collected daily until the required sample size was reached.

Data was coded and entered into Epidata software (version 4.6), and analysed using Stata (version 16) with results reported as frequencies and proportions for categorical data and medians with interquartile ranges for continuous data that were not normally distributed. Fisher's exact tests were used where appropriate to determine the probability that categorical independent variables were related to the immediate outcomes among children undergoing procedural sedation and analgesia for painful and distressful procedures. The statistical significance was determined at a p-value <0.05.

Ethical approval was given by the School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (SOMREC) of Makerere University-reference no Mak-SOMREC-2023–636. Proxy-informed consent was obtained from the parents/guardians of the children <8 years of age. Assent was obtained for all children aged 8–17 years.

The majority of procedures performed were orthopaedic, 149(81.4 %), and this included fracture reduction, plaster of paris application, and skin traction for femoral fractures. General surgery procedures included surgical toilet and suture, wound debridement, dressing of burn wounds, and incision and drainage. Ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgical procedures included foreign body removal from the ear and nose. Cardiothoracic procedures primarily involved chest tube insertions. Ophthalmology and oral maxillofacial procedures included eye irrigation and mandibular fracture reduction, respectively.

During the study period, based on the case logs, approximately two PPSA procedures were performed daily and about fourteen (14) to twenty (20) weekly. Analgesia-only administration was the most common approach, and it was provided in 164(89.6 %) of the cases. Only 15(8.2 %) received both sedation and analgesia, while 4(2.2 %) received sedation alone. Pre-procedural assessments were limited; the parameters assessed for included: ASA classification, fasting status, airway assessment, allergies, weight, NPO status, vital signs, medication and surgical history. Full assessments were completed in only 7(3.8 %), with informed consent for PPSA being documented in 40 % of the cases. Intra-procedural practice was equally sub-optimal, with full-time monitoring conducted in only 16 (8.7 %) children. Post-procedural monitoring occurred in just 7 (3.8 %) cases.

The providers of PPSA included anaesthetic officers, nurses, and various surgical specialists. Anaesthetic officers administered sedation in 75 % of sedated cases and were also the primary providers for children who received both sedation and analgesia (46.7 %). Pain control was generally inadequate. Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) scores were assessed continuously throughout the procedure, from initiation to completion, and scoring was done for the average pain score during the procedure was recorded (Fig. 4). A total of 92(50.3 %) recorded NRS scores of ≥8 during the procedure, indicating severe pain. Higher pain scores were predominantly observed among children who received analgesia alone, while those who received both sedation and analgesia demonstrated comparatively lower pain levels. There was no statistically significant association between pain scores and patient age (p = 0.655) or sex (p = 0.167).Fig. 4Average pain scores attained using the Wong-Baker FACES Scale. .Fig. 4

Adverse events were recorded in 3.3 % (6/183) of children, all of whom had received both sedation and analgesia. No adverse events occurred among children who received sedation only or analgesia only. In a subgroup analysis of the sedation and analgesia group, 6 children (40 %) experienced adverse events. Based on the pre-set classification, there were two mild adverse events, including transient hypoxia and vomiting, and two moderate adverse events, which included bradycardia and prolonged hypoxia requiring oxygen supplementation. Two children developed severe adverse events, where they progressed to general anaesthesia with persistent hypotension, necessitating intensive care unit (ICU) admission for cardio-respiratory support and monitoring. Among the six events, four (66.7 %) occurred when anaesthetic officers were the PPSA providers, while one was overseen by an orthopaedic officer and the other by a nurse. No deaths occurred during the study period.

Discussion

Paediatric procedural sedation and or analgesia (PPSA) is the recommended standard of care for children undergoing painful procedures outside the operating theatre [2], and prioritising how it is done is important in improving paediatric emergency care. To our knowledge, this study is the first to analyse and provide data on PPSA in this unit. It highlights critical gaps in the PPSA practices in a busy emergency unit of a tertiary referral hospital in Uganda. This data provides a picture of the current paediatric procedural pain management at the unit, and we hope that findings from this study can inform future standardization of care.

Our study reveals that majority of children received basic analgesia alone, with only a small fraction receiving both sedation and analgesia, despite undergoing painful procedures. This approach likely contributed to the high incidence of perceived severe pain, with over 50 % of children scoring ≥8 on the Numeric Rating Scale during procedures. These findings align with a South African audit where procedural pain was reported as the most undertreated cause of paediatric pain [11]. In contrast, studies from high-income settings have reported notable improvements in PPSA practices even among neonates, underscoring inequities in global paediatric pain management [12]. The suboptimal pain control observed in our study setting may be partially attributed to knowledge gaps regarding the appropriate use of local anaesthetics. This is supported by prior research conducted in the same emergency unit, which revealed a limited understanding of local anaesthetic use among non-anaesthesia providers, despite it being the most commonly employed form of analgesia during the study period [13].

The overall adverse event rate in our study was low, a finding comparable to prior studies from Pakistan, South Africa, and the UK, which reported rates below 5 % [[14], [15], [16]]. These findings suggest that PPSA can be a safe practice even in low- and middle-income countries when performed with appropriate care.

However, it is notable that, in our setting, all recorded adverse events occurred among children who received both sedation and analgesia. The clustering of adverse events exclusively in this group is of particular concern, with an observed rate of 40 %. The limited use of pre-, intra-, and post-procedure monitoring, as well as incomplete pre-sedation assessments and preparation, likely contributed to this higher rate. This underscores the critical importance of structured guidelines, trained personnel, and appropriate equipment to ensure safe sedation and analgesia practices among children.

The most commonly used sedative was propofol, and its use was most likely driven by the anaesthetic officers, who were the primary PPSA providers among sedated cases. The predominance of propofol in this study is noteworthy and raises concerns. While the ACEP supports the use of propofol for procedural sedation, its administration necessitates advanced training and vigilant monitoring owing to its narrow therapeutic index and the risk of rapidly progressing to deep sedation or general anaesthesia, as reported in some of the children in our study [3,4]. This practice diverges from international trends, where ketamine is more commonly utilised for paediatric procedural sedation due to its favourable safety profile [1,[17], [18], [19]].

A unique finding in our context is the distribution of PPSA providers. Anaesthetic officers were the principal providers of sedation, a practice rarely seen in other emergency units globally, where sedation is often performed by non-anaesthesia providers [[20], [21], [22]]. This study could not establish why they provided the most sedation; however, given their skill set, they were most likely to be called upon where sedation was needed. Their involvement may represent an opportunity to leverage existing skill sets to support safe PPSA in emergency units in Uganda. Additionally, the involvement of nurses in providing sedation is noteworthy and reflects a growing role that can be explored as it aligns with literature supporting the safety of nurse-led sedation protocols under structured supervision [23].

Our study could not fully determine why some children received both sedation and analgesia, while others received only analgesia or none at all, despite undergoing procedures of similar nature and intensity. Notably, some children who underwent procedures without any form of procedural sedation or analgesia were excluded as they did not meet the study’s inclusion criteria. However, their exclusion highlights further inconsistencies in the delivery of procedural pain management and likely leads to an underestimation of the true burden of untreated procedural pain in this setting. The reasons for this variability remain unclear and likely multifactorial. Potential explanations include the absence of trained providers during certain shifts, time constraints, clinical perceptions of whether sedation or analgesia was necessary or safe, concerns about patient instability, or resource limitations. These hypotheses underscore the need for further investigation to understand practice patterns and inform targeted quality improvement interventions and guideline development.

Lastly, the study identifies a substantial gap in the standardisation of PPSA. The absence of institutional guidelines, formal training programs, and documentation systems contributes to inconsistent and sometimes unsafe practices. Incorporating existing PPSA frameworks, such as those from ACEP or SASA and contextualising them for local implementation may be a feasible first step in improving practice.

To our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind to establish the practice of PPSA at an emergency unit in Uganda, and it will be pivotal in defining policy and drawing guidelines for PPSA practice in our setting. The data was collected prospectively by trained research assistants, and blinding the clinical team limited bias.

Limitations of this study include the study being a single-centre study conducted at the emergency unit of MNRH, where institutional and administrative issues may have affected the results, thus affecting their generalisation to emergency units in other settings. The study did not document the exact doses, timing, or routes of medications, which could have limited a comprehensive report of PPSA drug use in this setting (and in addition we note that ketamine providing both sedation and analgesia makes some of the results difficult to intepret). Pain scores were assigned by proxy using the Wong-Baker FACES scale, which may have led to over- or under-estimation. Additionally, data collection was done by in-unit nurses, introducing a potential for observer bias despite their prior training. The study did not explore time-of-day variations, sedation provider decision-making processes, or conduct post-discharge follow-up. Nonetheless, this study offers important insight into the realities of PPSA practice in a high-volume, low-resource emergency setting.

Conclusion

This study reveals that PPSA practice in this setting diverges significantly from recommended standards, with most children receiving only basic analgesia and limited monitoring. Although the overall adverse event rate was low, they all occurred among children who received both sedation and analgesia, raising important safety concerns. These findings highlight the need to prioritise safer sedative choices, clear provider roles, and consistent monitoring standards. Developing local guidelines and targeted training will be essential to improve the safety and quality of PPSA.
