Effects of proactive or synchronous anchorage preparation of posterior teeth during anterior en masse retraction with clear aligners: a three-dimensional finite element study
Authors: Ruomei Li, Wenxuan Shi, Yixin Li, Zhiyu Zhu, Ningjuan Ouyang, Yufeng Duan, Peng Mei, Ting Jiang, Xuehui Chen, Luyao Qu, Ziduan Ding, Chengxiao Liu, Zitong Yang, Yu Jin, Cheng Zhu, Dian Jing, Lunguo Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe increasing use of clear aligners for anterior retraction following first premolar extraction has highlighted undesirable movements of posterior teeth, impeding efficacy and predictability. Although the anchorage preparation of posterior teeth has been proposed to counteract this effect, the optimal methods and extent remain undefined. Effective anchorage preparation strategies to mitigate undesirable movements of posterior teeth during anterior en masse retraction were investigated in this study.MethodologyA finite element study based on a maxillary first premolar extraction model was conducted to examine posterior tooth movement with different anchorage preparation methods: (1) without any anchorage preparation (control); (2) proactive anchorage preparation (PAP), where 1°, 2°, and 4° distal tipping of posterior teeth were set prior to anterior tooth retraction; and (3) synchronous anchorage preparation (SAP), where 1°, 2°, or 4° distal tipping of posterior teeth was processed with anterior tooth retraction.ResultsCompared with the control group, the PAP group exhibited a similar tooth displacement pattern (P > 0.1). SAP effectively controlled mesial tipping when applied at 1.245° for U5, 1.386° for U6, and 2.706° for U7, whereas the optimal resistance to mesial movement required 1.258° (U5), 2.148° (U6), and 2.976° (U7) of SAP, respectively. A 1.25° SAP for U6 successfully prevented lingual torque. However, mesial rotation of the posterior teeth, vertical displacement, torque, and transverse displacement of U5 and U7 remained difficult to control despite SAP adjustments.ConclusionCompared with PAP, SAP demonstrated superior effectiveness in controlling mesial tipping and displacement, while PAP showed better performance in managing mesial rotation and transverse movements. For optimal anchorage control during SAP implementation, clinicians should pay particular attention to posterior tooth mesial rotation as well as U7 extrusion and buccal torque development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-025-00604-z.Keywords: Clear aligners, Tooth extraction, Finite element analysis, Anchorage preparation, Anterior en masse retraction

Background

Clear aligner therapy (CAT), a highly regarded and rapidly evolving orthodontic treatment modality, is favored because of its superior aesthetics, comfort, short chair time, and favorable compatibility with maintaining dental hygiene and periodontal health [1, 2]. The theoretical underpinnings of CAT can be traced back to Kesling’s pioneering work in 1945 [3], with subsequent pivotal contributions from Nahoum [4], Ponitz [5], McNamara [6], and other investigators [7] collectively shaping the fundamental principles of contemporary CAT. Numerous clinical endeavors and biomechanical investigations have enhanced the understanding of CAT after its widespread application. This technique was originally developed to address mild to moderate anterior crowding [8, 9]. Understanding of and advancements in material properties, biomechanics, materials science technology, biomechanical modeling, and sophisticated staging algorithms have progressively expanded its application to more complex malocclusions. However, the biomechanical challenge of anterior en masse retraction remains a persistent clinical concern [10, 11].

While CAT is clinically feasible for postextraction anterior retraction, practitioners frequently encounter the “roller coaster” phenomenon during space closure [12, 13]. This phenomenon is characterized by anterior tooth extrusion with lingual inclination, posterior tooth mesial inclination, intrusion in the middle of the dental arch, and extrusion at the ends of clear aligners (CAs) [11]. Comprehensive studies have documented multiple undesirable movements in maxillary first molars, including mesial tipping [14], mesial displacement [15], buccal torque, and intrusion of the mesiobuccal cusp [16]. Such complex tooth movements often necessitate midcourse correction or conversion to fixed appliances, ultimately prolonging treatment duration and increasing the risk of periodontal complications.

The underlying cause of these challenges can be traced to the material properties of CAs, which are typically fabricated from viscoelastic polymer materials. Comparative analysis revealed that the elastic modulus of these materials was approximately 300 times lower than that of stainless steel and 20 times lower than that of nickel-titanium archwires [17, 18]. During retraction, when CAs undergo contraction, they exhibit significant deformation. CAs embracing stress levels of 0.5–1 MPa might be sufficient for tipping movements, but they fall short of controlling root movements. As the treatment time continues, the CAs undergo stress relaxation and creep; then, it becomes difficult to provide sufficient and constant force, and the tooth usually deviates from the desired track. Once the “roller coaster” effect occurs, repositioning posterior teeth, particularly the first molars, to their proper positions becomes exceptionally challenging, and the inclination away from the extraction site is often underrepresented [19, 20]. While mini-implants can provide counteractive forces to mitigate the “roller coaster” effect, they do not offer a complete solution.

To fundamentally address this clinical challenge, a comprehensive understanding of the biomechanics and efficacy of anterior tooth retraction is essential [21, 22]. Current clinical approaches predominantly focus on anchorage preparation, involving either anterior or posterior teeth. As demonstrated by Liu et al. [23], overtreatment with respect to torque control of anterior teeth can prevent side effects. The concept of “anchorage preparation”, which was first proposed by Tweed in 1966, remains a cornerstone in preventing mesial tipping and extrusion of molars during anterior retraction. In contemporary clear aligner therapy, two primary molar anchorage preparation techniques are widely adopted [24]: proactive preparation (achieving 5° molar distal inclination prior to anterior retraction) and synchronous preparation (implementing 2° per step molar distal inclination during anterior retraction). While these approaches have shown clinical efficacy in managing this condition, their fundamental biomechanical mechanisms and comparative effectiveness remain to be fully elucidated.

Three-dimensional (3D) finite element modeling (FEM) is a powerful technique for simulating complex craniofacial dental systems in orthodontics [25]. This technique enables precise analysis of the stress distribution, strain patterns, and initial tooth displacement upon force application [26]. In previous studies, researchers have utilized mainly thermos-manufactured aligners with elastic moduli of approximately 500 MPa [27]. In contrast, direct 3D printing of aligners represents an innovative advancement, offering enhanced precision in appliance design—including customizable borders, thickness, and undercuts—while eliminating potential errors associated with traditional cast fabrication. Despite these advantages, finite element analyses of directly 3D-printed aligners remain limited in the literature. Dental clear aligner (DCA) material, a resin specifically formulated for direct 3D printing with a Young’s modulus of 990 MPa [28], was employed in this study. An extraction model was developed to accurately simulate clinical conditions during direct 3D-printed aligner therapy.

The aim of this study was to investigate tooth displacement patterns and stress distribution during en masse anterior retraction in a first premolar extraction model and to compare the biomechanical effects of proactive anchorage preparation (PAP) and synchronous anchorage preparation (SAP) on posterior teeth to determine the optimal anchorage preparation method and degree.

Materials and methods

Finite element model establishment

A three-dimensional (3D) finite element (FE) model of the maxillary arch with first premolar extraction was constructed from a single clinical case. A 30-year-old male (skeletal class I, angle class II malocclusion) who met the following inclusion criteria was selected from Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital: (1) permanent dentition with normal crown morphology; (2) absence of dental crowding; (3) normal anterior torque; (4) symmetrical arch form with a standard curve of Spee (5) absence of periodontal disease; and (6) absence of systemic diseases that could alter bone metabolism. The mesiodistal angulation of the upper second premolar (U5), upper first molar (U6), and upper second molar (U7) were 80.0°, 84.3° and 88.9°, respectively— all within the normal range [29]. Cephalometric values were ANB 3.89°, MP-SN 36.98°, U1-SN 109.05°, and IMPA 96.02° (Fig. S1).

CBCT data (0.25 mm isotropic voxel, 90 kV, 10 mA, 12-inch FOV) and intraoral scan data (Trios 3, 3Shape, Denmark) were acquired after obtaining informed consent. The two datasets were superimposed using mutual information-based rigid registration in Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Belgium) to create a maxillary arch containing tooth and alveolar bone. Geomagic Studio 2016 (3D Systems, USA) was used for surface optimization, including noise reduction and artifact removal, to generate a high-fidelity dental model. Afterward, the first premolars were digitally extracted.

Crown-specific attachments were algorithmically matched and positioned on the dental model on the basis of dimensional analysis. The periodontal ligament model was modeled as a 0.30 mm uniform solid layer created by the outward normal offset of the root surface. The attachments and clear aligner were constructed according to available products. For the aligner model, the target teeth were designed to move according to the experimental protocol, and the aligner was modeled by uniformly expanding the crown surface outward by 0.75 mm to generate shell elements. The gingival portion of the aligner was then removed along the gingival margin (the boundary between the crown and root), ultimately producing a clinically applicable clear aligner model.

Establishment of the coordinate system and observation

For every tooth, a local coordinate system was embedded at its center in stage 0 of the respective group; thus, all displacements quoted are relative to that group-specific baseline. The three-dimensional displacement data from all the models were systematically analyzed along the x (sagittal), y (transverse), and z (vertical) axes, with a comprehensive evaluation of the displacement patterns and Von Mises stress distribution in the upper second premolar (U5), first molar (U6), and second molar (U7), respectively. The x-axis represents the displacement in the mesial-distal (sagittal) direction on the model. Negative (-) values indicate the distal direction, and positive (+) values indicate the mesial direction. The y-axis represents the displacement in the buccal-palatal (transverse) direction on the model. Negative (-) values indicate the palatal direction, and positive (+) values indicate the buccal direction. The z-axis represents the displacement in the occlusal-gingival (vertical) direction of the model. Negative (-) values indicate the occlusal direction, and positive (+) values indicate the gingival direction (Fig. 1B).

Tooth-movement protocol

The therapeutic goal for all groups was to close the extraction space. The retraction force was introduced by activating 0.20 mm of aligner deformation in the lingual direction of the anterior segment (canine-to-canine). In this study, three primary space closure groups were categorized according to their anchorage preparation approaches (Fig. 1): (1) a control group without any anchorage preparation; (2) an experimental group with proactive anchorage preparation (PAP, involving 1°, 2°, and 4° distal tipping of posterior teeth) prior to anterior tooth retraction; and (3) an experimental group with synchronous anchorage preparation (posterior distal tipping of 1°, 2° or 4° was generated simultaneously with 0.20 mm anterior retraction per stage) during anterior tooth retraction (Fig. 1C).

Statistical analysis

Prism (version 10.1.2; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for the statistical analyses. Descriptive analysis and linear regression were employed to fit and calculate the optimal degree of posterior tooth distal tipping for anchorage preparation.

To evaluate the mesial-distal (sagittal) and tipping movement tendencies of the posterior teeth, the buccal and mesial views of the posterior teeth are presented in Fig. 3. Mesial movement was consistently observed in the PAP groups (Fig. 3A). In the SAP groups, as the SAP increased from 0° to 4°, the posterior teeth gradually transitioned from mesial to distal movement. The precise transition points from mesial to distal movement for U5, U6, and U7 were identified at SAPs of 1.258°, 2.148°, and 2.976°, respectively (Fig. 3A; Table 4).

Significant mesial tipping was evident in the PAP groups (Fig. 3B). In the SAP groups, when the SAP was small, the posterior teeth exhibited mesial tipping. The shift from mesial to distal tipping for U5, U6, and U7 occurred at SAPs of 1.245°, 1.386°, and 2.706°, respectively (Fig. 3B). These findings highlighted the critical role of the SAP in modulating the direction of tooth movement and tipping behavior.

With respect to U5, the stress distribution was characterized by a greater magnitude at the crown and a smaller magnitude at the root in the control and SAP of the 2° and 4° groups. However, when the SAP = 1°, the stress distribution became more uniform. This finding suggested a transition from a tipping force to a more generalized bodily movement (Fig. 3D). When the SAP = 1°, the stress was distributed uniformly for U6. Regardless of the SAP value, it was difficult for U7 to achieve an even distribution of stress.

The results above indicate that an increase in the PAP had a minimal effect on the mesial displacement and tipping tendency of posterior teeth during anterior tooth retraction (Fig. 3F). However, as the SAP increased, the mesial displacement or tipping of the posterior teeth is significantly decreased and even transitioned to distal mesial displacement or tipping, suggesting that the SAP played a regulatory role in controlling the displacement and tipping movement tendency and stress distribution of the posterior teeth. Compared with U5 and U6, U7 required a larger SAP to resist the forces of mesial tipping and mesial displacement (Fig. 3G).

Amount of vertical displacement and moment of torque

To evaluate the gingival–occlusal (vertical) movement and the moment of torque, mesial views of the posterior teeth are presented in Fig. 4C. In the PAP groups, U5 exhibited intrusive movement, whereas U6 and U7 demonstrated extrusive displacement (Fig. 4A). In the SAP groups, as the anchorage preparation increased from 0° to 4°, three distinct patterns emerged: (1) progressive intrusion of U5; (2) transition from extrusion to intrusion of U6; and (3) maximum extrusion magnitude in U7. Notably, U6 achieved a neutral vertical position at a specific SAP of 0.5887° (Table 4).

In the PAP groups, U5 displayed buccal torque, whereas U6 and U7 exhibited lingual torque tendencies (Fig. 4B, E). However, in the SAP groups, U6 transitioned from palatal to buccal torque at a SAP of 1.25°. U5 and U7 demonstrated enhanced buccal torque and lingual torque, respectively (Fig. 4B, F).

At a SAP ≤ 2°, the occlusal surfaces of U5 and U6 carried lower vertical stress compared with the PAP group, whereas U7 experienced increased force concentrated mesially, which might be related to the active anchorage of the SAP; at SAP = 4°, the vertical stress increased markedly on the occlusal surfaces of U5, U6 and U7 (Fig. 4D).

These findings underscored the dynamic influence of the SAP on vertical displacement and torque behavior in posterior teeth. As the SAP increased, U5 and U6 exhibited greater intrusion and buccal torque compared with those in the PAP group. It was difficult to limit the unwanted extrusion and lingual torque of U7 (Fig. 4E, F).

In the PAP groups, U5 exhibited buccal movement, whereas U6 and U7 exhibited palatal movement (Fig. 5A). U5 and U7 in the SAP groups displayed increasing buccal or palatal displacement from SAPs of 0° to 4° (Fig. 5C). The transition from palatal to buccal movement for U6 occurred at an SAP of 1.226° (Fig. 5A).

With respect to rotation, in the PAP groups, U5, U6, and U7 demonstrated mesial rotation (Fig. 5B). In the SAP groups, U5, U6, and U7 sustained mesial rotation, which was greater than that in the PAP groups (Fig. 5B).

The stress distribution diagram revealed that in both the PAP and SAP groups, U5 had the highest stress distribution, whereas U6 experienced relatively lower stress. Compared with PAP, at SAP = 1°, stress in U5 and U7 was localized near the buccal cusp, whereas U6 had a uniform stress distribution; at SAP = 4°, stress shifted to the lingual cusp in U5 and U6 and to the labial surface in U7 (Fig. 5D).

In the SAP groups, U5 and U6 exhibited greater buccal movement and mesial rotation. When the SAP angle was set to an appropriate value, the movement of U6 was optimized. However, the control over U7 remained suboptimal in both the PAP and SAP groups (Fig. 5E-F).

Discussion

With the increasing number of cases involving anterior retraction following first premolar extraction using clear aligners, the “roller coaster” effect has been observed to compromise treatment outcomes and prolong treatment duration. In the present investigation, the groups without anchorage preparation (control) exemplified a typical moderate anchorage extraction case. This scenario was characterized by first molars exhibiting mesial tipping and mesial displacement, lingual torque and displacement, rotation, and extrusion. According to our current understanding, the tooth extraction site of the aligner is not under support; therefore, when retraction occurs, it is prone to deformation. This issue is exacerbated by the uncontrolled tipping movement of both anterior and posterior teeth, especially when bodily movement is the intended treatment design. Investigations have demonstrated that with the utilization of mini-implants as anchorage devices, molar mesial tipping remains a persistent complication, which may inadvertently intensify the severity of this phenomenon [15]. Moreover, different types of attachment did not affect the movement of the first molar. In clinical practice, orthodontic professionals typically prefer using a two-step movement strategy (commonly referred to as the “frog-jump” model) rather than en masse retraction when performing tooth retraction. The drawback is that this approach requires more steps to retract anterior teeth [13, 30]. Jin et al. [31] enhanced the rigidity of the posterior segment of the aligners, which rendered the force closer to the central resistance of the tooth. Anchorage preparation of the posterior teeth has been proposed as a potential solution to counteract this effect. There remains a lack of consensus regarding the optimal methods and the extent of anchorage preparation needed. The aim of the present study is to address this gap by investigating the optimal anchorage strategies to mitigate the roller coaster effect and enhance the predictability of orthodontic treatment in such cases. The aligners tested herein, with a Young’s modulus of 990 MPa, fell within the range reported for conventional thermoformed aligners (528–1000 MPa) [14, 16, 31–37]. Uniformly thick and edge-trimmed to the digital margin, they generated smoother crown-level force systems—distinct from currently used thermoformed aligners but closely resembling 3D-printed aligners—offering an early quantitative benchmark for the rapidly emerging, more precise 3D-printed aligner technology.

Mesial tipping of the posterior teeth is the most undesirable, as uprighting molars distally is relatively complex and challenging. Previous research has demonstrated that the first molars exhibit a mesial inclination of 5.86°±3.51° and mesial displacement of 2.26 ± 1.58 mm compared with the predicted outcomes [38]. Compared with the fixed appliance treatment, U6 and U7 exhibit 3.6° and 7.0° more mesial tipping, respectively [39]. U5 has the most severe mesial tipping among the posterior teeth [33]. Such mesial tipping and mesial displacement can have significant clinical implications, including loss of interproximal contacts, development of abnormal occlusal contacts, and inefficient use of extraction space. To resist the mesial tipping of the posterior teeth, Jiang et al. [40] proposed anterior tooth intrusion during retraction. A 6.6° proactive distal crown tipping of posterior teeth might counteract unwanted crown tipping [38, 41]. Our results further indicated that proactive anchorage preparation exhibited limited efficacy in mitigating the associated side effects compared with the control. Wang et al. [42] demonstrated that synchronous anchorage preparation of 2.8° distal tipping of U5 can achieve bodily movement of U5 and reduce mesial tipping of U6. Yang et al. [34] reported that synchronous anchorage preparation of 2° could resist mesial tipping, lingual tipping and intrusion of mandibular molars. In the present study, we demonstrated that incorporating synchronous anchorage preparations of 1.245°, 1.386°, and 2.706° into U5, U6, and U7, respectively, could effectively prevent mesial tipping of the posterior teeth, offering a promising solution to the clinical challenges shown in Table S1.

Unwanted transverse displacement and torque may dampen the maxillary arch width and posterior overjet. Our findings revealed that the maxillary molars exhibited lingual torque and displacement of the molars and buccal torque and displacement of U5. As calculated in this study, approximately 1.2–1.3° of synchronous anchorage preparation of U6 could resist the tendency of lingual torque and displacement of the crown. It was difficult to adjust the SAP of U5 or U7 to counteract their unwanted transverse movement and torque. Under the same anchorage preparation values, the PAP demonstrated better control over the transverse displacement and torque movements of U5 and U7, but its control over U6 was inferior to that of the SAP. Notably, when we adopted the aforementioned SAP values to counteract the mesial tipping of posterior teeth, U5 would exhibit 2.4 times the buccal torque and 3.3 times the buccal displacement, whereas U7 would exhibit 4.8 times the lingual torque and 12.6 times the palatal displacement (Table S1). Therefore, additional overcorrection needs to be designed to compensate for these movements.

In terms of vertical displacement, Xia et al. [43] reported that the second premolar and the first molar show a tendency to intrude, whereas the second molar tends to extrude. Researchers have reported the efficacy of extraction using the Align Technology G6 protocol. Ren et al. [15] reported 0.45 mm greater intrusion of U6 than predicted. Dai et al. [44] reported that mesial and distal buccal cusps intrude more than predicted by 0.61 ± 0.89 mm and 0.01 ± 0.91 mm, respectively. Our FEA indicated that U6 presented more extrusion than predicted. When the synchronous anchorage preparation reached 0.6°, U6 remained stationary in the vertical direction. Notably, if one aimed to adopt an SAP of 1.2–1.4° to prevent mesial tipping and palatal displacement of U6, U6 would exhibit more intrusion than predicted (Table S1). It was comparably difficult to control the vertical displacement of U5 and U7 by anchorage preparation. Therefore, it might be necessary to apply heavy occlusal contact of U7 to counteract unwanted movements.

Overall, the selected SAP activation produced a more evenly distributed sagittal stress pattern for U5, U6, and U7, reduced the vertical load on U6 and rendered its transverse stress field more uniform. All groups in this study consistently exhibited certain degrees of mesial rotation, and anchorage preparation of the posterior teeth did little to counteract this movement. These findings suggested that additional methods, such as enhanced torque control or the use of auxiliary anchorage devices, might be necessary to effectively resist mesial rotation. Clinically, mesial rotation of U6 is often witnessed in extraction cases; fortunately, the efficacy of derotation of U6 was approximately 75%, which is highly predicted [45].

In terms of limitations, the model utilized in this study was derived from an individual case and might not comprehensively represent diverse conditions, such as variations in tooth morphology, periodontal status, and other clinical factors. These features could limit the generalizability of the findings to broader patient populations. Further studies involving a more diverse range of cases are recommended to validate and enhance the applicability of the results. The assumption of homogeneous alveolar bone is computationally efficient and has been extensively employed in analogous investigations [23, 31–34, 36]; this simplification inherently disregards the distinct biomechanical properties between cortical and trabecular bone. Subsequent research incorporating micro-CT-derived bone stratification would be valuable for precisely characterizing these biomechanical discrepancies. In this study, while we analyzed the initial loading conditions, certain critical factors, such as long-term interactions between the aligner and the tooth and material fatigue, were not considered. The single-step distal tipping of molars of 1°, 2°, and 4° was adopted, but achieving 4° single-step tooth tipping is currently challenging. Additionally, as the SAP increases from 0° to 4°, the anterior teeth tend to experience bodily retraction. However, whether this phenomenon reduces the actual retraction capacity of the anterior teeth remains to be investigated. In this study, the U1-SN angle was set at 101.0°. Kang et al. [36] suggested that the proclination of incisors could influence the movement of both anterior and posterior teeth. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the optimal synchronous anchorage preparation in relation to varying degrees of incisor proclination, which could provide valuable insights for anterior tooth retraction.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The tooth movement patterns observed with proactive anchorage preparation (PAP) closely resemble those of the control group without additional anchorage preparation. In contrast, synchronous anchorage preparation (SAP) demonstrates superior efficacy in controlling mesial tipping and mesial displacement. Notably, the PAP group exhibits minimal posterior tooth rotation and transverse displacement.

The tooth movement patterns observed with proactive anchorage preparation (PAP) closely resemble those of the control group without additional anchorage preparation. In contrast, synchronous anchorage preparation (SAP) demonstrates superior efficacy in controlling mesial tipping and mesial displacement. Notably, the PAP group exhibits minimal posterior tooth rotation and transverse displacement.

When the optimal SAP is implemented to counteract the mesial tipping of posterior teeth, clinicians should closely monitor the mesial rotation of posterior teeth, as well as the extrusion and buccal torque of the upper second molar (U7).

When the optimal SAP is implemented to counteract the mesial tipping of posterior teeth, clinicians should closely monitor the mesial rotation of posterior teeth, as well as the extrusion and buccal torque of the upper second molar (U7).

In the SAP groups, controlling the mesial rotation of posterior teeth remains a challenge. Additionally, managing the vertical and transverse movements, as well as the torque of the upper second premolar (U5) and U7, is difficult.
