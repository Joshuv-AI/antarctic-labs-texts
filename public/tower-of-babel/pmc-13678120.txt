Outcomes of Simultaneous Three- or Four-Horizontal Muscle Surgery for Very Large-Angle Convergence Insufficiency-Type and Basic-Type Exotropia
Authors: Ayaka Yagasaki, Teiji Yagasaki, Kazuo Ichikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe investigated the outcomes of simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscle surgery for very large-angle exotropia (> 60 prism diopters [Δ]).Subjects and MethodsThis retrospective study included 24 patients (16 with convergence insufficiency-type and 8 with basic-type exotropia). The deviation angle was measured using the alternate prism and cover test, and stereopsis was evaluated. Patients were classified into three groups (F: ≤ 60 arcsec (“), C: 60”<, and N: nil stereoacuity). A successful motor outcome was defined as ≤ 10Δ of orthotropia. Pre- and postoperative results were compared.ResultsMean preoperative exodeviation at distance and near were 75.2 ± 13.0Δ and 89.2 ± 18.0Δ, respectively. Mean age at surgery was 47.8 ± 16.5 years, and mean follow-up period was 3.6 ± 4.2 years. Thirteen and 11 patients underwent four- and three-muscle surgeries, respectively. The mean distant and near deviations at 3 months postoperatively and at the final visit were 5.3 ± 10.4Δ and 8.4 ± 9.5Δ, 5.5 ± 8.6Δ and 9.4 ± 9.0Δ, respectively. No differences were found in the near and distant deviations between the two time points. The motor success rates at both time points were 63%. Stereopsis at the final visit improved significantly from F:4%, C:38%, and N:58% before surgery to F:38%, C:50% and N:13% at the final visit (P = 0.002, McNemar–Bowker test).ConclusionsThree- or four-horizontal muscle surgeries for very large-angle exotropia are effective in achieving favorable motor outcomes and stereopsis.Keywords: large-angle exotropia, three- or four-horizontal muscle surgery, motor outcome, sensory outcome, stereopsis

Introduction

Success rates for exotropia greater than 50Δ with two-muscle surgery are low.1,2 However, several studies of simultaneous three- or four- horizontal rectus muscle surgery for very large‑angle exotropia exceeding 50Δ have reported relatively higher motor success rates of 42–85.4%.3–9 However, standard guidelines and recommended surgical dosages for simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscles surgery for very large-angle exotropia are limited.4,7,8 Therefore, consensus on surgical methods for exotropia ≥ 50Δ has not been established.

Although another goal of strabismus surgery is to improve binocularity, few reports have described outcomes of binocularity after simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscle surgery.4,6,7 Although those three studies compared fine stereopsis outcomes before surgery and at the final visit, no comparisons of coarse stereopsis or the absence of stereopsis have been reported.4,6,7

By comparing decisions regarding surgical procedures and dosages for very large-angle exotropia between our report and those in previous reports, we investigated the effects of simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscle surgery for very large-angle exotropia on motor outcomes, the dose–response ratio, and sensory outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Chukyo Eye Clinic (approval no. 20250811101). The study protocol and data collection procedures complied with all local laws, and informed consent was obtained from all patients after all study details were explained.

The medical records of patients with primary very-large-angle exotropia of 60Δ or greater who underwent three- or four-horizontal muscle surgery at Yagasaki Eye Clinic from 2014 to September 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Exclusion criteria included a history of strabismus surgery; paralytic, restrictive, or pattern strabismus; or coexisting ocular diseases other than refractive errors and amblyopia.

The angle of deviation was measured using the alternate prism cover test with single loose prisms ranging from 1Δ-50Δ at 5 m and 33 cm, with all patients wearing their full corrective glasses. The 50Δ prism was routinely placed in front of one eye, and another prism of increasing power was placed before the other eye until eye movements were neutralized.

Based on the definition of convergence insufficiency (CI)-type exotropia as having a near-distance disparity (NDD) of ≥ 10Δ, 16 patients were classified as CI-type exotropia and the remaining 8 as having basic-type exotropia. No patients with divergence excess-type exotropia were included. The surgical dose was calculated as the objective strabismus angle (distance deviation plus 5Δ) divided by 3Δ/mm. For patients with basic-type exotropia of ≤ 75Δ, bilateral lateral rectus recession (BLRrec) plus unilateral medial rectus resection (UMRres) with equal surgical doses on the three muscles was selected. For patients with CI-type exotropia of ≤ 75Δ, bilateral medial rectus resection (BMRres) plus unilateral lateral rectus recession (ULRrec) with equal surgical doses on the three muscles was selected. For patients with basic-type exotropia of > 75Δ, BLRrec and BMRres with equal surgical doses on the four muscles were selected. For patients with CI-type exotropia > 75Δ, BLRrec and BMRres were selected with the BMRes dose 2.0 mm larger than the BLRrec dose.

Binocular function and stereopsis were evaluated using the Bogolini striate lens test and the RANDOT Stereotests.10,11 Stereopsis was recorded as nil in cases with unmeasurable stereopsis, and these values were evaluated 10,000“ for proper statistical assessment.12 All patients were grouped into F group (fine stereopsis: ≤ 60 arcsec (”)), C group (coarse stereopsis: 60 “<, 3000” ≤), and N group (nil stereoacuity).13,14 Results were evaluated at 1 week and 3 months after surgery and at the last follow-up visit. A successful motor outcome was defined as horizontal esotropia or exodeviations of ≤ 10Δ.

Collected data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft; Redmond, WA, USA), and statistical analyses were performed with StutPlus 2 (AnalystSoft Inc, Brandon, FL, USA) and Mac Toukeikaiseki Ver. 3.0 for Macintosh (ESUMI Co., Tokyo, Japan). Statistical descriptions are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for numerical variables and as the frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. A dependent sample t-test or an independent sample test was used to compare continuous variables with a normal distribution, and the Wilcoxon matched pairs test or the Mann–Whitney U-test was used to analyze data that were not normally distributed. For continuous variables, the mean postoperative exodeviation angles and mean dose–response ratios at distance and near were compared among the three groups; when overall differences were significant, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using the Scheffé test. In addition, the McNemar and McNemar–Bowker tests were used to assess the association between qualitative variables before and after surgery. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 24 patients (13 males and 11 females) were included in this study. As variables before surgery, the mean best-corrected log MAR visual acuities were 0.06 ± 0.05 in the right eye and 0.04 ± 0.10 in the left eye. The mean cycloplegic refractive errors spherical equivalent were −2.27 ± 3.13 diopters (D) in the right eye and −1.94 ± 2.48 D in the left eye. The mean age at surgery was 47.8 ± 16.5 years. In total, 11 patients had intermittent exotropia, and 13 had constant exotropia. The mean preoperative exodeviation at distance and at near were 75.2 ± 13.0Δ and 89.2 ± 18.0Δ, respectively. The mean preoperative exodeviation at near was significantly larger than that at distance (P < 0.001), and the mean NDD was 14.0 ± 9.5Δ. According to the angle at distant and NDD, four-muscle surgeries were performed in 13 patients (BLRec and BMRes), and three-muscle surgeries were performed in 11 patients (BLRec and ULRes: 8 patients, BMRes and ULRec: 3 patients). The mean total surgery amount was 26.5 ± 4.4 mm (range, 20–40 mm). The mean age at surgery was 47.8 ± 16.5 years, and the median and the interquartile range of postoperative follow-up periods were 2 years and 3.5 years (1–4.5 years).

Table 1 shows a comparison of motor outcomes 1 week after surgery, 3 months after surgery, and the final visit. The mean angles of exodeviation for distance at 1 week after surgery, 3 months after surgery, and last visit were −6.7 ± 7.4Δ, 5.3 ± 10.5Δ, and 5.5 ± 8.6Δ, respectively, and those for near were −2.0 ± 10.1Δ, 8.4 ± 9.5Δ and 9.4 ± 9.0Δ, respectively. One-way ANOVA showed significant differences in the three mean angles of postoperative exodeviation at distant (P < 0.001) and near (P < 0.001). In both postoperative angles at distant and at near, although angles between 3 months after surgery and at the last visit were not different (at distant; P = 0.998, and at near; P = 0.932), angles between 1 week after surgery and 3 months after surgery, and 1 week after surgery and the last visit were significantly different (at distant; P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, at near; P = 0.002 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Table 1).

The mean dose–response ratios for distance at 1 week after surgery, 3 months after surgery, and last visit were 3.10 ± 0.39Δ/mm, 2.64 ± 0.42Δ/mm and 2.62 ± 0.40Δ/mm, respectively, and the dose–response ratios for near at 1 week after surgery, 3 months after surgery, and last visit were 3.45 ± 0.61Δ/mm, 3.04 ± 0.50Δ/mm and 3.00 ± 0.50Δ/mm, respectively. Although one-way ANOVA showed significant differences in the three dose–response ratios at distant (P < 0.001), no significant differences in those three dose–response ratios at near were found (P = 0.082). In both dose–response ratios at distant and at near, although angles between 3 months after surgery and at the last visit were not different (at distant; P = 0.980, at near: P = 0.959), angles at distance between 1 week after surgery and 3 months after surgery and between 1 week after surgery and at the last visit were significantly different (P = 0.003 and P = 0.001, respectively), and angles at near 1 week after surgery and at the last visit were significantly different (P = 0.036) (Table 1). Successful motor outcomes were obtained in 75.0% at 1 week after surgery, in 62.5% at 3 months after surgery, and in 66.8% at the last visit. In contrast, the McNemar–Bowker test demonstrated no differences among the three successful motor outcomes (P = 0.688) (Table 1). The NDDs were 14.0 ± 11.3Δ before surgery, 4.7 ± 7.6Δ at 1 week after surgery, 3.8 ± 5.2Δ at 3 months after surgery, and 4.0 ± 7.2Δ at the last visit. The one-way ANOVA showed significant differences among the mean NDDs (P < 0.001). Although the Scheffé test revealed significant differences before and after surgery (P = 0.002 at 1 week after surgery, P = 0.001 at 3 months after surgery, and P = 0.001 at the last visit), no significant differences throughout the postoperative period were found (P = 0.983: at 1 week after surgery vs, at 3 months after surgery, P = 0.991: at 1 week after surgery vs at the final visit, and P = 1.000; at 3 months after surgery vs at the final visit, respectively). However, the Wilcoxon matched-pairs test revealed significantly larger angles at near than at distant throughout the postoperative period (P = 0.014: at 1 week after surgery, P = 0.017: at 3 months after surgery, P = 0.025: at the final visit).

All 11 patients (46%) showing preoperative positive binocular response at distant kept a positive binocular response at both distant and near throughout the postoperative period. The positive binocular response at both distant and near sites in 13 patients, showing preoperative negative binocular response at distant sites, improved in 6 patients at 1 week after surgery, 9 patients at 3 months after surgery, and 11 patients at the last visit. Finally, 22 patients (92%) showed positive binocular response both at distant and at near.

The mean values of stereopsis (log arcsec: mean ± SD) were 2568″ (3.4 ± 0.9) before surgery, 1301″ (3.1 ± 0.9) at 1 week after surgery, 398″ (2.6 ± 1.0) at 3 months after surgery, and 198″ (2.3 ± 1.0) at the last visit. Although no differences were observed in stereopsis before surgery and at 1 week after surgery, at 3 months after surgery and at the last visit (P = 0.749 and P = 0.733, respectively), significant differences were observed in stereopsis before surgery and at 3 months after surgery, at 1 week after surgery and at the last visit (P = 0.001 and P = 0.030, respectively) (Table 2). Figure 2 shows the relationship between stereopsis outcomes and surgery. The frequency of stereopsis improved from F:4%, C:38%, and N:58% before surgery to F:25%, C:50%, and N:25% at 3 months after surgery; and F:38%, C:50%, and N:13% at the final visit (P = 0.016, P = 0.001, respectively). Thus, a positive relationship between the grade of stereopsis and surgery was found (P = 0.002). In the comparisons of stereopsis outcomes between constant exotropia (CXT: n = 13 patients) and intermittent exotropia (IXT: n = 11 patients), no patients with CX and 10 patients (91%) showed stereopsis before surgery. Although only 5 patients with CX (38%) showed recovery of stereopsis at 1 week after surgery, 10 patients with CX (77%) showed significant recovery of stereopsis at the final visit (P = 0.025). The mean stereopsis also improved from 3553″ (3.6 ± 0.5) at 1 week after surgery to 289″ (2.5 ± 0.9) at the last visit (P = 0.008), which was not different from the mean stereopsis in patients with IXT at the last visit (126″ (2.1 ± 0.7), P = 0.304). Regardless of the type of exotropia, stereopsis recovery did not occur soon after surgery. However, sufficient time guaranteed significant recovery of stereopsis after three- or four-horizontal muscle surgeries, even in cases with very large-angle exotropia of more than 60Δ.

Discussion

As two traditional horizontal muscle surgeries were insufficient to correct very large-angle exotropia ≥ 60Δ completely,1 large (8–9 mm) BLRrec or unilateral supermaximal R&R (10–14 mm) have been reported as other options for two-muscle surgeries.15,16 Although severe symptomatic postoperative eye movement deficit was not observed after both procedures, the safety of both procedures has not been established. Therefore, one or two additional muscle surgeries are necessary to obtain full correction of such a very large-angle exotropia. As options for very large-angle exotropia, two-stage horizontal muscle surgery or simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscle surgeries should be selected.3–8

Most reference tables are largely designed for two muscle surgeries, and a few reports described the standard guidelines and recommended surgical dosage for simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscle surgeries.4,5,8 A surgical approach by Lau et al was maximal BLRrec of 9 mm for a correction of 50Δ and 1 mm UMRres for every 5Δ of exotropia exceeding 50Δ.4 However, they did not consider NDD in the decision of surgical dosage and adopted a surgical approach with minimal UMRres. Moreover, all the patients underwent three-muscle surgery, and their surgical approach was not suitable for four-muscle surgery. Nabie et al reported the outcomes and dose–response ratio of surgery on three muscles in very large-angle exotropia.8 Their surgical methods were BLRrec and UMRres in the non-dominant eye. Their surgical doses appear to be effective in achieving a successful motor outcome of 84.5% and an effective dose–response ratio of 2.5 ± 0.3Δ/mm. However, all the patients underwent three-muscle surgery, and their surgical approach was also unsuitable for four-muscle surgery.

To our knowledge, only three studies on multiple four-muscle surgery have been published.3–5 In a report by Currie et al, although their motor success rate was 77%, they did not describe the guidelines for surgical dosage, and 25 of 26 patients had an adjustable suture technique.3 In a report by Raiyawa et al, their high motor success rates were 69% at the last follow-up and 75% at the follow-up at 2 years.4 However, their procedures were bilateral lateral rectus recession ranging from 4–11 mm plus unilateral medial rectus recession ranging from 5–6 mm for three-muscle surgery, or bilateral lateral rectus recession ranging from 2–11 mm with bilateral medial rectus recession ranging from 4.5–6 mm for four-muscle surgery. We cannot understand their selection of all horizontal rectus recession. If their medial rectus recessions are medial rectus resections, they did not describe the guidelines for surgical dosage, and 32 of 36 patients had an adjustable suture technique.4 Therefore, it is also difficult to compare the effectiveness of our guidelines for surgical dosage with their reports. Chen et al described the guidelines for surgical dosage in detail.5 However, 18 of 47 patients had an adjustable suture technique, and 29 patients had traditional sutures. Moreover, their choice of three vs four muscle surgery was occasionally tailored to surgeon preference with the goal of undercorrection, and their outcomes of four-muscle surgery depended on mixed procedures of adjustable suture technique and traditional suture technique. In their report, successful motor outcomes were obtained in 75.0% at 1 week after surgery, in 62.5% at 3 months after surgery, and in 66.8% at the last visit without adjustable suture technique, which were similar to successful motor rate with adjustable suture surgeries in a report by Chen et al5 In our report, the mean angles of exodeviation for distance and near at 1 week after surgery were −6.7 ± 7.4Δ and −2.0 ± 10.1Δ and 18 of 24 patients (75%) were overcorrected within 10Δ of small-angle esotropia. We believe that the small angle of overcorrection 1 week after surgery was the reason for the highly successful motor outcomes in our study without the adjustable suture technique. As one of the factors affecting successful motor rate, they pointed out that rates of successful alignment and overcorrection decreased, and rates of undercorrection increased as drift occurred over the postoperative period.5

Although the mean response to four muscle surgery ranged from 2.5 ± 0.5Δ/mm at the 2-month follow-up visit to 2.4 ± 0.6Δ/mm at the final visit, the response to three muscle surgery decreased from 2.3 ± 0.5Δ/mm at the 2-month follow-up visit to 1.7 ± 0.8Δ/mm at the final visit. In contrast, our study demonstrated no differences in the mean dose–response ratios for distance and near at 3 months after surgery and at the final visit. We believe that the considerations of NDD in the decision of surgical dosage may not affect the dose–response ratios for distance and near over the postoperative period.

A few previous studies have reported the outcome of the binocular response in very large-angle exotropia.3,6,7 Currie et al reported that binocularity was restored in 32% undergoing two-, three-, and four-muscle horizontal recti surgery with adjustable sutures to correct large-angle exotropia.3 However, because details of binocularity have not been explained, it is difficult to compare the results with ours. Excluding this report, only two studies regarding stereopsis outcome remain.6,7 In a report by Raiyawa et al, bifoveal stereopsis (≤ 60″) was detected in 4.3% preoperatively and 30.4% at the final visit.6 However, no results for gross or coarse stereopsis have been described, and it may be difficult to compare with our sensory outcomes. In our study, 11 patients showing a preoperatively positive binocular response at distant maintained a positive binocular response both at distant and near throughout the postoperative period. Additionally, 11 of 13 patients showing a preoperative negative binocular response at distant improved their positive binocular response both at distant and at near during the last visit. Finally, 22 patients (92%) reported a positive binocular response both at distant and near. The remaining two patients who reported a negative binocular response at the last visit had a history of astigmatic or hyperopic anisometropic amblyopia. In our study, the mean values of stereopsis (log arcsec: mean ± SD) significantly improved from 2568″ (3.4 ± 0.9) before surgery to 198″ (2.3 ± 1.0) at the last visit. In addition, the frequency of stereopsis after surgery significantly improved (P = 0.002, the McNemar–Bowker test). In a report by Yang et al,7 surgical success rate, defined as postoperative deviation within 8Δ and stereoacuity <60″, was 30%, similar to that in our study. However, Wu et al stated that patients with constant exotropia reported worse surgical sensory outcomes in both fine and gross stereopsis when compared with patients with intermittent exotropia.17 Because our patients were mixed, with 11 intermittent-type patients reporting preoperatively positive binocular response and 13 constant-type patients reporting preoperatively negative binocular response, and 21 patients (88%) reported measurable stereopsis at the final visit, we believe that our surgical success may be rather ideal.

Our study has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Second, the definition of the NDD of ≥ 10Δ or < 10Δ to differentiate CI-type exotropia from basic-type exotropia is usually applied for medium- and large-angle (20–45Δ) exotropia, and it is unclear whether this definition is appropriate for very large-angle exotropia. However, our results of significant decreases in the NDD demonstrate the validity of our surgical procedures and dosages based on the NDD of ≥ 10Δ or < 10Δ. Third, the onset of exotropia was based on patient history, which can be subject to recall bias, and it was difficult to distinguish the intermittent phase from the constant phase to restore binocular response and stereopsis.

Conclusion

Simultaneous three- or four-horizontal muscle surgeries for very large-angle convergence insufficiency-type and basic-type exotropia more than 60Δ are effective in achieving favorable motor outcomes and better stereopsis. Therefore, this procedure can avoid secondary surgery and is less stressful for patients.
