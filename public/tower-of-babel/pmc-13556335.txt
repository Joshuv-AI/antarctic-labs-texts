Enteroviruses antagonize the host restriction factor ABL2 via proteasomal degradation to facilitate viral replication
Authors: Mengran Yuan, Xiaorong Qiao, Hua Wang, Nuo Chen, Yan Wang, Yuhan Li, Xiaoxiang Zhou, Zengjun Ji, Hongxing Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnteroviruses, including Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), are significant human pathogens that cause severe diseases, such as viral myocarditis, pancreatitis, and encephalitis. ABL proto-oncogene 2 (ABL2), a non-receptor tyrosine-protein kinase, regulates diverse physiological processes and participates in virus infection; however, its role in enterovirus infection remains uncharacterized. Here, we demonstrate a novel host-virus interaction: enteroviruses degrade ABL2 via the ubiquitin-proteasome system through their non-structural protein 2B. Furthermore, ABL2 functions as an antiviral restriction factor during enterovirus infection, specifically inhibiting the early stages of viral replication. Mechanistically, ABL2 directly interacts with RAC1, a Rho family GTPase, and downregulates RAC1 protein levels, thereby suppressing RAC1-dependent activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In summary, our study reveals a post-translational mechanism by which enteroviruses evade host antiviral defenses, providing a rationale for therapeutic development against enteroviral diseases.Keywords: Enterovirus, Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), 2B, ABL2, RAC1

Introduction

Enteroviruses are single-stranded, non-enveloped RNA viruses belonging to the Picornaviridae family, which include over 300 identified serotypes capable of causing various acute and chronic diseases (Chi et al., 2024). Among these, Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) is particularly noted for its ability to damage heart tissue (Dong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025b). CVB3 accounts for 25%–27% of viral myocarditis and dilated cardiomyopathy cases in children and young adults, representing a significant public health burden (Fung et al., 2016; Kang et al., 2025). The enterovirus genome (∼7.4 kb) encodes a single polyprotein, which is cleaved by viral non-structural proteases (2A and 3C) into mature structural (VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4) and non-structural (2A, 2B, 2C, 3A, 3B, 3C, and 3D) proteins (Badorff et al., 1999). Viral proteins such as 2B, 2C, and 3A modulate vesicular trafficking and alter cellular membrane permeability to establish replication-friendly organelles (Mu et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2025). The 3D protein functions as an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), catalyzing the synthesis of a negative-strand RNA intermediate that serves as the template for genomic RNA replication (Li et al., 2025c). Proteases 2A and 3C are indispensable for viral polyprotein processing and the production of infectious virions, executing the majority of proteolytic cleavage events throughout the replication cycle (Andrews et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023).

Host cells deploy “restriction factors” to counter viral invasion. For CVB3, known restriction factors include TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) and ARE/poly(U)-binding/degradation factor 1 (AUF1), which inhibit viral RNA stability or translation (Wong et al., 2013; Fung et al., 2015). However, the role of non-receptor tyrosine kinases in CVB3 infection remains unclear. ABL2, also known as ABL-related gene (Arg), is a member of the Abelson family that regulates actin cytoskeleton reorganization via Rho family GTPases (Zandy et al., 2007; Zandy and Pendergast, 2008). Recent studies have shown that ABL2 participates in virus infection: ABL2 facilitates coronavirus entry via virus-cell fusion (Strobelt et al., 2022) and supports human papillomavirus 16 (HPV16) endocytosis (Bannach et al., 2020). In contrast, its role in enterovirus infections has not been investigated.

RAC1, a highly conserved Rho GTPase, is a key upstream activator of the PI3K/AKT pathway and is required for the replication of multiple RNA viruses (Mustafa et al., 2013; Ravi et al., 2021). Hepatitis C virus (HCV) has been shown to require RAC1 activation for its intracellular replication (Tan et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2014). Similarly, rotavirus, Newcastle disease virus, and Coxsackievirus A9 have been reported to utilize RAC1 activity to support their replication (Huttunen et al., 2014; Tan et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2018). However, its role in CVB3 infection remains unclear.

This study investigated the interaction between ABL2 and enterovirus, focusing on CVB3. CVB3 infection led to a significant reduction in ABL2 expression levels, primarily mediated by the viral protein 2B, which degraded ABL2 via the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS). Further investigations revealed that ABL2, as a host restriction factor, inhibited CVB replication and proliferation by interfering with the early stages of viral replication, downregulating RAC1 expression, and suppressing the AKT/PI3K signaling pathway. The degradation of ABL2 by CVB3 2B protein counteracted this antiviral effect, thereby promoting viral replication. These findings reveal a sophisticated host-virus arms race, in which a host restriction factor is actively targeted and degraded by viral proteins as an immune evasion strategy.

Results

Enterovirus infection downregulates ABL2 protein levels in a MOI- and time-dependent manner

HeLa cells were infected with CVB3 at various multiplicities of infection (MOI), and cellular proteins were analyzed using Western blotting. ABL2 protein levels decreased in a time-dependent manner, with the most significant reduction observed at 12 h post-infection (hpi) (Fig. 1A). At an MOI of 5, ABL2 levels began to decrease at 3 hpi and continued to decline over time, with the most pronounced reduction observed at 7 hpi (Fig. 1B). At an MOI of 10, ABL2 expression was significantly reduced at 3, 5, and 7 hpi compared to that in the mock-infected group (Fig. 1C).

These results demonstrated that ABL2 expression was downregulated in CVB3-infected HeLa cells in an MOI- and time-dependent manner, with the most consistent and significant changes observed at an MOI of 5. Based on these results, an MOI of 5 was selected for CVB3 infection in the subsequent experiments.

To further validate the effect of CVB3 on ABL2 expression, HL-1 cells were infected with CVB3 at an MOI of 50. Western blot analysis showed that ABL2 expression began to decrease at 12 hpi and continued to decline progressively over time (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, HeLa cells infected with CVB5 at an MOI of 1 for up to 24 h or at an MOI of 5 for up to 7 h exhibited a significant reduction in ABL2 levels compared to uninfected controls (Fig. 1E and F). Similarly, RD cells infected with enterovirus A71 (EV-A71) or coxsackievirus A16 (CVA16) showed reduced ABL2 expression at 5 and 7 hpi compared with mock-infected controls (Fig. 1G and H).

Non-structural protein 2B mediates ABL2 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system at the K331 site

Next, to determine which CVB3 proteins are involved in the degradation of ABL2, HeLa cells were transfected with plasmids encoding FLAG-tagged CVB3 non-structural (2A, 2B, 2C, 3A, 3C, and 3D) proteins. Western blotting showed that only 2A, 2B, and 2C significantly reduced ABL2 protein levels, whereas the other viral proteins had no obvious effect (Fig. 2A). 2A effectively shuts down cellular translation; thus, the decrease in ABL2 in 2A-expressing cells may be attributed to translational arrest rather than specific protein degradation. Non-structural protein 2B was selected as the representative for subsequent experimental research. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays failed to detect a stable direct interaction between ABL2 and 2B (Supplementary Fig. S1), suggesting that 2B-induced ABL2 degradation may reflect an indirect effect of their interference with cellular processes. HeLa cells were transfected with increasing amounts of FLAG-2B plasmids, and cell lysates were analyzed by Western blotting. As shown in Fig. 2B, the expression of 2B induced a dose-dependent decrease in ABL2 protein levels. However, no significant reduction in ABL2 mRNA levels was observed (Fig. 2C), suggesting that 2B modulates ABL2 expression at the post-transcriptional level. To identify the protein degradation pathway involved in 2B-mediated ABL2 degradation, the following inhibitors were used: Z-VAD-FMK (a pan-caspase inhibitor), chloroquine (CQ; a lysosomal inhibitor), and MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor). As shown in Fig. 2D, MG132 effectively restored ABL2 protein levels. These results indicate that the viral non-structural proteins 2B target ABL2 for proteasomal degradation.

Ubiquitination, as a post-translational modification (PTM), serves as a critical trigger for proteasomal degradation of target proteins via the UPS. To identify potential ubiquitination sites on ABL2, we employed the PhosphoSitePlus database. This analysis predicted three candidate lysine (K) residues: K331, K578, and K809 (Fig. 2E). Among these, K331 was selected for functional validation based on its highest predicted likelihood. An ABL2 mutant bearing a K331R substitution, in which lysine was replaced with arginine, was generated to verify whether this mutation could prevent degradation of ABL2. The results showed that the K331R mutant was resistant to ubiquitin-mediated degradation induced by 2B (Fig. 2F). These findings suggest that the non-structural protein 2B mediates ABL2 degradation through the UPS at the K331 residue.

ABL2 negatively regulates enterovirus replication

Given that enterovirus infection influences ABL2 expression, the role of ABL2 in CVB replication was investigated. To assess the impact of ABL2 on CVB replication, stable HeLa cell lines were generated via lentiviral transduction to overexpress ABL2 (designated oeABL2) or to silence ABL2 (designated shABL2). Western blotting confirmed successful ABL2 overexpression in oeABL2 cells and efficient silencing in shABL2 cells, relative to their respective negative controls (designated NC and shNC) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Following infection of these stable lines with CVB3 (MOI = 5), Western blot analysis showed that the expression level of the CVB3 capsid protein VP1 was significantly lower in oeABL2 cells than in the NC group (Fig. 3A). Viral plaque assays revealed an approximately 70% reduction in plaque number in oeABL2 cells relative to the control group (Fig. 3E). Fluorescence microscopic analysis of cells transfected with the CVB3-eGFP plasmid revealed significantly weaker fluorescence intensity in the oeABL2 group than in the control group, consistent with reduced viral replication (Fig. 3H). In contrast, shABL2 enhanced the expression level of the CVB3 capsid protein VP1 at 7 hpi, increased viral plaque formation, and augmented the fluorescence intensity of CVB3-eGFP (Fig. 3B, F, and I).

To determine the universality of this effect, the experiments were extended to other enteroviruses. Following infection with CVB5, shABL2 cells showed increased VP1 expression at 5 and 7 hpi relative to control cells (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, oeABL2 cells exhibited significantly reduced VP1 expression levels and viral plaque formation following infection with EV-A71 (Fig. 3D and G). Collectively, these findings indicated that ABL2 acted as a negative regulator of enterovirus replication.

ABL2 specifically inhibits the early stage of enterovirus replication

To identify which stage of the CVB3 life cycle was affected by ABL2, we sequentially analyzed viral binding, entry, and replication kinetics. For viral binding assays, shABL2 and shNC HeLa cells were chilled to 4 °C (to block endocytosis) and infected with CVB3 (MOI = 5) for 1 h qRT-PCR detection of viral RNA revealed no significant difference in CVB3 binding between the two groups, indicating that ABL2 does not influence viral attachment to the host cell membrane (Fig. 4A). For viral entry assays, cells were first incubated with CVB3 on ice (1 h) to allow binding, then washed to remove unbound virus and shifted to 37 °C for 1.5 h to initiate entry. qRT-PCR of intracellular viral RNA revealed no significant difference between shABL2 and shNC cells, confirming that ABL2 does not interfere with viral internalization (Fig. 4B).

Subsequently, intracellular CVB3 RNA levels were measured to analyze viral replication kinetics. At 1 hpi, there was no difference in CVB3 RNA levels between shABL2 and shNC cells. However, at 3 hpi, CVB3 RNA levels in shABL2 cells were higher than those in shNC cells (Fig. 4C). Conversely, oeABL2 cells had lower CVB3 RNA levels at 3 hpi (Fig. 4D). Similarly, compared to NC cells, oeABL2 cells exhibited a marked decrease in viral RNA levels 3 h post-infection with CVB5 (Fig. 4E) or EV-A71 (Fig. 4F). These data demonstrated that ABL2 specifically inhibited the early replication stage of enterovirus, which occurs after viral entry and before extensive viral RNA synthesis.

ABL2 directly interacts with RAC1 and downregulates RAC1 protein levels

Since ABL2 is downregulated by enterovirus infection (Fig. 1) and negatively regulates viral replication (Fig. 3), we next explored the molecular mechanism underlying this antiviral effect. The STRING database predicted a potential interaction between ABL2 and RAC1. Reciprocal Co-IP assays confirmed the ABL2-RAC1 interaction: anti-RAC1 antibody efficiently precipitated ABL2, and anti-ABL2 antibody reciprocally precipitated RAC1. Normal rabbit IgG failed to pull down either protein, confirming specificity (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence analysis further showed that ABL2 and RAC1 were primarily localized in the cytoplasm, supporting their interaction in a physiological cellular context (Fig. 5B).

Next, we investigated whether ABL2 regulates RAC1 expression. HeLa cells infected with CVB3 showed a time-dependent increase in RAC1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S3). In contrast, ABL2 overexpression significantly reduced RAC1 protein levels, while ABL2 silencing increased RAC1 protein levels (Fig. 5C and D). The same changes were also observed in CVB5 and EV-A71 infections (Fig. 5E and F). These results suggested that ABL2 negatively regulates RAC1 expression during enterovirus infection.

RAC1 promotes CVB3 replication and activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

To investigate the role of RAC1 in CVB3 replication, HeLa cells were transfected with either a RAC1-overexpression plasmid or siRNA targeting RAC1 (siRAC1). Western blot analysis and viral plaque assays showed that RAC1 overexpression enhanced CVB3 replication, as indicated by increased VP1 protein levels and plaque formation (Fig. 6A and C). In addition, co-transfection of the CVB3-eGFP plasmid with the RAC1-overexpression plasmid showed that RAC1 significantly promoted CVB3 replication, as indicated by increased fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6E). Conversely, RAC1 knockdown inhibited CVB3 replication, reducing both viral plaque formation and fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6B, D, and F).

Previous studies have implicated RAC1 in the regulation of viral replication via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Tan et al., 2008; Fan et al., 2020). To explore this connection, HeLa cells transfected with the RAC1-overexpression plasmid or siRAC1 were infected with CVB3 at 36 h post-transfection. Western blot analysis showed that RAC1 overexpression enhanced the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT in uninfected (mock) cells compared to the control. Upon CVB3 infection, the levels of phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K) and AKT (p-AKT) were further elevated in a time-dependent manner compared to those in the control group. The levels of p-AKT and p-PI3K closely mirrored the expression of CVB3 capsid protein VP1 (Fig. 6G). Conversely, RAC1 knockdown suppressed PI3K and AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 6H). These results indicated that RAC1 positively correlates with CVB3 replication and activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, which may contribute to RAC1's pro-viral effect.

ABL2 modulates CVB3 replication through the RAC1/PI3K/AKT axis

To determine whether ABL2 mediates its antiviral effect via the RAC1/PI3K/AKT pathway, the impact of ABL2 on pathway activation was first analyzed. ABL2 overexpression significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, and this inhibition was enhanced following prolonged CVB3 infection (Fig. 7A). Conversely, ABL2 silencing enhanced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 7B).

To further explore the functional interaction between ABL2 and RAC1, RAC1 was overexpressed in oeABL2 stable cell lines. The cells were divided into four groups: 1) pcDNA3.1 empty vector (Vec) + NC, 2) oeRAC1 + NC, 3) oeABL2 + Vec, and 4) oeRAC1 + oeABL2. Western blot analysis showed that in the absence of CVB3 infection (mock), the oeRAC1 + oeABL2 group had higher p-PI3K and p-AKT levels than the oeABL2 + Vec group. These results indicate that RAC1 overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effect of ABL2 on PI3K and AKT phosphorylation. Following CVB3 infection, VP1 expression in the oeRAC1 + oeABL2 group was higher than that in the oeABL2 + Vec group but lower than that in the oeRAC1 + NC group, suggesting that ABL2 attenuates the promoting effect of RAC1 on CVB3 replication and PI3K/AKT activation (Fig. 7C).

The combined effects of ABL2 silencing and RAC1 overexpression on CVB3 replication and the PI3K/AKT pathway were assessed. RAC1 was overexpressed in ABL2-silenced stable cells in the following groups: 1) Vec + shNC, 2) oeRAC1 + shNC, 3) shABL2 + Vec, and 4) oeRAC1 + shABL2. Western blot analysis revealed that in uninfected cells, the oeRAC1 + shABL2 group exhibited significantly higher levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT than the shABL2 + Vec group, indicating that RAC1 overexpression in ABL2-silenced cells further enhanced PI3K/AKT phosphorylation. After 7 h of CVB3 infection, VP1 expression in the oeRAC1 + shABL2 group was significantly higher than that in the shABL2 + Vec and oeRAC1 + shNC groups (Fig. 7D). These results suggest that ABL2 silencing and RAC1 overexpression together promote CVB3 replication and PI3K/AKT phosphorylation.

Discussion

Enteroviruses have been implicated in various severe diseases, including myocarditis, meningoencephalitis, and pancreatitis. Among these, CVB3 is a common cause of myocarditis and sudden cardiac death, and its complex pathogenic mechanisms pose significant challenges for clinical treatment (Li et al., 2025a). Current research on enterovirus-host interactions primarily focuses on two aspects: (1) direct virus-mediated infection leading to organ damage (McManus et al., 1993) and (2) the activation of an abnormal host immune response to combat viral invasion (Panetti et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). In this study, we identified the non-receptor tyrosine kinase ABL2 as a novel host restriction factor against enterovirus and elucidated a sophisticated viral countermeasure, thereby providing new insights into the dynamic “arms race” between enteroviruses and their hosts.

Initially, ABL2 downregulation was documented following infection with both CVB and CVA. This observation led us to systematically characterize ABL2 as a broad-spectrum antiviral factor against enteroviruses. Its inhibitory effect was consistent across multiple cell types, including HeLa and HL-1 cardiomyocytes. Crucially, time-course assays revealed that ABL2 acts during the early, post-entry stage of the viral life cycle. This timing is strategically significant, as intervention at this initial phase can prevent the exponential amplification of the viral genome before the host cell's defense and integrity are irreversibly compromised (Lockhart et al., 2022). These findings expand the known arsenal of host proteins capable of restricting enteroviruses.

Mechanistically, we delineated that ABL2 exerts its antiviral function through the RAC1/PI3K/AKT signaling axis—a pathway co-opted by many viruses to facilitate their replication (Ehrhardt et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2021). We demonstrated that ABL2 directly interacts with the small GTPase RAC1, leading to its downregulation and consequent suppression of PI3K/AKT phosphorylation. The rescue of viral replication upon RAC1 overexpression confirmed a causal relationship within this pathway. The context-dependent nature of ABL2 regulation of PI3K/AKT is noteworthy. For instance, ABL2 can enhance PI3K/AKT signaling in gastric cancer (Miao and Liu, 2024) yet suppress it in acute myeloid leukemia (Kazi et al., 2017). In the context of CVB3 infection, this apparent paradox may be resolved by virally induced alterations in the cellular environment. The CVB3 2B protein, known to disrupt cellular calcium homeostasis (Campanella et al., 2004), may be a key instigator. We propose that, given ABL2 kinase activity is modulated by calcium/calmodulin signaling (Piwocka et al., 2006), the 2B-induced calcium flux could redirect ABL2's activity towards RAC1, favoring pathway suppression over activation and thereby creating an environment less conducive to viral replication.

Another key finding is the identification of a viral counter-strategy. The enteroviral non-structural protein 2B serves as the critical viral factor responsible for ABL2 downregulation, acting through the UPS specifically at lysine 331. Notably, among the six non-structural proteins screened, only 2A, 2B, and 2C exhibited the capacity to reduce ABL2 protein levels. The dose-dependent reduction of ABL2 by 2B, coupled with unchanged mRNA levels, strongly supports a post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism. This counter-strategy mirrors mechanisms employed by other enteroviruses; for example, EV-A71 uses its 2A protease to degrade the innate immune adapter MAVS (Wang et al., 2013), and its 3C protease cleaves TRIM25 to blunt the interferon response (Xiao et al., 2021).

This study, while revealing a novel host-virus interaction, has several limitations that warrant further investigation. The E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for catalyzing K331 ubiquitination remains unknown. We have conducted preliminary screening for several candidate E3 ligases, including Parkin, SYTL4, CBL, and SMURF1; however, no definitive E3 ligase has been identified to date. Identifying the precise E3 ligase that mediates ABL2 ubiquitination will be a key focus of our future research. Furthermore, the physiological relevance of ABL2-mediated defense should be validated in vivo using ABL2 conditional knockout or transgenic mouse models challenged with CVB3, which would clarify its role in mitigating myocarditis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ABL2 functions as a host antiviral factor during enterovirus infection by inhibiting viral replication through the negative regulation of its interacting partner, RAC1, and the subsequent suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 8). Additionally, enterovirus degrades ABL2 through the ubiquitin-proteasome system mediated by its non-structural protein 2B to counteract this defense response. These findings raise the possibility that stabilizing or enhancing the activity of ABL2 may reinforce antiviral defenses in susceptible populations, such as children with enteroviral myocarditis, providing a new theoretical basis for the development of antiviral treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, viruses and reagents

HeLa, HL-1, and RD cells were maintained at the School of Medicine, Jiangsu University. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. CVB3 (Nancy strain, GenBank No. JX312064) was kindly provided by Professor Ruizhen Chen from the Department of Cardiology, Zhongshan Hospital, Shanghai, China. CVB5 (GenBank No. KP266578), CVA16 (GenBank No. KP266573) and EV-A71 (GenBank No. KP266579) were generously donated by the Jiangsu Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Nanjing, China. The CVB3-eGFP construct, which contains the CVB3 genomic sequence and expresses enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP), was kindly provided by Professor Zhong Zhaohua (Harbin Medical University, China). Chloroquine (CQ, HY-17589), MG132 (HY-13259) and Z-VAD-FMK (HY-16658B) were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, Shanghai, China).

Lentiviral vector transduction

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured overnight at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. HeLa cells were transduced with ABL2-overexpressing (sc-417779-LAC; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) or ABL2-silencing lentiviral particles (Asia-Vector Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), along with their respective control plasmids, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The viral supernatant was collected and used to infect HeLa cells. Puromycin (Biosharp, Hefei, China) selection was initiated 48 h post-transduction and maintained for 14 days to establish stably transduced cell lines. Western blotting was then performed to verify transduction efficiency.

Cell transfection

Transfection of siRAC1, negative control (NC; Jima, Suzhou, China, C03002), pcDNA3.1-RAC1 plasmid (Miao Ling Biotech, Wuhan, China, P71465), and pcDNA3.1 empty vector (Vec) was performed using the Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, L3000001) according to the manufacturer's instructions. CVB3-eGFP plasmids were co-transfected with P3000 reagent and Lipofectamine 3000.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from the cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and blocked with 0.5% skim milk. Membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were as follows: ABL2 (1:2000; Abcam, ab134134), RAC1 (1:1000; Proteintech, 24072-1-AP), protein kinase B (AKT, 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology [CST], USA, 9272), phospho-AKT (1:1000; CST, 4060), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K, 1:1000; CST, 4249), phospho-PI3K (1:1000; CST, 13857), VP1 (1:3000; Genetex, USA, GTX641870), and GAPDH (1:20,000; Genetex, GTX100118). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA, 309-035-003) was used as the secondary antibody for immunoblotting. After incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, the protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini; Pittsburgh, PA, USA).

RNA preparation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total cellular RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA, 15-596-026) and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan, RR037A) for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with TB Green Premix Ex TaqII (Takara, Japan, RR820L). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Viral plaque assay

HeLa cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well and incubated overnight. Virus dilution was added to the wells, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The cells were overlaid with 0.75% low-melting-point agarose and incubated for 72 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Following microscopic examination of the plaques, the cells were fixed with glacial acetic acid for 30 min. The agarose overlay was then removed, and the cells were stained with crystal violet for 30 min. The plaques were photographed and quantified.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

Cells were lysed with mild RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on ice for 30 min. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation and incubated with 2 μg of anti-RAC1 antibody overnight at 4 °C. Input (positive control) and IgG (negative control) samples were also included. Protein A/G agarose beads (20 μL) were added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with shaking to facilitate antibody-bead coupling and washed three times with lysis buffer containing 150 mM NaCl. Immune complexes were collected by centrifugation and analyzed using western blotting.

Immunofluorescence analysis

The cells were seeded on glass coverslips and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde the following day. Cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). After overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-RAC1, 1:500; anti-ABL2, 1:500), the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488/594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200; Genetex, GTX54529) for 30 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, China, C1006). The coverslips were mounted, and images were acquired using confocal fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in three independent replicates (n = 3). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA): Student's t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance was used for comparisons among multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, P < 0.01 highly significant, and P < 0.001 extremely significant.
