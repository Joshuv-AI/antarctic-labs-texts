Buddy Wire–Augmented Intravascular Lithotripsy in Refractory Calcified Coronary Lesions: A Five‐Case Series
Authors: Alfredo Marchese, Mattia Squillace, Pierluigi Demola, Giuseppe Speziale, Francesco Bedogni, Robert J. Chilton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHeavily calcified coronary lesions remain a major challenge in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), often limiting the effectiveness of intravascular lithotripsy (IVL). We report a five‐case series in which IVL failed to achieve adequate lesion modification but was successfully augmented by the addition of one or more buddy wires. Lesion subsets included severe proximal right coronary artery calcification, IVL balloon rupture with preserved efficacy, nodular calcification refractory to atherectomy, stent underexpansion causing recurrent ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction, and a concentric lesion assessed with optical coherence tomography (OCT). In the OCT‐guided case, buddy wire–augmented IVL produced multiple additional calcium fracture lines and significant luminal gain without further balloon dilation. These findings support a hypothesis‐generating mechanism of mechanical–acoustic coupling, whereby guidewires may facilitate localized amplification of shockwave energy. Buddy wire–augmented IVL may represent a simple and effective adjunctive strategy in resistant calcified lesions.

Introduction

Coronary artery calcification is a major determinant of adverse outcomes in PCI, contributing to stent underexpansion, restenosis, and thrombosis [1, 2]. Adequate lesion preparation is therefore critical for procedural success.

Intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) has emerged as a safe and effective modality for calcium modification, generating pulsatile acoustic pressure waves that induce controlled calcium fracture [3, 4]. Despite its favorable safety profile, IVL may be ineffective in lesions with severe, concentric, or nodular calcification [5].

The buddy wire technique is traditionally used to improve device deliverability and support, but its role in enhancing IVL efficacy has not been systematically described. We present a five‐case series demonstrating the potential utility of buddy wire–augmented IVL and propose a mechanistic hypothesis supported by intravascular imaging (Central Illustration Illustration 1, 1).

Discussion

Heavily calcified coronary lesions remain a major determinant of adverse procedural and long‐term outcomes in PCI, contributing to stent underexpansion, restenosis, and thrombosis [1]. IVL has emerged as an effective and safe modality for calcium modification, utilizing pulsatile acoustic pressure waves to induce controlled calcium fracture while minimizing vessel injury [5]. However, IVL efficacy may be limited in lesions characterized by severe calcium burden, concentric distribution, or nodular morphology [6].

This five‐case series demonstrates that buddy wire–assisted IVL can successfully modify calcified coronary lesions that have failed to respond to conventional IVL therapy. Across each case, the adjunctive placement of one or two buddy wires alongside the primary guidewire produced a measurable and clinically meaningful improvement in lithotripsy efficacy. Beyond their procedural outcomes, these cases collectively advance a mechanistic hypothesis that may reframe how operators conceptualize guidewire interactions with acoustic energy delivery systems [7].

IVL functions by translating acoustic pressure waves—generated by a piezoelectric emitter housed within the balloon—into circumferential shear stresses at the calcium–tissue interface. This produces both superficial and deep calcium fractures while preserving the structural integrity of the intima and media, a safety advantage validated across major registries and the DISRUPT CAD trial program [6, 8]. Despite this favorable profile, IVL is not universally effective. Lesions characterized by extreme calcium density, concentric circumferential distribution, or protruding nodular morphology represent a recognized subset in which standard IVL may fail to generate adequate plaque modification—precisely the substrate encountered in the cases described here [9, 10].

The buddy wire technique itself is not new to interventional cardiology. It has long been used to improve balloon deliverability in tortuous anatomy, provide supplementary rail support, and augment dilation force by altering the mechanical environment within the coronary lumen. What has not previously been characterized is its potential to interact with and augment acoustic energy delivery. The present series suggests this interaction is not only real but mechanistically exploitable.

Debonding

In IVL, debonding describes the separation of calcified plaque from surrounding vessel wall tissue at the calcium‐tissue interface—a mechanism that complements, rather than replaces, direct calcium fracture. IVL‐generated acoustic pressure waves (~5 MPa) exploit the differing acoustic impedances of calcium and soft tissue, concentrating energy at their shared interface and disrupting the bond between rigid deposits and the compliant vessel wall. The result is a calcified plaque architecture that can shift during balloon inflation, facilitating luminal gain and more uniform stent expansion.

Histologic evidence confirms that IVL produces multi‐depth fractures and discrete separation planes at calcium‐tissue boundaries. Notably, OCT detects only ~57% of fractures confirmed on histology, implying that subclinical debonding—occurring without visible fracture lines—independently contributes to improved vessel compliance. This likely explains why stent expansion outcomes in Disrupt CAD III were comparably optimized irrespective of OCT‐visible fracture. Critically, IVL achieves this degree of structural disruption while preserving medial integrity, representing a meaningful safety advantage over cutting balloons and ultra‐high‐pressure balloon strategies.

The Mechanical–Acoustic Coupling Hypothesis

We propose that the benefit observed with buddy wire–assisted IVL is explained by a phenomenon of mechanical–acoustic coupling. When metallic guidewires are co‐positioned within the IVL balloon environment, an impedance mismatch develops between the surrounding fluid medium and the higher‐density metallic structures. This mismatch preferentially channels shockwave‐derived vibrational energy into the guidewires themselves. Within the rigid, NC environment of a heavily calcified coronary segment, these oscillating metallic conductors act as focal stress concentrators at the wire–calcium interface—generating tangential shear forces in a pattern we describe as a fulcrum‐cutting effect.

In a double‐wire configuration, this vibrational energy propagates simultaneously along two parallel metallic pathways. The result is a conversion of predominantly radial acoustic energy into spatially distributed circumferential and tangential shear stresses across a broader arc of the calcified plaque. This amplification, spread across two discrete contact points, likely explains the superior calcium fracturing observed with dual buddy wire assistance compared to single‐wire or conventional IVL delivery.

Angiographic support for this hypothesis comes from Case 4, where StentBoost imaging demonstrated a visible reduction in calcified plaque thickness localized precisely between the two buddy wires—a spatial distribution consistent with concentrated mechanical stress at wire–calcium contact points. Quantitative coronary analysis confirmed a 27% reduction in maximal extra‐stent calcium thickness, providing objective angiographic evidence of focused plaque modification.

The most direct mechanistic evidence, however, comes from Case 5, in which sequential OCT imaging was performed before and after each discrete IVL phase. Standard IVL without buddy wire assistance produced a single calcium fracture line and increased the minimum lumen area (MLA) from 4.3 to 5.2 mm2. Following co‐placement of two buddy wires, an additional four IVL cycles—delivered without any intervening NC balloon dilation—generated two or more new, spatially distinct fracture lines and expanded MLA to 7.6 mm2. The appearance of additional fractures attributable exclusively to the buddy wire–assisted IVL phase, in the complete absence of supplementary mechanical dilation, constitutes compelling evidence that the guidewires were actively concentrating and directing shockwave energy to discrete points of wire–calcium contact.

Efficacy After Balloon Rupture

Case 2 presents what may be the most conceptually striking observation in this series. The IVL balloon ruptured during the initial lithotripsy burst following buddy wire placement, yet the operator proceeded to complete the full shockwave delivery cycle. Subsequent NC balloon inflation achieved complete and uniform lesion expansion—despite the loss of intact balloon‐mediated hydraulic pressure. If conventional IVL efficacy depends substantially on balloon inflation to transmit acoustic energy to the vessel wall, balloon rupture should theoretically compromise that transmission. However, there are two possible physical mechanisms to explain that effect.

First, the acoustic pressure waves are known to propagate efficiently through fluid media, including blood, whose acoustic properties are largely determined by its high water content [11]. Therefore, residual transmission of lithotripsy energy from functional emitters to adjacent calcified plaque may remain possible despite balloon disruption. While this hypothesis has not been formally investigated in coronary IVL, it is supported by established principles of acoustic wave propagation in biological fluids and may explain the continued plaque modification observed in our case. In our perspective, case 3 is actually a very interesting “natural experiment” because: IVL pulses continued after rupture, subsequent lesion modification occurred, and no other plaque‐modifying device was used. So, the clinical observation itself becomes indirect evidence that acoustic energy was still being transmitted through the blood‐filled lumen.

Second, the fact that effective calcium modification persisted suggests that the buddy wires themselves had assumed the role of primary conduits for focused acoustic–mechanical energy delivery along the longitudinal axis. This would represent a fundamentally different energy transmission pathway from standard IVL and, if confirmed in future cases, may have implications for how we understand the relative contributions of the balloon and the guidewire environment to overall IVL efficacy.

Calcified Nodular Morphology

Case 3 extends the applicability of buddy wire–assisted IVL to one of the most refractory substrates in contemporary interventional practice: nodular calcification. Calcified nodules resist conventional fracturing strategies due to their three‐dimensional luminal protrusion and structural rigidity. This patient had already undergone maximal multi‐modal plaque preparation including rotational atherectomy and cutting balloon angioplasty before buddy wire–assisted IVL was employed as a bailout strategy. The incremental benefit achieved in this setting suggests that mechanical–acoustic coupling may be most valuable precisely when conventional and advanced calcium modification modalities have been exhausted—when residual plaque rigidity is at its greatest and additional fracture propagation requires a more targeted mechanism.

Addressing the Counterpoint

A legitimate conceptual concern deserves direct acknowledgment. One of IVL's well‐recognized advantages over rotational atherectomy is its delivery of unfocused acoustic energy independent of guidewire position, producing circumferential calcium modification without the eccentric bias that limits atherectomy. The introduction of buddy wires may risk partially reintroducing that wire bias, observed with atherectomy, by concentrating energy at specific contact points and potentially leaving intervening calcium arcs unmodified.

The OCT findings from Case 5 address this concern directly. Buddy wire–assisted IVL did not replace the circumferential fracture pattern generated by the balloon; it added focal fractures at wire contact points on top of the existing circumferential mechanism. The result was an additive fracture pattern that produced the largest MLA gain in the series—suggesting complementarity rather than substitution of mechanisms.

Safety and Limitations

No procedural complications attributable to the buddy wire technique occurred across any of the five cases, providing preliminary reassurance regarding its safety profile. Theoretical concerns remain, including the possibility of wire‐mediated vascular trauma or misdirected acoustic energy, and systematic safety evaluation in larger series will be necessary before formal adoption.

The principal limitations of this report are its small sample size, retrospective design, and inconsistent use of intravascular imaging across cases. Prospective studies incorporating systematic OCT or IVUS assessment before and after each IVL phase will be essential to rigorously characterize fracture patterns, quantify lumen gain, and validate the mechanical–acoustic coupling hypothesis in a controlled setting.

Conclusions

Buddy wire–augmented IVL may represent a simple and effective adjunctive strategy for the treatment of severely calcified coronary lesions resistant to standard IVL. Further studies are warranted.
