Association among Gestational Weight Gain, Fucosylated Human Milk Oligosaccharides, and Breast Milk Microbiota—An Evidence in Healthy Mothers from Northwest China
Authors: Sijin Jiang, Jiale Qin, Lu Shi, Jiayu Feng, Jianhui Mo, Wanghong Su, Yue Cheng, Jia Lv, Qiang Li, Shaoru Li, Lingxia Zeng, Bei Han, Jing Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the relationship among maternal secretor status, human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), and the composition of breastmilk microbiota in a cohort of healthy mothers from Shaanxi province, China. The results demonstrated that 78.9% of the mothers were secretors, exhibiting an active fucosyltransferase 2 gene (fut2) and producing α-1,2 fucosylated HMOs, which significantly affected the HMO profile. Secretor mothers had higher levels of 2′-FL and LNFPI in contrast to nonsecretors who displayed high levels of 3′-FL, LNFPII, and LNT. Furthermore, secretor mothers exhibited greater diversity in HMOs compared with nonsecretors, although no significant differences were observed in the breast milk microbiota composition. A correlation was identified between specific HMOs (2′-FL, 3′-FL, 6′-SL, and LNFPI) and the microbiota composition. Notably, mothers with normal weight gain during pregnancy demonstrated higher microbial diversity, with increased abundance of beneficial genera such as Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Ligilactobacillus. These findings contribute to the development of potential guidelines for providing personalized nutrition.

Introduction

Human milk is the optimal food for infants. Evidence suggests that breast-fed infants are more resilient than formula-fed infants in reduced susceptibility to a range of diseases and infections, including diarrhea, necrotizing enterocolitis, allergies and asthma, enterovirus infection, diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease.1,2 The protective mechanisms of breastfeeding may be partially explained by its benefit to infant’s gut microbiota.1 Human breast milk contains a complex community of microbes,1−4 which are also an important source of bacteria for infant gut,5 and this subsequently shows in current and long-term health effects.6 The gut microbiota of breastfed infants exhibits lower diversity and a higher relative abundance of Bifidobacterium spp. and Lactobacillus spp. than that of formula-fed infants, which exhibit a higher relative abundance of Clostridia spp.3

Recent studies have indicated that the composition of human milk microbiota is highly diverse,1,4,6 with potential influences from both infant and maternal factors, including delivery mode, maternal body mass index (BMI), gestational age, infant gender, and so forth.1,7−10 However, the results of these studies have been inconsistent. For maternal BMI, some studies have reported no correlation,4,9 whereas others have indicated that increased maternal BMI affects the diversity of breast milk microbiota.8,10 For the delivery mode, no statistically significant differences were reported in bacterial profiles between cesarean section and vaginal delivery.11 In contrast, another study concluded that the breast milk microbiota was shaped by the delivery mode.7,9 Consequently, further research is required to elucidate the association between breast milk microbiota and maternal and infant factors.

In addition to the abundance of microbiota present in human milk, human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) deserve further attention. HMOs, a specific bioactive component, are the third most abundant component after lactose and lipids in human milk.12 HMOs are structurally diverse, unconjugated carbohydrates composed of five monosaccharides, including d-glucose (Glc), d-galactose (Gal), N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), l-fucose (Fuc), and sialic acid existed in humans as N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac).5,12 Although neither carbohydrate nor oligosaccharide synthesis is directly genetically encoded, HMO composition is largely determined by the expression of the secretor (Se) gene, fut2, which codes for α-1,2-fucosyltransferase2 (FUT2). Mothers with an active allele of fut2 are called “secretors,” while those with an inactivated homozygous allele are referred to as “non-secretors”.13 These are phenotypic differences between secretors and nonsecretors in glycans presented in breast milk.14 The milk of secretor mother contains no or only minimal quantities of α-1,2-fucosylated HMOs, such as 2′-fucosyllactse (2′-FL) and lacto-N-fucopentase I (LNFPI).15 For secretor mothers, 2′-FL is the most abundant HMO.16 The α-1,2-fucosylated HMOs function as prebiotics in infant gut, where they are the preferred substrate for Bifidobacterium spp. and Lactobacillus spp.14 Furthermore, these bacteria can utilize HMOs in vitro.17 Most studies have investigated the influence of secretor status and HMOs on infant gut microbiota.14,18,19 Nevertheless, few studies have investigated the impact of secretor status and HMO composition on breast milk microbiota. It is of great interest to determine whether the differences in HMOs based on the maternal secretor status affect milk microbiota.

The aim of this study is to explore the associations in mother secretion status, HMOs, and breastmilk microbiota and analyze the influencing factors of breastmilk microbiota, so as to provide a theoretical basis for the in-depth exploration of the interrelationships between the breastmilk composition and infants’ health in the early stage of life, optimize feeding recommendations for infants and young children, and develop formulas that are closer to breastmilk compositions.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This descriptive cross-sectional study is a subset of Maternal and Child Health Cohort Study of Shaanxi Province conducted in Lintong, Xi’an, Shaanxi province, China, from October 2019. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University, China (no. 2018-293) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry platform (ChiCTR2100047531). Mothers were enrolled in the study if they met the following criteria: they were between 18 and 45 years old and had a full-term, singleton pregnancy. Mothers with pre-existing chronic health conditions, use of prebiotics, alcohol, and smoking during pregnancy, and pregnancy complications were excluded. Sociodemographic and clinical information was collected, including maternal age, blood type, BMI, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, newborn gender, and birth weight.

Mothers were classified into four groups according to their pregestational BMI: low weight (LW, <18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (NW, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (OW, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (OB, ≥30 kg/m2). This classification was based on criteria set out by the World Health Organization (WHO).20 Furthermore, mothers before delivery with different weight gains were classified into three categories according to their prepregnancy BMI: normal weight gain (NORWG), low weight gain (LOWWG), and excessive weight gain (EXEWG) as recommended.21 NORWG was defined as follows: 12.5–18.0 kg weight gain for LW mothers, 11.5–16.0 kg weight gain for NW mothers, 7.0–11.5 kg weight gain for OW mothers, and 5.0–9.0 kg weight gain for OB mothers. Weight gain above the upper values of the recommended ranges was designated as EXEWG, while weight gain below the lower values of the recommended ranges was designated as LOWWG. LOWWG and EXEWG are all regarded as abnormal weight gain (UNOWG).

Collection of Human Breast Milk Samples

The breast milk samples from 71 healthy mothers 42 days postpartum in this cohort were collected from March 2021 to May 2022. The milk samples (10–20 mL) were collected from the breast that had not been recently used for breastfeeding on the morning of the indicated collection day postpartum. Following cleaning of the nipple, areola, and hands with 75% alcohol wipes, each mother provided a milk sample in a sterile container, with the initial few drops being discarded. After homogenization and aliquoting, milk samples were shipped to the laboratory of Biological Sample Library, School of Public Health, Xi’an Jiaotong University, China, on dry ice and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Analysis of fut2 SNP and Secretor Genotyping

Maternal genomic DNA was extracted from breast milk using the E.Z.N.A. Food DNA Kit (Omega, Norcross, Georgia, USA) and following the manufacturer’s protocol. The fut2 gene was genotyped using PCR to determine the secretor status. Genomic DNA was amplified with the fut2-F (5′-GTTCAGATGCCTTTCTCCTTT) and fut2-R (5′-GCAATCCCTGTCCACTCCG) primers, which produced a 992 bp amplicon containing the rs601338 SNP (G to A), rs1047781 SNP (A to T), and rs602662 SNP (G to A) alleles of fut2. The reaction mixture was settled and PCR conditions were 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 32 cycles at 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1.5 min, with a final elongation proceeding at 72 °C for 5 min. The successful amplification of the target sequence was confirmed by gel electrophoresis, and the PCR products were sent to Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for DNA sequencing. Sequences were aligned using Chromas software (version 2.3.0.0) and manually reviewed by two independent investigators to identify heterozygous positions. The maternal secretor genotype was defined as nonsecretor (nonsecretor mothers, NS) if the fut2 SNP allele was a homozygous mutation and as secretor (secretor mothers, SE) if fut2 SNP was a heterozygous mutation or nonmutation. The Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium was employed to assess the representativeness of the population, with results of this assessment being determined by the chi-square test (P > 0.05).

HMO Profiling Analysis by HPLC-MS/MS

The frozen milk samples were thawed overnight at 4 °C. Following homogenization, the lipids and proteins were separated and removed by centrifugation. The supernatant, which consisted mainly of oligosaccharides and residual lactose, was diluted 1:1 (v/v) with acetonitrile after passing through a 0.22 μm filter and then analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS).

The absolute concentrations of seven HMOs, including 2′-fucosyllactose (2′-FL), 3′-fucosyllactose (3′-FL), 3′-sialyllactose (3′-SL), 6′-sialyllactose (6′-SL), lacto-N-tetraose (LNT), lacto-N-fucopentaose I (LNFP I), and lacto-N-fucopentaose II (LNFP II), were quantified. An absolute quantitative determination was conducted utilizing an external calibration curve comprising HMO standards (GLYCOSCI; Shanghai, China). The HMO stock standards (1 mg/mL) were diluted in 90% acetonitrile to generate an eight-point calibration curve. HPLC-MS/MS in the negative ion mode was selected, which permitted the detection of neutral HMO isomers, including LNFP I and LNFP II, 2′-FL, and 3′-FL, without labeling. The method was described by Siziba et al.22 The separation of HMOs was conducted using a LC-20ADXR series HPLC (Shimadzu, Japan) by using an XBridge BEH Amide column (3.5 μM, 3 × 100 mm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The aqueous phase A consisted of 5 mM ammonium acetate, and organic phase B consisted of acetonitrile (HPLC grade, Macklin, Shanghai, China). Gradient elution of HMOs was performed with a flow rate of 300 μL/min, with the column maintained at a constant temperature of 40 °C throughout the process. HMOs were eluted from column and injected into an API3200 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) using an electrospray ionization source. They were subsequently analyzed through multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) for qualitative and quantitative purposes. Ions were produced and concentrated by applying a negative ion spray voltage of −4500 V and a source temperature of 600 °C, while the ion source gases 1 and 2 were set to 30 and 35 psi, respectively. Furthermore, the curtain gas was set to 35 psi, the collisional activated dissociation gas to 5 psi, and the entrance potential to −10 V. The optimal mass spectrometric parameters for each compound were evaluated by infusing HMO standards individually into the mass spectrometer (at 0.1 mg/mL in 80% acetonitrile) and selecting [M – H]− ions. An overview of the MRM conditions and selected transitions is provided in Table S1.

The method was validated in terms of its linearity, sensitivity, accuracy, and precision. The linearity was investigated by utilizing eight distinct solutions of known concentrations. Sensitivity was determined by the limit of detection and the limit of quantification, which were defined as analyte concentrations with signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios of 3 and 10, respectively. The accuracy was evaluated by spiking recovery experiments, which entailed the addition of three distinct levels of standards (high, medium, and low) to breast milk samples. Each spiked and nonspiked sample was analyzed in duplicate. Spiking recoveries were calculated by subtracting the amounts of HMOs measured in the nonspiked samples from the amounts of HMOs measured in the corresponding spiked samples and comparing them to the known amount of the spiked standard. The precision was evaluated in terms of intra- and interday reproducibility using breast milk samples.

Breast Milk Microbiota Sequencing

Microbiota metagenomic DNA was extracted from 5 mL of milk samples using a QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands). Barcoded specific bacterial primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′)23 were used to PCR amplify the V3–V4 of 16S rRNA gene. Sequencing libraries were constructed, and paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform at Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Trimmomatic software was used to search and remove residual adaptors, and Cutadapt (v19.1) was used to identify and eliminate primer sequences.24 The DADA2 method in QIME2 (v2020.6) was used for quality filtering, sequence joining, and chimera removal, resulting in the generation of amplicon sequence variants. Taxonomic assignments were achieved using the Silva v138 database.25

The raw sequence data were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database under the project accession number BioProject ID PRJNA1085391.

Bioinformatic and Statistical Analysis of Microbiota Sequencing Data

The microbiota composition of each sample was calculated at the phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels. The abundance of each species in samples was obtained using QIIME2.0. Alpha diversity was analyzed using indices of ACE and Chao1 for richness and Shannon and Simpson for diversity. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was employed using both Bray–Curtis and weighted-Unifrac distances, with PERMANOVA being used to analyze the statistical significance between groups. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LDA LEfSe) algorithm is a high-dimensional approach for discovering and explaining of biomarkers. It identifies genomic features (genes, pathways, or taxa) that characterize differences between two or more biological conditions. The analysis was primarily designed to identify the species that exhibited a significant difference in abundance between groups. The threshold for significance was set at an LDA score >3. Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) is performed using Vegan (version 2.6-4) and ggplot2 (version 3.4.4) packages in R to analyze between human milk microbiota and HMOs. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to calculate the correlations between milk microbiota and HMOs, with the results visualized in correlation heat maps using Python 2.7.8. Adonis analyses based on Bray–Curtis distance were conducted using Adonis 3 function of the GUniFrac (version 1.8) package in R to ascertain the impact level of maternal and infant factors on milk microbiota composition.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Prior to selecting the most appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests, the Shapiro–Wilk test (for normality) and Levene test (for homogeneity of variances) were performed. For continuous variables, data are expressed as mean ± SD. If data were normally distributed, a t-test was employed; if not, they were expressed as a median (Quartile1, Quartile3). The Mann–Whitney test was employed to compare differences between two groups. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and analyzed using the chi-square test. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Figures were visualized using GraphPad Prism v8.3.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) and R software v4.3.1. Adjusted P (q) using the Benjamini–Hochberg’s false discovery rate method for microbiota differential analysis.

Information of Participants

A total of 235 mothers participated in a 6 week postpartum visit in the Maternal and Child Health Cohort of Shaanxi Province, China from 2021.1 to 2021.8. For the 95 exclusive breastfeeding mothers, there were 24 mothers excluded, including insufficient provision of breast milk (n = 8) and refusal to provide breast milk (n = 16). Finally, 71 mothers were suitable for analysis (Figure S1). The detailed information on maternal-infant characteristics is presented in Table 1. The maternal age and pregestational BMI were 29.0 ± 3.82 years and 22.2 ± 3.44 kg/m2, respectively. In this study, 52.1% of infants were born via vaginal delivery, 63.4% of pregnancies were first births, 49.3% of mothers had normal weight gain throughout the entire pregnancy, and 43.7% of infants were male.

Maternal Secretor Status Distribution Based on fut2 SNPs

To determine the secretor status of each mother, we sequenced the rs601338G > A, rs1047781A > T, and rs60266261G > A SNP sites on the fut2 gene. No SNPs were identified at the rs601338 or rs60266261 sites, whereas the A > T mutation at rs1047781 was observed in all 71 mothers. Of the 71 mothers, 56 mothers (78.9%, 22 AA genotyping and 34 AT genotyping) were secretor-positive, and 15 mothers (21.1%, genotype of TT) were nonsecretors. The genotype distribution was consistent with the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (χ2 = 0.077, P = 0.962).

Analysis of Microbiota in Breast Milk of 42 Days Postpartum

A brief analysis of human milk microbiota revealed that the microbial communities were dominated by the phylum of Proteobacteria (34.83%) and Firmicutes (20.52%), followed by Bacteroidetes (14.34%) and Actinobacteria (7.80%) (Figure 2A). At the genus level, the most prevalent genus was unclassified_Enterobacteriaceae (3.49%) and Streptococcus (2.17%), followed by Sphingomonas (1.93%) and Acinetobacter (1.92%) (Figure 2B).

Maternal Secretor Status Is Not Significantly Associated with Human Milk Microbiota

The status of maternal secretion had no effect on the alpha diversity of human milk microbiota. The microbiota richness in SE was found to be slightly lower than that of NS in Chao 1 (secretor vs nonsecretor: 1151 vs 1519, P = 0.066) and ACE (secretor vs nonsecretor: 1154 vs 1521, P = 0.064). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in microbiota diversity between two groups in terms of Simpson (secretor vs nonsecretor: 0.99 vs 0.99, P = 0.897) and Shannon (secretor vs nonsecretor: 8.85 vs 8.94, P = 0.884) (Figure S3). β-Diversity was employed to investigate the association between maternal secretion status and the overall microbial composition of breast milk samples. No differences in microbial profiles were observed between SE and NS using Bray–Curtis distances (R2 = 0.012 and P = 0.938). To further confirm the above results, the weighted-Unifrac dissimilarity metric was applied, which considers the phylogenetic relatedness of the biological community, and no significant difference was found (R2 = 0.011 and P = 0.666) (Figure S4). The relative abundances of bacteria at each taxonomic level were not statistically different. The composition of the top 10 bacteria at each taxonomic level is presented in Figure S5.

Relationship between HMOs and Human Milk Microbiota

The gradient length of DCA axis 1 (DCA1) was 5.14, indicating that the typical correlation analysis (CCA) was the most appropriate method for the relationship between human milk microbiota and HMOs. Seven HMOs were employed as explanatory variables, with the relative abundance of milk microbiota as an outcome variable. Samples were grouped by the secretion status (Figure 3). The results of the envfit test indicated that 2′-FL (R2 = 0.317 and P = 0.001), 3′-SL (R2 = 0.265 and P = 0.001), 6′-SL (R2 = 0.269 and P = 0.001), and LNFPI (R2 = 0.218 and P = 0.002) had a statistically significant effect on human milk microbiota. However, it revealed that 3′-FL (R2 = 0.004 and P = 0.873), LNFPII (R2 = 0.052 and P = 0.174), and LNT (R2 = 0.025 and P = 0.404) did not have a statistically significant effect on human milk microbiota. The seven HMOs were found to account for only 9.3% of the variation observed in the human milk microbiota, with the remaining 90.7% of the variation still unexplained.

Breast Milk Microbiota Was Affected by Pregnancy Weight Gain

The core milk microbiota, defined as a genus present in at least 90% of human milk samples with a mean relative abundance of ≥1%, are listed in Table S6. Thirteen core genera were identified and clustered using k-means clustering with Bray–Curtis distance. Two clusters were identified (Figures S6 and S7), and the highest mean relative abundance in cluster 1 (n = 20) was for unclassified Enterobacteriaceae, followed by Acinetobacter and Streptococcus, while the mean relative abundance of core genera in cluster 2 (n = 51) was highest for unclassified Muribaculaceae, followed by unclassified Lachnospiraceae and Bacillus (Figure S8). Notably, the relative abundance of the most common probiotics, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, was significantly higher in cluster 2 (Lactobacillus, 0.31% vs 0.07%, q = 0.006; Bifidobacterium, 0.26% vs 0.02%, q = 0.015) (Figure S8).

To identify maternal-infant factors associated with the milk microbiota composition, we first assessed their associations with the core milk microbiota cluster membership. Indeed, 62.7% of mothers in cluster 2 exhibited normal pregnancy weight gain, in contrast to 15% of mothers in cluster 1 (χ2 = 13.10 and P < 0.001). The mothers in cluster 2 were also older than in cluster 1 (27.4 ± 2.76 vs 29.7 ± 4.00, t = −2.320, P = 0.023). Pregestational BMI, mode of delivery, maternal ABO blood types, infant birth weight, gestational age, infant gender, and parity were not associated with cluster membership (Table S8). Next, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (Adonis) using the Bray–Curtis distance metric was conducted to determine the contributions of maternal and infant factors to the variation in breast milk microbiota (Table 3). Only the types of gestational weight gain significantly influenced breast milk microbiota (P = 0.019).

To investigate the effect of weight gain type during pregnancy on the overall structure of breast milk microbiota, CCA was performed using normal and abnormal weight gains during pregnancy. The results indicated that 1.8% of breast milk microbiota structure was explained by weight gain during pregnancy, while the remaining 98.2% was unexplained. The permutation replacement test demonstrated a statistically significant effect of the type of weight gain during pregnancy on the structure of breast milk microbiota (F = 1.259 and P = 0.038).

Discussion

Human breast milk contains a complex bacterial community and biomolecular active compounds that are essential for infants’ development. The findings of our study indicate the associations among fut2 polymorphisms, breast milk bacteria, and HMOs. Furthermore, our results provide additional evidence for the influence of maternal factors (gestational weight gain) on the composition of breast milk bacteria.

SNPs of fut2 in Chinese mothers were reflected at rs1047781 (A385T)19 and validated in our data. The secretor status according to the genotype was also consistent with the production of fucosylated oligosaccharides of 2′-FL and LNFPI, except 2 mothers in the nonsecretor genotype who showed secretor phenotype (2′-FL of 148 and 1050 μg/mL, respectively). In a study of 44 mothers conducted by Lewis et al.,14 discrepancy was observed between the phenotypes of HMOs and maternal fut2 genotypes at four time points. In two cases, the genotype was a secretor, yet the phenotype was a nonsecretor. Nevertheless, this phenomenon was observed at only a single time point. At subsequent time points, the HMOs of both mothers were shown to be secretors. This study indicates that the nonsecretor phenotype may undergo changes during lactation in some individuals, which need to be validated in the follow-up studies.

Our study indicated that 3′-FL and LNFPII in breast milk were significantly higher in nonsecretor mothers than in secretor mothers, which is similar to previous studies.13,15,26 The relative levels of fucosylated HMOs are determined by the competition of fucosylases for a limited substrate. Other fucosyltransferases compete with FucT-II for the fucosylated substrate. In the absence of the FucT-II enzyme in nonsecretor mothers, other fucosylases are responsible for the production of additional fucosylated HMOs, including α-1,3/4 fucosylated HMOs 3′-FL and LNFPII, which are essential for infant health. It may be a kind of compensatory mechanism from the mother to offspring.

For the breast milk microbiota, the reports were different in studies.1,4,27 The existing studies have focused on the association between maternal secreting status and infant gut microbiota, while the findings have been inconsistent.28,29 There have been few studies investigating the correlation between maternal secreting status and breast milk microbiota. It is interesting to find that the diversity of breast milk microbiota was not significantly affected by the mother secreting status. This finding is consistent with previous reports.4,30 The breast milk microbiota of NS exhibited lower levels of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus SE.

Our result provides new evidence for potential interactions between HMOs and breast milk bacteria. The concentration and constitution of HMOs influence the composition of the breast milk microbiota. 2′-FL, LNFPI, 3′-SL, and 6′-SL exert a statistically significant influence on both the overall compositional structure of microbiota and specific genera in breast milk. The significant associations between the four HMO concentrations and the relative abundances of the majority of the top 40 genera were positive. In a previous study,31 negative correlations were observed between unclassified Enterobacteriaceae, Staphylococcus, and overall HMOs. Conversely, Lactobacillus demonstrated a significant positive correlation with both 2′-FL and LNFPI. Nevertheless, in our study, only Staphylococcus demonstrated a significant positive correlation only with 3′-SL regardless of the secretor status. The genera Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Akkermansia, as commonly beneficial probiotics, are associated with single HMOs. However, after stratification by secretor status and correlation analysis, the correlation between HMOs and breast milk microbiota was strengthened in secretor mothers and weakened in nonsecretor mothers. The presence of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in secretor breast milk exhibits a significant positive correlation with LNT and LNFPI. While Bifidobacteria can utilize HMOs, not all strains of this bacterium possess this ability. B. bifidum and B. infantis are capable of utilizing HMOs with different structures modified by different motifs. B. longum and B. breve are only able to metabolize LNT, lactose-N-disaccharide (LNB), and lactose-N-neotetrasaccharide (LNnT) and are unable to utilize fucosylated or sialylated HMOs.32 Further macrogenomic and/or culturomics studies are necessary to confirm the specific strains. As a mixture, there was no association between HMOs composition with breast milk microbiota, while the individual HMO showed a notable association with breast milk microbiota. This is likely due to the inverse effect of different single HMOs on the breast milk microbiota, which resulted in the disappearance of associations of the overall.

Except HMOs, the nine maternal and infant factors affecting breast milk microbiota were also analyzed. The composition and diversity of breast milk microbiota are significantly influenced by weight gain during pregnancy. The low and excessive weight gain were all considered abnormal weight gain, and their breast milk microbiota are more similar (Figure S11). Mothers who experience abnormal weight gain during pregnancy exhibit a reduction in the diversity of their breast milk microbiota. Furthermore, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Ligilactobacillus are significantly reduced, while Staphylococcus and Prevotella are significantly elevated in mothers with abnormal weight gain. Bifidobacteria and Lactobacilli are common probiotics that have been demonstrated to promote human health. Ligilactobacillus is also a probiotic with antimicrobial activity, immunological effects and the capacity to regulate intestinal microbiota.33 For the diversity of breast milk microbiota and pregnancy weight gain, Cortes-Macias et al.34 reported that breast milk microbiota diversity was negatively impacted by excess weight gain. Nevertheless, our study showed a more consistent difference in the bacterial composition. In mothers with excess weight gain, Cortes-Macias et al.34 observed lower levels of Bifidobacterium and Ralstonia in breast milk; Cabrera-Rubio et al.31 found higher levels of Staphylococcus and lower levels of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium.

The composition of maternal gut microbiota and maternal diet may explain the minor effects of maternal weight status on breast milk microbiota. Individuals with obesity have gut microbiota with decreased Bacteroidetes and increased Firmicutes, which alter microbial gene expression, resulting in increased energy uptake. Maternal weight is associated with alterations in the gut microbiota during pregnancy and the postpartum period. A higher prevalence of Staphylococcus and a lower prevalence of Bifidobacterium had been observed in gut microbiota of pregnant women with obesity and excessive gestational weight gain.35,36 It has been demonstrated that maternal gut microbiota associated with excess weight gain may be transferred to breast milk microbiota through the enteromammary pathway.37 Conversely, the maternal diet may influence the composition of milk microbiota, either directly or indirectly through alterations in milk composition such as milk macronutrient profiles and HMOs. Dietary intake during pregnancy is associated with gestational weight gain,38 which may explain weight-related differences in the breast milk microbiota. The management of weight during pregnancy is of great significance for the health of both the mother and infant. The results presented here provide new evidence from a breast milk perspective. Given the complexity of breast milk composition and the high diversity of breast milk microbiota structures, further research is required to ascertain which factors are more likely to influence the breast milk microbiota and how these changes affect neonatal and maternal health during pregnancy and childbirth.

In conclusion, the secretion of fut2 significantly and only affects the fucosylated HMOs. There are notable discrepancies in the total amount of HMOs, the quantity, and composition of fucosylated HMOs in secretor and nonsecretor mothers. While alternations in fucosylated HMOs are strongly correlated with the presence of Bifidobacterium spp. and Lactobacillus spp. in breast milk microbiota. Mother’s secretor status does not influence the overall structure of milk microbiota, especially the core microbiota. Abnormal weight gain during pregnancy has been demonstrated to significantly affect the composition of breast milk microbiota with lactic acid bacteria being particularly susceptible. Further research is necessary to elucidate the potential effects of these maternal factors on breast milk composition, infant development, and health.
