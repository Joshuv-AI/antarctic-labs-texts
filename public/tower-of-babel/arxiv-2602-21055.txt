Adjacency Spectral Embeddings of Correlation Networks
Keith Levin

Abstract
In many applications, weighted networks are constructed based on time series data: each time series is associated to a vertex and edge weights are given by pairwise correlations. The result is a network whose edge dependency structure violates the assumptions of most common network models. Nonetheless, it is common to analyze these “correlation networks” using embedding methods derived from edge-independent network models, based on a belief that the edges are approximately independent. In this work, we put this modeling choice on firm theoretical ground. We show that when the time series are expressible in terms of a small number of Fourier basis elements (or in some other suitably-chosen basis), correlation networks correspond to latent space networks with dependent edge noise in which the vertex-level latent variables encode the basis coefficients. Further, we show that when time series are observed subject to noise, spectral embedding of the resulting noisy correlation network still recovers these true vertex-level latent representations under suitable assumptions. This characterization of embeddings as learning Fourier coefficients appears to be folklore in the signal processing community in the context of principal component analysis, but is, to the best of our knowledge, new to the statistical network analysis literature.

In all of these example settings, the networks under study have weighted edges, with weights given by correlations between sequences.

The goal of the present work is to put this folklore on firm ground by studying the properties of these correlation networks.

2
Model Setup and Main Results

Consider a collection of
n
length-
T
discrete-time time series, so that for each
i\in[n]
and
t\in[T]
,
Z^{\star}_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}
is the value of the
i
-th time series at time
t\in[T]
.
We write
{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
to denote the
i
-th time series, written as a
T
-dimensional vector.
We model these time series as subject to noise, so that our observed data takes the form
{\mathbf{Z}_{i}}={\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}+\mathbf{N}_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
, where
\mathbf{N}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
is a vector of measurement noise (precise noise assumptions are given in Section
2.2
).
For example, in our neuroimaging example discussed in Section
1
, the true signal
{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}
corresponds to underlying brain activity in the brain region indexed by
i\in[n]
, while the noisy signal
{\mathbf{Z}_{i}}
corresponds to the measured blood oxygen level in brain region
i
.
Collecting the true signals
{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}
into the rows of
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
and forming matrices
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
and
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
analogously,
we may write

\mathbf{Z}={\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}+\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}.

(2)

In the applications discussed in Section
1
, it is common to form a weighted network with vertices
i\in[n]
by assigning edge weights via correlation among these time series.
That is, ignoring measurement noise for now, we form a weighted network with adjacency matrix

\mathbf{R^{\star}}=\left[R^{\star}_{i,j}\right]=\left[\operatorname{Corr}({\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}},{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{j}})\right]=\left[\frac{\operatorname{Cov}({\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}},{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{j}})}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

(3)

where

{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Z^{\star}_{i,t}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}Z^{\star}_{i,s}\right)^{2}.

(4)

We refer to a network with adjacency matrix as in Equation (
3
) as a
correlation network
, in light of the fact that its edge weights encode correlations between time series.

Remark 1
.

On first glance, it is tempting to interpret
{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}
as simply a variance.
However, since we interpret
{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
as encoding network structure, rather than random noise,
{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}
is better viewed as a measure of signal strength.
Indeed,
{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}
corresponds precisely to the power in the signal
{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}
after centering about its time-average
(
50
)
.

Defining the “centering” matrix

\mathbf{M}=\frac{1}{T}\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{J}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T},

(5)

we may write
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
as a matrix product,

\mathbf{R^{\star}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}\mathbf{M}{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2},

(6)

where

\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}=\operatorname{diag}\left({\sigma^{\star}_{1}}^{2},{\sigma^{\star}_{2}}^{2},\dots,{\sigma^{\star}_{n}}^{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

(7)

Of course, in practice, we do not observe the true signals in
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
, but instead observe
\mathbf{Z}
as in Equation (
2
), and our resulting correlation network has an adjacency matrix given by

\mathbf{R}=\frac{1}{T}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Z}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2},

(8)

where

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\sigma_{1}^{2},\sigma_{2}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(9)

is the diagonal matrix of (noisy) variances,

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(Z_{i,t}-\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}Z_{i,s}\right)^{2}.

(10)

In the applications in Section
1
, one observes a collection of time series and forms a correlation network with adjacency matrix as in Equation (
3
) or (
8
).
We then apply an embedding method to this network.
Our goal in the present work is to address two questions:

1.

Is there a sense in which
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
is driven by a low-dimensional latent structure, analogous to the role played by
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
in Equation (
1
)?

2.

Under what conditions does an embedding of the noisy correlation network
\mathbf{R}
in Equation (
8
) recover this latent structure, and at what rate?

Below, we address both of these questions as they pertain to the adjacency spectral embedding.
Given a network with adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the
d
-dimensional ASE produces an
n
-by-
d
matrix whose rows encode
d
-dimensional embeddings of the vertices:

\operatorname{ASE}(\mathbf{A},d)=\mathbf{V}~|\mathbf{D}|^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

(11)

where
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the diagonal matrix of the
d
largest-magnitude eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
contains as its columns the corresponding eigenvectors.
Under a variety of network models,
\mathbf{A}
can be expressed as in Equation (
1
), and
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
is naturally viewed as a low-rank structure underlying the observed network.
As such,
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
is a natural target for estimation and inference.
Of course, estimation of
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
is possible only up to right-multiplication by an orthogonal matrix, since for any
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{O}_{d}
,

{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}^{\top}={\mathbf{X^{\star}}}\mathbf{Q}({\mathbf{X^{\star}}}\mathbf{Q})^{\top}.

Under suitable assumptions on
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
and
\mathbf{E}
in Equation (
1
)
(
45
;
5
;
54
;
53
;
39
;
21
, see, e.g.,)
,

\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{O}_{d}}\left\|\operatorname{ASE}(\mathbf{A},d)\mathbf{Q}-{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}\right\|_{{2,\infty}}=o_{P}\left(1\right).

That is, the ASE recovers the latent structure
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
, up to orthogonal non-identifiability, uniformly over the vertices
i\in[n]
.
Our goal is to identify a low-dimensional structure analogous to
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
that gives rise to
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
, and to show that the ASE of
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
recovers this structure (up to non-identifiability constraints) under suitable assumptions.
We focus here on the ASE owing to its comparative simplicity, but we anticipate that much of our analysis can be extended to other spectral embeddings and related methods
(
62
;
49
;
54
;
24
;
42
;
56
;
73
, e.g.,)

2.1
Latent structure in correlation networks

To understand the low-dimensional structure encoded in
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
, we will aim to mimic the construction in Equation (
1
) and write
\mathbf{R^{\star}}={\mathbf{X^{\star}}}{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}^{\top}
for some choice of
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where
d\ll n
.
Toward this end, define

{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T},

(12)

noting that the
i
-th row of this matrix,
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}}^{\star}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
, corresponds to a centered, normalized version of the
i
-th observed time series
{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
Since
\mathbf{M}
is idempotent, we may rewrite Equation (
3
) as

\mathbf{R^{\star}}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}\mathbf{M}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}\mathbf{M}\right)^{\top}={\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star\top}}}.

(13)

Applying the discrete Fourier transform, we can express, for any
i\in[n]
and
t\in[T]
,

\widetilde{Z}^{\star}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{T}F^{\star}_{i,k}\exp\left\{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{T}(k-1)(t-1)\right\},

(14)

where
F^{\star}_{i,k}
denotes the
(k-1)
-th Fourier coefficient of the
i
-th standardized time series
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}}^{\star}_{i}}
for each
k\in[T]
.
That is,

F^{\star}_{i,k}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\widetilde{Z}^{\star}_{i,t}\exp\left\{\frac{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{T}(k-1)(t-1)\right\}.

(15)

We note that under this convention,
F^{\star}_{i,1}
is the Fourier coefficient of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}_{i}
associated to frequency zero, which is
T
times its mean.
Since
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}_{i}
is standardized, we have
F^{\star}_{i,1}=0
.

Denote by
\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{T\times T}
the (unitary) inverse discrete Fourier transform matrix, with entries

\Omega_{k,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\exp\left\{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{T}(k-1)(t-1)\right\},~k,t\in[T].

Examining Equation (
14
), we see that we can express
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
as

{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{\Omega}.

Substituting this into Equation (
6
),

\mathbf{R^{\star}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{\Omega}\right)^{\top}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{\Omega}\right)^{\text{H}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}^{\text{H}},

(16)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
is real
and the last equality follows from the fact that
\mathbf{\Omega}
is unitary.

Since
\mathbf{F^{\star}}
encodes the Fourier transforms of real sequences, basic symmetry properties of the discrete Fourier transform
(
50
, Chapter 8)
, along with the fact that the rows of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
are centered, imply

\overline{F^{\star}_{i,k}}=\begin{cases}F^{\star}_{i,k}=0&\mbox{ if }k=1\\
F^{\star}_{i,T-k+2}&\mbox{ if }k\in\{2,3,\dots,T\}.\end{cases}

(17)

Define a matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T}
according to

K_{s,t}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if }s=t=1\\
1&\mbox{ if }s+t=T+2\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

(18)

and observe that by Equation (
17
),
\mathbf{F^{\star}}^{\text{H}}=\left(\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}\right)^{\top}
. Substituting this into Equation (
16
) and observing that
\mathbf{K}
is a permutation matrix and thus has a square root
\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{C}^{T\times T}
,

\mathbf{R^{\star}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\left(\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right)^{\top},

(19)

Basic properties of the Fourier transform yield the following result.
A detailed proof is given in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 1
.

If the matrix
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
has all real entries, then so does the matrix
\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}
, and these entries are given by

\left[\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right]_{i,k}=\Re\left(F^{\star}_{i,k}\right)-\Im\left(F^{\star}_{i,k}\right)~~~i\in[n],k\in[T].

(20)

In light of Lemma
1
and Equation (
19
), we see that
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
is indeed encoded by a low-rank structure if
\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}
is low rank.
Suppose that there exist at most
d_{0}
non-zero columns of
\mathbf{F^{\star}}
.
That is, suppose that there exists a collection of
d_{0}
Fourier basis elements whose span contains all of the rows in
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
.
Equations (
17
) and (
20
) imply the existence of
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{T}
such that the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}
encodes, up to scaling, the real and imaginary parts of the Fourier coefficients of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}_{i}
, and
\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}
has at most
2d_{0}
non-zero columns.
It follows that
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
has rank
d\leq 2d_{0}
, since from Equation (
19
), we have

\mathbf{R^{\star}}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\left(\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\right)^{\top}=\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\left(\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{W}\right)^{\top}.

Thus, we may further write

\mathbf{R^{\star}}={\mathbf{U^{\star}}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\star}}{\mathbf{U^{\star}}}^{\top},

(21)

where
{\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\star}}=\operatorname{diag}({\lambda^{\star}_{1}},{\lambda^{\star}_{2}},\dots,{\lambda^{\star}_{d}})
contains the
d\leq 2d_{0}
non-zero eigenvalues of
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
on its diagonal and
{\mathbf{U^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
has the
d
corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors as its columns.
It follows that we may define

{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}={\mathbf{U^{\star}}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\star}}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

(22)

to be the “population embedding” of our (centered, scaled) time series data
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
based on the correlation matrix
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
, analogous to the low-rank matrix in Equation (
1
).
Since

{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}^{\top}={\mathbf{U^{\star}}}{\mathbf{\Lambda}^{\star}}{\mathbf{U^{\star}}}^{\top}=\mathbf{R^{\star}}={\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star\top}}}=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right)^{\top}

and all entries of
\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}
are real,
there must exist
\mathbf{\widetilde{W}}\in\mathbb{O}_{T}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{\widetilde{W}}.

We have shown the following.

Lemma 2
.

If the standardized time series in the rows of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
can be represented in a
d_{0}
-dimensional Fourier basis,
then there exists a
d\leq 2d_{0}
and
\mathbf{\widetilde{W}}\in\mathbb{O}_{T}
such that

\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{ASE}(\mathbf{R^{\star}},d)&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{\widetilde{W}}.

That is, the
d
-dimensional ASE of
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
corresponds precisely to the Fourier representation of the signals in
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
, up to orthogonal non-identifiability.

Said another way, Lemma
2
states that the Fourier coefficients of the signals in
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
correspond to a low-rank latent representation of the vertices in the correlation network
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
.

Remark 2
.

While our discussion above focused on representing the standardized time series
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
in the Fourier basis, the same basic argument applies to any basis that we might consider (e.g., wavelets or the discrete cosine transform).
While we restrict our attention to the Fourier basis for the sake of concreteness, our results and analysis are largely agnostic to this choice of basis.
Key to our results is that there exist some unitary matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}
such that the rows of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}\mathbf{\Omega}^{\text{H}}
encode the observed time series in the chosen basis.

2.2
Recovering low-dimensional latent structure

Lemmas
1
and
2
indicate that the noiseless correlation network
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
has a low-rank latent structure given by Fourier transforms of the time series in the rows of
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
.
We turn our attention now to the matter of recovering this latent structure from
\mathbf{R}
, the noisy version of
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
, as given in Equation (
8
).
Following previous work on network embeddings
(
58
;
38
;
62
;
40
)
, we wish to study the adjacency spectral embedding of the observed correlation matrix
\mathbf{R}
as defined in Equation (
8
).
In particular, write

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^{\top}+\mathbf{U}_{\perp}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\perp}\mathbf{U}_{\perp}^{\top},

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}({\lambda_{1}},{\lambda_{2}},\dots,{\lambda_{d}})
contains the

3
Experiments

We turn now to a brief exploration of our theoretical results.
In particular, we are interested in verifying the estimation bound in Theorem
1
, which predicts the rate at which
\operatorname{ASE}(\mathbf{R},d)
should recover
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
once we account for orthogonal non-identifiability.
When evaluating an estimate
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
of
{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}

2

{\mathbf{X^{\star}}}
and
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
are equal up to orthogonal rotation, and assess estimation error by

\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{O}_{T}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{Y}&{\bf 0}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{W}-{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}\right\|_{{2,\infty}},

{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}

In what follows, we compare the ASE against two baseline methods for estimating
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}

The first is based on applying

\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}

Note that we perform a scaled PCA, with the eigenvectors scaled by the square roots of the eigenvalues.
In essence, this PCA-based estimate reverses the steps of the ASE by applying eigenvalue truncation
before
performing row normalization.
Our second comparison is with a naïve method, whereby we simply use

\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T},

as our estimate of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}

While such an estimate fails to yield dimensionality reduction the way that the ASE or other PCA-based methods might, it serves as a baseline comparison to indicate whether an estimator is successfully removing noise from the observed signals.

4
Discussion and Conclusion

Network embedding methods with implicit edge independence assumptions are frequently applied to correlation networks that clearly violate edge independence.
Practitioners nonetheless frequently apply these methods to correlation networks, under the intuition that the dependence among the edges is ignorable.
We have presented an analysis of the behavior of spectral embeddings as applied to correlation networks that puts this intuition on firm ground.
We have shown that correlation networks built from collections of time series have a clear interpretation as having latent positions encoding the Fourier coefficients of the time series, and we have shown that when noisily-observed time series are used to build a correlation network, the ASE provably recovers the true underlying low-rank structure.

There are several interesting directions for future work.
We conjecture that the rate in Theorem
1
is minima optimal, up to logarithmic factors and dependence on the condition number
\kappa
.
Establishing a minimax lower bound is the focus of ongoing follow-up work, starting from the arguments developed in
66
;
67
.
We expect that the dependence on
\kappa
can be relaxed, and perhaps removed entirely, with suitably careful bookkeeping
(
51
, see, e.g.,)
.
If dependence on
\kappa
cannot be eliminated, future work should investigate whether approaches similar to
74
can be used as a preprocessing step to remove the effect of condition number.
As alluded to when comparing our results to the heteroscedastic PCA literature, it is interesting to note that Theorem
1
does not suggest a bias introduced by heteroscedasticity in the rows of
\mathbf{N}
.
Future work should more carefully analyze how, if at all, heteroscedasticity contributes to the estimation rate in Theorem
1
and whether, in the event that there is structure present in the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{R^{\star}}
(i.e., the left singular vectors of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
), this structure can be leveraged to achieve improved estimation, analogously to recent results in low-rank matrix estimation under independent noise
(
67
;
68
)
.
Finally, as discussed in Section
2
, embeddings of correlation networks and PCA applied directly to
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
both provably learn low-rank representations of the time series.
Future work should characterize when one or the other of these two methods is preferable, analogous to the study in
(
52
)
comparing the ASE to the Laplacian spectral embedding
(
62
)
.

\ell\in\{2,3,\dots,\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor\}

\left[\left(\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)\right]_{i}=F^{\star}_{i,\ell}-F^{\star}_{i,T-\ell+2}+\overline{F^{\star}_{i,\ell}}-\overline{F^{\star}_{i,T-\ell+2}}=0,

and therefore, using the decomposition in Equation (
31
) and recalling Equation (
33
),

\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lceil(T-1)/2\rceil}\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}+\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}+\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell}\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}.

(35)

We note that this quantity is purely real, since
(\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}})/2
is the real part of
\mathbf{F^{\star}}
.

Similarly, for any
i\in[n]
and any
\ell\in\{2,3,\dots,\lceil(T-1)/2\rceil\}
,

\left[\left(\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}+\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)\right]_{i}=F^{\star}_{i,\ell}+F^{\star}_{i,T-\ell+2}-\left(\overline{F^{\star}_{i,\ell}}+\overline{F^{\star}_{i,T-\ell+2}}\right)=0,

from which

\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor}\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)^{\top},

(36)

which is purely real, since
\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}
is purely imaginary.
Decomposing
\mathbf{F^{\star}}
into its real and imaginary parts as

\mathbf{F^{\star}}=\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}+\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2},

Equations (
35
) and (
36
) imply that

\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}=\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)\mathbf{K}^{1/2}+\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)\mathbf{K}^{1/2}

has no imaginary component, and thus
\mathbf{F^{\star}}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}
has all real entries.

Turning our attention to Equation (
20
), we again use the fact that

\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor}\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)^{\top}

to write, for
i\in[n]
and
k\in\{2,3,\dots,T\}
,

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right]_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor}\sum_{m=1}^{T}\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)_{i,m}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)_{m}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor}\left[\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)_{i,\ell}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)_{k}-\left(\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\right)_{i,T-\ell+2}\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor}\left(F^{\star}_{i,\ell}-F^{\star}_{i,T-\ell+2}\right)\left(\mathbf{e}_{\ell}-\mathbf{e}_{T-\ell+2}\right)_{k},

where the last equality follows from Equation (
34
).
Noting that the only contributing terms in the sum occur when
k=\ell
or
k=T-\ell+2
, only one of which is possible, since
\ell\leq\lfloor(T-1)/2\rfloor
, and using Equation (
34
) again,

\left[\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}-\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right]_{i,k}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{2}\left(F^{\star}_{i,k}-\overline{F^{\star}_{i,k}}\right)=-\Im\left(F^{\star}_{i,k}\right).

A parallel argument yields that

\left[\frac{\mathbf{F^{\star}}+\overline{\mathbf{F^{\star}}}}{2}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right]_{i,k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(F^{\star}_{i,k}+\overline{F^{\star}_{i,k}}\right),

the real part of
F^{\star}_{i,k}
.
Combining the above two displays yields Equation (
20
).
∎

Appendix B
Additional Numerical Experiments

Here we collect additional numerical experiments in support of our theoretical findings.

B.1
Effect of Noise Tail Decay

Our experiments in the main text focus on the setting where the entries of the noise matrix
\mathbf{N}
are generated from a Gaussian.
While our theoretical results require that each row
\mathbf{N}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
be a subgaussian random variable, we suspect that this can be relaxed substantially.
In what follows, we explore the effect of replacing this Gaussian noise with Laplacian noise.
We generate the rows of
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
by drawing i.i.d. from a standard normal and renormalizing so that
\|{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}\|_{F}=n
, so that the average row norm of
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
is
1
, but note that the row norms of
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}
are not necessarily of the same order.
We then generate
\mathbf{Z}={\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}+\mathbf{N}
, with the entries of
\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
drawn i.i.d. from either a Gaussian or a Laplacian distribution with variance
\nu
.
Note that unlike the experiments in Section
3.1
, we do not change the variance of the entries of
\mathbf{N}
from row to row to fix
\gamma
.
We conducted this experiment with
n=100
and
T=100,500,1000
for varying choices of variance
\nu
, with
50
independent Monte Carlo trials for each combination of conditions.
Figure
5
summarizes the results of this experiment, showing estimation error in
({2,\infty})
-norm as a function of
\nu
for the ASE as well as the PCA-based estimate and naïve baseline described in Section
3
.
Note that the positive direction of the x-axis corresponds to
decreasing
values of the SNR-like quantity
\gamma
.
Examining the figure, we note that the performance of the ASE is largely insensitive to whether the observation noise is Gaussian or Laplacian, supporting our conjecture that our results can be extended to subexponential or subgamma noise.
It is interesting to note, as an aside, that the poor performance of the PCA-based method at smaller variances only appears in the case of Gaussian noise.
We suspect that this is due to the fact that our PCA-based method renormalizes the rows of
{\mathbf{Z^{\star}}}

after
performing rank truncation.
In the situation where one or more rows of
\mathbf{Z}
are much smaller than the others and the noise is small, rank truncation may be sending one or more of these rows to be close to zero, with the result that they are especially bad estimates of the corresponding rows of
{\mathbf{\widetilde{Z}^{\star}}}
once the rows are renormalized.
The heavier tails of the Laplacian noise prevent this from happening.

Appendix C
Estimating Signal Variances

Here we collect results relating the signal powers
{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}
as defined in Equation (
4
) to their empirical estimates
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, as defined in Equation (
10
).
These technical results will be used in the technical appendices to follow, culminating in the proof of Theorem
1
given in Appendix
F
.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
Then with probability at least
1-O(n^{-2})
,

\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{\|\mathbf{N}_{i}\|^{2}}{\nu_{i}}\leq CT\log n.

Proof.

By assumption, for each
i\in[n]
,
\mathbf{N}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
is a
\nu_{i}
-subgaussian random vector.
By a standard
\varepsilon
-net argument
[see, e.g.,
64
, Chapter 4]
, for any
\tau>0
,

\Pr\left[\left\|\mathbf{N}_{i}\right\|\geq\tau\right]\leq C_{0}\exp\left\{T-\frac{C_{1}\tau^{2}}{\nu_{i}}\right\},

for suitably-chosen positive constants
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
.
Taking
\tau=\sqrt{C\nu_{i}T\log n}
for
C>0
suitably large and rearranging,

\Pr\left[\frac{\left\|\mathbf{N}_{i}\right\|^{2}}{\nu_{i}}\geq CT\log n\right]\leq Cn^{-3}.

A union bound over all
i\in[n]
then yields that with probability at least
1-O(n^{-2})
,

\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{N}_{i}\right\|^{2}}{\nu_{i}}\leq CT\log n,

as we set out to show.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
Then with high probability, it holds uniformly over all
i\in[n]
that

\left|\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\right|\leq C\nu_{i}\log n+\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\nu_{i}\log n}{T}}

Proof.

For
i\in[n]
, recalling
{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}
and
\sigma_{i}^{2}
from Equations (
4
) and (
10
), respectively,

\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{i,t}^{2}+\frac{2}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{i,t}Z^{\star}_{i,t}-\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{i,t}\right)^{2}-\frac{2\mu_{i}}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}N_{i,t}.

Rearranging and recalling the centering matrix
\mathbf{M}
from Equation (
5
),

\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}=\frac{1}{T}\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{N}_{i}\|^{2}+\frac{2}{T}\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}.

(37)

By assumption, each
\mathbf{N}_{i}
is a
\nu_{i}
-subgaussian random vector.
Noting that
\|\mathbf{M}{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}\|^{2}=T{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}
,
standard concentration inequalities
[
64
]
yield that with high probability,

\left|\frac{2}{T}\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{M}{\mathbf{Z}^{\star}_{i}}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\nu_{i}{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\log n}~\text{ for all }~i\in[n].

Taking absolute values in Equation (
37
) and applying the above bound,

\left|\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\right|\leq\frac{1}{T}\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{N}_{i}\|^{2}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{T}}\sqrt{\nu_{i}{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\log n}~\text{ for all }~i\in[n].

(38)

Applying submultiplicativity followed by Lemma
3
,

\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{N}_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|\mathbf{N}_{i}\right\|^{2}\leq C\max_{i\in[n]}\nu_{i}T\log n.

Applying this bound to Equation (
38
), it holds with high probability that

\left|\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\right|\leq C\left(\nu_{i}\log n+\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\nu_{i}\log n}{T}}\right)~\text{ for all }~i\in[n],

as we set out to show.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
Then with high probability, it holds uniformly over all
i\in[n]
that

\max\left\{\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}-1\right|,\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}-1\right|\right\}\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{\gamma}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{T\gamma}}\right).

(39)

Further, with high probability,

\max_{i\in[n]}\max\left\{\left|\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}{\sigma_{i}}\right|,\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}\right|\right\}\leq C.

(40)

Proof.

By Lemma
4
, it holds uniformly over all
i\in[n]
that

\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}-C\left(\nu_{i}\log n+\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\nu_{i}\log n}{T}}\right)={\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\left(1-\frac{C\nu_{i}\log n}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}-C\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}\frac{\log n}{T}}\right).

Applying our growth assumption in Equation (
28
), it follows that uniformly over
i\in[n]
,

\sigma_{i}^{2}\geq{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\left(1-o_{P}\left(1\right)\right).

(41)

For all
i\in[n]
, multiplying through by appropriate quantities, we have

\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}-1=\frac{\sigma_{i}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}\left({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\sigma_{i}\right)}.

(42)

Again using Lemma
4
,

\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}-1\right|\leq\frac{C}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}\left({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\sigma_{i}\right)}\left(\nu_{i}\log n+\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\nu_{i}\log n}{T}}\right).

Applying Equation (
41
) yields that

\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}-1\right|\leq C\left(\frac{\nu_{i}\log n}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{i}\log n}{T{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}}\right).

To see the same bound for
{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}/\sigma_{i}
, note that

\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}{\sigma_{i}}-1=\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}/{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}-1=\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}\right)\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}/{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}.

Applying Equation (
42
) and using our growth assumption in Equation (
28
) yields Equation (
39
).
Equation (
40
) then follows from the growth assumption in Equation (
28
).
∎

Lemma 6
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.
Then it holds with high probability that, uniformly over all
i,j\in[n]
,

\left|\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}-1\right|=O_{P}\left(\frac{\log n}{\gamma}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{T\gamma}}\right).

Proof.

We begin by noting that

\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}-1=\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}-\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}=\frac{({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}-\sigma_{i}){\sigma^{\star}_{j}}+\sigma_{i}({\sigma^{\star}_{j}}-\sigma_{j})}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}.

Multiplying through by appropriate quantities,

\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}-1=\frac{({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}){\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\sigma_{i})}+\frac{(\sigma_{j}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}^{2})}{\sigma_{j}({\sigma^{\star}_{j}}+\sigma_{j})}.

Applying the triangle inequality followed by Equation (
40
) from Lemma
5
,
it holds uniformly over all
i,j\in[n]
that

\left|\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}-1\right|\leq\frac{C\left|{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}-\sigma_{i}^{2}\right|}{\sigma_{i}({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\sigma_{i})}+\frac{\left|\sigma_{j}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}^{2}\right|}{\sigma_{j}({\sigma^{\star}_{j}}+\sigma_{j})}.

(43)

For ease of notation, for each
i\in[n]
define

B_{i}=\nu_{i}\log n+\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\nu_{i}\log n}{T}}.

(44)

By Lemma
4
, uniformly over all
i\in[n]
,

\left|\sigma_{i}^{2}-{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}\right|\leq CB_{i}.

Thus, applying this bound to Equation (
43
), it holds uniformly over all
i,j\in[n]
that

\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}-1\right|\leq\frac{CB_{i}}{\sqrt{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}-CB_{i}}\left({\sigma^{\star}_{i}}+\sqrt{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}-CB_{i}}\right)}+\frac{CB_{j}}{\sqrt{{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}^{2}-CB_{j}}\left({\sigma^{\star}_{j}}+\sqrt{{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}^{2}-CB_{j}}\right)}.

(45)

By our assumption in Equation (
28
),

\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{B_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}=o_{P}\left(1\right).

Applying this fact to Equation (
45
), it holds with high probability that,

\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}-1\right|\leq\frac{CB_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}+\frac{CB_{j}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}^{2}}~\text{ for all }~i,j\in[n].

Taking a maximum over all
i,j\in[n]
and recalling the definition of
B_{i}
from Equation (
44
),

\left|\frac{\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}{\sigma^{\star}_{j}}}-1\right|\leq C\left[\max_{i\in[n]}\frac{\nu_{i}\log n}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}+\max_{i\in[n]}\sqrt{\frac{\nu_{i}\log n}{T{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}}\right].

Recalling the definition of
\gamma
from Equation (
26
) completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold.

\|\mathbf{N}_{i}\|^{2}\leq C\nu_{i}T\log T.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.

\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{N}\right\|=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{T\frac{\log n}{\gamma}}\right).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Since the rows of
\mathbf{N}

[

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{u_{i}\mathbf{N}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}}\right|\leq C\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}\frac{\nu_{i}}{{\sigma^{\star}_{i}}^{2}}v_{i}^{2}\log n}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{\gamma}}.

Modifying the concentration inequality to encompass a union bound over
\varepsilon

\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{T}

[

]
, it holds with high probability that

\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{N}\right\|=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{(n+T)\log n}{\gamma}}\right).

Using our assumption that

completes the proof.

Lemma 10

Suppose that Assumption
1

\max\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathbf{N}\right\|,\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma^{\star}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{N}\right\|\right\}=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{T\log n}{\gamma}}\right)

and Lemma

completes the proof.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that Assumption
1
holds.
Then

\left\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R^{\star}}\right\|_{F}=o_{P}\left(\|\mathbf{R^{\star}}\|_{F}\right).

Proof.

For

i,j\in[n]
, noting that

and
\mathbf{R^{\star}}

have all diagonal entries equal to

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R^{\star}}\right\|_{F}^{2}
