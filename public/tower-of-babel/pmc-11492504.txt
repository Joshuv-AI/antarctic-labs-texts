Genomic identification, characterization, and stress-induced expression profiling of glyoxalase and D-lactate dehydrogenase gene families in Capsicum annuum
Authors: Md Sakil Arman, Asifur Rob Bhuya, Md. Rihan Kabir Shuvo, Md. Afser Rabbi, Ajit Ghosh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCapsicum annuum, a significant agricultural and nutritional crop, faces production challenges due to its sensitivity to various abiotic stresses. Glyoxalase (GLY) and D-lactate dehydrogenase (D-LDH) enzymes play vital roles in mitigating these stresses by detoxifying the stress-induced cytotoxin, methylglyoxal (MG).MethodsA genome-wide study was conducted to identify and characterize glyoxalase I (GLYI), glyoxalase II (GLYII), unique glyoxalase III or DJ-1 (GLYIII), and D-LDH gene candidates in Capsicum annuum. The identified members were evaluated based on their evolutionary relationships with known orthologues, as well as their gene and protein features. Their expression patterns were examined in various tissues, developmental stages, and in response to abiotic stress conditions using RNA-seq data and qRT-PCR.ResultsA total of 19 GLYI, 9 GLYII, 3 DJ-1, and 11 D-LDH members were identified, each featuring characteristic domains: glyoxalase, metallo-β-lactamase, DJ-1_PfpI, and FAD_binding_4, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis revealed distinct clades depending on functional diversification. Expression profiling demonstrated significant variability under stress conditions, underscoring their potential roles in stress modulation. Notably, gene-specific responses were observed with CaGLYI-2, CaGLYI-7, CaGLYII-6, CaDJ-1 A, and CaDLDH-1 showed upregulation under salinity, drought, oxidative, heat, and cold stresses, while downregulation were shown for CaGLYI-3, CaGLYII-1, CaDJ-1B, and CaDJ-1 C. Remarkably, CaGLYI-1 presented a unique expression pattern, upregulated against drought and salinity but downregulated under oxidative, heat, and cold stress.ConclusionThe identified GLY and D-LDH gene families in Capsicum annuum exhibited differential expression patterns under different abiotic stresses. Specifically, CaGLYI-2, CaGLYI-7, CaGLYII-6, CaDJ-1 A, and CaDLDH-1 were upregulated in response to all five analyzed abiotic stressors, highlighting their critical role in stress modulation amidst climate change. This study enhances our understanding of plant stress physiology and opens new avenues for developing stress-resilient crop varieties, crucial for sustainable agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05612-5.

Introduction

The glyoxalase pathway is an ancient metabolic pathway, conserved and ubiquitous among biological systems, such as humans, plants, and microbes, underlying its functional significance [1–3]. The primary function of this enzymatic pathway is to detoxify methylglyoxal (MG) into D-lactate [4]. MG, a renowned mutagen, is produced in biological systems as a derivate of a common metabolic byproduct that induces cellular damage and toxicity by generating free radicals and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [5]. In plant systems, the conventional glyoxalase I (GLYI) and glyoxalase II (GLYII) catalyze the conversion of MG-derived hemithioacetal (HTA) into D-lactate in two sequential reactions, utilizing reduced glutathione (GSH) as cofactor [3]. Plants also feature unique glyoxalase (GLYIII), a homolog of DJ-1 protein, which catalyzes the reaction in a single step without GSH [6–8]. The detoxification process culminates in converting D-lactate to pyruvate, a TCA-cycle intermediate, by D-lactate dehydrogenase (D-LDH) [9].

Plant GLYI proteins have been characterized by their reliance on metal ions, specifically bivalent Zn²⁺ or Ni²⁺, as co-factors [10, 11]. The activity and functionality of Ni²⁺-dependent OsGLYI-11, possessing two glyoxalase domains (PF00903), have been extensively characterized in rice [10]. Conversely, OsGLYI-8, a Zn²⁺-dependent glyoxalase with a single domain, regulates MG levels in the nucleus, similar to its orthologue AtGLYI-2 in Arabidopsis [11]. Characterized GLYII enzymes exhibit either conventional enzymatic activity, as OsGLYII-2 and OsGLYII-3 [12], or sulfur dioxygenase (SDO) activity, as OsGLYII-1 and AtGLYII-5 [13]. Meanwhile, glutathione-independent GLYIII (DJ-1 like) enzymes operate without any metal ion co-factors [8]. This reflects the divergence of the functionality and activity of glyoxalase in response to the physiological environment. D-LDH is classified by co-factor use: D-lactate ferricytochrome c oxidoreductase with cytochrome c or D-lactate NAD oxidoreductase with NAD. As part of the oxidoreductase protein superfamily, D-LDH enzymes feature the FAD_binding_4 domain (PF01565), which confers distinct catalytic functions [14]. However, OsD-LDH2 protein in rice, featuring both FAD_binding_4 and FAD-oxidase-C, was associated with enhanced MG detoxification, converting D-lactate to pyruvate, thus contributing to stress tolerance [15].

Accumulation of MG can result from environmental stresses (drought, salinity, temperature, and others), and reduce the growth and development of plants, ultimately affecting yields and productivity [3]. Previous literature has reported that the overexpression of GLY enzymes increased plant resistance to salinity, heat, and heavy metal stress [16–18], indicating the functionality of these genes in mitigating stresses. Overexpressed GLYIII (DJ-1) in transgenic sugarcane promoted plant growth under drought and salinity conditions [19]. Besides, silencing D-LDH genes in transgenic rice led to MG and D-Lactate accumulation and growth retardation, underlying the significance of D-LDH in MG detoxification [20]. Recent research involving gene pyramiding, which overexpressed D-LDH, GLYI, and GLYII genes in transgenic Arabidopsis, conferred enhanced stress modulation, highlighting their functional significance in plant survival [21]. Also, upregulation of GLYI and GLYII mitigates beryllium-induced toxicity in pepper when treated with pineapple fruit peel biochar and potassium silicate [22].

Prior research has identified MG detoxifying genes in diverse plant species, including Zea mays, Corchorus capsularis, Triticum sp., Aloe vera, Glycine max, Spinacia oleracea, Cicer sp., Nicotiana sp., Brassica sp., Pisum sativum, Amaranthus sp., Sorghum bicolor, and Solanum lycopersicum, highlighting their role in combating abiotic and biotic stress [14, 23–30]. A genome-wide study in Sorghum bicolor identified 15 GLYI, 6 GLYII, and 4 D-LDH family members, showing homology to functionally characterized GLYs and D-LDHs from Arabidopsis and rice [14]. In durum wheat, 27 GLYI candidates were identified, with significant upregulation under salt and osmotic stress [31]. Four GLYIII (DJ-1) genes had been characterized in date palm [32], which showed upregulation in drought and salinity. The OsD-LDH2 enzyme in rice also conferred abiotic stress tolerance, by maintaining cellular homeostasis and enhancing free radical scavenging [15]. Recently, 13 putative GLYI, 4 GLYII, 3 GLYIII (DJ-1), and 4 D-LDH genes were identified in tomato, and found to be upregulated in various stress and pathogenic infections [30].

This study focuses on characterizing the MG detoxifying gene family in Capsicum annum (Hot pepper), an economically significant crop extensively used in the food and pharmaceutical industries [33, 34] that are experiencing productivity losses due to environmental stresses including drought [35], extreme heat [36], and salinity [37]. Our genome-wide investigation has identified 19 GLYI, 9 GLYII, 3 GLYIII/DJ-1, and 11 D-LDH members through bioinformatics analysis. Detailed characterization of these genes and proteins, including their structure, domains, and evolutionary relationships, has yielded insights into their distinctive features. Expression analyses under various stress conditions have elucidated their roles in the stress response of plants. Therefore, this preliminary study might lay the groundwork for further investigation into the physiological purposes of GLY and D-LDH genes in plants for mitigating environmental stress.

Materials and methods

Prediction of MG detoxifying members in Capsicum annuum

For the identification of potential GLYI, GLYII, DJ-1, and D-LDH members in pepper, Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles specific to Glyoxalase (PF00903), Metallo-β-lactamase (PF00753), DJ-1/PfpI (PF01965.19), and FAD_binding_4 (PF01565) domains were retrieved from Pfam 36.0 (http://pfam.xfam.org) database [38]. The cultivated pepper Zunla-1 (C. annuum L.) sequence dataset (CaZL1.pep), as initially reported by Quin et al. 2014 [39], was retrieved from the Pepper Genome Platform (http://peppergenome.snu.ac.kr). HMMER v3.3.1 [40, 41] was employed to analyze the datasets, utilizing ‘hmmsearch’ with an E-value threshold of 0.1 to search against the HMM profiles. The identified sequences were subsequently verified for domain presence using SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de) [42, 43] and InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro) [44]. Classification of members was based on domain characteristics: GLYI with Glyoxalase domain, GLYII with Metallo-β-lactamase and HAGH_C domains, GLYIII or DJ-1 with DJ-1_PfpI, and D-LDH with FAD_binding_4 and FAD-oxidase_C domains. These identified members were named following the ‘Ca’ prefix, indicative of Capsicum annuum, and numerically ordered according to their similarity score from ‘hmmsearch’, with CaGLYI-1 assigned to the sequence with the highest score, followed by CaGLYI-2 for the next highest, and so on. Additionally, protein features e.g., molecular weight, isoelectric point, and subcellular localizations were predicted using Expasy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam) [45] and DeepLoc 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.0) [46], respectively.

Analysis of multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic relationship

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships of GLYI, GLYII, DJ-1, and D-LDH families among various plant species, their full-length amino acid sequences were aligned with corresponding orthologues using ClustalW in MEGA-11 [47]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed by applying the Maximum Likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates (cut-off value: 40%) to determine protein functional divergence and evolutionary lineage. The resulting phylogenetic trees were further refined and visualized with iTOL (https://itol.embl.de) [48]. Additionally, the amino acid sequences of specific protein domains were extracted from the SMART (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de) platform [42, 43] and were visualized using Jalview [49].

Gene structure and protein architecture analysis

The genomic structures of GLYs and D-LDH family members were elucidated by Gene Structure Display Server (https://gsds.gao-lab.org) [50] by comparing their genomic and coding DNA sequences (CDS). This analysis included details on UTRs, exon-intron organization, and intron phase locations. Domain positions for glyoxalase (PF00903), Metallo-β lactamase (PF00753), DJ-1/PfpI (PF01965.19), FAD_binding_4 (PF01565), and FAD-oxidase C (PF02913) were ascertained from SMART. The domain architecture of the identified members was visualized schematically using TBtools-II [51].

Gene expression profiling of putative glyoxalases and D-LDH genes

The expression pattern of the MG detoxifying gene families was investigated in Capsicum annuum by analyzing Illumina RNA-seq data sourced from PepperHub (http://lifenglab.hzau.edu.cn/PepperHub/index.php) [52]. The expression data encompassed a comprehensive range of 57 anatomical tissues and various abiotic stress conditions, including cold, heat, drought, and salinity, measured at 1, 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h, respectively. Relative expression changes were determined by comparing each time point to a 0 h control sample. Gene expression levels were standardized via Z-score normalization. The heatmaps were generated from this normalized data using MeV software [53] employing hierarchical clustering using Pearson correlation.

Plant materials, stress treatment, and quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Sterilized Capsicum annum L. seeds were cultivated in controlled growth chamber conditions, maintaining a day/night temperature regime of 26/20°C and a light/dark cycle of 16/8 hours, as reported by Guo et al. [54]. In the light of previous studies, 40-days-old mature seedlings, having at least 6–8 true leaves (n ~ 200), were selected for stress treatments as they exhibit better overall seedling quality and physiological responses [52, 55]. Approximately 10 seedlings were selected for each stress or control group, exposed to 200 mM NaCl (salt stress), 260 mM mannitol (drought stress), 10 mM H2O2 (oxidative stress), 4 °C (cold stress), and 42 °C (heat stress) for optimal responsiveness [56–58], with untreated seedlings serving as experimental control. Plants were stressed by watering them with additional stress inducers. Chilling/heating stresses were achieved by transferring plants to an identical chamber set to 4/42 ℃, respectively. Following a 12-hour stress exposure, leaf samples were harvested for RNA extraction using the SV Total RNA Isolation System (Promega Corporation, USA). RNA quantity and quality were ascertained via a NanoDrop device (Thermo Scientific). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed using 9 µg of RNA with the GoScript™ Reverse Transcriptase kit (Promega Corporation, USA). Primers targeting GLY and D-LDH genes were designed via NCBI Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) and synthesized by Macrogen (Table S1), with Actin (GenBank: GQ339766) as an internal control [59]. The quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses were executed using the QUANTSTUDIO® 3 REAL-TIME PCR System with SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RK21203), and the data was calculated using the 2−Δ ΔCT method [60].

Statistical analysis

Student’s t-test was employed to analyze the statistical significance of the gene expression value (n = 3) by comparing the treated samples with those of the untreated control samples. The p values ≤ 0.05, ≤ 0.01 or ≤ 0.001 were represented with *, **, or ***, respectively.

Results

Identification and analysis of putative GLYs and D-LDH members in pepper

A genome-wide analysis using HMM profiles PF00903, PF00753, PF01965.19, and PF01565 identified 19 GLYI, 9 GLYII, 3 GLYIII, and 11 D-LDH members with diverse gene and protein features (Table 1). The members in these gene families diverge in transcript length, CDS, isoelectric point, and subcellular localization, indicating their divergent roles within their respective families. The identified GLYI proteins in pepper (CaGLYI) displayed variations in size, comprising amino acids (aa) ranging from 363 aa to 137 aa, with an average length of 212 aa, corresponding to an average molecular weight of 23.87 kDa (Table 1). This similar scenario was observed for CaGLYII (max: 844 aa, min: 227 aa, average length: 497 aa, and average weight: 55.17 kDa, and CaDJ-1 (max: 456 aa, min: 375aa, average length: 423aa, and average weight: 45.20 kDa proteins, indicating potential functional divergence within these families (Table 1). Interestingly, five members of the CaGLYI gene family (CaGLYI-3, CaGLYI-9, CaGLYI-10, CaGLYI-12, and CaGLYI-13) were clustered in chromosome 8, while 4 members of the CaGLYII family (CaGLYII-3, CaGLYII-5, CaGLYII-7, and CaGLYII-9) were located at chromosome 3 (Table 1). The CaDJ-1 members, CaDJ-1 A, CaDJ-1B, and CaDJ-1 C, were distributed across chromosomes 2, 7, and 8, respectively (Table 1). Chromosomes 8 and 3 exhibit the highest density of glyoxalase genes (CaGLYI, CaGLYII, and CaDJ-1), with each harboring occurrences of six genes. Remarkably, a majority of the CaGLY proteins are predominantly predicted to be localized in the cytoplasm (n = 21), underscoring their essential cytoplasmic function, with others found in the plastid (n = 11), nucleus (n = 6), and mitochondria (n = 5).

In an initial screening for D-LDH in Capsicum annum, a total of 49 members were identified, all featuring the FAD_binding_4 domains, with some possessing additional domains. While these members share a common FAD-binding region, the presence of these additional domains might influence their catalytic functions. Previous research in rice and sorghum has demonstrated that an additional FAD_oxidase C domain alongside FAD_binding_4 can significantly enhance the catalytic activity of functional D-LDH [14, 15]. Based on this criterion, 11 putative D-LDH members were selected for more detailed analysis. These selected members contain only the FAD_binding_4 domain, while CaDLDH-1 uniquely possesses an additional FAD_oxidase C domain. The CaDLDH proteins featured differential sizes in the amino acid chain, ranging from 124 aa to 571 aa, with an average of 311 aa in length and 35 kilo Dalton in Molecular weight (Table 1). Six CaDLDH members are clustered in chromosome 2, while CaDLDH-1 and CaDLDH-2 are predicted to be localized in mitochondria (Table 1), relative to their functional importance in D-Lactate metabolism. All of the identified gene family members featured both positively (pI > 7) and negatively (pI < 7) charged proteins, indicating they coexist and function under specific physiological conditions.

Phylogenetic analysis of conventional glyoxalase members among various plant species

To infer the evolutionary relationship of conventional glyoxalases (GLYI and GLYII) among different plant species, full-length amino acid sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Glycine max, Solanum lycopersicum, Medicago truncatula, and Capsicum annuum was analyzed in a phylogenetic tree. The GLYI tree exhibited three major clades, which may represent Ni2+-dependent (Clade I), Zn2+-dependent (Clade II), and functionally inactive GLYI (Clade III) based on the previously identified members (Fig. 1A). Clade-I featured three GLYI proteins from rice (OsGLYI-2, OsGLYI-7, and OsGLYI-11) and two from Arabidopsis (AtGLYI-3 and AtGLYI-6), characterized as Ni2+-dependent in prior studies [10, 61]. On the contrary, OsGLYI-8 and its closest orthologue, AtGLYI-2, previously identified as Zn2+-dependent GLYI, belonged to Clade II [11]. Clade III encompassed the most substantial number of GLYI members while featuring OsGLYI-10, indicating a potential functional divergence (unpublished data). Notably, the clade consisting of Ni2+-dependent GLYI members predominated over the Zn2+-dependent ones (Fig. 1A), which aligns with findings previously reported in Arabidopsis, Medicago, Sorghum, and soybean [14, 26, 27, 62].

Phylogenetic analysis of GLYII members also resulted in three clades. Clade II featured previously characterized OsGLYII-1 and AtGLYII-3, known for possessing sulfur dioxygenase (SDO) activity [13]. Clade III contained the mitochondrial protein AtGLYII-2 [63], alongside cytosolic active OsGLYII-2, with characteristic GLYII catalytic activity [12], suggesting species-specific variations in evolutionary events (Fig. 1B). Conversely, Clade I also includes functionally active OsGLYII-3, which is expressed in seeds and confers MG tolerance. Therefore, this clade may encompass functionally active cytoplasmic GLYII proteins across these six plant species (Fig. 1B). The phylogenetic relationships observed in the tree imply that both GLYI and GLYII members within each clade may share similar characteristics with their experimentally validated counterparts within the same clade.

Gene organization, and domain architecture and active sites of conventional glyoxalases in pepper

Utilizing the Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 (GSDS 2.0), we investigated the exon-intron organization of GLYI and GLYII genes in Capsicum annuum. The CaGLYI gene lengths varied significantly, from 682 to 7489 base pairs, averaging approximately 2366 bp. Most of the CaGLYI genes typically comprised three exons, except CaGLYI-1 with ten, while CaGLYI-2 and CaGLYI-3 have nine exons (Fig. 2A). Domain analysis of CaGLYI proteins showed the universal presence of at least one glyoxalase domain (PF00903), with CaGLYI-1, CaGLYI-2, and CaGLYI-3 featuring an additional C-terminal domain (Fig. 2B). These glyoxalase domains of identified CaGLYI proteins were comprised of around 122 amino acids on average, with variations in lengths possibly linked to metal ion dependency (Table 2).

Alignment of CaGLYI protein glyoxalase domains with known OsGLYI-11 (Ni2+-dependent) and OsGLYI-8 (Zn2+-dependent) [61] proteins revealed significant similarities (Fig. 2C). Specifically, CaGLYI-1, CaGLYI-2, and CaGLYI-3, each with approximately 120 amino acids in their N and C-terminal glyoxalase domains, closely matched the Ni2+-dependent OsGLYI-11 [61]. Their conserved H/QEH/QE motif, essential for enzymatic function [10, 27], suggests a likely Ni2+-dependency and catalytic activity for these GLYI members. Similarly, CaGLYI-7, CaGLYI-8, and CaGLYI-17 had a glyoxalase domain of ~ 120 amino acids, which contain key catalytic residues that are anticipated to possess glyoxalase activity. In contrast, CaGLYI-4, although aligning with the Zn2+-dependent OsGLYI-8, lacks essential residues due to a truncated sequence (Fig. 2C), indicating its potential functional inactivity.

Further, the nine identified CaGLYII genes in pepper exhibited varying lengths, ranging from 3,216 bp (CaGLYII-3) to 24,199 bp (CaGLYII-9), with an average of ~ 11,114 bp (Fig. 3A). Domain organization showed the presence of a metallo-β-lactamase domain in all CaGLYII proteins, while CaGLYII-1 uniquely featured an additional hydroxyacylglutathione hydrolase domain (Fig. 3B). The Metallo-β-lactamase domains across these proteins spanned 156 to 206 amino acids, averaging around 187aa (Table 3). Sequence alignment with the previously characterized OsGLYII-2 and AtGLYII-2 indicated that only CaGLYII-1, CaGLYII-3, CaGLYII-4, and CaGLYII-6 contained two essential motifs for glyoxalase II functionality: the metal-binding site (THXHXDH) and the active site motif (C/GHT) [12]. This suggests their potential as functionally active members of the glyoxalase II gene family. Conversely, the remaining members, lacking either the conserved metal-binding site or the active site, are likely functionally inactive (Table 3).

Analysis of unique glyoxalase III family members in pepper

Our comprehensive analysis has identified three unique GLYIII genes in Capsicum annuum, each with distinct features. They exhibited variable length, with CaDJ-1 C being the longest at 6,699 bp, followed by CaDJ-1B (5,432 bp) and CaDJ-1 A (4,545 bp), averaging around 5,559 bp. Exon-intron distribution showed that CaDJ-1 A comprised five exons, while both CaDJ-1B and CaDJ-1 C contained eight exons (Fig. 4A). Each CaDJ-1 protein was found to have two DJ-1 domains (Fig. 4B), characteristic of plant glyoxalase III, as observed in rice and Arabidopsis (8). However, the length of the DJ-1/PfpI domains in CaDJ-1 proteins was heterogeneous (Table 4), spanning ~ 169 amino acids on average.

Evolutionary comparison of DJ-1 proteins from various species, including Oryza sativa, Glycine max, Sorghum bicolor, Capsicum annuum, Medicago truncatula, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Solanum lycopersicum, resulted in two distinct clades in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4C). Clade I includes the functionally active AtDJ-1D and OsDJ-1 C [7, 8], suggesting that orthologous CaDJ-1 A might exhibit significant glyoxalase III activity. Previous studies have reported weak glyoxalase activities for AtDJ-1 A and AtDJ-1B in clade II [7], suggesting that the closely related CaDJ-1B may exhibit a similar weak catalytic function. Moreover, AtDJ-1 C, also in clade II, was found to lack essential catalytic activity due to missing core cysteine residues [7]. This suggests that its ortholog, CaDJ-1 C, may be functionally less active, indicating divergent functions within clade II members. Sequence analysis of the DJ-1/PfpI domain, aligned with OsDJ-1 A, revealed that all identified CaDJ-1 proteins contained conserved catalytic residues: aspartate/glutamate, cysteine, and tyrosine/histidine - crucial for enzyme activity [8], particularly the central cysteine residue [64]. Thus based on the phylogenetic relationship and sequence data, CaDJ-1 A may be the most functionally active GLYIII enzyme in pepper, followed by potentially less active CaDJ-1B and CaDJ-1 C.

Evaluation of D-LDH members in Capsicum annuum

By screening the HMM profile of the FAD_Binding_4 domain (PF01565), our analysis identified 11 D-LDH candidates in pepper, each exhibiting distinct genetic and protein characteristics. These genes varied in length from 393 bp (CaDLDH-10) to 13,372 bp (CaDLDH-2), averaging around 2,906 bp. The shorter genes, such as CaDLDH-10, might encode truncated proteins with limited or no functional activity, as functional D-LDH genes in Sorghum (SbDLDH-1) exceeded 2,000 bp in length [14]. Analysis of gene structure revealed varied exon numbers: four CaDLDH genes (CaDLDH-4, CaDLDH-7, CaDLDH-8, CaDLDH-11) comprised two exons, while another four (CaDLDH-3, CaDLDH-5, CaDLDH-9, CaDLDH-10) contained a single exon, and the remaining ones (CaDLDH-1, CaDLDH-2, CaDLDH-6) had 13, 7, and 3 exons, respectively (Fig. 5A). Each D-LDH protein in pepper possessed a single FAD_Binding_4 domain, with CaDLDH-1 uniquely comprising both FAD_Binding_4 and FAD-oxidase C domains (Fig. 5B), suggesting a potentially active form with catalytic function as observed in rice OsD-LDH [15, 20]. The FAD_binding_4 domain length in most CaDLDH members was around 139–140 amino acids, with some exceptions (Table 5).

The analysis demonstrated distinct expression patterns for various genes within these tissues. Among the CaGLYI family, the Ni2+ dependent CaGLYI-1 and CaGLYI-2 were notably expressed in all tissues, contrasting with CaGLYI-3, with relatively lower tissue-specific expression. CaGLYI-17 showed the highest expression in seedlings and flowers, while CaGLYI-8 and CaGLYI-7 exhibited moderate expression (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Capsicum sp., or pepper, is an economically and nutritionally valuable vegetable [65], with a global production of approximately 36.97 million metric tons in 2022, as per FAOSTAT data. This crop is susceptible to various abiotic stresses, significantly impacting its yield [66, 67]. The glyoxalase pathway plays a critical role in mitigating these abiotic stresses in various organisms by enzymatically detoxifying MG, produced under stress, into D-lactate [3]. Subsequently, D-lactate dehydrogenase converts D-lactate into the metabolic fuel pyruvate [68].

While the glyoxalase system has been a focus of research for over a century, its functional roles in many plant species remain to be fully elucidated [23]. Prior studies have identified glyoxalase genes in a variety of plants, including Zea mays, Corchorus capsularis, Triticum sp., Aloe vera, Glycine max, and others, highlighting their contribution to abiotic stress tolerance [14, 23–29, 69]. Additionally, The MeGLYI-13 member in cassava enhances the tolerance of yeast and Arabidopsis to zinc and copper stresses, providing resistance to these heavy metals [70].

Previously, functional characterization of D-LDH was performed in Sorghum bicolor and rice [14, 15]. Therefore, comprehensive genome-wide analyses and functional characterizations of the GLY I, GLY II, unique GLY III (DJ-1), and D-LDH genes in Capsicum annuum were undertaken in this study to explore their potential roles in the plant’s adaptive responses to abiotic stresses. This study aims to extend the existing knowledge base on these gene families, previously characterized in other plant species, highlighting their conserved yet diverse roles in stress physiology. A total of 19 GLYI, 9 GLYII, 3 DJ-1, and 11 D-LDH members were identified in Capsicum annuum, each exhibiting unique gene and protein characteristics within their respective families (Table 1). This diversity contrasts with the identified members in other species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana (10 GLYI, 5 GLYII, 6 DJ-1, 2 D-LDH) [7, 28, 71], Glycine max (24 GLYI, 12 GLYII, 7 DJ-1) [27], M. truncatula (29 GLYI, 14 GLYII, 5 DJ-1) [26], Oryza sativa (11 GLYI, 3 GLYII, 6 DJ-1, 3 D-LDH) [8, 15, 28], Sorghum bicolor (15 GLYI, 6 GLYII, 4 D-LDH) [14], Avena sativa (26 GLYI, 14 GLYII, 15 DJ-1) [72] and Solanum lycopersicum (13 GLYI, 4 GLYII, 3 DJ-1, 4 D-LDH) [30]. This variation may arise from evolutionary adaptations, with GLYI genes consistently outnumbering GLYII and DJ-1. This pattern likely arises from gene duplication events, a common strategy in sessile organisms plants to evolve new isoforms or increase gene copies, enabling adaptation to diverse environmental stresses and developmental processes [73, 74].

Phylogenetic analysis revealed distinct clades among GLYI members, suggesting functional divergence of the homologs. (Fig. 1A). Based on the evolutionary relationship with Ni2+-dependent GLYI members from rice and Arabidopsis [10, 61] and subsequent sequence analysis, we identified six Ni²⁺-dependent members in pepper: CaGLYI-1, CaGLYI-2, CaGLYI-3, CaGLYI-7, CaGLYI-8, and CaGLYI-17. The dominance of Ni²⁺-dependent members aligns with previously identified glyoxalases in Glycine max, Oryza sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, Medicago truncatula, Sorghum bicolor, and Solanum lycopersicum, with 8, 3, 2, 6, 5, and 3 Ni²⁺-dependent isoforms, respectively [14, 26–28, 30]. However, only three Zn²⁺-dependent GLYI members were identified in Glycin max and only a single member in the rest, this study did not find any Zn²⁺-dependent members. Although CaGLYI-4 was present in the Zn²⁺-dependent clade in the phylogenetic tree, the lack of catalytic residues suggests potential protein devolution or pseudogenization from Zn²⁺-the dependent activity [75].

Among active GLYII members in Capsicum annuum, (Table 3) CaGLYII-1, and CaGLYII-6 may be conventional glyoxalase II members while CaGLYII-3, CaGLYII-4 may be functionally diverge with SDO activity, reflecting adaptations to distinct cellular environments or physiological processes. Sequence comparison with the active OsGLYII-2 [12] further confirmed this notion, by the presence of conserved metal-binding and active site motif (Fig. 3C). Interestingly, in tomato, two GLYII members, SlGLYII-3A and SlGLYII-3B, were identified as conventional active glyoxalases, while SlGLYII-1A and SlGLYII-1B were functionally divergent SDO enzymes. This suggests a homology in the functional GLYII enzymes between pepper and tomato.

Phylogenetic analysis also revealed a close relationship between pepper DJ-1 and glyoxalase III of Arabidopsis. CaDJ-1A revealed a strong evolutionary relationship with highly active AtDJ-1D and OsDJ-1 C [7, 8] while CaDJ-1B was closely related with AtDJ-1 A and AtDJ-1B (partial active) (7) and CaDJ-1C were closely associated with AtDJ-1C (inactive) (Fig. 4C). Therefore, we inferred CaDJ-1A may be the most active, followed by CaDJ-1B and CaDJ-1C. Notably, CaDLDH-1, CaDLDH-2, and CaDLDH-6, closely related to active OsDLDH and SbDLDH-1 [14, 20], may have functional importance (Fig. 5C). Among them. CaDLDH-1, with both FAD_Binding_4 and FAD-oxidase C domains (Fig. 5B), was predicted as the most active D-LDH in pepper, emulating functional D-LDH in rice [15, 20]. This suggests conserved functional mechanisms across species, with species-specific variability also observed, implying possible functional divergence throughout evolution.

This study further delved into the developmental roles of CaGLYI, CaGLYII, CaDJ-1, and CaDLDH in Capsicum annuum by examining their transcript abundance across 57 distinct anatomical tissues using data from the PepperHub transcriptomic database. We observed moderate to high expression levels for most active CaGLYI-1 and CaDJ-1 genes across all analyzed tissues, while CaGLYII genes generally exhibited lower expression (Fig. 6). Notably, CaDLDH genes showed the least expression in these tissues in response to developmental cues (Fig. 6). This contrasted with the prior studies in Medicago and soybean, in which GLYI genes displayed varied expression patterns against development cues. Notably, some members showed tissue-specific expression (e.g., MtGLYI-1, MtGLYI-7, GmGLYI-20, GmGLYI-2), and others being highly expressed across all tissues (e.g., MtGLYI-4, GmGLYI-12) [26, 27]. Likewise, some GLYII genes (e.g., MtGLYII-4, MtGLYII-8, GmGLYII-11, GmGLYII-10) were expressed highly in Medicago and soybean, while others did not (e.g., MtGLYII-11, MtGLYII-13, GmGLYII-8, GmGLYII-6). Similar tissue-specific expressions were noted for GLYIII genes in these species. However, unlike the broad expression of SbDLDH in Sorghum bicolor, CaDLDH genes in pepper were less expressed [14]. This variation in gene expression across species and tissues suggests potential functional diversification among these gene families.

Plants are experiencing an increase in both the frequency and intensity of abiotic stresses due to global warming and climate change [76]. The major stresses encompass salinity, drought, extreme high and low temperatures, ozone exposure, and metal toxicity. All abiotic stress often leads to oxidative stress as one of its key effects [77]. Various factors such as reduced stomatal conductance, damage to the photosynthetic machinery or pigments, malfunctioning of the Calvin cycle and photosystem, inactivation of RuBisCO, decreased carboxylation efficiency, and impaired electron transport chain function are some of the primary contributors to the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under stress conditions [78]. Thus, under stress conditions, the level of ROS increases exponentially, exceeding the potential of scavenging machinery and instigating oxidative bursts [79]. Alongside ROS, plants accumulate significant amounts of MG by 2- to 6-fold compared to normal conditions, depending on the species, under stress [17]. ROS serves as a critical indicator of how well a plant is mitigating stress conditions. So, the effect of different abiotic stresses including oxidative stress in transcript alteration of the glyoxalase and D-LDH gene family members could be an essential aspect of plant stress physiology.

The differential expression patterns of CaGLYI, CaGLYII, CaDJ-1, and CaDLDH genes in Capsicum annuum, as observed through RNA-seq data from PepperHub and validated by the qRT-PCR analysis, highlighted their integral roles in modulating abiotic stresses. There are some differences observed in the two expression profiling data, which might be due to the variations in plant growth conditions, age of the plant, stress intensity, stress exposure time, and plant material extraction protocol. This analysis revealed gene-specific expression among various groups; for instance, Ni2+-dependent CaGLYI-2 was consistently upregulated in stress conditions, while CaGLYI-3 was downregulated (Fig. 8A), demonstrating the complex regulatory mechanisms within these gene families. This pattern contrasts with prior research in soybean, where all Ni2+-dependent glyoxalase I (e.g., GmGLYI-1, GmGLYI-2) were downregulated under salinity and drought [27], and complement observations in Sorghum, where most Ni2+-dependent genes were upregulated (e.g. SbGLYI-7, SbGLYI-10, SbGLYI-14) in response to abiotic stresses [14]. In durum wheat, putative active TdGLYI genes were upregulated by salt and osmotic stress (31), indicating a similar stress response pattern [31].

The selective upregulation of CaGLYII-6 and CaDJ-1 A and downregulation of CaGLYII-1, CaDJ-1B, and CaDJ-1 C within the GLYII and GLYIII families further substantiate the complexity of stress response pathways in pepper (Fig. 8, B and C). This phenomenon was also noted in soybean, where GLYII genes showed varied responses (e.g., upregulation of GmGLYII-4 and downregulation of GmGLYII-10) in contrast to the consistent upregulation of functional GLYII members in sorghum (e.g., SbGLYII-3, SbGLYII-4) under stress conditions. In date palm, the GLYIII isoform PdDJ-1 was upregulated upon salinity treatment, suggesting its role in MG detoxification and redox balance [32]. The upregulation of PdDJ-1 is in alignment with CaDJ-1 A, which is upregulated against diverse abiotic stressors (Fig. 8C). Notably, the high expression of functional SbDLDH-1 and SbDLDH-2 genes in Sorghum mirrors the expression pattern of CaDLDH-1 in our study (Fig. 8D), underscoring the potential of these genes to confer stress tolerance.

In summary, these findings elucidate the differential expression and regulatory mechanisms of glyoxalase and D-LDH genes in Capsicum annuum, contributing to our understanding of their roles in abiotic stress responses. Further, this study facilitated the screening of the appropriate candidate gene(s) for functional characterization to develop transgenic plants through the over-expression of selected members. Additionally, exploring their roles in this economically significant crop could provide insights into developing stress-resilient plant varieties, which is crucial in the face of changing climatic conditions and increasing agricultural challenges.

Conclusion

Taken together, this comprehensive study in Capsicum annuum provides valuable insights into the glyoxalase and D-LDH gene families, highlighting their significant roles in plant stress modulation. Through in-silico genome-wide analysis, 19 CaGLYI, 9 CaGLYII, 3 unique GLYIII (CaDJ-1) and 11 D-LDH members were identified. The identified members were further characterized by expression patterns across anatomical tissues and under various stress conditions. These findings may extend the current understanding of plant stress physiology while offering potential avenues for developing stress-tolerant crop varieties. Future work focusing on the functional validation of these genes could further elucidate their specific roles in stress mitigation, contributing to sustainable agricultural practices in the face of global climate challenges.
