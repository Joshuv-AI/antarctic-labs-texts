TET3 promotes preeclampsia by regulating PDK4-mediated glucose metabolism reprogramming in trophoblasts
Authors: Xiaokang Deng, Yi Liu, Meilikang Li, Weichun Tang, Lingling Jiang, Juveria Rahman, Matthew Sagnelli, Hongtao Hu, Lizhou Sun, Qing Zuo, Dan Wu, Yetao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExtra villous trophoblasts (EVTs) dysfunction impairing spiral artery remodeling (SAR) is a key preeclampsia (PE) factor. ScRNA-seq and bioinformatics analyses of preeclamptic placentas revealed the upregulation of TET3, a newly recognized regulator of epigenetic reprogramming. Untargeted metabolomics further associated TET3 with the trophoblast glucose metabolism. In vitro functional and metabolic assays demonstrated that elevated TET3 levels inhibited EVT proliferation and migration by dysregulating the mitochondrial glucose metabolism. Through integrated ScRNA-seq, microarray analysis, metabolic profiling, and chromatin immunoprecipitation assays, we found that in human trophoblasts, TET3 suppressed the expression of PDK4 by upregulating the transcription factor FOS. Mechanistically, TET3 bound to the FOS promoter and induced DNA demethylation, chromatin remodeling, and transcriptional activation. As a downstream effector, FOS bound directly to the PDK4 promoter and repressed its expression. This regulatory axis was validated in various animal models and in clinical placental specimens. Collectively, our study established that the TET3/FOS/PDK4 pathway is a critical driver of PE pathogenesis, thus offering a novel therapeutic target for PE.Subject areas: pregnancy, epigenetics, metabolomics

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a multisystem pregnancy-specific disease. PE and related hypertensive conditions affect approximately 8% of pregnancies in China and 5%–10% globally. Annually, this condition leads to approximately 75,000 maternal deaths and over 500,000 infant fatalities worldwide, making it the second leading cause of both short- and long-term morbidity and mortality among newborns and pregnant women.1,2 The diverse prevalence of PE among various populations can be explained by geographical, social, economic, and racial influences.3

The clinical hallmark of PE is the onset of hypertension, which usually occurs after the 20th week of pregnancy, along with signs of end-organ dysfunction.4,5 PE can lead to diverse end-organ complications, including proteinuria, acute kidney injury, liver dysfunction, hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, liver rupture, seizures (eclampsia), stroke, and death.5 Several risk factors have been identified for PE, including a history of PE, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and multiple pregnancies.6 In China, recent implementation of the three-child policy has led to a substantial increase in the proportion of older and high-risk mothers, consequently increasing the incidence of PE. Currently, the most effective intervention is pregnancy termination,7,8 underscoring the critical need for further research on the clinical diagnosis and management of PE.

The placenta, a crucial endocrine organ that influences fetal growth and maternal metabolism,9 plays a pivotal role in the etiology of various pregnancy-related disorders, including PE, when its formation or functional metabolism is compromised.9 Extra villous trophoblasts (EVTs) are functional placental cells. Abnormalities in trophoblast function, such as aberrant migration, invasion, and angiogenesis, can lead to SAR, resulting in increased blood resistance and reduced placental perfusion, exacerbating the onset and progression of PE.10,11 Thus, elucidating the mechanisms underlying abnormal placental EVT function is crucial for understanding SAR disorders and providing a theoretical foundation for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of PE.

The ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of enzymes, comprising TET1, TET2, and TET3, is a novel class of DNA demethylases. These enzymes catalyze stepwise oxidation of 5-methylcytosine in DNA to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine and other oxidation products, which are subsequently converted to unmethylated cytosine.12,13,14 Although significant advances have been made in understanding the physiological functions of TET proteins and their roles in regulating DNA methylation and transcription during development and cancer,15,16 their involvement and molecular mechanisms in PE progression remain unclear. Our research group previously reported considerable elevated expression of TET3 in the placental tissue (EVT) of women with PE17; however, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying PE warrant further investigation.

It is well established that trophoblasts modulate mitochondrial glucose metabolism to fulfill their energy requirements.18 Previous studies have also shown that trophoblasts demand substantial energy for their biological functions, with adenosine triphosphate (ATP) primarily derived from active mitochondrial oxidative and anaerobic metabolism.19,20 We hypothesized that the abundant expression of TET3 might regulate the biological functions of trophoblasts by influencing their metabolism. In this study, we identified a previously unidentified molecular regulatory pathway related to glucose metabolism that plays a crucial role in determining the physiological functions of trophoblasts.

Results

Elevated expression of TET3 in EVTs promoted PE progression

PE is predominantly attributed to placental dysfunction, during which differential gene expression dysregulates EVT function, ultimately resulting in impaired uterine SAR. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we first performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on three paired placental tissue samples obtained from patients with PE and healthy controls (Figure 1A). The scRNA-seq results revealed that the expression level of HLA-G in EVTs was significantly reduced in the PE group compared with the control group, strongly indicating a compromised EVT functionality (Figure 1B).

Subsequent analysis of TET mRNA levels (including TET1, TET2, and TET3) in placental tissues demonstrated that TET3 was markedly upregulated in the PE group, with particularly elevated expression observed in the EVTs (Figures 1A and 1B). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization illustrates the differential expression of TET3 across diverse cell types, including neutrophils, syncytiotrophoblasts, and macrophages (Figure 1C). Conversely, no statistically significant differences were detected in the mRNA levels of TET1 and TET2 (Figures 1B, S1A, and S1B). GO analysis further indicated that differentially expressed genes in the EVTs may be closely related to cellular metabolic processes (Figure 1E).

TET3 regulated glucose metabolism reprogramming in trophoblasts

To investigate the role of TET3 in trophoblast cell metabolism, untargeted metabolomic analyses were performed in HTR-8/SVneo cells overexpressing TET3. The results revealed significant perturbations in multiple metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate, amino acid, organic acid, and fatty acid metabolism, following TET3 overexpression (Figure 2A). Further analysis of metabolites related to glucose metabolism revealed significantly lower levels of glycolytic-derived metabolites (e.g., d-glucose-6-phosphate, lactic acid, and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate), tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycling intermediates (e.g., succinic acid, isocitric acid, and malic acid), and ATP in the PE group compared to the control group (Figure 2B).

To characterize the impact of TET3 on trophoblast glucose metabolism, glucose consumption, lactate production, ATP generation, and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured. TET3 overexpression led to decreased glucose consumption (i.e., increased residual glucose levels), reduced lactate production, diminished ATP generation, and impaired glycolytic capacity. In the context of placental development, especially for extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs), proliferative capacity and invasive/migratory potential are not mutually exclusive but are sequentially linked and both are critical for successful uterine remodeling and spiral artery transformation. Conversely, TET3 knockdown exhibited reciprocal effects (Figures 2C and 2D). Colony formation, CCK8, and Transwell invasion assays further demonstrated that TET3 overexpression inhibited trophoblast cell proliferation and invasion, whereas TET3 knockdown enhanced these functions (Figure 2E, 2F and 2G).

Previous studies have established that EVT-mediated vascular remodeling operates through three principal mechanisms10,11: protease-driven degradation of the extracellular matrix via secretion of enzymes such as MMP2 and MMP9; induction of endothelial apoptosis through direct cellular contact or secreted factors; and immune escape-facilitated clearance of endothelial cells. To model the direct trophoblast-endothelial interaction and subsequent endothelial injury, we established a co-culture system (Figure 2H). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were pre-labeled with CMTPX (green), and transfected HTR-8/SVneo cells were labeled with CMFDA (red). After 12 h of HUVEC seeding, labeled HTR-8/SVneo cells were added and co-cultured for an additional 12 h under carefully controlled conditions to limit confounding effects from nutrient depletion or spatial competition. The results demonstrated that TET3 knockdown in trophoblasts led to a marked reduction in HUVEC numbers alongside an increase in HTR-8/SVneo cells, whereas TET3 overexpression produced the opposite effect. Consistent with this, ELISA quantification of culture supernatants revealed significantly elevated levels of MMP2 and MMP9 in the TET3-knockdown group and reduced levels in the overexpression group (Figure 2I). Taken together, these data indicate that TET3 modulates trophoblast-mediated vascular remodeling by restraining glucose metabolic reprogramming and associated functional behaviors.

TET3 regulated trophoblast glucose metabolism reprogramming via PDK4 inhibition

To decipher the molecular mechanism by which TET3 governs glucose metabolism, we performed PCR array-based gene expression profiling of glucose metabolic pathways. Employing a 384—well real—time quantitative PCR assay plate, this array concurrently analyzed 84 key genes associated with cellular glucose metabolism. These genes were involved in glycogen metabolism, TCA cycle, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and pentose phosphate pathway. The results indicated that PDK4 expression was significantly upregulated following TET3 knockout (Figure 3A). This result was further verified via qPCR and western blot analyses in both HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cells and primary first-trimester human cytotrophoblasts (CTBs) (Figures 3B and S5).

PDK4 is a rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis. Among the dysregulated genes identified by the PCR array, PDK4 showed the most significant fold change. Given its role as a critical gatekeeper enzyme regulating the switch between glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, we selected PDK4 for further mechanistic investigation. It phosphorylates and inactivates pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), impeding the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA.21 After effectively downregulating and upregulating PDK4, we conducted relevant experiments on glucose metabolism. These results demonstrated that the knockdown of PDK4 led to reduced glucose consumption (i.e., an increase in the remaining glucose content), lactate production, ATP generation, and glycolytic capacity. In contrast, PDK4 overexpression enhanced these metabolic parameters (Figures 3C and 3D). Collectively, colony formation, CCK8 proliferation, Transwell invasion, and coculture assays revealed that PDK4 knockdown significantly inhibited trophoblast proliferation and invasion and impaired trophoblast-mediated endothelial cell ablation. In contrast, PDK4 overexpression reversed these phenotypes (Figures 3E–3H). These results implied that PDK4 boosted glucose metabolism, influencing trophoblast function in HTR-8/SVneo cells.

As a DNA-binding protein, TET3 activated gene transcription by facilitating DNA demethylation. Analysis of genome-wide single-nucleotide resolution DNA methylation data (GEO: GSE117190)22 from TET3-deficient human uterine cells revealed increased PDK4 promoter methylation. Nevertheless, pyrosequencing assays revealed no significant alterations in CpG methylation levels within the PDK4 promoter after TET3 silencing in HTR-8/SVneo cells (Figures S2A and S2B). These findings indicated that TET3 negatively regulated PDK4 expression in EVTs via mechanisms that were potentially independent of promoter demethylation.

TET3 inhibited trophoblast glucose metabolism reprogramming through epigenetic promotion of FOS

To elucidate the mechanism by which TET3 regulates PDK4 expression, we performed transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) of HTR-8/SVneo cells after TET3 knockout. This analysis identified widespread transcriptional alterations, with FOS emerging as a significantly downregulated gene (Figure 4A). Previous studies have established that FOS is critical for regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and maintaining cardiovascular homeostasis during pregnancy.23,24 GO enrichment analysis of the set of genes changed significantly upon TET3 knockdown in HTR-8/SVneo cells revealed that FOS is predominantly associated with biological processes linked to cellular metabolism, invasion, and migration (Figure 4B).

In HTR-8/SVneo and primary trophoblast cells, TET3 silencing significantly decreased FOS expression at both the RNA and protein levels, whereas TET3 overexpression had the opposite effect (Figures 4C and S5). Functional assays in HTR-8/SVneo cells with FOS knockdown or overexpression showed that FOS overexpression reduced glucose consumption, lactate production, and ATP generation while impairing glycolytic capacity (Figures 4D and 4E). Colony formation, CCK8 proliferation, Transwell invasion, and coculture assays further demonstrated that FOS overexpression significantly inhibited trophoblast proliferation, invasion, and trophoblast-mediated endothelial cell ablation. Conversely, FOS knockdown rescued these phenotypes (Figures 4G–4J). Collectively, these data indicated that TET3 suppressed trophoblast glucose metabolic reprogramming by upregulating FOS expression, modulating trophoblast function.

To investigate the epigenetic regulation of FOS by TET3 in human trophoblasts, we examined whether TET3 depletion affects DNA methylation in the FOS promoter region. Pyrosequencing analysis revealed significantly increased CpG dinucleotide methylation in the FOS promoter following TET3 knockdown (Figures 5A–5D). ChIP-qPCR assays further showed that TET3 overexpression enhanced its binding to the FOS promoter, accompanied by reduced H3K27me3 (a repressive histone marker) and increased H3K4me3 (an active histone marker) levels within the CpG island of the FOS promoter (Figure 5E). These findings suggested that TET3 drives dynamic epigenetic remodeling at the FOS locus, promoting a euchromatic state and enhancing chromatin accessibility in HTR-8/SVneo cells.

TET3 mediated PDK4 expression via FOS regulation

To validate the regulatory effect of FOS on PDK4 expression in trophoblasts, we transfected HTR-8/SVneo cells with a FOS-specific siRNA or an FOS-overexpressing plasmid. FOS overexpression significantly downregulated PDK4 expression at both the RNA and protein levels, whereas FOS knockdown upregulated PDK4 expression (Figures 6A–6C). A rescue experiment further confirmed the regulatory cascade linking TET3, FOS, and PDK4 (Figures 6D and 6E), indicating that TET3 deficiency impaired FOS activity, enabling PDK4 upregulation at both the transcriptional and translational levels.

Functional validation via glucose consumption, lactate production, and ATP generation assays in HTR-8/SVneo cells further supported this pathway; TET3 overexpression combined with FOS siRNA or TET3 knockdown combined with FOS overexpression reciprocally regulated metabolic parameters (Figure 6G). ChIP-qPCR assays demonstrated that FOS bound to the PDK4 promoter (Figure 6F), establishing it as a transcription factor that directly modulated PDK4 transcriptional activity. Collectively, these data define a molecular pathway through which TET3 epigenetically upregulates FOS expression, allowing FOS to bind to the PDK4 promoter and repress its transcription in trophoblasts (Figure 6H).

Verification of TET3-FOS-PDK4 regulatory pathway in animal models and clinical placenta

To validate our in vitro findings, we generated conditional Tet3 knockout mice (CKO-Tet3) and Tet3 overexpression mice (OE-Tet3) (Figures 7A and 7B). The CKO mouse was generated by crossing Tet3-floxed mice with Elf5-Cre mice, which drives Cre expression specifically in the trophoblast lineage of the placenta. Compared to the controls, CKO-Tet3 mice exhibited increased fetal and placental weights, whereas OE-Tet3 mice showed reduced weight. Blood pressure remained unchanged in CKO-Tet3 mice but was significantly elevated in OE-Tet3 mice compared to controls (Figures 7C–7E), suggesting that Tet3 deficiency may preserve blood pressure stability, whereas Tet3 overexpression drives hypertensive phenotypes. In placental tissues, CKO-Tet3 mice showed reduced TET3 and FOS expression but increased PDK4 expression, whereas OE-Tet3 mice exhibited the opposite pattern (Figure 7F). Morphological analysis revealed that CKO-Tet3 mice displayed improved uterine SAR with minimal surrounding smooth muscle cells, whereas control mice showed only a thin smooth muscle layer. In contrast, compared with the control group, OE-Tet3 mice had narrower blood vessel diameters, thicker layers of smooth muscle and endothelial cells, and impaired remodeling (Figure 7G). The immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) results confirmed the expected changes in the expression levels of TET3, FOS, and PDK4 across each group. (Figures 7H and 7I).

To further validate this pathway, we used a uterine artery ligation (UAL) mouse model of PE combined with a rescue experiment using tail vein injection of an adeno-associated virus (AAV) targeting Tet3 (Figure 8A). UAL mice exhibited reduced placental and fetal weights, which were partially restored by AAV-mediated Tet3 knockdown. UAL mice also showed significantly elevated blood pressure, which was normalized after AAV treatment (Figures 8B and 8C). Western blotting of human pre-eclamptic placentas revealed increased TET3 and FOS expression but decreased PDK4 expression compared to controls. Similarly, UAL mice exhibited upregulated TET3/FOS expression and downregulated PDK4 expression, whereas AAV treatment rescued PDK4 expression (Figure 8D). IHC and IF validated the protein expression patterns (Figures 8E and 8F). Correlation analysis of human preeclamptic placentas showed a significant positive correlation between TET3 and FOS levels and a significant negative correlation between TET3/PDK4 and FOS/PDK4 (Figure 8G).

Collectively, our findings established that TET3 induced PE pathogenesis by epigenetically upregulating FOS, which repressed PDK4 and reprograms trophoblast glucose metabolism (Figure 9). These results provide in vivo validation of the TET3/FOS/PDK4 axis and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target for placental disorders.

TET3 regulates glucose metabolism in PE

Elevated expression of TET3 binds to the FOS promoter, inducing DNA demethylation, chromatin remodeling, and transcriptional activation. Then FOS directly binds to the PDK4 promoter and inhibits its expression, which inhibited EVT proliferation and migration by dysregulating the mitochondrial glucose metabolism, further leading to the promotion of PE occurrence.

Discussion

Cellular physiological functions require substantial material and energy supply, often accompanied by the abnormal activation of metabolic pathways for substances, such as glucose, lipids, and amino acids. Conversely, metabolic alterations directly affect cellular function. Nutrients, such as glucose, lipids, and amino acids, are metabolized by cells to meet energy demands, with glucose metabolism serving as the primary energy-supply mechanism.25,26 Under normal conditions, cells primarily undergo aerobic oxidation, in which glucose is converted to pyruvate via a series of enzymatic reactions, which are then further decomposed into the mitochondria to generate ATP. Anaerobic glycolysis becomes dominant only in low-oxygen environments and rapidly supplies energy to facilitate cellular adaptation to hypoxic stress.27,28 Aerobic oxidation yields far more energy than anaerobic glycolysis does. Glycolysis alone produces only two ATP molecules and two lactic acid molecules per glucose molecule. Intriguingly, tumor cells often rely primarily on glycolysis for energy production, even under oxygen-rich conditions, a phenomenon termed aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect).29,30,31 Trophoblasts, which share phenotypic similarities with invasive tumor cells, exhibit metabolic reprogramming.32,33 Beyond energy production, glycolytic intermediates provide essential precursors for the biosynthesis of macromolecules and maintenance of cellular homeostasis.34,35,36 Thus, deciphering the molecular mechanisms governing trophoblast glucose metabolism and targeting glycolytic reprogramming in these cells represents a promising therapeutic strategy for PE treatment. Our study addressed the placental origin of PE by investigating how metabolic reprogramming in trophoblasts contributes to disease pathogenesis.

The TET family of enzymes has emerged as critical regulators of the epigenetic landscape.37,38 TET3, a key member of this family, catalyzes the Oxidation of 5-methylcytosine (5 mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5 hmC), 5-formylcytosine (5 fC), and 5-carboxylcytosine (5caC)—collectively termed the “sixth bases” of the genome.39 This enzymatic activity is pivotal for DNA demethylation and dynamic epigenomic regulation, and influences gene expression programs during development and cellular differentiation.40,41

In single-cell sequencing of preeclamptic placentas, we observed high TET3 expression in trophoblasts and in endothelial and NK cells. SAR disorder is central to the classical two-stage PE theory. Conventionally, trophoblasts and NK cells are thought to jointly modulate the immune microenvironment, inducing endothelial cell swelling, smooth muscle disruption, and eventual replacement of endothelial cells by trophoblasts during SAR.42 However, recent studies have challenged this model, demonstrating that endothelial cells are only transiently depleted, and trophoblasts do not fully replace them.43 We observed endothelial cell loss in the remodeled vessels; however, the intact endothelial layer could not be definitively traced to the original endothelial cells. Other studies have suggested that spiral artery endothelial cells may be derived from circulating endothelial progenitor cells,44 raising the possibility that SAR is a dynamic process of endothelial destruction and regeneration. Trophoblasts may drive this process by directly inducing endothelial apoptosis through cell-cell contact and secretion of proteases (e.g., MMP2 and MMP9) to degrade the endothelial basement membrane and intercellular junctions. To model this, we established a trophoblast-endothelial coculture system in which trophoblasts induce endothelial apoptosis under standardized conditions. However, the precise molecular mechanisms governing these interactions require further investigation.

Untargeted metabolomics revealed a strong association between TET3 expression and trophoblast glucose metabolism. Given that the abnormal activation of glucose metabolism in trophoblasts is often accompanied by upregulation of metabolic enzymes, we hypothesized that TET3 inhibits trophoblast glucose metabolism by regulating glucose-related gene expression. To test this hypothesis, we used a glucose metabolism PCR array to profile key genes in five metabolic pathways: glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway, and glycogen metabolism. This array identified PDK4 as the most significantly differentially expressed gene. PDK4, a member of the pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK) family, phosphorylates and inactivates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), blocking the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, which is the rate-limiting step in the TCA cycle and energy production. PDK4 is a well-established regulator of glucose/lipid metabolism, and its expression responds to metabolic cues, such as fasting and high-fat diets.45,46,47 By inhibiting PDC, PDK4 redirects cellular metabolism toward fatty acid and ketone body utilization during nutrient stress. Our experiments confirmed that PDK4 was negatively regulated by TET3, suggesting that high TET3 expression in trophoblasts suppressed glucose metabolism by downregulating PDK4.

Pyrosequencing ruled out direct TET3 binding to the PDK4 promoter, prompting us to investigate transcriptional intermediaries. Transcriptome sequencing following TET3 knockdown revealed that FOS was the most differentially expressed gene. FOS is a critical transcriptional regulator involved in apoptosis, DNA repair, and oxidative stress responses, with established roles in tissue development and organ homeostasis (e.g., skeletal, cardiovascular, and nervous systems).48,49 Mechanistic studies revealed that TET3 directly bound to the FOS promoter, whereas ChIP assays confirmed that FOS bound to the PDK4 promoter. These findings support a model in which TET3 upregulated FOS to repress PDK4 expression, impairing trophoblast glucose metabolism and promoting PE pathogenesis. We validated this pathway in human placental tissues, Tet3 overexpression/CKO mice, and in UAL/UAL+AAV mouse models.

In conclusion, TET3 reprogrammed trophoblast glucose metabolism by regulating the FOS/PDK4 axis, altering trophoblast function, and contributing to PE development (Figure 9). These findings established a novel epigenetic metabolic pathway involved in the pathogenesis of PE and highlighted potential therapeutic targets for placental disorders.

Limitations of the study

While our findings provide compelling evidence for the TET3/FOS/PDK4 axis in PE, several limitations should be considered. First, although we characterized the role of TET3 in EVT-mediated spiral artery remodeling, the exact lineage and provenance of the resulting endothelial layer in remodeled vessels remain to be fully elucidated, as this may involve a dynamic process of endothelial destruction and regeneration. Second, single-cell analysis revealed high TET3 expression in vascular endothelial and NK cells, suggesting that its role in PE may involve complex multicellular coordination beyond the trophoblast metabolic reprogramming described here. Finally, while validated across multiple clinical and animal models, these systems may not fully encapsulate the extensive clinical and genetic heterogeneity of human PE populations.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Human tissue samples comprised blood and decidual tissues. This study was approved by the People's Hospital of Jiangsu Province and conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (ethics approval number: 2021-SR-055). All study participants were duly informed and human tissue samples were collected with their consent through signed informed consent forms. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for tissue collection strictly adhered to the latest International Federation of Obstetricians' guidelines.

A cohort of 30 pregnant women diagnosed with PE and 30 control subjects who underwent cesarean delivery at the People's Hospital of Jiangsu Province between September 2020 and September 2022 was randomly selected for this study. Placental tissue specimens from women with normal pregnancies served as the control group, comprising patients who underwent cesarean section because of a scarred uterus or social factors. The diagnostic criteria for PE included the onset after 20 weeks of gestation, with a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥90 mmHg, accompanied by urinary protein levels of ≥0.3 g/24 h, random urinary protein (+), a urinary protein/creatinine ratio of ≥0.3, or the absence of proteinuria but with the presence of the following: abnormal changes in vital organs (such as the heart, lungs, liver, or kidneys), abnormalities in the hematological, digestive, or nervous systems, and placental-fetal involvement.

Animals

First, the genetic background of the mice was C57BL/6J. The loxp sequence was inserted on both sides of the exon 4 of Tet3 gene by Cas9 technology, and the fertilized egg was transplanted into the uterus of a recipient female mouse. F0 mice were born. DNA PCR and sequencing were performed on F0 mice to determine whether insertion-positive mice and loxp insertion positions were correct. F0 mice were back-mated with C57BL/6J mice to obtain stable genetic F1 generation heterozygous mice, and the genotype was determined to be Tet3flox/wt heterozygous by PCR. The Tet3flox/wt heterozygous mice were mated with Elf5-Cre T-positive mice (the same as the C57BL/6J background), and the Elf5-Cre mice functioned, specifically in the placenta. Owing to the reported problem of leakage of Elf5-Cre expression in sperm, male mice carrying the Elf5-Cre gene were avoided in actual breeding. The breeding process was as follows: Tet3flox/wt male mice were mated with Tet3flox/wt female mice to obtain Tet3flox/ flox homozygous mice, and Tet3flox/wt male mice were mated with Elf5-Cre T female mice to obtain Tet3flox/wt Elf5-Cre T double-gene hybrid female mice. We used Tet3flox/ flox homozygous male mice and Tet3flox/wt Elf5-Cre T dual-gene heterozygous female mice to generate Tet3flox/ flox Elf5-Cre T female mice. Female mice will get Tet3 placenta-specific knockout mice when pregnant. We injected an adenovirus (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. China) into mice via tail vein at 8.5 and 12.5 days, respectively. The detailed experimental procedure is illustrated in Figure 7B. Bilateral UAL was performed and sutured at 12.5 days of gestation, whereas the abdominal cavity was opened and sutured back in the control group. After 18.5 days of gestation, the placental and embryonic tissues were retrieved. We injected (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.(China).) into the mice via the tail vein after 12.5 days. CKO mice (6–9 weeks old, weighing 180–200 g) were obtained from Gempharmatech (Shanghai, China) for all animal experiments. Animal experiments were approved by the People's Hospital of Jiangsu Province Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were categorized into their respective groups and provided with nesting materials and sufficient food. After 18.5 days of gestation, pregnant mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg body weight).

All animal experiments were carried out according to the standards of the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Women and Children's Healthcare Hospital (2024KY-096-01).

Cell culture

The culture conditions for HTR-8/SVneo cells were as follows: RPIM-1640 culture medium containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics, cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 90% humidity; HUVECs were cultured in a DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. The complete culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days based on the cell growth conditions, and the cells were passaged and used for subsequent experiments when the degree of fusion was 80–90%. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection kit and confirmed to be negative before use. The cells were certified by the manufacturers, but were not further authenticated before use.

Method details

Isolation and cultivation of primary trophoblast cells

Placental tissue samples from gestational weeks 36–38 were obtained under sterile conditions and were washed with sterile physiological saline. Tissues were mechanically dissociated using ophthalmic scissors and transferred to sterile microcentrifuge tubes. Enzymatic digestion was performed using 1 ml of 1.25 g/L trypsin and 0.4 mg/L type I collagenase at 37 °C for 30 min. Digestion was terminated by adding 2 mL of DMEM containing 10% FBS. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 200-mesh sieve and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 30 mL serum-free DMEM. Purification of primary trophoblasts was conducted by discontinuous Percoll density layer separation. The cells were inoculated in a culture bottle and purified using the differential adhesion method. Using the SABC immunohistochemistry method, cultured cells were identified using monoclonal antibodies against cytokeratin 7 and vimentin, and brownish-yellow particles were identified as positive reactions.

Cell transfection

After three passages, the cells were digested to a density of approximately 80%. The cells were counted and 2×105 cells/well were plated in 6-well plates for culture. When the degree of cell fusion was 50–60%, siRNA and pDNA were transfected with Lip3000 and returned to the incubator after transfection. After 6–8 h, the complete culture medium was replaced. The cells were collected 24–48 h after transfection, and total RNA and protein were extracted for qPCR or western blotting to verify the transfection efficiency or for other experiments.

Plasmid construction

The plasmids and bacterial suspensions were prepared using RiboBro (Shanghai, China). The bacterial suspension was placed in 1 mL centrifuge tubes containing liquid LB medium (with AMP added) and incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator for 12–16 h. After 12 h, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension was transferred from the centrifuge tube to a 50 mL culture bottle and incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator for 12–16 h. An Endo-free Plasmid Mini Kit II (Omage, D6950-02B) was used to extract the plasmid, and a Nanodrop ultraviolet spectrophotometer was used to determine the DNA content and purity of the plasmid. The plasmid DNA was stored at -20 °C.

Detection of the remaining glucose

HTR-8/SVneo cells (2 × 105/well) from each experimental group were plated in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The glucose content of the medium was determined using Abcam (ab65333). Around 50 μL medium was added to the 96-well plate, and the reaction system was configured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated and used as a blank control. The mixture was then incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at an OD of 570 nm using a microplate reader. Data were obtained by converting the measured values.

Lactate secretion detection

HTR-8/SVneo cells (2 × 105/well) from each experimental group were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The lactate content of the medium was determined using an Abcam (ab65330; Cambridge, UK). Then, 50 μL medium was added to the 96-well plate, and the reaction system was configured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated and used as a blank control. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance was measured at an OD of 570 nm using a microplate reader. Data were obtained by converting the measured values.

ATP production detection

HTR-8/SVneo cells (2 × 105/well) from each experimental group were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The ATP content of the cells was detected using an Abcam (ab83355) after the cells were broken by ultrasound. Around 50 μL supernatant was added to a 96-well plate and the reaction system was configured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated and used as a blank control. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance was measured at an OD of 570 nm using a microplate reader. Data were obtained by converting the measured values.

ECAR

The Seahorse instrument was then turned on and preheated to 37 °C. Seahorse Xr calibration solution was added to the Utility plate and the test plate was placed back on the Utility plate, then the hydration probe was placed overnight in a 37 °C CO2-free incubator. The experimental cells were collected using the growth medium provided with the kit and inoculated into a Seahorse xr cell culture plate at 105 cells/well. After the cells were attached to the wall (usually 4–6 h), the growth medium was changed to the test solution, and the cells were placed in a CO2-free incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. The test solution was prepared using Seahorse XF Base Medium and the required substrate was added. The solution was heated to a pH value of 7.4 with NaOH and filtered. Before use, the test solution was preheated in a 37 °C water bath for 1 h. The cell culture plates were placed in the Seahorse apparatus to begin the experiments. Data were collected and analyzed after the experiments.

ELISA

ELISA kits (A102535 and A102527) were purchased from Shanghai Fusheng Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pre-coated microplates were prepared and 100 μL/well of standards (7-point serial dilution), samples, and a blank control were added. The, they were incubated at 37 °C for 90 min, washed 2× with PBST (0.05% Tween-20), 100 μL biotin-conjugated antibody was added, and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. After washing thrice, 100 μL of ABC reagent was added, incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, and washed five times. Around 100 μL TMB substrate was added and incubated in the dark at 37 °C until color developed (≤30 min), stopped with 100 μL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Concentrations were calculated using a standard curve and corrected for dilution factors.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay (ChIP-qPCR)

Merck Millipore EZ-Magna ChIP A/G (Merck, 17-10086). a. Formaldehyde-treated cells, cross-linked fixation: target protein-DNA complex; b. Cell lysis and ultrasonic interruption: 200–1000 BP chromatin; c. Antibody binds to the target protein-DNA complex: antibody-target protein-DNA complex; d. Protein A/G-binding antibody-target protein-DNA complex: e. Precipitate the complex and wash it to remove non-specific binding proteins; f. Elution to obtain enriched target protein-DNA complexes; g. Decrosslinking and purification of enriched DNA fragments; h. qPCR analysis. Relative primer sequences are presented in Data S1.

RNA isolation and qPCR analyses

Total RNA was extracted from placental tissues or cultured cells using a FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd.; (China) RC112). RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA in a 10 μL reaction volume using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd; (Chian) R222). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (High ROX Premixed) (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (China), and Q341) using a CFX 96 system. Target gene expression was normalized to that of the housekeeping gene GAPDH. Relative primer sequences are presented in Data S1.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining (HE)

The hydrated sections were stained with a hematoxylin staining solution. The stained sections were rinsed with tap water for several minutes. Immediately after differentiation, the sections were rinsed with tap water or placed in a saturated lithium carbonate solution to stain them blue. Next, the sections were stained blue with eosin for 2–5 min. The sections were successively passed through 95% alcohol I and 95% alcohol II for 2–3 min each, followed by 100% alcohol I and 100% alcohol II for 5–10 min each, for dehydration. Finally, the dehydrated sections were placed in xylene I and II for 5–10 min each to make them transparent, which facilitated the microscopic observation.

IHC

IHC was performed on paraffin sections using the EnVision G2 Double-Stain System (DAKO, K5361, DAB+/Permanent Red). Briefly, slides were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking endogenous enzymes, primary antibodies (TET3, 1:50, CST,99980; FOS, 1:50, CST,31254; PDK4, 1:100, Abcam, ab214938) or negative control IgG were applied. The subsequent steps included incubation with polymer/HRP, DAB solution, and antibodies against α-SMA (1:400, Abcam, ab293182). The slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. The IHC images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica SP5). Immunohistochemical staining (IHC) was performed using antibodies against TET3, FOS, and PDK4 in a human/mouse placental model.

IF

Suitable specimens, such as cell smears and tissue sections, were prepared using different detection objects. Cell smears were evenly smeared on the slide, and the thickness of the tissue section was 3–5 μm. Commonly used fixing agents were acetone, methanol, and formaldehyde. For example, cell smears were fixed with acetone at 4 °C for 10–15 min to maintain the morphology and antigenicity of the cells or tissues. Fluorescein-labeled specific antibodies were added dropwise to the immobilized specimens. The antibody concentration was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions or prior experiments. Briefly, cells were incubated at 37 °C in a wet box for 30 min. The specimen was washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline for 5 min each to remove unbound fluorescent antibodies. The plate was sealed with an anti-fluorescent quenching agent to prevent fluorescence quenching. Cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope. If there is a corresponding antigen in the specimen, specific fluorescence can be observed. The results were judged according to the intensity and distribution of fluorescence.

Western blotting analyses

For western blotting analysis, cell or placental tissue samples (5–10 μg) were homogenized in 2x SDS-sample buffer. Samples were heated, homogenized, and loaded onto 4–15% gradient or 10% SDS gels. The intensity of the protein bands was quantified using the Quantity One software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Protein bands were quantified using ImageJ or Quantity One software (Tanon, China). GAPDH served as an internal control. The uncropped western blot images are shown in Data S2. The antibodies used in this experiment and the conditions used for western blotting are shown in Data S2.

Colony formation assay

The cells were dispersed in a single-cell suspension at 48 h after being transfected. The colony formation assay was conducted by incubating 1×103 cells at 37 °C for 2 weeks in a culture dish containing 10% FBS. The cells were then stabilized and stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and colonies were counted manually. The experiments were repeated thrice for all groups.

CCK-8 assay

The CCK-8 assay was used to quantify the proliferation rate of the cells. Following inoculation into 96-well culture plates (1.5 × 103 cells/well), cells were grown in 100 μL DMEM containing 10% FBS. CCK-8 solution (10 μL) was added at 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h after cellular attachment. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm after 2 h of incubation using an enzyme label.

Transwell assays

Transwell chambers (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) used for cell invasion assays were first pretreated with 50 μL of a 1:9 Matrigel/DMEM solution (BD, New Jersey, USA). Subsequently, 1 × 105 cells were dispersed in DMEM without FBS (1 mL), and 200 μL of the cellular solution was placed in the upper chamber. Subsequently, DMEM containing 10% FBS (600 μL) was added to the lower chamber to act as a chemotactic agent. After 48 h, the residual cells in the upper chamber were scraped off, whereas the invading cells were immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 2% crystal violet. The invading cells were counted under a light microscope (D-35578; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Trophoblast-Endothelium coculture

Human umbilical vein ECs were grown on plastic microscope slide coverslips in 6-well plates until confluent and then labeled with 1 lM CMFDA (Cell Tracker Green; MKbio MX4107-100UG) for 12 h. Transfected HTR-8/SVneo cells were labeled with 1 lM CMTPX (Cell Tracker Red; MKbio MX4109-100UG), added to the HUVEC monolayer (2X105 cells/well), and cultured for 24 h. All the cocultures were washed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The coverslips were mounted on glass slides and examined under an IX71 confocal microscope (Olympus). The area of the HTR-8/SVneo cell ‘‘island’’ (red) within the HUVEC monolayer (green) was then quantified using ImageJ software, which was used to evaluate the ability of HTR-8/SVneo cells to displace HUVEC.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

The experiment was partially commissioned by the Wuhan Mateware Metabolic Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Untargeted metabolomic analysis of HTR-8/SVneo cells overexpressing TET3 and control cells, with six biological replicates per group, was performed. The sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD, https://sciex.com.cn/; MS, QTRAP® System, https://sciex.com/). Data acquisition was performed in information-dependent acquisition mode using the Analyst TF 1.7.1 Software (Sciex, Concord, ON, Canada). LIT and triple quadrupole scans were acquired on a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer (QTRAP), QTRAP® LC-MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled using Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the statistical function prcomp in R (www.r-project.org). The data were unit-variance scaled before unsupervised PCA. The hierarchical cluster analysis results of the samples and metabolites were presented as heatmaps with dendrograms. Pearson correlation coefficients between samples were calculated using the cor function in R. The Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score quantifies the contribution of each metabolite to the group separation in the PLS-DA model. Metabolites with a VIP score > 1.0 are generally considered significant for discriminating between the experimental groups. For the two-group analysis, differential metabolites were determined by VIP (VIP > 1) and P-value (P-value< 0.05, Student’s t test).

Targeted bisulfite sequencing (pyrosequencing)

Briefly, using pyrosequencing, the methylation level of a single base in a specific region of the target gene was detected and entrusted to the Nanjing Chengwen Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (China). Methylation was performed using the EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen59104). CpG islands in FOS and PDK4 promoter regions were selected for methylation sequencing. The selection criteria were as follows: 1. length ≥200 bp; 2. (G + C) content ≥50%, and 3. The observation expectation ratio of dinucleotide CpG was ≥0.60. Primers were designed and amplified using PCR, followed by high-throughput sequencing. Finally, pyrosequencing tools were used for detection, and the methylation status of each site was analyzed using the Pyro Q-CpG software provided with the pyrosequencing instrument. The results are shown in Data S4.

QuantiNova LNA PCR panel gene expression analysis

The PCR array chip used in this study was developed by the well-known German biotechnology company QIAGEN and can simultaneously detect the gene expression profile (84 key genes) involved in glucose metabolism. Using real-time quantitative PCR, a 384-well test plate was used to detect the key genes involved in glucose metabolism in tumor cells. The genes detected using this chip are involved in several key metabolic pathways, including glycogen metabolism, tricarboxylic acid cycle, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and pentose phosphate pathway. RNA extraction and quality testing were then performed, followed by cDNA synthesis. Then Real-time quantitative fluorescent PCR was performed, and cDNA was mixed with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix and added to 384-well plate for real-time qPCR amplification on the quantitative PCR instrument. Finally, the data were analyzed. The experiment was conducted at the Shanghai University Co., LTD (China).

RNA-seq analysis

After total RNA was extracted from the samples, mRNA was enriched with magnetic beads containing oligo (dT). Fragmentation buffer was added to the obtained mRNA to convert fragments into short fragments. The first cDNA strand was synthesized using random hexamers based on fragmented mRNA as a template. The second strand of cDNA was synthesized by adding buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA polymerase I, purified and eluted with EB buffer solution through terminal repair, base A, and sequencing, recovered by agarose gel electrophoresis, and amplified using PCR to complete the entire library preparation. The constructed library was sequenced using Illumina HiSeq2000. All experiments were performed by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (China).

Single-cell sequencing analysis

Single-cell sequencing analysis of the placental tissue was performed. First, the cell population in the tissue was isolated as single cells, and sequencing libraries were constructed independently. Then, whole genome amplification of single cells was performed, and high-coverage single-cell genes were obtained through efficient amplification of trace genomic DNA obtained by dissolving single cells, and the data were analyzed. Genesky Biotechnologies Inc. (Shanghai, China) commissioned the experiment.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All statistical analyses were performed using a two-tailed Student’s T test or ANOVA. Correlation analyses between variables were performed using the Pearson’s rank correlation test. p < 0.05, and the level of significance was assigned as ∗ if p < 0.05, and ∗∗ if p < 0.01.
