Quantum simulation with sum-of-squares spectral amplification
Robbie King, Guang Hao Low, Ryan Babbush, Rolando D. Somma, Nicholas C. Rubin

Abstract
We present sum-of-squares spectral amplification (SOSSA), a framework for improving quantum simulation relevant to low-energy problems. We show how SOSSA can be applied to problems like energy and phase estimation and provide fast quantum algorithms for these problems that significantly improve over prior art. To illustrate the power of SOSSA in applications, we consider the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev model, a representative strongly correlated system, and demonstrate asymptotic speedups over generic simulation methods by a factor of the square root of the system size. Our results reinforce those observed in [G.H. Low et al. , arXiv:2502.15882 (2025)], where SOSSA was used to achieve state-of-the-art gate costs for phase estimation of real-world quantum chemistry systems.

This setting is relevant in the study of matter at low temperatures, including quantum phase transitions and the computation of ground-state properties. During the SOS step,

This step relates to the well-known optimization task of finding the best SOS lower bound on the ground state energy, which can be solved efficiently in classical preprocessing using semidefinite programming. During the SA step we produce a different Hamiltonian, the ‘square root’ of

are now less stringent. Similarly, access to the square root of of

II
SOSSA

The goal of SOSSA is to improve the gate complexity of simulation tasks in the low-energy sector. To this end, SOSSA uses SA for reducing query complexities first. Quantum signal processing and the related quantum singular value transform provide the modern machinery for quantum simulation tasks, relying on access to a block-encoding of a Hamiltonian
H

[
4
,
23
,
24
]
. This is a unitary acting on an enlarged space that contains the matrix
H/\lambda
in one of its blocks, where
\lambda\geq\|H\|
is needed for normalization. In quantum simulation algorithms we are often interested in both, the query and gate complexities. We define the query complexity to be the number of times the block-encodings are used (including their inverses), and the gate complexity to be the total number of two-qubit gates to implement the algorithm, which includes the number of gates to implement the block-encodings in applications.

A standard approach to construct the block-encoding uses an efficient presentation of
H
as a linear combination of unitaries (LCU)
[
25
,
9
]
. Suppose a Hamiltonian
H
acts on a system of
N
qubits and is presented as a sum of terms as

H=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}g_{j}\sigma_{j}\;,

(1)

where
g_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are coefficients and
\sigma_{j}
are (tensor) products of
N
Pauli matrices (i.e. Pauli strings). The query complexity of various quantum simulation tasks through this LCU scales linearly in the so-called
\ell_{1}
-norm of the Hamiltonian:

\lambda_{\lcu}\coloneqq\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|g_{j}|\;.

(2)

The LCU column of
Table

1
presents some known results.

SA allows one to get around the linear cost in
\lambda_{\rm LCU}
in simulation tasks involving states of low energy. SA can only be applied to Hamiltonians that are positive semidefinite. One way to achieve SA is to produce a square root of the Hamiltonian, so that the low-energy spectrum is amplified. Intuitively, this amplification arises because the square root function is steep near zero, i.e.
\sqrt{x}\gg x
for
x\ll 1
, where
x
denotes a rescaled eigenvalue. See
Figure

1
for an illustration. For phase estimation, a precise estimate of a small eigenvalue of the original Hamiltonian can then be obtained by squaring a coarser estimate of an eigenvalue of its square root. For time evolution, the amplified low-energy spectrum allows for a more efficient approximation to the evolution operator using lower degree polynomials in quantum signal processing. In both cases, SA can lead to a lower query complexity than in the worst case. See Appendix
B
for results on SA.

The error bars illustrate that an estimate of an eigenvalue

The approach can be generalized to other functions

whose derivatives are large or divergent as
x\rightarrow 0
and that could arise from other related constructions, such as the quantum-walk where
f(x)=\arccos(x-1)
results in a similar quadratic amplification
[

.

The easiest way to apply SA to a Hamiltonian presented as in Eq. (
1
) is to add a shift to each Pauli string
\sigma_{j}
so that it becomes positive semidefinite; for example,
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\pm\sigma_{j}\succeq 0
, where
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}
is the identity matrix. We call this approach ‘termwise SA’, but also anticipate that this approach is not generally effective. During this preprocessing, the shift produces

\displaystyle H+\lambda_{\lcu}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|g_{j}|\Pi_{j}\;,

(3)

where
\Pi_{j}:=\frac{1}{2}({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+\text{sign}(g_{j})\sigma_{j})
are simple orthogonal projectors since the Pauli strings
\sigma_{j}
have eigenvalues
\pm 1
. We can define the spectral amplified operator for this specific example to be:

H_{\sa}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|g_{j}|^{\frac{1}{2}}|j\rangle\otimes\Pi_{j}=\sqrt{2}\begin{pmatrix}|g_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi_{1}\\
\vdots\\
|g_{R}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi_{R}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(4)

This is a rectangular matrix that acts as a square root of
H+\lambda_{\lcu}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}
since

H_{\sa}^{\dagger}H_{\sa}^{\!}=H+\lambda_{\lcu}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}.

(5)

Consider now a simulation problem where the corresponding quantum states are supported in the low-energy subspace. We can introduce a parameter
\Delta_{\lcu}
that quantifies the low-energy assumption. In particular, for energy estimation
we will assume our states of interest
|\psi\rangle

to satisfy
E=\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\leq-\lambda_{\lcu}+\Delta_{\lcu}
.
For phase estimation
we will assume
\langle\xi|\psi\rangle=0
for every eigenstate
|\xi\rangle
of
H
of eigenvalue
\langle\xi|H|\xi\rangle>-\lambda_{\lcu}+\Delta_{\lcu}
. For these problems, by using the block-encoding of
H_{\sa}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm LCU}}
instead of that of
H/\lambda_{\rm LCU}
, SA allows us to achieve an improvement in the query complexity. See the termwise SA results in
Table

1
, proven in Thms.
5
and
7
. The net effect is an improved query complexity linear in
\sqrt{\Delta_{\lcu}\lambda_{\lcu}}
rather than
\lambda_{\lcu}
. (We can think of
\sqrt{\Delta_{\lcu}\lambda_{\lcu}}
as the new effective
\ell_{1}
-norm when considering the low-energy subspace.) The improvement occurs when
\Delta_{\lcu}\ll\lambda_{\lcu}
, which is possible in specific instances where the Hamiltonian
H
is close to ‘frustration-free’
[
14
]
.

For this example, an improvement in query complexity ultimately gives an improvement in gate complexity, because the corresponding block-encodings can be implemented with similar gate costs (
Lemma

3
). This would readily achieve the goal of SOSSA, however, for general and frustrated
H
, the termwise preprocessing outlined might result in a
\Delta_{\lcu}
that is comparable to
\lambda_{\lcu}
. This severely limits the general applicability of termwise SA, and a different kind of preprocessing is desirable to have a significant reduction in gate costs.

Our strategy is then to use a different representation of the shifted Hamiltonian as an SOS of more general operators
B_{j}
:

\displaystyle H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}B_{j}^{\dagger}B^{\!}_{j}\;.

(6)

Using this representation, which implies
H+\beta\succeq 0
, gives also a path to
applying SA to general Hamiltonians. Indeed, the previous termwise SA is an example of SOS where
\beta=\lambda_{\lcu}
and
B_{j}=|g_{j}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\Pi_{j}
. However, we can now consider the
B_{j}
’s to be linear combinations of Pauli strings
\sigma_{j}
or more general operators. It is important, however, to constrain the
B_{j}
’s so that they can be efficiently block-encoded; see
Section

III
.

SOSSA then reduces the overall gate complexity by first reducing the query complexity via SA as much as possible. We do this by finding ‘good’ SOS representations that have the following properties. Let

H_{\sossa}:=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|j\rangle\otimes B_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}\\
\vdots\\
B_{R}\end{pmatrix}\;

(7)

be such that
H_{\sossa}^{\dagger}H^{\!}_{\sossa}=H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}
. Then, we wish to block-encode of
H_{\sossa}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm SOS}}
efficiently, for some normalization factor
\lambda_{\rm SOS}\geq\|H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\|
, which is often very different from
\lambda_{\rm LCU}
. Additionally, we wish for the lower bound
-\beta
to be as close to the ground state energy of
H
as possible.
For energy estimation, we will assume
\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\leq-\beta+\Delta_{\sos}
and for
phase estimation the low-energy state is supported on the subspace of energies in
[-\beta,-\beta+\Delta_{\sos}]
.
These parameters give
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}
, which determines the query complexity when using SA with the Hamiltonian
H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}
; see the SOSSA results in
Table

1
. The goal of the good SOS representation is to satisfy
\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}\ll\Delta_{\rm LCU}\lambda_{\rm LCU}
and hence improve upon the query complexity of termwise SA.

After obtaining improved query complexities from the SOS representation, we wish to determine the resulting gate complexities, and we need to account for the gate cost of implementing the block-encoding of
H_{\sossa}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm SOS}}
. Unfortunately, this gate cost might be higher than that of implementing the block-encoding of
H_{\rm SA}/\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm SA}}
, since the operators
B_{j}
are more general and possibly more difficult to simulate. Nevertheless, we find that these two competing effects —the improvement in query complexity versus the increase in the gate complexity of each query— can still give a significant improvement overall, as demonstrated by the examples we studied.

Hence, to obtain improved gate complexities, SOSSA uses SA in combination with good SOS representations to reduce the query complexity as much as possible, even when the gate cost per query can increase.

III
SOS optimization for SOSSA

To achieve greater SA, we would like
\Delta_{\sos}
to be as small as possible, so we would like
-\beta
to be a tight lower bound on the ground state energy. In this section, we show that optimizing the lower bound
-\beta
can be achieved efficiently in classical preprocessing using semidefinite programming. Optimization of
-\beta
alone does not directly optimize the total gate cost but can instead improve the query complexity. However, it can be a useful starting point as demonstrated in Ref.
[
16
]
. We will see in
Section

IV
that this optimization is sufficient to achieve asymptotic speedups in the total gate cost for the SYK model.

We can start by selecting an ansatz for the
B_{j}
’s as low-degree polynomials of some natural operator set
\cal B
, the ‘SOS algebra’, of size
L
. For example, given the Hamiltonian on
N
qubits, we can choose a constant
k\geq 1
and let
{\cal B}
be the set of
L={\cal O}(N^{k})
monomials, each being a product of
k
Pauli operators.
Then,
B_{j}(\vec{b}_{j})\in{\cal B}

denotes a polynomial of Pauli strings of weight or degree
k
, where
\vec{b}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
are the coefficients of the polynomial.
For a fermionic Hamiltonian, we could let
{\cal B}
be the set of degree-
k
products of fermionic creation and annihilation operators instead, or equivalently in the Majorana operators. Often, we may not want to include all degree-
k
terms in
{\cal B}
, but only a subset like nearest-neighbor products when considering a system in a lattice.

Having selected the set
\cal B
, we can optimize the lower bound on the ground state energy while constraining the coefficients
\vec{b}_{j}
so that Eq. (
6
) is satisfied. This optimization can be cast as the following program:

\displaystyle\min_{\vec{b}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}}\beta\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}B_{j}(\vec{b}_{j})^{\dagger}B_{j}(\vec{b}_{j}),

which can be directly translated into an SDP whose dimension is polynomial in the operator basis size
L
and whose linear constraints are derived from the algebra of the polynomials; see
Section

C.1
.
Mathematical problems of this form can be solved in polynomial time with respect to the number of variables and constraints
[
26
,
27
,
28
]
.
The resulting number of terms
R
is related to the rank of the primal variable of the SDP and is also polynomial in
L
.

The dual problem of this SDP is the pseudomoment problem
[
19
,
29
]
where it is well understood that increasing the complexity of
B_{j}
by, for example, allowing a larger set of monomials in
\cal B
, and thus the size of the SDP,
-\beta
can be made arbitrarily close to the true ground-state energy, implying a smaller energy gap
\Delta_{\sos}
.

Nevertheless, for the quantum algorithm,
we note that the savings in complexity from a smaller
\Delta_{\sos}

might be obscured by the higher complexity of simulating the terms
B_{j}
.
Additionally, the cost of preprocessing from solving the SDP also increases with
L
, and this classical complexity
can also be a factor of consideration in the overall optimization for a specific application.

IV
Application to SYK

Using the SOSSA framework, we observe an asymptotic speedup in phase estimation of the ground-state energy and energy estimation over traditional approaches that use the block-encoding of the full Hamiltonian, which is constructed from an LCU presentation in terms of Pauli strings. The speedup reduces the gate complexity by a factor
\sqrt{N}
, where
N
is the number of modes. Let
\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}\}
be Majorana operators satisfying anticommutation relations:

\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}+\gamma_{b}\gamma_{a}=2\delta_{ab}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\;.

(8)

The SYK model is described by a fermionic Hamiltonian containing all degree-
4
Majorana terms whose coefficients are random Gaussians; that is,

\displaystyle H_{\syk}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\choose 4}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}g_{abcd}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d}\;,

(9)

\displaystyle g_{abcd}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.}\;.

(10)

When applying SOSSA to the SYK model we will use degree-
2
or quadratic Majorana operators to construct the SOS representation.
In
Lemma

8
we demonstrate that this SOS achieves a lower bound of
\beta={\cal O}(N)
with high probability using random matrix theory to obtain a lower bound on the SOS dual problem.
Furthermore, the spectrum of
H_{\syk}
is known to be contained in the interval
[-c\sqrt{N},c\sqrt{N}]
for some constant
c>0
, and therefore the energy of any state is such that
\Delta_{\sos}=\mathcal{O}(N)
.

To compare the performance of SOSSA with standard approaches or termwise SA, we first note
H_{\syk}
has number of terms scales like
\sim N^{4}
. Hence, the asymptotic gate complexities to construct the necessary block-encodings for the LCU, termwise SA, and SOSSA approaches is
\Theta(N^{4})
in all cases, which are optimal since the number of independent terms in
H_{\syk}
is
\sim N^{4}
. We can thus focus on comparing the query complexities only, which are determined by
\lambda_{\lcu}
,
\sqrt{\Delta_{\lcu}\lambda_{\lcu}}
and
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}
. We have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\lcu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{N}{4}}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}|g_{abcd}|

(11)

for Eq. (
9
), and hence

\lambda_{\lcu}\sim N^{2}
with high probability. Termwise SA does not provide any asymptotic improvement, since the scaling of the ground state energy
E
is dominated by
\lambda_{\lcu}
and so
\Delta_{\lcu}
will asymptotically scale linearly with
\lambda_{\lcu}
. Nevertheless, in
Section

D.3
we show that we can implement a block encoding of
H_{\sossa}
with squared normalization factor
\lambda_{\sos}=\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
. Our approach involves the double factorization technique described in
Section

D.2

[
30
]
. Since the energy gap is
\Delta_{\sos}=\mathcal{O}(N)
, we have
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}={\cal O}(N^{\frac{3}{2}})
for SOSSA, giving an asymptotic speedup by a factor of
\sim\sqrt{N}
over LCU and termwise SA.

’s in the SOS representation are cubic in the
\gamma_{a}
’s, and which is able to achieve a tighter lower bound to the ground state energy where
\beta=\Omega(\sqrt{N})
. Their SOS algebra
\cal B
does not contain all degree-3 Majorana monomials, but rather uses only a particular fragment of the degree-3 terms of size
L=\mathcal{O}(N)
.

We can consider applying SOSSA with the degree-3 SOS from Ref.
[
31
]
. The energy gap will improve to
\Delta_{\sos}={\cal O}(\sqrt{N})
. However, the block-encoding normalization factor scales now as
\lambda_{\sos}\sim N^{\frac{7}{2}}
; see the bound on
\lambda_{\sos}
in
Section

C.2
. We obtain
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}={\cal O}(N^{2})
, recovering the query complexity scaling of LCU and termwise SA. Not considering gate cost of block-encoding, which is likely higher than the termwise LCU method, we recover the termwise SA query cost suggesting higher gate complexities.

This further illustrates that optimization of
-\beta
alone does not guarantee that we will ultimately obtain improved gate complexities. The example analyzed here, which regarded a more involved optimization,
readily provided a factor
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}
that is asymptotically comparable to

\sqrt{\Delta_{\rm LCU}\lambda_{\rm LCU}}
.
This is a consequence of considering more complex generators for the SOS representations.
In addition to the complexity scalings due to these factors, we often expect
the block-encodings of the
SOS representations to be less efficient to implement.
These effects are important when comparing the overall gate complexity of SOSSA to that of termwise SA.

V
Conclusion

We described a framework for fast quantum simulation of low-energy states based on SA and SOS representations. To this end, we developed quantum algorithms for energy and phase estimation that improve over prior art and showed that SOSSA gives asymptotic improvements in gate costs with respect to traditional methods when applied to the SYK Hamiltonian. With the addition of Ref.
[
16
]
, where SOSSA already provided the state of the art for ground state energy estimation in chemical systems, we expect this framework to be generally useful and applicable to other quantum systems.

A.1
Block-encodings from linear combination of unitaries

We discussed quantum simulation when having access to
\textsc{{Be}}[H/\lambda]

25

9

H=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}g_{j}\sigma_{j}

(13)

where the
\sigma_{j}

g_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
are coefficients. When applying LCU,

Definition 1

(
\ell_{1}
-norm in LCU)
.

Define
\lambda_{\lcu}
to be the
\ell_{1}
-norm

\lambda_{\lcu}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|g_{j}|\;.

(14)

Lemma 2

(Compilation of LCU
[
35
]
)
.

Let
H
be as in
Equation

13
and
\lambda_{\rm LCU}\geq\|H\|
be as in
Equation

14
.
Then, it is possible to construct a block-encoding of
H
,
\textsc{{Be}}[H/\lambda_{\rm LCU}]
, using
{\cal O}(RC_{\sigma})
quantum gates, where
C_{\sigma}
is the gate complexity of the
\sigma_{j}

\sigma_{j}
, which are not necessarily unitary, as long as
\|\sigma\|\leq 1
and quantum circuits for the
\textsc{{Be}}[\sigma_{j}]
’s are given.

Proof.

The proof can be found in Ref.
[
35
]
and here we present a version that uses the notation of this work for completeness. First, we attach a ‘clock register’ of dimension
R

\text{SELECT}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|j\rangle\mskip-2.0mu\langle j|_{\rm a}\otimes\sigma_{j}\;,

(15)

where the

are basis states of

qubits. Also, define the state preparation unitary

\displaystyle\text{PREPARE}|0\rangle_{\rm a}\mapsto|\alpha\rangle_{\rm a}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\lcu}}}\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\sqrt{g_{j}}|j\rangle_{\rm a}\;.

∎

Often, we will be interested in instances where the

\sigma_{j}

36

Alternatively, more specialized constructions
[
37

.

Appendix B
Quantum simulation by spectral amplification

In this section we introduce the basic idea of spectral amplification (SA) and provide the results that give Termwise SA and SOSSA in
Table

1
. The first version of SA was put forward in Ref.
[
14
]
under the name spectral gap amplification, in which the goal was to amplify the spectral gap for faster adiabatic quantum computing of frustration-free Hamiltonians rather than amplifying the whole low-energy spectrum for arbitrary Hamiltonians as we consider here. More recently, Ref.
[
5
]
used SA to speedup time evolution for the low-energy subspace, in the block-encoding framework, which gives the third row in
Table

1
. We refer to the approach as SA because it amplifies all the small eigenvalues of eigenvectors in the low part of the spectrum of a positive semidefinite Hamiltonian.

We give the basic results of SA using block-encodings since this is a natural framework for this approach. Later, we will discuss how to construct these block-encodings. In SA we consider first a Hamiltonian of the form

H^{\prime}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}h_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}\;,

(18)

where
h_{j}\succeq 0
are positive semidefinite Hermitian operators satisfying
\|h_{j}\|\leq g_{j}
. We consider a factorization of the
h_{j}
’s so that
h_{j}=A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}
for some operators
A_{j}
. In the following we assume
A_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
to simplify the exposition.
Next we define the corresponding ‘spectral amplified’ operator
H_{\sa}
by

H_{\sa}:=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|j\rangle_{\rm a}\otimes A_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{MR\times M}\;.

(19)

Note that we have enlarged the space and attached an
R

|j\rangle_{\rm a}
can be replaced by any set of
R
mutually orthogonal quantum states and does not necessarily have to be a computational basis state. As a block matrix,
H_{\sa}
is given by

H_{\sa}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{0}\\
\vdots\\
A_{R-1}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(20)

We will see that the main property that makes SA useful is

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}A^{\dagger}_{j}A^{\!}_{j}=H_{\sa}^{\dagger}H_{\sa}^{\!}\;.

(21)

That is,
H_{\sa}
acts as a square root of
H^{\prime}
and now we can use it to produce other operators where the eigenvalues are changed. SA will then use access to
H_{\sa}

\textsc{{Be}}[A_{j}/a_{j}]

Define

\displaystyle\lambda:=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}|a_{j}|^{2}\;.

We can efficiently implement an appropriate block-encoding of

Define the unitary

B.3
Optimality of spectral amplification

Some results in

Definition 3

Appendix C
SOS optimization and example SOSSA block-encoding

In this section we provide necessary details for constructing the mathematical program that determines an SOS representation of a Hamiltonian given an operator basis for the SOS generators such that the lower bound energy is maximized. Recall, that maximizing the lower bound is an important algorithmic component in the SOSSA protocol that can lower the query complexity and end-to-end gate complexity if balanced with the cost of the block-encoding implementation. We also provide an example block-encoding for
H_{\sossa}
expressed as a polynomial of Pauli operators.

C.1
SOS optimization

Section
III
advocates for an SOS representation with increased complexity beyond termwise SA, which resulted in a loose lower bound that potentially negates an advantage through SA. In the following, we will restrict our exposition to Hamiltonians composed of Pauli strings but the construction can be applied more generally. We will also start with the formulation of the SOS optimization as a mathematical program and return later to the motivation of this form. Termwise SA uses a restricted algebra to form an SOS operator (in that case a projector) made from the linear combination of an identity operator and the Pauli operator of the Hamiltonian (Eq. (
3
)). The lower bound
-\beta
on the ground state energy can be improved by providing more variational freedom in the SOS generators, the
B_{j}
operators of Eq. (
6
). The variational protocol can be formulated as a semidefinite program (SDP). As an illustrative example, consider the set of operators constituting the span of one and two-qubit Pauli operators over
N
-qubits organized into a column vector

\vec{X}^{(2)}=\big({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}},X_{1},\dots,Z_{N},X_{1}X_{2},\dots,Z_{N-1}Z_{N}\big)^{T}.

(104)

If our Hamiltonian is
2
-local, then this algebra is sufficient to represent the Hamiltonian. In the following, we consider
\vec{X}^{(k)}
which involves operators involving degree-
k
products of Pauli operators. A particular SOS generator is defined as
B_{j}\coloneqq\vec{b}_{j}\vec{X}^{(k)}
for a row vector,
\vec{b}_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{\binom{N}{k}}
, of complex coefficients to be optimized in order to satisfy Eq. (
6
). To show the optimization protocol is an SDP, we form the SOS representation of the Hamiltonian from a set of operators
\{B_{j}\}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}B_{j}^{\dagger}B^{\!}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\left(\vec{b}_{j}\vec{X}^{(k)}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\vec{b}_{j}\vec{X}^{(k)}\right)

(105)

\displaystyle=\left(\vec{X}^{(k){\dagger}}\right)^{T}\Big(\sum_{j}\vec{b}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\vec{b}_{j}\Big)\vec{X}^{(k)}

(106)

\displaystyle=\left(\vec{X}^{(k){\dagger}}\right)^{T}G\vec{X}^{(k)}\;,

(107)

which can be used to construct the program

\displaystyle\min_{G}\;\beta

(108)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\;\;H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}=\left(\vec{X}^{(k){\dagger}}\right)^{T}G\vec{X}^{(k)}

\displaystyle G\succeq 0.

The above SDP equality constraints are short-hand for relating the coefficients of the operators obtained by expanding the right-hand side of the equality to the left-hand side (the Hamiltonian and the shift). This computationally the equality constraint is represented by letting the Hamiltonian coefficients in matrix form,
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L}
where
L=|\vec{X}^{(k)}|
provide the constants resulting from the Hilbert-Schmidt inner products,
\langle\mathbf{A}|G\rangle
, between constraint matrix
\mathbf{A}
and
G
that encodes the coefficient relationship of the equality constraint. Similarly, the cost function corresponds to minimizing the coefficient associated with the identity operators, which in this case corresponds to the diagonal elements of the Gram matrix
G
. The SDP of Eq. (
108
) can be solved in polynomial time with respect to the linear dimension of
G
. Once the SDP is solved an SOS representation can be recovered by expressing

in its eigenbasis

, where
\{\vec{a}_{j}\}
which yields a definition for each

\vec{X}^{(k){\dagger}}G\vec{X}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}(\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\vec{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\vec{X}^{(k)})^{\dagger}(\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\vec{a}_{j}^{\dagger}\vec{X}^{(k)})=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}B_{j}^{\dagger}B^{\!}_{j}.

(109)

The number of
B_{j}

R
of the Gram matrix
G
.

The intuition behind providing more variational freedom through the form of the SOS generator is that for any
-\beta
that is less than the ground state energy

of some Hamiltonian

\tilde{H}=H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}

\tilde{H}=\sqrt{\tilde{H}}\sqrt{\tilde{H}}
which is potentially a different, and potentially more complicated, many-body body operator. Thus if the equality of Eq. (

[

29

21

Application to SYK model

In this section we describe the application of the SOSSA framework to the SYK model. We will see a factor of
\sqrt{N}
speedup in both query and gate complexities when compared to the standard LCU compilation. This is accomplished by showing that
\Delta_{\sos}
scales linearly in system size with high probability and
\lambda_{\sos}
scales quadratically.

Let
\{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}\}
be Majorana operators satisfying anticommutation relations:

\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}+\gamma_{b}\gamma_{a}=2\delta_{ab}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\;.

(114)

The SYK model is described by a fermionic Hamiltonian containing all degree-4 Majorana terms whose coefficients are random Gaussians where

\displaystyle H_{\syk}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N\choose 4}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}g_{abcd}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d}\;,

(115)

\displaystyle g_{abcd}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{i.i.d.}\;.

(116)

To implement SOSSA, we will use the degree-2 Majorana SOS, which we describe in
Section

D.1
. We will then compile the resulting SOSSA Hamiltonian using the double factorization technique, which we recap in
Section

D.2
. In
Section

D.3
, we use random matrix theory to analyze the energy gap
\Delta_{\sos}
of the degree-2 Majorana SOS on the SYK model, and we also analyze the normalization factor
\lambda_{\sos}
resulting from the double factorization block encoding. Putting these together demonstrate our asymptotic improvements.

D.1
Degree-2 Majorana SOS

For fermionic systems, a natural SOS ansatz is the degree-2 Majorana SOS, which we will use for the SYK model. In this section we describe how to implement degree-2 Majorana SOS for a general fermionic Hamiltonians of the form:

H=i\sum_{a,b=1}^{N}K_{ab}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}-\sum_{a,b,c,d=1}^{N}J_{abcd}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d}

(117)

Our
\sos
is defined by the basis
\{{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}},\gamma_{a},\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}\}
. Using
Section

C.1
, we can write the SOS relaxation as (
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
)

\displaystyle\sos(H)\quad=\qquad

\displaystyle\min{\beta}

(118)

s.t.

\displaystyle H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}=\vec{X}^{\dagger}G\vec{X}

\displaystyle G\succeq 0

where

\vec{X}=\big({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N},i\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2},\dots,i\gamma_{N-1}\gamma_{N}\big)

(119)

and
G
is the Gram matrix with dimension
1+N+{N\choose 2}
. Comparing the coefficients of
{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}
in the polynomial constraint equation reveals that
\beta=\Tr{G}
, since all other operators in the generating set are traceless. Comparing coefficients of the other monomials gives us the linear constraints on the SDP. Thus we can explicitly write the SDP

\displaystyle\min{\Tr{G}}

(120)

s.t.

\displaystyle 0=G({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}},\gamma_{a})+G(\gamma_{a},{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}})+i\sum_{c}G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c},\gamma_{c})-i\sum_{c}G(\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c})

\displaystyle\forall a

\displaystyle K_{ab}=G({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b})+G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b},{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}})-iG(\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b})+iG(\gamma_{b},\gamma_{a})

\displaystyle\qquad-i\sum_{c}G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c})+i\sum_{c}G(i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c})

\displaystyle\forall a,b

\displaystyle 0=G(\gamma_{a},i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c})+G(\gamma_{b},i\gamma_{c}\gamma_{a})+G(\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b})

\displaystyle\qquad+G(i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c},\gamma_{a})+G(i\gamma_{c}\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b})+G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b},\gamma_{c})

\displaystyle\forall a,b,c

\displaystyle J_{abcd}=G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b},i\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d})+G(i\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b})

\displaystyle\qquad-G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{d})-G(i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{d},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c})

\displaystyle\qquad+G(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{d},i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c})+G(i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{d})

\displaystyle\forall a,b,c,d

\displaystyle G\succeq 0

D.2
Double factorization

The degree-2 Majorana SOS leads to a representation of the Hamiltonian that takes the form

\displaystyle H+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}=\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}^{\!}\;,

(121)

\displaystyle B_{j}^{\!}=e_{j}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+\sum_{a}f_{j,a}\gamma_{a}+\sum_{ab}g_{j,ab}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}

(122)

A similar factorization occurs in the 2-body term of quantum chemistry Hamiltonians. Using the direct block encoding strategy,
Definition

4
gives for this SOS representation a SOS
\lambda
of

\lambda_{\sos}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\Big(|e_{j}|+\|f_{j}\|_{1}+\sum_{ab}|g_{j,ab}|\Big)^{2}

(123)

where
R
is the rank of the Gram matrix
G
.

We can achieve a much better
\lambda_{\sos}
using the concept of double factorization from quantum chemistry
[
30
]
, which we now describe. See
[
45
]
for a comprehensive discussion of quantum algorithms techniques for quantum chemistry. Given a quadratic polynomial in the Majorana operators
\sum_{ab}g_{ab}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}
, let’s first decompose
g_{ab}
into it’s real and imaginary parts
g_{ab}=g^{(R)}_{ab}+ig^{(I)}_{ab}
. Without loss of generality,
g^{(R)}_{ab}
and
g^{(I)}_{ab}
are real antisymmetric, since
\gamma_{a}
and
\gamma_{b}
always anticommute. There are Gaussian unitaries
U^{(R)},U^{(I)}
which rotate the quadratic polynomials into the block-diagonal forms

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{ab}g^{(R)}_{ab}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}\Big)

\displaystyle={U^{(R)}}^{\dagger}\sum_{a}\tilde{g}^{(R)}_{a}\gamma_{2a-1}\gamma_{2a}U^{(R)}

(124)

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{ab}g^{(I)}_{ab}\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}\Big)

\displaystyle={U^{(I)}}^{\dagger}\sum_{a}\tilde{g}^{(I)}_{a}\gamma_{2a-1}\gamma_{2a}U^{(I)}

(125)

This is because a Gaussian unitary
U
acts on the
N
-vector of Majorana operators
(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N})
via a real orthogonal matrix
O
, and we can always design
U
so that
O
block diagonalizes a given antisymmetric matrix
g_{ab}
:

\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}&\dots&g_{1,N}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\\
g_{N,1}&\dots&&g_{N,N}\end{pmatrix}=O^{T}\begin{pmatrix}0&g_{1}&&&\\
-g_{1}&0&&&\\
&&\ddots&&\\
&&&0&g_{N}\\
&&&-g_{N}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}O

(126)

Let’s find such Gaussian unitaries
U^{(R)}_{j},U^{(I)}_{j}
for each
g_{j,ab}
. This let’s us write
B_{j}
as

B_{j}=e_{j}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+\sum_{a}f_{j,a}\gamma_{a}+{U^{(R)}}^{\dagger}\sum_{a}\tilde{g}^{(R)}_{(j,a)}\gamma_{2a-1}\gamma_{2a}U^{(R)}+{U^{(I)}}^{\dagger}\sum_{a}i\tilde{g}^{(I)}_{(j,a)}\gamma_{2a-1}\gamma_{2a}U^{(I)}

(127)

We can efficiently implement the Gaussian unitaries
U_{j}
on the quantum computer. Thus we can follow
Definition

4
to derive
\lambda_{\sos}
for the double factorized SOS representation, and the proof of
Corollary

3
will go through. We get

\lambda_{\sos}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\big(|e_{j}|+\|f_{j}\|_{1}+\|\tilde{g}^{(R)}_{j}\|_{1}+\|\tilde{g}^{(I)}_{j}\|_{1}\big)^{2}

(128)

This could be significantly cheaper than directly block encoding
B_{j}
, which would have
\lambda_{\sos}
as give in
Equation

123
.

D.3
Application to SYK model

We can now bring all of these ingredients together to analyze the performance of degree-2 Majorana SOSSA with double factorization on the SYK model, when compared to the standard LCU approach.

The asymptotic gate complexities to construct the necessary block-encodings for LCU and SOSSA are both
{\cal O}(N^{4})
. First, notice that the number of terms in
H_{\syk}
is
\sim N^{4}
, with each coefficient independently random, and each of the terms are independent. This creates an
{\cal O}(N^{4})
gate cost for block encoding
H_{\syk}
using known methods based on
SELECT
and
PREPARE
unitaries which is expected to be optimal.
Double factorization also allows for a
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
gate cost: the cost of the
B_{j}
are dominated by the costs of the Gaussian unitaries, which are
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
, and the number of
B_{j}
is at most
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
. The asymptotic equivalence of the block encoding gate costs means that we can focus on comparing the query complexities
\lambda_{\lcu}
and
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}
.

For the SYK model,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\lcu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{N}{4}}}\sum_{a,b,c,d}|g_{abcd}|

(129)

which scales like
\lambda_{\lcu}\sim N^{2}
. We will see that
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}\sim N^{\frac{3}{2}}
, giving a factor of
\sqrt{N}
improvement in the query complexity.

We begin by analyzing the scaling of
\Delta_{\sos}
. This will depend on the quality of the SOS lower bound
-\beta
arising from the degree-2 Majorana SOS described in
Section

D.1
. The SYK Hamiltonian is traceless,
\Tr(H_{\syk})=0
, implying that the ground state energy is negative, and it is known that with high probability the minimum and maximum eigenvalues
are proportional to
\sqrt{N}

[
31
]
.
The following lemma shows that applying degree-2 Majorana SOS to the SYK models gives a lower bound
-\beta
, where
\beta=\mathcal{O}(N)
, giving an energy gap of
\Delta_{\sos}=\mathcal{O}(N)
.

Lemma 8
.

Degree-2 Majorana SOS achieves a lower bound of
-\beta
on the ground energy of
H_{\syk}
, for
\beta=\mathcal{O}(N)
, with high probability.

Proof.

Our proof strategy is to use the dual problem to the SOS optimization. The SOS optimization obeys Slater’s condition and thus exhibits strong duality – see Section 5.2.3 of
[
26
]
. Thus, in order to show the lower bound where
\beta=\mathcal{O}(N)
, it is sufficient to show that the lowest achievable energy in the dual problem is at least
-cN
for some constant
c>0
. That is, no solution to the dual problem can take a value lower than
-cN
.

Appendix B of
[
19
]
allows us to express the dual optimization problem to the
\sos
. Define a degree-2 pseudoexpectation to be a matrix
\tilde{\rho}
whose rows and columns are indexed by quadratic Majorana operators, and where we impose that
\tilde{\rho}
is positive semi-definite and obeys algebraic constraints

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b},i\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d})=-\tilde{\rho}(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{d})=\tilde{\rho}(i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{d},i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c})

(130)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{c},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{d})=-\tilde{\rho}(i\gamma_{b}\gamma_{d},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{c})=\tilde{\rho}(i\gamma_{c}\gamma_{d},i\gamma_{a}\gamma_{b}).

(131)

Let
J
be the matrix with rows/columns indexed by subsets of
\{1,\dots,N\}
of size 2, with entries

J(ab,cd)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{N\choose 4}}}g_{abcd}

(132)

where
g_{abcd}
are the SYK coefficients. The dual problem is to minimize
\Tr(J\tilde{\rho})
over degree-2 pseudoexpectations
\tilde{\rho}
.

To complete the proof, we show that any degree-2 pseudoexpectation
\tilde{\rho}
obeys
\Tr(J\tilde{\rho})\geq-cN
with high probability. We have

\Tr(J\tilde{\rho})\geq-\|J\|\cdot\|\tilde{\rho}\|_{1}=-\|J\|\cdot\Tr(\tilde{\rho})=-{N\choose 2}\|J\|\;,

(133)

where
\|.\|
is the operator norm.
The first inequality was an application of matrix Holder’s inequality
[
46
]
. Random matrix theory tells us that
\|J\|=\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})
with high probability. For example, see Theorem 4.4.5 in Ref.
[
47
]
. Thus
\Tr(J\tilde{\rho})\geq-cN
for any degree-2 pseudoexpectation
\tilde{\rho}
with high probability over the SYK disorder.
∎

Next we analyze
\lambda_{\sos}
, which will involve the double factorization block encoding technique described in
Section

D.2
. The degree-2 Majorana SOS with double factorization writes the SYK Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H_{\syk}+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}=\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}

(134)

\displaystyle B_{j}=e_{j}{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}+\sum_{a}f_{j,a}\gamma_{a}+{U^{(R)}}^{\dagger}\sum_{a}\tilde{g}^{(R)}_{(j,a)}\gamma_{2a-1}\gamma_{2a}U^{(R)}+{U^{(I)}}^{\dagger}\sum_{a}i\tilde{g}^{(I)}_{(j,a)}\gamma_{2a-1}\gamma_{2a}U^{(I)}

(135)

where the
U_{j}
are Gaussian unitaries. From
Equation

128
, we can calculate

\displaystyle\lambda_{\sos}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\big(|e_{j}|+\|f_{j}\|_{1}+\|\tilde{g}^{(R)}_{j}\|_{1}+\|\tilde{g}^{(I)}_{j}\|_{1}\big)^{2}

(136)

\displaystyle\leq 4N\Big(\sum_{j}|e_{j}|^{2}+\sum_{j}\|\tilde{f}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j}\|\tilde{g}^{(R)}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}+\sum_{j}\|\tilde{g}^{(I)}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\Big)

(Cauchy-Schwarz)

(137)

\displaystyle\leq 4N\cdot\overline{\Tr}\Big(\sum_{j}B_{j}^{\dagger}B_{j}\Big)

(138)

\displaystyle=4N\cdot\overline{\Tr}\big(H_{\syk}+\beta{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\big)

(139)

\displaystyle=4N\beta

(140)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(N^{2})

(
Lemma

8
)

(141)

where
\overline{\Tr}
denotes the normalized trace so that
\overline{\Tr}({\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}})=1
. Putting this together with the above analysis showing
\Delta_{\sos}=\mathcal{O}(N)
gives
\sqrt{\Delta_{\sos}\lambda_{\sos}}=\mathcal{O}(N^{\frac{3}{2}})
.

Appendix E
Bonus: Low-depth amplified amplitude estimation

In this section, we use spectral amplification the reduce the sample complexity of estimating to standard deviation
\sigma
, the expectations of observables
E=\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle
represented by a sum-of-squares
H=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}A^{\dagger}_{j}A_{j}

Eq.

21
, using circuits of very low-depth, and given copies of
|\psi\rangle
.
The expectation estimation results of in
Section

B.1
perform phase estimation using circuits with query and depth complexity
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\Delta\lambda}/\epsilon)
.
Here, we instead perform Hadamard tests on the block-encoding
\textsc{Be}\left[\frac{A^{\dagger}_{j}A_{j}}{\frac{1}{2}|a_{j}|^{2}}-\mathbbm{1}\right]
, which can be formed by
Lemma

4
using only one query to
\textsc{Be}[A_{j}/a_{j}]
and its inverse.
This leads worse scaling with accuracy, but the low-depth circuits make this application more relevant to near-term applications.
By estimating terms with smaller normalization
a_{j}
to lower precision and thus fewer samples, previous approaches would have a sample complexity
{\cal O}\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}{|a_{j}|^{2}}/\sigma\right)^{2}\right)

[
48
]
.
However, using prior knowledge of upper bounds on the expectation of each term, say
\Delta_{j}\geq\langle\psi|A^{\dagger}_{j}A_{j}|\psi\rangle
, SA allows us to achieve a sample complexity of
{\cal O}\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}{\sqrt{\Delta_{j}}|a_{j}|}/\sigma\right)^{2}\right)
as follows from the following theorem.

Theorem 9

(Expectation estimation by sums of squares at the standard quantum limit)
.

Let
H
be any operator with a SOS decomposition
H=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}
.
Let
\textsc{{Be}}[A_{j}/a_{j}]
be a block-encoding of
A_{j}
.
Let each
\Delta_{j}\geq\langle\psi|A^{\dagger}_{j}A_{j}|\psi\rangle=\langle H_{j}\rangle
, be any upper bound on the respective expectations.
Then the expectation
\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle
can be estimated with variance at most
\sigma^{2}
and no bias by repeating
N_{j}
times the Hadamard test on
\textsc{{Be}}[2A^{\dagger}_{j}A_{j}a^{-2}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}]
for each
j
for total number of times

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}N_{j}=2\left(\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\sqrt{a_{j}^{2}\Delta_{j}}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}.

(142)

Proof.

Let
\Lambda_{j}:=\frac{1}{2}a_{j}^{2}
,
\phi_{j}:=\langle H_{j}\rangle/\Lambda_{j}
, and
p_{j}:=1-(1-\phi_{j})^{2}

Using one query to
\textsc{{Be}}[A_{j}/a_{j}]
and
\textsc{{Be}}[A_{j}/a_{j}]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\textsc{CBe}_{j}:=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes I+|1\rangle\langle 1|\otimes\textsc{{Be}}_{j},

(143)

where
\textsc{Be}_{j}=\textsc{{Be}}\left[\frac{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\right]
is self-inverse.

The Hadamard test applies a controlled unitary
U

|+\rangle
and
|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle(H\otimes{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}})U|+\rangle|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(|0\rangle(I+U)|\psi\rangle+|1\rangle(I-U)|\psi\rangle\right).

(144)

The probability of measuring
|0\rangle
is
p=\frac{1}{4}\langle\psi|(I+U)^{\dagger}(I+U)|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(1+\langle\psi|\mathrm{Re}[U]|\psi\rangle)

Now choose

\textsc{{Be}}\left[\frac{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\right]

|+\rangle|0\rangle|\psi\rangle
.
Then we obtain
|0\rangle
with probability

\displaystyle p_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\langle 0|\langle\psi|\mathrm{Re}\left[\textsc{{Be}}\left[\frac{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\right]\right]|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\langle 0|\langle\psi|\left[\textsc{{Be}}\left[\frac{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\right]\right]|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\langle 0|\langle\psi|\left[\frac{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}}-{\mathchoice{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.0mul}{\rm 1\mskip-4.5mul}{\rm 1\mskip-5.0mul}}\right]|0\rangle|\psi\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\psi|\left[\frac{A_{j}^{\dagger}A_{j}}{\Lambda_{j}}\right]|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\phi_{j}

(145)

Let
X_{j,x}\sim\mathcal{B}(p_{j})
be identical independently distributed random variables following the Bernouli distribution.
If we repeat
N_{j}
times, let us estimate
\phi_{j}
using the estimator
\hat{\phi}_{j}=\frac{2}{N_{j}}\sum_{x\in[N_{j}]}X_{j,x}
.
This estimator has expectation
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{j}]=\phi_{j}
and variance
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\phi}_{j}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{j}])^{2}]=\frac{4}{N_{j}}p_{j}(1-p_{j})=\frac{2}{N_{j}}\phi_{j}(1-\frac{1}{2}\phi_{j})\leq\frac{2}{N_{j}}\phi_{j}.
.

Let us estimate
\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle

\langle\hat{H}\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\Lambda_{j}\hat{\phi}_{j}
.
Then this estimator has expectation and variance

\displaystyle\langle\hat{H}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\Lambda_{j}{\phi}_{j}=\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle,

(146)

\displaystyle\langle(\hat{H}-\langle H\rangle)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\Lambda^{2}\mathrm{Var}[\hat{\phi}_{j}]\leq\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\frac{4\Lambda_{j}^{2}\phi_{j}}{N_{j}}=\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\frac{4\Lambda_{j}\langle H\rangle}{N_{j}}

(147)

Let us now choose
N_{j}
so that the variance is bounded by
\sigma^{2}
.
We also want to choose
N_{j}
in a way that minimizes the overall number of measurements

The optimal

\lambda

\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\frac{4\Lambda_{j}\langle H_{j}\rangle}{N_{j}}=\sigma^{2}

However, we do not know

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial N_{j}}

\displaystyle=1-\lambda\frac{4\Lambda_{j}\Delta_{j}}{N_{j}^{2}}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad N_{j}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\lambda}\sqrt{{4\Lambda_{j}\Delta_{j}}},

(148)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\sqrt{4\Lambda_{j}\Delta_{j}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sqrt{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\sqrt{4\Lambda_{j}\Delta_{j}},

(149)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cost}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{R-1}\sqrt{4\Lambda_{j}\Delta_{j}}\right)^{2}=\lambda\sigma^{2}.

(150)
