STSA: Federated Class-Incremental Learning via Spatial-Temporal Statistics Aggregation
Zenghao Guan, Guojun Zhu, Yucan Zhou, Wu Liu, Weiping Wang, Jiebo Luo, Xiaoyan Gu

Abstract
Federated Class-Incremental Learning (FCIL) enables Class-Incremental Learning (CIL) from distributed data. Existing FCIL methods typically integrate old knowledge preservation into local client training. However, these methods cannot avoid spatial-temporal client drift caused by data heterogeneity and often incur significant computational and communication overhead, limiting practical deployment. To address these challenges simultaneously, we propose a novel approach, S patial- T emporal S tatistics A ggregation (STSA), which provides a unified framework to aggregate feature statistics both spatially (across clients) and temporally (across stages). The aggregated feature statistics are unaffected by data heterogeneity and can be used to update the classifier in closed form at each stage. Additionally, we introduce STSA-E, a communication-efficient variant with theoretical guarantee, achieving similar performance to STSA with much lower communication overhead. Extensive experiments on three widely used FCIL datasets, with varying degrees of data heterogeneity, show that our method outperforms state-of-the-art FCIL methods in terms of performance, flexibility, and both communication and computation efficiency.



1
Introduction

Federated Learning (FL)
(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
is an emerging distributed machine learning framework that enables multiple parties to participate in collaborative learning under the coordination of a central server. The central server aggregates the model updates uploaded by clients rather than the private data. This mechanism addresses the data silos problem and provides privacy benefits for distributed learning
(
Kairouz et al. 2021
)
.

Existing FL methods
(
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Dong et al. 2022
)
typically assume that data categories and domains remain static throughout the entire training and testing phases. However, this assumption fails to align with real-world environments, where data distributions are dynamic and continuously evolving
(
Yu et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024c
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2024b
)
. To tackle FL tasks in dynamic environments, recent research has incorporated Continual Learning (CL) techniques into the FL framework, giving rise to the approach known as Federated Continual Learning (FCL)
(
Dong et al. 2022
;
Ma et al. 2022
;
Yoon et al. 2021
)
. In FCL, Federated Class-Incremental Learning (FCIL)
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
;
Tran et al. 2024
)
has gained significant attention in recent years due to its wide applicability
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Kaissis et al. 2021
)
. This setting allows new classes to be added flexibly at any time. In this paper, we focus on global FCIL
(
Dong et al. 2022
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Tran et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
, where a single global model is collaboratively trained across clients to learn new classes over time, aiming to enhance the generalization of the global model across all encountered classes.

In FCIL, we need to simultaneously address two core challenges: catastrophic forgetting and data heterogeneity. Existing FCIL methods typically follow a paradigm where the retention of old knowledge is integrated directly into local client training through techniques such as experience replay
(
Dong et al. 2022
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
, weight regularization
(
Li & Hoiem 2017
)
, generative replay
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Qi et al.
;
Babakniya et al. 2023
;
Tran et al. 2024
)
, or network expansion
(
Ma’sum et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. However, this paradigm still suffers from
spatial-temporal client drift
caused by data heterogeneity. Specifically, each client has a distinct data distribution, leading to different balances in the optimization process between learning new tasks and preserving old knowledge. This disparity causes inconsistent local updates, degrading the global model’s generalizability to both new and old tasks. Moreover, existing methods often necessitate iterative local updates and numerous communication rounds between clients and the server. This imposes significant computational burdens on client devices and leads to high communication overhead, posing practical limitations, especially for resource-constrained clients. These challenges motivated us to explore whether it is possible to break this paradigm and design a new method that effectively mitigates
spatial-temporal client drift
caused by data heterogeneity while maintaining low computational and communication overhead.

In this paper, we propose a novel approach named
S
patial-
T
emporal
S
tatistics
A
ggregation (STSA). Instead of using aggregated local updates to update the global model, STSA aggregates feature statistics spatially (across clients) to learn new tasks and temporally (across stages) to retain old knowledge, using a fixed global feature extractor. Specifically, in the first stage of STSA, all clients engage in federated training to obtain a global model. The trained backbone is then fixed and distributed among all clients for feature statistics extraction at each stage. These statistics are first aggregated across clients and then across stages. Notably, the aggregated feature statistics are equivalent to those derived from all data samples across both dimensions due to their linear characteristics, so they are not affected by data heterogeneity. Finally, these aggregated statistics are used to update the classifier in closed form at each stage. Since our method requires no training after the first stage and only needs one round of communication to upload feature statistics, it significantly improves computation and communication efficiency. Moreover, we propose a more communication-efficient variant, termed STSA-E, which enables the server to approximate global second-order feature statistics based on first-order statistics transmitted from clients. Theoretical analysis shows that STSA-E achieves similar statistical performance to STSA while considerably reducing communication overhead. Extensive experiments on three widely used FCIL datasets demonstrate that our method achieves compelling performance, greater flexibility, and significantly reduced communication and computation overhead compared to state-of-the-art baselines. In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a novel FCIL method called STSA, which offers a unified way to aggregate feature statistics both spatially (across clients) and temporally (across stages). The aggregated feature statistics are immune to data heterogeneity and can be used to update the classifier in closed form at each stage.

•

We propose STSA-E, a communication-efficient variant where the server approximates global second-order feature statistics using first-order statistics sent by clients. Theoretical and experimental results show that it achieves comparable performance to STSA while significantly reducing communication overhead.

•

We conduct extensive experiments to validate our method. The results demonstrate significant improvements in performance, flexibility, and both communication and computational efficiency compared to state-of-the-art FCIL methods.

2
Related Work

Class-Incremental Learning.
Continual learning (CL) enables a learning system to acquire knowledge from sequentially arriving tasks without catastrophic forgetting
(
Zhou et al. 2024d
)
. In CL, Class-Incremental Learning (CIL) is the most popular setting due to its high relevance to real-world applications
(
Zhou et al. 2024d
;
Babakniya et al. 2023
)
. In this setting, each new task introduces additional classes to the output space, progressively increasing the total number of classes. Existing CIL methods address catastrophic forgetting using various strategies, such as data replay
(
Aljundi et al. 2019
;
Shim et al. 2021
)
, weight regularization
(
Ahn et al. 2019
;
Pan et al. 2020
)
, knowledge distillation
(
Li & Hoiem 2017
;
Simon et al. 2021
)
, and network expansion
(
Wang et al. 2022
;
Smith et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024c
)
. Although current CIL methods have made significant progress, they often struggle to perform well in the FCIL setting. This is due to the new challenges introduced by FL, such as data heterogeneity, communication constraints, and privacy concerns.

Federated Class-Incremental Learning.
GLFC
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
is the first to introduce the FCIL setting and utilizes stored historical exemplars for local relation distillation. Various strategies are then proposed to make the best use of historical exemplars to mitigate catastrophic forgetting
(
Dong et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
. However, storing data from previous tasks is prohibited due to strict privacy regulations and policies in many privacy-sensitive scenarios, e.g., hospitals and medical research institutions
(
Kaissis et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tran et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, the focus has shifted to more challenging exemplar-free scenarios. Existing state-of-the-art exemplar-free FCIL methods can be broadly classified into two categories: (1) Data-Free Knowledge Distillation (DFKD) approaches
(
Qi et al.
;
Babakniya et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tran et al. 2024
)
and (2) Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) approaches
(
Ma’sum et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Bagwe et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2024
)
. DFKD-based FCIL methods train a generator to generate synthetic samples based on the predictions of the global model. These synthetic samples are stored for training subsequent tasks. However, storing and transferring large volumes of synthetic data between the server and clients would incur significant storage and communication costs. PEFT-based FCIL methods store knowledge from previous stages in prompt
(
Ma’sum et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Bagwe et al. 2023
)
or LoRA
(
Guo et al. 2024
)
parameters and retain it using similarity matching or task arithmetic. However, these methods fail to fully leverage the generalization capabilities
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
)
of pre-trained models and still perform poorly under high data heterogeneity. Besides, they suffer from computational inefficiency due to iterative backpropagation steps, placing a significant burden on client-side resources.

3
Method

3.1
Problem Definition

In FCIL, we have a set of
T
tasks
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathcal{T}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
shared among
K
clients, where the set of classes
\mathcal{C}_{t}
(
|\mathcal{C}_{t}|=c_{t}
) available at each time step
t
is disjoint. Each client
k
only have access to private dataset
\small\mathcal{D}_{t,k}

with size
n_{t,k}
at task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
. Here we denote
\bm{n}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{t}}
as the label frequency. During the training of task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, each local client
k
trains its local model
\theta_{t,k}
using its local data
\mathcal{D}_{t,k}
and then uploads the updated model parameters to the server. The server aggregates the local models to obtain the global model
\theta_{t}
and distribute it to all clients as the initialization for the next round of training. This process is repeated for multiple rounds, and the final global model
\theta_{t}
can distinguish the samples belonged to the classes set
\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

3.2
Spatial-Temporal Statistics Aggregation (STSA)

First Stage Adaptation.

At task
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, each client participates in federated training to obtain a global model
\theta_{1}
. Any federated learning algorithm can be used in this phase. After training, we freeze the feature extractor
F
from the global model
\theta_{1}
for further use.

Spatial Statistics Aggregation.

For each task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
(
t>0
), each client
k
uses this fixed feature extractor
F
to extract features from its local dataset. Given that the extracted features may lack sufficient expressiveness for clear class separation
(
Cover 2006
;
McDonnell et al. 2024
)
, we apply a nonlinear random mapping to project the extracted features into a higher-dimensional space (from
d
to
M
,
M>d
):

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{t,k}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Phi(x_{1}),\Phi(x_{2}),\cdots,\Phi(x_{n_{t,k}})\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(1)

where
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n_{t,k}}=\mathcal{D}_{t,k}
,
\Phi=R\circ F
represents the composition of the feature extractor
F
and the random mapping layer
R
, and
\bm{X}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t,k}\times M}
denotes the concatenation of all feature vectors. Here,
R
consists of a random matrix followed by a ReLU function. This
R
is fixed after initialization and shared across all clients. To reduce storage overhead in the clients, the server can distribute a common
seed
to all clients. This allows clients to generate the same random matrix locally using the
seed
. After obtaining high-dimensional features, clients can delete it and regenerate it when needed.

Subsequently, each client computes the local gram matrix
\bm{G}_{t,k}\!=\!\!\bm{X}_{t,k}^{\top}\bm{X}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
and the feature-label correlation matrix
\bm{C}_{t,k}\!=\!\!\bm{X}_{t,k}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times c_{t}}
. Here
\bm{Y}_{t,k}
denotes the corresponding label matrix stacked by one-hot vectors. The Spatial Statistics
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t,k}=\{\bm{G}_{t,k},\bm{C}_{t,k}\}
of each client
k
is then uploaded to the server and aggregated as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{G}_{t,k},\quad\bm{C}_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{t,k}.

(2)

Due to the linear additivity of these statistics, we have:

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{\text{centralized}}=\!\!\!\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\;\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t,k}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\Phi(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{G}_{t,k}=\bm{G}_{t}.

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\text{centralized}}=\!\!\!\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\text{OneHot}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\;\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{t,k}}\!\!\!\Phi(x)

{}^{\top}\text{OneHot}(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{t,k}=\bm{C}_{t}

(3)

This demonstrates that the statistics derived from Spatial Statistics Aggregation are consistently equivalent to those from the centralized setting (with access to all data from all clients at stage
t
). Therefore, these statistics are invariant to varing levels of data heterogeneity.

Temporal Statistics Aggregation.

The server then aggregates the Aggregated Spatial Statistics
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{t}=\{\bm{G}_{t},\bm{C}_{t}\}
with Temporal Statistics
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{1:t-1}=\{\bm{G}_{1:t-1},\bm{C}_{1:t-1}\}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{1:t}=\bm{G}_{1:t-1}+\bm{G}_{t},\quad\bm{C}_{1:t}=[\bm{C}_{1:t-1},\bm{C}_{t}],

(4)

where
\bm{G}_{1:t-1}\!\!=\!\!\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\bm{G}_{i}
and
\bm{C}_{1:t-1}\!=\![\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2},\!\cdots,\!\bm{C}_{t-1}]
are accumulated along
1\sim(t-1)
incremental stages.
\bm{G}_{1:t}
and
\bm{C}_{1:t}
are the latest Temporal Statistics need to store for further use. Given the linear additivity of these statistics, we have:

\displaystyle\small\bm{G}_{\text{joint}}

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{1:t}}\!\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\Phi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\Phi(x)=\bm{G}_{1:t}

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\text{joint}}

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{1:t}}\!\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\text{One-Hot}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\!\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}\!\!\!\Phi(x)^{\top}\text{One-Hot}(y)=\bm{C}_{1:t}.

(5)

This indicates that the statistics derived through Temporal Statistics Aggregation are consistently equivalent to those obtained in the joint setting (with access to all data from task
\mathcal{T}_{1}
to task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
).

Classifier Update.
After the first stage adaptation, the backbone remains fixed, and only the classifier is updated. The objective of our STSA is to build a strong classifier capable of accurately distinguishing all previously encountered classes. We formulate the solving procedure of the classifier as a regularized least squares form (ridge regression):

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{W}}\|\bm{X}_{\text{joint}}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}_{\text{joint}}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\gamma\|\bm{W}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

(6)

Here,
\bm{X}_{\text{joint}}
represents the complete feature matrix,
\bm{Y}_{\text{joint}}
represents the label matrix of all data samples from tasks
\mathcal{T}_{1}
to
\mathcal{T}_{t}
,
\gamma
controls the strength of the regularization. The close-formed solution of Eq.
6
is:

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\star}

\displaystyle=(\bm{G}_{\text{joint}}+\gamma\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{C}_{\text{joint}}

(7)

Based on Eq.
5
, we have:

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{t}

\displaystyle=(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{C}_{1:t}

\displaystyle=(\bm{G}_{\text{joint}}+\gamma\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{C}_{\text{joint}}=\bm{W}^{\star}.

(8)

Through our Spatial-Temporal Statistics Aggregation, we can obtain the feature statistics required for ridge regression on all data at any stage. Finally, the global model at the end of each stage
t
is
\theta_{t}=\{F,R,\bm{W}_{t}\}
.

Discussion.

Built on the widely observed finding that the feature extractor trained in the first stage has a certain level of generalizability to new classes
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
;
Zhuang et al. 2022
;
McDonnell et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024c
)
, our
STSA extends online least squares
(
Farhang-Boroujeny 2013
;
Camoriano et al. 2017
;
Zhuang et al. 2022
;
McDonnell et al. 2024
)
to the FCIL scenario by providing a unified way to aggregate feature statistics across both temporal and spatial dimensions. In any scenario with restricted access to the complete dataset, the required statistics for ridge regression on the full dataset can be effectively obtained through our aggregation scheme.

3.3
A communication-efficient variant (STSA-E)

The communication overhead of uploading
\bm{G}_{t,k}
(
M\times M
) increases significantly as
M
grows. To address this, we propose a communication-efficient variant of STSA, named STSA-E. It enables the server to approximate global second-order feature statistics
\bm{G}_{t}
using first-order statistics uploaded from clients.

Proposition 3.1

For each task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{G}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{c_{t}}\Bigg[\frac{n^{(i)}_{t}-1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\big(\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\big)\big(\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\big)^{\top}}{n_{t,k}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle-\frac{n^{(i)}_{t}-K}{n^{(i)}_{t}(K-1)}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\bigg)^{\top}\Bigg]

(9)

is an unbiased plug-in estimator of the global gram matrix
\bm{G}_{t}
when the global feature set

\Omega=\{(\Phi(x),y)\mid(x,y)\in\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{D}_{t,k}\}
is i.i.d. Here,
\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}
represents the
i
-th column of
\bm{C}_{t,k}
,
n_{t}^{(i)}
is the total number of samples from class
i
at stage
t
, and
n_{t,k}^{(i)}
is the number of samples from class
i
for client
k
at stage
t
.

Proposition 3.1 indicates that each client
k
only needs to transmit the correlation matrix
\bm{C}_{t,k}
and the label frequency vector
\bm{n}_{t,k}
(
\bm{n}_{t,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{t}}
) for task
t
, significantly reducing communication overhead. The server leverages these uploaded statistics to compute the estimated gram matrix
\hat{\bm{G}_{t}}
and performs temporal statistics aggregation (Eq.
4
) to derive the accumulated global gram matrix
\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}
. Then
\bm{G}_{1:t}
is used in Eq.
3.2
to update the classifier:

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{t}=(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{C}_{1:t}.

(10)

Then we provide two key theoretical results to analyze the estimation error of STSA-E in comparison to STSA. The full descriptions and proofs can be found in
Appendix
B
.

We consider a noisy linear regression model, where
n=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i}
and
n>M
. Specifically, we assume the existence of a ground-truth weight matrix
\bm{W}_{t}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times c_{\text{~joint}}}
and noise
\mathcal{E}_{joint}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c_{\text{~joint}}}
such that
\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}=\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}^{0}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{~joint}}
, where
c_{\text{~joint}}=\sum_{i}^{t}c_{i}
.

For each task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, and for every
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,c_{t}\right\}
, we make the following assumptions:

Assumption 1 (Data Structure).

\|\bm{X}_{t}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n_{t}}C_{\bm{X}}
,
\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{t}^{(i)}\right)\leq C_{\Sigma}
and
\|\mu_{t}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{\mu}
for some constants
C_{\bm{X}},C_{\Sigma},C_{\mu}>0
, where
\mu_{t}^{(i)}
and
\Sigma_{t}^{(i)}
are the class-wise mean and covariance of the global feature set
\Omega
at stage
t
.

Assumption 2 (Class Balance).
There exists a constant
0<C\ll c_{t}
such that
c_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{t}}n_{t}^{(i)2}\leq Cn_{t}^{2}
.

Assumption 3 (Stability).

\|\bm{W}_{t}^{0}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{M}r
for some constant
r>0
.

Assumption 4 (Noise).
The
i
-th row of noise satisfies the following conditions:
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{E}_{\text{~joint},i}^{\top})=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbb{E}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\text{~joint},i}^{\top}\mathcal{E}_{\text{~joint},i}\right)=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{M}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\text{~joint}}
is independent of
\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}
.

Theorem 3.3 (STSA-E plug-in error).

Under Assumptions 1-2, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}\leq O\left(\frac{\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+1)}\right).

(11)

Remark 1.
Theorem 3.3 shows that the plug-in error decreases as the average per-stage sample size
\frac{n}{t}
increases.

Corollary 3.4 (Estimation error).

Under Assumptions 1-4, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}\leq O\left(\frac{\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+1)}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(12)

Remark 2.
Corollary 3.4 reveals that our STSA-E achieves
O\left(\mathrm{tn}^{-1}\right)
estimation efficiency, contrasting with the oracle estimator’s
O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
rate (Eq.
3.2
)
(
Hsu et al. 2012
)
. Thus, STSA-E maintains comparable statistical performance to the STSA with significantly reduced communication overhead, achieving a great performance-resource trade-off.

Dummy Clients.
Since the number of clients,
K
, affects the quality of the estimated
\hat{\bm{G}_{t}}
, we introduce dummy clients to improve estimation when
K
is small (cross-silo setting). Specifically, we divide each client’s local dataset into multiple subsets, treating each subset as a separate dummy client. For instance, client
k
in task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
splits its dataset
\mathcal{D}_{t,k}
into
K_{D}
subsets. Each dummy client
k_{j}
(
j=1,2,\ldots,K_{D}
) holds a subset
\mathcal{D}_{t,k_{j}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{t,k}
and uploads the corresponding spatial statistics
\bm{C}_{t,k_{j}}
and
\bm{n}_{t,k_{j}}
to the server. This effectively increases the number of clients from
K
to
K\times K_{D}
.

Discussion.
Introducing dummy clients brings extra communication overhead for uploading first-order statistics (from
\bm{C}_{t,k}
to
\{\bm{C}_{t,k_{j}},\bm{n}_{t,k_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{K_{D}}
). However, this overhead is typically much smaller than the cost of uploading second-order statistics (
\bm{G}_{t,k}
) in STSA (see Section
4.4
). It is worth noting that dummy clients are used to adapt STSA-E to the cross-silo setting, where the number of clients
K
is small. In the cross-device setting (
K
is large), they are not needed (see Section
4.3
).

Experimental Setting

Datasets and Models.

We conduct experiments under two settings: training from scratch and training with a pre-trained model. For the scratch setting, we use ResNet18
(
He et al. 2016
)
as the backbone on CIFAR100
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
)
and Tiny-ImageNet
(
Le & Yang 2015
)
. For the pre-trained setting, we use ViT-B/16
(
Dosovitskiy 2020
)
(pre-trained on ImageNet-21K) as the backbone on CIFAR100
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
)
and ImageNet-R
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
. By default, we divide dataset classes into 10 non-overlapping tasks (
T\!\!=\!\!10
) and distribute them among 5 clients (
K\!\!=\!\!5
) using a Dirichlet distribution with varying
\beta
.

Comparison Baselines.

Following the benchmark in
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Tran et al. 2024
)
, we compare our STSA and STSA-E with Fed-EWC
(
Kirkpatrick et al. 2017
)
, Fed-LWF
(
Li & Hoiem 2017
)
, MFCL
(
Babakniya et al. 2023
)
, and LANDER
(
Tran et al. 2024
)
under the training from scratch setting. We also evaluate our method against PEFT-based FCIL methods, including Fed-DualP
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, Fed-CODAP
(
Smith et al. 2023
)
, Fed-CPrompt
(
Bagwe et al. 2023
)

(
Guo et al. 2024
)
, using a pre-trained ViT-B/16. For our method, we integrate an adapter
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
)

Evaluation Metrics.
The evaluation follows traditional CIL metrics
(
Smith et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2024a
)
, including: (1) average incremental accuracy
A_{\text{avg}}

A_{T}
and (3) average forgetting
F_{T}
. Refer to
Appendix
D.1
for further details on these metrics.

Experimental Details.
For ResNet18, we set the batch size to 128, local training epoch to 2, and communication rounds to 100. For pre-trained ViT-B/16, we use 2 local epochs with a batch size of 128, conducting 10 communication rounds on CIFAR100 and 20 communication rounds on ImageNet-R. The projection dimension of the adapter is set to 64. The dimension of the random feature
M
is set to 5000 for ResNet18 and 1250 for ViT-B/16. The number of dummy clients
K_{D}
is set to 50 for ResNet18, 10 for ViT-B/16. The ridge coefficient is set to 1e4 for ResNet18 and 1e6 for ViT-B/16. More details on configurations can be found in
Appendix
D.2

STSA typically slightly outperforms STSA-E, though the reverse can occur, such as on Tiny-ImageNet (

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We propose a novel FCIL method, STSA, which unifies feature statistic aggregation across spatial and temporal dimensions. The aggregated statistics are immune to data heterogeneity and enable closed-form classifier updates at each stage. We also introduce STSA-E, a variant that achieves similar performance with lower communication costs. Extensive experiments show that our method outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, offering greater flexibility while reducing communication and computation overhead. In the future, we plan to extend STSA to more FCL settings (e.g., asynchronous scenarios) and explore strategies for more efficient use of feature statistics.

Impact Statement

This paper aims to advance the field of Federated Class-Incremental Learning (FCIL). FCIL has the potential to enable privacy-preserving continual learning in decentralized environments. It allows edge devices (e.g., smartphones, wearables, and IoT sensors) to collaboratively build powerful models that can incrementally learn new tasks or classes over time without sharing raw data. This strengthens user privacy and ensures compliance with data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR). Our research enhances the effectiveness and efficiency of FCIL, enabling broader applications.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition 3.1

Proposition 3.1.

For each task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{G}}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{c_{t}}\Bigg[\frac{n^{(i)}_{t}-1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)\top}}{n_{t,k}^{(i)}}-\frac{n^{(i)}_{t}-K}{n^{(i)}_{t}(K-1)}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}\bigg)^{\top}\Bigg],

(13)

is an unbiased plug-in estimator of the global gram matrix
\bm{G}_{t}
when global feature set

\Omega=\{(\Phi(x),y)\mid(x,y)\in\cup_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{D}_{t,k}\}
is i.i.d. Here,
\bm{C}_{t,k}^{(i)}
denotes the
i
-th column of
\bm{C}_{t,k}
and
n_{t}^{(i)}
denotes the total number of data samples from class
i
in stage
t
, and
n_{t,k}^{(i)}
denotes the number of samples from class
i
for client
k
at stage
t
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we omit the subscript
t
in the proof. Under the i.i.d assumption of the global feature set
\{(\Phi(x),y)\mid(x,y)\in\cup_{k=1}^{K}D_{k}\}
, and letting
n_{k}^{(i)}
denote the total number of data samples from class
i
in client
k
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]=\sum_{(x,i)\in D_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\Phi(x)]:=n_{k}^{(i)}\mu^{(i)},\quad\mathbb{D}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]=\sum_{(x,i)\in D_{k}}\mathbb{D}[\Phi(x)]:=n_{k}^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)}.

(14)

Here,
\mu^{(i)}
and
\Sigma^{(i)}
are the class-wise mean and covariance of the global feature set
\Omega
. Since
\bm{G}=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\bm{G}^{(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{G}^{(i)}_{k}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}]=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}^{(i)}]
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}^{(i)}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}^{(i)}_{k}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{(i)\top}_{k}\bm{X}^{(i)}_{k}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{(x,i)\in D_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\Phi(x)\Phi(x)^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(n_{k}^{(i)}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}+n_{k}^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)})=n^{(i)}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}+n^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)}\Big.

(15)

For clarity in the proof, we expand
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{G}}]
and divide it into several parts as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{G}}]=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{G}}^{(i)}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Big[\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{K-1}A_{1,i}-\frac{n^{(i)}-K}{n^{(i)}(K-1)}A_{2,i}\Big],

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad A_{1,i}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)\top}]}{n_{k}^{(i)}},A_{2,i}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)^{\top}\Big].

(16)

To proceed, we compute each term in above equation separately:

\displaystyle A_{1,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)\top}]}{n_{k}^{(i)}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]\mathbb{E}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]^{\top}+\mathbb{D}[\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]}{n_{k}^{(i)}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(n_{k}^{(i)}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}+\Sigma^{(i)})=n^{(i)}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}+K\Sigma^{(i)}

(17)

\displaystyle A_{2,i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]\mathbb{E}[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]^{\top}+\mathbb{D}[\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}]

\displaystyle=(\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}^{(i)}\mu^{(i)})(\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}^{(i)}\mu^{(i)})^{\top}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)}=(n^{(i)})^{2}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}+n^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)}

(18)

Therefore, by substituting
A_{1,i}
and
A_{2,i}
back into the equation for
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{G}}]
, we get:

\displaystyle\hskip-30.0pt\mathbb{E}[\hat{\bm{G}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Big[\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{K-1}(n^{(i)}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}\;\;+\;K\Sigma^{(i)})-\frac{n^{(i)}-K}{n^{(i)}(K-1)}((n^{(i)})^{2}\mu\mu^{\top}+n^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)})\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\Big[n^{(i)}\mu^{(i)}\mu^{(i)\top}+n^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)}\Big]=\sum_{i=1}^{c}\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}^{(i)}]=\mathbb{E}[\bm{G}]

(19)

∎

Appendix B
Estimation Guarantee of STSA-E

Notation:
Let
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denote the spectral norm,
\|\cdot\|_{F}
represent the Frobenius norm,
\mathbb{E}
indicate the expectation operator,
\mathbb{D}
signify the variance operator,
\mathbf{0}
be the zero vector,
\mathbf{I}_{M}
denote the
M
-dimensional identity matrix, and
\operatorname{tr}(\cdot)
represent the matrix trace operation.
We consider a noisy linear regression model and let
n
represent the total number of collected samples until stage
t
, that is,
n=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i}
and
c_{\text{joint}}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}
. In the subsequent discussion, we assume that, through sufficient continual learning and accumulation, the number of samples
n
exceeds the feature dimension
M
, i.e.,
n>M
. Specifically, we assume there exist ground-truth weight matrix
\bm{W}_{t}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times c_{\text{joint}}}
and noise
\mathcal{E}_{joint}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c_{\text{joint}}}
satisfying

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}=\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{0}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{~joint}}.

(20)

For each task
\mathcal{T}_{t}
, and for every
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,c_{t}\right\}
, we assume:

Assumption 1 (Data Structure).

\|\bm{X}_{t}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n_{t}}C_{\bm{X}}
,
\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{t}^{(i)}\right)\leq C_{\Sigma}
and
\|\mu_{t}^{(i)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{\mu}
for some constants
C_{\bm{X}},C_{\Sigma},C_{\mu}>0
, where
\mu_{t}^{(i)}
and
\Sigma_{t}^{(i)}
are the class-wise mean and covariance of the global feature set
\Omega
at stage
t
.

Assumption 2 (Class Balance).
There exists a constant
0<C\ll c_{t}
such that
c_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{c_{t}}n_{t}^{(i)2}\leq Cn_{t}^{2}
.

Assumption 3 (Stability).

\|\bm{W}_{t}^{0}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{M}r

.

Assumption 4 (Noise).
The
i
-th row of noise satisfies the following conditions:

\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}
.

Proposition 3.2.

Under Assumption 1-2, when considering
\hat{\bm{G}}_{t}
as described in Proposition 3.1, we obtain the following results:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\bm{G}}_{t}-\bm{G}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{F}\leq C^{\prime}C_{\Sigma}^{2}(\frac{K+1}{K-1})^{2}n_{t}^{2}=O((\frac{K+1}{K-1})^{2}n_{t}^{2}),

(21)

where
C^{\prime}
represents a constant that is indenpendent of (
n_{t}
,
K
).

Proof.

For simplicity, we omit the subscript
t
in the proof.

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\bm{G}}-\bm{G}\right\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq c\sum_{i=1}^{c}\left\|\hat{\bm{G}}^{(i)}-\bm{G}^{(i)}\right\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=c\sum_{i=1}^{c}\left\|\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{K-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)\top}}{n_{k}^{(i)}}-\frac{n^{(i)}-K}{n^{(i)}(K-1)}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)^{\top}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{X}^{(i)\top}_{k}\bm{X}^{(i)}_{k}\right\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq(K+1)c\sum_{i=1}^{c}\left((\frac{n^{(i)}-K}{K-1})^{2}A^{(i)}_{1}+(\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{K-1})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}A^{(i)}_{2,k}\right),

(22)

where
A^{(i)}_{1}
measures global random error,
A^{(i)}_{2,k}
measures local random error,

\displaystyle A^{(i)}_{1}=\left\|\bm{X}^{(i)\top}\bm{X}^{(i)}-\frac{1}{n^{(i)}}\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bigg)^{\top}\right\|^{2}_{F},\quad A^{(i)}_{2,k}=\left\|\bm{X}_{k}^{(i)\top}\bm{X}_{k}^{(i)}-\frac{1}{n_{k}^{(i)}}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)}\bm{C}_{k}^{(i)\top}\right\|^{2}_{F}.

(23)

Noticing that the forms of these two terms are identical (except for the constants in front), we can use Lemma 3.5 to analyze them, which we assume that
\mathbb{E}[\bm{C}^{(i)}_{k}]:=n_{k}^{(i)}\mu^{(i)},\mathbb{D}[\bm{C}^{(i)}_{k}]:=n_{k}^{(i)}\Sigma^{(i)}
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[A^{(i)}_{1}]\leq\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{n^{(i)}}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{(i)})^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}[A^{(i)}_{2,k}]\leq\frac{n^{(i)}_{k}-1}{n^{(i)}_{k}}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{(i)})^{2},

(24)

then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\hat{\bm{G}}-\bm{G}\right\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Sigma}^{2}(K+1)c\sum_{i=1}^{c}\left((\frac{n^{(i)}-K}{K-1})^{2}\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{n^{(i)}}+(\frac{n^{(i)}-1}{K-1})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n^{(i)}_{k}-1}{n^{(i)}_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=C^{\prime\prime}C_{\Sigma}^{2}\frac{(K+1)^{2}}{(K-1)^{2}}c\sum_{i=1}^{c}n^{(i)2}\leq C^{\prime}C_{\Sigma}^{2}\frac{(K+1)^{2}}{(K-1)^{2}}n^{2}=O((\frac{K+1}{K-1})^{2}n^{2}),

(25)

where we scale some constants such as
\frac{n^{(i)}}{n^{(i)}-1}
as
C^{\prime\prime}
, which is a product factor of a finite number of quantities approximately equal to 1. Moreover, we utilize our class balanced condition to get another constant
C^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark.
Proposition 3.2 indicates that the error between
\hat{\bm{G}}_{t}
and
\bm{G}_{t}
is highly sensitive to small values of K, but the sensitivity diminishes as K increases.

After quantitatively analyzing the relationship between
\hat{\bm{G}}_{t}
and
\bm{G}_{t}
, we can use Proposition 3.1 and 3.2 to further explore more profound theoretical properties between
\bm{W}_{t}=\left(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\bm{C}_{1:t}
and
\bm{W}^{\star}=\left(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\bm{C}_{1:t}
.

Theorem 3.3 (STSA-E plug-in error).

Under Assumption 1-2, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}C_{\bm{X}}(C_{\Sigma})^{3/2}C_{\mu}^{1/2}\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+C^{\prime}\right)}=O\left(\frac{\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+1)}\right),

(26)

where
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}
are the constants that are independent of
(n,t,K,\gamma)
.

Proof.

Firstly, we expand the term in expectation:

\displaystyle\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}

\displaystyle-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\|_{F}\leq\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}\left\|\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}\|\left[(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}-(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}\right]\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}\|(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}(\bm{G}_{1:t}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t})(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}\|(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}\|_{2}\|\bm{G}_{1:t}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}\|_{F}\|(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}\|_{2}\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\|_{F}\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}\|\bm{G}_{1:t}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}\|_{F}}{\gamma^{2}+C^{\prime}n^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{\bm{X}}\sqrt{tn}\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}}{\gamma^{2}+C^{\prime}n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|\bm{G}_{i}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{i}\|_{F},

(27)

where the first inequality is based on the property
\|AB\|_{F}\leq\|A\|_{F}\|B\|_{F}
, and the second inequality follows from
\|AB\|_{F}\leq\|A\|_{2}\|B\|_{F}
. The second equality holds because
A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}
.

Regarding the third inequality, we consider
\|(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}\|_{2}
firstly. Notice that

\displaystyle\|(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma I)^{-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\operatorname{min}_{i}{\lambda_{i}+\gamma}}\qquad\text{where}\ \lambda_{i}\ \text{is the $i$-th eigenvalue of}\ \bm{G}_{1:t},

(28)

Where
\bm{G}_{1:t}
is, in fact, the sum of the outer products of
n
vectors. According to Lemma 3.7, when the number of vectors
n
exceeds the dimension
M
, with high probability, we can derive that
\min_{i}\lambda_{i}\geq O(n)
. The analysis result for
\left\|\left(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{2}
is similar by Lemma 3.7-3.8. Therefore, we can postulate the existence of a certain positive constant
C^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(\bm{G}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\cdot\left\|\left(\hat{\bm{G}}_{1:t}+\gamma\bm{I}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{C^{\prime}n^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

(29)

Regarding the fourth inequality, it is derived by considering the Cauchy inequality and the fact that
\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{joint }}\right\|_{F}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left\|\bm{X}_{i}\right\|_{F}
, where
\bm{X}_{\text{joint }}
is the matrix obtained by concatenating the matrices
\bm{X}_{i}
.

By Cauchy inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|\bm{G}_{i}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{i}\|_{F}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}\|\bm{G}_{i}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{i}\|_{F}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|^{2}_{F}]\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{G}_{i}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{i}\|^{2}_{F}]}

(30)

Then, we bound
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|^{2}_{F}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{C}_{1:t}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bm{C}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^{t}\|\bm{C}_{t}\|_{F}]^{2}\leq t\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{C}_{t}\|^{2}_{F}]

\displaystyle=t\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{Y}_{i}\bm{Y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i})=t\sum_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(\bm{Y}_{i}\bm{Y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}\bm{X}_{i}^{\top})

\displaystyle=t\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{c_{i}}n_{i}^{(j)}\left(\mu_{i}^{(j)\top}\mu_{i}^{(j)}+\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma^{(j)}\right)\right)\leq C_{\Sigma}C_{\mu}tn.

(31)

By taking the result in Proposition 3.2, and combining the above parts, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{C_{\bm{X}}C^{1/2}_{\Sigma}C^{1/2}_{\mu}t}{(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n}+C^{\prime}n)}\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{G}_{i}-\hat{\bm{G}}_{i}\|^{2}_{F}]}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}C_{\bm{X}}(C_{\Sigma})^{3/2}C_{\mu}^{1/2}\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+C^{\prime}},

(32)

where
C^{\prime\prime}
is the constant in Proposition 3.2.
∎

Remark.
Theorem 3.3 establishes that the plug-in error decreases as the average per-stage sample size
\frac{n}{t}
increases.

Corollary 3.4 (Estimation error).

Under the Assumption 1-4, with high probability, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}C_{\bm{X}}(C_{\Sigma})^{3/2}C_{\mu}^{1/2}\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+C^{\prime}\right)}+\frac{MRC_{\bm{X}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{n}},

(33)

where
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}
are the constants that are independent of
(n,t,K,\gamma)
.

Proof.

Consider the error decomposition:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}+\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|^{2}_{F}},

(34)

where the first term has been analyzed in Theorem 3.3, and we introduce Lemma 3.6 to analyze the second term. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}_{t}-\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{C^{\prime\prime}C_{\bm{X}}(C_{\Sigma})^{3/2}C_{\mu}^{1/2}\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+C^{\prime}\right)}=O\left(\frac{\frac{K+1}{K-1}t}{n\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{n^{2}}+1\right)}\right).

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\|\bm{X}_{\text{~joint}}\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{Y}_{\text{~joint}}\right\|^{2}_{F}}\leq\frac{MRC_{\bm{X}}+\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(36)

Combing this two parts, we can proof the Corollary 3.4.
∎

Remark.
The most significant implication of Corollary 3.4 lies in the rate analysis: our STSA-E incurs an estimation efficiency loss of order
O\left(tn^{-1}\right)
, whereas the oracle estimator achieves the standard rate
O\left(n^{-1/2}\right)
. Crucially, when
t\ll\sqrt{n}
(a mild scaling condition), the efficiency loss becomes asymptotically negligible. Thus, our method achieves comparable statistical performance to the oracle approach while requiring substantially lower communication overhead, establishing a favorable trade-off between precision and resource efficiency.

Lemma 3.5.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{n}
be i.i.d. random vectors such that
\mathbf{x}_{i}\sim N(\mu,\Sigma)
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
, where
\Sigma=(\sigma_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}
is a
d\times d
positive covariance matrix. Define the matrix
\mathbf{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}-\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)^{\top}
. Then, the expected value of the Frobenius norm of
\mathbf{M}
satisfies the following equality:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{M}\|_{F}^{2}\right]=\frac{n-1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)^{2}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
\mu=\mathbf{0}
.

By definition of Frobenius norm, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbf{M}\|^{2}_{F}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{2}\right)\right]
, then we expand
\mathbf{M}^{2}
obtain,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right)+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right).

(37)

(1). We compute
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{i}
are independent and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Sigma,&i=j\\
\mathbf{0},&i\neq j\end{array}\right.
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{2}\right)\right]+\sum_{i\neq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\right],

(38)

then we calculate
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{2}\right)\right]
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\left(x_{i1},x_{i2},\cdots,x_{id}\right)^{\top}
,
\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)_{jk}=x_{ij}x_{ik}
, and
\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(x_{ij}x_{ik}\right)^{2}
. For a multivariate normal distribution
\mathbf{x}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\Sigma)
, the fourth-moment formula is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x_{j}x_{k}x_{u}x_{v}\right]=\sigma_{jk}\sigma_{uv}+\sigma_{ju}\sigma_{kv}+\sigma_{jv}\sigma_{ku},

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x_{ij}x_{ik}\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{jk}^{2}+\sigma_{jj}\sigma_{kk}\right).

(40)

Then we calculate
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]
for
i\neq k
. Since
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
are independent,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right]=\Sigma^{2}
. Then we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]=\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{jk}^{2}
.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=n\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{jk}^{2}+\sigma_{jj}\sigma_{kk}\right),

(41)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq k}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=n(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{jk}^{2}.

(42)

(2). We compute
E\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]
. We consider different cases based on the indices. When
k=l
, we have terms related to
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right]
. When
k\neq l
, we use the independence of
\mathbf{x}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}_{l}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]=\frac{2}{n}\left(n\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{jk}^{2}+\sigma_{jj}\sigma_{kk}\right)+2n(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{jk}^{2}\right).

(43)

(3). We compute
E\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right)\right)\right]
. By considering all possible combinations of indices and using the independence of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and the properties of the normal distribution, we can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\operatorname{tr}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{l}^{\top}\right)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(n\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\sigma_{jk}^{2}+\sigma_{jj}\sigma_{kk}\right)+4n(n-1)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{jk}^{2}+\left(n^{2}-3n+2\right)\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{jk}^{2}\right).

(44)

After combining the above three parts, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{2}\right)\right]=\frac{n-1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sigma_{jj}\sigma_{kk}=\frac{n-1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)^{2}.

(45)

∎

Lemma 3.6.

Suppose that
\bm{Y}=\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{\epsilon}
, where
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c},\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times M}
is a random matrix whose rows
\bm{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
for
i=1,\cdots,n,\bm{W}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times c}
, and
\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
is a noise matrix with
\mathbb{E}(\bm{\epsilon})=\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{\epsilon}\bm{\epsilon}^{\top}\right)=\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{n,}
and
\bm{\epsilon}
is independent of
\bm{X}
. Let
\bm{W}=\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}+\gamma\bm{I}_{M}\right)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}
be the ridge estimator. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{2}\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}\bm{W}_{0}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\right]}_{\text{Bias }}+\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]\sigma^{2}}_{\text{Variance }}.

(46)

where
\bm{H}=\bm{X}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}+\gamma\bm{I}_{M}\right)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}
. Besides, suppose further that: (1). The covariate matrix
\bm{X}
satisfies
\|\bm{X}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n}C_{\bm{X}}
for some constant
C_{\bm{X}}>0
. (2). The true parameter matrix
\bm{W}_{0}
satisfies
\left\|\bm{W}_{0}\right\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{M}R
for some constant
R>0
. Then, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}\leq n(M^{2}R^{2}C_{\bm{X}}^{2}+\sigma^{2}).

(47)

Proof.

First, substitute the expression of
\bm{W}
into
\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}
. Given
\bm{W}=\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}+\gamma\bm{I}_{M}\right)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}
and
\bm{Y}=\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{\epsilon}
, we denote
\bm{H}=\bm{X}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}+\gamma\bm{I}_{M}\right)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}
, then we have

\displaystyle\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}+\gamma\bm{I}_{M}\right)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{Y}-\bm{Y}=\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{Y}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\left(\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}+\bm{\epsilon}\right)=\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}+\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{\epsilon}.

(48)

Consider
\mathbb{E}\|\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left[(\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y})^{\top}(\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y})\right]
, since
\mathbb{E}(\bm{\epsilon})=\mathbf{0}
and
\bm{\epsilon}
is independent of
\bm{X}
, the cross-terms involving
\bm{\epsilon}
and
\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}
will have zero expectation. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{X}\bm{W}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\left[\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}\right]^{\top}\left[\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}\right]\right\}+\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\left[\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{\epsilon}\right]^{\top}\left[\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)\bm{\epsilon}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left[\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{2}\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}\bm{W}_{0}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\right]}_{\text{Bias }}+\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]\sigma^{2}}_{\text{Variance }}.

(49)

Bias Term.
Recall
\bm{H}=\bm{X}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\bm{X}+\gamma\bm{I}_{M}\right)^{-1}\bm{X}^{\top}
. Using the identity:
\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}=\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}-\bm{I}_{n}=-\gamma\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}
. We have
\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{2}=\gamma^{2}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}
. The bias term becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}\left[\gamma^{2}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}\bm{W}_{0}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{W}_{0}^{\top}\bm{X}^{\top}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\bm{X}\bm{W}_{0}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\bm{X}\right)\|\bm{W}_{0}\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}MR^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\bm{X}\right)\right]

Using properties of semi-positive matrices and the identity:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{X}^{\top}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\bm{X}\right)\right]\leq\frac{nMC_{\bm{X}}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}

(50)

Then, the bias term is bounded by:

\displaystyle\text{~Bias}\leq\gamma^{2}M^{2}R^{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}^{\top}\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\bm{X}\right]\right)\leq nM^{2}R^{2}C_{\bm{X}}^{2}

(51)

Variance term.
Using the identity:
\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}=-\gamma\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left[\mathbb{E}\left(\bm{H}-\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]\sigma^{2}=\gamma^{2}\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\right)\right].

(52)

For semi-positive matrix
\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}
, we have
\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{X}\bm{X}^{\top}+\gamma\bm{I}_{n}\right)^{-2}\right]\right)\leq\dfrac{n}{\gamma^{2}}
.
Thus, the variance term is bounded by:

\displaystyle\text{~Variance}\leq n\sigma^{2}.

(53)

Combining the bias and variance bounds, we will get the final bound.
∎

Remark.
The result obtained in Lemma 3.6 aligns with the works of
Hsu et al. 2012
and
Mourtada & Rosasco 2022
. For clarity of exposition and to stay within the theoretical framework of
Peng et al. 2024
, we have rederived a rough bound.

Lemma 3.7(
Wainwright 2019
, Theorem 6.1).

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be drawn according to the
\Sigma
-Gaussian ensemble. Then for all
\delta>0
, the maximum singular value
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})
satisfies the upper deviation inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{X})}{\sqrt{n}}\geq\lambda_{\max}(\sqrt{\Sigma})(1+\delta)+\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{n}}\right]\leq e^{-n\delta^{2}/2}.

(54)

Moreover, for
n\geq d
, the minimum singular value
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})
satisfies the analogous lower deviation inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{X})}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\sqrt{\Sigma})(1-\delta)-\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{n}}\right]\leq e^{-n\delta^{2}/2},

(55)

where
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot),\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
means the maximum and minimum eigenvalue respectively.

Remark.
Lemma 3.7 demonstrates that the following statement holds, which is used in the proof of Theorem 3.3. With a probability of at least
1-e^{-n\delta^{2}/2}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top})\geq n\left(\lambda_{\min}(\sqrt{\Sigma})(1-\delta)-\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{n}}\right)^{2}=O(n).

(56)

Lemma 3.8(
Wainwright 2019
, Exercise 6.1).

Given two symmetric matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A})-\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{B})\right|\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})\right|\leq\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

(57)

Remark.
Lemma 3.8 demonstrates that the following statement, which is used in the proof of Theorem 3.3.

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})-(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A}))\right|\geq\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})\right|-\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\right|\geq\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})\right|-\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

(58)

Appendix D
Experimental Details

D.1
Evaluation Metrics

In this work, we employ three widely-used metrics in CIL: average incremental accuracy (
A_{\text{avg}}
), final average accuracy (
A_{T}
), and average forgetting (
F_{T}
).
A_{\text{avg}}
denotes the average performance across all incremental stages,
A_{T}
is the accuracy averaged over all
T
tasks after completing task
\mathcal{T}_{T}
, and
F_{T}
represents the average performance decline on previous tasks over all
T
tasks. Let
A_{t,\tau}
represent the classification accuracy on the
\tau
-th task after training on the
t
-th task. After training on the
t
-th task, we calculate the evaluation metrics as follows:

\displaystyle A_{\text{avg}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}A_{t,\tau},

(59)

\displaystyle A_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\tau=1}^{T}A_{T,\tau},

(60)

\displaystyle F_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{\tau=1}^{T-1}\max_{\tau^{\prime}\in\{1,\cdots,T-1\}}A_{\tau^{\prime},\tau}-A_{T,\tau}.

(61)

D.2
Client Training Details

For the training-from-scratch setting, we follow the configurations from
Tran et al. 2024
, using ResNet18
(
He et al. 2016
)
as the backbone. Each client is trained using a batch size of 128 for 100 communication rounds, with 2 local training epochs per round. For CIFAR100
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
)
, we employ the SGD optimizer with a learning rate of 0.04, a momentum of 0.9, and a weight decay of
5\times 10^{-4}
. For Tiny-ImageNet
(
Le & Yang 2015
)
, we use a learning rate of 0.1, a weight decay of
2\times 10^{-4}
, and a multi-step learning rate scheduler that reduces the learning rate by a factor of 10 at the 50th and 75th communication rounds.

When training from pre-trained model, we incorporate an adapter into each layer of pre-trained ViT via residual connections to facilitate parameter-efficient tuning
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
)
. Specifically, an adapter typically consists of a downsampled MLP layer
W_{down}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
, a non-linear activation function ReLU, and an upsampled MLP layer
W_{up}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
. We formulate the output of the MLP when using the adapter as follows:

\mathbf{x}_{o}=\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+s*\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{x}_{i}W_{down})W_{up},

(62)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i}
represent the input,
\mathbf{x}_{o}
denote the output, and
s
be the scale factor.
By default, the scale factor
s
is set to 0.1, and the projection dimension
r
for the adapter is fixed at 64. For clients’ local training, we use a learning rate of 0.2 and a weight decay of
5\times 10^{-4}
. The model is trained with a batch size of 128 for 2 local epochs. It runs for 10 communication rounds on CIFAR100
(
Krizhevsky et al. 2009
)
and 20 communication rounds on ImageNet-R
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
.

Appendix E
Privacy Discussion

In our STSA, uploading Spatial Statistics may pose privacy concerns. Specifically, STSA uploads
\bm{G}_{t,k}
and
\bm{C}_{t,k}
, while STSA-E uploads
\bm{C}_{t,k}
and
\bm{n}_{t,k}
(assuming no dummy clients are used for simplicity). Feature statistics
\bm{G}_{t,k}
and
\bm{C}_{t,k}
are the aggregation of feature statistics from all local data samples. In real-world scenarios, each client
k
typically has many data samples across various classes, making it difficult to reconstruct specific samples from the uploaded feature statistics. To support this, we conduct feature inversion experiments
(
Ulyanov et al. 2018
;
Luo et al. 2021
)
to validate this claim. Label frequency
\bm{n}_{t,k}
has been widely used in numerous FL methods
(
Luo et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Gong et al. 2021
;
Zhu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Heinbaugh et al. 2023
)
, and label frequency presents fewer privacy risks compared to data samples. Moreover, we introduce two compatible privacy-preserving techniques to enhance the privacy-preserving capabilities of our method.

E.1
Feature Inversion Details

Here, we consider the scenario where the target client
k
has only a few samples (3 samples) from the same class (”apple” in CIFAR100), and the attacker utilizes the uploaded Spatial Statistics to attempt reconstruction of a specific training data sample using feature inversion technology, as shown in
Ulyanov et al. 2018
;
Luo et al. 2021
. In this case, the uploaded Spatial Statistics is a mixture of features from a few samples (3 samples) within the same class, making it less mixed and thus more advantageous for the attacker. In addition to using the uploaded Spatial Statistics for feature inversion, we also provide experimental results for inversion using raw representations, where each raw feature corresponds to a single input sample. Table
6
presents the quantitative similarity metrics between the ground truth and the reconstructed results. Higher PSNR and SSIM values and lower LPIPS values indicate greater similarity to the ground truth, demonstrating better attack performance. For our STSA, we compute similarity measures between the inversion result and each real sample, then average them to derive the final results. Figure
8
presents the visualization results of feature inversion. When using the raw feature of a specific data sample, the inversion produces clear visual outputs with higher quantitative similarity metrics. In contrast, using our uploaded Spatial Statistics for feature inversion leads to significantly worse metrics. The visual results consist of irregular color patches, making it hard to discern information related to the original data samples.

E.2
Compatibility with Privacy-Preserving Techniques

Secure aggregation is especially suitable for our STSA, since the server only needs the aggregated Spatial Statistics, not the individual value from each client.

Additionally, we can add random noise to the uploaded Spatial Statistics to enhance the privacy-preserving capabilities of our method. Following
Tan et al. 2022
and
Zhang et al. 2024b
, we add Gaussian noise
q\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})
to the uploaded Spatial Statistics as follows:

\displaystyle(STSA)\quad

\displaystyle\bm{G}_{t,k}\leftarrow\bm{G}_{t,k}+q\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2}),\quad\bm{C}_{t,k}\leftarrow\bm{C}_{t,k}+q\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})

(63)

\displaystyle(STSA-E)\quad

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{t,k}\leftarrow\bm{C}_{t,k}+q\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2}),\quad\bm{n}_{t,k}\leftarrow\bm{n}_{t,k}+q\cdot\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})

(64)

Table
7
presents the results of adding random noise to CIFAR100 (
\beta=0.5
) with different noise parameter configurations. According to
Zhang et al. 2024b
, setting
q=0.2
and
s=0.05
is sufficient to ensure privacy protection. From table
7
, we have the following observations: (1) In the range of moderate noise intensity, we observe that the performance of our STSA remains stable and is not significantly affected by the added noise. (2)
For STSA-E, adding noise to label frequency (
\bm{n}_{t,k}
) has a greater impact on performance than adding noise to feature statistics (
\bm{C}_{t,k}
). (3) Performance only decreases to a certain extent when the noise is large enough (
q=0.1
and
s=5
). (4) In some cases, performance even surpasses that of the original models without noise. These observations indicate that adding random noise can achieve a good privacy-accuracy tradeoff for our method.
