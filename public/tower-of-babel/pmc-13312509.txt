Value of CT in quantitatively diagnosing and assessing collapse in patients with femoral head necrosis based on finite element mechanics analysis
Authors: Tong Yu, Limin Xie, Zhihai Zhang, Shan Shi, Wenjuan Tan, Zhixiu Hao, You Zhou, Xin Li, Zhennan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMechanical analysis of the femoral head is mostly conducted through finite element analysis (FEA), the operation of which is relatively complex. In this study, the correlation between computed tomography (CT) quantitative analysis and FEA in the diagnosis of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) was analyzed.MethodsThis retrospective study involved 30 hips from patients with Steinberg stage II ONFH who had not received treatment. The average CT density was measured, and an FEA model was constructed on the basis of the CT images of each case. The correlation between the average CT density and the FEA measurements was analyzed.ResultsThe average CT density of the coronal midline plane was 370.47 ± 16.85 HU, and that of the maximum necrotic layer was 420.73 ± 22.24 HU. According to the FEA results, the maximum total displacement was 0.42 ± 0.04 mm, the maximum displacement in the Z direction was 0.25 ± 0.02 mm, the maximum equivalent stress in the stress concentration zone of the head was 1.81 ± 0.02 MPa, and the failure coefficient was 0.34 ± 0.02. The average CT density of the maximum necrotic layer was not correlated with the maximum total displacement or maximum displacement in the Z direction but was correlated with the maximum equivalent stress (r = 0.65; P < 0.1001) and failure coefficient (r=-0.58; P < 0.001). The average CT intensity of the maximum slice (P = 0.004) and the average failure coefficient (P = 0.020) significantly differed between the collapse and noncollapse groups.ConclusionCT quantification can reflect the load distribution and bearing capacity of ONFH to some extent. The higher the average CT density of the maximum slice, the lower are the average failure coefficient of the femoral head and the clinical risk of collapse.

Background

Prevention and treatment of collapse have long been difficult problems in the treatment of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH). Therefore, scientifically and accurately assessing the risk of collapse and preventing the occurrence of collapse are key points in the early stages of ONFH [1–3]. Two types of activities coexist, i.e., osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis. During the repair process of ONFH, they often become imbalanced in response to microenvironmental changes, various cytokines, and so forth, with osteoclastogenesis being more active than osteogenesis. This condition results in a decrease in the support strength of the femoral head and changes its mechanical distribution, which becomes the key to the development of collapse after ONFH [4–6].

Finite element analysis (FEA) is a technique that is used to simulate real physical systems (geometric and load conditions) on the basis of the principle of mathematical approximation. FEA was originally applied in the study of elasticity and structure in engineering. With the integration of medicine, biomechanics, and mechanical engineering, it has been increasingly applied in medical research. This application expansion helps achieve the simulation and construction of various tissue structure models under human physiological and pathological conditions, thereby creating conditions for analyzing the stress situation of each structural model. Using finite element simulation, a model can be constructed on the basis of the acquired imaging data of ONFH. The mechanical load of the obtained model can be simulated to determine the mechanical support strength and stress distribution of the femoral head, and the mechanical bearing capacity of the femoral head can then be evaluated, which provides an important basis for enhancing the accuracy of prediction regarding ONFH collapse. At present, research on the mechanical analysis of ONFH has focused mostly on finite element simulations [7–9]. Huang et al. [10] evaluated the mechanical support of the femoral head via FEA and reported that the anterior lateral sclerosis zone plays a role in enhancing the mechanical support of the femoral head in fibular transplantation surgery. Zhang et al. [7] used FEA to assess the stability of femoral neck fracture fixation with screws implanted at different angles, as well as the corresponding biomechanical risks of ONFH. Wen et al. [11] employed FEA to observe the influence of the mechanical environment on ONFH collapse. When the necrotic area is located in the lateral column, the risk of femoral head collapse is high. As the mechanical properties of the necrotic area change, both bone strength and stiffness decrease. Under these conditions, high hip flexion increases the likelihood of excessive deformation in the necrotic area. Therefore, patients with ONFH should reduce or avoid large-angle hip flexion during weight-bearing training in their rehabilitation activities. In a previous study, the current team constructed ONFH models with varying proportions of proximal sclerosis via FEA, with guidance from Tsinghua University [12]. We found that patients with proximal sclerosis effectively bear compressive loads, enhancing the structural resilience and deformability of the femoral head. This phenomenon creates a “mechanical umbrella” on the surface of the necrotic tissue, providing effective support for both the femoral head and the necrotic area, which can help prevent collapse of the femoral head to some extent. Although FEA has unique advantages in predicting collapse because of its ability to assess the mechanical distribution and support strength in ONFH, its operational complexity and the requirement for specialized knowledge pose challenges for clinical practitioners; therefore, research on its application for predicting the prognosis of ONFH remains at the theoretical stage [13], and no reliable collapse prediction indicators have been extensively used in clinical practice.

Computed tomography (CT) is a technique that utilizes X-ray beams to scan body parts at a specific thickness. As this technique offers high density resolution and clear anatomical relationships, it can reveal changes in the bone structure within the femoral head and thus provide a sound foundation for measuring bone density in necrotic areas, which reflects the strength of the bone [14]. Zhang et al. [15] used CT images to predict ONFH collapse via ABC classification, which demonstrated high predictive value. Lu et al. [16] established finite element models of ONFH with different preservation angles via CT images for biomechanical analysis and reported that when the preservation angle is below a critical value, the stress concentration in the femoral head becomes more noticeable. These findings provide mechanical evidence for predicting the collapse risk of ONFH associated with the anterior preservation angle and lateral preservation angle. Sun et al. [17] used CT perfusion parameters to predict ONFH collapse and reported that these parameters are associated with the risk of collapse and patient prognosis. CT serves as the gold standard for staging ONFH [18] and thus plays a significant role in predicting collapse. Its importance has been demonstrated by many radiomic studies in which the models were based on CT images [19–23].

Therefore, the current study aimed to evaluate the correlation between CT measurements and FEA results in the diagnosis of ONFH. To achieve this goal, a three-dimensional FEA model was constructed on the basis of thin-slice CT images of ONFH, after which mechanical analysis was performed. In addition, CT density was measured on the CT images.

This study might provide an easy way to determine the mechanical distribution of the femoral head and therefore improve the accuracy of quantitative diagnostic predictions regarding the risk of collapse in ONFH using visual imaging.

Methods

General data

This retrospective study involved 30 untreated ONFH hips at Steinberg stage II that were collected from the orthopedic outpatient department of Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences. All cases were screened, diagnosed, and enrolled by senior orthopedic specialists with expertise in osteonecrosis of the femoral head. The 30 hips (24 cases) included 9 left hips and 21 right hips, and the 24 patients included 18 males and 6 females, with an average age of 40 years (ranging from 18 to 63 years). ONFH was diagnosed according to the diagnostic criteria indicated in The 2019 Revised Version of Association Research Circulation Osseous Staging System of Osteonecrosis of the Femoral Head [24], and the staging criteria were in accordance with the Steinberg system [25].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) met the ONFH diagnostic or definitional standards; (2) were aged between 18 and 65 years; (3) were classified as Steinberg stage II; (4) did not receive any systemic treatment; (5) underwent bilateral hip CT examination; and (6) voluntarily participated in this study and provided informed consent. Those who met any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) had undergone hip joint surgery; (2) had severe congenital hip deformities; (3) had a history of hip fractures or trauma (including femoral neck, femoral head, intertrochanteric fractures, and hip dislocations); (4) had other concomitant joint diseases, such as bone tumors, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, joint tuberculosis, or pyogenic arthritis; (5) had osteoporosis, and (6) were unwilling to participate in this study. In addition, patients whose imaging examination indicated other abnormalities in addition to femoral head necrosis (e.g., bone cysts and femoral head synovial hernias), which might affect the construction of the FEA model for mechanical analysis, were also excluded.

CT scanning

Scanning methods

Continuous axial scans were performed on both hips via Siemens second-generation dual-source CT machines, SOMATOM Definition Flash and Emotion 16. The scanning parameters were as follows: a scanning voltage of 140 kV, an automatically adjusted tube current of 90–130 mA, bone tissue window settings, and a slice thickness of 0.75 mm. The obtained images were exported in BMP format.

The observational indices included the average CT density of the necrotic area in the coronal mid-slice plane and that of the plane with the largest necrotic area (hereafter referred to as the average CT density of the mid-slice plane and average CT density of the maximum slice, respectively).

Determination of the average CT density of the mid-slice plane. The coronal mid-slice plane was determined by locating the centers of both femoral heads. The free-form function of the medical imaging information management system of Guang'anmen Hospital (developed by Haina Medical Technology) was used, and the boundary of the femoral head was manually plotted. The average density of this area was automatically generated (Fig. 1a, b).

CT measurements were performed by a senior radiologist who was unaware of the finite element analysis results, collapse status, and the specific data analyses to be conducted. FEA

Basic data acquisition

Continuous axial scans were performed on both hips via Siemens second-generation dual-source CT machines, SOMATOM Definition Flash and Emotion 16. The scanning parameters were as follows: a scanning voltage of 140 kV, an automatically adjusted tube current of 90–130 mA, bone tissue window settings, and a slice thickness of 0.75 mm. The obtained images were exported in DICOM format.

Establishment of the geometric model of the hip joint

Mimics software was employed for U-Net image segmentation and contour extraction. U-Net is a deep learning architecture, which adopts an encoder–decoder architecture with skip connections. The encoder progressively reduces the image resolution to extract high-level abstract features, while the decoder gradually restores the feature maps to the original resolution through upsampling. This architecture helps preserve both global and local information during learning, thereby enabling more accurate image segmentation and improving the accuracy of femoral head modeling. The detailed segmentation is shown in Fig. 2. The obtained models were subjected to refinement processing with Geomagic Studio 2021 (3D Systems Corporation, NA, US) reverse modeling software (i.e., the U-Net segmented models were converted from the STL format to the IGES format), followed by model assembly processing with SolidWorks. Afterward, the IGES models assembled by SolidWorks 2022 (Dassault Systèmes, MA, US) were introduced into the Part module of Abaqus 2021 (Dassault Systèmes Simulia, RI, US), and meshes were generated under the Mesh command, with the mesh type set to C3D10 (second-order tetrahedral elements) (Fig. 3).

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed via SPSS software. Distribution normality (S‒W) tests were performed for all measurement data. If the data were normally distributed, Pearson analysis was performed to test the correlation between the CT measurements and FEA outcomes; otherwise, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted. A comparative analysis was conducted between the collapse and noncollapse groups. For normally distributed data, an independent t test was used; for nonnormally distributed data, a nonparametric test was applied. All the statistical tests were two-tailed, and a difference of P ≤ 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

The mean density of the coronal mid-slice plane of the 30 hips was 370.47 ± 16.85 HU, and the S‒W test revealed an abnormal distribution (P < 0.001). The average density of the plane with the largest necrotic area was 420.73 ± 22.24 HU, and the S‒W test revealed an abnormal distribution (P = 0.01) (Table 2).

Comparative analysis between the collapse and noncollapse groups

CT measurement indicators

The mean CT density of the coronal median plane in the collapse group was 330.38 ± 38.98 HU, with a Shapiro‒Wilk normality test result of P = 0.67. The mean density of the coronal median plane in the noncollapse group was 398.18 ± 58.98 HU, with a Shapiro‒Wilk test result of P = 0.01. The mean CT density of the coronal maximum plane in the collapse group was 367.31 ± 35.90 HU (P < 0.001), while the average density of the maximum necrotic plane in the noncollapse group was 434.00 ± 67.00 HU (P = 0.10). Since the average density of the median necrotic plane in the noncollapse group and the average density of the coronal maximum plane in the collapse group did not follow a normal distribution, nonparametric tests were performed. The Mann‒Whitney U test revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of the average density of the median necrotic plane (P = 0.20), whereas the difference in the average density of the maximum necrotic plane was significant (P = 0.004), with higher density values observed in the noncollapse group (Table 6).

Comparisons of CT measurement indicators between the collapse and noncollapse groups

FEA measurement indicators

The maximum total displacement in the collapse group was 0.35 ± 0.02 mm, with a Shapiro‒Wilk normality test result of P = 0.63. In the noncollapse group, the maximum total displacement was 0.48 ± 0.25 mm (P = 0.01). The average maximum displacement in the Z direction was 0.21 ± 0.05 mm in the collapse group, with a Shapiro‒Wilk normality test result of P = 0.67, and 0.28 ± 0.13 mm in the noncollapse group, with a value of P = 0.01. The maximum equivalent stress was 1.82 ± 0.93 MPa in the collapse group, with a Shapiro‒Wilk normality test result of P = 0.19, and 1.81 ± 0.77 MPa in the noncollapse group, with a value of P = 0.84. The average failure coefficient was 0.38 ± 0.06 in the collapse group (P = 0.04) and 0.30 ± 0.09 in the noncollapse group (P = 0.30). Since the maximum equivalent stress followed a normal distribution, an independent t test was used for group comparison, which yielded t = 0.21 and P = 0.84. The maximum total displacement in the noncollapse group, maximum Z-direction displacement in the noncollapse group, and average failure coefficient in the collapse group did not follow a normal distribution; therefore, nonparametric tests were performed. The Mann‒Whitney U test revealed no significant difference between the two groups in terms of maximum total displacement (P = 0.08) or maximum Z-direction displacement (P = 0.16). However, the difference in the mean failure coefficient was significant (P = 0.02), with the noncollapse group showing a lower mean failure coefficient compared with the collapse group (Table 7).

Discussion

Unlike traditional methods for establishing finite element models, we first used CT scan data to capture the internal structure of the femoral head accurately and then map it into a realistic and detailed three-dimensional model. This model not only considers the geometric shape of the bone but also accurately reflects the distribution of different tissue densities. Therefore, it provided us with more realistic and detailed material properties. On the basis of these conditions, we constructed a finite element model of ONFH and applied FEA to simulate the stress conditions of the femoral head to explore the internal mechanical distribution characteristics of the ONFH in detail. In selecting finite element indices, we set out from a multidimensional perspective and chose several key indices for analysis, which included the maximum displacement, maximum displacement in the Z direction, maximum equivalent stress in the stress concentration region of the head, and failure coefficient.

The total displacement represents the cumulative deformation of the femoral head in all directions, whereas the maximum displacement in the Z direction indicates the deformation that occurs in the collapse direction under stress. Therefore, these two indices reflect the deformation characteristics of ONFH. The equivalent stress indicates the stress distribution within the femoral head. Under normal conditions, the forces on the femoral head are transmitted evenly downward along the principal compressive trabecular beams until the femoral neck is reached, without the formation of any stress concentration. However, once the femoral head undergoes necrosis, the trabecular bone experiences uneven force transmission to some extent. The maximum equivalent stress indicates the location of stress concentration within the femoral head as well as the corresponding stress value. To quantify the stress condition of the femoral head, we performed sampling at six points within the proximal half of the femoral head in the stress concentration area along the distribution of the principal compressive trabecular beams (Fig. 8d), and the stress value of the femoral head was measured. Moreover, we calculated the strength values of the sampling points via a formula and determined the failure coefficients for each point. Next, we averaged the failure coefficients and used them to quantify the stress state of the femoral head. A smaller failure coefficient indicated a safer load-bearing condition for the femoral head. Therefore, the four indices involved in the FEA in this study reflect the mechanical distribution and load-bearing conditions of the femoral head after necrosis in terms of three dimensions, i.e., deformation, stress distribution, and stress state [28]. Herein, we obtained the following results: the maximum total displacement was 0.42 ± 0.04 mm, the Z direction was 0.25 ± 0.02 mm, the maximum equivalent stress was 1.81 ± 0.02 MPa, and the failure coefficient was 0.34 ± 0.02.

Bone density can reflect bone strength to a great extent. Imaging observations of bone density and quality after ONFH are typically conducted using X-rays or CT scans. Compared with X-ray images, CT images generated through tomographic scanning provide more comprehensive and precise information. In particular, they are more sensitive to bone defects and changes in bone volume; therefore, they can accurately present the extent of bone damage and the formation of new bone after repair in cases of ONFH. Because of these advantages, in this study, CT scan images were selected for quantitative measurement of the density of the femoral head. It is extensively assumed that the midline plane of the femoral head is the plane that experiences the greatest stress, considering the characteristics of the force distribution of the trabecular beams. The area of maximal necrosis in the femoral head more accurately reflects the actual condition of necrosis; it also represents the mechanical weak point after necrosis and becomes the area that is most likely to collapse [29]. Therefore, in this study, both the coronal midline plane and the plane with maximum necrosis were selected for density measurements. Our study revealed that the average density of the coronal midline plane was 370.47 ± 16.85 HU and that of the plane with maximum necrosis was 420.73 ± 22.24 HU. The correlation of these measurements was subsequently analyzed with the FEA results. The results revealed that the average CT density of the midline area was not correlated with any of the measurement indices of the FEA. Although the average CT density of the plane with maximum necrosis was not correlated with the maximum total displacement or the maximum displacement in the Z direction (P = 0.97 and P = 0.95, respectively), it was significantly correlated with the maximum equivalent stress in the stress concentration region of the head (r = 0.65, P < 0.001) and the failure coefficient (r=-0.58, P < 0.001).

The cystic areas formed because of absorption after necrosis, and the sclerotic regions that developed after repair represent the distribution zones of stress concentration in ONFH. The maximum equivalent stress reflects the changes in bone quality after necrosis, and these changes lead to uneven loading on the femoral head and the development of subsequent stress concentrations. Conversely, the plane of maximum necrosis provides insights into the actual density changes in the necrotic regions of the femoral head. Therefore, a correlation exists between these two factors. In our previous work [30], we applied the same classification method to the midline plane and the plane of maximum necrosis in the femoral head and compared the collapse rates of necrosis predicted on these two planes. The results indicated that the classification based on the midline plane tended to underestimate the extent of the necrotic lesions, whereas the classification based on the plane of maximum necrosis was more closely aligned with the actual pathological conditions of ONFH and thus possessed greater value in predicting ONFH. Our study provided a consistent result in this respect. The mean density of the coronal maximum plane in the collapse group was 367.31 ± 35.90 HU, while the mean density of the maximum necrotic plane in the noncollapse group was 434.00 ± 67.00 HU. The difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P = 0.004). The failure coefficient is a calculated result, and its value quantifies the stress state of the femoral head, which indirectly reflects the risk of collapse after ONFH. A smaller failure coefficient indicates safer stress on the femoral head and a lower risk of collapse. Additionally, a higher average CT density value suggests stronger bone strength and better quality of the femoral head; that is, a higher CT density value denotes less bone absorption and more reconstruction and repair after necrosis, which indicates that the mechanical support capability of the femoral head is stronger and that collapse is less likely to occur. Therefore, this study revealed a negative correlation between the average CT density of the plane with maximum necrosis and the failure coefficient. Furthermore, the mean failure coefficient in the collapse group was 0.38 ± 0.06, whereas that in the noncollapse group was 0.30 ± 0.09, indicating a significant difference (P = 0.020).

The primary aim of this study was to determine whether CT quantitative diagnosis and FEA results were correlated and whether direct measurements from CT images could indirectly reflect the mechanical loading conditions of the femoral head. However, the mechanical mechanisms underlying ONFH and collapse are complex, and ONFH is the result of multiple factors. Therefore, the average CT density value reflects only one aspect of this situation. The finite element model was simplified with isotropic and homogeneous material properties and a static axial load, which does not fully represent the anisotropic, viscoelastic, and dynamic loading characteristics of the femoral head during daily activities. The HU–modulus conversion formula has not yet been validated against mechanical testing. Furthermore, this study was a preliminary experiment with a small same size and aimed to identify potential CT-based predictive indicators. Although we analyzed the difference in mean CT density between the collapse and noncollapse groups, the precise predictive criteria, standardized measurement protocol, and accuracy of these predictive standards remain to be investigated in studies with larger sample sizes. Therefore, we did not perform a statistical power analysis. In future research, multidimensional imaging measurement indicators can be selected, and a “dimension-on-dimension” analysis with the multidimensional indicators of FEA can then be performed. Additionally, methods such as radiomic analysis can be integrated into our comprehensive analysis. These methods may aim to select clinically applicable indicators that align with real clinical situations and needs, with the expectation of optimizing prediction indicators for ONFH collapse on the basis of a deep understanding of its mechanical mechanisms, accurately predicting collapse risk and its influencing factors and ultimately reducing the rate of femoral head collapse and increasing the success rate of preserving the hip in cases of ONFH.

In conclusion, the average CT density of the plane with maximum necrosis was positively correlated with the maximum equivalent stress and negatively correlated with the failure coefficient obtained from FEA. The higher the average CT density of the maximum plane, the lower were the mean failure coefficient of the femoral head and the clinical risk of collapse. Quantitative CT measurements could partially reflect the mechanical distribution and load-bearing capacity of the femoral head in patients with ONFH, thereby providing a scientific basis for the use of quantitative imaging measurements to elucidate the mechanical mechanisms of ONFH collapse. However, its specific predictive clinical criteria remain to be determined in studies with larger sample sizes.
