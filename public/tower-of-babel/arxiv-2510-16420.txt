Exact Quantum Circuit Optimization is co-NQP-hard
Adam Husted Kjelstrøm, Andreas Pavlogiannis, Jaco van de Pol

Abstract
As quantum computing resources remain scarce and error rates high, minimizing the resource consumption of quantum circuits is essential for achieving practical quantum advantage. Here we consider the natural problem of, given a circuit C C , computing an equivalent circuit C ′ C^{\prime} that minimizes a quantum resource type, expressed as the count or depth of (i) arbitrary gates, or (ii) non-Clifford gates, or (iii) superposition gates, or (iv) entanglement gates. We show that, when C C is expressed over any gate set that can implement the H and TOF gates exactly, each of the above optimization problems is hard for co- ​ 𝐍𝐐𝐏 \text{co-}\mathbf{NQP} , and hence outside the Polynomial Hierarchy, unless the Polynomial Hierarchy collapses. This strengthens recent results in the literature which established an 𝐍𝐏 \mathbf{NP} -hardness lower bound, and tightens the gap to the corresponding 𝐍𝐏 𝐍𝐐𝐏 \mathbf{NP}^{\mathbf{NQP}} upper bound known for cases (i)-(iii) over Clifford+ T and (i)-(iv) over H + TOF circuits.



1
Introduction

Despite rapid progress in quantum hardware, limited resources and high error rates remain fundamental bottlenecks, making the optimization of quantum circuits a key challenge on the path to quantum advantage
[
Karuppasamy2025QuantumCircuitOptimization
]
.
Quantum computations rely on multiple types of resources, and depending on the underlying hardware, different resources (e.g., the total circuit size, or the number of a particular gate) can become the primary bottleneck by introducing noise or increasing execution time
[
Muroya2025HardwareOptimal
]
.

In high-level, a circuit optimization problem is defined wrt a function
f
mapping a circuit
C
to a cost
f(C)
.
Then, given some fixed gate set
\mathcal{A}
, the task is to find a circuit
C^{\prime}
over some gate-set
\mathcal{B}
(possibly different from
\mathcal{A}
) such that
C
and
C^{\prime}
are equivalent, and
f(C^{\prime})
is minimized.
Here we focus on exact equivalence, written as
C\equiv C^{\prime}
, where
C^{\prime}
behaves identically to
C
on all inputs.
Although quantum computation is often approximate
[
Dawson2005solovay_kitaev_thrm
,
Nielsen_Chuang2010
]
, focusing on exact equivalence remains important: it is the direct quantum analogue of classical computing, and it is useful for canonicalizing circuits, i.e., building a database of the most efficient circuits

[
Bravyi2022_6_qubit_optimal_Clifford_circuits
]
, making fast peephole optimizations compositional (i.e., without the need to keep track of how approximation errors may accumulate).

In complexity-theoretic terms, the optimization problem is typically phrased as a decision problem, where the input also specifies some budget
k
, and the task is to decide whether there exists an equivalent circuit
C^{\prime}
with
f(C^{\prime})\leq k

[
Wetering2023OptimisingQuantumCircuitsHard
]
.
In the classical reversible setting, the problem is in
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}^{\mathbf{P}}
(guess a circuit, then verify all inputs), under the reasonable assumption that
f
can be evaluated in polynomial time.
Moving towards quantum circuits, the same problem over the Clifford gate-set is in
\mathbf{NP}
, since circuit equivalence is known to be in
\oplus\mathbf{L}\subseteq\mathbf{P}

[
Aaronson04sim_of_stabilizer_circuits
]
.
Interestingly, whether the problem is
\mathbf{NP}
-hard when
f
simply counts the size of the circuit is an open question, to our knowledge
[
doi:10.1137/1.9781611975994.13
]
.

To capture the full computational power of quantum computing, the circuit optimization problem has also been studied wrt (approximately) universal gate sets beyond the Clifford fragment.
In
[
Tanaka2010ExactNonIdentityCheckNQPComplete
]
, it was shown that there are fixed gate-sets for which even deciding whether it equals identity (i.e., it can be implemented with
0
gates) is
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
-hard under Karp reductions.
More recently, the optimization problem was studied for fixed gate sets and wrt minimizing either the circuit size/depth or the count/depth of some particular gate
G
(from now on referred to as
G
-count/depth)
[
Wetering2023OptimisingQuantumCircuitsHard
]
.
In particular, the optimization problem was shown to be
\mathbf{NP}
-hard, under
\mathbf{P}
-time Turing reductions, over the Clifford+
T
and
H
+
TOF
gate sets,
and where minimization is wrt the circuit size/depth or the
H
/
CX
/
T
-count/depth for the former, and the
H
/
TOF
-count/depth for the latter.
The same work also showed that these problems are in
\mathbf{NP}^{\mathbf{NQP}}
, except for the case of
CX
gates, for which an upper bound is not known.
As
\mathbf{NQP}
is believed to be outside the Polynomial Hierarchy
\mathbf{PH}

[
fenner1998determiningacceptancepossibilityquantum
]
, there is a large gap between the
\mathbf{NP}^{\mathbf{NQP}}
upper bound and the
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness lower bound.
How difficult is exact quantum circuit optimization?

In this work, we consider the optimization problem in the following general form: the input is a circuit
C
that comes from a fixed gate set
\mathcal{A}
.
The task is to implement
C
in some fixed gate set
\mathcal{B}
in a way that minimizes either
(i) the number of all gates, or
(ii) the number of non-Clifford gates (e.g., the
T
gates), or
(iii) the number of gates that generate superposition (e.g., the
H
gates), or
(iv) the number of gates that generate entanglement (e.g., the
CX
gates).
While case (i) deals with the standard objective of minimizing circuit size, cases (ii), (iii) and (iv) deal with the minimization of various resources that are needed for universal quantum computation
[
Aaronson04sim_of_stabilizer_circuits
]
.
Towards this, we prove a strong lower bound for a conceptually simpler
promise problem
, that unifies the hardness of the above optimization tasks.
This promise problem is based on two semantic properties.
We say that a circuit
C

generates superposition
if
C
maps a basis state to a superposition of two or more basis states.
Similarly, we say that
C

generates non-affine correlation
if the measured value of one qubit is correlated with those of the others in a non-affine (e.g., quadratic) manner.
We consider the following promise problem.

Input:
A circuit
C
over gate-set
\mathcal{A}
.

Promise:
Either
C\equiv\text{I}
or
C
generates both superposition and non-affine correlation.

Question:
Is it the case that
C\equiv\text{I}
?

\textbf{{Promise\text{-}IsIdentity}}\textbf{(}\mathcal{A}\textbf{)}

Note that the identity circuit
I
does not generate superposition, nor non-affine correlation, meaning the promise indeed separates the two cases.
In full generality, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For any gate-set
\mathcal{A}
that can implement
H
and
TOF
exactly,
\textsc{Promise\text{-}IsIdentity}(\mathcal{A})
is
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
-complete.

In particular, the conditions of
Theorem

1
are satisfied by the Clifford+
T
gate set as well as the gate sets of common hardware platforms
[
IBMQuantum2025_QPUInformation
,
IonQ2025_NativeGatesGuide
,
Quantinuum2025_H2_Data_Sheet
]
.
The
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
upper bound follows from
[
Tanaka2010ExactNonIdentityCheckNQPComplete
]
, which showed that the check
C\not\equiv\text{I}
is in
\mathbf{NQP}
, thus our technical focus is on the lower bound of
Theorem

1
.

Clearly, any circuit that generates superposition must make use of at least one gate that generates superposition.
Likewise, any circuit that generates non-affine correlation must make use of non-Clifford resources
[
Nielsen_Chuang2010
]
.
Furthermore, any circuit that generates non-affine correlation clearly generates correlation, while any non-identity circuit must make use of at least one non-identity gate.
Thus, a potential algorithm for the minimization problem for any such type of gates could be used to solve
\textsc{Promise\text{-}IsIdentity}(\mathcal{A})
, by specifying a budget
k=0
.
In particular,
Theorem

1
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Consider any specification gate set
\mathcal{A}
that can implement
H
and
TOF
exactly, and any implementation gate set
\mathcal{B}
.
The problem of receiving a circuit
C
over
\mathcal{A}
and a budget
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and deciding whether
C
can be implemented over
\mathcal{B}
with count/depth
\leq k
,
counting all
(i) non-identity gates, or
(ii) non-Clifford gates, or
(iii) superposition gates, or
(iv) entanglement gates,
is
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
-hard.

As a concrete example of this, the problem of minimizing
T
-count over Clifford+
T
is
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
-hard due to (ii), as
T
gates are the only non-Clifford gates available.
Another example is that of minimizing
H
-count over
H
+
TOF
due to (iii), as
H
gates are the only superposition gates available.

It is known that
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
-hard problems lie outside
\mathbf{PH}
unless
\mathbf{PH}
collapses, since
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}=\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}

[
Yamakami1999nqp
]
and
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}
has this property
[
TodaOgiwara1992
]
.
Overall,
Theorem

1
strengthens significantly the recent
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness lower bounds of
[
Wetering2023OptimisingQuantumCircuitsHard
]
, and tightens the gap to the corresponding
\mathbf{NP}^{\mathbf{NQP}}
upper bound proven in that work.
In addition,
Theorem

1
generalizes the
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
-hardness bound of
[
Tanaka2010ExactNonIdentityCheckNQPComplete
]
to circuits over fixed gate families and when minimization is wrt specific gate families, each expressing a different type of quantum resource, as opposed to all gates.
Finally, the reduction behind
Theorem

1
is based on a small and simple gadget.
This indicates that strong hardness can arise out of standard quantum operations, and hints that it may be prevalent in practice.

2
Definitions

From now on, we use
a,b,\dots\in\{0,1\}
to denote single (classical) bits,
and
x,y,\dots\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to denote bit-strings of length
n
, where
n
is either explicit or clear from the context, and
x_{j}
gives the
j
’th bit of
x
.
We denote by
x\cdot y
the bitwise inner product of
x
and
y
, modulo two.

Quantum states.

An
n
-qubit quantum state is a
2^{n}
-dimensional vector

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi_{x}\ket{x}

where the set of basis states
\{\ket{x}\}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
form an orthonormal basis for a
2^{n}
-dimensional complex vector space,
and
\psi_{x}\in\mathbb{C}
are complex numbers satisfying
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\psi_{x}|^{2}=1
.
For quantum states
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
, we write their (tensor) product state as
\ket{\psi_{1}}\ket{\psi_{2}}
.

Quantum gates and circuits.

A quantum state evolves by applications of quantum gates.
A quantum gate represents a
(2^{n}\times 2^{n})
-dimensional unitary matrix
U
. Applying
U
to a state
\ket{\psi}
means multiplying
U
onto
\ket{\psi}
, obtaining the state
U\ket{\psi}
.
We write
\ket{\psi}\xrightarrow{U}\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
to mean that
U\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
.
By linearity,

U\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi_{x}U\ket{x},

so a quantum gate can be described by how it acts on the basis states.

A circuit
C
is a sequence of gates.
We say that two circuits
C
and
C^{\prime}
are equivalent (up to a global phase), written as
C\equiv C^{\prime}
, if there is a real number
\theta
such that for all
x
, we have
C\ket{x}=e^{i\theta}C^{\prime}\ket{x}
.
Given a quantum circuit
G
, we define
\Lambda^{k}G
as the
k
-controlled version of
G
, applying
G
iff the control bits equal
\ket{1^{k}}
.

Clifford gates.

The Clifford gate set consists of the Phase (
S
), Hadamard (
H
), and CNOT (
CX
) gates, and constitutes an important family of quantum gates, each acting on the basis states as follows.

\displaystyle\text{S}\colon\ket{a}\xrightarrow{}i^{a}\ket{a}\qquad\text{H}\colon\ket{a}\xrightarrow{}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{b=0}^{1}(-1)^{ab}\ket{b}\qquad\text{CX}\colon\ket{a,b}\xrightarrow{}\ket{a,a\oplus b}

In the following, we discuss different quantum computing resources.

Non-Clifford gates.

Access to non-Clifford resources is necessary for quantum universality.
It is well-known that extending the Clifford gates with any gate not expressible with Clifford gates yields an (approximately) universal gate set for quantum computing
[
aharonov2003simple
,
Dawson2005solovay_kitaev_thrm
]
.
Two commonly considered non-Clifford gates are the
T
and
TOF
gates, each acting on the basis states as follows.

\displaystyle\text{T}\colon\ket{a}\xrightarrow{}\sqrt{i}^{a}\ket{a}\qquad\qquad\text{TOF}\colon\ket{a,b,c}\xrightarrow{}\ket{a,b,c\oplus a\wedge b}

In later proofs, we use the fact that Clifford gates alone cannot generate non-affine measurement correlation
[
dehaene2003clifford
]
.
A quantum state exhibits non-affine measurement correlation, if the measurement correlation cannot be described as a linear function of measurement outcomes.
A state on which non-affine correlation can be observed is the state

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b}\ket{a,b,a\wedge b}
, which is s.t. the 3rd qubit measures to
1
if, and only if, the first two measure to
1
.

Superposition gates.

Superposition is a fundamental resource in quantum computing.
A superposition state is any state
\sum_{y}\psi_{y}\ket{y}
with
|\{y\colon\psi_{y}\neq 0\}|\geq 2
, that is, it is a weighted sum of two or more basis states.
We say that a gate
G
generates superposition, if there exists an
x
s.t.
G
maps
\ket{x}
to a superposition state.
An example is the
H
gate, as
\text{H}\colon\ket{0}\xrightarrow{}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
.
In contrast,
CX
,
T
and
TOF
are not superposition gates, as they map between basis states.

Entanglement gates.

An entangled state is any state
\ket{\psi}
which cannot be written as a product of two states, i.e., for all
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
, we have
\ket{\psi}\neq\ket{\psi_{1}}\ket{\psi_{2}}
.
We say that a gate
G
generates entanglement, if there exists a product state
\ket{\psi_{1}}\ket{\psi_{2}}
s.t.
G
maps
\ket{\psi_{1}}\ket{\psi_{2}}
to an entangled state.
An example is the
CX
gate, as
\text{CX}\colon\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})\ket{0}\xrightarrow{}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})
. Also
TOF
is entangling.
In contrast,
H
and
T
are not entanglement gates, as they only affect one qubit.

In later proofs, we use the fact that entanglement gates are needed to generate measurement correlation, i.e. that the measurement probability distribution of one qubit changes conditioned on what another qubit measures to.

Boolean predicates.

Let
\varphi\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\xrightarrow{}\{0,1\}
be a Boolean predicate.
Let
|\{x:\varphi(x)\}|
be the number of satisfying assignments to
\varphi
.
We say that
\varphi
is balanced, if
|\{x:\varphi(x)\}|=|\{x:\neg\varphi(x)\}|=2^{n-1}
, meaning it has the same number of satisfying and unsatisfying assignments.
A Boolean formula is a Boolean predicate inductively defined over conjunctions, disjunctions, and negations.

Complexity classes.

We recall a number of complexity classes that are central to this work.

One can think of
\mathbf{NP}
as a setting where we accept if a given randomized Turing machine has a non-zero chance of accepting.

\mathbf{NQP}
, a class originally defined by Adleman et al.
[
adleman1997quantum
]
, is a “quantum” generalization of
\mathbf{NP}
wherein we accept if a given quantum machine has a non-zero chance to accept.

Definition 1

(
\mathbf{NQP}
)
.

A problem
A
is in
\mathbf{NQP}
iff there exists a
\mathbf{P}
-time uniform family of quantum circuits
\{C_{j}\}
with polynomial ancillae where
C_{j}
takes
j
qubits as input, and
x\in A
iff the probability of measuring an accepting state on
C_{|x|}
applied to
\ket{x}
is
>0
.

Given a randomized Turing machine, one can define other complexity classes in terms of its acceptance probability.
The class
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}
, defined by Wagner
[
wagner1986complexity
]
, requires the acceptance probability to be exactly
1/2
.

Definition 2

(
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}
)
.

A problem
A
is in
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}
iff there exists a
\mathbf{P}
-time nondeterministic TM
M
for which
x\in A
iff exactly half of the computation paths of
M(x)
accept.

Although
\mathbf{NQP}
and
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}
are phrased in terms of different computational models, it turns out that they are closely linked; in fact,
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}=\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}

[
Yamakami1999nqp
]
.
We now turn to a problem that is complete for
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}

[
wagner1986complexity
]
(and thus for
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}
) under Karp reduction, which will be important for our development.

Definition 3

(
IsBalanced
)
.

Given a Boolean formula
\varphi\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\xrightarrow{}\{0,1\}
, accept iff
|\{x:\varphi(x)\}|=|\{x:\neg\varphi(x)\}|=2^{n-1}
.

We use
IsBalanced
as our source of hardness in later proofs.
Finally, we recall the definition of the Polynomial Hierarchy.

Definition 4

(
\mathbf{PH}
)
.

The Polynomial Hierarchy is defined as

\mathbf{PH}:=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\Sigma_{k}^{\mathbf{P}}\ ,

where
\Sigma_{0}^{\mathbf{P}}:=\mathbf{P}
, and
\Sigma_{k+1}^{\mathbf{P}}:=\mathbf{NP}^{\Sigma_{k}^{\mathbf{P}}}
.

It is known that
\mathbf{C}_{=}\mathbf{P}\not\subseteq\mathbf{PH}
, and consequently
\text{co-}\mathbf{NQP}\not\subseteq\mathbf{PH}
, unless
\mathbf{PH}
collapses
[
TodaOgiwara1992
]
.

3
A Deutsch-Josza-Based Gadget

Towards the proof of
Theorem

1
, we introduce a gadget
\text{DJ}_{\varphi}
, which is a circuit implementing the Deutsch-Josza algorithm
[
DeutschJozsa1992RapidSolutionProblemsQuantumComputation
]
.
The algorithm takes as input a Boolean formula
\varphi
, and under the promise that
\varphi
is either balanced or constant (meaning all outputs are equal), the algorithm efficiently decides which of these cases applies.
The
\text{DJ}_{\varphi}
gadget is captured in
:
