Comparing the effectiveness and drug persistence of methotrexate, cyclosporine, and acitretin for psoriasis
Authors: Chayada Chaiyabutr, Teerapat Paringkarn, Narumol Silpa-archa, Chanisada Wongpraprarut, Leena Chularojanamontri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Conventional systemic agents remain the cornerstone treatment for psoriasis because of their availability and cost-effectiveness. However, few studies have compared the effectiveness and drug persistence of these agents in Asian populations.Objectives:To evaluate the effectiveness and drug persistence of methotrexate, cyclosporine, and acitretin, and to identify factors associated with these outcomes.Methods:We reviewed data from 338 psoriasis patients treated with methotrexate, cyclosporine, or acitretin.Results:Out of 473 treatment courses, 239 (50.5%) involved methotrexate, 123 (26%) involved acitretin, and 111 (23.5%) involved cyclosporine. After 1 year, the proportion of patients who achieved absolute Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) ≤ 2 was greater with methotrexate (30.6%) and cyclosporine (22.2%) than with acitretin (9.5%). For absolute PASI ≤ 4, methotrexate (57%) and cyclosporine (47.2%) showed greater effectiveness than did acitretin (34.9%), with a significant difference only between methotrexate and acitretin (P = .017). The effectiveness findings were consistent in both the 1- and 3-year analyses. Multivariate analysis revealed that a high baseline PASI score significantly reduced the effectiveness of both methotrexate and cyclosporine. For methotrexate, a high body mass index was also associated with reduced effectiveness, whereas for acitretin, scalp involvement and male sex were key factors. Methotrexate demonstrated the longest drug survival at 1 year. Higher ages at psoriasis onset and systemic treatment-naive status were correlated with longer drug survival. Conversely, a higher body mass index and psychiatric comorbidities were linked to shorter survival.Limitations:Methotrexate is typically the first-line systemic therapy in Thailand, which may underestimate the true efficacy of cyclosporine and acitretin when used subsequently.Conclusion:Methotrexate was more effective and persistent than were cyclosporine and acitretin in a real-world setting.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a common chronic inflammatory skin disease affecting 0.14 to 1.99% of the global population, with regional variations.1 Despite the high efficacy and safety of biologic therapies, conventional systemic treatments remain the primary standard for managing moderate to severe psoriasis and are the most commonly prescribed drugs worldwide for this patient group.2–4 The choice of treatment in moderate to severe psoriasis is determined by a range of factors, including disease severity, the comorbidome, frequent access to the hospital for phototherapy, and lifestyle-related considerations, such as dietary habits, which have recently been reported to influence the minimal erythema dose and phototoxic reaction in patients with psoriasis undergoing phototherapy.5–7

While randomized controlled trials are considered the gold standard for evaluating treatment efficacy, their findings may only partially represent real-world clinical practice due to their strict inclusion criteria and controlled environments.8 Real-life studies of drug effectiveness and persistence offer valuable insights into practical use. Combining data from randomized controlled trials and real-world studies is essential to comprehensively understand the performance of each drug.

Drug persistence, or drug survival, is another key indicator of effectiveness. It measures the duration from treatment initiation to discontinuation and can reflect patient satisfaction or adherence to the prescribed medication.9

A recent systematic review highlighted the lack of studies on the effectiveness and persistence of conventional systemic drugs for psoriasis,10 particularly in Asian populations. The present investigation addressed this gap by comparing the efficacy and drug persistence of 3 conventional systemic medicines: methotrexate, cyclosporine, and acitretin.

Patients and methods

This study was a retrospective cohort study including patients treated at the Psoriasis Clinic, Department of Dermatology, Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand, from 1995 to 2023. Eligible participants were adults diagnosed with psoriasis by a dermatologist and treated with methotrexate, cyclosporine, or acitretin. Only the first treatment course for each drug was analyzed if patients underwent multiple courses. Patients treated with combinations of systemic medications or with both systemic drugs and phototherapy were excluded. The Siriraj Institutional Review Board approved this study (COA no. Si 022/2024).

We collected data on demographics, comorbidities, clinical severity, and treatment details. Baseline disease severity was determined by the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) score at the start of each systemic treatment. If the PASI score at the start date was unavailable, the closest score within 6 months before starting the drug was used. Follow-up PASI scores at 3 months (± 1 month), 6 months (± 2 months), and 1, 2, and 3 years (± 3 months) were collected. The nearest PASI values on those dates were recorded. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who achieved absolute PASI (aPASI) scores of ≤2 and ≤4, corresponding to relative PASI 90 (90% reduction) and PASI 75 (75% reduction), respectively.11 The analysis followed an as-observed approach, including only patients who remained on treatment and completed the respective follow-up evaluations. Patients who discontinued treatment due to ineffectiveness, adverse events, or other reasons were not included in the effectiveness analysis.

Drug persistence, or drug survival, was defined as the duration from treatment initiation to discontinuation, with discontinuation marked by a gap of >90 days.12 We assessed survival for each drug, including overall survival, cessation due to ineffectiveness, and discontinuation due to adverse effects.

The statistical analyses were conducted with Predictive Analytics Software (PASW) Statistics, version 29 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Factors with a P value < .1 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis to identify associations with aPASI scores of ≤2 and ≤4. A P value of < .05 indicated statistical significance. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to determine drug survival times, and the log-rank test was used to assess differences in survival times between drugs. Forward stepwise Cox regression was used to analyze factors associated with drug survival.

Results

A total of 338 patients were included in the study, with 51.2% being male (Table 1). The mean age was 43.2 ± 15.6 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 26.3 ± 5.3 kg/m2. The mean age of psoriasis onset was 36.0 ± 16.3 years. Chronic plaque psoriasis was the most common type, affecting 77.2% of the patients. The majority reported no history of alcohol consumption (67.8%) or smoking (69.5%). Psoriatic arthritis was present in 27.5% of the patients. The median baseline PASI score was 12.4.

A total of 473 treatment courses were analyzed: 239 (50.5%) with methotrexate, 111 (23.5%) with cyclosporine, and 123 (26%) with acitretin. Patients on acitretin had a greater mean age (47.3 ± 16.2 years) than did those on methotrexate (41.4 ± 15.3 years) or cyclosporine (41.9 ± 13.3 years). The median baseline PASI score was higher in the cyclosporine group (14.1) than in the methotrexate (10.2) and acitretin (11.6) groups. The methotrexate group had the highest proportion of systemic-naive patients (90.4%), followed by the acitretin (11.5%) and cyclosporine (9.9%) groups. The median duration of psoriasis before starting systemic treatment was longer in the cyclosporine group (122 months) than in either the methotrexate (57 months) or acitretin (96 months) groups.

During the study period, 331 (70%) of the 473 treatment courses were discontinued for various reasons. The most common reason for discontinuation was drug ineffectiveness (35.6%), followed by adverse effects (20.8%), remission (14.5%), and other reasons (29%). The most common adverse effect leading to discontinuation of methotrexate (n = 29) was transaminitis (58.6%), followed by liver fibrosis (13.8%) and cytopenia (6.9%). Meanwhile, for cyclosporin (n = 25), the most common effect was raising creatinine levels (68%), followed by high blood pressure (20%). As for acitretin (n = 15), transaminitis (33.3%) and hyperlipidemia (13.3%) were the most common adverse effects leading to discontinuation.

Table 2 shows the proportions of patients who achieved aPASI scores of ≤2 and ≤4, as well as drug survival rates. Methotrexate and cyclosporine were significantly more effective than acitretin in achieving aPASI scores of ≤2 at both 1 and 3 years of treatment. Within 1 year of treatment, 30.6% of patients on methotrexate and 22.2% on cyclosporine achieved aPASI ≤2, whereas 9.5% on acitretin did. After 3 years, 30.2% of patients on methotrexate and 23% on cyclosporine achieved this outcome, while the proportion for patients on acitretin was 10.3%.

Regarding aPASI score ≤4, 57% of patients on methotrexate achieved this outcome within 1 year, whereas 34.9% of patients on acitretin did. After 3 years, 59.7% of methotrexate patients achieved aPASI ≤4, which was significantly greater than the 38.2% reported for acitretin. Cyclosporine had a moderate effect, with 47.2% of patients achieving aPASI ≤ 4 within 1 year and 48.6% after 3 years.

The median time to discontinuation was 16 months for methotrexate, 15 months for cyclosporine, and 9 months for acitretin. The overall drug survival rates (95% confidence interval [CI]) at 12 months were estimated to be 69.1% (63.0–75.2) for methotrexate, 61.0% (51.4–70.6) for cyclosporine, and 52.1% (42.9–61.3) for acitretin.

Multivariate analysis revealed that patients with a high baseline PASI score were less likely to achieve aPASI ≤2 with methotrexate (adjusted odds ratio of 0.926) and cyclosporine (adjusted odds ratio of 0.890) within 1 and 3 years (Table 3). For aPASI ≤4, a high BMI was associated with reduced effectiveness during methotrexate treatment at 1 year, whereas a high baseline PASI score was a significant factor for reduced effectiveness in cyclosporine treatment within 1 and 3 years. In the acitretin group, scalp psoriasis and male sex were associated with a lower likelihood of achieving aPASI ≤4 within 1 and 3 years, respectively.

Discussion

This study examined the effectiveness and drug persistence of conventional systemic psoriasis treatments in a real-life setting in Thailand, where many patients may lack access to biologic therapies. Methotrexate demonstrated the highest effectiveness and drug persistence in our study.

We chose the aPASI score as the primary outcome measure to determine drug effectiveness. Although a relative improvement in the PASI score is considered the gold standard for assessing treatment response in psoriasis patients, achieving a percentage PASI score improvement of 75, 90, or even 100 from baseline has become the benchmark for evaluating new treatments. However, the relative PASI response (percentage of PASI reduction) has limitations, particularly when baseline PASI scores are unavailable or when evaluating patients with low baseline disease severity. An aPASI score may more directly reflect the severity of the disease at the time of the visit.11,13 Using both measures in conjunction would likely provide a more comprehensive assessment of disease activity.

Previous studies in Europe have compared the effectiveness of methotrexate, cyclosporine, and acitretin directly. A recent large-scale study from the United Kingdom reported that 32% of patients on methotrexate, 34% on cyclosporine, and 21% on acitretin achieved an aPASI score of ≤2.14 Our study revealed similar effectiveness for methotrexate (30.6%) but lower rates for cyclosporine (22.2%) and much lower rates for acitretin (9.5%). This difference may be because cyclosporine and acitretin were mainly used as second-line treatments after methotrexate failure in our cohort. The proportions of systemic-naive patients receiving cyclosporine and acitretin in our cohort were much lower (9.9% and 11.5%, respectively) than the corresponding values in the UK cohort (42% and 47%). In contrast, the proportion of systemic-naive patients receiving methotrexate in our cohort was much higher (90.4% vs 46%).

In terms of drug persistence, our study revealed that methotrexate had the highest survival rate among the 3 conventional systemic drugs, similar to findings from the UK study. However, our drug persistence rates were much higher despite lower effectiveness. At 12 months, the drug persistence rates for methotrexate, cyclosporine, and acitretin were 69.1%, 61.0%, and 52.1%, respectively. In comparison, the UK study reported rates of 46.1%, 30.0%, and 31.9%, respectively.14 A study from Spain also reported that, at 1 year of treatment, methotrexate (50.3%) had the longest drug persistence compared with cyclosporine (23.3%) and acitretin (42.3%), with lower persistence rates than those reported in our study.9 These differences may be due to disparities in access to biologics between European countries and Thailand. In Thailand, biologics are only reimbursed for patients in the civil service welfare system who have failed at least 2 of 4 conventional treatments (methotrexate, cyclosporine, acitretin, and phototherapy).

Regarding factors associated with effectiveness, the baseline PASI score (for methotrexate and cyclosporine) is crucial in determining the success of reaching the target goal. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies.14 Importantly, starting treatment with conventional systemic drugs in patients with very high baseline PASI scores may result in lower success rates. In such cases, early intervention with biologics could be a reasonable alternative to achieve the treatment target. Our study also revealed that a high BMI in the methotrexate group was associated with unfavorable treatment outcomes, affecting both drug effectiveness and drug persistence. This finding aligns with previous research.15,16 The mean BMI in our cohort was 26.3 kg/m2, which was classified as obese by the Asian cutoff,17 indicating that most patients were obese. In particular, for obese patients with severe disease, the effectiveness of methotrexate may be compromised.

Previous studies have shown that females tend to have better treatment responses than males do to many systemic drugs and biologics for psoriasis,18 which is consistent with our finding that females in the acitretin group had a better response. This difference may be due to higher drug adherence rates among women or their lower body weight, which could result in higher dosing per kilogram and may improve effectiveness.18 Although our study did not find a direct association between a high baseline PASI score and lower success rates in the acitretin group, we did observe that scalp psoriasis was associated with the ineffectiveness of acitretin. The prevalence of scalp psoriasis generally increases with psoriasis severity,19 so its presence may indicate overall greater disease severity and reflect potential acitretin ineffectiveness.

Our study also revealed that drug survival is associated with psychiatric morbidity. Psoriasis is known to be linked with various psychiatric comorbidities, including depression and anxiety disorders.20 Reduced drug survival in psychiatric patients may result from poorer medication adherence.21

Our study showed that methotrexate was commonly selected as the first-line systemic therapy, consistent with recommendations from Italy. According to the Italian Delphi consensus, methotrexate is considered the traditional first-line treatment for moderate to severe psoriasis. To optimize its efficacy, it should be initiated at an appropriate dose of 15 mg per week, preferably administered via the subcutaneous route. Lung and liver fibrosis are rare, idiosyncratic, non-dose-dependent adverse effects, and no cumulative dose threshold needs to be considered for safety.22 However, in special populations with certain comorbidities, methotrexate may not be the optimal first choice, and other treatment options should be considered.23

In addition, cyclosporine, which has potent immunosuppressive properties, has limited data supporting its use in psoriasis patients with hepatitis B or C infection. Our published data showed that among 66 psoriasis patients with hepatitis B infection, 3 individuals experienced viral reactivation—2 while receiving methotrexate and 1 while on cyclosporine.24 None of the 34 psoriasis patients with hepatitis C infection experienced viral reactivation; notably, 3 of them were treated with cyclosporine.25 Given the limited evidence, cyclosporine should be used with caution in psoriasis patients with hepatitis B or C infection.26

A limitation of this study is that methotrexate is typically the first-line systemic drug in Thailand because of its cost-effectiveness and widespread availability. In contrast, cyclosporine and acitretin are generally used as second-line treatments. Consequently, the effectiveness of cyclosporine and acitretin may appear lower when they are used after methotrexate than when they are used as initial therapies. This limitation could obscure a complete evaluation of the true efficacy of cyclosporine and acitretin. However, it also reflects the effectiveness of these 3 systemic conventional drugs in real-life practice within resource-limited settings, such as in Thailand. Another limitation is that the effectiveness analysis followed an as-observed approach, including only patients who remained on treatment and had follow-up assessments. Therefore, the efficacy outcomes might be overestimated, as patients who discontinued treatment due to lack of efficacy or adverse events were not captured in the analysis. Furthermore, dosage data were not included in the efficacy analysis, as frequent dose adjustments during the treatment course made it difficult to analyze.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study evaluated the effectiveness and drug persistence of 3 traditional systemic psoriasis treatments in a real-world context in Thailand, where access to biologic therapies is restricted. Methotrexate showed the highest effectiveness and persistence among the 3 drugs, underscoring its role as a first-line treatment in our country. The findings also emphasize the importance of baseline characteristics, including disease severity, BMI, and the presence of psychiatric disorders, in predicting treatment outcomes. Future research should focus on exploring optimal treatment strategies in specific patient subgroups, which may help guide personalized therapy and improve long-term management.
