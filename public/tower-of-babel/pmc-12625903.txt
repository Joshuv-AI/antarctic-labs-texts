Examining healthcare inequality for non-communicable diseases in Malawi: a hierarchical geospatial modelling approach
Authors: Yanjia Cao, Jiashuo Sun, Sali Ahmed, Pakwanja Twea, Jonathan Chiwanda Banda, David A Watkins, Yanfang Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is rising in low- and middle-income countries, including Malawi, yet spatial inequalities in NCD healthcare coverage remain poorly understood. In this research, we aim to: (1) develop a novel hierarchical geospatial framework to assess population coverage and accessibility of NCD services in Malawi and (2) identify underserved areas and provide evidence for targeted resource allocation.MethodsUsing 2019 Malawi Harmonized Health Facility Assessment Survey, hierarchical catchment areas were defined by facility type—primary healthcare (PHCs), district-level and central hospitals, with distance thresholds of 5 km walking, 25 km driving and 100 km driving, respectively. Incorporating facility readiness, we computed population coverage at the third administrative level. When estimating spatial accessibility, we used enhanced two-step floating catchment area, applying Gaussian distance decay for chronic conditions and inverse power for acute conditions.ResultsSecondary and tertiary facilities (STFs) covered over 60% of population, providing broader NCD service than PHCs, where coverage was lower than 20%, particularly for acute conditions. Population coverage was higher in central and southeastern Malawi, notably around Mzuzu, Lilongwe and Blantyre. However, at least 24% of the population were not covered for any NCD conditions. Additionally, only 11.9% of the population lived in regions of high or very high accessibility to PHCs.DiscussionWe found substantial geographic inequalities in NCD service coverage and access, highlighting underserved regions and the demand to strengthen PHC readiness.ConclusionThis hierarchical geospatial approach offers insights for resource allocation and improving healthcare equity in other low-resource settings.Keywords: Health Services Accessibility, Global Health, Health Equity, Computing Methodologies

Background

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are a leading global health challenge, responsible for approximately 41 million deaths annually, with 74% attributed to chronic conditions such as cancer, chronic respiratory diseases, diabetes and cardiovascular diseases.1 While NCDs are often associated with older populations, they affect individuals of all ages, with 17 million premature deaths occurring before the age of 70 years, 86% of which occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 The major factors for the global burden of NCDs include the ageing population, urbanisation, environmental pollution and unhealthy lifestyles.2 In addition, easier access to tobacco and poor diets have worsened the situation in LMICs.3 Younger populations, particularly those under 60 years, are increasingly affected by NCDs, adding economic pressure due to lost productivity and high medical expenditures.4 5 The Astana Declaration in 2018 reaffirmed the pivotal role of primary healthcare (PHC) in enhancing health outcomes.6 However, limited coverage and accessibility of healthcare facilities, particularly low performance of PHCs in LMICs, present another major contributor to NCD burden.7

Malawi, a low-income African nation, is experiencing a rise in NCD prevalence due to sociocultural changes and increasing urbanisation.8 Approximately 30% of the NCD incidence in Malawi is categorised as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer and chronic lung illness.9 Despite this growing burden, Malawi’s healthcare system faces significant challenges in addressing NCDs. Health clinics and centres score poorly on the service readiness index, with only 52% meeting basic standards.10 Furthermore, less than 40% of these PHC facilities are ready to provide NCD services.11 Reliable supply chains are required for necessities including blood, asthma medications and nasogastric tubes, as well as increased capacity for time-sensitive referral.12 In rural Malawi, there is a lack of basic health commodities for the screening, diagnosing and treating of diabetes in health centres, hindering disease management and highlighting the need for adequate equipment and supplies in PHC centres.13 Additionally, approximately 30% of Malawians lived beyond 120 min from an emergency room-equipped hospital, with Central regions having better accessibility than North and South regions.12

The assessment of healthcare access and the development of effective public health interventions heavily rely on measuring spatial accessibility.14,18 However, LMICs, particularly those in sub-Saharan Africa, still suffer from major challenges in this regard. Studies in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Ethiopia and Ghana have revealed alarming disparities in healthcare access, with large portions of the population living beyond recommended distances to healthcare facilities.19,22

Despite the growing NCD burden in LMICs, including Malawi, there is a notable absence of studies examining the spatial accessibility and population coverage of NCD services, two key indices for assessing healthcare equity. In particular, no prior research investigated NCD services while accounting for facility readiness (ie, service availability and functionality) for these conditions. To address this gap, this research developed a novel hierarchical geospatial model to evaluate healthcare inequality in Malawi. We classified catchment areas based on facility location and category (ie, health centres, rural/community hospitals, district-level hospitals and central hospitals). Considering service readiness and population demand, this work analysed spatial accessibility and population coverage for NCD services. The findings provide evidence for Malawi public health officials and stakeholders to address resource allocation to alleviate NCD burdens. The estimation also presents a landscape of NCD service in Malawi, as an LMIC example country in Africa, to acquire attention from WHO to address corresponding policy for the global NCD burden.

Method

Data

Data were sourced from the 2019 Harmonized Health Facility Assessment Survey (HHFA), focusing on 564 publicly financed facilities (512 PHCs, 52 secondary and tertiary facilities (STFs)) providing NCD care services. Facility readiness was assessed based on availability and functionality for chronic and acute NCD conditions.11 Population data were obtained from WorldPop (2020) and road network data from OpenStreetMap for spatial accessibility analysis, with details in online supplemental material method S1.

Study design

Following the research framework (figure 1), we computed population coverage and spatial accessibility to healthcare facilities in Malawi. We used travel distance for the former and travel time for the latter—aiming to improve metric precision and guide resource allocation. Facility coverage employs a supply-side, static distance metric to define a facility’s service area and population reach, providing geographic stability but without accounting for variables such as traffic dynamics. By contrast, spatial accessibility adopts a demand-side perspective, using travel time to reflect dynamic barriers (eg, road networks and transport modes) for patients, thus more accurately capturing utilisation and decision-making in NCD care.

Research framework for population coverage and spatial accessibility.

In addition, we applied a hierarchical catchment area computation based on the facility category: PHC services (including health centres and rural/community hospitals), district-level hospitals and central hospitals. To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses using extended travel distance and travel time thresholds for each facility category. Definition details can be found in online supplemental material method S2.

Population coverage of healthcare facilities

Accessibility to healthcare facilities

Our study used the E2SFCA method to calculate a composite accessibility index for each third-level administrative (ADM3) region. To enhance the accuracy of our analysis, we employed the population centroid based on the 2020 population distribution data as the origin, aligning with the demand unit representation used in previous accessibility studies. 14 17 25Online supplemental material method S4 described computation details.

Results

Readiness weighted population coverage

Overall population coverage revealed that acute epilepsy had the highest coverage among acute conditions at 76.20%, while acute diabetic event had the lowest coverage at 43.44% (figure 2a). For chronic conditions, oral pain care achieved the highest coverage at 79.57% and injectable pain care had the lowest at 52.59%. Although the majority of conditions had at least 64% population coverage (approximately 12.6 million people), at least 20% population (approximately 3.7 million people) did not reside within any catchment area and thus were not covered by any healthcare facilities for the listed NCD conditions.

Comparably, STFs (figure 2c) showed better service coverage for all conditions than PHCs (figure 2b). Among PHCs, chronic conditions are better covered, although the highest population coverage was only around 30% for oral pain care. And their coverage for chronic conditions such as heart failure and injectable pain care was as low as 0.5%. Their coverage for acute conditions was also limited, with only acute epilepsy slightly surpassing 1% (1.17%), while other coverages were equal to or below 0.7%. It is noteworthy that PHCs were considered unready to handle the conditions. Hence, figure 2b reflected only the population coverage provided by ready rural/community hospitals.

In contrast, STFs covered at least 60% of the population for most NCD conditions, except acute diabetic events (43%) and injectable pain care (52%). The highest coverage reached approximately 76%, for acute epilepsy, oral pain care, type 2 diabetes and type 1 diabetes. Therefore, at least 24% of the country’s population or 4.4 million people were not covered by STFs for any of the NCD conditions.

The geographic pattern of readiness-weighted population coverage showed most PHCs had low population coverage for acute conditions (figure 3a). Only one of the 471 health centres was ready for one or more acute conditions. Additionally, only 18 out of 41 rural/community hospitals offered some population coverage after considering readiness. Regarding chronic conditions, figure 3b emphasised the concentration of PHCs with relatively high population coverage in the country’s central regions (Lilongwe and Dowa) and southeastern areas (Zomba, Blantyre, Chiradzulu, Phalombe, Mulanje and the northern part of Thyolo). Within the PHC category, rural/community hospitals outperform health centres in population coverage for chronic conditions, averaging 0.03% and 0.01%, respectively. Interestingly, despite the large number of health centres, only eight surpassed all rural/community hospitals in population coverage. Malabada Health Centre, situated in Blantyre City in the Southern Region, held the highest population coverage (0.59%) among PHCs.

Regarding STFs (figure 3c and d), seven district-level hospitals were unready for any of the acute conditions. On average, within a 25 km driving distance, district-level hospitals had a population coverage of about 1.16% under acute conditions. Among these, the most extensive coverage was offered by ABC Mission Hospital (7.61%) and Daeyang Luke Hospital (7.31%), both situated in Lilongwe City, Malawi’s capital and one of its most populous areas. Concerning chronic conditions, the average population coverage was 1.71%. The lowest coverage was observed at Likoma/St Peters, located in Likoma in the Northern region, while Daeyang Luke Hospital continued to provide the highest coverage at 8.13%. Among the four central hospitals, Mzuzu Central Hospital, situated in the Northern region, had population coverage of merely 3.91% for acute conditions and 5.06% for chronic conditions. In contrast, the other three central hospitals had population coverage exceeding 16.5% for both conditions. Kamuzu Central Hospital, also in Lilongwe City, exhibited the top performance, covering 23.67% for acute conditions and 26.3% for chronic conditions, the highest levels in both aspects. The spatial distribution of STFs in Malawi also indicated a concentration in the central and southeastern regions, where healthcare facilities exhibited extensive population coverage.

Accessibility to healthcare facilities

Our findings (figure 4) revealed that access to both facility types (ie, PHC and STF) was high in Lilongwe City and Dowa (situated in the central region), the central areas of the Southern region (Zomba, northeast of Blantyre, Chiradzulu) and the central portions of Mzuzu City and Nkhata Bay in the Northern region. Furthermore, when evaluating overall accessibility, we observed high access to healthcare facilities in the southern border regions, such as the southwestern section of Thyolo, the southern part of Chikwawa, the northern part of Nsanje and the northern border zones, including the northeastern areas of Chitipa and Karonga. By comparison, regions with very low or low accessibility were often near Malawi’s borders, such as those adjacent to Zambia in the northern border region and Mozambique in the southeastern border region. Concurrently, figure 4 revealed that merely a minor percentage, 20% of the population in Malawi lived in regions marked by high or very high accessibility indices (top 40% scores) in relation to healthcare facilities.

Figure 5 demonstrated a thorough analysis of spatial accessibility to PHCs and STFs (categorised into district-level and central hospitals). The figure revealed that accessibility was comparatively higher for both PHCs and STFs in the Central region (Lilongwe and Dowa), the central part of the Southern region (Zomba, Blantyre, Chiradzulu and the northern section of Thyolo), and Mzuzu City in the northern region.

Spatial accessibility in each third-level administrative (ADM3) zone, showcasing spatial inequalities throughout different areas and types of facilities (a) primary healthcare services; (b) district-level hospitals; (c) central hospitals.

For PHCs, only a mere 11.91% of the population resided within ADM3 regions characterised by high or very high accessibility. These regions were particularly pronounced in the Southern region, encompassing districts such as Blantyre, Zomba and Chiradzulu.

Access to district-level hospitals presented analogous findings, with 11.79% population residing within ADM3 regions marked as high or very high accessibility. These regions were concentrated within Blantyre City, Lilongwe City and Mzuzu City.

In the context of central hospitals, a discernible trend emerged: accessibility gradually decreased from central zones encompassing Blantyre City, Lilongwe City and Mzuzu City. Among these ADM3 regions, 10.55% population resided in areas with high or very high accessibility.

Sensitivity analysis

To further validate the robustness and scalability of our model settings and findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative catchment thresholds, including an 8 km walking distance (approximately 96 min), a 50 km driving distance (approximately 60 min) and a 150 km driving distance (approximately 180 min) (online supplemental result S5). These analyses revealed patterns consistent with the primary findings. Notably, increasing travel distance and time thresholds led to higher overall population coverage and spatial accessibility across Malawi.

Discussion

This study introduced a novel hierarchical model to evaluate healthcare inequality in an LMIC, Malawi. Varying catchment areas were defined based on different facility types. Additionally, our methodology considered service readiness and used separate distance decay functions for acute and chronic NCD conditions, providing an accurate representation of healthcare service accessibility in the country. Our dual method using travel distance to measure facility coverage and travel time to measure facility accessibility offers a comprehensive view of healthcare service in Malawi, supporting targeted allocation for facility capacity and patient needs. This novel research framework using geospatial modelling enabled the identification of underserved regions in Malawi and provided valuable insights for healthcare resource allocation in the country.

The results emphasised considerable disparities in the weighted population coverage of healthcare facilities throughout Malawi, with STFs playing a vital role in providing specialised healthcare services to a broader population (figure 3). This suggests that efforts to improve access to healthcare services should consider the distribution and interplay of different facility types. Moreover, among the 512 PHCs, only 19 facilities were ready for at least one acute condition (figure 3a). While this can be primarily attributed to PHCs in Malawi not being mandated to provide specialised services for acute conditions,11 it leads to lower coverage for acute conditions than chronic ones. This underscores the need to reinforce PHCs by enhancing their readiness and expanding their networks, especially in underserved regions. Notably, for acute conditions, district-level hospitals within a 25 km driving distance achieved an average population coverage of approximately 1.16%. Among these, ABC Mission Hospital (7.61%) and Daeyang Luke Hospital (7.31%), both located in Lilongwe City, demonstrated the most extensive coverage. This exceptional coverage can be attributed to two key factors. First, these hospitals were well-equipped with the necessary diagnostic tools and medications to effectively manage acute NCD conditions, resulting in a higher service readiness ratio than other facilities. Second, their strategic location in Lilongwe, a densely populated urban centre, allows them to serve larger population within their catchment area, amplifying their coverage impact. Regarding chronic conditions (figure 3b), we found that PHCs with larger population coverage were mainly located in the country’s central regions and southeastern areas, aligning with the highest population density found south of Lake Malawi. By comparison, STFs displayed extensive population coverage (figure 3c and d) and have clusters around Mzuzu, Lilongwe and Blantyre city. Notably, although only 22% of PHCs and 38% of STFs are ready to provide heart failure services, the service coverage reached approximately 67% of population. This is because most of those ready facilities are STFs situated in high-population-density areas, offering substantial population coverage within 25 km of district-level hospitals and 100 km of central hospitals.

The analysis reveals substantial disparities in healthcare accessibility across Malawi, with greater access in the Central region, the central part of the Southern region, and Mzuzu City in the Northern region. Conversely, some areas had limited healthcare access, particularly in remote locations. This discrepancy may be attributed to varying catchment area sizes, with STFs generally having larger catchment areas than PHCs. Despite PHCs outnumbering STFs, their low service readiness reduces their capacity, lowering supply-to-demand ratios in accessibility calculations and thus limiting their contribution to accessibility. The clustering of ADM3 regions with high or very high accessibility around Lilongwe, Blantyre and Mzuzu illustrated the degree to which concentrated medical resources within compact urban catchments can improve accessibility outcomes.26 Although the population density was higher in these regions, the total population within each ADM3 was not exceedingly high, resulting in a more favourable supply-to-demand ratio. This finding explained why most ADM3 regions appeared to have low or very low accessibility, as shown in figure 5. The uneven distribution of healthcare facilities in these regions led to lower supply-to-demand ratios, consequently reducing access. Meanwhile, although approximately 20% of Malawi’s population resided in regions with combined high or very high accessibility indices, only about 11% lived in high or very high accessible ADM3 regions when considering each healthcare type separately—10.55% for central hospitals, 11.79% for district-level hospitals and 11.91% for PHCs. The geographic pattern emphasised the importance of targeted interventions and resource allocation to enhance healthcare access in those underserved, low-access areas. Our sensitivity analysis further demonstrates that the proposed hierarchical framework is both robust and scalable. By increasing the travel distance and time thresholds, a greater proportion of the population became covered by healthcare facilities for NCD conditions and resided in areas with high or very high accessibility. This expansion in coverage and spatial access underscores the potential benefits of enlarging catchment areas, especially in densely populated urban centres. The consistency of these findings attests to the reliability of our approach and highlights its value in guiding resource allocation and policy interventions aimed at reducing geographic disparities in healthcare access in LMICs.

This study has several limitations that may introduce bias and suggest areas for future research. First, the HHFA survey lacked data on all necessary equipment and medicines for NCDs, limiting the measurement of service readiness.11 Second, travel time and distance parameters were based on literature review, rather than Malawi-specific household surveys. Therefore, obtaining relevant surveys on the average travel time and distance of residents could help validate our models. Additionally, this study did not account for the topography of Malawi, which could adversely affect walkability and increase the travel time.27 Furthermore, 74% of the Malawian road network comprises earth/gravel surfaces and remains unpaved.28 This suggests that travel times to hospitals for residents in remote or rural areas might be longer than those estimated by the model. Moreover, due to HHFA data limitations, our calculations of spatial accessibility did not incorporate facility capacity metrics (eg, number of beds and doctors). Acquiring such data in an LMIC such as Malawi has been challenging. Incorporating such data when available in future analyses could potentially enhance the accuracy of observed spatial patterns and inform more precise resource allocation strategies. Finally, the referral system in Malawi involves referral hospitals serving both a primary catchment area (ie, local residents) and a secondary catchment area (ie, populations from referring facilities). Therefore, the current travel time and distance parameters only accounted for the primary catchment area and may underestimate the population coverage for some district-level or central hospitals. Future studies are suggested to incorporate this referral system to improve validation and accuracy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study introduced a novel hierarchical model to evaluate healthcare inequality in Malawi by integrating facility readiness, spatial accessibility and population coverage for NCD services. Our approach offered a nuanced and accurate assessment to identify key underserved regions. These findings provided critical evidence for policymakers and stakeholders to guide resource allocation, strengthen health facility networks, and implement targeted interventions, ultimately advancing healthcare equity in low-resource settings.
