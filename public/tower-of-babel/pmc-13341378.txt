Clinical Implications and Research Progress of Salivary Biomarkers for Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders
Authors: Chuyan Yu, Jiaying Ni, Qian Dou, Hang Su, Ziyi Zhang, Xiaojing Ma, Qing Dai, Jian Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsSimilar to blood, saliva contains a broad range of biomarkers and may offer a non‐invasive, repeatable specimen for endocrine and metabolic assessment. This review aims to synthesise and evaluate recent advances in the clinical translation of salivary biomarkers, while providing a critical assessment of the diagnostic reliability and clinical potential of key salivary biomarkers.Materials and MethodsPubMed, Web of Science and ScienceDirect were searched for English‐language articles published up to July 2025, with emphasis on studies from 2020 to 2025. We included articles with a focus on the clinical translation of salivary biomarkers for endocrine and metabolic disorders. The relevant studies on the physiology underlying the synthesis and transport of salivary biomarkers were also identified.ResultsLate‐night salivary cortisol is the most clinically established marker and is guideline‐endorsed for Cushing's syndrome, while salivary cortisone may improve the diagnostic accuracy. Salivary glycaemic biomarkers, such as glucose, 1,5‐anhydroglucitol, fructosamine, glycated albumin and α‐amylase, show potential for diabetes screening or monitoring. Salivary uric acid, inflammatory factors and adipokines may reflect hyperuricaemia, obesity and metabolic inflammation. However, most biomarkers remain exploratory and their clinical translation is limited by inconsistent collection procedures, oral disease, medication effects and analytical platforms.ConclusionsSalivary biomarkers offer a promising non‐invasive approach for assessment of endocrine and metabolic disorders. Standardised procedures and large‐scale multicentre validation are needed to define reliable clinical cutoffs and perform comparisons with blood‐based reference standards. Applications for machine learning and wearable sensing technology can further promote clinical translation.Keywords: Cushing's syndrome, diabetes, diagnostics, hyperuricemia, obesity, salivary biomarkers

Introduction

The progression of endocrine diseases involves complex interactions among multiple biomarkers (e.g., hormones, cytokines and metabolic mediators). Monitoring biomarker changes is crucial for screening, diagnosis and disease monitoring. The management of endocrine and metabolic disorders has long relied on blood‐based biomarkers. For instance, patients with diabetes require regular blood glucose monitoring, but the invasive nature of blood sampling causes pain and discomfort, particularly in paediatric and geriatric populations. These limitations have fuelled a persistent demand for non‐invasive diagnostic alternatives.

In this context, saliva has garnered interest as a promising diagnostic biofluid. It contains a rich profile of serum‐derived biomarkers, such as hormones, metabolites and proteins, whose concentrations closely correlate with systemic pathophysiological conditions [1]. Crucially, saliva collection is a non‐invasive procedure that reduces infection risks and facilitates repeated and convenient sampling. Its simplicity and ease of operation enable self‐testing at home.

Salivaomics is defined as the integrative study of saliva, its constituents and functions using omics technologies [2, 3]. In particular, salivary biomarkers for endocrine and metabolic disorders are now at a pivotal stage of translation. Based on the completeness of evidence and clinical translatability, salivary biomarkers can be categorised by clinical readiness into hierarchical tiers. Tier 1 refers to guideline‐endorsed markers with standardised protocols. For example, late‐night salivary cortisol (LNSC) is recommended in formal guidelines for diagnosis and monitoring of Cushing's syndrome (CS) [4]. Tier 2 includes biomarkers supported by multicentre validation and unified reference cut‐offs. Tier 3 covers candidates supported only by single‐centre or limited‐cohort data. Representative indicators include salivary glucose and salivary 1,5‐anhydroglucitol (1,5‐AG) for glycaemic assessment, with provisional thresholds and unresolved pre‐analytical heterogeneity [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Emerging biomarkers, including salivary uric acid (UA), inflammatory factors and adipokines, require further external validation before clinical implementation [10, 11]. Depending on clinical application scenarios, the statistical and evidence‐based requirements for screening, diagnosis and monitoring differ distinctly. Currently, only a limited number of salivary biomarkers have been formally recognised in clinical guidelines, with most remaining in the clinical research phase. This review therefore focuses on the clinical translation of salivary biomarkers, synthesises the evolving evidence in this field and provides a critical evaluation of the diagnostic reliability and clinical readiness of selected key biomarkers.

Methods

In preparation for this review, we searched the PubMed, Web of Science and ScienceDirect databases for articles published up to July 2025, with a particular focus on studies published in the past 5 years (2020–2025). We identified articles on salivary biomarkers for endocrine and metabolic disorders using the terms ‘salivary biomarker’, ‘Cushing's syndrome’, ‘diabetes’, ‘hyperuricemia’ and ‘obesity’. Articles on clinical studies for each salivary biomarker discussed in this review were further identified using the terms ‘salivary cortisol’, ‘late‐night salivary cortisol’, ‘salivary cortisone’, ‘salivary glucose’, ‘salivary 1,5‐anhydroglucitol’, ‘salivary fructosamine’, ‘salivary glycated albumin’, ‘salivary uric acid’, ‘salivary inflammatory factor’, ‘salivary adipokine’, ‘diagnosis’, ‘implication’, ‘detection’ and ‘treatment’. We also reviewed the reference lists of articles to identify additional relevant studies on the physiology underlying the synthesis and transport of salivary biomarkers. Non‐English articles were excluded. High‐quality clinical studies aligned with this review's focus on diagnostic reliability and clinical readiness were prioritised.

Physiology Underlying Secretion and Transport of Salivary Biomarkers

Saliva is an exocrine fluid composed of approximately 99% water, along with proteins and inorganic substances [12, 13]. Saliva is mainly secreted by three major pairs of salivary glands, including the submandibular, parotid and sublingual glands. Non‐glandular components, including gingival crevicular fluid, serum transudate from the oral mucosa, inflammatory exudates, epithelial and immune cells and microorganisms, also contribute to saliva composition [14]. Salivary secretion is regulated by the autonomic nervous system [15]. Parasympathetic activation stimulates the secretion of serous, watery saliva, whereas sympathetic activation mainly evokes saliva rich in proteins, including salivary amylase [12].

The diagnostic potential of saliva is attributed to its molecular profile, which reflects the individual's physiological state at the time of sampling. Molecules enter saliva from serum mainly through passive diffusion across capillaries, active transport by secretory cells and paracellular ultrafiltration between acinar and ductal cells [14]. The most common route by which molecules migrate from blood to saliva is passive diffusion. The ability of a molecule to diffuse passively through cell membranes depends partly on its size and electrical charge. Steroid hormones are relatively small in size and mostly lipid‐soluble, so they tend to pass relatively easily by diffusion [14]. Studies have shown that stimulation of salivary flow does not substantially affect the concentrations of these neutral steroids entering salivary circulation [16]. Charged steroids such as dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA‐S) are not able to diffuse through the lipid membranes of salivary gland cells [16].

Serum proteins (e.g., albumin and immunoglobulins) are too large to pass through salivary gland cell membranes and are believed to enter saliva via active transport by transmembrane protein ligands [16]. In addition, plasma compounds can enter the oral cavity through gingival crevicular fluid or by permeation through the oral mucosa [14].

Ultrafiltration represents another mechanism by which ions and unconjugated steroid molecules are transported from blood to saliva. Biomolecules with low molecular weights (e.g., water and UA) are transported between the gap junctions of acinar and ductal cells [14, 17]. For molecules transported by ultrafiltration, transport is more dependent on salivary flow rate, resulting in substantially lower concentrations in saliva than in serum [16, 18].

Given the diverse pathways of molecular transport into saliva, standardised pre‐analytical strategies are critical to reduce biological variability and guarantee valid biomarker measurement. Controlling sampling conditions and oral health status can help reduce confounding factors related to salivary flow and oral contaminants, and enhance the stability and reproducibility of salivary biomarker analysis.

Pre‐Analytical Factors and Standardization

Despite the promising clinical potential of salivary biomarkers, standardised protocols for saliva collection and component measurement remain insufficient, which is a major source of heterogeneity across studies. Key pre‐analytical factors include the stimulation status of saliva, collection method, sampling time, fasting status, salivary gland sources, sample storage conditions and blood contamination.

Unstimulated saliva is generally preferred to minimise potential biomarker dilution. Compared with absorbent methods (e.g., Salivette collection tubes), the draining and spitting methods tend to more accurately reflect the original composition of saliva [19]. Although the absorbent method may improve participant compliance, it may interfere with the measurement of some biomarkers, such as cortisol and sex hormones [20, 21]. Some salivary biomarkers (e.g., cortisol) exhibit obvious diurnal rhythms, and therefore, the sampling time should be standardised to reduce biological variability [22]. For salivary glucose measurement, samples collected in the morning after an overnight fast are recommended to ensure the stability and reproducibility of results. The composition of saliva differs across salivary glands, and unstimulated parotid saliva tends to show relatively higher glucose concentrations and closer correlations with blood glucose [23, 24]. In addition, inadequate centrifugation and blood contamination caused by gingival bleeding may affect measurement accuracy and should therefore be strictly controlled [19, 25]. Clinical Applications of Salivary Biomarkers for Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders

Salivary Cortisol and Cortisone for Cushing's Syndrome

The measurement of cortisol in saliva was pioneered in the 1980s [26]. The mechanism of passive diffusion ensures that salivary cortisol levels reliably reflect the unbound serum fraction and offers a direct window into hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity [27]. The physiological foundation underlies the current status of LNSC as a consensus‐endorsed diagnostic tool for CS [4]. A meta‐analysis of 58 studies by Galm et al. [28] reported high diagnostic accuracy in detecting CS (95.8% sensitivity, 93.4% specificity). Despite its advantages, the critical limitations of LNSC should be acknowledged. Its low concentration demands highly sensitive assays [29], and pre‐analytical factors like medication interference (e.g., liquorice and topical hydrocortisone) and blood contamination can confound results [25]. These vulnerabilities have spurred the search for more robust biomarkers, leading to the rise of salivary cortisone.

Emerging evidence supports salivary cortisone as an adjunctive measurement to cortisol. Cortisone is the inactive metabolite generated from cortisol within the salivary glands by the enzyme 11β‐hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 [30]. A key advantage, as demonstrated by Perogamvros et al. [31], is that salivary cortisone is present at an approximately 4‐ to 10‐fold higher concentration than cortisol, making it less susceptible to analytical lower limits of detection. In 2020, a Swedish study involving 155 healthy volunteers and 22 patients with CS established reliable reference intervals for both late‐night and 1‐mg dexamethasone suppression test (DST)‐suppressed salivary cortisol and cortisone levels [32]. The study revealed that when using the upper limit of reference intervals for CS, late‐night salivary cortisone (LNSE) demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic accuracy (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.985) than LNSC (AUC = 0.976). Notably, salivary cortisone showed superior pre‐analytical stability to cortisol, as its concentrations remained within the reference interval even in samples excluded due to potential contamination (indicated by high cortisol: cortisone ratios). To optimise diagnostic sensitivity, the combined use of LNSC and LNSE has emerged as a new strategy. In 2021, according to a retrospective analysis of 23 patients with CS, Garrahy et al. [33] demonstrated that parallel testing (considering a positive result as either LNSC ≥ 2.6 nmol/L or LNSE ≥ 18.0 nmol/L) achieved higher sensitivity compared with the measurement of LNSC or LNSE alone. This finding was corroborated by Efthymiadis et al. [34], who confirmed that the combined LNSC/LNSE approach (AUC = 0.850) exhibited significantly higher accuracy than LNSC alone (AUC = 0.762). Further refinement of this model suggests that incorporating DST into the combined LNSC/LNSE model could further improve diagnostic efficacy, whereas adding urinary free cortisol reduces overall accuracy, potentially due to its higher pre‐analytical variability. A study by Ceccato et al. [35] indicates that individuals with metabolic syndrome have elevated LNSC, particularly those with multiple risk factors, which may explain the mild increase in LNSC observed in the overweight/obesity population. Additionally, the overweight/obesity population often exhibits clinical manifestations similar to CS, such as hypertension and insulin resistance. Therefore, biochemical testing may be considered in selected individuals with overweight/obesity when CS is clinically suspected. Recent studies have compared salivary cortisol levels between the overweight/obesity population and those with normal body weight and have proposed diagnostic cut‐off values, thereby supporting further evaluation for the overweight/obesity population. Measuring salivary cortisone in the overweight/obesity population might be a promising method for differential diagnosis, but few related studies have been conducted. Critically, the implementation of these advanced diagnostic strategies is dependent on the analytical methodology. Salivary cortisol is commonly analysed using mass spectrometry or immunoassays. Mass spectrometry can detect both cortisol and cortisone [4]. A study by Ponzetto et al. [40] indicates that the detection results of salivary cortisol by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) are significantly different from those obtained by immunoassays. Furthermore, LC–MS/MS offers lower bias and superior analytical performance compared with immunoassay, making it a highly accurate analytical platform for measuring salivary steroids. However, other challenges exist in the application of LC–MS/MS. Its high cost and operational complexity limit its accessibility and implementation in clinical practice. In summary, different detection methods can yield significantly varying salivary cortisol levels. Therefore, each laboratory should establish its own reference intervals based on its specific detection methodology [4].

The utility of LNSC extends beyond initial diagnosis to therapeutic monitoring. Evidence from a multicentre phase III trial demonstrates its value in assessing pharmacological efficacy. Specifically, 22.8% of patients with Cushing's disease achieved normalised LNSC levels (≤ 3.2 nmol/L) after 1 year of treatment with long‐acting pasireotide. The subgroup (17.4% of the cohort), whose LNSC and urinary free cortisol were both under control, exhibited significantly greater improvements in blood pressure and body weight [41]. This finding indicates that biochemical control, reflected by normalised salivary cortisol, is associated with greater improvement in metabolic outcomes of hypercortisolism.

Salivary Glycaemic Biomarkers for Diabetes

Salivary glycaemic biomarkers are not currently recommended in clinical guidelines. However, studies have shown correlations between salivary biomarkers (e.g., glucose and glycated albumin [GA]) and blood concentrations, suggesting their potential as alternatives to blood biomarkers. Other biomarkers, such as salivary 1,5‐AG and salivary α‐amylase, may provide complementary information on glycaemic status.

Salivary Glucose

Early studies have demonstrated the basic correlation between salivary glucose and serum glucose levels, which were found to be moderate to strong [42, 43]. This association is particularly pronounced in hyperglycaemic populations [44]. Based on this physiological association, multiple studies have further explored the potential clinical value of salivary glucose in diabetes diagnosis. However, the diagnostic performance of salivary glucose for diabetes showed significant heterogeneity across studies, with reported AUCs ranging from 0.718 to 0.928 [9, 45, 46]. Therefore, the diagnostic efficacy of salivary glucose requires further validation. The heterogeneity mainly originates from several key factors, including saliva sources (whole saliva/gland‐specific), sampling status (fasting/postprandial), baseline population characteristics (e.g., body mass index [BMI] distribution and oral health conditions) and inconsistent analytical platforms. Current evidence is still insufficient to support clinical use of salivary glucose. Recent studies have revealed key factors influencing salivary glucose levels. In 2023, Patel et al. [48] identified a strong positive correlation (r = 0.896) between salivary glucose levels and BMI in patients with diabetes. Notably, the study also revealed that the strength of the correlation between salivary glucose and serum glucose increased proportionally with BMI. This suggests that predictive models may need to be tailored to patient subgroups.

Given these aforementioned confounding factors, the diagnostic performance of salivary glucose alone is limited. Therefore, recent studies have increasingly incorporated it into multivariate predictive models, such as those based on machine learning, to integrate multiple risk factors and improve accuracy. In 2024, Chopra et al. [50] developed a machine learning model for serum glucose prediction. The model included salivary glucose levels alongside other relevant variables (anthropometric measures, dietary factors, stress, physical activity and family history of diabetes). Through a wrapper method, the random forest regressor model was selected as the best model, with salivary glucose level as the most significant predictive feature. Such models may help account for known sources of variability in salivary glucose measurement.

Notably, the local oral environment is also associated with variations in salivary glucose measurements. Diabetes and periodontitis may exacerbate each other [51], and oral inflammation could alter salivary composition independently of systemic glycemia. A meta‐analysis including 3092 patients with diabetes showed that the overall prevalence of periodontitis was 67.8% [52]. Periodontal pathogens promote the release of inflammatory mediators and induce tissue insulin resistance [53]. In addition, xerostomia is common in diabetes and closely related to degenerative changes in salivary gland structure. A meta‐analysis of 2486 patients with type 2 diabetes showed a 42.49% prevalence of xerostomia [54]. One study found biochemical disturbances in saliva, including elevated salivary urea and total protein, lower albumin levels and salivary flow rate, in patients with diabetes, among whom xerostomia was present in 58% [45].

However, there remains a lack of standardised protocols for sample collection, storage conditions and detection. These limitations reduce reproducibility across studies, making it difficult to establish reliable clinical cut‐offs that could be equivalent to plasma glucose or glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Meanwhile, the stability of data for long‐term longitudinal monitoring in individuals has not been fully verified. Many existing studies also have small sample sizes, which may lead to overestimation of diagnostic performance. Standardizing the experimental procedure and conducting larger multicentre studies are necessary to obtain stable and repeatable results. Salivary 1,5‐AG

1,5‐AG is the 1‐deoxy form of glucopyranose, and its serum levels are significantly reduced in patients with diabetes. Serum 1,5‐AG is sensitive to postprandial hyperglycaemia and may serve as an indicator of glycaemic evaluation over approximately 1 week [55, 56]. Studies have reported significant correlations between salivary and serum 1,5‐AG levels [6, 57, 58]. These findings suggest that salivary 1,5‐AG may also have potential for glycaemic evaluation. Based on this association, Kedarnath et al. [59] investigated whether salivary 1,5‐AG has similar predictive utility for postprandial hyperglycaemia as serum 1,5‐AG. The concentrations of 1,5‐AG in both serum and saliva were quantified via LC–MS/MS in 45 patients with type 2 diabetes and 45 healthy controls. The results showed that salivary 1,5‐AG was positively correlated with serum 1,5‐AG (r = 0.648), and negatively correlated with postprandial plasma glucose (PPG, r = −0.539) in patients with diabetes. A salivary 1,5‐AG concentration of < 0.054 μg/mL was proposed for predicting PPG > 10.0 mmol/L (86.4% sensitivity, 87.2% specificity). Hyperglycaemia that exceeds approximately 10.0 mmol/L competitively inhibits the reabsorption of 1,5‐AG in the renal proximal tubules, leading to its depletion. These findings support the utility of salivary 1,5‐AG as a non‐invasive biomarker, particularly for monitoring postprandial hyperglycaemia in patients with diabetes.

Recent studies have further explored the clinical utility of salivary 1,5‐AG for diabetes screening. In 2020, Jian et al. [6] first proposed an optimal cut‐off value (0.44 μg/mL) of salivary 1,5‐AG for diabetes screening among the Chinese population. A combination of salivary 1,5‐AG and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) or HbA1c could enhance the efficiency of diabetes screening and reduce the need for oral glucose tolerance tests. Furthermore, a study by Ying et al. [60] found that salivary 1,5‐AG could be a novel non‐invasive biomarker for early‐phase insulin secretion. However, these studies were conducted in high‐risk populations, so further validation in general populations is required. Additionally, the quantification of salivary 1,5‐AG via mass spectrometry, while precise, is operationally complex and costly. More accessible enzymatic assays currently lack sufficient stability. Therefore, additional research is imperative to overcome these analytical challenges.

Salivary Fructosamine and GA

Salivary fructosamine (FA) reflects glycated proteins formed through non‐enzymatic reactions between glucose and plasma proteins, primarily albumin. It reflects the average serum glucose levels over the recent 2–3 weeks and could be a complementary indicator to HbA1c. To date, there are relatively few studies on salivary FA in relation to diabetes and its complications. Kandavel et al. [7] demonstrated a significant positive correlation between salivary FA levels and HbA1c, FPG and PPG (r = 0.893–0.934) in patients with diabetes. The results may support salivary FA as a potential indicator for glycaemic status. However, this study was cross‐sectional in design, and longitudinal investigations are required to validate the use of salivary FA for the monitoring of glycaemic control in patients with diabetes.

GA is calculated as the ratio of GA to total albumin. An LC–MS/MS analysis revealed that salivary GA levels were strongly correlated with serum GA levels [61]. Aihara et al. [8] collected saliva and serum samples from 56 patients with diabetes and measured GA levels using high‐performance liquid chromatography. The study revealed strong correlations between salivary and serum GA levels at three time points: fasting and 2‐h postprandial levels at admission and fasting at discharge (R 2 = 0.973–0.985). Multiple regression analysis confirmed that these associations remained significant even after adjustment for age, BMI and stage of nephropathy. The strong correlation between salivary and serum GA provides evidence supporting the potential of salivary GA as an alternative to serum GA.

However, the reliability of salivary FA and GA measurements requires further evaluation in conditions involving protein loss (e.g., hepatic and renal diseases) or salivary disorders. There is uncertainty regarding FA (or GA) measurements and controversy exists whether measurements should be corrected for total albumin, total protein, or neither. Periodontal inflammation increases local capillary permeability, leading to excess albumin leakage and falsely elevated salivary FA (or GA) levels. However, no correction methods for periodontitis currently exist, and standards for albumin correction of salivary FA (or GA) need to be established in the future [62].

Salivary α‐Amylase

Salivary α‐amylase is a key digestive enzyme secreted by acinar cells of the salivary glands. In patients with diabetes, persistent hyperglycaemia alters blood vessels and permeability of the basement membrane of salivary glands, leading to abnormal secretion and expression of salivary α‐amylase [5]. This mechanism may explain the association between salivary α‐amylase activity and long‐term glycaemic control. However, salivary α‐amylase does not directly reflect blood glucose. It is also influenced by factors such as autonomic nervous activity, stress, pain, sleep status and oral health [63, 64, 65, 66, 67]. Therefore, salivary α‐amylase is associated with glycaemic metabolism rather than serving as a direct indicator of glycaemic status. A study showed that salivary α‐amylase activity is significantly elevated in patients with poorly controlled diabetes, whereas in well‐controlled patients (mean HbA1c of 6.49%), salivary α‐amylase levels are markedly lower [68]. In a study by Anjum et al. [5], salivary α‐amylase levels were found to be significantly higher in patients with type 2 diabetes (80% with HbA1c > 8%) compared with healthy controls. An optimal cut‐off value of 5 U/L was proposed for differentiating between the groups, demonstrating an AUC of 0.98 with 95% sensitivity and 100% specificity. However, the generalizability of these findings is limited, as the study population consisted predominantly of individuals with poor glycaemic control. Current evidence better supports the use of salivary α‐amylase for monitoring glycaemic control trends rather than as a diagnostic tool. Further validation in populations with a more balanced distribution of glycaemic control levels is needed to determine its clinical utility.

Furthermore, it is well established that psychological and physiological stress stimulates the autonomic nervous system, thereby increasing salivary amylase secretion [69]. Therefore, salivary amylase could also reflect autonomic dysfunction mediated by stress or other factors, and its role in glucose evaluation should be interpreted with caution [70].

Salivary Uric Acid for Hyperuricemia

UA is the end product of purine metabolism. When UA excessively accumulates in the circulation, it leads to hyperuricemia. Recent studies have highlighted the potential clinical value of salivary UA measurement as a non‐invasive alternative to serum testing [71]. Patients with hyperuricemia show significantly higher salivary UA levels compared with healthy individuals [10]. Riis et al. [72] revealed a significant positive correlation between salivary and serum UA levels in healthy adults (R 2 = 0.48), with salivary UA exhibiting high stability across both short‐ and long‐term measurements. Using latent state–trait modelling, they found that approximately 60% of salivary UA variability was attributable to individual physiological traits, while about 30% stemmed from short‐term fluctuations (e.g., diet, exercise). These results can partially explain the instability of salivary UA levels. However, few studies have controlled for the confounding factors affecting salivary UA levels, which leads to decreased accuracy in salivary UA measurement and makes it difficult to establish reliable reference intervals. In summary, although current evidence supports an association between salivary UA and metabolic disorders, salivary UA cannot yet replace serum UA as a diagnostic tool for hyperuricemia. Key confounding factors must be clarified and controlled to improve measurement accuracy.

The interpretation of salivary UA levels is further complicated by pharmacological interventions. For instance, a study by Shibasaki et al. [73] observed that treatment with benzbromarone, a uricosuric agent, resulted in paradoxically higher salivary UA concentrations in treated individuals compared with untreated ones, at times even exceeding serum levels. This phenomenon is hypothesised to result from benzbromarone's inhibitory effect on urate transporter 1, potentially enhancing UA excretion into saliva. Bilancio et al. [74] detected parallel fluctuations between salivary and serum UA in seven dialysis patients, but with unknown pharmacological conditions. In conclusion, due to the unique mechanisms of uricosuric agents, salivary UA has limited utility for treatment evaluation in patients with hyperuricemia receiving pharmacological therapy. However, it still holds potential for assessing therapeutic outcomes in dialysis patients.

Salivary Inflammatory Factors and Adipokines for Obesity

Another group of biomarkers closely related to metabolism is inflammatory factors and adipokines. During the development of obesity, multiple cells secrete inflammatory factors and adipokines that are involved in inflammation induction and insulin resistance [75]. These observations highlight the potential of measuring inflammatory factors and adipokines to assess obesity‐related metabolic disorders. Recent studies have indicated that inflammatory factors and adipokines, which are present in serum, are also detectable in saliva and correlate with metabolic alterations. For example, Tvarijonaviciute et al. [76] reported a strong correlation between serum and salivary C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels (r = 0.77), and salivary CRP in the overweight/obesity group was significantly elevated compared with the normal‐weight group. However, as this is a cross‐sectional study, the causal relationship between salivary CRP and BMI cannot yet be determined. Multiple studies have established the association of adipokines with type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome [77, 78, 79, 80]. Asprosin is a newly discovered adipokine, first identified by Romere et al. [81] in 2016. Asprosin is closely linked to metabolism [82]. Gozel et al. [83] reported decreased salivary asprosin levels in patients with diabetes after 3 months of metformin treatment, suggesting that asprosin could be a potential therapeutic target for diabetes. Further studies are needed before salivary inflammatory factors and adipokines can be applied clinically, as interfering factors in measurements cannot be entirely ruled out.

Salivary inflammatory factors and adipokines are influenced by both systemic metabolic status and local oral conditions. A systematic review and updated meta‐analysis reported a significant positive association between obesity and periodontitis, with a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 1.35 [84]. Local oral inflammation can independently induce abnormal changes in salivary signals regardless of systemic metabolic status. Xerostomia is frequently reported in patients taking sibutramine, with a reported prevalence of 18%–48% [85, 86, 87]. Xerostomia may be associated with periodontal inflammation and the development of rampant caries [87].

Future studies of salivary inflammatory factors and adipokines should carefully assess periodontal health status. Comprehensive oral examinations should include periodontal parameters, such as plaque index, gingival index, probing depth and clinical attachment loss, to support correlation analyses. Acute oral infections may increase local inflammatory activity and salivary inflammatory signals. Participants with acute oral infections should therefore be excluded from study enrolment. Stratified analysis by periodontitis stage and quantification of local oral inflammation severity may help distinguish the separate effects of local oral inflammation and metabolic status on salivary inflammatory factors and adipokines.

Future Directions for Salivary Biomarkers

Current research highlights the emerging potential of salivary biomarkers in diagnosing and monitoring endocrine and metabolic disorders, such as hypercortisolism, diabetes and obesity. Advances in analytical methods, assay platforms, machine learning and wearable sensing technologies may further support clinical evaluation and implementation. Furthermore, the combined use of salivary biomarkers may provide a more comprehensive assessment of disease conditions.

Machine Learning Models for Predicting Blood Glucose Using Salivary Glucose

The relationship between salivary and blood glucose is complex and modulated by multiple confounding factors, which may explain the inconsistent results across studies. Machine learning has emerged as a potential strategy to improve the accuracy and reliability of salivary glucose‐based glucose prediction. Previous studies have employed diverse machine learning algorithms along with demographic, anthropometric and biomarker features to predict diabetes onset or blood glucose levels [88]. A systematic review by Liu et al. [89] further confirmed that neural network models are the most effective for blood glucose prediction. By quantifying and adjusting for confounding factors, such models could improve the reliability of salivary glucose and support the future development of portable, mobile application–integrated tools to accelerate clinical translation.

Wearable Device‐Based Salivary Monitoring Systems

An important future direction for salivary biomarkers is integration with wearable sensing technologies and multimodal monitoring platforms. Researchers have developed intraoral sensors of various forms. In 2017, the first wearable device was integrated into a mouthguard and offered the possibility of real‐time, non‐invasive saliva glucose monitoring via a wireless measurement system [90]. In 2018, a tooth‐mounted sensor based on resonators and hydrogel was proposed as a new way to detect glucose in the oral cavity [91]. In subsequent work, researchers demonstrated the possibilities offered by pacifiers as glucose monitors in neonates for their metabolic control [92]. In addition, diverse devices such as electric toothbrush‐integrated sensors have emerged [93]. However, this technology still faces challenges, including food interference, device stability, biofouling and wearing comfort [94].

Combined Use of Salivary Biomarkers

Salivaomics approaches, including proteomics, metabolomics, epigenomics and transcriptomics, represent important strategies for identifying novel biomarkers. A study revealed that salivary DNA methylation profiles and gene expression signatures could effectively identify biomarkers for various diseases, including type 2 diabetes and coronary heart disease [95]. As more salivary biomarkers are identified, combined biomarker assessment may become an important research direction.

For instance, diabetes is commonly accompanied by multiple complications, including cardiovascular disease, retinopathy and renal insufficiency. Salivary glucose alone could not reflect the progression of diabetic complications, and the incorporation of additional salivary biomarkers serves as a feasible solution. In the cross‐sectional study by Mrag et al. [45], the correlation between salivary urea and blood urea was analysed among patients with diabetes (r = 0.586). The enrolled cohort presented poor glycaemic control (mean HbA1c = 8.68%), and 27% of participants had confirmed diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Since this was a single‐centre, cross‐sectional study, the findings remain preliminary. Even so, the study suggests that elevated salivary urea may reflect systemic urea accumulation related to renal impairment. Combining salivary urea with other metabolites may offer a promising approach to establishing a noninvasive evaluation system for DKD.

Conclusions

Several critical bottlenecks remain for clinical translation. Pre‐analytical factors lack standardised protocols, and variations in sampling methods, fasting status, sample storage, blood contamination and salivary flow rate can lead to inconsistent results. Analytical platforms differ substantially. Measurements are also susceptible to interference from oral diseases, systemic comorbidities and medications. Most studies are single‐centre with small sample sizes, resulting in unstable diagnostic thresholds and insufficient validation of intra‐individual reproducibility.

Future efforts should focus on establishing standardised protocols for sample collection, processing, storage and reporting, with strict control of key confounding factors. Multicentre, large‐scale and prospective studies are needed to define reliable clinical cutoffs, refine the stratification of biomarkers by clinical utility and perform comparisons with blood‐based gold standards. The future directions, including medical machine learning and wearable devices, may further promote the clinical translation of salivary biomarkers.
