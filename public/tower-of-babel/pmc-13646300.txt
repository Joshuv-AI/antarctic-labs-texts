Re-Imagining Male Infertility Guidelines through the Global Andrology Forum
Authors: Selahittin Çayan, Marlon Martinez, Aleksei Ryzhkov, Mert Basaranoglu, Germar-Michael Pinggera, Widi Atmoko, Tan V. Le, Taras Shatylko, Edoardo Pescatori, Quang Nguyen, Rupin Shah, Ashok Agarwal
Published: 2026

THE PRAGMATIC IMPERATIVE

Male infertility remains one of the few subspecialties in modern medicine where clinicians are routinely forced to choose between rigid evidence hierarchies and pragmatic patient care. Individualized clinical decisionmaking in andrology is often based on insufficient evidence. In the subspecialty of male infertility, findings from the prospective, randomized clinical trials remain limited in the diagnosis and treatment of males with poor reproductive potential. Gaps remain in the recommendations of the professional society guidelines. The Global Andrology Forum (GAF) guidelines seek to bridge this divide by transforming global clinical experience into structured, transparent, and actionable recommendations. This editorial has been based on five GAF guidelines: a global survey on oxidative stress (OS) testing and antioxidant use (over 1,300 participants from 88 countries) [1] and four other clinical practice guidelines addressing varicocele [2], non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) [3], sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) [4], and antioxidant therapy [5]. Collectively, these guidelines produced international expert opinions and over evidence-informed, consensus-based clinical recommendations in male infertility, as summarized in Fig. 1.

A distinguishing feature of these guidelines is the use of a simplified binary classification of recommendations. Recommendations are derived from a structured process that combines systematic global surveys with the best available evidence and expert opinion and is particularly applicable to male infertility, a field where prospective, randomized studies are limited. This provides clinically actionable guidance where traditional evidence hierarchies alone are lacking to address global practice uncertainties.

This editorial aims to contextualize these recommendations by highlighting their practical clinical implications and future directions, while underscoring the advantages of the GAF guideline format.

THE EVIDENCE GAP AND CLINICAL REALITY

The European Association of Urology (EAU) and American Urological Association (AUA)/American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) guidelines provide frameworks but leave operational gaps. The EAU recognizes SDF correlations with poor outcomes but offers limited actionable guidance [6]. The AUA/ASRM discourages routine SDF testing and expresses reservations about antioxidant supplements [7]. GAF guidelines address critical gaps: SDF assessment in cases of unexplained infertility and assisted reproductive technology (ART) failure; comprehensive NOA diagnostic protocols, including universal genetic testing; standardized antioxidant therapy protocols; and guidance applicable across diverse resource settings [12345].

Guideline variability reflects the challenges posed by incomplete evidence. The Canadian Urological Association (CUA) conditionally recommends observation for NOA with varicocele [8], while the Japan Society for Reproductive Medicine advocates microsurgical testicular sperm extraction (mTESE) for NOA [9], and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence advises against varicocele surgery in this population [10].

Varicocele is present in up to 40% of males with primary infertility [2]. The EAU and AUA/ASRM limit treatment recommendations to the presence of abnormal semen parameters [67]. GAF global survey data reveal practice variations: 91.6% of uro-andrologists treat clinical varicocele with abnormal parameters in the presence of a female partner’s age ≤35, but 34.7% of them consider varicocele repair in the presence of elevated SDF (>30% by Sperm Chromatin Structure Assay [SCSA]), even with normal parameters [2]. Antioxidant use (85.6% of practitioners) occurs without standardized guidance, regardless of the presence of varicocele [1].

PRAGMATIC RECOMMENDATIONS: WHERE EVIDENCE MEETS PRACTICE

1. Non-obstructive azoospermia: no longer a “terminal diagnosis”

NOA accounts for 5% to 15% of infertility cases and represents the most severe form of male infertility; therefore, evidence-informed, consensus-based standardization of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches is critical to optimize outcomes [3]. Based on 49 recommendations evaluated by 48 experts [3], key guidance includes: (1) universal genetic testing (karyotype and Y-microdeletion) for all NOA patients—addressing the gap where currently only two-thirds are tested [11]; (2) hormonal therapy is definitive for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism but may be considered for selected patients (especially normogonadotropic). Exogenous testosterone is contraindicated; instead, selective estrogen receptor modulators, aromatase inhibitors, or human chorionic gonadotropin should be used; (3) mTESE is strongly preferred over conventional TESE, with higher sperm recovery rates (52% vs. 35%) [12], supported by both AUA [7] and CUA [8]; (4) a minimum interval of six months should be allowed before repeating sperm retrieval procedures.

Surgical sperm retrieval (SSR) attempts should be avoided in complete azoospermia factor a/b (AZFa/AZFb) deletions, while azoospermia factor c (AZFc) deletion allows a reasonable chance of sperm retrieval but poses a certain risk of inheritance by the male offspring. In patients with Klinefelter syndrome, SSR may be achieved in up to 44% of cases, particularly with mTESE [313].

Although no biochemical or clinical marker currently allows reliable prediction of sperm retrieval in males with NOA, considerable expectations are placed on integrated, multifactorial models combining genetic and clinical data, as well as on the future development of complex algorithmic and artificial intelligence–based approaches [314].

2. Varicocele: beyond the palpable

Varicocele is detected in up to 40% of males with primary infertility; however, indications for intervention remain debated [2]. Diagnosis is primarily clinical; Doppler ultrasound is recommended when physical findings are inconclusive, using criteria of venous diameter>3 mm and/or reflux >2 seconds [26]. Based on 31 statements scored by 24 experts [2]: (1) strong indication for varicocele repair is given by males with clinical varicocele and oligo-astheno-teratozoospermia (OAT) in couples with normal female reproductive potential; (2) consider repair in males with normal conventional sperm parameters but elevated SDF, as recent meta-analyses report a mean reduction in SDF of 6.92% after varicocele repair [215]; (3) never treat subclinical (nonpalpable) varicocele; (4) varicocele repair may improve reproductive outcomes in couples undergoing intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), with meta-analyses demonstrating higher fertilization rates (mean difference 10.9, p<0.01), increased clinical pregnancy (odds ratio [OR] 1.38, p=0.01), and live birth rates (OR 2.07, p<0.01) compared with no repair. Decisions should be individualized based on SDF, prior in vitro fertilization failure, and female reproductive factors [216]; (5) microsurgical approach is preferred, given lower hydrocele and recurrence rates (<1%) compared with other techniques (7%–10%) [1718].

3. Sperm DNA fragmentation: from research to routine use

SDF testing is used by 79.6% of clinicians managing male infertility [19]. Elevated SDF is associated with recurrent pregnancy loss, adverse ART outcomes, and failure to achieve natural pregnancy [4]. The established clinical relevance of SDF supports its potential inclusion in the standard evaluation of male infertility; however, high costs and unresolved methodological standardization issues currently limit its implementation [20]. The latest GAF guidelines comprehensively address the role of SDF in reproductive medicine. The key recommendations, based on expert consensus (87.5% agreement) [4], include: (1) SDF testing in cases of unexplained infertility, recurrent pregnancy loss, varicocele evaluation, and repeated ART failure; (2) the use of validated assays (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling [TUNEL], Comet assay, sperm chromatin dispersion [SCD], sperm chromatin structure assay [SCSA]); (3) a commonly applied clinical threshold of >30% by SCSA; and (4) a stepwise management approach, starting with lifestyle interventions and considering testicular sperm for ICSI when elevated SDF persists in ejaculated sperm [4].

Beyond lifestyle modification, recommended strategies for reducing SDF include treatment of clinical varicocele and antioxidant therapy administered for 3 to 6 months [115]. Importantly, the use of testicular sperm should be regarded as a last-resort strategy, considered only after correction of potential underlying causes and in the setting of repeated ART failure. Couples should be clearly informed that this approach is supported by low-quality evidence [4].

4. Antioxidant therapy: from empiricism to evidence

Antioxidant therapy is increasingly transitioning from empiric prescription toward a more evidence-based approach. The GAF guidelines [5] provide the first standardized framework, developed through rigorous dual-phase validation: 151 experts in a modified Delphi consensus, followed by 84 senior physicians applying the GRADE methodology. These guidelines address a critical gap: although 85.6% of clinicians prescribe antioxidants [1], practice remains inconsistent and often not guided by objective testing.

The guidelines present 14 evidence-informed, consensus-based recommendations (7 strong and 7 weak). Key strong recommendations include: thorough evaluation to identify underlying causes before starting empiric therapy (100% agreement); treatment duration of 3–6 months aligned with spermatogenesis cycle (94%); lifestyle modifications combined with antioxidants to improve sperm parameters (97%—highest agreement); standardized OS testing to identify appropriate candidates (80% consensus); and OS/SDF assessment in unexplained or idiopathic infertility may identify treatable causes (89%). Antioxidant therapy is not expected to restore sperm production in NOA (82% consensus) [5].

Weak recommendations address clinical scenarios with lower certainty: incorporating antioxidants for OS risk factors (varicocele, infections, obesity, advanced age, smoking); empirical use in idiopathic OAT; balanced formulations combining lipid-soluble (CoQ10, vitamins A/E) and water-soluble antioxidants (L-carnitine, vitamin C); and antioxidants as adjunct or standalone therapy in varicocele management when surgery is declined [5].

These guidelines harmonize clinical practice and establish a foundation for future research [5]. While the AUA expresses reservations [7], the GAF framework emphasizes documented OS assessment as essential before initiating therapy, thereby transforming antioxidant use from indiscriminate empirical use to a targeted, evidence-informed, consensus-based intervention.

THE GLOBAL PERSPECTIVE

The GAF guidelines on Male Infertility are distinctive in several respects. Other existing guidelines typically attempt to cover the entire field of male infertility in one document, sometimes combining it with male sexual dysfunction and even with female infertility. However, the issues of male reproductive health may be too heterogeneous for this approach, and the landscape of andrology evolves fairly quickly. The GAF guidelines, dedicated to specific topics and conditions, such as varicocele [2] or SDF [4], are more progressive in this aspect. Such guidelines allow clinicians to fully appreciate the complexity of conditions affecting the male reproductive function, sometimes masked by an all-encompassing label of “male infertility”. Furthermore, condition-specific guidelines on male infertility, pioneered by GAF, could be updated more quickly and incorporate vital emerging evidence.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THE GUIDELINES

Strengths include simplified binary classification (strong vs. weak), which improves accessibility and allows straightforward interpretation. This would allow timely guidance in rapidly evolving fields, where waiting for large, randomized trials would otherwise delay clinical direction, thus providing practical guidance for current practice while recognizing existing gaps in the evidence. These guidelines benefit from global representation (48 NOA experts from 25 countries, 52 SDF experts, 151 Delphi participants, and 84 GRADE evaluators for antioxidants, along with 1,327 survey participants from 88 countries) and capture diverse practice settings [12345]. Transparent grading acknowledges the quality of evidence: strong recommendations (≥80% expert agreement) indicate that most physicians would choose the intervention, while weak recommendations recognize value-dependent choices [35]. Dual-phase validation for antioxidant guidelines demonstrates methodological rigor [5].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As in much of male infertility research, a proportion of the recommendations relies on structured expert consensus, reflecting the limited availability of large randomized trials in this field. While this pragmatic approach enables clinically relevant guidance, survey-based inputs may partially reflect prevailing practice patterns, such as the continued use of antioxidant therapy without objective testing in some settings [1]. Formal cost-effectiveness analyses and broader couple-based considerations were beyond the scope of the current documents. Ongoing multicenter and prospective studies remain important to further strengthen the evidence base, particularly for antioxidant therapy [5].

CONCLUSIONS

These five guidelines, developed by the GAF, provide pragmatic, consensus-based recommendations for the management of male infertility, for which high-quality evidence remains limited. They reflect global clinical experience systematically collected through large-scale surveys, which are translated into multiple practical guidelines developed through a modified Delphi process [21] and validated through a rigorous GRADE evaluation [22]. GAF guidelines offer actionable, standardized guidance for clinicians managing NOA, varicocele, SDF, and antioxidant therapy across diverse practice settings. The antioxidant guidelines represent a paradigm shift from empiric prescription to evidence-informed, consensus-based, targeted therapy with documented assessment of OS [5].
