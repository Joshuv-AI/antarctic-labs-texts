Mechanical stress from the hyoid bone as a potential etiology of bony-related ischemic stroke secondary to carotid artery dissection: A case report and systematic review
Authors: Kawtar Lahlali, Jihad Karimi, Hajar Ouazzani, Ismail Chaouche, Amal Akammar, Nizar El Bouardi, Siham Bouchal, Naima Chtaou, Badr Alami, Meryem Boubbou, M. Faouzi Belahsen, Mustapha Maaroufi, M. Youssef Alaoui Lamrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ischemic strokes resulting from internal carotid artery (ICA) dissection are typically associated with trauma, connective tissue disorders, or spontaneous causes. However, rare mechanical factors—including well-documented compression by the styloid process and, more exceptionally, by the hyoid bone—have been identified as potential triggers of such injuries. The close proximity or elongation of the hyoid bone relative to the ICA may exert pressure on or cause injury to the arterial wall, leading to hemodynamic or embolic ischemia in the anterior cerebral circulation, or to a transient ischemic attack (TIA). Carotid dissection may also occur as a result of repeated or sudden microtrauma. We report the case of a 46-year-old woman with no significant medical history, admitted 2 hours after the onset of ischemic stroke symptoms, which included left-sided heaviness and facial paralysis. The remainder of the clinical examination was unremarkable. Radiological investigations revealed an acute right sylvian ischemic infarction with abrupt occlusion of the ipsilateral middle cerebral artery on CT angiography. Imaging of the supra-aortic trunks demonstrated a dissection of the right ICA in direct contact with the posterior horn of the hyoid bone.

Introduction

The hyoid bone anatomy demonstrates significant interindividual variability. Certain morphological anomalies—including excessive elongation or abnormal positioning at the carotid bifurcation—may result in direct contact between the hyoid bone and the internal carotid artery (ICA). This anatomical configuration may contribute to the pathophysiology of carotid artery dissection (CAD) and other carotid pathologies unrelated to dissection [1,2].

While such anomalies are frequently asymptomatic, they may occasionally manifest as intermittent and recurrent neurological symptoms, including transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), or progress to acute ischemic stroke when accompanied by significant cerebral blood flow reduction or embolic phenomena [3].

Diagnosis of bone-artery conflicts and their associated complications, particularly dissection, can be established through various imaging techniques, including doppler ultrasonography, computed tomography angiography (CTA), and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA).

A comprehensive literature search using PubMed and Google Scholar identified 25 articles reporting cases of ischemic stroke secondary to hyoid bone-carotid artery conflict, published between 1999 and 2025. No publications addressing this topic were found prior to 1999.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old woman with no significant personal or family medical history—including no cardiovascular risk factors, no valvular heart disease, no anticoagulant therapy, and no systemic warning signs—was admitted to our department for the sudden onset of left-sided hemibody heaviness associated with facial asymmetry, without seizure or spontaneous improvement, prompting medical consultation 2 hours and 30 minutes after symptom onset.

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically and respiratorily stable, with a blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg and a heart rate of 95 beats/min. Peripheral pulses were symmetrical, capillary blood glucose was 1.2 g/L (6.7 mmol/L; normal range: 0.70-1.10 g/L), and she was afebrile at 36.5°C.

Neurological examination revealed a severe ischemic stroke, with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 12. The deficits included a proportional left hemiparesis (upper limb: 3/5; lower limb: 3/5), dysarthria (1 point), cognitive impairment (2 points), oculomotor involvement (2 points), and anosognosia (1 point). Swallowing disturbances were also present.

The clinical presentation was consistent with a sudden-onset proportional left hemiparesis associated with facial involvement, suggestive of a right-hemispheric syndrome.

An additional occlusion of the right internal carotid artery was identified, extending from its petrous portion to the cavernous and intracranial segments, with a ``flame-sign'' appearance associated with a parietal hematoma, suggestive of dissection.

Imaging also demonstrated an interposition of the greater horn of the hyoid bone at the level of the right carotid bifurcation, in direct contact with the ipsilateral internal carotid artery.

The patient was eligible for neither intravenous thrombolysis nor mechanical thrombectomy, owing to a low ASPECTS score.

Laboratory findings were unremarkable: hemoglobin 12 g/dL (12-16 g/dL), white blood cell count 10,000/mm³ (4,000-10,000/mm³), platelets 155,000/mm³ (150,000-450,000/mm³), negative C-reactive protein, and a prothrombin time of 80% (70%-100%).

The electrocardiogram showed regular sinus rhythm.

Transthoracic echocardiography was unremarkable, revealing a left ventricle of normal size with preserved systolic function estimated ejection fraction: 60% (55%-70%), without dilation of the right cavities or pericardial effusion.

The patient was admitted to the vascular neurology unit and received medical treatment combining aspirin, prophylactic-dose enoxaparin, along with physiotherapy and speech therapy.

However, the etiological work-up could not be completed, as the patient died within the following days from aspiration pneumonia.

Discussion

Mechanical compression of the carotid vessels by bony structures is rare, and the occurrence of associated neurovascular events is even more exceptional. The stylohyoid complex—comprising the styloid process, the stylohyoid ligament, and the hyoid bone—lies in close anatomical proximity to the internal carotid artery (ICA) [4].

The hyoid bone is a solitary, U-shaped bone consisting of a body, 2 greater horns, and 2 lesser horns, with its tip located anterior and superior to the carotid bifurcation. Its anatomy shows considerable variability [5].

Fakhry et al. [5] analyzed 180 intact hyoid bones and found that the characteristics of the hyoid bone were highly heterogeneous, with a hyoid bone width of 40.78 ± 7.09 mm and a hyoid bone length of 36.38 ± 4.88 mm. Duan et al. [6] analyzed 74 intact hyoid bones and found that the length of the right greater horn was 31.4 ± 2.6 mm, of the left was 31.0 ± 2.5 mm.

In our patient, the length of the greater horn of the hyoid bone was 5 mm, the width of the body was 30 mm, and the distance between the hyoid bone and the midpoint of the artery was 6.5 mm. We hypothesized that repeated or sudden mechanical conflict between the hyoid bone and the carotid arterial wall led to dissection with subsequent cerebral embolization.

The hyoid bone undergoes positional changes during swallowing, phonation, and cervical rotation [7,8]. When these physiological movements occur in the context of unfavorable anatomical conditions—particularly marked hyoid bone elongation or intimate proximity to the internal carotid artery—they may precipitate direct arterial wall injury and subsequent dissection [9]. Such vascular lesions may subsequently lead to thromboembolic events, as demonstrated in the present case.

Carotid pathologies related to the hyoid bone may arise either directly, through compression inducing structural changes in the arterial wall, or indirectly, through alterations in blood flow, shear stress, and/or vessel wall rupture. Such mechanisms can lead to dissection, pseudoaneurysm, stenosis, or occlusion through direct compression, as well as pressure-induced plaque formation. Each of these processes may ultimately result in stenosis, occlusion, or artery-to-artery embolism [10].

In a retrospective study of patients with ICA dissection, Renard et al. demonstrated that a shorter distance between the stylohyoid complex and the ICA predisposes to dissection through a traumatic mechanism [11].

Following a comprehensive literature search, we compiled all reported cases of cervical artery involvement (vertebral artery, common carotid artery, and internal carotid artery) associated with a hyoid bone conflict and complicated by ischemic stroke, published between 1999 and 2025. No articles prior to this period were identified. This evolution in knowledge, initiated by the seminal observation of Abdelaziz et al. (1999) who reported the first case of transient ischemic attack (TIA) secondary to hyoid bone compression, demonstrated that the hyoid bone was responsible for 90% carotid stenosis at the site of arterial injury caused by the greater horn [1].

Since this landmark publication, it has been established that intramural arterial mechanical stress, combined with endothelial injury, smooth muscle hyperplasia, and increased connective tissue synthesis, lead to progressive alteration of the vascular surface and promote thrombus formation [3].

Accurate diagnosis and appropriate management can not only reduce but also potentially eliminate the risk of stroke recurrence. The reported therapeutic options are diverse and include antithrombotic therapy and surgical removal of the compressive structure [12].

Partial surgical resection of the hyoid bone has been performed in selected patients without complications. This procedure appears to be a safe option in patients with low surgical and anesthetic risk, in combination with anticoagulation during the acute phase of artery-to-artery embolism or ICA dissection [10].

However, in cases of carotid stenosis, stent placement is not recommended, as external mechanical compression may lead to in-stent restenosis [12].

In our case, the patient was managed exclusively with medical treatment prior to her death, consisting mainly of an oral anticoagulant (rivaroxaban).

Conclusion

In young patients presenting with carotid artery dissection involving the intrapetrous and suprabulbar segments, an underlying bony conflict should be considered. Potential anatomical contributors include elongation of the styloid process, calcification of the stylohyoid ligament, and, more rarely, contact with the greater horn of the hyoid bone. Imaging studies, including those performed with provocative maneuvers, are essential to confirm the diagnosis.

Early recognition of this rare etiology is crucial, as it may guide specific therapeutic strategies—including surgical resection of the offending bony structure—to prevent recurrent ischemic events.
