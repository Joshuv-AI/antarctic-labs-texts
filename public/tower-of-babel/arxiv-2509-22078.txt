An Inverse Problem for the Prescribed Mean Curvature
Tony Liimatainen, Janne Nurminen

Abstract
. We extend the study of inverse problems for minimal surfaces by considering the inverse source problem for the prescribed mean curvature equation ∇ ⋅ [ ∇ u ( 1 + | ∇ u | 2 ) 1 / 2 ] = H ⁡ ( x ) . \nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\nabla u}{(1+|\nabla u|^{2})^{1/2}}\right]=H(x). We prove that in two dimensions, the source function H H is uniquely determined by the associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. A notable feature of this problem is that although the equation is posed on an Euclidean domain, its linearization yields an anisotropic conductivity equation where the coefficient matrix corresponds to a Riemannian metric g g depending on the background solution. This work represents the first treatments of inverse source problems for quasilinear equations. The proof relies on the higher order linearization method. The main methodological contribution is the development of an approach to decouple the resulting system of nonlinear algebraic and geometric equations, which enables the complete determination of the source term from boundary measurements. The decoupling uses a Liouville type uniqueness result for conformal mappings. Keywords. Inverse problems, inverse source problems, prescribed mean curvature equation, quasilinear elliptic equations.



1.
Introduction

We consider an inverse problem for the prescribed mean curvature equation for
2
-dimensional surfaces living in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The equation in
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

(1.1)

\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\nabla u}{(1+\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\right]=H(x),

where
H\in C^{\alpha}(\Omega)
.
Let us assume for now that the Dirichlet problem for (
1.1
) is well-posed on an open subset
\mathcal{N}\subset C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
.
In this case, the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
(DN map) is defined by the usual assignment

(1.2)

\Lambda_{H}:\mathcal{N}\to C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega),\qquad f\mapsto\left.\partial_{\nu}u_{f}\right|_{\partial\Omega},

where
u_{f}
is the unique (small) solution to
(
1.1
)
with boundary value
f
.
Here
\nu
denotes the unit outer normal on
\partial\Omega
.

The well-posedness holds if
H\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and is small enough when compared to the isoperimetric constant and mean curvature of the boundary of
\Omega
, see
[
GT01
, Theorem 16.10]
. By the same theorem for some

f_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\text{ there is a unique solution }u_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}).

Now we can make a small perturbation by
f\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
to the boundary value
f_{0}
to obtain a unique solution
u_{f}
of (
1.1
) with
u_{f}|_{\partial\Omega}=f_{0}+f
. Then we can use the implicit function theorem to say that this solution depends smoothly on
f
. Notice that by
[
GT01
, Theorem 16.10]
we do not need to restrict ourselves to unique solutions in some neighborhood of
u_{0}
, sometimes called small solutions in previous works. We refer to
[
LL24
, Theorem 2.1]
for a related well posedness proof.

We ask the following question:

Inverse problem:
Can we determine
H
from the DN map?

This type of an inverse problem is called an inverse source problem. We will give a positive answer to this:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
H,\tilde{H}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
be such that
H|_{\partial\Omega}=\tilde{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
to high order and
f_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Assume that
u_{0}
and
\tilde{u}_{0}
solve

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\nabla u}{(1+\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\right]=H(x),&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
u=f_{0},&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

for
H
and
\tilde{H}
on the right hand side respectively, and with
u_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=\tilde{u}_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=f_{0}
. Suppose that the DN maps of (
1.1
) satisfy

\Lambda_{H}f=\Lambda_{\tilde{H}}f,\quad\text{for all }f\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)

with
\lVert f_{0}-f\rVert_{C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\delta
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Then
H=\tilde{H}
in
\Omega
.

1.1.
Motivation

The prescribed mean curvature (PMC) equation is a prototypical quasilinear elliptic equation of the form

(1.4)

\nabla\cdot\left(A(\nabla u)\nabla u\right)=H(x),

where
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. The main motivation of the present work is to serve as a first step in solving inverse problems for equations of the above. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first to consider an inverse source problem for a quasilinear elliptic equation.

Another key motivation for our study concerns the method we employ to solve the inverse problem. Our approach is based on the higher order linearization technique. The first step of this method involves solving the Calderón problem for the first linearized equation

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot\left(g^{-1}\nabla v\right)=0,&\text{in }\Omega,\\
v=f,&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
g
is a positive definite symmetric matrix field, thus a Riemannian metric. This linearized equation is an anisotropic conductivity equation. Therefore, our study provides a new application for the theory of the anisotropic Calderón problem, even though the original nonlinear equation is posed on a Euclidean domain.

Regarding the PMC equation itself, it generalizes the minimal surface equation (
H=0
) by requiring a surface to have a specific, pre-assigned mean curvature
H
at every point, rather than zero curvature. Physically, this corresponds to modeling surfaces in a state of balanced tension under external forces, such as soap bubbles with internal pressure, rather than area-minimizing soap films. Consequently, the PMC equation serves as a fundamental model in diverse areas, including capillary surfaces, biological membranes, and general relativity, where it arises in the initial data formulation of Einstein’s equations
[
Sei97
,
CGP10
]
.

Beyond these classical applications, we mention that inverse problems for minimal surfaces have recently gained surprising significance within the AdS/CFT duality theory in theoretical physics. There, techniques from inverse problems have proven effective in such cases
[
JLST25
]
where the mathematical
tools traditionally used in physics have been ineffective.
Due to this surprising application, we mention the works
[
HMS11
,
Don14
]
that study surfaces with prescribed curvature in holography.

1.2.

Our work is a new addition to a growing branch of inverse problems for nonlinear partial differential equations. In

,

This method has been applied to many problems. Quasilinear elliptic isotropic conductivity equations have been considered in
[
CFK+21
,
KKU20
,
LW23
]
. In these works the goal is to recover the nonlinear conductivity. Also inverse problems for the minimal surface equation, quasilinear in nature, on Riemannian manifolds and Euclidean domains have been considered in

ABN20
,

,

,

,

,

]
, and in the context of AdS/CFT duality in physics in
[

]
. Also in
[

]
the author studies a Calderón type problem for harmonic maps. A map between two Riemannian manifolds is harmonic if it satisfies a certain quasilinear equation.

Inverse problems for elliptic second order semilinear equations have been considered in

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
and inverse problems for nonlinear magnetic Schrödinger equations in

]
,
p
-harmonic equation

,

1.3.
Method of proof

The methods we use can be divided into two main parts. First, we combine information from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map with the higher-order linearization technique and complex geometric optics (CGO) solutions for the Schrödinger equation to derive equations for quantities related to the background solution
u_{0}
(corresponding to the boundary value
f_{0}
). These equations are highly coupled, and the second step involves solving for
\nabla u_{0}
from this system. A key ingredient at this stage is a Liouville-type uniqueness result for conformal mappings.

To show that
H=\tilde{H}
in
\Omega
, we proceed as follows. If
u_{0}
and
\tilde{u}_{0}
are the solutions of the prescribed mean curvature equation corresponding to the sources
H
and
\tilde{H}
respectively, and if we establish that
\nabla u_{0}=\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}
, then it follows immediately from the structure of the equation that

H=\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\nabla u_{0}}{(1+|\nabla u_{0}|^{2})^{1/2}}\right]=\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}}{(1+|\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}|^{2})^{1/2}}\right]=\tilde{H}.

We now provide a detailed outline of the argument. The first linearization of (
1.1
) yields an anisotropic conductivity equation, equivalent to a Schrödinger equation involving the Laplace–Beltrami operator associated with the Riemannian metric whose inverse is

g^{-1}=\frac{1}{(1+|\nabla u_{0}|^{2})^{1/2}}\left(I_{2\times 2}-\frac{\nabla u_{0}\otimes\nabla u_{0}}{1+|\nabla u_{0}|^{2}}\right).

This metric explicitly depends on the background solution
u_{0}
. Using boundary information about
H
, we show that the DN maps for the corresponding Schrödinger equations coincide. Applying
[
CLLO24
, Theorem 1.1]
, we conclude that the metrics associated with
u_{0}
and
\tilde{u}_{0}
, along with their potentials, agree up to natural conformal and diffeomorphism gauges; see Section
2
.

The next step is to break these gauge invariances. Using unique continuation and the specific functional dependence of the potential on the metric
g
, we deduce (see Lemma
2.2
and (
2.15
)) that

(1.6)

|\nabla u_{0}|=|\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}|\circ\phi,

where
\phi:\Omega\to\Omega
is a diffeomorphism that fixes the boundary.

To strengthen this to full gradient equality, we employ the second linearization, which yields the integral identity:

(1.7)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\Omega}T\cdot\bigg(

\displaystyle\nabla v_{0}\,g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})

\displaystyle+\nabla v_{1}\left(\Delta_{g}(v_{0}v_{2})-v_{2}\Delta_{g}v_{0}-v_{0}\Delta_{g}v_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\nabla v_{2}\left(\Delta_{g}(v_{1}v_{0})-v_{0}\Delta_{g}v_{1}-v_{1}\Delta_{g}v_{0}\right)\bigg)dV_{g},

where
T
is a

T:=\left(\frac{\lambda J_{\phi}\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}-\frac{\nabla u_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)\lvert g\rvert^{-1/2},

and
v_{k}
(
k=0,1,2
) are solutions to the first linearized equation. The analysis of this identity relies on carefully constructed CGO solutions
[
GT11b
]
, where we choose two solutions without critical points and one with a critical point. Using asymptotic analysis techniques similar to
[
CLLO24
,
LW23
]
, we ultimately conclude that (see (
4.4
))

(1.8)

\nabla u_{0}=\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}.

The final step is to show that (
1.6
) and (
1.8
) together imply that
\phi
is the identity map on
\Omega
. This follows from a uniqueness theorem for conformal mappings, which forces
\phi=\text{id}
, see Section
4.2
. Consequently,
\nabla u_{0}=\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}
throughout
\Omega
, and thus
H=\tilde{H}
as required.

2.
First linearization

To make notation more convenient, we let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},

F(p):=\frac{p}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}

and thus (
1.1
) can be written as

(2.1)

\nabla\cdot F(\nabla u)=H\text{ in }\Omega.

We assume that there exists a solution
u_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\Omega)
to (
2.1
) corresponding to boundary value
f_{0}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
and then we linearize at this solution. Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
,
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
and
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0,
be such that
f=f_{0}+\epsilon_{1}f_{1}+\epsilon_{2}f_{2}
is sufficiently close to
f_{0}
. Now the first linearization, denoted by

v_{1}=\partial_{\epsilon_{1}}u|_{\epsilon=0},

satisfies the first linearized equation

(2.2)

\partial_{a}(\partial_{b}F^{a}(\nabla u)|_{u=u_{0}}\partial_{\epsilon}\partial_{b}u|_{\epsilon=0})=\partial_{a}(\partial_{b}F^{a}(\nabla u_{0})\partial_{b}v)=0\text{ in }\Omega,

where

(2.3)

\partial_{b}F^{a}(p)=\frac{\delta^{ab}}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}-\frac{p_{a}p_{b}}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}.

From now on, we choose to put the indexes up and define the Riemannian metric

(2.4)

g^{ab}=\frac{1}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\left(\delta^{ab}-\frac{\partial^{a}u_{0}\partial^{b}u_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)=\partial_{b}F^{a}(\nabla u_{0}).

For a proof that
g^{ab}
is indeed positive definite symmetric matrix we refer to
[
GT01
, Chapter 10]
. Thus the first linearization is the elliptic equation

(2.5)

\partial_{a}(g^{ab}\partial_{b}v)=0\text{ in }\Omega.

For boundary values
f
close to
f_{0}
, the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot F(\nabla u)=H,&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
u=f_{0}+f,&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

is well-posed and the DN map is smooth in the Fréchet sense:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary,
H\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
f_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
. Assume that
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is a solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot F(\nabla u_{0})=H,&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
u_{0}=f_{0},&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Then there are
\delta,C>0
such that for any

f\in B_{\delta}=\{f\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}):\lVert f_{0}-f\rVert_{C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\delta\}

there exists a unique small solution
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot F(\nabla u)=H,&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
u=f_{0}+f,&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

so that
\lVert u_{0}-u\rVert_{C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\delta
. Furthermore there are
C^{\infty}
maps, in the Fréchet sense,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\colon B_{\delta}\to C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad f\mapsto u

\displaystyle\Lambda_{H}

\displaystyle\colon B_{\delta}\to C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),\quad f\mapsto\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}.

The proof of this is similar to the one in
[
LL24
]
and we omit the proof. We note that the linearized equation (
2.5
) is well-posed (since it does not contain a zeroth order term) and thus we do not need to make assumptions regarding the well-posedness of the linearization of (
1.1
). If
H
is small enough compared to the isoperimetric constant of
\Omega
and the mean curvature of the boundary
\partial\Omega
, then
u_{0}
exists and the solution
u
corresponding to boundary value
f+f_{0}
is globally unique. For these facts, see
[
GT01
, Theorem 16.10, and (16.61)]
.

We modify (
2.2
) to a Laplace-Beltrami type equation.
For
\gamma=\lvert g\rvert^{-1/2}
we have

0=\nabla\cdot\left(g^{-1}\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}v)\right)=\gamma^{-1/2}(\Delta_{g}+q)v

where
q=\dfrac{\Delta_{g}(\gamma^{1/2})}{\gamma^{1/2}}
. Thus
v
is a solution of the boundary value problem

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\nabla\cdot\left(g^{-1}\nabla v\right)=0,&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
v=f,&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

if and only if
\hat{v}=\gamma^{1/2}v
is a solution to

(2.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(\Delta_{g}+q)\hat{v}=0,&\text{in}\,\,\Omega\\
\hat{v}=\gamma^{1/2}f,&\text{on}\,\,\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

If the DN maps corresponding to two sources
H
and
\tilde{H}
agree, it follows by linearization that the DN maps of the equation (
2.6
) for the coefficients
(g,q)
and
(\tilde{g},\tilde{q})
agree. In this case there exists a conformal diffeomorphism
\phi\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\overline{\Omega}
such that

(2.8)

g=\lambda\phi^{*}\tilde{g}\quad\text{and that}\quad q=\lambda^{-1}\phi^{*}\tilde{q}

with
\lambda|_{\partial\Omega}=1
and
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=\Id
, see
[
CLLO24
]
.

Let us then denote by
v
and
\tilde{v}
the solutions to the first linearizations
(\Delta_{g}+q)v=0
and
(\Delta_{\tilde{g}}+\tilde{q})\tilde{v}=0
corresponding to a given boundary value
f\in C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
.
Now

(2.9)

\displaystyle(\Delta_{g}+q)(\tilde{v}\circ\phi)=(\Delta_{\lambda\phi^{*}\tilde{g}}+\lambda^{-1}\phi^{*}\tilde{q})(\tilde{v}\circ\phi)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}(\Delta_{\phi^{*}\tilde{g}}+\phi^{*}\tilde{q})(\tilde{v}\circ\phi)=\lambda^{-1}\phi^{*}(\Delta_{\tilde{g}}\tilde{v}+\tilde{q}\tilde{v})=0.

Since
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=\mathrm{Id}
, we have
v=\tilde{v}\circ\phi
by the uniqueness of solutions to the Dirichlet problem.

Using the Sylvester determinant rule and (
2.4
) we have the following

\displaystyle\det(\lambda^{-1}(D\phi)^{-1}\tilde{g}^{-1}(D\phi)^{-T})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}J_{\phi}^{-2}\frac{1}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}\left(1-\frac{\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2}J_{\phi}^{-2}\frac{1}{(1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi})^{2}}.

On the other hand

\displaystyle\det(g^{-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{2}}.

Thus, the identity

g^{-1}=\lambda^{-1}(D\phi)^{-1}\tilde{g}^{-1}|_{\phi}(D\phi)^{-T}

gives

(\lambda J_{\phi})^{-2}=\frac{(1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi})^{2}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{2}},

or equivalently

(2.10)

\lambda J_{\phi}=\frac{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}.

For the next result we recall that

(2.11)

q=\dfrac{\Delta_{g}(\gamma^{1/2})}{\gamma^{1/2}}\text{ and }\tilde{q}=\dfrac{\Delta_{\tilde{g}}(\tilde{\gamma}^{1/2})}{\tilde{\gamma}^{1/2}},

where

\gamma=\lvert g\rvert^{-1/2}\text{ and }\tilde{\gamma}=\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{-1/2}.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a domain with smooth boundary,
g,\tilde{g}

\Omega
,
\phi\colon\Omega\to\Omega

and

. Assume that

(2.12)

\det(g^{-1}\tilde{g}|_{\phi})=1\quad\text{to first order on }\partial\Omega

and

(2.13)

q=\lambda^{-1}\phi^{*}\tilde{q},

where

and
\tilde{q}
are as in (

).
Then

(2.14)

J_{\phi}\lambda=1.

Proof.

By using the conformal invariance of the Laplacian,
\Delta_{cg}v=c^{-1}\Delta_{g}v
, together with (

\displaystyle q=\lvert g\rvert^{1/4}\Delta_{g}(\lvert g\rvert^{-1/4})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\phi^{*}\tilde{q}=\lambda^{-1}\phi^{*}\left(\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{1/4}\Delta_{\tilde{g}}(\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{-1/4})\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{1/4}|_{\phi}\Delta_{\phi^{*}\tilde{g}}(\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{-1/4}|_{\phi})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{1/4}|_{\phi}\Delta_{\lambda^{-1}g}(\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{-1/4}|_{\phi})

\displaystyle=\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{1/4}|_{\phi}\Delta_{g}(\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{-1/4}|_{\phi}).

Let us denote

\tilde{D}=\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert^{1/4}|_{\phi}

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\Delta_{g}D}{D}-\frac{\Delta_{g}\tilde{D}}{\tilde{D}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{D}}\Delta_{g}(D-\tilde{D})+\Delta_{g}D\left(\frac{1}{D}-\frac{1}{\tilde{D}}\right).

\displaystyle\lvert\Delta_{g}(D-\tilde{D})\rvert=\lvert\tilde{D}\Delta_{g}D\rvert\lvert\frac{D-\tilde{D}}{D\tilde{D}}\rvert\leq C\lvert D-\tilde{D}\rvert

for some positive constant

and

D=\tilde{D}\ \text{ or equivalently }\ \lvert g\rvert=\lvert\tilde{g}\rvert|_{\phi}.

By the assumption

and

, we obtain

∎

3.
Boundary information

Before moving to the second linearization in this section we discuss the needed information on the boundary. As seen in the previous section, the metric
g
introduced in (
2.4
) depends on the initial solution
u_{0}
. Thus in order to have knowledge of
g
on the boundary, we need to have knowledge of
u_{0}
on the boundary.

First of all, since we know
u_{0}
and
\partial_{\nu}u_{0}
on the boundary, we know
\nabla u_{0}
on the boundary. Thus we know
g
on the boundary to zeroth order.

In Section
6
we will need that the conformal diffeomorphism
\phi
from the previous section is the identity to high order on the boundary which follows from
[
CLLT23
, Lemma 5.1]
. We only need to check that
\phi
satisfies the properties required by that Lemma. From (
2.9
) we know that
\phi
is a morphism of solutions. We also know that
\Lambda_{H}=\Lambda_{\tilde{H}}
and combining this with the fact that
g,\tilde{g}
agree on the boundary further implies that the DN maps for the first linearizations agree.

Thus in order to use
[
CLLT23
, Lemma 5.1]
we need to have that
g,\tilde{g}
agree on the boundary in all orders. This follows, if we can show that
u_{0}
and
\tilde{u}_{0}
agree on the boundary in all orders.

We know
H|_{\partial\Omega}=\tilde{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
and thus

(3.1)

\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\right)\bigg|_{\partial\Omega}=\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\right)\bigg|_{\partial\Omega}.

Now

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\nabla u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\right)=\frac{\Delta u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}-\frac{\nabla^{2}u_{0}(\nabla u_{0},\nabla u_{0})}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}

which can be divided into terms containing tangential derivatives of
u_{0}
, which we already know on
\partial\Omega
, and terms containing only normal derivatives. For the moment let us assume that the normal direction is in the direction
x_{2}
. The ones with only normal derivatives are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}_{2}u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}_{2}u_{0}\partial_{2}u_{0}\partial_{2}u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}=\partial^{2}_{2}u_{0}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}-\frac{(\partial_{2}u_{0})^{2}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}\right).

Then from (
3.1
) it follows

\frac{\partial^{2}_{2}u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\left(1-\frac{(\partial_{2}u_{0})^{2}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}_{2}\tilde{u}_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\left(1-\frac{(\partial_{2}\tilde{u}_{0})^{2}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)

and using
\nabla u_{0}=\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}
on
\partial\Omega
implies

\left(\partial^{2}_{2}u_{0}-\partial^{2}_{2}\tilde{u}_{0}\right)\left(1-\frac{(\partial_{2}u_{2})^{2}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)=0.

This is true when
\partial^{2}_{2}u_{0}=\partial^{2}_{2}\tilde{u}_{0}
since otherwise we would have
(\partial_{2}u_{0})^{2}=-1
.
Thus from
H|_{\partial\Omega}=\tilde{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
it follows
\nabla^{2}u_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=\nabla^{2}\tilde{u}_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}
.

For higher order derivatives of
u_{0}
we again only need to focus on terms with only higher order normal derivatives. Since we know
\partial_{2}H|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{2}\tilde{H}|_{\partial\Omega}
, we compute
\partial_{2}H
:

\displaystyle\partial_{2}H

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial_{2}\Delta u_{0}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle-(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{-3/2}\left(\partial^{3}_{211}u_{0}(\partial_{1}u_{0})^{2}+\partial^{3}_{212}u_{0}\partial_{1}u_{0}\partial_{2}u_{0}+\partial^{2}_{221}u_{0}\partial_{1}u_{0}\partial_{2}u_{0}+\partial^{3}_{2}u_{0}(\partial_{2}u_{0})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+R(\nabla^{2}u_{0},\nabla u_{0})

where the term
R
does not include third order derivatives. Again we are interested in the term with only
\partial_{2}
derivatives in them:

\displaystyle\partial^{3}_{2}u_{0}\left(\frac{1}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}-\frac{(\partial_{2}u_{0})^{2}}{(1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}\right).

This is similar to situation above and thus we would have
\partial^{3}_{2}u_{0}=\partial^{3}_{2}\tilde{u}_{0}
.
Hence we get that the third order derivatives of
u_{0}
and
\tilde{u}_{0}
agree on
\partial\Omega
. Proceeding inductively we obtain that
u_{0}
and
\tilde{u}_{0}
agree to high order on
\partial\Omega
.

Thus we can use
[
CLLT23
, Lemma 5.1]
to say that
\phi
is the identity on
\partial\Omega
to infinite order. This together with (
2.10
) implies that
\lambda
is equal to one on
\partial\Omega
to infinite order.

4.
Second linearization

Using the same notation as in Section
2
, we calculate the second linearization of (

). Now the second linearization is

(4.1)

\partial_{a}\big(\partial_{p_{c}p_{b}}F^{a}(\nabla u_{0})\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}+\partial_{p_{b}}F^{a}(\nabla u_{0})\partial_{b}w\big)=0,

where

\partial_{p_{c}p_{b}}F^{a}(p)

\displaystyle\partial_{p_{c}p_{b}}F^{a}(p)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{c}}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}\left(\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{1+\lvert p\rvert^{2}}p_{a}p_{b}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{1/2}}\left(\frac{2p_{a}p_{b}p_{c}}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{2}}-\frac{\delta_{ac}p_{b}+\delta_{bc}p_{a}}{1+\lvert p\rvert^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p_{c}}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}\left(\delta_{ab}-\frac{1}{1+\lvert p\rvert^{2}}p_{a}p_{b}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(1+\lvert p\rvert^{2})^{3/2}}\left(-p_{b}\left(\delta_{ac}-\frac{p_{a}p_{c}}{1+\lvert p\rvert^{2}}\right)-p_{a}\left(\delta_{bc}-\frac{p_{b}p_{c}}{1+\lvert p\rvert^{2}}\right)\right).

Using (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) gives

\displaystyle\partial_{p_{c}p_{b}}F^{a}(\nabla u_{0})=-\frac{1}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}(\partial_{a}u_{0}g^{bc}+\partial_{b}u_{0}g^{ac}+\partial_{c}u_{0}g^{ab}).

Define, again with indices up,

C^{abc}:=\partial_{p_{c}p_{b}}F^{a}(\nabla u_{0}).

Notice that
\partial_{p_{c}p_{b}}F^{a}(p)
is symmetric.

4.1.
Integral identity

Here we derive an integral identity for the second linearization. Integrating (

) against a solution

of the first linearization to obtain

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{a}\big(C^{abc}\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}+g_{0}^{ab}\partial_{b}w\big)v_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}C^{abc}\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}\partial_{a}v_{0}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}g_{0}^{ab}\partial_{b}w\partial_{a}v_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\big(C^{abc}\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}+g_{0}^{ab}\partial_{b}w\big)v_{0}\nu_{a}\,dS

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}C^{abc}\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}\partial_{a}v_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\big(C^{abc}\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}+g_{0}^{ab}\partial_{b}w\big)v_{0}\nu_{a}-wg_{0}^{ab}\partial_{a}v_{0}\nu_{b}\,dS

For the moment let us only focus on

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\frac{1}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}(\partial_{a}u_{0}g^{bc}+\partial_{b}u_{0}g^{ac}+\partial_{c}u_{0}g^{ab})\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}\partial_{a}v_{0}\,dx

Next consider two sources

as in the previous section. Now by making a change of coordinates with

\displaystyle\tilde{I}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\frac{1}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}}(\partial_{a}\tilde{u}_{0}\tilde{g}^{bc}+\partial_{b}\tilde{u}_{0}\tilde{g}^{ac}+\partial_{c}\tilde{u}_{0}\tilde{g}^{ab})\partial_{c}\tilde{v}_{1}\partial_{b}\tilde{v}_{2}\partial_{a}\tilde{v}_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle\times[D\phi^{-1}]^{c^{\prime}}_{c}[D\phi^{-1}]^{b^{\prime}}_{b}[D\phi^{-1}]^{a^{\prime}}_{a}\partial_{c^{\prime}}v_{1}\partial_{b^{\prime}}v_{2}\partial_{a^{\prime}}v_{0}J_{\phi}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\frac{\lambda J_{\phi}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}((\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0})^{a^{\prime}}g^{b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+(\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0})^{b^{\prime}}g^{a^{\prime}c^{\prime}}+(\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0})^{c^{\prime}}g^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\times\partial_{c^{\prime}}v_{1}\partial_{b^{\prime}}v_{2}\partial_{a^{\prime}}v_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\frac{\lambda J_{\phi}\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}\cdot(\nabla v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})+\nabla v_{1}g(\nabla v_{0},\nabla v_{2})+\nabla v_{2}g(\nabla v_{0},\nabla v_{1}))\,dx,

where we used (
2.8
) for

i.e.

. Let

(4.2)

Then

(4.3)

\displaystyle I-\tilde{I}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}T\cdot(\nabla v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})+\nabla v_{1}g(\nabla v_{0},\nabla v_{2})+\nabla v_{2}g(\nabla v_{0},\nabla v_{1}))\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}T\cdot\bigg(\nabla v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})+\nabla v_{1}\left(\Delta_{g}(v_{0}v_{2})-v_{2}\Delta_{g}v_{0}-v_{0}\Delta_{g}v_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\nabla v_{2}\left(\Delta_{g}(v_{1}v_{0})-v_{0}\Delta_{g}v_{1}-v_{1}\Delta_{g}v_{0}\right)\bigg)\,dV_{g}.

Now if we can show that

which further implies, using (

) and (

),

(4.4)

\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}=\nabla u_{0}.

4.2.
Showing that
\phi
is the identity

From the first linearization we know that
\lambda D\phi^{-1}\tilde{g}^{-1}|_{\phi}D\phi^{-T}=g^{-1}
or equivalently (using the explicit formula (
2.4
) for

and
\tilde{g}
)

\displaystyle\lambda D\phi^{-1}D\phi^{-T}-\frac{D\phi^{-1}(\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\otimes\nabla\tilde{u}_{0})|_{\phi}D\phi^{-T}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}=I-\frac{\nabla u_{0}\otimes\nabla u_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}.

Here we used (
2.15
). Using the above together with (

) and (

) implies

\displaystyle\lambda D\phi^{-1}D\phi^{-T}=I\quad\text{or}\quad D\phi^{-1}D\phi^{-T}=\lambda^{-1}I.

That is

is a conformal mapping in Euclidean space.
Combining this with

a Liouville typer result (see e.g.
[

, Proposition 3.3.]
) gives that

is the identity in

.

4.3.
Boundary terms

When deriving the integral identity above we encountered the following integral on the boundary

B=\int_{\partial\Omega}\big(C^{abc}\partial_{c}v_{1}\partial_{b}v_{2}+g_{0}^{ab}\partial_{b}w\big)v_{0}\nu_{a}-wg_{0}^{ab}\partial_{a}v_{0}\nu_{b}\,dS.

We know the boundary values of

and

, hence we know
\nabla u_{0}
on the boundary. Thus by the definition of

we know
g
on the boundary. This further implies that we know
C
on the boundary. Since we know that the DN map is smooth and we know the DN map related to (
1.1
), we thus know the DN map of the linearized equation. This also uses that

is known on the boundary.

Using these and that

is the identity on the boundary to high order (this follows from
[
CLLO24
, Lemma 6.1]
, see Section

)
we have that
\lambda
is equal to

on the boundary. Putting all of the above together we can conclude that

5.
Complex geometrical optics solutions

In this section, we construct
CGO solutions for the first linearized equation (
2.6
)
\nabla\cdot\left(g^{-1}\nabla v\right)=0
. As already noted in (
2.7
) it is sufficient to construct solutions to the Shrödinger equation

(\Delta_{g}+q)\mathbf{v}=0\text{ in }\Omega

and then redefine
v=\gamma^{-1/2}\mathbf{v}
.
The construction is based on
[
GT11b
,
GT11a
]
and the estimates we recall also borrows from
[
CLLO24
,
LW23
]
. We accomplish the construction on Riemannian surfaces
(\Sigma,g)
. While we do this, we explain the corresponding definitions and concepts in
\mathbb{C}
. When we apply the CGOs, we will pass to global isothermal coordinates. The reader can think that
\Sigma=\Omega
and that the metric is conformally Euclidiean
g=cI_{2\times 2}
. We extend
(\Sigma,g)
to a slightly larger Riemannian surface
(\widetilde{\Sigma},g)
, and
q
onto it so that
q\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
. (We denote the extensions of
g
and
q
using the same letters.)

Let us then recall the standard holomorphic calculus on Riemannian surfaces. The complexified cotangent bundle
\mathbb{C}T^{*}\widetilde{M}
has the splitting

\mathbb{C}T^{*}\widetilde{M}=T^{*}_{1,0}\widetilde{M}\oplus T^{*}_{0,1}\widetilde{M}

determined by the eigenspaces of the Hodge star operator
\star
. In local holomorphic coordinate
z
the space
T^{*}_{1,0}\widetilde{\Sigma}
is spanned by
dz
and
T^{*}_{0,1}\widetilde{\Sigma}
is spanned by
d\overline{z}
. The invariant definitions of
\overline{\partial}
and
\partial
operators are
\pi_{0,1}d
and
\pi_{1,0}d
respectively.
In holomorphic coordinates
z=(x,y)
these operators are

\overline{\partial}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y})\ \text{ and }\ \partial=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}).

By
[
GT11b
, Proposition 2.1]
there is a right inverse
\overline{\partial}^{-1}
for
\overline{\partial}
in the sense that

\overline{\partial}\hskip 0.5pt\hskip 0.5pt\overline{\partial}^{-1}\omega=\omega\text{ for all }\omega\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Sigma},T_{1,0}^{*}\widetilde{\Sigma})

such that
\overline{\partial}^{-1}
is bounded from
L^{p}(T_{1,0}^{*}\widetilde{\Sigma})
to
W^{1,p}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
for any
p\in(1,\infty)
. We have analogous properties for

\overline{\partial}^{*}=-\mathsf{i}\star\partial:W^{1,p}(T_{0,1}^{*}\widetilde{\Sigma})\to L^{p}(\widetilde{\Sigma}),

which is the Hermitean adjoint of
\overline{\partial}
. In holomorphic coordinates the operator
\overline{\partial}^{*}
is just
\partial
. If
\Sigma
admits global holomorphic coordinates, then in those coordinates
\overline{\partial}^{-1}
and
\overline{\partial}^{*-1}
equal

(\overline{\partial}^{-1}\mathbf{u})(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathbf{u}(w)}{z-w}\mathrm{~d}w\ \text{ and }\ ({\partial}^{-1}\mathbf{u})(z)=\int_{\mathbb{C}}\frac{\mathbf{u}(w)}{\overline{z}-\overline{w}}\mathrm{~d}w

with the understanding that the function
\mathbf{u}\in L^{p}(\Sigma)
in the coordinates are continued onto
\mathbb{C}
in
L^{p}
(and similarly if
\mathbf{u}
has higher regularity). We also define

\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}:=\mathcal{R}\overline{\partial}^{-1}e^{-2i\psi/h}\mathcal{E}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}:=\mathcal{R}\overline{\partial}^{*-1}e^{2i\psi/h}\mathcal{E},

where
\mathcal{E}:W^{l,p}(\Sigma)\to W_{c}^{l,p}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
is an extension operator from functions on
\Sigma
to functions on the extended surface
\widetilde{\Sigma}
and
\mathcal{R}
is the restriction operator back to
\Sigma
. By
[
GT11b
, Lemma 2.2 and Lemma 2.3]
we have for
p>2
and
2\leq q\leq p
the following estimate

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}f\rVert_{L^{q}(M)}&\leq Ch^{1/q}\lVert f\rVert_{W^{1,p}(M,T_{0,1}^{*}M)}\end{split}

Moreover, there is
\epsilon>0
such that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}f\rVert_{L^{2}(M)}&\leq Ch^{1/2+\epsilon}\lVert f\rVert_{W^{1,p}(M,T_{0,1}^{*}M)}\end{split}

We have similar estimates for
\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}f
.

The CGOs on a Riemannian surface
(\Sigma,g)
for the equation
(\Delta_{g}+q)\mathbf{v}=0
have the form

(5.3)

\mathbf{v}=e^{\Phi/h}(a+r_{h}).

Here
\Phi

is holomorphic function, which is Morse (i.e. Hessian at possible critical points of
\Phi
is invertible). Here also
a
is a holomorphic function and
r=r_{h}
is a correction term. The precise form of
r_{h}
is

(5.4)

r_{h}=-\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}T_{h}^{j}\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}(qa),

where the operators
\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}
and
\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}
were defined above and
T_{h}
is defined as

(5.5)

\displaystyle T_{h}:=-\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}q\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}.

We also denote

(5.6)

s_{h}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}T_{h}^{j}\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}(qa)

so that
r_{h}=-\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}s_{h}
.

By taking complex conjugate and changing the sign of the phase function, we also have CGO solutions with antiholomorphic phase of the form

(5.7)

\mathbf{v}=e^{-\overline{\Phi}/h}(b+\tilde{r}_{h}),

where
\overline{\Phi}
is antiholomorphic Morse function,
b
antiholomorphic and
\tilde{r}_{h}
given by:

(5.8)

\tilde{r}_{h}=-\partial_{\psi}^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\tilde{T}_{h}^{j}(\partial_{\psi}^{*-1}(qa))=-\partial_{\psi}^{-1}\tilde{s}_{h}.

Let us then recall estimates from
[
GT11b
,
GT11a
,
CLLO24
,
LW23
]
. If
\Phi
has a critical point, then

(5.9)

||r_{h}||_{L^{p}},||s_{h}||_{L^{p}},||\nabla r_{h}||_{L^{p}}=O(h^{1/p+\epsilon}),

for any
p\in[2,\infty)
and
0<\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{p}
depending on
p
. Moreover, we have

(5.10)

\displaystyle\lVert T_{h}\rVert_{L^{r}\to L^{r}}=O(h^{1/r})\text{ and }\lVert T_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}=O(h^{1/2-\epsilon}),

for any
0<\epsilon<1/2
and
r>2
.
See
[
CLLO24
, Section 4.1]
for the above. If

f\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
vanishes to order
1
on
\partial\Sigma
and
\hat{\psi}
is the imaginary part of holomorphic Morse function on
\Sigma
, then

(5.11)

\int_{\Sigma}e^{i\hat{\psi}/h}fr_{h}=-\int_{\Sigma}e^{i\hat{\psi}/h}f\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}s_{h}=\int_{\Sigma}\overline{\partial}^{-1}(e^{i\hat{\psi}/h}f)e^{-2i\psi/h}s_{h}=O(h^{1+\epsilon})

by (
5.2
).
Here
\overline{\partial}^{-1}(e^{i\hat{\psi}/h}f)
means the Cauchy-Riemann operator
\overline{\partial}^{-1}
applied to the zero extension of
f
onto
\widetilde{\Sigma}
, which is
C^{1}(\widetilde{\Sigma})\subset W^{1,p}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
for all
p>1
. The same estimate holds when
r_{h}
is replaced by
\tilde{r}_{h}
.

If
\Phi
has no critical points, we have the better estimates

(5.12)

\displaystyle\|r_{h}\|_{p}+\|s_{h}\|_{p}+\|dr_{h}\|_{p}\leq Ch

for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. Also, in this case we have the expansion

(5.13)

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{-1}f=e^{-2i\psi/h}\frac{ih}{2}\frac{f}{\bar{\partial}\psi}+\frac{ih}{2}\overline{\partial}^{-1}\left(e^{-2i\psi/h}\bar{\partial}\left(\frac{f}{\bar{\partial}\psi}\right)\right),

and the
L^{2}
norm second order derivatives of
r_{h}
multiplied by any
H\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Sigma})
satisfy

(5.14)

||H\nabla^{2}r_{h}||_{L^{2}}=O(1)

see
[
CLLO24
, Section 4.1]
and
[
LW23
, Eq. (3.14)]
.

We have similar estimates for
\tilde{r}_{h}
,
\tilde{s}_{h}
and
\tilde{T}_{h}
, and expansion for
\overline{\partial}_{\psi}^{*-1}
, to the ones above.

6.
Proving that

Here we assume that

vanishes to high order on the boundary

. The integral identity (

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}v_{k}

\displaystyle=2g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{k}\right)+\left(\Delta_{g}(\gamma^{-1/2})-\gamma^{-1/2}q\right)\hat{v}_{k},

where

\displaystyle I-\tilde{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}T^{k}\Big(\partial_{k}v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})

\displaystyle+\partial_{k}v_{1}(\Delta_{g}(v_{0}v_{2})-\gamma^{-1/2}\hat{v}_{2}\Delta_{g}(\gamma^{-1/2}\hat{v}_{0})-\gamma^{-1/2}\hat{v}_{0}\Delta_{g}(\gamma^{-1/2}\hat{v}_{2}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})

\displaystyle+T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{1}\left(\Delta_{g}(v_{0}v_{2})+2\left(g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{0}\right)\hat{v}_{2}+g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{2}\right)\hat{v}_{0}\right)+2Q\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{2}\left(\Delta_{g}(v_{0}v_{1})+2\left(g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{0}\right)\hat{v}_{1}+g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{1}\right)\hat{v}_{0}\right)+2Q\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\right)\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle:=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}.

Here

.
We choose the functions
\hat{v}_{k}
as follows: Let
\Psi,\Phi,a_{k}
be holomorphic Morse functions,
k=1,2
, and
a_{0}
antiholomorphic such that
\pm\Psi+\Phi
has no critical points and
\Phi
has critical points, see Section

. Then

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\overline{\Phi}/h}(a_{0}+r_{0})

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{(-\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{2}+r_{2})

and

v_{k}=\gamma^{-1/2}\hat{v}_{k}\quad\text{ for }k=0,1,2.

At this point it is beneficial to use holomorphic coordinates around an interior point
p\in\Omega
. In these coordinates
z=x+iy
, we have
g(z)=c(z)dzd\overline{z}
where
c
is a smooth function,
dz=dx+idy
and
d\overline{z}=dx-idy
. Recall also the operators

(6.1)

\partial=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\partial}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}),

thus
\nabla_{(x,y)}=(\partial+\overline{\partial},i(\partial-\overline{\partial}))
.
In these coordinates

(6.2)

g(\nabla u,\nabla v)=2c^{-1}(\partial u\overline{\partial}v+\overline{\partial}u\partial v),\quad\Delta_{g}u=-2ic^{-1}\overline{\partial}\partial u\quad\text{and}\quad dV_{g}=c(z)\frac{i}{2}dzd\overline{z}.

Since
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
have a holomorphic phase, they act similarly in the asymptotic analysis, and thus it is enough to do analysis for
I_{2}
as the analysis for
I_{3}
is the same and in the end we get the same terms from stationary phase (see Lemmas
6.1
,
6.2
,
6.3
below). Using the above, we have for
I_{2}
after integrating by parts

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{1}\overline{\partial}\partial(v_{0}v_{2})\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+i\int_{\Omega}cT^{k}\partial_{k}v_{1}\left(g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{0}\right)\hat{v}_{2}+g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{2}\right)\hat{v}_{0}\right)\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+i\int_{\Omega}cT^{k}\partial_{k}v_{1}Q\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=I_{2,1}+I_{2,2}+I_{2,3}

and similarly for
I_{3}
. Here we make a slight abuse of notation by writing again
T^{k}
for its representation in holomorphic coordinates.

In the analysis of the above integrals we will use stationary phase arguments for terms including
T
. For this we need that
T
vanishes to high order on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. Recall that

T:=\left(\frac{\lambda J_{\phi}\phi^{*}\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\rvert^{2}|_{\phi}}-\frac{\nabla u_{0}}{1+\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}}\right)\lvert g\rvert^{-1/2}.

As explained in Section
3
we have that
\Phi
is the identity,
\lambda=1
and
u_{0}=\tilde{u}_{0}
to high order on
\partial\Omega
. Thus
T
vanishes to high order on
\partial\Omega
. We are now ready to prove the following:

Lemma 6.1
.

Define
A_{5}=2T^{k}c^{-1}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\gamma^{-1/2}
. Then

\lim_{h\to 0}hI_{2,1}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}Ci^{k-1}(A_{5}\gamma^{-1})|_{z=z_{0}}

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Integrating by parts in
I_{2,1}
we have

\displaystyle I_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\partial}\partial(T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{1})v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\partial}\partial(T^{k})\partial_{k}v_{1}v_{0}v_{2}+T^{k}\overline{\partial}\partial(\partial_{k}v_{1})v_{0}v_{2}+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\partial\partial_{k}(v_{1})v_{0}v_{2}+\partial T^{k}\overline{\partial}\partial_{k}v_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\partial}\partial(T^{k})\partial_{k}v_{1}v_{0}v_{2}+T^{k}\partial_{k}(2g\left(\nabla(\gamma^{-1/2}),\nabla\hat{v}_{1}\right)+Q\hat{v}_{1})v_{0}v_{2}

\displaystyle+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\partial\partial_{k}(v_{1})v_{0}v_{2}+\partial T^{k}\overline{\partial}\partial_{k}v_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}.

Using (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) we can group the integral
I_{2,1}
as follows:

\displaystyle I_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\overline{\partial}\partial(T^{k})\partial_{k}(\gamma^{-1/2})+T^{k}\partial_{k}Q+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\partial\partial_{k}(\gamma^{-1/2})+\partial T^{k}\overline{\partial}\partial_{k}(\gamma^{-1/2})\Big)\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}

\displaystyle+\Big(i^{k-1}\overline{\partial}\partial(T^{k})\gamma^{-1/2}+2T^{k}\partial_{k}(c^{-1})\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})+2T^{k}c^{-1}\partial_{k}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})+i^{k-1}T^{k}Q

\displaystyle+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\partial_{k}(\gamma^{-1/2})+i^{k-1}\overline{\partial}T^{k}\partial(\gamma^{-1/2})+i^{k-1}\partial T^{k}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})\Big)\partial\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}

\displaystyle+\Big((-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}\partial(T^{k})\gamma^{-1/2}+2T^{k}\partial_{k}(c^{-1})\partial(\gamma^{-1/2})+2T^{k}c^{-1}\partial_{k}\partial(\gamma^{-1/2})+(-i)^{k-1}T^{k}Q

\displaystyle+(-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}T^{k}\partial(\gamma^{-1/2})+\partial T^{k}\partial_{k}(\gamma^{-1/2})+(-i)^{k-1}\partial T^{k}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})\Big)\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}

\displaystyle+\Big(2T^{k}c^{-1}\partial(\gamma^{-1/2})+\partial T^{k}\gamma^{-1/2}\Big)\overline{\partial}\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}

\displaystyle+\Big(2T^{k}c^{-1}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\gamma^{-1/2}\Big)\partial\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}+A_{2}\partial\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}+A_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}+A_{4}\overline{\partial}\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}+A_{5}\partial\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}.

All of the terms
A_{j}
depend on the summation index
k
.

In the following we do not write the summation over
k
. Next we further analyze
I_{2,1}
term by term, starting from the term including
A_{1}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{1}e^{(2\Phi-2\Phi)/h}(a_{0}+r_{0})(a_{1}+r_{1})(a_{2}+r_{2})\,dzd\overline{z}=O(1)

as

, since the functions
\gamma,A_{1},a_{j}
are bounded in
\Omega
and by the estimates for

(see (
5.9
) and (
5.12
)). Next the term with
A_{2}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\partial\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{2}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}(a_{0}+r_{0})(a_{1}+r_{1})(a_{2}+r_{2})\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{2}e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(\partial a_{1}+\partial r_{1})\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{2}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}+O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})+O(1),

where the term

comes from the fact that all the terms include at least one
r_{j}
and the term
O(1)
similarly as for the term with
A_{1}
. The last term will be
O(1)
by stationary phase and hence

(6.3)

\int_{\Omega}A_{2}\partial\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}=O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon}).

The term with
A_{3}
is a little bit simpler since
\overline{\partial}\Phi=\overline{\partial}\Psi=\overline{\partial}a_{1}=0
by the holomorphicity of these functions. Thus

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}=\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{3}e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}\overline{\partial}r_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}=O(h)

because of the term
\overline{\partial}r_{1}
, see (
5.12
). Continuing with
A_{4}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{4}\overline{\partial}\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{4}\left(i^{k-1}\overline{\partial}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}+(-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}^{2}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\right)\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{4}\frac{i^{k-2}}{2}cq\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{4}(-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}^{2}\left(e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})\right)\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=O(1)+\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{4}(-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}^{2}\left(e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})\right)\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

and this can be seen by the same arguments as for the term with
A_{1}
. For the rest, we notice that each time the operator
\overline{\partial}
hits

we get zero since
\overline{\partial}\Phi=\overline{\partial}\Psi=0
. Also
\overline{\partial}a_{1}=0
, hence

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{4}(-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}^{2}\left(e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})\right)\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\gamma^{-1}A_{4}(-i)^{k-1}e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}\overline{\partial}^{2}r_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=O(1)

by the same arguments as before and using the Calderón-Zygmund type estimate in (
5.14
) for
\overline{\partial}^{2}r_{1}
.

The term with
A_{5}
is the most complicated of these. First of all

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\partial\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A_{5}i^{k-1}\partial^{2}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}+\int_{\Omega}A_{5}(-i)^{k-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}\partial\left(\frac{1}{h}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})+e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(\partial a_{1}+\partial r_{1})\right)\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+O(1)

by the same arguments as for the integral with
A_{4}
. Expanding further

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\partial\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}\frac{1}{h^{2}}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}\frac{1}{h}\partial^{2}(\Psi+\Phi)e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+2\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}\frac{1}{h}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(\partial a_{1}+\partial r_{1})\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(\partial^{2}a_{1}+\partial^{2}r_{1})\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}+O(1).

Focusing first on the terms with
\frac{1}{h}
, we get that they are
O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})
by stationary phase (for terms with no remainders) and that the remainders
r_{k}
satisfy the estimates (
5.9
), (
5.12
). For the ones with
\frac{1}{h^{2}}
we have three kinds of terms: one without any
r_{k}
, ones with just one
r_{k}
and ones with two or more
r_{k}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}\frac{1}{h^{2}}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(\Psi+\Phi)/h}(a_{1}+r_{1})\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\Big(a_{1}a_{0}r_{2}+a_{1}r_{0}a_{2}+a_{0}r_{1}a_{2}

\displaystyle+a_{1}r_{0}r_{2}+a_{0}r_{1}r_{2}+r_{0}r_{1}a_{2}+r_{0}r_{1}r_{2}\Big)\,dzd\overline{z}.

Because
\Psi
and
\Phi
were chosen so that
\partial(\Psi+\Phi)(z_{0})\neq 0
we can use stationary phase to conclude that the first term is

\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}=C\frac{1}{h}i^{k-1}(A_{5}\gamma^{-1})|_{z=z_{0}}+O(1)

for some constant
C>0
. For the terms with at least two
r_{k}
, we can use the estimates (
5.9
), (
5.12
) for these to conclude that they are
O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})
as
h\to 0
. We are then left with the terms that have only one
r_{k}
. Let us consider the case with
r_{0}
. The other two are exactly the same and the estimates for
r_{1},r_{2}
are better than for
r_{0}
. Now by the definition of
r_{0}
we have

(6.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}a_{1}a_{2}r_{0}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\gamma^{-1}i^{k-1}(\partial(\Psi+\Phi))^{2}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}a_{1}a_{2}\partial^{-1}_{\Imm(-2\overline{\Phi})}s_{0}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=O(h^{-1+\epsilon}).

Here we used that one can integrate by parts with the operator
\partial^{-1}_{\psi}
, see (
5.11
).
All in all we have

(6.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{5}\partial\partial_{k}\hat{v}_{1}v_{0}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}=C\frac{1}{h}i^{k-1}(A_{5}\gamma^{-1})|_{z=z_{0}}+O(h^{-1+\epsilon})

which concludes the proof.
∎

Now we move to analyze the integral

(i.e. to (

)).

Lemma 6.2
.

For
I_{2,2}
we have, using the same calculations as for
I_{2,1}
,

(6.6)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}I_{2,2}

O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})
. Then we are left with the following:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}B_{7}\partial v_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{2}\hat{v}_{0}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}B_{7}\gamma^{-1/2}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)\partial(-\Psi+\Phi)\big(a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}+a_{1}a_{2}r_{0}+a_{0}a_{2}r_{1}\big)\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle+O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon}).

For the last two terms we use the definitions of
r_{0}
and
r_{1}
, as we did in (
6.4
), and for the first one we use stationary phase to obtain for
C>0

(6.7)

\int_{\Omega}B_{7}\partial v_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{2}\hat{v}_{0}\,dzd\overline{z}=C\frac{1}{h}(B_{7}\gamma^{-1/2})|_{z=z_{0}}+O(h^{-1+\epsilon}).

This ends the proof.
∎

Next we consider the last term in
I_{2}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For
I_{2,3}
we have
\lim_{h\to 0}hI_{2,3}=0.

Proof.

For
I_{2,3}
we get the following:

\displaystyle-iI_{2,3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}i^{k-1}cT^{k}Q\partial v_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}+(-i)^{k-1}cT^{k}Q\overline{\partial}v_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}C_{1}\partial v_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}+C_{2}\overline{\partial}v_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}.

These are at most
O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})
by the same arguments as for the terms with
A_{1},A_{2}
and
A_{3}
(see before and after equation (
6.3
)).
∎

Then we return to the integral
I_{1}
, that is to

I_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})\,dV_{g}.

Lemma 6.4
.

For
I_{1}
we have
\lim_{h\to 0}hI_{1}=0.

Proof.

We immediately use (
6.2
) to have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\Omega}T^{k}\partial_{k}v_{0}g(\nabla v_{1},\nabla v_{2})\,dV_{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}i\int_{\Omega}T^{k}\left(i^{k-1}\partial v_{0}+(-i)^{k-1}\overline{\partial}v_{0}\right)\left(\partial v_{1}\overline{\partial}v_{2}+\overline{\partial}v_{1}\partial v_{2}\right)\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}i\int_{\Omega}D_{1}\partial v_{0}\partial v_{1}\overline{\partial}v_{2}+D_{2}\partial v_{0}\overline{\partial}v_{1}\partial v_{2}+D_{3}\overline{\partial}v_{0}\partial v_{1}\overline{\partial}v_{2}+D_{4}\overline{\partial}v_{0}\overline{\partial}v_{1}\partial v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}.

Here all
D_{j}
again depend on the summation index
k
.

Starting with the
D_{1}
term we expand further (and as before we do not write the summation):

(6.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}D_{1}\partial v_{0}\partial v_{1}\overline{\partial}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle+\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}.

Here we have abused notation using
\tilde{D}_{1}
for all the terms that do not depend on
h
. The first six terms are similar to
A_{1},A_{3},A_{2},B_{5},B_{4}
and
B_{3}
, in that order, and they are
O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})
. For the first remaining term we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\overline{\Phi}e^{-2\overline{\Phi}/h}(a_{0}+r_{0})\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{1}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\partial r_{0}(a_{1}+r_{1})(a_{2}+r_{2})\,dzd\overline{z}.

The first term is
O(1)
by stationary phase and the fact that
\partial\overline{\Phi}(z_{0})=0
and the second term is
O(h^{1/2+\epsilon})
because of the term
\partial r_{0}
(see (
5.9
)). For the last term in (
6.8
) we use that
\partial a_{0}=\overline{\partial}a_{2}=\overline{\partial}(-\Psi+\Phi)=0=\overline{\partial}\overline{\Phi}(z_{0})
and stationary phase to have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{1}\partial\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\partial r_{0}\left(\frac{1}{h}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)(a_{1}+r_{1})+\partial a_{1}+\partial r_{1}\right)\partial r_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=O(h^{1/2+\epsilon}).

In the last equality we used the estimates for
\partial r_{0},\partial r_{2}
(see (
5.9
), (
5.12
)).

The term with
D_{2}
goes similarly as above with the term
D_{1}
because
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
have holomorphic phases.

Moving to the term with
D_{3}
we have (using similar notation as with
D_{1}
)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}D_{3}\overline{\partial}v_{0}\partial v_{1}\overline{\partial}v_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{3}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{3}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{3}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle+\tilde{D}_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}+\tilde{D}_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}.

Again the first six terms are similar to ones before. These are
O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})
by the arguments made for
A_{1},A_{3},A_{2},B_{5},B_{6}
and
B_{1}
. The first remaining term is

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{0}\hat{v}_{1}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\left(\left(-\frac{2}{h}\overline{\partial}\overline{\Phi}(a_{0}+r_{0})+\overline{\partial}a_{0}+\overline{\partial}r_{0}\right)(a_{1}+r_{1})(a_{2}+r_{2})\right)\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=O(1)

by stationary phase and the estimates for
r_{k}
(see (
5.9
), (
5.12
)). The last remaining term is, by using again that the functions
a_{2},\Phi
and
\Psi
are holomorphic,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\left(-\frac{2}{h}\overline{\partial}\overline{\Phi}(a_{0}+r_{0})+\overline{\partial}a_{0}+\overline{\partial}r_{0}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1}{h}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)(a_{1}+r_{1})+\partial a_{1}+\partial r_{1}\right)\overline{\partial}r_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\overline{\partial}\overline{\Phi}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)a_{1}a_{0}\overline{\partial}r_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}+O(h^{-1/2+\epsilon})

because of the estimates for
r_{1},r_{0}
and
\overline{\partial}r_{2}
(see (
5.9
), (
5.12
)). For the last term, we recall that (see (
5.4
) - (
5.6
))

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\partial}^{-1}_{\Imm(-\Psi+\Phi)}s_{2}=\overline{\partial}^{-1}e^{i\Imm(-\Psi+\Phi)/h}s_{2}

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=-\overline{\partial}_{\Imm(-\Psi+\Phi)}^{*-1}(qa)-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}T_{2}^{j}\overline{\partial}_{\Imm(-\Psi+\Phi)}^{*-1}(qa)

and thus in holomorphic coordinates, denoting
\eta=\Imm(-\Psi+\Phi)
,

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}r_{2}

\displaystyle=-e^{i\eta/h}s_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{i\eta/h}\partial^{-1}(e^{i\eta/h}qa)+e^{i\eta/h}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}T_{2}^{j}\partial^{-1}(e^{i\eta/h}qa)

\displaystyle=e^{i\eta/h}\left(\frac{ih}{2}e^{i\eta/h}\frac{qa}{\partial\eta}+\frac{ih}{2}\partial^{-1}\left(e^{i\eta/h}\partial\left(\frac{qa}{\partial\eta}\right)\right)\right)+e^{i\eta/h}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}T_{2}^{j}\partial^{-1}(e^{\eta/h}qa).

Here we used the expansion (
5.13
).
Now

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{h^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}e^{(2\Phi-2\overline{\Phi})/h}\overline{\partial}\overline{\Phi}\partial(\Psi+\Phi)a_{1}a_{0}\frac{ih}{2}e^{2i\eta/h}\frac{qa}{\partial\eta}\,dzd\overline{z}=O(1)

by stationary phase.
Recall that

\partial^{-1}\left(e^{i\eta/h}\partial\left(\frac{qa}{\partial\eta}\right)\right)=O(h^{1/2+\epsilon}),\quad\partial^{-1}(e^{i\eta/h}qa)=O(h)

(by the estimate (
5.2
) and the expansion (
5.13
)) and
T_{h}=O(h^{1/2-\epsilon})
. Then the remaining terms in
\int_{\Omega}\tilde{D}_{3}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{0}\partial\hat{v}_{1}\overline{\partial}\hat{v}_{2}\,dzd\overline{z}
are
O(h^{3/2-\epsilon})

Again the term with
D_{4}
goes similarly as above with the term
D_{3}
because
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
have holomorphic phases.
∎

Recall the definition of
T
in (
4.2
) and that the arguments in Lemmas
6.1
,
6.2
,
6.3
can be applied to the integral
I_{3}
with the same results. Then we have the following for
T
:

Lemma 6.5
.

The components
T^{1},T^{2}
of
T
satisfy the elliptic equation

(6.9)

\partial\overline{\partial}(T^{1}+iT^{2})+E\partial(T^{1}+iT^{2})+\partial E(T^{1}+iT^{2})=0\quad\text{in }\Omega

for a smooth function
E
.

Proof.

Multiplying
I-\tilde{I}
by
h
and taking the limit as
h\to 0
we get after using Lemmas
6.1
,
6.2
,
6.3
,
6.4

\sum_{k=1}^{2}2Ci^{k-1}(A_{5}\gamma^{-1})|_{z=z_{0}}+i\tilde{C}(B_{7}\gamma^{-1/2})|_{z=z_{0}}=0.

Recall that
A_{5}=2T^{k}c^{-1}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\gamma^{-1/2}
and
B_{7}=i^{k-1}T^{k}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})
.
Doing the above argument for a dense set of points implies that
T_{1},T_{2}
satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2}i^{k-1}\overline{\partial}T^{k}+\frac{i^{k-1}}{2C}\gamma^{3/2}\left(4Cc^{-1}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})\gamma^{-1}+i\tilde{C}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})\gamma^{-1/2}\right)T^{k}=0.

Letting
E:=\frac{1}{2C}\gamma^{3/2}\left(4Cc^{-1}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})\gamma^{-1}+i\tilde{C}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})\gamma^{-1/2}\right)
we have

\displaystyle\overline{\partial}(T^{1}+iT^{2})+E(T^{1}+iT^{2})=0.

Operating with
\overline{\partial}
on both sides gives (
6.9
).
∎

Next we choose solutions
v_{k}=\gamma^{-1/2}\hat{v}_{k}
,
k=0,1,2
, such that

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=e^{2\Phi/h}(\overline{a}_{0}+r_{0})

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=e^{-(\overline{\Psi}+\overline{\Phi})/h}(\overline{a}_{1}+r_{1})

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-(-\overline{\Psi}+\overline{\Phi})/h}(\overline{a}_{2}+r_{2})

where
\Phi,\Psi,a_{k}
,
k=0,1,2
, are as before. Then we look at the integral
I-\tilde{I}
with these solutions. The analysis is in most parts the same. The differences come from the terms
I_{2,1}
and
I_{2,2}
(and of course
I_{3,1}
and
I_{3,2}
but they are identical to
I_{2,1}
and
I_{2,2}
). With the solutions above we get

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}hI_{2,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}C(-i)^{k-1}(A_{4}\gamma^{-1})|_{z=z_{0}}

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}hI_{2,2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2}i\tilde{C}(B_{6}\gamma^{-1/2})|_{z=z_{0}},

where

\displaystyle A_{4}=\Big(2T^{k}c^{-1}\overline{\partial}(\gamma^{-1/2})+\overline{\partial}T^{k}\gamma^{-1/2}\Big),\quad B_{6}=(-i)^{k-1}T^{k}\partial(\gamma^{-1/2}).

Then, similarly as for Lemma
6.5
we have the following:

Lemma 6.6
.

The components
T^{1},T^{2}
of
T
satisfy the elliptic equation

\partial\overline{\partial}(T^{1}-iT^{2})+\tilde{E}\partial(T^{1}-iT^{2})+\partial\tilde{E}(T^{1}-iT^{2})=0\quad\text{in }\Omega

for a smooth function
\tilde{E}
.

Since we assume that
T
is known on the boundary to high order, we get by combining Lemma
6.5
and Lemma
6.6
with unique continuation that
T^{1}=T^{2}=0
.
