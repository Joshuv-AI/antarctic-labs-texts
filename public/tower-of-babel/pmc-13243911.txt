Non-Thermal Plasma Technologies for Plant Virus Inactivation: Sources, Mechanisms, and Practical Applications
Authors: Mi-Ri Park, Min-Jae Kim, Sang-Yun Cho, Rae-Dong Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant virus diseases reduce crop yield and quality worldwide; moreover, curative options once systemic infection is established remain scarce. Non-thermal plasma (NTP; cold atmospheric plasma) is a residue-free, near-ambient technology that generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) with strong virucidal activity. This review summarizes major sources of NTP, including dielectric barrier discharges, plasma jets, and corona/streamer discharges; plasma chemistry in gas and liquid phases; molecular mechanisms of virus inactivation, including capsid protein oxidation/denaturation, limited lipid peroxidation in enveloped plant viruses, and genome damage that suppresses replication. Evidence from plant virus studies indicates that highly stable viruses lose infectivity and that waterborne inactivation is efficient, supporting the use of NTP and plasma-activated water as practical tools for sanitizing irrigation water, recirculating hydroponic solutions, and contact surfaces. Emerging greenhouse studies have suggested that plasma-generated oxidants, including ozone, can curb water-mediated dissemination. Hence, we compare NTP with thermotherapy, irradiation, and chemical antiviral approaches, and discuss safety, scalability, and economic considerations. Key priorities for translation include standardized dose and chemistry metrics, infectivity-based endpoints in realistic matrices, scalable reactors that ensure uniform treatment at high throughput, and multi-site validation under commercial production conditions.Keywords: cold atmospheric plasma, plasma-activated water, reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, plant virus sanitation

Plant viruses are among the most consequential biological constraints on crop productivity and quality, undermining global food and fiber systems and amplifying vulnerability in both intensive and subsistence agriculture. Moreover, the impact of plant viruses is magnified by international trade in seed and planting materials, the expansion of protected cultivation, and climate-driven changes in host and vector distributions, all of which increase the probability of emerging outbreaks and large-scale epidemics (Jones and Naidu, 2019; Jones, 2021). Recent syntheses further emphasize that major agriculturally important viruses continue to cause substantial losses and remain a persistent challenge for sustainable virus disease management (Scholthof et al., 2011; Tatineni and Hein, 2023). Unlike many fungal or bacterial diseases, viral infections in plants are generally incurable once systemic infection is established; therefore, management relies on prevention, early diagnosis, exclusion and eradication, vector suppression, and the deployment of resistant cultivars (Tatineni and Hein, 2023). Additionally, the remarkable genetic diversity of plant viruses, frequency of mixed infections, and rapid evolutionary change complicate durable control and can lead to unexpected disease phenotypes and yield penalties (Jones and Naidu, 2019; Scholthof et al., 2011).

Current antiviral and sanitation strategies, such as thermotherapy and meristem culture for producing virus-free planting stocks, chemotherapy in tissue culture, and disinfection of contact surfaces and tools, can be effective but are often labor-intensive, crop-specific, or constrained by phytotoxicity, regulatory constraints, and limited scalability (Panattoni et al., 2013; Tatineni and Hein, 2023). These limitations motivate the search for rapid, residue-free physical approaches that can complement integrated plant virus management, especially in production systems where hygiene and water reuse are critical. Physical inactivation approaches generally operate either by directly damaging viral macromolecules and/or through indirect oxidative pathways mediated by reactive species (Fig. 1).

This schematic summarizes representative physical control strategies used to suppress plant viruses, including ionizing radiation (e.g., gamma rays), electromagnetic/ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, and other energy-based treatments that act through direct and indirect pathways. Direct effects include structural disruption and oxidation/denaturation of viral proteins (capsid components) and damage to viral genomes (RNA/DNA), leading to loss of infectivity. Indirect effects are mediated by the generation of reactive species, particularly reactive oxygen species (ROS), which further promote oxidative degradation of viral macromolecules and accelerate virion disassembly. Together, these mechanisms converge to inactivate the virus by impairing particle integrity and replication competence.

Non-thermal plasma (NTP) has emerged as a promising platform for virus inactivation because NTP operates at near-ambient temperatures and generates reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) that oxidize viral proteins and damage nucleic acids (Filipić et al., 2020). Proof-of-concept studies have demonstrated a loss of infectivity in highly stable tobamoviruses, such as tobacco mosaic virus (TMV), following atmospheric-pressure plasma irradiation (Hanbal et al., 2018). Subsequent studies extended cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) inactivation to waterborne plant viruses and operationally relevant matrices, including potato virus Y (PVY) in irrigation or nutrient solutions and pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) in water, supporting the potential of plasma-enabled sanitation for recirculating systems (Filipić et al., 2019, 2021). Recent greenhouse-scale investigations have further indicated that plasma-generated oxidants (e.g., ozone) may help interrupt water-mediated transmission of tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV) in hydroponic production (Zhou et al., 2024).

Thus, this review summarizes NTP source types and operating principles, the reactive chemistry most relevant to plant virus inactivation, and the molecular mechanisms underlying virion and genome damage. We then synthesize evidence for agricultural applications (water and solution disinfection, propagation and seed sanitation, and greenhouse applications), discuss safety, scalability, and economic considerations, and highlight research gaps that must be addressed to translate plasma-based antiviral strategies into routine plant protection practice.

Principle of Non-Thermal Plasma Technology

Types of non-thermal plasma sources

NTP, when used in antiviral and sanitation applications, is most commonly generated at atmospheric pressure using electrical discharges. Moreover, the selected plasma source strongly determines discharge uniformity, spatial reach, gas temperature, and the composition and flux of reactive oxygen and RONS, which, in turn, govern treatment efficacy and material compatibility (Filipić et al., 2020; Rajakumar et al., 2024; Tendero et al., 2006). For agricultural use, these source-dependent properties are particularly important because targets range from flat surfaces (tools and packaging) to porous materials (seed coats) and aqueous matrices (irrigation and hydroponic solutions). Atmospheric-pressure NTP used in agricultural and biological applications is commonly generated using dielectric barrier discharges (DBDs), plasma jets, corona/streamer discharges, and gliding arc discharges (Fig. 2, Table 1).

These platforms generate reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) and, depending on the configuration, UV photons and charged particles that collectively damage virions and reduce infectivity, thereby providing practical routes for disinfecting seeds, contact surfaces, and irrigation/hydroponic systems.

DBDs generate plasma between electrodes separated by one or more dielectric layers, thereby suppressing thermal arcing and enabling stable non-equilibrium operation in air or in controlled gas mixtures (Kogelschatz, 2003). DBD configurations include volume DBD, surface DBD, and packed-bed or microdischarge arrays, providing engineering flexibility for treating large areas and irregular surfaces. DBD-type and related atmospheric discharges have been used in plant virus-relevant studies and are well suited to surface sanitation and to producing plasma-activated liquids with virucidal activity (Hanbal et al., 2018; Filipić et al., 2019, 2021).

Atmospheric-pressure plasma jets (APPJs) produce plasma inside a nozzle or tube and deliver a reactive effluent (afterglow) to the target with minimal thermal load (Schutze et al., 1998; Tendero et al., 2006). APPJs offer high controllability of dose through gas composition, power, and exposure geometry, enabling localized treatment of complex surfaces and efficient generation of plasma-activated water for indirect application in agricultural systems (Filipić et al., 2020).

Intense electric fields near sharp electrodes in ambient air typically generate corona and streamer discharges. These sources can efficiently generate ozone and nitrogen oxides, which exhibit strong antimicrobial and potentially virucidal activity; however, the inherent non-uniformity and oxidant byproducts of these sources necessitate careful process control and ventilation in practical settings (Nijdam et al., 2012; Pekárek, 2003).

Gliding arc and microwave/surface-wave plasma sources can deliver high reactive fluxes at high throughput; however, these sources may operate at higher gas temperatures than DBDs or APPJs, which can limit direct use on living plant tissues. Consequently, these sources are more often considered for water treatment or decontamination tasks where thermal constraints are less restrictive, and for hybrid processes that separate plasma generation from the treated biological material (Fridman et al., 1999; Shimizu et al., 2010).

Characteristics of non-thermal plasma that influencing plant virus inactivation

NTP, often referred to as cold atmospheric plasma, is a nonequilibrium ionized gas in which energetic electrons drive chemistry while the bulk gas temperature remains close to ambient, enabling treatment of heat–sensitive biological materials and plant-derived commodities. NTP contains a complex mixture of charged particles (electrons and ions), neutral radicals, excited atoms/molecules, transient electric fields, and ultraviolet (UV)/visible emission; the composition and reactivity of NTP depend strongly on the plasma source (e.g., dielectric barrier discharge or plasma jet), the working gas (air, N2, O2, Ar, He), humidity, and the treated matrix (Laroussi et al., 2017; Nijdam et al., 2012; von Woedtke et al., 2022).

A defining characteristic relevant to antiviral action is the efficient generation of reactive oxygen and RONS. Short-lived primary species formed in the discharge (e.g., O, •OH, O2(1Δg), NO, and electronically excited N2/O2) rapidly react in the plasma–air–surface boundary layer and in plasma-treated liquids to yield longer-lived secondary products such as ozone (O3), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), nitrite (NO2−), and nitrate (NO3−). These long-lived species can act as reservoirs that sustain oxidative/nitrosative chemistry after exposure, while also seeding the formation of secondary short-lived radicals (e.g., •OH, ONOO−) in aqueous environments (Gorbanev et al., 2018; Jablonowski et al., 2018; Rezaei et al., 2019).

For plant virus inactivation, the relative contributions of these effectors depend on the virus (enveloped vs. non-enveloped), the carrier matrix (water, seed coat, tool/surface, plant tissue), and the treatment configuration. Across many systems, oxidative and nitrosative processes mediated by RONS are consistently implicated as dominant drivers of loss of infectivity through capsid protein modification and genome damage, and the antiviral activity can persist when plasma-activated water is applied as a secondary treatment (Filipić et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2018).

In addition to direct virucidal activity, plasma-activated water (PAW) has been reported to modulate plant redox signaling and defense pathways, including altered levels of salicylic acid/jasmonic acid and induction of pathogenesis-related genes, suggesting potential utility as a priming input within integrated disease management—provided that dose windows are defined to avoid oxidative stress or phytotoxicity (Adhikari et al., 2019, 2020; Zambon et al., 2020).

Mechanism of Virus Inactivation by Non-Thermal Plasma

Direct mechanistic evidence in plant-virus systems is still relatively limited and currently comes from only a small number of experimentally accessible pathosystems. Even so, the available studies point to three recurring outcomes following NTP treatment: disruption of TMV particles accompanied by RNA degradation and loss of infectivity under atmospheric-pressure plasma exposure; infectivity loss in PMMoV together with particle damage and genome injury during waterborne CAP treatment; and strong suppression of infectivity in aqueous PVY and ToBRFV systems, although the precise sequence of molecular damage has not yet been fully resolved in every case (Guo et al., 2018; Hanbal et al., 2018; Filipić et al., 2019, 2020, 2021). At the same time, important mechanistic questions remain open in plant viruses, including the relative contributions of specific reactive species, UV photons, and charged particles, as well as whether capsid damage precedes genome injury or occurs in parallel. Comparative susceptibility across plant virus families

Systematic comparisons of NTP susceptibility across virus families remain limited; however, available evidence suggests that highly stable, non-enveloped viruses—particularly tobamoviruses—represent the most resistant targets and, therefore, serve as conservative “worst-case” surrogates for sanitation validation. Indeed, tobamoviruses, such as TMV, PMMoV, and ToBRFV, possess rigid rod-shaped capsids with extreme environmental stability (surviving for years on surfaces and in soil). Nonetheless, atmospheric-pressure and cold plasma treatments have been shown to inactivate these viruses over time in both surface and aqueous matrices (Filipić et al., 2021; Hanbal et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2024). This observation is mechanistically consistent with the hypothesis that RONS-mediated capsid oxidation can destabilize even highly robust protein assemblies, thereby enabling subsequent genome access and degradation.

In contrast, limited data exist for other non-enveloped plant virus families (e.g., potyviruses, ilarviruses, comoviruses) and for the small subset of enveloped plant viruses (orthotospoviruses, rhabdoviruses). Potyviruses (e.g., PVY) appear similarly susceptible to plasma treatment in water matrices (Filipić et al., 2019), although direct head-to-head comparisons under standardized plasma conditions remain lacking. For the small subset of enveloped plant viruses, an additional susceptibility to plasma-induced lipid peroxidation may be plausible in principle because membrane lipids provide a potential oxidative target beyond capsid proteins and the viral genome. However, this remains an extrapolation from plasma studies on non-plant enveloped viruses and has not yet been directly demonstrated in orthotospoviruses or plant rhabdoviruses. Therefore, research should prioritize controlled comparative studies that challenge multiple virus types (varying capsid architecture, genome type, and envelope presence/absence) under identical plasma exposure conditions, coupled with structural and infectivity endpoints, to enable evidence-based risk stratification and process design.

Lipid peroxidation in viral envelopes

Although most plant viruses are non-enveloped, a small number of plant-infecting taxa, such as orthotospoviruses and plant rhabdoviruses, possess a host-derived lipid envelope containing viral glycoproteins that are important for entry and vector–host interactions (Bejerman et al., 2020; Buchmann et al., 2015). Drawing on plasma inactivation studies of enveloped viruses outside the plant-virus field, it is reasonable to propose that oxidative damage to envelope lipids could lead to membrane disruption, increased permeability, and loss of envelope-associated functions. However, this possibility has not yet been directly demonstrated in plant-virus NTP studies and should therefore be considered a mechanistic inference rather than an experimentally established outcome in enveloped plant viruses (Filipić et al., 2020).

Protein oxidation and capsid denaturation

In non-enveloped plant viruses, capsid proteins are likely to serve as the main extracellular targets of NTP-derived reactive oxygen and RONS, rather than lipid membranes. One plausible route of inactivation is that oxidative modification of capsid proteins weakens virion stability, increases particle permeability, and thereby makes the viral genome more vulnerable to subsequent damage in plasma-treated liquids. Susceptibility may also differ according to particle architecture and genome type, although these relationships have not yet been clearly resolved in plant-virus systems and require further comparative study. At the molecular level, RONS can oxidize susceptible amino acid residues such as Cys, Met, Trp, and Tyr, while also promoting carbonylation, nitration, and cross-linking reactions that disrupt capsid structure and may impair key processes such as attachment or uncoating. Consistent with this view, studies using model viruses have linked singlet oxygen-driven oxidation with concurrent capsid and genome injury and substantial losses of infectivity (Aboubakr et al., 2015; Guo et al., 2018). Consistent with these mechanisms, atmospheric-pressure plasma irradiation induced time-dependent fragmentation of TMV particles, as observed by electron microscopy, indicating disruption of higher-order capsid structure even when coat-protein subunits remained detectable (Hanbal et al., 2018).

Plant virus capsids exhibit remarkable structural diversity, ranging from rigid helical rods (tobamoviruses) to quasi-equivalent icosahedra (comoviruses, sobemoviruses) and bacilliform particles (rhabdoviruses), which may confer differential susceptibility to plasma-derived RONS through variations in amino acid exposure, inter-subunit contacts, and genome accessibility. Capsid proteins rich in solvent accessible Cys, Met, Trp, and Tyr are expected to be preferentially oxidized by •OH, 1O2, and ONOO−. Meanwhile, tobamoviruses (TMV, PMMoV, ToBRFV) have compact helical assemblies with deeply buried genomes. Nonetheless, atmospheric-pressure plasma rapidly fragments TMV particles and degrades viral RNA (Hanbal et al., 2018), suggesting that even highly ordered, stable capsids can be destabilized through cumulative oxidative lesions at subunit interfaces or at regions of structural flexibility (e.g., interhelical loops). Icosahedral viruses stabilized by extensive inter-subunit salt bridges and hydrophobic cores (e.g., comoviruses) may resist initial oxidative insult; however, once a critical threshold of subunit damage is reached, cooperative disassembly can be rapid. In contrast, filamentous viruses may lose infectivity through localized breaches in the capsid that expose internal genome segments without complete particle disintegration. Viruses with loosely packed genomes or natural “breathing” dynamics (transient capsid opening) may facilitate faster penetration of RONS into nucleic acids. Single-stranded RNA viruses, which constitute most plant viruses, have genomes that are more susceptible to oxidative base modification and strand breaks than those of double-stranded DNA or RNA viruses, potentially reducing the plasma dose required for replication-incompetent lesions.

Nucleic acid damage and inhibition of viral replication

Genome damage is frequently a decisive lesion in NTP-mediated virus inactivation. Oxidants generated during plasma exposure or delivered via plasma-activated water can access viral nucleic acids directly (after capsid weakening) or indirectly (through diffusion of reactive species through capsid channels), causing strand breaks and oxidative base modifications. Meanwhile, genomic RNA degradation occurs rapidly under atmospheric plasma irradiation (partial within 0.5–1 min and complete within 5–10 min) in the TMV, coinciding with the disappearance of infectivity in bioassays (Hanbal et al., 2018). In water matrices relevant to irrigation, cold atmospheric plasma has also been shown to reduce infectivity of PVY and PMMoV, thereby supporting a mechanism in which RONS-driven genome injury complements capsid destabilization (Filipić et al., 2019, 2020, 2021).

Matrix effects and reactive species transport in aqueous systems

The efficacy of plasma-mediated virus inactivation in water is strongly modulated by matrix composition, which governs RONS generation, stability, and transport to viral targets. Gorbanev et al. (2018) established critical analytical protocols demonstrating that the composition of short-lived reactive species (e.g., •OH, 1O2) in plasma–air–water systems depends on sampling method, quenching kinetics, and interfacial mass transfer, and cautioned against overinterpreting bulk measurements that may miss transient boundary-layer chemistry.

Organic matter (proteins, phenolics, humic substances) competes with virions for oxidants, thereby increasing the dose required to achieve an equivalent log reduction. Filipić et al. (2019) observed that PVY inactivation in infected plant homogenate required ≥5 min plasma exposure, whereas the purified virus in a nutrient solution was completely inactivated within ~1 min, demonstrating a >5-fold matrix penalty. Additionally, pH shifts were found to redistribute reactive nitrogen species between HNO2/NO2− and ONOO−/ONOOH, changing both stability and reactivity toward viral proteins and genomes (Gorbanev et al., 2018; Rezaei et al., 2019). Acidic pH generally favors peroxynitrite formation and may enhance virucidal activity, but can also accelerate H2O2 decomposition. Transition metal ions (Fe2+, Cu2+) can catalyze Fenton-like reactions that amplify •OH generation from H2O2, although excessive metal concentrations may also promote unproductive radical scavenging or precipitate as oxides (Jablonowski et al., 2018). High ionic strength or multivalent cations can induce viral aggregation, shielding internal particles from oxidants while potentially concentrating surface-exposed virions, thereby enhancing inactivation kinetics (context-dependent).

Comparison with Other Antiviral Techniques

Plant virus management has traditionally relied on various physical and chemical treatments, including heat, ionizing radiation, and different antiviral chemicals. While these methods have been effective to some extent, limitations also exist with respect to scalability, environmental impact, and potential phytotoxicity. NTP has emerged as an alternative antiviral technique that offers several advantages over conventional treatment (Table 2).

Heat treatment (thermotherapy)

Thermotherapy remains a cornerstone of plant virus sanitation, particularly for vegetatively propagated crops, and is typically implemented in vivo or in vitro by exposing infected material to sublethal elevated temperatures (typically ~35–40°C) for days to weeks, frequently followed by shoot tip/meristem culture, micrografting, or chemotherapy. Heat suppresses viral replication and cell-to-cell movement and can shift viral distribution away from the apical dome, thereby increasing the probability of recovering virus‐free regenerants rather than relying solely on direct thermal denaturation of virions. Although broadly effective across many virus–host systems, thermotherapy is constrained by host genotype-specific heat tolerance, variable eradication efficiency for deeply systemic infections, and the need for prolonged, labor-intensive handling in vitro (Maliogka et al., 2015; Panattoni et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2018). In contrast, NTP can deliver rapid virucidal action at near-ambient temperatures and may be used as a surface or aqueous treatment; however, the performance of NTP must be calibrated to avoid oxidative injury to sensitive tissues and to ensure uniform dose delivery.

Radiation-based methods (Gamma irradiation and UV treatment)

Radiation-based approaches target viral genomes and structural proteins via direct energy deposition and, in hydrated matrices, indirect oxidative chemistry. Gamma irradiation (ionizing) can inactivate plant viruses in a dose-dependent manner, with reports of complete loss of TMV infectivity at high doses accompanied by virion disruption and protein degradation (Jeong and Choi, 2017), and reduced infectivity of cucumber green mottle mosaic virus following irradiation of inocula (Hong et al., 2017). However, the high doses often required for robust inactivation may compromise plant viability, and implementation typically requires licensed facilities and strict dosimetry and safety controls, increasing capital and operating costs (Zimek et al., 2021). UV-C (non-ionizing) is commonly used for surface decontamination by inducing pyrimidine dimers in viral nucleic acids; however, efficacy is strongly limited by poor penetration depth, shadowing, and the optical properties of plant tissues and substrates (Tchonkouang et al., 2023). Compared with these methods, NTP provides a chemically rich, low-temperature alternative that can be engineered for short treatment times; however, standardization of dose metrics and process validation remains essential to ensure reproducible outcomes.

Chemical antiviral treatment

Chemical interventions fall into two main categories: (i) antiviral chemotherapy used during in vitro sanitation and (ii) defense elicitors that enhance host immunity. The nucleoside analog ribavirin and plant-derived compounds are widely used in tissue culture to suppress replication of a broad range of RNA viruses and is often combined with thermotherapy or meristem culture to improve eradication frequency (Kang et al., 2025; Kidulile et al., 2018; Panattoni et al., 2013). In parallel, salicylic acid (SA)-dependent signaling and synthetic functional analogs such as acibenzolar-S-methyl/benzothiadiazole (ASM/BTH) can prime systemic acquired resistance and reduce symptom development or virus accumulation in some pathosystems (Conrath, 2006; Klessig et al., 2018; Tripathi et al., 2019), including BTH-induced resistance reported against the cucumber mosaic virus in tomato (Anfoka, 2000). Despite the utility of these chemical approaches, cultivar-dependent efficacy, phytotoxicity under sanitation conditions, incomplete virus clearance for systemic infections, and regulatory or residue considerations for commercial deployment may remain. Meanwhile, compared with chemical treatments, NTP can achieve virucidal activity without introducing persistent synthetic residues, although NTP similarly requires optimization to balance antiviral efficacy with plant safety and process economics.

Overall, NTP occupies a distinct niche among antiviral technologies by combining low-temperature operation with the in situ generation of RONS, UV photons, and transient electric fields. This multi-modal stress can rapidly compromise viral capsids and genomes (e.g., tobamoviruses) and has been demonstrated across several model plant viruses and matrices, including the TMV and PMMoV in aqueous systems and on surfaces (Filipić et al., 2020, 2021; Hanbal et al., 2018; Milusheva et al., 2020). Compared with thermotherapy, irradiation, or chemotherapy, NTP treatments can be shorter and potentially more compatible with heat-sensitive materials; however, translation beyond laboratory demonstrations requires robust dose–response datasets, standardized reporting of plasma chemistry, and scalable reactor designs that ensure uniform exposure on complex agricultural substrates (Filipić et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024).

Applications of Non-Thermal Plasma in Agriculture

NTP has demonstrated significant promise as an innovative tool for managing plant viruses in diverse agricultural settings. Indeed, the broad-spectrum antiviral activity, chemical-free nature, and ability to operate at ambient temperature make NTP particularly attractive for applications ranging from seed disinfection to postharvest treatment and potential field deployment (Table 3, Fig. 4).

Antiviral efficacy against specific plant viruses

A small but growing body of peer-reviewed studies provides direct evidence that NTP can inactivate plant viruses by rapidly impairing virion integrity and infectivity. Using TMV as a highly stable model tobamovirus, Hanbal et al. (2018) demonstrated that atmospheric-pressure plasma irradiation of purified TMV suspension reduced local lesion formation in a dose-dependent manner and abolished visible lesions after sufficient exposure, consistent with plasma-driven damage to both capsid structure and viral RNA. The study further linked the effect to reactive nitrogen/oxygen chemistry generated in the liquid during irradiation, supporting a mechanistic framework in which oxidative/nitrosative reactions destabilize virions and promote nucleic acid degradation. In addition to tobamoviruses, NTP has been validated for inactivating PVY in water matrices relevant to agricultural reuse. In closed-irrigation context, Filipić et al. (2019) reported complete loss of PVY infectivity in most treatments, with markedly shorter treatment requirements for “cleaner” matrices: ~1 min for purified virus in nutrient solution, versus a longer minimum (≥5 min) for infected homogenates containing more organic matter, thereby highlighting the practical importance of matrix effects when translating NTP protocol to real irrigation stream.

Disinfection of irrigation water and recirculating hydroponic solutions

Among plausible agricultural deployments, water/solution disinfection in recirculating irrigation and hydroponic systems currently represents the most evidence-supported and engineering-compatible application. Tobamoviruses are increasingly detected and discussed in waterborne pathways, raising concern that recirculation loops may amplify inoculum if decontamination is absent. In this setting, Filipić et al. (2019) showed that NTP can completely inactivate PMMoV in water within minutes: 5-minute NTP exposures fully eliminated infectivity across independent repeats, and 3-minute exposures achieved complete inactivation in most repeats with occasional partial survival, indicating both strong efficacy and the need for conservative operating margins. Importantly, the study directly coupled infectivity assays with TEM evidence of particle damage and molecular assays of RNA integrity, strengthening causal interpretation beyond genome copy reduction alone.

A complementary greenhouse case has been reported for ToBRFV, in which ozone generated by cold plasma was evaluated as a water treatment in a recirculating hydroponic system. Zhou et al. (2024) documented that the inactivation outcome depended on ozone concentration, exposure time, and contamination load, and concluded that the approach could be operationally relevant for preventing ToBRFV spread through recirculating systems in commercial greenhouse tomato production. Together, these studies support a practical implementation concept: NTP can be integrated as an inline or batch treatment unit for nutrient solutions/runoff, with performance targets defined by “worst-case” organic load, flow rate, and virus concentration scenarios.

Sanitation of propagation materials and in vitro production systems

In addition to water treatment, NTP is being explored for sanitizing plant propagation materials, including tissue culture systems in which virus-free stock is critical. A notable example is the application of NTP to micropropagated plum plantlets naturally co-infected by plum pox virus (PPV) strains (M and D). Milusheva et al. (2020) investigated NTP exposure regimes, including a “torch tip” approach, and reported that iterative treatments combined with subculture reduced the proportion of PPV-positive microplants, with evidence of differential strain persistence (PPV-D became undetectable, whereas PPV-M remained detectable in a subset). While this line of work remains in the early stages and requires careful evaluation of phytotoxicity and stability, the findings are significant as these data advance plasma-based demonstrations from “virus in liquid” to biologically complex living plant material—an essential step for virus-free certification pipelines.

Seed disinfection and planting material sanitation

Seedborne transmission of plant viruses represents a critical route for disease dissemination, as viruses present on or within seeds can establish systemic infections in developing seedlings, often without visible early symptoms. Consequently, the development of effective, residue-free seed disinfection strategies is essential for preventing vertical transmission of viral pathogens. NTP has emerged as a highly promising technique for this purpose, offering a non-chemical, non-thermal alternative that is both environmentally sustainable and effective (Zhou et al., 2024).

In principle, NTP is attractive for seed sanitation because NTP can be utilized at near-ambient temperatures and leaves no conventional chemical residue. However, robust, peer-reviewed demonstrations of plasma eliminating plant viruses specifically from seeds (with downstream infectivity confirmation in seedlings) remain comparatively scarce, relative to the extensive literature on plasma control of seedborne fungi/bacteria. Therefore, in a review focused on plant viruses, it is scientifically safer to present seed disinfection as a high-value target application, supported by a clear biological rationale and feasible engineering pathways, while explicitly emphasizing the need for validation trials that integrate (i) seed surface virome quantification, (ii) standardized infectivity bioassays, and (iii) assessments of germination/seedling vigor and potential seed coat damage.

Greenhouse and field applications

The application of NTP technology in greenhouse and open-field settings represents a frontier area of research with substantial potential for integrated plant virus management. While most current studies on plasma-based disinfection focus on in vitro or postharvest systems, recent advances in portable plasma devices and plasma-activated media have created new opportunities for in vivo and in situ treatments under agricultural conditions.

For greenhouse integration, the most realistic near-term pathway is indirect treatment, such as water/solution disinfection (plasma-generated ozone, or plasma-activated water), rather than direct whole-canopy plasma exposure, as the treated medium can be dosed and monitored using conventional sensors (e.g., oxidant concentration, ORP, and contact time); the treatment process can be decoupled from plant tissue sensitivity. The ToBRFV hydroponic study explicitly supports this operational framing by demonstrating parameter-dependent inactivation in water solutions relevant to greenhouse practice (Zhou et al., 2024). In open-field conditions, the main research gap is not conceptual virucidal chemistry but the engineering of scalable delivery (throughput, energy cost per m3 of water, oxidant decay kinetics, and safety compliance) coupled with realistic epidemiological endpoints (disease incidence reduction across seasons). Accordingly, future field readiness will likely depend on standardized performance reporting across studies, device types, gas compositions, power densities, contact times, matrix organic loads, and on multi-site validation that compares plasma-enabled disinfection with established sanitation baselines.

Safety, Scalability, and Economic Considerations

The utilization of NTP in crop production must be guided by dose- and context-specific safety assessments. Although NTP operates at near-ambient temperatures and can reduce reliance on conventional chemical disinfectants, the antiviral activity of NTP is mediated by RONS and, in some configurations, by ozone and NOx. These oxidants can be beneficial at virucidal doses; however, these oxidants may also induce oxidative stress or tissue injury when exposure is excessive or poorly controlled, underscoring the need for crop-specific optimization and delivery precision. In greenhouse-relevant water treatment, cold plasma ozone has shown promise for inactivating ToBRFV in hydroponic runoff; however, the authors explicitly note that practical adoption will require confirmation of efficacy and careful evaluation of plant growth, nutrient stability, and potential phytotoxicity under production conditions (Zhou et al., 2024).

Beyond phytotoxicity and nutrient stability, the potential non-target effects of NTP and PAW on beneficial plant-associated microbiota should also be considered. Because plasma treatments act through reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, pH shifts, and related oxidative chemistry, protocols designed for virus suppression or water sanitation may also alter endophytic, rhizosphere, or symbiotic microorganisms that contribute to nutrient acquisition, stress tolerance, and disease suppression. Emerging evidence suggests that these effects are dose-dependent rather than uniformly deleterious. For example, PAW has been reported to promote arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis in Lotus japonicus, whereas cold plasma seed treatment in legumes has been associated with improved nodulation-related traits under some conditions (Abeysingha et al., 2025; Binci et al., 2025). At the same time, microbiome-focused studies indicate that plasma treatment can shift plant-associated microbial community structure and that species-specific sensitivities among beneficial microbes remain possible, depending on plasma source, gas composition, and exposure time (Mohan et al., 2025). Therefore, future validation of NTP for plant virus management should incorporate microbiome-aware endpoints, including profiling of endosphere/rhizosphere communities, quantification of beneficial symbionts such as rhizobia and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and functional assays linked to nutrient cycling or induced resistance, to define operating windows that maximize virucidal efficacy while minimizing ecological disruption.

Operator safety is equally critical for postharvest and on-farm use. While NTP does not employ ionizing radiation, exposure risks can arise from high-voltage components and from the accumulation of ozone and nitrogen oxides in poorly ventilated environments. Accordingly, engineering controls (e.g., shielding, interlocks, local exhaust ventilation), routine gas monitoring, and task-specific standard operating procedures should be treated as core design requirements. Occupational exposure limits provide a practical benchmark; for example, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lists an 8-hour time-weighted average permissible exposure limit for ozone of 0.1 ppm (OSHA, 2024).

From an economic perspective, NTP and PAW are attractive because these treatments can be electricity-driven, avoid the continuous purchase of chemical disinfectants, and deliver rapid inactivation kinetics in water matrices. For instance, cold atmospheric plasma successfully inactivated PVY in water within minutes, including under higher organic backgrounds (Filipić et al., 2019), supporting the concept of integrating plasma-based sanitation into closed-loop irrigation or recirculating hydroponic systems. Nevertheless, robust techno-economic analyses remain scarce, and realistic cost-effectiveness will depend on reactor lifetime, energy efficiency, throughput, maintenance, and monitoring requirements (Filipić et al., 2020).

Scalability remains a principal barrier to widespread adoption in agriculture. Although laboratory-scale demonstrations often show strong performance, translating these results to field or commercial-scale operations requires uniform and reproducible RONS delivery across large areas and complex matrices, as well as effective management of process byproducts and emissions. The broader cold-plasma virus inactivation literature highlights that high ozone production and the limited number of pilot/industrial-scale deployments are key hurdles that must be addressed before these treatments can be adopted as routine practice (Filipić et al., 2020).

The economic viability of NTP for agricultural virus management depends on capital investment, operating costs, throughput capacity, and the avoided losses from virus outbreaks. While comprehensive techno-economic assessments remain scarce in the peer-reviewed literature, partial analyses and industry experience from related plasma applications provide useful benchmarks. Although formal plant-virus-specific techno-economic comparisons remain limited, available benchmarks from related plasma sanitation applications suggest that NTP may become competitive with ozone or UV systems in selected high-value uses, such as greenhouse hydroponics or virus-free propagation, where disease risk is high and treatment uniformity is critical. Capital costs are higher than those of chemical methods, but eliminating consumable purchases and achieving residue-free operation provides long-term economic and environmental advantages. Broader adoption will depend on: (1) demonstration of multi-season reliability and yield protection in commercial settings, (2) equipment standardization and competitive supply chains that reduce capital costs, and (3) integration of plasma treatment with precision irrigation management and disease forecasting to optimize when and where disinfection capacity is utilized.

Future Directions and Research Gaps

NTP is emerging as a promising, residue-free platform for limiting the spread of plant viruses; however, several scientific and implementation gaps must be addressed before NTP can be used routinely in agriculture. Current evidence on plant viruses is strongest in controlled matrices—especially water—where CAP inactivates PVY and PMMoV, and atmospheric-pressure plasma irradiation disrupts TMV particles and RNA (Filipić et al., 2019, 2021; Hanbal et al., 2018). Early translational studies further suggest the utility of disinfecting hydroponic and irrigation systems implicated in tobamovirus outbreaks, including ToBRFV (Zhou et al., 2024). However, robust in planta trials under field-realistic conditions remain scarce, and the durability of antiviral effects under fluctuating organic load, temperature, humidity, and ambient UV exposure remains poorly quantified.

Indeed, the priority remains the standardization of protocols and cross-study comparability. Many studies emphasize discharge settings (voltage, current, power, gas flow) but provide limited quantitative characterization of plasma-chemical drivers, including the identities and doses of RONS, UV output, and the mass-transfer constraints that govern delivery into liquid and porous matrices (Filipić et al., 2020). For agricultural translation, a minimal reporting set should include matrix properties (pH, conductivity, organic matter), measured stable products in liquids (e.g., H2O2, nitrite, nitrate), and infectivity endpoints based on plant bioassay rather than nucleic acid detection alone. Such standardization is essential for meaningful meta-analysis and for defining “plasma dose” concepts analogous to UV fluence or chemical CT values.

Mechanistic research should transition from qualitative attribution (“RONS effects”) to quantitative, virus- and matrix-resolved causality. Thus, key open questions include which specific RONS predominate in inactivation across different viral families, whether UV radiation acts synergistically with RONS, and which viral components (capsid proteins versus genome) are rate-limiting targets under realistic agricultural conditions (Filipić et al., 2020). Nonetheless, resolving these questions will require coupling plasma diagnostics and aqueous chemistry with structural virology (e.g., capsid integrity assays, high-resolution imaging, proteomics/oxidation mapping) and infectivity kinetics.

In addition to direct virucidal effects, an underexplored yet potentially high-impact approach is to leverage NTP—particularly PAW—as a defense-priming input to enhance crop antiviral readiness. Several studies in plant systems have shown that PAW irrigation can reshape redox signaling and activate immunity-associated pathways, including changes in SA/jasmonic acid-related signaling and induction of defense gene expression, consistent with a priming-like phenotype (Adhikari et al., 2019, 2020; Zambon et al., 2020). However, for plant virus management, the field still lacks rigorous evidence that such plasma-mediated immune activation translates into reduced viral establishment, systemic movement, or epidemic spread, and demonstrating that priming can be disentangled from nonspecific oxidative stress remains essential. Therefore, future work should (i) define dose windows in which PAW/NTP triggers beneficial signaling without phytotoxicity; (ii) quantify duration, systemicity, and trade-offs (growth/yield penalties) under realistic environments; (iii) benchmark plasma priming against established SAR elicitors and SA-centered frameworks (Conrath, 2006; Klessig et al., 2018; Tripathi et al., 2019), including virus-relevant models where induced resistance has been validated (e.g., BTH against CMV) (Anfoka, 2000). Well-controlled challenge assays, both mechanical and vector-mediated inoculations, coupled with transcriptomic/metabolomic analyses and infectivity-based endpoints, will be required to position NTP/PAW not only as a sanitation tool but also as an actionable component of integrated antiviral crop protection. In parallel, future field-realistic studies should move beyond plant safety and virucidal efficacy alone to quantify non-target microbiome responses, including persistence of beneficial endophytes and symbionts, shifts in rhizosphere/endosphere community structure, and potential functional consequences for nutrient cycling and plant resilience.

Scalability and delivery are the major engineering challenges. Liquid treatment is intrinsically challenging because plasma is generated in the gas phase, and the active species must dissolve and remain stable long enough to reach virions in the bulk; therefore, maintaining a similar composition of reactive species while increasing throughput is non-trivial (Filipić et al., 2020). Practical pathways likely include scale-out designs (multiple small reactors in parallel), inline modules for recirculating irrigation or hydroponic loops, and plasma-activated liquids that can be dosed where direct plasma exposure is impractical. In parallel, sanitation workflows for propagation systems, such as in vitro culture and grafting nurseries, merit systematic evaluation, as suggested by early CAP-based sanitation work on micropropagated material (Milusheva et al., 2020).

Safety and regulatory readiness require evidence obtained under real-world conditions. Although NTP does not introduce conventional pesticide residues, NTP can generate ozone, nitrogen oxides, and secondary oxidants in liquids that may be phytotoxic at excessive doses or pose inhalation risks in enclosed environments, necessitating dose limits, ventilation, and exposure monitoring (Filipić et al., 2020). Encouragingly, CAP-treated water used for PMMoV inactivation showed no cytotoxic or genotoxic activity in a mammalian cell model under the tested conditions, supporting the feasibility of safety-by-design approaches (Filipić et al., 2021).

Finally, adoption will depend on economics and systems integration, not only antiviral efficacy. Field-relevant studies should report energy consumption per treated volume or surface area, maintenance requirements, and component lifetimes, and cost comparisons against incumbent sanitation options (e.g., oxidants, UV, filtration, and heat) using transparent cost–benefit and life-cycle frameworks (Filipić et al., 2020). Well-designed pilot demonstrations in high-value greenhouse and vertical-farming systems—where water recirculation and tool-mediated transmission are key risk points—are likely to provide the strongest pathway to commercialization.
