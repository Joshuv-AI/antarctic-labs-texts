Assessment of buffalo and bovine ghee authenticity using fatty acid profile analysis and FTIR spectroscopy
Authors: Abdelmoneim H Ali, Abdelaziz Elbarbary, Shao Quan Liu, Mayssa Hachem, Jahirul Ahmed Mazumder, Fawzi Banat, Xingguo Wang, Mutamed Ayyash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMilk fat stands out for its superior quality, due to its bioactive lipids and rich flavor, unlike other plant and animal fats. The current study presents a novel approach to detect pork fat adulteration in buffalo and bovine ghee via fatty acid profiling, volatile compound analysis, and FTIR spectroscopy. Pure ghee samples were mixed with different levels (1, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 15%) of pork fat. By spiking ghee samples with pork fat at levels as low as 1%, we demonstrate that key indicators, such as butyric acid depletion, shifts in lipid quality indices, and distinctive FTIR spectral characteristics, can reliably signal adulteration. In addition, the principal component analysis revealed clear clustering patterns that differentiate pure and adulterated samples, highlighting the discriminatory power of the combined analytical methods. The findings of this study could offer a robust framework to ensure ghee authenticity and protect consumer trust in high-value dairy products.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Chemical biology, Chemistry

Introduction

Milk fat is a distinctive natural animal fat that plays a significant role in the human diet. It is essential not only for the chemical, physical, and nutritional properties of dairy products, but also for determining the price of milk1. Monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) make up about 25% of milk fat, while polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) account for 2.3%. In contrast, saturated fatty acids (SFA) approximately comprise 70%, with short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) contributing around 11%, nearly half of which is butyric acid2. Among edible fats and oils, ghee or anhydrous milk fat, is the most expensive (~6–7 times higher) and is a primary medium for frying and cooking in several countries. Ghee is a rich source of conjugated linoleic acid, which increases with higher clarification temperatures from 110 to 120 °C3,4. However, due to its high price, ghee is susceptible to adulteration with cheaper oils or fats. Therefore, quality assessment is crucial to ensure authenticity, benefiting both processors and consumers5. While several analytical methods exist for detecting vegetable oils adulteration in dairy fats, the rapid and specific detection of animal fat adulteration, mainly pork fat in ghee, remains a significant challenge due to compositional similarities and ethical concerns.

The addition of non-dairy fats to milk and its products is an illegal practice that has become increasingly complex and widespread6,7. Thus, for legal purposes and to maintain consumer confidence and protection, dairy products must be authentic and precisely labeled, especially for conventional/traditional products8,9. The milk fatty acids profile is used to detect adulteration with vegetable oils, as milk fat is characterized by its SCFA, while vegetable oils contain medium- and long-chain fatty acids10. Therefore, identifying non-dairy fats in dairy products remains a prominent research focus across many centers due to its significant economic importance in both national and international trades11,12. Destaillats et al.13 reported that unknown lipids in milk fat can be identified through the analysis of triacylglycerols by using the gas chromatography (GC). This method has been suggested to distinguish foreign fat mixtures in milk fat and has been approved by the European Community as an effective approach to evaluating the purity of bovine milk fat14. However, milk fat adulteration continues to be a global concern.

Several analytical techniques have been developed to detect milk fat adulteration, including fluorescence spectroscopy10, fatty acid and sterols analysis15,16, infrared spectroscopy17–19, and chromatographic methods20,21. We hypothesize that the inclusion of pork body fat in buffalo and bovine ghee alters the fatty acids composition, volatile compounds profile, and FTIR spectral attributes in a measurable manner, even at low adulteration levels. The main objective of this study was to investigate the adulteration of pure buffalo and bovine ghee with varying levels (1, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 15%) of pork body fat. The study was based on the analysis of fatty acids and volatile compounds by using the GC and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), respectively. In addition, lipid quality indices, including atherogenicity index (AI), thrombogenicity index (TI), desired hypocholesterolemic fatty acids (DFA), undesired hypercholesterolemic fatty acids (OFA), hypocholesterolemic and hypercholesterolemic fatty acid ratio (H/H), and nutritive value index (NVI) of pure and spiked ghee samples were calculated. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was also applied to analyze the functional groups in the different samples. By revealing the adulteration of buffalo and bovine ghee with pork fat, the present study could provide valuable knowledge to the dairy industry sector.

Results and discussion

SCFA as diagnostic markers

Figure 1 shows the total ion chromatograms obtained by the GC for the analysis of fatty acids in pork fat, pure buffalo ghee, and pure bovine ghee. The fatty acid content of buffalo and bovine ghee spiked with varying levels of pork body fat are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The results revealed significant (P < 0.05) differences in the fatty acid composition between pure ghee and pork fat. Notably, butyric, caproic, and caprylic acids were absent in the pork fat sample. It has been reported that SCFA are considered to be physiologically significant metabolites because of their association with gut microbiota regulation, obesity, immunology, in addition to other pathophysiological processes22. Furthermore, the SCFA profile has been studied as potential indicators of cancer, mainly colorectal cancer, due to their roles in inflammation and cellular metabolism23. This has raised the need for consistent analytical detection approaches in a diversity of biological matrices.

Changes in SFA, MUFA, and PUFA profiles

The results presented in Table 1 and Table 2 also show that oleic (34.37%), palmitic (27.07%), stearic (18.98%), and linoleic (13.69%) acids were the major fatty acids in pork fat, consistent with findings elsewhere24. In contrast, palmitic (33.13%), oleic (22.49%), stearic (13.07%), myristic (9.64%), and butyric (5.62%) acids were predominant in the pure buffalo ghee sample (Table 1). Pork fat contained 48.48% SFA, 36.60% MUFA, and 14.93% PUFA, compared to 70.05, 27.14, and 2.81%, respectively, in the pure buffalo ghee sample. Van Ruth et al.25 reported differences in the fatty acid profiles between milk fat and pork fat, particularly in the lower SCFA content of pork fat. The results showed that the fatty acid composition of ghee was significantly (P < 0.05) affected by the addition of pork fat. Butyric acid content significantly decreased in the ghee samples containing 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 15% pork fat, by 2.95, 2.88, 2.62, 2.30, 2.90, and 2.64%, respectively. Similarly, the spiked samples exhibited lower oleic acid content as compared to the pure buffalo ghee. In contrast, the levels of other fatty acids, such as myristic, palmitic, and stearic acids, increased with the addition of pork fat. This may be due to that these are also major SFA in the pork fat, and their relative proportion increases as the ghee’s own SFA are diluted.

As shown in Table 2, palmitic, oleic, stearic, myristic, and butyric acids were the major fatty acids detected in the pure bovine ghee accounting for 29.76, 28.76, 13.18, 7.89, and 4.28%, respectively. The pure bovine ghee contained 63.82% SFA, 32.09% MUFA, and 4.09% PUFA, compared to 48.48, 36.60, and 14.93%, respectively, in pork fat. The fatty acid composition was significantly (P < 0.05) altered by the addition of pork fat, even at low levels. MUFA, known for their beneficial effects, have been shown to lower cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein levels while negatively impacting high-density lipoprotein levels26. Myristic and palmitic acids, considered pro-atherogenic, are linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases due to their role in promoting left ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial infarction, and metabolic disorders, highlighting their clinical significance27. Furthermore, Kumar et al.28 found that the content of oleic acid in ghee samples from different milk species ranged between 29 and 35% of the total fatty acids. The variations in fatty acids profile across different samples, as well as discrepancies with literature, can be attributed to diverse factors, such as animal species, diet, lactation season, processing methods, and analytical techniques.

Lipid quality indices

Lipid quality indices provide insights into the nutritional quality of lipids and their potential impact on coronary disease development29. Due to their influence on serum lipid fractions, fatty acids are believed to either prevent or promote obesity-related atherosclerosis and heart diseases30. Dairy fats supply energy and play critical roles in human health, making it important to assess the dietary quality of lipids consumed daily. The results showed significant (P < 0.05) differences in lipid quality indices between pork body fat and pure ghee samples. Pork body fat exhibited a significantly (P < 0.05) lower AI value (0.64) compared to the other samples. In addition, buffalo ghee samples had higher AI values than bovine ghee samples. For optimal human health, the AI value should be below 0.531. It has been reported that AI values for seaweeds, crops, fish, and meat were in the range 0.03–3.58, 0.08–0.55, 0.21–1.41, and 0.17–1.32, respectively32. Another study reported that AI values less than 0.11 are associated with low risk of cardiovascular disease, and values between 0.11–0.21 and more than 0.21 are associated with intermediate and increased risks33. The mechanism behind AI and its roles in the cardiovascular disease is rooted in how it reflects the quality and behavior of lipoproteins, mainly small, dense low-density lipoprotein particles, which are highly atherogenic34. Similarly, TI values were lower in pork body fat and pure ghee samples than in spiked samples, with values of 4.07, 7.63, and 6.28 for pork body fat, buffalo ghee, and bovine ghee, respectively. TI reflects the balance between pro-thrombogenic and anti-thrombogenic fatty acids35,36. Regarding DFA, pork body fat had the highest value (70.50), and bovine ghee samples showed higher values than buffalo ghee samples. In contrast, pork body fat had a lower OFA value (28.59) compared to other samples. Pork body fat also displayed higher H/H (1.21) and NVI (1.97) values than the ghee samples. The variations in these indicators, when compared to previously reported data, can be attributed to differences in fatty acid profiles between the analyzed samples. ANOVA and Duncan’s test results reveal significant differences among the lipid quality indices of pure and spiked ghee samples. Nonetheless, it is crucial to note that many of the spiked samples do not differ significantly from each other for certain parameters, such as AI and TI. This indicates that while pork fat inclusion affects the lipids profile, the magnitude of change may plateau beyond definite adulteration levels. For example, AI values among the 4, 8, and 10% spiked samples are statistically similar, revealing a limited incremental effect at higher levels of adulteration.

Volatile compounds profile

Tables 4 and 5 show the content of volatile compounds in pork fat, pure ghee samples, in addition to the samples spiked with pork fat. Both buffalo and bovine ghee have distinct volatile compounds profile, which plays a functional role in their exceptional aroma and flavor. The analysis of volatile compounds by using the GC-MS revealed the detection of 10 carboxylic acids, 5 esters, 5 ketones, 3 lactones, 8 aldehydes, and 3 furans. The results show that there were significant (P < 0.05) differences in the content of volatile compounds in the pure ghee samples as compared to the pork fat sample. The level of aldehydes was significantly higher in pork fat than pure buffalo and bovine ghee samples. On the other hand, pure ghee samples displayed higher levels of carboxylic acids, esters, lactones, and furans as compared to pork fat. The content of volatile compounds in buffalo and bovine ghee samples was affected by the addition of pork fat.

Regarding carboxylic acids, buffalo ghee has been shown to contain higher levels of short-chain fatty acids, for instance butyric acid and caproic acid, which contribute to its characteristic strong aroma28. In addition, it has been reported that the content of esters in buffalo ghee is generally lower than their content in bovine ghee37. Methyl ketones, such as 2-nonanone and 2-heptanone exist in substantial levels in buffalo ghee, contributing to the creamy and buttery aroma38. Lactones, which reflect a creamy and coconut-like aroma, are found but in lower amounts compared to bovine ghee39. Furthermore, aldehydes, such as hexanal and nonanal are also found in buffalo ghee, contributing to the green and fresh aroma40. Furans are less prominent in buffalo ghee, but they do contribute to the overall complexity of flavor profile41. The variations in the volatile compounds content presented in our study and those previously reported in other literature could be attributed to several factors, such as animal species, diet, genetics, season of lactation, environmental conditions, ghee manufacture methods, and analytical techniques.

The volatile compounds identified in Tables 4 and 5 offer substantial potential for the detection of pork fat in ghee. Precisely, elevated levels of aldehydes, such as hexanal and nonanal in pork fat as compared to the pure ghee samples indicate their utility as markers of adulteration. Conversely, compounds like methyl ketones (2-nonanone, 2-heptanone) and lactones, which are more abundant in pure ghee, reduce with increasing levels of pork fat. These changes in the volatile profiles can serve as a reliable indicator for observing adulterated samples. To enhance the specificity and sensitivity of adulteration detection, the development of targeted volatile markers and chemometric models needs further investigation.

FTIR spectra

The spectral profiles of the pure and spiked ghee samples were comparable and exhibited distinctive absorption responses to edible fat42,43. Figure 2 shows the FTIR spectra and absorption values of the different samples. Figure 2A–C show the FTIR spectra of the pure fat samples, buffalo ghee samples spiked with pork fat, and bovine ghee samples spiked with pork fat, respectively. The results show that the different samples had similar responses for the fingerprint and functional group regions in the spectra, but the absorption values differed. The data revealed that the observed absorbance peaks, which were primarily attributed to the presence of different functional groups and bonds, displayed bending and stretching movements after IR light was applied to pork body fat and ghee samples. The absorbance signal observed at 2914.27 cm−1 primarily corresponds to the asymmetric stretching between the carbon and hydrogen of the functional ethyl and methyl groups. Likewise, the peak detected at 2850.45 cm−1 was attributed to the symmetric stretching between the carbon and hydrogen atoms of the same functional groups.

FTIR spectra of (A) pure buffalo ghee, pure bovine ghee, and pork body fat; (B) buffalo ghee spiked with pork body fat; (C) bovine ghee spiked with pork body fat at 4000 − 500 cm−1.

Free fatty acids containing carbonyl functional groups and the stretching of this functional group presented a distinctive absorbance response at 1745.27 cm−1. In addition, the peak observed at 1462.94 cm−1 is mainly attributed to the bending between the H-C-H bonds of the functional ethyl and methyl groups. The absorbance signal at 1249.25 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching of C-O in the O-C( = O)–CH2 of the ester. Moreover, the peaks detected at wavenumbers of 1154.49, 1097.44, 962.08, and 722.28 cm−1 were attributed to the stretching between the carbon and oxygen in HC-O-(C = O) of the ester, the carbon‒carbon link of the hydrocarbon chain, the carbon–hydrogen bending of the trans double bonds, and the carbon–hydrogen rocking of the ethyl groups and cis double bonds in the long-chain unsaturated fatty acids, respectively. Pork body fat showed a peak at 3006 cm−1, which was due to the carbon‒hydrogen stretching vibrations of the cis-CH out-of-plane deformation. This peak indicates the presence of oleic and linoleic acids, which are abundant in pork fat. Its absence or very low intensity in the pure ghee samples underscores a major chemical difference: ghee is mainly saturated with minimal cis-unsaturated fatty acids. Thus, the 3006 cm−1 peak serves as a key differentiator between pork fat and ghee, highlighting its utility as a diagnostic spectral characteristic for adulteration detection. Upadhyay et al.19 reported that in the case of pork body fat, 3030 − 2785 cm−1 could be considered an important region for determining the presence of pork body fat in control samples of pure ghee owing to the presence of signals in the wavenumber range of 3024 − 2996 cm−1. The variations in the spectral regions of pure and spiked ghee samples and pork body fat could be attributed to the differences in the composition of these fat types with regard to chain length and degree of unsaturation. A similar spectral interpretation was previously discussed by Antony et al.44. Additionally, FTIR has been employed to detect the presence of goat and sheep milk in bovine milk45 and to determine the presence of goat body fat in ghee using chemometric methods43.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA), a statistical technique for factor analysis, is widely recognized in multivariate statistical analyses and serves as a precise mathematical method for identifying dissimilarities within a dataset using a few principal factors46. The PCA results for the different samples are presented in Fig. 3. Figure 3A shows the loading plot of PCA for fatty acid composition, Fig. 3B presents the scoring plot of PCA for fatty acid composition, Fig. 3C illustrates the scoring plot for PCA of FTIR spectral analysis, and Fig. 3D displays the scoring plot for PCA of lipid quality indices. As shown in Fig. 3, ghee and pork body fat exhibited distinct clusters. As the proportion of added pork fat increased, the clusters of spiked ghee samples shifted away from the pure ghee samples and toward pork body fat. The loading plot of PCA of fatty acid composition in Fig. 3A revealed that PC1 and PC2 respectively represented 57% and 16.69% of the variance. The PCA scoring plot for fatty acid composition (Fig. 3B) shows pork fat positioned in the lower right-hand section, bovine ghee in the upper section, and buffalo ghee clustered in the lower left-hand section. PC1 and PC2 explained 57% and 16.69% of the variance, respectively. Samples with lower levels of pork fat formed separate clusters compared to the ghee samples. PC1 and PC2 explained 74.01% and 17.70% of the variance, respectively, indicating the significant role of FTIR in detecting pork fat adulteration in ghee (Fig. 3C). The scoring plot of PCA of lipid quality indices of fat sample presented in Fig. 3D revealed that PC1 and PC2 represented 96.52% and 1.91%, respectively.

Principal component analysis of buffalo and bovine ghee samples spiked with different levels of pork body fat.

A Loading plotof fatty acid composition; (B) scoring plot of fatty acid composition; (C) scoring plot of FTIR spectra analysis; (D) scoring plot of lipid quality indices of fat samples. B and C letters in (B–D) refer to buffalo ghee samples and bovine ghee samples, respectively.

For fatty acids composition, oleic, palmitic, and butyric acids were major contributors to sample differentiation. In the FTIR analysis, wavenumbers around 2914 cm−1 and 1745 cm−1 were influential in distinguishing pork fat from pure ghee samples. In a previous study, Upadhyay et al.19 effectively applied PCA in the spectral range of 3030–2785 cm−1 to detect ghee adulteration with pork fat, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 98% and 2% of the variance, respectively. Another study demonstrated that PCA in the spectral range of 1170–1141 cm−1 was successful in detecting coconut oil adulteration in ghee, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 94% and 6% of the variance, respectively47. Regarding lipid quality indices (Fig. 3D), the control samples of pork fat and ghee were well separated. Pork fat and bovine ghee samples clustered on the right-hand side, while buffalo ghee samples were located on the left-hand side. While PCA was applied in our study to visualize clustering and evaluate the separation between pure and adulterated samples, we acknowledge that PCA is mainly a qualitative or semi-quantitative approach. For practical applications, developing a regression model, such as partial least squares regression or multiple linear regression, based on FTIR spectral data or key fatty acids markers could be highly beneficial for predicting the exact level of pork fat adulteration. Although such modeling was beyond the scope of our study, we recognize its potential and recommend it as a valuable direction for future research.

Recent studies have highlighted the growing role of chemometric techniques in food authentication. The effectiveness of ATR-FTIR coupled with PCA has been demonstrated for the detection of vanaspati ghee adulteration in milk fat18. In addition, reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography combined with multivariate models for milk fat adulteration detection has been reported20. Similarly, ATR-FTIR and chemometrics were applied to identify coconut oil adulteration in ghee, achieving high classification accuracy47. Furthermore, a rapid, reagent-free, and accurate method was applied for the detection of raw milk adulteration with milk powder by using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy combined with machine learning algorithms48. Recently, a rapid qualitative and quantitative approach was developed to detect palm oil adulteration in cow milk by using ATR-FTIR spectroscopy with chemometric analysis49. These findings highlight the potential of integrating FTIR spectral data with advanced chemometric models for robust authentication protocols in dairy fats.

Although the present study focused on qualitative detection by using the FTIR and PCA, future work should aim to acquire a quantitative prediction model for practical applications. Based on our findings and previous reports, the spectral regions of 3030–2785 cm−1, which is associated with C–H stretching in unsaturated fatty acids in addition to 1170–1141 cm−1, which is related to the ester functional groups are highly informative for discriminating pork fat from pure ghee. Coupling these regions with partial least squares regression could enable precise quantification of the adulteration levels. Therefore, future research is recommended to validate this protocol by using calibration and external validation sets to verify prediction robustness and accuracy. Furthermore, the present study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the utilized pork fat for ghee adulteration was collected from a single location, which may not represent the full variability in fat composition across diverse breeds or areas. Second, the study was performed during the winter season, and seasonal variations in milk composition were not considered. Third, while the employed analytical methods (GC, GC-MS, and FTIR) are robust, they may have limited sensitivity in detecting ghee adulteration below 1%. Lastly, the study did not involve sensory evaluation, which could offer valuable perceptions into consumer perception of the adulterated ghee.

The present study demonstrates that combining fatty acid profiling, volatile compounds analysis, and ATR-FTIR spectroscopy offers a robust and sensitive approach to detect pork fat adulteration in buffalo and bovine ghee, even at levels as low as 1%. Beyond confirming compositional variations, the integration of lipids quality indices and multivariate analysis PCA provides a powerful framework for authenticating dairy fats. The findings of this study have important implications for food safety, consumer trust, and regulatory enforcement, specifically in regions where pork fat adulteration poses religious and ethical concerns. By establishing a rapid and reproducible detection procedure, this study contributes to the development of standardized tools for ghee authentication and sets the stage for broader applications in the field of dairy quality control. Despite the strength of the analytical methods employed, this study is limited using pork fat from a single source and the lack of seasonal variation in milk sampling. In addition, the absence of sensory analysis and potential detection limits below 1% adulteration should be considered when interpreting the findings and applying them to broader contexts.

Methods

During the winter season, fresh raw milk samples from water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis) and cattle (Bos taurus) were collected from a local farm in Zagazig (Egypt). A local abattoir provided the pork body fat. In addition, the starter cultures used for cream souring, which included Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis, Lactococcus lactis subsp. lactis biovar diacetylactis, and Leuconostoc mesenteroides subsp. cremoris were acquired from Chris. Hansen Lab. (Copenhagen, Denmark). All chemicals and reagents utilized in this research were of analytical grade and high purity.

Preparation of buffalo and bovine ghee spiked with pork body fat

Initially, unprocessed milk was heated to 70 °C for 5 min and subsequently cooled to 42 °C. An Alfa Laval separator (2010.1.21 A, Alfa Laval Separator Co., Ltd., Hamilton, New Zealand) was used to extract the cream from both buffalo and bovine whole milk. The extracted cream underwent heating to 90–95 °C for 15 sec, followed by cooling to 20 °C. It was then inoculated with 2% of the starter culture and incubated for 12 h until the pH reached 4.4–4.6. The cream was then churned at 8–10 °C in a butter churner, and buttermilk was removed once the butter is formed. Both buffalo and bovine butter were heated in a temperature-controlled water bath at 60 °C, followed by clarification at 110–120 °C with constant stirring. Any foam that developed on the oil surface was consistently removed. The oil phase was left to settle for 2 h before being filtered through amber-colored glass vials lined with three layers of cheesecloth. The resulting buffalo and bovine ghee were kept at 4 °C for later use and analysis. Separate batches of pure buffalo and bovine ghees were mixed with pork body fat at concentrations of 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, and 15%. These mixtures were kept at 4 °C under nitrogen to prevent oxidation.

Gas chromatography analysis of fatty acids

Firstly, 50 mg of the fat samples were placed in a sealable container for fatty acid methyl esters preparation. The samples were combined with 2 mL of hexane and 0.5 mL of 2 M methanolic-potassium hydroxide. After mixing the mixtures for 5 min, 3 mL of saturated sodium chloride solution was introduced to separate the phases. The upper surface layers were extracted, dried using anhydrous sodium sulfate, and then injected into an Agilent 7820 A GC system. This system featured a flame ionization detector, autosampler, and a TRACE TR-FAME ionic liquid capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Thermo Fisher, USA). The oven was set to 60 °C with a 40 min total run time. Both injector and detector were maintained at 250 °C. A gradient program was employed for fatty acid profile analysis, starting at 60 °C for 3 min, then increasing at 5 °C/min to 175 °C for 15 min, followed by a final 2 °C/min increase to 220 °C for 10 min. Nitrogen served as the carrier gas, flowing at 1.2 mL/min with a 1:100 split ratio. The detector used hydrogen gas at 30 mL/min, along with 400 mL/min of air and 25 mL/min of nitrogen. Fatty acid identification was accomplished by comparing the retention times of gas chromatographic peaks with those of reference fatty acid methyl ester standards.

Volatile compounds analysis by gas chromatography-gas spectroscopy

The profile of volatile compounds in the pure and spiked ghee samples was determined by using solid-phase microextraction-gas chromatography/mass spectrometry. The samples (2 g) were placed in 15 mL glass vials equipped with self-sealing septum. Then, the vials were incubated for 20 min at 45 °C with a magnetic stirrer to reach adsorption and desorption equilibrium for organic volatile compounds in carboxen®/polydimethylsiloxane, before the adsorption of volatiles for another 20 min using a 75 μm carboxen poly(dimethyl siloxane)- coated solid-phase microextraction fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA). Then, adsorbed volatiles were determined by using Thermo Scientific ISQTM GC-MS (Trace GC Ultra gas chromatograph equipped with ISQ, Thermo Scientific, USA). The injection temperature was set at 280 °C and the splitless mode was 5 min desorption time. Separation was carried out using Rtx-5Sil DB-5 MS capillary column 30 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness (Restek, Bellefonte, PA, USA). In addition, the oven temperature was programmed as follows: starting at 35 °C, 15 °C/min to 180 °C, then 20 °C/min to 260 °C, and held at 260 °C for 30 s. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min, and the detector temperature set at 280 °C. Identification of the chromatograms was accomplished by comparison with mass spectral database. Finally, the mass spectrometer was operated in the electron impact ionization mode at 70 eV, and mass spectral data were acquired in the mass range of 33– 300 atomic mass unit at 0.7 scans/s.

FTIR spectra acquisition

The neat, spiked samples of buffalo and bovine ghee were positioned on a diamond crystal cell engineered for attenuated total reflectance (ATR), featuring a 1.66 mm path length. Absorption spectra were captured using a Nicolet FTIR spectroscope (Nexus 670, Waltham, MA, USA), with settings of 4 cm−1 resolution and 0.2 cm/sec scan speed. The samples were initially warmed to 60 °C to ensure complete melting during spectral acquisition. Each sample underwent 16 scans within the wavenumber range of 4000 − 500 cm−1. Then, the resulting spectra were processed by eliminating the background air spectrum, utilizing OMNIC Nicolet software (version 8.1) for analysis. Before analyzing subsequent samples, the ATR crystal was meticulously cleaned in place using a soft tissue dampened with isopropanol and thoroughly dried.

Statistical analysis

The obtained data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and the statistical XLSTAT software (Addinsoft, New York, USA) was used for data analysis. A significant difference between the different fat samples was calculated using analysis of variance and Duncan’s test at a significance level of P-values lower than 0.05. The structural correlation between variables and observations was evaluated using PCA to clarify the relationships between the different samples.
