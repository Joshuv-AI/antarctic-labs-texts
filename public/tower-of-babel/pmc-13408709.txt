First evidence of microplastic contamination in surface waters of Loktak Lake, a Ramsar site in the Eastern Himalayas
Authors: Pallabi Borah, Naphibaniarlin Kshiar, Arun Jyoti Nath, Sneha Dutta Chowdhury, Demsai Reang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastic (MP) pollution in freshwater ecosystems is an emerging environmental threat yet remains poorly documented in ecologically sensitive wetlands. Here, we present the first integrated assessment of MP occurrence, distribution, polymer composition, and associated risks in the surface waters of Loktak Lake (Manipur, India), a globally unique floating lake system and Ramsar site. Surface-water samples were collected during the pre-monsoon season (March 2024) from the 0–20 cm surface layer from 13 geospatially distributed sites. MP abundance exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity, ranging from 8 to 82 particles L⁻¹ (mean 47 ± 23.8 particles L⁻¹). MP hotspots are concentrated near densely inhabited islands, floating hutments, market-associated zones, and active fishing areas. Spatial concentration variation was statistically supported through a strong monotonic association between MP abundance and the anthropogenic intensity. Size profiling showed a dominance of small particles, with particles < 2000 μm accounting for 91.38% of total MPs, highlighting high bioavailability potential. Predominant MP morphotypes included fibers and fragments. Polymer identification confirmed the presence of common consumer and activity linked polymers, including polypropylene (PP), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyamide (PA), and polyvinyl chloride (PVC). EDX analysis revealed associated trace metals including Pb, Ru, and Pt, indicating potential polymer-metal interactions. An environmental risk evaluation integrating indices indicated elevated ecological concern in high-activity zones. The estimated human health risks (non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic) via ingestion remained within acceptable thresholds under current exposure assumptions. However, the presence of carcinogenic polymers such as PVC raises significant concern. This study provides a critical baseline to guide monitoring, wetland management, and targeted source-control interventions in Ramsar and high-value freshwater ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54098-8.

Introduction

Globally, over 430 million tonnes (MT) of plastic are produced annually, with more than two-thirds of them becoming waste rapidly1. India alone generates ~ 3.9 MT annually, a substantial portion of which remains uncollected and eventually enters aquatic ecosystems2, posing threats to both marine and freshwater environments1.

While macroplastic pollution has been widely studied, recent attention has shifted toward microplastics (MPs), i.e., plastic particles smaller than 5 mm in diameter. MPs originate from a variety of sources and exist in multiple forms3–5. They are composed of diverse polymer types, each with varying environmental persistence and toxicity profiles6–8. Despite their tiny size, MPs can infiltrate the food chain. They have been detected in air, water, soil, and biota1,4,9, underscoring their ubiquitous nature. Recent studies have identified MPs even in remote and extreme environments, including polar regions, high-altitude ecosystems, and the deep ocean10–12.

Initially classified as marine pollutants, MP particles are now widely reported in freshwater ecosystems5. Aquatic species ingest these particles, bioaccumulate through the food webs, and potentially affect higher trophic levels, including humans1,4. Human exposure to MPs occurs through multiple pathways, particularly via food, drinking water, and inhalation3,13,14. With MPs increasingly detected in both tap and bottled water, drinking water has emerged as a significant route of exposures4,15. Of particular concern is the ability of MPs to act as vectors for toxic chemical additives, heavy metals, and pathogenic microorganisms7,16,17. In this context, freshwater bodies that serve as critical drinking water sources for both rural and urban populations require higher attention and urgent investigation.

Recent estimates suggest substantial human exposure to MPs globally, with heightened vulnerability in developing regions13,18. MPs have been detected in multiple human tissues, raising concerns about potential long-term health implications4,19. In addition to health concerns, ecosystem service losses linked to plastic pollution have been estimated at $500-2,500 billion year⁻¹ globally20. All these associated challenges underscore the urgency of addressing MP contamination in freshwater environments and demand immediate attention.

Despite their high ecological importance, freshwater ecosystems remain understudied compared to marine systems. Lakes, in particular, are more vulnerable to MP accumulation due to their proximity to urban centers, and anthropogenic influences21,22. MPs in these inland waters persist for long periods, adsorb toxic pollutants, and enter food webs, amplifying both environmental and human health risks5,23,24. Yet, baseline data from many sensitive freshwater environments, particularly in the Global South, remain limited25.

Among freshwater systems, Ramsar wetlands warrant particular attention because of their exceptional biodiversity, hydrological functions, and socio-economic importance. Although protected, Ramsar wetlands are not immune to emerging contaminants such as MPs. Loktak Lake, located in Manipur, Eastern Indian Himalaya, a region underrepresented in global MP data, exemplifies this vulnerability. As the world’s only floating lake and a designated Ramsar site since 1990, Loktak holds high ecological, hydrological, and cultural value. Yet, it was placed on the Montreux Record in 1993 due to escalating ecological stress3.

Over 55 rural and peri-urban settlements surround the Loktak. The lake supports over 220,000 people who depend on its waters for fishing, agriculture, drinking water, and tourism26. Loktak is interspersed with phumdis (floating biomass), which supports phumdi-based agriculture and settlements known as phumsangs, housing over 18,000 residents27,28. Loktak also sustains the endangered Sangai deer (Rucervus eldii eldii) within the Keibul Lamjao National Park, the world’s only floating wildlife sanctuary27. In recent years, however, rising urban encroachment, increasing plastic waste, land use changes, and hydrological alterations have drastically altered the lake’s ecological functioning29,30,31. In addition, Loktak acts as a terminal basin for nine sub-catchments31, with the Nambul River serving as a major conduit for urban plastic waste from Imphal city, frequently exacerbating pollution and monsoon flooding32.

Despite these well-documented pressures, no prior study has examined the occurrence and associated ecological and human health risks of MP contamination in the waters of Loktak Lake. Our previous work revealed MP ingestion in over 90% of sampled fish from the lake3, highlighting potential ecological risk. Building on this foundational evidence, the present study provides the first comprehensive assessment of MPs in the surface waters of this Ramsar wetland. The objectives of the study are: (i) quantifying MP abundance, size distribution, and morphotypes, (ii) analysing spatial distribution patterns and potential anthropogenic sources, and (iii) evaluating associated ecological and human health risks. This study establishes a critical baseline for future monitoring and provides evidence relevant to wetland conservation, water governance, and management in socio-ecologically sensitive lake systems.

Methods

Study area

The Ramsar site, Loktak, lies in the southern part of the Imphal valley, at 24°33′52.8″N and 93°40′54.0″E, in the Bishnupur district of Manipur, northeast India (Fig. 1). The lake is situated at an altitude of ~ 768 m above sea level and is part of the Indo-Burma global biodiversity hotspot. It is the largest freshwater lake among the Ramsar-designated wetlands in the eastern Himalayan region. The lake’s surface water area spans approximately 286 km², with depth ranging from 0.5 to 4.58 m33. The lake forms part of the larger Manipur river sub-basin and has a direct catchment area of 980 km² and an indirect catchment area of 7157 km²33. This region receives an average annual rainfall of 1,350 mm and experiences notable seasonal variability in both temperature and humidity. The annual temperature range from 12 °C to 31 °C, and relative humidity fluctuates between 51% and 81%31. The catchment landscape is primarily dominated by forest and agricultural land. Subtropical pine and moist deciduous forests together constitute nearly 73% of the catchment’s forest cover, playing a crucial role in maintaining the lake’s ecological balance and influencing sediment and nutrient dynamics. The lake is dotted with small inhabited islands, with Thanga and Karang being the most prominent and populated. The lake’s socio-economic relevance as a lifeline for local fishing communities and its ecological role as a biodiversity hotspot amplify the need and importance of monitoring emerging pollutants in this sensitive ecosystem3.

Sampling and quality control

Surface water samples were collected from 13 strategically selected sites (Figs. 1 and 2) across the open water body of the Loktak Lake. Sampling was conducted during the pre-monsoon season (March 2024), and surface water was collected from an approximate depth of 0–20 cm. Due to restricted access to the sensitive zones requiring special permission and site-specific logistical arrangements, sampling was limited to a one-time, single-season collection. The sampling design aimed to capture the diverse land use and land cover (LULC) types in the lake, thereby representing the full spectrum of potential contamination sourced from varying levels of human activity/pressure. Each site was carefully chosen to reflect distinct environmental and anthropogenic conditions. These include areas with active fishing, boating, agriculture, market activity, village settlement, tourism, and minimal human disturbance areas (restricted regions and relatively clear water) (Fig. 2).

Sampling points were subjected to bulk water sample collection using the grab sampling method (1 L grab). This sampling method was adopted considering the size of the lake compared to rivers and oceans, where manta trawls or neuston nets14 are generally used. Furthermore, grab sampling helps collect samples from a wide variety of locations, such as wastewater discharge points, areas with extensive macrophytes, and shallow water bodies34, suitable for the present study. This sampling method also helps capture a larger number of MPs within the smaller size range35. A wooden country boat was used to collect water samples, thereby preventing additional plastic contamination. The geographical coordinates of all collection points were recorded using a handheld GPS device (Garmin 12 H) to ensure spatial accuracy and reproducibility of sampling locations.

Water samples were collected in triplicate. Triplicates were obtained within a 3-meter radius of the individual collection point (Fig. 2), and a composite 1 L sample was prepared for further identification and analysis. This approach was adopted to obtain a more representative sample from every point. Samples were collected in stainless steel thermos flasks, pre-washed with analytical-grade acetone and distilled water, and then dried in an oven at 50 °C for 12 h to help maintain their physical characteristics, including temperature. The collected samples were transported to the Environmental Science Laboratory at the Assam Royal Global University, Guwahati (Assam), and stored at temperatures below 4 °C, as described by Lusher et al35.

Contamination control

To avoid MP contamination from external sources, strict laboratory protocols were followed throughout the experimental procedures. At every stage of sample handling, precautions were taken to minimise background contamination. Throughout the entire experiment, cotton lab coats and nitrile gloves were worn, and laboratory doors and windows were kept closed to minimise the risk of airborne MP contamination. Glass and metal apparatus were primarily used35. Only analytical-grade reagents were used throughout the entire procedure. All the containers and laboratory equipment were thoroughly rinsed three times with Milli-Q ultrapure water both before and after each use. To further reduce airborne contamination, all reagents and digested samples were covered with aluminium foil. The polarised microscope workspace was cleaned before sample analysis. After filtration, the filters were stored in glass petri dishes until FTIR and EDX analyses were performed.

Microplastic isolation

The sequence of sample processing followed in this study was organic digestion (WPO + Fenton reagent) → density separation → filtration which is consistent with the techniques supported by the NOAA (National Atmospheric and Oceanic Administration) and guidelines followed by several other reports36–39. All the samples were passed through a 5 mm mesh size stainless steel sieve. Each sample that passed through the sieve was collected and transferred into a 1-litre beaker.

Sample digestion (wet peroxide oxidation method). 20 mL of 30% H2O2 and 0.05 M Fe(II) were added to digest the organic matter. The 0.05 M Fe(II) solution was prepared by dissolving 7.5 g of FeSO4·7H2O in 500 mL of distilled water and 3 mL of concentrated H2SO4. The samples were then placed in a magnetic stirrer inside a fume hood for 10 to 15 min to achieve maximum removal of organic matter36.

Density separation. Density separation was performed using a saturated NaCl solution, as it is cost-effective and non-toxic37,38. This was followed by the addition of 300 g/L of NaCl (ρ ~ 1.2 g cm⁻³), i.e. (6 g NaCl per 20 ml sample)39. The solution was stirred manually for approximately 2 min and allowed to settle until no or minimal visible particulate matter was suspended in the solution38. The solution was then covered with a foil and kept for 24 h.

Next, samples were filtered using a vacuum pump. Whatman Grade 1 filter paper (pore size 11 μm) and Whatman Grade 42 filter paper (pore size 2.5 μm) were used for this purpose. The two-step filtration was used to reduce clogging caused by suspended particulates. This allows the separation and concentration of a wider range of MP particles to be analyzed under a microscope. The contents on the filter paper were washed with Milli-Q water, then digested with 20 mL H2O2, and filtered again. This method removed organic matter without degrading plastic particles. The filter papers were then placed in petri dishes, air-dried at room temperature, and stored until characterisation.

Microplastic analysis

The analysis of MPs involved two key steps: visual identification and polymer characterization. The visual identification of residues is crucial for distinguishing MPs from other materials, such as organic debris (e.g., dried plant and animal parts, shell fragments) and non-organic items (e.g., glass, tar, and metal paint layers)35. To reduce the risk of misclassification and undervaluation of MPs, particle selection was standardised according to the guidelines established by Kibria et al40. and Kumar et al41. This process involves direct examination under a microscope, followed by manual isolation using tools such as tweezers after an initial visual pre-sorting42,43. MPs on the larger end of the size spectrum (500 μm–5 mm) can often be visually distinguished by certain characteristics, such as the absence of cellular or organic structures, including knots, and uniform thickness in fibres along their entire length without tapering44. Only the particles that met these criteria with a well-defined shape were classified as potential MPs. The selected particles were then collected and stored separately. Following this, polymer identification was conducted using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) to confirm their composition as MPs.

Visual identification

All the samples were examined under a Polarized Light Microscope (Model: Nikon ECLIPSE Ci-POL) at 4-10X magnification. The potential of a polarised light microscope in identifying MPs was also demonstrated by Labbe et al45. and Sierra et al46. Characteristics of the identified MPs were recorded using the NIS-Elements software, which allowed for the acquisition, processing, measurement, and analysis of images, as well as data management and report creation. Finally, MPs were categorised based on their size and morphotypes42,44.

FTIR analysis

For the categorization of polymer composition of the MPs, FTIR spectroscopy (PerkinElmer; Model No: SPECTRUM 100 and FRONTIER IR) was applied. However, in this study, the potential of not being able to select all the MPs for spectroscopic analysis was rather limiting. The process had turned out to be too expensive and time consuming. Accordingly, a representative sample of (n= 154) particles that covered all the pertinent shapes and sizes was adopted to analyse them. MP samples were individually mounted on a specialised sample holder to minimise interference from the IR beam. The FTIR measurements covered the mid-infrared range (4000–400 cm⁻¹) with a spectral resolution of 2 cm⁻¹. Spectral analysis was performed in Spectrum software with baseline correction and normalisation to enhance comparability. The identification of the polymer was based on a wide reference database of infrared (IR) spectra of known polymers and associated additives. The acquired spectra were matched to reference spectra in SpectraBase™, an online spectral resource of John Wiley and Sons, Inc. The findings were validated by cross-referencing polymer absorption bands reported by Jung et al47. and Jaafar et al48. The distinct absorption peaks enabled precise determination of the polymer composition of the MP sample.

Energy Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis

Elemental analysis of MP particles was conducted using an Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) system (Model No. 7582, Oxford INCA, Oxford, U.K.). This technique was employed to differentiate carbon-rich plastic materials from non-plastic contaminants. Each sample was carefully mounted on Al stubs using conductive carbon adhesive tabs to ensure stability during analysis. Individual MP particles were examined using EDX, with X-ray spectra collected from various surface points. The elemental composition obtained helped confirm the presence of MPs while excluding non-polymeric substances. Operating the system at 14 keV in backscatter electron mode enabled the detection of trace elements, including toxic metals. The identification of elements was based on their characteristic X-ray emission peaks, allowing for the detection of inorganic plastic additives and potential toxicants within the MPs43.

Risk assessment

To date, risk evaluations for MP particles in aquatic ecosystems have been conducted by comparing exposure concentrations with threshold effect concentrations49. In this study, four well-established ecological risk assessment methods, as outlined by Lithner et al6. were employed: the Pollution Load Index (PLI), Pollution Hazard Index (PHI), Potential Ecological Risk Index (PERI), and Coefficient of Microplastic Impact (CMPI). Although a standardized model for MP pollution has not yet been established, preliminary risk assessments are done based on factors such as abundance, size, shape, and polymer type.

These values can be sourced from the Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS), maintained by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA).

Statistical analysis

In this study, since each sampling site was represented by a single composite sample prepared from three subsamples, inferential site-wise comparisons (e.g., ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis across sites) were not statistically appropriate due to a lack of within-site replication. Spatial heterogeneity was therefore summarised using descriptive statistics and geospatial interpolation. To quantitatively assess the relationship between MP abundance and its surrounding associated LULC, sampling locations were assigned an ordinal anthropogenic intensity score based on field observations (Supplementary Table S1). Next, the association between MP abundance and anthropogenic intensity was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ), and the statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using OriginPro (OriginLab Corporation, USA).

Spatial distribution mapping

To visualise the spatial distribution of MPs across Loktak Lake, the Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) interpolation method was employed using ArcGIS (Esri ArcMap 10.1; License no. ESU730665330). Given the limited number of sampling points (n = 13) in the present study, kriging interpolation was not applied27,58. Geostatistical methods such as kriging require sufficient data points to model spatial dependence reliably58. IDW was therefore adopted as a deterministic visualization approach to depict relative spatial patterns of MP abundance and to identify potential accumulation hotspots, rather than for predictive spatial modelling59. The IDW interpolation was conducted with default parameters, using a power value of 2 and a variable search radius that incorporated the 12 nearest neighbours. This approach emphasizes the influence of proximal observations while progressively diminishing the contribution of more distant points, thereby highlighting local spatial variability59.

Results and Discussion

Microplastic distribution and abundance

In recent years, accumulating evidence has established a strong link between MP contamination in freshwater ecosystems and varying intensities of human activity across aquatic landscapes21,22,60. In this study, a distinct spatial distribution of MPs was observed, closely associated with surrounding LULC types.

MP abundance exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity across Loktak Lake, ranging from 8 to 82 particles L⁻¹, with an average concentration of 47 ± 23.8 particles L⁻¹. Sites characterised by higher anthropogenic disturbance, including dense settlements, floating hutments, island-based habitation, fishing zones, and market areas, consistently showed elevated MP concentrations compared to sites with restricted or low disturbance. Although formal inferential comparisons among individual sampling sites were not conducted because single composite samples were used per site, the observed spatial trends were statistically validated through association-based analysis. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis revealed a strong positive association between MP abundance and anthropogenic intensity (ρ = 0.965, p = 1.01 × 10⁻⁷, n = 13). The findings statistically confirm that MP accumulation increases monotonically with surrounding land-use pressure. Seasonal and flood-driven processes are known to strongly influence MP transport in dynamic wetland systems12,61. So, pre-monsoon surface waters therefore represent baseline accumulation conditions before peak hydrological disturbance38,61–63,offering important insight into exposure pathways relevant for aquatic biota and human water use.

The MP concentrations detected in Loktak Lake suggest substantial contamination of this Ramsar wetland. When compared with other first reports across different geographical settings, marked differences in concentrations were observed. For instance, Loktak’s surface-water MP levels are markedly higher than those reported from Deepor Beel (Ramsar wetland in Assam, northeast India)38, where average surface-water concentrations were 0.55 ± 0.05 particles L⁻¹. In contrast, Loktak overlaps with or exceeds reported values from some urban-influenced lakes, such as Kodaikanal Lake, India, where surface-water MPs were reported at ~ 24.42 ± 3.22 items L⁻¹64. At broader global scales, freshwater MP concentrations span orders of magnitude depending on surrounding pressures and sampling/reporting approaches. For example, Lake Ulansuhai, China, was reported at 1,760 ± 710 to 10,120 ± 4090 items m⁻³ (~ 1.76–10.12 items L⁻¹), with higher levels near drainage canal inputs65. Likewise, Lake Guaíba in Brazil was reported at 11.9 ± 0.6 to 61.2 ± 6.1 items m⁻³ (~ 0.012–0.061 items L⁻¹), and distribution was linked to hydrological/geohydrological processes and urbanization gradients36. Taken together, these comparisons suggest that Loktak’s elevated concentrations are consistent with a system experiencing diverse MP inputs, while also highlighting that differences in sampling technique and reporting units can influence cross-study comparability. Therefore, the comparisons should be interpreted as contextual rather than strictly equivalent benchmarks.

The IDW interpolation map (Fig. 3) revealed clear variation of MP accumulation in areas characterized by concentrated anthropogenic activity. This provide critical insights for targeted mitigation efforts and the design of future cleanup strategies40,66. Higher concentrations of MPs were recorded in areas under intense anthropogenic pressure, including Sites S4, S5, S6, and S13. Elevated levels were also observed near market proximities (S8). These findings reflect the cumulative impact of direct waste disposal, fishing activities, and settlement-based plastic use3.

In contrast, lower MP concentrations were recorded in the minimal human interference zones, such as S1 and S2, supporting the idea that reduced anthropogenic disturbance correlates with lower plastic load21,36. Loktak Lake functions as a terminal basin for multiple inflowing rivers, receiving not only localized waste but also substantial inputs of agricultural runoff and untreated urban effluents from surrounding catchments27,67. This hydrological connectivity further amplifies MP accumulation3,32. Consequently, the observed spatial pattern aligns with global trends, in which elevated MP loads are typically reported in regions with urbanisation, tourism activity, and proximity to wastewater discharge points60.

The strong monotonic relationship between MP abundance and anthropogenic intensity reinforces the view that the observed heterogeneity is systematic and follows a disturbance gradient rather than representing isolated site-specific variability11,21. The detection of MPs even in lower-impact zones also suggests atmospheric deposition and hydrodynamic redistribution as contributing transport pathways7–9. MPs may be mobilized by wind-driven currents and wave action, facilitating dispersal from point sources to less-disturbed areas36,68,69. Importantly, MP presence across all sites highlights the systemic nature of plastic contamination and supports the need for targeted waste management interventions, especially in floating settlements such as the phumsangs. The high concentrations observed at heavily used sites raise concerns over ecological impacts on aquatic fauna through ingestion, trophic transfer, and bioaccumulation. Our previous study3 confirmed MP ingestion in commercially important fish species from Loktak Lake, highlighting potential implications for food safety and human exposure. Given that Loktak supports over 220,000 people who depend on the lake for water and food26,29, addressing MP contamination is both an ecological priority and a socio-economic imperative.

Microplastic size distribution and ecological implications

The MPs identified in this study were classified into five size categories: very small (VS; <1000 μm), small (S; 1000–1500 μm), medium (M; 1500–2000 μm), large (L; 2000–3000 μm), and very large (VL; >3000 μm). Overall, the smallest size class (VS) contributed 40.65% of the total MP count, followed by S (40.00%), M (10.73%), L (6.18%), and VL (2.44%) (Fig. 4). Notably, particles < 2000 μm (VS + S+M) together accounted for 91.38% of total MPs, indicating a clear dominance of smaller particles in Loktak Lake. Although site-wise size-class distributions showed minor variation, smaller MPs (< 2000 μm) dominated across all sampling locations. The predominance of small-sized MPs observed in this study is consistent with reports across different regions36,69,70.

The dominance of smaller size fractions suggests that MPs in the lake largely originate from the progressive degradation and fragmentation of larger plastic debris4,8,71. Smaller MPs possess a higher surface-area-to-volume ratio, which enhances their capacity to adsorb toxic chemicals, heavy metals, and microbial contaminants, thereby increasing their potential toxicity to aquatic organisms16,72. The widespread occurrence of these small-sized MPs is of particular concern because their size enhances bioavailability to aquatic organisms. These particles are more readily ingested by fish and other biota, facilitating bioaccumulation and trophic transfer within the food web3,4,68. While organism uptake was not evaluated in the present study, our earlier investigation from Loktak Lake reported MP ingestion in over 90% of sampled fish species, providing independent empirical evidence that the exposure pathways inferred here are ecologically relevant3. Experimental and field studies have further shown that smaller MPs are more likely to translocate across biological membranes and persist within tissues18,68, increasing the likelihood of bioaccumulation and biomagnification. In a socio-ecologically sensitive lake such as Loktak, where fish constitute a major protein source for local communities, the predominance of small MPs raises concerns regarding food-web-mediated exposure and potential human health risks.

Morphotypes and polymer characterization

Understanding the shape composition of MPs is crucial for identifying their sources and pathways, helping develop effective strategies to mitigate MP pollution in the environment73. In this study, the MP shapes identified in the water samples were classified into four primary categories: fiber, fragment, foam, and sphere. A percentage composition doughnut chart was created to illustrate the distribution of these shapes for all sites (Fig. 5). The variation in morphotype abundance reflects the influence of land use and human activity across the lake. Among them, fibres and fragments were the most dominant forms of MPs across sites. In S1 (managed boating area), fibres accounted for 62.5% of the total MPs, while in S11 (agricultural lands), they accounted for 53.7%. Fragments, which are irregularly shaped pieces derived from larger plastic items, accounted for 37.5% in S1 and 44% in S9 (pisciculture farms). In the present study, foam- and sphere-shaped microplastics (MPs) were detected in relatively low proportions across the sampling sites. Foam-shaped MPs ranged from 17.1% at Site S11 to non-detectable (ND) levels at Site S1, while sphere-shaped MPs were present at up to 20% at Site S3 and were similarly undetected at S1. The absence of these morphotypes at certain locations may reflect localized differences in plastic usage patterns, waste input types, and hydrodynamic conditions that influence MP distribution and deposition. Although shape-wise percentage varied between samples, fibers and fragments were predominantly observed. This pattern is consistent with findings reported by Jian74.

Fibers typically originate from textiles, while fragments result from the degradation of larger plastic items. Foam particles may derive from foam-based products, and spheres from various plastic materials. The prevalence of these MPs can be attributed to site-specific anthropogenic activities. In S1, sources likely include wear and tear on synthetic ropes, nets, and boat materials, as well as litter from recreational use. In S9, plastic-based materials used in pisciculture, such as feeding bags, nets, and enclosures, may contribute to MP release. In S11, representing agricultural land, MPs may originate from the breakdown of plastic mulch films, irrigation equipment, and the use of contaminated sludge or compost71. Additionally, fibres may form during the washing of synthetic textiles75, while surface runoff and atmospheric deposition from nearby human settlements can further increase MP accumulation9. The proximity of these sites to human settlements and roadways likely increases MP contamination due to increased plastic waste and MP release from tyre wear. Similar observations have been documented by Kibria et al40. The construction of the Ithai Barrage is one of the most significant anthropogenic activities that led to the deterioration of Loktak ecosystem32. It was designed to regulate water levels for continuous hydropower generation by maintaining a constant water level throughout the year, permanently altering the lake’s hydrology. Additionally, the barrage has obstructed the lake’s natural outlet, which previously helped flush out waste, leading to increased pollution and deteriorating water quality. Another contributing factor to the lake’s declining water quality is the presence of phumsangs (floating huts)28. While these structures traditionally serve as homes for native fishers, the growing population has led to increased domestic waste being disposed of directly into the water system29. Surrounding the lake, there are vast agricultural fields locally known as pat-lou (pat meaning lake and lou meaning agricultural field), which further contribute to runoff pollution mixed with pesticides and fertilizers32. Additionally, the Ithai Barrage has disrupted the migratory routes of various fish species that once travelled from Myanmar, causing a decline in fish populations impacting the livelihoods of local fishers76. This led to the adoption of unsustainable fishing practices, abandoning traditional methods in favour of harmful techniques such as the use of pesticides or poisons in the lake.

FTIR analysis

The polymer composition of the MPs from the surface water of Loktak Lake was identified using FTIR. The spectra of the analyzed samples were compared with reference spectra (SpectraBase™). The analysis revealed the presence of multiple polymer types, including polypropylene (PP) of 15%, polyamide (PA) of 39%, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) of 31%, and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) of 15%, along with evidence of surface degradation and functional group modifications indicative of environmental ageing and contaminant interactions (Fig. 6). The spectra for PP have characteristic absorption peaks in the reference spectra at 2950 cm⁻¹, 2920 cm⁻¹, and 2870 cm⁻¹, indicating the alkyl C-H stretching vibrations were found in some of the samples - S1, S4, S5, S7, S11 and S13 (Fig. 6), with peaks in the range of 2914–2923 cm⁻¹41,77. PP is used in fishing gears, nets, and ropes78, which are commonly used by the local people in the studied area. However, peaks observed in the 3431–3460 cm⁻¹ range suggest surface oxidation or the presence of hydroxyl groups (-OH), which are indicative of environmental weathering and potential interaction with pollutants. Furthermore, the band indicates surface oxidation of the PET films. This finding corroborates those of Ainali8.

The sites - S1, S2, S3, S5 and S9 show consistent peaks at 3445 cm⁻¹ (N-H stretch), 1633 cm⁻¹ (C = O) corresponding to amide I, and 1045 cm⁻¹ (C-N stretch), confirming the presence of polyamide (PA), supported by other peaks as 609, 631, and 681 cm⁻¹. 1633 cm⁻¹ strongly supports the presence of a PA like Nylon-6 or Nylon-6,6, especially when found alongside peaks like 3445 cm⁻¹. The presence of -C ≡ N and -OH functional groups in the samples indicates polymer degradation resulting from prolonged environmental exposure, oxidative breakdown, and potential interactions with other pollutants. The peaks around 1631–1647 cm⁻¹ indicate the presence of amide (C = O stretching) or aromatic C = C bonds, which are characteristic of polyamide (PA)47 or polyethene terephthalate (PET)79. Similar findings were also reported by Shao et al80. The detection of PA confirms the presence of synthetic textiles, such as nylon, which may have originated from washing machine effluents or the degradation of fishing gear. PET suggests contamination from textile fibres, plastic bottles, and food packaging, which often break down into MPs. Likely local sources of PET in Loktak Lake include improper disposal of PET bottles and food packaging from surrounding settlements. Untreated sewage and road runoff from Imphal, Singjamei, and Sagolband via the Nambul River contribute domestic plastic waste to the lake81. Also of great importance is the fishing and tourism activities, along with the use of PET-based materials on floating phumdis, further contribute to plastic fragmentation and release into the lake water. The peak at approximately 1041–1050 cm⁻¹, found in most samples, is attributed to C-O stretching vibrations and indicates the presence of PET, which corresponds to the findings of Chen et al79. Abdel-Fattah et al82. also reported peaks in the range of 591–731 cm⁻¹, indicating the presence of PVC, which is attributed to C-Cl stretching vibrations. The wavenumber at 731 cm⁻¹, also attributed to C–H bending in ethyl, further suggests the presence of PET. The detection of PVC could be associated with construction materials, pipes, and electronic waste, which contribute to MP pollution through weathering and leaching processes. Additionally, plasticised PVC is widely used in the production of fishing nets, as it has a low specific gravity (~ 1.12 g cm3), high tensile strength, and is resistant to natural abrasional forces38, indicating its association with Loktak lake, where fishing is one of the primary activities. The presence of hydroxyl (-OH) and cyanate (-C ≡ N) groups suggests potential degradation or interaction with other environmental contaminants.

EDX analysis

The MP samples contained EDX-detected traces of key elements such as oxygen (O), sodium (Na), chlorine (Cl), potassium (K), ruthenium (Ru), lead (Pb), and platinum (Pt), with some also exhibiting sulfur (S) and nickel (Ni) (Fig. 7). This suggests they are likely of surface related or adsorbed constituents on the MPs, although the additive constituents within the polymer matrix cannot be entirely ruled out72. During the manufacturing of plastics, several additives are blended into base polymers as accelerators, biocides, colourants, fire-retardants, and plasticisers. They may also retain residual substances from the synthesis process, including monomers, solvents, and catalysts23. Na and K are commonly used as catalysts or additives in polymer synthesis, playing a role in shaping the formation and properties of MPs83. A significant variation is observed across the samples, with a high Na and Cl content in most, suggesting the presence of NaCl (salt-based) compounds. MPs originating from laundry, cleaning agents, or industrial processes may accumulate salts and other chemicals present in detergents, wastewater, or processing fluids84. This may indicate the presence of PA and PET, as these polar polymers have functional groups such as carbonyl and amide groups85 and ester linkages72, which enhance their affinity for ionic compounds like Na⁺ and Cl⁻. It may also be consistent with PVC, as it is inherently flame-resistant due to its high chlorine content, which helps prevent ignition and slow combustion86. There are reports that polymers, particularly PA-based textiles and PET fibres from clothing, can accumulate salts from aquatic ecosystems16.

The presence of Pb in the samples may indicate the association with polymers like PET and PP, as Pb is known to have high affinity for these polymers87. Pb is often used as a colourant, stabiliser, catalyst, and ink88. The findings suggest that metal(loid)s may interact with MPs through physical adsorption, resulting in a loose attachment and easy release into aquatic environments. With ageing, exposure to ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) light can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (•OH), superoxide anions (O₂•⁻), and singlet oxygen (¹O₂). This leads to changes in the surface physicochemical properties of MPs, the formation of oxygen-containing functional groups, and eventual polymer degradation8,71. These surface transformations enhance MPs’ ability to adsorb metal(loid)s80, likely leading to the formation of metal oxides such as RuO₂, PbO₂, and SiO₂ through adsorption and surface interactions. Fenton ageing, in which hydrogen peroxide reacts with iron(II) ions to produce hydroxyl radicals, is another process that influences the metal(loid) adsorption capacity of MPs by generating additional adsorption sites on their surfaces89. PA, used in industrial applications, can adsorb metals due to its polar functional groups. These contaminants likely originate from e-waste leachates or industrial wastewater90,91. Pt can be deposited onto MPs, through environmental exposure, particularly in areas influenced by automotive emissions. Pt can adsorb onto MPs in natural lake water, with surface ageing enhancing this process. At the same time, pH levels and biofilms influence sorption, emphasising the role of environmental factors in Pt accumulation91. Ru, used in electronic components such as resistive circuits and contacts92, can be adsorbed by MPs from e-waste or contaminated environments through direct interaction or via leachates. Overall, the elemental composition detected by EDX indicate possible polymer-metal interactions influenced by environmental exposure, industrial processes, and material degradation pathways. Nonetheless, the reason of particular metal oxides development, or the establishment of the concrete polymer-metal interactions, cannot be established without the advanced elemental mapping or depth-profiling analyses. In this regard, such findings may be considered as having the potential of metal association with MPs rather than mechanistic incorporation.

Risk assessment of microplastic pollution

The ubiquitous presence of MPs poses serious risks to aquatic ecosystems due to their persistence, small size, and complex chemical composition. MPs are composed of synthetic polymers that often contain residual monomers, stabilizers, plasticizers, flame retardants, and other hazardous additives7,25,86. According to the United Nations’ Globally Harmonised System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (GHS), over 50% of commonly used plastics are classified as hazardous owing to their potential toxicity, bioaccumulative behaviour, and long-term environmental persistence6,25. Once released into aquatic systems, these particles not only cause physical harm to aquatic organisms but can also leach toxic substances or act as carriers for heavy metals and persistent organic pollutants, further amplifying ecological risks68. While numerous studies have investigated MP pollution in marine environments20 and estuarine systems50, comprehensive risk assessments in freshwater ecosystems remain comparatively limited25. This knowledge gap is particularly concerning given the increasing evidence of MP accumulation in lakes, rivers, and wetlands, which are critical for biodiversity, water supply, and livelihoods.

The present study assessed the ecological risk of MPs in Loktak Lake using the PHI, PLI, and PERI. PHI values showed PP (PHI = 15.4) and PET (PHI = 123) falling in the “High” and “Danger” categories, respectively, while PA (PHI = 1925) and PVC (PHI = 154, 015.4) indicate the “Extreme Danger” category (Table 1). MPs can adsorb and release hazardous substances, such as additives, persistent organic pollutants (POPs), and heavy metals7,23,72. Therefore, even MPs like PP and PET, which exhibit lower hazard scores or are present in lower concentrations as noted in this study, should not be overlooked in ecological risk assessments.

PLI, on the other hand, has been utilised to assess the risk level associated with MP contamination. The results show that all the sites have PLIs categorised in level I, indicating a low pollution load (0–10). This suggests that the chemical composition of the identified MPs has a limited impact on PLI levels93. PLI values increase with increasing MP abundance, indicating a direct dependence of PLI on MP concentration50. MP abundance is strongly associated with socio-economic factors such as population (density, lifestyle and activities), urbanization and industrialization level23. Areas with economic activities, such as developed tourism, can boost MP concentrations22. The study area, Loktak Lake, is a prominent hub for tourism and fisheries, with widespread use of plastic-based aquaculture and boating rafts. As a result, tourism, agricultural practices, and fish farming have become major contributors to MP pollution in the region, thereby affecting the PLI values. The PLIs observed in this study, however, were relatively lower than those reported in other regions by Pan et al69. and Xu et al94., but showed an almost similar trend to Shekoohiyan and Akbarzadeh93, where all the samples showed PLI < 10. Warrier et al63. reported PLI values corresponding to hazard level I in Lake Manipal. This may be attributed to the limited presence of high-risk MPs in the lake.

Notably, polymers such as PVC (hazard score: 10,001), acrylonitrile-butadiene-styrene (ABS; hazard score: 6,552), and polycarbonate (PC; hazard score: 610), identified as high-hazard polymers in MPs by Lithner et al6., were either absent or present in very few points. PVC was detected in only two sampling points (S4 and S13), while ABS and PC were absent. However, it is also crucial to note that when assessing MP risk using the PLI, the selection of the background MP concentration (C₀i) is a significant factor. In this study, the lowest observed concentration of 8 MPs L− 1 was taken as the background concentration due to the lack of available baseline data, which can significantly influence the PLI outcomes. Other studies, such as Luo et al95. have also adopted up a similar methodology. This approach may lead to an underestimation of the risk, as a lower C₀i value can artificially reduce the PLI. Moreover, this method is inconsistent with the natural variability of background concentrations, which are known to fluctuate across spatial and temporal scales50. A similar finding was reported by Saikia and Handique38, who observed low PLI values in water samples from Deepor Beel, another Ramsar site of ecological importance in the eastern India region, primarily due to the absence of MPs with high hazard scores. Nonetheless, in the present study, although the overall hazard level remains consistent with that of Saikia and Handique38(Level I), the PLI values were slightly higher at most sampling points. This difference may be attributed to variations in MP abundance and the influence of site-specific socio-economic activities, such as tourism and aquaculture. Despite these variations, PLI remains one of the most effective indices for assessing MP contamination levels. However, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of MP-associated risks, PERI must be evaluated together with PLI, as the latter is sensitive to the choice of background concentration, which may not always accurately reflect the actual baseline environmental conditions50,53.

PERI has been used to assess risk by considering the hazard scores of polymer types and the abundance of MPs. In this study, the PERI values for five out of the thirteen points (S1, S2, S3, S5 and S9) were categorized as medium danger (150–300) (Table 1; Fig. 8A). While S4 and S13 showed extreme danger (PERI > 1200) (Fig. 8A), corroborated with the findings of Radhakrishnan et al96. in the estuary sediments. While the remaining sites below 150 are similar to the studies by Pan et al39., which detected a minor risk index in Dongshan Bay. This suggests minimal potential ecological risk from combined MP polymers. The PLI and PERI results differ because the impact of MP abundance and toxicity varies widely across the polymer types recorded at the sampling points. Therefore, the extremely high PERI scores at sites S4 and S13 are attributed to the presence of PVC, a polymer with a hazard score over 10,000. Despite being detected at only two sites, its toxicological weight significantly elevated the index values. On the other hand, low detection concentrations and the non-hazardous major polymer types detected in the rest of the points resulted in low-risk scores (PP: 1, PA: 50, PET: 4). Due to existing limitations in analytical methods, the actual MP-related risks at the studied points may be higher than reported. Therefore, advanced, standardised MP analysis techniques are crucial for improving the precision of such risk assessments. Moreover, MPs’ tendency to accumulate and carry environmental contaminants may further intensify their adverse effects. In this study, the integrated approach of PHI, PLI, and PERI offered a preliminary assessment of the ecological risks associated with MP contamination in Loktak’s freshwater ecosystem. However, the PLI and PERI indices were applied here for comparative assessment only and should not be interpreted as definitive quantification of ecological risk.

Similarly, the CMPI was calculated, and the values for the identified shapes (fibre, fragment, foam, and sphere) were observed to be 0.47, 0.34, 0.13, and 0.05, respectively (Fig. 8B), indicating impact levels ranging from minimal to average. Among these, fibres demonstrated an “average” impact across all sampling points. Although fibres dominate the morphotype composition, their CMPI falls into the “average” category because the calculation is based on relative abundance rather than hazard. This underscores the need for morphotype-weighted impact scores in future assessments. However, fibres tend to float on the water surface due to their buoyancy, thus posing a significant threat to aquatic organisms that mistake these particles for food68. Spheres recorded the lowest CMPI among the detected shapes in Loktak Lake (see Table 1and Fig. 8B). Similar patterns were observed by Afzal et al60. However, Rangel-Buitrago et al52. reported an “extreme” CMPI for fibres, which contrasts with the observation of the current investigation. This discrepancy may result from variations in sampling methods, hydrodynamic conditions, geographical location, MP input sources, and local socio-economic factors.

Human health risk assessment

In assessing the human health risks associated with MPs, the Chronic Daily Intake (CDI) via ingestion was calculated for both carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic effects. For this purpose, RfD and SFi were determined for the four types of MP polymers identified in the samples: PP, PA, PET, and PVC. Based on existing literature, PET is not considered to exhibit carcinogenic properties6,23. Likewise, PP and PA are also regarded as non-carcinogenic materials97. The RfD for PET has been reported as 0.5 mg/kg-day12. Accordingly, the non-carcinogenic risk, expressed as the hazard quotient (HQ), was calculated for PET, and the overall hazard index (HI) was derived using Eq. (11). Due to the absence of reported RfD values for PP and PA in the literature, the HI was estimated solely from PET. The calculated HI remained below 1, indicating a negligible non-carcinogenic health risk under current exposure assumptions57,98. In contrast, PVC is classified as a carcinogen and has an oral slope factor (SF) of 0.72 (mg/kg-day)-1, as per USEPA. Using this value, the carcinogenic risk (RI) was evaluated following Eq. 12. The resulting risk estimate was found to be below the acceptable threshold for carcinogens, typically set at 1 × 10⁻²99,12, indicating that there is no significant carcinogenic risk associated with the presence of PVC-based MPs in the water samples. Though RI for PVC was within acceptable thresholds, the polymer’s known toxicity and environmental persistence present a latent health concern, particularly with increasing exposure over time.

Beyond documenting the presence of MPs, this study advances freshwater MP research by integrating spatial distribution, size-class dominance, polymer-specific ecological risk indices, and human health risk assessment within a globally significant Ramsar wetland. Unlike most Indian freshwater MP studies, which primarily report abundance or morphotypes, this work demonstrates that small MPs dominate across anthropogenic gradients and confer elevated ecological risk when polymer toxicity is considered. Also, in comparison to global Ramsar wetland studies that often focus on sediments or biota, the present study provides one of the first surface-water-based, risk-integrated assessments from a floating lake system. Loktak Lake, as a monsoon-driven terminal basin with floating settlements, therefore serves as a model system for understanding MP accumulation, transformation, and exposure pathways in tropical wetlands under increasing human pressure.

Limitations and future direction

While the study offers valuable insights into MP contamination, the findings of the current research have to be seen in light of its limitations. Sampling was conducted during a single season (pre-monsoon, March 2024) and limited to the surface layer (0–20 cm), due to logistical constraints. As such, this study provides a seasonal snapshot rather than capturing full temporal or vertical variability. Given the strong influence of monsoon-driven inflows and post-flood remobilisation in Loktak Lake, MP abundance and spatial patterns may differ during other seasons.

This study focused exclusively on surface waters and did not include sediments, riverine inflows, or biota, which are important reservoirs and pathways for MPs in large lake systems, particularly terminal basins such as Loktak. The spatial statistical comparisons among individual sites were further constrained by a single composite sample per site, limiting site-specific inferential testing. Having a large sample size could promote the results. The lack of reliable baseline data on MP concentrations led us to use the lowest observed concentration as the background value for PLI calculations. While the adopted baseline provides a comparative framework, relying on the lowest observed MP value may have underestimated actual contamination levels due to natural variability. Finally, the use of the Polarised Light Microscope in characterization of MPs presented additional challenge. Although selected for its advantages such as rapid analysis, higher magnification, and enhanced illumination control, it restricted our ability to categorize MPs by colour.

Because activity-specific pressure indicators (tourist footfall, fishing effort, or waste discharge rates) were not available, our source attribution relies on LULC context and a field-based ordinal intensity proxy rather than quantified flux estimates. Despite these constraints, the study provides a robust baseline for MP occurrence, size dominance, spatial trends, and associated risks in a globally unique Ramsar wetland. Future studies that capture seasonal and vertical variability would further refine understanding of MP dynamics in Loktak Lake and comparable freshwater systems.

Conclusion

This study provides the first risk-integrated baseline of MP contamination in the surface waters of Loktak Lake, a globally significant Ramsar wetland and uniquely dynamic floating lake system. MPs were detected across all sampled locations, with zones under higher anthropogenic pressure exhibiting higher concentrations. The dominance of small particles (< 2000 μm) underscores heightened ecological relevance, as these fractions are more readily ingested and can facilitate trophic transfer, particularly in Loktak where fish are central to local diets and livelihoods. Polymer identification and risk indices further indicate that MP impacts cannot be interpreted from abundance alone; polymer-specific hazard and persistence shape ecological concern and exposure implications.

Beyond Loktak, these findings align with growing evidence that freshwater wetlands, especially in densely inhabited and monsoon-influenced regions can act as important reservoirs and redistribution hubs for MPs. From a policy and management perspective, the findings point to clear priorities: strengthening waste collection and segregation along the lake perimeter and on the islands, reducing direct disposal from floating settlements, improving riverine and urban effluent management, and targeting cleanup and awareness interventions in the identified hotspot zones. Future research can build on this baseline by using multi-season sampling, including sediments and inflow channels, and by using hydrodynamic-informed assessments better to resolve transport, accumulation, and exposure pathways.
