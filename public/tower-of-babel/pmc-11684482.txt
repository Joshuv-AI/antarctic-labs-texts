Progressive Evolution of Flow and Heat Transfer Channels in Hot Dry Rock Stimulated by Liquid Nitrogen Cold Shock
Authors: Yong Sun, Long Feng, Hongrui Xu, Cheng Zhai, Wei Tang, Yuzhou Cong, Xu Yu, Jizhao Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hot dry rock (HDR) is a novel green, low-carbon energy. Its development requires the creation of fracture channels in deep thermal reservoirs. Traditional methods such as hydraulic fracturing have limited effectiveness in reservoir stimulation, so a method of liquid nitrogen cold shock was proposed. The ultralow temperature of liquid nitrogen induces a quenching effect on hot rock, thereby promoting the formation of a complex fracture network. In this study, hot rocks at different temperatures were subjected to cyclic liquid nitrogen cold shocks. The cascading evolution of the “temperature field-thermal stress-pore fracture development-three-dimensional seepage network” was analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows: As the cold-shock duration increased, the core temperature decreased exponentially, with a slower cooling rate at the center and a faster rate at the edges. Additionally, local temperature fluctuations existed at the edges. The maximum radial thermal stress at 200–600 °C ranged from 2.57 to 9.29 MPa. The initial cold shock and temperatures above 300 °C contributed the most to the reduction in wave velocity, with maximum decreases of 70.25 and 55%, respectively. The more developed the pores and fractures within the damaged core, the more pronounced the arrival time lag, frequency shift, and energy attenuation will be. Microscopic pore evolution followed two patterns. At 200–300 °C, the first peak increased significantly, with a few new micropores forming along grain boundaries. At 400–600 °C, the second peak grew and expanded to be dominant. Numerous mesopores formed both along grain boundaries and within grain interiors, interconnecting with the original pores. As the temperature shock effect intensified, the fracture increased and expanded. Branch fractures developed from the main fractures, ultimately forming a fracture network. The orientations of these fractures became more randomly distributed. What’s more, the proportion of larger-sized and higher-connectivity pores increased. The flow velocity and flow rate increased significantly, with the maximum values in the representative elementary volume reaching 0.167 m/s and 1.75 × 10–7 m3/s, respectively. When HDR underwent liquid nitrogen cold shock, the mineral grains contracted. Due to the various thermal expansion coefficients of different types of grains, local stress was concentrated. When the stress exceeded the tensile strength, tensile fractures were induced. The cyclic shock effect not only intensified the stress concentration but also lowered the damage threshold of the grains. The convergence of these two factors led to the continuous development of pore-fracture channels in the HDR.

Introduction

Hot dry rock (HDR) refers to high-temperature rock buried deep with temperatures exceeding 150 °C and containing little to no water.1 HDR geothermal energy is a new type of green, low-carbon energy. It provides efficient and stable energy output, with a power generation capacity factor 2.6–5 times higher than wind and solar energy.2 The global HDR resource is estimated to be equivalent to 50 000 trillion tons of standard coal, which is 30 times the amount of all fossil fuels.3 Amid the dual context of global carbon reduction and the energy crisis triggered by the Russia–Ukraine conflict, the development of HDR has emerged as a potential solution.

HDR, primarily deep granitic formations confined under high stress, is characterized by low porosity and permeability. Generally, fracturing techniques are required to create flow pathways between production and injection wells in the thermal reservoir, forming an enhanced geothermal system (EGS)44. Current thermal reservoir modification techniques rely on hydraulic fracturing supplemented by thermal and chemical stimulation. Hydraulic fracturing requires high breakdown pressure and often induces simple fracture geometries, which is not conducive to efficient heat exchange with the reservoir.5 Chemical stimulation6 dissolves hydrothermal minerals in existing fractures to increase connectivity. Thermal stimulation7 induces thermal damage in the reservoir by injecting cold water, but its effectiveness is limited. Liquid nitrogen, with a boiling point of −196 °C at atmospheric pressure, can serve as a novel waterless fracturing fluid.8 Leveraging the temperature difference between ultralow-temperature liquid nitrogen and high-temperature rocks triggers strong thermal stress, producing a quenching effect on the rock and inducing a complex three-dimensional fracture network.7 McDaniel9 and Grundmann10 et al. conducted liquid nitrogen fracturing on coalbed methane and shale gas wells in the United States and found that the liquid nitrogen cold shock stimulated orthogonal fracture planes in the reservoir. Overall, this method shows great potential for thermal reservoir modification.

The core of liquid nitrogen fracturing lies in the temperature shock effect. Cong et al.11 captured temperature field changes during liquid nitrogen injection into coal boreholes, discovering a significant radial temperature gradient along the borehole, with a maximum thermal stress of 1.3 MPa near the borehole wall. Zhang et al.12 simulated the thermal stress distribution in high-temperature rock under a liquid nitrogen jet using a local nonthermal equilibrium model, finding that the thermal stress was concentrated at the solid–liquid contact interface, manifesting as tensile stress, with a maximum of several tens of MPa. Current research on heat and mass transfer between liquid nitrogen and high-temperature rock focuses on thermal stress calculations but does not consider how subsequent thermal stress causes rock failure. Sun et al.13 tested the changes in porosity, resistivity, and ultrasonic velocity of high-temperature granite after liquid nitrogen cold shock, analyzing correlations among different damage variables. Wu,14 Kang,15 and Shao16 et al. investigated macroscopic mechanical damage characteristics of granite after high-temperature and liquid nitrogen cooling treatment, establishing relationships between mechanical parameters, temperature, and cycle variables. Mahesar et al.17 captured micro- and nanoscale pore morphology of sandstone after liquid nitrogen treatment using scanning electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy, finding that liquid nitrogen cooling increased fracture width and connectivity. The macroscopic mechanical damage originates from the accumulation of microscopic pores and fractures,18 but specific correlations between multiscale damage are rarely addressed. More importantly, when liquid nitrogen contacts hot rock, it causes temperature field changes, which in turn stimulate thermal stress, induce the development of microscale pores and fractures, and further degrade the macroscopic mechanical properties, ultimately affecting fluid flow and heat exchange. This chain-like progressive failure process and the coupling relationships among damage variables remain unclear. No scholars have yet established a cascade mechanism linking “temperature field–stress field–damage field–flow field” from the perspective of temperature shock-induced damage in HDR.

In this study, HDR samples at different temperatures (200–600 °C) were subjected to cyclic liquid nitrogen cold-shock treatment. The spatial-temporal evolution of the temperature field was captured by using an infrared thermal imager, and the thermal stress distribution was calculated. Ultrasonic testing was performed to assess the overall pore-fracture development and evolution patterns. Furthermore, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) was used to characterize the changes in the microscopic pore-fracture components. Based on CT scanning, a three-dimensional pore-fracture network was reconstructed, followed by seepage simulations. Finally, the thermal failure process of HDR induced by liquid nitrogen cold shock was replicated by using PFC2d modeling. In this way, by following the path of “temperature field distribution–mineral grain fracture–microscopic pore composition–3D pore-fracture network–seepage and heat transfer characteristics”, the evolution characteristics of pore and fracture networks and the underlying cascade failure mechanism were revealed. This research can provide theoretical guidance for the field application of liquid nitrogen fracturing technology.

Experimental Procedure

(1)Sample preparation: Since HDR is generally buried deep and difficult to sample, granitic outcrops on the surface were used as substitutes. Large granite blocks from Yantai, Shandong, were cored and processed into cylindrical samples with a diameter and height of 50 mm. The granite samples exhibited a pinkish hue with coarse grains, containing relatively large biotite and pink feldspar particles and white quartz. The measured density of the granite was 2.654 ± 0.008 g/cm3, with a P-wave velocity of 3.617 ± 0.057 km/s and a porosity of 0.34 ± 0.04%. Additionally, granite powder finer than 325 mesh was prepared for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. The primary minerals identified were quartz, feldspar, and mica, which are typical constituents of granite, with potassium feldspar being the most abundant and mica the least. Further, samples with large deviations were eliminated through ultrasonic velocity testing, and finally, 15 core samples were selected and divided into 5 groups according to different HDR temperatures (200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 °C), with each group containing 3 replicate samples.(2)Slow heating: Each group of core samples was placed in a muffle furnace set to the target reservoir temperature. The temperature increased at a rate of 2 °C/min to minimize thermal damage during heating. The samples were then kept at a constant temperature for 1 h to ensure uniform heating throughout.(3)Liquid nitrogen cold shock: The high-temperature core samples were quickly removed from the muffle furnace and immersed in a Dewar flask filled with liquid nitrogen. The liquid level was maintained above the core samples. This was left for 10 min, during which the core approached the temperature of the liquid nitrogen.(4)Infrared thermal imaging: One core sample from each group was selected. Starting from the moment of immersion, the sample was quickly removed every 5 s for rapid infrared thermal imaging of the same end face. This process was repeated until the overall temperature of the end face fell below the measurement limit of the infrared thermography device (−30 °C).(5)Ultrasonic transmission and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tests: The samples, approaching the temperature of liquid nitrogen, were removed from the Dewar flask and allowed to return to room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were placed in a vacuum saturation device and saturated with water under a pressure of −0.95 MPa for 12 h to ensure the internal pores and fractures were fully filled with water. Low-field NMR tests were performed on these saturated samples. Following this, the saturated samples were then dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h, and ultrasonic testing was conducted on the dried cores. Steps (2), (3), and (5) were repeated until 5 cycles of liquid nitrogen thermal shock and corresponding tests were completed.(6)CT scanning: After 5 cycles of liquid nitrogen cold shock, the damaged cores were scanned using CT to obtain two-dimensional images of different internal layers (Figure 1).

Results and Discussion

Spatiotemporal Changes in the Temperature Field of HDR during Liquid Nitrogen Cold Shock

Infrared thermography was used to capture the evolution of the end-face temperature field of different high-temperature rock cores during liquid nitrogen cold shocks, as shown in Figures 2, 3, 4. The temperature changes of rock cores at 200, 400, and 600 °C were highlighted at different moments (20 s intervals). Since the lower temperature limit of the infrared thermal imager was −30 °C, imaging was stopped when the overall end-face temperature dropped below −30 °C. In the infrared images, regions tending toward red represent relatively high temperatures, while those tending toward blue represent relatively low temperatures.

Temperature changes of the 200 °C core during liquid nitrogen cold shock.

Temperature changes of the 400 °C core during liquid nitrogen cold shock.

Temperature changes of the 600 °C core during liquid nitrogen cold shock.

As the cold-shock duration increased, the end-face temperature decreased. The temperature dropped more rapidly near the edges and more slowly near the center. The edge regions were in direct contact with the liquid nitrogen, enabling faster cooling through boiling heat transfer (heat transfer coefficient fluctuated between 100 and 10 000 W/m K).19 When a liquid (such as water or liquid nitrogen) comes in contact with a high-temperature solid, wall and liquid nitrogen contact, it leads to the formation of a layer of gas. This gas layer separates the liquid from the solid surface, preventing direct contact, which is known as the Leidenfrost effect. When low-temperature liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) comes into contact with high-temperature granite, the significant temperature difference between liquid nitrogen and granite causes rapid vaporization of the nitrogen, forming a thin nitrogen gas film on the granite surface. This film acts as a barrier, preventing direct contact between liquid nitrogen and the rock, meaning that heat must pass through the gas film, which has a relatively high thermal resistance. During the heat exchange process, a boundary layer forms near the surface of the HDR due to viscous forces. Within this boundary layer, the flow velocity of nitrogen is reduced and the primary mode of heat transfer shifts from convection to conduction, which lowers the overall heat transfer efficiency. In summary, the boundary layer effect inhibits the heat transfer process. However, due to the high flow velocity of liquid nitrogen and the large temperature difference, nitrogen tends to exhibit turbulent flow, which enhances the heat transfer process. Moreover, turbulence was formed at the interface between the surface and liquid nitrogen, further enhancing the convective heat transfer. However, the central region was cooled through heat conduction with the edge region. Due to the longer heat conduction path and the inherently low thermal conductivity of granite (2.5–4 W/m K),20 the central temperature decreased more slowly. The isotherms approximated concentric circles, initially densely distributed at the edge and gradually enclosing toward the center. This reflected a transition of the temperature gradient from steep to gentle.

From a local perspective, the isotherms appeared to be jagged, reflecting the influence of mineral grains on heat transfer. Granite is composed of irregularly shaped quartz, feldspar, and mica grains, with thermal conductivities of 6–8, 2–3, and 0.5–2.5 W/m K,21 respectively. The shape and thermal conductivity differences of adjacent interlocking grains led to temperature fluctuations, which differed markedly from the smooth circular isotherms of a homogeneous medium. In the mid-to-late stages, rapid cooling was observed in certain directions along with localized rapid cooling zones at the edges (as shown in Figures 2e, 3j, and 4m). There are two main reasons for this: first, the anisotropy of granite, where combinations of large-sized, high-thermal-conductivity mineral grains in certain directions accelerate heat transfer. Additionally, the thermal conductivity of grains also varies with crystal orientations. For example, the thermal conductivity of quartz along the C-axis (10–12 W/m K) is about twice that along the A-axis (6.5–7.5 W/m K). Similarly, mica’s thermal conductivity parallel to the basal plane (0.5–2 W/m K) is higher than perpendicular to it (0.3–1 W/m K).22 Second, microfractures at the granite edges increased liquid nitrogen flow channels and nucleation sites, further accelerating heat escape. In later stages, off-center isotherms indicated high-temperature regions deviating from the center, indicating overall differences in the radial thermal conductivity.

Furthermore, the variation of the average end-face temperature of the core over time was analyzed, as shown in Figure 5. The end-face temperatures showed an exponential decay, with the cooling rate gradually diminishing until the temperature of liquid nitrogen. According to the heat conduction equation, the temperature change of the rock core is positively correlated to the temperature difference. As the temperature difference between the end face and liquid nitrogen decreased, so did the cooling rate. A higher temperature difference promoted collisions between high- and low-energy molecules, enhancing the driving force for heat conduction and accelerating the boiling rate of liquid nitrogen. The heat exchange between liquid nitrogen and high-temperature granite underwent four stages: film boiling, transition boiling, nucleate boiling, and natural convection boiling.23 In the film boiling stage, due to the high superheat of the rock core surface, liquid nitrogen rapidly vaporized, forming a nitrogen gas film that enveloped the rock core, hindering heat transfer and reducing heat flux density. In the transition boiling stage, as the surface superheat was not insufficient to sustain a continuous nitrogen gas film, the film began to rupture. Local areas came into direct contact with liquid nitrogen, which was accompanied by intense bubble nucleation. As the contact area with liquid nitrogen increased, the heat flux density rose. In the nucleate boiling stage, the core surface was longer, lasting several minutes or even longer. This indicated a full contact with the liquid nitrogen, with continuous bubble escape at multiple nucleation sites. Interference between bubble columns inhibited the flow of liquid nitrogen, reducing the number of nucleation sites and their activity. This led to a transition from continuous gas columns to isolated bubbles, decreasing the heat flux density. In the natural convection boiling stage, the surface temperature approached that of liquid nitrogen with superheat and heat flux density nearing zero. The film and transition boiling stages were relatively short, typically lasting only a few seconds. In contrast, the nucleate and natural convection boiling stage increases the trend in heat flux density, leading to an exponential drop in temperature.

Changes in the average temperature of HDRs over time.

Four radial lines (at 0, 90, 180, and 270°) were selected on the end face to analyze the changes in the average temperature gradient over time, as shown in Figure 6. At 0 s, there existed a certain radial temperature fluctuation (typically within 20 °C) due to heat exchange with air when the hot rock was removed from the muffle furnace. As the distance from the center increased, the temperature decreased in a horizontal parabolic pattern, with a greater temperature gradient closer to the edge. The edge surface exchanged heat vigorously with boiling liquid nitrogen, while the center received the cold slowly diffusing inward from the edge. The curve did not resemble smoothness, like those of a homogeneous medium, but showed fluctuations. The asynchrony between surface cooling and internal heat conduction and the discontinuity of the heat conduction process caused this. Additionally, thermal conductivity differences among various mineral grains exacerbated these fluctuations. Initially, the temperature gradient was maximal; from 200 to 600 °C, the maximum value increased from 18.90 to 103.85 °C. As the cold-shock duration increased, the deviation of the right side of the curve from the horizontal direction diminished and eventually flattened, indicating a gradual decrease in the temperature gradient.

Changes in the temperature gradient of HDRs during liquid nitrogen cold shock.

Thermal stress is positively correlated to the temperature gradient. When the radial temperature is uniformly distributed, the expansion and contraction of these regions are consistent and no thermal stress is generated. However, when there is a radial temperature gradient, different regions undergo different thermal deformations, and this nonuniform deformation induces thermal stress. Changes in the thermal stress of HDRs during liquid nitrogen cold shock.

For each core, the thermal stress curve followed a trend of “first increasing (within the first 20–40 s), then rapidly decreasing (40–150 s), and finally slowly decreasing (after 150 s)”, which can be well fitted using a double Gaussian function. At each moment, thermal stress always increases with the distance from the center. The thermal stress at the outer regions (3r/4 and r) was significantly higher than that at the inner regions (r/4 and r/2), with the former being 3.35–4.22 times greater than the latter. This resulted in thermal-induced fractures primarily forming near the outer sides of the core.24 The higher the initial temperature of the HDR, the greater the thermal stress induced by liquid nitrogen cold shock. For reservoir temperatures ranging from 200 to 600 °C, the maximum thermal stresses were 2.57, 3.97, 5.53, 8.45, and 9.29 MPa, while the corresponding tensile strengths were 6.35, 5.23, 4.75, 3.96, and 2.78 MPa. Notably, within the range of 400–600 °C, the induced thermal stress exceeded the tensile strength, causing tensile fractures in the granite.

When HDR was subjected to liquid nitrogen cold shock, it caused dramatic spatiotemporal changes in the temperature field.25 This manifested as a fast cooling rate, a large temperature gradient, and localized temperature fluctuation. The sharp spatial temperature gradients, combined with the differential thermal expansion of mineral grains, induce thermal stress. When this thermal stress exceeded the tensile strength, it led to the formation of tensile fractures.26 The cyclic high-temperature-rapid cooling process caused fatigue damage in granite. On one hand, newly formed microcracks accelerated local cooling; on the other hand, their plasticity increased while tensile strength decreased. As a result, the continuous increase in thermal stress and simultaneous decrease in the damage threshold resulted in more thermal fractures. This was particularly important for HDR at 200–300 °C.

Ultrasonic Transmission Wave Characteristics of Damaged HDR

To characterize the development of pores and fractures inside the damaged HDR, ultrasonic detection was used to examine the ultrasonic transmission wave characteristics of the damaged rock cores. The presence of discontinuities such as pores and fractures caused reflection, refraction, and scattering of the ultrasonic waves during transmission.27 This resulted in energy attenuation, increased arrival time, and frequency shift of the transmitted waves.5 The time-frequency spectra of the ultrasonic transmission waves for rock cores at different initial high temperatures after 0, 1, and 5 liquid nitrogen cold-shock cycles are shown in Figure 8. For ease of comparison, all images are set on the same scale.

Time-frequency spectra of ultrasonic transmission waves in HDRs after liquid nitrogen cold-shock cycles.

In the initial state, the primary frequency peak of the intact rock core appeared as a band, with an intensity exceeding 140, concentrated in the 30–50 kHz range. High-frequency signals appeared in a net-like pattern above 100 kHz, while a small number of low-frequency signals appeared as thin, elongated strips around 20 kHz. After one cold-shock cycle, the primary frequency peak attenuated and differentiated, showing significant temperature sensitivity. From 200 to 600 °C, the intensity of the primary frequency peak decreased successively, from 120 to 60. Specifically, at 500 and 600 °C, the peak splits into two distinct peaks. High-frequency signals diminished and shifted toward midfrequency, nearly disappearing above 500 °C. In contrast, the low-frequency bands exhibited only minor changes, with only slight increases in intensity and broadening of the frequency range. After 5 cold-shock cycles, the primary frequency peak further attenuated, with intensity reducing from 100 to 20 in the 200–600 °C range. Above 300 °C, the primary peak decomposed, with the middle and tail waves between 100–200 μs weakening. At 600 °C, the primary frequency peak shifted to 25.7 kHz. High-frequency signals further decreased, while the low-frequency band broadened.

The ultrasonic P and S wave velocities of various high-temperature cores subjected to 1–5 cycles of liquid nitrogen cold shock are shown in Figure 9. In the initial state, the granite’s P wave velocity ranged from 3.57 to 3.68 km/s, and the S wave velocity ranged from 1.28 to 1.44 km/s. As cycles increased, both P and S wave velocities exhibited a trend of “rapid decline at first, followed by a slower decline”, which was well fitted using a binomial exponential function. The first cold shock contributed the most to core damage, with initial P and S wave velocity decreases of 26.78–70.25 and 19.05–51.49%, respectively. This first shock accounted for 82.23–91.94 and 57.37–85.08% of the total decreases over 5 cycles. The influence of temperature on core damage was very significant, with a notable damage threshold existing between 300 and 400 °C, above which wave velocities notably decreased. Taking one cold shock as an example, the average drops in P and S wave velocities in the low damage temperature range (200–300 °C) were 30.43 ± 5.17 and 22.64 ± 5.08%, while in the high damage temperature range (400–600 °C), the average drops were 55.02 ± 13.73 and 40.88 ± 11.62%. High-temperature differences and multiple cycles caused significant damage to HDRs, whereas for rocks at relatively lower temperatures, cyclic cold shocks become particularly important in driving the damage process.

Changes in ultrasonic wave velocities of HDRs under liquid nitrogen cold-shock cycles.

The changes in ultrasonic transmission waves were divided into three stages: Stage I, the core was slightly damaged, characterized by only a small number of pores. The energy absorption effect of these pores led to a slight delay in the arrival time, a minor energy attenuation, and a small decrease in the frequency of high-frequency components. In stage II, the core was moderately damaged and was characterized by numerous pores and a few large-scale fracture faces. Due to the difference in wave impedance on both sides of these interfaces, the ultrasonic waves underwent multiple reflections, scattering, and mode conversions (P-waves converting to S-waves) when passing through these fractures. Additionally, the friction between air molecules and the rock matrix under wave vibrations converted mechanical energy into thermal energy. These resulted in a significant increase in transmission wave arrival time, substantial energy dissipation, and rapid attenuation of high-frequency components. Stage III, the core was severely damaged, characterized by a large number of penetrating and interwoven fracture faces. The fracture network made the propagation path of the waves more tortuous. The microsliding of fracture faces and the microvibrations of fragmented grains dissipated and absorbed energy through damping effects. Furthermore, the fracture network acted as a “low-pass filter”,28 absorbing high-frequency energy while allowing low-frequency energy to pass through, leading to a reduction, dispersion, and drift in frequency.29 The attenuation of the transmission wave was closely related to the number, orientation, and size of pores and fractures within the core: the more developed the pores and fractures, the more significant the lag in the arrival time, the drift in frequency, and the attenuation of energy.

Pore Size Distribution Characteristics of Damaged HDR

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance uses 1H (water) as a probe to invert pore distribution by measuring the relaxation characteristics of pore water under an external magnetic field.30 The relaxation characteristics of pore water are constrained by pore walls.31 When the pore size is large or the surface-to-volume ratio is low, the interaction between water molecules and pore walls is stronger, resulting in a longer transverse relaxation time.32 The T2 distribution curves of different high-temperature cores after 0, 1, and 5 liquid nitrogen shock cycles are shown in Figure 10. The T2 distribution curves can reflect the pore size distribution characteristics of damaged rock cores, where a larger T2 value on the horizontal axis indicates a larger pore size and a larger value on the vertical axis indicates a larger pore volume of the corresponding pore size. In the T2 distribution curves, each peak represents a group of pores with similar relaxation characteristics and morphological features. Notably, a Gaussian distribution was innovatively used for peak fitting of the T2 distribution curves, thereby enabling a further comparison of changes in pore components.

Changes in NMR T2 spectra of HDRs under liquid nitrogen cold-shock cycles.

In the initial state, the overall pore development of granite was minimal, with the peak values being only 0.02–0.04%. The pores showed a bimodal distribution, dominated by micropores, followed by mesopores. Granite, as an igneous rock, has a slow crystallization rate during its diagenetic process. Coupled with the immense pressure from the overlying rock strata, the grains are tightly arranged in an interlocking structure, resulting in a scarcity of pores. After one cycle of cold shock, the curve amplitude increased, showing strong temperature sensitivity; within the range of 200–600 °C, the peak value increased from 0.03 to 0.18%. Between 200 and 400 °C, the increase in the micropore peak was predominant. After five cycles of cold shock, the curve amplitude increased significantly, with peak values rising from 0.07 to 0.32% within the same temperature range of 200–600 °C. The increase and rightward expansion of the mesopore peak were most pronounced.

Gaussian multipeak fitting revealed two evolution patterns of the HDR pore structure during cold-shock cycles. Pattern I corresponds to 200 and 300 °C, where the micropore peak increased significantly. At this stage, thermal stress was relatively low, and only a small number of new intergranular micropores formed at the boundaries of differentially deformed grains. Pattern II corresponds to 400–600 °C, where the mesopore peak increased and expanded, with both amplitude and range exceeding those of the micropore peak. At this stage, greater thermal stress induced some new mesopores at grain boundaries and interiors along with the extension and expansion of existing pores. Cyclic thermal stress stimulated the generation of numerous mesopores and promoted the expansion and interconnection of existing micro- and mesopores, forming larger-sized pores.

Furthermore, the pores were categorized into micro-, meso-, and macropores, and the changes in total porosity and pore components were analyzed, as shown in Figure 11. The total porosity increased, with final porosities corresponding to 200–600 °C being 1.87, 1.67, 2.15, 3.61, and 7.54%, respectively. There was an abrupt point in the increase process: at 200–400 °C, a sharp increase occurs only after 2–4 cycles, whereas at 500–600 °C, a sharp increase occurs after just a single cycle.

Changes in pore components of HDRs under liquid nitrogen cold-shock cycles.

This indicated that at low cold-shock temperature differences, rock core damage must accumulate to a certain degree before causing a “qualitative change”, confirming the importance of cyclic cold shock. At high-temperature differences, severe damage occurred after the first cold shock. Mesopores consistently occupied the largest proportion of the pore space in this medium-grained granite. At 200–400 °C, the increase in micropores was secondary; at 500–600 °C, the increase in macropores was secondary. This corroborated the observation that micropores increased primarily under lower thermal stress, while macropores increased significantly under higher thermal stress.

Three-Dimensional Pore and Fracture Characteristics of Damaged HDR

The three-dimensional pore-fracture structures of various high-temperature rock cores after five cycles of liquid nitrogen cold shock were captured through CT scanning. The pore-fracture morphologies from different layers (100, 300, 500, 700, and 900 layers) of the damaged cores are shown in Figure 12.

Pore and fracture at different layers of HDRs after liquid nitrogen cold-shock cycles.

Due to the varying X-ray attenuation coefficients of minerals with different densities (3.2, 2.65, and 2.56 g/cm3 for mica, quartz, and feldspar, respectively), distinct brightness differences were observed among grains within the granite.33 Mica, quartz, and feldspar appeared to be progressively darker. In the CT slices, mica appeared as sparse fine dots and blocks, quartz as grayish-white flakes, and feldspar as dark gray flakes. At 200 °C, only a very small number of ultralarge pores existed at grain boundaries. At 300 °C, a few medium-length, finer microfractures formed. At 400 °C, these fractures extended and widened. At 500 °C, the number of fractures increased, and their sizes expanded, locally interconnecting to form interwoven nodes, primarily at the edges of quartz grains. At 600 °C, numerous fractures developed with visibly wider main fractures. Further, a large number of branch fractures derived around the main fractures, interweaving and interconnecting to form a mesh-like fracture network, widely distributed along both grain boundaries and interiors.

To further analyze the complexity and randomness of fracture, the fracture orientation distribution in the damaged core slices was statistically analyzed, as shown in Figure 13. The fracture distribution transitioned from a single direction to a multidirection random distribution. At 200 °C, the orientation distribution was elongated and conical, determined by the orientation of only a very small number of ultralarge pores. At 300 °C, the fracture angles resembled a scissor shape with a few intragranular microfractures approaching similar directions. At 400 °C, the range of fracture angles broadened further, with high-frequency peaks differing by nearly 90°, indicating the derivation of branching fractures. At 500 and 600 °C, the fracture angle curves approximated a gear-like shape, tending toward a uniform random distribution in multiple directions. At this stage, multilevel fracture networks formed in many locations, significantly enhancing the connectivity and complexity of fractures.

At 400 °C, the most probable pore size increased to 777.11 μm, with the number of pores rising to 83. The right branch slightly increased, mainly corresponding to the further expansion of existing microfractures and the formation of new ones. Activation Mechanism of Pore Fracture in HDR Induced by Liquid Nitrogen Cold Shock

The thermal fracturing process of various high-temperature HDR under liquid nitrogen cold shock was simulated by using PFC2D. A cluster model with parallel-bonded particles was chosen to simulate different mineral grains.35 In this model, each cluster unit is a collection of multiple particles with parallel bond contacts applied both within and between units. However, the bond strength within units is greater than that between units. This method combines circular single particles into irregularly shaped particle clusters, enabling the simulation of mineral grains with diverse morphologies.36 Tian et al.37 used the cluster model to simulate crack evolution in granite during the heating process, verifying its effectiveness. A granite model dominated by quartz, feldspar, and mica was established. Temperature shock leads to changes in particle radii and interparticle contact forces, which in turn affect the stress field distribution. To more accurately align the model properties with the experimental results, calibration of the mesoscopic parameters was conducted. Initial values were assigned to the mesoscopic parameters for quartz, potassium feldspar, plagioclase, and mica followed by uniaxial compression simulations. The simulated outcomes were then compared to those from uniaxial compression tests on granite. Simulation results of HDR fracture development during liquid nitrogen cold-shock processes.

When HDR is subjected to a liquid nitrogen cooling shock, the mineral grains contract, altering the positions and contact states of the particles. This leads to increased local particle contact forces and stress concentrations.39 Additionally, due to the differing thermal expansion coefficients among different types of grains, quartz deforms significantly more than other mineral grains.40 Consequently, stress concentrations are more likely to occur within quartz grains and at their interfaces with other grains. When the normal tensile stress between particles exceeds the normal strength, tensile fractures form. The irregular shape of the grains further exacerbates the stress gradient, making these tensile fractures more prone to appearing at the intersections of multiple grains.

As the cold-shock temperature difference increases, grain deformation enlarges, the number of regions with localized stress concentration increases, and the probability of normal stress exceeding the normal strength increases, leading to the formation of numerous tensile fractures. These fractures intersect and expand along preferential paths, gradually forming wide, throughgoing main fractures and multiple branch fractures. The multiplication of fracture numbers in adjacent regions increases the chances for fracture intersection, eventually leading to the interconnection of the main and branch fractures into a complex network. Furthermore, with the cyclic effect of cold shock, damage to the HDR gradually accumulates. On one hand, this intensifies the unevenness and concentration of stress distribution. On the other hand, it reduces the intergranular bond strength and the failure threshold of the HDR. The progressive convergence, or even intersection, of normal stresses and failure threshold lowers the damage threshold of the HDR and increases the degree of fatigue damage. This ensures the continuous development of thermally induced fractures in subsequent cycles.

Conclusions

Multiscale pore fracture development was characterized by ultrasonic detection and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance. Then, the 3D pore-fracture network was reconstructed using CT scanning for permeability evaluation. Based on the PFC thermal fracturing simulation, the pore-fracture evolution mechanism was revealed. Ultimately, a cascade mechanism of “temperature field distribution–thermal stress-induced failure–pore fracture development–three-dimensional pore network seepage” was established. The current study identified several limitations, including the restricted temperature range captured by the infrared thermal imager during liquid nitrogen immersion, which hindered complete data acquisition of the heat transfer process, the absence of an effective thermal–fluid–solid coupling model in the numerical simulations of hot dry rock permeability, and the use of relatively small granite samples, which resulted in a more idealized experimental setup. Future research will focus on optimizing the experimental platform for real-time monitoring of thermal shocks, developing advanced numerical models using realistic pore-fracture networks, and conducting large-scale liquid nitrogen fracturing experiments with improved high-temperature and high-pressure injection platforms to better simulate practical conditions. These planned improvements aim to enhance the understanding of liquid nitrogen’s effects on hot dry rock and support its potential application in geothermal systems.
