Latent iron deficiency as a silent driver of donor attrition in high-frequency plateletpheresis donors: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Liqin Jin, Bin Zhang, Ying Shen, Zilin Wang, Weiguo Wu, Zhihua Chen, Xinhan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-frequency plateletpheresis is crucial for meeting transfusion demands but poses a significant risk of cumulative iron depletion in donors. Current screening protocols relying solely on hemoglobin (Hb) fail to detect latent iron deficiency (LID), a condition where depleted iron stores coexist with normal Hb levels. This study investigated the longitudinal trajectory of iron parameters in high-frequency plateletpheresis donors and quantified the impact of LID on donor deferral and attrition. A retrospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted on 538 eligible plateletpheresis donors at a regional blood center in China from January 2021 to December 2024. Donors were stratified into high-frequency (≥ 10 donations/year) and low-frequency (< 10 donations/year) groups. Longitudinal changes in serum ferritin and Hb were modeled using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE), adjusting for time-varying inflammation (WBC) and demographics. The association between baseline LID (ferritin < 30 ng/mL) and temporary Hb deferral was assessed using a multivariable modified Poisson regression. Predictors of donor attrition (lapse > 12 months) were identified using a time-dependent Cox proportional hazards model. High-frequency donors exhibited a precipitous decline in serum ferritin over four years (β = -0.42, p < 0.001), with a significant time-by-frequency interaction (β = -0.15, p < 0.001), while Hb levels remained stable. Baseline LID was a potent predictor of future temporary deferral (Adjusted Relative Risk = 3.8, 95% CI: 2.6–5.5). Survival analysis revealed that both the experience of a deferral event (Hazard Ratio [HR] = 2.42, 95% CI: 1.85–3.16) and baseline LID (HR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.21–2.06) were independent predictors of donor attrition. Subgroup analyses confirmed these risks were consistent across sex, age, and residence status. High-frequency plateletpheresis induces progressive LID that is masked by stable Hb levels. LID serves as a silent precursor to both operational deferral and long-term donor attrition through physiological and psychological pathways. Integrating routine ferritin monitoring and personalized donation intervals into donor management strategies is essential to safeguard donor health and ensure a sustainable platelet supply.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06992-4.

Introduction

Platelet components are indispensable in modern medicine, providing critical support for patients with hematologic malignancies, those undergoing major surgery or organ transplantation, and trauma victims [1]. Consequently, the clinical demand for platelets has seen a steady increase, placing significant and sustained pressure on blood centers to maintain a safe and adequate supply. Apheresis technology, which allows for the collection of one or more therapeutic doses of platelets from a single donor, has become a cornerstone of this supply chain. This method enables more frequent donations compared to whole blood donation—up to 24 times per year in many countries, including China and the United States—making the recruitment and retention of dedicated, high-frequency apheresis donors a global public health imperative to meet transfusion needs [2].

While apheresis donation is highly efficient, a growing body of evidence highlights an important iatrogenic consequence: the depletion of donor iron stores. Each procedure, though designed to return red blood cells to the donor, results in an unavoidable loss of erythrocytes within the collection set and from samples drawn for testing. When combined with insufficient dietary iron uptake and inadequate inter-donation recovery intervals, this repeated erythrocyte loss leads to a cumulative iron deficit over time [3]. Studies have documented a high prevalence of iron deficiency (ID) among frequent plateletpheresis donors. For instance, Spencer et al. [3] found that 50% of male plateletpheresis donors had low ferritin levels, with the risk increasing in direct proportion to donation frequency [3]. Similarly, a 2022 study by Sab et al. confirmed that regular plateletpheresis donors exhibited significantly lower median serum ferritin levels compared to first-time donors, establishing a clear link between donation patterns and depleted iron reserves [4]. This link is particularly relevant in Asian populations; for instance, a landmark study of Hong Kong Chinese blood donors by Lee et al. [5] demonstrated a significant decline in iron stores as donation frequency increased, highlighting the vulnerability of this regional cohort to cumulative iron depletion [5].

This progressive iron depletion extends beyond laboratory findings and has tangible consequences for the donor pool’s stability. While the extent to which non-anemic iron deficiency causes overt clinical symptoms remains debated, it is undisputedly a primary precursor to hemoglobin-related temporary or permanent donor deferral [6]. Furthermore, these subclinical physiological shifts may lead to a donor voluntarily ceasing donation (lapsation), thereby undermining long-term retention efforts. A large-scale analysis has confirmed that lower ferritin levels are a significant predictor of lower return rates and shorter donation careers, compromising the availability of experienced and reliable donors [7]. Maintaining donor health is therefore intrinsically linked to maintaining the platelet supply.

Despite the established connection between frequent donation and iron loss, significant gaps remain in the literature. Most studies on the matter are cross-sectional and have not longitudinally tracked iron parameter trajectories over extended periods of high-frequency donation. As a recent evidence map by Schroyens et al. [8] focusing on plasma donors concluded, there is a scarcity of prospective, controlled studies with long-term follow-up to determine the health effects of different donation frequencies [8]. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant to dedicated apheresis platelet donor cohorts, where the precise relationship between evolving iron status and the propensity for donor lapsation has not been adequately quantified. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the longitudinal changes in iron metabolism in a cohort of high-frequency apheresis platelet donors and to determine the impact of these changes on donor retention.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center, retrospective, longitudinal cohort study was conducted to investigate the dynamic associations between donation frequency, iron metabolism trajectories, and donor retention among plateletpheresis donors at the Kunshan Blood Center in Eastern China. Data were systematically extracted from the center’s Laboratory Information System (LIS) covering a 4-year period that spans both the COVID-19 pandemic and the post-pandemic era, from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2024. The study protocol strictly adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kunshan Blood Station (Approval No. KSB-IRB-2025-003). Given the retrospective nature of the study, which involved the analysis of anonymized administrative and laboratory data without influencing clinical care or donor routine, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All personal identifiers were permanently removed prior to data analysis to ensure strict confidentiality and compliance with local data protection regulations. To provide operational context, our center adheres strictly to national guidelines for donor screening. Pre-donation capillary hemoglobin is a mandatory release criterion; donors failing this test face a mandatory temporary deferral (typically ranging from 3 to 6 months) and must present a normal venous CBC before resuming donation. Conversely, serum ferritin is not a statutory pre-donation requirement but is measured periodically as part of an advanced donor health surveillance initiative. During the study period, donors presenting with LID (ferritin < 30 ng/mL with normal Hb) were not statutorily deferred. However, they routinely received a digital health notification advising them of their depleted iron stores, recommending increased dietary iron intake, and suggesting a voluntary extension of their inter-donation intervals.

Study population

The study cohort was identified through a systematic query of the Blood Center’s LIS. Inclusion criteria comprised all of the following: (1) Validated history of at least one successful plateletpheresis donation during the recruitment period (2021), meeting the National Health Examination Standards for Blood Donors (GB 18467 − 2011) regarding hemoglobin levels (Hb ≥ 115 g/L for females, ≥ 120 g/L for males) and vital signs; (2) Age between 18 and 60 years at baseline; (3) Availability of complete baseline data including demographic information, complete blood count (CBC), and serum ferritin levels.

Exclusion criteria encompassed any of the following to ensure longitudinal cohort purity: (1) Incident or late-disclosed history of mild non-ID anemia (e.g., thalassemia trait) or other hematological disorders identified during the 4-year follow-up period that would fundamentally alter iron metabolism independent of donation frequency [9]; (2) Incident or newly identified chronic systemic diseases (e.g., early-stage chronic kidney disease, malignancy) during the study period; (3) Extreme outliers in iron parameters (e.g., Ferritin > 500 ng/mL or < 5 ng/mL) lacking clinical explanation, suggesting potential data entry errors or occult pathology [10]. Importantly, a cessation of donation (lapse > 12 months) was passively observed via the LIS and defined as “Donor Attrition” (a primary study outcome) regardless of the donors’ contact information status, thereby preventing any selection bias related to tracing capabilities.

Data collection and measurements

For the cross-sectional comparison of baseline characteristics, demographic and laboratory parameters were strictly extracted from the very first eligible donation record of each participant during the baseline year (2021). For the subsequent longitudinal modeling, all repeated measurements across the 4-year study period were utilized. Demographic variables were rigorously verified; specifically, residence status was dichotomized into “local” versus “non-local” based on the first six digits of the Resident Identity Card or validated residential addresses, serving as a critical proxy to control for attrition driven by population migration rather than donor intent. ABO blood group was also retrieved as a stable biological identifier. While body mass index (BMI) is a standard anthropometric measure, height data were occasionally incomplete in the general central donor registration database. However, accurate height and weight are mandatory inputs required by the apheresis devices (e.g., Trima Accel, Amicus) prior to initiating any procedure. Therefore, the Total Blood Volume (TBV)—automatically computed based on the donor’s real-time sex, height, and weight via the Nadler equation [11]—was directly extracted from the devices’ specific procedure logs, providing a complete and robust surrogate metric for body constitution even when general administrative records lacked anthropometric details. Crucially, peer-reviewed literature confirms that contemporary apheresis machines rely on this validated method for calculating total blood volume to safely monitor and restrict the extracorporeal volume during donation, thereby ensuring donor safety [12].

Socioeconomic indicators such as occupation and education level were excluded from the analysis due to high rates of missingness and self-reporting inconsistencies in the registration system. Hematological parameters obtained from the pre-donation Complete Blood Count (CBC) included hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (HCT), red blood cell count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), mean platelet volume (MPV), and red cell indices (MCV, MCH, MCHC). Red blood cell distribution width (RDW), a sensitive early marker of anisocytosis associated with iron restriction [13], was retrieved where available. Crucially, serum ferritin levels—the primary established biomarker for assessing whole-body iron stores—were extracted whenever periodic testing was performed during the follow-up period to construct the longitudinal iron profile. Consistent with the targeted and voluntary nature of this surveillance initiative, ferritin was not measured at every single donation episode, yielding an unbalanced repeated-measures dataset. Blood samples destined for serum ferritin testing were routinely collected directly from the diversion pouch immediately at the onset of venipuncture, prior to the initiation of the apheresis fluid cycle. Therefore, these measurements accurately and rigorously reflect the donor’s exact pre-donation baseline iron status without any hemodilution artifacts, even though the biochemical assays were conducted subsequently. To ensure measurement consistency and allow for the accurate interpretation of iron parameters, all Complete Blood Count (CBC) analyses were routinely performed using a Sysmex XN-1000 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corp., Kobe, Japan), and serum ferritin concentrations were quantified via electrochemiluminescence immunoassay on a Roche cobas e601 platform (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) utilizing manufacturer-provided standardized reagents.

The primary independent variables (exposures) were categorized by donation intensity and temporal context. Donation frequency was defined as the number of successful plateletpheresis procedures performed within a calendar year. To capture total iron burden and donor commitment, specific behavioral metrics including product yield (single vs. double unit), inter-donation interval (days), and cumulative career donations were also aggregated. Participants were stratified into “high-frequency” donors (≥ 10 donations/year) and “low-frequency” donors (< 10 donations/year). This threshold was established based on the median distribution of the cohort and is supported by extensive literature indicating a dose-dependent relationship between donation frequency and the risk of iron store depletion [14, 15]. The study timeline was segmented into two distinct phases to assess the environmental impact on donor retention: the “Pandemic Phase” (January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2022), characterized by strict “Zero-COVID” policies that were officially dismantled in December 2022 [16], and the “Post-Pandemic Phase” (January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024), representing the return to routine blood center operations and societal normalcy following this major policy transition. Seasonality was harmonized by categorizing donation dates into Spring (March-May), Summer (June-August), Autumn (September-November), and Winter (December-February) to adjust for known seasonal fluctuations in donor availability and hemoglobin levels [17, 18].

Outcomes were defined using established transfusion medicine criteria to ensure comparability with international benchmarks. The primary physiological outcome was ID, defined as a serum ferritin level < 30 ng/mL. This threshold is widely recognized to represent depleted iron stores in blood donors even in the absence of overt anemia, a definition supported by authoritative guidelines and large-scale donor studies [19, 20]. The primary behavioral outcome was Donor Attrition (Lapse), defined as an inactivity period of greater than 12 consecutive months following the last donation, a standard interval used to classify donors as “lapsed” in contemporary retention studies [21]. A secondary intermediate outcome was “Temporary Deferral due to Low Hemoglobin,” recorded when a donor presented for donation but was rejected solely because their capillary or venous hemoglobin fell below the national standard. Adverse reactions were encoded according to the International Society of Blood Transfusion (ISBT) standard reaction codes extracted from the LIS, with the absence of a code interpreted as a reaction-free donation.

Data quality control

To ensure the fidelity and reproducibility of the retrospective dataset, a rigorous multi-tiered data verification protocol was implemented prior to statistical processing. First, automated logical range checks were programmed into the extraction algorithm to filter biologically implausible values, including hemoglobin levels below 60 g/L or exceeding 200 g/L for healthy donors, and serum ferritin concentrations below 0 ng/mL. Temporal inconsistencies, such as a subsequent donation date preceding a previous one, were also flagged and corrected.

Second, to validate the accuracy of the automated LIS extraction, a random stratified sample comprising 10% of the total cohort underwent manual cross-verification against the original paper-based donor health questionnaires and laboratory logbooks. This auditing process was conducted independently by two trained data managers blinded to the study objectives. Inter-rater reliability for categorical variables, including adverse reaction codes and deferral reasons, was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient, yielding a robust agreement of 0.92. For continuous variables such as hemoglobin, ferritin, and Total Blood Volume, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated, achieving an excellent consistency of 0.98. Any discrepancies identified during this audit were adjudicated by a senior transfusion medicine specialist to reach a consensus.

Third, regarding missing data management, a complete-case analysis strategy was adopted for the primary outcome variables; participants missing baseline serum ferritin or hemoglobin data were excluded from the cohort as defined in the inclusion criteria. Crucially, because Total Blood Volume (TBV) is a mandatory functional parameter required for the apheresis devices to initiate any procedure, this variable was extracted directly and completely from the procedural logs with zero missingness, eliminating the need for any retroactive estimation. For other minor laboratory covariates displaying missingness of less than 5% (such as occasional missing specific red cell indices), Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) was employed under the assumption of data being missing at random (MAR). Five imputed datasets were generated, and pooled estimates were derived according to Rubin’s rules to minimize bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R software, version 4.5.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms; normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using the independent Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To model the longitudinal trajectories of serum ferritin and hemoglobin, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were constructed. We selected an exchangeable working correlation matrix to obtain robust standard errors, prioritizing marginal population-averaged effects over individual correlation structures. Given the right-skewed distribution of serum ferritin data, a Gamma distribution with a log link function was specified; for hemoglobin, a normal distribution with an identity link was used. The primary fixed effects included time, donation frequency group (High vs. Low), seasonality, and the “Time × Frequency” interaction term to assess divergent trends. The model was adjusted for baseline covariates including age, sex, ABO blood group, residence status, inter-donation intervals, and cumulative career donations, while white blood cell count (WBC) was entered as a time-varying covariate to control for the confounding influence of acute-phase reactions on ferritin levels.

Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate donor retention probabilities stratified by baseline ferritin levels (< 30 vs. ≥30 ng/mL), with differences between survival curves assessed via the Log-rank test. Subsequently, a time-dependent multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to identify independent predictors of donor attrition. Variables yielding a P-value < 0.10 in univariate analysis, alongside clinically significant a priori predictors such as baseline ID, high-frequency status, and cumulative career donations, were entered into the multivariate model to calculate Hazard Ratios (HR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Crucially, the experience of a temporary deferral was modeled dynamically as a time-varying covariate to strictly control for immortal time bias.

Finally, to ensure the robustness of the findings, subgroup analyses were performed by stratifying the cohort by sex and age group to test for interaction effects. Sensitivity analyses were conducted across three domains: redefining the attrition threshold from 12 to 18 months, excluding data from the strict lockdown period (March–May 2022) to rule out bias caused by external environmental restrictions, and applying a stricter baseline iron deficiency diagnostic threshold (ferritin < 20 ng/mL) to evaluate the prognostic stability of the cut-off. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant selection and baseline characteristics

During the baseline year (January 1 to December 31, 2021), our blood center registered a total of approximately 2,455 active plateletpheresis donors. Because serum ferritin testing is not a mandatory statutory pre-donation requirement but rather part of a targeted, voluntary health surveillance initiative for established donors, routine testing was not performed universally across the entire donor base. Consequently, an initial pool of 715 donors—representing approximately 29.1% of the total active apheresis population—who participated in this baseline ferritin screening program was identified from the LIS. Following the application of strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, 177 individuals were excluded: 89 for incomplete baseline iron or hematological data, 42 for a documented history of non-iron deficiency anemia (e.g., thalassemia) or other disqualifying hematological disorders, 35 for age < 18 or > 60 years, and 11 for biologically implausible outliers in serum ferritin levels. Consequently, a final cohort of 538 eligible donors was enrolled and longitudinally tracked through December 31, 2024. Data extraction and validation were completed by February 1, 2025. Based on their donation status during the post-pandemic phase (2023–2024), the cohort was stratified into the Retained Group (n = 336, 62.5%) and the Lapsed Group (n = 202, 37.5%). The baseline characteristics of these two groups are comprehensively presented in Table 1. Regarding biological identifiers, the overall distribution of ABO blood groups differed significantly (p = 0.032), driven largely by an overrepresentation of type O donors in the Lapsed Group (40.6% vs. 31.3%). In terms of donation behavior, the Lapsed Group exhibited a higher intensity of donation during the recruitment year, characterized by a higher mean annual frequency (13.8 vs. 9.2 donations, p < 0.001) and a greater proportion of double-unit collections (44.6% vs. 32.1%, p = 0.004), despite having a lower cumulative career donation history (p < 0.001). Longitudinal trajectories of iron parameters

To delineate the dynamic impact of donation intensity on iron stores and erythropoiesis, GEE were constructed to model the longitudinal trajectories of serum ferritin and hemoglobin over the 4-year study period (2021–2024).

For serum ferritin, after adjusting for time-varying inflammation (WBC), demographics (age, sex, residence), and biological traits (ABO blood group), the multivariate model presented in Table 2 revealed a significant main effect of donation frequency. Furthermore, specific donation behaviors significantly influenced iron stores: longer inter-donation intervals were positively associated with ferritin recovery (β = 0.04 per 10-day increment, p < 0.001), whereas a higher number of cumulative career donations exerted a persistent suppressive effect on ferritin levels (β = −0.03 per 10-donation increment, p = 0.008). Donors in the High-Frequency group (≥ 10 donations/year) exhibited consistently lower ferritin levels compared to the Low-Frequency group (β = −0.42, p < 0.001). Crucially, the Time × Frequency interaction term was statistically significant (β = −0.15, p < 0.001), indicating that the rate of iron depletion was substantially accelerated in high-frequency donors over time. As visualized in Fig. 2A, while the Low-Frequency group maintained relatively stable iron stores, the High-Frequency group demonstrated a precipitous decline, particularly during the Pandemic Phase (2021–2022), with mean ferritin levels dropping toward the ID threshold (< 30 ng/mL). Association between iron depletion and temporary deferral

To assess the operational impact of compromised iron stores on donor eligibility, we analyzed the incidence of temporary deferrals due to low hemoglobin during the follow-up period. The cohort was stratified based on baseline iron status into an Iron-Deficient (ID) group (Ferritin < 30 ng/mL, n = 159) and an Iron-Normal group (Ferritin ≥ 30 ng/mL, n = 379). As illustrated in Fig. 3, baseline ID served as a potent indicator of future deferral risk. In the univariate analysis, donors in the ID group experienced a significantly higher cumulative incidence of Hb-related deferrals compared to their iron-replete counterparts (24.5% vs. 5.8%; χ² = 48.21, p < 0.001). To control for potential confounders identified in the baseline characteristics, a multivariable modified Poisson regression model was constructed, adjusting for age, sex, residence status, and baseline donation frequency. The analysis confirmed that baseline ID remained an independent predictor of future deferral, with an Adjusted Relative Risk (aRR) of 3.8 (95% CI: 2.6–5.5, p < 0.001). Furthermore, regarding the severity of ineligibility, donors in the ID group were significantly more likely to experience recurrent deferrals (≥ 2 rejection episodes). Predictors of donor attrition: survival analysis

To identify the determinants of donor loss, survival analysis was conducted with “donor attrition” (lapse > 12 months) as the primary endpoint. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, with no significant violations detected for the included covariates. Kaplan-Meier survival estimates revealed significant stratifications based on physiological and biological baselines. As shown in Fig. 4A, donors with baseline ID (Ferritin < 30 ng/mL) exhibited a significantly steeper decline in retention probability compared to iron-replete donors (Log-rank χ² = 18.45, p < 0.001). Similarly, exploratory analysis by blood group (Fig. 4B) indicated that donors with blood type O had a significantly shorter median retention time compared to non-O types (p = 0.042). To isolate independent risk factors while strictly controlling for immortal time bias, a time-dependent multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was constructed (Table 3). In this model, “Temporary Deferral” was treated as a time-dependent covariate, meaning donors contributed person-time to the “exposed” group only from the date of their first deferral event onward. First, to rule out the possibility that the standard 12-month inactivity threshold might misclassify donors who merely took an extended break as “lapsed,” Model B (Strict Definition) redefined donor attrition as a lapse of > 18 consecutive months. Under this more conservative criterion, the total number of attrition events decreased from 202 to 168. Despite the reduced event rate, the predictive value of Baseline ID remained robust and statistically significant (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.15–2.01, p = 0.003). Similarly, the impact of Temporary Deferral remained high (HR = 2.35, p < 0.001), confirming that physiological barriers lead to long-term disengagement rather than temporary pauses.

Second, to address the potential bias introduced by the strict “Zero-COVID” lockdown measures implemented in the Kunshan/Shanghai region during the spring of 2022, Model C (Excluding Lockdown Period) was conducted by removing all person-time and event records from March 1 to May 31, 2022, from the analysis. The results corroborated the primary findings; the association between Baseline Iron Deficiency and attrition remained consistent and statistically significant (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.24–2.14, p < 0.001) after excluding the environmental noise of the lockdown period.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of high-frequency plateletpheresis donors, revealing a critical pathway from intensive donation schedules to latent ID and, ultimately, to donor attrition. While previous studies have focused on iron depletion, this is one of the first longitudinal cohorts to quantify the direct trajectory from high-frequency donation to ID, subsequent operational deferral, and final donor attrition in a Chinese population. Our principal findings, derived from robust generalized estimating equation and time-dependent Cox proportional hazards models, demonstrate that high-frequency donation induces a precipitous decline in serum ferritin levels while hemoglobin concentrations remain deceptively stable. More importantly, we identified both ID at baseline and the experience of a temporary deferral as powerful, independent predictors of long-term donor loss. These results challenge the prevailing reliance on hemoglobin alone for donor monitoring and uncover a crucial, yet often overlooked, mechanism driving the attrition of dedicated apheresis donors.

The kinetics of iron depletion observed in our cohort underscore the substantial physiological cost of high-frequency plateletpheresis. Unlike whole-blood donation, where a single large iron loss of 200–250 mg is well-recognized, the attrition of iron in apheresis is more insidious. Each procedure involves a smaller, incidental loss of red blood cells and plasma, estimated to be around 30–50 mg of iron [4]. However, the significantly shorter inter-donation intervals permitted for plateletpheresis create a cumulative deficit that rapidly outpaces the body’s capacity for iron absorption and replenishment [14]. Our GEE model’s significant time-by-frequency interaction term visually represented in Fig. 2, vividly illustrates this dynamic; the high-frequency group exhibited a much steeper ferritin decline compared to the low-frequency group, whose hemoglobin levels remained unchanged, masking the developing iron-deficient state. This finding is consistent with large-scale observational studies, which established the high prevalence of ID and long-term ferritin decline among frequent donors [4, 22, 23]. Furthermore, our observation of seasonal ferritin troughs during summer months aligns with reports from other latitudes, which posit that factors such as hemodilution, dietary changes, or low-grade inflammatory responses could contribute to this cyclical pattern, adding another layer of complexity to iron homeostasis in this population [24].

A key finding of this research is the establishment of latent ID as a silent precursor to hemoglobin-related deferral, transforming a subclinical biochemical deficit into a significant operational challenge. The adjusted relative risk of 3.8 demonstrates that donors with baseline ID are nearly four times more likely to be deferred for low hemoglobin in the future. This occurs because serum ferritin is a far more sensitive barometer of iron stores than hemoglobin. When ferritin levels fall below a critical threshold, typically cited as < 30 ng/mL, the bone marrow is deprived of the iron required for efficient red blood cell production, a state known as iron-deficient erythropoiesis (IDE) [25]. The consequences of IDE extend beyond the donor, as preclinical studies suggest it can also impair the quality and post-transfusion recovery of the resulting red blood cells [26]. As Kiss J.E. highlighted, hemoglobin is a poor indicator of early-stage ID; IDE begins at ferritin levels well within the ‘normal’ range for many laboratories [4]. Our data strongly support this concept, showing that once a donor enters this state, their ability to maintain a stable hemoglobin level above the deferral cutoff is compromised. The extremely high odds of recurrent deferral (OR = 7.6) for those deferred once suggests the establishment of a vicious cycle. Once a donor’s iron reserves are depleted to the point of IDE, recovery is slow, making them highly susceptible to subsequent deferrals, which ultimately contributes to donor frustration and attrition.

Our Cox model compellingly disentangled two distinct, yet synergistic, mechanisms driving donor attrition: psychological frustration and physiological exhaustion. The first pathway, mediated by deferral, is psychological. A temporary deferral was the single strongest predictor of attrition (HR = 2.42), supporting the notion that being turned away is a significant demotivating event. This experience can undermine a donor’s sense of self-efficacy and altruistic purpose, consistent with studies showing that deferral negatively impacts donor retention by creating feelings of rejection and inconvenience [27]. The second, and perhaps more novel, pathway is purely physiological. Baseline ID independently predicted attrition (HR = 1.58), even after accounting for the effect of deferral. This finding implies that donors are ceasing to donate not just because they are told they cannot, but because they subtly feel they cannot. This may be explained by the potential, though clinically debated, subclinical effects of LID, as iron is a critical cofactor in cellular bioenergetics and neurotransmitter synthesis, essential for maintaining energy levels and neurological function [28]. While some studies suggest that low ferritin, even with normal hemoglobin, may correlate with symptoms such as fatigue or sleep disturbances [29, 30], a direct causal link remains a subject of ongoing discussion. Nonetheless, a donor experiencing such malaise—regardless of its definitive etiology—may have a reduced propensity to attend donation appointments, leading to silent, self-driven attrition. Furthermore, we must acknowledge the operational paradox highlighted by our setting: because our center actively notified donors of their periodic ferritin results, this very health communication might act as a psychological deterrent. Receiving a medical notification about depleted iron reserves, even accompanied by benign dietary advice, can induce health anxiety. Consequently, donors may voluntarily cease donation out of an abundance of caution, independently of any actual physiological symptoms, adding a complex psychological layer to the observed attrition.

The subgroup analyses revealed specific demographic vulnerabilities that challenge conventional assumptions in donor management. The increased attrition risk among Type O donors (HR = 1.34), for example, is a novel finding. We hypothesize this is not an intrinsic biological vulnerability, but a consequence of operational pressure. Given the universal demand for O-type platelets, these donors may be subjected to more frequent and persistent recruitment efforts, potentially leading to a higher rate of donation-related burnout. This suggests that a one-size-fits-all recruitment strategy may inadvertently exhaust the most needed segment of the donor panel. Concurrently, our finding that male donors with ID have a significant attrition risk (HR = 1.62) serves as a critical rebuttal to the prevalent bias that iron health is primarily a concern for female donors. While women are more susceptible to baseline deficiency, high-frequency donation is a powerful equalizing force, capable of depleting iron stores regardless of sex, a fact corroborated by studies showing high rates of iron depletion in male apheresis donors [4, 31]. Finally, the persistence of these risks after excluding non-local residents confirms that the observed attrition is primarily driven by the physiological and psychological sequelae of donation itself, rather than being an artifact of population mobility.

Our findings quantitatively align with and expand upon existing literature. For instance, the adjusted hazard ratio of 2.42 for attrition following a deferral event strongly echoes the work of Custer et al. (2007), who identified deferral as a primary driver of donor dropout, although their analysis was not specific to high-frequency apheresis donors [32]. More directly, our finding that baseline LID is a significant independent predictor of attrition (HR = 1.58) complements the study by Goldman et al., which demonstrated that notifying donors of low ferritin results reduced their return rates [7]. Crucially, our study distinguishes itself by employing a time-dependent Cox model that separates the physiological impact of LID from the psychological sting of deferral, confirming they are distinct pathways to attrition. This horizontal comparison validates that our results are not an anomaly but rather a robust quantification of a widely recognized, yet undermanaged, phenomenon in a dedicated donor population [33].

The findings from this study have significant implications for blood center policy and practice. The most pressing recommendation is the integration of ferritin testing into the routine monitoring of high-frequency plateletpheresis donors. Relying solely on pre-donation hemoglobin checks is insufficient for identifying donors on a trajectory towards ID and subsequent attrition. International bodies like the AABB have already recommended that blood centers implement strategies to mitigate ID, including the use of ferritin measurement to guide interventions [19]. Secondly, a more nuanced approach to donation intervals is warranted. Instead of a uniform interval for all donors, our data advocate for a personalized strategy. For donors whose ferritin levels fall below 30 ng/mL, extending the inter-donation interval may be a more effective long-term strategy to preserve their donation career than simply recommending over-the-counter iron supplements, whose adherence and efficacy can be variable [6]. Finally, our results call for more precise and sustainable recruitment strategies. Blood centers should move away from models that risk exhausting their most committed donors, particularly those with universal blood types like Type O. Implementing tracking systems to monitor individual donation intensity could prevent burnout and promote long-term donor loyalty.

To balance clinical safety with operational costs, we propose a tiered screening strategy. First, baseline ferritin testing should be prioritized for all first-time apheresis donors, as it serves as a critical ‘early warning’ for future attrition. Second, for established donors, annual or semi-annual monitoring is more cost-effective than per-donation screening. Third, resources should be focused on high-frequency donors (e.g., > 12 donations/year) and those with baseline ferritin between 30–50 ng/mL, who are in the ‘transition zone’ toward deficiency.

This study has several notable strengths, including its longitudinal design with a large cohort of dedicated plateletpheresis donors and an extended four-year follow-up period, which allowed for the robust analysis of long-term outcomes like attrition. The application of advanced statistical methods, specifically the GEE model to account for correlations in repeated measures of ferritin and hemoglobin, and the time-dependent Cox model to mitigate immortal time bias, enhances the validity of our conclusions. Furthermore, the quality of our data was strengthened by the use of calculated total blood volume instead of BMI for a more accurate adjustment of physiological variables, and the inclusion of white blood cell count as a proxy to control for potential confounding by inflammation. However, the study is not without limitations. As a single-center investigation, the findings may have limited generalizability to other populations or healthcare systems with different donor management protocols. Its retrospective nature also means we could not control for unmeasured confounding variables, such as donors’ dietary habits or their personal use of iron supplements, which could influence iron status. Furthermore, our reliance on a single serum ferritin threshold (< 30 ng/mL) to define latent iron deficiency reflects a limitation, given the ongoing clinical debate regarding optimal cut-offs. To address this, we conducted a post-hoc sensitivity analysis defining iron deficiency at a stricter threshold of < 20 ng/mL; reassuringly, baseline iron deficiency remained a significant, independent predictor of donor attrition, confirming the robustness of our primary conclusions. Additionally, our longitudinal study is inherently subject to the “healthy donor effect,” a pervasive epidemiological bias where individuals who maintain frequent donation schedules possess greater baseline health and physiological resilience. This is further compounded by a degree of selection bias inherent in our cohort formation; our initial pool of 715 subjects represents approximately 29% of the total apheresis base, specifically those who opted into the voluntary health surveillance program. These individuals likely represent a more health-conscious and highly committed subset of the donor pool. Therefore, while our results robustly characterize the trajectories of established, dedicated donors, they may not be fully representative of or generalizable to first-time, sporadic, or less compliant donor populations. Donors experiencing the most severe symptomatic effects of iron depletion may have self-selected out of the cohort early. Consequently, this informative drop-out suggests that our GEE models might actually underestimate the true severity and incidence of iron decline across the broader donor population. Lastly, while white blood cell count was used to adjust for inflammation, the absence of a more specific marker like C-reactive protein (CRP) remains a minor limitation.

Conclusion

This study illuminates the critical role of latent ID as a key driver of donor attrition among high-frequency plateletpheresis donors. We demonstrate that intensive donation schedules lead to a progressive and clinically significant depletion of iron stores, which in turn elevates the risk of donor loss through both the physiological strain of LID symptoms and the psychological frustration of donation deferral. These findings carry a clear mandate to extend the ongoing paradigm shift in donor care specifically to the management of high-frequency plateletpheresis programs. Rather than relying solely on standard protocols designed for general blood procurement, apheresis programs must prioritize the long-term health and sustainability of this dedicated donor panel. By incorporating proactive iron status monitoring and adopting personalized, donor-centric intervals tailored to apheresis kinetics, blood centers can better protect these highly committed individuals who make advanced transfusion medicine possible.
