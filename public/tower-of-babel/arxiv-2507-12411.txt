Linearization-Based Feedback Stabilization of McKean-Vlasov PDEs
Dante Kalise, Lucas M. Moschen, Grigorios A. Pavliotis

Abstract
We study the feedback stabilization of the McKean-Vlasov PDE on the torus. Our goal is to steer the dynamics toward a prescribed stationary distribution or accelerate convergence to it using a time-dependent control potential. We reformulate the controlled PDE in a weighted-projected-space and apply the ground-state transform to obtain a Schrödinger-type operator. The resulting operator framework enables spectral analysis, verification of the infinite-dimensional Hautus test, and the construction of Riccati-based feedback laws. We rigorously prove local exponential stabilization via maximal regularity arguments and nonlinear estimates. Numerical experiments on well-studied models (the noisy Kuramoto model for synchronization, the O ⁡ ( 2 ) O(2) spin model in a magnetic field, and the Gaussian/von Mises attractive interaction potential) showcase the effectiveness of our control strategy, demonstrating convergence speed-ups and stabilization of otherwise unstable equilibria.

Introduction

The
McKean-Vlasov
equation is a nonlinear, nonlocal Fokker-Planck-type partial differential equation (PDE) describing the evolution of a probability density
\mu(t,x)
that arises in the mean-field limit of a system of weakly interacting particle systems driven by noise. In this paper, we will consider the overdamped McKean-Vlasov PDE with an external potential
V:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and an interaction potential
W:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
:

\partial_{t}\mu(t,x)=\nabla\cdot\Bigl[\sigma\nabla\mu(t,x)+\mu(t,x)\bigl(\nabla V(x)+(\nabla W*\mu)(x)\bigr)\Bigr],

(1)

where
*
denotes the convolution

(\nabla W*\mu(t,\cdot))(x)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\nabla W(x-x^{\prime})\,\mu(t,x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime},

(2)

and
\sigma>0
is the diffusion coefficient.
Equation (
1
) was first introduced in
[
39
,
38
]
and has been studied extensively since then, because of the many applications to physics, biology, models for collective behaviour and machine learning. It governs the evolution of the transition probability density of the McKean stochastic differential equation (SDE)

dX_{t}=-\nabla V(X_{t})\,dt-(\nabla W*\mu(t,\cdot))(X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}\,dB_{t},

(3)

where
\mu(t,\cdot)
is the density of the stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{B_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is the standard Brownian motion.

The goal of this work is to study feedback stabilitization for the McKean-Vlasov PDE. We consider the case where the state space is the unit torus,
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}=(\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{d}
. In particular, the convolution (
2
) is taken modulo
2\pi
and the PDE (
1
) is equipped with periodic boundary conditions. For the derivation of the McKean-VLasov PDE from the underlying interacting particle system as the number of particles
N\to\infty
, by proving the propagation of chaos property we refer to
[
41
,
45
]
. Standard models that can be described by the PDE and our choice of periodic domains naturally arise are models of phase synchronization (e.g. noisy Kuramoto oscillators
[
1
]
), opinion dynamics
[
31
,
30
]
, and spin systems on the circle, e.g. the
O(2)
model
[
10
]
.

As is well known, equation (
1
) is the 2-Wasserstein gradient flow of the free energy

\mathcal{F}[\mu]\coloneqq\sigma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mu(x)\log\mu(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}V(x)\mu(x)\,dx+\frac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}W(x-x^{\prime})\,\mu(x)\mu(x^{\prime})\,dx\,dx^{\prime},

which allows us to write it as
[
4
,
22
]

\partial_{t}\mu(t,x)=\nabla\cdot\left(\mu(t,x)\nabla\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta\mu}\right).

Stationary solutions of (
1
) on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
are characterized by vanishing probability flux, i.e.,

\nabla\cdot\Bigl[\sigma\nabla\bar{\mu}+\bar{\mu}\bigl(\nabla V+(\nabla W*\bar{\mu})\bigr)\Bigr]=0,

which integrates to the self-consistency relation

\bar{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\bigl(V(x)+(W*\bar{\mu})(x)\bigr)\Bigr),\qquad Z=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\sigma}(V(y)+(W*\bar{\mu})(y))\Bigr)\,dy.

Under mild regularity and symmetry assumptions on
V
and
W
, existence of at least one such
\bar{\mu}
follows from variational arguments, while uniqueness holds under additional convexity hypotheses
[
23
]
.
Namely, if
\nabla^{2}V(x)\succeq\alpha I
, and
\nabla^{2}W(x)\succeq 0
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the free energy
\mathcal{F}[\mu]
is displacement-convex, and one obtains a logarithmic Sobolev inequality that yields

\mathcal{F}[\mu(t)]-\mathcal{F}[\bar{\mu}]\leq e^{-2\alpha t}\bigl(\mathcal{F}[\mu(0)]-\mathcal{F}[\bar{\mu}]\bigr)

implying exponential convergence in entropy and hence in
L^{1}
to the unique steady state
\bar{\mu}

[
37
]
.
In the nonconvex case, one may have multiple
\bar{\mu}
, leading to bifurcations and metastable behavior
[
28
,
46
,
24
,
22
]
.
For instance, in the noisy Kuramoto model on
\mathbb{T}
, one observes a critical temperature
\sigma
at which the uniform state loses stability through a pitchfork bifurcation, giving rise to synchronized states
[
13
]
.

Research on controlling the McKean-Vlasov dynamics has predominantly followed stochastic-variational routes-mean-field games
[
35
,
32
,
21
,
20
]
, forward-backward optimality systems and master-equation methods
[
5
]
, and stochastic maximum-principle approaches
[
12
]
.
A recent controllability result for McKean-Vlasov SDEs can be found in
[
9
]
.
In contrast, direct PDE feedback control of the nonlocal Fokker-Planck equation remains underdeveloped.
By comparison, the linear Fokker-Planck equation (i.e.
W\equiv 0
) has been extensively studied from a PDE-control standpoint: controllability and Schrödinger-bridge connections in
[
16
]
, open-loop optimal control in
[
29
,
8
]
, and Riccati-based feedback stabilization in
[
18
,
19
]
.
First-order optimality conditions for the McKean-Vlasov PDE have been previously studied in
[

,

]
.
An optimal control problem for the noisy Kuramoto model is studied in
[

]
.
Related feedback and backstepping designs for parabolic drift-diffusion PDEs can be found in
[

A first PDE-centric attempt in the McKean-Vlasov setting with a feedback control built via nonlinear model predictive control appears in
[

, and an information-theoretic perspective on feedback-control for the noisy Kuramoto model is developed in
[

A recent work on optimal control of the McKean-Vlasov PDE and related kinetic PDEs is presented in

Nevertheless, a stabilization-theoretic framework for deterministic feedback laws on

Aim and contributions.

We consider the McKean-Vlasov PDE on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with periodic boundary conditions; our goal is steer the solution of the PDE toward a chosen equilibrium
\bar{\mu}
, or to accelerate its convergence towards the (unique) steady state, via a time-dependent potential

V(x)\mapsto V(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)\alpha_{j}(x),

following a similar approach to
[
18
,
33
]
.
Our main contributions are:

(i)

Bilinear reformulation.
We recast the controlled PDE as a bilinear system in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d},\bar{\mu}^{-1})
and, via the ground-state transformation, as a Schrödinger-type system in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

(ii)

Riccati feedback.
we verify an infinite-dimensional Hautus test, solve the algebraic Riccati equation, and derive a local, exponentially stabilizing feedback law.

(iii)

Numerical benchmarks.
We use Fourier-spectral numerical method in one dimension to illustrate the efficiency of our control methodology. In particular, we consider the noisy Kuramoto,
O(2)
in a magnetic field, and attractive (periodized) Gaussian interaction models, demonstrating significant speed-ups in the convergence rates.

Outline.

We begin in
Section 2
by reviewing the variational formulation and well-posedness of the McKean-Vlasov equation on the torus, including convergence to equilibrium under convexity assumptions.

Subsection 2.4
introduces the linearization around a steady state, the Schrödinger-type reformulation via ground-state transformation, and the spectral properties of the linearized operator.
In
Section 3
, we formulate the optimal control problem, establish stability via the Hautus test, and derive a Riccati-based feedback law.

Section 4
presents numerical experiments on several models illustrating the efficacy of our feedback design.

2.1
Model and standing assumptions

Let
V\in C^{2}(\Omega)
and
W\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
be the confining and interaction potentials, respectively, with
W
even.
Fix a diffusion coefficient
\sigma>0
.
We rewrite equation (
1
) as

\partial_{t}\mu=\sigma\Delta\mu+\nabla\cdot\bigl(\mu\nabla v[\mu]\bigr),\quad v[\mu](x)=V(x)+(W*\mu)(x),

(4)

under periodic boundary conditions, where
\mu(0,\cdot)=\mu_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}(\Omega),\;\|\mu_{0}\|_{L^{1}}=1
.

2.2
Variational solutions

Definition 2.1

(Variational solution)
.

For each time
T>0
, a curve
\mu:[0,T)\to L^{1}_{+}(\Omega)
with
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{*})
is a
variational solution
of (
4
) if, for almost every
t
, for all
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\mu\varphi=-\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\sigma\nabla\mu+\mu\nabla v[\mu]\bigr)\cdot\nabla\varphi,\quad\mu(0)=\mu_{0}.

Theorem 2.2

(Global well-posedness)
.

Under the above assumptions on
V,W,\sigma
and any
\mu_{0}\in L^{1}_{+}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique variational solution
\mu(t)
of (
4
).
Moreover:

•

Mass and positivity:
for all times
t\geq 0
,
\int_{\Omega}\mu(t,x)\,dx=1
and
\mu(t,x)>0
for all
x\in\Omega
.

•

Energy dissipation:

\dfrac{d}{dt}\mathcal{F}[\mu(t,\cdot)]\leq 0
.

Proof.

Existence, uniqueness, positivity and energy dissipation follow from the Picard-iteration construction and a priori estimates in the McKean-Vlasov setting.
In particular, see
[
40
, Theorem 5]
for the torus case, as well as the linearization estimates in
[
42
]
.

2.3
Stationary states and exponential convergence

A stationary solution
\bar{\mu}
of (
4
) satisfies

\nabla\cdot\bigl(\bar{\mu}\nabla v[\bar{\mu}]\bigr)=\sigma\Delta\bar{\mu}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\bar{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\sigma}v[\bar{\mu}](x)\bigr),

The following
Subsection 2.3
and
Subsection 2.3
follow from the logarithmic Sobolev inequality induced by displacement convexity, which is rigorously proved in
[
37
]
for the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and is carried over the torus with periodic boundary conditions.

Proposition 2.3

(Existence and uniqueness of stationary states)
.

Assume that
V,W\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
W
is even and is either
H
-stable, meaning that it has nonnegative Fourier modes, or is such that
\|{W}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}<\beta^{-1}
.
Then
\mathcal{F}

admits a unique minimizer
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{+}(\Omega)
, which is the only stationary state of the McKean-Vlasov dynamics.

Proposition 2.4

(Exponential convergence)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
2.3
, and assuming that the initial condition has finite relative entropy with respect to
\bar{\mu}
, the solution of (
4
) satisfies

\|\mu(t,\cdot)-\bar{\mu}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\|\mu(\cdot,\cdot)-\bar{\mu}\|_{L^{1}}\,e^{-\alpha t},

for an appropriate constant
\alpha>0
.

Extensions of
Subsection 2.3
can be found in
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
and in
[
42
, Section 2.3]
.

Remark 2.5

(Non-convex case and bifurcations)
.

If the interaction potential
W
in not
H-
stable,
\mathcal{F}
will have multiple local minima at sufficiently low temperatures, leading to phase transitions and metastability
[
22
]
.
A classic illustration is the noisy Kuramoto model on
\mathbb{T}
, with
V\equiv 0
and
W(x)=-K\cos(x)
.
For
K\leq 1
the uniform density
\mu\equiv(2\pi)^{-1}
is the unique (and stable) equilibrium.
At the critical coupling

, multiple non-uniform steady states bifurcate, all translations of

\bar{\mu}(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\bigl(Kr\cos x\bigr),\quad r=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{T}}\cos x\,e^{Kr\cos x}\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{Kr\cos x}\,dx},

while the uniform state remains a steady state but is now unstable
[
44
,
13
]
.

2.4
Spectral analysis of the linearized McKean-Vlasov operator

From now on, set
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq L^{2}\bigl(\Omega,\bar{\mu}^{-1}\bigr)
, and define the operators, for all
y\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathcal{A}\mu\coloneqq\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla V+\sigma\nabla\mu)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{W}(\mu)\coloneqq\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla W*\mu),

so that equation (
4
) can be written as
\partial_{t}\mu=\mathcal{A}\mu+\mathcal{W}(\mu)
, where
\mathcal{W}
is a nonlinear and nonlocal operator.
The domain of
\mathcal{A}
is given by
D(\mathcal{A})
, while the domain of
\mathcal{W}
is given by
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Fix a stationary state
\bar{\mu}
, which later will be the target distribution.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{W}
the Fréchet derivative of
\mathcal{W}
at
\bar{\mu}
, which can be computed as

\mathcal{D}_{W}y\coloneqq\nabla\cdot(y\nabla W*\bar{\mu}+\bar{\mu}\nabla W*y),\quad y\in\mathcal{X},

by noticing that
\mathcal{W}(y+\bar{\mu})=\mathcal{D}_{W}y+\mathcal{W}(y)+\mathcal{W}(\bar{\mu})
.
Then, if we define the variable
y\coloneqq\mu-\bar{\mu}
, it satisfies

\partial_{t}y=\mathcal{L}y+\mathcal{W}(y),

where
\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{D}_{W}
is the linearized operator around
\bar{\mu}
.

Ground-state transform.

We perform the (ground state) unitary transformation to map the Fokker-Planck operator
\mathcal{L}
to a
Schrödinger operator
defined in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Specifically, define the
unitary operator

\mathcal{U}\colon\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega),\qquad(\mathcal{U}y)(x)=\frac{y(x)}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}(x)}}

Verifying that
\mathcal{U}
is an isometric isomorphism with a well-defined inverse is straightforward.
Then, set

\mathcal{H}=-\mathcal{U}\mathcal{L}\,\mathcal{U}^{-1}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\mathcal{K},

where

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}=-\sigma\,\Delta+\Psi(x),

is a Schrödinger operator with potential
\Psi(x)=\tfrac{1}{4\sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta v
with
v[\bar{\mu}]=V+W*\bar{\mu}
, and
\mathcal{K}
is the integral operator

(\mathcal{K}\phi)(x)=-\bar{\mu}(x)^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{\Omega}\bigl[\nabla\bar{\mu}(x)\cdot\nabla W(x-y)+\bar{\mu}(x)\Delta W(x-y)\bigr]\bar{\mu}(y)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\phi(y)\,dy.

Remark 2.6
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}\coloneqq-\mathcal{U}^{-1}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}\,\mathcal{U}
is a linear and local Fokker Planck operator with diffusion
\sigma>0
and drift
v[\bar{\mu}]
.

In the following, we establish results regarding the spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}
based on the spectral properties of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
and the boundedness of the operator
\mathcal{K}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
W\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
, then
\mathcal{K}
is Hilbert-Schmidt, hence compact, on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Define the kernel

k(x,y):=-\frac{\bar{\mu}(y)^{1/2}}{\bar{\mu}(x)^{1/2}}\Bigl(\nabla\bar{\mu}(x)\!\cdot\!\nabla W(x-y)+\bar{\mu}(x)\,\Delta W(x-y)\Bigr),\qquad x,y\in\Omega,

so that
\mathcal{K}[\varphi](x)=\int_{\Omega}k(x,y)\varphi(y)\,dy
.
An integral operator is Hilbert-Schmidt iff its kernel lies in
L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
.
Because
W\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists
C_{W}>0
such that

\|\nabla W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C_{W},\quad\|\Delta W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C_{W}.

Additionally, notice that
\nabla\bar{\mu}=-\frac{\bar{\mu}}{\sigma}\nabla(V+W*\bar{\mu})
and define

a(x)=\sqrt{\bar{\mu}(x)}\left(\sigma^{-1}|\nabla(V(x)+(W*\bar{\mu})(x)|+1\right),\quad b(y)=C_{W}\sqrt{\bar{\mu}(y)}

so that
|k(x,y)|\leq a(x)b(y)
.
It is clear that
b\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with
\|b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C_{W}
because
\bar{\mu}
is a probability density.
Furthermore,

\begin{split}\|a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{\sigma}\|\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\nabla W*\bar{\mu}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{Z}}\|e^{-V/(2\sigma)}\nabla V\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{C_{W}}{\sigma}+1,\\
&=\frac{2}{\sqrt{Z}}\|\nabla(e^{-V/(2\sigma)})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{C_{W}}{\sigma}+1,\end{split}

which implies that
a\in L^{2}(\Omega)
since
\nabla\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
for
\phi\coloneqq e^{-V/(2\sigma)}
because
\nabla\phi
is a continuous function defined in the compact set
\Omega
.
Therefore,

\iint_{\Omega}|k(x,y)|^{2}\,dx\,dy=\|a\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\|b\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}<\infty.

Hence
\mathcal{K}
is Hilbert-Schmidt and therefore compact on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

∎

Proposition 2.8

(Pure-spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
)
.

The operator
\mathcal{H}
has a compact resolvent.
Its spectrum consists of isolated real eigenvalues of finite multiplicity accumulating only at
\pm\infty
, i.e.,
\sigma(\mathcal{H})=\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
,
\lambda_{n}
isolate, finite algebraic multiplicity,
|\lambda_{n}|\to\infty
.
Furthermore, there exist (generalized) eigenfunctions
\{\varphi_{n}\}
of
\mathcal{H}
and
\{\varphi_{n}^{*}\}
of
\mathcal{H}^{*}
such that
\langle\varphi_{n},\varphi_{m}^{*}\rangle=\delta_{nm}
, and the linear spans of
\{\varphi_{n}\}
and
\{\varphi_{n}^{*}\}
are both dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

On the compact torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the operator
-\Delta
with domain
H^{2}(\Omega)
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and has compact resolvent, since
\sigma(-\Delta)=\{|k|^{2}:k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
is discrete and unbounded above.
Adding the operator
\psi\mapsto\Psi\psi
to
-\sigma\Delta
, with the bounded function
\Psi
, yields
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, which remains self-adjoint on
H^{2}(\Omega)
and still has compact resolvent by
Appendix A
.
Combining Lemmas
A
and
2.4
concludes the result.
We concludes the proof because
\mathcal{H}
is bounded from below given that it is the sum of a bounded operator
\Psi+\mathcal{K}
with a bounded from below operator
-\Delta
.
∎

Proposition 2.9
.

The spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{H}
is the negative of the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,
\sigma(\mathcal{H})=-\sigma(\mathcal{L})
.
Further,
\varphi
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{H}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if
\mathcal{U}^{-1}\varphi
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
-\lambda
.
Finally,
0\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{U}
is unitary, the similarity
\mathcal{H}=-\mathcal{U}\mathcal{L}\,\mathcal{U}^{-1}
immediately gives
\sigma(\mathcal{H})=-\sigma(\mathcal{L})
.
In particular, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
(\mathcal{H}-\lambda I)\varphi=0
if and only if
(\mathcal{L}+\lambda I)(\mathcal{U}^{-1}\varphi)=0
.
To see that
0\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})
, observe that

0=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}y\,dx=\langle(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{D}_{W})y,\bar{\mu}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\bar{\mu}^{-1}}},\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{X},

which implies
\mathcal{L}^{*}\bar{\mu}=0
so
\bar{\mu}\in\ker(\mathcal{L})
.
Hence
\bar{\mu}
is a nontrivial element of the kernel of the adjoint, and
0
lies in the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Before turning to the control design, we state two spectral properties that will be necessary in the proof of
Theorem 3.1
.
Namely, although the full operator
\mathcal{H}
acts on an infinite-dimensional space, only finitely many of its eigenvalues can lie above any prescribed real level.
This ensures that only a finite number of modes need to be stabilized by feedback.
We also include the properties of the operator

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}y=\sigma\Delta y+\nabla\cdot\bigl(y\nabla v[\bar{\mu}]\bigr),

with
v[\bar{\mu}]\in W^{2,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 2.10

(Finiteness of spectrum above
-\delta
)
.

For any
\delta>0
, the set

\bigl\{\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L}):\Re(\lambda)\geq-\delta\bigr\}

is finite.

Proof.

Because
\mathcal{H}_{\rm loc}
is self-adjoint with compact resolvent, its eigenvalues
\{\mu_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are real, with finite multiplicity, and
\mu_{n}\to+\infty
.
Additionally, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
satisfy
|\lambda_{k}|\to\infty
when
k\to\infty
by
Subsection 2.4
.
By a standard perturbation argument, one shows that

\sigma(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bar{D}(\mu_{n},\|K\|),

where
D(a,r)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-a|<r\}
.
Hence, for all
\mu\in\sigma(\mathcal{H})
, we have that
|\mu-\mu_{n}|\leq\|\mathcal{K}\|
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\Im(|\mu|)<=\|\mathcal{K}\|
, which imply that
\Re(\mu)\geq\min_{n}\mu_{n}-\|\mathcal{K}\|\geq C>-\infty
for a constant
C
and
\Re(\mu_{k})\to\infty
.
It follows that there are finitely many eigenvalues
\mu\in\sigma(\mathcal{H})
such that
\Re(\mu)\leq-\delta
, and, therefore, there are finitely many eigenvalues
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{L})
such that
\Re(\lambda)\geq-\delta

∎

By the same arguments as in the Neumann-boundary setting (see Lemma 3.1 of
[
18
]
), one shows that on the compact manifold
\Omega
, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{X}
having compact resolvent.
In particular, its spectrum is purely discrete and nonpositive, and zero is a simple eigenvalue.

Lemma 2.11

(Spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}
)
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is self-adjoint with compact resolvent,
\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}})\subseteq(-\infty,0]
, and
\ker(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}})=\operatorname{span}\{\bar{\mu}\}
, so
0
is a simple eigenvalue and
\bar{\mu}
is the unique (probability) steady state.

Having established that our linearized operator has a discrete spectrum, we now turn in the next section to how to steer the dynamics via time-dependent control functions.

2.5
Decoupling via the zero-mean projection

As noted in
Subsection 2.4
, the linear operator
\mathcal{L}
has an eigenvalue at
0
arising from mass conservation.
In particular, if
y(t,x)=\mu(t,x)-\bar{\mu}(x)
, then
\int_{\Omega}y(t,x)\,dx=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Consequently, instead of the full space
\mathcal{X}
, our dynamics and control restrict to the zero-mean subspace

\mathcal{X}_{0}\coloneqq\left\{y\in\mathcal{X}:\int_{\Omega}y(x)\,dx=0\right\}.

In the sequel we introduce the projection
\mathcal{P}
onto
\mathcal{X}_{0}
so that

\mathcal{X}_{0}=\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{P})
, and derive the reduced dynamics on this subspace.

Define the projections on
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{P}y=y-\Bigl(\int_{\Omega}y\Bigr)\bar{\mu},

so that
\mathcal{X}_{0}\coloneqq\operatorname{im}(P)
, and decompose
y
via the projections
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}\coloneqq I-\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,

y_{p}\coloneqq\mathcal{P}y,\quad y_{q}\coloneqq\mathcal{Q}y,\quad\mathcal{X}=\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{P})\oplus\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{Q}).

Applying
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
to the evolution

\partial_{t}y_{p}+\partial_{t}y_{q}=\mathcal{L}(y_{p}+y_{q})+\mathcal{W}(y_{p}+y_{q}).

yields

\partial_{t}y_{q}=0\implies y_{q}(t)=y_{q}(0)=\mathcal{Q}\mu_{0}-\bar{\mu}

since
\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{L}=0
and
\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{W}=0
,and

\partial_{t}y_{p}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}y_{p}+\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}(y_{p}),\qquad y_{p}(0)=\mathcal{P}\mu_{0}.

Given that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}
on
D(\mathcal{L})
and
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}
on
H^{1}(\Omega)
,

\partial_{t}y_{p}=\mathcal{L}_{0}\,y_{p}+\mathcal{W}_{0}(y_{p}),\qquad y_{p}(0)=\mathcal{P}\mu_{0},

where

\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}I_{\mathcal{P}},\qquad\mathcal{W}_{0}(y_{p})=\mathcal{W}(I_{\mathcal{P}}y_{p}),

and
I_{\mathcal{P}}:\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}
is the canonical inclusion.

Remark 2.12
.

By working on the space
\mathcal{X}_{0}=\operatorname{im}(P)
, we have that, for all
f\in\mathcal{X}_{0}
,

\langle f,\bar{\mu}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\bar{\mu}^{-1}}}=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,dx=0

so
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{X}_{0}^{\perp}
.
The eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
cannot belong to
\operatorname{span}(\bar{\mu})
.

Under the ground-state transform
\mathcal{U}
, the zero-mean projection
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{X}
induces the orthogonal projector

\mathcal{P}_{H}=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{P}\,\mathcal{U}^{-1}\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to\{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\}^{\perp}.

Since
\mathcal{P}
commutes with
\mathcal{L}
(mass conservation), it follows that
\mathcal{P}_{H}
commutes with
\mathcal{H}
.
Hence the reduced Schrödinger operator

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{P}_{H}\mathcal{H}

is well-defined on the codomain
\operatorname{im}(\mathcal{P}_{H})=\{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\}^{\perp}\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega)
, and corresponds to the projection
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}
on the weighted side.

3
Control Problem Formulation and Riccati Feedback

In this section we pose the feedback-stabilization problem for the McKean-Vlasov dynamics on the torus and state the Riccati-based control design using the linearization step discussed in the previous section.

3.1
Formulation

We steer equation (
4
) by adding a time-dependent potential of the form

V(x)\mapsto V(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)\alpha_{j}(x),

where each
\alpha_{j}\in H^{1}(\Omega,\bar{\mu}^{-1})
is a prescribed
shape function
, which will be chosen in an ad hoc manner to meet the stability condition, and
u_{j}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
are scalar controls.
The controlled PDE becomes

\partial_{t}\mu=\mathcal{A}\mu+\mathcal{W}(\mu)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)\,\mathcal{N}_{j}\mu,

(5)

where
\mathcal{N}_{j}:H^{1}(\Omega,\bar{\mu}^{-1})\to L^{2}(\Omega,\bar{\mu}^{-1})
is defined as
\mathcal{N}_{j}\mu\coloneqq\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla\alpha)
.
The equation for
y
around a steady state
\bar{\mu}
is

\partial_{t}y=\mathcal{L}y+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)\mathcal{N}_{j}y+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}_{j}u_{j}(t)+\mathcal{W}(y),

(6)

where
\mathcal{B}_{j}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}_{j}\bar{\mu}
.
The first order approximation of this equation is given by

\partial_{t}y=\mathcal{L}y+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}_{j}u_{j}(t).

(7)

We apply the zero-mean projection
\mathcal{P}
onto
\mathcal{X}_{0}
and define

\mathcal{N}_{0,j}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}_{j}I_{\mathcal{P}},\qquad\mathcal{B}_{0,j}\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{j}\quad j=1,\dots,m

to obtain operators defined on
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.
Since
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{N}_{j}=\mathcal{N}_{j}
,
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{B}_{j}=\mathcal{B}_{j}
,
\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{N}_{j}=0
, and
\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{B}_{j}=0
, on the respective domains, the projected dynamics read

\partial_{t}y_{p}=\mathcal{L}_{0}y_{p}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}(t)\mathcal{N}_{0,j}y_{p}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}_{0,j}u_{j}(t)+\mathcal{W}_{0}(y).

Notational convention.
From now on we write

y\equiv y_{p},\quad\mathcal{L}\equiv\mathcal{L}_{0},\quad\mathcal{W}\equiv\mathcal{W}_{0},\quad\mathcal{H}_{0}\equiv\mathcal{H},\quad\mathcal{N}_{j}\equiv\mathcal{N}_{0,j},\quad\mathcal{B}_{0,j}\equiv\mathcal{B}_{j}

with the understanding that all of these objects act on the zero-mean space
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, or, after the ground-state transform, on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

3.2
Linear-quadratic control problem

For a given rate
\delta>0
and a self-adjoint, positive operator
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}_{0}\to\mathcal{X}_{0}
, define the cost

\mathcal{J}(y,u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{2\delta t}\Bigl(\langle y(t),\mathcal{M}y(t)\rangle_{L^{2}_{\bar{\mu}^{-1}}}+\|u(t)\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr)\,dt.

(8)

Under the (zero-mean) linearized dynamics (
7
), we set
z(t)=e^{\delta t}y(t)
,
v_{j}(t)=e^{\delta t}u_{j}(t)
, obtaining the standard linear-quadratic problem

\displaystyle\min_{v}\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(\langle z(t),\mathcal{M}z(t)\rangle+\|v(t)\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)\,dt,

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}+\delta I)z+\mathcal{B}v,

(10)

where
\mathcal{B}u\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{B}_{j}u_{j}\;(t)
.

Theorem 3.1

(Hautus
\delta
-stabilisation)
.

Let
\delta>0
.
Assume the first
m
eigenpairs
\{(\lambda_{j},\psi_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
of
\mathcal{L}
satisfy
\Re\lambda_{j}\geq-\delta
.
If the shape functions
\alpha_{j}
solve

\nabla\cdot\bigl(\bar{\mu}\nabla\alpha_{j}\bigr)=\psi_{j},\quad j=1,\dots,m,

(11)

with periodic boundary conditions, then
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
satisfies the infinite-dimensional Hautus test and is
\delta
-stabilizable.

Proof.

By
Subsection 2.4
, the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
in the half-plane
\Re\lambda\geq-\delta
consists of exactly
m<\infty
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\}
.
The equation (
11
) can be rewritten as

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}\beta_{j}=-\sigma\mathcal{U}\psi_{j}=-\sigma\varphi_{j},\quad\text{where }\beta_{j}=\mathcal{U}^{-1}\alpha_{j},

and
\varphi_{j}
denotes the corresponding eigenfunction of the operator
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}+\mathcal{K}
.
To derive this form, we apply the ground-state transform and rewrite in Schrödinger form.
The equation has a solution
\beta_{j}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for any
p>0
, if and only if the right-hand side is orthogonal to the kernel of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
given that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is self-adjoint over
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
By
Subsection 2.4
, we know that

\ker(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\dagger})=\ker(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}})=\operatorname{span}\{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\}.

Therefore, the equation is solvable if and only if
\varphi_{j}\notin\operatorname{span}\{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\}
, or equivalently,
\psi_{j}\notin\operatorname{span}\{\bar{\mu}\}
, which is true by
Subsection 2.5
.
Additionally, the solution is unique up to a constant, which we don’t define since we are only interested by its derivative.

Next, we verify the infinite-dimensional Hautus test

\ker\left(\lambda I-\mathcal{L}^{*}\right)\cap\ker(\mathcal{B}^{*})=\{0\}

for all eigenvalues
\lambda
of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
with real part at least
-\delta
.
Let
(\lambda_{i},\phi_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,m
be the eigenpairs of
\mathcal{L}^{*}
, chosen so that

\langle\psi_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\bar{\mu}^{-1}}}=\delta_{ij}.

Then, we compute

\mathcal{B}^{*}\phi_{i}=\Bigl(\langle\psi_{1},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega,\bar{\mu}^{-1})},\cdots,\langle\psi_{m},\phi_{i}\rangle_{L^{2}_{\bar{\mu}^{-1}}}\Bigr)\neq 0,

which gives us Hautus test.
Since we assumed
\mathcal{M}
to be positive definite, the pair
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{M})
is trivially detectable and proof follows from
[
27
]
[Theorem 5.2.11].
∎

3.3
Algebraic Riccati equation and feedback law

We now derive the operator-Riccati equation whose solution yields the optimal control for problem (
9
)-(
10
).
Recall that
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{A}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{0,W}
acts on the zero-mean space
\mathcal{X}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{B}_{0}
is the control operator on
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.
We consider two equivalent algebraic Riccati equations:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}_{0}^{*}+\delta I)\Pi_{0}+\Pi_{0}(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\delta I)-\Pi_{0}\mathcal{B}_{0}\mathcal{B}_{0}^{*}\Pi_{0}+\mathcal{M}=0,\quad\Pi_{0}=\Pi_{0}^{*}\in\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{X}_{0}),

(R
0
)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}^{*}+\delta\mathcal{P}^{*})\Pi+\Pi(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{P})-\Pi\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi+\mathcal{P}^{*}\mathcal{M}\mathcal{P}=0,\quad\Pi=\Pi^{*}\in\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{X}),,

(R
full
)

where equation (
R0
) is posed directly on the zero-mean subspace, while (
Rfull
) is its extension to the full space.

A standard decomposition argument shows that any solution
\Pi
of (

\Pi_{0}=I_{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\Pi I_{\mathcal{P}}
and conversely any solution
\Pi_{0}
of (
R0

Rfull

\Pi=\mathcal{P}^{*}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{P}+\gamma 11^{*}
, for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, where
1
indicate the constant functions.
In either case the feedback law

v(t)=-\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi z(t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u(t)=e^{-\delta t}v(t)=-\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi y(t)

is the same, since
\mathcal{B}^{*}1=0
.
For more details, check

.

We conclude by stating the connection between the Riccati equation and the optimal control problem (

), which is guaranteed by

coupled with

Proposition 3.2

(Algebraic Riccati Equation)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem 3.1
, there exists a unique self-adjoint, nonnegative
\Pi\in\mathbb{L}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
solving

(\mathcal{L}+\delta I)^{*}\Pi+\Pi(\mathcal{L}+\delta I)-\Pi\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi+\mathcal{M}=0.

The resulting optimal feedback is

v(t)=-\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi z(t),\quad u(t)=e^{-\delta t}v(t)=-\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi y(t).

Additionally, the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}+\delta I-\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi
generates an exponentially stable semigroup on
\mathcal{X}_{0}
.

3.4
Local exponential stabilization

After computing the optimal control in feedback form
v(t)=-\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi z(t)
for the linearized model, we insert it into the full nonlinear dynamics to obtain the closed-loop equation

\dot{z}=\mathcal{L}_{\Pi}z-(\mathcal{B}^{*}\Pi z)\mathcal{N}^{\delta}(t)z+\mathcal{W}^{\delta}(t)(z),

where
\mathcal{N}^{\delta}(t)z=(\mathcal{N}_{1}^{\delta}(t)z,\cdots,\mathcal{N}_{m}^{\delta}(t)z)
,
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{\delta}(t)=e^{-\delta t}\mathcal{N}_{j}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\delta}(t)=e^{-2\delta t}\mathcal{W}
.
By doing the change of variables
\psi=\mathcal{U}z
, we obtain the equation in
L^{2}(\Omega)
:

\dot{\psi}=-\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}\psi-(\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}\psi)\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{\delta}(t)\psi+\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(\psi),

(12)

where

\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}=\mathcal{H}-\delta I+\hat{\mathcal{B}}\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi},\quad\hat{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B},\quad\hat{\Pi}=\mathcal{U}\,\Pi\,\mathcal{U}^{-1},

\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{\delta}_{j}(t)=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{N}^{\delta}_{j}\,\mathcal{U}^{-1},\qquad\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}=\mathcal{U}\,\mathcal{W}^{\delta}\,\mathcal{U}^{-1}.

By
Subsection 3.3
and
Subsection 2.4
, both
\hat{\mathcal{B}}\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}
and the integral perturbation
\mathcal{K}
are bounded on
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Hence

D(\mathcal{H}_{\Pi})=D(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}})\,\cap\,L^{2}_{0}(\Omega),\quad D(\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}^{*})=D(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{*})\,\cap\,L^{2}_{0}(\Omega).

(13)

Next, taking
\lambda\in\rho(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}})
and setting
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc},\lambda}\coloneqq\lambda I-\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
, one shows that the real-interpolation spaces satisfy

[D(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc},\lambda})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega),L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)]_{1/2}=H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)=[D(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc},\lambda}^{*})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega),L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)]_{1/2},

assuming that the fractional powers of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc},\lambda}
are well-defined.
Moreover, it holds that

[[D(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}),L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)]_{1/2},[D(\mathcal{H}_{\lambda}^{*}),L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)]_{1/2}^{\prime}]_{1/2}=L^{2}_{0}(\Omega).

Defining the evolution space

W(Q_{\infty})\coloneqq L^{2}\bigl(0,\infty;H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\bigr)\cap H^{1}\bigl(0,\infty;[H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)]^{\prime}\bigr),

equipped with the norm

\|y\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\coloneqq\sqrt{\int_{0}^{\infty}\|y(t)\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\|y^{\prime}(t)\|_{[H^{1}]^{\prime}}^{2}\,dt},

one obtains the following maximal-regularity result.

Theorem 3.3

(Maximal regularity)
.

Let
f\in L^{2}\bigl(0,\infty;\,[H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}]^{\prime}\bigr)
and
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then the Cauchy problem

\begin{cases}\dot{\psi}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}\,\psi(t)=f(t),&t>0,\\
\psi(0)=\psi_{0},\end{cases}

admits a unique mild solution
\psi\in W(Q_{\infty})
satisfying

\|\psi\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\;\leq\;C\Bigl(\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Bigr).

In particular,
\psi\in C_{b}\bigl([0,\infty);L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\bigr)
.

Proof.

Write

\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}=A+B,\quad A\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc},\lambda},\quad B\coloneqq-\bigl(\delta I-\hat{\mathcal{B}}\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}+\mathcal{K}\bigr),

acting on
L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
By
Subsection 2.4
,
A
is sectorial with angle
0
, has compact resolvent and
D(A)=D(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}})\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Since
B\in\mathbb{L}(X)
, and by (
13
) we have the interpolation identity

[D(A),X]_{1/2}=H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L^{2}_{0}(\Omega)=[D(A^{*}),X]_{1/2},

so that the operator
A+B
meets the hypotheses of the maximal-
L^{2}
-regularity theorem (
[
11
, Ch. 3 Thm. 2.2]
; see also
[
36
, I.4.2]
).
The conclusion follows immediately.
∎

The closed-loop evolution in equation (
12
) contains two nonlinear (quadratic-type) perturbations:

F_{1}(\psi)(t)\coloneqq\bigl(\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}\psi(t)\bigr)\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{\delta}(t)\psi(t),\qquad F_{2}(\psi)(t)\coloneqq\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}\bigl(\psi(t)\bigr).

In order to run the contraction argument in
W(Q_{\infty})
, we estimate each one separately.
The next lemma bounds the
feedback term

F_{1}
; its proof is essentially identical to
[
18
, Lemma 4.7]
, so we only state it here.

Lemma 3.4

(Feedback-term estimate)
.

There exists
\widetilde{C}_{1}>0
such that for all
y_{p},z_{p}\in W(Q_{\infty})
,

\bigl\|F_{1}(y_{p})-F_{1}(z_{p})\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;\,[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{\prime})}\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}\bigl(\|y_{p}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}+\|z_{p}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\bigr)\|y_{p}-z_{p}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}.

We now propose an estimate for
\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}
of the the same type.

Lemma 3.5

(Nonlinear remainder estimate)
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
y,z\in W(Q_{\infty})
,

\|\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(y)-\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(z)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\;\leq\;C\bigl(\|y\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}+\|z\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\bigr)\;\|y-z\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}.

Proof.

Set
u=\mathcal{U}^{-1}y
,
v=\mathcal{U}^{-1}z
. Then

\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(y)-\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(z)=e^{-2\delta t}\mathcal{U}\Bigl[\nabla\cdot\bigl(u\nabla W*(u-v)\bigr)+\nabla\cdot\bigl((u-v)\nabla W*v\bigr)\Bigr].

Since
e^{-2\delta t}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathcal{W}}(y)-\hat{\mathcal{W}}(z)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\leq\;\|\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla W*

\displaystyle(u-v))\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}

\displaystyle+\|\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot((u-v)\nabla W*v)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}

Consider the first term.
For a fixed
t
and any
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}}=1
,

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla W*(u-v)),\varphi\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\nabla\cdot(u\nabla W*(u-v)),\mathcal{U}^{*}\varphi\bigr\rangle_{L^{2}_{\bar{\mu}^{-1}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\cdot\bigl(u\nabla W*(u-v)\bigr)\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\frac{\varphi}{\bar{\mu}}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u\nabla W*(u-v)\cdot\nabla\Bigl(\frac{\varphi}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\Bigr)dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}u\nabla W*(u-v)\cdot\Bigl[\nabla\varphi+\nabla\bigl(\tfrac{V+W*\bar{\mu}}{2\sigma}\bigr)\varphi\Bigr]\frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\frac{u}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\frac{\nabla W*(u-v)}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\cdot\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\Bigl[\nabla\varphi+\nabla\bigl(\tfrac{V+W*\bar{\mu}}{2\sigma}\bigr)\varphi\Bigr]\,dx.

Hence by Cauchy-Schwarz,

\bigl\|\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla W*(u-v))\bigr\|_{(H^{1})^{\prime}}\leq\left\|y\tfrac{\nabla W*(u-v)}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\right\|\,\left\|\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\nabla\varphi+\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\nabla\!\bigl(\tfrac{V+W*\bar{\mu}}{2\sigma}\bigr)\,\varphi\right\|.

On the compact torus,
W\in W^{2,\infty}
, and
V
and
\bar{\mu}
are continuous, thus bounded.
Therefore,

\left\|\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\nabla\varphi+\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}\nabla\bigl(\tfrac{V+W*\bar{\mu}}{2\sigma}\bigr)\varphi\right\|\leq C_{1}\|\varphi\|_{H^{1}}=C_{1},

and, by applying Young’s inequality,

\left\|y\tfrac{\nabla W*(u-v)}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\right\|\leq\|y\|\bigl\|\tfrac{\nabla W*(u-v)}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\|y\|\bigl\|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigl\|\nabla W\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigl\|u-v\bigr\|_{L^{1}}\leq C_{W}\|y\|\bigl\|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}}\bigr\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigl\|y-z\bigr\|,

where we are using Cauchy-Schwartz to obtain

\|u-v\|_{L^{1}}=\int_{\Omega}|\sqrt{\bar{\mu}}(y-z)|\leq\|y-z\|.

Hence

\bigl\|\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla W*(u-v))\bigr\|_{(H^{1})^{\prime}}\leq C\|y\|\|y-z\|.

Integrating in time gives

\bigl\|\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot(u\nabla W*(u-v))\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\leq C\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}\|y-z\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

For the second term, we use exactly the same argument, swapping the roles of
u
and
v
, yielding

\bigl\|\mathcal{U}\nabla\cdot((u-v)\nabla W*v)\bigr\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\leq C\|z\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}\|y-z\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Since
W(Q_{\infty})\hookrightarrow C_{b}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\Omega))
and

W(Q_{\infty})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
, we have

\|y\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}
,
\|z\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}
,
\|y-z\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\|y\|_{W(Q_{\infty})},\|z\|_{W(Q_{\infty})},\|y-z\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}
,
and the lemma follows with a suitable constant
C
.
∎

Combining the three presents estimates contributes with the concluding theorem.

Theorem 3.6

(Existence via contraction)
.

Let
C>0
,
\widetilde{C}_{1}>0
, and
\widetilde{C}_{2}>0
be the constants from
Theorem 3.3

Proof.

We follow the proof from
[
18

closely.
Define the map

\mathcal{F}:B\to B,\quad B\coloneqq\Bigl\{w\in W(Q_{\infty}):\|w\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\leq\tfrac{1}{4C(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})}\Bigr\},

by letting
\psi=\mathcal{F}(w)
be the unique mild solution of

\partial_{t}\psi+\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}\psi=-(\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}w)\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{\delta}(t)w+\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(w),\quad\psi(0)=\mathcal{P}\psi_{0}.

Denote

so that

By

and
Subsection 3.4
,

\|F(w)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\leq\widetilde{C}_{1}\|w\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}^{2}+\widetilde{C}_{2}\|w\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}^{2}=(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})\|w\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{16C^{2}(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})}.

Hence Theorem
3.3
yields for the mild solution
\psi=\mathcal{F}(w)
, the inequality

\|\psi\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\leq C\|F(w)\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}+C\|\psi_{0}\|\leq\frac{1}{16C(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})}+C\|\psi_{0}\|.

On the assumption of the initial datum, we guarantee that
\|\mathcal{F}(w)\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\leq 2/(16\,C(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2}))
.
Thus
\mathcal{F}
maps
B
into itself.

We follow to verify that
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction.
To check the contraction, let

and
\psi_{i}=\mathcal{F}(w_{i})
for

Then,

\partial_{t}\psi_{1}-\partial_{t}\psi_{2}+\mathcal{H}_{\Pi}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})=F(w_{1})-F(w_{2}),\quad(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})(0)=0,

so again by Theorem
3.3
and the two lemmas,

\begin{split}\|\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}&\leq C\Bigl(\Big\|(\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}w_{2})\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{\delta}(t)w_{2}-(\hat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\hat{\Pi}w_{1})\hat{\mathcal{N}}^{\delta}(t)w_{1}\Big\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt+\Big\|\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(w_{1})-\hat{\mathcal{W}}^{\delta}(w_{2})\Big\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty;[H^{1}]^{\prime})}\Bigr)\\
&\leq C(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})\bigl(\|w_{1}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}+\|w_{2}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\bigr)\,\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}\\
&\leq C(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})\frac{2}{4C(\widetilde{C}_{1}+\widetilde{C}_{2})}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}=\frac{1}{2}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{W(Q_{\infty})}.\end{split}

Hence
\mathcal{F}
is a strict contraction on

\psi\in B
with
\psi=\mathcal{F}(\psi)
, completing the proof of local existence and decay.
∎

Numerical Experiments

In all tests below, we solve the controlled McKean-Vlasov equation (
5
) on
\Omega
by a spectral method (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
).
Given the periodic boundary conditions, a natural basis to work on is the Fourier basis.
Another advantage is the possibility of using the Fast Fourier Transform to compute inner products.
Using a truncated Fourier series expansion with
2L+1
modes, we approximate

y(x,t)\approx\hat{y}_{L}(x,t)\coloneqq\sum_{k=-L}^{L}a_{k}(t)\phi_{k}(x),\quad\bar{\mu}(x)\approx\hat{\mu}_{L}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{k=-L}^{L}\bar{\mu}_{k}\phi_{k}(x),

where, on
\mathbb{T}
we consider
\phi_{k}(x)=e^{ikx}/\sqrt{2\pi}
so that
\langle\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\rangle=\delta_{jk}
.
The projection on
\mathcal{X}_{0}
implies that
a_{0}\equiv 0
.
The controlled McKean-Vlasov equation is projected onto the finite-dimensional space spanned by these modes, yielding a system of
2L+1
ODEs for the coefficients
a_{k}(t)
, namely, for each
i=1,\dots,2L+1
,

\partial_{t}a_{i}=\sum_{k=-L}^{L}\Bigl\langle\mathcal{L}\phi_{k},\phi_{i}\Bigr\rangle a_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\Bigl\langle\mathcal{N}_{j}\phi_{k},\phi_{i}\Bigr\rangle a_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\langle\phi_{i},\mathcal{B}_{j}\rangle+F_{i}(a),

where
F_{i}(a)\coloneqq-\langle\hat{y}_{L}\nabla W*\hat{y}_{L},\nabla\phi_{i}\rangle
.
We then define the inner product matrices

\displaystyle{}_{ik}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\phi_{k}\nabla V,\nabla\phi_{i}\bigr\rangle,

\displaystyle[D]_{ik}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\nabla\phi_{k},\nabla\phi_{i}\bigr\rangle,

\displaystyle[L_{W}]_{ik}

\displaystyle=\bigl\langle\phi_{k}\nabla W*\hat{\mu}_{L}+\hat{\mu}_{L}\nabla W*\phi_{k},\nabla\phi_{i}\bigr\rangle,

\displaystyle[N_{j}]_{ik}

\displaystyle=\langle\phi_{k}\nabla\alpha_{j},\nabla\phi_{i}\rangle,

\displaystyle[B_{j}]_{i}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{B}_{j},\phi_{i}\rangle=\bigl\langle\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mu}_{L}\,\nabla\alpha_{j}),\phi_{i}\bigr\rangle,

so that, in vector form,

\partial_{t}a=-\left[L_{V}+\sigma D+L_{W}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}N_{j}\right]a+Bu+F(a).

(14)

We also approximate the initial condition by
\hat{\mu}_{0}
with Fourier coefficients
\bar{\mu}_{0,i}
for
i=-L,\dots,L
.
Finally, we approximate the operator
\mathcal{M}
by the matrix
M

M_{ik}=\langle\mathcal{M}\phi_{k},\phi_{i}\rangle.

With these definitions, set
A=-(L_{V}+\sigma D+L_{W}-\delta I)

We solve the finite-dimensional algebraic Riccati equation

A^{*}\Pi+\Pi A-\Pi BB^{*}\Pi+M=0,

where
A^{*}

A

\Pi
.
This leads to the optimal feedback control
u=-B^{*}\Pi

14

For simplicity, we consider
M=\nu I
with
\nu=O(10^{6})

In practice, for the next examples, we bypass computing the first few eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{L}
and instead take a simple Fourier-basis Ansatz for the control shapes.
Concretely we set

\alpha_{1}(x)=\frac{\sin(x)}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\quad\alpha_{2}(x)=\frac{\cos(x)}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\quad\alpha_{3}(x)=\frac{\sin(2x)}{\sqrt{4\pi}},\quad\alpha_{4}(x)=\frac{\cos(2x)}{\sqrt{4\pi}},

Appendix A
Technical operator results

Lemma A.1
.

Let
T
be a self-adjoint closed operator on a Hilbert space
H
with compact resolvent, and let
A:D(A)\subseteq H\to H
be a bounded operator in
H
, so that
D(A)\supset D(T)
.
Then
S\coloneqq T+K
is closed also has a compact resolvent.

Proof.

By Kato’s perturbation theorem,
[
34
, Theorem IV-3.17]
, we need to verify that there exists some
\zeta
in the resolvent set of
T
such that

\|A\|\|R(\zeta,t)\|<1.

(15)

By
[
34
, Theorem V-3.16]
, the self-adjointness of
T
implies that
R(x,T)
is defined for all
x\in\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}
and

\|R(a+bi,T)\|\leq\frac{1}{a}

for all
b>0
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
, where
i
is the imaginary number.
By taking
\zeta=a+i(\|A\|+1)
, we obtain the inequality (
15
).
∎
