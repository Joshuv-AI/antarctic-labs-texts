Developing an Accreditation Framework for Postgraduate Medical Speciality Education in Low-Resource Settings: A Qualitative Study from Sudan
Authors: Kamal Khalaf Alla Maki, Omer Eladil Abdalla Hamid Mohammed, Suresh Kumar Srinivasamurthy, Elsheikh Elsiddig Badr, Nagla El Tigani Mahmoud, Dalia Yahia Mohamed Haddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPostgraduate medical education (PGME) is essential for producing competent specialists and strengthening health systems. Although Sudan has an established undergraduate accreditation system through the Sudan Medical Council (SMC), PGME accreditation under the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB) remains fragmented and inconsistently aligned with international standards. This study assessed the status of PGME accreditation in Sudan, examined the SMSB experience, and developed a framework to strengthen accreditation practices.MethodsA qualitative case study design was employed between 2023 and 2025. Data were collected through a structured review of international literature, accreditation-related documents, and semi-structured interviews with 16 purposively selected stakeholders, including accreditation experts, SMSB leaders, trainers, specialty council representatives, and policymakers. Data were analyzed using the Framework Approach, involving familiarization, thematic framework development, indexing, charting, and interpretation.ResultsThe findings revealed a substantial gap between the mature undergraduate accreditation system of the SMC and the evolving PGME accreditation system of the SMSB. Strengths included SMSB’s legal mandate, national reach, experienced specialists, and emerging WFME-informed standards. Challenges included the absence of a dedicated accreditation department, inadequate surveyor training, unpublished standards, weak stakeholder coordination, limited monitoring and evaluation, financial constraints, and political instability. These findings informed a multi-level framework focused on governance reform, role separation, capacity building, stronger monitoring systems, accreditation standards and processes, and continuous quality improvement.ConclusionSudan has substantial experience in PGME but lacks a coherent and standardized accreditation system. This study proposes a context-sensitive accreditation framework that emphasizes governance strengthening, role separation, capacity building, and continuous quality improvement. Its implementation may enhance training quality, institutional accountability, and the credibility of postgraduate medical education programs, while also providing guidance for other countries facing similar challenges.

Introduction

Background

Postgraduate medical education (PGME) is a crucial component of the continuum of medical education, alongside undergraduate medical education and continuous professional development. Due to its role in preparing specialist cadres who lead health care provision, PGME has gained special focus over the decades, and specialty programs are now widespread. Residency education has become a term to describe the enrollment of trainees in practice-based training for professional certificates in health specialties’, and specifically in medicine.

Accreditation in the context of medical education is identified as one main strategy to ensure the quality and standardization of institutions and programs. Van Zanten et al (2008) define accreditation as “a process by which a designated authority reviews and evaluates an educational institution using a set of clearly defined criteria and procedures”.1

In this study, accreditation, quality assurance, and monitoring and evaluation are regarded as related but distinct concepts. Quality assurance refers to the overarching system of policies, standards, and activities designed to maintain and improve the quality of educational programs. Accreditation is a formal external quality assurance process through which an authorized body evaluates an institution, program, or training site against predetermined standards and makes a judgment regarding its compliance and quality. Monitoring and evaluation involves ongoing collection, assessment, and use of information to track performance, identify gaps, and support continuous improvement. Accordingly, accreditation serves as a key mechanism within the broader quality assurance system, while monitoring and evaluation provide the evidence needed to inform accreditation decisions and sustain quality improvement effort.

There are currently over 3000 medical colleges worldwide providing education and training to aspiring physicians.2 The medical education curricula, programs provided, resources available, duration of the study, and many others vary widely, depending on local, political, and other contextual factors. This variability in academic models, combined with the rapid increase in the number of medical colleges globally and growing global mobility for trainees, necessitates quality-assurance oversight, including formal accreditation systems, to ensure that medical educational establishments are characterized appropriately. Although in higher education, Hamad defined accreditation as “a collegial process based on self and peer evaluation for public/social accountability and improvement of academic quality”.3 In the PGME domain, accreditation has grown in importance and application over the past period. Nowadays, most medical residency programs have moved from an unstructured apprenticeship type to a model of structured training characterized by requirements mandated by accreditation systems, and this includes a shift from process to outcomes.4 In this study, accreditation, quality assurance, and monitoring are viewed as related but distinct concepts. Quality assurance refers to the broader system of policies, standards, processes, and activities designed to maintain and improve educational quality. Accreditation is a formal external quality assurance process through which an authorized body evaluates an institution, program, or training site against predefined standards and determines its compliance. Monitoring and evaluation, in contrast, refers to the ongoing collection, review, and assessment of information to track performance, identify gaps, and support continuous improvement. Thus, accreditation is one component of the broader quality assurance system, while monitoring and evaluation provide the evidence required to sustain quality and inform accreditation decisions. Since 2003, the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME)5 has developed quality standards for the accreditation of PGME that provide a framework for use and adaptation by countries (WFME, 2003).6,7 Subsequently, the WFME has updated its standards in response to new developments in medical education.

In many countries, accredited residency programs have become the hallmark for effective postgraduate medical education systems, and accreditation is increasingly linked to licensing and professional classification of graduates.5 From a global perspective, the momentum for accreditation is increasing, and countries worldwide are establishing and operating accreditation systems and practices in both undergraduate and postgraduate domains. Yet, the challenge of ensuring effective and sustainable accreditation of medical education is still encountered in several places, most notably in smaller countries of Europe and South America. Nevertheless, many countries in Africa lack an accreditation system as a benchmark.8 In countries where medical education accreditation exists, the scope and organisation of the agencies vary significantly; regional cooperation and global agencies seem to be emerging trends. The records described in my study can serve as a vital resource for similar investigations of the effectiveness of accreditation activities worldwide. Our research also highlights some areas and countries that may need focused guidance on accreditation improvement. Accreditation systems in medical education aim to ensure that various stakeholders, such as student educators in postgraduate programs and patients, can be confident that graduates are prepared to further their training or start practice. Oversight of educational content and pedagogical strategies is important to ensure that students’ learning needs are met and to ultimately impact the quality of medical care provided to patients. At the same time, there needs to be significant consequences for educational institutions that do not meet the requirements. An important goal of the accreditation system should be to encourage ongoing institutional improvement and foster the dissemination of high-quality practices, both locally and globally.

Establishing accreditation systems for postgraduate medical education (PGME) is often more challenging than accrediting undergraduate medical education, particularly in low-resource settings. Unlike undergraduate programs, which are usually delivered within universities under relatively centralized governance structures, PGME occurs across multiple hospitals and training sites and is closely integrated with healthcare service delivery. Consequently, PGME accreditation requires coordination among diverse stakeholders, including training institutions, ministries of health, professional councils, trainers, and trainees. In many low-resource settings, these efforts are further constrained by limited financial and human resources, weak governance and regulatory structures, inadequate accreditation expertise, and insufficient monitoring and evaluation systems. Political instability and broader health-system challenges may also disrupt accreditation activities and limit the sustainability of quality assurance initiatives. These factors help explain why postgraduate accreditation systems often develop more slowly than undergraduate accreditation systems despite their critical importance for ensuring the quality of specialist training.

Problem Statement and Justification

Despite fast developments in embracing accreditation systems for PGME globally, many developing countries are lagging in terms of establishing and maintaining effective accreditation systems and practices.2,9,10 The situation analysis prior to the study revealed that in Sudan, there is a strong heritage and tradition of PGME with a good reputation, but the accreditation system is not well developed or well documented. The expansion of both undergraduate and postgraduate medical education in the face of thin resources has created challenges for quality and accreditation.11,12

Despite the existence of efforts and practices for accreditation within the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB), there is still not an adequately established and well-documented accreditation system for residency training/medical speciality education, as seen in the best practice examples in the literature. In a world witnessing a focus on quality and rapid developments in accreditation systems and practices, the SMSB would certainly need to devote special attention to strengthening and promoting its accreditation system. The author could not locate any previous research addressing the PGME accreditation system in Sudan; hence, the importance of this study in filling the gap. There is also a need to enhance accreditation in the context of PGME to match the progress achieved in undergraduate medical education, where the Sudan Medical Council has advanced accreditation of medical schools and gained international recognition by the WFME.13 The main implication of the study is to support and strengthen the accreditation system and practice for PGME in Sudan under the SMSB. The study also envisions conceptual framework to guide the establishment and strengthening of a PGME accreditation system applied to the context of Sudan which may also be relevant to other similar contexts. Further set of relevant recommendations to promote the quality of PGME in Sudan is also discussed.

Despite growing international interest in quality assurance and accreditation of postgraduate medical education (PGME), evidence from low-resource settings remains limited, and little is known about how accreditation systems can be effectively developed and sustained in such contexts. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically examine the accreditation of medical specialty education in Sudan and to explore the experiences of the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB) as the national accrediting body. Beyond assessing the current state of accreditation, the study makes an original contribution by developing an evidence-informed, context-sensitive accreditation framework derived from documentary analysis, stakeholder perspectives, and international accreditation principles. The framework extends existing literature by identifying governance, capacity-building, stakeholder engagement, and implementation considerations that are particularly relevant to resource-constrained settings and may inform accreditation strengthening efforts in similar contexts.

Research Question and Hypothesis

The overarching research question for this study is: how can the accreditation of medical speciality education in Sudan be standardised and strengthened? This question is based on the hypothetical link between accreditation and quality improvement in medical education. By standardizing the quality of training programs, aligning curricula with population health needs, and improving the learning environment, accreditation can enhance healthcare outcomes.14,15 The following are the detailed questions emanating from the overarching research question: What is the current situation of accreditation in the context of medical speciality education in Sudan?How is the accreditation system governed and applied in practice? Materials and Methods

Qualitative Methodology

This study adopts a qualitative paradigm of research due to the nature of the study and the research questions posed. The qualitative research approach is suitable for exploring in-depth natural phenomena and providing rich analysis of non-numeric data and insight.

Theoretical Orientation: This study was informed by an interpretivist qualitative paradigm, which assumes that social reality is shaped by individual experiences, perceptions, and contextual influences. Given the study’s aim of exploring how stakeholders understand and experience accreditation practices in postgraduate medical education, an interpretivist approach was considered most appropriate. This paradigm enabled an in-depth exploration of meanings, experiences, and institutional processes related to accreditation within the Sudanese context and informed the selection of a qualitative case study design, semi-structured interviews, documentary review, and framework analysis.

Study Design

The study was designed as a qualitative case study. Case study research is increasingly used as a popular form of qualitative, in-depth research in the field of health professions education.16 Accreditation in the context of medical speciality education represents the case which is explored through multiple data collection methods, including documentary review, observation, and interviews.

Study Area

This study was conducted in Sudan, mainly in Khartoum, as it addresses the accreditation system of the SMSB as a national instrument for quality assurance.

Study Population

The study population comprised key stakeholders involved in the accreditation system for postgraduate specialty education, including leaders, managers, and beneficiaries. It also incorporated relevant accreditation-related documents and events to provide contextual and process-oriented insights.

Inclusion Criteria

Participants and data sources were included if they were related to postgraduate medical education (specialty training); were involved in professional training programs; were engaged with or informed by the SMSB accreditation system.

Exclusion Criteria

The study excluded participants and data sources that pertained to undergraduate medical education or continuing professional development (CPD); focused on academic education within universities or medical schools; involved accrediting bodies other than the SMSB.

Sampling Techniques

Since this is a qualitative case study, a convenience or purposive sampling approach was adopted. Purposeful sampling is a technique widely used in qualitative research to identify and select information-rich cases for the most effective use of limited resources17 It entails identifying and selecting individuals or groups who are particularly knowledgeable about or experienced with a phenomenon of interest.

Sample Size

It was provisionally envisaged that 20 key informants would be interviewed to obtain insights into the accreditation system and experiences. However, given the flexible and evolving nature of qualitative research and the influence of context and individual availability, 16 key informants were identified through prior identification and snowball sampling, as suggested by participants. For the documentary review, there was no specific sampling frame, and the researchers obtained several documents from published and unpublished sources, including records, reports, and website content.

Key informant interviews: 16 interviews (based on purposive sampling and snowballing) were conducted with resource persons with experience in the accreditation of medical speciality education. Participant recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Saturation was assessed through ongoing review of interview data and documentary evidence, with later interviews yielding no substantially new themes, categories, or insights and largely confirming previously identified findings. The final sample of 16 participants provided adequate breadth and depth, representing diverse stakeholder groups involved in postgraduate medical education and accreditation, including SMSB, SMC, the Ministry of Health, universities, teaching hospitals, specialty training programs, and accreditation experts. An interview guide was developed based on the questions and objectives of the study and was piloted and refined (a copy is included in annex 2).

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in English by the principal investigator and audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Interviews lasted for 45 minutes to 60 minutes and were transcribed verbatim; no translation was required. Consistent with the interpretivist paradigm, data quality was ensured using a standardised interview guide, verbatim transcription, verification of transcripts against audio recordings, systematic framework analysis, and regular discussions among the research team to enhance the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings.

Reflexivity and Trustworthiness

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study in recognition of the researchers’ professional involvement in postgraduate medical education and the SMSB context. Although some members of the research team were involved with SMSB as trainers and contributors to accreditation activities, none held an administrative or decision-making role within the organization. The researchers acknowledged that these affiliations could influence data collection and interpretation. To enhance credibility and minimize potential insider bias, triangulation of interview and documentary data, respondent validation, peer review of findings, reflexive discussions among the research team, and maintenance of an audit trail were employed throughout the study. Reflexive notes were kept during data collection and analysis to document assumptions, positionality, emerging interpretations, and potential sources of bias. These reflections informed coding and theme development, enhancing confirmability and reducing the undue influence of researchers’ prior experience in postgraduate medical education.

Field Work

Fieldwork for this study was conducted in three main stages: preparation, piloting, and data collection. For preparations, the researchers contacted authorities and potential participants to gain access to information sources, including documents and informants. The response was high due to the appreciation of the study’s importance and the good working relationship of the author with most of the people in charge. The researchers also identified a small team of assistants composed of colleagues with knowledge and experience in qualitative research to support data management and analysis. The researchers also contacted key informants to explain the purpose of the study, obtain their consent, ensure their voluntary participation, and assure them of confidentiality.

At this stage, the researchers tested the developed interview guide to check clarity, suitability, and validity. The researchers conducted a pilot interview with one of the key informants. Some minor issues were noted, and the resultant interview data was later used for the study as it was deemed suitable.

Data Management and Analysis

The qualitative dataset, including a summary of documents and interview transcripts, was managed and organised with the support of computer programs such as Excel. The dataset was cleaned and stored in a separate folder to be ready for analysis. Specific software for qualitative data analysis, such as NVivo, was not used due to the manageable size of the interviews and the lack of training on such programs. Data analysis followed the roles of qualitative research analysis and adopted the framework approach.18 Data were analysed using the framework analysis approach, comprising five stages: familiarization with the transcripts, development of an initial thematic framework, manual coding (indexing), charting of coded data into thematic matrices, and mapping and interpretation to identify overarching themes. Two researchers independently performed manual coding of the transcripts, after which codes were compared and reconciled through discussion. The principal investigator reviewed the consolidated codes, finalized the thematic framework, and interpreted the findings.

Validity and Reliability

There are challenges associated with ensuring validity and reliability in qualitative studies due to the flexible and evolving nature of methods and procedures.19 Generally, standards for rigour in qualitative research differ from those in quantitative research.20 Suggest a four-indicator framework to reflect validity and reliability in qualitative studies. Components of the framework are:

Credibility: establishing that results are valid and believable from the perspective of participants

Transferability: referring to the degree to which results can be transferred to other contexts

Dependability: referring to whether we would obtain the same result if we could observe the same thing twice

Confirmability: referring to the degree to which the results could be confirmed by others21 describes several strategies to enhance the validity and reliability of qualitative research studies. These include triangulation, respondent validation, audit trail, reflexivity, and thick description.

Informed by the literature and the framework above, we addressed trustworthiness in this research through the following:

Internal validity or credibility: to ensure that findings are credible given the data presented, I adopted triangulation, respondent validation, and peer examination.

Reliability, consistency, or dependability denotes the extent to which research findings can be replicated. Reliability is problematic in social sciences as human behaviour is never static; that is why qualitative research usually refers to the phenomenon in terms of whether results are consistent with the data collected- dependable (Merriam, 2009). In this study, I adopted triangulation, reflexivity, and an audit trail.

External validity/transferability in terms of the extent to which findings can be applied to other situations. Generalisability in qualitative research is not statistical; it is rather conceptual or theoretical and depends on readers to judge the study’s applicability in their own context.22 The researchers, therefore, needs to provide enough description of data and context to enable transferability. To address transferability in this research, we opted for thick description and maximum variation in the sample.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this research was obtained from the research ethics committee of the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (20/09/SMSB/EDC). The research purpose was explained to participants and information providers, who were asked to volunteer and provide informed consent. They were assured that their privacy and confidentiality would be protected. The informed consent also contained consent for publication of anonymised responses, including direct quotes. The letters and documentation required in this regard were obtained. Anonymity and confidentiality were observed through careful data protection and management, as earlier explained. The researchers discussed with participants suitable ways of expressing their views and opinions, which were observed in the data presentation and writing. The participants were approached to obtain their consent and volunteer to take part in the study. They were informed of their right to quit at any time if they wish not to continue cooperating. The data were coded, and participants’ names were anonymized. While writing, quotations were anonymized with reference only to participants’ codes. The whole dataset were kept safely and in encrypted computer files, providing access to the researchers only.

Results

These analytical themes were subsequently synthesized into six interrelated accreditation framework domains: Governance and Leadership, Accreditation Standards, Accreditation Processes, Stakeholder Engagement, Monitoring and Evaluation, and Continuous Quality Improvement. Theme 1: Conceptualization of Accreditation

Accreditation in the Context of Medical Speciality Education

Accreditation serves as a mechanism for quality assurance, involving the formal recognition that an institution, program, or service complies with established standards. As one respondent explained, [“When you accredit something, you either say it is ok or good”.] Within medical education, accreditation was described as a collaborative process that engages multiple stakeholders in integrating education, healthcare services, and research to improve health outcomes. Participants emphasized that accreditation is essential for ensuring the quality of medical training, standardizing educational practices, and developing competent specialist physicians. These functions contribute to improvements in healthcare quality, health system management, patient safety, and quality of care. The findings also highlight the collaborative nature of accreditation and the importance of multisectoral cooperation among relevant stakeholders. In addition to its quality assurance function, accreditation is viewed as an important pathway to international recognition. As noted by a Sudan Medical Council representative, [“Accreditation will affect recognition of graduates when travelling abroad”,] which [“will enhance their credibility and international reputation”.] These perspectives underscore the strategic importance of aligning national medical education programs with international standards. Participants viewed accreditation as a mechanism for quality assurance, accountability, continuous quality improvement, and international recognition rather than a one-time inspection exercise.

Theme 2: Governance and Regulatory Bodies

SMSB Leadership, Organization, and Management

Participants identified weaknesses related to SMSB leadership commitment, staffing, and accountability. Although SMSB has established a unit to oversee accreditation procedures, respondents noted the absence of a dedicated accreditation department with full-time personnel. As one SMSB leader stated, [“SMSB needs full-time personnel.]

SMSB also benefits from a decentralized structure through regional satellites governed by the central office, enabling oversight across different regions of Sudan. Participants viewed its nationwide mandate and government support as important strengths, contributing to the credibility and consistency of postgraduate medical education accreditation.

Nevertheless, respondents expressed concerns regarding the highly centralized nature of accreditation governance. They argued that excessive dependence on the central authority may limit the engagement and autonomy of regional states in maintaining accreditation standards. One Ministry of Health representative explained that [the centralization of accreditation here in the center will make the other states more dependent on the center rather than to have an active role”.]

Theme 5: Accreditation Processes

SMSB Standards and Guidelines

The early phase of postgraduate medical education accreditation in Sudan, when universities were responsible for accrediting specialist training programs, was characterized by weak organization and limited documentation. As one expert trainer recalled, [“Initially, universities were accrediting bodies, but there were no written guidelines and no published standards”.] This absence of formal guidance persisted for many years, resulting in informal and fragmented accreditation practices with limited transparency and consistency.

In recent years, SMSB has made considerable progress in strengthening postgraduate accreditation by developing accreditation standards in collaboration with the Sudan Medical Council (SMC) and aligning them with WFME principles. Respondents identified the availability of accreditation standards as one of SMSB’s key strengths and noted that the current framework provides a foundation for evaluating training institutions and programs.

Between 2014 and 2018, SMSB further advanced the accreditation process through workshops that led to the development of an accreditation manual. Participants viewed the manual positively and considered it a useful resource for guiding accreditation and re-accreditation activities within training institutions.

Despite these developments, respondents consistently highlighted concerns regarding the formal publication and dissemination of accreditation standards, procedures, and regulations. Several participants indicated that while accreditation frameworks and manuals exist and have been applied in some public and private hospitals, they have not been adequately institutionalized or widely circulated. This has led to differing perceptions regarding their availability and use.

Participants also emphasized that the accreditation requirements applied in accredited hospitals were broadly comparable to international expectations, suggesting that the standards themselves are appropriate. However, the lack of a formal accreditation department and a fully institutionalized accreditation system was perceived as a major limitation, undermining consistency, accountability, and the effective implementation of accreditation activities across training sites.

Accreditation Process and Procedures

The accreditation process established by SMSB reflects an effort to align postgraduate medical education with international standards under the guidance of the Sudan Medical Council (SMC). Participants indicated that the process draws on the experiences of other accreditation bodies and generally includes several stages: familiarization with accreditation standards, institutional self-assessment, identification and correction of gaps, site visits by accreditation teams, and a final accreditation decision. Despite this well-defined framework, respondents highlighted substantial weaknesses in implementation. Participants frequently described the accreditation process as lacking sufficient structure, consistency, and transparency. As one expert trainer stated, [“Inspection of training sites was informal, unstructured, and lacked transparency”.] Several respondents also argued that accreditation activities were often limited to site visits and checklist-based assessments rather than comprehensive evaluations against established standards. Concerns were further compounded by deficiencies in documentation and reporting. Participants noted the absence of systematic records of accreditation activities, which undermines transparency, accountability, and opportunities for continuous quality improvement. One respondent observed, [“I don’t think there is any report or survey, to my knowledge, they don’t exist”.]

These findings suggest a considerable gap between the intended accreditation framework and its practical implementation. While the process incorporates key elements of internationally recognized accreditation systems, stronger institutionalization, more rigorous assessment procedures, and improved documentation mechanisms are needed to enhance the credibility and effectiveness of postgraduate medical education accreditation in Sudan.

Theme 7: Future Directions

Prospects for Medical Speciality Accreditation in Sudan

Despite the challenges facing postgraduate medical education accreditation in Sudan, respondents expressed considerable optimism about its future development. Participants generally believed that accreditation is a gradual process that requires time to mature and become fully effective. As one expert trainer stated, [“I feel a lot of expectation that things are going to take some time to develop”.] Similar views were shared across stakeholder groups, including SMSB leadership, university representatives, and specialty training directors, who anticipated continued growth and improvement in the accreditation system.

This optimism was grounded in several enabling factors. Respondents highlighted the availability of committed personnel, the growing number of trainees, and increasing institutional support for accreditation activities. As one SMSB leader noted, [“With goodwill, resources, and the huge number of trainees, we have the opportunity to have a good accreditation system; the future is very promising”.]

Participants also emphasized the importance of ongoing research in informing reforms and strengthening accreditation practices. Furthermore, the recognition of the Sudan Medical Council (SMC) by the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME) was viewed as an important milestone that provides momentum for future accreditation initiatives in postgraduate medical education. Respondents additionally observed growing awareness of accreditation concepts among consultants, professors, and other healthcare professionals, largely driven by increasing international expectations and standards.

Collectively, these findings indicate that, despite current limitations, stakeholders remain confident in the long-term prospects of postgraduate medical education accreditation in Sudan. Their optimism is underpinned by increasing awareness, institutional experience, research engagement, and the successful accreditation achievements already attained within undergraduate medical education.

Discussion, Limitations, Conclusion and Recommendations

Discussion

Principal Findings

This study explored the current status of postgraduate medical education (PGME) accreditation in Sudan, assessed the accreditation practices of the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB), and developed a contextually relevant accreditation framework. Several important findings emerged.

First, the study identified substantial gaps in the existing PGME accreditation system when compared with the more established undergraduate accreditation system in Sudan. While undergraduate medical education has benefited from structured standards, external recognition, and well-defined accreditation processes, PGME accreditation remains less developed and is characterized by fragmented processes, limited institutionalization, inadequate monitoring mechanisms, and insufficient stakeholder engagement. These findings suggest that accreditation development within postgraduate medical education has not progressed at the same pace as undergraduate medical education. This is in concurrence with the findings of a previous study that elaborated the need for reforms in assessments for postgraduate exams.23

Second, governance emerged as a central issue influencing accreditation effectiveness. Participants consistently emphasized the need for stronger governance arrangements, greater organizational accountability, clearer roles and responsibilities, and enhanced operational independence of accreditation functions. A particularly important finding was the perceived need to separate accreditation and regulatory functions from training provision activities to strengthen transparency, credibility, and stakeholder confidence in accreditation decisions. The findings further highlighted the importance of establishing dedicated accreditation structures supported by appropriate legal, administrative, and institutional mechanisms.

Third, the study identified stakeholder engagement and institutional capacity as critical determinants of accreditation implementation. Effective accreditation was perceived as requiring active collaboration among SMSB, health authorities, universities, training institutions, trainers, trainees, and community representatives. At the same time, significant barriers were identified, including financial constraints, workforce shortages, inadequate surveyor training, limited institutional readiness, and broader contextual challenges affecting the Sudanese health and education sectors. These findings indicate that successful accreditation reform requires both organizational commitment and sustained capacity development.

Fourth, participants highlighted the need for more structured accreditation standards, processes, monitoring systems, and quality assurance mechanisms. The findings demonstrated strong support for standardized accreditation procedures, self-assessment processes, external reviews, accreditation cycles, performance monitoring, and continuous quality improvement approaches. Furthermore, monitoring and evaluation were viewed as essential components for maintaining accreditation standards and ensuring the sustainability of quality improvement efforts.

Finally, the study integrated these findings into a six-domain accreditation framework consisting of governance and leadership, accreditation standards, accreditation processes, stakeholder engagement, monitoring and evaluation, and continuous quality improvement. The framework combines internationally recognized accreditation principles with context-specific findings derived from the Sudanese experience. In particular, the framework emphasizes governance reform, role separation, capacity building, stakeholder collaboration, and sustainable monitoring systems as key requirements for strengthening postgraduate medical education accreditation in resource-constrained settings.

Governance as the Foundation of Accreditation Reform

Governance emerged as one of the most influential factors shaping the effectiveness and sustainability of postgraduate medical education (PGME) accreditation in Sudan. Across stakeholder groups, participants consistently highlighted the importance of leadership, accountability, institutional arrangements, regulatory authority, and organizational structures in determining the success of accreditation activities. The findings suggest that weaknesses in governance underpin many of the challenges currently facing PGME accreditation, including fragmented accreditation processes, inconsistent implementation, limited monitoring systems, weak stakeholder coordination, and inadequate institutionalization of accreditation functions. The study revealed a substantial disparity between the well-established undergraduate accreditation system led by the Sudan Medical Council (SMC) and the less mature postgraduate accreditation system overseen by the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB). While undergraduate accreditation has evolved through clearly articulated standards, structured accreditation procedures, and international recognition mechanisms, postgraduate accreditation remains underdeveloped and insufficiently institutionalized. Participants attributed this situation partly to historical differences in regulatory development and partly to organizational, financial, and contextual challenges affecting postgraduate medical education in Sudan. Despite these challenges, respondents identified important governance strengths within the SMSB. These included its national mandate for postgraduate medical education, centralized oversight of training programmes, nationwide reach, and access to a large pool of experienced specialists and educators. Participants acknowledged that these attributes provide a potentially strong foundation for developing a more comprehensive and sustainable accreditation system. However, these strengths were perceived to be constrained by several governance weaknesses, including the absence of a dedicated accreditation department, inadequate staffing, unclear operational structures, weak documentation systems, limited accountability mechanisms, and insufficient collaboration with external stakeholders. A particularly important finding was the recurring concern regarding the concentration of educational, regulatory, and accreditation functions within the same organizational structures. Participants frequently argued that combining responsibility for training provision with responsibility for accreditation may create actual or perceived conflicts of interest, potentially undermining trust in accreditation decisions. Consequently, respondents emphasized the need for clearer separation between training provision and accreditation activities. Although complete institutional separation may not be immediately feasible within the Sudanese context, stakeholders supported the establishment of a dedicated accreditation structure with greater operational independence and clearly defined responsibilities. The importance of role separation is consistent with international accreditation principles and broader governance theory. Internationally recognized accreditation systems generally distinguish between organizations responsible for delivering educational programmes and those responsible for evaluating and accrediting them. Such arrangements are intended to promote transparency, reduce conflicts of interest, strengthen accountability, and enhance confidence in accreditation outcomes. The findings of this study suggest that similar principles may be particularly relevant in Sudan, where participants identified governance overlap as a significant barrier to accreditation credibility and effectiveness.

The findings further indicate that governance should not be viewed solely as an administrative requirement but as a prerequisite for successful accreditation reform. Effective governance provides the institutional environment within which accreditation standards can be implemented, monitoring systems can operate, accountability can be enforced, and quality improvement can be sustained. Participants consistently linked stronger governance to improved transparency, increased stakeholder confidence, and greater legitimacy of accreditation decisions. These observations support the view that accreditation functions not only as a quality assurance mechanism but also as a governance instrument capable of promoting organizational learning, accountability, and continuous improvement.

Taken together, the findings suggest that strengthening governance should constitute a central pillar of accreditation reform in Sudan. Priority actions include establishing dedicated accreditation structures, clarifying institutional roles and responsibilities, strengthening accountability mechanisms, formalizing stakeholder collaboration, and promoting greater functional separation between accreditation and training provision activities. Addressing these governance issues is likely to enhance the credibility, transparency, and sustainability of postgraduate medical education accreditation and provide a stronger foundation for implementation of the proposed accreditation framework.

Stakeholder Engagement and System Capacity

Accreditation emerged as a collective rather than an institutional activity, requiring the active involvement of multiple stakeholders, including the SMSB, training institutions, trainers, trainees, healthcare facilities, and policymakers. Participants emphasized that effective stakeholder engagement promotes shared ownership of accreditation processes, facilitates implementation, and strengthens the legitimacy of accreditation decisions. These findings reinforce the view that accreditation is not solely a regulatory exercise but a collaborative quality improvement process that depends on broad institutional commitment.

The findings also highlighted the importance of system capacity in supporting accreditation implementation. Human resource limitations, insufficient numbers of trained surveyors, variability in trainer preparation, and financial constraints were identified as important barriers to effective accreditation. Participants consistently stressed the need for targeted capacity-building initiatives to strengthen accreditation expertise, enhance institutional readiness, and support sustainable implementation. Without adequate human, organizational, and financial resources, even well-designed accreditation systems may struggle to achieve their intended objectives.

Taken together, these findings suggest that successful accreditation reform requires both meaningful stakeholder collaboration and sustained investment in system capacity. Strengthening partnerships among key stakeholders, while simultaneously developing the expertise and resources required for accreditation activities, is likely to improve implementation, credibility, and long-term sustainability of the proposed accreditation framework.

Strengthening Accreditation Processes and Quality Assurance

The findings highlighted the importance of establishing standardized accreditation processes to ensure consistency, transparency, and credibility across postgraduate medical education programmes. Participants emphasized the need for clearly defined accreditation standards, structured accreditation cycles, self-assessment procedures, site visits, and documented decision-making processes. These components were viewed as essential for promoting accountability and reducing variability in accreditation practices.

Monitoring and evaluation emerged as critical elements of an effective accreditation system. Participants considered continuous monitoring necessary for assessing compliance with accreditation standards, identifying areas for improvement, and supporting ongoing quality enhancement. Regular evaluation was also perceived as important for ensuring that accredited programmes maintain expected educational and training standards over time.

The findings further demonstrated strong support for documentation systems and evidence-based accreditation practices. Comprehensive documentation was viewed as necessary to support accreditation decisions, facilitate monitoring activities, and enhance transparency throughout the accreditation cycle. Consistent with internationally recognized accreditation approaches identified during the framework review, participants highlighted the value of integrating standards, periodic reviews, monitoring mechanisms, and continuous quality improvement processes within a coherent accreditation system. Together, these findings suggest that strengthening accreditation processes and quality assurance mechanisms is fundamental to improving the effectiveness, sustainability, and credibility of postgraduate medical education accreditation in Sudan.

Developing a Contextually Adapted Accreditation Framework

The proposed accreditation framework was developed through the integration of findings from the situational analysis, stakeholder perspectives, and the review of international accreditation models. Rather than adopting an existing accreditation system without modification, the framework was designed to reflect the specific realities, needs, and challenges of postgraduate medical education (PGME) in Sudan while remaining aligned with internationally recognized accreditation principles. The findings demonstrated that accreditation in Sudan requires not only standards and procedures, but also stronger governance arrangements, stakeholder participation, monitoring mechanisms, and capacity-building strategies to support implementation.

The six domains of the framework were selected because they consistently emerged across the study findings and were identified as essential components of a functional accreditation system. Governance and Leadership was included in response to participants’ concerns regarding accountability, organizational structures, leadership, and the need for greater separation between accreditation and training functions. Accreditation Standards and Accreditation Processes reflected stakeholder emphasis on the need for clearly defined standards, transparent procedures, self-assessment processes, site visits, accreditation cycles, and evidence-based decision-making. Stakeholder Engagement emerged as a distinct domain because participants repeatedly highlighted the importance of collaboration among SMSB, training institutions, trainers, trainees, healthcare facilities, and other relevant stakeholders.

Similarly, Monitoring and Evaluation was incorporated to address the identified need for continuous oversight, performance assessment, compliance monitoring, and quality assurance. Continuous Quality Improvement was included because accreditation was widely perceived not as a one-time regulatory exercise, but as an ongoing process aimed at enhancing educational quality, institutional performance, and programme outcomes over time. Together, these domains represent the major themes that emerged from the study and provide a coherent structure for strengthening PGME accreditation in Sudan.

The framework therefore establishes a clear link between the study findings and the proposed accreditation model. Governance-related findings informed the Governance and Leadership domain; findings related to standards, accreditation cycles, and site reviews informed the Accreditation Standards and Accreditation Processes domains; stakeholder-related findings informed the Stakeholder Engagement domain; monitoring and accountability findings informed the Monitoring and Evaluation domain; and findings concerning quality enhancement and sustainability informed the Continuous Quality Improvement domain. By synthesizing these interconnected findings into six domains, the framework offers a structured and contextually relevant approach for strengthening accreditation systems while addressing both international expectations and local priorities within the Sudanese PGME context.

Implications for Policy and Practice

The findings of this study have important implications for policy and practice at multiple levels of the postgraduate medical education (PGME) system in Sudan. The identified governance, capacity, stakeholder engagement, and quality assurance challenges highlight the need for coordinated action among the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB), the Ministry of Health, training institutions, and relevant professional bodies.

For the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB), the findings suggest the need to strengthen accreditation governance and institutional capacity. Priority actions include establishing a dedicated accreditation structure, clarifying accreditation policies and procedures, strengthening monitoring and evaluation mechanisms, investing in surveyor development, and enhancing stakeholder engagement throughout the accreditation process. The proposed framework also highlights the importance of promoting greater accountability, transparency, and consistency in accreditation decision-making to improve the credibility and effectiveness of the accreditation system.

For the Ministry of Health, the findings underscore the importance of providing strategic and policy support for accreditation reform. Effective accreditation requires an enabling environment that supports quality improvement, workforce development, and institutional capacity building. The Ministry can play an important role by supporting accreditation implementation, facilitating collaboration among stakeholders, and promoting alignment between health workforce development priorities and postgraduate medical education standards.

For training centres and healthcare institutions, the findings highlight the need to strengthen institutional readiness for accreditation. This includes developing internal quality assurance mechanisms, supporting self-assessment activities, maintaining appropriate documentation systems, and ensuring compliance with accreditation standards. Training centres should also invest in faculty development, educational resources, and continuous quality improvement processes to support sustainable accreditation outcomes.

For professional councils and regulatory bodies, the findings emphasize the importance of collaboration and alignment across the broader medical education system. Strengthened coordination between organizations responsible for undergraduate education, postgraduate education, professional regulation, and accreditation may enhance coherence, promote quality standards across the educational continuum, and support national efforts toward greater accountability and recognition of medical education programmes.

Overall, the findings suggest that successful accreditation reform requires coordinated governance, sustained capacity development, stakeholder collaboration, and institutional commitment to continuous quality improvement. The proposed accreditation framework provides a practical foundation for guiding these efforts and strengthening the quality, credibility, and sustainability of postgraduate medical education in Sudan.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it represents one of the first empirical investigation of postgraduate medical education (PGME) accreditation in Sudan and provides original evidence from a context where accreditation research remains limited. The study employed methodological triangulation using multiple data sources, including documentary review and stakeholder interviews, which enhanced the depth and credibility of the findings. The inclusion of diverse stakeholder groups, including representatives from the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB), the Sudan Medical Council (SMC), universities, the Ministry of Health, trainers, and training institutions, enabled the study to capture a broad range of perspectives on accreditation practices and challenges. Furthermore, the use of Framework Analysis provided a systematic and transparent approach for organizing, interpreting, and synthesizing qualitative data, ultimately facilitating the development of an empirically informed accreditation framework grounded in stakeholder experience and contextual realities.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the framework may be relevant to other low-resource settings, its transferability should be interpreted cautiously, as differences in governance structures, regulatory environments, educational systems, and healthcare contexts may require local adaptation. The limited availability of published literature and conceptual frameworks addressing accreditation of health professions education in Sudan also restricted opportunities for comparison with previous studies. In addition, resources and time constraints influenced the range of research methods employed and the number of stakeholders who could be included. While the study generated rich qualitative data, inclusion of additional participants and methods such as direct observation may have further enriched the findings. The researchers’ professional backgrounds in medicine, where quantitative approaches are traditionally more prominent, may also have influenced aspects of study design and interpretation, although efforts were made to mitigate this through training, consultation, and adherence to established qualitative research procedures. Finally, the prolonged study period, influenced by contextual instability and national challenges, may have affected some organizational circumstances; however, the central accreditation issues identified remain highly relevant and continue to warrant attention.

In addition, although the study included diverse stakeholders involved in accreditation and postgraduate medical education, the sample was weighted toward leadership, regulatory, and institutional representatives. Consequently, the perspectives of frontline trainees and stakeholders from peripheral or remote training sites may be underrepresented.

Novel Contribution and Future Research

This study makes several novel contributions to the literature on postgraduate medical education (PGME) accreditation in low-resource and fragile settings.

First, to our knowledge, it represents the first empirical investigation of accreditation needs, challenges, and system requirements for PGME programs in Sudan. While previous accreditation models have largely been developed and evaluated in high-income or relatively stable healthcare and educational environments, evidence from conflict-affected and resource-constrained contexts remains limited.

Second, this study proposes a comprehensive accreditation framework that integrates governance, accreditation standards, continuous quality improvement (CQI), and implementation mechanisms within a single model. Rather than adopting international accreditation systems without modification, the framework systematically adapts global best practices to the specific institutional, regulatory, and workforce realities of Sudan.

Third, the framework identifies and operationalizes the separation of regulatory, educational, and accreditation functions as a central governance principle. This role separation emerged as a key finding from stakeholder perspectives and is proposed as a mechanism to enhance transparency, accountability, and credibility within the accreditation process.

Fourth, the framework introduces a conflict-sensitive and context-responsive approach to accreditation implementation. By incorporating phased implementation, capacity-building strategies, stakeholder engagement, and continuous monitoring mechanisms, the proposed model responds to challenges commonly encountered in fragile health systems and resource-limited educational environments.

Taken together, the study extends existing accreditation literature beyond framework adaptation by providing an empirically informed, governance-oriented, and operationally implementable model for strengthening postgraduate medical education quality assurance in Sudan and similar settings.

Future Research

Further research is needed to evaluate and strengthen the proposed accreditation framework. First, formal framework validation should be undertaken through expert consultation and stakeholder review to assess its relevance, feasibility, and applicability across different training contexts. Second, pilot implementation studies are recommended to examine the practicality of applying the framework within selected postgraduate medical education programs and training centres in Sudan. Finally, longitudinal outcome evaluations are needed to assess the framework’s impact on accreditation performance, educational quality, institutional accountability, continuous quality improvement, and postgraduate training outcomes. Such studies would provide important evidence to support further refinement, scale-up, and broader application of the framework in Sudan and other resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

Main Findings

This study demonstrates that, despite Sudan’s long-standing experience in postgraduate medical education (PGME), accreditation remains fragmented, inconsistently implemented, and only partially aligned with international standards. Although the Sudan Medical Specialization Board (SMSB) possesses the legal mandate, national reach, and institutional potential to lead accreditation for specialty training, its effectiveness is constrained by organizational, financial, procedural, and governance-related challenges. At the same time, stakeholders expressed strong commitment to quality improvement and increasing recognition of the importance of accreditation, creating a favorable foundation for reform and strengthening quality assurance within PGME.

Novel Contribution

The principal contribution of this study is the development of an empirically informed, contextually adapted accreditation framework for postgraduate medical education in Sudan. Designed in response to identified system needs, the framework provides a structured approach to strengthening accreditation through governance enhancement, continuous quality improvement, accountability, and standardized training processes. To facilitate implementation, a multilevel implementation matrix was developed, clearly defining stakeholder roles, responsibilities, accountability mechanisms, and performance indicators across national, institutional, program, and individual levels. While developed for the Sudanese context, the framework may also offer insights for other resource-constrained settings seeking to establish or strengthen accreditation systems for specialist training.

Policy Implications and Future Validation

The successful implementation of accreditation reform in Sudan will require sustained stakeholder engagement, institutional commitment, capacity building, adequate resource allocation, and effective coordination across the health and education sectors. Given the challenges posed by political instability, conflict, workforce shortages, and broader health-system constraints, accreditation reform should be approached as a gradual and adaptive process. Future work should focus on piloting, validating, and evaluating the proposed framework in practice to assess its feasibility, effectiveness, and contribution to educational quality, workforce development, and health-system strengthening.

Theoretical Implications

Beyond its practical implications, this study contributes to accreditation governance literature by highlighting the importance of regulatory independence, institutional accountability, organizational readiness, and stakeholder engagement within postgraduate medical education accreditation systems. The findings suggest that accreditation effectiveness depends not only on standards and procedures but also on governance arrangements, organizational capacity, and contextual implementation conditions. In conflict-affected and resource-constrained settings, role separation, stakeholder coordination, and institutional readiness may be critical determinants of successful accreditation reform.
