Causal effect of urinary sodium-to-creatinine ratio on gastrointestinal diseases: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yan Jiang, Zhang Zhang, Yining Ding, Shuling Xu, Rumeng Chen, Zhiwei Zheng, Meihua Bao, Binsheng He, Sen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Given the epidemiological evidence suggesting an association between sodium intake and various gastrointestinal (GI) disorders, this study aimed to investigate the causal effect of genetically predicted urinary sodium-to-creatinine ratio (UNaUCr), a proxy for sodium intake, on GI diseases. Employing a Mendelian Randomization (MR) approach, genetic variants closely associated with UNaUCr were identified by extracting data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). To validate the results, a comprehensive set of statistical methodologies, encompassing inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, and MR-Egger approaches was employed. Moreover, sensitivity analysis was performed, encompassing examinations of heterogeneity, investigations into horizontal pleiotropy, MR-PRESSO, and leave-one-out analysis. The findings of the IVW method indicated a positive causal association between genetically predicted UNaUCr and 2 GI traits: diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum (odds ratio = 1.76; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.35, 2.30), and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) (odds ratio = 3.05; 95% CI: 1.64, 5.65). These associations remained significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Other GI outcomes showed no significant associations after multiple testing correction. Results from weighted median and MR-Egger analyses were generally consistent. Although no substantial evidence of horizontal pleiotropy or heterogeneity was detected, residual pleiotropy cannot be completely excluded. This MR study provides evidence supporting a potential causal association between genetically predicted UNaUCr and 2 GI traits after FDR correction. However, as UNaUCr is a proxy for sodium intake and residual pleiotropy cannot be entirely excluded, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require further validation.

1. Introduction

With progress in medical technology, socioeconomic advancement, and shifts in lifestyle patterns, the occurrence of age-related conditions, particularly those of the digestive system, has demonstrated a marked increase, consequently influencing human lifespan.[1,2] The spectrum of gastrointestinal (GI) diseases is broad, encompassing a multitude of disorders affecting the GI tract and its ancillary organs, inclusive of gastroesophageal reflux disease, liver cirrhosis, peptic ulcers, colorectal cancer, and gallstones.[3,4] The diagnostic processes for these ailments primarily hinge on imaging techniques, biochemical assessments, and histopathological analysis.[5–7] Projections indicate that the financial burden on global health systems is escalating in response to continuous surge in the number of patients with GI diseases.[8] Hence, it is imperative to identify the risk factors associated with these diseases and evaluate high-risk cohorts for prompt preventive measures and early intervention.

The etiology of GI disorders is multifaceted, with a complex interplay of factors, such as genetic predisposition, environmental influences, lifestyle habits, and microbial constituents, contributing to the onset of these diseases.[9] Risk factors suggested for GI diseases include smoking, excessive alcohol intake, dietary choices, sedentary behavior, GI infections, and inadequate intake of calcium and vitamin D.[10–12] GI disorders, which are common chronic and recurrent diseases, often exhibit similar symptoms and demographic characteristics. Their pathogenesis remains ambiguous, with potential contributing factors including genetic elements, neurological aspects (gut-brain axis), psychological factors (e.g., stress and anxiety), and environmental components (e.g., diet).[13,14] In recent years, the potential connection between sodium intake and prevalent digestive diseases such as esophageal cancer, gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcers, and colorectal cancer has gained interest; however, epidemiological evidence remains indecisive.[15]

Accurate assessment of sodium intake remains challenging in large-scale epidemiological studies. Although 24-hour urinary sodium excretion is regarded as the gold standard for estimating salt intake, its collection is labor-intensive and subject to potential measurement error due to incomplete sampling.[16] Therefore, spot urinary sodium measurements are often used as a practical alternative, but they can be influenced by hydration status. Expressing sodium relative to urinary creatinine (UNaUCr) partially corrects for urine dilution and has been shown to correlate moderately with 24-hour sodium excretion in diverse populations, as demonstrated by Mente et al.[17] Such an approach provides a biologically plausible and feasible proxy for habitual sodium intake in population-based studies. Despite growing epidemiological interest, the potential causal relationship between sodium exposure and specific GI diseases remains unclear, and few studies have systematically evaluated this association using an MR framework.[18] The Mendelian randomization (MR) method, which emulates a randomized controlled trial design, serves as an advantageous tool for scrutinizing the causal association between risk elements (e.g., UNaUCr) and outcomes (e.g., digestive disorders), thereby avoiding the common drawbacks of observational studies.[19] The application of MR necessitates the consideration of 3 critical assumptions: the genetic variations display a strong connection with the exposure under investigation; the instrumental variables (IVs) derived from these genetic variations are independent of confounding factors that may be linked to both the exposure and outcome; and the genetic variations exclusively affect the outcome through the chosen exposure, without any horizontal pleiotropy effects or involvement in other biological pathways.[20] In this context, we conducted an MR analysis to evaluate the potential causal effects of genetically predicted UNaUCr on a wide range of GI diseases, based on the hypothesis that sodium intake may play a role in the pathogenesis of digestive disorders.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are used as genetic IVs to elucidate the causal effect of an exposure on phenotypic outcomes in MR studies. Using SNP data extracted from genome-wide association studies (GWASs) for both exposure and outcome and harmonizing the effect allele of the SNPs, this MR study explored potential causal relationships between UNaUCr and a comprehensive set of GI traits.

2.2. Data source

The UK Biobank (UKBB), conducted between 2006 and 2010, is a large multicenter cohort study that recruited 500,000 participants from the UK.[21] The FinnGen study aimed to comprehensively analyze the genetic and health data of over 500,000 individuals from Finland.[22] GWAS summary statistics for UNaUCr were obtained from UKBB (N = 327,616). GWAS data for GI diseases were primarily derived from the FinnGen consortium. Detailed dataset characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Because the exposure GWAS (UKBB) and outcome GWAS (FinnGen) were derived from largely independent cohorts, substantial sample overlap is unlikely.

2.3. Selection of instrumental variables

SNPs associated with UNaUCr at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) were selected. Variants with effect allele frequency < 0.01 were excluded. Independent instruments were identified using linkage disequilibrium clumping (window = 10,000 kb; r2 < 0.001). Harmonization was conducted using the TwoSampleMR R package (version 4.1.2) to align effect alleles. Palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies were removed to prevent strand ambiguity. Instrument strength was evaluated at the instrument-set level using the F-statistic. An F-statistic > 10 indicated sufficient instrument strength.[23–25] F-statistics for final instrument sets are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

2.4. Statistical analysis

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary analysis. Complementary methods included weighted median and MR-Egger.[26,27] Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger intercept, and heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran Q statistic. MR-PRESSO was applied to detect and correct outlier variants.[28] We employed leave-one-out analyses to assess robustness, as previously reported.[29–31] To further assess potential horizontal pleiotropy, a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) was conducted for the instrumental SNPs using the IEU OpenGWAS repository. Genome-wide significant associations (P < 5 × 10-8) with predefined factors, including renal function, blood pressure, BMI, and other cardiometabolic traits, were identified. Given that UNaUCr may reflect renal sodium handling, hydration status, and creatinine-related physiology in addition to sodium intake, additional analyses were conducted to address potential violations of instrumental variable assumptions. MR-RAPS was applied to obtain causal estimates robust to weak instrument bias and systematic balanced pleiotropy.[32] Steiger filtering was performed to verify the causal direction by testing whether each SNP explained more variance in the exposure than in the outcome.[33] Causal estimates are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To control for multiple comparisons, a 5% false discovery rate (FDR) threshold was applied. All analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR,[34] MendelianRandomization,[27] and MR-PRESSO R package[28] in R software (version 4.1.2).

3. Results

3.1. Primary MR analyses

All IVs had F-statistics exceeding 10, indicating adequate instrument strength (Supplementary Table 2). Causal associations with FDR-adjusted P values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

3.2. FDR-significant findings

Using the IVW method, we identified 2 FDR-significant outcomes. Genetically predicted UNaUCr was positively associated with “Diseases of esophagus, stomach and duodenum” (OR = 1.76; 95% CI: 1.35–2.30) and “irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)” (OR = 3.05; 95% CI: 1.64–5.65). MR-Egger and weighted median estimates were directionally consistent with IVW results (Figs 1–3; Supplementary Table 3). The composite endpoint “Diseases of esophagus, stomach and duodenum” includes conditions such as gastro-esophageal reflux disease, gastritis, and duodenitis. Given the heterogeneity within this category, the overall association should be interpreted as an average effect across diagnoses and may not reflect uniform effects for each specific condition.

3.3. Nominal associations

Genetically predicted UNaUCr showed nominal associations (P < .05) with several additional GI conditions (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 3). As these did not survive multiple-testing correction, they should be interpreted as exploratory findings.

3.4. Sensitivity analysis

Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed, and MR-Egger intercept tests indicated no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 4). Cochran Q test showed no significant heterogeneity (Supplementary Table 5), and MR-PRESSO detected no outliers. Lookup in the IEU OpenGWAS database (P < 5 × 10-8) identified several instrumental SNPs associated with phenotypes such as BMI, suggesting potential pleiotropic pathways. However, leave-one-out analyses confirmed that results were not driven by single SNPs (Fig. 5). MR-RAPS, which provides causal estimates robust to weak instruments and balanced pleiotropy, yielded results consistent with the primary IVW analysis: UNaUCr remained associated with Diseases of esophagus, stomach and duodenum (OR = 1.74; 95% CI: 1.32–2.29) and IBS (OR = 3.45; 95% CI: 1.85–6.42). Steiger tests confirmed that the causal direction was from UNaUCr to the outcomes, with all instruments explaining significantly more variance in the exposure than in the outcomes (P = 1.21 × 10−47 and 5.43 × 10−12).

4. Discussion

Our MR analysis indicates that genetically predicted higher UNaUCr is associated with an increased risk of 2 FDR-significant outcomes: diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, and IBS. Nominal associations with 13 additional GI disorders were observed but should be interpreted as exploratory. These findings provide insight into potential links between sodium-related physiology and GI health, but do not support a universal causal role of sodium across all GI diseases.

The composite endpoint “Diseases of esophagus, stomach and duodenum” encompasses heterogeneous clinical entities, including gastro-esophageal reflux disease, gastritis, and duodenitis. Consequently, the observed association represents an average genetic effect across related but pathophysiologically distinct conditions, and should not be interpreted as evidence of uniform effects on each individual disease. For example, reflux disease may be more sensitive to sodium-related alterations in lower esophageal sphincter function or gastric acid exposure, whereas gastritis and duodenitis may be more strongly influenced by inflammatory and microbial mechanisms. Epidemiological studies have linked high-salt dietary patterns to reflux symptoms and upper GI mucosal pathology,[35,36] and high salt intake has been associated with increased risks of atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia in endoscopy-based studies.[35] However, heterogeneity across individual diseases within this composite category should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Several biological mechanisms may plausibly connect sodium-related physiology with upper GI disorders and IBS. Experimental evidence suggests that high sodium exposure can promote pro-inflammatory immune responses, including Th17 cell differentiation via SGK1/NFAT5/p38 MAPK signaling pathways.[37–39] High-salt diets have also been shown to alter gut microbiota composition, reduce beneficial commensals such as Lactobacillus, and enhance intestinal inflammatory activity in animal and translational studies.[40–42] In addition, sodium intake may influence GI hormones, including gastrin and GLP-1, which regulate gastric acid secretion, motility, and mucosal protection.[43–46] Clinical dietary intervention studies report that higher salt intake is associated with increased circulating gastrin concentrations and altered postprandial hormonal responses.[47] Furthermore, high salt exposure has been shown experimentally to enhance Helicobacter pylori colonization and gastric mucosal injury.[48–50] These pathways, including immune modulation, microbiota disruption, hormonal regulation, and microbial susceptibility, are likely interconnected and may collectively contribute to sodium-related GI pathophysiology.

Importantly, UNaUCr represents a proxy phenotype rather than a direct measure of dietary sodium intake. Spot urinary sodium measures are influenced by urine concentration and physiological variability, and may not precisely reflect true sodium intake.[51,52] UNaUCr is also influenced by creatinine excretion variability, which may confound its interpretation as a sodium intake proxy. Renal sodium handling is tightly regulated by mechanisms including the intrarenal renin–angiotensin system.[53] Therefore, genetic variants associated with UNaUCr may partly capture pathways involved in renal sodium regulation rather than intake per se. Such mechanisms could potentially introduce violations of the exclusion restriction assumption in MR analyses.[27] Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting genetically predicted variation in sodium-related physiology rather than pure dietary sodium exposure alone, and caution is warranted when extrapolating to individual GI diseases.

This study has several notable strengths. First, the instrumental variables were selected under stringent genome-wide significance thresholds and underwent comprehensive sensitivity analyses, including heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and leave-one-out assessments, which enhanced the robustness of the causal inference. Second, the 2-sample MR framework minimized reverse causation and residual confounding, thereby strengthening the credibility of the observed associations between genetically predicted sodium intake and GI diseases. Third, the use of large-scale GWAS summary statistics increased statistical power and reduced the likelihood of weak instrument bias.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be recognized. The genetic data were derived predominantly from individuals of European ancestry, which may limit extrapolation of the findings to other populations with different genetic backgrounds and dietary patterns. In addition, although multiple approaches were applied to detect horizontal pleiotropy, unrecognized pleiotropic pathways cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, this study evaluated the effect of lifelong genetically proxied sodium intake on disease risk, rather than short-term dietary modification or disease progression in patients with established GI conditions. Therefore, future prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the underlying biological mechanisms.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified 2 significant associations between genetically predicted UNaUCr and GI outcomes: diseases of the esophagus, stomach, and duodenum, and IBS. Other observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. These results highlight the potential role of sodium-related physiology in GI health, but replication in other consortia and validation using alternative sodium exposure instruments or direct dietary measurements are needed to confirm these findings.
