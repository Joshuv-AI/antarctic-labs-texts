Barrier breakdown: lung-brain crosstalk in systemic inflammation
Authors: Nuria Villalba, Richard S Beard, Sarah Y Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lung and brain engage in a dynamic, bidirectional communication that influences immune homeostasis both locally and systemically, shaping overall health and disease progression. Lung infection is increasingly recognized for its role in triggering or exacerbating neurological dysfunction, ultimately worsening clinical outcomes. Although the brain is shielded by the blood-brain barrier (BBB), it remains vulnerable to secondary injury during pulmonary infection, primarily through lung-driven systemic inflammation, immune cell activation, and the release of cytokines, chemokines, and other circulating mediators. This review explores the biological and immunological mechanisms underlying lung-to-brain communication, with a focus on cerebral alterations following pulmonary bacterial infection. We examine the contributions of circulating mediators such as degradation products of the endothelial glycocalyx (eGC), endothelial proteins, inflammatory cytokines, and others originating from the lung and impacting the central nervous system (CNS). We highlight emerging insights into how lung-triggered systemic inflammation causes neurological sequelae, including cognitive deficits and behavioral changes. Finally, we discuss recent experimental findings and clinical evidence that advance our understanding of the detrimental interplay between pulmonary inflammation and brain dysfunction. We then outline the clinical relevance, therapeutic translation and opportunities for future research. The mediators discussed here point to emerging therapeutic targets and highlight the clinical challenges of preventing long-term neurological complications of bacterial lung infections.Keywords: Inflammation, Lung infection, Blood-brain barrier, Brain dysfunction, Sepsis

Background

The intricate connections among multiple organs through the circulation system are essential for sustaining physiological balance and overall health. While the gut-brain axis has gathered significant attention, the equally vital yet less explored lung-brain axis warrants deeper investigation [1–3]. Communication between these two organs is particularly critical for neurobehavioral health such as memory and cognition during bacterial infections, given the susceptibility of the lungs to bacterial invasion. Although progress has been made in characterizing this inter-organ crosstalk, our understanding of the mediators linking lung infection to brain dysfunction remains limited. Acute pulmonary infections can trigger robust local and systemic inflammatory responses, often escalating to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [4, 5]. These responses, and the following cytokine storm, not only impair lung function but can also extend systemically to affect distant organs, including the brain [6].

In this review, we examine the cerebral consequences of systemic inflammation triggered by lung infections. We synthesize current evidence on how pulmonary-derived mediators including glycocalyx components, endothelial proteins, cytokines, extracellular histones and extracellular vesicles (EVs), interact with the BBB and influence brain homeostasis. We highlight research directions aimed at elucidating the pathways underlying lung-induced brain dysfunction and identify potential strategies for intervention and recovery. By integrating findings across preclinical and clinical studies, we summarize key knowledge gaps, methodological limitations, and new avenues for therapeutic advance. Our goal is to provide an integrative approach to identify key mechanisms. These insights can help support the design of future studies to develop new clinical strategies to mitigate neurological consequences of lung injury and inflammation.

Air−blood and blood−brain barriers: structure and function

The air-blood barrier (ABB) or alveolar-capillary barrier and the BBB are specialized barrier systems that maintain physiological homeostasis by regulating the movement of substances and immune cells; however, they differ in structure, function and immune activity (Fig. 1; Table 1). The ABB prioritizes gas exchange while balancing permeability to maintain fluid homeostasis. This barrier is composed of the alveolar epithelium, the capillary endothelium, and a thin basement membrane (BM) that lies in between them. In the brain, the capillary endothelium is in proximity and supported by a BM, astrocyte end-feet and pericytes within the neurovascular unit (NVU). This structural configuration helps reinforce endothelial junction integrity by limiting the free passage of molecules and immune cells from the blood into neural tissue while establishing connections with other brain cells such as neurons and microglia [7]. Structurally, the alveolar epithelium, formed by type I and type II pneumocytes, serves as the interface for gas exchange. The secretion of surfactant by alveolar type II cells not only helps reduce surface tension to prevent alveolar collapse but also plays a key role in regulating innate immunity [8, 9]. Tight junctions between epithelial cells mostly include various claudins and occludin. Claudins interact with zonula occludens (ZO) scaffold proteins [10], which anchor tight junctions to the actin cytoskeleton, a central regulator of paracellular permeability [11, 12]. Beneath the epithelium lies the capillary endothelium, a thin delicate single layer of vascular endothelial cells that controls blood flow and permeability. The endothelial barrier of the lung and brain exhibit distinct structural specializations. While both express adherens junctions, tight junctions, and gap junctions [13], these cell-cell junctions are less dense and more permissive in the pulmonary endothelium than those in the brain endothelium. The lung endothelial junctions are also highly responsive to injury and inflammatory stimuli allowing the lung to adapt to inhaled pathogens and toxicants [14]. In contrast, brain endothelial cells form a highly restrictive barrier and unlike the vascular endothelium that lines peripheral blood vessels, possess unique characteristics. These include continuous and complex tight junctions, dominated by claudin-5, followed by occludin, ZO-1 and junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs). They also express vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin), which effectively contributes to seal the paracellular space conferring low paracellular passage and high electrical resistance [15–17]. These cells display low levels of transcytosis and are framed by a thick, collagen-rich BM [7]. The absence of fenestrations helps maintain the integrity of this barrier which is essential for maintaining neuroprotection. Additionally, the ABB and BBB express selective transport systems to facilitate nutrient supply from the blood to the brain while restricting the passage of harmful substances [18–20]. Resident immune cells further reinforce these barrier systems. In the lung, alveolar macrophages patrol the pulmonary tissue and eliminate bacteria efficiently on their own via phagocytosis [21] and resolve inflammation by clearing apoptotic cells [22]. Interstitial macrophages, located in the connective tissue surrounding the airways, blood vessels, and alveolar interstitium, help maintain tissue homeostasis and vascular integrity [23–25]. In the brain, microglia cells serve as the resident macrophage population responsible for immune surveillance, regulate BBB permeability and keep immune responses to a minimum maintaining a low-inflammatory milieu [26, 27]. Together, these structural differences show how the ABB and BBB control permeability and regulate inflammation in response to environmental and systemic challenges.

From systemic inflammation to cognitive decline: mechanisms of Sepsis-Associated Encephalopathy (SAE) in pneumonia-induced sepsis

Sepsis, whether originating from the lung or other tissues, frequently leads to neurological complications, with chronic neurocognitive impairment being one of the most prevalent long-term consequences [28]. This form of cognitive dysfunction, occurring in the absence of clinical evidence of encephalitis or apparent structural damage, is termed SAE [29–31]. In its acute phase, SAE is characterized by altered mental status, ranging from lethargy and agitation to delirium and coma. Over the long term, cognitive deficits persist in up to 70% of sepsis survivors, manifesting as memory loss, attention difficulties, reduced verbal fluency and disability, each of which can severely impact patients’ ability to live independently [32]. Patients with bacterial pneumonia exhibit a 3-fold increased risk of developing cognitive dysfunction compared to those with non-bacterial pneumonia [33]. Although SAE is common in patients with pneumonia-induced sepsis [33], the specific mechanisms underlying the association between pneumonia and dementia have not been fully elucidated. Several pathophysiological mechanisms seem to be involved. Animal models demonstrate structural compromise or functional alteration of the BBB, and the extent of BBB leakage correlates with pneumonia severity [6]. The lung infection triggers systemic inflammation with circulating cytokines, immune cells and bacterial products (e.g., lipopolysaccharide, LPS) mediating BBB breakdown and neuroinflammation [34, 35]. This cognitive decline observed in SAE is also thought to result from inflammation and hypoperfusion-induced injury to neurons and neuronal fibers [36]. Specifically, systemic hypotension and reduced cerebral perfusion may directly contribute to brain injury and cognitive impairment [37–39] and overtime may even alter amyloid precursor protein processing, a major contributor to the development of Alzheimer’s disease [40]. In the brain, activated microglia and astrocytes, main cell types involved in neuroinflammation, release inflammatory mediators that further destabilize the BBB, promoting peripheral immune cell infiltration via chemokine gradients (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and upregulated adhesion molecules. Endothelial expression of intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and vascular adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) mediate the firm adhesion of immune cells to endothelial cells, facilitating immune cell transmigration and amplifying brain inflammation [41]. Other pathological alterations have been linked to SAE including impaired cerebral autoregulation, dysregulated neurotransmission, and reduced metabolic activity [30]. Changes in microglia density and morphology in cortical and hippocampal regions have been described in lung-induced systemic inflammation [42].

Brain regions critical to cognition and autonomic control including the hippocampus, amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and brainstem appear particularly susceptible to inflammation-induced damage during pneumonia and sepsis [43, 44]. Hippocampal atrophy correlates with memory loss in sepsis survivors [45, 46], while postmortem studies show structural abnormalities in the brainstem, including hypoplasia, edema, and calcification, which may impair alertness, cardiovascular control, and immune regulation [47, 48]. Neuroimaging studies report that post-sepsis cognitive impairment, attention deficiency, and reduced verbal fluency correlate with decreased volume in cerebral and cerebellar white matter, the cerebral cortex, and hippocampus [46]. These alterations are typically assessed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or standard neurobehavioral assays, including novel object recognition and the Morris water maze for memory, the open field and elevated plus maze for anxiety-like behavior, and locomotor activity [49]. The selective vulnerability of certain brain regions may be influenced by cytokine distribution and glial cell heterogeneity and distribution within the brain [26]. Although direct lung-derived signals that cause specific activation of astrocytes and microglia are lacking in the context of lung infection, pathophysiological mechanisms involve a combination of cytokine release, vascular permeability changes and BBB breakdown, glial activation and once prolonged chronically, brain dysfunction.

Pulmonary-derived mediators as drivers of brain dysfunction

Pulmonary infections can trigger a cascade of systemic responses that extend beyond the lungs, leading to neurological dysfunction such as memory loss, cognitive decline and speech disturbances [33, 50–52]. The mechanisms underlying this lung-brain crosstalk are multifaceted, given that there are various routes of communication between the lung and the CNS. These include neural connections through the vagus nerve, endocrine signaling mainly through the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis which influences stress hormones causing behavioral changes like fatigue or anxiety, and immune-mediated pathways involving the release of cytokines into the bloodstream and the activation and migration of immune cells to CNS compartments [53]. Recent investigations have associated pulmonary infections caused by bacteria such as Bordetella pertussis (whooping cough) and Clostridium pneumonia with amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition and other markers of CNS pathology in Alzheimer’s disease [54–56]. Furthermore, pneumonia-induced sepsis by Streptococcus pneumoniae and Klebsiella pneumoniae have been linked to cognitive impairment in both animal models and clinical studies [57, 58]. Another illustrative example of lung-brain communication is lung viral infection. Infections by SARS-CoV-2 offer compelling evidence of pulmonary-neurological crosstalk. The virus binds to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors expressed in both pulmonary and brain tissues, causing a spectrum of neurological manifestations including headache, altered mental status and encephalopathy among others [59] with BBB impairment as the main pathological mechanism as demonstrated in models of COVID-19 infection [60–63]. Collectively, these findings reinforce the concept that respiratory illnesses are not only confined to the lungs; rather, they exert systemic effects that influence brain homeostasis. Key signaling mediators in lung-to-brain communication

eGC degradation products

Systemic inflammation during sepsis or pneumonia can impair neurological function by disrupting specific brain regions, however, the soluble mediators responsible remain incompletely defined. A growing area of interest centers on bioactive molecules released from the pulmonary endothelium, particularly fragments resulting from degradation of the eGC. The eGC plays a critical role in vascular integrity, and its breakdown contributes significantly to the pathophysiology of sepsis [64–67]. Notably, eGC fragments such as heparan sulfate resulting from eGC degradation, have been shown to accumulate in the hippocampus, a brain region highly sensitive to BBB compromise [68–70]. In patients with sepsis, elevated plasma levels of sulfated heparan sulfate have been correlated with long-term cognitive impairment [70]. Increased concentration of heparan sulfate in the hippocampus have also been reported in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, reflecting its potential role in CNS pathology [71]. However, whether these circulating eGC fragments can serve as early biomarkers for sepsis-associated cognitive decline remains to be determined, requiring further validation for specificity, sensitivity, and diagnostic thresholds [72]. Experimental research using murine models has offered mechanistic insights [70]. Highly sulfated heparan sulfate fragments inhibit brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling by binding to BDNF and blocking activation of its TrkB receptor. This interaction disrupts hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP), a key process in learning and memory. Although researchers were unable to prevent heparan-BDNF interaction directly, TrkB receptor agonists and exogenous BDNF administration successfully restored LTP in vitro, offering a potential therapeutic pathway to protect or recover cognitive function after sepsis [70, 73]. Whether similar mechanisms apply to other eGC fragments and what threshold concentrations are required for their selective penetration into the hippocampus remain open questions.

Lung-derived tau and amyloids

Another emerging and still incompletely defined mechanism of lung-brain communication involves what we termed endothelial proteinopathy. In this process, lung endothelial cells aberrantly express, process, or release cytotoxic forms of tau or amyloids oligomers during infection [74–80]. This mechanism is distinct from classical neuroproteinopathies (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease-related tauopathy), which arise from intrinsic neuronal protein misfolding associated with neurodegenerative disease. Here, lung-derived tau refers specifically to endothelial-expressed tau released from the infected lung parenchyma into the circulation.

In vivo, lung endothelium expresses big tau (~ 110 kDa) and smaller tau isoforms (0N4R, 1N4R, 2N4R) that share aggregation-prone motifs with cerebral tau proteins implicated in Alzheimer’s disease [77]. In clinical and experimental models, these tau and amyloid variants have been detected in the bloodstream, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) during pneumonia caused by pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus [74, 75, 78, 81, 82]. Lung-derived tau and amyloids impair hippocampal LTP and alter dendritic morphology in ex vivo studies, suggesting a direct impact on synaptic function [83]. Intracerebral injection of endothelial tau and amyloid replicates these effects in mice, further implicating these proteins in cognitive function [81]. A key concern is whether circulating endothelial tau acts as a molecular seed, triggering misfolding and aggregation of neuronal tau. Some studies have provided evidence that tau isoforms released by pulmonary endothelial cells infected with Pseudomonas aeruginosa can initiate neuronal tau generation and release via a sirtuin 1-mediated mechanism [84] and tau aggregation in vitro [77]. Moreover, tau aggregates were detected in the brain of mice with Pseudomonas aeruginosa pulmonary infection, specifically following exposure to ExoY-expressing strains. Notable, circulating tau persisted in plasma for at least a month post-infection, and phosphorylated forms, especially at Ser214, a modification associated with cytotoxicity, were identified [85].

Importantly, recent findings revealed that cystatin C, a cysteine protein inhibitor released by pulmonary endothelial cells during infection, can neutralize the cytotoxicity of endothelial-derived Aβ. This modulation of its prion-like toxicity may help mitigate subsequent neurodegenerative effects. Mass spectrometry and immunoprecipitation confirmed that cystatin C acts as a key cytoprotective factor released from endothelial cells during Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection, offering a novel therapeutic target to counteract infection-induced amyloid pathology [86]. While its direct impact on tau remains under investigation, its ability to suppress Aβ toxicity may reduce the burden of circulating amyloids during infection and protect against synaptic dysfunction.

Although soluble tau can enter and accumulate in brain microvascular endothelial cells and induce endothelial cell senescence [87], it remains unclear whether endothelial-derived proteins cross the BBB and deposit into the brain parenchyma in vivo. Under conditions of BBB compromise, this mechanism is possible and based on in vitro models, but direct in vivo evidence remains limited [88]. Circulating cytotoxic proteins may be sufficient to drive endothelial dysfunction and initiate neuronal toxicity even without parenchyma deposition. Given the novelty of this mechanism, further investigation is needed to determine if other inflammatory stimuli (e.g., sterile inflammation) or non-bacterial pathogens (e.g., viruses, fungi) can cause similar infectious proteinopathy. In addition, it is important to identify whether circulating cytotoxic proteins undergo post-translational modifications that enhance their brain-penetrant properties and affect their pathogenicity. New interventions aimed at limiting endothelial protein release of lung origin and/or their clearance from the circulation may provide promising avenues to prevent infection-related proteinopathy and cognitive dysfunction.

Cytokines

An extensive body of evidence indicates that the immune system can regulate behavior indicating the neuromodulatory role of cytokines and immune cells [89–95]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines released during pulmonary infection can influence brain function by either entering the CNS or by being produced locally by activated glial cells. Circulating cytokines reach the CNS through permeable regions of the BBB, such as the circumventricular organs (CVOs), where the endothelial cell junctions are sparse [96]. Once cytokines bind to receptors on endothelial cells, downstream mediators amplify BBB disruption, upregulate adhesion molecules, and promote leukocyte infiltration, hallmarks of neuroinflammation. A previous study carried out by our group investigated the impact of pulmonary infection on BBB integrity and function using a murine model of Pseudomonas aeruginosa-induced pneumonia. We identified a clear link between lung infection and secondary neurological effects, primarily driven by increased lung permeability, a cytokine-mediated inflammatory response and bacterial dissemination [97]. Cytokines not only disrupted tight and adherens junctions but also initiated recruitment of immune cells into the brain and neuroinflammation. We also observed behavioral changes (hyperactivity and anxiety-like behavior) which highlights the functional significance of this lung-driven systemic inflammation-brain crosstalk. Importantly, our study demonstrates that brain dysfunction can occur independently of microbial CNS invasion, challenging traditional assumptions about CNS infection and creating opportunities for more refine diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Another study using a Pseudomonas aeruginosa pneumonia model similarly reported pneumonia-induced BBB leakage, reduced tight junction expression and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines [80]. Therapies aimed at modulating systemic cytokine levels or regulating neuroinflammation may represent promising strategies to mitigate long-term neurobehavioral consequences after severe pneumonia. These studies complement other reports demonstrating that pneumosepsis caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae results in increased vascular permeability in the lung and brain, along with microgliosis, elevated cytokine levels, neuroinflammation and behavioral alterations in infected animals [58]. A different mechanism for lung-induced brain dysfunction has linked severe pneumonia to the translocation of endogenous bacteria into the brain [98]. Those findings show how leaky lung-blood and brain-blood barriers during pneumonia cause microglia and astrocyte activation, another example of bacterial-induced disruption of brain homeostasis. Together, these studies highlight two distinct but converging mechanisms of lung-brain axis disruption, where pulmonary infections compromise brain homeostasis.

Extracellular histones

Beyond their canonical role in the nucleus to compact DNA, histones also have extracellular functions as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). During systemic inflammatory diseases including sepsis, trauma, infection and sterile inflammation, histones are released from dying or necrotic cells as free proteins into the extracellular space [99–103]. Extracellular histones are also released from activated neutrophils in a citrullinated form of histone H3 through NETosis, the process by which neutrophils release neutrophil-extracellular traps (NET) [104]. Among histones, H3 and H4 are the most cytotoxic, with their cationic and sticky nature contributing significantly to this toxicity through binding to anionic phospholipids in lipid bilayers [99, 105]. Extracellular histones bind to Toll-like receptors (TLRs) promoting systemic inflammation, endothelial injury, and thrombus formation [99, 102, 106–110]. Their deleterious effects on the vascular endothelium, including the cerebrovasculature, has been documented [111, 112]. Once in circulation, histones bind to negatively charged endothelial membranes, induce large aberrant intracellular calcium events and impair vasodilatory responses [111–113]. In addition to disrupting endothelial junctions [111, 114], extracellular histones, especially citrullinated H3, reorganize the cytoskeleton with increased F-actin fiber formation [114] which contributes to increasing paracellular permeability. While specific endothelial receptors for histones remain unidentified, members of the C-type lectin domain family, such as Clec2d [115, 116] and purinergic signaling pathways [117] have been identified as potential mediators of histones-induced effects. In the brain, histones exert neurotoxic effects. Experimental infusion of calf thymus histones in mice causes region-specific BBB disruption, particularly in the hippocampus, with increased permeability to small molecules (< 1 kDa) but not to larger tracers [111]. Recent studies using a CLP model show that activated neutrophils and NET formation exacerbate neuroinflammation and BBB dysfunction. These effects are reversed by peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) inhibition, which restores tight and adherens junction protein expression via Wnt3/β-catenin/TCF4 signaling [118]. Neutralization of circulating histones using anti-histone antibodies [99, 119–121], chelating agents such as non-anticoagulant heparins [122–124] or small polyanions such as suramin [125, 126] represent a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent BBB disruption and neuroinflammation. Upon lung injury, histones are released into the circulation alongside other lung-derived inflammatory mediators, including high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a non-histone nuclear protein [127]. HMGB1 activates TLR2/4 and RAGE receptors, initiating NF-κB-mediated transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and CXCL10 [128]. HMGB1 is considered a player in neuroinflammation due to its ability to cross the BBB [129]. Together, these mediators do not act in isolation but as part of a coordinated DAMP cascade that exacerbates BBB injury and inflammation and primes the brain and its vasculature for secondary insults.

Extracellular vesicles

EVs originating from inflamed lung tissue may play a key role in lung-to-brain communication. These vesicles carry nucleic acids, proteins, and DAMPs to distant organs [130]. EV composition varies under pathological conditions including inflammation. While the pulmonary endothelium is a major source of EVs, they can also be released by lung epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells. Once in circulation, EVs can be internalized by brain endothelial cells, influencing vascular integrity and promoting neuroinflammation and BBB leakage. Although both in vitro models and in vivo studies support this process [131–133], the mechanisms by which lung endothelial-derived EVs target brain endothelial cells and the BBB remain poorly understood. EVs can enter endothelial cells via multiple pathways, including membrane fusion, clathrin-dependent endocytosis, caveolin-mediated uptake, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis and lipid raft-mediated pathways [134]. The heterogeneity in EV size and cargo composition may determine the specific internalization mechanism. Lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1)-positive EVs demonstrate enhanced internalization by brain endothelial cells through specific binding to intracellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) [135]. Membrane-bound C-type lectin receptors have also been implicated in the internalization of macrophage-derived EVs [135]. Additionally, heparan sulfate proteoglycans act as receptors on the endothelial surface to facilitate EV uptake [136]. Several EV-associated ligands such as fibronectin, are known to bind heparan sulfate proteoglycans, facilitating vesicle docking and uptake into endothelial cells [137–139]. The use of integrin-blocking peptides has been shown to reduce EV internalization in endothelial cells [140]. Once internalized, EVs can trigger intracellular signaling and change endothelial functions. Mechanistic insights into how EVs alter endothelial permeability have been provided by studies showing that EVs carry and transfer the non-receptor tyrosine kinase c-Src, a key signaling molecule in permeability regulation [141], into endothelial cells promoting tyrosine phosphorylation of myosin-light chain and VE-cadherin, which triggers cytoskeleton contraction increasing endothelial permeability [142]. Other studies using other infection models such as E. coli, Candida albicans and SARS-CoV2 have shown that EVs can disrupt the endothelial barrier through additional mechanisms, including the delivery of virulent factors, and pathogen-associated molecular patterns that activate endothelial receptors and promote permeability changes [143, 144]. These findings complement our observations and highlight that EV-mediated endothelial dysfunction is a broader phenomenon across infections. Future studies are warranted to monitor the dynamics of brain endothelial cell uptake of lung-derived EVs, to identify transcriptional changes the brain endothelial cells undergo post-EV exposure, and to understand the mechanisms underlying the receptor-ligand specificity in EV uptake by endothelial cells (e.g., integrins and ICAM-1) during inflammatory conditions.

Clinical relevance of pulmonary-derived mediators: biomarkers and therapeutic strategies

Research on eGC degradation products is supported by strong clinical evidence: patients with pneumonia or sepsis consistently show elevated circulating eGC fragments, which correlate with organ failure and mortality [70]. These studies, however, establish just association (no causation) and no clinical trials have tested whether preventing eGC shedding, neutralizing eGC fragments, or restoring glycocalyx integrity improves BBB function or neurological outcomes. Preclinical studies have focused primarily on heparan sulfate without addressing the neurovascular effects of other eGC components (e.g., syndecan-1). Therapeutic strategies to enhance TrkB signaling (and increase BDNF levels) have gained attention in translational research for neurodegenerative diseases, but current evidence suggests they do not reverse disease pathology. Clinical trials using BDNF itself have been disappointing including in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [145], potentially due to its poor penetration into the brain [146]. Small-molecule TrkB agonists such as 7,8-dihydroxyflavone (7,8-DHF) have shown the ability to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine release in LPS-stimulated microglia [147]. Similarly, catalpol has demonstrated promising effects on long-term neurological sequelae of sepsis (cognitive decline/SAE) in experimental models; however, these findings remain preclinical and no active clinical trials are currently evaluating TrkB agonists for sepsis [148]. Only one Phase I clinical trial (NCT05040217) is testing BDNF delivery via gene therapy in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease, but no data have yet been released. For endothelial-derived amyloid and tau proteins, evidence is dominated by in vitro and in vivo studies. Human BALF from pneumonia patients contain tau and Aβ [74, 82], but do not distinguish which cytotoxic variants are present as well as their cellular origin (pulmonary endothelial cells or circulating). It is also unclear how much of the detected tau and Aβ reaches the systemic circulation and contributes to BBB dysfunction versus acting locally in the lung. Cystatin C has been shown in vitro to bind and neutralize Aβ, but its interaction with tau and its relevance in bacterial pneumonia remains untested; no clinical data support cystatin C administration as a therapeutic strategy.

Extracellular histones represent one of the most extensively studied mediators listed here. Since their initial detection in the plasma of septic patients [101], histones have been investigated both as biomarkers and as drivers of inflammation and organ injury. Numerous clinical studies report elevated circulating histones levels in sepsis, critical illness, and other inflammatory conditions; also, extensive in vitro and in vivo data have shown their effects on immune cells, platelets, and the vascular endothelium. Several anti-histone strategies have shown efficacy in animal models, including murine anti-histone antibodies and non-anticoagulant heparin derivates. Murine anti-histone antibodies are not suitable for clinical use, but a humanized anti-CitH3 monoclonal antibody has demonstrated promising preclinical activity, reducing cytokine levels, restoring macrophage function, and protecting against infection-induced lung injury [149]. Also, a non-anticoagulant heparin derivate (M6229) has completed phase I evaluation for safety and pharmacokinetics in sepsis [150].

For EVs, evidence of lung-to-brain transport suggests a role in the pathogenesis of pulmonary infection. However, clinical data on EV-derived therapeutic strategies for neurological complications following lung infection are extremely limited. Most studies focus on mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs, and nearly all supporting therapeutic evidence comes from in vitro and in vivo models [151].

Although these mediators and their mechanistic pathways are increasingly well characterized, they also point toward emerging therapeutic opportunities. Experimental strategies involving TrkB agonists, cystatin C administration, anti-histone therapies, or EVs have not yet advanced to clinical trials in bacterial pneumonia, but preclinical research consistently show that these approaches reduce neuroinflammation, stabilize barrier integrity, and attenuate cytokine-driven injury. These findings illustrate how targeting specific molecular pathways may ultimately translate into clinically meaningful treatments for patients with severe pneumonia or systemic inflammation.

Brain-to-lung pathways involved in lung immunity during bacterial pneumonia and systemic inflammation

Under pathological conditions such as bacterial pneumonia, the brain can actively regulate immune responses in peripheral organs, including the lungs. Although our review emphasized lung-to-brain signaling, here we outline the major brain-to-lung pathways that regulate pulmonary immunity: (1) the endocrine signaling; (2) neuronal efferent pathways, and (3) sensory peripheral pathways.

The endocrine system, coordinated by the hypothalamus, modulates lung immunity through the hypothalamic-neurohypophyseal system (oxytocin and arginine-vasopresin release) and the hypothalamic-hypophyseal portal system. Oxytocin has been shown to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production, limiting neutrophil infiltration, and promoting inflammation resolution [152, 153] and alleviate cognitive impairment in a mouse model of SAE [154]. Vasopressin, in parallel, exerts anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing macrophage-derived cytokines and mitigating sepsis-induced pulmonary edema [155]. Together, these neuroendocrine signals, provide a systemic mechanism through which the brain can prevent uncontrolled inflammation during pneumonia. Sensory and autonomic neurons innervating the respiratory tract also influence immune activity. During Staphylococcus aureus pneumonia, transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1)+ vagal sensory neurons detect infection and release neuropeptide calcitonin-gene-related peptide (CGRP), which inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokine production and limits T cell and neutrophil recruitment [156]. Finally, the lungs receive dense autonomic innervation that mediates efferent CNS control. In severe pneumonia, GABAergic neurons in the central amygdala (CeA) activate a descending CeA-rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM)-sympathetic circuit, increasing norepinephrine release and stimulating ADRB2+ interstitial macrophages, thereby amplifying cytokine production and lung injury [157]. Collectively, these pathways highlight the CNS as a central regulator of lung immune responses during pneumonia.

Limitations and opportunities for lung-brain axis research

Although significant progress has been made in identifying the factors, mechanisms, and animal models that elucidate lung-brain communication, the field remains constrained by several limitations. Mechanistic insights into lung-brain interactions are predominantly derived from animal studies, which, while informative, may not fully recreate the complexity of human disease. Several clinical studies have reported neurological sequelae of lung infections but offer limited information on signaling pathways, mechanisms or factors that might contribute to brain effects. The diverse etiology of lung infections and the disproportionate impact on vulnerable populations, such as older adults, further complicate translational relevance. Additionally, the potential role of the lung microbiome in neurological disease is an emerging area of interest. Whether therapeutic strategies targeting the lung microbiome could be effective in treating CNS disorders remains unclear [158]. Clinically, the neurological complications often seen in sepsis, ARDS, and COVID-19 serve as examples indicating the importance of better understanding lung-brain signaling and identifying new therapeutic targets. Longitudinal data on cognitive outcomes are scarce post-lung infection and there are no accepted biomarkers for lung-brain axis dysfunction. Emerging circulating mediators such as extracellular histones and lung endothelial-derived EVs open new opportunities for novel mechanisms beyond traditional neural pathways of communication between the lung and the brain such as vagus nerve signaling. Technological advances in EV profiling, single-cell transcriptomics, advanced co-culture systems and organoids, and in vivo imaging may enable better characterization of mediators involved in lung-brain crosstalk. Together, these limitations and strengths define a rapidly evolving field that demands advancing our knowledge on the mechanisms linking lung infection to neurological health (Table 4).

Lung-brain axis research: limitations and opportunities

Conclusions

Clinical and experimental investigations into cerebral dysfunction following pulmonary infection have significantly expanded our understanding of the neurological consequences of pulmonary exposure to environmental pathogens. Although not the primary focus of this review, it is important to recognize that lung-brain communication is bidirectional, and signals originating from the brain may also exacerbate pulmonary pathology. Cognitive impairment triggered by lung infection and systemic inflammation such as that observed in sepsis, can range in severity and has been associated with lasting neurological deficits that profoundly affect the patient’s quality of life and, in some cases, survival. The heterogeneity of the neurological manifestations emphasizes the complexity of the underlying mechanisms. A combination of increased vascular permeability, systemic immune responses, BBB disruption, and neuroinflammation contribute to lung-induced cerebral pathology. Despite significant research progress made in this field, signaling pathways remain insufficiently characterized. Future studies are needed to identify key mediators as potential therapeutic targets, and to develop translationally relevant animal models that can recapitulate the spectrum of CNS disorders observed in patients with pulmonary infection.
