Adverse childhood experiences mediate the association between ADHD symptoms and severe psychological distress in the Japanese general population: a causal mediation analysis of 29,268 participants
Authors: Keita Tokumitsu, Norio Sugawara, Takahiro Tabuchi, Norio Yasui-Furukori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) include various forms of trauma or chronic stress during childhood, e.g., physical, emotional, and sexual abuse; neglect; and household dysfunction. ACEs have been linked to long-term psychological difficulties. Emerging research suggests that individuals with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may be more frequently exposed to ACEs due to familial conflict or behavioral dysregulation. However, it remains unclear which specific ACE types mediate the association between ADHD symptoms and adult psychological distress.MethodsWe analyzed data from 29,268 adults in a nationwide Japanese internet-based survey. ADHD symptoms were measured using the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS-J-6), and severe psychological distress (SPD) was measured using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) scale. Participants reported exposure to ten types of ACEs. Causal mediation analysis was performed to identify ACEs that mediated the ADHD–SPD association, adjusting for demographic variables.ResultsAmong participants, 3.69% screened positive for ADHD symptoms, 12.81% met the criteria for SPD, and 5.09% reported four or more ACEs. Both ADHD symptoms and greater ACE exposure were significantly associated with SPD. Five ACE indicators, including emotional neglect, emotional abuse, physical abuse, household physical violence, and having four or more ACEs, significantly mediated the ADHD–SPD relationship. Emotional neglect had the strongest mediating effect (11.19%).ConclusionsEmotion-related ACEs partially mediate the association between ADHD symptoms and adult psychological distress. While preventing childhood adversity remains essential, addressing ADHD symptoms through psychoeducation and intervention may reduce psychological burden in adulthood. These findings underscore the importance of trauma-informed approaches in the assessment and treatment of individuals with ADHD.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07656-9.Keywords: Adverse childhood experience, ACE, Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, ADHD, Psychological distress, Causal mediation analysis

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) refer to various forms of trauma and chronic stress experienced before the age of 18, including physical, emotional, and sexual abuse; neglect; exposure to domestic violence; and parental mental illness or substance abuse [1]. ACEs are alarmingly prevalent, with only 39.9% of adults reporting no history of ACEs, thus highlighting their widespread nature [2]. A large body of evidence demonstrates that ACEs have profound long-term effects on both physical and mental health, as they contribute to increased risks of depression, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, and suicidality in adulthood [1, 3, 4]. These outcomes follow a dose–response pattern, such that a greater number of ACEs is associated with a greater likelihood of adverse health outcomes [5]. Given the prevalence and consequences of ACEs, both prevention strategies and ACE-informed clinical care are public health priorities.

Although the general association between ACEs and poor mental health in adulthood is well established, less is known about how ACEs interact with neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), in terms of shaping long-term psychological outcomes. ADHD is a common neurodevelopmental disorder that is characterized by inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity; its global prevalence rates are 5.9% in youth and 2.5% in adults [6–8]. Individuals with ADHD often experience chronic functional impairments and increased risks of psychiatric comorbidities, including depression, anxiety, and suicidality [9]. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and intellectual disability are more likely to be bullied than their typically developing peers, whereas children with ADHD tend to have a lower prevalence of being bullied despite being perceived as less accepted by teachers and more difficult to manage in Asian contexts [10, 11]. These findings suggest that the social experiences of children with neurodevelopmental disorders vary by diagnostic category and cultural background.

Recent studies suggest that individuals with ADHD are disproportionately exposed to ACEs compared with their peers without ADHD. This increased exposure may stem from multiple mechanisms, including behavioral dysregulation, increased family conflict, maladaptive parent–child interactions, and parental stress [12]. Moreover, longitudinal findings indicate that children with ADHD may be more vulnerable to subsequent adversities over time, creating a cycle of risk [12]. The co-occurrence of ACEs and ADHD raises important clinical concerns, as early trauma can exacerbate core ADHD symptoms, impair emotional regulation, and complicate diagnosis and treatment. Conversely, untreated ADHD may increase a child’s risk of encountering adversity in the home environment. These bidirectional dynamics suggest that trauma-informed approaches are essential in the care of individuals with ADHD.

Despite the growing recognition of the relationships among ADHD, ACEs, and poor psychological outcomes, the specific mediating role of different types of ACEs remains underexplored. Studies often treat ACEs as a cumulative score without examining whether distinct forms of adversity (e.g., emotional abuse vs. household dysfunction) have differential impacts. Identifying which ACE types most strongly mediate the relationship between ADHD symptoms and psychological distress in adulthood could enhance clinical assessment and guide targeted interventions. This is particularly relevant in psychiatric and developmental care settings, where unaddressed trauma histories may hinder therapeutic outcomes. Trauma-informed care, including screening for ACEs, offering psychoeducation, and providing appropriate referrals, has been proposed as a best-practice approach for individuals with ADHD [13].

To address this knowledge gap, the present study aimed to investigate the mediating effects of specific ACE categories on the relationship between ADHD symptoms and psychological distress in a large, community-based sample of adults in Japan. We used data from the Japan Society and New Tobacco Internet Survey (JASTIS), a nationwide internet cohort study [14]. ADHD symptoms were assessed using the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS-J-6), and psychological distress was measured using the validated Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) scale [15]. The participants retrospectively reported their exposure to ten types of ACEs, including emotional and physical abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction. Using causal mediation analysis, we quantified the extent to which each ACE type mediated the association between ADHD symptoms and severe psychological distress (SPD). By identifying which childhood experiences exert the strongest indirect effects, our study seeks to clarify the mechanisms linking neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities and adult mental health. These insights may inform trauma-sensitive strategies in ADHD care, promote early intervention, and help break generational cycles of adversity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design to explore the associations among ADHD symptoms, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), and severe psychological distress. The data were collected from the JASTIS database [14]. The JASTIS has been conducted annually since 2015. The 2024 survey was carried out between January 24 and February 27 by Rakuten Insight, Inc., headquartered in Tokyo, Japan. The survey provider maintains a panel of approximately 2.2 million members, which has been used in multiple research studies. For this study, a nationally representative sample of 32,000 individuals was selected to encompass a broad range of demographic and socioeconomic distributions. Predefined quotas based on gender, age, and prefecture ensured that each category was adequately represented, with data collection ceasing once the target for each group was reached. These quotas were established in accordance with Japan’s 2019 population statistics. The methodology of the JASTIS has been previously described in the published literature [14, 16, 17]. Since all survey questions required responses, there were no missing data. A stratified random sample of 50,850 panelists received an invitation to participate, and ultimately, 32,000 individuals aged 16 to 83 years, covering all 47 prefectures, completed the survey. The response rate was 62.9%.

Prior to participation, individuals provided online informed consent and had the option to withdraw at any stage. The study followed ethical guidelines established by national and institutional research committees and aligned with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki as amended in 2008. Ethical approval was granted by the Osaka International Cancer Institute’s Research Ethics Committee on June 19, 2020 (Approval No. 20084). The survey agency adhered to Japan’s Act on the Protection of Personal Information, and participants were compensated with “E points,” which could be exchanged for cash or online shopping credits. Of the 32,000 respondents in the JASTIS 2024 survey, 2,732 were excluded due to predetermined exclusion criteria, such as a short response time (< 600 s), failing an attention-check question, reporting all listed drug use categories, listing all health conditions, or reporting more than 15 family members. Consequently, the final analysis included data from 29,268 participants (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

Severe psychological distress

To evaluate SPD, we utilized the Japanese version of the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) [15]. The K6 is a short self-report questionnaire designed to measure an individual’s level of psychological distress. This scale is widely applied in both research and clinical contexts to efficiently identify individuals who may be experiencing significant emotional or psychological difficulties. The questionnaire comprises six items assessing the frequency of various distress-related feelings and symptoms over the past 30 days. The total K6 score ranges from 6 to 24, with higher scores reflecting greater psychological distress. In this study, we used a cutoff of K6 ≥ 13 points to indicate the presence of SPD. The Japanese version of the K6 has been validated, with studies indicating that a score of 13 or higher is the optimal threshold for detecting severe mood and anxiety disorders [18, 19]. Furthermore, a validation study by Furukawa et al. reported that the Japanese version demonstrated excellent screening performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.94 for identifying mood and anxiety disorders, which is comparable to that of the original English version [18].

ADHD symptoms

ADHD symptoms over the preceding six months were assessed using the 6-item Japanese version of the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS-J-6) [20]. This scale, which is based on DSM-IV Criterion A, has total scores ranging from 0 to 24. For this study, a score of 15 or higher was considered indicative of ADHD symptoms. The ASRS-J-6 has demonstrated satisfactory validity in Japan, with a specificity of 0.84 and a sensitivity of 0.67 [20].

Covariates

The covariates included age, sex (female/male), educational background, number of family members and household income.

Mediators

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)

The participants were asked to retrospectively report whether they had experienced any of the following ACEs before the age of 18 [21, 22]: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, physical neglect, parental separation/divorce, household physical violence, household substance abuse, household mental illness, and incarcerated household members. Responses for each item were recorded as either “yes” or “no”.

Statistical analyses

To align the characteristics of internet-based survey respondents with those of the general population, we applied the inverse probability weighting (IPW) method [16]. We used the 2019 Comprehensive Survey of Living Conditions (CSLC) dataset as a reference; this survey was conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW). We merged data from the JASTIS and CSLC surveys to estimate the likelihood of participating in an internet-based survey. The data were stratified by sex and age (14 strata), and propensity scores were calculated for each group. We employed a multivariable logistic regression model to calculate propensity scores (PSs) separately for each stratum. The variables used in the adjustment process were those common to both surveys. In most strata, adjustments were made for factors such as residential area, marital status, educational background, homeownership, self-rated health, and smoking status. However, for individuals aged 16–19 years, marital and smoking status were not adjusted due to uniformity in marital status within this age group and the discontinuation of smoking data collection for teenagers in 2013. Furthermore, for women aged 16–19 years, regional adjustments were omitted, as geographical differences were considered to have a minimal impact on this subgroup, given their uniformly lower personal computer ownership rates regardless of city size [23]. For women aged 70 and older, educational background was excluded as an adjustment factor because of historically limited access to higher education.

We conducted chi-square tests (for categorical variables) and Student’s t tests (for continuous variables) to compare sample characteristics based on severe psychological distress levels and ADHD symptoms. Multivariable binomial logistic regression was employed to investigate associations between severe psychological distress, ADHD symptoms, and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs). To explore whether ADHD symptoms contribute to an increased risk of severe psychological distress via various types of ACEs, we performed causal mediation analysis using the “mediate” command in Stata 18.0 [24].

The conceptual illustration of the primary mediation model used in this study is shown in Fig. 2. In this model, ADHD symptoms were specified as the independent variable, severe psychological distress as the dependent variable, and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) as the mediator. This model was designed to estimate the natural indirect, direct, and total effects of ACEs mediating the relationship between ADHD symptoms and psychological distress through causal mediation analysis. All analyses were adjusted for covariates, including age, sex, educational background, household income, and number of family members. To assess the robustness of the results, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we examined potential effect modification by age, sex, and ADHD symptoms on the associations between each ACE and SPD. Interaction terms (ACE × age, ACE × sex, and ACE × ADHD symptoms) were added to the logistic regression models, and interaction odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Second, in addition to the primary model (ADHD → ACE → SPD), we tested an alternative causal direction (ACE → ADHD → SPD) to estimate the proportion of the effect of each ACE on SPD that was mediated through ADHD symptoms. This alternative mediation analysis allowed us to evaluate the indirect contribution of ADHD to the relationship between each ACE and SPD (Figure S1). Finally, to address potential multicollinearity among ACE items, we also performed separate multivariable binomial logistic regression analyses for each ACE item to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) for SPD while controlling for all covariates.

The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, Texas) and IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corporation, Version 28.0.0.0).

Ethical considerations

The participants provided online informed consent, confirming their willingness to participate before they responded. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and Japan’s Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Osaka International Cancer Institute (Approval No. 20084).

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the general public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research.

Results

The sample included 29,268 individuals aged 16 years and older. The mean (SD) age of the study participants was 48.80 (17.64). The sample was 50.6% female (14,816/29,268). The prevalence of ADHD symptoms was 3.69% (1,081/29,268). Additionally, 12.81% (3,749/29,268) were classified as having severe psychological distress. Furthermore, 5.09% (1,489/29,268) of the participants had four or more ACEs, and within this subgroup, 35.06% (522/1,489) were classified as having severe psychological distress (Table 1). The participants were divided into two groups based on ADHD symptom status, and the prevalence of ACEs was compared. The results revealed that all types of ACEs were significantly more common in the group with ADHD symptoms (Fig. 3 and Table S1). Bivariate analysis based on the presence of severe psychological distress revealed significant differences in age, household income, number of family members, ADHD symptoms, and all types of ACEs. All types of ACEs were more prevalent among individuals with severe psychological distress (Table 1). A multivariable binomial logistic regression analysis revealed that younger age, male sex, low educational background, low household income, ADHD symptoms, and ACEs (emotional abuse, emotional neglect, parental separation/divorce, household substance abuse, household mental illness) were significantly associated with SPD (Table 2). Among these factors, the strongest association with severe psychological distress was found in individuals with ADHD symptoms (odds ratio [OR] = 3.91; p < 0.001), followed by emotional neglect (OR = 2.01; p < 0.001). Furthermore, another multivariable binomial logistic regression analysis was conducted by replacing the presence or absence of each of the 10 individual ACEs with a categorical variable indicating whether participants had four or more ACEs. The results revealed that having ADHD symptoms was the strongest predictor of severe psychological distress (OR = 4.07; p < 0.001), followed by having four or more ACEs (OR = 3.27; p < 0.001) (Table S2).

Causal mediation analysis of the primary model (ADHD → ACE → SPD) indicated that emotional abuse, physical abuse, emotional neglect, household physical violence, and having four or more ACEs were statistically significant mediators of the relationship between ADHD symptoms and SPD. The natural indirect effects (NIEs) were substantial for emotional abuse (NIE = 11.02%), physical abuse (NIE = 7.17%), emotional neglect (NIE = 11.19%), household physical violence (NIE = 8.39%), and having four or more ACEs (NIE = 8.35%) (Table 3 and Figure S2). To assess the robustness of these findings, sensitivity analyses were performed. Interaction analyses revealed significant moderating effects of sex and ADHD symptoms on the relationship between ACEs and SPD (Tables S3–S5). For example, the effect of emotional abuse on SPD was weaker among males (interaction OR = 0.71, p = 0.012), whereas the effect of physical abuse was stronger (interaction OR = 1.41, p = 0.022) (Table S3). In addition, physical abuse (interaction OR = 1.77, p = 0.014) amplified the effect of ADHD symptoms on SPD, while sexual abuse (interaction OR = 0.55, p = 0.013), physical neglect (interaction OR = 0.43, p = 0.003), and household mental illness (interaction OR = 0.63, p = 0.046) showed attenuated effects (Table S5). No significant interaction ORs were observed for age (all p > 0.05) (Table S4).

Furthermore, an alternative mediation model (ACE → ADHD → SPD) was analyzed to examine the mediating role of ADHD symptoms in the relationship between ACEs and SPD (Table S6, Figures S1 and S3). The largest mediation proportion of ADHD symptoms was observed for household physical violence (15.47%), followed by physical abuse (10.62%). ADHD symptoms also exerted significant indirect effects on SPD through emotional abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect.

Additionally, separate multivariable logistic regression analyses for each ACE type confirmed consistent positive associations with SPD. Emotional neglect (aOR = 3.40), emotional abuse (aOR = 3.05), and household physical violence (aOR = 2.92) showed the strongest associations, while all other ACE categories were also significant predictors. ADHD symptoms remained an independent predictor of SPD (aOR = 4.46) (Table S7).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated a robust association between ADHD symptoms, ACEs, and SPD in a large nationally representative sample. Individuals with ADHD symptoms reported significantly greater exposure to all types of ACEs, particularly emotional abuse and emotional neglect. Notably, 16.0% of individuals with ADHD symptoms had experienced four or more ACEs compared to only 4.67% of those without ADHD symptoms. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies suggesting that individuals with ADHD are more vulnerable to childhood adversity, potentially because of behavioral dysregulation, family conflict, or parental mental health conditions [25]. However, the lower prevalence of ACEs observed compared with surveys conducted among children and adolescents suggests possible recall bias [26]. We observed that the prevalence of severe psychological distress was notably higher among participants with ADHD symptoms and those with multiple ACEs, particularly in individuals who had experienced four or more ACEs. According to the multivariable binomial logistic regression analysis, ADHD symptoms were the strongest predictor of severe psychological distress (OR = 3.91), followed by emotional neglect (OR = 2.01). These results indicate that ADHD is closely associated with emotional ACEs and heightened vulnerability to mental health issues.

Using causal mediation analysis, we quantitatively examined the extent to which specific types of ACEs mediate the relationship between ADHD symptoms and SPD. Emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical abuse, household physical violence, and having a history of four or more ACEs emerged as statistically significant mediators. Among them, emotional neglect had the highest NIE (11.19%), followed closely by emotional abuse (NIE = 11.02%). This pattern highlights the disproportionate impact of emotionally harmful environments on long-term psychological well-being in individuals with ADHD traits. Notably, not all ACE categories acted as mediators in the ADHD–SPD pathway; the mediating effects were largely confined to ACEs involving direct emotional or physical harm, whereas several household dysfunction items showed no significant mediation effects. Our study is among the first to use causal mediation analysis to disentangle the indirect effects of distinct ACE categories on psychological distress in adults with ADHD symptoms. Despite the significant mediating effects of ACEs, the moderate size of the NIEs suggests that ADHD symptoms themselves have a direct effect on severe psychological distress in adulthood. The core characteristics of ADHD, such as emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and executive dysfunction, can contribute to persistent social and occupational challenges, thereby intensifying severe psychological distress independently of childhood adversity [27]. As a complementary analysis, we also tested the alternative causal direction (ACE → ADHD → SPD). ADHD symptoms significantly mediated the association between ACEs and SPD, with the largest proportion mediated for household physical violence (15.47%), followed by physical abuse (10.62%). Significant indirect effects of ADHD symptoms on SPD were also found through emotional abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. These findings suggest that early exposure to violence or neglect may increase the risk of psychological distress partly through exacerbating ADHD-related vulnerabilities.

Interestingly, although childhood sexual abuse is a well-established risk factor for mental health problems [28–30] and was significantly more prevalent among participants with ADHD symptoms (14.2% vs. 3.8%, p < 0.001; Table S1), it did not emerge as a significant mediator in our model. Given our large sample size (n = 29,268) and adjustment for confounding variables such as age and sex, statistical power was unlikely to be a limiting factor. One possible interpretation is that sexual abuse contributes to psychological distress through pathways that are independent of ADHD symptoms. That is, sexual abuse may function as a direct risk factor for poor mental health rather than as an indirect risk factor through ADHD. Previous studies have shown that childhood sexual abuse leads to elevated risks of PTSD, depression, and suicidality regardless of the presence of neurodevelopmental disorders [28–30]. Although underreporting of sexual abuse remains a concern, especially owing to stigma and gender-specific disclosure barriers [31–33], the relatively high reporting rate observed in this study suggests that measurement bias alone is unlikely to explain the lack of mediation. This finding emphasizes the need to consider sexual abuse as a distinct clinical target for trauma-informed intervention.

Supporting this interpretation, separate multivariable binomial logistic regression models confirmed that sexual abuse independently predicted SPD when analyzed singly with covariates (aOR = 2.34). Thus, the absence of mediation may reflect intercorrelations among ACEs rather than a true lack of association.

Sensitivity analyses revealed sex- and ADHD-specific interaction ORs indicating differences in how ACEs influence psychological distress. For instance, the effects of emotional and physical abuse varied between men and women, implying that demographic and neurodevelopmental characteristics modify the impact of childhood adversity. These findings suggest that interventions should be individualized, taking into account both the type of adversity and patient characteristics.

These findings underscore the need for integrated care that combines trauma-informed therapy with ADHD-specific psychoeducation and individualized symptom management. In addition to addressing trauma history, treatment strategies should involve helping individuals with ADHD develop effective strategies to manage core symptoms and cope with social and emotional challenges. Evidence-based interventions, such as ADHD-adapted cognitive‒behavioral therapy (CBT) and mindfulness-based approaches, have shown promise in enhancing emotional regulation and reducing psychological distress [34, 35]. Given the heritability of ADHD and its impact on family functioning, supporting caregivers, especially parents with ADHD, is also essential. Children with ADHD traits not only face increased risks of maltreatment due to behavioral challenges but are also more likely to be raised in environments where one or both parents have ADHD symptoms [36]. Undiagnosed or untreated parental ADHD can result in inconsistent caregiving, family conflict, and emotional neglect, all of which may contribute to the accumulation of ACEs. Moreover, parents with their own history of childhood adversity may inadvertently perpetuate trauma across generations. Targeted family-focused interventions, such as parent training, early identification of parental ADHD, and supportive psychoeducation, can help disrupt this cycle and promote more stable, nurturing home environments [37].

Our study highlights the critical mediating role of ACEs in the relationship between ADHD symptoms and severe psychological distress while also emphasizing the direct effect of ADHD symptoms themselves. The intergenerational transmission of ADHD may further increase the risk of ACEs, increasing the psychological burden in adulthood. However, studies have shown that clinicians often overlook patients’ histories of childhood adversity, conduct inadequate screening, and may lack awareness of its mental health consequences [38]. ADHD not only predisposes individuals to adverse experiences but may also be exacerbated by such experiences. Therefore, clinicians should screen for ACEs among patients with ADHD and, conversely, assess ADHD symptoms among individuals with a history of ACEs. Incorporating ACE information into ADHD care could enable the early identification of high-risk individuals and facilitate more targeted and personalized interventions [12]. Moreover, given the modest mediation proportions and observed sex-specific moderation, clinicians should tailor assessments and treatment priorities according to individual ACE patterns, ADHD severity, and demographic background. Future research should examine long-term ADHD-distress trajectories and investigate protective factors such as social support, self-compassion, and emotional literacy. A comprehensive, developmentally informed approach that integrates trauma sensitivity with ADHD-specific care has significant potential to improve the mental health and resilience of individuals with ADHD.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, ADHD symptoms were assessed via self-report using the ASRS-J-6 rather than through clinical evaluations based on DSM-5 diagnostic criteria, which may have led to misclassification. As the diagnosis of ADHD requires structured clinical interviews conducted by trained professionals, future studies incorporating such diagnostic assessments are warranted to further clarify the association between ADHD, ACEs, and psychological distress. Second, all data on ADHD symptoms, ACEs, and psychological distress were obtained from self-report questionnaires, which are subject to recall bias, particularly for retrospective reports of childhood adversity. Such bias may have resulted in under- or overestimation of the observed associations. Third, potential confounders such as substance use, social support, loneliness, chronic physical illness, and psychiatric comorbidities were not assessed. In addition, unmeasured familial and genetic factors may have influenced the observed associations. For example, parental ADHD traits could increase both the risk of child maltreatment and the likelihood of ADHD symptoms in offspring, creating intergenerational confounding effects [39]. Given the heritable nature of ADHD, future longitudinal research incorporating parental ADHD assessment and family background is warranted. Furthermore, psychosocial variables not captured in this study—such as social isolation, substance use, and chronic disease burden—may also affect psychological distress through mechanisms like reduced help-seeking and poorer emotional regulation. Fourth, dichotomizing continuous measures (ASRS-J-6 ≥ 15, K6 ≥ 13) may obscure gradations in symptom severity, potentially underestimating the dimensional nature of ADHD and psychological distress. Fifth, ACEs were recorded as binary variables without information on frequency, duration, or severity. Previous research has shown that children exposed to chronic maltreatment exhibit more pronounced emotional problems such as anxiety and depression, as well as greater behavioral difficulties and social withdrawal, compared to those exposed to transient or isolated incidents of maltreatment [40]. This limited measurement approach may underestimate dose–response relationships and the cumulative impact of chronic adversity. Sixth, cultural factors unique to Japan, such as parenting norms that emphasize social harmony, reluctance to disclose trauma, and stigma surrounding mental illness, may have led to underreporting of ACEs [41–43]. These sociocultural influences should be considered when generalizing the findings to populations with different cultural or familial contexts. Finally, because this study employed a cross-sectional design, causal inference remains limited, and the temporal sequence among ADHD symptoms, ACE exposure, and psychological distress cannot be definitively established. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these temporal pathways and to identify sensitive periods for intervention. From a methodological standpoint, longitudinal and structural equation modeling approaches that account for binary mediators and sampling weights could further elucidate the causal interplay among distal factors (e.g., socioeconomic adversity), ACEs, ADHD symptoms, and psychological outcomes. This underscores both a methodological limitation of the current cross-sectional design and a promising direction for future research.

Conclusion

Our study highlights that emotional types of ACEs significantly mediate the relationship between ADHD symptoms and severe psychological distress in adulthood. However, given the modest mediation effect sizes, ADHD symptoms themselves likely play a substantial direct role. These findings underscore the need for trauma-sensitive, ADHD-specific, and family-centered interventions to reduce the long-term psychological burden on affected individuals.
