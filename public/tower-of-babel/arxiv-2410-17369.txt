Convergence of a Critical Multitype Bellman–Harris Process with Finite-Mean Lifetimes
E.T. Kolkovska, J.A. López-Mimbela, J.H. Ramírez-González

Abstract
. We study a critical multitype Bellman–Harris branching particle system in ℝ N \mathbb{R}^{N} with a finite type space 𝐊 = { 1 , … , K } \mathbf{K}=\{1,\dots,K\} . Particles of type i i move according to a symmetric α i \alpha_{i} -stable process, have non-arithmetic lifetimes with finite mean, and reproduce according to a critical offspring law whose mean matrix is irreducible and stochastic. The branching mechanism is assumed to be in the domain of attraction of a ( 1 + β ) (1+\beta) -stable law, with β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \beta\in(0,1] . We prove that the particle system converges, as t → ∞ t\to\infty , to a limiting random measure which is nonzero. We also show that the limiting population preserves the initial intensity measure. Thus, the system persists with full intensity. These results complement the local extinction results of Kevei and Lopez-Mimbela (2011) for critical multitype Bellman–Harris systems.

1.
Introduction and previous work

A central question for population models based on branching processes is the classification of
their long-time behaviour as random measures. In critical branching systems, where the mean
number of offspring per individual is one, different asymptotic regimes may occur and should be
kept distinct.

Let
E
be a locally compact state space. A particle system on
E
will be denoted by

(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and viewed as an
\mathcal{M}(E)
-valued stochastic process, where

\mathcal{M}(E)
is the space of nonnegative Radon measures on
E
, endowed with the vague
topology. For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
and a measurable function
\varphi:E\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\langle\mu,\varphi\rangle:=\int_{E}\varphi\,d\mu.

We also set

C_{c}^{+}(E):=\left\{\varphi:E\to[0,\infty):\ \varphi\text{ is continuous and has compact support}\right\}.

We say that the particle system
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
survives in the limiting sense if

X_{t}\Rightarrow X_{\infty},\qquad t\to\infty,

in
\mathcal{M}(E)
, for some random measure
X_{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}(E)
such that

\mathbb{P}\{X_{\infty}\neq 0\}>0.

If, in addition, the limiting random measure preserves the initial mean measure, in the sense that

\mathbb{E}\langle X_{\infty},\varphi\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle X_{0},\varphi\rangle,\qquad\varphi\in C_{c}^{+}(E),

then we say that the system persists with full intensity. Finally, we say that the system undergoes
local extinction if

\langle X_{t},\varphi\rangle\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0,\qquad\varphi\in C_{c}^{+}(E).

In
Gorostiza and Wakolbinger (1991)
, a monotype system in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is studied in which particles perform
symmetric
\alpha
-stable motions,
0<\alpha\leq 2
, and branch at the end of exponential lifetimes.
The offspring law has generating function

H(s)=s+\frac{1}{2}(1-s)^{1+\beta},\qquad 0<\beta\leq 1.

Starting from a homogeneous Poisson population, they prove local extinction when

N\leq\alpha/\beta
, and convergence to a nonzero limiting equilibrium of full intensity when

N>\alpha/\beta
. Later,
Vatutin and Wakolbinger (1999)
considered general non-arithmetic lifetimes with finite mean
and obtained the same critical dimension. Moreover, for heavy-tailed lifetimes with tail exponent

0<\gamma\leq 1
, they proved local extinction if
N<\alpha\gamma/\beta
, convergence to a
Poisson equilibrium of full intensity if
N>\alpha\gamma/\beta
, and a nonzero subsequential
limit in the critical case
N=\alpha\gamma/\beta
; see also
Fleischmann et al. (2003)
for further results at the
critical dimension.

We now turn to multitype systems. Consider a branching population in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with types

i\in\mathbf{K}:=\{1,\dots,K\}
. A type-
i
particle moves according to a symmetric

\alpha_{i}
-stable motion, lives for a non-arithmetic time with distribution
F_{i}
, and then branches
according to the multitype generating function
f_{i}(\mathbf{s})
,
\mathbf{s}\in[0,1]^{K}
. Let

M=(m_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{K}
be the mean matrix, where

m_{i,j}=\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial s_{j}}(\mathbf{1}),\qquad\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1).

In the Markovian case, where type-
i
lifetimes are exponential with mean
1/\lambda_{i}
, set

r_{i,j}:=\lambda_{i}m_{i,j}
for
i\neq j
, and consider a Poisson initial field with intensity

\bar{\Lambda}:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}(\lambda\otimes\delta_{i}),

where
\lambda
is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. A standard requirement for

\bar{\Lambda}
to be a stationary intensity is that

\theta=(\theta_{i})_{i=1}^{K}
be a stationary distribution for the type chain with jump rates

(r_{i,j})_{i\neq j}
, equivalently,

(1.1)

\sum_{i\neq j}\theta_{i}r_{i,j}=\theta_{j}\sum_{i\neq j}r_{j,i},\qquad j=1,\dots,K.

For
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbf{K})
and

(x,i)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbf{K}
, write

W_{t}(x,i):=E\!\left(e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\,\middle|\,X_{0}=\delta_{(x,i)}\right).

If
X_{0}
is Poisson with intensity
\bar{\Lambda}
, then
Gorostiza and Wakolbinger (1991)
;
Gorostiza et al. (1992)
identify the Laplace
functional as

(1.2)

E\!\left(e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)=\exp\{-\langle\bar{\Lambda},1-W_{t}(\cdot)\rangle\}.

Using this representation, together with asymptotic estimates and tightness, they obtain
convergence in distribution of
X_{t}
to an equilibrium
X_{\infty}
. In
López-Mimbela and Wakolbinger (1996)
, assuming that for each type
i
the total offspring number belongs to the normal domain of attraction of a
(1+\beta_{i})
-stable law (
0<\beta_{i}\leq 1
), they recover the same extinction/convergence dichotomy with critical threshold expressed through the parameters
\alpha:=\min_{i}\alpha_{i}
and
\beta:=\min_{i}\beta_{i}
.

When lifetimes are not exponential, the process is no longer Markovian on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbf{K}
and one is led to multitype Bellman–Harris systems. In
Kevei and Lopez-Mimbela (2011)
, the mean matrix
M
is assumed to be an ergodic stochastic matrix (hence critical, with Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue
1
), lifetimes are non-arithmetic with finite mean, and the offspring mechanism satisfies regular-variation assumptions. More precisely, if

(1.3)

x-\langle v,1-\mathbf{f}(1-ux)\rangle\sim x^{1+\beta}L(x)\ \ (x\to 0)

and

(1.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n(1-F_{i}(n))}{\langle v,1-\mathbf{f}^{(n)}(0)\rangle}=0\ \ (i=1,\dots,K),

where
u
and
v
are respectively the normalized right and left eigenvectors of

M
associated with the eigenvalue
1
,
L
is slowly varying at
0
, and

\beta\in(0,1]
, then local extinction holds whenever
N<\alpha/\beta
with
\alpha:=\min_{1\leq i\leq K}\alpha_{i}
. Thus, under fairly general hypotheses, the extinction side is understood for multitype non-Markovian systems, while convergence to nonzero limiting equilibria is much less developed.

The purpose of the present paper is to address this convergence problem for a
critical multitype Bellman–Harris branching particle system with finite mean
lifetimes. We consider a finite type space
\mathbf{K}=\{1,\dots,K\}
, type-dependent
symmetric stable motions, and a critical offspring mechanism whose mean matrix is
irreducible and stochastic. The branching mechanism is assumed to be in the domain
of attraction of a
(1+\beta)
-stable law, in the sense of (
1.3
).
For a Poisson initial population with the stationary intensity weights determined by
the finite mean lifetimes, we prove that, under the Condition
2.1
and if

q\wedge\frac{N}{\alpha_{*}}>\frac{1}{\beta},\qquad\alpha_{*}:=\alpha_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_{K},

the system converges, as
t\to\infty
, to a nonzero limiting random measure. We
also prove that the limiting population preserves the initial intensity measure, and
therefore the system persists with full intensity. Finally, we provide a sufficient
condition for the occupation-time estimate in terms of polynomial tail bounds for
the lifetime distributions.

2.
The model and main results

2.1.
The model

Let
K\geq 2
and let

\mathbf{K}:=\{1,\dots,K\},\qquad S:=\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbf{K}.

The population at time
t\geq 0
is represented by the locally finite counting measure

X_{t}=\sum_{k}\delta_{(X_{t}^{k},K_{t}^{k})}\in\mathcal{M}(S),

where
X_{t}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the position of the
k
-th particle alive at time
t
, and

K_{t}^{k}\in\mathbf{K}
is its type.

Motion.

A particle of type
i\in\mathbf{K}
moves according to a symmetric

\alpha_{i}
-stable process
(\xi_{t}^{(i)})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, started from its birth
position. The motion indices satisfy

\alpha_{i}\in(0,2],\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

The motions of distinct particles are independent, and are also independent of the lifetimes and
offspring variables.

Lifetimes.

The lifetime of a type-
i
particle is denoted by
\tau_{i}
and has distribution function

F_{i}
on
[0,\infty)
. We assume throughout that

F_{i}(0)=0,\qquad F_{i}\text{ is non-arithmetic},\qquad m_{i}:=E(\tau_{i})=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,dF_{i}(t)<\infty,\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

Lifetimes are independent of each other and of all motions and offspring variables.

Branching mechanism.

At the end of its lifetime, a type-
i
particle is replaced by an offspring vector

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}=(\zeta_{i,1},\dots,\zeta_{i,K})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{K},

where
\zeta_{i,j}
is the number of type-
j
children. Its generating function is

f_{i}(\mathbf{s}):=E\left(\prod_{j=1}^{K}s_{j}^{\zeta_{i,j}}\right),\qquad\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}.

Let

\mathbf{f}:=(f_{1},\dots,f_{K}),

and let
M=(m_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{K}
be the mean offspring matrix, where

m_{i,j}:=\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial s_{j}}(\mathbf{1}),\qquad\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1).

We assume that
M
is irreducible and stochastic, and that

m_{i,i}>0,\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

Thus the system is critical. Let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K})
be the stationary distribution of
M
:

\pi M=\pi,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{i}=1,\qquad\pi_{i}>0,\quad i=1,\dots,K.

Since
M
is stochastic, we set

u:=\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1),\qquad v:=\pi.

Then

Mu=u,\qquad vM=v,\qquad\langle v,u\rangle=1.

We assume that, for some
\beta\in(0,1]
and some positive slowly varying function
L
at zero,

x-\langle v,1-\mathbf{f}(1-ux)\rangle\sim x^{1+\beta}L(x),\qquad x\downarrow 0.

Initial configuration.

We consider a Poisson initial population on
S
. The weights of the initial intensity are chosen
according to the stationary occupation proportions of the type process in continuous time.

Set

\bar{m}:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{i}m_{i},\qquad\theta_{i}:=\frac{\pi_{i}m_{i}}{\bar{m}},\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

Equivalently, these weights satisfy the balance relation obtained from the continuous-time rates

r_{i,j}:=\frac{m_{i,j}}{m_{i}},\qquad i\neq j.

Indeed, for every
j=1,\dots,K
,

\sum_{i\neq j}\theta_{i}r_{i,j}=\theta_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}r_{j,k}.

To verify this, fix
j
. Since
\theta_{i}=\pi_{i}m_{i}/\bar{m}
,

\sum_{i\neq j}\theta_{i}r_{i,j}=\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\pi_{i}m_{i}}{\bar{m}}\frac{m_{i,j}}{m_{i}}=\frac{1}{\bar{m}}\sum_{i\neq j}\pi_{i}m_{i,j}.

Using
\pi M=\pi
, we have

\pi_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{i}m_{i,j}=\pi_{j}m_{j,j}+\sum_{i\neq j}\pi_{i}m_{i,j}.

Hence

\sum_{i\neq j}\pi_{i}m_{i,j}=\pi_{j}(1-m_{j,j})=\pi_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}m_{j,k}.

Therefore,

\sum_{i\neq j}\theta_{i}r_{i,j}=\frac{\pi_{j}}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k\neq j}m_{j,k}.

On the other hand,

\theta_{j}\sum_{k\neq j}r_{j,k}=\frac{\pi_{j}m_{j}}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{m_{j,k}}{m_{j}}=\frac{\pi_{j}}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k\neq j}m_{j,k}.

This proves (
1.1
).

Thus, throughout the finite-mean setting, we take
X_{0}
to be a Poisson random
measure with intensity

\bar{\Lambda}:=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\,\lambda\otimes\delta_{i},

where
\lambda
denotes Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The initial population is
independent of all motions, lifetimes, and offspring variables.

We shall use the following quantitative condition on the associated type process. Let

Z=(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Markov renewal process on
\mathbf{K}
such that, when
Z
is in
state
i
, the holding time has distribution
F_{i}
, and after this holding time the process jumps
from
i
to
j
with probability
m_{i,j}
. Let

L_{i}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z_{s}=i\}}\,ds

be the time spent by
Z
in state
i
up to time
t
.

Condition 2.1
.

For each
i\in\mathbf{K}
, there exist constants
c_{i}\in(0,1)
,
C>0
, and
q>0
such that,
for all sufficiently large
t
and every initial type
j\in\mathbf{K}
,

(2.1)

P_{j}\left(\frac{L_{i}(t)}{t}\leq c_{i}\right)\leq Ct^{-q},

where
P_{j}
denotes the law of
Z
started from
j
.

For
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
, define

W_{t}(x,i):=E_{(x,i)}\left(e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right),\qquad V_{t}(x,i):=1-W_{t}(x,i),\qquad(x,i)\in S.

We also write

\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}V_{t}(x,i)\,dx,\qquad G_{i}(t):=\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1},\qquad g_{\phi}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}G_{i}(t).

2.2.
Main results

Throughout this subsection, the lifetime distributions are assumed to be in the finite-mean
regime specified in Section
2.1
: for each
i=1,...,K
,
F_{i}
is non-arithmetic and satisfies

m_{i}:=E(\tau_{i})<\infty
.

Proposition 2.1

(Finite-mean first-moment bound and Poisson Laplace functional)
.

Consider the Bellman–Harris branching particle system described in
Section
2.1
, with Poisson initial intensity
\bar{\Lambda}
. For

\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
, let
V_{t}
,
G_{i}(t)
, and
g_{\phi}(t)
be defined as in
Section
2.1
. Then the following properties hold.

(a)

For every
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
,

\sup_{t\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}E_{(x,i)}\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big)\,dx<\infty.

Consequently, for each
i=1,\ldots,K
, the function

G_{i}(t)=\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}
is bounded on
[0,\infty)
. Moreover, for every

\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
,

(2.2)

E\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big)\longrightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\|\phi_{i}\|_{1},\qquad t\to\infty.

In particular,
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in the space of Borel–Radon measures on

S
, endowed with the vague topology.

(b)

For every
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
,

(2.3)

\begin{split}E\left(e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)&=\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}E_{(x,i)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)\,dx\right\}\\
&=\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}\right\}=e^{-g_{\phi}(t)}.\end{split}

The following result gives a simple sufficient condition for Condition
2.1
in terms of polynomial
tail bounds for the lifetime distributions.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume the model described in Section
2.1
. Assume moreover that the
lifetime distributions satisfy

(2.4)

F_{i}(0)=0,\qquad F_{i}(t)<1\text{ for all }t\geq 0,\qquad 1-F_{i}(t)\leq At^{-\eta^{(i)}}\text{ for all }t>0,

for some
A>0
and some
\eta^{(i)}>1
,
i=1,\dots,K
. Set

\eta:=\min_{1\leq i\leq K}\eta^{(i)}.

Then Condition
2.1
holds with
q=\eta-1
.

Theorem
2.2
shows that Condition
2.1
holds for a broad class of finite-mean lifetime
distributions.

Theorem 2.3
.

Consider the Bellman–Harris branching particle system described in
Section
2.1
. Assume that Condition
2.1
holds for some

q>0
.

Assume that the branching mechanism satisfies

(2.5)

x-\langle v,1-\mathbf{f}(1-ux)\rangle\sim x^{1+\beta}L(x),\qquad x\downarrow 0,

for some
\beta\in(0,1]
and some positive slowly varying function
L
at
0
. Let

\alpha_{*}:=\alpha_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_{K},\qquad\rho:=q\wedge\frac{N}{\alpha_{*}}.

Assume that

\rho>\frac{1}{\beta}.

For
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
, let
V_{t}
,
G_{i}(t)
, and
g_{\phi}(t)
be defined as in
Section
2.1
. For
i=1,\dots,K
, define

R_{i}(\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}y_{j}-\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-\mathbf{y})\bigr),\qquad\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{K},

and

B_{i}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{i}\bigl(V_{t}(x,1),\dots,V_{t}(x,K)\bigr)\,dx.

Then, for every nonzero
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
, the limit

(2.6)

G(\phi):=\lim_{t\to\infty}g_{\phi}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}G_{k}(0)-\frac{1}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{k}(s)\,ds

exists and satisfies
G(\phi)>0
.

Moreover, if

\phi_{\epsilon}:=-\log\bigl(1-\epsilon(1-e^{-\phi})\bigr),\qquad 0<\epsilon<1,

then

(2.7)

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{G(\phi_{\epsilon})}{\epsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\|1-e^{-\phi_{i}}\|_{1}.

Recall that
S=\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{\mathbf{}}K
, where

\mathbf{K}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
is finite. Endowing
\mathbf{K}
with the
discrete topology, it follows that
S
is a locally compact second
countable Hausdorff space. Hence the convergence criterion for random
measures in the vague topology, Theorem 4.2 of
Kallenberg (1986)
, applies on

S
.

By Proposition
2.1
and Theorem
2.3
, for every
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
,

\mathbb{E}\exp\{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{E}\exp\{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi\rangle\},

where
X_{\infty}
is a random Radon measure on
S
whose Laplace
functional is given by

\mathbb{E}\big(e^{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi\rangle}\big)=e^{-G(\phi)}.

Thus the Laplace functionals converge on
C_{c}^{+}(S)
, the class of
nonnegative continuous functions with compact support. Therefore, by
Theorem 4.2 of
Kallenberg (1986)
,

X_{t}\Rightarrow X_{\infty},\qquad t\to\infty,

in the space of Radon measures on
S
endowed with the vague topology.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
X_{\infty}
be the limiting random measure in
Theorem
2.3
, characterized by

E\bigl(e^{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi\rangle}\bigr)=e^{-G(\phi)},\qquad\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S).

The limiting population preserves the initial intensity measure. Indeed, let
\psi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
. Choose
a>0
such that

0\leq a\psi<1,

and define

\phi_{a}:=-\log(1-a\psi).

Then

1-e^{-\phi_{a}}=a\psi.

For
0<\varepsilon<1
, set

\phi_{a,\varepsilon}:=-\log\bigl(1-\varepsilon(1-e^{-\phi_{a}})\bigr)=-\log(1-\varepsilon a\psi).

By (
2.7
),

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{G(\phi_{a,\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\|a\psi_{i}\|_{1}=a\int_{S}\psi\,d\bar{\Lambda}.

Since
G(\phi_{a,\varepsilon})\to 0
, it follows that

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1-e^{-G(\phi_{a,\varepsilon})}}{\varepsilon}=a\int_{S}\psi\,d\bar{\Lambda}.

On the other hand, by the Laplace functional of
X_{\infty}
,

1-e^{-G(\phi_{a,\varepsilon})}=E\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi_{a,\varepsilon}\rangle}\right).

Therefore,

(2.8)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}E\left[\frac{1-e^{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi_{a,\varepsilon}\rangle}}{\varepsilon}\right]=a\int_{S}\psi\,d\bar{\Lambda}.

For each fixed Radon measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(S)
,

\frac{\phi_{a,\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}=\frac{-\log(1-\varepsilon a\psi)}{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow a\psi

pointwise, and since
\psi
has compact support,

\frac{1-e^{-\langle\mu,\phi_{a,\varepsilon}\rangle}}{\varepsilon}\longrightarrow a\langle\mu,\psi\rangle.

Applying this with
\mu=X_{\infty}
, Fatou’s lemma and
(
2.8
) give

aE\langle X_{\infty},\psi\rangle\leq a\int_{S}\psi\,d\bar{\Lambda}<\infty.

Hence
E\langle X_{\infty},\psi\rangle<\infty
.

Moreover, since
a\|\psi\|_{\infty}<1
, there exists
C_{a}>0
such that, for all

0<\varepsilon<1
,

\frac{\phi_{a,\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\leq C_{a}\,a\psi.

Using
1-e^{-x}\leq x
, we obtain

0\leq\frac{1-e^{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi_{a,\varepsilon}\rangle}}{\varepsilon}\leq\left\langle X_{\infty},\frac{\phi_{a,\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\right\rangle\leq C_{a}a\langle X_{\infty},\psi\rangle.

The right-hand side is integrable. Thus, by dominated convergence,

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}E\left[\frac{1-e^{-\langle X_{\infty},\phi_{a,\varepsilon}\rangle}}{\varepsilon}\right]=aE\langle X_{\infty},\psi\rangle.

Combining this identity with (
2.8
), we obtain

aE\langle X_{\infty},\psi\rangle=a\int_{S}\psi\,d\bar{\Lambda}.

Since
a>0
,

E\langle X_{\infty},\psi\rangle=\int_{S}\psi\,d\bar{\Lambda}.

Thus the limiting population preserves the initial intensity measure:

E(X_{\infty})=\bar{\Lambda}.

Example 2.5
.

Consider the case
K=2
. Let the type-
1
lifetime distribution be

F_{1}:=F_{p,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
, where
F_{p,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}
has density

f_{p,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}(x):=\Big(p\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x}+(1-p)\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x}\Big)\mathbf{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x),\qquad p\in[0,1],\quad\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0.

Then

(2.9)

1-F_{1}(x)=pe^{-\lambda_{1}x}+(1-p)e^{-\lambda_{2}x}\leq e^{-(\lambda_{1}\wedge\lambda_{2})x},\qquad x>0.

Let the type-
2
lifetime distribution be Weibull with parameters
r>0
and
\lambda>0
, with distribution function

F_{2}(x):=\left(1-e^{-(x/\lambda)^{r}}\right)\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x).

Then

(2.10)

1-F_{2}(x)=e^{-(x/\lambda)^{r}},\qquad x>0.

It follows from (
2.9
)–(
2.10
) that, for every
\eta>1
, there exists

A_{\eta}>0
such that

1-F_{i}(x)\leq A_{\eta}x^{-\eta},\qquad x>0,\quad i=1,2.

Indeed, the exponential tail in (
2.9
) and the Weibull tail in (
2.10
)
decay faster than any negative power. Thus (
2.4
) holds for every
\eta>1
, and
Theorem
2.2
implies that Condition
2.1
holds with arbitrarily large
q
.
Hence, if the branching mechanism satisfies (
2.5
) with exponent

\beta\in(0,1]
, then Theorem
2.3
gives persistence with full intensity whenever

N>\frac{\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}}{\beta}.

For the extinction side, suppose in addition that the offspring mechanism belongs to the particular
class considered in Remark 1 of
Kevei and Lopez-Mimbela (2011)
, namely

f_{i}(s,s)=s+c_{i}(1-s)^{1+\beta},\qquad 0\leq s\leq 1,\quad i=1,2,

where
c_{i}\in(0,(1+\beta)^{-1}]
.
Moreover, (
2.9
)–(
2.10
) imply that, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1+\frac{1}{\beta}+\varepsilon}\bigl[1-F_{i}(n)\bigr]=0,\qquad i=1,2.

Therefore, by Remark 1 of
Kevei and Lopez-Mimbela (2011)
, condition (
1.4
) is satisfied, and
Theorem 1 of
Kevei and Lopez-Mimbela (2011)
gives local extinction whenever

N<\frac{\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}}{\beta}.

Consequently, for these two lifetime distributions and for the above particular offspring mechanism,
the extinction–persistence dichotomy is determined outside the critical dimension

N=\frac{\alpha_{1}\wedge\alpha_{2}}{\beta}
, which is not treated here.

3.
Proofs of main results

Proof of Proposition
2.1
:

We first prove (a). Fix
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
. Suppose that the
system starts at time
0
with one particle of type
i
at position

x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. A first-step decomposition gives

(3.1)

\begin{split}E_{(x,i)}\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big)&=(1-F_{i}(t))P_{t,\alpha_{i}}(\phi_{i})(x)\\
&\quad+\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}E_{(\cdot,j)}\big(\langle X_{t-s},\phi\rangle\big)\right)(x)\,F_{i}(ds),\end{split}

where
\phi_{i}(x):=\phi(x,i)
. This identity only uses the mean offspring
matrix
M
.

Set

H_{i}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}E_{(x,i)}\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big)\,dx,\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

Integrating (
3.1
) over
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and using
the invariance of Lebesgue measure under the
\alpha_{i}
-stable semigroup,
gives

(3.2)

H_{i}(t)=(1-F_{i}(t))\|\phi_{i}\|_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{[0,t]}H_{j}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds),\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

Equivalently,

H_{i}(t)=z_{i}^{\phi}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{[0,t]}H_{j}(t-s)\,F_{i,j}(ds),

where

z_{i}^{\phi}(t):=(1-F_{i}(t))\|\phi_{i}\|_{1},\qquad F_{i,j}(t):=m_{i,j}F_{i}(t).

We first justify local boundedness on compact time intervals. This is done
without assuming continuity of the lifetime distribution functions. Since
each distribution function is right-continuous and
F_{i}(0)=0
, there exists

h>0
such that

\kappa_{h}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq K}F_{i}(h)<1.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{h}
be the space of bounded Borel maps

\mathbf{u}:[0,h]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K},

endowed with the norm

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty,h}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq h}\max_{1\leq i\leq K}|u_{i}(t)|.

This is a complete metric space. Define, for

\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{K})\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,

(\mathcal{T}\mathbf{u})_{i}(t):=z_{i}^{\phi}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{[0,t]}u_{j}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds),\qquad 0\leq t\leq h.

The map
\mathcal{T}\mathbf{u}
is Borel measurable. Indeed, after extending

u_{j}
by zero outside
[0,h]
, the function

(t,s)\mapsto u_{j}(t-s)\mathbf{1}_{\{0\leq s\leq t\}}

is Borel measurable on
[0,h]\times[0,h]
, and
F_{i}
is a finite measure
on compact intervals. Since
z_{i}^{\phi}
is bounded on
[0,h]
, the operator

\mathcal{T}
maps
\mathcal{B}_{h}
into itself. Moreover, for

\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}\mathbf{u}-\mathcal{T}\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty,h}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq K}\sup_{0\leq t\leq h}\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{[0,t]}|u_{j}(t-s)-v_{j}(t-s)|\,F_{i}(ds)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq K}F_{i}(h)\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty,h}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{h}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty,h}.

Thus
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{B}_{h}
.

To connect this contraction argument with the first moment
H_{i}
, let

\mathbf{H}^{[0]}\equiv 0,

and define recursively

\mathbf{H}^{[n+1]}:=\mathcal{T}\mathbf{H}^{[n]},\qquad n\geq 0.

Equivalently,

H_{i}^{[n+1]}(t)=z_{i}^{\phi}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{[0,t]}H_{j}^{[n]}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds),\qquad 0\leq t\leq h.

The sequence
(\mathbf{H}^{[n]})_{n\geq 0}
is the increasing sequence of
integrated first moments obtained from finite-depth truncations of the
branching construction. Hence, by monotone convergence,

H_{i}^{[n]}(t)\uparrow H_{i}(t),\qquad 0\leq t\leq h,\quad i=1,\ldots,K.

On the other hand, since
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{B}_{h}
,
the iterates
\mathbf{H}^{[n]}
converge uniformly on
[0,h]
to the
unique fixed point
\mathbf{H}^{[*]}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
. Therefore

H_{i}(t)=H_{i}^{[*]}(t),\qquad 0\leq t\leq h,\quad i=1,\ldots,K,

and consequently

\sup_{0\leq t\leq h}\sum_{i=1}^{K}H_{i}(t)<\infty.

Repeating the argument on consecutive intervals gives local boundedness on
every compact interval. Indeed, suppose that
H_{i}
is bounded on

[0,a]
, for every
i=1,\ldots,K
. For
r\in[0,h]
, put

Y_{i}(r):=H_{i}(a+r).

From (
3.2
), for
0\leq r\leq h
,

Y_{i}(r)=q_{i}^{(a)}(r)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{[0,r]}Y_{j}(r-s)\,F_{i}(ds),

where

q_{i}^{(a)}(r):=z_{i}^{\phi}(a+r)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{(r,a+r]}H_{j}(a+r-s)\,F_{i}(ds).

The function
q_{i}^{(a)}
is bounded on
[0,h]
, because

z_{i}^{\phi}
is bounded on compact intervals and, for
s\in(r,a+r]
, one
has
a+r-s\in[0,a)
, where the functions
H_{j}
are bounded by the
induction hypothesis. Therefore, on
[0,h]
, the unknown vector

\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{K})
satisfies a renewal equation with bounded
Borel inhomogeneous term
q^{(a)}
and with the same contraction constant

\max_{1\leq i\leq K}F_{i}(h)=\kappa_{h}<1.

Starting from
\mathbf{Y}^{[0]}\equiv 0
and iterating this equation, the
iterates converge uniformly on
[0,h]
to a bounded fixed point, because the
contraction constant is again
\kappa_{h}<1
. These iterates are precisely the first moments obtained from the corresponding
finite-depth truncations. Hence, by monotone convergence,

Y_{i}^{[n]}(r)\uparrow Y_{i}(r),\qquad 0\leq r\leq h,\quad i=1,\ldots,K.

Thus
\mathbf{Y}
coincides with the bounded fixed point. Therefore

\sup_{0\leq r\leq h}\sum_{i=1}^{K}Y_{i}(r)<\infty.

Equivalently,

\sup_{a\leq t\leq a+h}\sum_{i=1}^{K}H_{i}(t)<\infty.

After finitely many steps, the functions
H_{i}
are bounded on any prescribed
compact interval.

We now apply the renewal theorem. The functions
z_{i}^{\phi}
are Borel
measurable, locally bounded, and directly Riemann integrable, since

1-F_{i}
is nonincreasing and

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-F_{i}(t))\,dt=m_{i}<\infty.

Moreover,

F(\infty)=M,\qquad p(F(\infty))=p(M)=1,\qquad F(0)=0,\qquad p(F(0))=0<1.

Since
m_{i,i}>0
and
F_{i}
is non-arithmetic, the diagonal measure

F_{i,i}=m_{i,i}F_{i}
is non-arithmetic. Hence the matrix of measures

(F_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{K}
is non-lattice. Therefore,
Theorem 2.2(iii) of
Athreya and Murthy (1976)
applies to
(
3.2
).

Since
M
is stochastic, its right Perron–Frobenius eigenvector associated
with the eigenvalue
1
is

u=\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1),

and its normalized left Perron–Frobenius eigenvector is

v=\pi,\qquad\langle v,u\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\pi_{i}=1.

Furthermore,

b_{k,r}:=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\,dF_{k,r}(t)=m_{k,r}m_{k}.

Thus the denominator appearing in the renewal theorem is

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{r=1}^{K}v_{k}u_{r}b_{k,r}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{r=1}^{K}\pi_{k}m_{k,r}m_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}m_{k}=\bar{m},

because
\sum_{r=1}^{K}m_{k,r}=1
. Also,

\int_{0}^{\infty}z_{k}^{\phi}(s)\,ds=\|\phi_{k}\|_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-F_{k}(s))\,ds=m_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{1}.

Consequently, Theorem 2.2(iii) of
Athreya and Murthy (1976)
yields, for every

i=1,\dots,K
,

(3.3)

H_{i}(t)\longrightarrow\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}m_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{1}}{\bar{m}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{1},\qquad t\to\infty.

Combining this convergence with the local boundedness proved above, we obtain

(3.4)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{K}H_{i}(t)<\infty.

Since
X_{0}
is Poisson with intensity
\bar{\Lambda}
,

E\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}H_{i}(t).

Using (
3.3
) and
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}=1
, we obtain

E\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big)\longrightarrow\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|\phi_{k}\|_{1},\qquad t\to\infty,

which proves (
2.2
).

Moreover,

(3.5)

0\leq V_{t}(x,i)=E_{(x,i)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)\leq E_{(x,i)}\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big).

Integrating (
3.5
) over
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and using
(
3.4
), gives

(3.6)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}<\infty.

Therefore each
G_{i}(t)=\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}
is bounded on
[0,\infty)
.

It remains to prove tightness. Let
\mathcal{K}\subset S
be compact, and
choose
\psi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
such that
\psi\geq 1_{\mathcal{K}}
. Then, by
Markov’s inequality,

P\big(X_{t}(\mathcal{K})>a\big)\leq P\big(\langle X_{t},\psi\rangle>a\big)\leq\frac{E(\langle X_{t},\psi\rangle)}{a}.

Since
E(\langle X_{t},\psi\rangle)
is bounded uniformly in
t
, it follows
that

\lim_{a\to\infty}\limsup_{t\to\infty}P\big(X_{t}(\mathcal{K})>a\big)=0.

Thus, by Lemma 4.5 of
Kallenberg (1986)
, the family
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is tight in
the vague topology. This proves (a).

We now prove (b). Similarly to
(
Vatutin and Wakolbinger, 1999
, page 622)
, which in turn
relies on Remark 1.4.7 in
Liemant et al. (1988)
, since the initial population

X_{0}
is a Poisson population with intensity
\bar{\Lambda}
and is
independent of the branching mechanism, the Poisson exponential formula gives

\begin{split}E\left(e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)&=\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}E_{(x,i)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)\,dx\right\}\\
&=\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}\right\}.\end{split}

This proves (
2.3
).

\hfill\square

Proof of Theorem
2.2
:

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the embedded Markov chain of the Markov renewal process

(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, with transition matrix
M
, and with
Y_{0}
equal to the
initial type. Let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K})
denote its stationary
distribution. Since
M
is irreducible and stochastic,
\pi_{j}>0
for every

j=1,\dots,K
. For
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, define

t_{j}(n):=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{m}=j\}},\qquad n\geq 1.

By the large deviation theorem for finite Markov chains, see Lemma 2.13 of

Goldstein (1971)
, for every
\epsilon>0
there exist constants
C,c>0
such that

(3.7)

P_{i}\left(\left|\frac{t_{j}(n)}{n}-\pi_{j}\right|>\epsilon\right)\leq Ce^{-cn},\qquad n\geq 1,\quad i,j\in\{1,\dots,K\}.

Conditionally on the embedded chain
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, let

(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be independent holding times such that
S_{n}
has
distribution
F_{Y_{n}}
. Define

\sigma_{0}:=0,\qquad\sigma_{n}:=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}S_{r},\quad n\geq 1,

and let

n_{t}:=\max\{n\geq 0:\sigma_{n}\leq t\}

be the number of renewals up to time
t
. Then the occupation time of state

j
up to time
t
is

L_{j}(t)=\sum_{r=0}^{n_{t}-1}S_{r}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{r}=j\}}+(t-\sigma_{n_{t}})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{n_{t}}=j\}}.

Equivalently, if
T_{1}^{j},T_{2}^{j},\ldots
denotes the sequence of holding times
attached to visits of the embedded chain to state
j
, then

L_{j}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{t_{j}(n_{t})}T_{k}^{j}+r_{j}(t),

where

r_{j}(t):=(t-\sigma_{n_{t}})\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{n_{t}}=j\}}\geq 0.

By construction,
T_{1}^{j},T_{2}^{j},\ldots
are i.i.d. with distribution
F_{j}
.
Moreover, they are independent of the visit-count process

(t_{j}(n))_{n\geq 1}
, because the holding times are attached independently to
the visits of the embedded chain.

Fix
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. Choose
\epsilon>0
such that

\pi_{j}-\epsilon>0.

Let

\eta:=\min_{1\leq r\leq K}\eta^{(r)}.

Let
T^{1},\ldots,T^{K}
be independent random variables such that
T^{r}
has
distribution
F_{r}
, and set

T^{*}:=\max\{T^{1},\dots,T^{K}\},\qquad\bar{\mu}:=E(T^{*})<\infty.

The finiteness of
\bar{\mu}
follows from Lemma
3.2
, since

\eta>1
.

Choose
c_{(j)}\in(0,1)
sufficiently small so that

d_{j}:=\frac{2c_{(j)}}{m_{j}(\pi_{j}-\epsilon)}<\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}.

Equivalently, it is enough to take

0<c_{(j)}<1\wedge\frac{m_{j}(\pi_{j}-\epsilon)}{2\bar{\mu}}.

We decompose

\begin{split}P_{i}\big(L_{j}(t)\leq tc_{(j)}\big)&\leq P_{i}\big(L_{j}(t)\leq tc_{(j)},\,n_{t}>d_{j}t\big)+P_{i}\big(n_{t}\leq d_{j}t\big).\end{split}

We first estimate the first term. By (
3.7
),

\begin{split}P_{i}\left(\frac{t_{j}(n_{t})}{n_{t}}<\pi_{j}-\epsilon,\,n_{t}>d_{j}t\right)&\leq\sum_{n\geq\lfloor d_{j}t\rfloor}P_{i}\left(\frac{t_{j}(n)}{n}<\pi_{j}-\epsilon\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{n\geq\lfloor d_{j}t\rfloor}Ce^{-cn}\leq C_{1}e^{-c_{1}t}\end{split}

for all sufficiently large
t
.

Next, we estimate the probability that the average of the type-
j
lifetimes is too
small. Since
m_{j}=E(T_{1}^{j})>0
, choose
R>0
such that

E(T_{1}^{j}\wedge R)>\frac{3m_{j}}{4}.

Set
Y_{k}:=T_{k}^{j}\wedge R
. Then
0\leq Y_{k}\leq R
, the variables

Y_{k}
are independent, and

E(Y_{k})>\frac{3m_{j}}{4}.

Moreover, since
Y_{k}\leq T_{k}^{j}
,

\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}^{j}<\frac{m_{j}}{2}\right\}\subset\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}<\frac{m_{j}}{2}\right\}.

Hence, by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\begin{split}P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}^{j}<\frac{m_{j}}{2}\right)&\leq P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}<\frac{m_{j}}{2}\right)\\
&\leq P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-E(Y_{1})<-\frac{m_{j}}{4}\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{2n(m_{j}/4)^{2}}{R^{2}}\right\}.\end{split}

Therefore, there exist constants
C_{2},c_{2}>0
such that

P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}^{j}<\frac{m_{j}}{2}\right)\leq C_{2}e^{-c_{2}n},\qquad n\geq 1.

Therefore,

\begin{split}&P_{i}\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t_{j}(n_{t})}T_{k}^{j}}{t_{j}(n_{t})}<\frac{m_{j}}{2},\,\frac{t_{j}(n_{t})}{n_{t}}\geq\pi_{j}-\epsilon,\,n_{t}>d_{j}t\right)\\
&\qquad\leq\sum_{n\geq\lfloor(\pi_{j}-\epsilon)d_{j}t\rfloor}P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{k}^{j}<\frac{m_{j}}{2}\right)\leq C_{3}e^{-c_{3}t}\end{split}

for all sufficiently large
t
.

On the complement of the two exceptional events above, if
n_{t}>d_{j}t
, then

\frac{t_{j}(n_{t})}{n_{t}}\geq\pi_{j}-\epsilon>0

and

\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t_{j}(n_{t})}T_{k}^{j}}{t_{j}(n_{t})}\geq\frac{m_{j}}{2}.

Since
r_{j}(t)\geq 0
, we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{L_{j}(t)}{t}&=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t_{j}(n_{t})}T_{k}^{j}+r_{j}(t)}{t_{j}(n_{t})}\frac{t_{j}(n_{t})}{n_{t}}\frac{n_{t}}{t}\\
&\geq\frac{m_{j}}{2}(\pi_{j}-\epsilon)\frac{n_{t}}{t}>\frac{m_{j}}{2}(\pi_{j}-\epsilon)d_{j}=c_{(j)}.\end{split}

Thus, on this event,
L_{j}(t)>tc_{(j)}
. Consequently,

P_{i}\big(L_{j}(t)\leq tc_{(j)},\,n_{t}>d_{j}t\big)\leq C_{4}e^{-c_{4}t}

for suitable constants
C_{4},c_{4}>0
.

It remains to estimate
P_{i}(n_{t}\leq d_{j}t)
. Since
d_{j}<1/\bar{\mu}
,
Lemma
3.4
gives

P_{i}(n_{t}\leq d_{j}t)\leq C_{5}t^{1-\eta}

for all sufficiently large
t
. Combining the last estimates, we obtain

P_{i}\big(L_{j}(t)\leq tc_{(j)}\big)\leq C_{4}e^{-c_{4}t}+C_{5}t^{1-\eta}\leq C_{6}t^{1-\eta}

for all sufficiently large
t
.

Since
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and the initial type
i\in\{1,\dots,K\}
were arbitrary,
and since
K
is finite, the constants can be chosen uniformly after increasing

C_{6}
. Therefore, for each
j=1,\dots,K
, there exists
c_{(j)}\in(0,1)
such that

P_{i}\left(\frac{L_{j}(t)}{t}\leq c_{(j)}\right)\leq Ct^{-(\eta-1)},\qquad t\text{ sufficiently large},\quad i=1,\dots,K.

Hence Condition
2.1
holds with

q=\eta-1.

\hfill\square

Proof of Theorem
2.3
:

By Proposition
2.1
, for every
\psi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
,

(3.8)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}E_{(x,i)}\big(\langle X_{t},\psi\rangle\big)\,dx<\infty.

We shall use this estimate with
\psi=\phi
and later with

\psi=C_{\phi}\bar{f}
.

Define, for
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}
,

R_{i}(\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}y_{j}-\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-\mathbf{y})\bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,K,

where
1-\mathbf{y}=(1-y_{1},\dots,1-y_{K})
. For an offspring vector

\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{K})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{K}
, set

A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y}):=\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}y_{j}-1+\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}},\qquad\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{K},

with the convention
a^{0}=1
for
a\in[0,1]
.

We first show that
A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0
. Let

m:=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{K}.

If
m=0
, then
A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})=0
. Assume now that
m\geq 1
. Let

a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in[0,1]
be the list obtained by repeating
y_{j}
exactly

n_{j}
times, for
j=1,\dots,K
. Then

\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}=\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-a_{r}),\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}y_{j}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}.

The elementary inequality

1-\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-a_{r})\leq\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r},\qquad 0\leq a_{r}\leq 1,

follows by induction on
m
. Indeed, it is an equality for
m=1
. If it
holds for
m
, then, writing
P_{m}:=\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1-a_{r})
, we have

1-P_{m}(1-a_{m+1})=1-P_{m}+P_{m}a_{m+1}\leq\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}+a_{m+1},

because
0\leq P_{m}\leq 1
. Hence

1-\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}y_{j}.

Therefore

A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}y_{j}-\left[1-\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}\right]\geq 0.

Moreover, since

0\leq 1-\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}},

we also have the upper bound

0\leq A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}y_{j}.

We next prove that
A_{\mathbf{n}}
is nondecreasing in each coordinate. Fix

\ell\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, and keep all coordinates except
y_{\ell}
fixed. If

n_{\ell}=0
, then
A_{\mathbf{n}}
does not depend on
y_{\ell}
, and hence it is
constant in that coordinate. If
n_{\ell}\geq 1
, then, for
0<y_{\ell}<1
,

\frac{\partial A_{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial y_{\ell}}(\mathbf{y})=n_{\ell}-n_{\ell}(1-y_{\ell})^{n_{\ell}-1}\prod_{j\neq\ell}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}.

Equivalently,

\frac{\partial A_{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial y_{\ell}}(\mathbf{y})=n_{\ell}\left[1-(1-y_{\ell})^{n_{\ell}-1}\prod_{j\neq\ell}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}\right].

Since
0\leq 1-y_{j}\leq 1
for every
j
, we have

0\leq(1-y_{\ell})^{n_{\ell}-1}\prod_{j\neq\ell}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}\leq 1.

Thus

\frac{\partial A_{\mathbf{n}}}{\partial y_{\ell}}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0,\qquad 0<y_{\ell}<1.

By the mean value theorem,
A_{\mathbf{n}}
is nondecreasing in
y_{\ell}
on

(0,1)
. Since
A_{\mathbf{n}}
is continuous on
[0,1]^{K}
, the same
monotonicity extends to the boundary. Therefore
A_{\mathbf{n}}
is
nondecreasing in each coordinate on
[0,1]^{K}
.

We now average with respect to the offspring distribution of a type-
i

particle. Let

\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}=(\zeta_{i,1},\dots,\zeta_{i,K})

be its offspring vector. By the definition of the offspring generating function,

f_{i}(\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{K}s_{j}^{\zeta_{i,j}}\right],\qquad\mathbf{s}\in[0,1]^{K}.

Taking
\mathbf{s}=1-\mathbf{y}
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{\zeta_{i,j}}\right]=f_{i}(1-\mathbf{y}).

Since

\mathbb{E}_{i}(\zeta_{i,j})=m_{i,j},

it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}\bigl(A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{K}\zeta_{i,j}y_{j}-1+\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{\zeta_{i,j}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}y_{j}-1+f_{i}(1-\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}y_{j}-\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-\mathbf{y})\bigr)

\displaystyle=R_{i}(\mathbf{y}).

Furthermore, by the bound already proved,

0\leq A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\zeta_{i,j}y_{j}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\zeta_{i,j}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{i}(\zeta_{i,j})=m_{i,j}<\infty
for each
j
, the expectation

\mathbb{E}_{i}(A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{y}))
is finite. Consequently,

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0.

Finally, if
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in[0,1]^{K}
satisfy
y_{j}\leq z_{j}
for every

j
, then the coordinatewise monotonicity of
A_{\mathbf{n}}
gives

A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})\leq A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{z})

for every realization of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}
. Taking expectations yields

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq R_{i}(\mathbf{z}).

Thus
R_{i}
is nonnegative and nondecreasing in each coordinate on

[0,1]^{K}
. Furthermore, for
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in[0,1]^{K}
, we claim that

|A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})-A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{z})|\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|.

Indeed,

\displaystyle|A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})-A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{z})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|

\displaystyle+\left|\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-z_{j})^{n_{j}}\right|.

Writing the products as products of
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{K}
factors in
[0,1]
, the
elementary telescopic estimate

\left|\prod_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}-\prod_{r=1}^{m}b_{r}\right|\leq\sum_{r=1}^{m}|a_{r}-b_{r}|,\qquad 0\leq a_{r},b_{r}\leq 1,

gives

\left|\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-y_{j})^{n_{j}}-\prod_{j=1}^{K}(1-z_{j})^{n_{j}}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|.

Hence

|A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{y})-A_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{z})|\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{K}n_{j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|.

Taking expectations with respect to the offspring distribution of a type-
i

particle yields

\displaystyle|R_{i}(\mathbf{y})-R_{i}(\mathbf{z})|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})-A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{z})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\left|A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{y})-A_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{i}}(\mathbf{z})\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}_{i}(\zeta_{i,j})|y_{j}-z_{j}|

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|.

Thus
R_{i}
is Lipschitz on
[0,1]^{K}
.

By the first-step decomposition for the Laplace functional,

(3.9)

\begin{split}V_{t}(x,i)&=(1-F_{i}(t))P_{t,\alpha_{i}}V_{0}(\cdot,i)(x)\\
&\quad+\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\left(1-f_{i}\bigl(1-V_{t-s}(\cdot,1),\dots,1-V_{t-s}(\cdot,K)\bigr)\right)(x)\,F_{i}(ds).\end{split}

Integrating over
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, using invariance of Lebesgue measure under

P_{t,\alpha_{i}}
, and using the definition of
R_{i}
, we obtain

(3.10)

G_{i}(t)=(1-F_{i}(t))G_{i}(0)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\int_{[0,t]}G_{j}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds)-\int_{[0,t]}B_{i}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds),

where

B_{i}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{i}\bigl(V_{t}(x,1),\dots,V_{t}(x,K)\bigr)\,dx.

We prove that
B_{i}
is directly Riemann integrable. Let

D_{\phi}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a ball such that

\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K}.

By Lemma
3.1
, applied to

\mathcal{A}=D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K},

there exists
C_{\phi}>0
such that,

\begin{split}V_{t}(x,i)&=E_{(x,i)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)\\
&\leq P_{(x,i)}\left(X_{t}(\mathcal{A})>0\right)\leq C_{\phi}t^{-\rho},\qquad t\geq 1.\end{split}

After increasing
C_{\phi}
, this gives

(3.11)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ 1\leq i\leq K}V_{t}(x,i)\leq C_{\phi}\min\{t^{-\rho},1\},\qquad t\geq 0.

Also,

0\leq V_{t}(x,i)\leq E_{(x,i)}\big(\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle\big).

Applying (
3.8
) with
\psi=\phi
, we get

(3.12)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\|V_{t}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}<\infty.

We now use the branching assumption. Since
u=\mathbf{1}
and
M
is stochastic,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}u_{j}=1,\qquad i=1,\ldots,K.

Hence, for
x\in[0,1]
,

R_{i}(ux)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}x-\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-ux)\bigr)=x-\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-ux)\bigr).

Therefore

\displaystyle\langle v,R(ux)\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}v_{i}R_{i}(ux)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}v_{i}\left[x-\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-ux)\bigr)\right]

\displaystyle=x\sum_{i=1}^{K}v_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{K}v_{i}\bigl(1-f_{i}(1-ux)\bigr).

Since
\langle v,u\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}v_{i}=1
, this gives

\langle v,R(ux)\rangle=x-\langle v,1-\mathbf{f}(1-ux)\rangle.

By the assumption on the branching mechanism,

x-\langle v,1-\mathbf{f}(1-ux)\rangle\sim x^{1+\beta}L(x),\qquad x\downarrow 0.

Consequently,

\langle v,R(ux)\rangle\sim x^{1+\beta}L(x),\qquad x\downarrow 0.

Since
v_{i}>0
for every
i
and
R_{i}\geq 0
, it follows that, for each fixed

i
,

0\leq v_{i}R_{i}(ux)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}R_{k}(ux)=\langle v,R(ux)\rangle.

Therefore, for
x>0
sufficiently small,

R_{i}(ux)\leq\frac{1}{v_{i}}\langle v,R(ux)\rangle\leq C_{i}x^{1+\beta}L(x).

Since the type space is finite, increasing the constant if necessary, we may write

R_{i}(ux)\leq Cx^{1+\beta}L(x),\qquad i=1,\dots,K,

for all sufficiently small
x>0
.

Now let
\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{K}
, and put

x_{\mathbf{y}}:=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}.

If
x_{\mathbf{y}}=0
, then
\mathbf{y}=0
and
R_{i}(\mathbf{y})=R_{i}(0)=0
, so the
desired estimate is immediate. Assume
x_{\mathbf{y}}>0
. Since
u=\mathbf{1}
,
we have the coordinatewise inequality

\mathbf{y}\leq ux_{\mathbf{y}}.

Using the coordinatewise monotonicity of
R_{i}
, we obtain

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq R_{i}(ux_{\mathbf{y}}).

Thus, whenever
x_{\mathbf{y}}=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}
is sufficiently small,

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq Cx_{\mathbf{y}}^{1+\beta}L(x_{\mathbf{y}})=C\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}^{1+\beta}L(\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}).

Finally, since
L
is slowly varying at zero, Potter’s bound implies that, for
every
\delta>0
, there exist constants
C_{\delta}>0
and
r_{\delta}>0
such
that

L(r)\leq C_{\delta}r^{-\delta},\qquad 0<r<r_{\delta}.

Taking
r=\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}
, we obtain, for

0<\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}<r_{\delta}
,

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq C_{\delta}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}^{1+\beta-\delta}.

Hence, for every
\delta\in(0,\beta)
, after possibly changing the constant,

(3.13)

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq C_{\delta}\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}^{1+\beta-\delta},\qquad\|\mathbf{y}\|_{\infty}\text{ sufficiently small}.

Since
\rho>1/\beta
, we can choose
\delta\in(0,\beta)
sufficiently small so
that

\rho(\beta-\delta)>1.

Indeed, the inequality
\rho>1/\beta
is equivalent to
\rho\beta>1
; hence,
by taking
\delta>0
small enough, we still have

\rho\beta-\rho\delta>1.

For all sufficiently large
t
, (
3.11
) implies that

\max_{1\leq j\leq K}V_{t}(x,j)

is uniformly small in
x
. Hence, by (
3.13
),

\begin{split}B_{i}(t)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{i}\bigl(V_{t}(x,1),\dots,V_{t}(x,K)\bigr)\,dx\\
&\leq C_{\delta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq K}V_{t}(x,j)\right)^{1+\beta-\delta}\,dx\\
&\leq C_{\delta}\left(\sup_{x,j}V_{t}(x,j)\right)^{\beta-\delta}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|V_{t}(\cdot,j)\|_{1}\\
&\leq C_{\delta}\min\{t^{-\rho(\beta-\delta)},1\}.\end{split}

On bounded time intervals, the same domination follows from

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}y_{j}

and (
3.12
). Thus, after increasing
C_{\delta}
,

(3.14)

B_{i}(t)\leq C_{\delta}\min\{t^{-\rho(\beta-\delta)},1\},\qquad t\geq 0.

The right-hand side is nonincreasing and integrable on
[0,\infty)
.

It remains to justify local Riemann integrability of
B_{i}
.
Fix
T>0
, and set

\mathcal{E}:=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{K},\qquad\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathcal{E}}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq K}\|u_{j}\|_{1}.

Since
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is a Banach space,
\mathcal{E}
, endowed with

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{E}}
, is also a Banach space.

We first prove that, for each
i=1,\ldots,K
,

t\mapsto V_{t}(\cdot,i)

is càdlàg from
[0,T]
into
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Write the integral
equation (
3.9
) as

V_{t}(\cdot,i)=(1-F_{i}(t))P_{t,\alpha_{i}}V_{0}(\cdot,i)+\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}\bigl(V_{t-s}(\cdot,1),\ldots,V_{t-s}(\cdot,K)\bigr)\,F_{i}(ds),

where

\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{y}):=1-f_{i}(1-y_{1},\ldots,1-y_{K}),\qquad\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}.

For
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in[0,1]^{K}
,

|\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{y})-\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{z})|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|.

Consequently, for functions
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}
taking values in

[0,1]^{K}
a.e.,

\|\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{u})-\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{v})\|_{1}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathcal{E}},

because
M
is stochastic.

The term

t\mapsto(1-F_{i}(t))P_{t,\alpha_{i}}V_{0}(\cdot,i)

is càdlàg in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, since
1-F_{i}
is càdlàg and

(P_{t,\alpha_{i}})_{t\geq 0}
is strongly continuous on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Let
\mathbf{U}\in D([0,T];\mathcal{E})
, with
0\leq U_{j}(t,\cdot)\leq 1

a.e., and define

I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}(t):=\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}\bigl(U_{1}(t-s),\ldots,U_{K}(t-s)\bigr)\,F_{i}(ds).

We claim that
t\mapsto I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}(t)
is càdlàg in

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Let
t_{n}\downarrow t
. Then

\displaystyle I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}(t_{n})-I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\left[\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(t_{n}-s))-\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(t-s))\right]F_{i}(ds)

\displaystyle+\int_{(t,t_{n}]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(t_{n}-s))F_{i}(ds).

The first term converges to zero in
L^{1}
by the right-continuity of

\mathbf{U}
, the Lipschitz bound for
\Phi_{i}
, the contractivity of

P_{s,\alpha_{i}}
on
L^{1}
, and dominated convergence with respect to the
finite measure
F_{i}
. For the second term, since

\mathbf{U}\in D([0,T];\mathcal{E})
, it is bounded on
[0,T]
. Hence

\sup_{0\leq r\leq T}\|\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(r))\|_{1}<\infty,

and therefore

\left\|\int_{(t,t_{n}]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(t_{n}-s))F_{i}(ds)\right\|_{1}\leq C\,F_{i}((t,t_{n}]).

Since
F_{i}
is right-continuous,
F_{i}((t,t_{n}])\to 0
. Thus

I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}
is right-continuous.

Similarly, if
t_{n}\uparrow t
, then
I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}(t_{n})
converges in

L^{1}
to

\int_{[0,t)}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}\bigl(\mathbf{U}((t-s)-)\bigr)F_{i}(ds),

where
\mathbf{U}(r-)
denotes the left limit of
\mathbf{U}
at
r>0
.
Indeed, for
s<t
,
t_{n}-s\uparrow t-s
, so

\mathbf{U}(t_{n}-s)\longrightarrow\mathbf{U}((t-s)-)\quad\text{in }\mathcal{E},

and dominated convergence applies with respect to the finite measure

F_{i}
restricted to
[0,t)
. This proves the existence of left limits.
Hence
I_{i}^{\mathbf{U}}
is càdlàg.

Now choose
h>0
such that

\max_{1\leq i\leq K}F_{i}(h)<1,

which is possible because
F_{i}(0)=0
and
F_{i}
is right-continuous at
zero. Let
\mathcal{B}_{h}
denote the space of bounded Borel maps

\mathbf{U}:[0,h]\to\mathcal{E}
, endowed with the norm

\|\mathbf{U}\|_{\infty,h}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq h}\|\mathbf{U}(t)\|_{\mathcal{E}},

and satisfying
0\leq U_{j}(t,\cdot)\leq 1
a.e. for every
j
and
t
.
This is a complete metric space. Indeed, since
\mathcal{E}
is a Banach
space, the space of all bounded maps from
[0,h]
into
\mathcal{E}
,
endowed with the supremum norm, is complete. The subspace of bounded Borel
maps is closed under uniform convergence, because uniform limits of Borel maps
with values in a metric space are Borel. Finally, the constraint

0\leq U_{j}(t,\cdot)\leq 1
a.e. is closed under
L^{1}
-convergence for each
fixed
t
. Hence
\mathcal{B}_{h}
is complete. Moreover, the operator
\mathcal{T}
defined by the right-hand side of
(
3.9
) maps
\mathcal{B}_{h}
into itself. Indeed, if

\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
, then
\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U}
is Borel
measurable. To see this, extend each
U_{j}
by zero outside
[0,h]
. Then

(t,s)\mapsto U_{j}(t-s)\mathbf{1}_{\{0\leq s\leq t\}}

is Borel measurable from
[0,h]\times[0,h]
into
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
Since
\Phi_{i}
is Lipschitz as a map on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{K}
, and

(P_{s,\alpha_{i}})_{s\geq 0}
is strongly measurable on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
it follows that

t\mapsto\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}\bigl(\mathbf{U}(t-s)\bigr)\,F_{i}(ds)

is Borel measurable as an
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
-valued map. Hence

\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U}
is Borel measurable.

Furthermore,
0\leq V_{0}\leq 1
and
0\leq\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq 1
for

\mathbf{y}\in[0,1]^{K}
. Hence, for
0\leq t\leq h
,

0\leq(\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U})_{i}(t)\leq(1-F_{i}(t))P_{t,\alpha_{i}}\mathbf{1}+\int_{[0,t]}P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\mathbf{1}\,F_{i}(ds)=(1-F_{i}(t))+F_{i}(t)=1\qquad\text{a.e.}

Moreover, for
0\leq t\leq h
,

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U})_{i}(t)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(1-F_{i}(t))\|P_{t,\alpha_{i}}V_{0}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}+\int_{[0,t]}\|P_{s,\alpha_{i}}\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(t-s))\|_{1}\,F_{i}(ds)

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{0}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}+\int_{[0,t]}\|\Phi_{i}(\mathbf{U}(t-s))\|_{1}\,F_{i}(ds)

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{0}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}+\int_{[0,t]}\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\|U_{j}(t-s)\|_{1}\,F_{i}(ds)

\displaystyle\leq\|V_{0}(\cdot,i)\|_{1}+F_{i}(h)\|\mathbf{U}\|_{\infty,h}<\infty.

Consequently,
\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
.
Furthermore, for
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{B}_{h}
,

\sup_{0\leq t\leq h}\|(\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U})(t)-(\mathcal{T}\mathbf{W})(t)\|_{\mathcal{E}}\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq K}F_{i}(h)\sup_{0\leq t\leq h}\|\mathbf{U}(t)-\mathbf{W}(t)\|_{\mathcal{E}}.

Thus, by the contraction mapping theorem, the integral equation has a unique
bounded Borel solution on
[0,h]
. To see that this solution is càdlàg,
start the iteration from
\mathbf{U}^{[0]}\equiv 0
. Since
\mathcal{T}
maps

D([0,h];\mathcal{E})\cap\mathcal{B}_{h}
into itself, all iterates

\mathbf{U}^{[n+1]}=\mathcal{T}\mathbf{U}^{[n]},\qquad n\geq 0,

are càdlàg. The contraction estimate implies that
\mathbf{U}^{[n]}
converges
uniformly in
\mathcal{E}
to the unique fixed point. Since the uniform limit
of càdlàg functions with values in a Banach space is càdlàg, the unique
solution is càdlàg. Moreover, by
0\leq V_{t}\leq 1
and (
3.6
), the
probabilistic function

\mathbf{V}(t):=(V_{t}(\cdot,1),\ldots,V_{t}(\cdot,K))

belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{h}
. It satisfies the same integral equation, and
therefore, by uniqueness in
\mathcal{B}_{h}
, it coincides with the fixed
point. Hence

t\mapsto V_{t}(\cdot,i)

is càdlàg on
[0,h]
, for each
i=1,\ldots,K
.

Repeating the argument on consecutive intervals gives the same conclusion on

[0,T]
. Indeed, when the equation is solved on an interval
[a,a+h]
,
the unknown part of the convolution corresponds to
0\leq s\leq t-a\leq h
,
and is therefore controlled by the same contraction constant

\max_{i}F_{i}(h)<1
, while the remaining part only involves values already
constructed on earlier intervals. Therefore, for each
i=1,\ldots,K
,

t\mapsto V_{t}(\cdot,i)

is càdlàg from
[0,T]
into
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Since
R_{i}
is Lipschitz on
[0,1]^{K}
,

|R_{i}(\mathbf{y})-R_{i}(\mathbf{z})|\leq 2\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}|y_{j}-z_{j}|,\qquad\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\in[0,1]^{K},

we have, for
s,t\in[0,T]
,

|B_{i}(t)-B_{i}(s)|\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{K}\|V_{t}(\cdot,j)-V_{s}(\cdot,j)\|_{1}.

Therefore
B_{i}
is càdlàg on compact intervals. Since every real-valued
càdlàg function on a compact interval is regulated,
B_{i}
is locally
Riemann integrable. In particular,
B_{i}
is Borel measurable and locally
bounded.

Together with (
3.14
),

0\leq B_{i}(t)\leq C_{\delta}\min\{t^{-\rho(\beta-\delta)},1\},\qquad t\geq 0,

where
\rho(\beta-\delta)>1
, this implies that
B_{i}
is directly Riemann
integrable on
[0,\infty)
. Indeed,
B_{i}
is locally Riemann integrable
and is dominated by an integrable nonincreasing function on
[0,\infty)
.

Define

z_{i}(t):=(1-F_{i}(t))G_{i}(0)-\int_{[0,t]}B_{i}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds).

The function
1-F_{i}
is nonincreasing and integrable on
[0,\infty)
,
since

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-F_{i}(t))\,dt=m_{i}<\infty.

Hence
1-F_{i}
is directly Riemann integrable. Since
B_{i}
is nonnegative
and directly Riemann integrable, and
F_{i}
is a finite measure on

[0,\infty)
, the convolution

t\mapsto\int_{[0,t]}B_{i}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds)

is directly Riemann integrable; see
(
Sgibnev, 2023
, Theorem 1)
.
Therefore
z_{i}
, being the difference of two directly Riemann integrable
functions, is directly Riemann integrable in the real-valued sense.

Moreover,
z_{i}
is locally bounded. Indeed, for every
T>0
,

0\leq(1-F_{i}(t))G_{i}(0)\leq G_{i}(0),\qquad 0\leq t\leq T.

Since
B_{i}
is càdlàg, it is bounded on compact intervals. Therefore

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\int_{[0,t]}B_{i}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds)\leq\sup_{0\leq r\leq T}B_{i}(r)\,F_{i}([0,T])\leq\sup_{0\leq r\leq T}B_{i}(r)<\infty.

Thus
z_{i}
is Borel measurable, locally bounded, and directly Riemann
integrable.

With this notation, (
3.10
) becomes

(3.15)

G_{i}(t)=z_{i}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{[0,t]}G_{j}(t-s)\,F_{i,j}(ds),

where

F_{i,j}(t):=m_{i,j}F_{i}(t).

As in Proposition
2.1
, the matrix of measures

(F_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{K}
satisfies

F(\infty)=M,\qquad p(F(\infty))=1,\qquad F(0)=0,\qquad p(F(0))=0<1,

and is non-lattice because
m_{i,i}>0
and
F_{i}
is non-arithmetic. Since each

z_{i}
is directly Riemann integrable, Theorem 2.2(iii) of
Athreya and Murthy (1976)

applies to (
3.15
). Thus, for every
i=1,\dots,K
,

(3.16)

\lim_{t\to\infty}G_{i}(t)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}z_{k}(s)\,ds}{\bar{m}}.

By Tonelli’s theorem,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{[0,t]}B_{i}(t-s)\,F_{i}(ds)\,dt&=\int_{[0,\infty)}\int_{s}^{\infty}B_{i}(t-s)\,dt\,F_{i}(ds)\\
&=\int_{[0,\infty)}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{i}(r)\,dr\,F_{i}(ds)=\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{i}(r)\,dr.\end{split}

Therefore,

\int_{0}^{\infty}z_{i}(t)\,dt=m_{i}G_{i}(0)-\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{i}(s)\,ds.

Substituting this into (
3.16
), one obtains

\lim_{t\to\infty}G_{i}(t)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}m_{k}G_{k}(0)-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{k}(s)\,ds}{\bar{m}}.

Since

\theta_{k}=\frac{\pi_{k}m_{k}}{\bar{m}},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}=1,

we conclude that

(3.17)

G(\phi):=\lim_{t\to\infty}g_{\phi}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}G_{k}(0)-\frac{1}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{k}(s)\,ds.

It remains to prove the first-order identity and the positivity of
G(\phi)
. Let

\bar{f}_{i}(x):=1-e^{-\phi_{i}(x)},\qquad i=1,\dots,K.

For
0<\epsilon<1/2
, define

\phi_{\epsilon,i}(x):=-\log(1-\epsilon\bar{f}_{i}(x)),\qquad\phi_{\epsilon}(x,i):=\phi_{\epsilon,i}(x),

and set

V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,i):=E_{(x,i)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi_{\epsilon}\rangle}\right).

Then

V_{0}^{\epsilon}(x,i)=\epsilon\bar{f}_{i}(x).

Moreover,
0\leq\phi_{\epsilon}\leq\phi
, and hence

0\leq V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,i)\leq V_{t}(x,i).

Since
x\mapsto-\log(1-x)/x
is bounded on
[0,1/2]
, there exists

C_{\phi}>0
such that

\frac{\phi_{\epsilon,i}(x)}{\epsilon}\leq C_{\phi}\bar{f}_{i}(x).

Using
1-e^{-y}\leq y
, we obtain

(3.18)

\frac{V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,i)}{\epsilon}\leq E_{(x,i)}\left(\left\langle X_{t},C_{\phi}\bar{f}\right\rangle\right).

Let

B_{i}^{\epsilon}(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}R_{i}\bigl(V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,1),\dots,V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,K)\bigr)\,dx.

We prove that

(3.19)

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{i}^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds=0.

For all sufficiently large
t
, using (
3.13
),

V_{t}^{\epsilon}\leq V_{t}
, and (
3.18
),

\begin{split}\frac{B_{i}^{\epsilon}(t)}{\epsilon}&\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq K}V_{t}(x,j)\right)^{\beta-\delta}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,j)}{\epsilon}\,dx\\
&\leq C\left(\sup_{x,j}V_{t}(x,j)\right)^{\beta-\delta}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}E_{(x,j)}\left(\left\langle X_{t},C_{\phi}\bar{f}\right\rangle\right)\,dx\\
&\leq C\min\{t^{-\rho(\beta-\delta)},1\}.\end{split}

The right-hand side is integrable. On bounded time intervals, domination follows from

R_{i}(\mathbf{y})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}y_{j},

from (
3.18
), and from (
3.8
). Thus

B_{i}^{\epsilon}(t)/\epsilon
is dominated by an integrable function independent of

\epsilon
.

For each fixed
t\geq 0
, we now show that

\frac{B_{i}^{\epsilon}(t)}{\epsilon}\longrightarrow 0.

For each fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
,

V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,j)=E_{(x,j)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi_{\epsilon}\rangle}\right)\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\epsilon\downarrow 0,

by dominated convergence. In addition, (
3.18
) gives

V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,j)\leq\epsilon E_{(x,j)}\left(\left\langle X_{t},C_{\phi}\bar{f}\right\rangle\right).

Hence, for fixed
t,x,j
,

V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,j)=O(\epsilon),\qquad\epsilon\downarrow 0.

Using (
3.13
), we obtain

\frac{R_{i}\bigl(V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,1),\dots,V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,K)\bigr)}{\epsilon}\longrightarrow 0.

Furthermore,

\frac{R_{i}(V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,1),\dots,V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,K))}{\epsilon}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}\frac{V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,j)}{\epsilon}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}m_{i,j}E_{(x,j)}\left(\left\langle X_{t},C_{\phi}\bar{f}\right\rangle\right),

and the right-hand side is integrable in
x
by (
3.8
), applied
with
\psi=C_{\phi}\bar{f}
. Therefore, dominated convergence in
x
gives

\frac{B_{i}^{\epsilon}(t)}{\epsilon}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{for every fixed }t\geq 0.

A second application of dominated convergence in
t
, using the integrable domination
obtained above, proves (
3.19
).

Applying (
3.17
) with
\phi
replaced by
\phi_{\epsilon}
, we obtain

G(\phi_{\epsilon})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|V_{0}^{\epsilon}(\cdot,k)\|_{1}-\frac{1}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{k}^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds.

Since

\|V_{0}^{\epsilon}(\cdot,k)\|_{1}=\epsilon\|\bar{f}_{k}\|_{1},

division by
\epsilon
gives

\frac{G(\phi_{\epsilon})}{\epsilon}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|\bar{f}_{k}\|_{1}-\frac{1}{\bar{m}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}B_{k}^{\epsilon}(s)\,ds.

Using (
3.19
), we obtain

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{G(\phi_{\epsilon})}{\epsilon}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|\bar{f}_{k}\|_{1}.

Since

V_{0}(x,k)=1-e^{-\phi_{k}(x)}=\bar{f}_{k}(x),

the right-hand side equals

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|V_{0}(\cdot,k)\|_{1}.

This proves (
2.7
).

Finally, if
\phi\neq 0
, then

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\|\bar{f}_{k}\|_{1}>0,

because
\theta_{k}>0
for every
k
. Hence

G(\phi_{\epsilon})>0

for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Since
0\leq\phi_{\epsilon}\leq\phi
, monotonicity gives

0\leq V_{t}^{\epsilon}(x,i)\leq V_{t}(x,i),\qquad t\geq 0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\ i=1,\dots,K.

Therefore

g_{\phi_{\epsilon}}(t)\leq g_{\phi}(t),\qquad t\geq 0.

Letting
t\to\infty
, and using the existence of both limits, yields

G(\phi_{\epsilon})\leq G(\phi).

Consequently,

G(\phi)>0.

\hfill\square

Auxiliary Results

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that Condition
2.1
holds and set

\alpha_{*}:=\alpha_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_{K},\qquad\rho:=q\wedge\frac{N}{\alpha_{*}}.

Then, for every
\phi\in C_{c}^{+}(S)
, there exists
C_{\phi}>0
such that, for all
t\geq 1
and all
(x,i)\in S
,

V_{t}(x,i)\leq P_{(x,i)}\bigl(X_{t}(D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K})>0\bigr)\leq C_{\phi}t^{-\rho},

where
D_{\phi}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is any ball such that

\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K}
.

Proof.

Following the argument of Proposition 4.2 of
Gorostiza et al. (1992)
, and using
Condition
2.1
, there exists
C_{\phi}>0
such that, for all
t\geq 1
and all

(x,i)\in S
,

P_{(x,i)}\bigl(X_{t}(D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K})>0\bigr)\leq C_{\phi}\left(t^{-q}+t^{-N/\alpha_{*}}\right)\leq C_{\phi}t^{-\rho},

where
\alpha_{*}=\alpha_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha_{K}
and

\rho=q\wedge N/\alpha_{*}
. Since

1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\leq 1_{\{X_{t}(D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K})>0\}},

we obtain

V_{t}(x,i)=E_{(x,i)}\left(1-e^{-\langle X_{t},\phi\rangle}\right)\leq P_{(x,i)}\bigl(X_{t}(D_{\phi}\times\mathbf{K})>0\bigr)\leq C_{\phi}t^{-\rho}.

This proves the lemma.
∎

The following lemmas provide auxiliary results that will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T^{(1)},\dots,T^{(K)}
be independent nonnegative random variables, where

T^{(i)}
has distribution
F_{i}
. Assume that

1-F_{i}(t)\leq At^{-\eta^{(i)}},\qquad t>0,\quad i=1,\dots,K,

for some
A>0
and
\eta^{(i)}>1
. Set

\eta:=\min_{1\leq i\leq K}\eta^{(i)}

and

T^{*}:=\max_{1\leq i\leq K}T^{(i)}.

Then there exists a constant
\bar{A}>0
such that

(3.20)

P(T^{*}>t)\leq\bar{A}t^{-\eta},\qquad t>0.

In particular,
E(T^{*})<\infty
.

Proof.

By the union bound,

P(T^{*}>t)=P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}\{T^{(i)}>t\}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}P(T^{(i)}>t).

For
t\geq 1
, since
\eta\leq\eta^{(i)}
for every
i
,

P(T^{*}>t)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}At^{-\eta^{(i)}}\leq KAt^{-\eta}.

For
0<t<1
, we have
P(T^{*}>t)\leq 1\leq t^{-\eta}
. Hence

P(T^{*}>t)\leq\bar{A}t^{-\eta},\qquad t>0,

with
\bar{A}:=KA\vee 1
. Since
\eta>1
,

E(T^{*})=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(T^{*}>t)\,dt<\infty.

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. nonnegative random variables with mean

m<\infty
. Assume that

P(Y_{1}>t)\leq At^{-\eta},\qquad t>0,

for some
A>0
and
\eta>1
. Then, for every
c>0
, there exists

C>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
and all
x\geq cn
,

(3.21)

P\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-nm\geq x\right)\leq Cnx^{-\eta}.

Consequently, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
C_{\delta}>0
such that,
for all sufficiently large
n
,

(3.22)

P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-m\geq\delta\right)\leq C_{\delta}n^{1-\eta}.

Proof.

Apply Theorem 2 of
Nagaev (1982)
to the centered variables

X_{k}:=Y_{k}-m,\qquad k\geq 1.

Then
E(X_{1})=0
. Moreover, for every
y>0
,

P(X_{1}>y)=P(Y_{1}>y+m)\leq A(y+m)^{-\eta}\leq Ay^{-\eta}.

Therefore, Theorem 2 of
Nagaev (1982)
gives, for each fixed
c>0
, a constant

C>0
such that, for all sufficiently large
n
and all
x\geq cn
,

P\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\geq x\right)\leq Cnx^{-\eta}.

Since

\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-nm,

this proves (
3.21
).

Taking
x=\delta n
in (
3.21
), we obtain

P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-m\geq\delta\right)=P\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-nm\geq\delta n\right)\leq Cn(\delta n)^{-\eta}.

Hence

P\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}Y_{k}-m\geq\delta\right)\leq C_{\delta}n^{1-\eta},

where

C_{\delta}:=C\delta^{-\eta}.

This proves (
3.22
).
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume (
2.4
). Let
Z=(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Markov renewal process on

\mathbf{K}=\{1,\dots,K\}
, and let
n_{t}
be the number of renewals up to time
t
.
Let
T^{(1)},\dots,T^{(K)}
be independent random variables such that
T^{(r)}
has
distribution
F_{r}
, and define

T^{*}:=\max_{1\leq r\leq K}T^{(r)}.

Set

\bar{\mu}:=E(T^{*})<\infty.

If
c>0
satisfies

c<\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}},

then, for every initial type
i\in\mathbf{K}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that,
for all sufficiently large
t
,

(3.23)

P_{i}(n_{t}\leq ct)\leq Ct^{1-\eta},

where

\eta:=\min_{1\leq r\leq K}\eta^{(r)}.

Proof.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the embedded Markov chain of the Markov renewal
process. Thus
Y_{0}
is the initial type. Conditionally on

(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, let the holding times be independent random variables

(\widetilde{T}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, where
\widetilde{T}_{n}
has distribution

F_{Y_{n}}
. Define

\sigma_{0}:=0,\qquad\sigma_{n}:=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{T}_{r},\qquad n\geq 1.

Then

n_{t}=\max\{n\geq 0:\sigma_{n}\leq t\}.

Put

n:=\lfloor ct\rfloor+1.

Then

\{n_{t}\leq ct\}\subseteq\{\sigma_{n}>t\}.

We now compare the holding times of the Markov renewal process with
independent copies of
T^{*}
. Let
F_{*}
be the distribution function of

T^{*}:=\max_{1\leq r\leq K}T^{(r)}.

Since
T^{(r)}\leq_{\mathrm{st}}T^{*}
for every
r=1,\ldots,K
, we have

F_{r}(x)\geq F_{*}(x),\qquad x\geq 0,\quad r=1,\ldots,K.

Let
U_{0},U_{1},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables uniformly distributed on

(0,1)
, independent of the embedded chain
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. Using the
usual generalized inverse

F^{-1}(u):=\inf\{x\geq 0:F(x)\geq u\},\qquad 0<u<1,

define

\widetilde{T}_{n}:=F_{Y_{n}}^{-1}(U_{n}),\qquad T_{n}^{*}:=F_{*}^{-1}(U_{n}),\qquad n\geq 0.

Then, conditionally on
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, the variables

(\widetilde{T}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are independent and
\widetilde{T}_{n}
has
distribution
F_{Y_{n}}
. Hence this construction has the law of the holding
times of the Markov renewal process. Moreover, since

F_{Y_{n}}\geq F_{*}
, the monotonicity of generalized inverses gives

\widetilde{T}_{n}\leq T_{n}^{*},\qquad n\geq 0,

almost surely. The variables
T_{0}^{*},T_{1}^{*},\ldots
are i.i.d. with
distribution
F_{*}
, that is, with the distribution of
T^{*}
. Consequently,
for every
n\geq 1
,

\sigma_{n}=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{T}_{r}\leq\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}T_{r}^{*}=:\bar{S}_{n}\qquad\text{a.s.}

Therefore,

P_{i}(n_{t}\leq ct)\leq P_{i}(\sigma_{n}>t)\leq P(\bar{S}_{n}>t).

Since
n=\lfloor ct\rfloor+1
, we have
n/t\to c
. Because

c<1/\bar{\mu}
,

\frac{t}{n}\longrightarrow\frac{1}{c}>\bar{\mu}.

Therefore, there exists
\delta>0
such that, for all sufficiently large

t
,

t-n\bar{\mu}\geq\delta n.

Thus,

P(\bar{S}_{n}>t)=P(\bar{S}_{n}-n\bar{\mu}>t-n\bar{\mu})\leq P(\bar{S}_{n}-n\bar{\mu}\geq\delta n).

By Lemma
3.2
, the distribution of
T^{*}
satisfies

P(T^{*}>x)\leq\bar{A}x^{-\eta},\qquad x>0.

Applying Lemma
3.3
to the i.i.d. sequence

Y_{k}:=T_{k-1}^{*},\qquad k\geq 1,

with mean
\bar{\mu}
, we obtain

P(\bar{S}_{n}-n\bar{\mu}\geq\delta n)\leq Cn^{1-\eta}

for all sufficiently large
n
. Since
n=\lfloor ct\rfloor+1
, it follows
that

P(\bar{S}_{n}-n\bar{\mu}\geq\delta n)\leq C^{\prime}t^{1-\eta}

for all sufficiently large
t
. Combining the preceding estimates gives

P_{i}(n_{t}\leq ct)\leq C^{\prime}t^{1-\eta},

which proves (
3.23
).
∎
