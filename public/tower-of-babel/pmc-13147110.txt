Longitudinal changes and predictors of happiness in South Korean early adolescents: a panel study using latent growth modeling
Authors: Hyesun Jeong, Mi-Ae You, Yoonju Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEarly adolescence is a pivotal developmental stage characterized by rapid physical, emotional, and social changes. Happiness during this period is crucial as it influences later life outcomes. This study examined 4-year changes in happiness and identified predictors among Korean early adolescents.MethodsA secondary analysis of longitudinal panel data was conducted using latent growth modeling to assess changes in happiness over 4 years, utilizing data from the Panel Study on Korean Children (11th–14th waves) with a total of 1,177 participants. Descriptive statistics were computed using IBM SPSS ver. 29.0, and latent growth modeling, including unconditional and conditional models with associated factors variables, was conducted using IBM AMOS ver. 29.0.ResultsA nonlinear trajectory was identified with a sharp decline in happiness, particularly between the ages of 11 and 12 years. Self-esteem, peer attachment, and a creative school environment emerged as protective factors mitigating this decline, whereas parental attachment influenced baseline happiness but not its trajectory. Higher self-esteem, stronger peer attachment, and supportive school environments were associated with a slower decline in happiness.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of early interventions beginning around the age of 11 years, a critical period when happiness decreases more steeply. Practical implications include developing school-based and psychosocial programs that strengthen self-esteem and positive peer relationships, and fostering autonomy-supportive, creative educational environments. Coordinated efforts between schools and families are essential to promote adolescents’ emotional well-being and ensure balanced development in academic and socio-emotional domains.Keywords: Adolescent, Happiness, Latent class analysis, Longitudinal studies

INTRODUCTION

Adolescence is a transitional period between childhood and adulthood, characterized by rapid physical and emotional development, as well as considerable social changes [1]. Early adolescence, generally considered to encompass the ages of 10–13 years, is marked by substantial changes across physical, cognitive, and social domains. During this period, individuals begin to form identities and explore values and social roles that can influence their adult lives [2]. The onset of early adolescence, typically beginning by the fourth grade of primary school, introduces challenges related to secondary sexual characteristics [3] and increased psychological stress and anxiety resulting from expanding relationships with peers, teachers, and others outside the family [4,5].

Happiness refers to a subjective experience that reflects an individual’s cognitive appraisal and evaluation of their own life, resulting in positive emotions [6]. Happiness is more than a positive emotional state; it plays a fundamental role in psychological and physical development [7]. Unhappiness or a decline in happiness during early adolescence can lead to various difficulties during middle school. Difficulties in peer relationships, feelings of social exclusion, and emotional instability at home can exacerbate mental health problems such as depression and anxiety [8]. In particular, Korean adolescents have been reported to exhibit the lowest levels of happiness among 22 Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development countries [9]. Such low levels of happiness represent a serious concern, as they are closely associated with adverse mental health outcomes [4], including an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behaviors [8]. Notably, suicide rates among adolescents in the Asia-Pacific region, including Korea, have shown a continuous upward trend [10]. Therefore, supporting the happiness of early adolescents in Korea may serve as a preventive measure against suicidal ideation and attempts, ultimately contributing to better mental health [11].

Happiness during early adolescence exerts a significant influence on physical, mental, and social well-being [6]. It is directly linked to mental health and serves as a foundation for overall development into adulthood [1]. Therefore, efforts to promote happiness during this critical period are essential for enhancing population health and social well-being [8]. Happier adolescents are more likely to engage in community activities and contribute positively to society [1]. In terms of physical health, happiness influences health behaviors such as physical activity. Unhappy adolescents are less likely to engage in physical activity [12]. Moreover, happiness is closely related to a reduction in problematic behaviors such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and school violence [13]. For instance, adolescents who consume alcohol are 68% more likely to report unhappiness, whereas smokers are 61% more likely to report lower happiness than that among their non-smoking peers [12]. Consequently, promoting happiness from late primary school years may provide a protective buffer against the physical and mental health challenges of adolescence and serve as a fundamental strategy for fostering social adaptation while reducing risk behaviors [14].

Despite its importance, happiness among adolescents has shown a global decline [15]. The Health Behavior in School-Aged Children study, conducted across 43 countries with adolescents aged 10–16 years, reported a noticeable decrease in happiness levels [15]. In Korea, cross-sectional research comparing happiness levels between fourth graders in primary school and first-year middle school students found that the younger group reported higher happiness and better teacher–student relationships than did their older peers [7]. Similarly, primary school students showed relatively higher levels of happiness, whereas middle school students reported markedly lower levels [16]. Although some studies reported temporary improvements in happiness between certain grades [17], recent longitudinal evidence indicates a consistent decline during the transition from late primary to early middle school [7]. However, systematic longitudinal studies on happiness changes during this transition remain scarce, highlighting the need for further research.

Guided by an ecological systems perspective and informed by prior research, we selected predictors representing multiple developmental contexts that have been linked to happiness in early adolescence [2,12,17-21]. At the individual level, personal characteristics, such as self-identity formation and emotional experiences, contribute to self-esteem, which is strongly associated with higher happiness levels, effective stress management, and better mental health outcomes [2,12,14,22]. Within the family context, parental attachment has been shown to provide emotional security and positively affect adolescents’ happiness [17,18]. In the peer domain, positive peer attachment and supportive friendships are important predictors of happiness [19,20]. At the school level, a creative and supportive educational environment fosters school adaptation and further enhances happiness [21]. Collectively, these findings suggest that self-esteem, parental attachment, peer attachment, and a creative school environment are critical protective factors that shape happiness during early adolescence.

This study aimed to examine the changes in happiness among early adolescents in South Korea using data from the Panel Study on Korean Children (PSKC). Specifically, the objective was to investigate how happiness in early adolescence is influenced by individual-, family-, and school-related factors. By capturing individual changes in happiness over time and conducting a multidimensional analysis of the effects of various predictors on these changes, this study sought to provide foundational evidence for developing programs and public health interventions aimed at enhancing early adolescent happiness. Furthermore, it aimed to improve the effectiveness of such interventions and contribute to promoting mental well-being during this critical developmental period.

METHODS

Ethical statements: This study was deemed exempt from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Pusan National University (IRB No. PNU IRB/2024-98-HR) because it was a secondary analysis of existing data. The original Panel Study on Korean Children (PSKC) received annual IRB approval from the internal IRB of the Korea Institute of Child Care and Education (KICCE), and informed consent was obtained from participants and their legal guardians for each wave of data collection.

1. Study Design and Data Source

In this study, we conducted a secondary analysis of longitudinal data from the PSKC, with the aim of examining changes in happiness among early adolescents from the age of 10 years over a 4-year period and identifying predictors of these changes, focusing on individual-, family-, and school-related factors.

The PSKC data used in this study were de-identified secondary data; therefore, no direct contact with participants occurred in the present study. The original PSKC data collection procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The PSKC investigates the developmental pathways of children and adolescents in South Korea, assessing factors that contribute to their emotional, social, and academic well-being. The dataset captures multidimensional influences, such as family relationships, peer interactions, and school environments, which are essential for understanding adolescent flourishing. Its longitudinal structure provides a robust basis for examining positive developmental outcomes, such as happiness, with a focus on individual-, family-, and school-related factors. For this study, data from the 11th to 14th waves (2018–2021) of the PSKC were analyzed, targeting adolescents aged 10–13 years. These periods represent a critical developmental period characterized by rapid physical, emotional, and social changes. By leveraging the longitudinal nature of the PSKC, this study examined how self-esteem, parental attachment, peer attachment, and creative school environment contribute to happiness changes, offering insights into interventions that promote adolescent well-being. The reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [23].

2. Participants

The study sample included adolescents who participated in all four waves of the PSKC from the 11th to 14th year and met the following criteria: (1) enrollment in regular middle schools and (2) provision of complete responses to the variables of interest. Adolescents attending specialized schools (such as arts or sports-focused middle schools) or alternative educational institutions were excluded to ensure comparability of school environments. The initial eligible sample included children who participated in the 11th PSKC wave (age 10 years; N=1,428). Among them, participants who missed at least one follow-up assessment between Waves 12 and 14 were excluded (n=100), resulting in n=1,328 participants with data available across the target waves. We further excluded cases with missing values in study variables (n=132), those not enrolled in middle school (n=3), and those enrolled in special middle schools (n=16). After applying these criteria, the final sample comprised 1,177 participants, allowing for robust analyses of happiness changes and related protective factors.

3. Measures

Each measure was selected to capture critical components of adolescent flourishing, emphasizing personal strengths and supportive environments:

1) General characteristics

General characteristics included participants’ sex and subjective socioeconomic status (SES). SES was assessed at baseline (age 10 years; Wave 11) using the corresponding item from the PSKC. Participants were asked a single question: “Where do you think your family’s living standard (economic status) falls on the following ladder?” Responses were recorded on a ladder scale ranging from 1 (“very poor”) to 10 (“very well”), yielding scores from 1 to 10.

2) Self-esteem

Self-esteem, a foundational component of resilience and positive self-concept, was assessed at baseline (age 10 years; Wave 11) using a validated five-item adaptation of Rosenberg’s Self-Esteem Scale, tailored for adolescents [22]. Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater self-esteem. The internal consistency of the scale in this study was Cronbach’s α=.84.

3) Parental attachment

Parental attachment, reflecting emotional security and relational support, was measured at baseline (age 11 years; Wave 12) using a modified version of the Inventory of Parent and Peer Attachment [19]. The tool included 12 items assessing communication, trust, and alienation, with separate evaluations for mothers and fathers. Scores were averaged, with higher scores indicating stronger parental attachment. Cronbach’s α values were .79 and .81 for father and mother attachments, respectively.

4) Peer attachment

Peer attachment, a key resource for social integration and emotional well-being, was evaluated at baseline (age 10 years; Wave 11) using nine items from the same inventory [24]. The scale assessed communication, trust, and alienation, with negatively worded items reverse-coded. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating stronger peer attachment. Cronbach’s α for this scale was .80.

5) Creative school environment

A creative school environment, which fosters autonomy, intrinsic motivation, and self-expression, was measured at baseline (age 10 years; Wave 11) using a 10-item instrument adapted by the Korea Institute of Child Care and Education (KICCE). This instrument was revised by KICCE based on the 37-item “Characteristics of My Surrounding Environment Questionnaire” originally developed by Kim [25] in 2012. The scale included four reverse-coded items reflecting restrictive environments [25]. Higher scores indicated a more creative and supportive school environment. The internal reliability was Cronbach’s α=.81.

6) Happiness

Happiness, a core construct of subjective well-being and flourishing, was measured from Waves 11–14 (ages 10–13 years) using the Subjective Happiness Scale [26], adapted for the PSKC. The four-item scale was rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting greater subjective happiness. Cronbach’s α for this measure was .77 at Wave 11, .75 at Wave 12, .78 at Wave 13, and .75 at Wave 14.

4. Statistical Analysis

To analyze the developmental changes in happiness and identify their predictors, data were processed using IBM SPSS/WIN ver. 29.0 (IBM Corp.) and IBM AMOS ver. 29.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviation (SD), skewness, and kurtosis, were calculated to summarize participant characteristics and normality. All variables met the criteria for normal distribution, supporting subsequent modeling.

Latent growth curve modeling (LGCM) was applied to examine changes in happiness over time, offering a robust framework for estimating initial levels (intercept) and rates of change (slope) across the study period [27]. An unconditional model was first established to investigate the overall changes in happiness and assess individual differences in initial status and growth rates. Based on prior research and theoretical considerations, predictors such as self-esteem, parental attachment, peer attachment, and creative school environment were then included in a conditional model to examine their influence on happiness changes. Covariates were selected to reflect environmental and psychological significance in adolescent development.

Model fit was evaluated using standard indices, including χ2/degrees of freedom (df), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). For model adequacy, RMSEA values below .08 and CFI/TLI values above .90 were indicative of good fit. Maximum likelihood estimation was employed to estimate parameters, and significant pathways within the conditional model provided insights into the relative contribution of each predictor to changes in happiness over time. Comparative evaluations of the models revealed the most suitable framework for understanding the developmental dynamics of happiness in early adolescence.

RESULTS

1. Baseline Characteristics of Participants and Variables

In total, 1,177 participants completed all four waves of the PSKC. Table 1 presents demographic and baseline characteristics of participants. Among the participants, 51.1% were male, and all were 10 years of age. Participants’ SES was 7.18 (SD=1.82), indicating a moderately high level. The mean scores for self-esteem, father attachment, mother attachment, peer attachment, and creative school environment were 17.5 (SD=2.29), 43.2 (SD=6.94), 46.6 (SD=6.58), 31.3 (SD=4.21), and 38.2 (SD=5.95), respectively. Happiness, the primary outcome, declined over time, with mean scores decreasing from 19.9 (SD=2.76) at the age of 10 years (Wave 11) to 17.6 (SD=2.68) at the age of 13 years (Wave 14). Notably, the largest decline was observed between ages of 11 (Wave 12) and 12 (Wave 13) years (Figure 1). These patterns suggest a substantial decline in happiness during early adolescence. Correlation analysis of the main variables revealed that all variables were significantly correlated. First, happiness at the age of 10 years (Wave 11) was significantly and positively correlated with all predictors. The strongest correlation was observed with self-esteem (r=.68, p<.01), followed by creative school environment (r=.55, p<.01) . Second, regarding inter-correlations among the predictors, the strongest association was found between creative school environment and peer attachment (r=.60, p<.01) (Table 1).

2. Changes in Happiness: Model Fit and Growth Patterns

LGCM was employed to examine the changes in happiness over the four waves. In the linear LGCM, slope weights were fixed to 3, 2, 1, and 0 for Waves 11–14. In the nonlinear LGCM, the slope weights were fixed to 3 (Wave 11) and 0 (Wave 14), while the intermediate loadings (Waves 12 and 13) were freely estimated to capture nonlinear change. A negative slope indicates a decline in happiness over time. Compared with the no-change and linear models, the nonlinear model demonstrated superior fit (χ2/df=3.4, p<.001; CFI=.99; TLI=.99; RMSEA=.04). The model revealed significant variance in the intercept (baseline happiness) and slope (rate of change over time), indicating individual differences in initial happiness levels and decline rate (Table 2).

3. Predictors of Happiness Changes

The conditional LGCM, which incorporated predictors such as self-esteem, peer attachment, parental attachment and creative school environment demonstrated a good fit (χ2/df=7.90, p<.001; CFI=.98; TLI=.93; RMSEA=.07). The results revealed that self-esteem (β=.55, p<.001), father attachment (β=.11, p<.001), mother attachment (β=.15, p<.001), peer attachment (β=.17, p<.001), and creative school environment (β=.24, p<.001) were significant positive predictors of the baseline level of happiness.

Regarding slope, self-esteem (β=.47, p<.001), peer attachment (β=.15, p=.018), and creative school environment (β=.23, p<.001) were associated with slower declines in happiness, indicating that these factors serve as protective resources. In contrast, father attachment and mother attachment were insignificant (Figure 2, Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study systematically analyzed the developmental changes in happiness during early adolescence using LGCM and examined the effects of personal and environmental factors, including self-esteem, parental attachment, peer attachment, a creative school environment.

The findings of this study indicated that happiness declined over time, consistent with that of previous research. Prior studies have demonstrated a downward trend in happiness during early adolescence, linked to psychological development, stress factors, and changes in peer relationships [28]. The slope of happiness gradually decreases with age, with the sharpest decline occurring between the ages of 12 and 15 years [29]. Similarly, this study revealed a decline in happiness over time during early adolescence, with a nonlinear change indicating a sharp decrease between the ages of 11 and 12 years. This finding reflects the significant social and psychological changes characteristic of early adolescence. Such changes are associated with reduced happiness and increased complexity in peer relationships, academic burden, and challenges in identity formation [3]. The pronounced nonlinear decline in happiness observed between the ages of 11 and 12 years may also be closely related to the educational structure in Korea [28]. Korean adolescents report relatively low levels of happiness, and this pattern is associated with the stark differences in academic intensity and evaluation methods between elementary and middle school. Upon entering middle school, they experience a sudden increase in academic pressure and competition, which is associated with the sharp nonlinear decline in happiness observed before middle school entry [11]. Therefore, implementing programs that foster happiness before the age of 11 years is crucial to enhancing the well-being of early adolescents in Korea.

The predictors of happiness changes identified in this study include self-esteem, creative school environment, and peer attachment; parental attachment was not found to be significant. First, self-esteem was confirmed as a key predictor of changes in happiness during early adolescence. The results showed that self-esteem was highly correlated with happiness across all waves from the ages of 11 to 14 years. Higher self-esteem was associated with higher initial levels of happiness and a slower decline over time, indicating that it acts as a protective factor against the decline in happiness as adolescents’ progress through school grades. This finding aligns with that of previous research, highlighting self-esteem as a critical psychological factor for enhancing happiness after entering middle school [17,30]. Adolescents with high self-esteem are more likely to maintain a positive self-concept and demonstrate emotional resilience in stressful situations [5,7,30]. Therefore, fostering self-esteem during early adolescence is essential, and systematic programs should be actively integrated into both school education and public health policies.

Second, creative school environment were identified as a protective factor against the decline in happiness as adolescents progress through grades. The study also revealed a strong association between creative school environment and peer attachment, highlighting the importance of supportive school settings in fostering positive peer relationships and sustaining adolescent happiness. Well-established creative school environments provide students with opportunities to explore their interests and exercise autonomy, which enhances self-esteem and intrinsic motivation, ultimately improving happiness [21]. Therefore, creating educational environments that focus on academic achievement as well as fostering creativity and student-centered learning is important [28]. In the highly competitive educational environment in Korea, providing creative educational activities and opportunities for autonomous learning could have a positive impact on enhancing early adolescent happiness. Additionally, fostering a creative school environment requires positive teacher-student relationships and a cooperative classroom atmosphere, necessitating efforts from the government, schools, and teachers [21].

Third, peer attachment significantly influenced both the initial levels and the rate of change in happiness. Peer attachment impacts adolescents’ life and school satisfaction. This finding supports the notion that positive peer relationships play a crucial role in maintaining happiness by promoting emotional stability and providing social support during early adolescence [30]. Particularly during the transition from upper elementary school to middle school, peer relationships become more important than parental relationships [31]. As students spend more time at school, peer interactions become an essential component of their school life [28]. Therefore, implementing school-based programs that promote positive peer interactions and prevent negative peer influences before student’s transition to middle school is necessary [31].

In contrast, parental attachment was not a significant predictor of changes in happiness. This finding may reflect developmental characteristics of early adolescence, during which rapid biological and psychosocial changes and expanding social contexts can influence happiness trajectories [3]. Beyond parental influences, changes in adolescents’ happiness during this period may be increasingly linked to other salient factors, including peer relationships and school-related experiences [20]. These results align with those of previous research indicating that peer relationships and self-esteem are closely associated with adolescent well-being and life satisfaction [18,20], and that family-related factors may be more strongly related to overall levels of happiness than to short-term changes during the transition to adolescence [17]. As adolescents transition from late childhood to early adolescence, the formation of self-identity and increased independence diminish parental influence, whereas peer relationships and school environment gain prominence [17]. Although parental attachment did not predict changes in happiness, it may still contribute indirectly by shaping adolescents’ self-esteem and peer attachment, which were associated with changes in happiness [18,20]. Consequently, parental roles may be limited to providing basic emotional stability [21]. However, positive parenting during adolescence can potentially influence peer attachment and self-esteem [18], and stable attachment to parents during early childhood may mitigate declines in happiness during early adolescence [18]. Further research is needed to verify the relevance of parental attachment by investigating its impact on happiness from early childhood through early adolescence.

Based on these findings, several practical strategies are proposed to enhance the happiness of early adolescents. First, school-based mental health promotion programs aimed at improving self-esteem should be actively implemented from the age of 11 years [7,30]. Universal self-esteem enhancement programs should be provided for all students to prevent the decline in happiness after transitioning to middle school, whereas targeted interventions should be designed for students with low self-esteem by considering family and school environments. Second, peer attachment, identified as a significant predictor, should be strengthened through activities such as group-based social skills training [31]. These programs promote peer attachment and enhance students’ sense of belonging and self-esteem, ultimately improving their overall happiness. Third, educational environments emphasizing creativity and autonomy, such as project-based learning and arts education, can provide early adolescents with opportunities for self-expression and stress relief [21]. In the educational system of Korea, where academic achievement is highly emphasized, academic pressure significantly associated with decline in happiness [28]. Therefore, to promote healthy adolescent development and ensure sustainable improvements in happiness, programs that enhance self-esteem are needed, along with efforts that take into account peer relationships and environmental factors within the school setting. Accordingly, schools and communities should develop programs that support happiness in early adolescents, specifically focusing on fostering self-esteem and peer attachment.

This study had some limitations. First, although variable selection was informed by an ecological systems perspective, the present study did not formally test ecological systems theory as a theory-driven framework. Future studies are needed to evaluate theory-based pathways among multi-level determinants (individual, family, peer, and school factors) and their associations with happiness trajectories. Second, the reliance on self-reported measures introduces the potential for response bias, particularly for constructs such as happiness and self-esteem. In addition, missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, which may have introduced selection bias by excluding participants with incomplete responses or follow-up data. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Third, subgroup analyses, such as differences based on sex or SES, were not conducted. These factors likely influence happiness changes and their predictors and should be explored in future research.

Furthermore, future research should investigate the long-term effect of resilience-building interventions on adolescent happiness. Longitudinal studies should explore how cultural contexts moderate the effects of self-esteem, peer attachment, and creative school environment on well-being changes. Additionally, testing adaptive interventions, including social-emotional learning programs and digital tools such as augmented reality games, across diverse educational and cultural settings could provide new insights into sustaining adolescent happiness.

CONCLUSION

This study identified a nonlinear change in happiness, with a significant decline observed between the ages of 11 and 12 years. Notably, this was likely related to the academic burdens faced by Korean early adolescents during the transition to middle school. Furthermore, this study highlights the critical predictors of self-esteem, peer attachment, and creative school environment in shaping the developmental changes of happiness in early adolescence. By providing insights into the dynamic interplay of these predictors, this research provides a foundation for early, targeted strategies to promote and sustain well-being, offering a foundation for practical applications and future investigations into adolescent happiness.
