Approach and Versatility of Multiple Options to Harvest Fibular Vascularized Corticoperiosteal Flap
Authors: Edgardo R. Rodriguez-Collazo, Jose Antonio Delgado, Matthew Hans, Sitong Chen, Shawn Ward
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Successful tibial and hindfoot reconstruction requires more than technical skill; it demands a surgical strategy capable of adapting to intraoperative findings. Considering that intraoperative anatomical and vascular findings may differ from preoperative imaging, a flexible approach is essential to managing varying patient cases. Although multiple well-established fibular flap techniques currently exist, they are traditionally approached as independent procedures with separate preoperative planning and dissection approaches. Rather than preoperatively committing to 1 flap technique, our unified harvest approach enables surgeons to choose 1 of 3 proven techniques—vascularized fibula free flap, vascularized fibular medialization, and distal myocutaneous fibular flap—once intraoperative vascularity, tissue quality, and defect assessment have been made. Harvest approach is performed through a shared lateral compartment exposure alongside dual osseous windows. This approach reframes the reconstructive workflow by preserving flap commitment until intraoperative confirmation has been made. Our innovation lies not in the individual flap techniques but in the integration of all 3 into a unified harvest strategy. This approach seeks to enhance reconstructive flexibility, reducing the need for multiple plans while mitigating intraoperative risk when preoperative assumptions prove inaccurate.

Takeaways

Question: How can surgeons optimize fibular flap harvesting to accommodate varying intraoperative findings without preoperatively committing to a single flap technique?

Findings: This report introduced a unified harvest approach that enables intraoperative selection among 3 well-established fibular flap options: vascularized fibula free flap, fibular medialization, and distal myocutaneous fibula flap.

Meaning: This harvest approach enhances intraoperative adaptability, allowing surgeons to tailor flap selection based on intraoperative findings. The added flexibility may help improve outcomes while reducing risk when preoperative assumptions prove inaccurate.

INTRODUCTION

Reconstruction of extensive lower extremity defects remains a complex challenge in limb salvage interventions. Indications for reconstruction can range from osteosarcoma, chronic osteomyelitis, trauma, congenital deformities, and failed previous reconstruction attempts.1 Vascularized fibular autografts have emerged as a prominent reconstruction choice due to their reliable vascularity, infection resistance, and potential for hypertrophic remodeling.2,3 These flaps include a segment of fibular cortical bone and periosteum sleeve alongside the preserved vascular pedicle. Among vascularized fibular autografts, 3 flap types are well recognized in lower extremity reconstruction: the vascularized fibula free flap (VFFF), vascularized fibular medialization (VFM), and the distal myocutaneous fibula flap (DMFF). Each flap offers distinct advantages and limitations, depending on the defect location, geometry, and patient vascular status. Although practices may vary, each technique is often planned with its own dissection approach and preoperative planning. However, intraoperative realities may vary from preoperative imaging, rendering previous plans impractical or unsafe. In such cases, premature commitment can result in unnecessary dissection, leading to prolonged operative time or selection of a flap that is unviable. To address these limitations, we proposed a unified harvest approach that allows efficient access to 3 fibular flap techniques.

SURGICAL ACCESS TECHNIQUE

A longitudinal incision is made along the lateral aspect of the leg, typically centered over the fibular shaft between the anterior and posterior borders of the fibula. Preoperative planning includes consideration of all viable techniques with a definitive flap selected on intraoperative examination. The deep fascia is incised, and subfascial dissection is carried out to expose the peroneal compartment. Peroneus longus and brevis muscles are identified and are mobilized inferiorly, providing direct access to the floor of the compartment and fibula. At this point, proximal and distal osseous windows are created allowing for elevation of the flap using the most appropriate harvest technique based on the defect. Osteotomies are performed at a slight oblique angle using an oscillating saw with care taken to protect surrounding structures. Typical window length is approximately 1.5 cm proximal and distal to the desired graft length. Window segments may also be used as additional autografts.

FLAP OPTIONS

Vascularized Fibula Free Flap

The VFFF is best suited for large or complex tibial defects, including those with multiplanar geometry, long segmental loss, or cases requiring harvest from the contralateral leg.3,4 This flap is harvested alongside its peroneal pedicle and transferred to the defect site for microsurgical anastomosis (Fig. 1).5 Proximal osseous window allows for easy identification and assessment of the peroneal artery and associated pedicle before flap elevation. Limitations of the VFFF are often associated with the need for microsurgical anastomosis. Anastomosis typically lengthens surgical time compared with flap techniques that preserve native vasculature, whereas anastomotic failure can elevate the risk of complications.4 However, even with these limitations, the VFFF does offer extensive versatility in reconstruction of large or irregular defects (Table 1). This flap has an option to incorporate a myocutaneous island for cases requiring additional soft tissue reconstruction.

Vascularized Fibular Medialization

VFM is generally best suited for linear mid-diaphyseal tibial defects when the ipsilateral fibula and vasculature are uncompromised.6 Graft length typically ranges from 6 to 14 cm. VFM is generally not recommended for proximal or distal third defects due to limited arc of rotation and joint destabilization concerns.6 This technique preserves the native peroneal pedicle and avoids the need for microsurgical anastomosis. VFM involves meticulous dissection of the interosseous membrane to allow the fibula to rotate medially in the tibial defect site where it is secured with internal fixation (Fig. 2).7 VFM may offer reduced operative time compared with the VFFF due to avoiding the need for microsurgical anastomosis (Table 1). Additionally, VFM may limit donor-site morbidity due to the preservation of native vascular supply.6 A strong benefit of this technique is its availability to be performed across many surgical settings. In lower resource surgical centers without microsurgical capacity, this method may hold power across many lower extremity salvage scenarios.

Distal Myocutaneous Fibula Flap

The DMFF is commonly associated with reconstruction of distal tibial or hindfoot defects with related soft tissue loss.8,9 This flap relies on retrograde perfusion through communicating branches between the posterior tibial and peroneal arteries. Meticulous dissection and preservation of these distal anastomotic branches is essential. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound is necessary to confirm adequate retrograde perfusion before transection of the proximal peroneal pedicle to ensure flap viability. The DMFF is harvested alongside the flexor hallucis muscle and skin paddle (Fig. 3).10 The added muscularity contributes to soft tissue bulk replacing dead space. This flap is applicable to a variety of cases involving trauma, osteosarcoma, and osteomyelitis.8,9 Limitations include contraindication in patients with peripheral vascular disease, disrupted posterior circulation, or significant distal vascular trauma as retrograde perfusion may be compromised (Table 1).8

DISCUSSION

The core innovation presented in our work lies in unifying access to 3 different flap techniques through a single lateral compartment exposure. Rather than relying on a preoperative commitment to a single technique, our approach embraces intraoperative adaptability. Considering that intraoperative realities may differ from preoperative imaging and plans, our approach enables surgeons to efficiently select a flap option based on real-time intraoperative findings. By reducing the need for multiple flap dissection approaches, our unified approach limits unnecessary dissection. Minimizing dissection contributes to reducing morbidity, potentially helping limit complication rates. Additionally, our approach broadens reconstructive viability across diverse clinical environments. In settings without microsurgical capacity or reliable imaging, the ability to deter final flap selection until intraoperative assessment offers critical flexibility. This adaptability can help make our approach applicable to both high- and low-resource environments. This report describes our harvest strategy but does not present clinical data. Future clinical studies are needed to formally test outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Our unified harvest approach reframes lower extremity reconstruction from preoperative commitment to intraoperative versatility. This method offers surgeons the choice among 3 well-established flap techniques through a unified harvest approach. The central innovation is not modifying these effective flaps but changing the way they are accessed. As reconstructive cases grow increasingly complex, preserving commitment to flap choice until intraoperative assessment has been made may help improve future patient outcomes.
