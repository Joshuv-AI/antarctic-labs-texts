Nonendoscopic Screening for Barrett’s Esophagus: Current Methods, Evidence, and Future Directions
Authors: Ankoor H. Patel, Michael Ma, Petros C. Benias, Molly Stewart, Arvind J. Trindade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incidence of esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) has steadily risen over the past several decades. Screening for EAC and its metaplastic precursor, Barrett’s esophagus (BE), remains a challenge to prevent EAC development and decrease EAC-attributed mortality. Unfortunately, most BE is diagnosed at the time of diagnosis of EAC in the majority of patients with EAC, showcasing that current screening paradigms are neither widely accepted nor adequate. Historically, screening for BE with upper endoscopy was endorsed by major gastrointestinal society guidelines in the presence of gastroesophageal reflux disease symptoms and related risk factors. However, this method has limitations, including accessibility, cost, and tolerability. Nonendoscopic technologies, such as swallowable cell collection devices, methylated DNA marker assays, esophageal capsule endoscopy, and emerging molecular and physiologic biomarkers, represent newer technologies that may offset many of the limitations of traditional endoscopic screening and facilitate translation to broader settings to capture higher-risk patients. In this review article, we aim to provide a comprehensive, up-to-date summary of nonendoscopic screening tools for BE, contextualizing their advantages, limitations, and prospects for clinical implementation.

Introduction

Barrett’s esophagus (BE), defined by the replacement of normal squamous mucosa in the distal esophagus with columnar-lined epithelium containing intestinal metaplasia, is the only recognized histologic precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC). Currently, EAC is ranked as the seventh most common cancer and the sixth in terms of mortality.1 Over the past several decades, the incidence and prevalence of EAC in Western countries have risen significantly, with 5-year survival rates of 15% to 20% due to the aggressive nature of the malignancy and most diagnoses occurring in late/advanced stages.2,3

Despite this worrisome rising trend in EAC and the well-known premalignant process of BE, only a small minority of patients with EAC have a documented prior BE diagnosis. Notably, in a meta-analysis including over 15,000 EAC cases, fewer than 12% had a prior diagnosis of BE, and just under 30% had concurrent BE at the time of cancer diagnosis, despite histopathologic BE being present in up to 90% of resected tumors,4 underscoring widespread under recognition of this premalignant state. This diagnostic gap is compounded by limitations of current screening and surveillance strategies, which fail to capture many high-risk individuals, including patients who are clinically silent, resulting in most EACs being diagnosed in advanced stages with poor outcomes. In contrast, outcomes improve markedly when BE-related neoplasia or EAC is identified at an early, endoscopically or surgically treatable stage,5 highlighting the significance and urgent need to optimize risk stratification, screening, and surveillance paradigms.

Challenges and Limitations of Traditional Endoscopic Screening

Traditional sedated upper endoscopy remains the gold standard for detecting BE, but its invasiveness, cost, and need for specialized equipment and personnel substantially limit population-level implementation. As a result, only a minority of eligible individuals—often cited as roughly 10% to 30%—undergo screening, even though BE is found in the vast majority of patients with EAC, representing a major missed opportunity for early detection and intervention.6, 7, 8

Screening strategies that hinge on the requirement of chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) symptoms (eg, American College of Gastroenterology [ACG], American Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy [ASGE], and European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy [ESGE]) further narrow the screened population, yet up to 40% of patients with EAC report no antecedent GERD symptoms (despite having physiologic reflux), revealing a fundamental mismatch between current criteria and the true at-risk population9 (Table 1). In a multicenter study from the United States, only 61% of patients with EAC and 38% with adenocarcinoma of the cardia reported heartburn or regurgitation with a duration longer than 5 years before their diagnosis.10 In addition, a study from the United States found that societal guidelines requiring GERD inclusion for eligibility for BE/EAC screening have poor sensitivity (39%–43%) and modest specificity (67%–77%).11 As a result, the 2022 American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Clinical Practice Update removed GERD as a requirement for BE screening, recommending screening patients with 3 risk factors (eg, male, non-Hispanic white, age >50 years, have a history of smoking, chronic GERD, obesity, or a family history of BE or EAC)12 (Table 1). Health system and patient-level barriers further constrain the impact of traditional endoscopic screening programs. Limited patient awareness of BE and EAC risk, especially in underserved communities, combined with insurance gaps, out-of-pocket costs, and long endoscopy wait lists, prevents many eligible individuals from accessing screening in the first place.17 As previously mentioned, adhering to screening guidelines that have removed mandatory GERD-based criteria would dramatically increase the number of screen-eligible adults and strain already limited endoscopic capacity. For example, in countries such as the United Kingdom, there are currently ≥60,000 people awaiting a diagnostic gastroscopy, with 27% waiting for ≥6 weeks, underscoring the impracticality of relying solely on conventional sedated upper endoscopy for population-wide BE detection.18

Even when endoscopic screening is performed, several procedural and technical limitations reduce its effectiveness. Accurate diagnosis of BE requires visualization of columnar-lined epithelium extending at least 1 cm above the gastroesophageal junction with biopsies demonstrating intestinal metaplasia, but the patchy distribution of metaplastic tissue and variable adherence to biopsy protocols create substantial sampling error and false-negative examinations. In addition, variability in endoscopist expertise, inspection time, and adherence to standardized classification systems contributes to missed diagnoses. In a study using 2 large national databases totaling >350,000 patients, rates of BE on repeat esophagogastricduodenoscopy (EGD) ranged from 1.7% to 3.4% and were higher in those with multiple risk factors for BE, suggesting that most lesions were likely missed during initial endoscopy, highlighting the importance of a high-quality initial endoscopic examination.19 Recently, a new term coined, post-endoscopy esophageal cancer, or PEEC, is a quality measure of endoscopic screening/surveillance in which high-grade dysplasia or EAC is detected within a year after a “negative” surveillance exam. A multicenter study reported the pooled proportion of post-endoscopy esophageal cancer to be 26% (95% confidence interval (CI): 19%–34%, I2 = 93.4%),20 further emphasizing inadequate endoscopic evaluation, incomplete EET, and/or rapidly progressive cancer as possible explanations.

BE Screening in the Primary Care Setting

Screening for BE is arguably best positioned in the primary care setting, where most patients with reflux symptoms and BE-specific risk factors are encountered. Primary care–based strategies can address the current reality that only a small proportion of patients who develop EAC are diagnosed through screening, and that roughly 90% of patients who meet current screening criteria are not screened. In a study including 3535 patients deemed high risk for BE by primary care chart review, only 1077 (30%) were screened for BE with endoscopy.8 In another retrospective study, only 39% of the 1127 screening-eligible patients, as determined by primary care chart review, underwent EGD.7 Most endoscopies were ordered for refractory symptoms rather than proactive screening, reflecting a disconnect between guidelines and real-world practice.

For primary care–based screening to capture the largest number of patients, several implementation gaps must be addressed. Gastroenterologists are substantially more likely than primary care physicians to recognize BE risk profiles and recommend guideline-concordant screening, while primary care clinicians report barriers such as limited familiarity with recommendations, time constraints during visits, concerns about insurance coverage, and doubts about cost-effectiveness. In a web-based survey of gastroenterologists and primary care providers (PCPs) at 4 academic medical centers and 2 affiliated county hospitals, approximately half (54.17%) of gastroenterologists order an upper endoscopy for BE screening per month compared to only 17.7% of PCPs reported ordering endoscopy, and 25.6% reported referring patients to gastroenterologists. After being presented with clinical vignettes, GI providers were more likely to recommend guideline-concordant screening than PCPs.21 Educational initiatives, integrated risk-assessment tools in electronic health records (EHRs), and clear referral pathways that include minimally invasive screening options can help close this gap and ensure that high-risk patients identified in the primary care setting are offered appropriate screening and subsequent surveillance programs.

Transnasal Endoscopy

Transnasal endoscopy (TNE) is a minimally invasive technique that uses an ultrathin (<6 mm in diameter) endoscope passed through the nostril under topical anesthesia, eliminating the need for intravenous sedation and recovery monitoring, and can be performed in an office-based setting or mobile vans. There are 2 available TNE devices: EndoSheath and EG-Scan. The EndoSheath device has a working channel for biopsy sampling, whereas the EG-Scan system does not. In multiple studies, unsedated TNE has demonstrated diagnostic performance for BE that is comparable to standard sedated EGD, with reported sensitivities and specificities around 90% for BE and intestinal metaplasia and even up to 98%–100% for endoscopic diagnosis in crossover trials.22 Randomized data also show that TNE can achieve similar BE detection rates to EGD while offering shorter procedure and recovery times and higher patient participation, with many patients expressing a preference for TNE over conventional endoscopy.23 Economic analyses suggest that uTNE, particularly mobile or office-based models, can be cost-effective or even the most cost-effective strategy compared with standard sedated endoscopy because of lower direct and indirect costs.24

Despite these advantages, TNE has not gained widespread traction as a screening tool for BE. TNE still requires specialized equipment, decontamination facilities for reusable devices or ongoing procurement of disposable systems, and trained operators with endoscopic expertise, which makes large-scale, population-based deployment challenging outside of specialized centers, mobile programs, or well-resourced primary care practices. Image quality with some ultrathin scopes can be inferior to that of standard high-definition endoscopes, and certain platforms lack a full working channel, reducing the ability to perform extensive biopsies or detect and sample subtle short-segment BE, which may lower histologic yield for intestinal metaplasia even when endoscopic impressions are accurate. Provider and patient factors have also slowed adoption. Gastroenterologists and primary care clinicians may perceive TNE as technically more demanding, less familiar, or insufficiently superior to standard EGD to justify practice change, especially when established endoscopy workflows already exist. Surveys highlight physician concerns about patient discomfort (nasal pain, gagging), limited availability, uncertain reimbursement, and the need for additional training, while patients may still associate endoscopic procedures—whether transoral or transnasal—with anxiety and invasiveness, blunting the potential participation gains. As a result, although TNE represents a patient-friendly, accurate alternative to conventional EGD, these practical, technical, and perceptual barriers have prevented it from being widely embraced as a primary screening modality for BE.

Benefits of Nonendoscopic Screening Options

Nonendoscopic screening modalities provide a minimally invasive, scalable way to detect BE and BE-related neoplasia, addressing many of the cost and capacity limitations of traditional endoscopic screening. Swallowable cell collection devices (CCDs) such as capsule sponges or balloon-based systems are tethered devices that sample esophageal cells as they are withdrawn and then analyze these specimens for BE-specific biomarkers, including trefoil factor 3 (TFF3) and methylated DNA markers (MDMs), allowing accurate detection of intestinal metaplasia with generally good tolerance and the option for performance by nonphysicians in primary care. Current guidelines from major societies, such as the AGA and ACG, endorse CCDs as acceptable alternatives to endoscopy for BE screening. CCDs may serve as both screening and triage tools because their scalability supports deployment in broad clinical settings, such as primary care, while also enabling identification of patients at increased risk for BE who should be referred for confirmatory EGD. During the COVID-19 era, Cytosponge-based biomarker panels (eg, atypia, p53 overexpression) combined with clinical factors such as age, sex, and segment length were used to triage patients by neoplasia risk,25 illustrating how noninvasive testing can support ongoing surveillance while safely reducing the need for unnecessary endoscopy and thereby freeing up resources for those with high-risk findings that may benefit from endoscopic therapy. Studies have shown nonendoscopic tests have doubled the detection yield of histologically confirmed BE in targeted campaigns compared with routine pathways.18 Noninvasive tools that can be performed in a wide array of settings improve access for underserved groups or under-resourced settings. Economic evaluations consistently show that swallowed CCDs with biomarkers (such as Cytosponge-TFF3 or sponge-on-a-string-MDMs), followed by confirmatory sedated endoscopy only when indicated, are cost-effective or more cost-effective than endoscopy alone, with high probabilities of being the preferred strategy at standard willingness-to-pay thresholds and documented per-patient cost savings in health systems like the National Health Service.26 By reducing the number of endoscopic procedures, these modalities also lessen environmental impact—an emerging priority in “green endoscopy”—while still enabling effective screening and risk-stratified surveillance for BE and EAC.

Nonendoscopic Screening Modalities

Cytosponge With Trefoil Factor 3

Cytosponge (Figure 1)27 is a minimally invasive, “sponge-on-a-string” device developed to sample esophageal cells for BE in the outpatient setting. It consists of a 30 mm polyurethane sponge compressed within a gelatin capsule and attached to a tether; after the capsule is swallowed and reaches the stomach, the shell dissolves and the rough-textured sponge expands, and gentle retraction over 5 to 7 minutes brushes more than a million cells from the esophageal mucosa, which are then fixed and analyzed immunohistochemically for TFF3, a goblet-cell–associated marker of intestinal metaplasia. BEST1 was a proof-of-concept trial conducted in primary care to assess the feasibility of the Cytosponge among patients prescribed acid-suppressant medications for >6 months and showed that it is safe and feasible in primary care.28 The procedure is generally safe and well tolerated, with more than 90% of patients able to swallow the capsule on the first attempt and adverse events largely limited to transient throat discomfort, gagging, or very rare tether detachment that can be managed endoscopically.27,29Figure 1Cytosponge (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) in gelatin capsule (right) and expanded (left).[Reprinted from “Safety and acceptability of a non-endoscopic esophageal sampling device – Cytosponge: a systematic review of multicenter data,” by Januszewicz et al, Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Volume 17 (Issue 4), p. 647–656 (doi: 10.1016/j.cgh.2018.07.043). Multiple case-control studies, systematic reviews, and randomized trials have demonstrated that Cytosponge TFF3 has favorable efficacy for detecting BE, with sensitivities ranging from 73% to 87% and specificities of 77% to 94%, making it suitable as a triage test before confirmatory endoscopy.28, 29, 30, 31, 32 The test's sensitivity is higher in longer-segment BE, as evidenced by the BEST2 trial, which showed an overall BE sensitivity of 80.0%; however, it increased to 87.2% for patients with circumferential BE longer than 3 cm.30 Interestingly, the analysis performed in the BEST2 trial was per-protocol and included cases where the Cytosponge did not reach the stomach, in which no gastric cardia cells were sampled, which is now identified as a quality control measure by which the lack of gastric cardia mucosa indicates an unsuccessful swallow. After excluding cases that did not meet these quality control measures, the sensitivity improved to 94% and the specificity to 92.4.30 While there are concerns regarding the nonspecificity of TFF3 to BE, as it is increased in over 50% of patients with negative Helicobacter pylori and intestinal metaplasia and increased in those with GERD, several studies have evaluated other MDMs that may offer more specific markers of BE, resulting in improved sensitivity and specificity. In a retrospective validation study using Cytosponge specimens collected from patients included in the BEST2 case-control trial, 4 MDMs (TFPI2, TRIST1, ZNF345, and ZNF569) were found to be hypermethylated and could discriminate BE biopsies from squamous esophagus and gastric cardia between patients with BE and reflux controls, with TFPI2 found to have the best accuracy (sensitivity of 82.2%, specificity of 95.7%).33

Several studies have shown the ability for Cytosponge to be associated with significant clinical utility and impact. The BEST3 trial was a multicenter, pragmatic, randomized controlled trial that recruited >13,000 patients from 109 United Kingdom general practice clinics who were randomized to an offer of a Cytosponge vs usual care (acid-suppressant therapy and endoscopy only if deemed necessary by the PCP). Those randomized to the Cytosponge arm had a 10-fold higher number of diagnosed BEs (rate ratio = 10.6; 95% CI: 6.0–18.8; P < .0001) compared with those in the standard of care arm. As a secondary endpoint, the Cytosponge group was also diagnosed with earlier-stage BE-related neoplasia, although the number of cases was small, confirming its utility in the earlier detection of dysplasia and early neoplasia.29

Several studies have shown that Cytosponge-based screening is cost-effective compared with usual care or endoscopy alone.34, 35, 36 In a microsimulation of a 50-year-old male in the United Kingdom with GERD symptoms, Cytosponge screening followed by treatment of dysplasia or intramucosal cancer cost an additional $240 per screening participant compared with no screening, yielding a mean gain of 0.015 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of $15.7K per QALY. By comparison, endoscopy screening followed by treatment and surveillance had an additional mean cost of $299 per participant, a gain of 0.013 QALYs, and an ICER of $22.2K per QALY. Therefore, Cytosponge screening was both less expensive per participant and more cost-effective per QALY gained than endoscopy.36 A systematic review of 13 cost-effectiveness studies showed that nonendoscopic screening is more cost-effective than standard endoscopic screening, with greater cost-effectiveness in populations with higher disease prevalence.26

Cyted Device

EndoSign (Figure 2)18 is another “sponge-on-a-string” device developed by Cyted Health, closely resembling CytoSponge; however, it was developed with a new applicator to streamline community rollout, making it easier to administer and for patients to swallow. After the device is swallowed by the patient, the outer capsule dissolves in the stomach, releasing a small sponge that collects cells from the esophageal lining as the sponge is withdrawn using the attached string. It is then analyzed with a biomarker panel, such as TFF3, p53, and atypia, to detect BE. In a study using the EndoSign device, the device had a positive predictive value of 72.7% (95% CI: 43.5%–91.7%) for the detection of BE.18 Another study identified patients with chronic GERD using electronic medical records from a primary care setting. A total of 1815 patients completed the test, of whom 266 (14.7%) underwent a subsequent endoscopy following a positive test. Tethered capsule/balloon (EsoCheck/EsoGuard)

EsoCheck (EC) (Figure 3)38 and EsoGuard (EG) are manufactured by Lucid Diagnostics for screening for BE. EC is a swallowable, silicon-based balloon catheter that is ingested in a deflated state and then inflated once it reaches the stomach; as it is withdrawn across the distal esophagus, the textured balloon surface captures epithelial cells, after which it is retracted back into its sheath to protect the sample during removal. Collected cells undergo DNA extraction and are then analyzed with EG, a targeted next-generation sequencing assay that quantifies aberrant methylation of vimentin (VIM) and cyclin A1 (CCNA1), generating a binary positive or negative result for BE and EAC based on prespecified methylation thresholds.Figure 3EsoCheck (Lucid Diagnostics, New York, NY).38[Reprinted from "Innovations in Screening Tools for Barrett’s Esophagus and Esophageal Adenocarcinoma,” by Bell et al, Current Gastroenterology Reports, Volume 23, (doi: 10.1007/s11894-021-00821-6). Several studies suggest integrating EC/EG as a triage tool in current screening strategies can prognosticate patients for risk of BE and optimize referrals and resources to improve diagnostic yield. Studies have reported the range of sensitivity and specificity of EC/EG to be ∼85%–100% and 72%–92%, respectively.39, 40, 41, 42 Early pilot studies reported a sensitivity and specificity of 90.3% and 91.7%, respectively.40 In a large, multicenter, prospective study assessing the performance of EC/EG, the sensitivity of EG for BE was 87.5% (95% CI: 47.4–99.7), specificity was 81.2% (95% CI: 71.2–88.8).42 In a multicenter study in which patients with nondysplastic BE, dysplastic BE, EAC, and health controls were prospectively enrolled, the overall sensitivity was 85% (95% CI: 0.78–0.93), and specificity was 85% (95% CI: 0.79–0.90). EC/EG showed 100% detection of EAC cases and a sensitivity of 91% for the long segment BE.39 The sensitivity of EC/EG for the detection of BE compares well with other noninvasive screening tests for cancers, as recommended and used by the United States Preventive Services Task Force.41,43, 44, 45 A prospective study including 124 patients at the Cleveland Veterans Affairs (VA) Medical Center demonstrated EG sensitivity and negative predictive value of 92.9% and 98.6%, respectively. The data suggest that the diagnostic yield of EGD increased 2.5-fold among patients who initially tested positive for EG.41 These data support using this approach to identify and prioritize higher-risk patients while safely reducing unnecessary procedures in lower-risk individuals, thereby maintaining an accurate, well-tolerated screening pathway for BE and early EAC.

EsophaCap

The EsophaCap (Figure 4),46 like the Cytosponge, consists of a sponge inside a dissolvable capsule; however, it is slightly smaller (25 mm). After the capsule reaches the stomach, the outer shell dissolves, allowing the sponge to expand before it is withdrawn, collecting cells as it is withdrawn. Studies utilizing EsophaCap have analyzed the cells obtained with panels of MDMs. Across multiple investigations pairing EsophaCap with 2-, 3-, or 5-marker MDM panels, reported sensitivities for BE detection range from 79% to 93% with specificities of 90% to 94%.46, 47, 48 Initial whole-methylome discovery work identified a broad panel of candidate MDMs, which was subsequently condensed via random forest modeling to a 5-marker panel (including ZNF682, VAV3, NDRG4, FER1L4, and ZNF568) that achieved cross-validated sensitivity and specificity of about 92% and 94%, respectively, for discriminating BE from controls, with similar performance reproduced in independent cohorts.48 Further studies demonstrated that 3-marker subsets centered on ZNF682 could maintain high diagnostic accuracy, suggesting the possibility of streamlined, cost-efficient assays.46 Another study utilized an alternative panel of MDMs consisting of p16, NELL1, TAC1, and AKAP12, which showed the ability to discriminate BE from controls with a sensitivity of 78.6%, a specificity of 92.8%, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.93.49 Several studies have shown excellent tolerability with no major complications and patient preference over more invasive tests such as endoscopy.48 However, the device was recalled by the food and drug administration due to safety concerns related to sponge detachment from the tether during removal and is no longer manufactured.Figure 4EsophaCap (Lucid Diagnostics, New York, NY) in both the intact (A) and the expanded (B) configuration.From “Validation of a methylated DNA marker panel for the nonendoscopic detection of Barrett’s esophagus in a multisite case-control study” by Iyer PG et al, Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, Volume 94 (Issue 3), p. 498–505 (DOI: 10.1016/j.gie.2021.03.937). Exact Science technology (using sponge on string device)

Exact Sciences is developing a nonendoscopic BE screening test that uses a swallowable “sponge-on-a-string” device to collect esophageal cells for analysis of an MDM. Data suggest a refined 3-MDM algorithm (NDRG4, VAV3, and ZNF682) shows excellent sensitivity and specificity in independent validation samples. The 3-MDM panel produced a sensitivity of 100% for high-grade dysplasia and EAC.47 Development is ongoing that will offer patients another minimally invasive screening method for earlier detection of EAC and its precursors.

Esophageal Capsule Endoscopy

Esophageal capsule endoscopy (ECE) (Figure 5)50 is a minimally invasive imaging modality that employs a swallowable capsule that captures high-resolution images of the esophageal mucosa as it passes through the esophageal lumen. The technology is similar to that of the video capsule endoscopy, which is used to image the small bowel. A meta-analysis of 9 studies including 618 patients reported pooled sensitivity and specificity of 78% and 86%, respectively, using EGD as the reference standard.51 Two cost-effectiveness analyses comparing ECE with standard esophagogastroduodenoscopy for screening middle-aged men with gastroesophageal reflux (GER) symptoms found standard esophagogastroduodenoscopy to be more cost-effective, with ICERs remaining below standard willingness-to-pay thresholds.52,53 The limited cost-effectiveness of ECE was attributed to its suboptimal accuracy, likely in part due to rapid esophageal transit for BE detection and the need for confirmatory EGD in many cases. Newer versions of ECE have been developed to improve upon the accuracy by overcoming identified issues. The food and drug administration first approved the PillCam ESO (Medtronic Inc, Minneapolis, MN) in 2004. The second-generation device, the PillCam ESO2, captured images at 18 frames per second with cameras located at both ends of the capsule.50 More recently, the third-generation PillCam UGI, with an expanded 174° angle of view and a 35-fps capture rate, is under investigation; however, pilot studies have suggested it remains inferior to standard endoscopy in BE detection.54 Other measures and technologies have been studied to counteract the rapid capsule transit. One technique for slowing capsule transit is the deployment of the device while the patient is supine. Another innovation involves the use of magnetically controlled or tethered capsule systems. In a study including 16 patients, magnetic-assisted capsule endoscopy facilitated the correct identification of BE in 15, achieving BE detection sensitivity and specificity of 93.6% and 100%, respectively.55 Wireless magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy, which has demonstrated feasibility and tolerability, achieving sensitivities as high as 92% and specificities around 80% in a prospective, multicenter study evaluating for the presence of high-risk esophageal varices.56 Cost analysis studies continue to show ECE lacks cost-effectiveness, limiting its adoption as a viable screening modality for BE.52,57Figure 5PillCam ESO2 (Given Imaging Ltd, Yoqneam, Israel) capsule.50From “Current and Future Use of Esophageal Capsule Endoscopy” by Park et al, Clinical Endoscopy, Volume 51 (Issue 4), p. 317–322 (DOI: https://doi.org/10.5946/ce.2018.101. Beyond ECE, optical coherence tomography-tethered capsule endomicroscopy (OTC-TCE) is an emerging technique that provides high-resolution, 3-dimensional, cross-sectional imaging of the entire esophageal wall using optical-frequency-domain imaging technology. In a proof-of-concept study including 13 subjects (7 normal volunteers and 6 with known BE), there were no complications reported, and 12/13 patients preferred OTC-TCE over EGD.58 In a multicenter study of 147 patients with known BE, OTC-TCE demonstrated a strong correlation with the maximum extent of BE measured by EGD (r = 0.77–0.79, P < .05), with high-quality microscopic images obtained in 93.7% of cases.59 Similarly, TCE has shown promising feasibility and patient tolerability in both specialty and primary care settings, enabling virtual microscopic visualization of the esophageal mucosa without sedation.58, 59, 60 Despite advances, a key challenge remains the correlation of TCE-derived imaging abnormalities with histopathologic confirmation—still the diagnostic gold standard for BE.

Other Emerging Techniques

MicroRNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as promising liquid biopsy tools for nonendoscopic screening of BE. These short (approximately 18–25 nucleotides in length), noncoding RNA molecules regulate gene expression and are released into the circulation during cellular stress, apoptosis, and neoplastic transformation, where they exhibit both tissue and disease specificity. For example, multiple studies have identified BE-associated miRNAs in serum and tissue samples, demonstrating their potential as noninvasive biomarkers.61,62 The EMERALD multicenter study developed and externally validated a 6-miRNA serum panel that distinguished BE/EAC from controls with an area under the receiving operating characteristic (AUROC) of 0.919, sensitivity of 82.5%, and specificity of 90.5%. The panel was also able to discriminate symptomatic GERD from BE with an AUROC of 0.95 and from HGD/EAC with an AUROC of 0.95.63 Few other studies have evaluated miRNAs for BE diagnosis with sensitivities ranging from 78.4% to 100% and specificities of 85.7% to 95%.61,64

An analysis of samples derived from patients with disease (BE or esophagitis) or normal tissue has shown consistent dysregulation of specific miRNAs (including higher levels of miR-143, miR-145, miR-194, miR-215, miR-192-5p, and miR-130a) in BE tissue and blood as opposed to other miRNAs (such as miR-203 and miR-205), which were found to be downregulated in BE tissue samples.65 Serum miR-130a was also shown to be elevated in BE and EAC patients.66 Other miRNAs, such as miR-320e and miR-199a-3p, were found to be significantly lower in BE compared to patients with normal epithelium,67 supporting this technology to provide profiles that can accurately discriminate between disease and normal tissue in patients with BE. Interestingly, investigators have recently shown miR-4485-5p as a novel biomarker of esophageal dysplasia.68 Nonetheless, large-scale prospective studies are still required to confirm performance, standardize assays, and define how miRNA panels might be integrated into population-level screening pathways.

Volatile Organic Compounds

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), exhaled gaseous byproducts of cellular metabolism and microbiome activity, represent another emerging noninvasive technology for BE screening. Two techniques have been used for VOC analysis in patients with BE or esophageal cancer: gas chromatography-mass spectrometry and an electronic nose (e-nose) apparatus.69 A study using the gas chromatography method was used to analyze exhaled breath samples from 81 patients (including 48 esophageal cancer patients) and 129 controls (including 16 patients with BE) showed the ability to discriminate esophageal cancer from controls (AUC = 0.97); however, it was not able to identify patients with BE.70 The e-nose apparatus, such as the Aeonose (eNose company, Zutphen, the Netherlands), uses a metal-oxide sensor which interacts with VOCs to generate a digital signal, which is analyzed across an artificial neural network to detect breath print differences, allowing for discrimination of patients with BE, GERD, and controls. In proof-of-concept studies of 122 and 402 patients, e-nose breath testing identified BE with sensitivities of 82% to 91%, specificities of 74% to 80%, and AUCs of approximately 0.79 to 0.91; performance improved further for long-segment BE and appeared largely independent of proton pump inhibitor use, reflux symptoms, or hiatal hernia.71,72 Advantages of the e-nose apparatus over gas chromatography-mass spectrometry include cost and portability. Cost-effectiveness modeling suggests that VOC-based strategies (eVOC) may reduce EAC incidence and mortality, but at higher incremental cost-effectiveness ratios than some alternative nonendoscopic tests,26,73 and implementation barriers remain, including device cost, need for external validation, and uncertainty regarding confounders such as diet, medications, and environmental exposures. Although VOC breath analysis exemplifies a promising, minimally invasive approach that could expand BE screening beyond endoscopy, additional studies are required to confirm diagnostic performance, reproducibility, and feasibility across different settings (eg, outpatient, primary care).

Oral microbiome

The microbiome is increasingly recognized as a potential biomarker for BE. Sampling the esophageal microbiome can be challenging, as the bacterial density within the esophagus is low, and there is also the risk of contamination from the oral microbiome. However, studies have suggested that sampling the oral microbiome may provide an accurate profile of the more distant esophageal microbiome. A randomized controlled trial consisting of 20 patients who were divided among the chlorhexidine mouth rinse arm or no treatment arm for 2 weeks prior to upper endoscopy demonstrated that altering the oral microbiome induced similar changes to the esophageal microbiome within individuals, supporting the concept that the 2 environments are strongly linked.74

Previous studies have noted changes associated with the development of BE and neoplasia in the esophageal microbiome, highlighting its utility as a diagnostic or screening tool.75, 76, 77 In a nested case-control study (81 EAC; 160 matched controls) analyzing the microbiome composition from oral rinse samples collected from patients enrolled in 2 cancer screening cohort studies (Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian Cancer Screening Trial cohort by the National Cancer Institute and the Cancer Prevention Study II Nutrition cohort by the American Cancer Society) found a higher relative abundance of a periodontal pathogen, Tannerella forsythia, to be associated with an increased risk of developing EAC.78 Several studies have reported differences in the global composition of the oral microbiome between patients with and without BE. In a case-control study of patients with and without BE, patients with BE had a distinct salivary microbiome compared with controls, characterized by increased Firmicutes and decreased Proteobacteria, along with shifts in specific genera, including increased Streptococcus and decreased Lautropia. Using 16S rRNA sequencing and quantitative PCR, one cross-sectional study of 49 patients with and without BE revealed a 3-taxon salivary signature (including Lautropia, Streptococcus, and a Bacteroidales genus) that distinguished BE from non-BE controls with high accuracy (AUROC 0.94).76 Different microbial profiles were noted in patients with HGD and EAC, such as decreased Veillonella and increased Enterobacteriaceae compared to non-dysplastic BE patients.76 Similarly, a cross-sectional study of 244 patients with and without a history of BE revealed that patients with HGD and early EAC had the most distinct shifts in salivary microbiome, independent of tooth loss, which strongly influences oral microbiome composition.79 The 4 bacterial taxa with the greatest increase in relative abundance in patients with HGD or EAC and associated with dysplastic transformation were all in the genus Streptococcus. A machine learning model developed using data derived from the salivary microbiome was able to discriminate HGD/early EAC with an AUROC of 0.84, which increased to 0.88 when combined with established EAC risk factors.79 Further studies are required to determine the role and utility of the salivary microbiome in screening populations across diverse geographic regions, ensuring practicality and generalizability.

Machine Learning, Digital Health Tools, and Artificial Intelligence

Machine learning and other artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled digital tools are being developed to address key shortcomings in risk-based screening strategies for BE. Traditional clinical risk scores that weigh factors such as age, sex, obesity, and reflux symptoms equally have only modest discriminatory ability, with reported AUCs in the 0.65 to 0.80 range and limited clinical implementation.80 AI and machine learning models leverage large-scale EHR data sets to integrate both conventional and novel predictors and have demonstrated substantially higher performance compared to traditional screening measures. In a recent EHR-based transformer model including approximately 6 million patients (including 8476 patients with BE and 250,000 propensity-matched controls), the BE risk model achieved an AUC of 0.84 with 76% sensitivity and 76% specificity, while the EAC model reached an AUC of 0.84 with 84% sensitivity and 70% specificity.81 These models incorporated readily available variables, such as demographics, reflux history, body mass index, comorbidities, medications, and laboratory values, and are designed to be embedded in EHRs as automated “trigger tools” that flag high-risk patients and prompt clinicians to consider BE screening.

AI systems are being studied and applied to improve the quality and yield of endoscopic screening, surveillance of BE, and identification of areas suspicious for dysplastic changes. Deep learning models trained on endoscopic images, including narrow-band imaging and other advanced modalities, have improved the accuracy and sensitivity of BE detection. In a study that utilized narrow-band imaging to train an AI prediction system, the model predicted histologic BE with an accuracy of 94.4%, sensitivity of 94.3%, and specificity of 94.4%.82 Another study using AI- and computer-aided systems to identify areas for dysplasia showed a sensitivity of 90% and, specificity of 88%, and accuracy of 89% in detecting nondysplastic (NDBE) and BE-related dysplasia, highlighting the ability for this technology to detect mucosa changes and guide targeted biopsies, thereby reducing sampling error and intraobserver variability.83 Together, the implementation of AI and machine-learning models to improve EHR-based risk prediction tools and endoscopic image analysis illustrates how these technologies can evolve and advance strategies for patient selection for screening, detection of dysplasia, and surveillance using both endoscopic and non-endoscopic tools.

Clinical Implementation

A future BE screening paradigm could be centered in the primary care clinic, using integrated risk tools within the electronic medical record to automatically identify and triage at-risk patients. At each visit, demographic and clinical variables (age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, GERD symptoms, family history of BE/EAC, and other comorbidities) would feed into a validated risk calculator or AI model that assigns patients to lower or higher risk categories and triggers point-of-care reminders for clinicians. Lower-risk patients would be offered non-endoscopic screening—such as swallowable CCDs or other minimally invasive tests—with clear pathways to schedule these in primary care or community settings. High-risk patients, particularly those with multiple risk factors, concerning symptoms, or strong family history, would be referred directly for upper endoscopy. Patients who meet a threshold number of risk factors and are scheduled for a colonoscopy can consider endoscopic screening at the time of colonoscopy. Results from non-endoscopic tests would be routed back through the electronic medical record, automatically generating endoscopy referrals for positive or indeterminate findings and reassuring messaging plus future rescreening reminders for negative tests, creating a closed-loop, risk-stratified system that efficiently channels endoscopic resources to those most likely to benefit. Conclusion

Although current endoscopic screening programs are equipped to identify BE and advancements in therapy can prevent the progression of BE to EAC, their overall effectiveness in preventing EAC and improving EAC-related mortality remains unclear. Nonendoscopic screening tools have the potential to revolutionize the screening paradigm for BE and EAC by expanding evaluation beyond the constraints of GERD-based risk criteria and traditional sedated upper endoscopy. Innovative technologies, such as swallowable CCDs combined with MDM assays, ECE, and emerging molecular and physiologic biomarkers such as miRNAs, VOCs, and the oral microbiome, collectively demonstrate that accurate detection of BE and BE-related neoplasia is achievable using minimally invasive approaches that are feasible in primary care and other community settings. When paired with AI and machine learning models to develop risk prediction models that can be integrated within an EHR, these tools offer a foundation for risk-stratified, tiered screening pathways in which simple, low-cost tests are used to identify high-risk individuals for confirmatory endoscopy and endoscopic eradication therapy, while safely deferring invasive procedures for those at low risk. However, many of the nonendoscopic screening modalities reviewed still need large-scale, prospective validation and cost-effectiveness analyses across diverse populations, settings, and systems. Practical barriers—including patient and provider acceptance, device availability, training requirements, regulatory approvals, and reimbursement structures—must be addressed to exacerbate existing disparities in BE screening. Future research should prioritize the standardization and implementation of risk-based screening programs utilizing a tiered approach using nonendoscopic modalities. This strategy aims to identify individuals who require more evaluation by more invasive methods, ultimately facilitating and expediting diagnosis and treatment to prevent disease progression.
