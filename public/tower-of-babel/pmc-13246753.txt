MARCH2 prevents doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy by stabilizing NR1H2 and promoting clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes
Authors: Shuolin Liu, Yiran E. Li, TingFang Zhu, LaiHai Zhang, Cong Yang, YaGuang Bi, Chenglong Bao, Ronggui Hu, Junbo Ge, Yingmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy (DiCM) involves impaired clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes (efferocytosis) by cardiac macrophages. This study reveals a central role for the MARCH2-NR1H2 axis in this process. We find that MARCH2 expression is significantly reduced in cardiac macrophages from DiCM mice and human dilated cardiomyopathy patients. Genetic ablation of MARCH2, either globally (MARCH2−/−) or specifically in resident cardiac macrophages (MARCH2f/f; CX3CR1Cre), exacerbates DiCM, impairs efferocytosis, and increases inflammation. Mechanistically, MARCH2 enhances the protein stability of the nuclear receptor NR1H2 via K27-linked polyubiquitination, leading to upregulation of the efferocytosis receptor MERTK. Conversely, macrophage-specific NR1H2 deficiency (NR1H2f/f; CX3CR1Cre) suppresses efferocytosis and worsens cardiac dysfunction. Importantly, pharmacological activation of NR1H2 attenuates DiCM progression. These findings identify the MARCH2-NR1H2 axis as a key regulator of macrophage efferocytosis and a potential therapeutic target for DiCM.

Introduction

Doxorubicin (DOX) is a widely used chemotherapeutic drug effective against various cancers1. However, its clinical application is limited by dose-dependent cardiotoxicity2. Doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy (DiCM)–manifested by dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) and heart failure–represents a severe complication of DOX therapy3–5. Although accumulating evidence has identified cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis as central mechanisms driving DiCM1,6, effective strategies to halt its progression remain elusive1.

In patients with DiCM, excessive cardiomyocyte apoptosis and necroptosis trigger inflammatory cascades, further damaging healthy cardiomyocytes and promoting fibrosis, and ultimately impairing cardiac function. This deleterious interplay between apoptosis and inflammation creates a vicious cycle that accelerates left ventricular dilation and dysfunction.

Efferocytosis, the process by which macrophages engulf apoptotic cells, critically prevents the release of cytotoxic intracellular contents7. As an essential innate immunity mechanism, it facilitates cardiac injury repair8,9. Impaired efferocytosis causes apoptotic cell accumulation, sustained inflammation, and subsequent cardiac dysfunction9, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic strategies targeting this process.

Membrane-associated RING finger protein 2 (MARCH2), a RING finger -type E3 ubiquitin ligase10, has been implicated in the regulation of innate immunity and inflammatory responses11,12. Beyond immune modulation, MARCH2 also governs endocytosis, vesicle trafficking, membrane protein localization, and protein quality control via ubiquitination13,14. However, its role in macrophage efferocytosis and DiCM is unexplored. In particular, the involvement of MARCH2-mediated K27-linked polyubiquitination in efferocytosis and heart failure pathogenesis remains to be elucidated.

Here, we identified nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group H member 2 (NR1H2), also known as liver X receptor beta (LXRβ), as a direct interacting partner of MARCH2. NR1H2 is a nuclear receptor that regulates lipid and cholesterol metabolism, glucose homeostasis, and inflammation15–17. Previous studies have indicated that NR1H2 participates in the clearance of apoptotic cells and promotes immune tolerance18,19. Specifically, internalization of apoptotic cells activates NR1H2, which induces the expression of Tyrosine-protein kinase Mer (MERTK), a key efferocytosis receptor. Notably, macrophages deficient in NR1H2 exhibit defective efferocytosis and sustained proinflammatory responses18. However, regulatory mechanisms controlling NR1H2 levels in heart failure remain undefined.

Here, we demonstrate that MARCH2 in cardiac-resident macrophages protects against DiCM by stabilizing NR1H2 through K27-linked polyubiquitination, thereby enhancing apoptotic cardiomyocyte clearance. NR1H2 transcriptionally induces MERTK expression and mitigates DiCM progression. Moreover, pharmacological activation of NR1H2 alleviates DiCM. These findings highlight the MARCH2/NR1H2 axis as a therapeutic target for the treatment of DiCM.

Results

Characterization of MARCH2 expression in dilated cardiomyopathy and doxorubicin-induced cardiomyopathy

To identify genes implicated in DiCM, RNA sequencing was performed using heart tissues from saline- and DOX-treated wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 1A). Gene ontology analysis revealed enrichment of phagocytosis-related genes in hearts of DOX-treated mice (Fig. 1B). Efferocytosis (a specialized form of phagocytosis targeting apoptotic cells) dysfunction is a key contributor to persistent cardiac inflammation, progressive structural deterioration, and cardiac fibrosis20,21. Screening of differentially expressed genes associated with efferocytosis identified 25 upregulated and 20 downregulated genes. Among them, MARCH2 (a downregulated gene) was prioritized due to its established role in endocytosis and phagocytosis13,14,22 (Fig. 1C). Western blotting analysis demonstrated significantly reduced MARCH2 protein levels in cardiac macrophages from heart tissues of DCM patients compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1D). Immunohistochemical staining validated predominant MARCH2 reduction in cardiac macrophages of DCM patients (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Table Ⅰ). MARCH2 expression was further assessed in mice subjected to DOX challenge. Consistent with human data, cardiac MARCH2 mRNA and protein levels were significantly decreased in the DiCM mouse model (Fig. 1F, G). A positive correlation was noted between MARCH2 expression levels and efferocytotic macrophage counts in mouse hearts (Fig. 1H). Critically, a reduction of MARCH2 expression occurred predominantly in macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S1C, Fig. 1I, J), with no significant changes in endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S1D), fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S1E), T cells (Supplementary Fig. S1F), neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. S1G), or cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig. S1H) isolated from hearts of DOX-treated mice.

MARCH2 deficiency exacerbates DiCM

To determine the role of MARCH2 in DiCM in vivo, global MARCH2 KO mice were generated (Supplementary Fig. S2A). DOX challenge significantly lowered heart weight in WT mice, with a more pronounced decline in MARCH2 KO mice (Fig. 2A). Serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin- 1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were notably increased after DOX treatment, a response that was markedly exacerbated by MARCH2 KO (Fig. 2B–D). Echocardiography was performed to assess cardiac functional changes (Fig. 2E). DOX-treated WT mice exhibited a reduction of left ventricular ejection fraction (EF, Fig. 2F) and fractional shortening (FS, Fig. 2G), an increase of left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD, Supplementary Fig. S2B), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD, Supplementary Fig. S2C), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV, Supplementary Fig. S2D), and left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV, Supplementary Fig. S2E). These impairments were overtly exacerbated in MARCH2 KO mice, with a subtle effect on heart rate (Supplementary Fig. S2F). Plasma creatine phosphokinase-MB (CK-MB, Fig. 2H) and cardiac troponin T (cTnT, Fig. 2I) levels were elevated in DOX-treated WT mice and further increased in MARCH2 KO mice. Furthermore, cardiac atrophy and interstitial fibrosis were evaluated by H&E staining, wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining, and Masson trichrome staining. DOX-treated WT mice exhibited pronounced reductions in heart size (Fig. 2J), cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area (Fig. 2K, L), and prominent interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 2M, N), while these effects were overtly accentuated in DOX-treated MARCH2 KO mice. A reminiscent effect was observed in DOX-treated female WT and MARCH2 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. S3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that MARCH2 KO led to downregulation of phagocytosis (recognition, engulfment) and phagocytic vesicle processes (Fig. 3A). Next, we verified the possible effect of MARCH2 KO on the clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes. MARCH2-deficient mice exhibited elevated levels of pro-apoptotic molecules, BAX and cleaved caspase-3, alongside decreased levels of Bcl-2 following DOX challenge compared with WT mice (Fig. 3C–F). The uncleared terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP-biotin nick-end labeling (TUNEL)-positive cardiomyocytes were also significantly higher in MARCH2 KO mice following DOX treatment compared with WT mice (Fig. 3G, H). However, apoptosis levels in endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. S4B, C), leukocytes (Supplementary Fig. S4D, E), and fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S4F, G), assessed by TUNEL staining and protein level of cleaved caspase-3, were comparable between MARCH2−/− and WT mice in the realm of DOX challenge. To verify the effect of MARCH2 knockout on cardiac macrophage efferocytosis in vivo, we lineage-traced cardiomyocytes using tdTomato (Rosa-LSL-tdTomato; Myh6-cre mice). The colocalization of tdTomato+ signal and CD68+ macrophages was examined, and the percentage of efferocytotic macrophages was calculated in the heart tissues from WT; Rosa-LSL-tdTomato; Myh6-cre mice and MARCH2−/−; Rosa-LSL-tdTomato; Myh6-cre mice (Supplementary Fig. S5) after doxorubicin treatment. As expected, MARCH2 deficiency led to a notable decrease in colocalization of efferocytosis signal (CD68+ macrophages containing tdTomato+ cardiomyocyte debris, Fig. 3I). Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis showed that CX3CR1+CCR2− macrophages engulfed fewer tdTomato+ signals in the hearts of MARCH2−/−; Rosa-LSL-tdTomato; Myh6-cre mice compared to those in WT; Rosa-LSL-tdTomato; Myh6-cre mice after DOX treatment (Fig. 3J). In contrast, CX3CR1−CCR2− macrophages or CX3CR1−CCR2+ macrophages in WT and MARCH2−/− groups engulfed comparable tdTomato+ signals (Fig. 3K).

MARCH2 knockout potentiates the inflammatory response following DOX treatment

Inflammation plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of DiCM, while macrophage efferocytosis is essential for the inflammation resolution following cardiac injury23,24. Therefore, we assessed inflammatory cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokine expression. Firstly, flow cytometry revealed little difference in cardiac macrophages between WT and MARCH2−/− mice. Next, MARCH2 deficiency markedly enhanced cardiac infiltration of Ly6G+ neutrophils and Ly6C+ monocytes following DOX insult (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Additionally, MARCH2−/− mice exhibited increased recruitment of MHC-IIhighCCR2+ macrophages and MHC-IIlowCCR2+ monocytes compared with WT mice after DOX treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6B). However, MARCH2 knockout did not significantly affect macrophage proliferation, as assessed using Ki67+ cells (Supplementary Fig. S6C, D). Gene expression analyses of proinflammatory mediators, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (Supplementary Fig. S6E–H), demonstrated compromised inflammation resolution in MARCH2−/− mice compared to WT mice following DOX challenge. These results indicate that MARCH2 deficiency impaired inflammation resolution in the DiCM model.

MARCH2 directly interacts with NR1H2

Next, the yeast two-hybrid system was used to identify MARCH2-interacting proteins, and 22 proteins were identified (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table Ⅱ). Among these proteins, NR1H2 and ARHGAP44 were closely associated with endocytosis and co-immunoprecipitated with MARCH2 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 4B). However, ARHGAP44 was minimally expressed in macrophages (Supplementary Fig. S7). Thus, we further examined the interaction between MARCH2 and NR1H2 in cardiac macrophages transfected with HA-NR1H2 or an empty vector together with Flag-MARCH2. Flag-MARCH2 was detected in the HA-NR1H2 immunoprecipitants (Fig. 4C). Additionally, MARCH2 co-immunoprecipitated endogenous NR1H2 but not control IgG in cardiac macrophages (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, purified glutathione S-transferase (GST)-MARCH2 could pull-down His-NR1H2 (Fig. 4E), suggesting their direct interaction. To map the precise molecular domains mediating MARCH2–NR1H2 interaction, truncated MARCH2 mutants were generated for the GST-pull-down assay. Deletion of amino acids 56–116 or 117–214 abolished MARCH2 binding to NR1H2, indicating essential roles for the RING finger and TM domains of MARCH2 in the observed interaction (Fig. 4F). Corroborating these findings, immunofluorescence microscopy revealed colocalization of Flag-MARCH2 and HA-NR1H2 in cardiac macrophages (Fig. 4G). MARCH2 E3 ligase increases NR1H2 protein stability through K27-linked polyubiquitination

Next, we investigated whether MARCH2 catalyzes the ubiquitination of NR1H2. Western blot analysis revealed that NR1H2 protein levels were significantly increased in cardiac macrophages overexpressing MARCH2 (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. S8A, B), whereas they were reduced following MARCH2 knockdown under DOX challenge (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S8C). MARCH2 overexpression or knockdown did not alter NR1H2 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. S8D, E). As expected, MG132 treatment restored NR1H2 protein levels after MARCH2 knockdown under DOX treatment (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, macrophages were incubated with cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit protein synthesis, and NR1H2 protein levels and stability were assessed using Western blotting. MARCH2 knockdown significantly shortened the half-life of NR1H2 compared to controls under DOX treatment (Fig. 5D, E). Thus, MARCH2 stabilizes NR1H2 protein via the proteasome pathway in an E3 ligase activity-dependent manner. Ubiquitination assays confirmed that MARCH2 facilitated NR1H2 ubiquitination, whereas its catalytically inactive mutant (C64/67S) failed to do so under DOX conditions (Fig. 5F). Further screening with ubiquitin chain-specific mutants revealed that MARCH2 selectively conjugated WT-Ub and K27-Ub, but not K6, K11, K29, K33, K48, or K63 chains, to NR1H2 under DOX treatment (Fig. 5G). Moreover, MARCH2 failed to facilitate ubiquitination when using K27R ubiquitin, underscoring its preference for K27-linked ubiquitination chains on NR1H2 (Fig. 5H). Ubiquitin mass spectrometry (Supplementary Table Ⅲ) identified lysine 163 (K163) of NR1H2 as a putative ubiquitination site. Mutation of K163 to arginine (K163R) abolished MARCH2-mediated ubiquitination, confirming K163 as the specific site for MARCH2-catalyzed ubiquitination under DOX treatment (Fig. 5I). Consistently, MARCH2-mediated stabilization and prolonged half-life of NR1H2 were abolished by the K163R mutation (Fig. 5J, K). NR1H2 promotes macrophage efferocytosis and inhibits inflammation by inducing MERTK expression, a receptor tyrosine kinase essential for apoptotic cells recognition18. However, whether NR1H2 transcriptionally regulates MERTK remains unknown. We observed that NR1H2 overexpression enhanced MERTK-luciferase reporter activity in DOX-treated macrophages (Fig. 6G). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (CHIP) confirmed direct binding of NR1H2 to the MERTK promoter in cardiac macrophages (Fig. 6H). Consistently, RT-qPCR revealed upregulated MERTK mRNA in NR1H2-overexpressing macrophages (Fig. 6I). When co-cultured with pHrodo™ Red-labeled cardiomyocyte debris, NR1H2-overexpressing macrophages showed enhanced efferocytosis (measured by pHrodo™ Red+ macrophages via confocal microscopy), which was abolished by MERTK knockdown (Supplementary Fig. S9A). We next investigated whether MARCH2 regulates efferocytosis via the NR1H2-MERTK axis. MARCH2 increased MERTK-luciferase activity dose-dependently in DOX-treated macrophages (Fig. 6J). Critically, MARCH2-mediated enhancement of MERTK activity and mRNA was blocked by NR1H2-siRNA (Fig. 6K, L). MARCH2 overexpression augmented efferocytosis, which was reversed by NR1H2 or MERTK knockdown (Fig. 6M, Supplementary Fig. S9B). Flow cytometry confirmed that MARCH2 enhances cardiac macrophage efferocytosis of cardiomyocyte debris following DOX treatment, an effect abolished by NR1H2/MERTK knockdown (Fig. 6N, O). Conversely, MARCH2 knockdown impaired efferocytosis, which reversed by NR1H2 agonist RGX-104 or the MerTK ligand GAS6 (Supplementary Fig. S9C). Besides, MARCH2 deficiency significantly reduced MERTK protein without affecting other macrophage efferocytotic receptors (CD36, LRP1, BAI1, TIM4; Supplementary Fig. S10). Thus, the NR1H2/MERTK axis mediates MARCH2-regulated efferocytosis in cardiac macrophages.

The protective effect of MARCH2 on DiCM is not mediated by bone marrow-derived macrophages

To determine the specific macrophage subset involved in MARCH2-mediated protection against DiCM, chimeric mice were generated via bone marrow transplantation (BMT). Both wild-type (WT) and MARCH2 knockout (KO) recipient mice received bone marrow (BM) from either wild-type (wt) or MARCH2 knockout (ko) donor mice. Subsequently, chimeric mice were treated with DOX one week post-BMT (Supplementary Fig. S11A). Parameters related to cardiac injury and remodeling were analyzed, including cardiac function (Supplementary Fig. S11B, C), cardiac interstitial fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. S11D, E), cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area (Supplementary Fig. S11F, G), heart weight-to-tibia length ratio (Supplementary Fig. S11H), serum inflammatory cytokine levels (Supplementary Fig. S11I, J), and cardiac inflammatory gene expression (Supplementary Fig. S11K–M). Results revealed no significant differences in these parameters between WT/wt vs. WT/ko or KO/wt vs. KO/ko groups. However, cardiac injury and remodeling were more severe in KO/wt and KO/ko mice compared to that in WT/wt and WT/ko mice. Collectively, these findings indicate that MARCH2 in cardiac-resident macrophages, not BM-derived macrophages, mediates substantial protection against DiCM.

CX3CR1+ macrophage transfer rescues DiCM

Besides, we assessed whether the protective effect of MARCH2 in DiCM is mediated by cardiac-resident CX3CR1+ macrophages. We isolated CX3CR1⁺ and CX3CR1⁻ cardiac macrophage subsets from WT mice and transferred them via intramyocardial injection into MARCH2 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. S15A), followed by confirmation of their phenotype and function (Supplementary Fig. S15B, C). Flow cytometry and Immunofluorescence verified macrophage engraftment based on the observed Calcein-AM+CX3CR1+ or Calcein-AM+CX3CR1− macrophage population in the heart (Supplementary Fig. S15D–F).

Notably, injection of CX3CR1+ macrophages increased heart weight (Supplementary Fig. S16A), reduced serum inflammatory cytokines (Supplementary Fig. S16B), improved cardiac function parameters (Supplementary Figs. S16C–H), suppressed cardiac atrophy (Supplementary Fig. S16I) and attenuated interstitial fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. S16J) compared with CX3CR1− macrophage injection in MARCH2 KO mice post-DOX treatment, as demonstrated by Masson trichrome staining, H&E, and WGA staining.

Cardiac macrophage-specific MARCH2 overexpression mitigates DiCM

To investigate the in vivo protective effect of MARCH2 in DiCM, we overexpressed MARCH2 using an ADV-CD68-MARCH2 adenoviral vector. Notably, ADV-CD68-MARCH2 administration significantly upregulated MARCH2 expression in cardiac macrophages compared to the control ADV-CD68-Vector (Supplementary Fig. S17A). Following DOX treatment, cardiac macrophages from ADV-CD68-MARCH2-injected mice exhibited increased K27-linked ubiquitination of NR1H2 (Supplementary Fig. S17B) and enhanced protein levels of NR1H2 and MERTK (Supplementary Figs. ADV-CD68-MARCH2-injected mice showed significantly increased mcherry+ macrophages post-DOX treatment (Supplementary Fig. S17F, G), and CX3CR1+CCR2− cardiac macrophages in these mice internalized more mcherry-labeled cardiomyocyte-derived debris (Fig. S17H, I). TUNEL staining revealed that ADV-CD68-MARCH2 injection promoted clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes in DiCM (Supplementary Fig. S17J, K). Remarkably, ADV-CD68-MARCH2-injected mice displayed improved DiCM pathology compared with ADV-CD68-Vector-injected mice, including enhanced cardiac function (Supplementary Fig. S17M, N), ameliorated interstitial fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. S17O, P), and suppressed cardiac atrophy (Supplementary Fig. S17Q–S), as validated by Masson trichrome staining, H&E staining, and WGA staining.

Pharmacological activation of NR1H2 ameliorates DiCM

Next, we examined whether pharmacological activation of NR1H2 could alleviate DiCM. Oral administration of the NR1H2 agonist RGX-104 (100 mg/kg) significantly improved heart weight (Supplementary Fig. S20A) and reduced levels of proinflammatory cytokines (Supplementary Fig. S20B) in WT mice following DOX treatment. RGX-104 treatment preserved left ventricular EF and FS (Fig. 8J, K), attenuated interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 8L, M), and suppressed cardiac atrophy (Fig. 8N–P) in the DiCM mouse model. Collectively, these results indicate that RGX-104 is a promising therapeutic candidate for the prevention and treatment of DiCM.

Discussion

Efficient clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes by macrophage efferocytosis is essential for cardiac repair and resolution of inflammation. However, the role of efferocytosis in the pathogenesis of DiCM has remained largely unexplored. In this study, we demonstrate that MARCH2-dependent efferocytosis by cardiac-resident macrophages mitigates adverse cardiac remodeling and inflammation in DiCM. Conversely, MARCH2 deficiency compromises the efferocytotic capacity of cardiac macrophages, leading to impaired clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes, heightened inflammatory responses, and exacerbated myocardial injury and remodeling in DiCM. Mechanically, MARCH2 promotes NR1H2 protein stability through K27-linked polyubiquitination, thereby enhancing efferocytosis and suppressing inflammation via the NR1H2/MERTK signaling axis (Fig. 8Q). These findings underscore the critical role of the MARCH2–NR1H2–MERTK pathway in regulating macrophage efferocytosis and highlight its importance in modulating the progression of DiCM.

DiCM is characterized by progressive cardiomyocyte apoptosis, followed by inflammation and cardiac fibrosis. If apoptotic cardiomyocytes are not efficiently cleared by macrophage-mediated efferocytosis, they can initiate adverse cardiac remodeling and heart failure25. Efferocytosis is a tightly regulated process in which phosphatidylserine, exposed on the surface of apoptotic cells, acts as an “eat me” signal, recognized by specific macrophage receptors, such as MERTK, TIM4, and others26. Upon recognition, macrophages undergo cytoskeletal rearrangement to engulf the apoptotic cells, followed by lysosomal degradation26,27. Efficient efferocytosis prevents secondary necrosis and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which would otherwise trigger inflammatory cascades28, while simultaneously promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines and facilitating tissue repair26,29,30.

MARCH2 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase known to regulate endocytosis and endosomal trafficking31. In this study, we identified a critical role of MARCH2 in macrophage-mediated endocytosis. Specifically, MARCH2 deficiency impaired the clearance of apoptotic cardiomyocytes and aggravated cardiac injury in DiCM mice, whereas cardiac macrophage-specific MARCH2 overexpression enhanced efferocytosis and alleviated myocardial damage.

Heterogeneous macrophage populations are involved in the pathogenesis of DiCM32. Pro-inflammatory macrophages are increased early on following DOX insult, followed by a subsequent decline with the onset of dilated heart failure, while reparative macrophages expand during chronic phases32. Unlike infiltrating monocytes, cardiac-resident macrophages are a distinct immune cell population that resides within myocardial tissue33. They interact closely with cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, and play essential roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis, immune modulation, and post-injury repair34. As expected, DOX treatment led to marked damage of cardiac-resident macrophages, while microenvironmental cues stimulated their compensatory proliferation. Despite this, the specific role of cardiac-resident macrophages in DiCM remained largely unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that efferocytosis by cardiac-resident macrophages significantly alleviates DOX-induced cardiac injury. Furthermore, we found that MARCH2 expression was predominantly downregulated in MHC-IIlowCCR2− resident macrophages.

Using BMT and cardiac-resident CX3CR1+ cell transplantation models, we demonstrated that MARCH2 expression in cardiac-resident macrophages–rather than in BM-derived macrophages–plays a pivotal role in modulating DiCM progression. Specific knockout MARCH2 in resident cardiac macrophages using MARCH2f/f; CX3CR1Cre mice impaired macrophage efferocytosis and aggravated DiCM, as evidenced by enhanced infiltration of inflammatory cells and elevated expression of proinflammatory cytokines in cardiac tissues, thereby confirming the cardioprotective role of MARCH2. However, it is important to note that the CX3CR1Cre model may introduce systemic effects due to its activity in bone marrow progenitors, which could potentially obscure the specific role of resident macrophages35. Future studies employing alternative lineage-tracing models will be necessary to more precisely distinguish the contributions of progenitor-derived versus tissue-resident macrophages.

Although macrophages engulf debris from leukocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts36,37, this study focused specifically on cardiomyocytes as the primary targets of efferocytosis in the context of DiCM. Our preliminary data revealed no significant difference in the proportions of TUNEL+ leukocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts between MARCH2 KO and WT mice during DiCM. Nonetheless, further investigation is warranted to clarify whether MARCH2 also contributes to the clearance of apoptotic non-cardiomyocyte populations.

Ubiquitination, a post-translational modification involving the covalent attachment of ubiquitin to substrate proteins, plays a critical role in regulating endocytosis and cardiac failure14,38. Distinct polyubiquitin linkage confers specific cellular outcomes, including protein degradation, DNA repair, signal transduction, and cellular trafficking39–46. Among them, K27-linked ubiquitin chains possess a unique spatial structure that may influence substrate conformation and stability by masking degradation signals and delaying proteasomal recognition47,48. Previous studies have demonstrated an association between K27-linked ubiquitin and enhanced protein stability49,50. However, the functional role of K27-linked polyubiquitination in macrophage efferocytosis or dilated heart failure remains unclear. In this study, we identified NR1H2 as a substrate of the E3 ubiquitin ligase MARCH2. MARCH2 directly interacted with and stabilized NR1H2 through K27-linked polyubiquitination. NR1H2 expression positively correlated with MARCH2 levels in DCM patients and mediated MARCH2-dependent regulation of MERTK expression during macrophage efferocytosis. Mutation of lysine 163 in NR1H2 accelerated its proteasomal degradation, while MARCH2 promoted K27-linked polyubiquitination at lysine 163 of NR1H2 to enhance its protein stability. These findings identify MARCH2-mediated K27-polyubiquitination of NR1H2 as a key mechanism regulating innate immune responses in dilated heart failure pathogenesis and highlight the broader relevance of K27-linked ubiquitin signaling in regulating cardiac injury and repair.

NR1H2, a transcription factor involved in cholesterol metabolism and immune regulation, plays a critical role in macrophage efferocytosis51. However, its specific role in DiCM was unknown. In this study, we demonstrated that selective deletion of NR1H2 in resident cardiac macrophages (NR1H2f/f; CX3CR1Cre) impaired efferocytosis and exacerbated DiCM. Notably, pharmacological activation of NR1H2 has been shown to suppress tumor progression52,53, suggesting that NR1H2 exhibits dual therapeutic potential - ameliorating DiCM while synergizing with DOX to enhance anti-tumor efficacy.

Efferocytosis deficiency in cardiac injury arises from multiple mechanisms, including impaired receptor-mediated recognition of apoptotic cells. For instance, loss of the macrophage receptor MERTK reduces apoptotic debris engulfment and amplifies inflammation36,54. Using MERTK-luciferase reporter assays, ChIP, and RT-qPCR, we showed that NR1H2 directly binds to the MERTK promoter, thereby enhancing its transcription. Importantly, MARCH2 stabilizes NR1H2 through K27-linked polyubiquitination, further promoting MERTK expression and facilitating macrophage efferocytosis to protect against DiCM.

Consistently, the NR1H2 agonist RGX-104, a synthetic compound, protected against DOX-induced cardiac injury by enhancing resident macrophage efferocytosis. Oral administration of RGX-104 also exhibited potent anti-tumor activity55. These findings further support the therapeutic potential of pharmacological NR1H2 activation in the treatment of DiCM.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that MARCH2 promotes NR1H2 protein stability via K27-linked polyubiquitination, thereby facilitating MERTK-dependent efferocytosis to ameliorate DiCM. Targeting the MARCH2/NR1H2 axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating DiCM.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

This study was performed in both female and male mice to minimize potential sex-based biases in experimental outcomes.

Animal studies

All animal procedures were carried out according to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. In animal studies, treatment/genotype groups were blinded during the experimental procedures.

Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J mice aged 7–8 weeks were used in the experiments. MARCH2 global knockout mice were generated via CRISPR/Cas9 system56. MARCH2flox/flox and NR1H2flox/flox mice were constructed by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. CX3CR1Cre mice (Stock No.C001032), Myh6Cre mice (Stock No.C001041), and Rosa-LSL-tdTomato mice (Stock No.C001181) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Guangzhou, China). Cardiac-resident macrophage-specific conditional MARCH2 knockout mice (MARCH2flox/flox; CX3CR1Cre) were generated by crossing MARCH2flox/flox mice with CX3CR1Cre mice. Cardiac-resident macrophage-specific conditional NR1H2 knockout mice (NR1H2flox/flox; CX3CR1Cre) were generated by crossing NR1H2flox/flox mice with CX3CR1Cre mice. The presence of fluorescent protein in cardiomyocytes was achieved by crossing Myh6Cre with Rosa-LSL-tdTomato.

Human subjects

This study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Experiments with human subjects and tissues were approved by the Rui Jin Hospital Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Heart tissues from patients with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) were obtained from individuals undergoing clinically indicated cardiac surgery. Control heart tissues were obtained from donors who died in traffic accidents and had no known history of cardiovascular disease. For the control hearts, written informed consent for research use was provided by the donors’ next of kin or legal guardians at the time of organ donation, in accordance with established national organ donation protocols. For DCM patients, written informed consent was obtained directly from the participants prior to surgery.

Generation of a doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity model

To induce doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity in C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 weeks, mice were intravenously injected with either doxorubicin (DOX, 5 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) or saline (vehicle control) once per weekly for 4 weeks. The animals were monitored daily for survival. Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography one week after the final injection. All surviving mice in the DOX group displayed a marked reduction in cardiac function and were thus enrolled in our investigation.

Echocardiography

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane for assessment of cardiac function and structure. Standard two-dimensional M-mode echocardiography was recorded before and 6 weeks after the first injection of DOX in mice. Transthoracic echocardiographic images were recorded when the heart rate of mice was at 400–500 bpm. End systole and end diastole left ventricular internal diameters were measured on M-mode recordings.

Bone marrow transplantation

Chimeric mice were generated using sex- and age-matched WT and MARCH2-KO mice. Recipient mice were lethally irradiated with 800 Gy X-ray. Bone marrow cells (1 × 107) extracted from the donor mice by flushing the femurs and tibias with PBS and injected into the recipient mice (tail vein injection). The mice were housed in a clean environment with acidified antibiotic water and sterilized food. 7 days later, the mice were injected with DOX to induce chronic doxorubicin cardiotoxicity.

Mouse cardiac macrophage MARCH2 overexpression

Mouse MARCH2-overexpressing adenovirus particles (ADV-CD68-MARCH2) containing the CD68 promoter and Flag tag were obtained from Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). WT mice received intramyocardial injection of ADV-CD68-MARCH2 or ADV-CD68-Vector with 4 × 109 viral particles in 30 μl sterile PBS57. ADV-CD68-Vector contains an empty macrophage CD68 promoter and served as the control.

Histological analysis

Heart tissues were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde buffer (PFA). The hearts were then embedded in paraffin and cut transversely into 5–6 μm sections for subsequent experiments. These sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome to evaluate the heart morphological effect and interstitial fibrosis. 6 randomly chosen fields in each section (n = 6) were analyzed. These data were calculated using the ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence and TUNEL

For immunofluorescence analysis, heart tissues were embedded in OCT compound and sliced into sections with a thickness of 6 μm. The sections were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. To block nonspecific binding sites, the sections were treated with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Next, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in the blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h. The primary antibodies are anti-MARCH2 (ab220292), anti-CD68 (ab283654), anti-CD3 (ab16669), anti-Ly6G (ab238132), anti-CCR2 (ab273050), anti-MHC-Ⅱ (ab55152), anti-cTNI (ab47003), anti-CD31 (28083-1-AP, proteintech), anti-CD45 (20103-1-AP, proteintech), anti-Vimentin (10366-1-AP, proteintech), anti-Ki67 (28074-1-AP, proteintech). Then, the samples were incubated with the Alexa fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, USA).

Heart tissue slides were treated with the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining using an in-situ cell death detection kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The number of TUNEL-positive nuclei was counted from microscopic images in a 40× objective to evaluate apoptosis. The tissue sections were then photographed using an Olympus IX71 or Zeiss Pascal confocal microscope.

Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) staining

Two weeks after the final administration of doxorubicin, the mice were humanely euthanized, and the hearts were embedded in OCT compound. The hearts were then sliced transversely into sections with a thickness of 5 μm. To detect the cross-sectional areas of cardiomyocytes, wheat germ agglutinin staining (#W11261; Invitrogen, CA, USA) was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions. This staining technique allows for the visualization and measurement of cardiomyocyte sizes.

Western blot analysis

Total protein lysates were extracted from heart tissues or cells. Equal amounts of protein samples (generally 25 or 50 μg) were resolved on SDS-PAGE gels and separated by electrophoresis. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. After blocking with Tris-buffered saline solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.5% Tween 20 for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight.

The primary antibodies against the following were utilized in this study: MARCH2 (#ab123136; Abcam), NR1H2 (#ab28479; Abcam,), Bcl-2 (#3498; Cell Signaling), Bax (#14796; Cell Signaling), cleaved-caspase-3 (#9661; Cell Signaling), MERTK (ab300136; Abcam), CD36 (ab252923; Abcam), TIM4 (ab194829; Abcam), LRP1 (ab92544; Abcam), BAI1 (ab135907; Abcam), and ARHGAP44 (#sc-390609; Santa Cruz), K27 Ubiquitin (ab181537; Abcam) Anti-Flag (#14793; Cell Signaling), Anti-HA (#2367; Cell Signaling), Anti-Myc (#2276; Cell Signaling), Anti-His (#12698; Cell Signaling), HRP-conjugated β-actin (#HRP-60008; Proteintech), HRP-conjugated α-Tubulin (#HRP-66031; Proteintech).

Following the primary antibody incubation, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence luminal reagents (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Images of the protein bands were captured and analyzed using Image Lab (Bio-Rad, USA) and ImageJ software.

Cardiac macrophages isolation

Mouse hearts were harvested and dissociated into single-cell suspensions according to the method mentioned above. The digested tissues were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. After lysing the red blood cells (RBCs), cell suspensions were enriched with Anti-F4/80-MicroBeads UltraPure (Miltenyi, #130-110-443). F4/80+ cells were isolated using a magnet following the manufacturer’s protocols. The cardiac macrophages were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1% FBS.

Human cardiac tissue was minced and digested in Collagenase IV (1.5–2 mg/mL) + DNase I (50 U/mL) at 37 °C for 45–60 min. The digested tissues were filtered through 70 μm strainers, then underwent lysing for RBCs, and resuspended in magnetic-Activated Cell Sorting (MACS) buffer. Cell suspensions were incubated with anti-CD14-MicroBeads (Miltenyi, #130-050-201). CD14+ cells were isolated via an LS column following the manufacturer’s protocols. The cardiac macrophages were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1% FBS.

Cardiac ECs isolation

Mice were euthanized humanely and underwent intracardiac perfusion with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to clear residual blood. Hearts were excised, minced into approximately 2-mm fragments, and enzymatically dissociated using a commercial enzyme kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat No. 130-110-201) according to the manufacturer’s protocol to generate a single-cell suspension. The single-cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm strainer to remove cellular aggregates, followed by incubation with CD31 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat No. 130-097-418) for 20 min (bead-to-cell ratio: 10 μL MicroBeads per 1 × 10⁷ total cells). After washing, ECs were isolated via LD Column (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat No. 130-042-901) using magnetic separation as specified by the manufacturer. Freshly isolated primary cardiac ECs were directly subjected to subsequent analyses.

Cardiomyocytes isolation

Adult cardiomyocytes were enzymatically dissociated from adult murine hearts following an established protocol56. The isolated cardiomyocytes were subsequently seeded onto culture plates pre-coated with laminin (5 μg/mL; Sigma, St. Louis, USA).

Cardiac fibroblasts isolation

Adult mouse hearts (8–12 weeks) were minced in ice-cold HBSS, and digested through 4–5 sequential 10-min cycles at 37 °C with agitation in HBSS containing 0.1% collagenase type II, 0.05% trypsin, and 40 µg/ml DNase I. After each digestion, supernatants were collected following centrifugation. Pooled cell suspensions were filtered (70 µm), digested, halted with 10% FBS, and centrifuged (300 g, 8 min). Cells were resuspended in culture medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin), plated, and incubated for 1.5 h (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Non-adherent cells were removed by washing, and adherent cardiac fibroblasts were maintained in fresh medium.

RNA extraction, and quantitative real-time (RT)-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from heart tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy RNA micro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The mRNA was subsequently transcribed to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Cat No.RR036A, Takara), and the resulting cDNA was amplified by semi-quantitative RT-PCR using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Cat No. RR820A, Takara) and a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Cell culture

HEK293T and Raw264.7 murine macrophages were obtained from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The cardiac macrophages were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/cm² in culture plates pre-coated with poly-L-lysine and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1% FBS.

Plasmid, small RNA interference, and adenovirus

Point mutations in the indicated ubiquitin chain expression plasmids were generated by site-directed mutagenesis58.

The selective siRNAs and negative controls were designed and synthesized by the Jiangsu Gencefe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Control siRNA was employed as a negative under similar conditions. siRNAs used in our experiment were

MARCH2-siRNA sequence: 5′-GCCACCUCAAUAUGUAGCACATT-3′

Mouse NR1H2-siRNA sequences: 5′- GGATTCAGAAGCAGCAA-3′

Mouse MERTK-siRNA sequences: 5′-GGUCUGUAAUGGAAGGAAATT-3′

Plasmid or siRNA transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Adenovirus expressing MARCH2, NR1H2, and MERTK was conducted by the OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cardiac macrophages were transfected with ADV-Vector or ADV-MARCH2 (1 × 108 V.g). Flow cytometry

Mice were euthanized and intracardially injected with cold PBS to eliminate blood cells. The hearts of mice were dissected and rinsed with cold PBS, minced into small pieces, and enzymatically digested to release the cells with the enzyme kit (Cat No. 130-110-201, Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the single-cell suspension was passed through 70-μm cell strainers, washed, and stained with appropriate fluorescently labeled antibodies at 4 °C for 1 h and washed with stain buffer to remove unbound antibodies. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted on a FACSAria™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed with FlowJo 10 software. The results were reported as the number of cells per microgram of heart tissue.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis (FACS)

The single-cell suspension from the heart was obtained using the enzyme kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Afterwards, the single-cell suspension was incubated with appropriate antibodies at 4 °C for 1 h and washed with stain buffer to remove unbound antibodies. The primary antibodies used were all from BD Pharmingen: CD45-APC-Cy7 (#561037), CD11b-FITC (#561688), CD64-APC (#558539), MHC-II- PERCP-CY5.5 (#562363), CCR2-PE (#568093), Ly6G-PE-CY7 (#560601), Ly6C-PE (#560592). Cell sorting was carried out using a FACSAria™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The isolated cardiac macrophages were used for RNA extraction and qPCR analysis.

Induction of apoptosis of cardiomyocytes

For the induction of apoptosis, cardiomyocytes were treated with 2 μM doxorubicin at 37 °C for 24 h.

pHrodoTM red conjugation to cardiomyocytes-derived material

pHrodo™ succinimidyl ester (#P36600, Invitrogen) was used to label cardiomyocyte-derived debris59. pHrodo™ succinimidyl ester was dissolved in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate buffer at 50 mg/mL. To label the apoptotic cardiomyocytes-derived debris, 0.4 mg of pHrodo™ succinimidyl ester was added to a suspension containing 400 μL of the apoptotic cardiomyocytes. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 h at room temperature with continuous mixing. The labeled apoptotic cardiomyocytes were separated from the reaction mixture by centrifugation at 3000 × g. The resulting pellet was washed three times by resuspending in 20 mL of PBS and then centrifuging. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at 4 °C. Microscopic examination was performed on the labeled dying cardiomyocytes to ensure that the background signal was not brighter than a control field without any pHrodo™ present.

In vitro efferocytosis assay

Cardiac macrophages were sorted from indicated groups of mice and were seeded in 24-well dishes at a density of 1–103 cells per well. Fluorescently labeled apoptotic cardiomyocytes were then added to the cardiac macrophages and co-incubated for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells underwent thorough washing three times using 1× PBS. After fixation with 4% formaldehyde, the cells were visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

RNA-seq and sequencing procedures

The total RNA was extracted from heart tissues using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Paired-end libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). In brief, the purified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq25. Q-value < 0.05 and foldchange >2 or foldchange <0.5 was set as the threshold for significantly differential expression gene (DEGs).

Proteomics

The heart samples were quickly homogenized in phenol extraction buffer and then treated with lysate solution containg 5 mM DTT. Proteins were labeled with TMT/iTRAQ reagent. The Proteomic data analysis was performed by Shanghai Luming Biological Technology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). RP separation was performed on an 1100 HPLC System (Agilent) using an Agilent Zorbax Extend RP column (5 μm, 150 mm × 2.1 mm). All analyses were performed by a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo, USA) equipped with a Nanospray Flex source (Thermo, USA). Samples were loaded and separated by a C18 column (15 cm × 75 µm) on an EASY-nLCTM 1200 system (Thermo, USA). ProteomeDiscoverer (v.2.4) was used to search all raw data thoroughly against the Uniprot Mus Musculus database. The thresholds of fold change (>1.5 or <0.5) and P-value < 0.05 were used to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells seeded on a confocal dish at an appropriate density were washed with PBS, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde buffer for 30 min, and permeabilized and blocked with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 1% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, samples were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing off unbound primary antibodies with PBS three times, cells were then stained with Alexa Fluor–conjugated fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclear was stained with DAPI. The following antibodies were used: Flag (Cell Signaling Technology, USA, #8146), HA (Cell Signaling Technology, #3724).

Dual-Luciferase Assays

Dual-Luciferase Assays were performed to investigate the transcriptional activity of the MERTK promoter. Raw264.7 were planted in 24-well plates and transfected with plasmids expressing MERTK-luciferase reporter (firefly luciferase, 0.5 μg per well), ADV-MARCH2, ADV-NR1H2, and pRL-CMV (Renilla luciferase, 0.01 μg per well) and then treated with doxorubicin for 24 h. Then, the cells were harvested, and the luciferase assay was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Cat No. 11402ES80, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This system involves sequential enzymatic reactions to measure the activities of both firefly and Renilla luciferases. The reporter gene activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

First, cells were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde to preserve the protein-DNA interactions. The cross-linking reaction was stopped by adding glycine. The cells were then harvested and lysed to isolate the chromatin. The chromatin was fragmented into smaller DNA fragments using sonication digestion. The nuclei were recovered and resuspended in IP Dilution Buffer, and then sonicated to break the nuclear membrane. Next, anti-NR1H2 antibody (Cat No.60345-1-Ig, Proteintech), or IgG control, was added to the chromatin solution. ChIP-grade Protein A/G Magnetic beads were added to the chromatin solution. Then, the cross-links were reversed by RNAse A and Proteinase K. The DNA fragments were purified and subjected to qPCR analysis.

ELISA

Mouse plasma levels of cardiac troponin T (cTnT) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) were determined as markers for cardiac injury and detected by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The and CK-MB were measured by cTnT Assay Kit (Cat No. H149-4, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) and CK-MB Assay Kit (Cat No. H197-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute).

Mass spectrometry analysis to map ubiquitylation modification Sites on substrates

Proteins were solubilized in 8 M urea, 100 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8.5), and reduced with TCEP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 204433), followed by alkylation with NEM. Gel bands (1 mm³) were decolorized in 50% ethanol overnight, washed with 50 mM NH₄HCO₃, and digested with trypsin (Promega, Cat. No. V5117; 20 ng/μL) at 37 °C overnight. Peptides were dehydrated twice with acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 204433), desalted using C18 ZipTips (Millipore, Cat. No. ZTC18M096), and analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an EASY-nLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer. Data were processed in Proteome Discoverer 2.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and searched against the UniProt Human database (http://www.uniprot.org/) for protein identification and ubiquitylation site determination58.

GST-pull-down assays

GST-MARCH2 protein was expressed in E. coli BL21 competent cells. Cells were lysed and clarified by centrifugation. GST-MARCH2 was affinity-purified with glutathione-agarose beads (GE Healthcare) following the manufacturer’s protocols. His-NR1H2 was expressed similarly in BL21(DE3) cells and purified using Ni-NTA agarose beads (Qiagen) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified proteins were incubated in pull-down buffer at 4 °C for 3 h. The beads were washed four times using the pull-down buffer and detected by Western blotting. The same procedures were followed for the pull-down assays of other proteins.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, all quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Initially, the Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to determine the normal distribution of the data. Statistical differences were conducted using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test for comparison between two groups. Furthermore, a two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni multiple comparison tests and a three-way ANOVA with Sidak post hoc multiple comparison was performed to compare differences among multiple groups. However, if the data did not exhibit a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney test was selected to evaluate the differences between the two groups. P < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and SPSS Version 19.0 unless otherwise indicated.
