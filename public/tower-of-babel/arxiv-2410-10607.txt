Sealed-bid Auctions on Blockchain with Timed Commitment Outsourcing
Jichen Li, Yuanchen Brian Tang, Jing Chen, Xiaotie Deng, Bin Xiao

Abstract
Sealed-bid auctions play a crucial role in blockchain ecosystems. Previous works introduced viable blockchain sealed-bid auction protocols, leveraging timed commitments for bid encryption. However, a crucial challenge remains unresolved in these works: Who should bear the cost of decrypting these timed commitments? This work introduces a timed commitment outsourcing market as a solution to this challenge. We first introduce an aggregation scheme for timed commitments, which combines all bidders’ timed commitments into one while ensuring security and correctness and allowing a varying number of bidders. Next, we remodel the utility of auctioneers and timed commitment solvers, developing a new timed commitment competition mechanism and combining it with the sealed-bid auction to form a two-sided market. The protocol includes bid commitment collection, timed commitment solving, and payment. Through game-theoretical analysis, we prove that our protocol satisfies Dominant Strategy Incentive Compatibility (DSIC) for bidders and Bayesian Incentive Compatibility (BIC) for solvers, and achieves optimal revenue for the auctioneer among a large class of mechanisms. Finally, in the forced-opening-only model in which bidders cannot self-open, we prove that no mechanism can achieve positive expected revenue for the auctioneer while satisfying DSIC and Individual Rationality (IR) for both bidders and solvers, together with ex-post IR for the auctioneer.



1
Introduction

Sealed-bid auction, where all bidders simultaneously submit their confidential bids to the auctioneer, is a cornerstone in the realm of auctions.
In the most widely known variant of the second-price sealed-bid auction, also known as the Vickrey auction
[
57
]
,
the auctioneer sells the item to the highest bidder at the second-highest price, and it is optimal for bidders to report their values truthfully. However, a fundamental premise of sealed-bid auctions is that the auctioneer must be a trusted party, or the mechanism must be credible, a condition that has been proven to be unattainable through mechanism design alone
[
6
]
.
This has impelled research into leveraging cryptography and blockchain to mitigate auctioneer misbehaviors
[
41
,
11
,
43
,
25
]
.

However, in these protocols, the inherent transparency of blockchain necessitates that users first submit encrypted commitments, which are then revealed after all bids have been collected.
This approach, however, introduces two critical vulnerabilities: (1) a bidder might maliciously refuse to reveal their commitment after observing others’ bids; and (2) miners could maliciously prevent bidders’ commitment-revealing transactions from being included in the blockchain (similar attacks have been studied in the payment-channel network (PCN) domain
[
58
]
). To simultaneously address both issues, Tyagi et al.
[
56
]
innovatively employed timed commitments combined with efficient range proofs to construct sealed-bid auctions on the blockchain.
Recent systems further study zero-deposit delayed execution, private auctions at scale, rational liveness, censorship resistance, and TEE-backed confidential compute
[
64
,
47
,
39
,
7
,
34
]
.
Yet, their study left open a critical problem:

Who bears the cost of computing these timed commitments?

The significance of this question stems from the fact that the decryption costs associated with the commitment mechanism profoundly influence the utility of both bidders and auctioneers, potentially altering the auction’s mechanism and bidding strategies, and also critically impacting the overall security of the entire auction protocol.
For instance, consider a scenario with a single solver for timed commitments, where each commitment invocation incurs an individual cost
c
.
If bidders are required to bear a portion of
c
, then running the Vickrey auction may no longer satisfy Individual Rationality (IR), as the second price plus the decryption cost might exceed the first price.
Conversely, if the auctioneer bears a portion of the individual cost
c
, a single bidder can collude with the solver to extract the auctioneer’s payment without actually solving the commitment.
Consequently, the design of a robust timed commitment solving mechanism is paramount for decentralized sealed-bid auctions on the blockchain, necessitating a meticulous examination of participant incentives.

Building on top of
[
56
]
, the contribution of this paper includes:

1.

We generalize the timed commitment scheme from Malavolta et al.
[
45
]
and propose an aggregatable timed commitment scheme. Compared to the scheme by Glaeser et al.
[
36
]
, which is also aggregatable, our scheme supports auction scenarios with varying numbers of bidders with only a one-time setup.

2.

We consider auctioneers who account for both the decryption cost and the decryption time and remodel the timed commitment sealed-bid auctions as a two-sided game by introducing a commitment outsourcing competition for solvers. We redesign the utility functions of the auctioneer and solver, drawing on Bayesian auction theory to design a decryption bidding mechanism.

3.

We propose solutions that satisfy the Bayesian Incentive Compatibility (BIC) and Bayesian Individual Rationality (BIR) properties for the solver while the bidder’s bidding strategies remain unaffected.
This ensures that our decryption mechanism supports any sealed-bid auction protocol without compromising the auction’s properties or the protocol’s security.

4.

Through game-theoretical analysis, we prove that our mechanism achieves maximum auctioneer revenue in a large class of mechanisms, and its optimality is also shown by a complementing impossibility result in achieving DSIC and IR for both bidders and solvers.

2.1
Our Model for Timed Commitment Auction

We consider a single-item auction scenario.
Our timed commitment auction model involves three types of participants:
bidder
,
auctioneer
, and
solver
.

Bidder.
A bidder is an entity participating in the auction, aiming to obtain the auctioned item by placing bids. In an auction, suppose there are
m
bidders, and each bidder
i\in[m]
has a private value
v_{i}
for the auctioned item.
A bidder should design a bidding strategy to determine her bid
b_{i}
according to her private value.

Auctioneer.
The auctioneer organizes the auction, aiming to sell the item and maximize revenue. However, unlike traditional auctions, where the result is immediately available after receiving all bidders’ bids, in a timed commitment auction, the auctioneer can only obtain the result and revenue after the commitments are revealed. Therefore, in our model, the auctioneer aims to maximize
revenue per unit time
, as defined in Definition
4
.

Solver.
Our model includes
n
solvers, numbered as
\{1,\dots,n\}
, responsible for providing computational power to decrypt the commitment and receive payment.
In practice, a solver may use rented generic compute or specialized hardware. The platform can benchmark a machine with hourly price
\theta
and measured speed
s(\theta)
modular squarings per second, then assign marginal cost
c(\theta)=\frac{\theta}{3600\,s(\theta)}
per squaring.
The public curve
\kappa
is fit from these speed–price pairs, with
\kappa(c(\theta))=s(\theta)
. We assume that
\kappa
is strictly increasing, so that achieving a higher speed requires accepting a higher marginal cost per squaring; this trade-off is what makes the solver’s choice of
c_{j}
strategic.
For example, if a benchmark reports
s=10^{8}
squarings per second at
\theta=\$0.32
per hour, then
c\approx 8.9\times 10^{-13}
dollars per squaring and a puzzle with
\mathcal{T}=10^{13}
costs about
\$8.9
to open. This calculation is illustrative; deployment should use measured prices for the actual machines.

In each auction, a solver chooses one point on the public curve, but its effective cost may differ because of electricity prices, idle capacity, bulk-rental discounts, or depreciation. Solver
j
therefore has a private discount ability
l_{j}\in[1,\bar{l}]
, drawn independently from a common prior distribution
F
, allowing it to use hardware of speed
\kappa(c_{j})
at marginal cost
c_{j}/l_{j}
.
Here,
\bar{l}
is an arbitrarily large upper bound used for convenience in the proof.
Thus, each solver
j
should strategically choose its computation cost
c_{j}
in order to maximize its profit.

A complete timed commitment auction mechanism requires the auctioneer to determine a tuple
<\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}^{b},\mathcal{P}^{s}>
which are the
Allocation Rule
,
Bidder Payment Rule
, and
Solver Payment Rule
, defined as follows:

Definition 1
(Timed Commitment Auction)

A timed commitment auction is defined by determining a tuple
<\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P}^{b},\mathcal{P}^{s}>
, where:

Allocation Rule

\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{m}
maps bidders’ reported bids
\vec{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
to an
m
-dimensional vector
(a_{1}(\vec{b}),\dots,a_{m}(\vec{b}))
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}(\vec{b})=1
, where
a_{i}(\vec{b})
is the probability of the item allocated to bidder
i
.

Bidder Payment Rule

\mathcal{P}^{b}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
maps bidders’ bid
\vec{b}
to an
m
-dimensional vector
(p^{b}_{1}(\vec{b}),\dots,p^{b}_{m}(\vec{b}))
, where
p^{b}_{i}(\vec{b})
is the amount of payment that bidder
i
should pay to the auctioneer.

Solver Payment Rule

\mathcal{P}^{s}:\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
maps bids
\vec{b}
and solvers’ computation costs
\vec{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
to an
n
-dimensional vector
(p^{s}_{1}(\vec{b},\vec{c}),\dots,p^{s}_{n}(\vec{b},\vec{c}))
, where
p^{s}_{j}(\vec{b},\vec{c})
is the amount of payment made by the auctioneer to solver
j
.

We now define the utility function for each type of participant.

Definition 2
(Bidder Utility)

Given the allocation rule
\mathcal{A}
and bidder payment rule
\mathcal{P}^{b}
, the utility of bidder
i\in[m]
under bids
\vec{b}
and private value
v_{i}
is defined as

\displaystyle u^{b}_{i}(\vec{b})=a_{i}(\vec{b})\cdot v_{i}-p^{b}_{i}(\vec{b}).

Definition 3
(Solver Utility)

Given the solver payment rule
\mathcal{P}^{s}
, the utility of solver
j\in[n]
under the puzzle complexity parameter
\mathcal{T}
, bids
\vec{b}
, computation costs
\vec{c}
and discount ability
l_{j}
is defined as

\displaystyle u^{s}_{j}(\vec{b},\vec{c})=p^{s}_{j}(\vec{b},\vec{c})-\frac{\mathcal{T}c_{j}}{l_{j}}.

The auctioneer receives the payment from bidders and pays solvers for commitment revealing.
We consider the auctioneer’s revenue per unit of time.

Definition 4
(Auctioneer Revenue)

Given the bidder payment rule
\mathcal{P}^{b}
and the solver payment rule
\mathcal{P}^{s}
, the revenue of the auctioneer under bids
\vec{b}
, computation cost
\vec{c}
, cost-speed function
\kappa
, and time parameter
\mathcal{T}
is defined as

\displaystyle R=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}p^{b}_{i}(\vec{b})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}p^{s}_{j}(\vec{b},\vec{c})\right)\cdot\frac{\kappa(\max{\vec{c}})}{\mathcal{T}}.

2.2
Incentive Properties

We use the standard notions of dominant-strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC), individual rationality (IR), Bayes-Nash equilibrium, Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC), and Bayesian IR for a common prior; see
[
46
,
50
]
. DSIC means truthful participation is optimal for every fixed profile of others’ actions, and IR means the prescribed action yields nonnegative utility. In the Bayesian case, the comparison is in expectation conditional on the participant’s type.

We analyze the economic game after accepting the timed-privacy guarantee of the puzzle. A polynomial-time solver obtains no advantage from trying to break the puzzle; the detailed argument is in Appendix
0.C.5
. Thus the strategic choice is the computation cost and submission time, not an attack on the cryptographic primitive.

Aggregatable Timed Commitment Scheme.

In this section, we introduce our timed commitment auction protocol on the blockchain, including the construction of aggregatable homomorphic time-lock puzzles, the bidding protocol for bidders, and the commitment unveiling protocol for solvers.

3.1
Aggregatable Homomorphic Time-Lock Puzzle

Our aggregatable time-lock puzzle is based on the homomorphic time-lock puzzle construction introduced in
[
45
]
, and the means of achieving aggregatability were also briefly discussed in
[
53
]
. The security of this construction relies on the complexity assumption of squaring operations in a finite field, as introduced by
[
49
]
.

Let
N=p\cdot q
be an RSA integer, where
p
and
q
are large primes.
Malavolta et al.
[
45
]
construct a linear homomorphic timed commitment as
(N,T,x^{r},(x^{N\cdot 2^{T}})^{r}\cdot(1+N)^{e})
, where
r
is uniformly sampled from
\{1,\cdots,N^{2}\}
, and its distribution modulo
\varphi(N)
is statistically close to uniform over
\{1,\cdots,\varphi(N)\}
.
This construction follows the idea that the group
\mathbb{Z}_{N^{2}}^{*}
can be represented as a product of the group generated by
(1+N)
and the
N
-th residue group
\{x^{N}:x\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}\}
.
Our scheme additionally works in the subgroup
\mathbb{J}_{N}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}
of elements whose Jacobi symbol is
+1
, so that the Jacobi symbol leaks no information about the exponent.

However, this construction does not yet satisfy the property of aggregatability (formally defined in Appendix
0.A
). We further extend it, observing that when
N
is a strong RSA integer, every element of the subgroup
1+N\mathbb{Z}_{N^{m+1}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{N^{m+1}}^{*}
admits a unique coordinate decomposition, as stated in the following Lemma, the proof of which is in Appendix
0.E.1
.

Lemma 1

Let
N
be a strong RSA integer and
m\geq 1
. Every element
y
of the subgroup
1+N\mathbb{Z}_{N^{m+1}}=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}_{N^{m+1}}^{*}:y\equiv 1\pmod{N}\}
admits a unique decomposition

y=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(1+N^{k})^{t_{k}},\qquad t_{k}\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}.

Moreover,
1+N\mathbb{Z}_{N^{m+1}}
is cyclic of order
N^{m}
, generated by
1+N
.

To prevent an attacker from gaining information through the Jacobi symbol, we also replace the group
\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{*}
with the subgroup
\mathbb{J}_{N}
when constructing the encryption.
We also note that the original algorithm
PSolve
is a special case of algorithm
PASolve
with
m=1
.
The scheme is shown in Fig.
1
.

Working level of the construction.

An element that is known only modulo
N
does not determine its
N
-th power modulo
N^{k+1}
for
k\geq 2
: for instance,
1+N\equiv 1\pmod{N}
, yet
(1+N)^{N}\equiv 1+N^{2}\not\equiv 1\pmod{N^{3}}
.
All values that enter computations modulo
N^{k+1}
must therefore be carried explicitly in
\mathbb{Z}_{N^{M+1}}^{*}
, where
M
is the per-auction capacity announced at auction initiation: the setup publishes
h=g^{2^{\mathcal{T}}}
reduced modulo
N^{L+1}
for a system capacity
L
, bidders commit modulo
N^{M+1}
with their auction’s
M\leq L
, and the solver’s repeated-squaring chain runs modulo
N^{M+1}
(still
\mathcal{T}
sequential squarings, on a larger modulus).
With this convention,
X=U^{2^{\mathcal{T}}}=h^{R}
holds as an exact identity in
\mathbb{Z}_{N^{M+1}}^{*}
(where
h
is reduced modulo
N^{M+1}
and
R=\sum_{i}r_{i}
), and the extraction equations of
PASolve
are well-defined.

Correctness and Security.

The correctness of this construction follows from the exact identities in
\mathbb{Z}_{N^{M+1}}^{*}
together with the coordinate uniqueness of Lemma
1
, as verified in Appendix
0.B
, and the security of our variant follows from the same hybrid sequence as the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
45
]
.
Crucially, our scheme ensures that as long as at least one bidder honestly constructs the timed commitment, their bid remains confidential even if the solver, all other bidders, and the auctioneer collude. This property effectively prevents potential bribery issues.

Range proofs for aggregatable time-lock puzzles.

Aggregatable time-lock puzzles are compatible with the range proof method presented in
[
56
]
; the details are discussed in Appendix
0.C.1
.

Comparing with other possible solutions.

A comparison with the packing protocol in Cicada
[
36
]
is provided in Appendix
0.C.1
.

Revenue-Optimal Mechanisms

In this section, we analyze the timed commitment auction mechanism that maximizes revenue for the auctioneer. We find that
in a large class of mechanisms with a natural structure, the optimal mechanism takes the form of a second price auction with a reserve price for the bidder and a winner-takes-all competition for the solver: see

Since this mechanism satisfies DSIC

Concretely, the bidder-side rules

4.1
Equilibrium Analysis

We first characterize the BNE of solvers in the proposed mechanism.
Building on the work of Chawla et al.
[
18

Definition 5
(n-maximum-payment-regular)

For a given distribution
F
with density function
f
and an integer
n
, we define the maximum payment virtual value,
\psi_{n}(z)
, as

\psi_{n}(z)=zF(z)^{n-1}-\frac{1-F(z)^{n}}{nf(z)}.

A distribution

is said to be n-maximum-payment-regular if
\psi_{n}(\cdot)
is a monotone non-decreasing function wherever it is non-negative.

This regularity condition covers standard smooth distributions used in auction theory rather than only pathological cases. For example, uniform distributions on a bounded interval and truncated exponential distributions on a bounded interval provide common instances; more generally, the condition holds whenever the distribution of the maximum solver discount ability is Myerson-regular.

Theorem 4.1

of each solver’s discount ability is n-maximum-payment-regular, a unique symmetric BNE exists with strategy

is the winning probability,
\mathcal{T}
is the timed-commitment difficulty parameter,
\vec{v}
is the bidders’ value profile, and

The detailed proof is shown in Appendix

4.3
Impossibility of two-sided DSIC

In the previous analysis, we only required that the solver satisfies the properties of Bayesian Incentive Compatibility (BIC) and Individual Rationality (BIR).
A natural question arises: Is there a mechanism that satisfies DSIC and IR for both bidders and solvers?
We prove in Theorem
4.4
that when the bid vector can be revealed only by a solver, no such mechanism can generate positive revenue for the auctioneer when the bidder’s value can be zero.

Theorem 4.4

When bidder values can be zero, the cost-speed function satisfies
\kappa(0)=0
, and the bid vector can only be revealed through the forced opening of the timed commitment, any mechanism that is DSIC and ex-post IR for both bidders and solvers, and additionally ex-post IR for the auctioneer, must have zero auctioneer revenue on every value profile.

The detailed proof is shown in Appendix
0.E.5
.

5
Conclusion and Future Directions

We studied blockchain sealed-bid auctions with a timed commitment outsourcing market.
We introduced an aggregatable timed commitment scheme that supports a varying number of bidders after a one-time setup.
Building on it, we designed a second-price auction with a reserve price for bidders and a winner-takes-all solving competition for solvers.
This mechanism satisfies DSIC and IR for bidders, BIR and BIC for solvers, and maximizes auctioneer revenue among payment-proportional, rank-based mechanisms when the solver types are
n
-maximum-payment-regular.
We also proved that, when bidder values can be zero,
\kappa(0)=0
, and the bid vector can only be revealed through the forced opening of the timed commitment, performed by the solvers, no mechanism can satisfy DSIC and IR for both bidders and solvers, together with auctioneer IR, while guaranteeing positive auctioneer revenue.

Future directions include non-payment-proportional mechanisms, mechanisms that are BIC for both bidders and solvers, dynamic interactions with reputation and strategy evolution, and stronger forms of solver collusion and bribery.

We introduce the timed commitment scheme, a key component of our protocol, which allows bidders to commit to a bid while keeping it sealed and confidential until the designated reveal time.

Definition 6
(Timed Commitment

A timed commitment scheme is a tuple of three algorithms

Correctness.

For all

in

\mathcal{T}(\cdot)\geq\tilde{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot)

, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|Pr[b^{\prime}=b:e\leftarrow e^{b},\ Z\leftarrow PGen(PSetup(1^{\lambda},\mathcal{T}(\lambda)),e),\ b^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(Z)]-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|\leq\mu(\lambda).

Additionally, we also consider other important functionalities of time-lock puzzles, including homomorphism, aggregatability, and non-malleability.

Homomorphic.
The homomorphic property of timed commitment was first introduced by Malavolta et al.
[
45
]
. It allows specific types of homomorphic operations on encrypted commitments, ensuring both correctness and security. The definition is as follows:

Z^{\prime}\leftarrow Eval(pp,C,Z_{1},...,Z_{k})
.
An algorithm takes public parameters, a circuit

Aggregatability.
We use the following aggregatability notion: a manager can aggregate multiple puzzles into one while maintaining correctness and security. It should contain two algorithms

PASolve

Z^{A}\leftarrow PAggr(pp,M,Z_{1},...,Z_{m})
.
An algorithm takes public parameters and commitments

as input, and outputs one commitment
Z^{A}

(e_{1},...,e_{m})\leftarrow PASolve(pp,M,Z^{A},m)

An algorithm takes public parameters, the capacity
M
, aggregate commitment
Z^{A}
and the number of aggregated puzzles
m
as input, and outputs
m

The non-malleability of timed commitment means that even if an adversary intercepts a commitment, they cannot create a new commitment whose solution would reveal any information about the original solution. This property is crucial for maintaining the security and integrity of timed commitment, particularly in scenarios where they are used to delay access to information or to secure timed releases

29
]

Comparing with other possible solutions.

Our aggregatable time-lock puzzle scheme differs from the packing protocol in Cicada
[

Consider a scenario where the maximum possible value for each bid is

Cicada’s scheme necessitates prior knowledge of

to prevent bid overflow.
Consequently, each commitment in Cicada’s scheme is constructed over the group

In contrast, our scheme only requires choosing once at setup a group size

, so that every bid in
[0,B]
admits a unique coordinate decomposition (Lemma

, and commitments in that auction are constructed over the group
\mathbb{Z}_{N^{M+1}}^{*}

When the announced capacity matches the number of bidders (

, whereas Cicada’s commitments for
m

with

, i.e., a group larger than

; moreover, Cicada must fix

0.C.2
Outsourcing Versus Alternative Opening Designs

Opening
m
concatenated puzzles separately requires
m
repeated-squaring chains, or
m\mathcal{T}
modular squarings in total. The aggregate requires one chain,
\mathcal{T}
squarings, followed by coordinate extraction costing
O(m\log N)
group operations. Thus aggregation is not merely a change of data layout: it removes the factor of
m
from the sequential-opening work.

Renting generic compute is not excluded by our market: a rented machine can participate as a solver. The difference is procurement. A fixed rental commits the auctioneer to one cost-speed choice before knowing solver availability, whereas the competition asks multiple providers to reveal their speed through submission time and pays only the first publicly valid solution.

Encrypted mempools address a complementary propagation-time problem: they can hide transactions while they are propagated for inclusion, but they typically rely on threshold decryption or trusted relay infrastructure and do not by themselves price the cost of forced opening or create an incentive-compatible outsourcing market. The construction here instead provides a contract-verifiable opening path that does not require every bidder to act again after bidding or require a separate threshold committee to release the auction data. Thus it is useful when encrypted-mempool infrastructure is unavailable or when the auction explicitly wants a publicly verifiable fallback opening path.

0.C.3
Bidder Verification

During the protocol’s execution, a solver may attempt to provide a false result to fraudulently obtain payment.
The primary check is public and automatic: the smart contract verifies the succinct repeated-squaring proof and every coordinate-extraction equation before accepting or rejecting the solution.
Thus a false result cannot produce a payment even if no bidder is watching.

Bidders and other relayers can still submit an invalid solution to the contract before regular execution; the first valid fraud report receives a publicly announced reward
\rho
from the solver’s deposit
D^{s}
.
Since validity is deterministic and publicly checkable, there is no “false accusation” proof to fabricate: an invalid report simply fails the same equations.
Consequently, a bidder need not reveal her own bid to challenge a malformed aggregate solution.

0.C.4
Collusion and Bribery Analysis

Proposition 1
(Conditional collusion resilience)

Suppose at least one participating solver is outside a bidder-solver coalition and follows the protocol. Then the coalition cannot alter or suppress the bid of a non-coalition bidder.

Proof

First, a coalition solver cannot alter a non-coalition bid: any accepted solution must pass the public extraction checks of Theorem
0.C.2
. Second, bidder-specific suppression is not available before opening. By Theorem
0.B.2
, bids remain hidden, so a coalition cannot identify which non-coalition bidder carries the competing bid. Since all accepted puzzles are multiplied into one aggregate, suppressing that bid requires suppressing the aggregate itself.

If at least one non-coalition solver participates, it can submit the valid aggregate solution and receive the winner-takes-all reward. A bribe that asks this solver to withhold must therefore exceed its expected reward from submitting. The automatic contract execution prevents the auctioneer from quietly ignoring a valid submission. Thus the non-coalition bid is revealed.

The proposition does not claim protection against a complete solver strike: if every solver colludes and withholds, no computation-procurement mechanism can force the delayed computation to happen. The economic analysis therefore assumes at least one competitive solver. The fraud reward
\rho
deters false submissions, but no on-chain reward by itself can prove and punish a privately promised bribe to withhold.

0.C.5
Honest Decryption of Solvers

Notice that one of the solver strategies is to try to break the cryptographic security of the timed commitment.
However, due to the security guarantees of our aggregatable time-lock puzzle scheme, the probability of a polynomial-time solver successfully doing so is negligible.
As the maximum revenue a solver can gain from an auction is bounded, the expected revenue from attempting to break the scheme is strictly less than the expected revenue from honestly decrypting the commitment.
Therefore, in the subsequent analysis, we only consider the solver to decrypt the timed commitment honestly.

0.C.6

Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
denote the bid-well-formedness relation

\{(pp,M,i,Z,b,r):Z=PGen(pp,M,i,b;r),\ b\in[0,B]\}.

If all accepted bids have sound well-formedness proofs and the smart contract computes

Appendix 0.D
Payment-Proportional and Rank-Based Payment Rules

Definition 10
(Payment-proportional)

.
For a total payment function
V
, a payment rule
\mathcal{P}^{s}
is
payment-proportional
if the payment function satisfies:

p_{j}^{s}(\vec{b},\vec{c})=V(\vec{b})\cdot x_{j}(\vec{c}),

where
(x_{1}(\vec{c}),\cdots,x_{n}(\vec{c}))
is the payment allocation function, with
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}(\vec{c})=1
.

Definition 11
(Rank-based)

A payment rule
\mathcal{P}^{s}
is rank-based if the function is only determined by
\vec{b}
and the rank of
\vec{c}
(ranked in decreasing order, equivalently by increasing submission time). Specifically, it requires
\forall i,j\in[n]
,

p_{j}^{s}(\vec{b},\vec{c})=p_{i}^{s}(\vec{b},\vec{c}^{\prime}),

if the solver
j
’s rank in
\vec{c}
is the same as solver
i
’s rank in
\vec{c}^{\prime}
.

Essentially, a rank-based payment rule predetermines the division of the payment into
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
, with
\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}=1
, where
y_{j}
is given to the solver whose final speed rank is
j
-th. Rank-based is a natural property in competition design, as in reality, the auctioneer only knows the solver’s rank rather than the exact solver’s cost. The bidder’s bid and the total reward function
V(\vec{v})
determine the total payment to solvers. Solvers are ordered by their submission time and awarded the corresponding fraction of the payment; specifically, the
j
-th earliest gets a
y_{j}
fraction of the total reward
V(\vec{v})
if submitted before the due time.

Essentially, the property of payment-proportionality separates the influence of the bidder and solver in the mechanism.
Although it is natural to design such payment rules, it is also worth studying whether the auctioneer can achieve a strictly larger revenue with a non-payment-proportional payment rule. We leave this as an open question for future study.

contradicting ex-post auctioneer IR. Hence no positive-cost opening action can be used on such a profile. Since bidder values can be zero, such a profile exists for every positive-cost opening action, so no positive-cost opening action is selected with positive probability. Without an opening action the mechanism can never determine the bids; by bidder IR it then collects no payments, so its revenue is zero on every value profile.

Bridging Game Theory and Cryptography.

The intersection of game theory and cryptography aims to develop more realistic security models by incorporating participant incentives and computational constraints.
Asharov et al.
[
8
]
formalized traditional cryptographic security concepts like secrecy, correctness, and relaxed fairness as properties of computational Nash equilibria, linking the concept in cryptography and game theory.
Building on this,
[
32
]
introduced a meta-game framework, rational protocol design, defining attacker utility via simulation and corruption costs, which enables composability and bypasses impossibility results in secure function evaluation.
In auction settings, Chung et al.
[
22
]
model platform-assisted auctions with strategic platforms and coalitions and use cryptographic tools to obtain mechanism-design guarantees.
On the other side, Halpern and Pass
[
38
]
laid the groundwork for game theory with costly computation, demonstrating how computational costs influence equilibrium existence and explain bounded rationality.
For practical protocols,
[
4
]
contributed to this intersection by developing k-resilient mechanisms for secret sharing and multi-party computation, achieving robustness against coalitions.
These works refine traditional security definitions to account for practical limitations and strategic behavior, paving the way for designing protocols that are both secure and incentive-compatible.
