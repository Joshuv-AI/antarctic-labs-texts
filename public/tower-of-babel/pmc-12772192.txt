Cannabis produces acute hyperphagia in humans and rodents via increased reward valuation for, and motivation to, acquire food
Authors: Catherine Hume, Carrie Cuttler, Samantha L. Baglot, Lucia Javorcikova, Ryan J. McLaughlin, Matthew N. Hill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCannabis stimulates feeding, though the mechanistic underpinnings are not well characterized. Studies incorporating translational strategies leveraging advantages of clinical and preclinical approaches are necessary to determine the appetite-stimulating therapeutic potential of cannabis. We undertook parallel human and rodent studies to show that vaporized delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol dominant cannabis acutely and robustly increases energy intake, food motivation, and reward value, irrespective of food type, satiety, food aversion, and gender/sex, but does not alter human macronutrient intake proportions and abolishes rat macronutrient preferences. Central cannabinoid receptor mechanisms underlie these feeding behaviors, but peripheral endocrine and cannabinoid receptor pathways do not. We provide insight into the energy balance consequences of cannabis and shed light on the behavioral and physiological mechanisms underlying these effects.

Despite cannabis legalization and decriminalization in many parts of the world, our understanding of its physiological effects remains limited. In 2020, 4% of the world’s population was estimated to use cannabis (1). However, use varies between countries, where 23.8 to 34.4% of North Americans and 1.3 to 15.7% of Europeans reported using cannabis in the previous 12 mo (2, 3). Further, 7.4 to 11.3% of North Americans report almost daily cannabis use (2). Inhalation is the most common route of cannabis use, whether from smoking or vaping (2), and the accessibility, range, and potency of cannabis products are ever increasing (3).

It is widely recognized that cannabis drives food consumption, colloquially referred to as “the munchies.” Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the primary psychoactive constituent in cannabis, is mainly responsible for mediating these appetitive effects (4, 5), which have therapeutic potential to stimulate weight gain in patients experiencing wasting associated with anorexia, chemotherapy, and HIV (6). Cannabis-driven feeding has been modeled in both humans and rodents. In human participants, oral THC or smoked cannabis increased hunger ratings (7, 8) and a single dose of oral THC acutely increased chocolate milkshake consumption (4). In rodents, injected or vaporized THC increased consumption of chow (9–12), sweetened high-fat food (10), and chocolate ensure (13). Beyond modeling these behaviors, research fully characterizing cannabis-driven feeding is sparse.

It remains unclear whether cannabis alters macronutrient intake and preference as detailed eating pattern analysis following cannabis use is currently lacking in both the human and rodent literatures. However, increased carbohydrate (14) or protein (7) intake was reported in cancer patient populations, but no changes in snack selection (15, 16) or food preference scores (17) were observed in healthy participants. On the other hand, when participants were given a variety of meal items, snacks, and beverages in residential laboratory settings, individuals smoking cannabis multiple times a day had higher consumption of sweet snack foods like bananas, cakes, and candy bars (18) and heavy cannabis users had increased sweet tastant preference ratings (19). This implies that cannabis may drive a preference for sweet or carbohydrate-rich foods; thereby, additional research is required to determine whether cannabis indeed influences food choice and preference.

Both the behavioral and physiological mechanisms underlying cannabis-driven feeding behaviors remain understudied. Cannabis may alter palatability and increase motivation for food, which is supported by human studies where oral THC made food “taste better” (7) and smoked cannabis increased desire to eat (20). In rodent models, injected or vaporized THC increased palatability readouts to sugar (21, 22) and increased effort expended to acquire chow in an operant paradigm (23). Thereby, the behavioral mechanism underlying cannabis-induced hyperphagia may be motivation based, where cannabis increases reward value, and may even reverse food reward devaluation to promote food intake in situations where there is no homeostatic drive to eat. It also remains unknown whether cannabis acts through peripheral or central cannabinoid 1 receptors (CB1R) to drive feeding, as peripheral CB1R can independently regulate feeding behaviors (24, 25). Furthermore, cannabis could alter circulating levels of hormones, like appetite-promoting ghrelin, to promote food intake; however, this may be dependent on route of administration (26). Thus, further investigation is warranted to delineate the mechanisms underlying the appetitive effects of cannabis.

Ultimately our understanding of the effects of cannabis on appetitive behaviors remains incomplete through lack of basic human studies and translational rodent models. As the most common route of cannabis use is inhalation (2) and with the pharmacokinetic profiles of inhaled cannabis being drastically different to injected or orally consumed cannabis (27), we validated cannabis inhalation animal models using natural cannabis products (9, 27) which allow us to study cannabis-driven behaviors alongside human cohorts. Here, we undertook parallel human and rodent studies to characterize effects of inhaled cannabis vapor on energy and macronutrient intake patterns over time, with a focus on potential gender or sex differences, respectively. Then using our rat model, we dove deeper into these appetitive effects, assessing the impact of vaporized cannabis on satiety, macronutrient-specific preference, food reward value and devaluation, and appetite hormone levels, then used pharmacological approaches to determine the involvement of peripheral and/or central CB1R in mediating these behaviors. This study advances the field by simultaneously assessing cannabis-driven feeding in both humans and rodents and using a rat model of cannabis inhalation to explore the behavioral and physiological mechanisms underlying these behaviors.

Results

Human Study.

To determine the extent to which cannabis alters energy and macronutrient intake patterns, male and female participants were randomly assigned to vaporize either a low-dose (20 mg THC), high-dose (40 mg THC), or placebo cannabis in a controlled manner. Following ~1 h of memory tests, participants rated intoxication levels, were given access to a variety of snacks and beverages, food intake was measured, and taste ratings were obtained. Full statistical analysis is shown in SI Appendix, Table S1 and participant demographics are shown in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Vaporized cannabis increased inhalations and intoxication.

During vapor inhalation, the length of inhalations or holds did not differ across vapor conditions, but both low (P = 0.034) and high (P < 0.001) cannabis participants took more puffs than placebo participants, and there were no differences between cannabis doses (SI Appendix, Table S3). For intoxication ratings, both low (P < 0.001) and high (P < 0.001) cannabis doses increased intoxication ratings compared to placebo, but there were no differences between cannabis doses (SI Appendix, Table S3).

Vaporized cannabis acutely increased energy consumption.

To assess whether cannabis vapor alters feeding patterns, post-vapor energy intake was measured over time. At the 30 min timepoint, both low (P < 0.001) and high (P = 0.002) dose cannabis increased energy intake compared to placebo, and there were no differences between cannabis doses or any main effect of cannabis at other timepoints (Fig. 1A). However, at the 180 min timepoint, high-dose cannabis increased energy intake compared to placebo in males only (P = 0.029; Fig. 1A). Irrespective of vapor, male participants had higher energy intake than female participants at the 150 min timepoint (P = 0.032; Fig. 1A). The proportion of participants eating over time is shown as a heat map in Fig. 1E; however, cannabis did not significantly influence this.

Vaporized cannabis acutely increased carbohydrate, fat, and protein intake, but did not alter the proportions of macronutrients consumed.

To determine whether cannabis vapor alters the proportions of macronutrients consumed, post-vapor macronutrient intake over time was calculated. At the 30 min timepoint, both low (P < 0.03) and high (P < 0.03) cannabis doses increased carbohydrate, fat, and protein intake compared to placebo, and there were no differences between cannabis doses, or a main effect of cannabis at other timepoints (Fig. 1 B–D). However, at the 180 min timepoint, high-dose cannabis increased carbohydrate intake compared to placebo in males only (P = 0.023; Fig. 1B). Irrespective of vapor condition, male participants consumed more carbohydrate (P = 0.017) and fat (P = 0.048) at the 150 min timepoint, and protein at the 60 min timepoint (P = 0.025), than female participants (Fig. 1 B–D). Further, as cannabis increased intake of all macronutrients at the 30 min timepoint, the proportion of macronutrients consumed was no different between placebo and cannabis conditions, regardless of sex (Fig. 1F).

Acute cannabis-driven feeding was not influenced by fasting duration.

Prior to vapor inhalation, participants reported when they last ate. We used these data to conduct a series of moderation analyses to assess whether fasting duration influences the effects of vaporized cannabis on energy or macronutrient intake at the 30 min timepoint. Fasting duration was not a significant moderator of total energy (P = 0.446), fat (P = 0.196), carbohydrate (P = 0.712), or protein (P = 0.157) intake.

Vaporized cannabis did not influence taste ratings.

To assess whether cannabis vapor alters taste perception and food liking, post-vapor taste ratings were obtained. Despite a significant vapor condition and gender interaction being detected for taste ratings at the 30 min timepoint, no cannabis effect was detected. However, males inhaling high-dose cannabis had higher taste ratings than females inhaling high-dose cannabis (P < 0.001; Fig. 1G).

Rodent Studies.

To model our human findings in rodents and explore these appetitive effects in a translational manner, male and female adult rats were exposed to vehicle (VEH) or cannabis vapor (10% THC); then, feeding or motivated behaviors were measured. Full statistical analyses are shown in SI Appendix, Table S4.

Vaporized cannabis acutely increased chow intake.

To assess whether cannabis vapor alters feeding patterns in rats, post-vapor chow intake was measured over time. Irrespective of sex, cannabis increased chow intake at the 30 (P < 0.001) and 60 min timepoints (P = 0.013; Fig. 2A), an effect that was consistent across exposure days (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Within this timeframe, cannabis altered feeding patterns (Fig. 2B) by decreasing the latency to eat and increasing the number of feeding bouts to increase overall time spent eating (all P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Table S5A). Cannabis also reduced satiety ratio (time spent not eating/kcal consumed (28), P = 0.027; SI Appendix, Table S5A). This acute cannabis-driven increase in feeding was followed by a reduction in intake, particularly at the 120 (P = 0.009) and 180 min timepoints (P = 0.021; Fig. 2A) and did not alter 24 h energy intake or body weight change (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C).

In rats, in standard and sated conditions, cannabis vapor acutely increased chow intake irrespective of sex, an effect that was blocked with preadministration of a universal CB1R inverse agonist (AM251) but not a peripherally restricted CB1R antagonist (AM6545) or inverse agonist (JD5037). Vaporized cannabis overrides satiety to increase chow intake.

To assess whether cannabis vapor can override satiety to drive food intake, rats were sated through consumption of a palatable preload (29), exposed to vapor, and post-vapor chow intake measured over time. In sated rats, irrespective of sex, cannabis increased chow intake at the 30 (P < 0.001) and 60 min timepoints (P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). This effect increased over the first two exposure days (P < 0.02) then plateaued (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). In this timeframe, cannabis altered feeding patterns (Fig. 2D) by reducing the latency to eat, increasing feeding bout number and duration to increase time spent eating (all P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Table S5B). Cannabis also increased ingestion rate (P = 0.035) and decreased satiety ratio (P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Table S5B). Further, compared to VEH rats, sated cannabis-exposed rats had lower intake at the 180 min (P < 0.001; Fig. 2C) and 24 h timepoints (P = 0.004; SI Appendix, Fig. S1G), and had lower body weight change (P = 0.049; SI Appendix, Fig. S1H).

Vaporized cannabis acutely increased intake regardless of food macronutrient content.

To determine whether cannabis-driven feeding is dependent on the types of food available, rats were given post-vapor access to high-carbohydrate or high-fat food, and intake was measured over time. Cannabis increased high-carbohydrate and high-fat food intake at the 60 min timepoint (both P < 0.001; Fig. 3 A and B), by decreasing latency to eat (high-carb: P = 0.003; high-fat: P = 0.012) and increasing bout number (both P < 0.001), to increase time spent eating (high-carb: P = 0.001; high-fat: P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Table S5 C and D). With high-carbohydrate food, cannabis also decreased feeding bout duration and satiety ratio, and increased the number of feeding bouts to a greater extent in males compared to females (all P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Table S5 C and D). With macronutrient-rich food, cannabis effects on feeding were acute and consistent between exposure days (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A, B, E, and F), but only with high-carbohydrate food access did cannabis reduce daily energy intake (P = 0.003) and cannabis did not influence body weight change with either food (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C, D, G, and H).

In rats, cannabis vapor acutely increased food intake regardless of macronutrient content and sex, and when given a food choice (high-fat versus high-carbohydrate), cannabis increased high-fat food preference in standard conditions and increased high-carbohydrate food preference in sated conditions to abolish preexisting food preferences. Vaporized cannabis acutely increased high-fat food preference to abolish preexisting high-carbohydrate food preference.

To assess whether cannabis vapor alters macronutrient-specific food preferences, rats were given a post-vapor choice between high-carbohydrate and high-fat food, and intake of both foods was measured over time. At the 60 min timepoint, VEH rats ate more high-carbohydrate food than high-fat food (P = 0.004; Fig. 3C) and thereby had a higher preference for the high-carbohydrate food (P = 0.003; Fig. 3D). In comparison to VEH, cannabis-exposed rats ate more high-fat food (P = 0.001; Fig. 3C), had a higher preference for high-fat food (P = 0.004) and a lower preference for high-carbohydrate food (P = 0.004), resulting in cannabis-exposed rats not preferring one food over the other (Fig. 3D). With this food choice, cannabis increased feeding bout number (P < 0.001), decreased high-carbohydrate feeding bout duration (P = 0.001) and decreased female high-fat ingestion rate (P = 0.027; SI Appendix, Table S5E). Further, cannabis effects were acute and consistent between exposure days (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–F). Cannabis-exposed rats had lower 24 h intake (P < 0.001; SI Appendix, Fig. S3G), but body weight change was unaffected (SI Appendix, Fig. S3H).

With satiety, cannabis acutely increased high-carbohydrate food preference to abolish preexisting high-fat food preference.

To assess whether cannabis vapor can override satiety to alter macronutrient-specific intake and preference in the sated state, rats were preload-sated (29), vaped, and then given a post-vapor choice between high-carbohydrate and high-fat food, and intake of both foods was measured over time. At the 60 min timepoint, sated VEH rats had a higher preference for the high-fat food than the high-carbohydrate food (P < 0.001; Fig. 3F). Compared to VEH, sated cannabis-exposed rats ate more overall (P = 0.007; Fig. 3E), had a higher preference for high-carbohydrate food (P = 0.046) and a lower preference for high-fat food (P = 0.046), resulting in cannabis-exposed rats not preferring one food over the other (Fig. 3F). With sated food choice, cannabis decreased latency to eat (P = 0.003) and increased the number of feeding bouts (P < 0.001), while increasing high-carbohydrate food bout duration (P = 0.021) to increase time spent eating the high-carbohydrate food (P = 0.004; SI Appendix, Table S5F). Further, cannabis effects were acute and consistent between exposure days and did not influence 24 h intake or body weight (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 I–P).

Vaporized cannabis increased effort expended to acquire a food reward and reduced food reward devaluation.

To determine whether cannabis vapor alters food motivation and reward value, rats were trained to lever press for sucrose on a progressive ratio schedule of reinforcement (30), then post-vapor operant responding for sucrose reinforcement was recorded. In food restricted conditions where the sucrose is “valued,” cannabis increased sucrose-paired “active” lever responses, the number of sucrose pellets earned and breakpoint (readout of effort rats are willing to expend to acquire sucrose, all P < 0.001; Fig. 4 A–C). In the operant task, rats had access to both a sucrose-paired active lever and an unpaired “inactive” lever, allowing discrimination for the active lever to be assessed. In these valued conditions, discrimination for the active lever was unaffected by cannabis as inactive lever responses also increased with cannabis vapor exposure (P = 0.003; SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B).

We showed earlier that cannabis can drive food intake in situations where there is no homeostatic drive to eat, for example during satiety. Thus, we assessed whether cannabis vapor reduces food reward devaluation to provide mechanistic insight into these motivated behaviors. To do this, the sucrose pellets in the operant task were devalued by satiety (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), then post-vapor operant responding recorded. In devalued by satiety conditions, cannabis increased active responses, number of reinforcers earned and breakpoint (all P < 0.001; Fig. 4 D–F), as well as active lever discrimination (P = 0.013; SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E and F). Further, with satiety, cannabis reduced revaluation index (calculated from valued and devalued operant responses, demonstrating the strength of devaluation (31), P < 0.001; Fig. 4G), reflecting a reduction in food reward devaluation.

Further, to assess whether the ability of cannabis to reduce food reward devaluation is dependent on the type of devaluation, the sucrose pellets were devalued by pairing the operant task with lithium chloride (LiCl)-associated malaise (32) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G), then post-vapor operant responding recorded. In devalued by LiCl aversion conditions, cannabis increased active responses (P = 0.007; Fig. 4H), number of sucrose pellets earned (P < 0.001; Fig. 4I) and breakpoint (P = 0.005; Fig. 4J); however, active lever discrimination was unaffected as cannabis also increased inactive lever responses (P = 0.017; SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H and I). Further, with LiCl-induced aversion, cannabis reduced revaluation index (P = 0.020; Fig. 4K), indicating a reduction in food reward devaluation.

Vaporized cannabis does not alter circulating appetite-associated hormone levels.

To evaluate if changes in peripheral appetite-regulating hormones are associated with cannabis-driven feeding, rats were exposed to vapor, blood sampled at immediate, 30 and 60 min timepoints, and plasma hormone concentrations measured. Irrespective of sex or timepoint, there was no difference between cannabis and VEH groups for the appetite-stimulating hormone ghrelin, or the appetite-suppressing hormones insulin, glucagon, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), leptin, amylin, c-peptide, pancreatic polypeptide (PP), or peptide tyrosine tyrosine (PYY) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–J).

Cannabis-induced hyperphagia is blocked by global but not peripheral CB1R antagonism.

To assess the involvement of central and peripheral CB1Rs in cannabis-driven feeding, rats were preadministered with AM251 (global CB1R inverse agonist), AM6545 (peripherally restricted CB1R antagonist), JD5037 (peripherally restricted CB1R inverse agonist), or vehicle, then exposed to vapor and post-vapor chow intake was measured over time. In VEH vapor-exposed rats, pretreatment with AM251, AM6545, JD5037, or vehicle did not influence chow intake (Fig. 2E). As expected, AM251 pretreatment prevented cannabis vapor from increasing chow intake (P < 0.001); however, cannabis vapor still increased chow intake in rats pretreated with the peripheral CB1R antagonist AM6545 or inverse agonist JD5037 (P < 0.001; Fig. 2E).

Following cannabis vapor exposure, THC and metabolite concentrations were higher in females than males.

THC, 11-OH-THC, and THC-COOH were detected in moderate concentrations in the plasma and hypothalamus, and female concentrations were higher than males, irrespective of sample (P < 0.02; SI Appendix, Fig. S6). CBD was undetectable.

Discussion

While the munchies are widely recognized as a cannabis-induced phenomenon, research characterizing the effects of cannabis on feeding and macronutrient intake patterns, and the behavioral and physiological mechanisms subserving this effect is remarkably scarce. To fill this knowledge gap, we ran parallel human and rodent studies and demonstrated that cannabis vapor inhalation had similar outcomes across species, acutely and robustly increasing energy intake. In human participants, these effects were observed in the first 30 min of food access, were irrespective of dose or gender, and cannabis vapor inhalation did not influence food taste ratings. In rats, these effects were observed in the first 60 min of food access, were irrespective of food macronutrient content, satiation, or sex, and were a result of cannabis vapor inhalation reducing latency to eat and increasing feeding bout number. Further, we found that cannabis vapor inhalation did not drive intake of specific macronutrients, instead, it proportionally increased macronutrient intake in human participants and abolished preexisting macronutrient preferences in rats. Here, we document energy and macronutrient intake patterns following cannabis use in healthy human participants and show that these cannabis-driven feeding effects can indeed be modeled in rodents. Rats also compensated for these acute appetitive cannabis effects by reducing subsequent food intake; thereby, body weight change was not significantly affected in most experiments. We then explored underlying behavioral mechanisms and demonstrated that cannabis may override homeostatic appetite regulation by increasing motivation to eat and reducing food reward devaluation to promote energy intake in situations where there would be no homeostatic drive to eat (e.g., during satiation), providing behavioral insight into cannabis-driven feeding and enhancing understanding of how cannabis interferes with food reward processing. Finally, we showed that vaporized cannabis does not alter circulating appetite-regulating hormone levels and mediates its feeding effects through central, but not peripheral, CB1R. We confirm that inhaled full-spectrum cannabis acts through CB1R and disentangle the contribution of central and peripheral CB1R in the appetitive effects of cannabis, paving the way for further mechanistic exploration into the neural circuits and cell types mediating these behaviors. The major strengths and findings of this study are summarized in SI Appendix, Fig. S7.

Acute Feeding Effects.

We consistently showed that cannabis vapor inhalation increases food intake on acute timeframes that align with the pharmacokinetic profile of cannabis vapor delivery, where blood THC concentrations peak immediately after vapor exposure (15, 27) and rodent brain THC concentrations are highest throughout the first 60 min following vapor exposure (27). This timeframe is also consistent with previous feeding studies administering THC or cannabis vapor in rodents (11–13) and oral THC in humans (4). Although there was a small delay between vapor inhalation and food access in our human cohort, intoxication scores remained significantly elevated, implying that these acute feeding effects were indeed a result of cannabis inhalation. Interestingly, we observed no differences in cannabis-driven feeding between low and high-dose participants, which is consistent with previous inhaled and oral THC studies (33, 34), suggesting that within this dose range, there is a threshold and/or ceiling effect for cannabis-driven feeding. As it is established that lower doses of THC have minimal feeding effects and that higher doses of THC can reduce energy intake (11), we can therefore assume that both cannabis doses were within a moderate range of THC dosing and not reflective of the higher doses seen to suppress feeding. It is possible that if a lower or higher dose of cannabis was administered, these acute appetitive effects would be lost. Further, we showed that these acute cannabis vapor feeding effects in rats are stable across days, which is consistent with previous human oral and smoked cannabis studies (18, 33, 34), implying that there are no additive or tolerance effects seen with this behavior and dosing schedule. Moreover, our studies investigated the acute feeding effects of THC-dominant whole-spectrum cannabis. THC is the main phytocannabinoid that drives the munchies (4, 5); however, other minor cannabinoids can also have feeding effects at high doses, including CBD, which has the opposite effect, reducing appetite and food intake (35, 36). Assessing the effects of cannabinoid balanced cannabis or cannabis with differing cannabinoid ratios on feeding behaviors has not yet been investigated. Future research into this is essential particularly as the range of cannabis products and formulations is ever expanding.

Feeding Patterns.

In rats, we undertook microstructural feeding pattern analysis to gain more insight into effects of cannabis vapor inhalation on feeding behaviors. Cannabis vapor decreased the latency to eat in most cohorts, providing more evidence that cannabis increases food motivation. Further, cannabis vapor consistently increased the number of feeding bouts regardless of food type, thereby increasing food intake by eating more frequently. Additionally, ingestion rate remained unchanged in most experiments. Human studies have also observed increased number of eating occasions and snacking with smoking cannabis (8, 18, 33); therefore, it appears that cannabis inhalation does not change meal duration or rate of food consumption but drives people to eat more frequently. We did not observe an effect of cannabis on the proportion of human participants eating over time; however, these data were collected into 30 min bins; thereby, we may not have had the resolution to detect changes in the number of eating occasions.

Macronutrient Intake and Preference.

The literature is unclear regarding the impact of cannabis use on macronutrient intake or preference, as some, but not all studies infer that cannabis may specifically alter carbohydrate intake. In cancer patients, oral THC was associated with higher consumption of carbohydrates (14) and cannabis smoked multiple times a day was associated with sweet snack food consumption (18). Conversely, other human studies did not find an effect of oral THC on snack selection (15, 16) or smoked cannabis on food preferences scores (17). In our human cohort, vaporized cannabis acutely increased intake of all macronutrients; thereby, the proportions of macronutrients consumed were no different to placebo. We did however observe that carbohydrate intake was higher in male high-dose cannabis participants at the 180 min timepoint, indicating that in certain conditions cannabis may drive carbohydrate intake, but this was not robust or consistent in our model. In our rat studies, cannabis vapor abolished preexisting macronutrient preferences, irrespective of rats being sated or not. These effects were a result of cannabis decreasing carbohydrate preference in standard conditions and increasing carbohydrate preference in sated conditions; thereby, we did observe differences in carbohydrate preference between vehicle and cannabis groups; however cannabis exposed animals did not prefer one macronutrient-rich food over the other. Further, cannabis did not influence the latency for rats to eat the different macronutrient-rich foods when rats were given this food choice. Therefore, data from our models imply that cannabis vapor may not selectively drive the consumption of individual macronutrients, but universally drive intake of all macronutrients to increase energy intake. Inconsistencies in previous findings could be explained by patient/participant sample, where cannabis might have more prominent effects in situations where appetite or other sensory factors are influenced, for example in patients undergoing chemotherapy.

Satiety, Aversion, and Food Reward Devaluation.

We also showed that cannabis vapor in rats can drive food intake in the sated state, whether given chow or a palatable food choice, demonstrating that cannabis can override homeostatic satiety mechanisms to alter feeding patterns. In line with this, moderation analyses on our human data showed that the magnitude of cannabis-driven feeding was not influenced by when participants ate last. Moreover, with chow and high-carbohydrate food access, cannabis vapor decreased satiety ratio implying that it reduced the satiating potential of food. We then uncovered the behavioral mechanisms underlying cannabis-driven feeding. Similar to previous findings (23), we showed that cannabis vapor inhalation increased effort exerted to acquire sucrose pellets, demonstrating that cannabis increases food motivation to drive feeding. Further, we devalued the sucrose in the operant task to assess whether cannabis alters food reward devaluation. Even when sucrose was devalued, cannabis was still able to increase motivation and sucrose reward value, and decrease revaluation index, suggesting that cannabis vapor reduces food reward devaluation to drive food intake in situations where food would not otherwise be consumed. Further, the method of devaluation, whether by satiety or LiCl aversion, did not influence the ability of cannabis vapor to reduce food reward devaluation, implying that cannabis can universally reverse food reward devaluation. Collectively, these data demonstrate that the effects of cannabis vapor on enhancing motivated appetitive behaviors are highly robust. This study sheds light on the behavioral mechanism of cannabis-driven feeding, providing insight into how cannabis can promote food intake in situations where appetite is compromised, and highlights possible reward-related brain regions and neuronal circuits that could be involved in mediating these behaviors. Due to ethical constraints, we were unable to conduct equivalent human tasks that directly measure satiety and food motivation; thereby, additional research is required to confirm that these behavioral mechanisms are also present in humans.

Further, other rodent studies have shown that injected THC increases hedonic orofacial responses to sucrose (21, 22) or aversive bitter solutions (22), implying that cannabis may also alter the palatability and liking of food. In our human cohort, we did not see any effects of cannabis vapor inhalation on food taste ratings, consistent with previous studies showing that oral or inhaled THC did not influence participant tastant liking ratings (16, 17). However, in cancer patients with chemosensory alterations, oral THC was reported to make food taste better (7), suggesting that in situations where sensory perception is altered, cannabis may have more prominent effects on palatability. Therefore, additional research is required to dissect effects of cannabis on orosensory experience, taste, and hedonic “liking.”

Daily Energy Intake and Body Weight Change.

Due to cannabis having therapeutic potential for stimulating appetite and the literature showing mixed efficacy for influencing body weight, we assessed daily energy intake and body weight change in our rodent model. We showed that rats compensate for this acute cannabis-induced hyperphagic response by reducing subsequent food intake regardless of sex or food type. We also showed that cannabis had no effect on 24 h energy intake in most cohorts but reduced 24 h intake in others (not associated with feeding state or food type), suggesting that some animals may have overcompensated for acute cannabis-driven feeding. Previous rodent studies are also variable, where oral THC had no effect on daily energy intake (37), but inhaled THC increased daily energy intake (8, 9). Further, in line with other reports (9), body weight change for all but one cohort was not influenced by cannabis. Thus, our data indicate that acute increases in food intake from once daily cannabis vapor exposure are compensated for to maintain body weight. Moreover, in this study, we did not directly study chronic cannabis exposure, specifically in relation to overall energy intake, body weight change, and tolerance. Our human participants reported to have used cannabis ~five times in the past week with use spanning for ~8 to 10 y on average; thereby, these data shed some light on experienced cannabis users. These participants showed robust cannabis-driven feeding responses, suggesting that regular cannabis use does not influence these acute effects. Additional research is required to explore other aspects of regular or long-term cannabis use from an energy homeostasis perspective.

The ability of cannabis to influence daily energy intake and body weight may be related to the frequency of use. In human studies, 24 h energy intake is increased in participants who use cannabis 4+ times a day (7, 15, 18, 20, 33, 34), but not in those who use cannabis 1 to 2 times a day (7, 15). Further, higher rates of cannabis use are associated with higher daily caloric intake (38), and weight gain has been reported in cachectic patients using cannabis products (20). Conversely, regular cannabis users are reported to have lower incidences of obesity compared to the general population (39), and chronically administered THC in rodents is associated with reduced body weight gain (40) and has protective effects on diet-induced obesity development (41). Emerging evidence suggests that this could be explained by a metabolic switch possibly involving hepatic CB1R-activated lipolysis (42), aligning with observations of reduced nonalcoholic fatty liver disease prevalence in regular cannabis users (43); however, this is yet to be directly studied. Thereby, the effects of cannabis use on metabolic health and obesity risks can be speculated to be dependent on several factors including differing patterns of cannabis use, specifically with regard to THC dose, the duration and frequency of use, and the length of use (i.e., acute versus chronic) (44). This highlights the necessity for conducting more appropriately designed, controlled, and highly powered preclinical and clinical studies assessing the effects of cannabis use regimens on energy balance in the short and long-term, as well as its potential for alleviating cachexia in the clinic.

Sex and Gender Differences.

Sex and gender differences in cannabis-induced feeding behaviors remain relatively unexplored in the literature. Overall, blood THC levels in our rat vapor model are consistent with human blood levels after consuming cannabis to the point of desired intoxication (45). Further, we showed that female rats had higher THC and metabolite concentrations compared to males. This is consistent with other rodent and human studies (27, 46) and is speculated to be a result of body weight differences between males and females, and THC being metabolized at a faster rate in females due to liver metabolic enzyme differences (47). We did not have the ability to measure blood THC levels in our human cohort. However, in both our human and rodent cohorts, we found no obvious sex differences in the ability of cannabis to alter food intake. We did not track estrus cycle stage. However, in rats, cannabis-driven feeding effects were consistent between vapor exposure days and previous reports did not detect THC vapor-induced behavioral differences between estrus and diestrus phases (48).

Potential Central Mechanisms.

We show that cannabis vapor inhalation does not influence circulating levels of the appetite-stimulating hormone ghrelin, nor appetite-suppressing hormones, including leptin, insulin, or GLP-1, implying that these behaviors are not mediated through a peripheral endocrine mechanism. Literature surrounding cannabis effects on appetite-stimulating hormones is sparse; thereby, we comprehensively investigated this, particularly using a translational rodent model and measuring numerous hormones. Human evidence suggests that the recruitment of endocrine pathways may be dependent on the route of cannabis administration, where ingestion might alter ghrelin levels, but inhalation might not (26). This warrants additional investigation and highlights the importance of route of administration in cannabis research.

Moreover, pharmacologically blocking peripheral CB1R in rats using a peripherally restricted antagonist or inverse agonist had no influence on the appetitive effects of cannabis vapor exposure, demonstrating that inhaled cannabis does not influence feeding through peripheral CB1R-mediated mechanisms. This is interesting as peripheral CB1R are involved in endocannabinoid regulation of energy balance, where blocking peripheral CB1R reduces food intake and operant responding (25), and the CB1R agonist anandamide only promotes food intake when administered peripherally (24). Additionally, we show that globally blocking CB1R inhibited cannabis vapor-driven feeding [aligning with previous reports administering SR-141716 prior to injected THC (49)], implying that cannabis vapor is acting directly on the brain to drive energy intake. Previous research into the involvement of CB1R used rodent models that do not reflect routes of human cannabis use and did not dissect the contribution of central and peripheral CB1R. Therefore, in this study, the involvement of peripheral versus central CB1R was investigated with respect to cannabis-driven feeding, expanding and complementing previous literature and paving the way for further mechanistic exploration.

Within the brain, the endocannabinoid system modulates numerous energy balance regulating circuits; thereby, these pathways are candidates for mediating cannabis-driven feeding. The hypothalamic arcuate nucleus is a key region of interest as inhibition of appetite-stimulating AgRP neurons reduces cannabis-driven food intake (12). Appetite-inhibiting arcuate nucleus POMC neurons may also be involved through CB1R mitochondrial mechanisms, where CB1R agonists increase POMC neuron activity, promoting hypothalamic β-endorphin release and food intake (50). Further, lateral hypothalamus appetite-stimulating GABA neurons could play a role, as inputs to these neurons are gated retrogradely by endocannabinoids and local administration of CB1R agonists reduces inhibitory tone and promotes food intake (51). Moreover, the main olfactory bulb is also a region of interest where CB1R agonists dampen excitatory inputs to granule cells and locally administered CB1R antagonists reduce food intake following injected THC (52). Further research is warranted to explore whether inhaled cannabis engages these different homeostatic circuits to promote feeding. Moreover, considering the behavioral mechanism we uncovered showing that inhaled cannabis reduces food reward devaluation, cannabis likely engages with brain regions or circuits involved in reward value and motivation. THC has been shown to stimulate striatal dopamine neurotransmission in rodents (21) and humans (53), potentially explaining how cannabis vapor influences motivation for food intake. CB1R are also densely expressed in the nucleus accumbens shell (54) which when activated stimulates food intake (55) and increases hedonic responses to sucrose (56), similar to the effects of THC (22). Additionally, the orbitofrontal cortex is involved in reward value and goal-directed behavior, where its inactivation reduces the devaluating ability of satiety, likely through connectivity with the striatum (57). Thus, cannabis-driven motivated feeding behaviors could involve dopaminergic, striatal, and/or cortical pathways. This study documents a translational rodent model for delineating the mechanisms of inhaled cannabis, and ongoing work is exploring the relative influence of each of these central pathways.

Further, it is possible that various internal and environmental conditions may influence if central or peripheral CB1R pathways are involved. Rodent literature demonstrates that the pathway recruited can be dependent on food type (chow versus high-fat diet) or the metabolic state of the animal (fasted or ad lib fed) (58). Moreover, as mentioned earlier, route of cannabis administration may also dictate its mechanism (26). Cannabis type (e.g., high versus low in CBD) could also be a factor, but this is yet to be determined. Therefore, further mechanistic research should consider these variables when delineating the actions of cannabis through CB1R.

Finally, cannabis could influence other aspects of the endocannabinoid system to drive feeding, where evidence suggests that cannabinoids alter brain levels of the endocannabinoids anandamide and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (59), as well as fatty acid amide hydrolase (the enzyme that metabolizes anandamide) (60). However, it remains unknown whether inhaled cannabis specifically influences the regulation and/or function of endocannabinoids or other aspects of the endocannabinoid system, or whether acute or chronic cannabis exposure differentially impacts this. Thereby, future mechanistic work should also assess potential alterations in endocannabinoid tone and downstream signaling, and their involvement in cannabis-driven feeding behaviors.

Conclusions

Overall, this study is important for assessing the effects of cannabis use on eating patterns and sheds light on its energy balance associated consequences and underlying behavioral and physiological mechanisms. This knowledge is necessary to determine whether cannabis-driven feeding in healthy individuals could be maladaptive, disrupting homeostatic feeding patterns and energy balance, as well as for assessing the appetite-stimulating therapeutic potential of cannabis to promote weight gain in patients experiencing wasting associated with anorexia nervosa, chemotherapy, and HIV. Further investigation is necessary to uncover the association between cannabis use regimens and appetitive/metabolic effects, and to further elucidate the central mechanisms underlying cannabis-driven feeding behaviors, with focus on both homeostatic and hedonic appetite-regulating circuits.

Methods

See SI Appendix, Methods for additional methods and details of statistical analysis.

Human Study.

Participants.

The study was approved by the Washington State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Food & Drug Administration (FDA). A double-blind, placebo-controlled design was used, where 37 female and 45 male participants were randomly assigned into treatment groups, and both experimenter and participants were blinded to drug condition. Participants were instructed to abstain from using cannabis on testing day and from eating 1 h prior to testing. Participants provided written informed consent and completed an online survey containing demographic questions and the Daily Sessions, Frequency, Age of Onset, and Quantity of Cannabis Use Inventory (61).

Vapor inhalation.

Participants vaporized low-dose cannabis (n = 26; 20 mg THC; 0.17 g of 11.86% THC cannabis flower), high-dose cannabis (n = 28; 40 mg THC; 0.34 g of 11.86% THC cannabis flower), or placebo cannabis (n = 28; 0.17 g of placebo plant material) in a controlled manner. Dried cannabis flower [barrel #1592; 11.86% Δ9-THC, 1.12% cannabidiol (CBD), 0.32% cannabichromene (CBC), 0.41% cannabigerol (CBG), 0.77% cannabinol (CBN), and 0.10% tetrahydrocannabivarin (THCV)], and placebo (Lot# 1004-1904-01; <0.025% Δ9-THC, 0.03% Δ8-THC, 0.14% CBD, <0.025% CBC, and <0.01% CBG, CBN, and THCV) were obtained from the National Institute on Drug Abuse Drug Supply Program.

Procedure.

Following ~1 h of memory tests and intoxication ratings [using a 0 (not at all) to 10 (extremely) visual analogue scale], participants were given access to a variety of snacks and beverages, and every 30 min, food intake was measured, and taste ratings obtained [using a 0 (strongly dislike) to 10 (strongly like) scale]. Moderation analyses were conducted to determine whether fasting duration influences cannabis feeding effects at the 30 min timepoint.

Rodent Studies.

Subjects.

Adult male and female Sprague-Dawley rats were single housed on a reverse 12 h dark light cycle with access to chow and water unless otherwise stated. Each experiment had 6 to 10 rats in each treatment/sex group.

Vapor exposure.

Rats were habituated to procedures, then exposed to vapor from polyethylene glycol vehicle (VEH) or a THC-dominant cannabis extract (10% Δ9-THC, 0.03% CBD, 0.06% CBC, 0.19% CBG, 0.15% CBN, 0.03% THCV in VEH, Aphria Inc., Ontario, Canada) for 15 min/d for 3-d as previously described (27). Rats were food restricted for 75 min prior to vapor exposure, and food returned immediately after vapor exposure.

Food access.

Rats were given post-vapor access to chow (Prolab® RMH 2500; 3.34 kcal/g), high-carbohydrate food (#D19102310, Research Diets Inc., New Jersey, USA; 3.8 kcal/g; 80% carbohydrate, 10% fat, 10% protein), or high-fat food (#D19102309, Research Diets Inc., New Jersey, USA; 6.1 kcal/g; 80% fat, 10% carbohydrate, and 10% protein) or both high-carbohydrate and high-fat foods, and intake was measured. Microstructural feeding patterns were scored within the first hour of food access.

Satiety.

A palatable preload (1 part sucrose : 4 parts chow : 5 parts water w/w) (29) was given for 2 h prior to vapor exposure.

Antagonist pretreatment.

30 min prior to vapor exposure, rats were subjected to IP injections of vehicle (5% DMSO, 5% tween, 90% saline), CB1R inverse agonist AM251 (1 mg/kg in vehicle; #1117, Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK), peripherally restricted CB1R antagonist AM6545 (2 mg/kg in vehicle, provided by K. Vemuri and A. Makriyanis, Northeastern University, Boston, MA, USA), or peripherally restricted CB1R inverse agonist JD5037 (3 mg/kg in vehicle, #1HY-18697, Cedarlane, Burlington, Ontario, Canada).

Post-vapor hormone measurement.

Following vapor exposure, blood was sampled at the immediate (0 min), 30 min, and 60 min timepoints. Plasma concentrations of ghrelin, insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, GIP, leptin, amylin, c-peptide, PP, and PYY were measured using a Rat Metabolic Hormone 10-Plex Discovery Assay (Eve Technologies, Calgary, AB, Canada).

Operant conditioning.

A within subject, balanced, cross-over design was used. Rats were maintained at 90% free-feeding body weight and trained to lever press for sucrose pellets on a progressive ratio (PR) schedule of reinforcement (1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 20, etc.) (30). For testing, rats were exposed to VEH or cannabis vapor then PR operant responding recorded. For devaluation by satiety testing, rats had 2 h sucrose pellet access prior to VEH or cannabis vapor exposure. For devaluation by aversion testing, the operant task was paired with lithium chloride injections (LiCl, 125 mg/kg, IP) (32). Revaluation index for devaluation was calculated (31).

Phytocannabinoid measurement.

Post-vapor plasma and hypothalami were collected and ran through tandem mass spectrometry as previously described (27).
