Photon-counting detector computed tomography compared with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging for myocardial extracellular volume quantification in spontaneous coronary artery dissection
Authors: Konstantin Klambauer, Andrea Biondo, Ernst Klotz, Lukas J Moser, Tristan Demmert, Victor Schweiger, Victor Mergen, Michael Würdinger, Davide Di Vece, Alexander Gotschy, Martin F Reiner, Jelena-R Ghadri, Verena C Wilzeck, Matthias Eberhard, Christian Templin, Robert Manka, Hatem Alkadhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) may cause myocardial infarction. Photon-counting detector computed tomography (PCD-CT) with late iodine enhancement (LIE) imaging enables myocardial extracellular volume (ECV) quantification. In this single-center, prospective study, we quantified myocardial ECV in patients with SCAD using LIE PCD-CT (n = 20 patients), and compared results with CMR (n = 13 patients). Global ECVCT was quantified in the entire myocardium; lesion ECVCT was quantified using a threshold-based approach with lesions exceeding an ECVCT of 40%. Relative lesion volume was calculated as the proportion of lesion to total myocardial volume. Global ECVCMR was calculated across all myocardial segments, lesion ECVCMR was derived from manual segmentation. In the 20 patients, lesion ECVCT was 45.2% (IQR 44.2–46.8%) compared to a global ECVCT of 28.5 ± 5.3%. Relative lesion volume strongly correlated with global ECVCT (ρ = 0.938, P < 0.001). A global ECVCT cut-off of 28.5% predicted relative lesion volumes < 5% with an AUC of 0.956 (95% CI 0.752–0.999) and a PPV of 82%. Global ECVCT (28.0 ± 5.2%) and global ECVCMR (29.3 ± 4.2%) was similar (P = 0.085, mean difference 1.4%, 95% limits of agreement: − 4.2 to 7.0%). Lesion ECVCT (45.2%, IQR 44.6–46.8) and lesion ECVCMR (45.0%, IQR 39.4–48.6) was similar (P = 0.340). In conclusion, LIE PCD-CT enables quantitative myocardial ECV assessment in SCAD patients, showing a good agreement with CMR.Keywords: Extracellular volume, Late iodine enhancement, Photon-counting detector computed tomography, Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, Spontaneous coronary artery dissectionSubject terms: Magnetic resonance imaging, Acute coronary syndromes, Vascular diseases, Computed tomography

Introduction

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) is a rare but relevant cause of myocardial infarction, primarily affecting younger women without traditional cardiovascular risk factors1. The modality of choice for the diagnosis of SCAD is invasive coronary angiography (ICA)2, although the diagnosis can be challenging due to its variable and often subtle appearance3. Early identification is essential as management differs from atherosclerotic coronary disease, favoring conservative treatment over revascularization in many patients1.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging is a useful modality in patients with SCAD for detecting myocardial edema and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) to confirm infarction and to distinguish it from other conditions such as myocarditis or takotsubo syndrome4–6. The extent of LGE is associated with worse long-term outcomes, including recurrent cardiac events and left ventricular dysfunction7. Beyond myocardial edema and LGE detection, extracellular volume (ECV) can be quantified providing additional insights into myocardial pathology8. ECV has shown to improve diagnostic confidence and refine risk stratification of both ischemic and non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, particularly when interpreted alongside LGE and ejection fraction information9,10.

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has been explored for diagnosis of SCAD, demonstrating accuracy in larger coronary arteries but limitations in detecting lesions in smaller, distal vessels11–14. However, computed tomography (CT) offers more than just coronary artery evaluation but also allows for myocardial tissue characterization via late iodine enhancement (LIE) imaging15,16, hereby representing a potential alternative to LGE with CMR particularly in the acute setting17–20. Conventional energy-integrating detector CT (EID-CT) enables ECV quantification using LIE images, usually requiring an additional non-contrast scan for image subtraction21,22. Compared to EID-CT, the recently introduced photon-counting detector CT (PCD-CT) provides intrinsic multi-energy data with high temporal resolution and reduced noise23, enabling more robust CT-based myocardial tissue characterization and ECV quantification21,24.

Most prior studies on CT- and CMR-based ECV have focused on acute myocardial infarction or non-ischemic cardiomyopathies24,25. SCAD represents a distinct clinical entity with unique pathophysiology and underexplored imaging strategies. Evaluating ECV in this population may provide novel insights and has potential clinical value, particularly when CMR is not feasible, and assessing agreement with CMR is therefore essential to establish the reliability of PCD-CT in this patient cohort.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to quantify myocardial ECV in patients with SCAD using LIE imaging with PCD-CT, and to compare the results with the reference standard CMR.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Cantonal Ethics Committee Zurich, Switzerland (approval date: 20th May 2020, BASEC-ID: 2020 − 01023). All patients provided written informed consent and were prospectively enrolled in the SCAD registry of our hospital. This prospective single-center study included consecutive patients diagnosed with SCAD by ICA who underwent LIE imaging using a PCD-CT between March 2021 and August 2024. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, and the exclusion criterion was nondiagnostic LIE CT image quality, defined as severe motion artifacts, inadequate contrast-to-noise ratio, or incomplete myocardial coverage precluding reliable ECV quantification.

As part of the study protocol, patients were planned to undergo both cardiac CT and CMR during index hospitalization and at a three-month follow-up. Still, some patients did not undergo CMR due to contraindications, scheduling constraints, and patient preferences. Thus, only a subgroup of patients was available for a secondary analysis comparing ECV quantification between CT and CMR (Fig. 1). Patient demographics were collected from electronic medical records. Part of the patients were included in previous publications on different topics20,26,27.

Study flowchart.

CT scan protocol

Scans were conducted using a first-generation dual-source PCD-CT (NAEOTOM Alpha, Siemens Healthineers AG, Forchheim, Germany) featuring two cadmium telluride detectors. The detector collimation was 144 × 0.4 mm and the gantry rotation time 0.25 s. Initially, a non-contrast scan was performed for coronary calcium scoring at 75% of the RR-interval. CT angiography was performed using an intravenous injection of 60–80 mL iodinated contrast medium (iopromide 370 mg I/mL, Ultravist; Bayer Pharma AG, Berlin, Germany), corresponding in this study population to approximately 296–395 mgI/kg, followed by a 20 mL saline flush (NaCl 0.9%). Injection rates ranged from 5 to 6 mL/s, adjusted for patient weight. CCTA was acquired in an ECG-triggered sequential spectral mode (QuantumPlus) and with variable ECG-pulsing based on the average and variability of heart rate. LIE CT was performed 5 min after contrast media administration, as this delay has been shown to provide stable myocardial iodine distribution and reliable lesion conspicuity. An ECG-triggered end-systolic sequential acquisition at 280 milliseconds of the RR-interval was chosen to minimize motion artifacts and to ensure consistent assessment at the phase of largest myocardial volume. Tube voltage was set at 120 kVp and the image quality level was 80. CCTA and LIE CT scans were reconstructed as virtual monoenergetic images (VMI) at 65 keV with a 1.5 mm slice thickness and 1 mm increment, which optimizes contrast-to-noise ratio in LIE imaging. All acquisition and reconstruction parameters followed previously published and established protocols21,24. No quantitative image quality metrics (e.g., contrast-to-noise ratio or signal-to-noise ratio) were obtained, however, all scans were visually assessed for image quality by two cardiovascular radiologists ([K.K.] and [H.A.] with 6 and 20 years of experience in cardiac CT, respectively). Spectral iodine maps were generated from the LIE scans.

Extracellular volume quantification with CT

Three-dimensional left ventricle (LV) models were generated from either end-systolic CCTA or LIE phase datasets. Using nonrigid registration, ECVCT volume data were aligned with the LV model and presented as polar maps with 16 American Heart Association (AHA) myocardial segments, color-coded to depict ECVCT levels in the subendocardium, midwall, and subepicardium. Midwall myocardium was defined as the inner 25% to outer 25% of the myocardial wall21. Additionally, high-resolution AHA subsegmental data (34 × 72) were calculated for the three transmural layers, subdividing the myocardium circumferentially into 72 angular segments and longitudinally into 34 short-axis layers, as previously described20.

Global ECVCT was determined by averaging all midwall subsegments over the entire myocardium, avoiding partial volume effects from the blood pool or epicardial fat. A threshold-based approach was employed to identify myocardial lesions and quantify total lesion burden. Analyses were performed on the median-filtered, subsegmental ECVCT data derived from the entire myocardial midwall, as previously described20. Subsegments with an ECVCT > 40% were classified as lesion myocardium. The threshold of 40% was selected based on prior work demonstrating excellent concordance with lesion extent identified on standard LGE CMR20. Relative lesion myocardial volume was calculated by summing all subsegment volumes above the threshold across the entire left ventricular myocardium and normalizing to the total volume. CMR scan protocol

CMR was performed on 1.5 T scanners (Achieva, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands, or MAGNETOM Sola, Siemens Healthineers AG, Erlangen, Germany), both with cardiac receiver arrays and vector ECG synchronization. Cardiac function was assessed using breath-hold balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) cine images in short-axis slices covering the LV and in two-, three-, and four-chamber orientations (note: Reported values reflect vendor-specific conventions; Philips: TR/TE = 3.3/1.6 ms, flip angle 60°, spatial resolution 1.5 × 1.5 mm², slice thickness 8 mm, ≥ 25 phases; Siemens: TR/TE = 30.9/1.34 ms, flip angle 70°, spatial resolution 1.5 × 1.5 mm², slice thickness 8 mm, ≥ 25 phases). Additional T1- and T2-weighted turbo spin-echo (TSE) sequences with fat saturation were acquired in short-axis orientation. Native T1 and T2 mapping were performed in end-diastole at three short-axis levels using single breath-holds (note: Reported values reflect vendor-specific conventions; Philips T1: TR/TE = 2.3/0.9 ms, flip angle 35°, voxel 1.2 × 1.2 × 10 mm³; Philips T2: TR/TE = 9.4/728 ms, flip angle 90°, voxel 1.1 × 1.1 × 10 mm³; Siemens T1: TR/TE = 325.4/1.09 ms, flip angle 35°, voxel 1.2 × 1.2 × 10 mm³; Siemens T2: TR/TE = 224.4/1.04 ms, flip angle 70°, voxel 1.1 × 1.1 × 10 mm³). T1 mapping employed a MOLLI 5(3)3 scheme (eight inversion-recovery images acquired in two blocks, separated by three recovery beats), applied both natively and post-contrast. LGE imaging was performed ~ 10 min after intravenous administration of 0.2 mmol/kg gadobutrol (Gadovist, Bayer Schering, Germany) using an inversion-prepared spoiled gradient-echo sequence, with inversion time determined by a Look-Locker scout. Post-contrast T1 mapping (MOLLI 5(3)3) was acquired 15–20 min after contrast, and hematocrit measured on the day of CMR was incorporated for ECV calculation.

Extracellular volume quantification with CMR

Measurements were performed in agreement by two cardiovascular radiologists ([A.B.] and [R.M.], with 4 and 20 years of experience in CMR, respectively) using dedicated software (Intellispace Portal 9.0, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands). All analyses were based on the 16-segment American Heart Association (AHA) model, which was automatically registered to both LGE and T1 mapping images and manually corrected when necessary. Motion correction was applied to pre- and postcontrast T1 mapping sequences. Myocardial segmentation was performed by manually drawing subendocardial and subepicardial contours on the T1 maps, carefully avoiding partial volume effects from the blood pool or epicardial fat. Global ECVCMR was calculated by averaging values across all 16 segments. Due to limited spatial resolution and signal uniformity in CMR, lesion quantification relied on visual identification rather than the automated thresholding used in CT. Lesion segments were identified on LGE images as any segment showing LGE. Lesion ECVCMR was then determined using two approaches: (i) placement of three small, manually drawn regions of interest (ROI) within visually identified areas of myocardial injury with LGE across basal, midventricular, and/or apical slices, and (ii) a segment-based method, in which the average ECVCMR of all segments showing a lesion was calculated. Blood pool T1 was measured from a centrally placed ROI in the LV cavity, avoiding papillary muscles and flow artifacts.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Correlation between global ECVCT and lesion volume was assessed using Spearman correlation across the entire study population. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with calculation of the area under the curve (AUC) was performed for acute phase CT to evaluate the ability of global ECVCT to predict relative lesion volumes. Thresholds of < 5% and < 10% lesion volume were formulated for this study to provide two exploratory boundaries and to demonstrate the significance of small infarcts, which have previously been associated with preserved LV function and favorable prognosis28,29. Due to the limited sample size, this analysis was not repeated for follow-up CT. In the subgroup with both CT and CMR (including acute and follow-up scans), comparisons of ECVCT and ECVCMR were conducted using the paired t-test or the paired Wilcoxon test, as appropriate. Bland-Altman analysis was used to evaluate agreement between ECVCT and ECVCMR. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using MedCalc (Version 19.1.3, MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium).

Results

Study population

Overall, 22 patients underwent LIE imaging with PCD-CT, of whom two were excluded due to nondiagnostic image quality resulting from severe motion artifacts. The remaining patients in the SCAD registry did not undergo LIE CT due to patient preference scheduling constraints or loss to follow-up. Among the final 20 patients (7 males [35.0%], 13 females [65.0%]), a total of 27 CT scans were available, comprising 19 in the acute phase and 8 at follow-up. Six patients underwent both acute and follow-up phase CT scans. A subgroup of 13 patients (2 males [15.4%], 11 females [84.6%]) additionally underwent CMR, yielding a total of 15 matching CT and CMR scans (7 in the acute phase and 8 at follow-up). Two patients underwent both acute and follow-up phase CMR (see Fig. 1).

The 20 patients of the final study population had a median age of 45 years (IQR 40–54 years) and a median body mass index (BMI) of 28.2 kg/m² (IQR 23.6–30.5 kg/m²). The most frequently affected coronary artery was the left anterior descending artery in 10 patients (50%), followed by the right coronary artery in 6 patients (30.0%) and the circumflex artery in 4 patients (20%). SCAD types 2 and 3 (Yip-Saw classification30 were most prevalent, observed in 7 patients (35%) and 6 patients (30%), respectively. Arterial hypertension was present in 9 patients (45.0%) and smoking history in 9 patients (45%), while 8 patients (40%) had dyslipidemia and 2 patients (10%) had diabetes. Physical stressors were noted in 13 patients (65%) and emotional stressors in 8 patients (40%). Median troponin T level at symptom onset was 88 ng/L (IQR 44–512 ng/L; normal values < 14 ng/L).

The median time from symptom onset to acute phase CT was 6 days (IQR 3–13), and to follow-up CT 120 days (IQR 118–123), with a median interval of 113 days (IQR 102–115) between acute and follow-up phase scans. The median time between acute phase CT and CMR was 0 days (IQR − 3–1), and 0 days (IQR 0–0) for follow-up. Median heart rate during acute and follow-up CT was 66 bpm (IQR 60–75) and 57 bpm (IQR 52–63), respectively. Median volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) was 7.8 mGy (IQR 5.8–8.5) in acute phase CT and 8.1 mGy (IQR 7.4–14.2) in follow-up scans. Hematocrit levels ranged from 0.35 to 0.42 L/L. CT extracellular volume and relative lesion volume: entire population

In the 20 patients with LIE PCD-CT (n = 27 scans), mean global ECVCT was 28.5 ± 5.3%. Median lesion ECVCT was 45.2% with an interquartile range of 44.2%–46.8%. Median relative lesion myocardial volume was 5.2%, with an interquartile range of 2.4%–19.6% (Table 2). Fourteen of the 27 scans (52.9%) showed a relative lesion volume < 5% (Fig. 4A). For the acute phase CT scans (n = 19), correlation between relative lesion volume and global ECVCT was high, with a Spearman correlation coefficient of 0.938 (95% CI 0.844–0.976, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

This prospective single-center study is the first to systematically quantify ECV in patients with SCAD using LIE imaging with PCD-CT, and to compare these results to CMR as the reference standard. We believe that this might be clinically relevant because SCAD is an underexplored condition in which myocardial injury is common, yet imaging strategies remain limited1. CT-based ECV measurement may therefore provide a practical alternative when CMR is not feasible or contraindicated, and it may further support follow-up imaging to monitor myocardial recovery. Building on this rationale, this study demonstrates (1) that PCD-CT may allow reliable detection of myocardial injury using a newly introduced, quantitative, threshold-based approach for detecting myocardial scar based on ECVCT; (2) a high correlation between lesion volume and global ECVCT, indicating that in this cohort elevated global ECV values reflect the presence and extent of focal myocardial injury (3) good agreement between PCD-CT and CMR for global and lesion-specific ECV, with no significant differences between modalities.

LIE CT imaging offers complementary myocardial tissue characterization to a standard CCTA protocol with minimal additional time (approximately 5 min) and radiation dose. In our study, LIE CT was performed 5 min after contrast media administration, with an average radiation dose (CTDIvol) of 7.9 mGy. An additional important advantage of PCD-CT is its lower image noise and higher iodine contrast-to-noise ratio, which enables the use of smaller amounts of contrast media for LIE imaging compared to conventional EID-CT21. In this study, we did not change our contrast media injection protocol for CCTA administering a weight-based volume of 60 to 80 mL per patient, which is considerably lower than that required for LIE CT with previous CT scanner generations25,31.

Previous studies have illustrated the potential of LIE CT for myocardial tissue characterization in acute chest pain. Palmisano et al.31 and Esposito et al.25 showed that conventional EID-CT with LIE and ECV maps enabled the diagnosis of myocarditis, myocardial infarction, and amyloidosis in troponin-positive patients. More recently, Gkizas et al.32 demonstrated that PCD-CT–derived ECV maps can reliably detect myocardial injury in acute myocarditis with good agreement to CMR. Together, these findings support the integration of LIE imaging into clinical CT workflows when myocardial injury is suspected, particularly in patients with acute chest pain.

Our results align with prior studies demonstrating agreement in ECV quantification between CT and CMR. Aquino et al.24 and Saberwal et al.33 investigated heterogeneous cohorts predominantly composed of patients with chronic cardiac conditions such as non-ischemic and ischemic cardiomyopathy, whereas our study focused exclusively on patients with SCAD. Global ECV values were nevertheless remarkably consistent: Aquino et al. reported that dual-energy CT overestimated global ECV by 1.9% with limits of agreement from − 2.9 to 6.7%, while single-energy CT underestimated ECV by 3.0% with limits of agreement from − 6.8 to 0.7%24. Saberwal et al. found a mean difference of 1.0% between CT and CMR at the mid-ventricular level, with limits of agreement from − 4.3 to 6.4%33. In our SCAD cohort, Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a mean difference of 1.4% with limits of agreement from − 4.2 to 7.0%, underscoring the good agreement of ECVCT and ECVCMR across different patient populations, disease phases, and CT technologies.

Lesion-specific ECV quantification was a key aspect of our study. While SCAD is pathophysiologically distinct from atherosclerosis, it can mimic atherosclerotic injury patterns in extent and transmurality1. In our cohort, median lesion ECVCT was 45.2%, notably higher than the mean global ECVCT of 28.0%, consistent with findings by Garg et al.34, who reported that ECVCMR values exceeding 46% in STEMI patients correlated with infarct extent and nonviable segments at follow-up. However, absolute thresholds may differ between modalities and disease types. Importantly, lesion-specific segment-based ECVCMR quantification yielded significantly lower values than both the ROI-based ECVCMR and the threshold-based ECVCT approach. This discrepancy likely results from methodological differences: segment-based ECVCMR includes the entire myocardial segment—even if only partially affected—thus diluting the lesion-specific signal. In contrast, the ROI-based ECVCMR method focuses on visually identified areas of injury, while the threshold-based ECVCT includes all voxels above a defined threshold (≥ 40%), capturing the full extent of the lesion but potentially overestimating focal severity. The close agreement between ROI-based ECVCMR and lesion ECVCT suggests that both methods target similar focal pathology and may be more suitable for intermodal comparisons. Moreover, the CT-based threshold approach enables fully automated lesion detection, offering a reproducible and observer-independent tool for quantifying myocardial injury.

We found that a global ECVCT below 28.5% in acute phase PCD-CT scans was strongly associated with lesion volumes below 5% (PPV 82%) or 10% (PPV 100%). This suggests that low global ECVCT values may reliably indicate only minimal focal myocardial injury, as frequently observed in SCAD patients1. Prior studies have shown that infarct size itself is an important prognostic marker, with larger infarcts being associated with impaired left ventricular function and adverse outcomes28,29. In this context, a low global ECVCT may serve as an objective marker of limited myocardial damage and could be of favorable prognostic significance. Beyond its diagnostic value, it may also complement the qualitative assessment of LIE images and visual ECV maps15,20. The very high correlation between lesion volume and global ECVCT in acute phase scans in our study may therefore support the use of LIE PCD-CT for quantitative assessment of myocardial injury in the acute clinical setting, where timely diagnosis is critical and CMR may not be feasible or available.

This study has several limitations. First, it was performed at a single center using one specific PCD-CT system, which may limit generalizability. Second, the cohort size was small, reflecting the low prevalence of SCAD, particularly for follow-up scans. Third, some patients underwent both acute and follow-up imaging, introducing non-independence of data. Fourth, only a subgroup had both CT and CMR, limiting the sample for direct ECV comparisons. Fifth, lesion ECVCMR was derived from manually placed ROIs, introducing potential variability. Sixth, outliers (including one global ECVCT value < 20%) and the exclusion of two cases due to motion artifacts indicate that the method is not yet fully robust and results should be interpreted with caution. Seventh, lesion classification and volume thresholds (< 5%, < 10%, and 40%) were applied in this study to provide exploratory boundaries, but they remain non-standardized and thus require further clinical validation. Eighth, ECVCT was acquired in end-systole whereas ECVCMR was obtained in diastole, which may have introduced variability. Ninth, the predominance of female patients, particularly in the CMR subgroup, may have influenced the results, as sex-related differences in ECV are known35 and could represent a confounding factor. Finally, ECVCT calculation and visualization were generated using prototype software not yet commercially available.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated good agreement between ECV quantification using LIE PCD-CT and CMR in patients with SCAD. Both global and lesion-specific ECVCT values were comparable to ECVCMR. Use of global ECV thresholds may facilitate the quantitative identification of myocardial injury and aid visual image interpretation. These results altogether underscore the potential of LIE imaging with PCD-CT for myocardial tissue characterization in SCAD, particularly when access to CMR is limited or contraindicated. Larger multi-center studies are warranted to validate these results and determine their broader applicability.
