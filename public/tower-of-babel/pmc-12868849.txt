Polyamide nanofilms with Janus microporous framework for sustainable solvent filtration
Authors: Fuxin Zheng, Zhenxiang Pan, Yu Liao, Jiang Zhan, Songjun Fang, Jiandong Pang, Tong Zhang, Gang Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Synergistic-tuning the permselectivity and chemical stability of polymer membranes with a scalable method is an imminent demand for sustainable and energy-efficient organic solvent nanofiltration, but remains an intricate challenge. Here, we report a bifunctional molecular engineering strategy to fabricate sub-20 nm fluorinated polyamide nanofilms via scalable interfacial polymerization. Experiments and molecular simulations reveal that in situ incorporation of noncoplanar contorted segments with hydrophobic fluorine-rich pendants into a flexible hydrophilic polyamide backbone constructs a highly crosslinked 3D interconnected Janus microporous framework with rigid–flexible and hydrophilic–hydrophobic duality. This Janus structure instigates simultaneous control over the dissolution and migration of polar and nonpolar organic solvents. The resulting membranes exhibit enduring chemical stability, unprecedentedly high solvent permeance, and concomitantly low molecular weight cutoff, rivaling state-of-the-art membranes. This work provides a promising paradigm for the development of high-performance membranes to promote the sustainability of energy-intensive filtration of organic solvents over a wide range of solvent polarity.

Introduction

Rapid and selective separation of target molecules from complex matrices of similar species in organic solvents is paramount for chemical and pharmaceutical industries1,2. Conventional molecular separation and purification processes, such as distillation and evaporation, primarily rely on energy-intensive thermal operations, which account for 10–15% of global energy consumption and emit about 4.9 gigatons of CO2 each year3, unaligned with the worldwide movement towards sustainable development and low-carbon economic practices. Membrane nanofiltration that performs physical separation without phase transformation offers a prominent alternative to provide more energy- and carbon-efficient separation of organic solvents4,5. However, extending the nanofiltration concept to organic solvent systems for strategic sustainability goals is challenging, mainly due to the lack of competent membrane materials that can simultaneously offer high solvent permeance at a targeted molecular weight cutoff, long-term stability, and good scalability6–8.

The organic solvent nanofiltration (OSN) performance of a membrane is fundamentally dictated by its pore architecture, which necessitates stringent control over pore size and pore microenvironment at the molecular level9,10. Traditional polymer materials are generally solution-processable and easy to scale up, but they tend to undergo pronounced swelling and plasticization in organic solvents, which leads to expanded pore sizes with an inevitably concomitant degradation in membrane selectivity11,12. Emerging microporous materials with advanced subnanometer micropores/nanochannels, such as amorphous microporous polymers13–15, crystalline microporosity materials16,17, and 2D materials18, hold great promise for achieving precise molecular sieving capabilities. However, the relatively low solvent permeance and technical problems associated with fabricating defect-free nanofilm membranes and the manufacturing scale-up required for industrial applications can be limiting challenges for their widespread applications19. Three-dimensional (3D) crosslinked polymers show excellent solvent-swelling resistance and well-confined micropores alongside superior designability20,21. Unfortunately, the ill-designed intrinsic chemical structures of conventional 3D crosslinked polymer materials generally suffer from low solvent permeance owing to their low microporosity with limited pore interconnectivity22,23. The introduction of rigid and contorted structures and/or intrinsic permanent cavities into the polymer framework confers high microporosity and interconnected through-pore networks24,25, but the membrane fabrication is generally confined by the complex functionalization process, poor scalability, low permeance for nonpolar solvents, and deleterious permeance–selectivity tradeoff26. Furthermore, the current microporosity-enhancement strategy mainly counts on entropic regulation of solvent transmembrane diffusion by providing more interconnected nanochannels for migration without giving sufficient attention to the attributes of entropy modulation27. We believe that synergistic regulation of the microporosity and solvent affinity without compromising chemical stability through rational molecular design of the polymer chemical structure represents an effective strategy for constructing high-performance OSN membranes, but a notable research gap remains.

Herein, a feasible bifunctional molecular engineering strategy that can synergistically tailor the microporosity and solvent affinity of polyamide (PA) nanofilms was developed to leverage the dual merits of enthalpy and entropy modulation by using versatile aromatic diamines as micropore-generating and pore-environment-modulating units through scalable interfacial polymerization (Fig. 1a). Ultimately, this bifunctional design strategy culminates in the de novo synthesis of highly crosslinked 3D microporous fluorine-rich PA membranes with excellent chemical stability, unprecedentedly high solvent permeance (i.e., methanol ~ 32.5, acetone ~ 78.7, and hexane ~ 47.4 L m‒2 h‒1 bar‒1) and concomitantly low molecular weight cutoff of ~ 300 Da over a wide range of solvent polarity. Results

Rational design and synthesis of microporous PA nanofilms with Janus frameworks

4,4’-oxybis[3-(trifluoromethyl)aniline] (6FODA), containing two 4-aminophenyl groups linked by a phenyl ether bond with one ortho trifluoromethyl (–CF3) substituent on each benzene ring, was identified as the building block for polymerization with trimesoyl chloride (TMC) to construct a 3D crosslinked microporous PA polymer. We envision that the steric hindrance effect of the two bulky –CF3 substituents at the ortho position of the para-linked ether bond in 6FODA would generate non-coplanar contorted aromatic fragments with subtle chain rigidity in the resulting PA, which resonate with flexible amide linkages to construct a PA framework with highly interconnected hourglass-shaped network voids, conferring efficient size-sieving capability and rapid migration of solvent molecules. Simultaneously, the fluorine-rich –CF3 pendants on the polymer backbone appropriately modulate the hydrophobicity and polarity of PA, providing another degree of freedom for tuning solvent dissolution and anti-swelling stability (Fig. 1b). In the same vein, 4,4’-diaminodiphenyl ether (ODA) and 2,2’-bis(trifluoromethyl)benzidine (PFMB) without ortho –CF3 substituents or flexible phenyl ether linkages were employed as model monomers for comparison, through which a better understanding of the intrinsic correlation between the membrane structure and solvent transport could be gained, critical for rational membrane design but remains a critical knowledge gap.

Molecular spatial configurations of the three aromatic diamines reveal that the torsion of the two benzene rings through the phenyl ether or biphenyl linkage generates a spiral noncoplanar conformation with an inter-benzene ring dihedral angle of 75.2–81.6°. The flexible phenyl ether bonds with a bond angle of about 118° in ODA and 6FODA offer additional polymer chain flexibility and torsional freedom (Fig. 1c). In-depth analysis of the dihedral angle distributions at different junctions of the PA backbones after crosslinking with TMC show that the torsion of the phenyl ether bonds in 6FODA is restrained by the steric hindrance of the ortho –CF3 substituents. The bond rotation angle is restricted within a range of 240°, while the ether bonds in ODA rotate 360° freely (Fig. 1d). The torsional energy barriers of the corresponding bond linkages in 6FODA-TMC PA are approximately one order of magnitude higher than those of ODA-TMC PA, and the torsion angles are mainly concentrated at 60° and − 120° (Supplementary Fig. 1). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further demonstrate the formation of noncoplanar conformations in the spatially stacked polymer network of 6FODA-TMC PA, which frustrates the efficient packing of the polyamide chains and thus results in relatively large microporosity with enhanced interconnectivity of network voids (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The contorted, noncoplanar hydrophobic segmental structures resonating with the flexible hydrophilic amide linkages ultimately form a 3D crosslinked Janus microporous PA framework that seamlessly integrates rigidity and flexibility as well as hydrophilicity and hydrophobicity (Fig. 1e).

The nucleophilic addition-elimination reactivity of the three diamine monomers was further examined using density functional theory (DFT) to calculate the electrostatic potential (ESP), average local ionization energy (ALIE), nucleophilicity index, and local electron attachment energy (LEAE). These multidimensional electronic descriptors indicate that they all possess sufficient reactivity to undergo rapid polycondensation with TMC, with the nucleophilicity indices following an order of ODA (4.26 eV) > 6FODA (3.68 eV) > PFMB (3.56 eV) (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5), enabling the fabrication of PA nanofilms via scalable liquid-liquid interfacial polymerization (IP). Unfortunately, the recruited aromatic diamine monomers are primarily composed of aromatic rings with hydrophobic substituents, and they exhibit poor solubility in water at ambient conditions. To tackle this issue, we developed an ionic liquid (IL) assisted IP process that leverages the superior dissolution ability of [C4mim]BF4 to enhance the solubility of the diamine monomers in water (Supplementary Fig. 6)28, allowing us to perform the IP synthesis via a highly scalable way (Fig. 1f). The high solubility of the aromatic diamines in a binary mixture of [C4mim]BF4 and water was confirmed at concentrations of more than 1.0% (wt/v) (Supplementary Fig. 7), which was further corroborated by the formation of the robust, self-sustaining PA nanofilms at the free interface (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Chemical and microporous structural characterization of PA nanofilm membranes

PA thin-film composite (TFC) membranes were prepared directly on porous polyacrylonitrile (PAN) ultrafiltration substrate via in situ IP, and the resulting membranes are denoted as 6FODA-M, ODA-M, and PFMB-M, respectively. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and optical microscope surface images confirm the formation of flat, continuous PA nanofilms devoid of visible cracks, pinholes, or other defects (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Figs. 9–11). Their intact structures are further substantiated by the uniform distribution of surface elements acquired by SEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS, Supplementary Fig. 12). Atomic force microscope (AFM) surface images reveal that the PA nanofilms have very smooth surfaces with low roughness (Ra) of 3.7–5.6 nm (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 13). AFM height profile analysis shows that the accurate thicknesses of the free-standing PA nanofilms are less than 20 nm, with the specific thickness of 6FODA-M PA being ~ 13 nm, approaching the thickness of the thinnest polymer film reported to date (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). The formation of such ultrathin and smooth PA nanofilms can be attributed to the retarded diffusion of the bulkier aromatic diamine monomers during IP and their relatively low reactivity with TMC relative to archetypical diamines like phenylenediamine and piperazine (Supplementary Figs. 16–21)29,30. Surface zeta potential measurements disclose that all three PA nanofilms are negatively charged at neutral pH with an isoelectric point of less than pH = 5 (Fig. 2e). Attenuated total reflection–Fourier transform infrared (ATR–FTIR, Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 20), Raman spectra (Supplementary Fig. 22), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. 2g) confirm their chemical structures as aromatic PAs. Quantification of elemental composition by high-resolution XPS spectra illuminates the crosslinking degrees of ODA-M, 6FODA-M, and PFMB-M PA nanofilms, which are determined to be 76.3, 68.1, and 30.0% (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Figs. 23–26 and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), respectively, aligning closely with the distinct amination reactivity of the diamine monomers. Owing to the presence of –CF3 groups on the PA skeletons of 6FODA-M and PFMB-M, these two membranes exhibit distinctly larger surface water contact angles of 95.5 and 100.4°, respectively, compared with ODA-M (72.9°) and conventional PA membranes (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 27 and Supplementary Table 3). Vapor sorption experiments reveal that hydrophilic ODA-M preferentially adsorbs polar methanol, while highly fluorinated 6FODA-M shows a stronger affinity toward nonpolar hexane, confirming the effective regulation of solvent affinity by the –CF3 substituents (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 28)31.Fig. 2Morphological and structural characterization of microporous PA nanofilms.a Surface SEM images of the PAN substrate and 6FODA-M PA nanofilm. b Optical microscope image of 6FODA-M PA nanofilm deposited on a silicon wafer. c 3D AFM image of 6FODA-M PA nanofilm with the calculated surface roughness. d AFM height images of PA nanofilms on a silicon wafer and the height profiles corresponding to the areas marked by the lines. e Surface zeta potential profiles as a function of pH value of PA TFC nanofilm membranes. f, g ATR–FTIR spectra (f) and XPS spectra (g) of PAN substrate and PA TFC nanofilm membranes. h Cross-linking degree and surface water contact angle of PA TFC nanofilm membranes. i Vapor sorption isotherms of ODA-M and 6FODA-M membranes in hexane.

The average interchain spacing of three PA nanofilms was estimated from the average d-spacing of their powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks32, which fall in the range of 4.7–5.6 Å with an order of PFMB-M > 6FODA-M > ODA-M (Fig. 3a). These values are relatively larger than those (i.e., ~ 3.9 Å) typically observed in conventional PA nanofilms derived from m-phenylenediamine (MPD) and TMC33. The specific surface areas of the PAs were obtained from their CO2 sorption isotherms at 273 K using the Langmuir model (Fig. 3b). 6FODA-M and ODA-M show remarkably higher Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of more than 110 m2 g‒1 relative to PFMB-M (86.6 m2 g‒1), indicating the compromised microporosity of the latter albeit it has a relatively larger average interchain distance (Fig. 3a, b). The corresponding pore size distribution analysis suggests that the PA nanofilms possess subnanometer micropores, predominantly featuring a pore diameter distribution around 4.7–8.9 Å (Supplementary Fig. 29). Similar trends in surface area and pore size distribution were observed from the N2 sorption isotherms of the three PAs at 77 K (Supplementary Fig. 30), which further manifest the effectiveness of the rigid and flexible Janus framework in improving membrane microporosity. MD simulations were further performed to construct realistic structural models to glean molecular-level insights into the microporous structure of the PAs. Compared with PFMB-M, 6FODA-M and ODA-M exhibit more fractional free volume (FFV) with elevated surface areas across divergent probe sizes (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 31), closely matching the experimental data. Notably, although 6FODA-M and ODA-M possess larger surface areas and more FFV than PFMB-M, they show more uniform distributions of microvoids (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 32). MD simulations of polymer chain dynamics upon solvation show that 6FODA-M PA exhibits significantly retarded dynamic changes in polymer chain conformations in hexane relative to ODA-M PA. The change in its root mean squared displacement (RMSD) curve is approximately three times smaller (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 33), implying exceptional resistance to undergo large polymer chain motions even in high-affinity solvents35. Further inspection of the trajectories and dynamical cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) of different regions in the PAs discloses that the orientational dynamics of the polymer backbone mainly originate from the torsion of the phenyl ether bonds, which was further corroborated by the strong correlation between the –CF3 group displacement and the movement of the benzene rings of 6FODA. Meanwhile, the –CF3 substituents acting as steric hindrances further suppress the rotation of the phenyl ether bonds, making 6FODA-M more resistant to large polymer chain motions in solvents (Fig. 1d). Benefiting from the rigid-flexible Janus structure, 6FODA-M combines high microporosity, narrowly distributed subnanometer micropores, and excellent chemical stability to facilitate ultrafast solvent permeation and precise molecular sieving in OSN applications (Fig. 3g).

The OSN performance of the PA TFC membranes prepared under the optimum conditions (Supplementary Figs. 34–39) was surveyed using a wide spectrum of organic solvents with different viscosities (η), solubility parameters (δ), and molar diameters (dm) (Supplementary Table 4). Coinciding with our hypothesis, bifunctional engineering of the PA microporous structure conspicuously enhances the solvent permeance over a wide range of polarity, with the effect being most pronounced when both microporosity and pore environment are finely tuned. As presented in Fig. 4a, b, 6FODA-M shows unprecedented acetone and methanol permeance of 78.7 and 32.5 L m‒2 h‒1 bar‒1 (abbreviated as LMH/bar), respectively, far exceeding those acquired by ODA-M and PFMB-M. 6FODA-M also exhibits substantially high permeance to nonpolar solvents, with a hexane permeance of 47.4 LMH/bar, representing 39.5 and 2.0 times as high as that of ODA-M and PFMB-M. Further analysis reveals that the permeance enhancement of 6FODA-M over ODA-M (P6FODA-M/PODA-M) strongly correlates with the dm and the inverse of δ of the solvents (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 41), with a more profound increase for low-polarity solvents. In contrast, the P6FODA-M/PPFMB-M shows a better correlation with the inverse of η, likely due to the similar solvent-membrane interactions of 6FODA-M and PFMB-M. Notably, 6FODA-M shows approximately 10 times larger permeance than ODA-M for polar solvents, whereas the permeation rates toward moderately polar solvents (i.e., acetone and toluene) and nonpolar solvents (i.e., hexane and heptane) are nearly 11-fold and 40-fold higher, albeit having similar pore size and microporosity. Conversely, 6FODA-M exhibits nearly doubled solvent permeance over PFMB-M with far better solute rejections, implying that dissolution-driven transport mechanisms may play a crucial role in the permeation of organic solvents.

The strikingly diminished surface free energies of 6FODA-M and PFMB-M relative to ODA-M signify that the abundant –CF3 groups in conjunction with hydrophobic benzene rings on the PA skeleton act as strong binding sites to promote the dissolution of low-polarity and nonpolar solvents. This conclusion was further corroborated by the substantially escalated Gibbs adhesion free energy to nonpolar solvents like hexane (Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Tables 5–7). Hansen solubility parameters (HSP) comparison of organic solvents with the aromatic diamines and the derived PAs in the ternary diagram further reveals that the fluorinated 6FODA-M and PFMB-M have similar HSP with low-polarity solvents37, while the HSP of ODA-M are closer to those of polar solvents (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 43 and Supplementary Table 8). The energy landscapes of membrane-solvent affinity obtained from DFT calculations reveal that the hydrophobic 6FODA-M and PFMB-M undergo stronger van der Waals interactions with nonpolar hexane, which ultimately leads to their greater negative adsorption energy and higher desorption energy than ODA-M (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 44). The mutual interaction analyses further demonstrate that the negative binding energies of ODA-M with polar solvents such as acetone are relatively higher than those with low-polarity solvents, whereas 6FODA-M and PFMB-M experience larger negative binding energies with low-polarity toluene and nonpolar hexane (Fig. 4e), resembling the trends observed in surface energy and HSP analyses. MD simulations reveal that the molecular density of hexane surrounding the 6FODA-M is dramatically higher than that of ODA-M, and the density of hexane molecules near the –CF3 groups is substantially larger than those around the benzene rings and amide linkages, substantiating that the strong affinity to nonpolar solvents mainly stems from the –CF3 functional moieties (Fig. 4f, g and Supplementary Fig. 45).

Precise and stable molecular separations

The rejection performance of the PA TFC membranes in different solvent systems was evaluated using an array of dye molecules as the model solutes (Supplementary Table 9). The membranes exhibit electrostatic exclusion selectivity toward charged molecules with similar molecular weights in polar solvents such as methanol (Supplementary Fig. 46), mainly due to the charge-charge Coulomb forces afforded by the abundant surface carboxyl groups (Fig. 2e). The strong negative electrostatic potentials imparted by highly polarized C–F bonds in 6FODA-M and PFMB-M further strengthen this effect (Supplementary Fig. 47)38. On the other hand, the order of solute retention rates generally aligns with the sequence of solute size, suggesting that steric sieving remains a critical mechanism for solute discrimination (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Figs. 48–50). The obtained MWCO for anionic molecules obeys the order of ODA-M (339 Da) > 6FODA-M (346 Da) > PFMB-M (424 Da), consistent with the pore size and cross-linking degree of the PA nanofilms. On the contrary, rejection profiles of 6FODA-M in moderately polar solvent acetone and nonpolar solvent hexane exhibit a good correlation with the molecular size of the solutes regardless of their charge (Fig. 5b, c and Supplementary Figs. 51 and 52), implying that molecular sieving dominates solute rejection, likely due to the limited dissociation of dye molecules in low-polarity solvents31. Collectively, 6FODA-M features unparalleled combinations of permeance and small MWCO over a wide range of solvent polarity (i.e., in methanol, acetone, and hexane/heptane), far exceeding those of previously reported OSN membranes (Fig. 5d–f and Supplementary Tables 10–12), which represents a step-change in breaking the pernicious permeance-selectivity tradeoff. Expanded solvent filtration tests demonstrate that 6FODA-M shows superior capability for discriminating ångström-sized molecules, irrespective of polar or nonpolar solvent systems (Fig. 5g and Supplementary Figs. 54–56). Taking the binary mixture of methyl red (MR, 269.3 Da) and indigo carmine (INCA, 466.4 Da) as an example, an exceptional separation factor of 65.9 and a high methanol permeance of 27.1 LMH/bar were achieved (Fig. 5h, i). More important, 6FODA-M performs equally well for molecular sieving in moderately polar acetone and nonpolar hexane, which exhibits an exceedingly high methylene blue (MB, 319.9 Da)/acid fuchsin (AF, 585.5 Da) separation factor of 78.6 with an acetone permeance of 73.3 LMH/bar (Fig. 5h). During the fractionation of binary mixtures of azobenzene (ABZ, 182.2 Da)/eriochrome black T (EBT, 461.4 Da) and ABZ/orange G (OG, 452.4 Da) in hexane, 6FODA-M showcases exceptional solvent permeance up to 36.6 and 37.8 LMH/bar, respectively, accompanied by unparalleled selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 56), making our membrane stand out among the reported polymer membranes.

We further challenged the 6FODA-M membrane to extract pharmaceutical active ingredients from organic solvents using small drug molecules with molecular weights ranging from 250.3 to 505 Da as the probe solutes and methanol as the representative solvent (Supplementary Table 14). The rejection profile reveals that a small MWCO of 375 Da was attained, implying that 6FODA-M can selectively fractionate coexisting pharmaceutical components (Fig. 5j). For example, carbenicillin disodium (CBD, 422.4 Da) was almost completely rejected with a retention rate of 94.0%, while sulfadiazine (SDZ, 250.3 Da) achieved a permeation of 17.8%, resulting in a high separation factor of 13.7 with high methanol permeance of more than 27.3 LMH/bar (Supplementary Fig. 57). It is worth noting that even when separating binary mixtures, the 6FODA-M membrane maintained a strong retention capacity of 85–95% for larger pharmaceutical molecules, while selectively permeating smaller ones (rejection < 30%) (Supplementary Fig. 58). These results confirm its powerful molecular sieving ability under competitive diffusion conditions, highlighting the promising feasibility of 6FODA-M in the ultrafast recovery of organic solvents and the precise molecular fractionation of similarly sized molecules in organic solvents.

The remarkable operation stability of the 6FODA-M membrane was evident by its consistent solute rejection and solvent permeance during continuous filtration tests over 120 h and the excellent adaptability to testing pressures (i.e., from 2 to 15 bar) and feed solute concentrations (i.e., from 25 to 200 ppm) (Supplementary Figs. 59–61). Furthermore, the 6FODA-M membrane and its free-standing PA nanofilm maintain their intact morphology and separation performance even after being immersed in many solvents of different polarities for 7 days (Supplementary Fig. 62), signifying their superior swelling resistance and chemical stability, which is of great significance for broadening their applications in different solvent systems. Alternating filtration tests in methanol, acetone, and hexane were performed to further illuminate the structural stability of 6FODA-M against solvent polarity gradients. As expected, the separation permeance remained stable over the cycle (Fig. 5k), underscoring the operational stability and flexibility of the developed PA TFC nanofilm membranes in the nanofiltration of organic solvents. The 6FODA-M membrane also exhibits comparable permselectivity and stability under highly turbulent crossflow filtration conditions, demonstrating its potential for practical applications (Supplementary Figs. 63–64).

Discussion

Membrane nanofiltration could potentially revolutionize the separation and purification of organic solvents as a sustainable and energy-efficient separation technology. This work underscores the feasibility of a bifunctional molecular engineering approach to synergistically tune the chemical stability, microporosity, and solvent affinity of 3D crosslinked Janus PA nanofilms through in situ IP. The resulting membranes exhibit enduring solvent stability along with exceptionally high solvent permeance and concomitantly low molecular weight cutoff over a wide range of solvent polarity. In light of the diverse array of monomer chemistry available for constructing a polymer nanofilm, the implemented design strategy and the fundamental understanding of the correlation between the material structure and separation performance hold broad prospects for the rational design and fabrication of next-generation polymer membranes for sustainable and economical liquid-phase organic solvent separations.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Trimesoyl chloride (TMC, 98.0%), 4,4’-diaminodiphenyl ether (ODA, 98.0%), 4,4’-oxybis(3-(trifluoromethyl)aniline) (6FODA, 99.0%), and 2,2’-bis(trifluoromethyl)benzidine (PFMB, 98.0%) ordered from Macklin (Shanghai, China) were used as the monomers for the synthesis of free-standing polyamide nanofilms and polyamide thin-film composite (TFC) membranes. Polyacrylonitrile (PAN) ultrafiltration membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 100 kDa from Lanjing Membrane Technology Co., Ltd., China was used as the substrate for preparing the polyamide TFC membranes. 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([C4mim]BF4, 99.0%) and hexane (98.0%) were purchased from Macklin. Methanol, ethanol, isopropanol (IPA), acetone, tetrahydrofuran (THF), toluene, hexane, and heptane obtained from Macklin were used as the organic solvents for membrane performance tests. Azobenzene (ABZ), methyl red (MR), methyl orange (MO), orange G (OG), eriochrome black T (EBT), indigo carmine (INCA), acid fuchsin (AF), congo red (CR), methylene blue (MB), crystal violet (CV), rhodamine B (RB), victoria blue B (VBB), alcian blue 8GX (AB), and vitamin B12 (VB12) from Macklin were used as the model molecules for molecular sieving experiments. Sulfadiazine (SDZ), ciprofloxacin (CIP), carbenicillin disodium (CBD), moxifloxacin hydrochloride (MHX), oxytetracycline (OTC), cefotaxime sodium salt (CS), tetracycline hydrochloride (TC), and clindamycin phosphate (CLP) from Macklin were used as the model molecules for drug ingredient separation experiments. Glycerol and diiodomethane were obtained from Macklin for contact angle measurements. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) purchased from Macklin was used to isolate the polyamide layer of the TFC membrane by dissolving the PAN substrate. Unless otherwise indicated, all chemicals and reagents were used as received without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was supplied by a Millipore-D 24 UV ultrapure water integrated system (Millipore Instruments, 18.2 MΩ cm).

Synthesis of free-standing polyamide nanofilms

Free-standing polyamide (PA) nanofilms were prepared at the aqueous solution/hexane interface by interfacial polymerization (IP). The diamine monomer was first dissolved in a binary mixture of water and [C4mim]BF4 ionic liquid (IL) (the volume ratio of IL to water was 1/1) at a concentration of 1.0 wt/v%. The TMC monomer was dissolved in hexane at a concentration of 0.1 wt/v%. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, the diamine solution was first poured into a beaker, and then the TMC solution was slowly added to form a stable aqueous solution/hexane interface. Afterwards, the beaker was covered, and the IP reaction was carried out at room temperature for 30 min. The formed PA nanofilm was extracted from the interface and washed alternately with ethanol and DI water.

Preparation of polyamide thin-film composite (TFC) nanofilm membranes

The polyamide TFC nanofilm membranes were prepared on the PAN ultrafiltration substrate by the in situ IP method. Specifically, the PAN substrate was immersed in IPA for 30 min and then thoroughly washed with DI water. The cleaned PAN substrate was air-dried and fixed on a glass plate with a Teflon frame. After that, an aqueous solution of [C4mim]BF4 ionic liquid (IL) and water (the volume ratio of IL to water was 1/1) containing 1.0 wt/v% of the diamine monomer (ODA, 6FODA, or PFMB) was poured onto the top surface of the PAN substrate. After 10 min of contact, the aqueous solution was drained, and the residual liquid was removed by filter paper. Subsequently, a 0.1 wt/v% TMC hexane solution was poured onto the diamine-saturated PAN substrate to initiate the IP reaction. After reacting at room temperature for 2 min, the excess TMC solution was drained, and the resulting membrane was cured at 60 °C for 10 min. The prepared polyamide TFC membrane was washed several times with water and then stored in deionized water at 4 °C for further characterization and performance tests. The polyamide TFC membrane prepared with 6FODA was named 6FODA-M. An array of 6FODA-M membranes was prepared to survey the optimal synthesis conditions by optimizing reaction parameters such as IP time and concentrations of diamine and TMC monomers. ODA-M and PFMB-M polyamide TFC membranes were prepared for comparison using ODA and PFMB as the diamine monomers, respectively. Of note, the preparation procedures of the three polyamide TFC membranes were identical, except that the reaction time of the PFMB-M membrane was extended to 20 min due to the relatively low reactivity of the PFMB monomer.

Preparation of polyamide powder for characterization

For the synthesis of powdered polyamides, 150 mL of an aqueous solution (the volume ratio of IL to water was 1/1) containing 1.0 wt/v% of the diamine monomer (ODA, 6FODA, or PFMB) was gradually mixed with 300 mL of a hexane solution containing 0.1 wt/v% of TMC in a beaker to initiate the polymerization reaction. The mixture was vigorously stirred at 300 rpm using a magnetic rotor at room temperature for 30 min. After the reaction was completed, the resulting polyamide powder was obtained through filtration and subsequently washed three times with hexane, ethanol, and water, respectively. The as-synthesized polyamide powder was dried in a vacuum oven at 100 °C for 24 h.

Characterization methods

The surface morphology, roughness, and thickness of the polyamide nanofilms and polyamide TFC membranes were observed by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Scientific, USA) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, NanoWizard 4, Bruker, Germany). The chemical features of the polyamide nanofilms were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet IN10, Thermo Fisher, USA), Raman spectroscopy (XploRA PLUS, Horiba, Japan), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher, USA). The specific surface area and microporous properties of the polyamide powders were evaluated by CO2 and N2 sorption isotherms using an automated gas adsorption analyzer (ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, USA). The d-spacing values of the polyamide polymer chains were determined by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D8 Discover, Germany). The surface hydrophilicity of the membranes was assessed via solvent contact angle measurements using a contact angle goniometer (HARKE-SPCA, HARKE, China). The solvent affinity of polyamide was determined using a gas and vapor adsorption analyzer (BELSORP-max II, MicrotracBEL, Japan) to obtain sorption isotherms for methanol and hexane vapors. The surface zeta potential was measured on a SurPASS electrokinetic analyzer (SurPASS 3, Anton Paar, Austria). The detailed procedures for each measurement are included in the Supporting Materials (Supplementary Text 2).

Organic solvent filtration performance tests

Unless otherwise specified, the organic solvent filtration tests were conducted on a lab-made dead-end filtration device with an effective membrane area of 12.57 cm2 at room temperature. Continuous stirring was maintained at 500 rpm throughout the whole test to reduce external concentration polarization. Supplementary tests were also conducted on a lab-made crossflow filtration system with a volumetric feed flow rate of 25 L h−1 and an effective membrane area of 25 cm2. Before the test, the membrane sample was pre-compressed in the test solvent for 30 min to stabilize the membrane. The pure solvent permeance of different organic solvents, including methanol, ethanol, IPA, acetone, THF, toluene, hexane, and heptane, was then measured. After that, solutions of various dye molecules (e.g., ABZ, MR, MO, OG, EBT, INCA, AF, CR, MB, CV, RB, VBB, AB, and VB12) dissolved in different organic solvents were used for permeation flux and solute retention rate measurements. Expanded organic solvent filtration tests

The organic solvent filtration performance of the benchmarking 6FODA-M TFC membrane for drug molecule separation in methanol was tested using the same procedure and operating parameters as the dye molecule rejection tests mentioned in the previous section. After the membrane was pre-stabilized, a methanol solution containing 5 ppm of drug molecule (e.g., SDZ, CIP, CBD, MHX, OTC, CS, TC, or CLP) was used as the feed. The stable methanol permeance was recorded, and the concentrations of drug molecules in the feed (Cf) and permeate (Cp) were tested at a specific wavelength using a UV-vis spectrometer.

For the binary mixture fractionation performance tests, the two molecules were dissolved in organic solvents of different polarities to prepare the feed solution. In the methanol system, the concentration of each solute was 25 ppm, and the binary solute pairs tested in this study included MR/INCA, MB/OG, MR/VBB, and ABZ/AF. In the acetone system, the concentration of each solute was 10 ppm, and the binary mixtures tested included MB/AF and MB/EBT. In the hexane system, the concentration of each solute was 5 ppm, and the binary solute mixtures tested included ABZ/EBT and ABZ/OG.
