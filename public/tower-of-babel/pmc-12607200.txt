Prevalence and risk factors of adverse drug events with dolutegravir-based regimens among Thai people living with HIV: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sutthipun Suriya, Siriyaporn Wanitchakorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDolutegravir (DTG)-based antiretroviral therapy (ART) is a preferred first-line treatment for HIV/AIDS, but concerns about adverse drug events (ADEs) persist. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of ADEs and identify associated independent risk factors among Thai people living with HIV (PLWH) on DTG-based regimens for at least one year.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study examined 1,270 Thai individuals living with HIV receiving a DTG/3TC/TDF regimen for ≥ 1 year at a secondary care hospital between 2021 and 2023. The primary outcome was any ADE (Grade ≥ 1) documented during the first 12 months of DTG therapy. Multivariate logistic regression, adjusted for confounders including sex, was used to identify independent predictors of ADEs.ResultsAmong 1,270 participants, 684 (53.86%) experienced at least one ADE. The cohort was predominantly ART-experienced, with a median duration since HIV diagnosis of 6.00 years (IQR 4.00–9.00 years). The most frequent ADEs included a decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (17.72%), anemia (15.43%), weight gain (9.29%), and nausea (7.80%). Independent risk factors associated with increased ADE odds were: older age (≥ 40 years vs. 18–29 years, global p = 0.040), psychiatric comorbidity (aOR 3.13; 95% CI 1.02–9.56; p = 0.046), history of cryptococcosis (aOR 1.45; 95% CI 1.05–2.13; p = 0.035) ,underweight status (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) (aOR 2.22; 95% CI 1.50–3.38; p < 0.001), and baseline eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73m2 (aOR 1.45; 95% CI 1.09–1.93; p < 0.011). Conversely, a longer duration on ART prior to DTG initiation (> 15 years) was associated with a 68% reduction in ADE odds (aOR 0.32; 95% CI 0.14–0.75; p = 0.008).ConclusionsOver half (53.86%) of patients on long-term DTG-based therapy experienced at least one ADE. Risk was significantly elevated in older adults, those with psychiatric illness, previous cryptococcosis, underweight status, or lower baseline eGFR. Healthcare providers should implement proactive monitoring and tailored management strategies for these high-risk patients to optimize ART safety and efficacy outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-025-00817-6.Keywords: Dolutegravir, HIV, Adverse drug events, Thailand, Antiretroviral therapy (ART)

Introduction

The advent of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has revolutionized the prognosis of people living with HIV (PLWH), significantly reducing HIV-related morbidity and mortality and enabling long-term viral suppression and immune restoration [1]. Among current ART options, dolutegravir (DTG)—a second-generation integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)—has become a cornerstone agent. Its potent antiviral efficacy, high genetic barrier to resistance, favorable tolerability, and convenient once-daily dosing contribute to rapid viral load suppression and a reduced risk of resistance development compared to older antiretrovirals [2, 3].

Consequently, DTG-based regimens are widely endorsed as preferred first-line therapy in numerous international and national HIV treatment guidelines, including recommendations from the World Health Organization (WHO) to improve global treatment outcomes. In Thailand, DTG was officially incorporated into the National HIV Treatment Guidelines in 2019 as part of the preferred first-line regimen, replacing efavirenz-based combinations. This transition marked a nationwide implementation of WHO-recommended standard of care, aimed at enhancing viral suppression, reducing drug resistance, and minimizing long-term toxicities. Since then, DTG-based therapy has become the standard regimen provided under the national universal health coverage program across all public hospitals [4, 5].

However, like all medications, DTG is associated with potential adverse drug events (ADEs) including neuropsychiatric effects such as insomnia, anxiety, depression [6], gastrointestinal disturbances [7], weight gain [8], and, less frequently, renal impairment [9]. Notably, neuropsychiatric events appear more prevalent in older individuals and those with pre-existing mental health conditions [10, 11]. Furthermore, observed weight gain, particularly in women and individuals of African ancestry, has raised concerns regarding long-term metabolic consequences [12, 13]. The precise clinical significance and underlying mechanisms of these ADEs, especially in diverse populations, warrant further investigation.

Current safety data are largely derived from studies in Caucasian and African cohorts. However, the generalizability of these findings to Southeast Asian populations is uncertain, given known pharmacogenomic differences that may influence DTG metabolism and toxicity profiles [14]. This knowledge gap is particularly relevant in Thailand, where rapid nationwide adoption of DTG as first-line therapy underscores the importance of validating its real-world safety profile in this population. Failure to address this evidence gap may lead to under-recognition of clinically significant ADEs, potentially undermining treatment outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and identify independent risk factors associated with ADEs among Thai PLWH receiving DTG-based regimens for at least one year at a secondary care hospital. The findings will provide actionable insights for risk stratification, enhance clinical monitoring, and support personalized management strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Pakchongnana Hospital, a secondary care hospital in Nakhon Ratchasima province, Thailand. The hospital serves as a major referral center for HIV/AIDS care within the region. The study utilized data from patient medical records spanning July 1, 2021, to July 31, 2023. All data, including baseline characteristics and documented ADEs, were retrospectively extracted from existing patient documentation following the initiation of the DTG regimen.

Study population, eligibility criteria, and sample size

The study population comprised Thai adults (≥ 18 years) living with HIV who initiated or transitioned to a DTG-based antiretroviral regimen at the hospital’s HIV/AIDS clinic and had at least one year of documented follow-up. Patients were excluded if they: (I) were younger than 18 years; (II) had documented non-adherence to ART (defined as < 95% adherence based on pill counts or self-reported data recorded in the medical records); (III) had incomplete information on antiretroviral therapy; (IV) had missing baseline clinical data at the time of DTG initiation; or (V) had incomplete documentation of ADEs during the one-year follow-up period.

A total of 1,923 medical records were screened. Of these, 499 records were excluded due to age < 18 years, non-adherence, incomplete ART data, or missing baseline information. An additional 154 records were excluded due to incomplete ADE reporting, defined as missing key follow-up documentation such as essential laboratory data (e.g., eGFR, ALT, lipid profile, hemoglobin) or clinical symptom records required to reliably determine the presence or absence of ADEs throughout the observation period. The final study cohort therefore included 1,270 eligible participants (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient inclusion for the analysis of adverse drug events among HIV patients receiving dolutegravir-based regimens

As a retrospective cohort study, all eligible patients within the defined study period were included to maximize representativeness. The adequacy of the final sample size (n = 1,270) was verified using the Kelsey formula for comparing two proportions in a cohort study, assuming a two-sided α = 0.05, 80% power, a baseline risk in the unexposed group of 33.1% (based on previous DTG cohort studies [15, 16]), and a relative risk of 1.5. The minimum required sample size was estimated at 740 participants, indicating that the achieved cohort size provided sufficient statistical power to detect clinically meaningful associations between risk factors and ADE occurrence.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received approval from the Ethical Committee for Human Research at Pakchongnana Hospital (Approval No.: ECPC-2566008). The study is registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20230904001).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics and ADE prevalence. Continuous variables were expressed as median (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test; categorical variables were presented as frequencies (%) and compared using the χ² test. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis and clinically relevant covariates were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of ADEs. Multicollinearity was assessed using VIF (< 2.5). Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Study population

Initially, 1,923 patient medical records were screened for eligibility. After applying exclusion criteria based on age, adherence, and completeness of baseline, treatment, and adverse drug event (ADE) information, a total of 1,270 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 1,270 study participants are presented in Table 1. The study population had a median age of 45.00 years (IQR: 36.00–52.00) and 57.10% were male. The median BMI was 21.88 kg/m² (IQR: 19.84–24.56), indicating that participants were generally within a healthy weight range at baseline. Lifestyle factors showed relatively low proportions of current smokers (3.86%) and herbal product or supplement users (2.05%). However, nearly one-quarter (24.25%) of the participants reported alcohol consumption. The most prevalent comorbidities among participants were hypertension (14.49%) and dyslipidemia (8.03%).

Regarding HIV-specific clinical characteristics, 53.00% of participants had received prior ART before switching to a DTG-based regimen, with treatment durations typically ranging from 5 to 10 years before enrollment. Among those who switched regimens, the median duration of prior ART was significantly longer in the AZT-based group (8.00 years, IQR: 6.00–11.00) compared with the TDF-based group (4.00 years, IQR: 2.00–6.00), reflecting differences in historical nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI) exposure patterns. Baseline laboratory and anthropometric parameters—including eGFR, Hb, ALT and body weight—were also analyzed across regimen groups. No significant baseline differences were observed, and the detailed comparisons are presented in Supplementary Table S1. None of the participants had active hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection at baseline.

Prevalence of adverse drug events (ADEs)

Overall, 684 participants (53.86%) experienced at least one documented ADE within the 12-month follow-up period. The prevalence of specific ADEs is shown in Fig. 2. The four most frequently reported ADEs were a decline in eGFR (17.72%), anemia (15.43%), weight gain (9.29%) and nausea (7.80%).

Prevalence of specific adverse drug events in the study population (n = 1270) within 1 year of Dolutegravir-based regimen initiation

ADEs were observed across all prior regimen groups (DTG-naïve, prior AZT, prior TDF) (Fig. 3). Decline in eGFR and anemia were numerically, though not statistically, more frequent in participants with prior exposure to TDF (19.59%) and AZT (17.91%) based-regimens, respectively, compared to DTG-naïve patients (14.89% and 13.16%). These patterns suggest potential cumulative regimen-related susceptibilities among ART-experienced individuals.

Prevalence of specific adverse drug events by prior antiretroviral regimen

Within the same patient cohort, longitudinal comparison of key laboratory and anthropometric parameters after 12 months of DTG therapy demonstrated significant clinical and biochemical changes (Table 2). Specifically, we observed significant immune recovery, with median CD4 count increasing from 385.00 to 431.00 cells/µL (p < 0.001). Corresponding to the clinical observations, median BMI rose significantly from 21.88 to 23.13 kg/m² (p < 0.001), reflecting mild weight gain. A small but statistically significant decline in eGFR was noted—from 105.00 to 88.00 mL/min/1.73 m² (p < 0.001) —consistent with the known benign DTG-related creatinine effect. ALT levels decreased slightly (p < 0.001), while Hb levels remained stable (p = 0.037).

Factors associated with adverse drug events

Results from the univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identifying factors associated with experiencing at least one ADE are presented in Table 3. After adjusting for clinically relevant covariates including sex, the multivariate analysis identified several independent factors significantly associated with the occurrence of ADEs. Older age was associated with a higher risk compared with the 18–29-year reference group (≥ 50 years: aOR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.08–2.38; p = 0.045). Psychiatric comorbidity (aOR = 3.13, 95% CI 1.02–9.56; p = 0.046), history of cryptococcosis (aOR = 1.45, 95% CI 1.05–2.13; p = 0.035), underweight status (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²; aOR = 2.22, 95% CI 1.50–3.38; p < 0.001), and baseline renal impairment (eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m²; aOR = 1.45, 95% CI 1.09–1.93; p = 0.011) were also significantly associated with increased ADE risk. Conversely, a longer duration of ART prior to DTG initiation (> 15 years) was associated with a protective effect (aOR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.14–0.75; p = 0.008).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study provides important real-world insights into the safety of DTG-based antiretroviral regimens among Thai PLHIV. Over half of the cohort (53.86%) experienced at least one ADE within the first year of DTG therapy. This prevalence is markedly higher than the 6.30% incidence reported in a recent prospective Tanzanian cohort, which followed 935 participants for three months after DTG initiation across 25 care clinics [16]. The Tanzanian study identified mostly mild dermatologic or neuropsychiatric symptoms—such as pruritus, rash, headache, and insomnia—with few grade 3–4 events, concluding that DTG was generally well tolerated. In contrast, our study included a longer 12-month observation window and captured both clinical and laboratory-defined toxicities, including renal decline, anemia, and weight gain. Therefore, the discrepancy in ADE prevalence likely reflects differences in study design, follow-up duration, ADE definitions, and data collection methods, rather than a true regional safety difference. Nonetheless, the substantially higher ADE burden in our Thai cohort underscores the need for context-specific monitoring strategies, as short-term safety data from African or Western populations may underestimate cumulative toxicities in long-term real-world use.

The most frequent ADEs in this cohort—decline in eGFR, anemia, and weight gain—are consistent with the established safety profiles of DTG and TDF [8, 20, 21, 31, 32]. Each of these events has distinct clinical implications. Weight gain may partly reflect the return-to-health phenomenon but could also signal emerging metabolic risks associated with integrase inhibitors. Anemia was often mild and likely multifactorial, involving baseline marrow suppression, nutritional status, or prior AZT exposure. Renal decline appears primarily related to TDF rather than DTG: while DTG can cause small, reversible increases in serum creatinine via inhibition of renal tubular transporters (OCT2 and MATE1), significant eGFR reductions are more consistent with TDF-associated nephrotoxicity [33, 34].

Our multivariate analysis identified several independent predictors of ADEs. Older age (≥ 40 years) and pre-existing psychiatric comorbidity were significant risk factors, consistent with literature linking increased drug susceptibility with age [22, 23] and neuropsychiatric vulnerability to integrase inhibitors [21, 24, 25]. Critically, our study identified a novel and robust predictor: a history of cryptococcosis. This association may extend beyond being a simple marker of prior immunosuppression. We hypothesize that the profound immune dysregulation required for such an opportunistic infection could lead to persistent systemic inflammation or residual end-organ damage, thereby altering drug pharmacokinetics or heightening sensitivity to ART-related toxicities [26–28]. Similarly, underweight status (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) were strong predictors, likely reflecting underlying frailty and reduced physiological reserve [29–32]. Baseline renal impairment (eGFR < 90 mL/min/1.73 m²) was associated with higher risk of renal ADEs. This finding aligns with known pharmacologic effects of DTG, which can cause a small, reversible increase in serum creatinine through inhibition of renal tubular transporters (OCT2 and MATE1) without true nephrotoxicity. However, in this cohort, participants receiving TDF-containing regimens are more likely to experience clinically relevant eGFR declines, suggesting that the observed renal ADEs may largely reflect TDF-related nephrotoxicity rather than DTG itself. For patients with pre-existing renal impairment or other risk factors, consideration of non-TDF backbones (e.g., TAF/FTC/DTG) or dual therapy (DTG/3TC or DTG/RPV) — when HBV co-infection is absent — may help mitigate renal risk [33, 34].

Conversely, a longer duration since duration on ART prior to DTG initiation was found to be protective. This intriguing finding requires careful interpretation as it may reflect survivor bias or treatment tolerance. This aligns with the concept of a healthy survivor effect, where individuals who have achieved long-term survival are inherently a more resilient subgroup. These survivors likely possess better long-term adherence to ART, more resilient immune systems, or fewer comorbidities, making them less susceptible to ADEs. Alternatively, long-term exposure to ART may lead to a degree of treatment tolerance in this patient group. Further research is warranted to fully characterize this resilient patient subgroup and differentiate between these potential mechanisms [35].

The strengths of this study include its large sample size and the inclusion of a comprehensive 12-month follow-up period, which is critical for observing both immediate and delayed adverse events. Crucially, the findings provide valuable real-world evidence from a setting where safety data on DTG remain sparse, thereby meaningfully contributing to the evidence base guiding DTG implementation.

Despite these strengths, several limitations inherent to the study design and data source must be acknowledged when interpreting the results. First, while 154 records (approximately 8% of the total dataset) were excluded due to inadequate ADE documentation, this proportion was relatively modest and therefore unlikely to have introduced substantial selection bias. Second, the retrospective nature of data collection precluded the precise determination of the temporal relationship between DTG initiation and the onset of ADEs, limiting detailed temporal analyses. Similarly, reliance on existing documentation led to inconsistent recording of ADE severity grading. By protocol, we defined any documented event of Grade (per CTCAE) as an ADE, meaning that mild or clinically insignificant events were included. Consequently, this study cannot report the proportion of Grade 3–4 ADEs, which may overestimate the overall prevalence of clinically significant toxicity. Third, the study could not reliably estimate the rate of DTG discontinuation attributable to ADEs, as patients who discontinued or switched therapy before completing one year of DTG were excluded due to incomplete documentation. Fourth, data regarding historical or chronic HBV/HCV co-infection and the concomitant use of potentially nephrotoxic agents (e.g., co-trimoxazole, amphotericin B) were unavailable. These unmeasured factors may act as significant confounders, particularly when evaluating renal outcomes. Fifth, because all participants received the fixed-dose combination of DTG + 3TC + TDF, it is impossible to attribute observed renal impairment to a single drug definitively. While mild, reversible increases in serum creatinine are compatible with DTG’s transporter inhibition, larger eGFR declines likely reflect TDF toxicity. Finally, consistent with the challenges of any retrospective chart review, ADEs dependent on subjective symptoms—such as insomnia or anxiety—were likely under-reported. To mitigate under-ascertainment, we included only participants with complete one-year follow-up and documented ADE data; nevertheless, a prospective multicenter design with standardized reporting and Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) is necessary to provide more complete safety data.

Ultimately, our findings have immediate clinical implications. They call for a shift towards more individualized patient assessment and proactive, targeted monitoring. Enhanced vigilance is particularly crucial for high-risk groups: older patients, those with psychiatric disorders, a history of severe opportunistic infections like cryptococcosis, underweight status, or baseline renal impairment. Tailored management for these vulnerable individuals is essential to enhance patient safety and optimize long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

In this large cohort of Thai PLWH, more than half experienced at least one ADEs (≥ Grade 1) within the first year of initiating a DTG-based regimen. The study identified several independent risk factors for experiencing an ADE, including older age, psychiatric comorbidity, a history of cryptococcosis, underweight status, and reduced baseline renal function. Conversely, a longer duration since ART initiation prior to switching to DTG was found to be protective. These findings underscore the urgent need for individualized risk assessment and enhanced, targeted monitoring for vulnerable patients to optimize the safety and effectiveness of this critical first-line therapy. Identifying these baseline clinical risk factors may ultimately guide safer prescribing and monitoring strategies in similar real-world settings.
