Contrast-Induced Nephropathy
Authors: Masaru Nemoto, Sohei Matsuura, Yusuke Sakurai, Dai Yamanouchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular diseases often require the administration of iodinated contrast media. However, it is sometimes difficult to use such contrast media in patients with renal dysfunction because direct mechanisms of tubular cell injury and indirect mechanisms of medullary ischemia, together with the contrast medium, may cause contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN). Although the frequency of developing CIN is said to be lower than previously estimated, once it develops, there is no effective treatment, and it may affect mortality. Therefore, before using a contrast medium, it is necessary to identify the risk factors of CIN in patients—including chronic kidney disease, advanced age, and diabetes mellitus—and understand the risk differences between intravenous and intra-arterial contrast medium administration. If there is any risk, intravenous saline or sodium bicarbonate treatments may be used to prevent CIN onset, and the use of minimum amounts of contrast media, within the corresponding range, may be recommended. Introduction

In clinical practice, examinations using iodinated contrast media are indispensable for accurately diagnosing diseases. In particular, their importance is high in the cardiovascular field, where they are applied not only for diagnosis but also for treatment. Patients with cardiovascular diseases frequently undergo contrast-enhanced examinations; however, many of them also have comorbid chronic kidney disease (CKD) and are therefore at risk of developing contrast-induced nephropathy (CIN). This article reviews the etiology, risk factors, prevention, and treatment of CIN.

I. Definition of CIN

CIN is defined as an increase in serum creatinine (SCr) of ≥0.5 mg/dL or ≥25% from the baseline value within 72 h after contrast media administration. Renal function should be evaluated using the most recent SCr value before contrast exposure whenever possible. However, because CIN is also considered a form of acute kidney injury (AKI), it is also referred to as contrast-induced AKI (CI-AKI) or contrast-associated AKI (CA-AKI) and may be evaluated using diagnostic criteria for AKI. Specifically, CIN is diagnosed when, within 48 h after contrast administration, the SCr level increases by ≥0.3 mg/dL from baseline, or when the SCr level increases to ≥1.5 times the baseline value that was known within the prior 7 days or is presumed, or when urine output decreases to <0.5 mL/kg/h for at least 6 h.1) In recent years, SCr has been considered unsuitable for the early diagnosis of CIN, and cystatin C (Cys-C), neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), and interleukin-18 (IL-18) have been reported as predictive biomarkers for early diagnosis.2–5)

II. Pathophysiology of CIN

The pathogenesis of CIN involves both direct and indirect mechanisms related to contrast media. Direct mechanisms include nephrotoxicity of contrast agents on tubular epithelial cells, which induces apoptosis and necrosis, leading to tubular epithelial injury and detachment from the basement membrane, resulting in tubular obstruction. This increases intratubular pressure, leading to renal function deterioration. In addition, increased sodium delivery to the distal tubules activates the tubuloglomerular feedback mechanism, inducing renal vasoconstriction and further impairing renal function. Indirect mechanisms include an increase in vasoconstrictive substances (such as the renin–angiotensin system and endothelin) and a decrease in vasodilatory substances (such as nitric oxide [NO] and prostacyclin [PGI2]) caused by contrast media, resulting in local or global impairment of renal blood flow. Ischemia of the outer medulla is likely to occur. Furthermore, increases in blood osmolality and viscosity promote microthrombus formation, which also contributes to renal hypoperfusion and subsequent renal dysfunction6) (Fig. 1).

III. Incidence and Clinical Course of CIN

The reported incidence of CIN after contrast exposure ranges widely from 1.3% to 33.3%, with significant variability among studies.7) However, recent large-scale cohort studies have shown no difference in the incidence of CIN between contrast-exposed and non-exposed groups (5.5% vs. 5.6%), suggesting that the incidence of CIN may have been overestimated in the past.8) Renal dysfunction caused by CIN is often reversible. SCr levels typically peak within 3–5 days and return to baseline within 7–21 days; however, dialysis may occasionally be required. The rate of progression to dialysis is reported to be 0.06%.9)

IV. Risk Factors for CIN

SCr elevation has been associated with increased 30-day in-hospital mortality, highlighting the importance of assessing CIN risk.10) Risk factors for CIN can be divided into patient- and procedure-related factors. Patient-related factors include CKD, advanced age, and diabetes mellitus, whereas procedure-related factors include the use of high-osmolality contrast media, excessive contrast volume, repeated contrast exposure within 72 h, and concomitant medications11) (Table 1). CKD is considered a risk factor for CIN, but no consensus has been reached regarding the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) threshold associated with increased risk. The European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR) 2018 guidelines define an eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 as conferring the risk of CIN, which is consistent with Japanese guidelines.12,13) Advanced age is also regarded as a risk factor for CIN, with many reports identifying age ≥70 years as high risk; however, age-specific risk increments have not been clearly defined. Therefore, risk assessment should consider underlying comorbidities in addition to age alone.14) Diabetes mellitus is associated with an increased risk of CIN in patients with CKD but is considered a risk-enhancing factor rather than an independent risk factor.15) Regarding contrast media type, the use of high-osmolality contrast agents increases the risk of CIN. Since 2001, intravascular administration of high-osmolality contrast media has no longer been indicated. Currently, the most commonly used contrast agents are iso- and low-osmolar contrast media, and no difference in CIN incidence has been demonstrated between these 2 types.16) An increased contrast volume is associated with a higher risk of CIN; therefore, the minimum necessary dose for an adequate diagnosis is recommended. If feasible, combining low tube voltage imaging with iterative reconstruction techniques can enhance contrast effects while preventing image quality degradation. Repeated administration of contrast within 72 h should also be avoided whenever possible. Renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors were previously thought to worsen renal function during contrast exposure due to reduced intraglomerular pressure; however, evidence that RAS inhibitors increase CIN risk is inconclusive.17) Diuretics, particularly loop diuretics, have also been suggested to worsen renal function, but clear evidence is lacking. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) reduce renal blood flow through cyclooxygenase inhibition; therefore, discontinuation of NSAIDs is recommended for 24 h before and after contrast exposure. Although CIN risk prediction models combining multiple risk factors have mainly been developed for coronary angiography (CAG) and percutaneous coronary intervention, such models are considered useful for pre-procedural risk stratification.18)

V. Arterial Versus Venous Contrast Studies

Many reports indicate that arterial contrast studies (invasive procedures) carry a higher risk of CIN than venous contrast studies (noninvasive procedures). This may be due to the higher burden of comorbidities in patients undergoing arterial studies and the direct influx of high-concentration contrast media into the kidneys. In addition, catheter manipulation increases the risk of renal thromboembolism. However, even with arterial administration, the risk is comparable to that of venous administration when contrast media reach the kidneys after passing through the pulmonary or peripheral circulation (e.g., lower-extremity endovascular therapy).

VI. Prevention of CIN

In patients at high risk of CIN, intravenous isotonic saline before and after contrast studies is recommended for prevention.19) In patients with CKD with an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, the risk of CIN associated with CAG is considered high.20) Preventive strategies and risk explanation are also required when performing contrast-enhanced CT in patients with an eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2. Although the risk of CIN after contrast-enhanced CT is considered low in this population, individual risk factors should be evaluated when determining preventive measures.21) However, the optimal eGFR threshold for fluid administration remains unclear. A commonly used hydration protocol is 1 mL/kg/h for 6–12 h before and after the examination, although the optimal regimen has not been clearly established. Evidence regarding oral hydration for CIN prevention remains insufficient.19) In emergency settings with limited hydration time, sodium bicarbonate reduces the risk of CIN. Urine alkalinization is thought to suppress oxidative stress and tubular injury. A typical regimen consists of administering sodium bicarbonate (~150 mEq/L) at 3 mL/kg/h for 1 h before contrast exposure and 1 mL/kg/h for 6 h afterward, although the optimal protocol remains uncertain. Sodium bicarbonate preparations are available at concentrations of 1.26%, 7%, and 8.4%. Medical accidents related to the overdose of high-concentration (8.4%) sodium bicarbonate injections have been reported; therefore, 1.26% sodium bicarbonate is preferable from a patient safety perspective. Although CIN is thought to be involved in oxidative stress, and N-acetylcysteine was expected to have a preventive effect due to its antioxidant properties, its efficacy has not been demonstrated.22) Statins possess pleiotropic effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic actions, and preventive effects against CIN have been reported for simvastatin and rosuvastatin; however, evidence remains insufficient.23,24) Administration of human atrial natriuretic peptide (hANP) with the expectation of antidiuretic effects is not recommended.25) Blood purification therapy after contrast administration does not reduce CIN risk.26)

VII. Treatment after the Onset of CIN

CIN may improve with fluid administration if renal hypoperfusion due to dehydration is present; however, careful monitoring of renal function is required to avoid fluid overload. Although loop diuretics were expected to exert renoprotective effects, no benefit has been demonstrated, and they may even be harmful due to reduced renal blood flow.27) Neither hANP administration nor blood purification therapy is recommended, and no effective treatment has been established for CIN.

VIII. Prognosis of CIN

Most CIN cases are transient, and renal function often recovers. However, From et al. reported significantly higher 30-day and 1-year mortality rates in patients with CIN than in those without CIN.28) Conversely, some reports indicate that CIN has no impact on mortality, and the association between CIN and prognosis remains unclear.21) Nevertheless, AKI generally affects prognosis, and considering CIN as a subtype of AKI, careful attention to CIN development is warranted.29)

Conclusions

The incidence of CIN due to contrast media appears to be lower than previously thought. However, no effective treatment is available once CIN develops, and it may adversely affect prognosis. Therefore, identifying risk factors and emphasizing preventive strategies are essential. The current guidelines provide few high-grade recommendations, and further evidence accumulation is needed.
