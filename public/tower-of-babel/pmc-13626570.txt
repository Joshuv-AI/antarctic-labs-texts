Cephalomedullary nail fixation of subtrochanteric femur fractures in older adults: Is 1 distal locking screw enough?
Authors: Manasa L. Kadiyala, Lauren A. Merrell, Abhishek Ganta, Sanjit R. Konda, Steven M. Rivero, Kenneth A. Egol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Subtrochanteric (ST) fractures are proximal femur fractures with strong deforming muscular forces, which complicate anatomical reduction and treatment. These fractures are fixed with intramedullary nails with 2 distal locking screws, although the need for both screws has never been researched.Purpose:This study's purpose was to investigate the use of 1 versus 2 distal locking screws on clinical and radiographic outcomes and complications.Study Design/Level of Evidence:Retrospective cohort, Level of Evidence III.Methods:An IRB-approved retrospective review of a consecutive series of ST femoral fractures was performed. Each patient's medical record was reviewed for demographics, injury and treatment information, perioperative details, radiographic parameters, hospital quality measures, and outcomes. Patients were divided into 2 cohorts: 1 distal locking screw (1-screw cohort) and 2 distal locking screws (2-screw cohort). Distal screw number (1 vs. 2 screws) was chosen by surgeon preference. Comparative and multivariable analyses were conducted.Results:Two hundred thirty-two patients met inclusion criteria with a mean age of 71.18 ± 19.05 years and average follow-up of 11 months. All fractures were treated with a long antegrade, reamed cephalomedullary nail. Fifty (21.6%) patients were fixed with one 5 mm distal locking screw, and 182 (78.4%) patients had 2 screws. Patients with 1 screw had a shorter operative time in minutes (156.64 ± 47.06 vs. 176.06 ± 41.32, P = .005), but there were no further perioperative differences. Patients treated with 1 screw were more frequently associated with being fixed in a valgus coronal alignment (20% 1 distal screw vs. 5% 2 distal screws, P = .003) and had higher degree of apex anterior angulation (19.7° ± 8.2° 1-screw vs. 10.6° ± 6.3° 2-screw, P = .011). However, this did not significantly affect healing outcomes because both cohorts experienced similar rates of fracture healing, medical and mechanical complications, mortality, and readmission (all P > .050).Conclusion:Clinically, a single distal locking bolt is sufficient for ST femur fractures fixed with a cephalomedullary nail. A second distal locking screw may be reserved for situations in which the first screw may not have achieved adequate fixation.

Introduction

Subtrochanteric (ST) femur fractures, defined as fractures that occur within 5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter, comprise 5–20% of all proximal femur fractures.1–3 The incidence of ST fractures has been rising because of high rates of diabetes and osteoporosis and their respective associated medications, such as bisphosphonates.2,4–6 They occur in a bimodal distribution, typically from high-energy injuries in the young and low-energy trauma in elderly patients. The complex biomechanics and high regional stress concentration make these fractures challenging to treat for orthopaedic surgeons.1,3,7–9 Management of these fractures frequently involves operative fixation with either an intramedullary nail or an extramedullary device, such as fixed-angle blade plate.1–3

Use of a cephalomedullary nail (CMN) is the current gold standard for fixation of ST femoral fractures.4 The ability of the load-sharing implant to provide rotational stability for unstable ST fractures while being minimally invasive makes it superior to the other fixation devices.4,10 Typically, a CMN is inserted with 1 large proximal lag screw and 1 or 2 distal locking screws because this has been shown to provide optimal stability against the various deforming forces.11 The most frequent complication associated with CMN treatment of these fractures is varus malunion or malalignment, followed by nonunion and implant failure. These complications typically lead to additional revision surgeries with associated risks and complications.3,7,10

Although hip fractures in general have been extensively researched and published in orthopaedic literature, ST fractures have not been as frequently studied. Previous studies have explored various types of fixation constructs for these fracture types and short versus long CMNs. Current management guidelines for ST fractures recommend the use of 2 distal locking screws, but no study exists that demonstrates that the use of 1 screw with an intramedullary nail leads to inferior results.12–14 Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of 1 versus 2 distal locking screws in CMNs used for fixation of ST fractures on patient outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This was an IRB-approved retrospective review of a consecutive series of ST femur fractures between October 1, 2014, and October 1, 2022. Criteria for inclusion was the following: operative management for AO/OTA Type 32Axa, 32Bxa, or 32Cxa15 ST fracture, use of long CMN for fixation, age 18 years or older, and minimum follow-up for 6-month postsurgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: reverse obliquity intertrochanteric (IT) fracture (AO/OTA 31A3.1, 31A3.2, and 31A3.3), incomplete atypical fracture pattern, or lack of adequate medical records and follow-up data. All patients included in this study were treated by the same group of orthopaedic surgeons at one of the 3 hospitals within an urban academic medical center and two level 1 trauma centers.

The electronic medical records for all patients were reviewed for baseline demographics including age at time of injury, sex, body mass index, Charlson Comorbidity Index without age adjustment, assistive device use such as a cane or walker, and preinjury ambulatory status. Data were collected regarding prior and current bisphosphonate use, Glasgow Coma Scale, injury mechanism, American Society of Anesthesiology score class, time from date of admission to date of surgery, and type of operative intervention. A low-energy mechanism was defined as a ground-level fall or fall from a height less than or equal to 2 stairs, while a high-energy injury was a fall from a height greater than 2 stairs, motor vehicle accident, pedestrian struck by motor vehicle, or gunshot wound. Fracture patterns were classified according to the system of the Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) by 3 fellowship-trained orthopaedic trauma surgeons.15 All atypical ST fractures were classified according to the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research guidelines.16

All surgeries were performed on a radiolucent table in the supine position with or without traction. The proximal lag screw was placed through a guide, and femoral rotation was confirmed using an intraoperative “scannogram” technique.17 The choice to use 1 versus 2 distal locking screws was based on surgeon preference at time of fixation. After this, the distal locking screws were placed using a freehand “perfect circles” technique.18 All patients were given chemical venous thromboembolism prophylaxis and allowed to be weight bearing as tolerated postoperatively. All procedures were performed by a board-certified orthopaedic surgeon and resident staff. Surgical approach, open versus closed reduction, implant type, operative time, postoperative hemoglobin, estimated intraoperative blood loss, the need for inpatient blood transfusion, and the quantity of units transfused were all recorded. Operative time was defined as time of patient entry into the operating room until time of patient exit. Postoperative radiographs were assessed by the study authors to identify implant characteristics including fixation construct (1 vs. 2 screws in the femoral head), diameter, alignment, and degrees of sagittal and coronal alignment.

Charts were reviewed to determine hospital outcomes and quality measures including length of stay, inpatient mortality and complications, and discharge disposition. Minor inpatient complications included acute kidney injury, urinary tract infection, and acute blood loss anemia. Major inpatient complications included sepsis or septic shock, pneumonia, acute respiratory failure, stroke, myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. In addition, records were evaluated to determine rates of 30- and 90-day readmission and mortality at 30 days and 1 year after hospital discharge. Time to radiographic healing, development of fracture nonunion, and implant fixation failure (broken screw or nail) were determined from expert evaluation of postoperative radiographs. Radiographic healing was defined as the presence of bridging callus or trabecular consolidation on 3 of 4 cortices on AP and lateral radiographs. It was evaluated by physicians based on the modified Radiographic Union Score for Tibia score and associated criteria.19

All patients who met inclusion criteria were grouped into 2 cohorts: those treated with 1 distal locking screw (1-screw cohort) and those treated with 2 distal locking screws (2-screw cohort). The primary outcomes of this study included time to radiographic union, nonunion, and fixation failure (broken screw or nail). Secondary outcomes included postoperative alignment, hospital quality measures, complications, mortality, and readmission. Univariable analysis including chi-square tests, independent sample t-tests, Fisher exact test, and Mann-Whitney U tests were used as appropriate. Regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between distal locking screw number and outcomes. Continuous outcomes were evaluated using univariable linear regression and categorical outcomes using univariable logistic regression with number of distal locking screws as the predictor; estimates are reported as beta coefficients or odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For significantly different outcomes, additional adjusted multivariable analyses were performed using predetermined covariates (age, sex, body mass index, Charlson Comorbidity Index, and American Society of Anesthesiology class). All statistics were calculated with IBM SPSS data software, version 25. Significance was defined with an alpha of 0.05.

Results

A total of 232 patients met inclusion criteria. Of these, 78 (33.6%) were male and 154 (66.4%) were female with a mean age of 71.18 ± 19.05 years. Fifty (21.6%) patients were treated with 1 distal locking screw, while 182 (78.4%) patients were treated with 2 distal locking screws. The mean follow-up time for all patients was 325 days. There were no differences in demographics between the 1-screw and 2-screw cohorts (Table 1). Both cohorts experienced similar mechanisms of injury (P = .714) and corresponding AO/OTA fracture patterns: 32A (P = .536), 32B (P = .636), and 32C (P = .188).

Additional univariable regression analyses (Supplemental Table 1, https://links.lww.com/BH9/A2 and Supplemental Table 2, https://links.lww.com/BH9/A3) demonstrated that 2-screw constructs were associated with longer operative time (beta 24.36 minutes; 95% CI 8.40–40.32; P = .003), lower odds of coronal valgus alignment (operating room [OR] 0.30; 95% CI 0.12–0.77; P = .012), and lower degrees of apex anterior deformity (beta −9.11°; 95% CI −15.92 to −2.31; P = .011), with no other significant clinical or radiographic findings. In adjusted multivariable analysis, 2-screw constructs remained associated with longer operative time (beta 23.47 minutes; 95% CI 7.59–39.36; P = .004), lower odds of coronal valgus alignment (adjusted OR 0.24; 95% CI 0.09–0.65; P = .005), and lower degrees apex anterior deformity (adjusted beta −10.80°; 95% CI −18.89 to −2.72; P = .012).

Discussion

This clinical study sought to determine whether the use of a single distal locking screw with a CMN is acceptable for fixation of a ST femoral fracture. Our data demonstrate that ST femur fractures can be effectively treated with CMNs using only 1 distal locking screw. Apart from operative time, there were no differences in perioperative and postsurgical outcomes when compared with the current standard of care with intramedullary nail fixation using 2 distal screws.

Subtrochanteric femur fractures typically undergo operative fixation with a CMN due to their unstable nature and large muscular deforming forces. This presents a challenge when attempting to reduce and fix the injury. Construct stability is needed for mobilization of patients with ST fractures because of the large associated axial, bending, and torsional forces.1,4,7 Without adequate surgical reduction and fixation patients have a high risk of symptomatic malunion or nonunion, reducing their ability to ambulate and leading to an increase in overall mortality.4,20–22 Several factors contribute to the stability of the implant, including the quality of the reduction, fracture comminution, bone mineral density, nail length, and the number of distal locking screws.

Drilling of medial and lateral cortical holes for insertion of distal locking screws, especially for ST fractures, can serve as potential sources of stress risers.8,9 The initial breakage or fixation failure of the construct frequently occurs at distal locking screws, making their stability important for prediction of fracture nonunion.8 Kwak et al. compared the strength of 2 different locking screw configurations in fracture models, 1 and 2 screws. They found the 2-screw construct had a higher load to failure and varying stress distribution when compared with the 1-screw construct. Some clinical studies have demonstrated single-screw constructs can increase the risk of fixation failure, autodynamization, and associated peri-implant fracture.3,7,22 Several other mechanical studies have also reported increased stability and rigidity with additional locking screws due to the shorter working length and reduced the stress on the proximal fixation of the construct.23,24

Although previous biomechanical research has demonstrated that the stability of ST fractures improves with additional points of fixation, the results from our clinical study reveal no difference in perioperative and surgical outcomes or patient safety between the 2 constructs.7,8,25 Instead, the 1-screw cohort had a shorter operative time and thus less radiation exposure for both the patient and surgical staff. The reduced operative time means less time spent by the patient under general anesthesia with its various associated risks, and more efficient use of hospital resources with the purchase of fewer screws and ability to perform more surgeries throughout the day. It also decreases the overall cost to the patient and healthcare system. Although the 1-screw cohort had a higher degree of angulation in apex anterior, this added deformity did not affect overall outcomes and was related to the reduction accepted at the time of surgery. Despite this, the time to radiographic union and surgical complication rates were comparable for both cohorts when compared with previous studies that examined similar outcomes for ST fractures.2,4,20,25

These findings align with those of Hajek et al., a clinical and biomechanical study that evaluated 16 cadaver femurs and 27 patients with femoral shaft fractures and found that 1 distal screw provided adequate fixation for femoral shaft fractures treated with intramedullary nailing. They found no difference in axial load to failure between 1 and 2 distal screw constructs, and the fixation with 2 screws potentially exceeded the nail strength at certain locations. There was an increased risk of migration of the distal screw with the 1-screw design, but this did not affect clinical outcomes or implant failure.18 There are a number of other potential causes for implant failure when treating these fractures such as a narrow medullary canal, unreamed nailing technique, lag screw cut-out, or malreduction.4,10,20,23,26 Brumback et al., on the other hand, supported the use of 2 distal locking screws in comminuted femoral shaft fractures due to higher implant fatigue strength. However, the study results were largely from biomechanical testing and lack clinical correlation.

This study is subject to the inherent limitations and biases of a retrospective study design. Our institution is a large urban academic medical center, and some of our findings may not be applicable to all hospital settings, such as those in rural or suburban areas. However, the diverse patients, providers, and injury types mitigate this limitation because our patient databases encompass a wide variety of both simple and complex cases. In addition, although our overall cohort size is large, our group sizes of 1 versus 2 screws differed. However, this was expected as the 2-screw design is the current standard of practice for fixation of ST fractures. Distal screw number (1 vs. 2 screws) was chosen by surgeon preference and may have been a source of selection bias. Furthermore, the operative duration variable collected in our institutional database was total OR time rather than skin to skin time, and this measure may have been influenced by nonoperative factors such as anesthesia induction and patient positioning. Finally, leg length measurements were also not available in our institutional database and therefore could not be evaluated.

Future studies may involve a randomized control trial with equal-sized patient cohorts to further assess the impact of the number of distal locking screws on long-term functional outcomes to corroborate these findings and guide clinical orthopaedic management for ST fractures.

Conclusion

This study found that patients treated with a 1 distal locking screw construct had shorter operative times and were more likely to demonstrate valgus coronal alignment and a greater degree of apex anterior angulation postoperatively. However, these differences did not significantly affect healing outcomes because both cohorts had similar rates of fracture union, medical and mechanical complications, mortality, and readmission. As such, the results of this study reflect that ST fractures can be safely and adequately treated with intramedullary nail fixation using 1 distal locking screw. Although 2 distal locking screws may show biomechanical superiority, this does not lead to superior patient outcomes. Therefore, the use of a 1-screw locking construct for these fractures demonstrated comparable radiographic and healing outcomes with the 2-screw constructs with the added advantage of decreasing operative time, radiation exposure, and healthcare costs. A 2-screw construct may be used for cases in which additional fixation is desired, such as perceived instability or inadequate fixation with 1-screw.
