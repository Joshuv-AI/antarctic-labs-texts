The microenvironment dictates glyco-immune surveillance via HSF1-mediated metabolism

ABSTRACT
Altered glucose metabolism is an auspicious feature of solid tumors, but is it an intrinsic property of tumor cells or a metabolic adaptation to the tumor microenvironment? Using normal epithelial cells cultured in a physiological culture medium under conditions that mimic the physical properties of healthy or cancerous tissues, we establish multiomics relationships between the biochemical and physical properties of the microenvironment and its impact on biosynthetic outputs of altered glucose metabolism. We find that microenvironmental properties, such as hyperglycemia, can affect the composition and thickness of the epithelial glycocalyx, in part through the activity of mechanosensitive stress responses associated with Heat Shock Factor 1 (HSF1). Because glycocalyx thickness alters immune surveillance of epithelial-origin tumor cells, we examined the relationship between the HSF1-hyperglycemia axis in human breast tumors and validate it as a druggable vulnerability to license natural killer cell lethality against cancer cells.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer cells use metabolic rewiring to satisfy demands of rapid proliferation and survival in the tumor microenvironment (TME) (1, 2). However, the extent to which this rewiring is influenced by intrinsic factors versus extrinsic adaptations to the TME remains incompletely understood (3–5). One hallmark feature of tumor-associated metabolism is reduced oxidative metabolism of glucose (6), but replication experiments indicate that this can be due to the nutrient availability within the TME and not necessarily an intrinsic feature of tumor cells (7, 8). Similarly, studies that examined how untransformed cells respond to microenvironments that recapitulate the physical properties of the TME suggest that glucose oxidation may also be suppressed by cellular mechanical responses (i.e., “mechanoresponse”) to microenvironmental cues (9, 10). These potential changes to glucose metabolism, triggered by the nutrient abundance or physical properties of the cellular microenvironment, may be important metabolic adjustments that support a tumor’s ability to proliferate, persist, and metastasize (3, 11, 12) with potential therapeutic relevance (13).

Specifically, tumor cells may gain a proliferative advantage at the expense of adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) production (14), by routing glucose-derived carbon into biosynthetic pathways such as the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP) (15). These pathways support redox homeostasis and mediate the production of nucleotide building blocks and glycosylation substrates (3, 16). While nucleotide building blocks are critical for rapidly proliferating cells that have high transcriptional and genomic replication rates, the role of glycosylation in tumor persistence and metastasis is becoming more evident (17–19). Glucose-derived intermediates from the HBP are required to synthesize the glycocalyx, a pericellular matrix composed of glycoproteins and glycolipids, which is particularly apparent on the surface of endothelial, epithelial, and tumor cells (20–22). The thickness and composition of the tumor glycocalyx is a critical determinant of immune evasion (19, 23, 24), which enables tumors to persist and metastasize by limiting immune surveillance mechanisms that naturally clear tumors from our bodies in primary and metastatic sites (22, 25–27).

Here, we investigate how microenvironmental cues intersect with glucose metabolism to regulate downstream glycosylation. We uncover synergistic effects of microenvironmental properties on the availability of glucose-derived intermediates required for glycosylation and show the complexity by which nutrient availability governs the composition of the glycome. We find that hyperglycemia promotes glycocalyx expansion in mammary epithelial cells in a Heat Shock Factor 1 (HSF1)–dependent manner, but only under physiological nutrient conditions. We find that suppression of HSF1 also limits sialic acid synthesis from glucose and thereby diminishes sialoglycan abundance. Because a thick and sialoglycan-rich glycocalyx prevents immune-mediated clearance of tumor cells, we examined how hyperglycemia and HSF1 affect natural killer (NK) cell surveillance and found that targeting HSF1 is a potential therapeutic target to diminish the immune evasive properties of the glycocalyx. Together, these findings reveal how physical cues and nutrient availability within the TME cooperate to shape the immunosuppressive properties of the glycocalyx and identify HSF1 as a potential therapeutic target to disrupt this tumor protective barrier.

RESULTS

Conventional in vitro culture media (e.g., Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, DMEM) does not accurately reflect in vivo nutrient availability of the human body (28–30), which is why we used a culture medium systematically designed to reflect the nutrient composition of human plasma (human plasma-like medium, HPLM) in comparison to conventional culture medium (DMEM) (fig. S1, A to C). We chose this comparison because most of our data are derived from models cultured in DMEM, but human tumors and tissues are fed by a nutrient composition that more closely resembles the circulatory system with and without postprandial concentrations of metabolites present in plasma (i.e., HPLM ± postprandial hyperglycemia). To determine whether our culture media influenced cellular responses to the physical properties of the microenvironment, we cultured immortalized mammary epithelial cells (MCF10A) on fibronectin presenting hydrogels that recapitulate the elastic modulus of the soft normal mammary epithelial stroma (400 Pa) and stiff fibrotic tumors (6000 to 60,000 Pa) (31–33). In either medium, we observed the characteristic mechanoresponse, where responding cells use actomyosin-supported cell-matrix adhesions to fibronectin to spread across the surface, whereas cells on soft substrates enforce cell-cell junctions and remain in colonies. Fluorescence microscopy revealed that these morphological dynamics were observable in both media (fig. S1D).

To characterize how culture media composition affects the cellular mechanoresponses, we used mass spectrometry–based proteomics to compare the same cells cultured on soft and stiff substrates for 24 hours (Fig. 1A). As expected, we found that responding to stiff microenvironments drives feed-forward production of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins and adhesion receptors [e.g., integrin receptors (ITGA6 and ITGB1) and fibronectin, FN1], but beyond a molecular explanation for the spreading phenotype observed (fig. S1D), proteomic changes induced by substrate elasticity showed little correlation between the two media (Fig. 1, B to E, and fig. S2A). Unexpectedly, we found that significantly induced (red box in Fig. 1C, transposed to Fig. 1D) or reduced (blue box in Fig. 1C, transposed to Fig. 1D) proteins in HPLM were dissimilar to the same comparison in DMEM (Fig. 1, D and E, and fig. S2, B and C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that cells cultured in DMEM were enriched in proteins characterizing R-HSA-2262752: cellular responses to stress, R-HSA-8953854: metabolism of RNA, and GO:0006091: generation of precursor metabolites and energy. Similarly, a top GO category enriched in cells cultured in stiff over soft HPLM microenvironments was also the “R-HSA-2262752: cellular response to stress,” which associates glucose metabolism, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), DNA damage, and HSF1-mediated adaptations to protein folding stress in multiple cellular compartments (fig. S2C). Further analysis suggested that transcriptional mediators of mechanoresponses were not associated with the expected Hippo signaling pathway, but instead poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), Krueppel-like factor 10 (KLF10), CREB-binding protein (CREBBP), and hypoxia-inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF1A) due to overlapping assignments with HSF1 (34, 35) (e.g., PARP1: CDKN1A|FN1|JUN|SERPINE1|VIM; KLF10: CDKN1A|EGFR|SERPINE1; CREBBP: CDKN1A|EGFR|PSMC5|S100A4; HIF1A: AREG|SERPINE1|VIM; and HSF1:CDKN1A|JUN|SERPINE1), which we found to be a mechanosensitive transcription factor (9, 36) that can influence cytoskeletal processes (37) and glucose metabolism (9, 38) (fig. S2D and table S4).

Synergy between microenvironmental factors enhances metabolic support of glycosylation

To explore how cells in these microenvironments respond to increased concentrations of glucose, a paradoxical mediator of suppressed oxidative metabolism of glucose (7, 8, 12), we used mass spectrometry to measure the metabolite levels of cells cultured in DMEM or HPLM with and without physiologically relevant hyperglycemia [15 versus 5 mM] on a gradient of adhesive substrate stiffnesses (400, 6000, and 60,000 Pa) (Fig. 2, A and B). Principal components analysis revealed that media composition (PC1) was the most deterministic factor of divergent intracellular metabolite profiles (fig. S3A), but we could also detect synergy between hyperglycemia and stiffness (PC2). We noticed that stiff and hyperglycemic DMEM conditions biased cells to dramatically accumulate several nucleotides, which significantly altered the ATP:AMP ratio (fig. S3B), whereas NADH (reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) levels increased with adhesive substrate stiffness in a medium-independent fashion (fig. S3C). Stress-induced PARP1 activity (fig. S2C) has been correlated with alterations in NAD(P)H (reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) photochemistry (39). Therefore, we used phasor fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) to monitor NAD(P)H dynamics, which we found was only sensitive to adhesive substrate stiffness when cells were cultured in HPLM, but not DMEM (fig. S3, D to F).

Examining the regulatory patterns of the composite metabolomic and proteomic effects of mechanoresponses in DMEM and HPLM with Metaboverse (40) indicated that the NAD(P)H photochemistry and stress response signatures of cells cultured in stiff HPLM microenvironments were also associated with increased glutathione oxidation, suggesting that stiff microenvironments elicit more oxidative stress and PARP1 activity (figs. S2, C and D, and S4, A to C). We also identified that stiffness increased the metabolism of arginine to ornithine (fig. S4D) (41) and creatinine to phosphocreatine (fig. S4E) (42) in either medium. Cells cultured in soft DMEM microenvironments were predicted to increase the synthesis of N-acetylglucosamine 6-phosphate (GlcNAc-6P) from endogenous N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) because N-acetylglucosamine kinase (NAGK) is enriched (2.3-fold at the protein level) (fig. S4, F and G). NAGK is a critical enzyme for the salvage/recycling of GlcNAc from lysosomal degradation of glycoconjugates (glycan-conjugated proteins or lipids) when exogenous nutrients are limiting programmed cellular objectives (43).

S4, F and G). To explore this, we measured isotopic glucose (13C) incorporation into UDP-GlcNAc on varied adhesion substrate stiffnesses in DMEM or HPLM. Both of these UDP-charged sugars are substrates for glycosylation in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus that enable the secretory pathway or glycocalyx synthesis (44). An explanation for why we detected a greater proportion of 13C incorporation without an increase in total pool size could be that these substrates were not detectable in the methanol extract because they had already been incorporated into macromolecules (e.g., glycoconjugates) used to form the epithelial glycocalyx (45–47), which has been linked to mechanosignaling and cancer progression (48) in stiff and fibrotic tumors (21, 49). To assay the glycocalyx abundance in these cells, we used scanning angle interference microscopy (SAIM) (fig. S5, A to C), an axial localization microscopy technique with nanoscale precision (23, 50–52). SAIM revealed that cells cultured in DMEM had a thicker glycocalyx than cells cultured in HPLM. Moreover, hyperglycemia was sufficient to increase the thickness of the glycocalyx in cells cultured in HPLM but not DMEM (Fig. 3, B and C, and fig. S5D). Effects of hyperglycemia on the glycocalyx corresponded to greater fractional labeling of glycosylation intermediates (e.g., UDP-Glc and UDP-GlcNAc) with 13C from glucose without an increase in the total abundance of UDP-Glc. These observations would fit a condition in which a greater proportion of newly synthesized UDP-Glc/GlcNAc was immediately incorporated into glycoconjugate synthesis and cell surface accumulation.

Microenvironmental control of glycome and glycocalyx dynamics

To test whether medium-dependent metabolic responses were associated with the formation of different glycoconjugates, we used lectin-based microarrays to survey the relative abundance of specific glycoconjugates formed (Fig. 3D and table S3) (53–56). Lectins are carbohydrate-binding proteins that can detect specific glycan epitopes, distinguishing between epimeric and isomeric structures that have identical masses but different stereo- and regiochemistry (e.g., Galβ1,3GalNAc and Galβ1,4GlcNAc) (57). Dual color lectin microarray analysis rapidly informs on the combinatorial structures of the glycome that have biological significance in evolution/physiology. We found stark separation between the lectin-substrate signatures of cells cultured in DMEM and HPLM. Cells cultured in DMEM were enriched in glycoconjugates containing high mannose [Griffithsin (GRFT) and Banana lectin (BanLec)], oligomannose (GNA), and terminal GalNAc [Vicia villosa agglutinin (VVA) and Artocarpus integrifolia (Jacalin) Lectin (AIA)]. A potential signal for core fucose [Pisum Sativum Agglutinin (PSA) and Lens culinaris Agglutinin (LcH)] was also observed; however, these lectins also have cross-specificity for high mannose. This indicates a less complex N-glycan set in DMEM-cultured cells. In contrast, cells cultured in HPLM were enriched with glycoconjugates containing polyLacNAc [Datura stramonium Lectin (DSA) and Griffonia simplicifolia Lectin II (GSL-II)], indicative of complex, branched N-glycans, and α2 fucose [Sambucus nigra lectin (SNA-II) and Trichosanthes japonica Agglutinin II (TJA-II)], associated with type 3/4 blood group H. Physiological hyperglycemia, which increased the thickness of the glycocalyx in HPLM but not DMEM (Fig. 3, C and D), increased the abundance of α1,2-fucosylated epitopes in HPLM [Psophocarpus tetragonolobus (PTA) and Lewis Y] and DMEM [Ulex europaeus agglutinin (UEA)] as well as an increase in core 1/3 O-glycans, commonly found on mucins [AIA and Maclura pomifera agglutinin (MPA)*] (Fig. 3E). Glycan diversity and abundance can have significant impacts on cellular physiology (46), which makes the effects of culture medium a startling revelation.

HSF1 enables hyperglycemia-induced NK resistance

Since hyperglycemia was sufficient to increase the glycocalyx thickness of cells cultured in HPLM, we decided to test whether we could identify positive regulators of glycocalyx construction by examining which proteins become more abundant in response to hyperglycemia (fig. S6, A to C). Promoter analysis of the hyperglycemia-enriched proteins revealed several putative transcriptional mediators, such as PPARGC1A, CREB, YY1, HSF1/2, ARNT2, and USF, and an unassigned motif (ACTAYRNNNCCCR) was correlated with increased glycocalyx abundance (Fig. 4A). When we separated these putative mediators by their effects on cells cultured in soft or stiff microenvironments, only proteins with the unassigned motif (ACTAYRNNNCCCR) were more abundant because of hyperglycemia (58). However, the ACTAYRNNNCCR-associated proteins induced by hyperglycemia were inconsistent and unrelated between soft (e.g., glycerolipid biosynthesis) and stiff microenvironments (e.g., cellular response to DNA damage stimulus and signaling by Rho guanosine triphosphatases). The only shared effect of hyperglycemia between the soft and stiff conditions was predicted to be driven by HSF1 and HSF2 (“HSF” assignment encompassed targets associated with both HSF1 and HSF2). HSF1 is a transcription factor associated with canonical (heat shock) and noncanonical responses that mediate mammary tumor progression and metastasis (59) possibly through effects on glucose metabolism to support stress remediation programs (9, 38, 60–62).

To test the potential role of HSF1 in hyperglycemia-induced glycocalyx construction, we assayed cells cultured in hyperglycemia with and without HSF1 inhibition (KRIBB11) using the lectin microarray. HSF1 inhibition prevented hyperglycemia from increasing the abundance of the [glucose]-responsive glycans (Fig. 4B) and specifically reduced the abundance of sialoglycans of cells in normal [glucose] concentrations (fig. S7A). Human sialoglycans are glycoconjugates capped with N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac), whose auspicious abundance in tumors has yet to be explained by an oncogene, transcription factor, or signaling pathway. Because of these associations, we assayed isotopic glucose (13C) incorporation into UDP-GlcNAc, UDP-Glc, and Neu5Ac in cells cultured in HPLM and found that HSF1 inhibition significantly reduced the contribution of 13C-glucose–derived carbons into UDP-Glc, UDP-GlcNAc, and Neu5Ac (Fig. 4C). Using SAIM, we observed that HSF1 inhibition was sufficient to prevent the thickening of the glycocalyx that occurred in response to hyperglycemia (Fig. 4, D and E). HSF1 inhibition did not reduce the thickness of the glycocalyx of cells cultured in 5 mM glucose HPLM, which could indicate that the HSF1 program is suited to regulate and route the metabolism of glucose beyond a baseline concentration threshold (i.e., which could explain why we detected an HSF1-driven response to hyperglycemia).

Staining of mammary epithelial cells with SNA and StcEE447D revealed that HSF1 inhibition reduced the abundance of cell surface sialic acid and mucin-domain containing glycoconjugates to a greater extent than treatment with a high concentration [2 μM] of the bacterial O-glycoprotease, StcE (63), which cleaves proteins conjugated with mucin-type O-glycosylation (Fig. 4F). To verify the relationship between HSF1 and sialic acid abundance in vivo, we generated a mouse model in which HSF1 was conditionally deleted in mammary epithelial cells and tumors [Hsf1 floxed alleles (64) crossed with the Mouse Mammary Tumor Virus (MMTV)–Cre (line D) and a model oncogene, polyomavirus middle T antigen, PyMT, to drive tumor formation]. In MMTV-Hsf1KO mammary tissues, we found no SNA staining of the lumens or cell-cell junctions of the mammary epithelial acini, consistent with the loss of this epitope observed by lectin microarray (Fig. 4G and fig. S7C). This effect was conserved in MMTV-Hsf1KO-PyMT tumors, which also displayed significantly reduced SNA staining of the tumor epithelium (fig. S7D) as well as expressing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against HSF1 in the human breast cancer model (MDA-MB-231, a model of triple-negative breast cancer) (fig. S7F). Overall, these results indicate a role for HSF1 in Neu5Ac synthesis, sialoglycan abundance, and hyperglycemia-induced glycocalyx expansion on the surface of mammary epithelial cells and tumors.

Neu5Ac-capped glycans play an essential role for tumor-immune interactions, especially NK-cell surveillance (65, 66). Because we found that hyperglycemia increased the amount of cell surface sialylation, and that HSF1 inhibition was sufficient to prevent this, we checked whether genes associated with the HSF1-associated hyperglycemia response (Fig. 5A) were correlated with immune cell programming (67) in human breast tumors with transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort (68). We observed that tumors with enrichment for these HSF1-target genes were negatively correlated with NK-cell activation signatures (Fig. 5B), suggesting that NK-cell activation and, in turn, killing of target cells, may be adversely affected by HSF1 and hyperglycemia. Direct evidence of this relationship must be carried out in reconstituted systems with human cells and not mouse models because mice use different sialic acid species from humans (69) (NeuAc in human and Neu5Gc in mice), and the cognate receptors on NK cells are different between mice and humans (65), so we assayed human NK cell–mediated killing of human mammary epithelial cells (MCF10A and MDA-MB-231) previously cultured in hyperglycemia with and without KRIBB11 (fig. S8A). NK cells kill target cells by first permeabilizing target cells with perforin, so that they are able to deliver granzymes, which activate apoptotic pathways in the target cell once they reach the cytosol (70). As predicted from differences in glycocalyx thickness, cells cultured in stiff HPLM microenvironments were less sensitive to NK cell–mediated killing if they were cultured in hyperglycemia 24 hours prior to the NK cell challenge.

To verify the potential anticancer utility of these findings, we examined the role of hyperglycemia and HSF1 inhibition in both media to determine whether hyperglycemia conferred NK cell resistance in a microenvironment/HSF1-dependent fashion. We found, consistent with the glycocalyx thickness results (Fig. 3, B and C), that resistance to NK cell killing conferred by hyperglycemia was an effect observable only in physiologic medium (Fig. 5, C to H, and fig. S8, B to D). HSF1 inhibition, which lowers the levels of sialoglycans in both media (Fig. 4B and fig. S7A), was sufficient to abolish the resistance to NK cells conferred by hyperglycemia. HSF1 knockdown in these cells phenocopied the effects of KRIBB11 and prevented the resistance conferred by hyperglycemia in knockdown cells and generally sensitized target cells to NK cell surveillance (Fig. 5H). Together, these functional data corroborate the inverse correlation of NK cell activation with the HSF1-hyperglycemia signature observed in human breast tumors (Fig. 5A) and demonstrate that targeting HSF1 may be sufficient to abolish the NK cell resistance conferred by hyperglycemia. Overall, these findings indicate synergy between ECM stiffness and hyperglycemia that affects metabolic programming necessary to construct an immune-suppressive glycocalyx, which is corroborated by clinical data (49).

DISCUSSION

We were motivated to explore the relationship between microenvironmental factors and their effects on cellular physiology. We found that microenvironmental factors can affect metabolic phenotypes, proteome dynamics, and the glycobiology of mammary epithelial cells. Previous work has shown that stiff and fibrotic mammary TMEs are associated with tumor progression and HSF1-mediated metabolic adaptations (9, 21, 49, 71), which is important since HSF1 is deterministic for metastatic progression (38, 59, 60). Our data indicate a new role for HSF1, in response to the physical properties of the cellular microenvironment and hyperglycemia, which affects hyperglycemia-driven glycocalyx expansion and routing of glucose into Neu5Ac for sialoglycan production. Functionally, these biosynthetic changes had direct consequences for immune surveillance because glycocalyx thickness and sialoglycan abundance are mediators of immune suppression (23). Hyperglycemia conferred resistance to human NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity in a manner dependent on physiological nutrient conditions, matrix stiffness, and HSF1 activity. Suppression of HSF1 reduced sialylation, thinned the glycocalyx, and restored NK cell killing across epithelial and breast cancer models.

Together, our results position the HSF1-glycocalyx pathway as a metabolically driven immune-evasion mechanism that emerges from the cooperation of hyperglycemia and tumor-associated mechanics. These findings have important implications for cancer patients with metabolic dysfunction and suggest that targeting HSF1, or downstream glycosylation pathways, may sensitize tumors to innate immune surveillance. This work highlights the necessity of integrating microenvironmental properties to model the physiological context of cells to identify new cancer vulnerabilities. Therefore, translation-focused preclinical studies, including efforts to augment antitumor immunity or drug cancer cell autonomous programming, should be mindful of the cellular context for the models used because cellular physiology and potential sensitivities are disposed through a dynamic interplay with the microenvironment (72, 73).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human cell culture

MCF10A (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) was originally passaged in [5 mM] glucose DMEM:F12 (1:1 mixture of F:12 [10 mM] glucose and [0 mM] glucose DMEM) (Gibco, 11765054 and 11966025) supplemented with 5% horse serum (HS) (Gibco, 16050-122), epidermal growth factor (20 ng/ml; Peprotech), insulin (10 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), hydrocortisone (0.5 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), cholera toxin (100 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, C8052-2MG), and 1× penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). For experiments comparing HPLM to DMEM, a biological replicate cryopreserved stock of MCF10A cells were thawed and expanded in either DMEM (Gibco,11966025, [5 mM] glucose) or HPLM supplemented with 5% HS (Gibco, 16050-122), epidermal growth factor (20 ng/ml; Peprotech), insulin (10 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), hydrocortisone (0.5 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich), cholera toxin (100 ng/ml; Sigma-Aldrich, C8052-2MG), and 1× penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Subsequent experiments (proteomics, metabolomics, and lectin microarrays) were carried out in the same mediums with 5% dialyzed fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, A3382001) replacing the standard HS used for MCF10A culture. Imaging experiments were carried out in both dialyzed FBS and HS with similar results obtained in either.

KHYG1 cells were provided by A. Karampatzakis and D. H. Raulet (UC Berkeley) and expanded in HPLM supplemented with 10% FBS, with recombinant human interleukin-2 (40 U/ml; Peprotech, 200-02). Cocultures of KHYG1 and MCF10A cells were carried out in MCF10A medium, which did not affect the viability of KHYG1 cells within 24 hours of transfer.

ECM-coated polyacrylamide hydrogel cell culture surfaces

Cleaned (10% ClO, 1 M HCl, then 100% EtOH) round #1 German glass coverslips (Electron Microscopy Services) were coated with 0.5% (v/v) (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES; Sigma-Aldrich, 440140), 99.2% (v/v) ethanol, and 0.3% (v/v) glacial acetic acid for 2 hours and then cleaned in 100% EtOH on an orbital shaker at 22°C. APTES-activated coverslips were coated with PBS-buffered acrylamide/bis-acrylamide (Bio-Rad, 1610140 and 1610142) solutions (3%/0.05% for 400 Pa, 7.5%/0.07% for 6000 Pa, and 10%/0.5% for 60,000 Pa) polymerized with TEMED (0.1%, v/v) (Bio-Rad, 1610801) and potassium persulfate (0.1%, w/v) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, BP180) to yield a final thickness of ∼85 μm. Polyacrylamide gels (PA-gels) were washed with 70% EtOH and sterile PBS before 3,4-dihydroxy-l-phenylalanine (DOPA CAS 59-92-7, Alfa Aeser, A1131106) coating for 5 min at 22°C protected from light with sterile-filtered DOPA in pH 10 [10 mM] tris buffer (76). DOPA-coated PA-gels were washed 2× with sterile PBS and ECM functionalized with human fibronectin (5 μg/ml; Millipore, FC010) in sterile PBS 1 hour at 37°C to generate an expected fibronectin coating density of 6 μM/cm2.

Phasor FLIM

Phasor FLIM (78) was carried out on MCF10A cells (50 to 100 cells) seeded onto fibronectin-coated PA-gels cast on 30-mm coverslips in 35-mm Tissue Culture dishes. Mitochondria labeling was achieved by adding 2 μl of a tetramethyl rhodamine methyl ester (TMRM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, T668) directly to the medium to achieve a final concentration of TMRM of 100 nM. The samples were then placed on the incubated and buffered stage (37°C and 5% CO2) of a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with a water immersion 40× 1.2 numerical aperture (NA) objective and MaiTai two-photon laser (Spectra Physics). FLIM images were acquired by averaging 30 frames of 256 × 256 pixels at 16 μs per pixel with a pixel size of about 200 to 300 nm. The laser excitation was set at 740 nm with laser power optimized to have negligible photobleaching (<10% initial TMRM intensity). FLIM images were acquired using an A320 FastFLIM box device (ISS) coupled to Hamamatsu photo multiplier tube detectors (Hamamatsu, H7422P-40). The fluorescence signal was acquired through a 690-nm dichroic filter and directed to the detectors. NADH signal was acquired through a band pass filter (460/80 nm), and TMRM signal was acquired through a long pass filter (520 nm) and band pass filter (641/75 nm). The signal was processed by SIM FCS software (SIM FCS 4, https://biii.eu/globals-images-simfcs-4) developed by the Laboratory for Fluorescence Dynamics, University of California, Irvine. The phasor transformation was carried out using SIM FCS software calibrating the instrument response with a 100 nM Coumarin 6 solution (mono exponential lifetime at 2.5 ns) as a reference for FLIM. A systematic and automated methodology was used to process and analyze fluorescence lifetime images by way of a custom Python script (https://github.com/aelefebv/lfdfiles). The workflow comprises several steps: file conversion, image segmentation, masking, G (real) and S (imaginary) components calculation, bound NADH fraction determination, and results exportation as CSV files. Initially, raw R64 files were converted to TIFF format for compatibility and image manipulation. The TMRM channel image underwent segmentation to distinguish mitochondrial from cytosolic signals, using a diffuse background subtraction removal technique, as described in our previous paper (79). Masks were applied to corresponding phase and modulation images, enabling the extraction of cellular compartment-specific FLIM information. G and S parameters, key fluorescence lifetime indicators, were calculated. Bound fluorophore fractions (FB) were calculated, providing insight into NADH metabolic trajectory within cellular compartments. Mean FB values were calculated for each sample and exported as CSV files. In addition, the script generated a composite colormap image visualizing NADH FB values and exported G and S coordinates as CSV files for downstream analysis. This custom Python script facilitated comprehensive processing and analysis of fluorescence lifetime images, ensuring reproducibility and accuracy in examining cellular interactions and microenvironments.

Proteomics (timsTOF)

One million cells were seeded on round 50-mm2 varied stiffness ECM-coated PA-gels cultured for 24 hours in HPLM- or DMEM-based MCF10A medium. Cells were washed with sterile PBS once, then cells were detached with cold PBS and a cell scraper (rubber policeman). Samples were prepared using the Sample Preparation by Easy Extraction and Digestion (SPEED) method (80). Briefly, cells were lysed through the addition 1:4 (v/v) of >99% trifluoruacetic acid (TFA) for 3 min. Neutralization buffer (2 M TrisBase) was added at 10× the volume of added TFA, followed by the addition of reduction/alkylation buffer [100 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine and 400 mM 2-chloroacetamide in H2O] at 1.1× the volume of added TFA. Samples were incubated at 95°C for 5 min. Protein concentration was determined using Bradford assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s directions. Trypsin (Promega) was added at a 1:50 enzyme:protein ratio and digested for 20 hours at 37°C and 1000 rpm. The samples were then acidified with TFA (∼1% final) to yield a sample pH of 2. Samples were then desalted on Ultra Micro C18 Spin Column (Nest Group). Samples were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA) and analyzed by DDA-PASEF on a timsTOFpro (Bruker) (81) interfaced with EASY-nLC 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were separated on a 25-cm pepSep column over a 55-min gradient at 0.5 μl/min. Following loading, peptides were analyzed over 65 min at a gradient of 5 to 30% B [0.1% FA in 80% acetonitrile (ACN)], followed by a 1-min ramp to 90% B at high flow (1 μl/min). B was held at 90% for 4 min and then ramped down to 5%. Peptides were sprayed through a 20-mm ZDV emitter held at 1700 V and 200°C. Samples were analyzed in positive ion mode using DDA-PASEF over a 400 to 1550 mass/charge ratio (m/z) range. Raw files were analyzed using the Andromeda search engine (82) in MaxQuant using the default TIMS-DDA settings (83). Variable modifications were set to oxidation of methionine and N-terminal acetylation, with carbamidomethylation of cysteines set as a fixed modification. Up to two missed tryptic cleavages were permitted. Peptide and protein identifications were filtered to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) (84). Searches were performed against a protein database of the human proteome (downloaded from UniProt on 2 February 2020). Quality control analysis was performed via artMS (http://artms.org), and statistical testing was performed with MSstats (85).

LC-MS metabolomics and 13C6-glucose LC-MS metabolomics

One million cells were seeded on 50-mm2 varied stiffness ECM-coated PA-gels cultured for 22 hours in 5 mM glucose DMEM- or HPLM-based MCF10A media. The medium was exchanged for medium with or without 5 mM 13C6-glucose (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories Inc., CLM-1396) in HPLM- or DMEM-based MCF10A media for 2 hours (for 13C6-glucose media, no-glucose DMEM or bespoke no glucose HPLM was used as a base medium that lacked 12C-glucose). Cells were washed twice with PBS and extracted with mass spectrometry–grade 80% methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A456-1) and 20% water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, W6500) supplemented with 1 nmol DL-Norvaline (Sigma-Aldrich, N7502). Protein concentrations of the methanol extract was determined via bicinchoninic acid (BCA; Pierce, 23225) with no significant variability assessed (5 μl transferred into 45 μl of RIPA buffer, 5 μl of the RIPA dissolved solution assayed). Insoluble material was pelleted in a 4°C centrifuge at 16,000g, and supernatant was transferred and dried in a SpeedVac. Dried metabolites were resuspended in 50% ACN:water, and 1/10th of the volume was loaded onto a Luna 3-μm NH2 100A (150 mm by 2.0 mm) column (Phenomenex). The chromatographic separation was performed on a Vanquish Flex (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with mobile phases A (5 mM NH4AcO, pH 9.9) and B (ACN) and a flow rate of 200 μl/min. A linear gradient from 15% A to 95% A over 18 min was followed by 9-min isocratic flow at 95% A and reequilibration to 15% A. Metabolites were detected with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Q Exactive mass spectrometer run with polarity switching (+3.5 kV/−3.5 kV) in full scan mode with an m/z range of 65 to 975. TraceFinder 4.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to quantify the targeted metabolites by area under the curve using expected retention time and accurate mass measurements (<5 parts per million). Values were normalized to cell number and sample protein concentration. Relative amounts of metabolites were calculated by summing up the values for all isotopologs of a given metabolite. Fractional contribution of 13C carbons to total carbon for each metabolite was calculated (86). Data analysis was accomplished using in-house developed R scripts, available at https://github.com/graeberlab-ucla/MetabR.

Metaboverse

Reaction representations and multicondition reaction component comparisons were prepared using Metaboverse (v0.9.0) (40). Input proteomics and metabolomics were prepared by calculating the log2 fold change and Benjamini-Hochberg corrected Student’s t test statistic for each comparison.

Scanning angle interference microscopy

The glycocalyx thickness was mapped in live cells using SAIM, a localization microscopy technique that uses standing waves of excitation light to probe the axial distances, or “heights.” of fluorescently labeled structures above a mirror substrate. Here, the glycocalyx thickness was reported as the height of the plasma membrane above the cell substrate, the intervening gap being occupied by the various macromolecular constituents of the glycocalyx (23, 50–52).

SAIM: Fabrication of silicon substrates

Cells were plated on silicon mirror substrates functionalized with fibronectin to support cell adhesion. To fabricate the substrates, silicon wafers with ∼1900-nm thermal oxide (Addison Engineering) were diced into 7 mm–by–7 mm chips, and the oxide layer thickness for each chip was measured with a FilMetrics F50-EXR. Prior to use, the chips were cleaned with 1:1 methanol and hydrochloric acid for 15 min, followed by 1 min of plasma cleaning (Harrick Plasma, PDC-001). The chips were incubated with 4% (v/v) (3-mercaptopropyl)trimethoxysilane in 100% ethanol for 30 min at room temperature. After washing with 100% ethanol, the chips were subsequently incubated with 4 mM 4-maleimidobutyric acid N-hydroxysuccinimide ester in 100% ethanol for 30 min and then rinsed with PBS. Human plasma fibronectin (50 μg/ml; Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated) in PBS was incubated with the functionalized chips overnight at 4°C. Unconjugated fibronectin was washed from the silicon substrates with PBS rinses.

SAIM: Cell seeding and imaging

Cells were seeded onto the chips at 2 × 104 cells/cm2 in MCF10A cell culture medium and incubated for 24 hours. Cells were labeled with MemGlow Dye 560 (MG02-02, Cytoskeleton) in serum-free cell culture medium for 10 min at 37°C. Samples were then rinsed 3× in PBS, inverted into a 35-mm glass-bottom imaging dish containing serum-free, phenol red–free DMEM/F12 medium, and imaged at 37°C. SAIM was conducted on a custom circle-scanning microscope. The core of the setup was an inverted fluorescence microscope (Ti-E, Nikon). The excitation lasers (488 nm, Coherent; 560 nm, MPB Communications Inc.; and 642 nm, MPB Communications Inc.) were combined into a collinear beam by a series of dichroic mirrors (Chroma). The combined output beam was attenuated and shuttered by an AOTF (AA Opto-Electronic). The beam was directed onto a pair of galvanometer scanning mirrors (Cambridge Technology). The image of the laser on the scanning mirrors was magnified and relayed to the sample by two 4 f lens systems, a beam expanding telescope, and a scan lens/objective lens combination. The beam expander was formed by f = 30 mm and f = 300 mm achromatic lenses with an m = 1 zero-order vortex half-wave plate positioned between them and positioned 2 f from the 300-mm achromatic scan lens (Thorlabs). SAIM experiments were performed with a 60× 1.27 NA water immersion objective (Nikon). Fluorescence emission was collected with a quad-band filter cube and single-band filters (TRF89901-EMv2, Chroma) mounted in a motorized filter wheel (Sutter). Images were acquired with a Zyla 4.2 sCMOS (Andor) camera using the microscope’s 1.5× magnifier for a total magnification of 90×. The open-source software Micro-Manager was used for camera and filter wheel control and image acquisition. The circle-scanning galvanometers were operated in an autonomous fashion using a custom-designed 16-bit PIC microcontroller. Thirty-two images were acquired at varying incidence angles for each excitation channel (i.e., 560 nm for the plasma membrane and 642 nm for the fibronectin layer). During a typical image acquisition sequence, changes in the scanned incidence angle were triggered by a transistor-transistor logic signal from the camera to the microcontroller. The incidence angles were evenly spaced from 5° to 43.75° with respect to the wafer normal in the imaging media.

Parallel plate shear rheology

Viscoelastic properties of tissues were determined using an oscillatory rheometer (Anton-Paar, M302e) with parallel-plate geometry (8 mm) and a gap height of ∼0.2 mm under 0.1% strain and 1-Hz frequency at 37°C in a humidity-controlled chamber.

Flow cytometry (WGA staining)

Data were analyzed with FlowJo (v10) to quantify mean fluorescence intensity of WGA staining on live single cells.

TCGA HSF1-hyperglycemia association

We investigated the relationship between the HSF1-associated hyperglycemia (HG) signature and immune cell populations in breast cancer using TCGA cohort (68), which provides an informed consent process that ensures patients understand how their genetic data and tissue samples will be used for cancer research, prioritizing ethical data sharing and privacy. The gene signatures were previously identified from cells cultured under stiff and soft microenvironment conditions under hyperglycemic conditions. We quantified the 60,000 Pa HSF1-associated HG-signature ST13|MORF4L2|STAG2|NUDT4|FOXP1|TFCP2L1|CREBBP|FLNC|PPP2R5C|HIP1R|SETD2|HIKESHI|GIGYF1|PPP2R5C| as well as the 400 Pa HSF1-associated HG-signature HSPA8|HSP90AB1|PRKAG1|RNF5|ST13|ZC3H11A|MBNL2|EHD1|ZNF280C|MRPS6|CCDC126 activity in each patient tumor using their gene expression profiles. Focusing on breast cancer patients in TCGA (UCSC Xenabrowser, PANCAN TPM) (88), for each patient, we computed the HSF1-associated HG-signature score as the geometric mean of the signature genes’ expression levels. This approach was chosen because geometric means effectively combine genes expressed at different scales while preventing highly expressed genes from disproportionately influencing the final score. We extracted immune cell levels from Thorsson et al. (67). Patient IDs were matched between the expression and immune datasets (11 samples were excluded due to missing immune data). The expression score and immune cell abundances were z-scaled, and a Pearson correlation was computed between them. Multiple hypothesis testing was corrected using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.
