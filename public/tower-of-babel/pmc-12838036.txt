Stratified patterns of healthcare-seeking behavior among older adults in Fujian, China: exploring the behavioral foundations of health inequality
Authors: Wei Luo, Jiaxin Liu, Hao Yang, Wenji Qiu, Jinghong Huang, Xiaojun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina is experiencing rapid population aging amid longstanding but gradually narrowing disparities in health resource allocation. As insurance coverage, primary care capacity, and basic service availability become increasingly equitable, variations in healthcare-seeking behaviors have become a key pathway through which socio-economic differences are reproduced as health inequalities. This study examined the distribution, latent patterns, and socio-demographic correlates of five representative healthcare-seeking behaviors among older adults in China.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,500 older adults in Fuzhou and Xiamen, Fujian Province. Five key healthcare-seeking behaviors were assessed: routine physical examination, use of online medical services, first visit in primary care, delay in seeking treatment, and concomitant prescription–over-the-counter drug use. Latent class analysis (LCA) was used to identify unobserved behavior patterns, and Two-Step Cluster Analysis (TCA) was applied as a robustness check for class stability. Multinomial logistic regression (MLR) was then performed to assess socio-demographic correlates of class membership, with the Passive Symptom-Management Group specified a priori as the reference category.ResultsThree distinct behavior patterns emerged: a Proactive Health Management Group with high engagement in preventive and digital health services; a Reactive Primary-Care Group characterized by high reliance on primary care but limited preventive behavior; and a Passive Symptom-Management Group marked by delayed care and self-medication. LCA and TCA produced largely comparable classifications. Class membership varied significantly by gender, age, income, education, household registration, and self-reported health.ConclusionsHealthcare-seeking behaviors among older Chinese adults are increasingly stratified along socio-economic lines, and these behavioral differences function as mechanisms through which structural health inequalities are sustained even when service availability is relatively equitable. Targeted and class-specific interventions are required, including efforts to strengthen digital access, improve health literacy, and promote proactive engagement with primary care among socio-economically disadvantaged groups. Such measures may help mitigate the emerging behavioral divides in later life.EthicsThe study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (IRB No. 2023 − 152). Introduction

Sustained economic development and continuous improvements in China’s healthcare system have substantially elevated population health, reflected in longer life expectancy, reduced disease burden, and expanded access to essential services. Yet these gains have not translated into improved equity; health disparities continue to widen across social groups [1]. While institutional reforms and technological progress have largely eased the “short supply” of medical resources, inequalities increasingly stem from differences in individuals’ capacity to access, interpret, and utilize available services. These disparities are now understood as “structural health inequalities”—systematic and avoidable differences shaped by social conditions rather than individual choice [2].

Building on Fundamental Cause Theory (FCT) [3], these disparities persist because individuals enter the healthcare system with unequal access to flexible resources (education, income, social networks, and institutional status) that shape their ability to recognize symptoms, navigate care pathways, and engage in preventive action. Healthcare-seeking behaviors are therefore not merely personal preferences but socially patterned responses rooted in broader structural conditions. Advantaged groups consistently convert newly available healthcare opportunities into effective action, whereas disadvantaged groups continue to face informational, cognitive, and institutional barriers that limit their capacity to act on health needs [4]. As healthcare accessibility improves, the primary mechanism of inequality shifts from resource scarcity to behavioral differentiation: those with more flexible resources adopt proactive and preventive behaviors, while others remain constrained in reactive or delayed patterns [5]. This shift highlights the need to move beyond supply-side explanations and attend to the behavioral pathways through which social structure shapes health outcomes, particularly since healthcare-seeking behavior represents the most proximal mechanism linking social conditions to health inequality.

Healthcare-seeking behavior refers to a series of decision-making processes through which individuals determine whether to seek care, when to seek it, where to go, and from whom to receive treatment, which reflect both individual preferences and structural conditions [6, 7]. Although prior studies have examined the influence of socioeconomic status, medical insurance, health literacy, and spatial accessibility on these decisions [8–10], little attention has been paid to a comprehensive reflection on its relation to health inequality. Existing evidence often isolates specific pathways, but a comprehensive explanation of how behavioral patterns mediate the effects of social determinants remains underdeveloped [11]. While social determinants serve as foundational drivers of health inequality, their effects are often mediated through individual-level behaviors rather than directly determining health outcomes [12]. When access to healthcare resources becomes relatively equitable, as is increasingly seen in urban China, variation in healthcare-seeking behavior emerges as the most immediate and observable expression of inequality. Thus, instead of only asking “which groups are healthier”, it becomes essential to ask “how different groups use health services” and “how these patterned behaviors reflect underlying social structures”. These considerations underscore the need to analyze healthcare-seeking not as isolated actions but as socially conditioned behavioral configurations that contribute to the persistence of health inequality.

These considerations underscore the need to analyze healthcare-seeking not as isolated actions but as socially conditioned behavioral configurations that contribute to the persistence of health inequality.

Furthermore, current research on health-seeking behaviors, especially the relevant literature in the Chinese context, also shows limitations in the identification and measurement of such behaviors. First, many studies are based on a single behavioral dimension or specific decision segments, making it difficult to describe the structural diversity of healthcare-seeking behavior patterns [13–16]. Second, the aims of these studies often center on the analysis of correlations between variables rather than identifying or interpreting patterns and combinations of healthcare behaviors [17, 18]. Healthcare-seeking behavior involves a series of decision-making processes; the differences in whether, when, and how to seek healthcare collectively constitute behavioral heterogeneity in service utilization. Single behavioral indicators cannot sufficiently capture such structural differences, nor can it intuitively reveal how health inequalities are produced and differentiated at the behavioral level.

As individuals age, older adults experience a gradual decline in physical and immune function, resulting in higher healthcare demands and more frequent, complex healthcare-seeking behaviors compared to other age groups. In China, which has the largest elderly population and the fastest aging rate [19], these patterns are particularly pronounced. The intersection of high individual-level need and macro-level demographic weight makes older Chinese adults a uniquely identifiable and intervention-worthy population in terms of health inequalities. Based on this, to comprehensively depict the behavioral heterogeneity in service utilization among older adults, this study concentrates on the Chinese elderly population.

Combining previous research and relevant definitions [16, 20–22], we selected five representative healthcare-seeking behavior indicators: Routine Physical Examination, Use of Online Medical Services, First Visit in Primary Care, Delay in Seeking Medical Treatment, and Concomitant Use of Prescription and Over-the-Counter Drugs. These indicators correspond to the most critical and policy-relevant decision nodes in the healthcare utilization pathway among older adults in China: Routine physical examination captures the proactive dimension of preventive care; online medical service use reflects digital pathway adoption; primary-care first visit choice indicates institutional preference within the tiered healthcare system; delay in seeking treatment reflects timing judgment essential for early intervention; and concomitant drug use represents risk-control capacity in medication management. Together, these indicators encompass five key behavioral dimensions (proactivity, technology adoption, institutional preference, timing judgment, and risk control) and capture the structural differentiation in how older adults engage with the healthcare system. Given the lack of discussion about the identification and interpretation of behavioral patterns, this study adopts latent category analysis (LCA) and two-step cluster analysis (TCA) to identify the potential patterns and structural differentiation among older adults’ healthcare-seeking behaviors. LCA is a statistical method for identifying unobserved classes based on patterns of categorical data [23]. Unlike the approaches that examine each behavior independently, LCA captures how multiple behaviors co-occur within the same individual. This perspective is crucial because single behaviors reveal only limited information when viewed in isolation. By uncovering their joint distribution, LCA identifies coherent behavioral profiles that reflect socially patterned responses rather than independent choices, thereby offering a window into the structural conditions shaping healthcare-seeking. TCA was subsequently employed as a cross-method robustness check to assess the stability of the broad behavioral structure identified by the LCA.

Building on this analytical framework, we outline the research objectives and hypotheses as follows. This study seeks to identify distinct patterns of healthcare-seeking behavior among older adults based on five key behavioral dimensions and to provide a comprehensive characterization of behavioral heterogeneity in their healthcare utilization. It further examines how these latent behavioral patterns differ across socio-demographic groups. Drawing on existing evidence, we hypothesize that:

Older adults with socio-demographic advantages are more likely to cluster into patterns characterized by proactive, timely, and well-structured healthcare use;

Materials and methods

Study setting and participants

We collected data from December 2024 to January 2025 in Fuzhou and Xiamen, the two major administrative and economic centers of Fujian Province, China. These two cities were selected because they share relatively developed healthcare infrastructures, broad insurance coverage, and comparable primary care capacity. Such conditions minimize structural access barriers, making them appropriate settings for examining behavioral rather than supply-side determinants of healthcare-seeking patterns. As provincial-level urban centers with established chronic disease management programs and dense primary care networks, Fuzhou and Xiamen also represent typical implementation environments for elderly health governance in Fujian, providing suitable sites for observing behavioral differentiation under relatively equitable service availability.

We used convenience sampling to recruit participants in public spaces to capture a diverse participant pool, including parks, senior community activity centers, and areas where older adults congregate around public buildings (e.g., shopping malls, bus/subway stations, hospital square). Participants who met the following selection criteria were included: (1) aged 55 years or older; (2) able to complete the questionnaire independently without assistance; and (3) voluntarily agreed to participate and provided written informed consent. Respondents were excluded if they had multiple unanswered items in the questionnaire. In total, 1,500 questionnaires were distributed, and 1,460 were retained for analysis (validity rate: 97.33%).

This sampling strategy enabled efficient access to older adults with diverse healthcare-seeking experiences. However, it may have overrepresented individuals who were more mobile, socially active, or in better health, while homebound, frail, institutionalized, or rural older adults were less likely to be captured. Thus, although the sample is appropriate for identifying latent behavioral patterns, the resulting class proportions should not be interpreted as population-level prevalence estimates. No weighting or post-stratification adjustments were applied, as the non-probability sampling design does not permit reliable calibration to the population structure.

Measures

Socio-demographic characteristics

In this study, socio-demographic variables included age (continuous), gender (male and female), marital status (married and others such as unmarried, divorced, separated), and self-reported health status (poor, fair, or good). Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed via three indicators: (1) monthly per capita household income (< 2,000 CNY, 2,000–4,999 CNY, 5,000–9,999 CNY, ≥ 10,000 CNY). (2) household registration status (rural or urban hukou). (3) education level (primary school or below, middle and high school, college and above).

Healthcare-seeking behaviors

Healthcare-seeking behaviors were assessed using selected items from the sixth National Health Service Survey (NHSS) [24], a nationally representative survey administered by the National Health Commission of China. Only items relevant to this study (e.g., first-contact institution, delay in seeking care, self-medication) were included, with minor wording adjustments for contextual relevance. These NHSS-derived items have been widely applied in population-based studies of older adults and have undergone repeated methodological validation across multiple survey waves and age groups [25–27]. As the five indicators in this study represent single-item behavioral measures covering heterogeneous domains (preventive examination, digital service use, first-contact choice, treatment delay, and self-medication), internal consistency metrics such as Cronbach’s α are not applicable and each item was treated as an independent manifest variable following standard LCA practice. The adapted English version is provided in the supplementary material.

In this study, healthcare-seeking behaviors comprised five factors, including Routine Physical Examination (RPE), Use of Online Medical Services (OMS), First Visit in Primary Care (FVPC), Delay in Seeking Medical Treatment (DSMT), and Concomitant Use of Prescription and Over-the-Counter Drugs (CPOD). Among these, RPE, OMS, and FVPC were treated as proactive behaviors, while DSMT and CPOD were considered negative or potentially adverse utilization patterns.

Routine Physical Examination reflects engagement in preventive health services. This behavior was assessed using the item: “Have you undergone a routine physical examination in the past year?” (Yes/No). Responses were coded as 1 for Yes and 0 for No.

Use of Online Medical Services captures engagement in digital health resources. It was measured through the question: “Have you utilized web-based consultations (e.g., hospital websites, mobile apps) in the past two weeks?” (Yes/No). Responses were coded as 1 for Yes and 0 for No.

First Visit in Primary Care reflects initial healthcare-seeking preference within China’s tiered health system. Participants were asked: “Where do you usually go first when experiencing illness, injury, or physical discomfort?”. Options corresponding to primary care facilities (community health service stations, township health centers, community health service centers, and local private clinics), which are part of China’s primary health-care system [28] were coded as 1; visits to general or specialized hospitals or other institutions were coded as 0.

Delay in seeking medical treatment was used to capture the timeliness dimension of healthcare-seeking behavior. The measure was based on the question: “How long after symptom onset did you first seek medical treatment?”. Responses of “More than 1 month” were coded as delayed care (1), and responses of “Within 1 month” were coded as no delay (0). This threshold is consistent with patterns of symptom recognition and decision-making among older adults reported in prior studies [29].

Concomitant Use of Prescription and Over-the-Counter Drugs captures self-medication practices involving simultaneous use of different medication types. It was assessed using two questions: “Have you self-medicated with prescription drugs (including antibiotics) in the past two weeks?” (Yes/No) and “Have you self-medicated with over-the-counter medications in the past two weeks?” (Yes/No). Participants who answered Yes to both items were coded as 1, indicating concomitant use. All other responses were coded as 0.

Analytic strategy

We conducted data analyses using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, United States) and Mplus version 8.3 (Los Angeles, CA, United States) for Windows. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The analysis consisted of four parts. First, we conducted descriptive statistics to describe the sample characteristics and the distribution of healthcare-seeking behaviors. Second, we performed Latent Class Analysis (LCA) to identify unobserved subgroups (latent classes) based on the five healthcare-seeking behaviors. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (ABIC), with lower values indicating better fit. Classification precision was evaluated using entropy (≥ 0.80 indicating high classification accuracy). In addition, we applied the Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT) and the Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) to compare differences in model fit, where a significant p-value indicated that the current model was superior to the previous one. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for the two-, three-, four-, and five-class models to verify robustness.

Third, we conducted a Two-Step Cluster Analysis (TCA) as an exploratory robustness assessment to examine whether broad behavioral distinctions similar to those identified by the LCA would also emerge using a non–model-based clustering approach. The analysis included the same five binary healthcare-seeking indicators (RPE, OMS, FVPC, DSMT, CPOD). The TCA procedure consisted of an initial pre-clustering step followed by hierarchical clustering based on the log-likelihood distance measure. Schwarz’s Bayesian Criterion was used to select the optimal number of clusters, and silhouette width was used to assess cohesion and separation. Because TCA does not account for classification uncertainty and cluster profiles did not form substantively coherent behavioral patterns, TCA-derived clusters were used only for descriptive comparison and were not included in inferential analyses.

Fourth, we applied chi-square tests (χ²) and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare demographics across the latent and cluster-based groups. Associations between sociodemographic variables and latent class membership were then estimated using multinomial logistic regression (MLR). The Passive Symptom-Management Group was specified as the reference category. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI).

Missing data were minimal. None of the behavioral indicators had missing values, and less than 1% of sociodemographic variables contained missingness. Given the negligible and non-systematic nature of missing data, listwise deletion was applied, and the analytic sample remained consistent across all models.

Latent class analysis of healthcare-seeking patterns among older adults

We estimated LCA models with two to five classes, and model fit statistics for competing solutions are presented in Table 2. The two-class solution showed a substantial decrease in AIC and BIC compared with the one-class model, indicating improved model fit. However, the two classes collapsed distinct behavioral mechanisms into overly broad polarity-based categories—one uniformly “positive” and one uniformly “negative”. For instance, respondents showing high primary-care utilization but low preventive engagement were forced into the same category as those delaying treatment and engaging in risky medication practices, despite reflecting qualitatively different decision-making patterns. Such dichotomization is a hallmark of under-extraction, where meaningful heterogeneity is masked by coarse grouping. These limitations are consistent with established criteria for rejecting under-fitted LCA solutions [30].

Although the four-class model yielded a marginally lower BIC value than the three-class solution, the magnitude of improvement was weak according to Kass and Raftery’s [31] standards (ΔBIC < 10). The four-class model also did not improve classification precision, as entropy declined slightly (0.834 vs. 0.836). More importantly, the additional fourth class displayed diffuse and inconsistent conditional probabilities that did not correspond to a coherent behavioral mechanism, indicating over-extraction consistent with the guidelines summarized by Nylund et al. [32].

The three-class solution offered the best balance of statistical fit, behavioral differentiation, parsimony, and theoretical interpretability. It produced three well-separated and policy-relevant behavioral profiles with adequate class sizes, making it the most appropriate representation of the latent heterogeneity in this population. Local independence was supported, with all bivariate standardized residuals below 3.00 (highest = 2.46). Therefore, the three-class model was retained for subsequent analyses.

Class 1 (reactive primary-care group; n = 266, 18.26%)

This class showed the highest probability of using primary care as the first point of contact (FVPC = 0.921) and a low probability of delaying medical care (DSMT = 0.037). However, this group exhibited low probabilities of proactive behaviors such as routine physical examination (RPE = 0.111) and use of online medical services (OMS = 0.068), along with a high likelihood of concomitant use of both prescription and over-the-counter drugs (CPOD = 0.750). This pattern reflects a “traditional treatment-oriented” healthcare strategy common among older adults who regard primary care as a convenient site for symptomatic treatment but do not actively invest in preventive check-ups or digital services. Their behavior is responsive rather than anticipatory—“seeing a doctor when sick,” sometimes preceded by self-medication, but rarely oriented toward sustained prevention or health monitoring.

Class 2 (passive symptom-management group; n = 538, 36.80%)

This group had the lowest probabilities of proactive behaviors (RPE = 0.017; OMS = 0.004) and the highest probabilities of passive or adverse behaviors, including delayed seeking medical treatment (DSMT = 0.929) and concurrent use of both prescription and over-the-counter medications (CPOD = 0.946). In contrast to Class 1, these individuals do not routinely seek timely primary-care services. Instead, they tend to postpone formal care, rely on self-directed symptom management, and delay professional intervention until symptoms become unavoidable. This latent class captures a behavior mechanism marked by avoidance, delay, and self-reliance, representing a distinct pathway of risk escalation different from the reactive-but-compliant pattern seen in Class 1.

Class 3 (proactive health management group; n = 656, 44.94%)

Members of this class displayed a clearly proactive orientation toward healthcare. In this class, proactive healthcare-seeking behaviors, including routine physical examination, use of online medical services and were highly probabilities (RPE = 0.850; OMS = 0.772) and reported lower probabilities of passive healthcare-seeking behaviors, especially in concurrent use of both prescription and over-the-counter medications (DSMT = 0.032; CPOD = 0.274). This pattern reflects a health-literate, digitally engaged group that actively invests in prevention, seeks timely professional care, and integrates online resources into health management. These individuals represent the most advantaged behavioral profile and form a clear contrast to Classes 1 and 2 in both agency and timeliness.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis of factors associated with the latent classes of healthcare-seeking behaviors

Table 6 presents the associations between socio-demographic factors and the likelihood of belonging to the Reactive Primary-Care Group or the Proactive Health Management Group, using the Passive Symptom-Management Group as the reference category. The analysis included gender, age, marital status, self-reported health, monthly per capita household income, household registration status, and education. The overall multinomial logistic regression model significantly improved model fit over the null model (χ² = 1430.18, df = 22, p < 0.001). The Nagelkerke pseudo R² was 0.718, indicating strong explanatory power. Deviance goodness-of-fit statistics suggested an adequate model fit (χ² = 903.60, df = 1270, p = 1.000).

Discussion

Based on Latent Class Analysis (LCA), three distinct healthcare-seeking behavior patterns were identified among older adult populations: the Proactive Health Management Group (44.94%), the Passive Symptom-Management Group (36.80%), and the Reactive Primary-Care Group (18.26%). These findings reveal that healthcare-seeking behavior among older adults does not conform to a simple dichotomous (positive/negative) structure but rather constitutes a complex spectrum incorporating active, passive, and mixed behaviors. This suggests that variations in healthcare-seeking behavior are not solely determined by differences in healthcare resource availability but are also closely associated with socio-demographic heterogeneity, even under relatively comparable institutional conditions [33–35]. To complement the LCA results, a two-step cluster analysis was additionally conducted as an exploratory cross-method check. The clusters showed a broadly consistent distributional structure with the LCA classes, supporting the overall stability of the classification, although the findings were not used for substantive interpretation.

Specifically, the Proactive Health Management Group displays a significant prevention-oriented characteristic. Within this group, 84.8% reported receiving RPE, and 77.2% proactively utilized online health services. These behaviors reflect not only active engagement with available services but also a forward-looking orientation toward health as a long-term resource that requires continuous investment [36]. This pattern aligns with findings from Bu et al., who identified a similar “high-utilization” profile among older migrants in China [37]. In comparison, the Passive Symptom-Management Group exhibited a symptom-triggered and reactive pattern of healthcare use, with extremely low uptake of preventive services and a high prevalence of delaying treatment and self-medication. This behavior pattern reveals that members of this group may encounter multiple constraints regarding resource access and health literacy. Consistent with previous studies [38], their behavioral profile reflects a concentration of structural disadvantage. The co-occurrence of passive healthcare-seeking behaviors within this latent class, accompanied by a higher prevalence of socio-demographic disadvantage, indicates that these behaviors may reflect broader structural constraints rather than independent individual choices.

Notably, an intermediate behavioral pattern, the Reactive Primary-Care Group, was identified between the polar behaviors of proactive prevention and reactive response. Individuals in this group demonstrated a clear reliance on primary healthcare institutions for initial care (FVPC = 91.9%), suggesting strong alignment with national efforts to promote a primary-care-first utilization pathway. Their choices indicate functional responsiveness to accessibility-oriented policies aimed at strengthening community-level gatekeeping. However, their engagement in preventive and technology-enabled behaviors remained low (RPE = 10.9%; OMS = 6.7%). At the same time, reactive behaviors displayed intermediate levels (DSMT = 3.7%; CPOD = 75.4%), forming a pattern best described as “basic-needs–oriented.” This combination reflects a form of compliance without prevention: older adults in this group follow recommended treatment pathways when symptoms arise but show limited initiative for prevention, long-term planning, and digital engagement. This configuration is consistent with long-standing features of China’s health system, where treatment-oriented norms have historically outweighed preventive practices. For many older adults, symptom-triggered utilization remains the default behavioral repertoire, even in contexts where preventive services are readily available. The Reactive Primary-Care Group therefore represents both a warning signal and a strategic opportunity. Their partial alignment with policy goals suggests behavioral flexibility; yet without targeted intervention, they may drift toward more passive and risk-prone patterns. Strengthening preventive literacy, improving digital health competence, and supporting long-term health planning may help guide this group toward more proactive behaviors. Because they already engage with primary care, interventions delivered through community health centers, such as preventive counseling, digital navigation assistance, and personalized health management plans, may yield substantial population-level benefits at relatively low cost.

When medical resources are relatively accessible, the formation of health disparities tends to shift from structural supply shortages toward differences in individuals’ healthcare-seeking behaviors. From the perspective of the Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) framework [39], these behavioral differences are not simply personal choices but patterned outcomes shaped by education, income, household registration, and other social conditions that affect individuals’ access to information, health literacy, and their ability to navigate the healthcare system. This pattern aligns with the Fundamental Cause Theory [3], which posits that socioeconomic inequalities in health persist because higher-SES groups possess greater flexible resources, enabling them to better leverage available health opportunities and adopt advantageous behaviors. Understanding these contextual determinants helps clarify why heterogeneous behavioral patterns emerge and how they may contribute to the reproduction of health inequalities. Guided by this framework, the present study further examined how key socioeconomic factors relate to the latent behavioral classes. We conducted a logistic regression analysis, focusing on socioeconomic status (SES) elements such as education level, income, and household registration, to investigate their roles in the formation process of different behavior patterns. Emphasizing SES enabled us to reveal the social structuring underlying the behavioral differentiation observed in the LCA and to highlight the structural roots of the three healthcare-seeking pathways.

At the income level, this study found that high-income individuals are more likely to be classified as the Proactive Health Management Group. Economic conditions play an important role in preventive healthcare behaviors. Income not only relates to the payment for medical services but also determines the level of resources and the quality of the environment available to individuals [40]. When resources are limited, basic survival priorities tend to crowd out investment in preventive care. Low-income older adults often face structural constraints that reduce their capacity to engage in prevention, including limited access to reliable health information, poorer living conditions, and greater time pressure. Even when awareness of health risks exists, these constraints make it difficult for health intentions to translate into action [41]. When faced with illness, the sensitivity of low-income groups to potential costs also reinforces the invisible barriers to healthcare decision-making, leading to passive behaviors (such as delaying medical treatment) that stem more from resource limitations than from individual preferences.

Compared with rural hukou holders, older adults with urban household registration were more likely to fall into the Reactive Primary-Care Group and the Proactive Health Management Group. This pattern aligns with well-documented structural differences embedded in the hukou system, which shapes long-term access to education, employment opportunities, social welfare, and healthcare resources [42]. Rural hukou holders often encounter reduced access to high-quality medical services [43, 44], and fewer opportunities to engage with preventive or digitally mediated care, contributing to lower levels of proactive healthcare seeking [45]. Moreover, primary healthcare facilities in rural areas typically have weaker service capacity [46, 47], and residents in remote regions may have limited exposure to emerging service models and authoritative preventive information [48–50]. Prior studies have described this constellation of disadvantages as a form of structural marginalization rooted in spatial and institutional inequalities, particularly those perpetuated by the hukou system [51, 52]. These contextual constraints provide a plausible explanation for the lower presence of rural hukou holders in behavior patterns characterized by proactive or well-structured healthcare engagement. Together, the objective resource disparities in rural areas and the behavioral disadvantages observed among rural hukou holders in the present study collectively reflect the comprehensive nature of urban-rural inequalities in China, which manifest not only in physical infrastructure but also in healthcare-seeking behaviors.

Education is also a key factor in the differentiation of healthcare-seeking behavior. Older adults with higher education levels are more likely to be categorized into the Proactive Health Management Group, exhibiting higher rates of RPE and OMS. Within the SDOH framework, education shapes individuals’ access to health information, cognitive processing capacity, and health-related decision-making, all of which contribute to more structured and proactive healthcare use [53]. Conversely, lower education can limit the ability to recognize health risks, interpret preventive information, and navigate increasingly digitalized service pathways, making symptom-driven care more common [54, 55]. Prior research has consistently shown that low educational attainment is associated with reduced health literacy and lower levels of digital competence [56], which may hinder participation in preventive or technology-mediated care. In this study as well, the rate of OMS use among low-education groups was notably low, suggesting reduced ability to take advantage of emerging access channels. Therefore, for older adults with low education levels, the lack of proactive health behaviors is not due to a lack of health awareness, but rather constrained by cognitive burdens, information barriers, and insufficient digital skills. These structural limitations ultimately evolve into health inequalities.

Taken together, the systematic stratification of healthcare-seeking behaviors observed across the three latent classes provides a clear illustration of the logic emphasized in Fundamental Cause Theory. The socioeconomic gradients in education, income, and household registration map onto unequal distributions of what FCT terms flexible resources. These resources help explain why the Proactive Health Management Group is able to sustain prevention-oriented and digitally enabled behaviors, while the constrained resources among the Passive Symptom-Management Group limit their options to delayed or symptom-driven responses. Even the behavior of the Reactive Primary-Care Group can be understood as reflecting partial access to institutional pathways without the additional resources required for proactive planning. In this way, the three behavioral patterns are not merely correlated with socioeconomic status; they represent the pathways through which structural advantages and disadvantages translate into differentiated health behaviors, even in contexts where overall service availability has improved.

Social structural factors do not operate independently; rather, they intersect to produce cumulative advantage or cumulative disadvantage that shapes healthcare-seeking patterns [57]. The multinomial regression findings in this study suggest a clear tendency toward cumulative disadvantage: older adults with rural household registration, lower income, and lower education were more likely to belong to the Passive Symptom-Management Group. These overlapping disadvantages can constrain access to information, reduce health decision-making capacity, and limit opportunities to engage in preventive care. Conversely, the Proactive Health Management Group was more often composed of individuals with higher education, stronger economic resources, and urban residence—conditions that provide greater access to health information, digital resources, and higher-quality services. Although self-reported health is not a structural determinant in the same sense as income or education, individuals reporting better health were more frequently represented in the Proactive Health Management Group. This pattern is plausible given the well-documented bidirectional relationship between self-reported health and health behaviors [58]. Nonetheless, these associations should be interpreted with caution due to the cross-sectional design. Overall, the observed behavioral patterns reflect the interaction of resources, opportunities, and constraints under varying social conditions, illustrating how health inequalities may be reproduced through differentiated behavioral pathways in later life.

Building on existing research on health inequalities and the healthcare-seeking behavior of older adults, this study identifies three patterned pathways of healthcare-seeking among older adults and examines the social structural mechanisms shaping these patterns. The latent class analysis reveals that even under a relatively unified and accessible healthcare system, substantial heterogeneity persists in how older adults recognize needs, seek care, and engage with preventive services. This distinction between resource accessibility and behavioral accessibility highlights an overlooked layer of inequality: individuals may face similar service availability yet remain differentiated by their ability to mobilize information, navigate institutions, and plan for long-term health. The observed patterns reflect the cumulative influence of education, income, and household registration—factors that shape cognitive resources, economic capacity, and institutional opportunity. These social conditions jointly form the structural context within which healthcare-seeking behaviors emerge. Accordingly, differences in healthcare-seeking are not merely expressions of individual preference but adaptive responses to unequal resources, information environments, and institutional constraints. These structurally patterned behaviors constitute a proximal mechanism through which health inequalities are generated and sustained. By identifying how distinct behavioral pathways map onto socioeconomic stratification, this study underscores the need for policies that address not only service provision but also the social conditions that organize and differentiate healthcare-seeking in later life.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between sociodemographic characteristics and healthcare-seeking behaviors. Second, clinical information such as disease severity and chronicity was not collected. Because illness acuity and complexity can substantially shape care-seeking decisions, the absence of these data may constrain the interpretation of certain behavioral patterns.

Third, the study used convenience sampling in public spaces across two urban centers with relatively equitable access to healthcare resources. This sampling approach favored the inclusion of more mobile, socially active, and healthier older adults, while homebound, frail, rural, or remote populations were underrepresented. Consequently, the latent class proportions identified by LCA and TCA (e.g., the 44.94% Proactive Health Management Group) reflect the distribution within this specific sample and should not be interpreted as population-level behavioral prevalence. The findings therefore may not generalize to older adults facing distinct access barriers or different contextual constraints.

Finally, the combined use of Latent Class Analysis and Two-Step Cluster Analysis introduces inherent classification uncertainty. Although the entropy value of the selected LCA model (0.837) indicates good class separation, some degree of misclassification is unavoidable in probabilistic modeling. This uncertainty may affect class-specific estimates and should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Future research would benefit from probability-based sampling, richer clinical data, and longitudinal or mixed-method designs to improve representativeness and deepen causal understanding.

Conclusion

The three latent classes reveal a clear socioeconomic gradient in healthcare-seeking behavior, identifying distinct behavioral mechanisms through which health inequalities are reproduced: the Proactive Health Management Group reflects accumulated advantages in education, income, and urban residence; the Reactive Primary-Care Group occupies an intermediate position characterized by partial access but limited preventive capacity; and the Passive Symptom-Management Group reflects concentrated socioeconomic disadvantage. Together, these stratified patterns indicate that healthcare-seeking is not merely an individual decision but reflects socially patterned pathways shaped by underlying socioeconomic conditions. To mitigate these inequities, policy interventions must directly target these core behavioral mechanisms. Linking these patterns to policy action highlights the need for stratified, behavior-specific intervention strategies. For the Passive Symptom-Management Group, reducing informational, spatial, and institutional constraints remains essential, particularly by improving access to preventive services and strengthening support for socioeconomically disadvantaged older adults. For the Reactive Primary-Care Group, whose reliance on primary care aligns with policy expectations but whose preventive engagement remains limited, targeted efforts to enhance preventive literacy, digital competence, and long-term health planning may help shift treatment-focused behaviors toward more proactive orientations. The Proactive Health Management Group already demonstrates strong preventive and digital behaviors; for this group, maintaining supportive environments can help preserve their existing advantages without requiring substantial additional intervention. By integrating behavioral typologies with the social determinants of health, this study underscores that advancing equity requires attention not only to the distribution of medical resources but also to the structural conditions that generate and sustain these differential behavioral pathways. These findings clarify the behavioral mechanisms underlying health inequality and provide an analytical foundation for designing stratified, behavior-specific public health interventions that address the very pathways through which social disadvantage becomes health disadvantage.
