Managing mitral annular calcification—high-end robotic mitral valve repair for experienced surgeons
Authors: Johannes Bonatti, Kei Kobayashi
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article and accompanying video by Pickering and coworkers on a robotic mitral valve reconstruction which involved detachment of the posterior leaflet, removal of mitral annular calcification (MAC), and re-attachment of the leaflet (1). This remains a procedure that many surgeons approach with caution, and often avoid in the conventional open approach through sternotomy, and even more so in robotic cardiac surgery.

The increased morbidity associated with this procedure is obviously the main reason for this common aversion. As reported by Ribeiro and coworkers in their systematic review and meta-analysis, patients with MAC undergoing mitral valve intervention had higher rates of complications, including reoperation for bleeding, permanent pacemaker implantation, and periprosthetic leak. In surgical mitral valve cases, perioperative mortality was not significantly different between patients with and without MAC, whereas midterm mortality was higher among patients with MAC (2). This analysis, however, included both mitral valve repair and replacement. The more recent systematic review by Hage and coworkers summarized contemporary early and late outcomes after mitral valve surgery in patients with MAC, highlighting the heterogeneity of operative strategies and patient risk profiles (3).

There are only a few centers with significant experience concerning robotic mitral surgery in the setting of MAC. The most prominent experience comes from the group of Drs. Didier Loulmet and Eugene Grossi at New York University. Even the title of their publication in 2021 stated that “advanced experience” allows robotic mitral valve repair in the presence of extensive MAC (4). In their cohort of 64 patients with MAC, 54 patients (84%) had Barlow’s disease, and they reported a median cross-clamp time of 122 minutes, conversion to replacement in 2 patients (3.1%), no perioperative stroke or pacemaker implantation, and a 30-day mortality of 3.1%. They emphasized in their conclusion that only a team involving two experienced robotic surgeons should tackle this operation because sewing a patch to the debrided myocardium with proper tension requires extreme finesse and adequate coordination between console surgeon and table side surgeon. Their preference for en bloc resection of MAC is justified by the fact that placing sutures around the calcium block may increase the risk of circumflex artery injury, fracture of the calcium, release of liquefied calcium into the left ventricle, and distortion of the implants. The same group subsequently expanded this experience to 217 patients with significant MAC, including 69 patients requiring pericardial patch ventriculoplasty, with comparable outcomes between the patch and non-patch group (5). These data provide important support for the feasibility of complete MAC resection and atrioventricular groove reconstruction in experienced robotic programs.

In our recent analysis of automated performance metrics in robotically assisted mitral valve repair, annular calcification and decalcification were associated with longer console time. Decalcification emerged as the strongest independent predictor of prolonged console time, supporting the idea that MAC is not only an anatomical challenge but also a measurable driver of robotic operative complexity (6). Longer console time was also associated with selected perioperative outcomes, including longer hospital stay, transfusion, reoperation for bleeding, and lower likelihood of extubation in the operating room, suggesting that increased technical complexity may have measurable clinical consequences.

Pickering and colleagues should be commended for sharing a highly informative video article on robotic MAC decalcification which will be very valuable for robotic cardiac surgeons who are seeking to adopt the technique. The authors emphasized the importance of dedicated robotic team with a skilled bedside assistant. They have also highlighted proper case selection. Special attention needs to be paid to the circumflex coronary artery, the extent of annular and leaflet calcification, and the feasibility of achieving a durable repair. In selected patients with prohibitive surgical anatomy, transcatheter mitral valve replacement in MAC may need to be considered, although this strategy carries important limitations and should not be viewed as a simple substitute for surgical reconstruction. The video also nicely shows that real en bloc resection may not always be possible and “piecemeal” decalcification is often necessary depending on the extent, density, and distribution of calcification. It is worth emphasizing that atrioventricular groove disruption or rupture remains one of the most devastating complications of extensive MAC decalcification. In a contemporary 25-year series by Arghami and collegues, five patients (0.3%) had atrioventricular disruption and all died during the index hospitalization (7). Risk mitigation begins with careful assessment of the location and depth of calcification, limiting decalcification when complete removal is unnecessary, and readiness to perform primary or patch reconstruction when atrioventricular groove integrity is compromised (8). Loulmet and colleagues were explicit regarding patient selection, stating that repair was considered only when the posterior leaflet was pliable, and patients with a totally calcified posterior leaflet were not considered candidates for totally endoscopic robotic repair (4).

Taken together, despite the substantial complexity of mitral valve repair in the setting of MAC, robotic surgery is emerging as a feasible option for carefully selected patients when performed by highly experienced teams. In the current era, however, it is important to note that outcomes may not be reproducible in lower-volume robotic programs and that the learning curve for robotic MAC surgery is likely substantially steeper than that for standard robotic mitral repair. Further reports from additional centers will be important to define reproducibility, durability, optimal patient selection, and whether these advanced robotic techniques can be safely disseminated beyond a small number of expert programs.
