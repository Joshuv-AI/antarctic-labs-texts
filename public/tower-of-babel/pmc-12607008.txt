Protein acylation in inflammatory diseases: from mechanisms to therapeutic strategies
Authors: Jiayi Ding, Huiyi Wang, Zhengkun Yang, Xiaoxuan Wang, Zhengguo Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Protein acylation, a critical subset of post-translational modifications (PTMs), serves as a dynamic regulatory mechanism linking cellular metabolism, epigenetic regulation, and inflammatory responses. This review systematically elucidates the roles of protein acylation modifications—including acetylation, lactylation, succinylation, propionylation, crotonylation, malonylation, butyrylation, S-palmitoylation, and myristoylation—in the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases. These modifications, orchestrated by acyltransferases (writers), deacylases (erasers), and recognition proteins (readers), regulate immune cell functionality and disease progression. In immune cells, specific acylation patterns govern inflammatory responses by modulating polarization, cytokine production, and signaling pathways. Furthermore, dysregulated protein acylation contributes to the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases such as sepsis, periodontitis, inflammatory bowel disease, atherosclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis through disrupting immune homeostasis, driving metabolic reprogramming, and impairing tissue repair. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting acylation-related enzymes or leveraging nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems show promise in restoring balanced PTM dynamics and alleviating disease progression. By systematically mapping protein acylation networks across immune and diseased cells, this review provides insights into novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic interventions for inflammatory diseases.

Introduction

Inflammation represents an internal defense response orchestrated by the immune system, aimed at eliminating harmful stimuli and initiating tissue repair processes [1]. Prolonged chronic and uncontrolled acute inflammation may progress into inflammatory diseases [2]. These inflammatory diseases exhibit high incidence rates and are challenging to treat, imposing significant burden on patients [3, 4]. Furthermore, current understanding of the pathogenesis of inflammation and inflammatory diseases remains insufficient, hindering the development of more effective therapeutic means for inflammatory diseases [5].

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) refer to biochemical modifications of proteins that occur after protein synthesis. PTMs include the introduction of functional groups such as methyl, glycosyl, and acyl groups, which can impact protein stability and function [6]. In recent years, protein acylation, as an important PTM, has been demonstrated to play a crucial role in both normal physiological as well as pathological conditions [7]. In particular, multiple studies have indicated that protein acylation can serve as a potential biomarker for the diagnosis of inflammatory diseases [8, 9]. Furthermore, therapeutic interventions targeting protein acylation, such as the use of histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, have exhibited potential in alleviating the progression of inflammatory diseases [10]. Thus, unraveling pathogenic mechanisms and identifying new therapeutic targets lies on comprehensive understanding of the contribution of protein acylation to inflammation and inflammatory diseases.

Since the discovery of histone acetylation in 1963 [11], the field of epigenetics has progressively expanded to include over a dozen short-chain acylation modifications such as lysine acetylation (Kac), propionylation (Kpr), butyrylation (Kbu), crotonylation (Kcr), succinylation (Ksucc), malonylation (Kmal), and lactylation (Kla) [12, 13]. These protein acylation modifications are present in both histones and non-histone proteins. Histone acylation modifications influence gene expression by affecting transcriptional regulation, whereas non-histone acylation modifications largely regulate protein functions. Moreover, it has been discovered that long-chain fatty acid acylation modifications, such as myristoylation and palmitoylation, are distributed among non-histone proteins [12]. Protein acylation modifications are predominantly regulated by enzymatic processes, with acyltransferases acting as “writers” responsible for adding acyl moieties from acetyl-CoA to amino acid residues, and deacylases functioning as “erasers” controlling the removal of acyl groups. Proteins capable of recognizing acylation marks are termed “readers”. Of note, both endogenous and exogenous metabolites can provide acyl moieties for protein acylation, establishing close connections between epigenetic processes and metabolism. Protein acylation related to inflammation-associated immune cells and inflammatory diseases requires different writers, erasers, readers, and metabolic sources (Fig. 1).

Currently, although the roles of protein acylation in inflammatory diseases have been increasingly studied, a systematic and comprehensive elucidation of its regulatory mechanisms and pathophysiological significance is yet to be established. This review uniquely synthesizes cutting-edge discoveries on emerging protein acylation types and their dynamic regulatory roles in inflammatory pathologies. In contrast to prior reviews, we systematically dissect the functions of distinct protein acylation modifications within immune and diseased cells under inflammatory conditions, particularly their roles in reshaping immune homeostasis and mediating tissue damage. Furthermore, we emphasize previously underexplored therapeutic strategies targeting protein acylation machinery, including enzyme-specific agents and precision-engineered nanoparticle delivery systems. ​​In defining the scope of this review, it is crucial to note that although chronic inflammation represents a known precursor to oncogenesis, we deliberately exclude cancer-related pathologies to maintain focus on non-malignant inflammatory mechanisms. Collectively​​, the insights obtained address critical gaps in understanding how acylation networks drive inflammatory pathogenesis while offering innovative perspectives for biomarker discovery and the development of next-generation therapies.

The role of protein acylation in inflammation-related immune cell functions

Immune cells act as active participants in inflammation, responding to inflammatory signals to regulate the progression or resolution of inflammation. First, immune cells activate inflammasomes by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger-related molecular patterns (DAMPs), which are key players in inflammatory responses. Furthermore, immune cells also regulate metabolic processes in inflammatory diseases. Finally, immune cells play a crucial role in inflammatory diseases by regulating cellular stress response systems [14–16]. Multiple protein acylation modifications have been observed to play a key role in modulating various aspects of the inflammatory response in inflammation-related immune cells. Given the significance of macrophages, T cells, and dendritic cells (DCs) in the inflammatory process and their demonstrated abundant protein acylation modifications, this chapter primarily discusses how protein acylation within these three cell types regulates their function and the inflammatory response (Fig. 2).

Macrophages

Macrophages play a critical role in regulating inflammation and immune responses, exhibiting remarkable plasticity and functional diversity [17]. All macrophages express pattern recognition receptors, enabling them to identify PAMPs and DAMPs, thus triggering responses to various stimuli [18]. Upon encountering pro-inflammatory stimuli, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), macrophages can polarize towards the M1 phenotype. Classically activated M1 macrophages are efficient producers of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), reactive oxygen species (ROS), and nitric oxide, which are crucial for host defense against pathogens and the initiation of inflammatory responses [17]. Conversely, M2 macrophages induced by IL-4, IL-10, and IL-13 are associated with tissue repair, immune regulation, and the resolution of inflammation [19]. Additionally, macrophages are involved in the coordination of multiple aspects of inflammation, including phagocytosis and antigen presentation [20].

Acetylation plays a pivotal role in modulating macrophage polarization and cytokine expression. Elevated expression levels of p300/CBP during M1 polarization have been noted to activate NF-κB via acetylation of p65 and histone H3K9, promoting M1 macrophage polarization [21]. Transcriptional activator 6 (Stat6) suppresses its transcriptional activity through CBP-mediated acetylation, thereby inhibiting macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype [22]. Conversely, hypoxia-induced acetylation of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) mediated by p300 [23], as well as histone H3 acetylation in compressed macrophages [24], have been shown to enhance M2 macrophage polarization. Additionally, acetylation of NLRP3 and α-tubulin has been identified as crucial for the full activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages [25]. The acetylation of macrophages is also regulated by several deacetylases. After LPS stimulation, the activity of HDACs 1–3 is enhanced in macrophages, which leads to the silencing of RGS10 through histone deacetylation, resulting in enhanced inflammatory signaling [26]. LPS also triggers the upregulation of HDAC2 in macrophages, resulting in decreased expression of c-Jun through the deacetylation of histones H3 and H4 on the c-Jun promoter, thus promoting the expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-12 and inducible nitric oxide synthase [27]. It is noteworthy that there exists a balance and interaction between various HDACs. For instance, HDAC11 has been shown to negatively regulate the expression of IL-10 in macrophages [28], while HDAC6 is a positive regulator of IL-10 expression in macrophages [29]. Furthermore, SIRT1 and SIRT2 inhibit the transcriptional activity of the NF-κB p65 subunit by deacetylating p65 [30, 31]; meanwhile, SIRT5 competes with SIRT2 to enhance p65 acetylation by inhibiting SIRT2 deacetylation, thereby promoting NF-κB activation [32].

Lactylation, succinylation, malonylation, and butyrylation also participate in regulating macrophage polarization and function. In 2019, Zhang et al. found that the levels of Kla of histones were elevated following LPS and IFNγ activation of M1-polarized macrophages, enhancing the expression of M2-like genes [13]. Subsequent studies showed that H3K18la induction promoted the expression of macrophage repair genes such as Lrg1, Vegf-a, and Il-10 [33]. Furthermore, lactylation can also modulate protein structure and activity through non-histone modification. Lactylation of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) significantly enhances the activity of PKM2 by inhibiting its transition from tetramer to dimer, thereby facilitating the transition of pro-inflammatory macrophages into a reparative phenotype [34]. Conversely, high succinylation promotes the dimeric form and nuclear translocation of PKM2, reducing its glycolytic enzyme activity but enhancing its protein kinase activity [35]. This phenomenon highlights that different acylation modifications occurring on the same protein may exert opposing functional effects, likely attributable to cell type-specific metabolic dependencies or divergent microenvironmental contexts. In addition, glutamine inhibits the desuccinylation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 component alpha (PDHA1), thereby maintaining the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and promoting M2 polarization in macrophages [36]. Further, γ-amino butyric acid (GABA) can also inhibit IL-1β production in M1 macrophages by downregulating succinyl-CoA and mitochondrial succinylation levels [37]. LPS-driven malonylation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) increases GAPDH activity and promotes the production of IL1β and IL6. Additionally, malonylation of GAPDH ensures the translation of TNFα [38]. Finally, exogenous crotonyl-CoA induces histone lysine crotonylation in macrophages and promotes the production of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and CXCL10 [39].

S-palmitoylation in macrophages is the focus of extensive current research. The palmitoyl acyl transferase zinc-finger and aspartate-histidine-histidine-cysteine 5 (ZDHHC5) and ZDHHC7 facilitate the palmitoylation of NLRP3 and promote the activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes [40, 41]. Upon NLRP3 inflammasome activation, ZDHHC12 also enhances the palmitoylation of NLRP3, but also promotes NLRP3 degradation via the chaperone-mediated autophagy pathway, forming a negative feedback loop to prevent excessive NLRP3 inflammasome activation [42]. The induction of GSDMD palmitoylation enhances the membrane translocation and binding of GSDMD, thereby promoting pore formation and cell pyroptosis [43]. Moreover, palmitoylation augments the activity of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and the IFN response [44], while myristic acid promotes the autophagic degradation of STING through N-myristoylation of ADP-ribosylation factor 1 [45].

T cells

T cells, also known as T lymphocytes, are a crucial component of the adaptive immune system. T cell progenitors undergo a series of developmental stages in the thymus to generate naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets, which express distinct lineage-specific surface markers [46]. Upon recognizing antigenic peptides presented via MHC class II molecules (CD4+ T cells) or MHC class I molecules (CD8+ T cells) in conjunction with costimulatory signals, these naïve T cells become activated and undergo clonal expansion and differentiation [47]. CD4+ T cells differentiate into functionally distinct subsets such as T helper (Th1, Th2, Th17) and regulatory T (Treg) cells, while CD8+ T cells develop into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [48]. Furthermore, a proportion of T cells differentiate into memory T cells, exhibiting a robust response upon re-exposure to specific antigens [46]. Different subtypes of T cells regulate the progression or remission of inflammation through various mechanisms.

The acetyltransferases CBP and p300 are essential for the differentiation and activation of T cells. CBP and p300 regulate the activity of Foxp3+ Treg cells. CBP and p300 induce acetylation of various transcription factors such as nuclear factor of activated T cells (NFAT), Runx1, and NF-κB, thereby promoting the transcriptional activity of Foxp3 [49]. Furthermore, acetylation enhances the expression level of Foxp3 by impairing its polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [50]. p300 is involved in the regulation of Th cell differentiation. It induces the acetylation of retinoic acid-related orphan receptor (RORγt), thus promoting RORγt expression and transcriptional activation, which plays a pivotal role in driving Th17 cell differentiation [51]. Further, p300 can promote the transcriptional activation of Th2 cell cytokine genes [52]. Notably, the acetyltransferase GCN5 facilitates the acetylation of histone H3K9, thereby enhancing the expression of IL-2 and subsequently promoting the differentiation of Th1 and Th17 cells [53].

Deacetylases are also crucial for T cells differentiation and activation. HDAC1 leads to the downregulation of miR-124, enhancing the expression of IRF1 and promoting the activation of CD4+ T cells [54]. Moreover, HDAC1 also aids in promoting the expression of genes related to leukocyte extravasation in CD4+ T cells, which is pivotal in governing cellular morphology and migration [55]. Besides, HDAC1 and HDAC2 jointly impede the expression of CD8+ effector genes in CD4+ T cells, thereby preserving the integrity of the CD4+ lineage [56]. HDACs also regulate the function and differentiation of CD8+ T cells. HDAC3 mediates the deacetylation of T cell receptor signaling regulatory factors, and surface receptors, inhibiting the differentiation of CD8+ T cells into cytotoxic effector cells [57]. HDAC6-mediated deacetylation of STAT4 impairs STAT4-Y693 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, thus suppressing the differentiation of Th1 cells [58]. Additionally, SIRT1/2-mediated deacetylation of RORγt enhances the transcription of IL-17 and the differentiation of Th17 cells [59, 60]. Finally, the majority of HDACs can positively or negatively regulate the differentiation and function of Foxp3+ Treg cells [61, 62].

Crotonylation and lactylation are also associated with T-cell differentiation. Histone crotonylation within the long-terminal repeat region of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in T cells promotes reactivation of latent HIV [63]. Lactylation modifications occurring on both histone and non-histone proteins also play a pivotal role in regulating T cell activation and differentiation. High levels of histone H3 lactylation have been observed in Th1, Th17, and Treg cells. Lactylation at the H3K18 site in Th17 cells is thought to potentially promote the reprogramming of Th17 cells into Treg cells [64]. Furthermore, the significant upregulation of lactylation at the K164 site of Ikzf1 notably promotes the expression of Th17-related genes [65]. Additionally, increased histone lactylation at the PD-L1 promoter enhances PD-L1 expression, ultimately weakening CD8+ T cell activation [66].

Palmitoylation and myristoylation play multiple roles in regulating T cells. The palmitoylation-depalmitoylation cycle mediated by ZDHHC7 and acyl-protein thioesterase 2 (APT2) activates STAT3, thereby promoting the differentiation of Th17 cells [67]. Moreover, ZDHHC3-mediated palmitoylation of PD-L1 inhibits its ubiquitination and lysosomal degradation, consequently weakening the immune response of T cells [68]. N-myristoyltransferase (NMT) primarily regulates T cell receptor signaling and T cell thymic development through myristoylation of Lck and protein kinase C θ substrates such as Marcks [69]. In γδ T cells, N-myristoylation inhibits the constitutive phosphatase activity of CaN and weakens IFN-γ transcription [70]. Furthermore, NMT1 downregulation in CD4+ T cells promotes the differentiation of pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 cells [71].

Dendritic cells

DCs are a type of antigen-presenting cells crucial for linking innate and adaptive immune responses, and are indispensable for the initiation of T-cell responses [72]. DCs can be broadly categorized into conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), which are further delineated into distinct subsets [73]. Their involvement in the recruitment of immune cells and the initiation of T-cell responses is considered to play a significant influence on inflammatory diseases [74].

Numerous studies have substantiated the presence of acetylation in DCs. Stimulation with LPS has been observed to activate p300-mediated acetylation of PKM2 and trigger the transition of PKM2 from tetramer to monomer, which is vital for the activation of DCs [75]. Additionally, p300-mediated acetylation of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in DCs has been shown to augment the nucleocytoplasmic translocation and extracellular release of HMGB1, thereby promoting the maturation and migration of DCs [76]. Conversely, research has revealed that mimic acetylated HMGB1 (HMGB1-M) can enhance its nucleocytoplasmic translocation while reducing the expression of MHC class II on DCs, thus inhibiting DC maturation [77]. Notably, HDAC3 has been found to reduce the expression of cDC1-related genes and increase pDC-related gene expression by deacetylating H3K27, thereby exerting a crucial influence on the differentiation of bone marrow hematopoietic progenitor cells into pDCs [78]. Additionally, HDAC4 has been observed to activate the transcription of Arg1 through the deacetylation of histone H3 and STAT6, thereby promoting the differentiation of monocytes into DCs [79]. SIRT1 attenuates the transcriptional activity of IRF1 to Il-27p28 by deacetylating IRF1 in DCs, consequently leading to the inhibition of IL-27 expression in these cells [80].

Succinylation is associated with the tolerogenic function of regulatory DCs. Splenic stroma has the ability to drive the differentiation of mature DCs (maDCs) into regulatory DCs (diffDCs) that exhibit immunosuppressive properties [81]. In comparison with maDCs, diffDCs demonstrate reduced succinic acid levels. In diffDCs, a significant increase is observed in the expression of the GFP-specific succinate-CoA ligase Suclg2, which inhibits the succinylation of mitochondrial protein lactamase beta; this leads to the reduced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in diffDCs, thereby maintaining the tolerogenicity of regulatory DCs [82].

The palmitoylation of CD80, CD86, and TLR2 has been observed in DCs. Palmitoylation positively regulates the activity of TLR2 and its localization on the cell surface [83]. Furthermore, the previously mentioned endosomal palmitoylation/de-palmitoylation cycle, which regulates TLR9 signal transduction, has also been detected in pDCs. PPT1 has been demonstrated to enhance the production of IFNα in pDCs [84]. S-palmitoylation has also been identified at three cysteine residues within the C-terminal region of CC chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5), contributing to the intracellular trafficking of CCR5 [85]. Additionally, the palmitoylation of interferon-induced transmembrane protein 3 (IFITM3) in DCs induces its aggregation and augments its antiviral activity [86]. In summary, protein acylation plays a critical regulatory role in inflammation-related immune cell function.​ (Table 1).

Protein acylation modifications and their functions in inflammation-related immune cells

The role of protein acylation in inflammatory diseases

Protein acylation is widely prevalent in pathophysiological processes, and, accordingly, diverse types of protein acylation modifications have been identified in inflammatory diseases. This section extensively elaborates on the crucial regulatory role of protein acylation in the pathogenesis and advancement of inflammatory diseases, exemplified specifically by sepsis, periodontitis, inflammatory bowel disease, atherosclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis (Fig. 3).

Sepsis

Sepsis defined as an organ dysfunction syndrome resulting from a dysregulated host response to infection has the potential to be life-threatening [87]. Sepsis can be initiated by a variety of microbial infections: when pathogens stimulate host cells leading to immune system imbalance, this can often escalate to an excessive inflammatory response and immune suppression, causing widespread tissue damage and secondary infections [88]. During the pathogenesis of sepsis, immune imbalance is orchestrated via the modulation of key proteins such as Beclin1, HMGB1, p53, and PDH; this leads to impaired autophagy, amplified inflammatory signaling, and multiorgan damage through mechanisms such as pyroptosis and ferroptosis.

Acetylation and lactylation play an important role in the regulation of sepsis. The tumor suppressor p53 is a critical regulator of autophagy. In septic cases, enhanced acetylation of p53 is observed in renal tubular epithelial cells (RTEC); this coincides with a transient elevation followed by a rapid decline in the level of autophagy, aggravating sepsis-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) [89]. Similarly, the acetylation of autophagy-associated protein Beclin1 is also heightened during sepsis, which inhibits autophagy and promotes AKI and cardiac dysfunction during sepsis [90, 91]. HMGB1 can exacerbate the progression of sepsis and resulting mortality [92]. Highly acetylated HMGB1 translocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it accumulates [93]. SIRT1 has been verified to inhibit the nucleocytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1 through its deacetylation, thereby suppressing sepsis-related AKI [94]. Additionally, a close relationship exists between lactylation and acetylation. PKM2 exhibits heightened acetylation in sepsis, leading to increased PKM2 activity and augmented lactate production [95]. During sepsis, lactate taken up by macrophages can mediate the lactylation of HMGB1 via the involvement of p300/CBP, and promote HMGB1 acetylation. Consequently, the lactylated/acetylated HMGB1 can translocate from the cellular nucleus to the cytoplasm and ultimately be released from macrophages [93]. Furthermore, lactate dehydrogenase (LDHA)-mediated histone H3K18 lactylation has been demonstrated to promote the transcription of HMGB1 and enhance its expression, thereby inducing cell pyroptosis [96].

In sepsis, an increase in Ksucc, and Kmal levels has been observed [97]. Research indicates that decreased levels of the metabolite α-ketoglutarate (αKG) inhibit SIRT5, suppressing desuccinylation of PDH. This inhibition enhances PDH activity and promotes M2 macrophage polarization, consequently mitigating inflammation and tissue injury in sepsis [36]. Following recovery from sepsis, elevated malonyl-CoA and heightened Kmal levels of voltage-dependent anion channel 2 (VDAC2) were detected. Inhibiting VDAC2 malonylation can alleviate sepsis-induced ferroptosis and myocardial dysfunction [98].

Palmitoylation may be implicated in intensifying the inflammatory signaling cascade and exacerbating tissue damage in sepsis. Bacterial pathogen-derived cGAMP has been observed to stimulate the palmitoylation of STING within thrombin-activated platelets, resulting in amplified platelet activation and increased septic thrombosis [99]. Within neutrophils, ZDHHC6 induces the palmitoylation of MYD88 to promote LPS-activated TLR signal transduction. Inhibiting the palmitoylation of MYD88 has been shown to improve survival rates in a mouse model of sepsis [100]. Moreover, ZDHHC21 has been demonstrated to induce the palmitoylation of vascular α1-adrenergic receptors, leading to an intensified vasoconstrictive response in the kidneys and subsequent renal tissue damage during sepsis [101]. Additionally, LPS stimulation-induced palmitoylation of GSDMD promotes pyroptosis, increasing sepsis severity [43].

Inflammatory bowel disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) represents a chronic inflammatory condition that predominantly affects the gastrointestinal tract. It encompasses two distinct subtypes, known as Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). While CD can impact any part of the gastrointestinal tract, UC is typically confined to the rectum and colon, primarily influencing the inner epithelial layer of the colon [102]. The pathogenesis of IBD is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, gut microbes, environmental factors, and immune system dysregulation [103]. The mechanism underlying IBD pathogenesis involves Treg differentiation, macrophage polarization, disruption of microbial metabolite crosstalk, and altered membrane signaling. These factors collectively drive chronic inflammation, epithelial oxidative stress, and impaired tissue repair.

Acetylation is associated with the progression of IBD. In IBD, a marked reduction in histone H3 acetylation has been observed in the colon epithelium. Furthermore, the level of acetylation is negatively correlated with colitis severity [104]. Metabolites of the gut microbiota, such as butyrate, exhibit inhibitory effects against HDAC [105]. Butyrate can induce the differentiation of Treg cells by upregulating histone H3 acetylation and suppress the production of pro-inflammatory mediators from neutrophils in a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi)-dependent manner, thereby ameliorating intestinal inflammation [106, 107]. In patients with CD, a decrease in the Kac level has been observed. This reduction may be associated with the depletion of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) producers and the development of CD [108]. Moreover, the intestinal microenvironment is rich in lactate. Lactate induces H3K27 acetylation through the generation of acetyl coenzyme A. This process leads to the transcriptional suppression of macrophage pro-inflammatory genes, presenting potential advantages for the management of IBD [109]. The aforementioned study highlights the occurrence of H3K27 acetylation during the early phase of LPS stimulation and the potential occurrence of lactylation in the latter stages of LPS stimulation [13].

Lactylation contributes to the expression of reparative macrophage genes and the repair of inflammatory tissues. Deficiency of B-cell adaptor for PI3K leads to reduced lactate generation, resulting in decreased histone lactylation and reduced expression of reparative macrophage genes, thereby enhancing the inflammatory state of colitis [110]. However, single-cell sequencing data from individuals suffering from CD revealed a marked elevation in lactylation levels within inflammatory regions. Elevated levels of lactylation may therefore be associated with the pathogenesis of CD [111].

Furthermore, succinylation, crotonylation, malonylation, and butyrylation may play pivotal roles in the regulation of IBD. A study reports that knocking down SIRT5 induced high succinylation of PKM2. Further, Sirt5-deficient mice show increased susceptibility to dextran sulphate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis [35]. Robust histone H3K18 crotonylation modifications have been identified within colonic epithelial cells. Microbial-derived butyrate can promote histone crotonylation in intestinal cells, implying the possible modulation of IBD by gut microbiota via Kcr regulation [112]. LPS-induced macrophage GAPDH malonylation leads to a significant increase in TNF-α expression levels, which is associated with the progression of DSS-induced mouse colitis [113]. Additionally, a heightened presence of histone H3K27 and H3K9 butyrylation and propionylation was evidenced within the cecum of mice. Butyrylation may contribute to gene expression regulation and play a role in the oxidative stress response of intestinal epithelial cells [114].

Palmitoylation also plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of IBD. In patients with IBD, ZDHHC7 and APT2 expression profiles show significantly elevated trends correlated with disease progression [67]. Furthermore, various NOD2 mutant proteins associated with CD show reduced palmitoylation levels, leading to membrane dissociation and the inability to recognize the invasion of bacterial pathogens [115]. Further research into the role of protein acylation in IBD is necessary to provide new insights for the prevention and treatment of IBD.

Periodontitis

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the periodontal supporting tissues caused by the dental plaque microbiome and their virulence factors. This condition can result in the destruction of the bone and soft tissues supporting the teeth, ultimately leading to tooth loss [116]. Periodontitis is the sixth most common human disease, with an overall prevalence of 45%–50% worldwide [117].​ According to data from the oral health survey in China, 69.3% of subjects in the 55–64-year-old group had periodontitis [118]. Moreover, periodontitis may cause or aggravate other chronic inflammation-driven diseases, making its prevention and treatment critical [119]. The progression of periodontitis is associated with periodontal pathogens and disrupted acylation in host tissues such as oral epithelial cells and periodontal ligament cells, involving histone deacetylase and inflammatory mediators such as p300/CBP.

Acetylation regulates the occurrence and development of periodontitis. Periodontal pathogens influence the acetylation of periodontal tissues by regulating the expression of HDACs. Following infection of periodontal tissues by Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum, the expression of HDAC1 and HDAC2 in gingival epithelial cells is downregulated. This leads to increased acetylation of chromatin histones, resulting in the upregulation of the gene expression of human β-defensin 2 and CC chemokine ligand 20 (CCL20) to regulate the immune response [120]. Conversely, infection with Treponema denticola decreases the expression of HDAC4, HDAC6, and HDAC10 in periodontal ligament cells, promoting the expression of MMP2 in periodontal tissues and thus exacerbating the progression of periodontitis [121]. However, some studies suggest that the expression levels of HDAC1, HDAC5, HDAC8, and HDAC9 are elevated in patients with periodontitis, possibly in association with the long-term chronic inflammation process [122]. HDAC3 has been demonstrated as a regulatory factor in the inflammatory response of gingival fibroblasts in periodontitis. HDAC3 may increase the expression of inflammatory mediators in periodontitis, such as CCL2, IL-1B, MMP3, and COX2 [123]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that P. gingivalis LPS and heat-inactivated F. nucleatum promote the activation and accumulation of p300/CBP, resulting in rapid but transient acetylation of H3K9 in oral epithelial cells. In contrast, Escherichia coli LPS induces slower but more effective histone acetylation [124]. Additionally, butyrate can be produced by periodontal pathogens and functions as an HDAC inhibitor, thereby enhancing histone acetylation [125]. Through this mechanism, periodontal pathogens manipulate histone acetylation in host cells and promote the reactivation of latent viruses, such as the Epstein-Barr virus [126] and HIV [127]. In addition to promoting virus reactivation, recent research suggests that Enterococcus faecalis lipoteichoic acid in the presence of butyrate inhibits HDAC, thereby facilitating inflammasome activation in macrophages and potentially contributing to the development of periodontitis [128].

Lactylation, crotonylation, and succinylation have also been associated with periodontal disease and periodontal pathogens. Periodontal disease (PD) tissues displayed heightened levels of lactic acid as well as a significant increase in the level of protein lysine Kla [129, 130]. It has been demonstrated that P. gingivalis increases lactate production and elevates protein Kla levels in macrophages through the transfection of msRNA P.G_45033, leading to the upregulation of amyloid-β expression and exacerbating periodontitis [131]. Nevertheless, periodontal tissues in rat models of periodontitis exhibit decreasing trends in lactate accumulation and Kla levels, while human periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) similarly demonstrate reduced Kla modification. The reinstatement of Kla levels in PDLSCs has been found to enhance their osteogenic differentiation in inflammatory conditions [132]. Under inflammatory conditions, the lactylation levels of macrophages and PDLSCs exhibit contrasting alterations. This phenomenon can potentially be attributed to two factors, the first being the disparity in stimulation duration between in vivo and in vitro experiments. Second, as immune cells, macrophages are highly sensitive to inflammatory stimuli. An elevation in lactylation levels within macrophages can trigger an inflammatory response, which serves to eliminate infections. In contrast, under inflammatory conditions, the functions of PDLSCs are inhibited, preventing the occurrence of lactylation. This remains under further investigation. Besides lactylation, lysine crotonylation is identified as a prevalent form of modification in PDLSCs, actively facilitating their osteogenic differentiation [133]. Lysine succinylation is primarily detected in P. gingivalis proteins associated with virulence factors, as well as in products of pathogen survival and fitness-related factors [134]. Notably, acetylation and succinylation modifications within P. gingivalis exhibit extensive overlap, particularly in proteins related to ribosomal function and metabolism [135]. The precise functions of these novel protein acylation modifications in the context of periodontitis remain to be further explored.

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the arterial intima [136]. A defining feature is the early-stage aggregation and oxidation of low-density lipoproteins (LDLs) within the arterial intima. This process triggers endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory response, and promotes the recruitment of leukocytes. Macrophages exhibit modified LDLs uptakes, leading to the formation of foam cells, which results in the accumulation of lipid-rich plaques within the arterial wall. As the disease progresses, these plaques pose a significant risk for adverse cardiovascular events [137]. Atherosclerosis progression is driven by dysregulated PTMs and their outcomes, including histone acetylation-mediated cholesterol efflux impairment, lactylation-induced M2 macrophage polarization, and CD36-eNOS axis modulation. These factors collectively exacerbate ox-LDL uptake and plaque instability through inflammatory amplification, oxidative stress, and disrupted cholesterol homeostasis.

Histone acetylation closely associated with the inflammatory processes in atherosclerosis. The level of H3K9 acetylation in late-stage atherosclerotic lesions correlates with the severity of atherosclerosis [138]. Activated p300 mediates STAT1 acetylation and interaction with PPARγ, inducing CD36 expression, oxidized LDL (ox-LDL) uptake, and foam cell formation [139]. Furthermore, ROS induces downregulation of 8-oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (OGG1) acetylation levels in vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), leading to decreased enzyme activity, which results in atherosclerotic formation [140]. Notably, all types of HDACs are significantly upregulated in atherosclerosis [141]. HDACs have been found to promote NADPH oxidase expression and ROS production, thereby mediating oxidative stress and the inflammatory response in atherosclerosis [141]. However, research also suggests that HDAC3 may induce the deactivation of NF-κB/p65 by upregulating PPARγ expression, thus exerting an anti-inflammatory effect in atherosclerosis [142]. Numerous studies have described the regulatory role of HDAC3 and HDAC9 in atherosclerosis, which can impact endothelial homeostasis, VSMC proliferation, macrophage phenotype, and foam cell formation. HDAC3 can change the migration and proliferation ability of human aortic smooth muscle cells [143]. In macrophages, HDAC3 reduces the expression of ATP-binding cassette transporters A1 (ABCA1), impairing cholesterol efflux and promoting foam cell formation [144]. Similarly, HDAC9 has been found to downregulate the expression of ABCA1 via histone H3K9 acetylation, thereby impeding macrophage cholesterol efflux and polarization towards an M2 phenotype [145]. HDAC9 can deacetylate inhibitory kappa B kinase (IKK)-α and β and induce NF-κB activation, which is detrimental to the stability of atherosclerotic plaques [146]. SIRT1 can enhance mitochondrial function by deacetylating PGC-1α, thereby attenuating VSMC senescence and atherosclerosis progression [147]. In lipid metabolism, SIRT1 promotes cholesterol efflux by upregulating ABCA1 in macrophages [148]. It has been demonstrated that SIRT6 can ameliorate lipid metabolism disorder in atherosclerosis, enhance lipophagy in macrophages, and promote lipid droplet degradation [149].

Advances in lactylation research have provided novel insights into the progression of atherosclerosis. For example, it has demonstrated that exercise induces lysine lactylation of methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MeCP2) in endothelial cells, suppressing of the expression of epiregulin and exerting an inhibitory effect on atherosclerosis progression [150]. Their most recent research reveals that exercise enables the demethylation of H3K36me3 in aortic root plaque macrophages through the lactylation of MeCP2 at K271, induces the polarization of M2 macrophages, and ameliorates atherosclerosis [151].

Palmitoylation and myristoylation are key participants in the regulation of atherosclerosis. The subcellular positioning of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) necessitates dual acylation involving N-myristoylation and S-palmitoylation. Specifically, DHHC-21 modulates eNOS palmitoylation and governs its nitric oxide (NO)-generative capacity [152]. Moreover, research suggests that oxHDL enhances CD36 palmitoylation, thus facilitating CD36 localization and signal transduction; this ultimately promotes the uptake of oxHDL by macrophages and contributes to foam cell formation [153]. Additionally, myristoylation has been shown to regulate the translocation of IL-1α to the cell surface, thereby promoting leukocyte adhesion and contributing to the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis [154]. These intricate acylation modifications serve as critical regulators of atherosclerosis, highlighting the potential for novel therapeutic interventions for this disease.

Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a progressive immune-mediated inflammatory disease. Its primary features include synovial inflammation and damage to the affected articular cartilage and bone [155]. Another characteristic of RA is the production of serum autoantibodies, such as anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies [156]. The pathogenesis of RA involves the orchestrated recruitment and activation of T cells, which progressively instigates immune cells, synoviocytes, chondrocytes, and osteoclasts to release pro-inflammatory and tissue-destructive mediators [157]. RA progression is driven by dysregulated acetylation modifications, including KAT2A-mediated H3K9 acetylation and HDAC1/SIRT1 imbalance, which alter macrophage polarization and Treg/Th17 dynamics. Additionally, altered lactylation and myristoylation exacerbate mTORC1/Th17 hyperactivation to fuel synovitis, autoantibody production, and joint destruction through epigenetic rewiring of immune-stromal crosstalk.

Current research underscores the involvement of protein acetylation in the genesis and progression of RA. Lysine acetyltransferase 2 A (KAT2A) mediates the transcriptional activation of the Il1b and Nlrp3 genes in macrophages by catalyzing H3K9 acetylation and suppressing the activity of nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2. This exacerbates the degree of synovitis and bone erosion [158]. In naïve T cells of patients with RA, Tip60 promotes the acetylation of Foxp3 and facilitates the differentiation of Treg cells, slowing the progression of RA [159]. A reduction in the expression of class I HDACs has been observed in the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with RA, resulting in augmented global histone H3 acetylation levels [160]. Nevertheless, studies have also suggested that increased expression of HDAC1 in fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) in RA may inhibit the production of MMP1 and promote the proliferation and survival of FLS [161]. In collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse models, mice with T cell-specific deletion of HDAC1 (HDAC1-cko) mice showed resistance to joint inflammation development [162]. Furthermore, butyrate can act as an HDACi in RA, promoting increased histone acetylation levels in the promoter region of follicular regulatory T (Tfr) cell marker genes, which facilitates Tfr cell differentiation and reduces the production of autoantibodies [163]. Additionally, SIRT1 plays an anti-inflammatory role in RA. The activation of SIRT1 can promote the expression of macrophage M2 genes through the phosphorylation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) α/acetyl-CoA carboxylase. Furthermore, SIRT1 can deacetylate p65 and suppress the expression of M1 genes [164].

At present, understanding of the role of other acylation modifications in RA is quite limited. Through proteome and malonylome analyses of PBMCs obtained from patients with RA and healthy individuals, 938 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and 42 differentially malonylated proteins (DMPs) have been identified. Further, two DEPs and one DMP have been identified within the neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETosis) pathway. Their malonylation may be involved in the pathogenesis of RA, requiring further experimental validation [165]. Furthermore, a study found that the antimalarial drug artemisinin enhances the lactylation of PKM2 in RA fibroblast-like synoviocytes (RA-FLS). This results in blocked nuclear translocation of PKM2 and cell cycle arrest at the S phase, thus inhibiting the proliferation of RA-FLS and the progression of RA. These findings imply a potential modulatory role of lactylation modifications in RA [166].

Notably, studies have reported on the role of myristoylation modifications in RA. Specifically, functional deficiencies in NMT1 were discovered within T cells of patients with RA, promoting mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signal transduction. Overactivation of the mTORC1 pathway results in the differentiation of T cells into pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 cells. Overexpression of NMT1 appears to counteract this effect, inhibiting T cell-induced synovitis and suggesting a protective role for myristoylation in RA [71]. These findings suggest that further investigation into acylation modifications may offer valuable insights into potential mechanisms and therapeutic targets related to RA. Targeting protein acylation as a therapeutic strategy

With the development of molecular docking, pharmacophore modeling, dynamics simulations and other technologies, the affinity and selectivity of ligands have been greatly improved, aiding the discovery and design of potential drug molecules targeting protein acylation regulatory enzymes. Targeting protein acylation readers as a therapeutic strategy for inflammatory diseases

Numerous BET inhibitors have demonstrated effective roles in cancer therapy, and have been substantiated through phase I clinical trials [170]. Current investigations suggest that BET inhibitors also show substantial potential in the treatment of inflammatory diseases. These inhibitors can be subcategorized into pan-BET inhibitors (such as JQ1, I-BET151, I-BET762, and PLX51107), which exhibit equal binding affinities to both the BD1 and BD2 domains. Selective inhibitors targeting either the BD1 or BD2 domains, including GSK040, GSK620, RVX-208, and RVX-297, have been reported [168, 169]. Furthermore, proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) proteins such as ARV-771, which induce the degradation of BRD proteins, have been described [183].

Pan-BET inhibitors such as JQ1 and I-BET151 have been demonstrated to confer protective effects against inflammatory diseases including sepsis [184], RA [185], colitis [186], atherosclerosis [187] and periodontitis [188, 189]. However, pan-BET inhibitors may lead to severe adverse effects [190]. Notably, gene expression induced by inflammatory stimuli has been shown to necessitate the involvement of both BD1 and BD2. BD1 inhibitors, acting as pan-BET inhibitors, mainly mediate steady-state gene expression, while selectively targeting BD2 may offer superior safety and efficacy for treating inflammation [191]. In periodontitis, the selective BD2-targeting BET inhibitor RVX-208 demonstrates the ability to hinder the expression of inflammatory genes and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in P. gingivalis infection. Additionally, it reduces alveolar bone resorption through the suppression of osteoclast differentiation [192]. Intriguingly, observed effects indicate that JQ1 treatment induces more widespread and pronounced gene inhibition compared with RVX-208, hinting at the potential for RVX-208 to exhibit fewer side effects than JQ1 [192]. This suggests that RVX-208 represents a promising therapeutic agent for periodontitis. Furthermore, pan-BET inhibitors such as JQ1 have been shown to broadly influence the differentiation of various T helper cell subtypes including Th1, Th2, and Th17. In contrast, selective inhibition of the BD1 domain using MS402 achieves significant suppression of Th17 cell differentiation with little impact on Th1, Th2, and Treg cells. This selective approach has demonstrated promise in the prevention and amelioration of colitis in mice [193].

Targeting protein acylation writers as a therapeutic strategy for inflammatory diseases

P300/CBP, the most common acyltransferase, can regulate almost all kinds of SCFA acylation modification. Therefore, therapeutic strategies focusing on protein acetylation writers primarily target p300/CBP. Inhibitors of p300/CBP are mainly categorized into inhibitors targeting the bromodomain (BRD) and inhibitors targeting the HAT domain, along with PROTAC degraders [194, 195]. Studies have identified p300 as the main HAT in synovial fibroblasts (SF), where silencing of p300 exhibits both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory effects. Treatment with the p300 HAT domain inhibitor C646 failed to achieve gene expression similar to p300 silencing, while the effects observed with the p300 BRD inhibitor I-CBP closely resembled those of silencing p300. However, CBP/p300 inhibitors, owing to their analogous dual functionality to p300 silencing, are not suitable as anti-inflammatory agents for synovial fibroblasts [171]. Another CBP/p300 BRD inhibitor, CBP30, has been shown to significantly reduce the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-17 A by Th17 cells in patients with ankylosing spondylitis and psoriatic arthritis, with minimal side effects relative to JQ1 [196]. Further, C646 downregulates the expression of pro-inflammatory genes in murine macrophages and lung tissues by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway. Interestingly, a slight increase in histone acetylation was observed after applying C646. This is attributed to the inhibitory effect of C646 on HDAC6 activity [172]. Moreover, the p300/CBP HAT domain inhibitor A-485 has been reported to significantly repress the expression of pathological inflammatory genes, reducing M1 polarization of macrophages and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and thereby exerting a protective effect against acute liver injury [173].

Therapeutic strategies for inflammatory diseases targeting protein acetylation and palmitoylation writers NMT and the ZDHHC family remain scarce. The compound 2-bromopalmitate (2-BP) serves as a pan-ZDHHC inhibitor, capable of suppressing the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages [41]. Treatment with 2-BP also demonstrates the potential to ameliorate burn-induced systemic inflammation and intestinal barrier dysfunction [174]. However, pan-ZDHHC inhibitors impede widespread signaling mechanisms and cellular processes, leading to cytotoxic effects [197]. Hence, further research is necessary to develop more selective ZDHHC inhibitors and validate their therapeutic utility in inflammatory diseases.

While studies have indicated the therapeutic potential of acyltransferase inhibitors in cancer, research concerning the targeting of protein acylation writers for the treatment of inflammatory diseases is limited. Furthermore, acyltransferase inhibitors may potentially exert cytotoxic effects and hinder the activity of HDAC or other enzymes. Consequently, the development of acyltransferase inhibitors with acceptable efficacy and safety to treat inflammatory diseases is a promising but pressing issue in need of resolution.

Targeting protein acylation erasers as a therapeutic strategy for inflammatory diseases

Targeting the HDAC family has emerged as a therapeutic approach in cancer treatment. Several HDAC-targeting drugs have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration of the United States and the China Food and Drug Administration for treating cutaneous T-cell lymphoma and peripheral T-cell lymphoma [7, 198]. Certain HDAC-targeting compounds also exhibit potential therapeutic effects in inflammatory diseases. The selective inhibitor of class I HDAC, zabadinostat, can modulate adaptive immunity, leading to increased expression of MHC genes and enhanced activity of CD4+, CD8+ T lymphocytes, and B cells [175]. Class I HDACi MS-275, Class IIb HDACi TubA, and pan-HDACi TSA have been reported to notably extend survival in rats with cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis [176]. These inhibitors and class IIa HDACi LMK-235 also significantly reduce histopathological scores and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [176]. SIRT1 inhibitor EX-527 and SIRT2 inhibitor AGK2 have been shown to reduce mortality in sepsis and decrease the production of sepsis-associated cytokines [180, 181]. In colitis, pan-HDACi SAHA inhibits the expression of pro-inflammatory genes such as Il-6, TNF-α, and Ccl2, protecting against DSS-induced colitis in mice [179]. Furthermore, polypharmacologic molecules that inhibit both HDAC and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase can also prevent DSS-induced colitis and colitis-associated colorectal cancer, reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, COX-II, and cyclin D1 [199]. HDAC-targeting drugs, including HDAC6i BML-281, HDAC8i SPA3074, and the SIRT3 activator honokiol, have been proven to ameliorate colitis inflammation in murine models [177, 178, 182]. Pan-HDACi and inhibitors targeting HDAC1, 2, and 6 (particularly HDAC6) have demonstrated promising roles as anti-inflammatory regulators in RA, alleviating synovial inflammation, bone, and cartilage destruction [200]. Notably, a study indicated that although HDACi vorinostat and BML281 inhibit the release of inflammatory mediators at low concentrations (< 3µM), they can potentiate the production of other pro-inflammatory mediators at higher concentrations (>3µM). Specifically, higher concentrations of BML281 exacerbate disease progression in a rat arthritis model [201].

Additionally, pan-HDACi (SAHA and ITF2357) and HDAC3/6i have been shown to significantly decrease the expression of inflammatory genes in gingival fibroblasts in periodontitis. Considering the crucial role of HDAC3 in gingival fibroblast inflammation mediator expression, selective HDAC3 inhibitors may serve as a therapeutic modality for ameliorating the inflammatory response associated with periodontitis [123]. Therapeutic targeting of the HDAC family has long been an area of intense interest and research. Moving forward, there is a critical need for further screening of selective HDAC inhibitors in inflammatory diseases and exploring their optimal dosage and more effective combination usage.

Targeting APT-mediated depalmitoylation may also be of value in the treatment of inflammatory diseases. Specifically, the selective APT2 inhibitor ML349 has demonstrated the ability to alleviate colitis and weight loss in DSS-induced mouse models of colitis, enhancing survival rates of the mice [67]. The effects of compounds targeting palmitoylation and myristoylation in inflammatory diseases require further exploration.

The new strategy of delivering protein acylation-related anti-inflammatory drugs

Enhancing bioavailability and achieving targeted release of these bioactive compounds remain substantial challenges in contemporary pharmaceutical research. The development of biomaterials such as nanoparticles (NPs), exosomes, and hydrogels will significantly improve therapeutic efficacy and reduce cytotoxicity associated with the delivery of acylation-related anti-inflammatory drugs through these materials.

Currently, the most frequently studied delivery system of epigenetic inhibitors mainly employs NPs. The HDACi valproic acid (VPA) is a potential anti-inflammatory drug, and a type of VPA-loaded NPs based on cellulose- and dextran VPA esters bearing sulfuric acid half ester moieties has been reported [202]. However, VPA has disadvantages such as a short serum half-life and hepatotoxic side effects when administered systemically. Loading VPA onto NPs can prolong the half-life, facilitate efficient intracellular release, and successfully induce high acetylation of histone H3. Moreover, loading VPA onto NPs has good biocompatibility and has been demonstrated to be non-toxic in both in vivo and in vitro models [202]. These cellulose-based sulphated VPA-coupled (CV-S) NPs exhibit stronger anti-inflammatory activity than free VPA in LPS-stimulated human macrophages [203]. Recently, dextran sulfate-based nanoparticles carrying the P300/CBP-related factor inhibitor garcinol have been designed, which can selectively target M1 macrophages in the inflamed joints of CIA mouse models and show higher therapeutic efficacy compared with free garcinol in the treatment of autoimmune arthritis [21]. Additionally, given that many acylation-related anti-inflammatory drugs are hydrophobic, encapsulating drugs with hyaluronidase-coated NPs can increase the drug loading capacity and drug plasma exposure [204]. The shape of NPs may also influence their ability to deliver acylation-related drugs to specific cell compartments [205]. Biomimetic NPs covered by cell membranes show enhanced homotypic targeting ability, and exosome membrane coatings possess similar characteristics. A study combined artificial exosomes with NPs to develop a novel drug delivery system that exhibits both targeting capabilities and spatiotemporal resolution within tumor tissues, thereby achieving excellent therapeutic effects [206].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) represent an emerging and promising tool for drug delivery, characterized by their excellent biocompatibility as well as unique homotypic targeting and recognition capabilities. In drug delivery applications, EVs can be loaded with bioactive compounds through methods such as ultrasonication, electroporation, and incubation, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy [207]. Exosomes derived from immune cells such as macrophages [208] and DCs [209], as well as non-immune cells like epithelial cells [210], have shown great potential as carriers for therapeutic agents in the treatment of inflammatory diseases. Additionally, plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles and milk-derived exosomes have been explored as drug delivery systems for the treatment of inflammatory diseases [211]. Compared with cell-derived exosomes, these exosomes offer abundant production volume. Recent studies have demonstrated that exosome-like nanovesicles derived from Sophora flavescens successfully achieved efficient oral delivery of RNA polymerase (Pol I) inhibitor CX5461, exhibiting strong targeting properties and alleviating colitis in a DSS-induced mouse model [212]. Milk exosomes, by modulating immune responses and the gut microbiota, can help reduce intestinal inflammation, providing benefits in the prevention and treatment of IBD [211]. Moreover, milk exosomes maintain structural integrity relatively well within the gastrointestinal tract, which makes them particularly suitable for oral drug encapsulation, as shown via the effective delivery of fibroblast growth factor 21 for sepsis treatment [213]. At present, systems for the delivery of protein acylation-related anti-inflammatory drugs via EVs remain underdeveloped, representing a promising area for future research and exploration.

Nevertheless, the bioavailability of locally administered EVs remains suboptimal, restricting their therapeutic efficacy. Hydrogels, as delivery vehicles, offer enhanced sustained drug release, excellent biocompatibility, and low invasiveness. Emerging research on thermosensitive hydrogels, specifically those composed of PEG-polyester copolymers, has demonstrated their capability to effectively encapsulate anti-inflammatory agents and EVs, leading to improved inhibition of inflammatory responses [214, 215]. This strategy has been shown to yield superior therapeutic outcomes compared with the administration of EVs alone [215]. Hydrogels could also be a valuable consideration in the design of delivery systems for protein acylation-related anti-inflammatory drugs, particularly for applications necessitating localized, sustained release, such as in the treatment of periodontitis.

In conclusion, the delivery of protein acylation-related anti-inflammatory drugs through these biomaterials offers better bioavailability, targeting ability, and lower cytotoxicity compared with traditional methods, and holds high promise for application in the treatment of inflammatory diseases (Fig. 4).

Conclusions and future perspectives

Protein acylation has emerged as a central regulatory mechanism bridging metabolic reprogramming, epigenetic regulation, and inflammatory responses in immune cells and inflammatory diseases. Clinically, these modifications hold dual potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. For instance, differential expression of HDAC4, 8, and 9 in saliva has been proposed as a potential biomarker for periodontitis [8]. Acetylation of tau protein may serve as a blood-based biomarker for traumatic brain injury [9]. Lactylated H3K18 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with septic shock demonstrate potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for disease severity [216]. These acylation modifications correlate with disease severity and progression, holding promise for enabling minimally invasive and effective disease monitoring through liquid biopsy. Therapeutically, as discussed in the previous section, targeting acylation-related enzymes to restore balanced PTM dynamics has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models, with emerging nanoparticle-based delivery systems further enhancing drug specificity. These findings underscore the clinical significance of protein acylation in inflammatory diseases, facilitating the translation of molecular mechanisms into diagnostic innovation and targeted therapeutic development.​.

The exploration of these acylation modifications contributes to a deeper understanding of the pathophysiological processes and facilitates the identification of novel drug targets. However, several challenges in mechanistic research remain to be addressed. First, while multiple protein acylation modifications may be regulated by the same writer, these do not occur simultaneously. The specific mechanism by which non-specific enzymes selectively regulate the occurrence of specific types of acylation remains an unresolved issue in this field. Second, there are potential interactions between different protein acylation modifications and other PTMs, partly because distinct modification types may be regulated by the same enzyme and partly because of the metabolic interconnections between the precursors and byproducts of different modifications. As discussed in this paper, PKM2 can undergo acetylation, succinylation, and lactylation under varying pathological conditions, modulating its glycolytic enzyme activity or protein kinase function [34, 35, 60]. These modifications may exhibit temporal hierarchies or interact with each other, and the relationships between them remain poorly understood. Given that such regulatory imbalances may be linked to disease progression, further investigation in this area is warranted. Furthermore, the interplay between protein acylation and other PTMs is crucial for maintaining enzymatic activity. For instance, class I and II HDACs regulate their deacetylase activity through SUMOylation [217], and HDAC5-mediated deacetylation of PP2Ac reduces its phosphatase activity [218]. Interestingly, HDAC4 also acts as a SUMO E3 ligase to mediate IκBα SUMOylation, thereby inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [219]. A comprehensive elucidation of these interactions will provide new insights valuable for identifying additional acylation regulatory targets and understanding disease mechanisms. Third, a recent study discovered that the monooxygenase LimB in Streptomyces roseosporus can irreversibly oxidize the side chain of crotonyl groups to regulate crotonylation, defining it as a post-PTM modification [220]. This discovery opens new avenues for research into protein acylation modifications and their potential implications in inflammatory diseases. Lastly, traditional methods for detecting epigenetic modifications lack ​​single-molecule resolution and quantitative analytical capabilities​​, often resulting in ​​the loss of critical localization information. This limitation makes it ​​challenging to investigate cellular heterogeneity​​. In contrast, recent breakthroughs in technologies such as ​​scNanoSeq-CUT&Tag​​, single-cell chromatin immunocleavage and unmixing sequencing (​​scChIX-seq)​​, and Target Chromatin Indexing and Tagmentation (TACIT) have enabled ​​high-precision mapping of histone covalent modifications—and even interactions between histone modifications—at single-cell resolution. Moving forward, these approaches will allow ​​unprecedentedly precise monitoring of acylation dynamics​​ throughout disease progression, ​​paving the way for mechanistic dissection of epigenetic regulatory networks​​.

While protein acylation holds therapeutic potential in inflammatory diseases, several critical issues must be addressed to bridge the gap between mechanistic insights and clinical translation. First, many findings remain confined to in vitro models, necessitating further in vivo validation to confirm the pathophysiological relevance of protein acylation within heterogeneous tissue microenvironments. Second, the development of highly selective modulators targeting distinct protein acylation writers, erasers, and readers remains a bottleneck. Current inhibitors often lack specificity, leading to off-target effects that obscure therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, certain enzymes involved in protein acylation are not only linked to disease pathogenesis but also regulate essential normal biological processes—inhibiting these enzymes may therefore result in unpredictable adverse effects. Third, current methods for identifying and detecting protein acylation, such as mass spectrometry and antibody-based assays, are time-consuming and costly, thus restricting biomarker discovery and clinical utility. Collectively, these obstacles impede the translation of acylation-centric therapies into clinical practice. Future efforts should prioritize the development of precision tools, including isoform-specific inhibitors, PROTACs, novel drug delivery systems, and advanced mass spectrometry workflows, for application to context-dependent acylation networks in inflammatory diseases. Large-scale, heterogeneous multicenter cohorts should be incorporated into clinical studies to validate the clinical utility of these acylation-related biomarkers. Concurrently, integrating multi-omics approaches and patient-derived models will facilitate elucidation of the clinical relevance of acylation dysregulation and guide the rational design of safe and effective therapeutic interventions. Acknowledging and systematically addressing these challenges will be pivotal in harnessing protein acylation mechanisms for the development of transformative diagnostics and therapies in inflammatory diseases.

In conclusion, this review summarizes the latest advances in the understanding of protein acylation in various immune cells and inflammatory diseases, offering new insights into the pathogenesis and progression of these diseases. The development of therapies targeting protein acylation in the treatment of inflammatory diseases holds important clinical significance, and further research is required to expand the mechanistic understanding of protein acylation and the application of related therapeutic strategies in inflammatory diseases.
