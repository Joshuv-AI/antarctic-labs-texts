Modes of Sequence Models and Learning Coefficients
Zhongtian Chen, Daniel Murfet

Abstract
We develop a geometric account of sequence modelling that links patterns in the data to measurable properties of the loss landscape in transformer networks. First, we cast conditional sequence distributions into a Hilbert-space framework and apply tensor decompositions to identify their principal modes . Truncating the small-amplitude modes yields an effective data distribution that preserves dominant structure while discarding statistical detail. Second, we show theoretically that Local Learning Coefficient (LLC) estimates are insensitive to modes below a data-dependent threshold. Consequently, the LLC calculated in practice characterises the geometry of the effective rather than the true distribution. This insight clarifies why reliable LLC estimates can be obtained even when a network parameter is not a strict minimiser of the population loss, and it highlights how the inverse temperature in SGLD acts as a resolution dial on the landscape structure.



1
Introduction

Statistical models of sequence distributions form the foundation of our mathematical theories of communication
(
Shannon, 1948
)
, artificial general intelligence
(
Hutter, 2005
)
, and natural language. The empirical success of transformer neural networks has brought sequence models once again to the center of both theoretical and practical aspects of artificial intelligence
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
. Recent work has suggested that the
geometry
of the loss landscape in these networks may be deeply related to the way that the models compute
(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Wang et al., 2024
;
Hoogland et al., 2024
)
, and therefore understanding this geometry and how it relates to structure in the data distribution is a promising path towards interpretability and applications in AI safety
(
Lehalleur et al., 2025
)
.

Within the framework of singular learning theory (SLT)
(
Watanabe, 2009
)
the main way we currently access this geometry empirically is through approximate Bayesian posterior sampling using stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD)
(
Welling & Teh, 2011
)
. This forms the basis for local learning coefficient (LLC) estimation
(
Lau et al., 2024
)
and related techniques like susceptibilities
(
Baker et al., 2025
)
. However there are many open conceptual and technical questions about this SGLD-based approach to studying the geometry of the loss landscape.

In this paper, we lay foundations for understanding the role of
modes of the data distribution
in shaping our measurements of the geometry of the loss landscape of sequence models like transformers. We do this by developing an
effective theory
of sequence modelling. Our approach uses function spaces and tensor decompositions to identify the fundamental patterns – which we call
modes
– in sequence distributions. This framework allows us to perform a principled coarse-graining of the true data distribution by truncating smaller modes while preserving the dominant structures, resulting in what we term the
effective true distribution
.

Our key insight is that the geometry of learning, as captured by LLC estimation, is inherently limited in its sensitivity to the full spectrum of modes. Due to constraints on the sensitivity of the model’s gradients to the long tail of modes, LLC estimation primarily measures the geometry of the effective true distribution rather than the complete distribution. This has several implications for how we interpret measurements of landscape geometry in deep sequence models.

The contributions we make in this paper:

•

Modal decomposition of sequence distributions
: Building on existing work in the mathematical literature on natural language processing, we use tensor decompositions to formulate a Hilbert space of sequence models with a natural basis. We show how patterns in the data distribution correspond to modes, and how tensor truncation provides a principled approach to coarse-graining.

•

LLC estimation measures effective distributions
: We prove that under reasonable assumptions, LLC estimation is insensitive to small modes in the distribution. Specifically, the LLC estimates we perform in practice (e.g., on transformer neural networks) effectively measure the geometry of a coarse-grained distribution rather than the complete distribution.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
, we review necessary background on tensor algebra and singular value decomposition. Section
3
introduces our formalism for sequence models and their corresponding data distributions. Section
4
develops the Hilbert space framework and modal decomposition. Section
5
makes these abstract definitions concrete with some examples of modes. Then in Section
6
and Section
7
we connect these concepts to Singular Learning Theory and the Local Learning Coefficient and prove our main results.

2.2
Singular Value Decomposition

For the reader’s convenience we give a minimal treatment of SVD that makes explicit the fact that SVD is an operation on linear transformations between inner product spaces. Let
V,W
be finite-dimensional inner product spaces over
\mathbb{R}
with pairings denoted
\langle-,-\rangle_{V}
and
\langle-,-\rangle_{W}
respectively. Given a linear transformation
A:V\longrightarrow W
the adjoint
A^{\dagger}:W\longrightarrow V
is uniquely defined by

\langle Av,w\rangle_{W}=\langle v,A^{\dagger}w\rangle_{V}\qquad\forall v\in V,w\in W\,.

Then
A^{\dagger}A,AA^{\dagger}
are self-adjoint operators on
V,W
respectively and thus we may apply the spectral theorem to obtain an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors for these spaces. Let
\{v_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha}
be an orthonormal basis of
V
consisting of eigenvectors of
A^{\dagger}A
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{\alpha}\geq 0
. We can divide the indices
\alpha
into those with positive and zero eigenvalues

\Lambda^{+}=\{\alpha\mid\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0\}\,,\qquad\Lambda^{0}=\{\alpha\mid\lambda_{\alpha}=0\}\,.

We write
\Lambda=\Lambda^{+}\cup\Lambda^{0}
for the complete set of indices, so
|\Lambda|=\dim V
. Then we define

u_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1/2}Av_{\alpha}\,,\qquad s_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}^{1/2}\,,\qquad\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}\,.

(6)

Lemma 2.1
.

The set
\{u_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}}
is an orthonormal basis in
W
for the image of
A
. We have

Av_{\alpha}=s_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\,,\quad A^{\dagger}u_{\alpha}=s_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}\,.

(7)

We call
s_{\alpha}
the
singular values
,
v_{\alpha}
the
right singular vectors
and
u_{\alpha}
the
left singular vectors
.

Proof.

If
\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0
define
u^{\prime}_{\alpha}=Av_{\alpha}
. Then

\displaystyle A^{\dagger}u^{\prime}_{\alpha}=A^{\dagger}Av_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}

and hence

AA^{\dagger}u^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}Av_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}u^{\prime}_{\alpha}

Moreover by adjointness

\langle u^{\prime}_{\alpha},u^{\prime}_{\beta}\rangle_{W}=\langle u^{\prime}_{\alpha},Av_{\beta}\rangle_{W}=\langle A^{\dagger}u^{\prime}_{\alpha},v_{\beta}\rangle_{V}=\lambda_{\alpha}\langle v_{\alpha},v_{\beta}\rangle_{V}=\lambda_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}\,.

If we set
u_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1/2}u^{\prime}_{\alpha}=\lambda_{\alpha}^{-1/2}Av_{\alpha}
then the claims follow.
∎

2.3
Learning Coefficient

In this section we formulate the problem of learning the conditional true distribution
q(y|x)
of sequences in the context of singular learning theory (SLT)
Watanabe (2009)
. We assume
k,l>0
and
x\in\Sigma^{k}
,
y\in\Sigma^{l}
. Given a set of
n
independent and identically distributed samples
D_{n}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn from the true data-generating distribution
q(x,y)
, we define the sample negative log likelihood
L_{n}(w)
associated with a model
p(y|x,w)
and its theoretical counterpart
L
as

\displaystyle L_{n}(w)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y_{i}|x_{i},w)\,,

\displaystyle L(w)

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{q(x,y)}\big[\log p(y|x,w)\big]\,.

Under some technical hypotheses the
local learning coefficient
(LLC)
(
Watanabe, 2009
)

(
Lau et al., 2024
)
can be defined at a local minimum
{w^{*}}
of
L(w)
is the
volume scaling exponent
of the loss near
{w^{*}}
in the following sense. Let
B({w^{*}})
be a closed ball centered on
{w^{*}}
such that for all
w\in B({w^{*}})
,
L(w)\geq L({w^{*}})
. For sufficiently small
\epsilon
, consider the set
B({w^{*}},\epsilon)=\{w\in B({w^{*}})\mid L(w)-L({w^{*}})<\epsilon\}
, the volume of which we define as
V(\epsilon):=\int_{B({w^{*}},\epsilon)}\varphi(w)\,dw
for some positive prior
\varphi(w)
. It can be shown that there exist a positive rational number
\lambda({w^{*}})
and a positive integer
m({w^{*}})
such that
V(\epsilon)\propto\epsilon^{\lambda({w^{*}})}(-\log\epsilon)^{m({w^{*}})-1}
asymptotically as
\epsilon\to 0
. The LLC is defined to be the rational number
\lambda({w^{*}})
.

For regular statistical models,
\lambda(w^{*})=d/2
, where
d
is the dimension of the parameter space. However, in general
\lambda(w^{*})
can be smaller than
d/2
. The LLC plays a crucial role in the asymptotic expansion of the local free energy

F_{n}(B_{\gamma}(w^{*}))=-\log\int_{B(w^{*},\gamma)}\exp\{-nL_{n}(w)\}\varphi(w)dw

(8)

The asymptotic expansion of the local free energy is given by Watanabe’s free energy formula
(
Watanabe, 2018
, §6.3)

F_{n}(B_{\gamma}(w^{*}))=nL_{n}(w^{*})+\lambda(w^{*})\log n-(m(w^{*})-1)\log\log n+O_{P}(1)

(9)

This expansion provides insights into the behavior of the posterior distribution and is the basis for one approach to understanding neural network development
(
Chen et al., 2023
;
Hoogland et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
.

2.4
LLC Estimation

It is technically challenging to derive theoretical values for the LLC even for deep linear neural networks
(
Aoyagi, 2024
)
. To this end,
Lau et al. (2024)
introduced an estimator of the LLC and we recall its definition below. Consider the posterior distribution in
w
localized to
{w^{*}}
by a Gaussian prior centered there with scale parameter
\gamma>0
:

p(w|w^{*},\beta,\gamma)\propto\exp\left\{-n\beta L_{n}(w)-\frac{\gamma}{2}||w-w^{*}||_{2}^{2}\right\}\,.

(10)

The larger
\gamma
is, the more tightly concentrated this distribution is around
{w^{*}}
. Let
\mathbb{E}_{w|{w^{*}},\beta,\gamma}
denote expectation over (
10
). The
LLC estimator
is defined by

\hat{\lambda}_{n}(w^{*})=n\beta\big[\mathbb{E}_{w|{w^{*}},\beta,\gamma}L_{n}(w)-L_{n}({w^{*}})\big].

(11)

We note that in the original work of
Lau et al. (2024)
we use the inverse temperature
\beta^{*}=1/\log n
derived in
Watanabe (2013)
. However empirically it has been found that other inverse temperatures are more convenient because the SGLD sampler is better behaved
(
Hoogland et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024
)
. That this is a valid estimator follows from
(
Watanabe, 2013
, Theorem 4)
which holds for
\beta=\beta_{0}/\log n
with
\beta_{0}\neq 1
. The effect of this choice of inverse temperature on the estimates is part of the subject of the present work (see Section
8
).

We approximate the expectation in this estimator with SGLD
Welling & Teh (2011)
. The SGLD update for sampling from the local posterior distribution
p(w|w^{*},\beta,\gamma)
is

\displaystyle\Delta w_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{t}}{2}\Bigg[\frac{\beta n}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nabla_{w}\log p(y_{l_{i}}|x_{l_{i}},w_{t})+\gamma(w^{*}-w_{t})\Bigg]+\eta_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{t}}{2}\Big[-\beta n\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w_{t})+\gamma(w^{*}-w_{t})\Big]+\eta_{t}\,,

(12)

where
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\epsilon_{t})
and
\{(x_{l_{i}},y_{l_{i}})\}_{i=1}^{m}
is a randomly sampled minibatch. Here the
batch-size

m
can be significantly smaller than
n
. Let
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{T}\}
be samples of the tempered posterior at inverse temperature
\beta
using the SGLD update (
12
). Then the
SGLD-based LLC estimator
is

\hat{\lambda}_{n}^{\SGLD}({w^{*}})=n\beta\bigg[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}L_{n}(w_{t})-L_{n}(w^{*})\bigg]\,.

(13)

Typically we will drop the superscript and write
\hat{\lambda}_{n}({w^{*}})
for estimates derived using SGLD samples.

Let
\Sigma
be a nonempty finite set which we refer to as the set of

\Sigma

\sigma,\tau,\ldots
. For

3.1
Data Distribution

We consider jointly distributed random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{K}
taking values in
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}

are identified as vectors in

(see Section

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{k}}[X_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes X_{k}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q_{k}(X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{k}=x_{k})x_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{k}\,.

We call

\displaystyle\varepsilon:\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{R},\quad

3.2
Tokenisation

Tokenisation is a fundamental preprocessing step in modern language model training, serving as the interface between raw text and the numerical representations required by neural networks. A tokeniser segments input text into discrete units called tokens, which form the vocabulary that the model operates on
(
Kudo & Richardson, 2018
;
Sennrich et al., 2016
;
Phuong & Hutter, 2022
)
. The choice of tokenisation strategy significantly impacts model performance, training efficiency, and downstream applicability
(
Mielke et al., 2021
)
.

Several tokenisation approaches have evolved alongside advances in language modelling:

Word-level tokenisation.

Early approaches split text on whitespace and punctuation, treating each word as a distinct token
(
Mikolov et al., 2013a
)
. While intuitive, this approach suffers from vocabulary explosion in morphologically rich languages and an inability to handle out-of-vocabulary words.

Character-level tokenisation.

Character-based approaches
(
Kim et al., 2016
)
use individual characters as tokens, ensuring a small vocabulary size and eliminating out-of-vocabulary issues. However, they create very long sequences and lose direct access to word-level semantics, requiring the model to learn these relationships from scratch.

Subword tokenisation.

Modern language models predominantly use subword tokenisation algorithms
(
Sennrich et al., 2016
;
Kudo & Richardson, 2018
)
, which strike a balance between word and character approaches. These methods adaptively split words into subword units based on frequency statistics in the training corpus.

The choice of tokenisation strategy creates implicit biases in how models learn linguistic patterns
(
Rust et al., 2021
)
. These biases manifest in several ways. For example subword algorithms like BPE create shorter tokens for frequent words or substrings and longer tokens for rare ones. The distributional properties we study, including modes, are shaped by how the continuous stream of text is discretised through tokenisation.

3.3
Transformers

Transformer neural networks
(
Vaswani et al., 2017
)
have become the dominant architecture for modelling sequential data, particularly in natural language processing. For a formal definition of a transformer-based sequence model see
Phuong & Hutter (2022)
.

For autoregressive modelling, transformers typically employ causal attention masks to ensure that the prediction for position
t
only depends on tokens at positions
<t
. Transformers are trained to
simultaneously
model the conditional distributions
q(X_{k+1}|X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
for all sequence lengths
1\leq k<K
and thus implicitly to model the joint distribution

q(X_{1}\cdots X_{K})=\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}q(X_{k+1}|X_{\leq k})q(X_{1})\,.

We assume
q(X_{1})
is given and does not need to be modelled and
X_{\leq k}=X_{1}\cdots X_{k}
. Here
K
is called the
maximum context length
. The training loss for such language models is usually defined for a sample
\{S^{i}_{K}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
contexts

S^{i}_{K}\in\Sigma^{K}
which are drawn i.i.d from a large corpus, by

\ell_{n}(w)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\log p(x^{i}_{k+1}|x^{i}_{\leq k},w)

where we write
S^{i}_{K}=x^{i}_{1}x^{i}_{2}\cdots x^{i}_{K}
. Here for any sequence
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma

p(y|x,w):=\operatorname{softmax}\big(g_{w}(x)\big)[y]

where
p[y]
denotes the
y
entry in the softmax vector
(
Phuong & Hutter, 2022
)
and
g_{w}
is the transformer parametrised by weights
w
. Thus the transformer is trained to model the distribution over sequences of length
K

given
the distribution over unigrams
q_{1}(x)
where we define

p(X_{1}\cdots X_{K}|w):=\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}p(X_{k+1}|X_{\leq k},w)q_{1}(X_{1})\,.

Note that then by definition

\ell_{n}(w)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\prod_{k=1}^{K-1}p(x^{i}_{k+1}|x^{i}_{\leq k},w)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(S^{i}_{K}|w)+\log q_{1}(x^{i}_{1})\,.

(18)

Of course it is not assumed that
q_{1}
is known, but since it does not depend on
w
it does not appear in gradients and so we may ignore it.

Although it is usually stated that transformers do
next-token
prediction, from a mathematical perspective they just model the distribution of contexts. In particular, given integers
k,l>0
with
k+l=K
it is just as accurate to describe the training loss of a transformer as

\ell_{n}(w)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\log p(x^{i}_{>k}|x^{i}_{\leq k},w)+\log p(x^{i}_{\leq k}|w)\Big]+\log q_{1}(x^{i}_{1})

(19)

where
x^{i}_{>k}=x^{i}_{k+1}\cdots x^{i}_{K}\in\Sigma^{l}
. That is, we can think of optimising this loss as training the transformer to predict sequences of length
l
given sequences of length
k
for any pair with
k+l=K
.

This observation is not an idle one: since the forward pass of the transformer on a token in position
i
produces a latent vector which is
used for prediction
in all sequence positions
>i
it is not surprising that these latent vectors contain information relevant for such prediction
Pal et al. (2023)
;
Wu et al. (2024)
. Indeed
Shai et al. (2024)
have argued this is optimal based on computational mechanics. This explains why in this paper we consider models
p(y|x,w)
which predict
y\in\Sigma^{l}
given
x\in\Sigma^{k}
(rather than fixing
l=1
) since from a mathematical perspective this is arguably the correct setting for studying transformers as language models.

4.1
Hilbert space

Definition 4.1
.

For

\langle y,y^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma^{l}}y(\sigma)y^{\prime}(\sigma)\,.

Here we use that
y,y^{\prime}
are functions
\Sigma^{l}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which we may evaluate on symbols. Recall that we assume
q(x)>0
for all
1\leq k\leq K
and
x\in\Sigma^{k}
.

Definition 4.2
.

For
k>0
let
\mathscr{V}_{k}
denote the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}
with the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}u(x)v(x)q(x)^{-1}.

(21)

When
k=1
we sometimes write
\mathscr{V}
for
\mathscr{V}_{1}
.

Remark 4.3
.

Given
x\in\Sigma^{k}
we have

\|x\|_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}^{2}=q(x)^{-1}

hence the unit vector in the direction of
x
is
q(x)^{1/2}x
. Hence the vectors
\{q(x)^{1/2}x\}_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathscr{V}_{k}
.

Given a measure space
(X,\mu)
and Hilbert space
\mathscr{K}
there is a Hilbert space of square-integrable
\mathscr{K}
-valued functions
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathscr{K})

(
Reed & Simon, 1980
, II.1)
. In this paper we make
\Sigma^{k}
into a measure space using
q_{k}
and consider the Hilbert space

\mathscr{H}_{k,l}=L^{2}(\Sigma^{k},q_{k};\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}})

with pairing for functions
f,g:\Sigma^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
in
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
defined by

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathscr{H}_{k,l}}=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\big\langle f(x),g(x)\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}\,q(x)

(22)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathscr{H}_{k,l}}=\Big(\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\|f(x)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}^{2}\,q(x)\Big)^{1/2}\,.

(23)

In the case
k=0
, we take
\mathscr{H}_{0}=\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
as a Hilbert space with the pairing in (
20
).

Recall that for a finite set
S
we write

\Delta S=\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{S}\,|\,\sum_{s\in S}g(s)=1\text{ and }g(s)\in[0,1]\text{ for all }s\in S\}\,.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}\subseteq\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
denote the subset of functions
f:\Sigma^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
satisfying

f(x)\in\Delta\big(\Sigma^{l}\big)\,,\quad\forall x\in\Sigma^{k}\,.

This is the subset of conditional probability distributions.

We note that
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}
is a product of probability simplices, and thus a smooth manifold with corners embedded into the affine space
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
viewed as a real manifold. In fact
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}
is a statistical manifold in the sense of
Amari (2016)
.

4.2
Converting Between Tensors and Linear Transformations

Since all functions on a finite set are integrable, the underlying set of
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
is just the set of all functions
\Sigma^{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
which is in bijection with the vector space of linear transformations
(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes k}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes l}
. This bijection identifies a function
f:\Sigma^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
with the linear transformation

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes k}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes l}\,,

(24)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{k}\longmapsto f(\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k})\,.

(25)

Note that we used the fact that
(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes l}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
are identified with each other via

\displaystyle(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes l}\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\,,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{l}\longmapsto(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{l})\,.

There is an isomorphism of vector spaces

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{k}\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}\big[\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}\big]^{*}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\,,

(26)

\displaystyle f\longmapsto\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}x^{*}\otimes f(x)\,.

Here if
x=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k}
we write
x^{*}
for the functional that sends a tensor in
(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes k}
to its coefficient in
\sigma_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{k}
, using the expansion in word basis. While the definition of dual vector
x^{*}
makes use of the word basis, the isomorphism (
26
) nonetheless doesn’t depend on a choice of basis (because
x
appears again in
f(x)
).

There is no canonical isomorphism between a vector space and its dual. A nondegenerate bilinear form provides such an identification. Using the bilinear forms
\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}
and
\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}
we have the isomorphisms of vector spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}})^{*}\,,\qquad u\longmapsto u^{*}:=\langle u,-\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}}\,,

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}})^{*}\,,\qquad u\longmapsto\hat{u}^{*}:=\langle u,-\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}\,.

(28)

Note that for
u,v\in\Sigma^{k}
we have

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{*}(v)=\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=\delta_{u,v}q(u)^{-1}

hence
\hat{u}^{*}=q(u)^{-1}u^{*}
as linear functionals.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\hat{u}^{*}(x)\hat{v}^{*}(x)\,.

Proof.

The vectors
\{q(x)^{1/2}x\}_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathscr{V}_{k}
by Remark
4.3
. Hence

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\big\langle u,q(x)^{1/2}x\big\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}\big\langle q(x)^{1/2}x,v\big\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\langle u,x\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}\langle x,v\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\hat{u}^{*}(x)\hat{v}^{*}(x)

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

The inverse of (
28
) sends a functional
\eta
to
\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\eta(x)x
.

Proof.

It suffices to show
\eta=\sum_{x}q(x)\eta(x)\hat{x}^{*}
. We check it on basis elements
u\in\Sigma^{k}

\displaystyle\sum_{x}q(x)\eta(x)\hat{x}^{*}(u)=\sum_{x}q(x)\eta(x)q(x)^{-1}x^{*}(u)=\eta(u)

as required.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

The composite isomorphism defined using
\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{k}\overset{\eqref{eq:curry}}{\cong}\big[\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}\big]^{*}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\overset{\eqref{eq:curry2}}{\cong}\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\cong(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes(k+l)}

(29)

is defined on
f\in\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
by

f\longmapsto\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\,x\otimes f(x)\,.

Proof.

Under (
26
) a function
f
is sent to
\sum_{u\in\Sigma^{k}}u^{*}\otimes f(u)
, and under (
28
),
u^{*}
is sent to
q(u)u
by Lemma
4.6
, which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

The fundamental tensor
A_{k+l}\in(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes(k+l)}
corresponds under (
29
) to
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}\in\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
defined by

\mathcal{C}_{k,l}(x)=\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}q(y|x)y\,,\qquad\forall x\in\Sigma^{k}\,.

(30)

Proof.

By Lemma
4.7
the function
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
corresponds to the tensor

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\,x\otimes\mathcal{C}_{k,l}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\,x\otimes\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}q(y|x)y

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k},y\in\Sigma^{l}}q(xy)x\otimes y

\displaystyle=A_{k+l}

using
q(xy)=q(y|x)q(x)
.
∎

4.3
Tensor Decomposition

Let
k,l>0
. To apply dimensional reduction we start with the fundamental tensor
A_{k+l}
and take the associated linear transformation (Lemma
4.8
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}:\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\,,

\displaystyle x\longmapsto\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}q(y|x)y\,.

It is natural to apply singular value decomposition (SVD), however, this construction is defined for linear transformations between inner product spaces (Section
2.2
) and so we must make a choice of inner product for the domain and codomain. The right and left singular vectors will have different interpretations. The left singular vectors are viewed as elements of the
output
space of the function space
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}=L^{2}(\Sigma^{k},q_{k};\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}})
while the right singular vectors are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}
.

We take the domain to be the inner product space
\mathscr{V}_{k}
of Definition
4.2
and the codomain to be the inner product space
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
with the dot product (
20
). That is, we consider
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
and its adjoint

\mathcal{C}_{k,l}:\mathscr{V}_{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\,,\quad\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}:\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}\longrightarrow\mathscr{V}_{k}

defined uniquely such that for all pairs
x\in\Sigma^{k},\sigma\in\Sigma^{l}

\langle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}x,\sigma\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}=\langle x,\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}\sigma\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}\,.

(31)

The singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
with respect to the given inner products on the domain and codomain gives (using the notation of Section
2.2
):

•

an index set
\Lambda_{k,l}
presented as a disjoint union of subsets
\Lambda^{+}_{k,l},\Lambda^{0}_{k,l}
,

•

an orthonormal basis of vectors
\{v_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}}
of
\mathscr{V}_{k}
, the
right singular vectors
(orthonormal with respect to the pairing of of
\mathscr{V}_{k}
),

•

an orthonormal basis of vectors
\{u_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}_{k,l}}
for the image of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
in
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
called the
left singular vectors
(orthonormal with respect to the dot product)

•

singular values

s_{\alpha}\geq 0
with
s_{\alpha}>0
for
\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}_{k,l}
and
s_{\alpha}=0
for
\alpha\in\Lambda^{0}_{k,l}
.

These have the property that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}\,v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}^{0}\,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}\,v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}^{+}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}\,u_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}^{+}\,.

By construction

\mathcal{C}_{k,l}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}}s_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\circ\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}_{k,l}}s_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\circ\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}

(32)

where we interpret
u_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
as the linear transformation sending
1
to
u_{\alpha}
, and
\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(v_{\beta})=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}
. We refer to
\Lambda_{k,l}
as the set of
modes
of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}_{k,l}
. For
x\in\Sigma^{k},y\in\Sigma^{l}
we define

q(y|x,\alpha):=s^{-1}_{\alpha}\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)u_{\alpha}^{*}(y)\in\mathbb{R}

(33)

which we call the
propensity
of
y
to follow
x
according to the mode
\alpha
. We adopt the convention that the propensity is zero if
\alpha\in\Lambda^{0}_{k,l}
. The
propensity
of a mode
\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}
is defined to be
q(\alpha)=s_{\alpha}^{2}
.

The preceding discussion is summarised by the
mode decomposition
of the conditional probability:

Lemma 4.10
.

For any
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
,

q(y|x)=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}}q(y\mid x,\alpha)q(\alpha)\,.

Proof.

It follows from (
32
) that

q(y|x)=y^{*}(\mathcal{C}_{k,l}(x))=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{k,l}}s_{\alpha}\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)u_{\alpha}^{*}(y)

(34)

from which the claim follows.
∎

The choice of the
s^{-1}_{\alpha}
factor in (
33
) is justified by Remark
5.3
.

Note that while these propensities sum to a probability, they do not have a clear interpretation as likelihoods outside of this sum over
\alpha
. This will not cause confusion, because in this paper there are no probabilities that appear conditioned on mode indices, only propensities.

Remark 4.11
.

If we define
D:\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}
by
D(x)=q(x)^{-1/2}x
then

\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=\langle Dx,Dx^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}}\,.

(35)

Let
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{*}
denote the adjoint with respect to the dot product on both the domain and codomain, so its matrix is the transpose of the matrix of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
in the token basis. Then using (
31
), (
35
) we have

\langle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}D^{-1}x,x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}}=\langle D^{-1}x,\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=\langle x,D\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}x^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}}\,.

Hence
D\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}=(\mathcal{C}_{k,l}D^{-1})^{*}=D^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{*}
, so
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}=D^{-2}\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{*}
. The singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
, viewed as a linear transformation between inner product spaces
\mathscr{V}_{k}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
, is thus the same as the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}D^{-1}
viewed as a linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
.

Remark 4.12
.

The matrix of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
is a (column) stochastic matrix since
\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}q(y|x)=1
. When
k=l
the Perron-Frobenius theorem applies to this matrix (noting that we have assumed all probabilities positive) and we can think of this as a Markov chain
Seabrook & Wiskott (2023)
.

4.4
Mode Basis

We make use of the notation established in Section
4.3
. Let
k,l>0
be given and let
\Lambda=\Lambda_{k,l}
,
\Lambda^{+}=\Lambda_{k,l}^{+}
as above. Note that the
u_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}
may span a proper subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
. Choose arbitrarily an extension of this to an orthonormal basis
\{u_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda^{++}}
for all of
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
where
\Lambda^{++}\supseteq\Lambda^{+}
.

Definition 4.13
.

Given
\alpha\in\Lambda
,
\beta\in\Lambda^{++}
we define
e_{\alpha\beta}\in\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
to be

e_{\alpha\beta}=u_{\beta}\circ\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}

(36)

that is,
e_{\alpha\beta}(x)=\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)u_{\beta}
and so as functions
\Sigma^{l}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

e_{\alpha\beta}(x)(y)=\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)u_{\beta}^{*}(y)\,.

(37)

Hence by (
32
)

\mathcal{C}_{k,l}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}_{k,l}}s_{\alpha}e_{\alpha\alpha}\,.

(38)

Lemma 4.14
.

The vectors
\{e_{\alpha\beta}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda,\beta\in\Lambda^{++}}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
.

Proof.

We compute that

\displaystyle\langle e_{\alpha\beta},e_{\gamma\delta}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}_{k,l}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta}(x),e_{\gamma\delta}(x)\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)\hat{v}_{\gamma}^{*}(x)\big\langle u_{\beta},u_{\delta}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\beta,\delta}\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)\hat{v}_{\gamma}^{*}(x)

which is by Lemma
4.5
equal to

\displaystyle=\delta_{\beta,\delta}\langle v_{\alpha},v_{\gamma}\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=\delta_{\beta,\delta}\delta_{\alpha,\gamma}\,.

This shows that the set is orthonormal and thus linearly independent. It is a spanning set since

\dim\mathscr{H}_{k,l}=\dim(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{k}})\dim(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}})=|\Lambda_{k,l}||\Lambda_{k,l}^{++}|

which completes the proof.
∎

Given a statistical model
p(y|x,w)
for
x\in\Sigma^{k},y\in\Sigma^{l}
and
w\in W
we denote by
f_{w}
the corresponding function in
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}

f_{w}(x)=\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}p(y|x,w)y

(39)

and we write
p(x,y|w)=p(y|x,w)q(x)
. Pairing an observable like
e_{\alpha\beta}
with the function space representation of a probability distribution like
p
gives an expectation
\mathbb{E}_{p}\big[e_{\alpha\beta}\big]
in the following sense:

Lemma 4.15
.

For any
\alpha\in\Lambda,\beta\in\Lambda^{++}
we have

\big\langle f_{w},e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}_{k,l}}=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k},y\in\Sigma^{l}}p(x,y|w)e_{\alpha\beta}(x)(y)\,.

(40)

Proof.

We calculate

\displaystyle\big\langle f_{w},e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\big\langle f_{w}(x),e_{\alpha\beta}(x)\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\big\langle f_{w}(x),\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)u_{\beta}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}q(x)\big\langle f_{w}(x),u_{\beta}\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}}\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}p(y|x,w)\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)u_{\beta}^{*}(y)q(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}p(x,y|w)e_{\alpha\beta}(x)(y)\,.

as claimed.
∎

The modes
\alpha
are
modes of variation
or “patterns” in the data distribution and we think of
e_{\alpha\beta}(x)(y)
as the weighting of
xy
in the mode, or more informally, as a measure of the degree to which
xy
“follows” the pattern. Note it is immediate from (
38
) that

\big\langle\mathcal{C}_{k,l},e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}_{k,l}}=\delta_{\alpha,\beta}s_{\alpha}

(41)

4.5
Effective True Distribution

In this section we discuss how to truncate the true distribution using the mode basis. After normalisation we obtain what we refer to as an
effective
true distribution. Let
k,l>0
be fixed and set
\Lambda=\Lambda_{k,l}
,
\Lambda^{+}=\Lambda_{k,l}^{+}
and
\Lambda^{++}=\Lambda_{k,l}^{++}
. We have defined above an orthonormal basis
\{e_{\alpha\beta}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda,\beta\in\Lambda^{++}}
for
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
(Lemma
4.14
). Suppose that we put a total order on
\Lambda
(for example putting the largest singular values first). Then for any
\chi\in\Lambda
we can define

\Lambda^{\leq\chi}=\{\alpha\in\Lambda\mid\alpha\leq\chi\}\,,\qquad\Lambda^{+,\leq\chi}=\{\alpha\in\Lambda^{+}\mid\alpha\leq\chi\}\,.

This defines a subspace

\mathscr{H}^{\leq\chi}_{k,l}:=\operatorname{span}\Big\{e_{\alpha\beta}\mid\alpha\in\Lambda^{\leq\chi},\beta\in\Lambda^{+,\leq\chi}\Big\}\,.

Note that this subspace is defined by linear equations in the coordinates of
\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
since the
e_{\alpha\beta}
are related to these coordinates by a change of basis. We let

P^{\leq\chi}:\mathscr{H}_{k,l}\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}_{k,l}^{\leq\chi}

denote the orthogonal projection onto this subspace. Recall that
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}
denotes the subspace of functions taking values in probability distributions on
\Sigma^{l}
(Definition
4.4
). We define

\mathscr{P}^{\leq\chi}_{k,l}=\mathscr{P}_{k,l}\cap\mathscr{H}^{\leq\chi}_{k,l}\,.

By construction this is an
m
-flat submanifold in the sense of
Amari (2016)
since it is cut out by linear equations in the statistical manifold
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}
. The true distribution
q
gives a point in
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}
(or equivalently
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
does). Hence the following is well-defined:

Definition 4.16
.

We define

q^{(\chi)}=\operatorname{argmin}_{p\in\mathscr{P}_{k,l}^{\leq\chi}}D_{KL}(q\,\|\,p)

to be the unique distribution in
\mathscr{P}_{k,l}^{\leq\chi}
which minises the KL divergence to the original distribution
q
. We refer to this as the
effective
true distribution associated to the mode cutoff
\chi
.

Remark 4.17
.

The uniqueness of the
q^{(\chi)}
is proved in
Amari (2009)
.

Remark 4.18
.

There is a more straightforward way to construct a truncation, but it has some subtleties. The
truncation
of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}:\mathscr{V}_{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{l}
defined by

\mathcal{C}^{\leq\chi}_{k,l}=P^{\leq\chi}\big(\mathcal{C}_{k,l}\big)=\sum_{\alpha\leq\chi}s_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}\circ\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}.

(42)

In the notation of propensities (Lemma
4.10
) we have for
x\in\Sigma^{k},\tau\in\Sigma

\mathcal{C}^{\leq\chi}_{k,l}(x)=\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\sum_{\alpha\leq\chi}q(y\mid x,\alpha)q(\alpha)\,y\,.

(43)

Note however that
\mathcal{C}^{\leq\chi}_{k+1}(x)(y)
is not necessarily a probability distribution on
\Sigma^{l}
. Indeed these quantities can be negative. We can remove these negatives and normalise to produce a conditional probability distribution over
y
given by

q^{(\chi,b)}(y|x):=\frac{\max\big\{0,\sum_{\alpha\leq\chi}q(y|x,\alpha)q(\alpha)\big\}}{\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\max\big\{0,\sum_{\alpha\leq\chi}q(y|x,\alpha)q(\alpha)\big\}}\,.

(44)

This has the advantage of being simpler to describe explicitly than
q^{(\chi)}
but is theoretically less well-justified. We do not consider the distribution
q^{(\chi,b)}
further in this paper.

Remark 4.19
.

Here we have treated a single pair
k,l
at a time. But we note that
q
satisfies the language condition (Definition
3.2
) which ties the
q_{k}
for all
k
to one another. We can define simultaneous truncations of all
q_{k}
by a choice of mode cutoff
\chi^{k,l}
for each
k,l
since this leads to an
m
-flat submanifold of the statistical manifold of all languages.

5
Examples

The purpose of this section is to briefly argue that the singular values of the SVD of the fundamental tensor, arranged as a linear transformation
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
, is a reasonable notion of “pattern” in sequence models, including tokenised natural language. First we exhibit some simple abstract modes, before giving empirical examples. We note that tensor decompositions are a common technique for representing structure in natural language; see
(
Anandkumar et al., 2012
)
and the references therein.

5.1
Theoretical

Definition 5.1
.

For
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, an
absolute bigram
is a sequence
xy
where
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
with the property that

(a)

q(xz)=0
for all
z\in\Sigma^{l}
with
z\neq y
.

(b)

q(ty)=0
for all
t\in\Sigma^{k}
with
t\neq x
.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
xy
is an absolute bigram, then
q(x)^{1/2}x
is a right singular vector with corresponding left singular vector
y
and singular value
s=q(x)^{1/2}
, of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}\in\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
.

Proof.

Let
xy
be an absolute bigram. Then it follows from the definition of absolute bigrams that
q(y|x)=1
. Note that
q(z|x)=0
if and only if
q(xz)=0
, and
q(y|t)=0
if and only if
q(ty)=0
. Note that by Remark
4.3
,
q(x)^{1/2}x
is a unit vector in
\mathscr{V_{k}}
and
y
is a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
which are respectively the domain and codomain (as inner product spaces) of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}(q(x)^{1/2}x)

\displaystyle=q(x)^{1/2}\sum_{z}q(z|x)z=q(y|x)q(x)^{1/2}y=q(x)^{1/2}y

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t}q(y|t)t=q(y|x)x=x\,.

Hence by Remark
4.11
,
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}(y)=q(x)x=q(x)^{1/2}(q(x)^{1/2}x)
as claimed in (a). ∎

Remark 5.3
.

In the terminology of Definition
4.9
, with
\alpha
denoting the mode index corresponding to an absolute bigram
xy
, the propensities are
q(\alpha)=q(x)=q(xy)
and

q(z|t,\alpha)=q(x)^{-1/2}y^{*}(z)q(x)^{1/2}\hat{x}^{*}(t)=\delta_{z,y}\hat{x}^{*}(t)=q(x)^{-1}\delta_{z,y}\delta_{t,x}\,.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
xy
is an absolute bigram with mode index
\alpha
then
\langle f_{w},e_{\alpha\alpha}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=p(xy|w)q(x)^{-1/2}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.2
we have
u_{\alpha}=y,v_{\alpha}=q(x)^{1/2}x
so by Lemma
4.15
we have

\displaystyle\langle f_{w},e_{\alpha\alpha}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}

\displaystyle=p(xy|w)\hat{v}_{\alpha}^{*}(x)=p(xy|w)\langle q(x)^{1/2}x,x\rangle_{\mathscr{V}_{k}}=p(xy|w)q(x)^{-1/2}

as claimed.
∎

While absolute bigrams may be rare, there is a generalisation which is more common:

Definition 5.5
.

A
collective bigram
is a pair
(S,y)
consisting of a nonempty set
S\subseteq\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
satisfying

(a)

q(y|s)=1
for all
s\in S
.

(b)

q(y|t)=0
if
t\notin S
.

As above we can prove collective bigrams give modes:

Lemma 5.6
.

If
(S,y)
is a collective bigram then
v=\frac{q(y)^{1/2}}{|S|^{1/2}}\sum_{s\in S}s
is a right singular vector with corresponding left singular vector
y
and singular value
q(y)^{1/2}|S|^{1/2}
of
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}:\mathscr{V}_{k}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
.

Proof.

We have
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}(s)=y
for
s\in S
, hence
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}(v)=q(y)^{1/2}|S|^{1/2}y
. On the other hand
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{*}(y)=\sum_{s\in S}q(y|s)s=\sum_{s\in S}s
and hence by Remark
4.11

\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{\dagger}(y)=q(y)\mathcal{C}_{k,l}^{*}(y)=q(y)\sum_{s\in S}s=q(y)\frac{|S|^{1/2}}{q(y)^{1/2}}v=q(y)^{1/2}|S|^{1/2}v\,.

This proves the claim.
∎

6
Mode Insensitivity

We develop two technical hypothesis which express that in a region of parameter space a model is
insensitive
to the information in higher modes. Consider a parametric model with parameters
w\in W
defining conditional probabilities
p(y|x,w)
for
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
. In this paper we assume
W
is compact and that
p(y|x,w)
is continuously differentiable with respect to
W
for all
x,y
.

We fix
k,l>0
and set
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
. We let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}
denote local coordinates at
w^{*}\in W
, so that the tangent space
T_{w^{*}}(W)
is spanned by partial derivatives
\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}
.

Definition 6.1
.

The log probability map
\Phi:W\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}
is defined by

\Phi(w)(x)=\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\log p(y|x,w)\cdot y

The Jacobian of
\Phi
at a point
w^{*}
is a linear transformation. Since
\mathscr{H}
is a (finite-dimensional) affine space we identify it with its tangent map at a given point.

Definition 6.2
.

The Jacobian of the log probability map is

\Psi_{w^{*}}:T_{w^{*}}(W)\longrightarrow\mathscr{H}

which sends each tangent vector to a function representing the corresponding direction of change in log-probabilities:

\Psi_{w^{*}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}\right)(x)=\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}\log p(y|x,w^{*})\cdot y

(50)

where we view
y\in\Sigma^{l}
as a basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma^{l}}
.

Any linear transformation from
T_{w^{*}}(W)
to
\mathscr{H}
can be viewed as a
\mathscr{H}
-valued cotangent vector and so sometimes we will write the Jacobian of the log probability map simply as

\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\nabla_{w}\log p(y|x,w^{*})\cdot y

(51)

where we assume
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given the canonical metric on its tangent space.

The image of
\Psi_{w^{*}}
characterizes which directions in function space the model can immediately move toward during gradient-based optimisation. If a particular mode in the data distribution lies outside this image, the model will struggle to capture it efficiently during learning.

We think of the conditional distributions of
q,q^{\prime}
as vectors in
\mathscr{H}
as in Section
4.3
, and abuse notation by writing
q
for
\mathcal{C}_{k,l}
. Given the vector
q-q^{\prime}
in
\mathscr{H}
the pairing with
\Psi_{w^{*}}
is a linear functional on
T_{w^{*}}(W)
defined by sending a tangent vector
\nu
to

\displaystyle\big\langle\Psi_{w^{*}}(\nu),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\big\langle\Psi_{w^{*}}(\nu)(x),q(-|x)-q^{\prime}(-|x)\big\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{l}}q(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\big(q(x,y)-q^{\prime}(x,y)\big)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{i}}\log p(y|x,w^{*})\,.

(52)

using the
L^{2}
-norm on
T^{w^{*}}(W)=T_{w^{*}}(W)^{*}
the norm of this is the
L^{2}
-norm of the vector

\big\|\big\langle\Psi_{w^{*}}(-),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}\big\|_{2}:=\bigg\|\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma}\big(q(x,y)-q^{\prime}(x,y)\big)\nabla_{w}\log p(y|x,w)\bigg\|_{2}\,.

Similarly we can pair
\Phi(w)
with
q-q^{\prime}

\big\langle\Phi(w),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\big(q(x,y)-q^{\prime}(x,y)\big)\log p(y|x,w)\,.

(53)

In the following two definitions the
model
means
p(y|x,w)
and implicitly we mean
insensitivity to the difference between
q,q^{\prime}
.

Definition 6.3
.

The model is
gradient-insensitive
for the constant
A
at
w^{*}
if

\big\|\big\langle\Psi_{w^{*}}(-),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}\big\|_{2}<A\,.

(54)

We say this condition hold in some set
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
if it holds at every point.

Definition 6.4
.

The model is
log-probability-insensitive
for the constant
B
at
w^{*}
if

\Big|\big\langle\Phi(w^{*}),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}\Big|<B\,.

(55)

We say this condition hold in some set
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
if it holds at every point.

6.1
Motivation for Gradient-Insensitivity

The intuitive content of the gradient-insensitivity condition is quite straightforward, and can be summarised by the phrase “can’t learn”. To say that
\langle\Psi_{w^{*}}(\nu),q-q^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
is small is to say that it is hard to change the model’s log probabilities in directions in the space of predictions of
y
given
x
, which are aligned with the difference between
q,q^{\prime}
.

For example suppose that
p(y|x,w)
is computed by a neural network. This model has a finite capacity to memorize absolute bigrams, so if
q
contains more absolute bigrams than
p
has capacity, and
q^{\prime}
represents truncating the true distribution at some
\chi
that is past this capacity, it is reasonable to suppose that
p
has no gradient for learning the difference
q-q^{(\chi)}
.

More generally we follow
Rogers & McClelland (2004)
in supposing that before models have learned coarse distinctions between categories it is hard to learn distinctions between finer categories; this hierarchical structure in the data distribution means that is reasonable to suppose the gradient for learning higher modes is small before lower modes are learned.

6.2
Motivation for Log-Probability Insensitivity

The motivation for log-probability-insensitivity is more subtle.

Naively to make
\big\langle\Phi(w),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
small in norm we would have to arrange that
\log p(y|x,w)
is not too large whenever
q(x,y)
is different from
q^{\prime}(x,y)
, for example in the case where
q^{\prime}
is obtained by mode truncation, whenever the prediction of
y
from
x
depends non-trivially on higher modes. It is not immediately clear that this is reasonable, and so we need a more careful analysis.

Note that using the basis of
\mathscr{H}
and writing
\langle-\rangle
for
\langle-\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
we have

\big\langle\Phi(w),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda,\beta\in\Lambda^{++}}\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle\,.

(56)

Suppose that
q^{\prime}=q^{(\chi)}
is produced by mode truncation as in Section
4.5
. Then the idea is that the support of
q-q^{(\chi)}
is mostly in the modes
>\chi
. To be more precise,
q^{(\chi)}
is the closest point of
\mathscr{P}^{\leq\chi}_{k,l}
to
q
, and this may be different from the projection
P^{\leq\chi}(q)
which is a vector in
\mathscr{H}^{\leq\chi}
but need not be a distribution. However it is reasonable to assume that
q^{(\chi)}
is not
too
different from
P^{\leq\chi}(q)
if
\chi
is large, since both operations should only have a small effect on most sequences.

Notice that for
\alpha,\beta\leq\chi

\displaystyle\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},q-q^{(\chi)}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},q-P^{\leq\chi}(q)\big\rangle+\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\big\rangle\,.

(57)

By Parseval’s identity

\|P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\leq\chi,\beta\leq\chi}\Big|\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\big\rangle\Big|^{2}

and so making this small, which amounts to taking
\chi
sufficiently large, makes all the terms in (
57
) simultaneously small. More formally

\displaystyle\big|\big\langle\Phi(w),q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\sum_{\alpha\leq\chi,\beta\leq\chi}\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big|\sum_{\alpha>\chi\text{ or }\beta>\chi}\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},q-q^{\prime}\big\rangle\Big|\,.

(58)

By Cauchy-Schwartz the first term is bounded by

\|\Phi(w)\|^{2}\|P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\|^{2}\,.

Let’s suppose this is
<A/2
. To bound the second summand in (
58
) note that

\sum_{\alpha>\chi\text{ or }\beta>\chi}\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle\big\langle e_{\alpha\beta},q\big\rangle\,=\sum_{\alpha>\chi\text{ or }\beta>\chi}\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\beta}\big\rangle\delta_{\alpha\beta}s_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha>\chi}\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\alpha}\big\rangle s_{\alpha}\,.

Now

\displaystyle\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\alpha}\big\rangle=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}e_{\alpha\alpha}(x)(y)\log p(y|x,w)q(x)\,.

To give a plausibility argument for bounding this term we now make the assumption that all the
\alpha
’s are absolute bigrams. Then
s_{\alpha}=q(\sigma\tau)^{1/2}
so

\big\langle\Phi(w),e_{\alpha\alpha}\big\rangle s_{\alpha}=q(\sigma)^{-1}\log p(\tau|\sigma,w)q(\sigma)q(\sigma\tau)^{1/2}=\log p(\tau|\sigma,w)q(\sigma\tau)^{1/2}\,.

To bound (
58
) by
A
we need

\log p(\tau|\sigma,w)<\frac{A}{2q(\sigma\tau)^{1/2}C}

where
C
is the number of modes
>\chi
. The total number of modes is
|\Sigma|^{l}
. Here we make an assumption on our data distribution, namely, that for sufficiently long sequences
x\in\Sigma^{z}
we have
q(x)\leq 2^{-zH}
for some entropy rate
H
with high probability
(
Cover & Thomas, 2006
, §3.1)
. Then since
q(\sigma\tau)
is a sequence of length
k+l
if the entropy rate is
H
we have
q(\sigma\tau)\leq 2^{-(k+l)H}
. Hence

\displaystyle q(\sigma\tau)^{1/2}C

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\frac{(k+l)}{2}H}|\Sigma|^{l}

\displaystyle=2^{-\frac{k+l}{2}H}2^{l\log_{2}|\Sigma|}

\displaystyle=2^{l\log_{2}|\Sigma|-\frac{k+l}{2}H}

and so

\frac{A}{2q(\sigma\tau)^{1/2}C}\geq 2^{\frac{k+l}{2}H-l\log_{2}|\Sigma|-1}A

(59)

So to recap, in order to get log-probability-insensitivity for
A
we need
\|P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\|
to be small enough relative to
\|\Phi(w)\|
and in addition it would suffice (if all the modes were absolute bigrams) to have

\log p(\tau|\sigma,w)<2^{\frac{k+l}{2}H-l\log_{2}|\Sigma|-1}A

(60)

for all these bigrams. Note that this is trivially true if the model perfectly predicts all the bigrams corresponding to modes
>\chi
since then these log probabilities are zero. However we do not want to rely on this. We would prefer to have (
60
) be a mild constraint because the right hand side is large, and have most of the pressure of the log-probability-insensitivity be on
\|P^{\leq\chi}(q)-q^{(\chi)}\|
being small.

Let us substitute some reasonable numbers. If
|\Sigma|=65k
then
\log_{2}|\Sigma|=16
and we take
H\in[1,10]
. Suppose
H=1
(this is the pessimistic case since it leads to slower decay in probabilities). Then the exponent is
\frac{k}{2}-\frac{31l}{2}-1
. Thus provided

k>2+31l

the bound in (
60
) depends more on
A
than it does on factors to do with the data distribution or
k,l
. For next-token prediction
l=1
and modern transformer language models are typically trained with context lengths
k
that are tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens, so this seems reasonable.

7
Modes and LLC estimation

As in Section
4.5
we fix
k,l>0
and set
\Lambda=\Lambda_{k,l}
,
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{k,l}
. We consider a parametric model with parameters
w\in W
defining conditional probabilities
p(y|x,w)
for
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
. We assume
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is compact with coordinates
w_{1},\ldots,w_{d}
and that
p(y|x,w)
is continuously differentiable with respect to
W
for all
x,y
. In the following
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
denotes a compact set. We assume throughout that
W^{\prime}
is large enough that all SGLD chains lie inside this set.

We consider the effective true distribution
q^{(\chi)}
associated to truncating at a mode
\chi\in\Lambda
. Associated to the two distributions
q,q^{(\chi)}
we have two empirical negative log likelihoods

\displaystyle L_{m}(w)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p(y_{i}|x_{i},w)\,,

(61)

\displaystyle L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p\big(y^{(\chi)}_{i}|x^{(\chi)}_{i},w\big)\,,

(62)

where
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\big\{(x_{i}^{(\chi)},y_{i}^{(\chi)})\big\}_{i=1}^{m}
are sampled from the true distribution
q(x,y)
of sequences of length
k+l
and the truncated distribution
q^{(\chi)}(x,y)
(
Definition

4.16
), respectively. Their theoretical counterparts are

\displaystyle L(w)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\log p(y|x,w)q(y|x)q(x)\,,

(63)

\displaystyle L^{(\chi)}(w)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma^{l}}\log p(y|x,w)q^{(\chi)}(y|x)q(x)\,.

(64)

We first enumerate the hypotheses we require on the model
p(y|x,w)
for our main theorem. If
W^{\prime}\subseteq W
is compact then define

F(x,y)=\sup_{w\in W^{\prime}}\|\nabla_{w}\log p(y|x,w)\|_{2}<\infty

for each
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
which is integrable as
\Sigma^{k}
and
\Sigma^{l}
are finite sets. Then the family

\mathcal{F}=\{-\nabla_{w}\log p(y|x,w)\,|\,w\in W^{\prime}\}

of functions on
x\in\Sigma^{k}
,
y\in\Sigma^{l}
has a integrable envelope function
F(x,y)
:
\|-\nabla_{w}\log p(y|x,w)\|_{2}<F(x,y)
for all
x\in\Sigma^{k}
,
y\in\Sigma^{l}
, and
w\in W^{\prime}
.
Then it follows from
Vaart (1998, §19.2)
that the family
\mathcal{F}
is Glivenko-Cantelli, which, by definition, says that

\sup_{w\in W^{\prime}}\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L(w)\|_{2}\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0\,,\text{\, as $m\to\infty$}.

(65)

We further assume that
p(y|x,w)
is bounded away from zero for all
x\in\Sigma^{k}
,
y\in\Sigma^{l}
, and
w\in W^{\prime}
by a fixed constant. Then it follows that the family

\mathcal{G}=\{-\log p(y|x,w)\,|\,w\in W^{\prime}\}

of functions on
x\in\Sigma^{k}
and
y\in\Sigma^{l}
is has a constant envelope function: there exists
G\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that
|-\log p(y|x,w)|<G
for all
x\in\Sigma^{k}
,
y\in\Sigma^{l}
, and
w\in W^{\prime}
. Then it follows from
Vaart (1998, §19.2)
that the family
\mathcal{G}
is Glivenko-Cantelli, which, by definition, says that

\sup_{w\in W^{\prime}}|L_{n}(w)-L(w)|\overset{P}{\longrightarrow}0\,,\text{\, as $n\to\infty$}.

(66)

Lemma 7.1
.

Suppose that the model is gradient-insensitive in
W^{\prime}
to the difference between
q
and
q^{(\chi)}
for some positive constant
A
. Then for any
\xi
,
\delta>0
, there exists a large enough
m
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<A+\xi\bigg)>1-\delta\,,

(67)

for all
w\in W^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L(w)\big\|_{2}+\big\|\nabla_{w}L(w)-\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\big\|\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}\,.

So

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<A+\xi\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L(w)\big\|_{2}+\big\|\nabla_{w}L(w)-\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\big\|\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<A+\xi\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L(w)\big\|_{2}<\frac{\xi}{2}\bigg)+\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L(w)-\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<A\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<\frac{\xi}{2}\bigg)+1-3\,.

Since
\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)\to L(w)
and
\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\to L^{(\chi)}(w)
uniformly as
m\to\infty
, for
\frac{\delta}{3}
and
\frac{\xi}{2}
, there exists large enough
m
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L(w)\big\|_{2}<\frac{\xi}{2}\bigg)>1-\frac{\delta}{3}\,,

and

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<\frac{\xi}{2}\bigg)>1-\frac{\delta}{3}\,.

By the hypothesis of gradient-insensitivity we have that

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla_{w}L(w)-\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{k}}\sum_{y\in\Sigma}\big[q^{(\chi)}(x,y)-q(x,y)\big]\nabla_{w}\log p(y|x,w)\bigg\|_{2}

is less than
A
. In particular,

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L(w)-\nabla_{w}L^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<A\bigg)=1>1-\frac{\delta}{3}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\big\|\nabla_{w}L_{m}(w)-\nabla_{w}L_{m}^{(\chi)}(w)\big\|_{2}<A+\xi\bigg)

\displaystyle>3\bigg(1-\frac{\delta}{3}\bigg)+1-3

\displaystyle=1-\delta

as claimed.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

The need to potentially take
m
large in the lemma is a weakness in our technical treatment. The idea of SGLD is that
m\ll n
but since we average over many SGLD steps the effect of the mini-batch gradients approaches the effect of Langevin dynamics with
\nabla_{w}L_{n}
.

As above
T
is the length of the SGLD chains,
\epsilon_{\max}=\max\{\epsilon_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
,
\epsilon_{\min}=\min\{\epsilon_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
are the maximum and minimum step-sizes, and

Let us now study the hypotheses and the bound (we set

\displaystyle p(x|w)

\displaystyle=p(x_{>k_{1}}|x_{1:k_{1}},w)p(x_{1:k_{1}}|w)

\displaystyle=p(x_{>k_{1}}|x_{1:k_{1}},w)p(x_{k_{2}+1:k_{1}}|x_{1:k_{2}},w)p(x_{1:k_{2}}|w)

That is, we can think of a transformer parameter

8
Discussion

In the previous section we showed, under some technical assumptions, that the geometry of the functions
L,L^{(\chi)}
cannot be distinguished by LLC estimation (Theorem
7.4
). In this sense
L^{(\chi)}
acts as an
effective potential
. In this section we make some general remarks on what this means for the practice of studying language models like transformers using LLC estimates.

Local Minima.

The technical conditions for LLC estimation
(
Lau et al., 2024
)
include that
w^{*}
is a local minima of the population loss
L
. However do not expect this to be typically the case for network parameters
w^{*}
that we wish to study in deep learning; in particular, it is unlikely to be true for language models like those studied in
Hoogland et al. (2024)
,
Wang et al. (2024)
. As observed in
(
Hoogland et al., 2024
, §A.6)
this makes it quite surprising that LLC estimation works at all; it was speculated there that “the LLC estimation procedure is effectively giving us the LLC of a different potential to the population loss – a potential for which the current parameter actually is at a critical point.” We do not completely resolve this point in the present paper, but if
L^{(\chi)}
represents the loss on all the modes that the model has learned, it seems certainly
more
reasonable that a parameter
w^{*}
found during training may be a local minima for this potential rather than
L
.

Inverse Temperature versus Mode Cutoff.

Suppose we have chosen values for all these hyperparameters, including
A,B
such that the hypotheses of Theorem
7.4
are satisfied and give a non-trivial bound on the difference between
\hat{\lambda}_{n}({w^{*}})
and
\hat{\lambda}_{n}^{(\chi)}({w^{*}})
. Suppose that this bound is small enough that it means “in practice” we can’t distinguish the LLC of
q
and
q^{(\chi)}
with the resolution of our probing of the posterior via SGLD. Note that the inverse temperature
\beta
is a common factor in the bound (
75
). This means that if we increase
\beta
but decrease
A,B
to match, we can maintain the same level of in-distinguishability. Decreasing
A,B
means intuitively that we need to shift the mode cutoff
\chi
higher, so that the insensitivity conditions become easier to satisfy (as
q-q^{(\chi)}
is smaller). Similarly if we decrease
\beta
then
A,B
can be allowed to increase, which relaxes the constraints and allows the cutoff to shift down (that is, our estimates capture coarser modes).

In practice what this means is that the inverse temperature at which we attempt to sample from the posterior with SGLD plays an important role in controlling the level of resolution in the geometry that we measure; here
L^{(\chi)}
is thought of as a higher resolution approximation of
L
as
\chi
increases. For example, the LLC estimate will count a contribution to the complexity of the model of rarer bigrams as
\beta
is increased since their singular value is the square root of their probability.

In the empirical literature on LLC estimation for transformer neural networks
(
Hoogland et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2024
;
Urdshals & Urdshals, 2025
;
Carroll et al., 2025
)
the effect of variations in
\beta
has not yet been well-studied, beyond hyperparameter selection. The potential importance of variations of inverse temperature for neural network interpretability have been emphasised by
Vaintrob (2025)
. More generally, we can think of variations in
\beta
as one of a number of perturbations we can make to the (tempered) posterior distribution viewed as an object of statistical mechanics. We can also perform variations in the data distribution itself. We read the main theorem of this paper as suggesting that such variations affect the LLC estimate
through the effect of these variations on individual modes
. The study of changes in LLC estimates in response to such variations as a foundation for interpretability is studied under the name
susceptibilities
in upcoming work by
Baker et al. (2025)
.

9
Related Works

Distributional structure and deep learning.

The study of distributional structure in language has a rich history in linguistics and computational linguistics.
Harris (1954)
pioneered the idea that the meaning of words could be inferred from their distribution in text, laying the groundwork for modern approaches to semantic analysis. This distributional hypothesis was further developed by
Firth (1957)
. In recent years, the advent of neural network-based approaches has revolutionised how we capture and utilize distributional information in language.
Bengio et al. (2003)
introduced neural probabilistic language models, which implicitly learn distributional representations of words. This work paved the way for more sophisticated models that explicitly aim to capture distributional semantics
(
Mikolov et al., 2013b
;
Pennington et al., 2014
)
.

Tensor decomposition in NLP.

In the context of natural language, tensor decompositions have been widely used both theoretically and for practical applications. For our purposes the canonical references are
Anandkumar et al. (2014)
and
Hsu et al. (2012)
.

Dimensionality Reduction for Markov Models.

Traditional approaches to approximating or coarse-graining Markov chains have utilized spectral methods to identify essential dynamics
(
Deuflhard & Weber, 2005
;
Deuflhard et al., 2000
;
Fritzsche et al., 2007
)
.
Geiger et al. (2014)
frames state aggregation as an information bottleneck problem, seeking to compress the current state while preserving information about future trajectories. There is an extensive literature on spectral methods for “compressing” Markov processes using tensor decompositions
(
Zhang & Wang, 2019
;
Ohta, 2021
;
Navarro et al., 2024
)
with the underlying hypothesis being that complex systems can often be lower rank than they appear
(
Thibeault et al., 2024
)
. The relation of these ideas to renormalisation group theory has also been recognised
(
Orioli & Faccioli, 2016
)
.

Tensor Renormalisation Group.

Our approach to mode truncation in sequence distributions shares conceptual foundations with tensor renormalisation group (TRG) methods in statistical physics
(
Levin & Nave, 2007
)
. TRG provides a framework for coarse-graining tensor networks by iteratively replacing tensors with their truncated SVDs. The truncation procedure defined in Definition
4.16
, where we construct
q^{(\chi)}(y|x)
by retaining only modes
\alpha\leq\chi
, directly corresponds to the truncated SVD operation central to TRG. In both cases, truncation serves to control computational complexity while preserving dominant patterns. Notably, research has shown that the “brute force” truncation of singular values in the original TRG approach, while effective, is not optimal for capturing the relevant physics near critical points
(
Evenbly & Vidal, 2015
)
. Some ideas from the TRG literature (such as hierarchical truncation) may inspire useful approaches for improved LLC estimation techniques.

Effective Actions in MCMC.

In lattice field theory, it is well-established that Langevin dynamics with finite step size
\varepsilon
samples from a modified distribution rather than the target distribution
(
Batrouni et al., 1985
)
. Specifically, when attempting to sample from a distribution
P[\phi]\propto e^{-S[\phi]}
using the update rule:

\Delta\phi(x)=-\varepsilon\frac{\delta S[\phi]}{\delta\phi(x)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\eta(x)

the algorithm actually samples from a distribution with an
effective action

S_{\text{eff}}[\phi]
which is analagous to our effective potential. The techniques used there suggest trying to approach the effective distribution
q^{(\chi)}
directly through analysis of the Fokker-Planck equation associated with SGLD. Techniques from physics for managing effective action corrections might enable more accurate LLC estimation across a broader spectrum of modes.

This connection also reframes our view of the “effective theory” presented in this paper. Rather than seeing mode truncation as an approximation, we can understand it as identifying the appropriate level of description given the computational procedure used for LLC estimation. This is analogous to how renormalisation group methods identify effective field theories valid at specific length scales
(
Polchinski, 1984
;
Efrati et al., 2014
)
.

10
Conclusion

We have shown that the geometry probed using SGLD for LLC estimation in sequence models is, in practice, the geometry of a
coarse-grained
or
effective
distribution obtained by truncating higher-order modes of the fundamental tensors. More precisely, using a mode decomposition based on singular-value analysis we proved that, under conditions of gradient- and log-probability-insensitivity, SGLD-based LLC estimates cannot distinguish the true potential
L
from the truncated potential
L^{(\chi)}
.

This perspective may help clarify longstanding puzzles about why LLC estimates appear stable even when the measured parameter is not a strict minimum of the population loss: the estimator may be effectively sampling a potential where the learned modes are stationary. The perspective of modes introduced here, and the relation of modes to LLC estimation, is also fundamental in our approach to neural network interpretability based on SLT
(
Baker et al., 2025
)
.

Future work should explore alternative tensor factorizations (such as CP, Tucker and tensor-train) to capture more complex hierarchies of modes, tighten quantitative bounds on the insensitivity constants for modern transformer architectures and develop a more sophisticated treatment of the main theorems that allows keeping
m
small, which is more consistent with how SGLD is used in practice.

This appendix describes the methodology used to analyse SVD of conditional probability matrices.

We used the Pile dataset

•

For

For

2.

For each document, we tokenised the text and extracted:

•

•

sequences: consecutive token sequences of length

that immediately follow an X sequence

•

3.

We filtered

4.

Similarly, we filtered

Next we construct a sparse matrix

Appendix D
Higher-Order Tensors

In the main text, we used SVD to provide a factorization of the fundamental tensor. For
k>2
, there is no canonical factorization of
A_{k}
and the choice of factorisation becomes an additional consideration. Natural candidates include the CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition
Harshman (1970)
;
Carroll & Chang (1970)
, the Tucker decomposition
Tucker (1966)
, and matrix-product states (MPS) or tensor-trains
Oseledets (2011)
. For more background see
(
Kolda & Bader, 2009
)
.

Even once a particular kind of decomposition is chosen, there are still additional choices that can be made, which affect the ultimate basis of function space that we end up with. We focus on the Tucker decomposition of
A_{k}\in(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes k}
. Set
V=\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma}
and write
V_{i}
for labelled copies of
V
so

(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes k}=V_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{k}\,.

We now allow for rearrangement and grouping of these tensor factors. More precisely we assume chosen a partition
P_{1},\ldots,P_{r}
of
\{1,\ldots,k\}
and set
V^{\prime}_{j}=\bigotimes_{i\in P_{j}}V_{i}
so there is a canonical isomorphism

(\mathbb{R}^{\Sigma})^{\otimes k}\cong V^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V^{\prime}_{r}\,.

(80)

Under this isomorphism we view
A_{k}
as a tensor in
\bigotimes_{j}V^{\prime}_{j}
. Using the word basis to identify vector spaces with their duals, we consider for each
1\leq j\leq r
the isomorphism

V^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V^{\prime}_{r}\cong\Big[\bigotimes_{k\neq j}V^{\prime}_{k}\Big]^{*}\otimes V^{\prime}_{j}\,.

As an element of the right hand side
A_{k}
determines a linear transformation with codomain
V^{\prime}_{j}
and we let
\{v^{(j)}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda_{j}}
be an orthonormal basis for
V^{\prime}_{j}
consisting of the left singular vectors of an SVD decomposition of this transformation. The tensor products of these bases over
j
give an orthonormal basis of
V^{\prime}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes V^{\prime}_{r}
with respect to which we can write, as elements of
\bigotimes_{j}V^{\prime}_{j}
,

A_{k}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\lambda_{\alpha}v^{(1)}_{\alpha_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes v^{(r)}_{\alpha_{r}}

(81)

where
\Lambda=\Lambda_{1}\times\cdots\times\Lambda_{r}
and
\lambda_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
. This is called the
Tucker decomposition
with respect to the grouping (
80
). Usually the Tucker decomposition is presented using the
core tensor

G=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\lambda_{\alpha}\alpha_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\alpha_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_{r}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda}

(82)

which can be contracted against tensors
U^{(j)}=\sum_{\alpha_{j}}\alpha_{j}^{*}\otimes v^{(j)}_{\alpha_{j}}
to produce
A_{k}
. The idea is that if the core tensor
G
is much lower rank than
A_{k}
, then the Tucker decomposition has succeeded in representing that tensor in a compressed form; moreover, since the sets
\Lambda_{j}
can be ordered by the corresponding singular values, by truncating the sum in (
82
) we obtain a natural way of discarding “higher order” information in
A_{k}
.

These more general tensor decompositions provide a “drop in” replacement for the orthonormal bases produced by SVD in the main text. Additionally, we can use
multiple
different tensor decompositions for
A_{k}
and use the union of the orthonormal bases thus obtained; in this way we get an overcomplete basis rather than a standard basis. Ultimately this may be the more appropriate way of thinking about modes in models like transformers, which are likely to learn modes for multiple distinct factorisations of the fundamental tensor.
