Collective Recourse for Generative Urban Visualizations
Rashid Mushkani

Abstract
Text-to-image diffusion models help visualize urban futures but can amplify group-level harms. We propose collective recourse : structured community “visual bug reports” that trigger fixes to models and planning workflows. We (1) formalize collective recourse and a practical pipeline (report, triage, fix, verify, closure); (2) situate four recourse primitives—counter-prompts, negative prompts, dataset edits, reward-model tweaks—within the diffusion stack; (3) define mandate thresholds via a mandate score combining severity, volume saturation, representativeness, and evidence; and (4) evaluate a synthetic program of 240 reports. Prompt-level fixes were fastest (median 2.1 2.1 – 3.4 3.4 days) but less durable (21–38% recurrence); dataset edits and reward tweaks were slower (13.5 and 21.9 days) yet more durable (12–18% recurrence) with higher planner uptake (30–36%). A threshold of 0.12 0.12 yielded 93% precision and 75% recall; increasing representativeness raised recall to 81% with little precision loss. We discuss integration with participatory governance, risks (e.g., overfitting to vocal groups), and safeguards (dashboards, rotating juries).



Keywords:
collective recourse, generative models, diffusion models, urban visualization, participatory governance, evaluation, civic technology

1
Introduction

Text-to-image diffusion models
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Rombach et al. 2022
)
are entering planning practice to render streetscape alternatives, infill options, and design guidelines. In this study, we focus on Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL) because it is open source; however, other image generators are also used in this domain. Emerging work in urban informatics and design indicates both promise and fragility
(
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
. Beyond aesthetic plausibility, audits of image generators report systematic social biases: demographic stereotyping and omission
(
Luccioni et al. 2023
)
, amplified occupational gender biases
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, prompt-surface vulnerabilities
(
Struppek et al. 2023
)
, and, in large-scale evaluations, toxic or racialized outputs
(
Schneider and Hagendorff 2025
)
. These issues are often linked to training data composition and uneven coverage of everyday civic life
(
Birhane et al. 2021
)
. In civic contexts, such distortions matter because renderings can influence deliberation when used as decision inputs.

Algorithmic
recourse
offers those affected by a model pathways to change outcomes, originally for classification
(
Üstün et al. 2019
;
Karimi et al. 2022
)
. Counterfactual explanations propose minimally altered alternatives
(
Wachter et al. 2018
)
, with later methods emphasizing diversity and feasibility
(
Mothilal et al. 2020
;
Poyiadzi et al. 2020
)
. Importantly, recourse fairness can diverge from prediction fairness
(
von Kügelgen et al. 2022
)
. In generative urban imagery, harms are typically
group-level
and recurring across prompts and contexts, suggesting a shift from individual to
collective
recourse tied to model lifecycle and municipal governance
(
Mushkani et al. 2025b
)
.

We propose
collective recourse
for generative urban visualizations: organized community inputs become structured
visual bug reports
that, when meeting clear thresholds, trigger specific fixes in the model stack and propagate into planning artifacts. Our design draws on traditions of participatory planning
(
Arnstein 1969
;
Healey 1997
;
Sieber 2006
)
, taxonomies of participatory power
(
Fung 2006
)
, and empirical studies of user-driven audits
(
Li et al. 2023
;
DeVrio et al. 2024
;
Mushkani et al. 2025a
)
. Rather than treating transparency as an end in itself
(
Ananny and Crawford 2018
)
, we specify a pipeline with service-level targets, subgroup verification, and independent citizen review.

Research questions.

We study two questions that bridge technical remedies and institutional mechanisms.
RQ1
: Which
recourse primitives
—counter-prompts, negative prompts, dataset edits, or reward-model tweaks—most quickly reduce
group-level visual harms
while limiting recurrence? This extends counterfactual insights
(
Wachter et al. 2018
;
Mothilal et al. 2020
;
Poyiadzi et al. 2020
)
to generative settings and engages recent work on aligning diffusion models with preferences
(
Christiano et al. 2017
;
Wallace et al. 2024
)
.
RQ2
: What
collective thresholds
constitute a
mandate
for mitigation, and how do representativeness and evidence quality trade off with speed and precision, given equity concerns in access to recourse
(
von Kügelgen et al. 2022
)
and institutionalizing participatory authority
(
Fung 2006
)
?

Contributions.

We: (1) formalize collective recourse for generative imagery and specify a practical pipeline from reporting to triage, fix selection, verification, and closure; (2) introduce a typology of four recourse primitives in the diffusion stack and discuss their mechanisms and limits; (3) define mandate thresholds that translate collective signals into enforceable actions with transparent precision–recall trade-offs; and (4) conduct a simulation-based evaluation on a synthetic program of 240 community reports, quantifying mitigation latency, recurrence, subgroup satisfaction, and downstream policy uptake, and comparing our approach to red-teaming and third-party audits
(
Ganguli et al. 2022
;
Longpre et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2023
;
Raji et al. 2022
;
Raji et al. 2020
)
.

2
Background and related work

2.1
Generative models in planning and their biases

Diffusion models
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Rombach et al. 2022
)
are now common tools for depicting alternatives. Surveys document their rise in urban design and digital twins
(
Jiang et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
)
, including street-level and satellite synthesis. Audits report persistent social biases: under-representation and stereotyping, skewed cultural markers, and occupational gender bias
(
Luccioni et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Prompt-surface vulnerabilities can elicit hidden behaviors
(
Struppek et al. 2023
)
, and evaluations note toxicity and racialized portrayals in some outputs
(
Schneider and Hagendorff 2025
)
. Training data analyses implicate multimodal corpora with problematic content and uneven coverage
(
Birhane et al. 2021
)
. Because civic renderings shape perceptions of safety, desirability, and legitimacy, these risks are salient for planning, where perception datasets such as Place Pulse have long probed visual judgments
(
Naik et al. 2014
;
Salesses et al. 2013
;
Mushkani et al. 2025c
;
Mushkani and Koseki 2025a
)
.

2.2
From individual recourse to collective recourse

Recourse research originally addressed individuals facing adverse automated decisions
(
Üstün et al. 2019
;
Karimi et al. 2022
)
. Counterfactual explanations communicate “what to change”
(
Wachter et al. 2018
)
, with later work prioritizing diverse and feasible options
(
Mothilal et al. 2020
;
Poyiadzi et al. 2020
)
. A purely individual framing does not capture
recurring, group-level
harms in generative systems, and equitable access to remedies is itself a fairness concern
(
von Kügelgen et al. 2022
)
. We extend this line by defining collective recourse: groups file structured visual bug reports; institutions adopt thresholds that
trigger
mitigations; and changes are verified on subgroup test sets and reviewed by citizen panels.

2.3
Documentation, governance, and the limits of transparency

Model Cards and Data Statements document scope and limitations
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
;
Bender and Friedman 2018
)
; Datasheets formalize dataset documentation
(
Gebru et al. 2021
)
. Our pipeline operationalizes these artifacts: dataset edits update datasheets; model-level changes update model cards; closures are logged publicly. We also adopt an end-to-end audit mindset
(
Raji et al. 2020
)
: structured intake, triage, verification, and independent review. Empirical work suggests that participatory audits can surface issues that experts miss and can precipitate institutional change
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Howell et al. 2024
;
DeVrio et al. 2024
)
. Our rotating citizen juries and mandate thresholds are informed by these lessons and by democratic design frameworks that specify
who
participates,
how
, and with
what authority

(
Fung 2006
)
.

2.4
Red-teaming and third-party oversight

Red-teaming formalizes adversarial testing for generative models
(
Ganguli et al. 2022
)
. Assessments identify blind spots (e.g., limited context coverage and weak ties to action)
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
. Safe-harbor provisions aim to broaden participation and protect testers
(
Longpre et al. 2023
)
. Outsider oversight and third-party audits help provide structural accountability
(
Raji et al. 2022
)
. We position collective recourse as complementary: community members act as a distributed red team through binding bug reports with clear escalation routes and institutional links.

Let a

b

group

(e.g., stratified alignment with population marginals), and

(

)
and aligns with participatory governance principles that link authority to participation
(

)
.

We define a

M(b)\;=\;s\cdot\underbrace{\bigl(1-\exp(-n/\tau_{n})\bigr)}_{\text{volume saturation}}\cdot r\cdot q,

(1)

3.1
Recourse primitives in the diffusion stack

We consider four primitives that operate at distinct layers and exhibit different speed–durability profiles.
Counter-prompts
add protective, context-aware language (e.g., “include universal design features; retain vernacular signage”), offering low-latency responses aligned with counterfactual reasoning
(
Wachter et al. 2018
)
.
Negative prompts
exclude unwanted motifs; their mechanics and limits are increasingly characterized
(
Ban et al. 2024
)
.
Dataset edits
curate and augment underrepresented scenes, objects, or communities; they are documented via datasheets and generally produce more durable changes
(
Bender and Friedman 2018
;
Gebru et al. 2021
;
Mushkani et al. 2025f
)
.
Reward-model tweaks
update preference or reward models to steer generation at sampling time, analogous to RLHF for language
(
Christiano et al. 2017
)
; direct preference optimization for diffusion models offers promising alignment leverage
(
Wallace et al. 2024
)
. Because prompt surfaces can be exploited
(
Struppek et al. 2023
)
, we treat prompt-level fixes as necessary first responses rather than complete solutions; structural fixes are prioritized when harms recur.

3.2
Verification on subgroup test sets

For each harm-type
\times
context, we maintain a
subgroup visual eval set
(SVES) of templated prompts and reference imagery, seeded by urban-perception cues
(
Mushkani and Koseki 2025b
)
. Verification re-generates the SVES pre- and post-mitigation and tests recurrence against harm-specific tolerances. Because community participation is central to surfacing harms
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Howell et al. 2024
)
, we encourage
safe harbor
reporting
(
Longpre et al. 2023
)
and publish aggregate dashboards with stratified breakdowns while suppressing small cells.

Coordinated advocacy channel with enforcement.

A dedicated intake and triage team (city, vendor, and community liaisons) publishes service-level targets by severity. Visual bug reports are clustered by harm type and context, then routed to candidate fixes. Mandate thresholds (Eq.
1
) transform collective signals into commitments. Each closure updates public-facing documentation: model cards for model-level changes; datasheets for data-level changes
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
;
Gebru et al. 2021
)
. We integrate these artifacts with planning workflows by linking verified fixes to design guides, procurement language, and participatory budgeting criteria, aligning governance with model improvement rather than isolating it as a separate audit silo
(
Raji et al. 2020
)
.

Citizen review and division of labor.

We convene rotating mini-publics (citizen juries) to review closures, adjudicate disputed cases, and audit representativeness. Evidence suggests that informal, qualitative audits by non-experts can surface issues that experts miss
(
Howell et al. 2024
)
and that user-driven investigations can catalyze organizational change
(
Li et al. 2023
;
DeVrio et al. 2024
)
. The jury model clarifies roles: communities
protect
by flagging harms; auditors and vendors
strengthen
by verifying fixes and updating documentation; administrators
pressure
by enforcing mandates—consistent with taxonomies of participatory power
(
Mushkani et al. 2025d
)
.

5.3
Comparison to red-teaming and third-party audits

Our pipeline can be viewed as an institutionalized, user-driven red team with explicit closure criteria. Traditional red-teaming is valuable for stress-testing
(
Ganguli et al. 2022
)
but can struggle to connect findings to persistent, context-specific harms and to formal authority for remedies
(
Ji et al. 2023
)
. Safe-harbor provisions
(
Longpre et al. 2023
)
and outsider oversight
(
Raji et al. 2022
)
broaden participation and sustain pressure. We embed these ideas into municipal workflows with mandate thresholds, subgroup verification, and public dashboards.

6
Methods in practice: from bug reports to planning

6.1
Report schema, triage, and fix selection

Each report captures prompts, generated images, context, harm type, a severity rubric, optional reporter demographics, substantiating evidence, and a proposed remedy. Triage proceeds in two passes. First, we detect cross-report patterns by clustering on context and harm type to identify candidates for structural fixes when harms recur. Second, we test prompt steerability on the SVES; if targeted counter- or negative prompts reliably reduce recurrence, we deploy them as first responses. Persistent omissions or misrepresentations traceable to data coverage trigger dataset curation and augmentation documented via datasheets
(
Gebru et al. 2021
)
. If misalignment remains despite adequate coverage, we update reward models, drawing on diffusion-specific alignment techniques such as direct preference optimization
(
Wallace et al. 2024
)
in addition to RLHF-style objectives
(
Christiano et al. 2017
)
. Model cards record scope and limitations
(
Mitchell et al. 2019
)
, and every change is visible on a public dashboard.

6.2
Verification, closure, and planning linkages

Verification re-runs the SVES for the affected subgroup; closure requires recurrence below a harm-specific tolerance, subgroup sign-off above a satisfaction threshold, and citizen-jury approval. Verified fixes propagate to design guides, RFQ language, and participatory budgeting criteria so that technical corrections are reflected in institutional artifacts. Public dashboards log case history, thresholds, decisions, and documentation updates, operationalizing an end-to-end accountability loop
(
Raji et al. 2020
)
. We view red-teaming signals and external audits as complementary inputs rather than stand-alone solutions
(
Longpre et al. 2023
;
Ji et al. 2023
;
Raji et al. 2022
)
.

7
Discussion

Answer to RQ1.

Prompt-level fixes act as
first responses
: fast, easy to operationalize in workshops, and amenable to templating
(
Mushkani et al. 2025e
)
. They are also more prone to recurrence and surface-level attacks
(
Struppek et al. 2023
)
. Structural fixes—dataset edits and reward-model alignment—are slower but appear more durable and easier to encode in institutional artifacts. Preference-optimization approaches for diffusion models
(
Wallace et al. 2024
)
may help correct subtle omissions that are hard to guardrail lexically.

Answer to RQ2.

Mandate thresholds translate collective signals into enforceable action and provide clear levers for governance. In our simulation,
\tau_{M}{=}0.12
balances urgency (75% recall) and rigor (93% precision);
\tau_{M}{=}0.16
suits resource-constrained settings prioritizing precision. Modestly increasing representativeness through rotating juries improves recall with limited precision loss, echoing concerns about equitable access to remedies
(
von Kügelgen et al. 2022
)
and empowered participation
(
Fung 2006
)
.

Risks, mitigations, and limits.

Risks include
overfitting to vocal groups
(mitigated by the representativeness index
r
, jury rotation, and stratified recruitment),
gaming and astroturfing
(addressed via rate-limiting, deduplication, and evidence-quality checks; safe harbor policies protect good-faith reporters
(
Longpre et al. 2023
)
), and the
transparency trap
where dashboards substitute for action
(
Ananny and Crawford 2018
)
. Our pipeline incorporates transparency alongside thresholds, verification, and citizen review. We also separate exploratory ideation from evidentiary renderings to avoid misleading inputs to deliberation.

Integration with municipal workflows.

Cities can embed collective recourse in existing engagement portals; link verified fixes to design guideline updates and RFQ evaluation criteria; and publish rolling dashboards with subgroup SLAs. In doing so, they align technical governance with collaborative planning traditions
(
Healey 1997
)
and with calls for structured outsider oversight
(
Raji et al. 2022
)
.

8
Limitations

Our evaluation uses a simulation to illustrate design trade-offs; effect sizes will vary by toolchain, data provenance, and civic capacity. While SVES verification draws inspiration from perception datasets
(
Naik et al. 2014
;
Salesses et al. 2013
)
, constructing robust subgroup test sets for each harm type and context remains a community-intensive endeavor. Future work will field-test the pipeline with partners; build shared SVES resources seeded with urban-perception cues; compare diffusion-specific preference optimization against data curation at scale
(
Wallace et al. 2024
)
; and study the labor and equity costs of citizen review, building on user-driven audits
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Howell et al. 2024
;
DeVrio et al. 2024
)
. We also plan to examine legal and organizational enablers for safe participation
(
Longpre et al. 2023
;
Mushkani 2025
)
and to evaluate dashboards as boundary objects for third-party auditors
(
Raji et al. 2022
)
.

9
Conclusion

Collective recourse reframes who gets to correct generative urban visualization systems and
how
those corrections propagate into both models and planning practice. Our pipeline makes harms reportable, fixes auditable, and closures accountable. Simulations suggest a division of labor: prompt-level interventions for speed; data- and reward-level changes for durability and policy impact. Mandate thresholds translate collective signals into enforceable action, especially when combined with rotating citizen juries. Situating this within established participatory planning traditions and contemporary audit practices may help cities realize the benefits of generative tools while reducing group-level harms.
