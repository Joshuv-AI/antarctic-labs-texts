Rates of convergence in the free multiplicative Central Limit Theorem Thanks: …
Marwa Banna, Nicolas Gilliers, Pei-Lun Tseng

Abstract
. We provide the first quantitative estimates for the rate of convergence in the free multiplicative central limit theorem (CLT), in terms of the Kolmogorov and r r -Wasserstein distances for r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 . While the free additive CLT has been thoroughly studied, including convergence rates, the multiplicative setting remained open in this regard. We consider products of the form π n g , n − 1 / 2 x := g ( x 1 n ) ⋯ g ( x n n ) , \pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}:=g\left(\frac{x_{1}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\cdots g\left(\frac{x_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right), where x 1 , … , x n x_{1},\dots,x_{n} are freely independent self-adjoint centered operators with common variance σ 2 \sigma^{2} and g : ℝ → ℂ g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C} satisfies certain regularity and integrability conditions. We quantify the deviation of the singular value distribution of π n g , x \pi_{n}^{g,x} from the free positive semicircular law, with bounds depending only on the moments of the underlying variables. Additionally, we present a combinatorial proof of the free multiplicative CLT that extends to the unbounded setting.

Introduction and main results

This work investigates the rate of convergence in the free multiplicative central limit theorem (CLT) in terms of the Kolmogorov and
r
-Wasserstein distances, for
r\geq 1
. While the rate of convergence for the central limit theorem in the context of free additive convolution has been studied by

,

in a tracial probability space

and let

from the free multiplicative semicircular law when

]
paved the way to limit theorems for products of free random variables. Subsequently, in
[

The explicit description of the limiting distribution, known as the free multiplicative semicircular law, for products of positive freely independent and identically distributed random variables, was provided by

This paper aims to provide the first quantitative estimates for the free multiplicative CLT in the general setting described in (

1.2.
Idea of the proof and outline of key results

We begin by outlining the key steps leading to the desired estimates. For the case of the
1
-Wasserstein distance, we make use of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality formula. Since no analogous duality holds for
r>1
, we instead rely on Rio’s inequality (
6
), which bounds the
r
-Wasserstein distance in terms of the
r
-th root of the
r
-Zolotarev distance. The definitions of these distances and the relationships between them are provided below in Section
1.3
.

While obtaining bounds on Wasserstein distances is generally challenging, working with the Zolotarev distance offers a useful alternative. By maximizing the relevant quantities over the class of Zolotarev functions, we gain access to a variety of analytic tools that enable us to derive the desired quantitative estimates. For a function
f
belonging to the Zolotarev class, we provide a quantitative bound for

\left|\varphi\big(f(|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}|)\big)-\varphi\big(f(e^{y})\big)\right|,

where
y
is the
\log
of a free multiplicative semicircular element. The above bound follows from the infinite divisibility of the free multiplicative semicircular distribution together with the following invariance principle:

(4)

\displaystyle\left|\varphi\big(f(|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}|)\big)-\varphi\big(f(|\pi_{n}^{h,n^{-1/2}y}|)\big)\right|,

where the function
h
and the sequence
y=(y_{i})_{i\leq n}
satisfy the same assumptions in Theorem
1.1
.

Our approach, which we refer to as the Fourier–Lindeberg method, involves regularizing Zolotarev functions to make them amenable to a Lindeberg-type replacement scheme. However, the inherent nonlinearity of the multiplicative setting presents significant challenges. To address this, we rely on the Hermitization trick, which helps linearize the problem to some extent. The price to pay is to prove an invariance principle within the framework of the noncommutative probability space
(M_{2}(\mathcal{A}),\mathrm{tr}_{2}\varphi)
of
2\times 2
matrices over
\mathcal{A}
. This is carried out in Section
2.1
. The final step is to estimate the divergence of the moments of the Fourier transform of kernel regularization of Zolotarev functions as illustrated in Lemmas
2.6
and
2.7
.

The Lindeberg method has proven to be a powerful technique for obtaining quantitative bounds in limit theorems. It has already been successfully applied to establish the only known quantitative estimates for the operator-valued free, Boolean, and monotone additive central limit theorems in terms of the Lévy distance, as shown in
[
ABT22
,
BM23
]
. However, the arguments required in the multiplicative setting cannot be reduced to adaptations of those used for the additive case.

The remaining part of this section aims to review essential concepts on distances between probability measures, and aspects of free probability theory that are relevant to this paper.

1.3.
Distances on probability distributions

On the set of all Borel probability measures on the real line
\mathbb{R}
, there are several well-established and useful notions of distance. In this paper, we consider the Kolmogorov, Wasserstein, and Zolotarev distances, and discuss some of the relationships between them. For a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
its
cumulative distribution function
, defined by

\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(t):=\mu((-\infty,t])
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\mu
and
\nu
are two Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, the
Kolmogorov distance
by

(
K
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{\mu}(t)-F_{\nu}(t)\big|.

For any
r\geq 1
and any
\mu,\nu\in P_{r}(I)
, the set of probability measures on the interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with moments up to the order
r
, the
Wasserstein distance
of order
r
or the
r
-
Wasserstein distance
is defined by

(
W_{r}
)

W_{r}(\mu,\nu)=\inf\{\|X-Y\|_{L^{r}}\mid X\overset{d}{\sim}\mu,\text{ and }Y\overset{d}{\sim}\nu\}.

It is known that convergence in the
r
-Wasserstein distance is equivalent to convergence in distribution along with the convergence of moments up to order
r

[
V+08
, Theorem 6.9]
. If we assume that
\nu
has a Lebesgue density bounded by
C
, then by
[
Ros11
, Proposition 1.2]
, the Kolmogorov distance can be bounded by the Wasserstein distance as follows:

(5)

\mathcal{K}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sqrt{2CW_{1}(\mu,\nu)}.

Computing the
r
-Wasserstein distance is generally not an easy task; however, in the particular case
r=1
, and thanks to the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality formula, the
1
-Wasserstein distance can be reformulated as the solution to a maximization problem over
1
-Lipschitz functions. Specifically, it can be written as

(
W_{1}
)

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\mu(\mathrm{d}y)\colon f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\|f\|_{1,\mathbb{R}}\leq 1\Big\}.

It is important to emphasize that this duality does not hold when
r>1
. Nevertheless, the
r
-Wasserstein distances can still be bounded above in terms of the so-called Zolotarev distances, using Rio’s inequality (
6
) below.

In
[
Zol78
,
Zol81
]
, Zolotarev introduced a metric for analyzing the class of probability measures that possess finite moments of order
r
and have matching moments up to and including order
r-1
. Here, we adopt an alternative definition of the Zolotarev metric, which extends its applicability to probability measures with moments of order
r
that do not necessarily coincide.

Let
r>1
and write
r=\ell+\beta
with
\beta\in(0,1]
and
\ell
an integer (in particular, if
r
is an integer,
\ell=r-1
and
\beta=1
). Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval containing
0
. We denote by
\Lambda^{0}_{r}(I)
the set of all
\ell
times differentiable functions
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

f^{(i)}(0)=0,\penalty\ 0\leq i\leq\ell,\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f^{(\ell)}(x)-f^{(\ell)}(y)|}{|x-y|^{\beta}}<+\infty.

For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{r}(I)
, the
Zolotarev distance
is defined by

(
Z_{r}
)

Z_{r}(\mu,\nu):=\sup\{|\mu(f)-\nu(f)|,\,f\in\Lambda^{0}_{r}(I),\|f^{(\ell)}\|_{\beta,I}\leq 1\},

where we recall that
\|f^{(\ell)}\|_{\gamma,I}
denotes the Hölder semi-norm on the interval
I
with exponent
\gamma
.
We extend the definition of
\Lambda^{0}_{r}(I)
by setting
\Lambda^{0}_{1}(I)
as the class of Lipschitz functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(0)=0
. For more details on ideal metrics and Zolotarev metrics, we refer to
[
BDS17
,
Bog18
]
.

Having recalled the Zolotarev distance, we illustrate Rio’s inequality
[
Rio98
]
that provides an upper bound for the Wasserstein distances of order
r
in terms of the Zolotarev metric. Whenever
r>1
, Rio proved that there exists a positive constant
c_{r}
such that, for any pair
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}_{r}(I)
,

(6)

W_{r}(\mu,\nu)^{r}\leq c_{r}Z_{r}(\mu,\nu).

1.4.
Free multiplicative semicircular distribution

For an introduction to free probability theory, including noncommutative probability spaces, free independence, free cumulants, moment-cumulant formula, operator-valued free probability, and other fundamental results and tools, we refer the reader to the books
[
MS17
,
NS06a
]
. This section is devoted to the free multiplicative semicircular distribution, denoted by
\mu_{p.s.c}
. Bercovici and Voiculescu showed that
\mu_{\text{p.s.c}}
is infinitely divisible with respect to free multiplicative convolution and has compact support. In particular,
\mu_{\text{p.s.c}}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Moreover, by combining this result with
[
Zho14
, Theorem 5.7]
, it follows that the support of
\mu_{\text{p.s.c}}
is a closed and bounded interval in
\mathbb{R}
. Additionally, as noted in
[
BV92
, Section 7]
the
S
-transform of
\mu_{\text{p.s.c}}
is given by

S_{\mu_{p.s.c}}(z)=\exp\Big(-4\sigma^{2}\big(z+\frac{1}{2}\big)\Big).

Ho
[
Ho11
]
explicitly determined the limiting distribution in the free multiplicative central limit theorem and showed that the free multiplicative semicircular law
\mu_{\text{p.s.c}}
has a logarithm
y_{\text{l.s.c}}(\sigma)
, which is given by the free additive convolution of a semicircular distribution and one-half the uniform distribution on
[-1,1]
. More precisely,

y_{\text{l.s.c}}(\sigma)=2\sigma s+2\sigma^{2}w,

where
s
is a standard semicircular element,
w
is uniformly distributed on
(-1,1)
, and
s
and
w
are freely independent.
Moreover, Ho gave the explicit expressions of the moments, see
[
Ho11
, Eq.(41)]
. Furthermore, the free cumulants
\kappa_{n}^{\mu_{p.s.c}}
were explicitly computed in
[
AFU24
, Proposition A.1]

(7)

\kappa_{k}^{\mu_{p.s.c}}=\frac{k^{k-1}}{k!}(2\sigma)^{2(k-1)}e^{2k\sigma^{2}}\quad\text{ for each }\quad k\geq 1.

See
[
AFU24
,
BV92
,
Ho11
]
for more details about the material presented in this section.

2.1.

As outlined in Section

We extend

to a symmetric function on
\mathbb{R}

\tilde{f}

\tilde{f}
is an even function, it admits the representation

\tilde{f}(x)=\int_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\cos(ux)\mathcal{F}{\tilde{f}}({\rm d}u),

\mathcal{F}\tilde{f}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{f}(x)e^{{i}ux}{{\rm d}u}=2\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}{f}(x)\cos(ux){{\rm d}u},\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

Let

be two sequences of self-adjoint elements and

two smooth functions. We first observe that, by Lemma

\displaystyle\varphi\big(f(|\pi_{n}^{g,x}|)\big)-\varphi\big(f(|\pi_{n}^{h,y}|)\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\big(\varphi\big(\cos(u|\pi_{n}^{g,x}|)\big)\big)-\varphi\big(\cos(u|\pi_{n}^{h,y}|)\big)\mathcal{F}{\tilde{f}}({\rm d}u)

\displaystyle=\int_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\big({\rm tr}_{2}\varphi^{(2)}\big(e^{{\sf i}uH({{\pi^{g,x}_{n}}})}\big)-{\rm tr}_{2}\varphi^{(2)}\big(e^{{\sf i}uH({\pi^{h,y}_{n}})}\big)\big)\mathcal{F}{\tilde{f}}({\rm d}u),

where

and

({{\pi^{g,x}_{n}}})^{\star}&0\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad H({\pi^{h,y}_{n}})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\pi^{h,y}_{n}\\
(\pi^{h,y}_{n})^{\star}&0\end{bmatrix}.

We proceed by applying the Lindeberg method in

to estimate the term

\big|{\rm tr}_{2}\varphi^{(2)}\big(e^{{\sf i}uH({{\pi^{g,x}_{n}}})}\big)-{\rm tr}_{2}\varphi^{(2)}\big(e^{{\sf i}uH({\pi^{h,y}_{n}})}\big)\big|.

\displaystyle\leq(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{4}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{4}_{\varphi}})^{4}(O(n^{-2})+O(n^{-3/2})+O(n^{-3/2+\gamma/2}))

\displaystyle=(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{4}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{g,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{4}_{\varphi}})^{4}(O(n^{-3/2})).

After summing over
1\leq i\leq n
and using Lemma
2.6
with
k=2
, we obtain that the order two term contributing to (
30
) is
(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{4}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{g,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{4}_{\varphi}})^{4}O(n^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{-2}\zeta^{-2})
.
We finally bound the contribution to (
30
) of the order three term. By using Proposition
2.3
with the same parameters as for the first and second order terms, we obtain, under the assumption of Theorem
1.1
:

\displaystyle|{\rm tr}_{2}(\varphi^{(2)}(L_{i,3}))|\leq(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}})^{6}O(n^{-3/2}).

After summing over
1\leq i\leq n
and applying Lemma
2.6
with
k=3
, we obtain that the contribution of the third-order term to (
30
) is

(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}})^{6}O(n^{-1/2}\zeta^{-2}\varepsilon^{-2}).

Collecting the leading-order terms, we now seek the asymptotic behavior in
n
as
n\to\infty
,
with
\varepsilon=\zeta=n^{-a}
for some
a>0
, of:

\varepsilon+\zeta+n^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}\varepsilon}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta\varepsilon^{3}}.

We seek to solve either
-\frac{1}{2}+4a=-a
or
-\frac{\gamma}{2}+3a=-a
depending on the sign of
-\frac{1}{2}+4a-(-\frac{\gamma}{2}+3a)
.
This last expression is negative when
\gamma\leq 1-2a
. Solving
-\frac{\gamma}{2}+3a=-a
yields
\gamma/8={a}
, which further implies
\gamma\leq\frac{4}{5}
. We conclude that whenever
\gamma\leq 4/5
,

\varepsilon+\zeta+n^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}\varepsilon}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta\varepsilon^{3}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{\gamma}{8}}}\Big).

Conversely, when
\gamma>4/5
, we have
-\frac{1}{2}+4a>-\frac{\gamma}{2}+3a
, and solving
-\frac{1}{2}+5a=-a
gives
a={1}/{10}
. In this case, we obtain

\varepsilon+\zeta+n^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}}\frac{\ln(\varepsilon)}{\zeta^{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta\varepsilon^{3}}=O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{1/10}}\Big).

Finally, from the above computations, we deduce that whenever
\gamma\leq 4/5
:

|\varphi(f(|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}|))-\varphi(f(|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}|))|=(1\vee(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}})^{6})O(n^{-\gamma/8}),

and otherwise when
\gamma>4/5
:

|\varphi(f(|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}|))-\varphi(f(|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}|))|=(1\vee(\|\pi_{n}^{g,n^{-1/2}x}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}}+\|\pi_{n}^{\exp,y^{(n)}}\|_{L^{8}_{\varphi}})^{6})O(n^{-1/10}).

This finishes the proof of the rate of convergence in the
W_{1}
-distance.

General Case

We now turn to the case
r>1
, and follow the same approach used for the
r=1
case to determine the asymptotic order in the Zolotarev metric. Subsequently, we apply Rio’s inequality (
6
) to derive the convergence rate for the Wasserstein distance of order
r
.

Case
r=2
:

Following an analogous approach to the proof of Theorem
1.1
for
r=1
, we obtain an upper bound of the form

\varepsilon^{2}+\zeta+n^{-\frac{\gamma}{2}}|\ln(\varepsilon)|\frac{1}{\zeta^{3}}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{3}\varepsilon}+n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\zeta^{3}\varepsilon^{2}}.

Setting
\varepsilon=n^{-a/2}
and
\zeta=n^{-a}
, the first two terms in the above expression are of order
n^{-a}
. The remaining terms are of order
|\ln(n)|n^{-\gamma/2+3a}
,
n^{-1/2+7a/2}
and
n^{-1/2+4a}
, respectively.
Since
-1/2+4a>-1/2+7a/2
, the sum of the last two terms is of order
n^{-1/2+4a}
.

If
-\gamma/2+3a>-1/2+4a
; that is, when
\gamma<1-2a
, the first of these terms dominates. Solving the equation
a=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+3a
yields
a=\gamma/4
, which implies
\gamma<2/3
.
When
\gamma\geq 1-2a
, the second term dominates. Solving
-1/2+4a=a
gives
a=1/6
, which holds for
\gamma\geq 2/3
. Finally, applying inequality (
6
) yields the rate of convergence for the Wasserstein distance
W_{2}
.
Now, let us consider the general case.

Case:
1<r<2
:
Setting
\zeta=n^{-a}
and
\varepsilon=n^{-a/r}
, we obtain the expression

\displaystyle\zeta+\varepsilon^{r}+\frac{n^{-\gamma/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}\varepsilon^{2-r}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}\varepsilon^{3-r}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}\varepsilon^{4-r}}

\displaystyle\sim n^{-a}+n^{-\gamma/2+(1+r)a+((2-r)a)/r}+n^{-1/2+(1+r)a+((4-r)a)/r}.

Observe that the inequality

-\frac{\gamma}{2}+(1+r)a+\frac{(2-r)a}{r}>-\frac{1}{2}+(1+r)a+\frac{(4-r)a}{r}

implies

\gamma<1-4a/r.

To determine
{\rm rate}_{-}
, we solve

-a=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+(1+r)a+\frac{(2-r)a}{r},\ \text{ which yields }\ a=\frac{\gamma}{2r+2+4/r}.

This leads to the critical value

\gamma_{\rm crit}=\frac{r^{2}+r+2}{r^{2}+r+4},\ \text{ and }\ {\rm rate}_{-}=\frac{\gamma}{2r^{2}+2r+4}.

To compute
{\rm rate}_{+}
, we solve

-a=-\frac{1}{2}+(1+r)a+\frac{(4-r)a}{r},\ \text{ which results in }\ a=\frac{1}{2r+2+8/r}.

Hence, we conclude
{\rm rate}_{+}=1/(2r^{2}+2r+8).

Case:
{2}<r<3
:
We set
\zeta=n^{-a}
and
\varepsilon=n^{-a/r}
, then the expression becomes

\displaystyle\zeta+\varepsilon^{r}+\frac{n^{-\gamma/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}\varepsilon^{3-r}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}\varepsilon^{4-r}}\sim n^{-a}+n^{-\gamma/2+(1+r)a}+n^{-1/2+(1+r)a+((4-r)a)/r}.

To compare terms, observe that the inequality

-\frac{\gamma}{2}+(1+r)a>-\frac{1}{2}+(1+r)a+\frac{(4-r)a}{r}

holds when

\gamma<1-8a/r+2a
.
To determine
{\rm rate}_{-}
, solving

-a=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+(1+r)a\ \text{ yields }\ a=\frac{\gamma}{2r+4}.

From this, we obtain the critical value

\gamma_{\rm crit}=\frac{r^{2}+2r}{r^{2}+r+4},\ \text{ and }\ {\rm rate}_{-}=\frac{\gamma}{2r^{2}+4r}.

For
{\rm rate}_{+}
, we solve

-a=-\frac{1}{2}+(1+r)a+\frac{(4-r)a}{r}\ \text{ leads to }\ a=\frac{1}{2(1+r+4/r)}.

Thus, we conclude

{\rm rate}_{+}=1/(2r^{2}+2r+8).

Case:
r=3
:
We set
\zeta=n^{-a}
and
\varepsilon=n^{-a/r}
, leading to the expression

\displaystyle\zeta+\varepsilon^{3}+\frac{n^{-\gamma/2}}{\zeta^{4}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}|\ln(\varepsilon)|}{\zeta^{4}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{4}\varepsilon}\sim n^{-a}+n^{-\gamma/2+4a}+n^{-1/2+4a+a/3}.

Notice that
-\gamma/2+4a>-1/2+4a+a/3
is satisfied when
\gamma<1-2a/3.

To determine
{\rm rate}_{-}
, we solve
-a=\gamma/2+4a
, which gives
a=\gamma/10
. Therefore, we find

\gamma_{\rm crit}=\frac{15}{16},\ \text{ and }\ {\rm rate}_{-}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{\gamma}{10}=\frac{\gamma}{30}.

For
{\rm rate}_{+}
, solving

-a=-\frac{1}{2}+4a+\frac{a}{3}\ \text{ yields }\ a=\frac{3}{32}.

Hence,

{\rm rate}_{+}=1/3\cdot 3/32=1/32.

Case:
3<r<4
:
Set
\zeta=n^{-a}
and
\varepsilon=n^{-a/r}
, which simplifies the expression to

\zeta+\varepsilon^{r}+\frac{n^{-\gamma/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{r+1}\varepsilon^{4-r}}\sim n^{-a}+n^{a(r+1)-\gamma/2}+n^{a(r+1)-1/2+a(4-r/r)}.

To compare terms, we note that

a(r+1)-\frac{\gamma}{2}>a(r+1)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a(4-r)}{r}\ \text{ holds if and only if }\ \gamma<1-2(1-\frac{4}{r})a.

Solving
-a=a(r+1)-\gamma/2
yields
a=\gamma/(2r+4)
. Therefore, we obtain

\gamma_{\rm crit}=\frac{r+2}{r+1+4/r}=\frac{r^{2}+2r}{r^{2}+r+4},\ \text{ and }\ {\rm rate}_{-}=\frac{1}{r}\frac{\gamma}{2r+4}=\frac{\gamma}{2r^{2}+4r}.

For
{\rm rate}_{+}
, solving

-a=a(r+1)-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a(4-r)}{r}\ \text{ gives }\ a=\frac{1}{2r+2+8/r}.

Thus, the rate is
{\rm rate}_{+}=1/(2r^{2}+2r+8).

Case:
r=4
:
We set
\zeta=n^{-a}
and
\varepsilon=n^{-a/4}
, leading to

\varepsilon^{4}+\zeta+\frac{n^{-\gamma/2}}{\zeta^{5}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{5}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}|\ln(\varepsilon)|}{\zeta^{5}}\sim n^{-a}+n^{-\gamma/2+5a}+n^{-1/2+5a}|\ln(\varepsilon)|.

Observing that
-\gamma/2+5a>-1/2+5a
holds if and only if
\gamma<1
. Thus the critical value is
\gamma_{\rm crit}=1
. Solving
-a=-\gamma/2+5a
yields
a=\gamma/12
, which gives
{\rm rate}_{-}=\gamma/48
. Similarly, solving
-a=-1/2+5a
yields
a=1/12
. Hence,
{\rm rate}_{+}=1/48.

Case:
r>4
:
Since the expression

\varepsilon^{r}+\zeta+\frac{n^{-\gamma/2}}{\zeta^{5}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{5}}+\frac{n^{-1/2}}{\zeta^{5}}

follows the same pattern as the case
r=4
, the results remain unchanged. Thus, for brevity, we omit the detailed computations.

Proposition A.1
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
is a tracial probability space.
Given an integer
m\geq 1
, let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
be elements in
\mathcal{A}
or affiliated to
\mathcal{A}
with enough moments for
\kappa_{m}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
to be well-defined. Define for each
1\leq i\leq m
,

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}&0\\
0&x^{\star}_{i}\end{bmatrix}

The
m^{th}
free
\star
-cumulants of
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
with respect to the state
\varphi^{(2)}
satisfies, with
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle\kappa_{m}^{\varphi^{(2)}}({\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{m}J^{\varepsilon_{m}})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{m}^{\varphi}\big({x}_{1},{x}_{2}^{(\iota_{1})},\ldots,{x}_{m}^{(\iota_{m-1})}\big)&0\\
0&\kappa_{m}^{\varphi}\big({x}_{1}^{\star},{x}_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)},\ldots,{x}_{m}^{(\iota_{m-1}+1)}\big)\end{bmatrix}J^{\iota_{m}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{\kappa}^{\varphi,\,\bm{x}}_{m}(\varepsilon)J^{\iota_{m}},

where
\iota_{i}=\varepsilon_{1}+\dots+\varepsilon_{i}
and the following conventions, for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
{x}^{(2l)}={x},{x}^{(2l+1)}={x}^{\star}
, and
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\dots,\varepsilon_{m})
.

Proof.

We prove by induction that for all
m\geq 1
:

(31)

{\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{m}J^{\varepsilon_{m}}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x_{m}^{(\iota_{m-1})}&0\\
0&x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{m}^{(\iota_{m-1}+1)}\end{bmatrix}J^{\iota_{m}}.

Observe that (
31
) holds when
m=1
. Now we assume (
31
) holds for
m=n
, and let us consider

{\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{n+1}J^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}
.
Our assumption implies that
{\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{n}J^{\varepsilon_{n}}{\bm{x}}_{n+1}J^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}
is equal to

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x_{n}^{(\iota_{n-1})}&0\\
0&x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{n}^{(\iota_{n-1}+1)}\end{bmatrix}\times J^{\iota_{n}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{n+1}&0\\
0&x^{\star}_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}J^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}.

We first consider the case
J^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}=1
(
\varepsilon_{n+1}
= 0). If
\iota_{n}
is even,
\iota_{n+1}=\iota_{n}+\varepsilon_{n+1}
is even and
J^{\iota_{n}}=J^{\iota_{n+1}}=1
. Besides
x=x^{(\iota_{n})}
and
x^{\star}=x^{(\iota_{n}+1)}
. We obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{n}J^{\varepsilon_{n}}{\bm{x}}_{n+1}J^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x_{n}^{(\iota_{n-1})}x_{n+1}&0\\
0&x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{n}^{(\iota_{n-1}+1)}x^{\star}_{n+1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x_{n+1}^{(\iota_{n})}&0\\
0&x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{n+1}^{(\iota_{n}+1)}\end{bmatrix}J^{\iota_{n+1}},

which implies that (
31
) also holds for
m=n+1
. In the case
\iota_{n}
is odd,
\iota_{n+1}
is also odd. Thus,
J^{\iota_{n}}=J^{\iota_{n+1}}=J
and
x^{\star}=x^{(\iota_{n})}
and
x=x^{(\iota_{n}+1)}
.
Hence

\displaystyle{\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{n}J^{\varepsilon_{n}}{\bm{x}}_{n+1}J^{\varepsilon_{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x_{n}^{(\iota_{n-1})}&0\\
0&x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{n}^{(\iota_{n-1}+1)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}0&x^{\star}_{n+1}\\
x_{n+1}&0\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x^{(\iota_{n})}_{n+1}&0\\
0&x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{n+1}^{(\iota_{n}+1)}\end{bmatrix}J^{\iota_{n+1}},

implies that (
31
) holds for
m=n+1
. The proof for the case
\varepsilon_{n+1}=1
is similar, and we leave it to the reader. By induction, we have proved that (
31
) holds for all
m\geq 1
.
We apply
\varphi^{(2)}
to both sides of equation (
31
) and obtain

\displaystyle\varphi^{(2)}({\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\epsilon_{1}}\cdots{\bm{x}}_{m}J^{\varepsilon_{m}})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi(x_{1}{x}^{(\iota_{1})}_{2}\cdots x_{m}^{(\iota_{m-1})})&0\\
0&\varphi(x_{1}^{\star}x_{2}^{(\iota_{1}+1)}\cdots x_{m}^{(\iota_{m-1}+1)})\end{bmatrix}J^{\iota_{m}}:=\varphi^{(2),{\bm{x}}}(\varepsilon)J^{\iota_{m}}.

Next, given
p,q\geq 0
,

\varphi^{(2)}({\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{p-1}J^{\varepsilon_{p-1}}\varphi^{(2)}\big({\bm{x}}_{p}J^{\varepsilon_{p}},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{p+q-1}J^{\varepsilon_{p+q-1}}\big),{\bm{x}}_{p+q}J^{\varepsilon_{p+q}},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{m}J^{\varepsilon_{m}})\\
=\varphi^{(2)}({\bm{x}}_{1}J^{\varepsilon_{1}},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{p-1}J^{\varepsilon_{p-1}}\varphi^{(2),\,\bm{x}_{[p,p+q-1]}}(\varepsilon_{[p,p+q-1]})J^{\varepsilon_{p}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{p+q-1}},{\bm{x}}_{p+q}J^{\varepsilon_{p+q}},\ldots,{\bm{x}}_{m}J^{\varepsilon_{m}}),

where we denote by
\bm{x}_{[p,p+q-1]}=(x_{p},\dots,x_{p+q-1})
and
\varepsilon_{[p,p+q-1]}=(\varepsilon_{p},\dots,\varepsilon_{p+q-1})

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon_{p-1}}\varphi^{(2),\,\bm{x}_{[p,p+q-1]}}(\varepsilon_{[p,p+q-1]})J^{\varepsilon_{p-1}+\dots+\varepsilon_{p+q-1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\varphi\big(x^{\varepsilon_{p-1}}_{1}{x}^{(\varepsilon_{p-1}+\varepsilon_{p})}_{2}\cdots x_{p+q-1}^{(\varepsilon_{p-1}+\varepsilon_{p}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{p+q-2})}\big)&0\\
0&\varphi\big(x_{1}^{\varepsilon_{p-1}+1}x_{2}^{(\varepsilon_{p-1}+\varepsilon_{p}+1)}\cdots x_{m}^{(\varepsilon_{p-1}+\varepsilon_{p}+\cdots+\varepsilon_{p+q-2}+1)}\big)\end{bmatrix}.

The moment-cumulant formula yields the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose
(\mathcal{A},\varphi)
is a tracial probability space, and
p\geq 0
. Let
X=g(x)
for some self-adjoint random variable
x\in\mathcal{A}
and
g\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
. Then for any
\epsilon_{i}\in\{1,\star\}
and
\pi\in NC(p)
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\kappa_{\pi}^{\varphi}\big(X^{\epsilon_{1}}-1,\dots,X^{\epsilon_{p}}-1\big)\big|\lesssim\big\|\|g\|_{1,[x^{-},x^{+}]}\big\|_{L^{p(1+\zeta^{-1})}_{\varphi}}^{p}\|x\|_{L^{p(1+\zeta)}_{\varphi}}^{p},

where
x^{-}:=\min(x,0)
,
x^{+}:=\max(x,0)
.

Proof.

We first observe by the moment-cumulant formula that

(32)

\displaystyle\big|\kappa_{\pi}^{\varphi}(X^{\epsilon_{1}}-1,\dots,X^{\epsilon_{p}}-1)\big|\leq\sum_{\sigma\in NC(p);\sigma\leq\pi}|\mu(\sigma,\pi)||\varphi_{\sigma}(X^{\epsilon_{1}}-1,\dots,X^{\epsilon_{p}}-1)|.

Then for any
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq p
, we note that

\displaystyle|\varphi((X^{\epsilon_{i_{1}}}-1)\dots(X^{\epsilon_{i_{k}}}-1))|

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(|X-1|^{k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|g(t)-1|^{k}\nu_{x}({\rm d}t)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|g\|_{1,[t^{-},t^{+}]}^{k}|t|^{k}\nu_{x}({\rm d}t)

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\|g\|_{1,[x^{-},x^{+}]}\big\|_{L^{k(1+\zeta^{-1})}_{\varphi}}^{k}\varphi(|x|^{k(1+\zeta)})^{\frac{1}{1+\zeta}}

\displaystyle=\big\|\|g\|_{1,[x^{-},x^{+}]}\big\|_{L^{k(1+\zeta^{-1})}_{\varphi}}^{k}\|x\|_{L^{k(1+\zeta)}_{\varphi}}^{k},

where the first and last inequalities follow from Hölder’s inequality and the second one from the fact that
g\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
and is hence Lipschitz. Since
L^{p}
-norms are increasing in
p
and the fact that
\sum_{V\in\sigma}|V|=p
, we infer

(33)

\displaystyle|\varphi_{\sigma}(X^{\epsilon_{1}}-1,\dots,X^{\epsilon_{p}}-1)|\leq\big\|\|g\|_{1,[x^{-},x^{+}]}\big\|_{L^{p(1+\zeta^{-1})}_{\varphi}}^{p}\|x\|_{L^{p(1+\zeta)}_{\varphi}}^{p}\text{ for any }\sigma\in NC(p).

By combining (
32
) with (
33
), we obtain the desired result.
∎

\displaystyle|(f-f_{\zeta})(x)|=|(1-\theta_{\zeta}(x))f(x)|=|(\theta_{\zeta}(0)-\theta_{\zeta}(x))f(x)|\leq\zeta|x||x|^{r}=\zeta|x|^{r+1}.

To proceed, we first give an upper bound on

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(z)-f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(x)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}\zeta^{k}|f^{(\ell-k)}(z)-f^{(\ell-k)}(x)|+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}|\theta^{(k)}_{\zeta}(x)-\theta^{(k)}_{\zeta}(z)||f^{(\ell-k)}(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|x-z|^{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}\zeta^{k}|x-z|\sup_{-R\leq u\leq R}|f^{{(\ell-k+1)}}(u)|+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}\zeta^{k}|x-z||f^{(\ell-k)}(z)|,

where the second and third inequalities follow by the fact that
|\theta_{\zeta}^{(k)}(x)|=|\zeta^{k}\theta^{(k)}(x)|\leq\zeta^{k}
.
To control the derivatives of
f
, we first note that
f
and all its derivatives up to the order
\ell
vanish at
0
. Then by Taylor-Lagrange, we obtain for some
\theta_{x}
between

\displaystyle\big|f^{(\ell-k)}(x)\big|

\displaystyle=\big|f^{(\ell-k)}(0)+f^{(\ell-k+1)}(0)x+\cdots+\frac{1}{(k-1)!}f^{(\ell-1)}(0)x^{k-1}+\frac{1}{k!}f^{(\ell)}(\theta_{x})x^{k}\big|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k!}|f^{(\ell)}(\theta_{x})-f^{(\ell)}(0)||x|^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k!}|x|^{r},

where we recall that

|x|,|z|\leq R

\displaystyle|f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(z)-f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|x-z|^{\beta}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}\zeta^{k}|x-z|\frac{R^{r-\ell+k-1}}{(k-1)!}+\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}\binom{\ell}{k}\zeta^{k}|x-z|\frac{R^{r-\ell+k}}{k!}

where we have used the fact that
R\geq\frac{1}{\zeta}
and
r\geq 1
to write the last inequality.
Noting that
\hat{\rho}^{(k)}(0)=0
for

\hat{\rho}(0)=1

which by the steps to get (

\displaystyle|

\displaystyle f_{\zeta}(x)-f_{\zeta}\star\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{\zeta}(x)-f_{\zeta}(x+y))\rho_{\varepsilon}(y){\rm d}y\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(f_{\zeta}(x)-f_{\zeta}(x+y)+f_{\zeta}^{\prime}(x)y+\cdots+\frac{f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(x)}{\ell!}y^{\ell}\big){\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y){\rm d}y\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(\xi_{x,y})-f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(x)||y|^{\ell}|{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)|{\rm d}y\quad\text{for some }\xi_{x,y}\text{ between }x\text{ and }x+y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sup_{z\in[x,x+y]}|f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(z)-f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}(x)||y|^{\ell}|{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)|{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|y|^{\beta}\wedge 1)(|y|\vee 1)(1+(|y|^{r}\vee|x+y|^{r})\zeta^{\ell})|y|^{\ell}|{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)|{\rm d}y.

To estimate the above integral, we consider the domains corresponding to
|y|\geq 1

|y|\leq 1

\displaystyle\int_{|y|\geq 1}(|y|^{\beta}\wedge 1)

\displaystyle(|y|\vee 1)(1+(|y|^{r}\vee|x+y|^{r})\zeta^{\ell})|y|^{\ell}|{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)|{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{|y|\geq 1}|y|(1+|x|^{r}+|y|^{r}\zeta^{\ell})|{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)|{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon^{-1}}\varepsilon|u|(1+|x|^{r}+|\varepsilon u|^{r}\zeta^{\ell})\varepsilon^{\ell}|u^{\ell}||{\rho}(u)|{\rm d}u

\displaystyle={\varepsilon^{\ell+1}}\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon^{-1}}{|u|}(1+|{x}|^{r}+|{\varepsilon}{u}|^{r}\zeta^{\ell})|{u^{\ell}}||{\rho}(u)|{\rm d}u

Since

goes to

,
and the bound is

.
As for the second part,

\displaystyle\int_{|y|\leq 1}

\displaystyle(|y|^{\beta}\wedge 1)(|y|\vee 1)(1+|x+y|^{r}\zeta^{\ell})|{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)|{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{|u|\leq\varepsilon^{-1}}\varepsilon^{\ell+\beta}|u|^{\beta}(1+|x|^{r}+|\varepsilon u|^{r}\zeta^{\ell})|u|^{\ell}|{\rho}(u)|{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{r}\int_{|u|>0}|u|^{\beta}(1+|x|^{r}+|u|^{r})|u|^{\ell}|{\rho}(u)|{\rm d}y.

Putting the above bounds together, we obtain (
28
).
Now by choosing

|\widehat{f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}}(y)|=\frac{1}{2}|\widehat{f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}}(y)-(\widehat{f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)})_{\frac{\pi}{y}}}(y)|\lesssim\|f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)}-(f_{\zeta}^{(\ell)})_{\frac{\pi}{y}}\|_{L^{1}([-\frac{1}{\zeta}-\frac{\pi}{|y|},\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{\pi}{|y|}])}\\
\lesssim\big(\frac{1}{|y|^{\beta}}\wedge 1\big)\big(\frac{1}{|y|}\vee 1\big)\Big(1+\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{1}{|y|}\big)^{r}\zeta^{\ell}\Big)\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{1}{|y|}\big).

Since for any

|\widehat{f_{\zeta}}(y)|\lesssim\big(\frac{1}{|y|^{\beta}}\wedge 1\big)\big(\frac{1}{|y|}\vee 1\big)\Big(1+\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{1}{|y|}\big)^{r}\zeta^{\ell}\Big)\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{1}{|y|}\big)\frac{1}{|y|^{\ell}}.

To estimate the integral

\displaystyle I_{k}=\int_{|y|\leq 1}|y|^{k}|\hat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)\hat{f}_{\zeta}(y)|\mathrm{d}y+\int_{|y|\geq 1}|y|^{k}|\hat{\rho}_{\varepsilon}(y)\hat{f}_{\zeta}(y)|\mathrm{d}y,

As for the other integral, we use the bound in (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{k+1}}

\displaystyle\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}|u|^{k}|\hat{\rho}(u)|\hat{f}_{\zeta}(\frac{u}{\varepsilon})|\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{k-\ell+1}}\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}|u|^{k-\ell}|\hat{\rho}(u)|\big(\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta}}{|u|^{\beta}}\wedge 1\big)\big(\frac{\varepsilon}{|u|}\vee 1\big)\Big(1+\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{\varepsilon}{|u|}\big)^{r}\zeta^{\ell}\Big)\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{\varepsilon}{|u|}\big)\ \mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{k-\ell+1}}\int_{|u|\geq\varepsilon}|u|^{k-\ell}|\hat{\rho}(u)|\frac{\varepsilon^{\beta}}{|u|^{\beta}}\Big(1+\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{\varepsilon}{|u|}\big)^{r}\zeta^{\ell}\Big)\big(\frac{1}{\zeta}+\frac{\varepsilon}{|u|}\big)\ \mathrm{d}u

\bullet
For

: It is easy to see that the integrals in

\bullet
For

. On the other hand, as

\bullet

A.4.
A combinatorial proof of the free multiplicative CLT

Theorem A.8
.

Suppose
(x_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of centered self-adjoint random variables with the same variance
\sigma^{2}
. For a fixed
k\geq 1
, assume

(44)

\sup_{i\geq 1}{\varphi(x^{k(1+\zeta)})}<+\infty

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
be such that
g\in\mathcal{C}^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{R})
and
g(0)=1
. Assume that there exists
0<\alpha<1
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 1}\|\||g^{2}|\|_{1,[\alpha x^{-}_{i},\alpha x^{+}_{i}]}\|_{k(1+\zeta^{-1})},\quad\sup_{i\geq 1}\|\||g^{2}|^{\prime}\|_{1,[\alpha x^{-}_{i},\alpha x^{+}_{i}]}\|_{k(1+\zeta^{-1})},

\displaystyle\sup_{i\geq 1}\|\||g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}\|_{\gamma,[\alpha x^{-}_{i},\alpha x^{+}_{i}]}\|_{k(1+\zeta^{-1})}<+\infty,

for some
\zeta>0
and
\alpha>0
, where
x_{i}^{-}:=\min(x_{i},0)
,
x_{i}^{+}:=\max(x_{i},0)
and
\|.\|_{L_{\varphi}^{p}}
denotes the
L^{p}
norm with respect to
\varphi
. Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\kappa^{\varphi}_{k}\big({\pi^{g,x}_{n}}({\pi^{g,x}_{n}})^{\star}\big)=\frac{k^{k-1}}{k!}(|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)\sigma)^{2(k-1)}e^{\frac{k}{2}|g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}(0)\sigma^{2}}.

Remark A.9
.

Note that if we take
g(x)=\exp(x)
, then
|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{2}|g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}(0)=2
, which yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}\kappa^{\varphi}_{k}\big({\pi^{g,x}_{n}}({\pi^{g,x}_{n}})^{\star}\big)=\frac{k^{k-1}}{k!}(2\sigma)^{2(k-1)}e^{2k\sigma^{2}},

in agreement with Equation (
7
) and Ho’s result.

Remark A.10
.

It is worth emphasizing that Theorem
A.8
, combined with the results of
[
BW08
,
CG08b
]
, establishes the free multiplicative central limit theorem (CLT) under the Wasserstein distance and even extends to the unbounded setting. Specifically,
[
BW08
,
CG08b
]
guarantee the existence and convergence of the limit, while Theorem
A.8
identifies the corresponding cumulants. In contrast to the approach of Ho, this combinatorial approach applies in the unbounded setting as well and yields convergence in the Wasserstein distance to a broader class of products involving functions of freely independent, not necessarily identically distributed elements.

To prove Theorem
A.8
, we begin by controlling the cumulants of
|g|^{2}(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}})
for all
i=1,\dots,n
as illustrated in the following lemma.

Lemma A.11
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
A.8
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa^{\varphi}_{1}\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=1+\big(\Re(g^{\prime\prime}(0))+|g^{\prime}(0)|^{2}\big)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}\Big),

\displaystyle\kappa^{\varphi}_{2}\Big(|g|^{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)^{2}+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{3}{2}}}\Big),

\displaystyle\kappa^{\varphi}_{k}\Big(|g|^{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4^{k-1}}{n^{k/2}}\big\|\||g|^{2}\|_{1,[\alpha x_{i}^{-},\alpha x_{i}^{+}]}\big\|^{k}_{L_{\varphi}^{k(1+\frac{1}{\zeta})}}\|x_{i}\|_{L_{\varphi}^{k(1+\zeta)}}^{k},\qquad\text{for }k\geq 3.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{p}
denote the
p
-th order Taylor polynomial of
|g|^{2}
around the origin. Then, for any
i=1,\dots,n
,
\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{2}(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}})
is given by

\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)=|g(0)^{2}|+|g(0)^{2}|^{\prime}\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{1}{2}|g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}(0)\frac{x^{2}_{i}}{n}.

We first note that by the Taylor-Lagrange theorem, we have

\displaystyle\Big|\varphi\Big(|g|^{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big||g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}\Big(\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)-|g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}(0)\Big|\frac{|t|^{2}}{n}\nu_{x_{i}}({\rm d}t),

for some
\xi
between
0
and
t
.
Using the fact that
g^{\prime\prime}
is
\gamma
-Hölder continuous then applying Hölder’s inequality, we deduce that for any
\zeta\geq 0

\displaystyle\Big|\varphi\Big(|g|^{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\||g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}\|_{\gamma,[\frac{t^{-}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{t^{+}}{\sqrt{n}}]}|t|^{2+\gamma}\nu_{x_{i}}({\rm d}t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}\Big\|\||g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}\|_{\gamma,[\frac{x_{i}^{-}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{x_{i}^{+}}{\sqrt{n}}]}\Big\|_{L_{\varphi}^{1+\frac{1}{\zeta}}}\|x_{i}\|_{L_{\varphi}^{(2+\gamma)(1+\zeta)}}^{2+\gamma},

under Assumption (

\varphi(x_{i})=0

\displaystyle\varphi\Big(\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=|g(0)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|g^{2}|^{\prime\prime}(0)\varphi\Big(\frac{x^{2}_{i}}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=|g(0)|^{2}+\big(\Re(g^{\prime\prime}(0)\bar{g}(0))+|g^{\prime}(0)|^{2}\big)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}=1+\big(\Re(g^{\prime\prime}(0))+|g^{\prime}(0)|^{2}\big)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.

Hence, we conclude from the above computations and bounds that

\kappa^{\varphi}_{1}\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)=\varphi\Big(|g|^{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)=1+\big(\Re(g^{\prime\prime}(0))+|g^{\prime}(0)|^{2}\big)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}}\Big).

For the cumulant of second order, we first write

\displaystyle\kappa^{\varphi}_{2}

\displaystyle\Big(|g|^{2}\Big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\varphi\Big(\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-1\Big)^{2}\Big)-\varphi\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-1\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\varphi\Big(\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)+|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{2}\Big)-\varphi\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)^{2}.

where the last equation holds because
x_{i}
is centered with respect to
\varphi
. Now, by Taylor-Lagrange theorem, we note that

\displaystyle\varphi\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\||g^{2}|^{\prime}\|_{1,[\frac{t_{i}^{-}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{t_{i}^{+}}{\sqrt{n}}]}|t|^{2}\nu_{x_{i}}({{\rm d}t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\Big\|\||g^{2}|^{\prime}\big\|_{1,[\frac{x_{i}^{-}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{x_{i}^{+}}{\sqrt{n}}]}\Big\|_{L_{\varphi}^{1+\frac{1}{\zeta}}}\|x_{i}\|_{L_{\varphi}^{2(1+\zeta)}}^{2},

where the last inequality follows for any choice of
\zeta\geq 0
by applying Hölder’s inequality.
As for the second-order moment, we first write

\displaystyle\varphi\Big(\big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-1\big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\varphi\Big(\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)+|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\varphi\Big(\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)^{2}\Big)+2|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)\varphi\Big(\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\Big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}|g^{2}|^{\prime}(0)^{2}.

We now show that the first two terms on the right-hand side above are each of order
O(n^{-3/2})
. To begin, we observe by Taylor-Lagrange theorem that

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\Big(\Big(|g|^{2}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)-\mathcal{T}^{|g|^{2}}_{1}\big(\frac{x_{i}}{\sqrt{n}}\big)\Big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\||g^{2}|^{\prime}\|_{1,[\frac{t^{-}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{t^{+}}{\sqrt{n}}]}^{2}|t|^{4}\nu_{x_{i}}({\rm d}t)\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big\|\||g^{2}|^{\prime}\|_{1,[\frac{x_{i}^{-}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{x_{i}^{+}}{\sqrt{n}}]}\Big\|_{L_{\varphi}^{1+\frac{1}{\zeta}}}^{2}\|x_{i}\|_{L_{\varphi}^{2(1+\zeta)}}^{4},

where the last inequality follows again for any
\zeta\geq 0
