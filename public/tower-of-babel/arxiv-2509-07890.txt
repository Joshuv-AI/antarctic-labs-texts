Quantum Walks for Chemical Reaction Networks
Seenivasan Hariharan, Sebastian Zur, Sachin Kinge, Lucas Visscher, Kareljan Schoutens, Stacey Jeffery

Abstract
We lay the foundation for a quantum algorithmic framework to analyse fixed-structure chemical reaction networks (CRNs) using quantum random walks (QRWs) via electrical circuit theory. We model perturbations to CRNs, such as, species injections that shift steady-state concentrations, while keeping the underlying species-reaction graph fixed. Under physically meaningful mass-action constraints, we develop quantum algorithms that (i) decide reachability of target species after perturbation, (ii) sample representative reachable species, (iii) approximate steady-state fluxes through reactions, and (iv) estimate total Gibbs free-energy consumption. Our approach offers new tools for analysing the structure and energetics of complex CRNs, and opens up the prospect of scalable quantum algorithms for chemical and biochemical reaction networks.

Perturbations to a CRN can affect its behaviour differently depending on whether its structure is fixed or variable. We focus on fixed-structure CRNs, defined by a finite set of species, e.g.

, and a finite set of reactions between these species, e.g.

Under standard assumptions on the mass action kinetics and the structure of the CRN, the resulting dynamics give rise to a natural thermodynamic interpretation of the system. In particular, the system’s evolution can often be associated with the dissipation of a free energy–like function, and its steady states correspond to thermodynamic equilibria where no net change in species concentrations occurs.

For the large-scale networks relevant in practical applications, however, it is reasonable to model access to the CRN’s structure and its precomputed thermodynamic properties in a manner analogous to an adjacency matrix. By adopting this perspective and assuming that the network data is stored in readable QRAM, we can leverage quantum walks to achieve a provable speedup over classical methods. Our analysis is predicated on a set of thermodynamic constraints that the CRN must satisfy. A prominent and practically relevant example of a CRN that meets these requirements is
competitive binding
, where an injected inhibitor species competes with the substrate for binding to an enzyme. This scenario can be represented within the bipartite structure of the CRN by introducing additional species and binding/unbinding reactions, preserving the network topology while modifying the equilibrium composition and flux distribution

[
1

Although we do not explicitly model enzyme–inhibitor dynamics here, the structural similarity between CRNs with competitive inhibitors and the bipartite graphs considered in this work suggests that our framework could be useful in analysing such perturbations. More broadly, our results indicate that quantum algorithms for CRNs may provide a scalable approach for studying mechanistic aspects of biochemical regulation, drug action, and energy dissipation in large molecular networks.

II.1
Graph theory and electrical networks

In this section, we define the necessary graph-theoretic concepts and provide a review of basic knowledge on electrical networks.

Definition II.1

(Network)
.

A network is a connected weighted graph
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
with a vertex set
V
, an (undirected) edge set
E
and some weight function
{\sf w}:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

{\overrightarrow{E}}
such that for all

{\overrightarrow{E}}

{\sf w}:{\overrightarrow{E}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and for all
(u,v)\in{\overrightarrow{E}}
, define
{\sf w}_{v,u}={\sf w}_{u,v}
. For convenience, we define
{\sf w}_{u,v}=0
for every pair of vertices such that
(u,v)\not\in E
. We write

{\sf W}:=\sum_{(u,v)\in{\overrightarrow{E}}}{\sf w}_{u,v},

for the total weight of the network.

For an implicit network
G
, and
u\in V
, we will let
\Gamma(u)
denote the
neighbourhood
of
u

\Gamma(u):=\{v\in V:\{u,v\}\in E\}.

We use the following notation for
the out- and in-neighbourhoods
of
u\in V

\begin{split}\Gamma^{+}(u)&:=\{v\in\Gamma(u):(u,v)\in{\overrightarrow{E}}\}\\
\Gamma^{-}(u)&:=\{v\in\Gamma(u):(v,u)\in{\overrightarrow{E}}\},\end{split}

(1)

To build intuition from physics and apply results from electrical network theory, it is useful to interpret our networks as

.

Definition II.2

(Electrical network)
.

Given a network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
with a weight function
{\sf w}
, we can interpret every edge
\{u,v\}\in E
as a resistor with resistance
1/{\sf w}_{u,v}
. This allows

to be modeled as an

.

Definition II.3

(Flow)
.

A
flow
on a network

is a real-valued function on the edges

, such that
\theta_{u,v}=-\theta_{v,u}

. For any flow

, we define the net flow leaving

. Flow is said to be

\theta_{u}=0
. A vertex

is called a

\theta_{u}>0
and a

if
\theta_{u}<0
.

Given a

and an

\sigma

(unit)
\sigma
-
M

is a flow
\theta

\sigma

, and each sink is in

.

The

of a flow

The

II.2
Chemical reaction networks

A chemical reaction network (CRN) provides a mathematical framework for representing and analysing a system of complex chemical reactions. It is particularly useful for studying the kinetics of large reaction networks. In this section, we adopt the definition of a CRN, as well as related notions, as introduced by Feinberg
[
1
]
.

Definition II.7

(Chemical Reaction Network)
.

A chemical reaction network (CRN) is a tuple
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R})
, where:

(i)

{\cal S}
is a finite set, whose elements are called the species of the network.

(ii)

{\cal C}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{\cal S}}
is a finite set of vectors called the complexes of the network.

(iii)

{\cal R}\subset{\cal C}\times{\cal C}
is a finite set of ordered pairs called
reactions
, satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

\forall y\in{\cal C}
,
(y,y)\notin{\cal R}
(no trivial reactions).

(b)

\forall y\in{\cal C}
, there exists
y^{\prime}\in{\cal C}
such that either
(y,y^{\prime})\in{\cal R}
or
(y^{\prime},y)\in{\cal R}
(every complex participates in at least one reaction either as a reactant or as a product.

For a reaction
(y,y^{\prime})\in{\cal R}
, we denote it by
y\to y^{\prime}
, where
y
is called the reactant complex and
y^{\prime}
is the product complex.

As an example, consider the CRN defined by

\displaystyle{\cal S}

\displaystyle=\{A,B,C,D,E\},

(2)

\displaystyle{\cal C}

\displaystyle=\{A,2B,A+C,D,B+E\},

(3)

\displaystyle{\cal R}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{A\to 2B,2B\to A,A+C\to D,D\to A+C,D\to B+E,B+E\to A+C\bigr\}.

(4)

This corresponds to the reaction system whose standard reaction diagram is displayed as

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\rightleftharpoons 2B,

\displaystyle A+C

\displaystyle\rightleftharpoons D,

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\rightarrow B+E,

\displaystyle B+E

\displaystyle\rightarrow A+C.

A CRN is not just a static system; it also has dynamical relationships between the various complexes. These dynamics are governed by the
kinetics
of the CRN. The kinetics act on a
concentrations vector

c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{\cal S}}
, whose components
c_{s}
(for each
s\in{\cal S}
) specify the number of
s
-molecules per unit volume of the mixture. In this work, we consider the following specific form of kinetics:

Definition II.8

(Mass Action Kinetics)
.

A
mass action kinetics
for a chemical reaction network

({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R})

is an assignment to each reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
of a
mass action rate function

{\cal K}_{y\to y^{\prime}}\colon\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{{\cal S}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

such that there exists a positive number
k_{y\to y^{\prime}}
with

{\cal K}_{y\to y^{\prime}}(c)=k_{y\to y^{\prime}}\prod_{s\in{\cal S}}c_{s}^{y_{s}}.

Here
k_{y\to y^{\prime}}
is the
rate constant
for the reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
, and the component
y_{s}
of
y
is the
stoichiometric coefficient
of species
s
in the reactant complex
y
.

In the above definition of the mass action rate function, we adopt the convention
0^{0}=1
when a particular concentration
c_{s}
is zero. For the CRN in (
2
), consider, for example, the reaction
A+C\to D
. Then

{\cal K}_{A+C\to D}(c)=k_{A+C\to D}(c_{A})^{1}(c_{B})^{0}(c_{C})^{1}(c_{D})^{0}(c_{E})^{0}=k_{A+C\to D}c_{A}c_{C}.

Similarly, for the reaction
2B\to A
, we have

{\cal K}_{2B\to A}(c)=k_{2B\to A}(c_{B})^{2}.

It is convention to abbreviate

\prod_{s\in{\cal S}}c_{s}^{y_{s}}=c^{y},

so that the mass action rate functions can be written more succinctly as

{\cal K}_{y\to y^{\prime}}(c)=k_{y\to y^{\prime}}c^{y}.

mass action kinetics for a CRN
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R})
is completely specified by assigning a positive rate constant
k_{y\to y^{\prime}}
to each reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in{\cal R}
. That is to say, a mass action kinetics is specified by an element
k\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\cal R}
. We therefore speak of a
mass action system (MAS)

({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R},k)
, which is the CRN
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R})
combined with the mass action kinetics uniquely determined by
k
.

II.3
The thermodynamics of an MAS

In this section, we delve deeper into the thermodynamics of an MAS, following the notions in
[
53
]
. When studying the kinetics of an MAS, it is convenient to study
reversible
reactions. For a CRN
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R})
, we say that a reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in{\cal R}
is said to be reversible if
y^{\prime}\to y\in{\cal R}
. For a reversible reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
, we can define its
net flux

J_{y\to y^{\prime}}(c)
as follows:

J_{y\to y^{\prime}}(c):={\cal K}_{y\to y^{\prime}}(c)-{\cal K}_{y^{\prime}\to y}(c).

(5)

This net flux is positive if the reaction proceeds predominantly in the forward direction and negative if the backward direction is dominant. The net flux can also be zero, reflecting a perfect balance of forward and backward processes in that reaction.

If all reactions in
{\cal R}
for a CRN
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R})
are reversible, we say that the CRN is reversible. To avoid listing both directions of each reversible pair of reaction, we fix a subset
\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}\subset{\cal R}
called the set of
oriented reactions
, containing exactly one direction from every reversible pair
y\to y^{\prime},y\to y^{\prime}\in{\cal R}
, similarly as to how the directed edge set
{\overrightarrow{E}}
is obtained from the edge set
E
in a graph. We can fully characterise the set of reversible reactions
{\cal R}
from just
\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
. If
r=y\to y^{\prime}
, we use
\overline{r}
to denote
y^{\prime}\to y
. This means that, depending on the chosen direction,
J_{r}(c)
would change sign.

For a reversible CRN, if the net flux of all reactions is zero, that is, there exists a vector of concentrations
c^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\cal S}
such that, for each reaction
r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
,

{\cal K}_{r}(c^{*})={\cal K}_{\overline{r}}(c^{*}),

(6)

then we say that the MAS is in
detailed balance
. Such a vector
c^{*}
is often referred to as a (thermodynamic)
equilibrium
of the MAS.

One may then ask what happens if we perturb this equilibrium by introducing or removing certain amounts of one or more species. Such a perturbation generally alters the exact cancellation of forward and backward fluxes, typically driving the system away from detailed balance. For a reaction
r=y\to y^{\prime}
, let
\nu_{r,s}:=y^{\prime}_{s}-y_{s}
denote the
net stoichiometric coefficient
of
s
in
r
. Then the system and new concentrations
c
will still satisfy the
steady‐state
condition for each species:

\frac{dc_{s}}{dt}=\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r,s}J_{r}(c)=\eta_{s},\quad\text{for all species }s\in{\cal S}.

(7)

Here
\eta_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the net
external injection/removal rate
of species
s
. By convention,
\eta_{s}>0
means species
s
is injected from an external source, while
\eta_{s}<0
indicates a net removal of
s
. Note that
(
7
)
is invariant under the choice of direction in
\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
, since

\nu_{r,s}J_{r}(c)=--\nu_{\overline{r},s}J_{\overline{r}}(c).

We now introduce
chemical potentials

\{\mu_{s}(c)\}_{s\in{\cal S}}
. For an MAS action system, these are defined as

\mu_{s}(c):=RT\ln(c_{s})+\mu_{s}^{0},

(8)

where
R
and
T
are the gas constant and absolute temperature, respectively. We can ignore the reference potential
\mu_{s}^{0}
, since we are only interested in what happens to
\mu_{s}(c)
under a change of concentration

\delta c_{s}:=c_{s}-c^{*}_{s}
. Applying the first‑order Taylor expansion to
\ln(c_{s})
around
c^{*}_{s}
gives

\delta\ln(c_{s})=\ln(c^{*}_{s}+\delta c_{s})-\ln(c^{*}_{s})\approx\frac{\delta c_{s}}{c^{*}_{s}},

so that

\delta\mu_{s}(c)=RT\delta\ln(c_{s})\approx RT\frac{\delta c_{s}}{c^{*}_{s}}.

(9)

It is standard in linear non-equilibrium
thermodynamics (see e.g. Chapter X in
[
53
]
) to assume
(
9
)
to be an equality, which is exact in the limit
\delta c_{s}/c^{*}_{s}\to 0
. We can apply the same first–order expansion to
J_{r}(c)
(and assume equality), with
r=y\to y^{\prime}
, obtaining

\begin{split}\delta J_{r}(c)=\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\left.\frac{\partial J_{r}(c)}{\partial c_{s}}\right|_{c^{*}}\delta c_{s}=\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\left(\frac{y_{s}}{c^{*}_{s}}{\cal K}_{r}(c^{*})-\frac{y^{\prime}_{s}}{c^{*}_{s}}{\cal K}_{\overline{r}}(c^{*})\right)\delta c_{s}=-{\cal K}_{r}(c^{*})\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\frac{\nu_{r,s}}{c^{*}_{s}}\delta c_{s}.\end{split}

(10)

By introducing the
affinity
of the reaction
r

\Delta\mu_{r}(c):=-\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\nu_{r,s}\delta\mu_{s}(c),

and the
Onsager coefficient

G_{r}:=\frac{{\cal K}_{r}(c^{*})}{RT}>0,

we have by
(
10
)
and
(
6
)
that

J_{r}(c)=J_{r}(c^{*})+\delta J_{r}=G_{r}\Delta\mu_{r}(c)

(11)

The last thermodynamic quantity we need is the
instantaneous rate of
Gibbs free–energy consumption

\Phi
:

\Phi(c)=\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}J_{r}(c)\Delta\mu_{r}(c),

which measures the free–energy dissipated, i.e. the chemical “power” irreversibly lost (or supplied) by the running reactions, per unit time. Near equilibrium, we have due to
(
11
)
that

\Phi(c)=\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}J_{r}(c)^{2}/G_{r}.

(12)

Due to the square, we find that
\Phi(c)
is invariant under the choice of direction in
\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
, since
G_{r}=G_{\overline{r}}
.

II.4
Quantum walks

In this work we employ the use of quantum walk algorithms to compute properties of our chemical reaction network. Quantum walk algorithms provide a fundamentally different model of computation that is particularly well-suited for analysing graph-structured systems. In contrast to classical random walks, which explore a graph diffusively, quantum walks exhibit interference and coherence, allowing in some cases for more efficient exploration. For example, quantum walks offer quadratic speed-ups in search tasks
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
]
and, in some cases, even exponential improvements for structured graph problems
[
58
]
. These features suggest that quantum algorithms could help overcome the combinatorial complexity inherent in CRNs, where the number of pathways, intermediates, and reaction configurations grows rapidly with the system size.

We adopt the electric network framework introduced by
[
43
]
and further refined by
[
59
,
44
]
to construct our quantum walks. These algorithms can likewise be derived via the span program formalism
[
60
,
61
]
.

While we primarily treat this framework as a black box to produce quantum walk algorithms with specified problem-solving capabilities and complexities, we also offer a brief overview of the principles underlying these quantum walks. Readers already familiar with the framework, or less interested in these details, may skip directly to the next section.

A quantum walk algorithm derived from the electrical network framework can be viewed as a specialised phase estimation algorithm
[
62
]
. Such an algorithm can be specified by the following parameters:

•

A finite-dimensional complex inner product space
H
,

•

a unit vector
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle\in H
,

•

two subspaces
{\cal A},{\cal B}
of
H
, such that
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle\in{\cal B}^{\perp}
.

These parameters define a quantum algorithm as follows. Let

U_{\cal{AB}}=(2\Pi_{\cal{A}}-I)(2\Pi_{\cal{B}}-I).

(13)

We then perform phase estimation of
U_{\cal{AB}}
on initial state
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
to a certain precision, measure the phase register, and output 1 if the measured phase is 0, and output 0 otherwise. Here ‘perform phase estimation’, means running a quantum subroutine that decides whether
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
has non-zero overlap with the
(+1)
-eigenspace of
U_{\cal{AB}}
, which, due to the structure of
U_{\cal AB}
, is precisely
({\cal A}\cap{\cal B})\oplus({\cal A}+{\cal B})^{\perp}.
Since by construction
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
is orthogonal to
{\cal B}
, the algorithm effectively decides whether
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle\in{\cal A}+{\cal B}
or not.

The exact initialisation of the phase estimation parameters depends on the application, but in all our applications these parameters relate to objects from the classical random walk on the underlying graph and the electrical properties of the graph as an electrical network. For simplicity, we consider the simplest initialisation here, where
\textrm{supp}(\sigma)={s}
. The marked set
M
is either empty or equal to
\{t\}
and it is the goal of the quantum walk to decide which case is true (assuming
t
is reachable from
s
).

The phase estimation algorithm takes place on the
edge space
of our network
G
:

{\cal H}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}u,v\rangle:\{u,v\}\in E(G)\}\}.

(14)

Note that each edge
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
“appears twice” in
\cal{H}
: both as
{\lvert}u,v\rangle
and
{\lvert}v,u\rangle
, which are orthogonal states in
\cal{H}
. This also means that intuitively our quantum walk will “walk on edges”, where as a classical random walk on a graph usually takes place on its vertices.

The space
{\cal A}
is constructed from the span of
star states
. For each vertex
u\in V(G)
, we define the normalised star state of
u\in V(G)
as

\begin{split}{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle&:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\sf w}_{u}}}\sum_{v\in\Gamma(u)}\sqrt{{\sf w}_{u,v}}{\lvert}u,v\rangle,\end{split}

(15)

where
{\sf w}_{u}:=\sum_{v\in\Gamma(u)}{\sf w}_{u,v}
is the
weighted degree
of
u
. This state can be seen as the quantum generalisation of the classical walk transition matrix: by measuring
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle
we obtain the edge
{\lvert}u,v\rangle
with probability
\frac{{\sf w}_{u,v}}{{\sf w}_{u}}
. These star states are used to construct

{\cal A}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle:u\in V\setminus\{s,t\}\}.

(16)

The other subspace
{\cal B}
is the
antisymmetric subspace
of
{\cal H}
:

{\cal B}=\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}u,v\rangle+{\lvert}v,u\rangle:(u,v)\in E(G)\}.

(17)

The last parameter
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
is the star state of
s
, projected onto the symmetric subspace of
{\cal H}
to ensure orthogonality with
{\cal B}
:

{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle=\sqrt{2}(I-\Pi_{\cal B}){\lvert}\psi_{\star}(s)\rangle.

(18)

Since by assumption
s
and
t
are connected, we know that the
s
-
t
electrical flow
\theta
exists and we can construct its corresponding (normalised)
flow state
, which is a quantum representation of the flow living in
{\cal{B}}^{\perp}
(see
(
17
)
):

{\lvert}\theta\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2{\sf E}(\theta)}}\sum_{(u,v)\in{\overrightarrow{E}}(G)}\frac{\theta_{u,v}}{\sqrt{{\sf w}_{u,v}}}\left({\lvert}u,v\rangle+{\lvert}v,u\rangle\right).

(19)

The key observation in
[
43
]
is that, if
M=\{t\}
, the flow
{\lvert}\theta\rangle
lies in
({\cal A}+{\cal B})^{\perp}
and has non-zero overlap with
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle
, meaning that if
s
and
t
are connected,
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle\notin{\cal A}+{\cal B}
. Conversely, if
M=\emptyset
, it is shown that
{\lvert}\psi_{0}\rangle\in{\cal A}+{\cal B}.

III
Representing an MAS as an electrical network

III.1
The mass action system graph

In this work, we represent an MAS as an electrical network, to allow us to run a quantum walk algorithm on this network. To create this electrical network, we base our approach on the bipartite graph representation by
[
63
]
. In this bipartite graph representation, the set of species,
{\cal S}
, forms one partition of the vertices and the set of reactions,
{\cal R}
, forms the other. A directed edge, or
arc
, is drawn between a species
s\in{\cal S}
and a reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in{\cal R}
if and only if species
s
occurs with non-zero stoichiometry in the reactant complex
y
, that is,
y_{s}\neq 0
.
Similarly, an arc is drawn between a reaction
y\to y^{\prime}\in{\cal R}
and a species
s\in{\cal S}
when
s
occurs with non-zero stoichiometry in the product complex
y^{\prime}
. Note that although the formal definition of a chemical reaction network (CRN) includes the set of complexes
{\cal C}
, this representation does not. In many analyses, including our graph-based approach, the essential information provided by the complexes is fully encoded within the reactions themselves. Each reaction inherently describes the transformation from a set of reactant species to a set of product species. Therefore, by representing the CRN as a bipartite graph with one partition corresponding to the species
{\cal S}
and the other to the reactions
{\cal R}
, we retain all critical stoichiometric information. Unlike other graph representations of CRNs, this bipartite graph ensures that each reactant is represented only once while remaining correctly connected to its associated reactions.

To represent an MAS as an electrical network, we are not allowed to have any directed edges (see
Definition
II.1
), meaning that this approach will not suffice. In addition, the thermodynamic machinery discussed in
Section
II.3
also needs our MAS to have additional structure. We will therefore assume in the rest of this work that our MAS
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R},k)
satisfies the following three conditions:

1.

The underlying CRN of our MAS is
reversible
(every reaction
y\to y^{\prime}
appears together with its backward reaction
y^{\prime}\to y
in
{\cal R}
), meaning we can fully characterise
{\cal R}
by the set of oriented reactions
\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
.

2.

The MAS admits a positive equilibrium concentrations vector
c^{*}
that realises
detailed balance
, i.e. the forward and reverse fluxes of every reaction pair coincide at
c^{*}
.

3.

Each reaction in
{\cal R}
is
particle conserving
, meaning that for every
y\to y^{\prime}\in{\cal R}
we have
\sum_{s}y_{s}=\sum_{s}y^{\prime}_{s}
.

In practice, many mechanistic models violate at least one of these assumptions: combustion schemes contain intrinsically irreversible steps, metabolic networks include “committed” reactions, and numerous biological or catalytic processes are deliberately driven far from equilibrium, so that neither a detailed‑balance concentration nor particle conservation holds exactly. Nevertheless, imposing these constraint is a standard assumption to simplify the analysis of chemical kinetics within the CRN framework. Additionally, we shall shortly see that under these assumption, the dynamical properties of our MAS will give rise to a duality with electrical networks.

Under the assumptions of reversibility, detailed balance, and particle conserving, our bipartite graph representation is formally defined as follows:

Definition III.1

(Mass Action System Graph)
.

For an MAS
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R},k)
and a concentrations vector
c\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\cal S}
its mass action system graph (MASG) is a weighted bipartite undirected graph
G=(V,\overline{E},{\sf w})
, where:

•

The vertex set
V
is the disjoint union of species and (reversible) reactions, given by
V={\cal S}\sqcup\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
, where
{\cal S}
is the set of species and
\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
is the set of oriented reactions.

•

The edge set
E\subseteq\{\{s,r\}\mid s\in{\cal S},r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}\}
consists of edges connecting species to reactions. An edge
\{s,r\}
is part of
E
if and only if
s
appears with non-zero stoichiometry in
r
, i.e. at least one of
y_{s},y^{\prime}_{s}
is non zero for
r=y\to y^{\prime}
. The directed edge set
{\overrightarrow{E}}
is obtained by considering the direction
(s,r)
for every
\{s,r\}\in E
.

•

The weight function

is defined on the edge set

, where each edge

is weighted by
\nu_{r}\left|\nu_{r,s}\right|G_{r}

\nu_{r}:=\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\left|\nu_{r,s}\right|

Note that

III.3
The computational cost

Using our electrical network representation of the MAS, we develop quantum walk algorithms to answer structural and kinetic questions about the underlying chemical reaction network. These constructions apply to MASs that are particle-conserving, reversible, and admit a detailed balance condition.

The asymptotic cost of these algorithms relies on the abstract costs from
Section
II.5
:
{\sf S}
, i.e. setting up the state
{\lvert}\sigma\rangle
, and
U_{\star}
, i.e. the cost of implementing the quantum walk operator
U_{\cal{AB}}
from
(
13
)
, or equivalently, the cost of implementing the star states
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle
. The states relevant to these costs are therefore

\begin{split}&{\lvert}\sigma\rangle=\sum_{s\in{\cal S}\setminus M}\sqrt{\eta_{s}}{\lvert}s\rangle,\\
&{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(s)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r}\left|\nu_{r,s}\right|G_{r}}}\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\sqrt{\nu_{r}\left|\nu_{r,s}\right|G_{r}}{\lvert}s,r\rangle,\\
&{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(r)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu_{r}}}\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\sqrt{\nu_{r,s}}{\lvert}r,s\rangle.\end{split}

(26)

For typical applications of our framework it is reasonable to assume that these quantum states can be prepared efficiently. Because we focus on near-equilibrium dynamics (see
Section
II.3
), we assume that the MAS is initially close to a known steady state
c^{*}
and that all thermodynamic quantities required with respect to this equilibrium can be pre-computed classically. Storing these values in read-only QRAM then allows efficient generation of the states in (
26
).

III.4

is reachable from
\sigma
with a constant probability of success in cost

\displaystyle{\sf S}+\sqrt{\Phi(c_{\sigma,M})\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r}^{2}G_{r}}.

Proof.

We invoke
Theorem
II.9
to obtain a quantum walk that runs on the MASG obtained from our MAS. By our choice of weights for the MASG, we know that

{\sf W}=\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\nu_{r}\left|\nu_{r,s}\right|G_{r}=\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r}^{2}G_{r}.

Lastly, we know by
(
25
)
that the energy of the MASG flow allows us to upper bound the effective resistance as follows:

{\sf R}_{\sigma,M}\leq{\sf E}(\theta)=\Phi(c_{\sigma,M}).\qed

Corollary III.4
.

Fix an MAS
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R},k)
that admits a detailed balance, that is particle conserving and whose underlying CRN is reversible. Fix an initial distribution of species
\sigma
on
{\cal S}
and a non-empty set of target species
M
, reachable from
\sigma
. Then there exists a quantum walk algorithm that returns any of the target species from
M
with a constant probability of success in cost

\displaystyle{\sf S}+\sqrt{\Phi(c_{\sigma,M})\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r}^{2}G_{r}}\log^{3}(\left|M\right|).

Proof.

This follows directly from
Theorem
II.10
, by applying the same parameters as in
Corollary

.

IV
Algorithms for the Gibbs free-energy consumption

At a first glance, one might hope to approximate the Gibbs free energy consumption
\Phi(c_{s,M})
using
Theorem
II.11
, since
(
25
)
guarantees that for the MASG flow we have

{\sf R}_{s,M}\leq{\sf E}(\theta)=\Phi(c_{s,M}).

However, the flow
\theta
constructed in
(
25
)
does not, in general, coincide with the true
\sigma
-
M
electrical flow. As a result, we typically have
{\sf R}_{s,M}<\Phi(c_{s,M})
. Consequently, applying
Theorem
II.11
yields only an approximation to a lower bound on
\Phi(c_{s,M})
, and the tightness of this bound is unclear. In this section, we show how we resolve this problem in certain cases.

IV.1
Multidimensional electrical networks

Ref.
[
45
]
introduces the multidimensional quantum walk framework to generalise the standard quantum walk framework, later further formalised in
[
46
]
. A key innovation in this generalisation is the introduction of
alternative neighbourhoods
.

Definition IV.1

(Alternative Neighbourhoods)
.

For a network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
, as in
Definition
II.1
, a set of
alternative neighbourhoods
is a collection of states:

\Psi_{\star}=\{\Psi_{\star}(u)\subset\mathrm{span}\{{\lvert}u,v\rangle:{\lvert}u,v\rangle:v\in\Gamma(u)\}:u\in V\}

such that for all
u\in V
,
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle\in\Psi_{\star}(u)
(see
(
15
)
).
We view the states of
\Psi_{\star}(u)
as different possibilities for
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle
, only one of which is “correct”.

By adding additional alternative neighbourhoods to the set
\Psi_{\cal A}
spanning
{\cal A}
(see
(
16
)
), we obtain
\Psi_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}}
. In the rest of this section, we assume that each
\Psi(u)
is a normalised basis
\{{\lvert}\psi_{u,0}\rangle={\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle,\dots,{\lvert}\psi_{u,a_{u}-1}\rangle\}
, so

\Psi_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}}=\{{\lvert}{\psi}_{u,i}\rangle:u\in V\backslash\left(\{s\}\cup M\right),i\in\{0,\dots,a_{u}-1\}.

Through the addition of these alternative neighbourhoods, we obtain the modified quantum walk operator

U_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}{\cal B}}=(2\Pi_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}}-I)(2\Pi_{{\cal B}}-I),

(27)

Here,
\Pi_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}}
denotes the orthogonal projector onto the modified star space
{\cal A}^{\sf alt}
.

Incorporating these additional neighbourhoods into
\Psi_{\star}(u)
alters the quantum walk operator, and thus the complexity of its implementation. This was central to their original introduction in
[
45
]
, as these constructions are necessary in applications where it is computationally much more efficient to generate the set
\Psi_{\star}(u)
rather than the individual star state
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle
. For example, this situation arises when
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle
is known to be one of a small collection of easily preparable states within
\Psi_{\star}(u)
, but it is computationally infeasible to determine precisely which one. We denote the cost of implementing
U_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}{\cal B}}
by
{\sf U}_{\star}^{\sf alt}
.

It was later shown in
[
47
]
, that the addition of alternative neighbourhoods structure in this way gives rise to the
\sigma
-
M
alternative electrical flow associated with
\Psi_{\star}
. This flow satisfies the
Alternative Kirchhoff’s Law
and
Alternative Ohm’s Law
, which generalise
Definition
II.5
and
Definition
II.6
, respectively.

Definition IV.2

(Kirchhoff’s Alternative Law)
.

For any
s
-
M
alternative flow
\theta^{\sf alt}
with respect to a collection of alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
on an electrical network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
, the corresponding flow state
{\lvert}\theta^{\sf alt}\rangle
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}(\Psi_{\star}(u))
for every
u\in V\backslash\{\{s\}\cup M\}
, that is,
\langle{\psi}_{u,i}|\theta\rangle=0
for each
i\in\{0,1,\cdots,a_{u}-1\}
.

Definition IV.3

(Alternative Electrical Flow)
.

For a collection of alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
on an electrical network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
, the
s
-
M
alternative electrical flow
is the unique alternative unit
s
-
M
flow
\theta^{\sf alt}
with minimal energy
{\sf E}(\theta^{\sf alt})
. We call this minimal energy the
alternative effective resistance

{\sf R}_{s,t}^{\sf alt}
.

Definition IV.4

(Alternative Ohm’s Law)
.

Let
\theta^{\sf alt}
be the
s
-
M
alternative electrical flow with respect to a collection of alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
on an electrical network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
. Then there exists an alternative potential vector
{\sf p}^{\sf alt}
corresponding to
\theta^{\sf alt}
, which assigns a unique potential
{\sf p}^{\sf alt}_{u,v}
to each edge
(u,v)\in E
such that
{\sf p}^{\sf alt}_{s,v}={\sf R}_{s,t}^{\sf alt}
for every
v\in\Gamma(s)
and
{\sf p}^{\sf alt}_{u,v}=0
for every
u\in M
and
v\in\Gamma(u)
. Additionally, the associated state
{\lvert}{\sf p}^{\sf alt}\rangle
(see (16) in
[
47
]
) satisfies
\Pi_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}}{\lvert}{\sf p}^{\sf alt}\rangle={\lvert}{\sf p}^{\sf alt}\rangle
and the potential difference between
(u,v)
and
(v,u)
is equal to the amount of electrical flow
\theta^{\sf alt}_{u,v}
along
(u,v)
multiplied with the resistance
1/{\sf w}_{u,v}
, that is,
{\sf p}^{\sf alt}_{u,v}-{\sf p}^{\sf alt}_{v,u}=\theta^{\sf alt}_{u,v}/{\sf w}_{u,v}
.

The alternative potential
{\sf p}^{\sf alt}
from
Definition
IV.4
gives rise to an alternative escape time, generalising
(
21
)
:

\mathsf{ET}^{\sf alt}_{s,M}:=\frac{1}{{\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}}\sum_{{u,v}\in E}{\sf p}_{u,v}^{2}{\sf w}_{u,v}.

(28)

By using these “alternative” electrical network definitions, Ref.
[
47
]
shows that we can approximate the alternative electrical flow, generalising
Theorem
II.12
to alternative neighbourhoods:

Theorem IV.5

(
[
47
]
)
.

Fix a network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
as in
Definition
II.1
, a collection of alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
, a non-empty marked set
M\subset V
and an initial vertex
s\in V\setminus M
, with the promise that there is a path in
G
connecting
s
to
M
. Let
\theta^{\sf alt}
be the
s
-
M
alternative electrical flow on
G
with corresponding flow state
{\lvert}\theta^{\sf alt}\rangle
as defined in
(
19
)
. Then there exists a quantum walk algorithm that returns a state
{\lvert}\widetilde{\theta}\rangle
satisfying

\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\lvert}\widetilde{\theta}\rangle{\langle}\widetilde{\theta}\rvert-{\lvert}\theta^{\sf alt}\rangle{\langle}\theta^{\sf alt}\rvert\right\|\leq\epsilon

with a constant probability of success in cost

O\left({\sf S}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{ET}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}}+\log({\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf w}_{s})\right){\sf U}_{\star}^{{\sf alt}}\right).

IV.2
Using alternative neighbourhoods to restrict the flow

The technique of alternative neighbourhoods has previously been employed in
[
45
,
47
,
46
]
to address the cases where it is computationally easier to generate
\Psi_{\star}(u)
, which includes additional alternative neighbourhoods, than to construct the star state
{\lvert}\psi_{\star}(u)\rangle
directly. In this section, we exhibit a novel use of this technique: we show how alternative neighbourhoods can be leveraged to approximate the Gibbs free-energy consumption, and to prepare a quantum state that is a superposition over all species-reaction pairs
(r,s)
, weighted by their contribution to the total Gibbs free-energy consumption.

Our approach constructs a collection of alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
such that the MASG flow defined in
(
24
)
is the unique
s
-
M
flow satisfying Alternative Kirchhoff’s Law (see
Definition
IV.2
). By construction (see
Definition
IV.3
), this guarantees that the MASG flow is also the alternative
s
-
M
electrical flow with respect to
\Psi_{\star}
.

This reverses the usual causal relationship between alternative neighbourhoods and the corresponding electrical flow: rather than beginning with a prescribed set
\Psi_{\star}
and checking which flows satisfy Alternative Kirchhoff’s Law, we start from the desired flow and reverse-engineer the alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
so that this flow becomes the alternative
s
-
M
electrical flow relative to
\Psi_{\star}
.

IV.2.2
The general case

Contrary to how alternative neighbourhoods are usually applied, we start with our target MASG flow
\theta
and reverse engineer our alternative neighbourhoods such that
\theta
is precisely the alternative
s
-
M
electrical flow. We start with
r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
, where the normalised projection of
{\lvert}\theta\rangle
(where
\theta
is our MASG flow) onto
\textrm{span}\{{\lvert}r,s\rangle:s\in{\cal S}\}
is equal to

{\lvert}\theta_{r}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\nu_{r,s}^{2}}}\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\nu_{r,s}{\lvert}r,s\rangle.

(31)

As discussed in
Section
III.3
, it is reasonable to assume that this state can be prepared efficiently in practical settings: the ratios of stoichiometric coefficients for the species involved in each reaction can be pre-computed classically and stored in QRAM.

In principle, performing the same preparation for every
s\in\mathcal{S}\setminus M
would always reproduce the MASG flow. Unfortunately, this is not computationally viable in general. For
s\in\mathcal{S}\setminus M
, the normalised projection of
{\lvert}\theta\rangle
onto
\operatorname{span}\{{\lvert}r,s\rangle:r\in\mathcal{R}\}
is

{\lvert}\theta_{s}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r,s}^{2}J_{r}(c_{\sigma,M})^{2}}}\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\nu_{r,s}J_{r}(c_{\sigma,M}){\lvert}r,s\rangle.

(32)

Preparing this state requires knowledge of the relative fluxes
J_{r}(c_{\sigma,M})
for all
r\in\mathcal{R}
. These fluxes depend on the steady-state concentrations
c_{\sigma,M}
, which can be obtained only by solving a global system of polynomial equations for the entire MAS, a task that is computationally infeasible. Consequently, we cannot generate the state in
(
32
)
and must restrict our alternative neighbourhood construction to vertices in
\mathcal{R}
.

The example in
Figure
4
shows that this restriction
can
be sufficient to recover the MASG flow uniquely, but it is not guaranteed for all networks. We therefore confine our attention to the following class of networks:

Definition IV.6

(
\sigma
-

.

Let
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
be a network as in
Definition

, let
M\subset V
be a non-empty marked set, and let
\sigma
be a probability distribution supported on

. We say that
G
is

if the following conditions hold:

•

is bipartite with vertex partition

,

•

is supported entirely on

,

•

For each

, we fix a ratio vector

, supported on the neighbours of

,

•

Under these conditions, there exists a
unique

s
-

flow
\theta
such that for all

and all

such that
(b,a_{1}),(b,a_{2})\in E
and

,

Provided the MASG is

IV.3
Approximating and sampling from the Gibbs free-energy consumptions

Our quantum algorithm to estimate the Gibbs free-energy consumption is an extension of
Theorem
II.11
that accommodates the use of alternative neighbourhoods:

Theorem IV.7

(
[
44
]
)
.

Fix a network
G=(V,E,{\sf w})
as in
Definition
II.1
, a collection of alternative neighbourhoods
\Psi_{\star}
, a non-empty marked set
M\subset V
and an initial vertex
s\in V\setminus M
, with the promise that there is a path in
G
connecting
s
to
M
. Then there exists a quantum walk algorithm that
\epsilon
-multiplicatively estimates
{\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}
with a constant probability of success in cost

O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left({\sf S}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\mathsf{ET}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}+\log({\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf w}_{s})\right){\sf U}_{\star}^{\sf alt}\right)\right).

Proof.

The proof combines the strategy of the proof of
Theorem
II.11
from
[
44
]
, with Theorem 1.1 and Lemma 2.12 from
[
47
]
. These theorems state that if we apply phase estimation with precision
O(\epsilon/({\sf ET}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf w}_{s})
to the modified quantum walk operator
U_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}{\cal B}}
from
(
27
)
, the probability of the phase estimation register yielding “0” is
p^{\prime}=(1\pm\epsilon)/({\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf w}_{s})
. Recall that
U_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}{\cal B}}
is designed such that the MASG flow (see
(
24
)
) matches the alternative electrical flow, per
Theorem
IV.5
.

Hence by combining this with quantum amplitude estimation
[
64
,
65
]
, we obtain an
\epsilon
-multiplicative estimate of
p^{\prime}
at the cost of
O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\log(\frac{1}{p^{\prime}})\right)
calls to the phase estimation routine.

The proof is concluded by modifying the quantum walk with the standard quantum walk technique that ensures that
{\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf w}_{s}=O(1)
, incuring an extra
\log({\sf R}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}{\sf w}_{s})
(see
[
43
,
44
]
)
∎

Once the alternative
s
-
M
electrical flow is forced to coincide with the MASG flow, we can estimate its energy, namely the quantity
\Phi(c_{s,M})
defined in
(
25
)
. Note that the quantity
{\sf w}_{s}
in this case is equal to
\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}:s\in r}\nu_{r}\left|\nu_{r,s}\right|G_{r}
, which can be precomputed with the thermodynamical information stored in the (Q)RAM. For ease of notation however, we shall keep it abbreviated as
{\sf w}_{s}
.

Corollary IV.8
.

Fix an MAS
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R},k)
that admits a detailed balance, that is particle conserving and whose underlying CRN is reversible. Fix an initial species
s
and a non-empty set of target species

and assume that the resulting MASG is

-

\epsilon
-multiplicatively estimates the instantaneous rate of Gibbs free–energy consumption
\Phi(c_{s,M})
with a constant probability of success in cost

O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left({\sf S}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left(\mathsf{ET}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}+\log\left(\Phi(c_{s,M}){\sf w}_{s}\right)\right){\sf U}_{\star}^{\sf alt}\right)\right).

Additionally, by employing
Theorem
IV.5
, we can approximate, and subsequently sample from, the state

{\lvert}\theta\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Phi(c_{s,M})}}\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\sum_{r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}}\frac{\theta_{s,r}}{\sqrt{{\sf w}_{s,r}}}\left({\lvert}s,r\rangle+{\lvert}r,s\rangle\right).

Corollary IV.9
.

Fix an MAS
({\cal S},{\cal C},{\cal R},k)
that admits a detailed balance, that is particle conserving and whose underlying CRN is reversible. Fix an initial species
s
and a non-empty set of target species
M
and assume that the resulting MASG is
s
-
M
rigid. Let
\theta
be the MASG flow (see

)
). Then there exists a quantum walk algorithm that returns a state
{\lvert}\widetilde{\theta}\rangle
satisfying

\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\lvert}\widetilde{\theta}\rangle{\langle}\widetilde{\theta}\rvert-{\lvert}\theta\rangle{\langle}\theta\rvert\right\|\leq\epsilon

with a constant probability of success in cost

O\left({\sf S}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{ET}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}}+\log(\Phi(c_{s,M}){\sf w}_{s})\right){\sf U}_{\star}^{{\sf alt}}\right).

Additionally, we can return an
\epsilon
-approximation of
J_{r}(c_{\sigma,M})^{2}/G_{r}
for any
r\in\overrightarrow{{\cal R}}
with a constant probability of success in cost

O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left({\sf S}+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\mathsf{ET}_{s,M}^{\sf alt}}+\log(\Phi(c_{s,M}){\sf w}_{s})\right){\sf U}_{\star}^{{\sf alt}}\right)\right).

Proof.

The first part of the corollary follows directly from applying
Theorem
IV.5
with our modified quantum walk operator
U_{{\cal A}^{\sf alt}{\cal B}}
, ensuring that the MASG flow (see
(
24
)
) matches the alternative electrical flow.

For the second part, note that measuring the resulting state

yields an edge adjacent to reaction

with a probability that is

\frac{1}{\Phi(c_{s,M})}\sum_{s\in{\cal S}}\frac{\theta_{s,r}^{2}}{{\sf w}_{s,r}}=\frac{J_{r}(c_{\sigma,M})^{2}}{G_{r}}.

We can therefore approximate this quantity by applying quantum amplitude estimation.
∎
