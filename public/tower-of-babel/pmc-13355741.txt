PACS-ILT: A proposed morphobiochemical scoring system for rupture risk stratification of asymptomatic abdominal aortic aneurysms
Authors: Mohammad Ghanem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIntraluminal thrombus (ILT) is increasingly recognized as a dynamic factor in abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) progression. It can play a dual role—providing mechanical cushioning while simultaneously destabilizing the aortic wall through its biochemical activity. Fissuring and bleeding within the ILT remain underinvestigated but may serve as early indicators of wall failure, independent of aneurysm size. Our aim was to propose a novel biology-driven framework—the PACS-ILT classification (Proteolytic Activity and Clinical Stability of Intraluminal Thrombus)—to refine the assessment of rupture risk by integrating thrombus biology, imaging characteristics, and patient-specific factors.MethodsA targeted literature review of biomechanical and biochemical studies was conducted to inform the development of the PACS-ILT system. The weighting of individual parameters was guided by their reported impact on aortic wall vulnerability.ResultsThe analysis indicates that diameter-based risk assessment alone does not capture the local pathophysiological changes of the aortic wall. Two key controllers of rupture risk beyond size were identified: (1) biomechanical effects, in which ILT thickness and morphology significantly influence wall stress distribution; and (2) biochemical effects, in which proteolytic activity, including matrix metalloproteinases and imbalances in their inhibitors, contributes to mural degradation. Integrating these findings with established clinical risk factors, the PACS-ILT algorithm stratifies rupture risk on a 1 to 5 scale (P1-P5). Scores are assigned across four domains: Geometric (G), Morphologic (M), Biochemical (B), and Clinical (C). A key insight is the recognition of “proteolytic hyperactivity” within thin ILTs, which may explain early rupture in small AAAs. Conversely, some larger aneurysms may remain relatively stable due to the “thrombus paradox,” where a thick, mature ILT serves as a mechanical shield.ConclusionsPACS-ILT provides a conceptual, hypothesis-generating framework that moves AAA risk assessment beyond size alone toward a more dynamic, biology-informed approach. Future validation—incorporating computed tomography-based morphology, fluid-structure interaction, and computational fluid dynamics simulations—is required to confirm its utility in identifying “small-but-unstable” aneurysms and preventing catastrophic rupture.

Although maximum diameter remains the gold standard in clinical management, vascular surgeons frequently encounter a paradox: “aggressive” small aneurysms that rupture unexpectedly and stable “giant” ones that remain asymptomatic for years. This reality proves that rupture risk is not just a linear function of size but rather a complex interplay of biomechanical and chemical forces. In clinical practice, an actual rupture is catastrophic, with mortality rates between 30% and 60% for those who reach the emergency room.1,2 Structural warning signs within the thrombus are often overlooked. Fissuring or internal bleeding within the intraluminal thrombus (ILT) is a major red flag that signals a dangerously weakened wall interface, yet it rarely takes center stage in modern guidelines.3, 4, 5, 6, 7

Our current reliance on diameter is rooted in landmark trials such as United Kingdom Small Aneurysm Trial (UKSAT), Aneurysm Detection and Management (ADAM), Comparison of Surveillance Versus Aortic Endografting for Small Aneurysm Repair (CAESAR), and Positive Impact of Endovascular Options for Treating Aneurysms Early (PIVOTAL), which collectively confirmed that immediate surgery for aneurysms under 5.5 cm generally offers no survival advantage over careful surveillance. Although the Endovascular Aneurysm Repair Trial 1 (EVAR-1) and Dutch Randomized Endovascular Aneurysm Management (DREAM) trials highlighted that endovascular repair is a safer short-term option for large aneurysms, the PIVOTAL investigators shifted the conversation toward a more nuanced assessment of individual rupture risk and patient life expectancy.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

This is where understanding the biology of the ILT becomes vital. Rather than a stagnant clot, the ILT acts as a “biological reactor.”14, 15, 16, 17, 18 It can provide a mechanical cushion, but it also harbors an “enzymatic bath” where proteases such as matrix metalloproteinases-9 (MMP-9) reach concentrations five times higher than those in the blood—effectively digesting the aortic wall from the inside out. Because current risk models largely ignore this biochemical assault, This paper proposes the Proteolytic Activity and Clinical Stability of Intraluminal Thrombus (PACS-ILT) scoring system. By integrating morphological imaging with these biological signals of decay, this paper aims to move toward a personalized, biology-informed strategy to identify high-risk small aneurysms before it is too late.

Methods

Literature review

Owing to the limited number of studies directly addressing this topic, a narrative literature review was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, ResearchGate, and ScienceDirect. Searches were performed using combinations of the terms “intraluminal thrombus,” “fissure,” “rupture,” “abdominal aortic aneurysm,” “MMP,” “TIMP,” and “elastase,” guided by relevant Medical Subject Heading terms. Reference lists of key articles were also screened to identify additional pertinent studies.

Discussion

This article proposes a new scoring system that accounts for morphological and biochemical changes in the aortic wall. This system integrates the established morphological features into a new classification framework and adds the biomarkers MMP-9 and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase (TIMP)/MMP as indicators of ILT proteolytic activity. ILTs form in the abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) due to sequential and biomechanical changes.

How the ILT starts: Flow and biology

To understand why a 4.8-cm aneurysm fails whereas a 7.0-cm one holds, one must look at how the thrombus forms and evolves.

The role of turbulent flow

Aneurysm dilation triggers the backward-facing step phenomenon, creating vortical structures at the inlet.22,42 This flow architecture activates a signal cascade between the endothelium and platelets under high wall shear stress (WSS). Biasetti et al22,42 noted that although these high-speed vortices activate platelets, they cannot adhere to the wall until reaching low-WSS43 “recirculation zones” where the flow slows (Fig 1).Fig 1Intraluminal thrombus (ILT) formation dynamics. The “biological reactor” phase

Once the initial platelet layer has adhered, the ILT transforms into an active biological environment. A transductive microinflammatory reaction on the luminal surface triggers neutrophil agglutination, which, along with activated platelets, forms an “enzymatic bath.” This process develops a network of microcanaliculi (a microscopic “plumbing system”) that funnels destructive proteases, such as MMP-9 and neutrophil elastase, from the surface directly to the aortic wall. Modeling the stress

To investigate the pathogenetic effects of WSS and hemodynamic changes on ILT development, fluid-structure interaction4,22 modeling is essential. Accurate simulations require treating blood as a non-Newtonian fluid, as its viscosity varies with the velocity. The ILT thickness paradox

The thrombus thickness paradox directly challenges the conventional “bigger is worse” paradigm in AAA management. Di Martino et al5, 6, 7 and Arslan and Salman45 demonstrated that a thick, old, and compacted thrombus can stabilize the aneurysm by acting as a protective mechanical buffer, or “shield,” for the aortic wall. However, this mechanical protection comes at a biological cost; Vorp et al19 reported that increasing ILT thickness significantly enhances hypoxic effects on the underlying tissue by attenuating oxygen diffusion. This localized mural hypoxia leads to tissue weakening, reducing wall strength by approximately 36%.

Conversely, Siennicka et al17 showed that a thin ILT (<10 mm) is often fresh and porous, facilitating a significant increase in the “enzymatic bath” effect and proteolytic activity against the wall. Kazi et al14,15 further demonstrated that these thrombus-covered walls exhibit higher levels of smooth muscle cell apoptosis and matrix degradation. Although some models, such as those by Creisher et al,40 showed that increased thrombus volume is associated with higher rupture risk, the PACS-ILT framework recognizes that a thin, highly “bioactive” ILT creates a direct path for proteolytic attack that standard 5.5 cm guidelines fail to capture (Figs 4 and 5).Fig 4Pathophysiology of thin intraluminal thrombus (ILT) and rupture risk. Fissuring and the enzymatic bath

ILT fissuring and “in-bleeding” indicate significant thrombus instability, resulting from the combined impact of mechanical loading and biochemical remodeling. Repetitive, pulsatile pressure fluctuations paired with proteolytic hyperactivity drive the development of microfissures within the thrombus matrix. Likewise, bleeding into the ILT without active rupture weakens the thrombus's protective role, allowing high-pressure blood jets to reach an aortic wall already compromised by the earlier “enzymatic bath” (Fig 6).4,46,47 Although a homogeneous ILT possesses a stable, concentric fibrin matrix, a heterogeneous architecture signals a dangerous, high-turnover environment defined by active thrombogenesis and internal microtunneling.Fig 6Intraluminal thrombus (ILT) structural failure modes. Beyond the paradigm of absolute diameter

Although diameter remains a cornerstone of risk assessment, its relevance varies across patient populations. This challenge was first addressed by introducing the aortic size index by Davies et al,29 which showed that rupture risk is more accurately a function of diameter relative to body surface area. However, as emphasized by Tanweer et al,28 aortic height index is a more robust clinical alternative to the aortic size index. Unlike weight, which can fluctuate significantly, height remains a fixed anatomical criterion.

The PACS-ILT framework

The PACS-ILT framework integrates multidomain factors into a single, biology-informed grading system for stable, asymptomatic patients. At its core, geometrical size remains the backbone of the score, but it is heavily refined by the primary modifier: biochemical proteolytic hyperactivity, in which elevated markers like MMP-9 function as an “engine” of destabilization. As a secondary modifier, ILT morphology determines mural stability; thin, porous, “bioactive” thrombi (<10 mm) facilitate a destructive “enzymatic bath,” whereas thick, “bioinactive” thrombi provide a protective “thrombotic tamponade” by physically blocking the transthrombotic canalicular system. Baseline clinical factors act as lesser modifiers that influence the interventional trajectory based on the patient frailty and systemic health. The pharmacological triad—antiplatelet therapy (ASA/clopidogrel), statins, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system/beta-blockers—promotes a compact, stabilized architecture. This is balanced against the dual effect of renal insufficiency (chronic kidney disease): Although moderate impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate >30 mL/min) adds a risk factor that could urge the repair (+1 point), severe chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min) acts as a negative modifier (−1 point) because the devastating 39% postoperative mortality rate may outweigh the benefit of repair. Similarly, overall life expectancy is integrated to prevent overtreatment in the high-risk cohorts; a predicted 5-year mortality of <50% reduces the score (−2 points), whereas an extreme 5-year mortality risk of <20% further de-escalates the intervention priority (−4 points). Risk categorization and clinical action

The total score determines the PACS-ILT grade, which dictates whether a patient follows a trajectory of intensified surveillance or immediate interventional repair. To demonstrate the model's clinical utility, case simulations (Figs 8 and 9) illustrate the system's ability to differentiate between divergent risk profiles. For example, the “mean devil” represents a small (4.8 cm) but high-risk aneurysm characterized by a thin, bioactive ILT and high proteolytic activity, triggering a P4 grade for planning a repair. In contrast, the “stable giant” represents a large (7 cm) AAA shielded by a thick, compact, “bioinactive” ILT. When further stabilized by the “pharmacological triad” and the “diabetes paradox,” this patient maintains a lower grade (P1), allowing for personalized surveillance despite exceeding traditional size thresholds (Tables III and IV). Economic foresight and diagnostic insurance

A preliminary cost-benefit analysis suggests that using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits for systemic biomarkers (MMP-1-MMP-9, TIMPs, and elastases) is economically justified for AAA monitoring. Collected via venous sampling during routine follow-ups, these moderate-cost assays act as “diagnostic insurance” by preventing the catastrophic expenses associated with aneurysm rupture, especially in high-risk small aneurysms. Biomarker-guided protocols further optimize patient management by increasing follow-up efficiency and reducing the frequency of unnecessary imaging or premature surgical interventions. Currently, most available kits are for research use only, necessitating further clinical validation before standard implementation.

Limitations and future research

The PACS-ILT framework is a conceptual starting point rather than a final clinical tool. Significant hurdles include the lack of standardized, low-cost biochemical assays for markers such as MMP-9 and TIMP-1, as well as the high cost and specialized nature of molecular magnetic resonance imaging for routine follow-ups. Current point weighting relies on retrospective and laboratory data, reflecting biological urgency rather than absolute rupture rates. Consequently, these scores require real-world prospective testing through dedicated, multicenter cohorts to confirm their ability to identify the high-risk cases before catastrophic failure.

Conclusions

The PACS-ILT model moves beyond simple geometry to capture underlying biology, identifying patients with high-proteolytic activity who remain at high risk even at small diameters. This framework aims to carve a path to solving the “size riddle” by distinguishing between the thin, aggressive ILT that threatens a small aneurysm and the thick, mature ILT that may stabilize a much larger one. By treating signs such as fissuring and biochemical “in-bleeding” as primary red flags, imminent ruptures that size-based criteria would miss could be caught. Future research integrating computed tomography morphology, fluid-structure interaction simulations through computational fluid dynamics/finite element analysis, and biochemical profiling will be key to refining this tool. Although prospective validation is still required, this system is a necessary step toward a more personalized, biology-informed approach to AAA management.
