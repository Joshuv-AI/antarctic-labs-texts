Universally Wheeler Languages
Ruben Becker, Giuseppa Castiglione, Giovanna D’Agostino, Alberto Policriti, Nicola Prezza, Antonio Restivo, Brian Riccardi

Abstract
The notion of Wheeler languages is rooted in the Burrows-Wheeler transform (BWT), one of the most central concepts in data compression and indexing. The BWT has been generalized to finite automata, the so-called Wheeler automata, by Gagie et al. [Theor. Comput. Sci. 2017]. Wheeler languages have subsequently been defined as the class of regular languages for which there exists a Wheeler automaton accepting them. Besides their advantages in data indexing, these Wheeler languages also satisfy many interesting properties from a language theoretic point of view [Alanko et al., Inf. Comput. 2021]. A characteristic yet unsatisfying feature of Wheeler languages however is that their definition depends on a fixed order of the alphabet. In this paper we introduce the Universally Wheeler languages 𝖴𝖶 {\mathsf{UW}} , i.e., the regular languages that are Wheeler with respect to all orders of a given alphabet. Our first main contribution is to relate 𝖴𝖶 {\mathsf{UW}} to some very well known regular language classes. We first show that the Striclty Locally Testable languages are strictly included in 𝖴𝖶 {\mathsf{UW}} . After noticing that 𝖴𝖶 {\mathsf{UW}} is not closed under taking the complement, we prove that the class of languages for which both the language and its complement are in 𝖴𝖶 {\mathsf{UW}} exactly coincides with those languages that are Definite or Reverse Definite Secondly, we prove that deciding if a regular language given by a DFA is in 𝖴𝖶 {\mathsf{UW}} can be done in quadratic time. We also show that this is optimal unless the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis ( 𝖲𝖤𝖳𝖧 \mathsf{SETH} ) fails.



1
Introduction

The probably most powerful single algorithmic primitive for indexing data is sorting. The Burrows-Wheeler transform (BWT) is a string transformation that is based on sorting all rotations of a given string. It is at the base of the most efficient text indexes developed in the last three decades such as, for example, the FM index
[
9
]
and the
r
-index
[
11
]
.
This technique of indexing compressed data based on sorting has been extended to sets of strings that can be described by finite automata, i.e., regular languages, by Gagie et al.
[
10
]
. The class of automata that permits sorting its states so that the underlying language can be indexed was termed
Wheeler automata
. Subsequently, there has been an interesting line of works
[
1
,
2
,
6
]
that aims at understanding the class of regular languages accepted by such automata, the so-called
Wheeler languages
.

The definition of Wheeler languages is, however, characterized by the peculiarity that it depends on a fixed total order of the alphabet. This peculiarity is inherited from the definition of Wheeler automata as follows. Given a totally ordered alphabet, a language is called Wheeler if there exists an automaton accepting that language, which is Wheeler with respect to this given order. This dependency of an alphabet order is unsatisfying for several reasons. Firstly, from a practical point of view, in terms of the original indexing problem, this alphabet order is not very meaningful as any order of the alphabet is equally interesting, e.g. after a suitable alphabet remapping. Secondly, from a more theoretical perspective, if we want to understand the characteristics of Wheeler languages by relating them to other classes of regular languages, those classes typically do not depend on alphabet orders and thus such a relation is not easily possible.

There are two canonical ways of removing this dependency of an alphabet order: to consider the languages for which there
exists
an alphabet order that makes
the language Wheeler or to consider those languages that are Wheeler with respect to
all
alphabet orders. The first approach was followed by D’Agostino et al.
[
7
]
leading to the class of
Existentially Wheeler
languages
{\mathsf{EW}}
(named
Generalized Wheeler
in
[
7
]
). However, their approach is unsatisfying from a computational viewpoint. While the Wheeler language recognition problem, i.e., the problem of checking if a language
\mathcal{L}
is Wheeler given a DFA accepting it, can be solved in quadratic time in the size of the DFA
[
2
]
, the problem of deciding whether
\mathcal{L}
is in
{\mathsf{EW}}
is NP-complete
[
7
]
, even when the language is given by a DFA.

Our contribution.

In this article we follow the second approach by defining the class of
Universally Wheeler languages

{\mathsf{UW}}
, i.e., those languages that are Wheeler with respect to every order of the alphabet. We present two main contributions in this area. (1) We manage to relate
{\mathsf{UW}}
to some very well known classes of regular languages, namely the
Definite
(
{\mathsf{DEF}}
),
Reverse Definite
(
{\mathsf{RDEF}}
), and the
Strictly Locally Testable
(
{\mathsf{SLT}}
) languages (Section
3
).
More precisely, we first show that
{\mathsf{SLT}}
is strictly included in
{\mathsf{UW}}
. As
{\mathsf{UW}}
is not closed under taking the complement, we then consider the class of languages
\mathcal{L}
such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
belong to
{\mathsf{UW}}
and prove that this class coincides with
{\mathsf{DEF}}\cup{\mathsf{RDEF}}
.
(2) From a computational point of view, we prove that
{\mathsf{UW}}
behaves better than
{\mathsf{EW}}
: checking whether the language accepted by a given DFA is in
\mathsf{UW}
can be done in quadratic time in the size of the DFA (Section
4
). We complete this algorithmic result with a fine-grained complexity lower bound showing that no truly subquadratic time algorithm is possible unless the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (
\mathsf{SETH}
) fails.

Due to space limitations, some of the proofs are moved to the appendix.

Preliminaries

Automata.

A
deterministic finite automaton
(DFA) is a tuple
\mathcal{D}=(Q,s,\Sigma,\delta,F)
, where
Q
is a finite non-empty set of
states
,
s
is the

initial state
(or
source
),
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\rightarrow Q
is the (possibly partial)

transition function
, and
F\subseteq Q
is the set of
final states
.

If
\delta
is not defined on
(q,\sigma)
we write
\delta(q,\sigma)=\bot
. If
\delta
is a total function we say that the DFA is
complete
. We sometimes omit the alphabet
\Sigma
and simply write a DFA as a tuple
\mathcal{D}=(Q,s,\delta,F)
. If we are dealing with a single DFA, the letters
Q,s,\delta,F
always refer to the set of
\mathcal{D}
-states, the
\mathcal{D}
-initial state etc. Sometimes we shall describe the transition function
\delta
using triples (edges), where
(q,q^{\prime},a)
stands for
q^{\prime}=\delta(q,a)
.

We denote with
|\delta|
and
|Q|
the number of edges and states of an automaton
\mathcal{D}=(Q,s,\delta,F)
, and with
|\mathcal{D}|=|Q|+|\delta|
its total size.

As customary, we extend
\delta
to strings as follows: for all
q\in Q
,
a\in\Sigma
and
\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}
, we define

\delta(q,\varepsilon)=q
and
\delta(q,\alpha a)=\delta(\delta(q,\alpha),a)
,
where
\delta(\bot,a)=\bot
.

Given a DFA
\mathcal{D}=(Q,s,\Sigma,\delta,F)
and any of its states
q\in Q
, we denote with
I_{q}
the set of all strings labeling paths starting from the source of
\mathcal{D}
and ending in
q
, i.e.,
I_{q}=\{\alpha\in\Sigma^{*}\ :\ \delta(s,\alpha)=q\}
. The language
accepted
(or
recognized
) by
\mathcal{D}
is then defined as
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})=\cup_{q\in F}I_{q}
.
Moreover, for any
q\in Q
, we denote by
\lambda(q)
the set of labels of all transitions whose endpoint is
q
, i.e.,

\lambda(q)\coloneq\left\{\,{a\in\Sigma}:{(\exists p\in Q)(\delta(p,a)=q)}\,\right\}
.

We mostly consider
trimmed
DFAs, where every state is reachable from the initial state and can reach at least one final state. This is not restrictive: every automaton can be turned into a trimmed and equivalent one by simply deleting unreachable states as well as states not reaching at least one final state.
For trimmed automata the following hold:
there can be at most one state without incoming edges, namely
s
, and
every string that can be read starting from
s
belongs to the set of prefixes,
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})
, of the language
\mathcal{L}
.

The languages accepted by automata form the class of
regular languages
.
Given a regular language
\mathcal{L}
, there exists a unique (up to isomorphism) state-wise minimum
complete
DFA recognizing
\mathcal{L}
.
The states of this minimum DFA correspond to the classes of the Myhill-Nerode equivalence relation
\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}
on
\Sigma^{*}
, defined as follows:

\alpha\equiv_{\mathcal{L}}\beta\iff\{\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\alpha\gamma\in\mathcal{L}\}=\{\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\beta\gamma\in\mathcal{L}\}.

We denote by
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
the
complete
DFA having as set of states the classes
[\alpha]_{\mathcal{L}}
of the Myhill-Nerode equivalence,
s=[\varepsilon]_{\mathcal{L}}
,

\delta([\alpha]_{\mathcal{L}},a)=[\alpha\cdot a]_{\mathcal{L}},

and
F=\{[\alpha]_{\mathcal{L}}\mid\,\alpha\in\mathcal{L}\}
.
Then it can be proved that
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
is the DFA with minimum number of states among all complete DFA recognizing
\mathcal{L}
and it is unique up to isomorphism.
Let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
be the DFA obtained from
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
by considering only the classes
[\alpha]_{\mathcal{L}}
with
\alpha\in\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})
and the transitions among them. Then one can easily check that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
recognizes
\mathcal{L}
, it is trimmed, and can differ from
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
for (at most) one non-final
absorbing
state — that is, a state
q\in Q
such that
\delta(q,a)=q
, for all
a\in\Sigma
.
The automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
is the DFA with minimum number of states among (complete or not) DFAs recognizing
\mathcal{L}
.

Orders.

If
(Z,\leq)
is a partial order, we denote by
(Z,<)
its corresponding strict partial order.

If
\preceq
is a total order on the alphabet
\Sigma
, we extend it to strings in
\Sigma^{*}

co-lexicographically
, that is, for
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma^{*}
, we have
\alpha\preceq\beta
if and only if either
\alpha
is a suffix of
\beta
(denoted by
\alpha\dashv\beta
), or there exist
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}
and
a,b\in\Sigma
, such that
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}a\gamma
and
\beta=\beta^{\prime}b\gamma
and
a\prec b
.

Wheeler Automata and Wheeler Languages.

Wheeler Automata are a special class of DFAs that leverage an a priori fixed order of the alphabet in order to achieve, among other things, efficient compression and indexing (see
[
10

Definition 1

(Wheeler Automaton)
.

Let

(W2) if

,

, then
p^{\prime}<q^{\prime}
.

, that is,

for all states

. Any DFA can be transformed into an equivalent input consistent DFA in
\mathcal{O}\big(|Q|\cdot|\Sigma|\big)
time by simply creating, for each state
q\in Q
, at most
|\Sigma|
copies of
q
, one for each different incoming label of
q
.

It can be proved (see

) that, if such a total order satisfying Definition

\preceq
on

in which the initial state has no incoming edges, we can define a partial order

.
Then it can be proved that

In this case, we can decide whether

Switching from automata to languages we define Wheeler languages
[
1
]
.

Definition 2
.

A language is
Wheeler
if there exists a WDFA recognizing it.

One can easily generalize Definition
1
to WNFA and
prove that WDFA and WNFA have the same expressive power in terms of accepted languages (see
[
1
, Theorem 3.16]
).
We denote by
{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
the class of all regular languages that are Wheeler w.r.t. the order
\preceq
.
In
[
2
]
a characterization of languages which are not in
{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
was based on the existence of particular cycles in the product automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}
.
Here we shall use a variant of this result based on the following definition:

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}=(Q,\Sigma,\delta,s,F)
be a DFA and let
\preceq
be an order of the alphabet. If
p,q\in Q
we define:

\displaystyle p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q

\displaystyle\iff\exists\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{p},~\exists\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in I_{q}~(\alpha\prec\beta)\wedge(\beta^{\prime}\prec\alpha^{\prime}).

\displaystyle p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}q

\displaystyle\iff\exists\preceq~p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q.

Recall that the automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}
is defined by
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}=(Q\times Q,\Sigma,\delta^{\prime},(s,s),F\times F)
where
\delta^{\prime}((p,q),a)=(\delta(p,a),\delta(q,a))
. Then we have:

Lemma 1

(
[
2
, Theorem 3]
)

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular language, let
\preceq
be an order of the alphabet, and let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
be the minimum trimmed DFA accepting
\mathcal{L}
. Then,

\mathcal{L}\notin{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
if and only if
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}
contains a cycle
(p_{1},q_{1})\rightarrow(p_{2},q_{2})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(p_{k},q_{k})\rightarrow(p_{1},q_{1})
such that
the following hold: (i)
p_{1}\neq q_{1}
, and (ii)
p_{1}\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q_{1}
.

It is not difficult to prove that the class
{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
is closed under intersection.
However, it is neither closed under complement nor union: indeed, there are examples of languages
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
for which no
complete
WDFA recognizing
\mathcal{L}
exists (see
[
1
]
) and this prevent the possibility of finding a WDFA for the complement by simply switching final and non-final states
[
5
]
.

One peculiarity of the Wheeler notion is that it depends on a fixed order of the alphabet.
In order to compare Wheeler languages with other sub-classes of regular languages, which do not depend on a fixed order of the alphabet, it is natural to ask for a definition that is independent of such an order. Two natural options are the following.

Definition 4
.

A regular language
\mathcal{L}
is:

-

Existentially Wheeler
if there exists an order
\preceq
such that
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
;

-

Universally Wheeler
if
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
for all orders
\preceq
of the alphabet.

The class of Existentially (Universally) Wheeler languages is denoted by
{\mathsf{EW}}
(
{\mathsf{UW}}
).

In
[
8
]
it is proved that deciding
\mathsf{EW}
is
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete. In Section
4
we shall prove that deciding
\mathsf{UW}
is in
{\mathsf{P}}
, but first in the next lemma we give a necessary condition that languages in
\mathsf{EW}
must satisfy.

Lemma 2

If the minimum trimmed automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
of a language
\mathcal{L}
contains three equally labeled cycles starting from three different states, then
\mathcal{L}\not\in{\mathsf{EW}}
.

Note that the condition of Lemma
2
can easily be checked in cubic time.

3
Definite and Reverse Definite Languages meet Wheelerness

We first notice that the class
{\mathsf{UW}}
is not closed under complement (see Example
2
in the appendix).
In this section we give a characterization of the class of languages
\mathcal{L}
for which both
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
are in the class
{\mathsf{UW}}
. This characterization involves two well known subclasses of star-free languages, the definite and reverse definite ones
[
3
]
. Remember that, given two languages
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
the set containing all concatenations of a word in
\mathcal{L}
with a word in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
(with
\mathcal{L}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
equal to the empty language if either one of
\mathcal{L},\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is).

Definition 5
.

A regular language
\mathcal{L}
is:

definite
(
\mathsf{DEF}
) if
\mathcal{L}=F\cup\Sigma^{*}G
, for finite
F,G\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
;

reverse definite
(
\mathsf{RDEF}
) if
\mathcal{L}=F\cup G\Sigma^{*}
, for finite
F,G\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
;

strictly locally testable
(
\mathsf{SLT}
, see
[
4
]
) if there are finite sets
H,K,W,F
s.t.

\mathcal{L}=F\cup(H\Sigma^{*}\cap\Sigma^{*}K)\setminus\Sigma^{*}W\Sigma^{*}
.

Our aim is to prove that the class
{\mathsf{DEF}}\cup{\mathsf{RDEF}}
consists in exactly the
{\mathsf{UW}}
-languages for which the complement is in
{\mathsf{UW}}
as well.
First, using a characterization due to Caron
[
4
]
, we compare
{\mathsf{SLT}}
and
{\mathsf{UW}}
languages.

Lemma 3

(
[
4
, Proposition 3.2 and 3.3]
)

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular language and let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
be its minimum trimmed DFA. Then
\mathcal{L}\not\in{\mathsf{SLT}}
if and only if there exists a pair of equally labeled cycles
\mathcal{C}_{p},\mathcal{C}_{q}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
starting respectively from states
p\neq q
.

Since a pair of equally labeled cycles in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
corresponds to a single cycle in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}
, combining the previous lemma with Lemma
1
we obtain:

Lemma 4

{\mathsf{SLT}}\subseteq{\mathsf{UW}}
. In particular, all languages in
{\mathsf{DEF}}\cup{\mathsf{RDEF}}
are in
{\mathsf{UW}}
.

In order to prove that the class of regular languages
\mathcal{L}
for which both
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
are in
{\mathsf{UW}}
coincides with the class
{\mathsf{DEF}}\cup{\mathsf{RDEF}}
we need a careful inspection, for a regular language
\mathcal{L}
, on the differences between the minimum complete DFA for
\mathcal{L}
,
D^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
, its trimmed version
D_{\mathcal{L}}
, the minimum complete DFA for
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
,
D^{c}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
, and its trimmed version

D_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
, as in the following remark.

Remark 1
.

Since the Myhill-Nerode equivalence on
\Sigma^{*}
is the same for
\mathcal{L}
and
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
, we have that
D^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
and
D^{c}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
only differ for the final states, which in
D^{c}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
is the complement of the one in
D^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
.
Let
Q
be the set of
D^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
-states.
Consider the subsets
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}\subseteq Q

defined as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{q\in Q\mid I_{q}\cap(\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}}))\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle Q_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{q\in Q\mid I_{q}\cap(\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}}))\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle Q_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{q\in Q\mid I_{q}\cap(\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}}))\neq\emptyset\}.

States in
Q_{1}
are final for
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{\mathcal{L}}
and can reach only final states, hence, by minimality,
|Q_{1}|\leq 1
. Moreover,
|Q_{1}|=1
iff
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})\neq\emptyset
, and if

then

is an absorbing state (that is,

, for all

) and it is final for
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{{\mathcal{L}}}
.
The analogous holds for

with respect to
\mathcal{D}^{c}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
:
|Q_{2}|=1
iff
\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\neq\emptyset
, and if

then

is an absorbing state which is final in

.
Finally,
D_{\mathcal{L}}
is obtained from

by (eventually) erasing the absorbing state

and all transition arriving in
\overline{q}
, while

is obtained from

by (eventually) erasing the absorbing state

and all transition arriving in

(see Example
1
in the appendix).

Considering a regular language
\mathcal{L}
with
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
we notice the following:

Remark 2
.

For every order
(\Sigma,\preceq)
, if
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
and

\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
then
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
.
This follows by the fact that if

, then any deterministic

and

\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
,
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\in{\mathsf{UW}}

(see

\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{DEF}}

Proof.

If

Suppose that

\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\neq\Sigma^{*}

If
G=\{\alpha x\in\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})~:~\alpha\in F,~x\in\Sigma\}

\mathcal{L}=(F\cap\mathcal{L})\cup G\Sigma^{*}

If
\beta\in G\Sigma^{*}
then
\beta=\alpha x\gamma

\alpha x\in\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})

Conversely, we prove that if
\beta\in\mathcal{L}

\beta\in(F\cap\mathcal{L})\cup G\Sigma^{*}

If
\beta\in\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})

.

If
\beta\not\in\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})

Since we proved that

with

Conditional Lower Bound.

First, we define the Orthogonal Vectors problem.

\mathcal{L}
be a regular language, let

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we established a link between the class of Wheeler languages and — well studied — (sub-)classes of
locally testable
languages. Our link is provided on both theoretical as well as algorithmic grounds. On the one hand, we proved that the class of languages that, together with their complement, are Wheeler with respect to
any
order of the alphabet corresponds exactly to the union of Definite and Reverse Definite languages — that is, the collections of words characterized either by a finite set of prefixes or suffixes. On the other hand we provided an optimal algorithm for characterizing the
universally
(with respect to the alphabet ordering) Wheeler languages.

The next steps along the path we started to follow here are, for example, to characterize languages that, together with their complement, are Wheeler with respect to a fixed order of the alphabet. Moreover, somehow along the style proposed in
[
6
]
, it would be interesting to reformulate and study analogues of the problems studied here for general — i.e. co-lexicographically
partially
ordered — languages/automata.

\mathcal{L}
is not Wheeler w.r.t.
\preceq
. Then, by Theorem
A.1
there are:

1.

a pair of equally labeled cycles
\mathcal{C}_{p},\mathcal{C}_{q}
(say labeled by
\gamma
) in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
starting starting respectively from states
p\neq q
;

2.

two words
\alpha,\beta
with
\alpha\in I_{p},\beta\in I_{q}
such that
\gamma
is not a suffix of
\alpha
or
\beta
;

3.

either
\alpha,\beta\prec\gamma
or
\gamma\prec\alpha,\beta
.

Let
p_{1}=p,q_{1}=q
and consider the
\gamma
-cycle
(p_{1},q_{1})\rightarrow(p_{2},q_{2})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow(p_{k},q_{k})\rightarrow(p_{1},q_{1})
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}
.
Then it is sufficient to prove that
p_{1}\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q_{1}
.
Suppose w.l.o.g. that
\alpha\prec\beta
. If

\alpha,\beta\prec\gamma
then
\alpha\gamma,\alpha\gamma^{2}\in I_{p_{1}}
,
\beta\gamma\in I_{q_{1}}
and
\alpha\gamma\prec\beta\gamma
while
\beta\gamma\prec\alpha\gamma^{2}
. If
\gamma\prec\alpha,\beta
then

\beta\gamma,\beta\gamma^{2}\in I_{q_{1}}
,
\alpha\gamma\in I_{p_{1}}
and

\beta\gamma\succ\alpha\gamma
and
\alpha\gamma\succ\beta\gamma^{2}
(the last disequality holds because
\gamma
is not a suffix of
\alpha
).
Hence in both cases we obtain
p_{1}\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q_{1}
.

Suppose now that we have two states
p_{1}\neq q_{1}
with
p_{1}\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q_{1}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
from which two cycles labeled by the same word
\gamma
start. From
p_{1}\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q_{1}
we know that there are
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{p_{1}}
,
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in I_{q_{1}}
with
\alpha\prec\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}\prec\alpha^{\prime}
. By using a power of
\gamma
, if necessary, we may suppose that
\gamma
is not a suffix of
\alpha,\beta,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
. If
\gamma\prec\alpha
or
\alpha^{\prime}\prec\gamma
then the conditions of Theorem
A.1
are satisfied (with
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
playing the role of
\alpha,\beta
in the second case).
Otherwise, we have
\alpha\prec\alpha^{\prime}
. Then, either
\beta\prec\gamma
or
\gamma\prec\beta
holds. In the first case we have
\alpha,\beta\prec\gamma
while in the second case we have
\gamma\prec\beta,\alpha^{\prime}
, so that, in both cases we fulfill the conditions of Theorem
A.1
.
∎

Lemma

(
2
)
.

If the minimum trimmed automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
of a language
\mathcal{L}
contains three equally labeled cycles starting from three different states, then
\mathcal{L}\not\in{\mathsf{EW}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
contains three different states
p,q,r
which are the starting point of three cycles labeled by the same word
\gamma
. Let
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\zeta_{3}
be words reaching
p,q,r
from the starting state. Using a power of
\gamma
, if necessary, we may suppose that
\gamma
is not a suffix of
\zeta_{i}
, for all
i=1,2,3
.
Fix an arbitrary order
\preceq
of the alphabet. Then
there must be two indices
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
such that
\zeta_{i},\zeta_{j}
are either both bigger or both smaller than

\gamma
. By Theorem
A.1
, we have
\mathcal{L}\not\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
and by the generality of the choice of
\preceq
it follows
\mathcal{L}\notin{\mathsf{EW}}
.
∎

Lemma

(
5
)
.

\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{DEF}}
iff
\mathcal{L}
is finite, or
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
and
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{DEF}}
then
L=F\cup\Sigma^{*}G
for finite sets
F,G
. Hence,
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
if
\mathcal{L}
is not finite. Moreover,
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
by Lemma
4
as definite languages are strictly locally testable.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{L}
is finite or
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
and
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
. If
\mathcal{L}
is finite then
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{DEF}}
and we are done.
If
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
and
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
, consider the minimum automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}=(Q,q_{0},\dots)
recognizing
\mathcal{L}
. Since
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
, the automaton
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
is complete and trimmed. We first prove that
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{SLT}}
. Suppose, by way of a contradiction, that this is not the case. Then, by Lemma
3
, we have that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
contains two states
p\neq q
which are both the starting point of a cycle labeled by a string
\delta\in\Sigma^{+}
. Let
\alpha\neq\beta
be words reaching
p,q
from the initial state, respectively. If
k
is such that
|\delta^{k}|>|\alpha|,|\beta|
, we are sure that no power of
\delta^{k}
is a suffix of
\alpha
or
\beta
.

Let
\gamma=\delta^{k}
and consider the sequence
q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{i},\dots
that we obtain, in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
, reading the powers of
\gamma
starting from the initial state

q_{0}\rightarrow^{\gamma}q_{1}\rightarrow^{\gamma}q_{2}\rightarrow^{\gamma}\dots\rightarrow^{\gamma}q_{i}\rightarrow^{\gamma}\dots\rightarrow^{\gamma}q_{j}\rightarrow^{\gamma}\dots

(here we are using the hypothesis
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
: this path has to be in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
).
Then, there exist
i,j
with
i<j
such that
q_{i}=q_{j}
.
Notice that
\rho=\gamma^{j-i}
labels cycles from the states
p,q
and
r=q_{i}
; if
\nu=\gamma^{i}
, by using an higher power of
\gamma
if necessary, we may suppose that
\rho
is not a suffix of
\nu
.

We now consider two cases:

1.

r\neq p,r\neq q
. In this case we have three cycles labeled
\rho
starting from three different
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{L}}
-states
p,q,r
. By Lemma
2
we obtain
\mathcal{L}\notin{\mathsf{EW}}
, contradicting
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
.

2.

r=p
or
r=q
. Suppose w.l.o.g. that
r=p
. In this case we have that
\nu=\gamma^{i}
reaches
r
and, since
\rho
is a bigger power of
\gamma
,
\nu
is always co-lexicographically smaller than
\rho
w.r.t. any order of the alphabet. Moreover, we know that
\rho
is not a suffix of
\beta
. This implies that we can always find an order
\preceq
of the alphabet such that
\beta\prec\rho
: if
\beta
is not a suffix of
\rho
(otherwise we already have
\beta\prec\rho
) then, since
\rho
is not a suffix of
\beta
either, we may consider the first letters
x,y
from the right in
\beta,\rho
, respectively, marking a difference between the two words; we may then consider an order
\preceq
having
x\prec y
in order to obtain
\beta\prec\rho
and
\nu\prec\rho
(the last one always holds).

Hence, we have two states
r,p
which are both the starting point of a cycle labeled
\rho
and are reached by strings smaller than
\rho
. This implies
\mathcal{L}\not\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
, contradicting
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
.

This proves that
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{SLT}}
, hence, there are finite sets
H,K,W,F
such that

\mathcal{L}=F\cup\left[(H\Sigma^{*}\cap\Sigma^{*}K)\setminus\Sigma^{*}W\Sigma^{*}\right].

We now prove that, since
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
, we have
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{DEF}}
.
We have that
\epsilon\not\in W
, otherwise
\Sigma^{*}W\Sigma^{*}=\Sigma^{*}
, and
L=F
would be a finite set, contradicting
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
.

To conclude the proof we check that
\epsilon\in H
(so that
H\Sigma^{*}=\Sigma^{*}
) and
W=\emptyset
.
Since
\mathcal{L}\subseteq F\cup H\Sigma^{*}
, all words in
\mathcal{L}
which are longer than
\max\left\{\,{|\alpha|}:{\alpha\in F}\,\right\}
must have have a prefix in the finite set
H
, contradicting
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
unless
\epsilon\in H
.
Suppose, by way of a contradiction, that
\xi\in W
, and consider a word
\beta
, longer than
\max\left\{\,{|\alpha|}:{\alpha\in F}\,\right\}
, such that
\beta
contains
\xi
as a prefix. Hence,
\beta\in W\Sigma^{*}
and, since
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})=\Sigma^{*}
, there exists
\delta\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
\beta\delta\in\mathcal{L}
. However, since
\beta\delta\not\in F
(it is too long), we should have
\beta\delta\not\in\Sigma^{*}W\Sigma^{*}
, contradicting
\beta\in W\Sigma^{*}
.
Hence,
W=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{L}=F\cup\Sigma^{*}K\in{\mathsf{DEF}}
.
∎

Lemma

(
6
)
.

Let
|\Sigma|\geq 2
and let
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
. If
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\neq\Sigma^{*}
, the following are equivalent:

1.

there exists an order
\preceq
such that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\not\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
;

2.

\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
is an infinite set.

Proof.

(
1\Rightarrow 2
) If there exists an order
\preceq
such that
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\not\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
but
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{UW}}
, then, in particular,
\mathcal{L}\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
.
Since
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\not\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
, then
\overline{\mathcal{L}}\not\in{\mathsf{SLT}}
. By Lemma
3
,
\mathcal{D}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
must contain two equally labeled cycles (say, with label
\gamma
) starting from two different states
p,q
.
One of them, say
p
, must be different from the absorbing state
\overline{q}
for
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
(see the discussion on minimum complete or trimmed DFA for a language and its complement). Since all words arriving in
p
are in
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
, we have

\alpha\gamma^{*}\in\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
and
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
is infinite.

(
2\Rightarrow 1
) Suppose
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
is infinite. Since
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\neq\Sigma^{*}
implies

\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})\setminus\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\neq\emptyset
, in
\mathcal{D}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}

there is an absorbing final state
\overline{q}
. Let
\beta
be a word arriving in
\overline{q}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
. From the hypothesis we get that

\beta\neq\epsilon
(otherwise
\overline{\mathcal{L}}=\Sigma^{*}
would contradict
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})\neq\emptyset
), and, since
\operatorname{Pref}({\mathcal{L}})\cap\operatorname{Pref}({\overline{\mathcal{L}}})
is infinite, in
\mathcal{D}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
there must be a cycle not passing through
\overline{q}
. Let
p\neq\overline{q}
be on this cycle,
\alpha
be a word arriving in
p
, and
\gamma\neq\epsilon
be a label of a cycle starting from
p
and avoiding
\overline{q}
.
We may suppose w.l.o.g. that
\gamma
is not a suffix of
\alpha
(if so, just consider a power of
\gamma
instead of
\gamma
). Notice that there is a cycle labeled
\gamma
both from
p
and from
\overline{q}
(
\overline{q}
is an absorbing state, hence there are cycles starting from
\overline{q}
labeled by any word).

We claim that there exists an alphabet order
\preceq
such that
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!\overline{q}
.
Consider two cases:

1.

\alpha\dashv\gamma
. Let
g
be the last letter of
\gamma
and let
x\in\Sigma
with
x\neq g
. Fix an order
\preceq
of the alphabet such that
x\prec g
. Since
\alpha
arrives in
p
and
\beta x
arrives in
\overline{q}
we have either
\alpha\prec\beta x
or
\alpha\succ\beta x
. In the first case, we may consider the words

\alpha\prec\beta x\prec\alpha\gamma
, while if
\alpha\succ\beta x

we may consider the words

\beta x\prec\alpha\prec\beta x\gamma
.
In both cases the words witness that
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!\overline{q}
.

2.

\alpha\not\dashv\gamma
. Then there is an order
\preceq
of the alphabet such that
\gamma\prec\alpha
holds. If
\preceq
is such an order, then, since we already supposed
\gamma\not\dashv\alpha
, we have
\beta\gamma\prec\alpha\prec\beta\alpha
, witnessing
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!\overline{q}
.

This proves the claim. Since a cycle labeled
\gamma
starts from both
p
and
\overline{q}
, in
\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}
there is a cycle starting from
(p,\overline{q})
, with
p\neq\overline{q}
and
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!\overline{q}
, and Lemma
1
applied to
\overline{\mathcal{L}}
yields

\overline{\mathcal{L}}\not\in{\mathsf{W}}(\preceq)
.
∎

Lemma

(
7
)
.

Let
p,q\in Q
with
p\neq q
. Then,
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}q
if and only if
|P[p,q]|\geq 2
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
If
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}q
, then there exists an order
\preceq
and strings
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in I_{p}
and

\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in I_{q}
such that
\alpha\prec\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}\prec\alpha^{\prime}
.
If
\alpha
is a suffix of
\beta
, then
(\dashv,\dashv)\in P[p,q]
. Otherwise, there exist

a,b\in\Sigma
, with
a\prec b
, and a string
\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}
such that
a\gamma\dashv\alpha
and
b\gamma\dashv\beta
, and thus
(a,b)\in P[p,q]
. Similarly, for
\beta^{\prime}\prec\alpha^{\prime}
we have either

(\vdash,\vdash)\in P[p,q]
or there exists
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\Sigma
, with
b^{\prime}\prec a^{\prime}
and
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
in
P[p,q]
. Furthermore, if such
a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
exists, we must have
(a,b)\neq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
because

a\prec b
and
b^{\prime}\prec a^{\prime}
. Hence,
|P[p,q]|\geq 2
.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
(a,b)\neq(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
be two elements in
P[p,q]
.
Suppose
\{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\}\cap\{(\dashv,\dashv)\}=\emptyset
. One of the pairs, say
(a,b)
, must be different from
(\vdash,\vdash)
.

If
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=(\vdash,\vdash)
, then any total order
\preceq
on
\Sigma
for which
a\prec b
is such that

p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q
. Otherwise, any total order
\preceq
on
\Sigma
for which
a\prec b
and
b^{\prime}\prec a^{\prime}
is such that

p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q
. Similarly, if
\{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\}\cap\{(\vdash,\vdash)\}=\emptyset
then there exists
\preceq
such that
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q
.
Finally, if
\{(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\}=\{(\dashv,\dashv),(\vdash,\vdash)\}
then
p\mathrel{\rhd\mspace{-10mu}\lhd}_{\!{}_{\preceq}}\!q
holds for any order
\preceq
of the alphabet
\Sigma
.

Lemma

(
8
)
.

Let
p,q\in Q
, with
p\neq q
. Then
|P[p,q]|\geq 2
iff
|P_{\lambda^{\prime}}[p,q]|\geq 2
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
We prove the statement by contraposition. Suppose
|P_{\lambda^{\prime}}[p,q]|<2
, hence
(P_{\lambda^{\prime}}[p,q]\setminus\{(\dashv,\dashv),(\vdash,\vdash)\})\subseteq\{(a,b)\}
, for some
a,b\in\Sigma
. Then, for every pair
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\in Q
such that there exists
\gamma\in\Sigma^{*}

for which
p=\delta(p^{\prime},\gamma)
and
q=\delta(q^{\prime},\gamma)
it holds
\lambda^{\prime}(p^{\prime})\subseteq\{a\}
and

\lambda^{\prime}(q^{\prime})\subseteq\{b\}
. Now recall that
\lambda^{\prime}(p^{\prime})
and
\lambda^{\prime}(q^{\prime})
where chosen as a maximal subset of
\lambda(p^{\prime})
and
\lambda(q^{\prime})
with at most two elements and thus also
\lambda(p^{\prime})\subseteq\{a\}
and

\lambda(q^{\prime})\subseteq\{b\}
must hold, from which it follows that
(P[p,q]\setminus\{(\dashv,\dashv),(\vdash,\vdash)\})\subseteq\{(a,b)\}
. Then
|P[p,q]|<2
since

(\dashv,\dashv)\in P[p,q]
implies
(\dashv,\dashv)\in P_{\lambda^{\prime}}[p,q]
and the same holds for
(\vdash,\vdash)
.

(\Leftarrow)
Since
P_{\lambda^{\prime}}[p,q]\subseteq P[p,q]
, the result is immediate.
∎
