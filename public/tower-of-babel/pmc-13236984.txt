Optimizing landfill siting for small towns in developing countries
Authors: Nobert Tafadzwa Mukomberanwa, Brian Chaipa, Honest Komborero Madamombe, Farai Cainos Mazonde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCities in developing countries often struggle to protect environmental quality and public health amid growing municipal solid waste pressures. Landfilling remains the dominant disposal option in resource-constrained settings, yet decisions on landfill locations are often made without systematic, science-based frameworks that adequately incorporate environmental, social, and ethical considerations. This study applies an integrated GIS–Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) framework to assess landfill site suitability in Chegutu, Zimbabwe, a rapidly urbanizing small town facing increasing waste management challenges. Multiple environmental, social, and geotechnical criteria were evaluated through structured pairwise comparisons and spatial overlay analysis, while acknowledging that additional considerations such as financial feasibility could further strengthen planning assessments. The results show that highly unsuitable areas dominate the town, accounting for approximately 85.6% of the total area, primarily due to proximity to built-up areas, incompatible land uses, and terrain-related constraints. Suitable and moderately suitable areas collectively represent about 14.1% of the landscape, while highly suitable land constitutes only about 0.3%, underscoring the severe spatial limitations for landfill development within the municipal boundary. The findings demonstrate that within the context of Chegutu Town, municipal-scale landfill self-sufficiency may be unrealistic, highlighting the need for regional waste management strategies and proactive land-use protection. The framework provides a locally relevant decision-support approach for evidence-based planning and contributes to broader discussions on responsible waste management in small towns across developing regions.Keywords: Solid waste management, GIS-based site selection, Multi-criteria decision analysis, Expert knowledge, Environmental planning, Developing regionsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

Global cities around the world continue to face mounting environmental pressures from rapidly increasing municipal solid waste driven by economic growth, urbanization, and changing consumption patterns31,38. Worldwide, more than two billion tonnes of solid waste are generated annually, a figure that continues to rise as population growth, urban expansion, and income levels intensify, particularly in low- and middle-income countries1,19. Cities that are responsible for protecting their inhabitants from garbage have a challenge from the solid waste generation and its treatment patterns14. The utility of novel technologies seems imperative to select a suitable site for landfill as new criteria are increased9. These challenges are particularly pronounced in small towns across the developing world, where limited technical capacity, financial constraints, and rapid urban growth continue to undermine effective municipal solid waste management systems2,27.

Urban centers are increasingly burdened by complex waste streams characterized by higher volumes, greater material heterogeneity, and elevated proportions of non-biodegradable components such as plastics and electronic waste23. These trends have intensified pressures on already strained municipal systems, resulting in widespread environmental degradation, including groundwater contamination, surface water pollution, air quality deterioration, greenhouse gas emissions, and heightened public health risks5. Although integrated waste management frameworks prioritize waste reduction, reuse, recycling, and energy recovery, landfilling remains the dominant and often unavoidable disposal method worldwide due to its relative simplicity, economic feasibility, and capacity to handle large waste volumes20. However, poorly sited, unengineered, unlined, or improperly managed landfills pose significant threats to environmental sustainability, particularly through leachate infiltration into aquifers, soil contamination, and impacts on nearby ecosystems. Devadoss et al.,10, Hajar17, . Consequently, the scientific and policy discourse has increasingly emphasized the importance of systematic, transparent, and environmentally sound landfill siting processes as a foundational element of sustainable waste management systems.

These challenges are particularly acute in developing countries, where rapid urbanization is occurring most intensely in small and intermediate towns rather than in large metropolitan areas29. Municipal authorities in these contexts often operate under severe financial, technical, and institutional constraints, with waste management expenditures consuming up to half of local government budgets while yielding limited environmental or economic returns25. In many cases, waste collection receives priority over treatment and disposal planning, resulting in the proliferation of open landfills that disregard environmental regulations and public health safeguards24. Although recycling and material recovery are increasingly promoted, their implementation remains limited due to inadequate infrastructure, weak markets, and insufficient policy enforcement7. Consequently, landfilling continues to dominate waste disposal practices, frequently in an unregulated or semi-controlled manner28. The selection of landfill sites in developing countries has traditionally relied on ad hoc decisions driven by land availability, political expediency, or distance from urban centers, rather than on systematic evaluation of environmental, social, and economic criteria6. Such approaches often neglect critical factors such as hydrogeology, soil characteristics, slope, proximity to water bodies, sensitive ecosystems, and long-term urban expansion, thereby exacerbating environmental health risks30. In response, the integration of Geographic Information Systems (GIS) with multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) techniques—particularly the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and fuzzy logic—has gained prominence as a scientifically robust approach for landfill siting27. These tools enable the structured evaluation of multiple, often conflicting criteria, facilitate spatial analysis, and enhance decision transparency4,26. Nevertheless, many existing models remain technically complex, data-intensive, and insufficiently grounded in local operational realities, limiting their practical adoption by municipal authorities in resource-constrained settings.

Within this broader context, small towns in sub-Saharan Africa face a distinct yet underexplored set of waste management challenges that warrant targeted investigation37. Unlike large cities, small towns often lack formal waste management strategies, technical expertise, and reliable spatial data, despite experiencing rapid population growth and spatial expansion18. Zimbabwean towns such as Chegutu exemplify this predicament, where increasing waste generation intersects with limited institutional capacity and growing environmental vulnerability. In such settings, expert knowledge—derived from municipal officials, environmental practitioners, planners, and local stakeholders—constitutes a critical but underutilized resource for informed decision-making37. Incorporating expert opinions into landfill siting frameworks offers an opportunity to contextualize scientific criteria within local socio-environmental realities, enhance stakeholder ownership, and improve policy relevance39. However, expert judgments are inherently subjective and uncertain, necessitating structured methods that can systematically capture, weight, and integrate diverse perspectives16. Hybrid GIS–MCDA approaches driven by expert input provide a promising pathway for optimizing landfill siting in small towns, balancing environmental protection, public health, economic feasibility, and social acceptability27. Despite extensive literature on landfill siting in metropolitan and regional contexts, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding GIS–Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) framework, context-sensitive siting models tailored to small towns in developing countries. Addressing this gap is essential for advancing sustainable waste management practices that are both scientifically rigorous and practically implementable. Against this background, the present study focuses on optimizing landfill siting in Chegutu, Zimbabwe, using an integrated GIS–Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) framework designed to support evidence-based planning and environmentally responsible waste disposal in small-town contexts. In this study, the focus is on planned municipal landfills, designed and managed according to engineering and environmental standards. The study specifically considers municipal solid waste, including household and small-scale commercial waste, as this represents the primary waste stream generated within the town and managed by local authorities.

Materials and methods

Study area

Chegutu Town (Fig. 1) is in Chegutu District, Mashonaland West Province of central Zimbabwe, approximately 100 km southwest of Harare. The town which is about 40.52 km2 in size lies within the geographic extent of about 30°06′–30°09′ E longitude and 18°06′–18°09′ S latitude. Chegutu occupies part of the Highveld plateau at elevations generally above 1,000 m above sea level, characterized by gently undulating terrain with predominantly low slope gradients. The dominant soil types in the area include Chromic Luvisols and Ferralic Cambisols, which are moderately fertile and widely used for agriculture. Geologically, the area is mainly underlain by metasediments, dolerites, intrusive granites, and ultramafic units, reflecting Zimbabwe’s complex Precambrian basement geology. Chegutu experiences a subtropical climate, with mean annual rainfall ranging between 700 and 900 mm, concentrated during the November–March rainy season21. Mean annual temperatures range from 15 to 18 °C in winter to 25–30 °C in summer.

Land use and land cover are dominated by grassland and cropland, interspersed with forest patches, built-up areas, and limited surface water bodies. The town has an estimated population exceeding 45,000 residents, driven by industrial activity, agriculture, and proximity to Harare. Rapid urbanization and population growth have intensified solid waste generation, overwhelming existing waste management infrastructure and resulting in indiscriminate dumping, environmental degradation, and public health risks8. The Chegutu municipal area currently faces challenges associated with unregulated waste disposal at informal dumpsites; however, this study evaluates potential sites for engineered landfills intended to receive municipal solid waste (MSW) from households and small commercial establishments.

Methodology flowchart

The methodology illustrated in the flowchart (Fig. 2) outlines a GIS-based multi-criteria decision analysis for identifying suitable landfill sites for Chegutu Town using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). Initially, key thematic spatial layers influencing landfill suitability—namely elevation, slope, soil type, geology, NDVI, land use/land cover (LULC), and distances from roads, railways, water bodies, and built-up areas—are prepared and standardized. AHP is then applied to assign relative weights to each criterion based on their environmental, technical, and socio-economic importance. These weighted layers are integrated through a weighted overlay analysis to generate a preliminary land suitability map27.

Methodological flowchart for developing a landfill site suitability model in Chegutu Town, Zimbabwe, integrating Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) datasets obtained from the USGS Earth Explorer (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/), spatial data processing, thematic map generation, multi-criteria evaluation, and the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to produce the final suitability map.

In parallel, exclusion criteria are defined to eliminate environmentally sensitive and restricted areas. Buffering and extraction operations are applied to features such as settlements, water bodies, and infrastructure, and all exclusion layers are merged to produce an exclusion zones map. The suitability map is subsequently masked using the exclusion zones to extract feasible candidate sites. The final output is a landfill suitability map highlighting optimal locations that minimize environmental risk while satisfying planning and operational requirements.

Geospatial datasets

In this study, ten spatial variables (Table 1; Fig. 3) were utilized to develop a landfill suitability map for Chegutu Town, Zimbabwe, using the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP). All spatial data processing, analysis, and map generation were conducted using ArcGIS Pro 3.0. The soil type layer was prepared by downloading the Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD) vector dataset and extracting soil mapping units covering Chegutu District. The dataset was projected to UTM Zone 36 S (WGS 84) to ensure spatial consistency with other layers. Attribute fields describing soil classification were reviewed and simplified to dominant soil groups present in the study area, namely Chromic Luvisols and Ferralic Cambisols. The vector data were clipped to the Chegutu town boundary and converted to raster using the Polygon to Raster tool at a common cell size. Reclassification was then performed to assign suitability ranks based on permeability, clay content, and leachate retention capacity relevant to landfill siting.

Spatial criteria maps used in the GIS-based Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) for landfill site suitability analysis in Chegutu Town, Zimbabwe. The figure presents ten thematic layers used as constraints and evaluation criteria: soil type, distance from built-up areas, geology, distance from railway lines, distance from water bodies, distance from roads, Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), land use/land cover (LULC), elevation, and slope. Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) data used for deriving NDVI and LULC were obtained from the USGS Earth Explorer (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/). All spatial datasets were processed and visualized using ArcGIS Pro version 3.0 (https://www.esri.com/arcgis).

The distance from built-up areas layer was derived by downloading settlement and building footprint data from OpenStreetMap (OSM). Built-up features were extracted, cleaned for topology errors, and merged into a single polygon layer representing urban areas. The dataset was projected to the study coordinate system and clipped to the area of interest. Euclidean Distance analysis was applied to generate a continuous distance raster in meters. The resulting raster was reclassified into distance zones of 1000–1499, 1500–1999, 2000–2499, 2500–2999, and above 3000 m, following environmental and public health buffer standards to minimize human exposure to landfill impacts.

The geology layer was prepared using geological map data obtained from the Geological Survey of Zimbabwe in vector format. The dataset was georeferenced where necessary, projected to UTM Zone 36 S, and clipped to Chegutu town. Lithological units were harmonized by dissolving similar rock types and correcting attribute inconsistencies. Dominant formations—dolerites, metasediments, ultramafic lavas, and intrusive granite—were retained due to their influence on groundwater vulnerability and structural stability. The vector geology map was converted to raster at a standardized resolution and reclassified by assigning suitability weights based on rock permeability, weathering behavior, and contamination risk associated with landfill development.

The distance from railway line layer was generated using railway network data extracted from OpenStreetMap. Railway polylines were inspected, edited to remove duplicates, and projected to the study coordinate system. The data were clipped to the Chegutu boundary and used as input for Euclidean Distance analysis to calculate continuous distances from railway infrastructure. This raster was subsequently reclassified into distance bands of 250–499, 500–749, 750–999, 1000–1249, and above 1250 m. These classes reflect safety, vibration, and land-use conflict considerations, ensuring landfill sites are adequately separated from railway corridors and associated transport risks.

The distance from water bodies layer was developed using rivers, streams, and surface water features extracted from OpenStreetMap. Water features were merged into a single hydrographic layer, cleaned, projected, and clipped to the study area. Euclidean Distance analysis was applied to compute distances from water bodies across the landscape. The resulting raster was reclassified into buffer zones of 500–999, 1000–1499, 1500–1999, 2000–2499, and above 2500 m. These thresholds align with environmental protection guidelines aimed at preventing leachate contamination of surface water and preserving aquatic ecosystems.

The distance from roads layer was produced using road network data downloaded from OpenStreetMap. All road classes were merged, corrected for connectivity issues, and projected to UTM Zone 36 S. The road network was clipped to Chegutu town, and Euclidean Distance analysis was performed to generate a continuous distance surface. The raster was reclassified into distance classes of 300–499, 500–749, 750–999, 1000–1199, and above 1200 m. These classes balance accessibility requirements for waste transport with the need to minimize noise, dust, and visual impacts on nearby communities.

The NDVI layer was derived from Sentinel-2 MSI imagery downloaded via USGS access portals. Cloud-free scenes were selected, atmospherically corrected using surface reflectance products, and clipped to the study area. The red (Band 4) and near-infrared (Band 8) bands were used to compute NDVI using the raster calculator. The resulting NDVI raster was normalized and inspected for noise. Values ranged from − 0.239405 to 0.577873, representing water, built-up surfaces, sparse vegetation, and dense vegetation. Reclassification was applied to assign suitability ranks, with lower vegetation density considered more appropriate for landfill siting.

The land use/land cover layer was generated from Sentinel-2 MSI imagery using supervised classification techniques. Pre-processing included atmospheric correction, band stacking, and clipping to the Chegutu boundary. Training samples for forest, grassland, water, cropland, and built-up areas were collected using high-resolution reference imagery. Classification was performed using a maximum likelihood algorithm, followed by accuracy assessment and post-classification smoothing. The final LULC raster was reclassified to reflect landfill suitability, with built-up and water areas restricted and grassland or degraded land assigned higher suitability.

The elevation layer was prepared using SRTM DEM data downloaded from USGS. The DEM tiles were mosaicked, projected to UTM Zone 36 S, and clipped to the study area. Sink filling was applied to correct surface anomalies. Elevation values were extracted and reclassified into five classes: below 1000, 1000–1100, 1100–1200, 1200–1300, and above 1300 m. These classes were evaluated for drainage potential, construction feasibility, and flood risk, with moderate elevations generally assigned higher suitability for landfill development.

The slope layer was derived from the processed SRTM DEM using the Spatial Analyst slope function in ArcGIS Pro. Slope values were calculated in degrees and clipped to the study boundary. The resulting raster was reclassified into slope categories of below 5, 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, and above 20 degrees. Lower slope areas were ranked as more suitable due to reduced erosion risk, easier construction, and improved landfill stability, while steeper slopes were assigned lower suitability due to increased runoff and engineering constraints.

While this study primarily focuses on environmental, social, and geotechnical criteria, it is acknowledged that the economic feasibility of potential landfill sites, including land acquisition, construction, and operational costs, is an essential factor for practical implementation. Future work should integrate available municipal resources and cost assessments to enhance the decision-making framework.

Analytical hierarchy process (AHP)

The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) was employed to derive criterion weights for landfill site suitability mapping in Chegutu Town, Zimbabwe. AHP is a multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) technique that structures complex decision problems into a hierarchical framework and quantifies the relative importance of decision criteria through pairwise comparisons32,36. In this study, AHP was integrated within a GIS environment to ensure an objective and reproducible weighting scheme for spatial suitability analysis. Ten environmental and infrastructural criteria relevant to landfill siting were considered: distance from water bodies, distance from roads, distance from built-up areas, soil type, geology, land use/land cover (LULC), elevation, distance from railway lines, slope, and Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI). Following the AHP framework, a pairwise comparison matrix was constructed using Saaty’s fundamental scale32 to express the relative importance of each criterion with respect to landfill suitability (Table 2).

The pairwise comparison matrix was normalized by dividing each element by its corresponding column sum, producing a standardized matrix (Table 3). Criterion weights were then derived by computing the average of each row in the normalized matrix. These weights represent the relative contribution of each criterion to overall landfill suitability and were subsequently used in the weighted linear combination during GIS-based overlay analysis. To evaluate the reliability of the judgments, consistency assessment was performed. The maximum eigenvalue (λ_max) was computed, and the Consistency Index (CI) and Consistency Ratio (CR) were calculated following Saaty’s method (Table 4). The CR was obtained by dividing the CI by the Random Consistency Index (CIr) corresponding to a matrix size of ten (Table 5). A CR value of 0.07, which is below the acceptable threshold of 0.10, confirmed that the pairwise comparisons were logically consistent. Study limitations

While this study provides a systematic assessment of landfill site suitability in Chegutu Town using a GIS–AHP framework, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis focuses primarily on environmental, social, and geotechnical criteria and does not incorporate detailed economic feasibility assessments, which are critical for practical implementation of landfill projects. Second, the study considers only municipal solid waste (household and small commercial waste) and does not account for other waste streams such as industrial or hazardous waste, which may have different siting requirements. Third, while AHP provides a structured multi-criteria approach, it relies on fixed criteria weights and assumes independence among factors, which may not fully capture complex interactions or temporal changes in urban and environmental systems. Fourth, the analysis does not replace in-depth field-based investigations such as geotechnical surveys, hydrological studies, or social impact assessments. Finally, the findings are specific to Chegutu Town and may not be directly transferable to other small towns with differing spatial, socio-economic, or environmental conditions, emphasizing the need for context-specific evaluations in similar settings.

Results

Analytical hierarchy process (AHP)

The AHP results indicate a clear hierarchy in the relative importance of criteria (Fig. 3) influencing landfill suitability in Chegutu Town. The derived weights (Table 3) show that distance from built-up areas is the most influential factor (19.4%), reflecting its critical role in minimizing human exposure, public health risks, and land-use conflicts. This dominance suggests that social and planning constraints strongly condition landfill siting decisions in the study area. Land use/land cover (LULC) (14.4%) and elevation (12.4%) also received high weights, emphasizing the importance of avoiding environmentally sensitive land-cover types and low-lying areas prone to flooding or poor drainage.

Moderately influential criteria include slope (11.8%), soil type (10.2%), and geology (10.1%). These factors collectively highlight the significance of geotechnical and hydrogeological conditions in controlling leachate migration, slope stability, and long-term landfill integrity. Lower weights were assigned to NDVI (7.0%), distance from water bodies (6.0%), and distance from roads (5.2%), indicating that while ecological sensitivity and accessibility are relevant, they are secondary to settlement protection and physical site suitability in this context. Distance from railway lines had the least influence (3.4%), suggesting minimal constraint relative to other infrastructures.

The consistency analysis yielded a Consistency Ratio (CR) of 0.07 (Table 4), which is below the acceptable threshold of 0.10, confirming that the pairwise judgments were coherent and reliable. Overall, the AHP results demonstrate a balanced weighting structure that integrates social, environmental, and physical considerations, providing a robust foundation for GIS-based landfill suitability mapping.

Landfill suitability assessment

The landfill suitability assessment for Chegutu Town, derived through the AHP-weighted GIS overlay, reveals a landscape that is overwhelmingly constrained for landfill development. The suitability map (Fig. 4) and area statistics (Table 6) indicate that highly unsuitable zones dominate the town, covering 34.69 km² (85.60%) of the total area. Spatially, these areas are extensive across the central, eastern, and southern parts of Chegutu, reflecting the cumulative influence of restrictive criteria such as proximity to built-up areas, unfavorable land use/land cover, sensitive environmental conditions, and terrain-related limitations. Given the high AHP weight assigned to distance from built-up areas and LULC, this dominance highlights the strong urban and environmental constraints characterizing the town.

Areas classified as suitable (7.90%; 3.20 km²) and moderately suitable (6.21%; 2.52 km²) are limited and spatially fragmented, occurring mainly along the peripheral northern and localized southwestern margins of the town boundary. These zones represent transitional landscapes where physical conditions (e.g., slope, elevation, soil, and geology) and infrastructural buffers are comparatively favorable, but not optimal. Their moderate extent suggests that landfill siting in Chegutu requires careful trade-offs and rigorous site-specific validation. Only 0.28% (0.11 km²) of the town is classified as highly suitable, underscoring the scarcity of locations that simultaneously satisfy environmental protection, engineering feasibility, and planning thresholds. Conversely, unsuitable areas (0.02%) are negligible, indicating that most land is either clearly constrained or clearly unsuitable rather than marginally restricted.

Overall, the results demonstrate that Chegutu Town has very limited spatial capacity for landfill development, emphasizing the need for strategic planning, potential regional waste management solutions, or consideration of engineered alternatives such as waste transfer stations or landfill siting beyond the municipal boundary. Given the limited proportion of highly suitable land (7.9%) within the municipal boundary, complementary waste management strategies such as recycling, reuse, and composting should be considered to reduce the volume of waste reaching the landfill and thereby increase the lifespan of selected sites. Integrating such initiatives would improve sustainability and enhance the practical applicability of landfill planning in small-town contexts.

Potential suitable sites

The spatial analysis of identified landfill candidate sites (A–C) reveals a highly uneven distribution of suitable land within Chegutu Town (Fig. 5). Site A dominates the suitability landscape, accounting for 3.58 km² (89.28%) of the total suitable area (Table 7). Spatially, Site A forms a relatively contiguous and extensive zone in the northern part of the town, which enhances its practical viability for landfill development. Its large areal extent suggests favorable cumulative conditions across multiple AHP-weighted criteria, including adequate separation from built-up areas, compatible land use, and acceptable terrain and environmental characteristics. The spatial continuity of Site A also allows for flexibility in detailed site design, buffer establishment, and phased landfill development. In contrast, Site B covers 0.36 km² (8.98%) and is spatially constrained, limiting its capacity and long-term operational potential. While it meets minimum suitability thresholds, its smaller size implies greater sensitivity to operational buffers and regulatory setbacks. Site C, representing only 0.07 km² (1.75%), is highly fragmented and marginal, making it unsuitable for large-scale landfill infrastructure despite meeting baseline suitability criteria.

Discussion

Analytical hierarchy process (AHP)

The AHP results reveal a clear and defensible hierarchy of criteria governing landfill suitability in Chegutu Town, with distance from built-up areas emerging as the most influential factor (19.4%). This finding aligns with established landfill siting literature, which consistently emphasizes settlement buffers as a primary safeguard against public health risks, social conflict, and environmental injustice22,33,34. However, the strength of this study lies not merely in confirming known priorities, but in quantifying their relative dominance within a rapidly urbanizing secondary African town, where informal settlement expansion often undermines conventional planning assumptions.

The relatively high weighting of LULC (14.4%) and elevation (12.4%) highlights the compounded influence of ecological sensitivity and terrain-driven hydrological risk. Unlike many African landfill siting studies that treat land cover and topography as secondary screening layers, this study demonstrates their near-equivalence to social exclusion criteria, reflecting the increasing vulnerability of urban systems to flooding, erosion, and climate variability. This represents a methodological advance by embedding climate-sensitive terrain considerations directly into the decision hierarchy, rather than addressing them post hoc.

Moderately weighted criteria—slope, soil, and geology (≈ 10–12%)—underscore the importance of subsurface controls on landfill performance. Their balanced weighting contrasts with studies that over-emphasize accessibility or hydrology alone, and instead supports a systems-based understanding of landfill risk, where surface and subsurface processes interact. The relatively lower weights assigned to distance from water bodies and NDVI do not imply irrelevance; rather, they indicate that in Chegutu’s context, settlement pressure and land-use configuration present more immediate constraints than vegetation density or surface hydrology alone.

The Consistency Ratio of 0.07 confirms strong internal coherence in the overlay analysis, enhancing confidence in the robustness of the weighting scheme. Importantly, this study advances existing AHP applications by explicitly integrating urban planning, environmental protection, and geotechnical risk within a single, internally consistent framework, rather than privileging one domain. This methodological integration constitutes a key novelty and produces transferable knowledge for other medium-sized African towns facing similar land-use and governance pressures.

While the current study focuses on environmental, social, and geotechnical criteria to identify suitable landfill sites, it is important to acknowledge that economic feasibility is a critical component of practical landfill planning. In resource-constrained small towns like Chegutu, factors such as land acquisition costs, construction and lining expenses, operational and maintenance costs, and long-term management requirements can significantly influence whether a site deemed environmentally suitable can actually be implemented. The absence of this assessment in the current study represents a limitation, as some high-ranking sites in terms of environmental suitability may not be financially viable for the municipality. Future research should integrate cost-benefit analyses or municipal budget assessments alongside the GIS–AHP framework, providing a more holistic approach to landfill siting that balances environmental safety with economic practicality. Incorporating economic feasibility would allow local authorities to make more informed, implementable decisions, ensuring that selected sites are both environmentally sustainable and financially manageable.

Landfill suitability assessment

The spatial dominance of highly unsuitable land (85.60%) reveals a critical structural constraint in Chegutu Town: landfill siting is not primarily a technical optimization problem, but a spatial scarcity challenge shaped by urban growth, environmental sensitivity, and planning legacies. While previous studies often report moderate to high availability of suitable land at municipal scale3,15, the results here demonstrate that secondary towns in rapidly urbanizing African contexts may face far more severe spatial constraints than large metropolitan areas.

The concentration of unsuitable zones across central, eastern, and southern Chegutu reflects the cumulative effect of high-weighted criteria—particularly proximity to built-up areas and incompatible land uses. This pattern highlights an important insight: urban expansion trajectories, rather than natural environmental limits alone, are the dominant drivers of landfill exclusion. This challenges the implicit assumption in many landfill siting studies that environmental factors are the primary limiting condition, and instead foregrounds urban governance and spatial planning failure as central risk multipliers.

The fragmentation of suitable (7.90%) and moderately suitable (6.21%) areas further complicates landfill planning. These peripheral patches may meet threshold criteria but lack the spatial coherence required for long-term waste management infrastructure. The extremely small proportion of highly suitable land (0.28%) is particularly significant, as it demonstrates that optimal landfill conditions are nearly absent within the municipal boundary. This finding constitutes new empirical evidence that municipal-scale landfill self-sufficiency may be unrealistic for many African towns.

The study results indicate that only a small fraction of land within the municipal boundary (approximately 7.9%) is suitable for landfill development, highlighting the extreme spatial constraints faced by small towns. In this context, complementary waste reduction initiatives—such as recycling programs, reuse of materials, and organic waste composting—could play a crucial role in reducing the volume of waste requiring disposal. Implementing such strategies would not only extend the operational lifespan of newly established landfills but also mitigate environmental pressures associated with limited landfill capacity. While this study does not directly evaluate the effectiveness or feasibility of these waste management initiatives, acknowledging their potential importance reinforces the need for integrated waste management planning, where landfill siting decisions are considered alongside municipal programs for waste reduction, resource recovery, and sustainability. Future planning efforts should assess how such initiatives can be quantitatively incorporated into suitability models, ensuring a more sustainable and resilient approach to municipal solid waste management.

From a policy perspective, this result directly supports a shift toward regionalized waste management systems, including inter-municipal landfill facilities and waste transfer stations. Such an approach aligns with SDG 11.6, which targets the reduction of environmental impacts of cities through improved waste management, and SDG 3.9, which focuses on reducing illness from environmental contamination. At the continental level, the findings speak directly to AU Agenda 2063, Aspiration 1, which emphasizes environmentally sustainable and climate-resilient economies. By spatially demonstrating the limits of conventional landfill siting, this study provides evidence-based justification for rethinking urban waste governance models across Africa.

Potential suitable sites

The identification of three candidate sites (A–C) reveals a highly skewed suitability structure, with Site A alone accounting for 89.28% of all suitable land. This concentration is not incidental; it reflects the spatial convergence of favorable AHP-weighted conditions—adequate settlement buffers, compatible land use, acceptable terrain, and manageable environmental risk. Unlike many studies that identify numerous dispersed candidate sites11,13,35, this research demonstrates that true landfill viability often collapses onto a single dominant spatial solution once cumulative constraints are applied rigorously.

Site A’s spatial contiguity represents a critical operational advantage. It allows for buffer zoning, phased landfill development, and adaptive management under future waste generation scenarios. In contrast, Sites B and C, despite meeting baseline suitability thresholds, are severely constrained by size and fragmentation. This distinction introduces an important conceptual contribution: suitability does not equate to feasibility. Many GIS-based landfill studies stop at binary or ordinal suitability classification; this study advances the field by explicitly interpreting spatial configuration and areal sufficiency as functional feasibility criteria.

The marginal contribution of Sites B and C highlights the risks of relying on fragmented parcels, which are highly sensitive to regulatory buffers, land tenure conflicts, and community opposition. Their identification is nevertheless valuable, as they may serve as contingency or auxiliary sites under emergency or transitional waste management scenarios.

Crucially, the dominance of a single viable site underscores the urgency of protecting suitable land from competing development pressures12. Without proactive zoning and policy intervention, even Site A may be rapidly encroached upon, rendering the town entirely dependent on external waste disposal solutions. This insight has immediate relevance for urban planners, environmental regulators, and policymakers.

In a broader context, the study contributes new spatial evidence to the global discourse on sustainable waste infrastructure in the Global South27. It demonstrates that advanced GIS–AHP integration can move beyond mapping to strategic foresight, offering concrete, spatially explicit guidance for infrastructure planning25. This positions the research as both methodologically and policy-relevant, with direct implications for achieving SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities) and SDG 12.5 (waste reduction and environmentally sound management), while supporting Africa’s long-term urban sustainability agenda.

Conclusion

This study evaluated landfill site suitability in Chegutu Town, Zimbabwe, using an integrated GIS–Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) approach to support informed and transparent waste management planning. The findings show that landfill siting within the municipal boundary is severely constrained, with suitability largely controlled by settlement proximity, land-use compatibility, terrain characteristics, and environmental considerations. Only a small number of spatially coherent areas satisfy the combined siting requirements, with one location emerging as the most practically viable when cumulative constraints are applied. The study demonstrates the value of structured multi-criteria decision-making tools in rapidly urbanizing secondary towns, where unplanned growth and competing land uses significantly limit infrastructure options. By systematically integrating social, environmental, and geotechnical factors, the GIS–AHP framework provides a defensible basis for preliminary landfill planning in data-limited urban contexts, while acknowledging that these findings are specific to Chegutu Town and may not directly apply to other municipalities with different spatial, social, or economic conditions.

However, the application of AHP has inherent limitations. The method assumes criteria independence and uses fixed weights, which may not fully capture complex interactions or temporal changes in urban and environmental systems. The analysis is also constrained by the quality and resolution of available spatial data and does not replace detailed geotechnical, hydrological, or social impact assessments. Future work should additionally consider economic feasibility, waste reduction initiatives, and local resource constraints to enhance practical applicability. Integrating dynamic urban growth models, uncertainty analysis, and complementary decision-support methods can further strengthen sustainable waste management planning in Chegutu and inform similar assessments in comparable small towns across Zimbabwe and other African contexts.
