Phase retrieval with rank d d measurements – descending algorithms phase transitions
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

Companion paper
[
118
]
developed a powerful
Random duality theory
(RDT) based analytical program to statistically characterize performance of
descending
phase retrieval algorithms (dPR) (these include all variants of gradient descents and among them widely popular Wirtinger flows). We here generalize the program and show how it can be utilized to handle rank
d
positive definite phase retrieval (PR) measurements (with special cases
d=1
and
d=2
serving as emulations of the real and complex phase retrievals, respectively). In particular, we observe that the minimal sample complexity ratio (number of measurements scaled by the dimension of the unknown signal) which ensures dPR’s success exhibits a phase transition (PT) phenomenon. For both plain and lifted RDT we determine phase transitions locations. To complement theoretical results we implement a log barrier gradient descent variant and observe that, even in small dimensional scenarios (with problem sizes on the order of 100), the simulated phase transitions are in an excellent agreement with the theoretical predictions.

Index Terms: Phase retrieval; Rank
d
measurements; Descending algorithms; Random duality theory
.

1.1
A first look at PR’s difficulty

In this paper we consider practically relevant scenario where the underlying problem dimensions are large. Moreover, we consider mathematically the most challenging
linear/proportional
regime

\displaystyle\alpha\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n},

(3)

where
\alpha
is the so-called
oversampling or sample complexity ratio
which remains constant as
n
and
m
grow. Two aspects of (
2
) are important. The first one is of predominantly theoretical character and relates to the “mathematical soundness”. It particularly focuses on algebraic characterizations of uniqueness, injectivity, and stability (see, e.g.,
[
30
,
6
,
5
,
10
,
125
]
and references therein). The second is of more practical relevance and relates to algorithmic solving of (
2
) (since both
\bar{{\bf x}}
and
-\bar{{\bf x}}
are admissible solutions, solving up to a
global
phase is assumed throughout the paper). The key algorithmic difficulty is the appearance of (squared) magnitudes. A naive removal of magnitudes transforms the problem into an easily solvable linear system but incurs an exponential complexity (on the order of
O(2^{m})
) and is practically useless.

1.2
PR with rank
d
measurements

Since the simple methods (along the lines of the above naive combinatorial approach) were not computationally satisfactory, development of computationally more efficient solvers ensued and a rather vast algorithmic theory has been built. Particularly relevant progress has been made over the last 10-15 years and is in large part due to the appearance of breakthrough papers
[
25
,
21
,
24
]
. One of these breakthroughs,
[

, uncovered that a simple gradient algorithm (also called Wirtinger flow) can often act as a very successful PR solver. Companion paper
[

[

provided

d
measurements phase retrieval (basically a rank

variant of (

As stated above, (

) is a phase retrieval real variant. Within the rank
d
measurements context it is obtained as a special case,

. For

one then obtains the emulation of the complex phase retrieval – a more relevant scenario in some imaging application. Higher ranks

[

While the standard phase retrieval is usually described through (
1
) and (

) can also be rewritten in the following (slightly different) form

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}=|A_{i,:}\bar{{\bf x}}|^{2}=\bar{{\bf x}}^{T}A_{i,:}^{T}A_{i,:}\bar{{\bf x}}=\bar{{\bf x}}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}\bar{{\bf x}},\quad{\cal B}_{(i)}=A_{i,:}^{T}A_{i,:},\quad i=1,2,\dots,m,

(4)

where
A_{i,:}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{1\times n}
is the
i
-th row of

. Moreover, the above matrix form of measurements allows one to further describe the data acquisition as

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf x}}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}\bar{{\bf x}},\quad{\cal B}_{(i)}^{T}={\cal B}_{(i)}\geq 0\mbox{ is rank $1$},\quad i=1,2,\dots,m.

(5)

Formulation from (

1
) is the so-called

phase retrieval (PR) with rank 1 positive-semidefinite (PSD) measurements. Its a rank

analogue is then naturally given as

\displaystyle\hskip-5.78172pt{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{\emph{Rank $d$ measurements (RdM):}}}\quad\quad\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{{\bf x}}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}\bar{{\bf x}},\quad{\cal B}_{(i)}^{T}={\cal B}_{(i)}\geq 0\mbox{ is rank $d$},\quad i=1,2,\dots,m.

(6)

Recovering
\bar{{\bf x}}
provided the access to
\bar{{\bf y}}
and rank
d
PSD
{\cal B}_{(i)}
assumes solving the following inverse feasibility problem (basically rank
d
measurements analogue to (

RdM phase retrieval (RdM PR):

find

\displaystyle{\bf x}

subject to

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}{\bf x}=\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}(=\bar{{\bf x}}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}\bar{{\bf x}}),\quad i=1,2,\dots,m.

(7)

To be able to make a clear parallel with the standard real and complex PR we take
{\cal B}_{(i)}
as PSD. In general, however, one can also consider the higher rank indefinite
{\cal B}_{(i)}
as well

,

]
. Moreover, (

find

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\,\left({\cal B}_{(i)}X\right)=\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}(=\bar{{\bf x}}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}\bar{{\bf x}}),\quad i=1,2,\dots,m

\displaystyle X^{T}=X\geq 0,\text{rank}(X)=1.

Problems of this type have been extensively studied throughout the low rank recovery literature

in PR and that PR usually deals with problem sizes on the order of a few tens of thousands, we here focus on the above mentioned dPRs as computationally more convenient alternatives.

1.3
Relevant PR prior work

1.3.1
Algorithmic methods

While the rank
d
structure of PR measurements is likely to affect associated theoretical and algorithmic analytical results, it leaves the choice of the employed algorithmic techniques to a large degree unaffected. In other words, many of the algorithms applicable in the standard rank 1 (
d=1
) scenario can be reutilized for larger
d
’s without much of a change. Previewing some of the most important algorithmic strategies typically employed in rank 1 scenario is therefore necessary and we do so next (for more detailed expositions, we also refer to
[
118
,
117
]
).

\star

Convex approaches
:
Since formulation in (
8
) has matrix rank-1 constraint as a key non-convexity feature, standard optimization techniques assume its full rank relaxation (see
[
25
,
21
]
for the so-called Phaselift and
[
127
]
for a closely related Phasecut relaxation). As
[
25
,
21
]
showed, when
m=O(n\log(n))
Phaselift exactly and stably solves standard PR (see, e.g.,
[
56
]
for further robustness discussion and
[
22
]
for order optimally improved sample complexity,
m=O(n)
; for utilization of Fourier instead of Gaussian measurements see, e.g.,
[
54
,
23
]
). Rank dropping SDP (semi-definite programming) relaxations perform well but their practical implementation becomes computationally challenging as dimensions grow. The search for numerically more efficient alternatives resulted in appearance of the so-called PhaseMax
[
52
,
4
]
which was shown to be successful provided that the starting point of the algorithm (initializer) is sufficiently close to the true solution (see
[
58
,
28
]
for further discussion on initializers and
[
98
]
for precise PhaseMax analysis; see, also
[
35
]
where PhaseLamp variant was introduced and shown to have better properties than PhaseMax).

Convex methods allow an easy incorporation of further signal structuring (say sparsity, positivity, low-rankness, and so on). One simply follows usual compressed sensing (CS) adaptations (see, e.g.
[
93
,
74
,
70
]
). For example,
[
74
]
shows that
m=O(k^{2}\log(n))

-sparse

which is (even in nonlinear

regime) weaker than the corresponding
m=O(k\log(n))
CS result. Adaptations of standard convex techniques are possible when additional structuring is present (for two-stage approaches and randomized Kaczmarz upgrades see
[
65
,
64
]
and
[

,

]
, respectively).

\star

Non-convex approaches
:
Early PR algorithmic approaches relied on the alternating minimization
[
51
,
43
,
32
]
. A big breakthrough, however, arrived when
[

uncovered that the so-called Wirtinger flow (a modified gradient descent) often significantly outperforms all other known techniques (a closely related amplitude flow variant was considered in, e.g.,
[

(the sparse variants of Wirtinger and

,
57

). ADMM (alternating direction method of multipliers or Douglas-Rachford within the PR context), was introduced in

After deep learning (DL) had shown a great promise within compressed sensing

,

,
16

[

[

1.3.2
Initializers of non-convex methods and types of performance analysis

\star

Initializers:
Almost all of the above non-convex techniques require a good starting point –
initializer
.
Consequently a fairly strong theory related to initializers has been developed in recent years. The so-called spectral initializers appeared within the PR context as integral components of alternating minimization
[
92
]
(for related and earlier considerations see
[
72
,
91
]
).
[
24
]
proposed starting Wirtinger flow with simple diagonal spectral initializers.
[
75
]
considered a more general nonnegative diagonal variant and
precisely
characterized “overlap vs oversampling ratio” (for further extensions, see also
[
77
]
). A sharp transition between zero and nonzero overlap phases was uncovered directly impacting performance of non-convex PR algorithms. Negative diagonals as well as complex domains were incorporated in
[
89
]
where the concept of “weak threshold” (critical
\alpha
below which no diagonal spectral initializer achieves nonzero overlap) was introduced and
precisely
determined. The spectral preprocessing from
[
89
]
was proven in
[
76
]
as optimal for any
\alpha
(i.e., not only for the weak threshold). Gaussian measurements related results from
[
75
,
89
]
, were complemented by practically often more attractive orthogonal ones in
[
78
]
where an Expectation Propagation paradigm was utilized to precisely characterize associated “overlap vs oversampling ratio” (for a study of more general measurement models via statistical physics tools, see also
[
3
,
80
]
). Results from
[
78
]
were established as mathematically rigorous in
[
39
]
.

\star

“Qualitative” vs “quantitative” performance characterizations:
Most of the theoretical results associated with the above discussed non-convex methods relate to
qualitative
performance characterizations. They usually provide a strong intuitive hint as how the algorithms behave, but are not fully precise and do not supply sufficiently convincing arguments to support the non-convex methods practical superiority. On the other hand, precise,
quantitative
, characterizations are usually much harder to obtain and the available results are fairly scarce. The few known examples relate to replica analysis and the so-called Bayesian contexts. For example, the vector AMP (VAMP) algorithms
[
100
,
95
]
are analyzed via replica methods in
[
121
]
and a similar Bayesian inference context is considered in
[
81
]
where a large set of replica predictions is confirmed (for a real version of the complex counterpart from
[
81
]
, see
[
12
]
; for a further work on utilization of Bayesian context and replica methods, see also
[
119
]
, where excellent results are obtained for so-called phase-selection algorithm). Since the Bayesian concept heavily relies on the presumed statistical identicalness of
\bar{{\bf x}}_{i}
’s and a perfect statistical prior knowledge of both
\bar{{\bf x}}
and the so-called channel/posterior, it does not conveniently lend itself for a fair comparison with the above discussed methods that do not require such assumptions. On the other hand, it does allow for precise, phase transition type of results substantially superior to the corresponding ones obtained via the simpler qualitative methods.

1.4
Our contributions

We consider rank
d
measurements phase retrieval problem (RdM PR) and theoretically study performance of the associated
descending
phase retrieval algorithms (dPR) (dPRs are a generic class of non-convex PR methods that, among others, encompasses all forms of gradient descents). As stated earlier, our focus is on a statistical
linear/proportional
high-dimensional regime where sample complexity ratio
\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}
remains constant as
n
and
m
grow.

•

Following into the footsteps of
[
118
]
, we design a convenient
fundamental RdM PR optimization
(fd-pro) formulation and recognize relevance of studying its objective (see beginning of Section
2
).

•

Paralleling the development of the rank 1
Random duality theory
(RDT) based generic analytical program in
[
118
]
, we here develop its rank
d
analogue and utilize it for statistical studying of fd-pro (see Section
2.1
).

•

A direct connection between fd-pro’s objective and dPR’s ability to solve PR is established (see Section
2.2
). The impact of oversampling ratio,
\alpha
, on fd-pro’s objective (and ultimately dPR) is numerically evaluated through the developed RDT program (see Figures
1
and
2
for visual illustrations). Emergence of a phase transition phenomenon is observed. In particular, as
\alpha
increases, fd-pro’s objective transitions from a multi to a single local minimum phase (see Figure
1
).

•

Since the so-called strong deterministic duality is not in place, a lack of strong random duality is anticipated as well. This ultimately implies that the plain RDT results from Section
2.1
are of strictly bounding nature. A powerful
Lifted
RDT based program is then developed to further lift plain RDT estimates (for theoretical lifted RDT considerations see Section
3
; for visualization of the “
lifting effect
” see Figures
3
–
5
).

•

The obtained theoretical predictions assume heavy concentrations which in
n\rightarrow\infty
regime indeed happen. Since dimensions are finite in practical applications, we introduce the concept of “
safer compression
” phase transition adjustment. It suggests that in practical scenarios, one relies on a sample complexity ratios,
\alpha
, slightly higher (say
10-20\%
) than the minimally needed one determined by the phase transitioning behavior. By doing so, the so-called
local jitteriness
of the fd-pro’s objective is mitigated (see Figure
5
).

•

For practically highly relevant rank 2 case (the one which effectively emulates the complex phase retrieval), we conduct numerical experiments as well and obtain an excellent agreement with the theoretical predictions. In particular, a log barrier variant of the gradient descent is implemented together with the optimal diagonal spectral initializers from
[
89
,
76
,
117
]
. Even in small dimensional context with
n=100
(where the presence of strong local jitteriness effects is highly likely), the simulated phase transition is fairly close to the safer compression adjusted theoretical prediction (see Figure
6
).

Analysis of
descending
phase retrieval algorithms (dPR)

Assuming statistical
{\cal B}_{(i)}
in (
7
), one can proceed as in
[
117
,
118
]
and (in a fashion similar to
[
116
,
108
,
106
,
104
]
) connect studying RdM PR theoretical and algorithmic properties to the analysis of the following random optimization problem (rop)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}({\cal B}_{(i)}):\qquad\qquad\min_{{\bf x}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}}-\sqrt{{\bf x}^{T}{\cal B}_{(i)}{\bf x}}\right)^{2}.

(9)

After readjusting dimensions so that
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dm\times dn}
and
\bar{{\bf x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dn}
, we set

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{(i)}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}A_{jm+i,:}^{T}A_{jm+i,:},

(10)

and rewrite (
9
) as

{\mathcal{R}}(A):\hskip 5.05942pt\min_{{\bf x}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}}-\sqrt{{\bf x}^{T}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}A_{jm+i,:}^{T}A_{jm+i,:}\right){\bf x}}\right)^{2}=\min_{{\bf x}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\bar{{\bf y}}_{i}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf x}^{T}A_{jm+i,:}^{T}A_{jm+i,:}{\bf x}}\right)^{2}.

(11)

It is also relatively easy to see that (
11
) can be further rephrased as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(A):\qquad\qquad\min_{{\bf x},{\bf z}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\bar{{\bf z}}_{jm+1}^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle A{\bf x}={\bf z}.

(12)

It will turn out as convenient to set

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\textbf{\emph{Fundamental RdM PR optimization (fd-pro):}}}\hskip 5.78172pt\xi(c,x)\triangleq\min_{{\bf x},{\bf z}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\bar{{\bf z}}_{jm+1}^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}

subject to

\displaystyle A{\bf x}={\bf z}

(13)

\displaystyle A\bar{{\bf x}}=\bar{{\bf z}}

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{T}\bar{{\bf x}}=x

\displaystyle\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}=c.

One can observe that PR optimization formulations usually seen throughout the literature are slightly different from (
9
), (
12
), and (
13
). A full analogy with the
squared magnitudes
common practice would be to use
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\bar{{\bf z}}_{jm+1}^{2}
and
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+1}^{2}
instead of their roots in the objective. In the standard rank 1 measurements contexts (
d=1
), such practice relates to the so=called
intensity
measurements whereas the non-squared magnitudes relate to the so-called
amplitude
measurements. As
[
117
,
118
]
demonstrated, not much of a conceptual difference between the two options actually exists. The
non-squared
option allows for more elegant and numerically less cumbersome treatment and as such will be our preferable choice in analytical considerations.

From (
13
) one also easily has
\xi(c,x)=\xi(c,-x)
. To avoid phase ambiguity trivialities, we throughout the presentation consider only

(all obtained results will automatically hold for corresponding
x<0
as well). As
[
118
]
observed, fd-pro and in particular

are important mathematical structures behind the study of two key PR problems.
(i)
The first one is the uniqueness of PR’s solution and directly corresponds to ensuring that
\xi(1,x)>0
for
x\neq 1
.
(ii)
The second one is the PR’s practical (algorithmic) solvability and directly relates to increasing/decreasing behavior of function
\xi(c,x)
. To make the presentation neater, we consider a statistical scenario with
A
comprised of iid standard normals. Moreover, following into the footsteps of
[
118
]
, we consider
Random duality theory
(RDT) based statistical analytical program developed therein and discuss how it can be adapted to handle fd-pro and ultimately RdM PR.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-13.73148pt\mbox{1) \emph{Finding underlying optimization algebraic representation}}&\hskip 0.0pt\mbox{2) \emph{Determining the random dual}}\\

\displaystyle A{\bf x}=A_{:,1}x+A_{:,2:n}{\bf x}_{2:n}={\bf z}

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n}{\bf x}_{i}=c-x^{2}=r^{2},

Utilizing the Lagrangian we have

\displaystyle\xi(c,x)=\min_{{\bf x},{\bf z}}\max_{{\bf y}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}A_{jm+1,1}^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}+{\bf y}^{T}A_{:,1}x+{\bf y}^{T}A_{:,2:n}{\bf x}_{2:n}-{\bf y}^{T}{\bf z}

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n}{\bf x}_{i}=c-x^{2}=r^{2}.

(18)

We then also set
{\bf g}^{(0)}=A_{:,1}
and write (
18
) in the following more compact form

\displaystyle\xi(c,x)=\min_{\|{\bf x}_{2:n}\|_{2}=r,{\bf z}}\max_{{\bf y}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\left({\bf g}_{jm+i}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf g}^{(0)}x+{\bf y}^{T}A_{:,2:n}{\bf x}_{2:n}-{\bf y}^{T}{\bf z}\right).

(19)

The above is a key algebraic characterization of fd-pro. It is summarized in the following lemma together with its implications regarding theoretical solvability of RdM PR.

Lemma 1
.

Consider rank
d
measurements phase retrieval (RdM PR) problem with
dn
unknowns and sample complexity
m
. Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dm\times dn}
,
{\bf g}^{(0)}\triangleq A_{:,1}
, and assume a high-dimensional linear (proportional) regime,
with sample complexity ratio
\alpha=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m}{n}
. Then, RdM PR is theoretically solvable (i.e., it has a unique (up to a global phase) solution) provided that

\forall x\neq 1\qquad f_{rp}(1,x;A)>0,

(20)

where

f_{rp}(c,x;A)\hskip-2.168pt\triangleq\hskip-3.61371pt\frac{1}{dn}\hskip-2.8903pt\min_{\|{\bf x}_{2:n}\|_{2}=r,{\bf z}}\max_{{\bf y}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\left({\bf g}_{jm+i}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf g}^{(0)}x+{\bf y}^{T}A_{:,2:n}{\bf x}_{2:n}-{\bf y}^{T}{\bf z}\right).

(21)

Proof.

Follows as an automatic consequence of the preceding discussion and the recognition that (
21
) is (
19
) cosmetically scaled by
\frac{1}{dn}
.
∎

The optimization on the right hand side of (
21
) is the so-called
random primal
. We determine the corresponding
random dual
next.

2)
Determining the random dual:
As usual within the RDT, the concentration of measure is utilized as well. This basically means that for any fixed

Theorem 1

Assume the setup of Lemma

being iid standard normals. For two positive scalars
c
and
x
(
0\leq x\leq c
) set
r\triangleq\sqrt{c-x^{2}}
. Let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\left(A,{\bf g}^{(1)},{\bf h}^{(1)}\right)=\left({\bf g}^{(0)},A_{:,2:dn},{\bf g}^{(1)},{\bf h}^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle\phi({\bf x},{\bf z},{\bf y})\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\left({\bf g}_{jm+i}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}\hskip-3.61371pt+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf g}^{(0)}x+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf g}^{(1)}\|{\bf x}_{2:dn}\|_{2}+\left({\bf x}_{2:dn}\right)^{T}{\bf h}^{(1)}\|{\bf y}\|_{2}-{\bf y}^{T}{\bf z}

\displaystyle f_{rd}(c,x;{\mathcal{G}})\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\frac{1}{dn}\min_{\|{\bf x}_{2:dn}\|_{2}=r,{\bf z}}\max_{\|{\bf y}\|_{2}=r_{y},r_{y}>0}\phi({\bf x},{\bf z},{\bf y})

\displaystyle\phi_{0}\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}f_{rd}(c,x;{\mathcal{G}}).\vskip 0.0pt

(22)

One then has

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}(f_{rp}(c,x;A)>\phi_{0})\longrightarrow 1,

(23)

and

\displaystyle\hskip-5.05942pt(\phi_{0}>0)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}\left(\frac{\xi(c,x)}{dn}=f_{rd}(c,x;{\mathcal{G}})>0\right)\longrightarrow 1\right)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}(f_{rp}(c,x;A)>0)\longrightarrow 1\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow

\displaystyle\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}\left(\mbox{PR is (uniquely) solvable}\right)\longrightarrow 1\right).

Proof.

It is an immediate consequence of Theorem 1 in
[

]
which follows through conditioning on
{\bf g}^{(0)}
and application of the Gordon’s comparison theorem (see, e.g., Theorem B in
[

,

]
(see Theorem 1, Corollary 1, and Section 2.7.2 in
[

]
as well as Theorem 1, Corollary 1, and Section 2.3.2 in
[

]
).
∎

\displaystyle f_{q}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}\left(\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}\|_{2}-\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}x+{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(1)}r\|_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(1+c)-2{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}\left(\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}\|_{2}\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}x+{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(1)}r\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle d(1+c)-2{\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}\left(\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}\|_{2}\|U{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}x+U{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(1)}r\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle d(1+c)-2r{\mathbb{E}}_{\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}\|_{2}}\left(\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}\|_{2}L_{\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\left(-\frac{\|{\bf g}_{(i)}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}x^{2}}{2r^{2}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(1+c)-2r\int_{0}^{\infty}u\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left((x_{\lambda}+1){\mathcal{I}}_{0}\left(\frac{x_{\lambda}}{2}\right)+x_{\lambda}{\mathcal{I}}_{1}\left(\frac{x_{\lambda}}{2}\right)\right)e^{-\frac{x_{\lambda}}{2}}ue^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}}du

\displaystyle 2(1+c)-2r\int_{0}^{\infty}u\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\left(\frac{u^{2}x^{2}}{2r^{2}}+1\right){\mathcal{I}}_{0}\left(\frac{u^{2}x^{2}}{4r^{2}}\right)+\frac{u^{2}x^{2}}{2r^{2}}{\mathcal{I}}_{1}\left(\frac{u^{2}x^{2}}{4r^{2}}\right)\right)e^{-\frac{u^{2}x^{2}}{4r^{2}}}ue^{-\frac{u^{2}}{2}}du,

where

and

3
Lifted RDT

A lack of strong random duality implies that the above bounds are

[

As discussed on many occasions in

1

Theorem 2

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\left(A,{\bf g}^{(1)},{\bf h}^{(1)}\right)=\left({\bf g}^{(0)},A_{:,2:dn},{\bf g}^{(1)},{\bf h}^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle\phi({\bf x},{\bf z},{\bf y})\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\left({\bf g}_{jm+i}^{(0)}\right)^{2}}-\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{\bf z}_{jm+i}^{2}}\right)^{2}\hskip-3.61371pt+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf g}^{(0)}x+{\bf y}^{T}{\bf g}^{(1)}\|{\bf x}_{2:dn}\|_{2}+\left({\bf x}_{2:dn}\right)^{T}{\bf h}^{(1)}\|{\bf y}\|_{2}-{\bf y}^{T}{\bf z}

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{rd}(c,x;{\mathcal{G}})\hskip-8.67204pt

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\hskip-8.67204pt\min_{\|{\bf x}_{2:dn}\|_{2}=r,{\bf z}}\max_{\|{\bf y}\|_{2}=r_{y},r_{y}>0}\phi({\bf x},{\bf z},{\bf y})

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\max_{r_{y}>0}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{dn}\left(\frac{c_{3}}{2}r^{2}r_{y}^{2}-\frac{1}{c_{3}}\log\left({\mathbb{E}}_{{\mathcal{G}}_{(2)}}e^{-c_{3}\bar{f}_{rd}(c,x;{\mathcal{G}})}\right)\right).\vskip 0.0pt

(39)

One then has

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}(f_{rp}(c,x;A)>\bar{\phi}_{0})\longrightarrow 1,

(40)

and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt(\bar{\phi}_{0}>0)\Longrightarrow\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}(f_{rp}(c,x;A)>0)\longrightarrow 1\right)\Longrightarrow\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{A}\left(\mbox{RdM PR is (uniquely) solvable}\right)\longrightarrow 1\right).

(41)

Proof.

Follows as a trivial extension of Theorem 2 in

5
Conclusion

We study rank
d
positive definite measurements phase retrieval (RdM PR) generalization and the associated
descending
algorithms (dPR).
[
118
]
developed
Random duality theory
(RDT) based analytical program to characterize performance of dPRs when measurements are of rank 1. We here show how the program can be extended so that it can handle rank
d
measurements. The impact of oversampling ratio,
\alpha
, on dPR’s performance is numerically evaluated through the developed extension of the RDT program and emergence of a phase transition is observed. In particular, as
\alpha
increases, the optimizing objective transitions from a multi to a single local minimum phase, resulting in an analogous dPR transition from a generically non-converging to a generically converging to a global optimum phase.

An anticipated lack of strong random duality (and consequent, strictly bounding, nature of plain RDT results) motivated implementation of a powerful
Lifted
RDT based mechanism. The “
lifting effect
” and the resulting sample complexity ratio phase transition adjustment are precise;y characterized as well. Since the obtained theoretical predictions assume heavy concentrations (which for
n\rightarrow\infty
indeed happen), they are a bit optimistic for practical considerations. To account for the finite dimensional effects and potential appearance of the objective’s local jitteriness, we introduce the “
safer compression
” phase transition adjustment concept. It assumes that in practical considerations one relies on a sample complexity ratios,
\alpha
, slightly higher (say
10-20\%
) than the minimally needed phase transitioning one.

Theoretical results are also complemented with the corresponding numerical experiments as well. For rank 2 measurements case (practically highly relevant one that effectively emulates the complex phase retrieval), we implement a log barrier variant of the gradient descent together with the optimal diagonal spectral initializers from
[
89
,
76
,
117
]
. Even for small dimensions (on the order of
n=100
) where strong local jitteriness effects are highly likely, the simulated phase transition fairly close matches the corresponding safer compression adjusted theoretical one.

Developed methodologies are fairly general and allow for many further extensions. Clearly, studying further impact of fl RDT is (from the theoretical point of view) the next logical step. On the other hand, studying signal and/or measurements further structuring, stability, and robustness appear as topics of great practical importance. Concepts presented here apply to any of these studies. Since the relevant technical details are problem specific, we present them in separate papers.
