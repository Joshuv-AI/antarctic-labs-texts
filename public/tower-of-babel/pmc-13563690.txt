ENT1 inhibition links oligodendrocyte lipid metabolism to connectivity in tauopathy
Authors: Ching‐Pang Chang, Ching‐Wen Chang, Ching‐Wen Wu, Jian Jing Siew, David Blum, Lee‐Way Jin, You‐Yin Chen, Yijuang Chern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMetabolic dysfunction, altered adenosine signaling, and white matter abnormalities are implicated in tauopathies, but their relationship to network disconnection remains unclear. Myelinating oligodendrocytes may represent a metabolically vulnerable hub linking these processes to circuit dysfunction.METHODSWe assessed human hippocampal tissue by immunofluorescence and profiled THY‐Tau22 mice using diffusion tensor imaging, metabolomics, lipidomics, and single‐nucleus RNA sequencing. Symptomatic mice were treated with the equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (ENT1) inhibitor J4 to evaluate therapeutic modulation.RESULTSHippocampal tissue from patients with tauopathy showed reduced oligodendrocyte‐ and myelin‐associated CNPase signal. THY‐Tau22 mice exhibited progressive reductions in hippocampal–prefrontal fractional anisotropy, myelin‐associated alterations, purine and lipid metabolic remodeling, and oligodendrocyte‐enriched lipid‐associated transcriptional changes. J4 treatment selectively modulated purine and lipid metabolic pathways and improved myelin‐associated markers and structural connectivity measures.DISCUSSIONENT1 inhibition links adenosine homeostasis to oligodendrocyte‐associated metabolic pathways and supports further investigation as a potential therapeutic approach for tauopathy‐associated network dysfunction.

BACKGROUND

Tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), are characterized by the progressive accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins, resulting in synaptic dysfunction, axonal degeneration, and impaired neural connectivity. 1 , 2 White matter abnormalities are increasingly recognized in both AD patients and tauopathy models. 3 , 4 Myelin deficits and oligodendrocyte dysfunction are emerging as early pathological features shared across neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, often appearing before measurable neuronal loss becomes evident. Convergent mechanisms, including chronic neuroinflammation, protein aggregation, lipid dysregulation, and organellar stress, have been implicated in these changes, positioning oligodendrocytes as potential contributors to disease progression. 3 In tauopathy models, early disease stages have been associated with localized myelin damage along axons and altered axoplasmic vesicle release, consistent with transient white matter dysfunction. Although compensatory myelin remodeling may partially restore axonal integrity, early white matter disruptions could contribute to impaired neuronal connectivity and cognitive decline as disease advances. 4 Notably, despite relatively stable firing activity in individual hippocampal neurons during early tauopathy, emergent network properties, including pairwise correlations and network criticality, are progressively impaired, reflecting broader disruptions in neural connectivity. 5 These network‐level disruptions closely associate with anatomical degeneration and behavioral deficits, and may serve as early predictive markers of disease progression. Although tau pathology has been classically linked to neuronal dysfunction, growing evidence reveals that glial and metabolic abnormalities emerge early and may contribute to disease progression. Integrated metabolomic and lipidomic analyses reveal that pathological tau perturbs brain energy metabolism and lipid homeostasis early in disease progression. 6 These disruptions have been linked to neuronal lipid droplet accumulation and the release of unsaturated lipids, which may exacerbate microglial dysfunction through pro‐inflammatory activation, oxidative stress, and impaired phagocytosis. 7 Together, these findings support the view that early glial and metabolic dysfunction may shape tauopathy progression and amplify neurodegenerative processes. Although recent studies have begun to uncover these early glial and metabolic changes, the molecular pathways by which tau accumulation disrupts glial energy homeostasis remain largely elusive.

Among neuromodulators involved in glial and metabolic regulation, adenosine signaling is a key modulator that coordinates neuronal excitability, myelination, glial responses, and cellular metabolism. Through G protein–coupled receptors such as A1R and A2AR, it fine‐tunes synaptic transmission, neurotransmitter release, mitochondrial function, and immune activity. 8 , 9 Both activation and blockade of A2AR influence oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination, underscoring the importance of adenosine receptor signaling in white matter regulation. 10 , 11 Dysregulation of adenosine signaling has been implicated in synaptic failure, impaired bioenergetics, and chronic neuroinflammation across several neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, Huntington's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. 12 , 13 , 14 However, the molecular pathways through which altered adenosine dynamics contribute to glial metabolic dysfunction remain incompletely understood. The equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (ENT1), which regulates adenosine availability by mediating its bidirectional flux across cell membranes, has recently emerged as a potential therapeutic target in several neurological disease contexts, including tauopathy, AD, spinal cord injury, and Dravet syndrome. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Notably, our prior work showed that pharmacological inhibition of ENT1 with J4, a brain‐penetrant ENT1 inhibitor, preserved cognitive function, reduced tau hyperphosphorylation, mitigated mitochondrial dysfunction, and attenuated glial activation in THY‐Tau22 mice, a well‐characterized model of tauopathy. 15 These findings established ENT1‐dependent adenosine homeostasis as a modulator of neuronal energy stress and tau‐associated pathology. However, whether this pathway also influences oligodendrocyte‐associated lipid metabolism, myelin integrity, and long‐range structural connectivity remains unknown.

The mechanisms linking oligodendrocyte metabolism to loss of white matter integrity during neurodegeneration remain incompletely defined. Building on our previous work, we integrate imaging, lipidomic, metabolomic, transcriptomic, and histological approaches to characterize oligodendrocyte‐associated metabolic alterations during tauopathy progression and after modulation of adenosine homeostasis. Our findings link oligodendrocyte‐associated metabolic dysfunction with altered white matter integrity and support further investigation of ENT1 inhibition as a potential therapeutic approach for tauopathy‐associated network dysfunction.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed literature on white matter abnormalities, oligodendrocyte dysfunction, lipid metabolism, adenosine signaling, and equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (ENT1) inhibition in Alzheimer's disease and tauopathies using PubMed and relevant primary studies. Prior work implicates myelin and glial metabolic disruption in neurodegeneration, but how these processes relate to adenosine homeostasis and network disconnection remains unresolved. Interpretation: This study links three disease‐relevant axes: oligodendrocyte‐associated metabolic dysfunction, structural connectivity loss, and ENT1‐regulated adenosine transport. Human tauopathy hippocampus showed reduced oligodendrocyte‐ and myelin‐associated signals, while THY‐Tau22 mice developed hippocampal‐prefrontal connectivity deficits, purine/lipid remodeling, and oligodendrocyte‐enriched transcriptional changes. Pharmacological ENT1 inhibition with J4 modulated purine/lipid pathways and improved myelin‐associated and structural connectivity measures. Future directions: Building on this ENT1‐linked metabolic‐connectivity framework, future studies should determine whether ENT1 inhibition directly modulates oligodendrocyte metabolism or acts indirectly through neurons, astrocytes, microglia, or network changes. Cell‐type–specific metabolic profiling, spatial metabolomics, and longitudinal imaging will help define causality and therapeutic relevance.

METHODS

Animals and drug administration

The THY‐Tau22 mouse model (B6.Cg‐Tg(Thy1‐MAPT)22Schd) carries a mutant human 4R tau (G272V and P301S) under the regulation of the neuron‐specific Thy1.2 promoter and is maintained on a C57BL/6J background. 19 All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Academia Sinica, Taiwan. Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (IVCs, two to five mice per cage) after genotyping and experimental group assignment, and had ad libitum access to water and a standard chow diet (LabDiet®, San Antonio, TX, USA). Animals were maintained under standard housing conditions with standard bedding and no additional environmental enrichment. Environmental conditions were controlled, including a 12‐hour light/dark cycle, within the Animal Care Facility at the Institute of Biomedical Sciences (Taipei, Taiwan). Unless otherwise specified, male mice were used for all experiments. Female mice were used for snRNA‐seq analysis, as described in the “Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing” subsection. A previous study reported no overt sex‐related differences in the phenotypic outcomes examined in THY‐Tau22 mice at a symptomatic stage. 20 To examine the effects of tauopathy on neuronal connectivity and metabolism, THY‐Tau22 mice at various ages (4, 6, 9, and 10 months) were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Additionally, to evaluate the impact of J4 treatment, male THY‐Tau22 mice and their littermate controls were randomly divided into groups receiving either J4 (0.02 mg/mL in 1% HPβCD; equivalent to approximately 3.04 mg/kg/day based on average water intake; designated as TauJ) or vehicle (1% 2‐hydroxypropyl‐β‐cyclodextrin [HPβCD]; designated as TauC and WTC) in drinking water for 1 month, starting at 9 months of age. This J4 concentration was selected based on our previous in vivo studies using the same drinking‐water administration regimen in amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1 (APP/PS1) and THY‐Tau22 mice, in which this concentration showed therapeutic efficacy. 15 , 18

Human cases

Post mortem human posterior hippocampal tissues were obtained from the UC Davis Alzheimer's Disease Center (USA) for immunofluorescence analysis. The cohort comprised 18 cases, including six neurologically normal controls, six individuals diagnosed with AD, and six patients with frontotemporal dementia with tau pathology (FTD‐Tau; corticobasal degeneration, progressive supranuclear palsy, and Pick's disease). Detailed case information is provided in Table S1.

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning was performed using a 7 Tesla Bruker Biospec system (Ettlingen, Germany) equipped with a linear volume coil for radiofrequency transmission and a planar surface coil (T7399V3; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) for signal reception positioned over the animal's head. To stabilize the mice during scanning and maintain physiological parameters, animals were secured in a custom‐built cradle with a water‐circulated heating pad, and their core body temperature was kept near 37°C. Respiration was continuously monitored using a pressure sensor (SA Instruments Inc., NY, USA) placed beneath the abdomen, with breathing rates maintained between 20–40 breaths per minute. Anesthesia was induced and maintained using 3% isoflurane vaporized in a gas mixture of 20% oxygen, 75% nitrogen, and 5% carbon dioxide.

Magnetic field homogeneity was optimized using the fast automated shimming technique by mapping along projections (FASTMAP) with first‐order shims on an isotropic voxel of 7 × 7 × 7 mm3 encompassing the imaging slices. Turbo spin echo (TSE) T2 images were acquired with repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 33 ms, matrix size = 256 × 256 × 15, field of view (FOV) = 20 × 20 mm3, voxel size = 0.08 × 0.08 × 0.4 mm3, slice thickness = 0.4 mm, 15 horizontal slices. For diffusion imaging, we applied the diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) ‐EPI spin–echo sequence with the following parameters: TR = 3,750 ms, TE = 40.28 ms, matrix size = 50 × 50, FOV = 20 × 20 mm2, slice thickness of 0.4 mm across 15 horizontal slices, and a diffusion time of 20 ms. The encoding duration for diffusion was 6 ms, and we acquired data from 12 diffusion sampling directions.

DTI analysis

Diffusion‐weighted imaging data were preprocessed and analyzed using DSI Studio (http://dsi‐studio.labsolver.org). The analysis pipeline included motion correction, mask generation, and diffusion map reconstruction utilizing a model‐based approach as described by Jiang et al. 21 To enable anatomical referencing, the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas 22 was resized in MATLAB (R2021a, MathWorks) to match the DTI acquisition dimensions, and co‐registration with the diffusion images was performed accordingly. Fractional anisotropy (FA) values were derived through both tractography and region‐of‐interest (ROI) analyses focused on memory‐associated areas. For tractography, the hippocampus (Hippo) and prefrontal cortex (PFC) were used as seed regions, while ROI analyses targeted the PFC and specific Hippo subfields.

Immunohistochemical staining

Mouse brain

Mice were anesthetized with Zoletil 50 (50 mg/kg; #05393, Virbac) administered intraperitoneally and subsequently underwent transcardial perfusion with 0.9% NaCl. Following perfusion, animals were decapitated, and the brains were rapidly dissected and post‐fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Brain sections (20 µm) were prepared from the desired mice as previously described. 15 For immunofluorescence staining, antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the brain slices in 1× citrate buffer (C9999, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 97.5°C for 20 minutes, followed by cooling to room temperature (RT). The sections were then washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X‐100 in 0.1 M PBS, and blocked with either 3% normal goat serum (NGS) or 3% normal donkey serum (NDS) in 0.1 M PBS for 2 h at RT. Following two additional washes with 0.1 M PBS, the sections were incubated with the anti‐myelin basic protein (MBP; mouse, 1:1000, BioLegend, SMI‐99P), anti‐2′,3′‐cyclic nucleotide 3′‐phosphodiesterase (CNPase; goat, 1:1000, antibodies, A270543), or anti‐aspartoacylase (ASPA; rabbit, 1:1000, GeneTex, GTX113389) antibody in a solution containing 1% NGS (or 1% NDS), 0.2% Triton X‐100, and 0.1% sodium azide in 0.1 M PBS at 4°C for 48 hours. After extensive rinsing, they were exposed to the corresponding secondary antibody (1:500) for 2 h at RT. Subsequently, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33258 (1:5000) for 10 minutes at RT, followed by another round of washes to remove excess dye. Finally, the stained sections were mounted onto silane‐coated slides (Muto Pure Chemicals Co., Tokyo, Japan) using mounting media (Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) and stored at 4°C until imaging. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an LSM 780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany), and image analysis was carried out with MetaMorph software (Universal Imaging, PA, USA). The coherency of myelination, which serves as an inverse indicator of myelinated axon complexity, was quantified using Fiji/ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Coherency measurements were obtained through the “OrientationJ measure” plugin in Fiji.

Human brain

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded human brain sections (6 µm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the sections in 1× citrate buffer (C9999, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 97.5°C for 20 minutes, followed by cooling to RT. The sections were then washed with 0.1 M PBS, permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X‐100 in 0.1 M PBS, and blocked with either 3% NGS or 3% NDS in 0.1 M PBS for 2 hours at RT. Following two additional washes with 0.1 M PBS, the sections were incubated with the anti‐CNPase (rabbit, 1:500, Invitrogen, PA5‐27972), anti‐CNPase (goat, 1:750, antibodies, A270543), anti‐ASPA (rabbit, 1:500, GeneTex, GTX113389), or anti‐pTauSer202, Thr205 (mouse, 1:250, Invitrogen, MN1020) antibody in a solution containing 1% NGS (or 1% NDS), 0.2% Triton X‐100, and 0.1% sodium azide in 0.1 M PBS at 4°C for 48 h. After extensive rinsing, the sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody (1:500) for 2 hours at RT; the sections were treated with 0.1% (w/v) Sudan Black B (199664, Sigma) in 70% ethanol for 15 minutes at RT to quench autofluorescence. Nuclei were then stained with Hoechst 33258 (1:5000) for 10 minutes at RT, followed by extensive washes with PBS. Finally, the sections were mounted using mounting media (Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) and stored at 4°C until imaging. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an LSM 780 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany). For each subject, eight images were captured from hippocampal brain slices under identical acquisition settings. Image analysis was conducted using MetaMorph software (Universal Imaging, PA, USA), and fluorescence intensity values were normalized to the overall average intensity of the normal control group.

Dissection of hippocampal subregions

Mice were anesthetized with Zoletil 50 (50 mg/kg; #05393, Virbac) administered intraperitoneally and subsequently underwent transcardial perfusion with 0.9% NaCl. Following perfusion, animals were decapitated, and the brains were rapidly dissected. Hippocampal subregions were dissected with modifications based on previous protocols. 23 The CA1 subregion was selected as the primary region of interest based on the prominent CA1 tau pathology previously reported in THY‐Tau22 mice. 19 Briefly, the hippocampi were carefully removed and placed on an ice‐cold Petri dish under a dissecting microscope. Each hippocampus was aligned with the anterior end facing upward. To create flat, blunt reference points, small vertical incisions were made at both the anterior and posterior tips using a scalpel. An additional incision was made approximately one‐third of the way from the anterior to posterior end under microscopic observation, resulting in three distinct segments. Using a scalpel, the CA1, CA3, or DG subregions were carefully isolated with a single curved downward cut. The dissected tissue was immediately transferred onto a Petri dish on dry ice to facilitate rapid freezing. Once all tissue segments were frozen, they were carefully detached from the Petri dish using a scalpel blade and transferred into labeled microtubes. Samples were stored in liquid nitrogen until further processing.

Lipidomic analysis

Tissue extraction

For lipidomic profiling, microdissected hippocampal CA1 tissues from THY‐Tau22 mice and littermate controls were used. The frozen CA1 tissues were weighed and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles in an extraction buffer composed of methanol and butanol in a 1:1 ratio, along with 10 mM ammonium formate and an internal standard (1 ppm EquiSPLASHTM LIPIDOMIX®, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA). To enhance extraction efficiency, the homogenate was subjected to vortex and sonication for a total of 30 minutes, consisting of three 10‐minute cycles with 5‐minute intervals between each cycle. The lysate concentration was standardized to 25 mg/mL. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 11,500 rpm for 10 minutes at 20°C, and the supernatant was collected for LC‐MS analysis.

UPLC‐MS

Lipidomic profiling was performed using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II ultra‐performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The system was directly coupled to an Agilent 6545 XT quadrupole time‐of‐flight (Q‐TOF) mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a Dual AJS electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Metabolite separation was conducted using a ZORBAX RRHD Eclipse Plus C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Agilent Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The mobile phases consisted of water:acetonitrile:isopropanol (5:3:2, v/v/v) as eluent A and water:acetonitrile:isopropanol (1:9:90, v/v/v) as eluent B, both containing 10 mM ammonium formate. The column was maintained at 45°C, with a flow rate of 400 µL/min. Data acquisition was performed in both positive and negative full‐scan modes, covering an m/z range of 60–1700. Chromatographic data processing, peak detection, and spectral waveform analysis were conducted using Agilent Qualitative Analysis 10.0 and Agilent Profinder B.10.00 software (Agilent). Lipidomic annotation was carried out using MS‐DIAL v5.5 after converting the MS1 and MS2 data to ABF format with the Reifycs Analysis Base File Converter.

Data analysis

Data acquired from positive and negative ionization full‐scan modes were combined following normalization to internal standards. For metabolites detected in both modes, the value with the higher raw abundance was retained for downstream analysis. Log2‐fold change was calculated as the difference in log2‐transformed mean abundances between experimental groups (e.g., Tau22 vs. wild‐type [WT]; Tau22_J4 vs. Tau22). Statistical significance was assessed using a two‐tailed Student's t‐test, with thresholds set at p < 0.05 and absolute log2‐fold change ≥ 0.263. Significantly altered metabolites were visualized using volcano plots (Instant Clue 24 ) and heatmaps (Morpheus software, https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/). To enable functional interpretation of lipidomic alterations, lipid information, including LION abbreviation, LIPID MAPS abbreviation, and lipid category, was standardized and exported via LipidSig 2.0 (https://lipidsig.bioinfomics.org) for compatibility with the LION/web ontology database (http://www.lipidontology.com). Lipid species without corresponding entries in the LION database were excluded prior to enrichment analysis. Functional enrichment analysis was then performed with LION/web, which classifies lipids according to their chemical properties, biological functions, and subcellular localization. 25

Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing

Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) was conducted using 12.5‐month‐old female mice. J4 was administered for 1 month starting at 11.5 months, corresponding to a late symptomatic stage. One mouse per condition was used for snRNA‐seq analysis. Hippocampal tissue was dissociated following the 10× Genomics Nuclei Isolation protocol (CG000366), and nuclei were processed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression kit. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000, and the output was analyzed with Cell Ranger v3.1.0, aligning reads to the mouse genome (mm10) while excluding non‐coding gene biotypes. Quality control in Seurat included filtering cells with low unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts (< 200), low gene counts (< 200), or high mitochondrial expression (> 10%), resulting in 11,390, 13,545, and 13,674 cells for WT, Tau22, and Tau22_J4 samples, respectively. Data were normalized and integrated using SCTransform, 26 selecting 3000 features for integration, followed by dimensionality reduction with principal component analysis (PCA; 150 components) and UMAP (40 components). Clustering was performed using FindNeighbors and FindClusters (67 components, resolution = 0.35), and visualized using UMAP with the “DimPlot” function, with labels applied to identify individual clusters and split visualizations based on sample conditions. Pseudobulk analysis was performed by aggregating normalized expression values across all nuclei from each experimental group using Seurat, providing group‐level averaged expression profiles for global comparison of transcriptional alterations. Differentially expressed genes in pseudobulk analyses were defined as genes exhibiting an absolute log2‐fold change ≥ 0.263 (corresponding to a fold change ≥ 1.2 in either direction). Module‐level enrichment analyses were then performed using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat to assess the relative enrichment and distribution of predefined gene sets across single nuclei. Cell annotation combined SingleR, PanglaoDB, and ScType tools, 27 , 28 , 29 with visualizations highlighting condition‐specific cell type distributions. All analyses were conducted in R using a uniform workflow across samples. Cluster‐ and condition‐level visualizations were generated to examine cell type composition and state‐specific alterations.

Statistics

All experiments were conducted in a blinded manner to minimize bias. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (La Jolla, CA, USA). For comparisons involving two groups, differences were assessed using unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐tests. For experiments involving three groups, statistical analyses were performed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with pre‐specified multiple comparisons. Two a priori contrasts were tested based on the study design: WT versus THY‐Tau22 (disease effect) and THY‐Tau22 versus THY‐Tau22 + J4 (treatment effect), with Holm–Sidak correction applied to adjust for multiple testing. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

RESULTS

ENT1 inhibition ameliorates tauopathy‐associated myelin alterations and hippocampal‐prefrontal connectivity deficits

Progressive disruption of brain connectivity is a hallmark of tauopathies and may precede overt cognitive decline. 30 , 31 , 32 Consistent with white matter degeneration, analysis of post mortem hippocampal tissue from tauopathy patients, including those diagnosed with AD and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) with tau pathology (FTD‐Tau), revealed a significant reduction in the oligodendrocyte marker CNPase (Figure 1; AD, 61.81 ± 8.20%, *, p < 0.05 vs. normal; FTD‐Tau, 62.91 ± 3.58%, *, p < 0.05 vs. normal). This decrease suggests an alteration in oligodendrocyte‐ and myelin‐associated signal in the human hippocampus, supporting the relevance of white‐matter–related pathology in tauopathies.

We next used immunofluorescence staining to investigate the levels of myelin basic protein (MBP), a structural component of myelin, in an experimental model of tauopathy (i.e., THY‐Tau22 mice). Previous studies have shown that pathological tau accumulation in this model is enriched in the hippocampal CA1 region, 19a region critically involved in memory processing and particularly vulnerable in AD. While tau pathology in THY‐Tau22 mice has been reported to emerge at early ages and progressively increase with disease progression, 19 , 33 more overt pathological and functional alterations become prominent at later stages. Based on this progression, MBP levels were examined at 4, 6, and 10 months of age, corresponding to presymptomatic, early symptomatic and late symptomatic stages, respectively. Myelin organization was quantified using coherency analysis. At 4 months, MBP expression and coherency in the hippocampal CA1 region were comparable between THY‐Tau22 and WT littermate controls (Figure 2A–C). At 6 months, MBP levels remained similar between genotypes. However, a reduction in coherency (*, p < 0.05 vs. WT) emerged in the CA1 region of THY‐Tau22 mice (Figure 2D‐F), indicating early signs of myelin disorganization. By 10 months, significant reductions in both MBP intensity (*, p < 0.05 vs. WT_Vehicle) and coherency (**, p < 0.01 vs. WT_Vehicle) were observed in the CA1 region (Figure 2G‐I), supporting progressive disruption of myelin‐associated organization during disease progression. To evaluate the potential therapeutic impact of ENT1 inhibition, THY‐Tau22 mice were treated with J4 (0.02 mg/mL in 1% HPβCD) for 1 month starting at 9 months of age. J4 treatment significantly increased MBP levels (#, p < 0.05 vs. THY‐Tau22_Vehicle) and improved myelin coherency (#, p < 0.05 vs. THY‐Tau22_Vehicle) in the CA1 region at 10 months (Figure 2G‐I), suggesting that ENT1 inhibition ameliorates myelin‐associated alterations when administered at a symptomatic stage.

Because DTI captures microstructural features of white matter organization, including but not limited to myelin‐related changes, we used DTI to assess structural connectivity and tissue organization. 34 We next performed DTI analyses across disease stages in THY‐Tau22 mice at 4, 6, and 9 months of age. Analysis of FA, an indirect measure of tissue anisotropy influenced by axonal organization, myelin integrity, and fiber architecture, along the Hippo‐PFC tract revealed progressive structural connectivity loss in THY‐Tau22 mice. FA was already reduced at 4 months (Figure 3A, 96.88 ± 0.93%, *, p < 0.05 vs. WT), declined further at 6 months (Figure 3A, 93.55 ± 1.43%, ***, p < 0.001 vs. WT), and reached 87.61 ± 2.17% at 9 months (Figure 3A, **, p < 0.01 vs. WT), indicating stepwise deterioration of structural connectivity. To evaluate whether ENT1 inhibition could modify this late‐stage deficit, symptomatic THY‐Tau22 mice were assessed at 9 months of age and then randomly assigned to receive either vehicle or J4 (0.02 mg/mL in 1% HPβCD) via drinking water for 1 month. After treatment, FA in vehicle‐treated mice further declined to 81.97 ± 2.70% at 10 months (Figure 3B, **, p < 0.01 vs. WT_Vehicle), whereas J4‐treated mice showed significantly higher FA compared with vehicle‐treated THY‐Tau22 mice (Figure 3B, 98.66 ± 2.04%, ##, p < 0.01 vs. THY‐Tau22_Vehicle). To determine whether these changes extended to another hippocampal circuit, we additionally examined hippocampal‐entorhinal cortex (Hippo‐EC) pathway. Hippo‐EC FA was comparable between THY‐Tau22 and WT mice at 4 months but was significantly reduced at 6 and 9 months. At 10 months, J4 treatment significantly increased Hippo‐EC FA relative to vehicle‐treated THY‐Tau22 mice (Figure S1). Consistent with these tract‐based findings, ROI‐based FA analyses in the whole hippocampus and hippocampal CA1 region demonstrated progressive reductions in FA during disease progression, with significant improvement following J4 treatment (Figure 3C–D, *, p < 0.05, **, p < 0.01, ***, p < 0.001 vs. WT or WT_Vehicle; ##, p < 0.01 vs. THY‐Tau22_Vehicle). In contrast, FA values in the PFC as well as in the hippocampal CA3 and dentate gyrus (DG) regions remained unchanged in THY‐Tau22 mice and were unaffected by J4 treatment (Figure 3C,D; Figure S2). Together, these findings suggest that ENT1 inhibition ameliorates tauopathy‐associated reductions in hippocampal‐prefrontal structural connectivity and improves hippocampal microstructural organization when administered during symptomatic stages.

ENT1 inhibition selectively modulates tauopathy‐associated metabolic and lipid remodeling

Because brain energy metabolism is closely linked to white matter organization and glial function, and oligodendrocytes require tightly regulated metabolic inputs to support axonal function, 35 , 36 we conducted comprehensive metabolomic and lipidomic profiling to characterize disease‐associated alterations and evaluate their responsiveness to J4‐mediated ENT1 inhibition. Given the preferential vulnerability of the hippocampal CA1 region in THY‐Tau22 mice, 19 our analyses focused on this region. To minimize potential dilution from less affected areas, all subsequent analyses were conducted using microdissected CA1 tissue from THY‐Tau22 mice at 4, 6, and 10 months of age.

Targeted metabolomic analysis (Figure S3) revealed progressive alterations in energy‐ and redox‐related metabolites, including changes in glycolytic intermediates and reductions in purine‐related metabolites, which were selectively normalized by ENT1 inhibition. These findings are consistent with altered nucleotide turnover and energy balance in tauopathy. Given the established link between lipid composition, oligodendrocyte function, and myelin integrity, we next performed targeted lipidomic profiling of CA1 tissue across disease stages. This analysis focused on five major lipid classes: fatty acyls, glycerolipids (GL), glycerophospholipids (GP), sphingolipids (SP), and sterol lipids (ST). To further characterize differential lipids (DELs), defined by fold change ≥ 1.2 (|log2FC| ≥ 0.263) and p < 0.05 between groups, we analyzed the overall profiling and applied LION/web, a lipid ontology enrichment tool. Lipidomic analysis revealed broad, class‐specific alterations in the hippocampal CA1 region of THY‐Tau22 mice across disease progression (Figure S4A,B). At 4 months, only a few DELs were detected (three upregulated and 19 downregulated), primarily reflecting reductions in GP, fatty acyls, and GL, with minor increases in SP (Figure S4A–S4D). Consistent with these limited changes, LION/web enrichment analysis showed no significant lipid ontology enrichment at this stage (Figure S4E). These reductions may reflect early alterations in lipid remodeling, which is essential for the maintenance of membrane homeostasis and neuronal function, as similarly reported in an AD model (APP/PS1). 37 By 6 months, lipid alterations became more pronounced (43 upregulated and 13 downregulated), with increased levels of SP, GP, and GL species, particularly ceramides, sphingomyelins, phosphatidylethanolamines, and triglycerides, alongside reductions in fatty acyls subclasses, including polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), and negatively charged phospholipids such as cardiolipins and phosphatidylinositols (Figure S4A, S4B, S4F, and S4G). LION/web analysis at this stage indicated enrichment in lipid storage pathways, particularly those associated with neutral headgroups and lipid droplets (Figure S4H). At 10 months, the number of altered lipid species increased substantially (105 upregulated and 53 downregulated), featuring marked elevations in SP and GL species, including multiple sulfatides, ceramides, and triacylglycerols. GP subclasses such as LPC, PE, PS, and PG also showed broad elevations, along with cholesterol esters and cardiolipins. In contrast, a range of free fatty acyls, including long‐chain polyunsaturated and acylcarnitine (CAR) species, were markedly reduced. Reductions were also observed in polyunsaturated GP species, particularly within the phosphatidylcholine (PC) and plasmalogen–PE subclasses (Figure 4A,B and Figure S5A,B). Ontology enrichment further highlighted accumulation of ceramides and sulfatides, along with reduced enrichment of long‐chain PUFAs (Figure 4C), in line with previously reported lipidomic changes in AD. 38 These findings suggest progressive lipid remodeling during disease advancement characterized by increased structural and storage lipid species together with reductions in metabolically active lipid species.

Lipidomic analysis following J4 treatment revealed selective alterations in lipid species, with 26 lipids upregulated and four downregulated, primarily within the fatty acyls, GP, and GL categories (Figure 4A,B). Although the PCA did not show a clear segregation between the J4‐treated (TauJ) and vehicle control (TauC) groups in THY‐Tau22 mice (Figure S5C), distinct changes in individual lipid species were observed. Among the upregulated species, CARs, PCs, and lysophospholipids, including LPC, LPE, and LPI, were prominently represented. In contrast, only four lipids were reduced by J4, mainly within the GP and GL subclasses, including two phosphatidylethanolamines (PE 21:3 and PE 21:4) and two triglycerides (TG 24:0 and TG 58:6). No significant alterations in sphingolipid or sterol subclasses were observed, suggesting that the lipid effects of ENT1 inhibition were selective rather than global (Figure S5D). Functional enrichment analysis further revealed that upregulated lipids were significantly enriched for long‐chain PUFAs (defined as those with at least 20 carbons and three double bonds), whereas downregulated species were mildly enriched for fatty acids with 21 carbons or three to five double bonds (Figure 4C). Lipidomic analysis revealed progressive and class‐specific lipid remodeling in the hippocampal CA1 region of THY‐Tau22 mice, with a shift toward structural and storage lipids during disease progression. ENT1 inhibition selectively restored lipid profiles, particularly by increasing long‐chain PUFA‐containing species and modulating fatty acyls, GP, and GL subclasses, suggesting partial normalization of membrane‐associated lipid metabolism.

J4‐responsive lipid changes are associated with oligodendrocyte‐enriched transcriptional profiles

To further investigate how the ENT1 inhibitor J4 modulates lipid metabolism, we sought to identify genes associated with J4‐responsive lipid species and assess whether these transcriptional changes exhibit cell‐type specificity. We focused on 16 of the 30 lipid species that were previously altered in THY‐Tau22 mice and effectively normalized by J4 treatment (Figure 4D and Figure S6). These lipids primarily belonged to the fatty acyls, GL, and GP categories, which are functionally associated with lipid storage, membrane architecture and signaling, and energy metabolism, respectively (Figure S6). The 16 lipids were further grouped into nine subclasses (fatty acyls, CAR, PS, PC, PE, LPC, LPS, LPI, and TG). Based on these lipid subclasses, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) was used to identify 185 candidate genes predicted to be involved in the regulation, metabolism, or transport of these lipids (File S1).

To evaluate whether these lipid‐associated genes were transcriptionally dysregulated in tauopathy and responsive to ENT1 inhibition, we first analyzed snRNA‐seq data (Figure 5A–5C). The dataset comprised 38,609 high‐quality nuclei obtained from hippocampal tissue of 12.5‐month‐old female mice (WT_Vehicle, WTC; THY‐Tau22_Vehicle, TauC; and THY‐Tau22_J4, TauJ), following 1 month of J4 administration via drinking water starting at 11.5 months of age. Nuclei were classified into 33 transcriptionally distinct clusters representing all major hippocampal cell types, including glutamatergic neurons (GLU_Neu) and gamma‐aminobutyric acid‐ergic (GABAergic) neurons (GABA_Neu), mature neurons (Mature_Neu), immature neurons (Immature_Neu), neural progenitor cells (NPC), astrocytes (Ast), oligodendrocytes (OLG), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC), microglia (Mic), endothelial cells (Endo), neuroepithelial cells, and tanycytes (Figure 5B), with comparable cellular composition across groups (Figure 5C).

We next performed differential expression analysis using the above‐described snRNA‐seq database at whole hippocampal tissue level. Whole‐tissue differential expression analysis identified 71 genes dysregulated in tauopathy (TauC vs. WTC) and 74 genes modulated by J4 treatment (TauJ vs. TauC). Enrichment analysis indicated that 21 genes downregulated in TauC were upregulated in TauJ (Figure 5D), and that these genes were enriched in cholesterol metabolism and peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR) signaling (Figure 5F). Conversely, 15 genes upregulated in TauC were downregulated by J4 treatment (Figure 5E), and these genes were enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway (Figure 5G). Together, these findings suggest that J4 may exert its therapeutic effects, at least in part, by modulating lipid metabolic pathways and the PPAR signaling axis, which are central to lipid metabolism and glucose homeostasis and are disrupted in the AD brain. 39 , 40 Mapping the J4‐responsive genes onto the snRNA‐seq dataset revealed that the 21 genes upregulated by J4 (i.e., downregulated in TauC) exhibited strong cell‐type‐specific enrichment, particularly in the oligodendrocyte subclass (OLG_1) as visualized by feature plot of these genes (Figure 5H). In contrast, the 15 genes downregulated by J4 (i.e., upregulated in TauC) did not show marked enrichment in any specific cell type (Figure 5I). These findings suggest that J4‐responsive lipid‐associated transcriptional changes are preferentially linked to oligodendrocyte‐enriched programs. To further investigate the functional characteristics of the OLG_1 subclass, we examined gene expression patterns corresponding to different stages of the oligodendrocyte lineage, ranging from OPCs to myelinating oligodendrocytes, based on established lineage markers from previous studies. 41 , 42 Specifically, we assessed OPC markers (Cspg4, Pdgfra, Gpr17, Vcan), premyelinating oligodendrocyte markers (Galc, Cemip2, Thbs3, Rras2), and myelinating oligodendrocyte markers (Mbp, Mobp, Mag, Mog, Aspa, Plp1, Myrf, Klk6, Hopx, Ptgds, Il33, Opalin). As shown in Figure S7, these marker genes were differentially enriched in OPC_1 (Figure S7A), OLG_2 (Figure S7B), and OLG_1 (Figure S7C) clusters, respectively, indicating a developmental progression across oligodendrocyte subclasses. Notably, the percentage of OLG_1 cells was reduced in the TauC group and increased following J4 treatment (Figure S7C).

In line with these findings, analysis of a public single‐nucleus RNA‐seq database from AD (ssREAD; https://bmblx.bmi.osumc.edu/ssread/) revealed significant reductions in multiple myelinating oligodendrocyte markers, including Mbp, Mobp, Mag, Mog, Aspa, Plp1, Myrf, Ptgds, and Opalin, in AD brains (Figure S8). Among these markers, aspartoacylase (ASPA) was of particular interest because it hydrolyzes N‐acetylaspartate to generate acetate, which serves as a substrate for lipid synthesis during postnatal central nervous system (CNS) myelination, 43 thereby functionally linking oligodendrocyte metabolism to myelin formation. Consistent with these transcriptomic observations, immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated markedly reduced ASPA expression in hippocampal sections from patients with AD and FTD‐Tau (Figures 6A,B). Similarly, immunofluorescence staining of hippocampal brain sections from Tau22 mice showed reduced signals for the myelinating oligodendrocyte marker ASPA (Figure 6C,D) and the pan‐oligodendrocyte marker CNPase (Figure 6C and 6E) in the Tau22_Vehicle group, both of which increased following J4 treatment. Additional cell‐counting analysis showed that CNPase‐positive cell density was not significantly altered among groups, whereas J4 treatment increased the proportion of ASPA‐positive cells within the CNPase‐positive population (Figures 6F,G). These results suggest that increased ASPA immunoreactivity after J4 treatment was not solely attributable to an increase in oligodendrocyte density. Collectively, these findings indicate that J4‐responsive lipid‐associated genes are predominantly enriched in myelinating oligodendrocytes, consistent with improvements in myelin‐associated markers and organization observed in Figure 2, and highlight the potential relevance of this pathway in both AD and FTD‐Tau.

Together, our findings reveal progressive disconnection of neural circuits, myelin impairment, and lipidomic remodeling across tauopathy stages, which precede overt cognitive decline. These alterations were especially prominent in lipid metabolism, with notable changes in oligodendrocyte‐related pathways. Importantly, short‐term ENT1 inhibition during the symptomatic stage ameliorated structural connectivity deficits and partially normalized metabolic and lipid alterations. This study highlights both the evolving metabolic landscape of tauopathy and the potential of ENT1 inhibition as a targeted therapeutic strategy.

DISCUSSION

Inhibition of ENT1 by J4 ameliorated structural and metabolic alterations associated with tauopathy. Specifically, short‐term J4 treatment during the symptomatic stage improved myelin‐associated markers and organization in the CA1 region (Figure 2) and increased structural connectivity along the Hippo‐PFC and Hippo‐EC pathways (Figure 3 and Figure S1), changes that emerged early in disease progression and worsened with age. These improvements were accompanied by selective modulation of metabolic disruptions, notably in lipid pathways (Figure 4), without inducing broad shifts in glycolysis (Figure S3), suggesting a pathway‐specific therapeutic effect. Transcriptomic analyses further showed that J4‐responsive lipid‐associated genes were enriched in myelinating oligodendrocytes, highlighting the contribution of glial lipid metabolism to maintaining network stability in tauopathy (Figure 5).

White matter abnormalities are a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases and have been linked to both cognitive performance and disease progression across clinical and preclinical models, including tauopathy. 30 , 31 , 32 , 44 , 45 , 46 Post mortem hippocampal tissue from patients with AD and FTD‐Tau showed a marked reduction in CNPase signal (Figure 1), consistent with oligodendrocyte‐ and myelin‐associated alterations. In line with these human findings, our DTI analyses across disease stages revealed a progressive decline in FA along the Hippo‐PFC tract, a circuit critical for memory processing, 47 , 48 in THY‐Tau22 mice. Notably, FA reductions were already detectable at the presymptomatic stage (4 months of age; Figure 3A and 3C), preceding detectable reductions in MBP signal or myelin coherency (Figure 2). By comparison, FA along the Hippo‐EC pathway, a circuit particularly vulnerable during AD progression, 49 remained relatively preserved at the presymptomatic stage but declined from disease onset and was further reduced at the symptomatic stage (Figure S1). Together, these findings indicate that structural connectivity deficits emerge and progress differently across the Hippo‐PFC and Hippo‐EC pathways during tauopathy progression. Beyond structural changes, our data reveal a gradual metabolic shift throughout tauopathy, with lipidomic alterations emerging before cognitive symptoms and advancing alongside white matter abnormalities (Figures 2, 3, 4 and Figures S3–S5). Consistent with findings in demyelinated and AD brains, 37 several polyunsaturated lipid species, including LPC 22:6 and LPI 20:4, were reduced in the hippocampus of 10‐month‐old THY‐Tau22 mice (Figure 4D), indicating altered lipid homeostasis associated with membrane composition. In this context, PUFA remodeling may be relevant to tauopathy‐related mechanisms. Previous studies indicate that long‐chain omega‐3 PUFAs, particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)‐containing lipids, can modulate Aβ accumulation, tau phosphorylation, and cognitive dysfunction in experimental AD models. For example, DHA/DPA supplementation ameliorated Aβ and tau pathology, 50 while endogenous enrichment of omega‐3 PUFAs reduced phosphorylated tau and improved neuropathology in AD‐related mouse models. 51 Endogenous n‐3 PUFA enrichment also exerted protective effects in a mouse model of tau‐associated AD pathology. 52 Together, these findings support the relevance of PUFA‐containing lipid species to tau‐related pathology. Therefore, the reduction of PUFA‐containing lipid species in aged THY‐Tau22 mice may reflect changes in membrane and inflammatory lipid homeostasis, and the J4‐associated partial restoration of long‐chain PUFA‐containing lipid species (Figure 4D) may be relevant to membrane and myelin homeostasis and network stability. Lipid droplet accumulation in glial cells has been linked to oxidative and metabolic stress responses, 53 implying that the lipid alterations observed here may reflect an early adaptive response to white‐matter instability and cellular stress.

In addition to the lipidomic alterations, our metabolite analysis uncovered early shifts in amino acid and glucose metabolism, preceding overt pathology and progressing to sustained glycolytic deficits (Figure S3). These changes were most pronounced in the hippocampal CA1 region, which exhibits prominent tau accumulation in THY‐Tau22 mice. 19 According to the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas, 22 Slc29a1 that encodes ENT1 is broadly expressed across hippocampal subregions without clear enrichment in CA1. Thus, these CA1‐based metabolomic and lipidomic changes likely reflect the regional vulnerability in THY‐Tau22 mice rather than preferential ENT1 expression. In the CA1 region, we observed accumulation of glycolytic intermediates, including F16BP, F6P/G1P, 3‐PG, PEP, and DHAP, indicating possible upregulation of glycolysis during disease progression (Figure S3). Such accumulation of glycolytic intermediates may reflect impaired metabolic flux and energetic stress rather than enhanced glycolytic activity.

When administered during a symptomatic stage, short‐term J4 treatment restored myelin integrity in the CA1 region (Figure 2), improved Hippo‐PFC and Hippo‐EC connectivity (Figure 3 and Figure S1), and selectively normalized dysregulated metabolic pathways (Figure 4 and Figure S3). These effects align with J4's role as an ENT1 inhibitor, given that ENT1 is a key regulator of adenosine transport. 15 , 18 Recent studies have shown that neuronal activity can promote ENT1 activity via calcium‐dependent mechanisms, thereby facilitating adenosine efflux. 54 , 55 Under pathological conditions such as AD, disrupted glutamate homeostasis 56 , 57 may promote excitotoxicity associated with membrane depolarization and elevated intracellular calcium 58 and potentially facilitate ENT‐dependent adenosine efflux. 54 , 55 These findings raise the possibility that enhanced ENT1‐mediated adenosine efflux may contribute to reduced intracellular adenosine availability in vulnerable brain regions of AD. 14 In support of this hypothesis, our data showed that treatment with J4 increased adenosine and several related purine metabolites (Figure S3H and S3K), supporting the notion that ENT1 inhibition partially restores adenosine and purine homeostasis. Collectively, ENT1 inhibition may help normalize purine homeostasis and support neuronal energy balance in the context of tauopathy.

In addition to modulating intracellular purine homeostasis, J4‐mediated ENT1 inhibition may also influence extracellular adenosine availability and thereby engage adenosine receptor‐dependent pathways. This extracellular mechanism may be relevant to oligodendrocyte and white‐matter biology. Earlier studies showed that OPCs and oligodendrocytes express functional A1 adenosine receptors (A1Rs), and that their activation can stimulate OPC migration. 59 In vivo studies further support a context‐dependent role of the A1R under hypoxic or injury‐related stress. 60 These findings raise the possibility that extracellular adenosine signaling may contribute to the oligodendrocyte‐ and myelin‐associated responses observed following ENT1 inhibition. In addition, extracellular adenosine signaling may intersect with lipid and cholesterol homeostasis. In Niemann–Pick type C models, stimulation of A2A adenosine receptors (A2ARs) has been shown to reduce intracellular cholesterol accumulation and improve mitochondrial abnormalities, 61 and to promote oligodendrocyte differentiation in cellular models of Niemann–Pick type C disease. 62 , 63 In lipid‐loading and foam‐cell models, extracellular adenosine acting through A2AR enhances reverse cholesterol transport by upregulating cholesterol‐handling proteins such as ABCA1 and CYP27A1. 64 , 65 These studies provide additional context for how adenosine receptors may regulate lipid availability and cholesterol handling in relation to the lipidomic remodeling observed in the present study.

In parallel, J4 corrected lipid abnormalities in the fatty acyls, GP, and GL subclasses, reversing alterations in fatty acid availability and phospholipid remodeling linked to membrane function and lipid storage (Figure 4). Human single‐nucleus transcriptomic analyses have shown that myelination‐related processes are recurrently perturbed across multiple cell types in AD, including oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells. 66 Within this broader disease context, our snRNA‐seq analysis revealed that J4‐responsive lipid‐related genes were predominantly enriched in myelinating oligodendrocytes (Figure 5 and Figure S7), reflecting their known roles in metabolic support and myelin stability. This interpretation aligns with emerging concepts that oligodendrocytes support neuronal circuits through both myelination and metabolic support of axons. 67

Furthermore, ENT1 inhibition by J4 appears to modulate PPAR signaling (Figures 5F and 5G). Recent studies have shown that the adenosine derivative IFC‐305 can reduce methylation of the PPAR‐γ promoter by modulating S‐adenosylmethionine (SAM)‐dependent methylation pathways, thereby enhancing PPAR‐γ transcription and protein expression. 68 Adenosine metabolism is closely linked to cellular methylation reactions through the SAM–S‐adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) cycle, which determines the cell's overall methylation potential. 8 Because ENT1 inhibition preserves intracellular adenosine levels (Figure S3), J4 may similarly influence methylation dynamics and, in turn, restore proper PPAR signaling. The normalization of this pathway following ENT1 inhibition (Figure 5F,G) likely reflects a broader rebalancing of metabolic (Figure 4 and Figure S3) and inflammatory homeostasis. 15

In light of these findings, the preferential effect of J4 on the OLG_1 population may be attributed, at least in part, to the distinct metabolic profile of myelinating oligodendrocytes. Because these cells are highly engaged in lipid synthesis to maintain and expand myelin membranes, 69 they are particularly sensitive to perturbations in energy and lipid metabolism. Although ENT1 expression is relatively low in oligodendrocytes, J4 may still influence their function directly through subtle modulation of intracellular adenosine‐dependent pathways, or indirectly by improving neuronal metabolic status and energy availability. This could help explain the apparent responsiveness of the OLG_1‐associated transcriptional profile to J4, including changes in cell proportion (Figure S7C) and myelin‐associated markers (Figure 2).

While our findings highlight a selective restoration of purine and lipid metabolism following ENT1 inhibition, it is important to note that metabolic profiling was performed on bulk CA1 tissue, limiting the ability to resolve cell‐type–specific contributions. Although transcriptomic data implicated myelinating oligodendrocytes in lipid‐related changes, direct metabolic resolution at the single‐cell level remains lacking. Emerging spatial metabolomic approaches may help resolve cell‐type‐specific metabolic alterations in the future.

In conclusion, our data demonstrate that ENT1 inhibition selectively modulates purine and lipid metabolic pathways, while improving myelin‐associated markers and structural connectivity measures. Together, these findings support further investigation of ENT1 targeting as a potential therapeutic approach for tauopathy‐associated network dysfunction.
