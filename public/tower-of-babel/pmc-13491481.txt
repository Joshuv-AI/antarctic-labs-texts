Microrheological Evolution of C–S–H and M–S–H Gels During Gelation and the Regulatory Mechanism of Cations
Authors: Weijia Cao, Mengyu Bai, Xin Song, Erlong Yang, Gengrui Zhang, Yongjiang Wang, Kun Xie, Kun Yan, Peng Xu, Jinxiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inorganic silicate gels, including calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and magnesium silicate hydrate (M–S–H) gel, exhibit notable advantages in profile control and water shutoff applications in high-salinity oilfields both domestically and internationally. To elucidate the influence of ions on the gelation state and process of silicate gels in high-salinity reservoirs, this study employs diffusing wave spectroscopy (DWS) to investigate the effects of common reservoir cations on the microrheological behavior of C–S–H gel and M–S–H gel. The results indicate that the gelation process of C–S–H gel can be divided into four stages: an initial viscosity-dominated stage; a rapid cross-linking stage (1–2 h) with sharply decreased mean square displacement (MSD) and emerging elastic dominance; a stable equilibrium stage (2–12 h) with stabilized network structure; and a long-term relaxation stage (12–72 h) featuring increased MSD, declining modulus, and local network relaxation. In contrast to C–S–H gel, M–S–H gel consistently exhibits viscosity-dominated fluid behavior throughout the gelation process, with the slope of the MSD curve remaining close to unity, indicating that it does not form a dense elastic network. Cation type exerts a significant regulatory effect on the microrheological behavior of the two silicate gels. For C–S–H gel, Ca2+ promotes particle aggregation through strong charge bridging, resulting in the lowest MSD and the highest storage modulus. The monovalent cations Na+, K+, and H+ exhibit weak cross-linking effects, leading to higher MSD values, with the H+ system being the loosest due to the acidic environment inhibiting polycondensation. Although Mg2+ is divalent, it tends to form a loose M–S–H phase, which increases MSD and reduces the modulus. For M–S–H gel, the modulus values under different cation systems follow the order of MgCl2 < HCl < NaCl < KCl < CaCl2. Keywords: C-S-H gel; M-S-H gel; gelation process; cations; micro-rheology; high-salinity reservoirs

Introduction

Inorganic silicate gels, including calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and magnesium silicate hydrate (M–S–H) gel, have significant application value for deep profile control and water shutoff in high-temperature and high-salinity reservoirs due to their excellent temperature resistance, salt tolerance, and long-term stability. − However, the formation water in actual reservoirs is characterized by high salinity and complex ionic compositions. − The widely present cations such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, and H+ can directly participate in the polycondensation of silicate ions through charge screening, ionic bridging, competitive adsorption, and phase transformation, thereby regulating the gel network structure and its viscoelastic properties. Current research on the micromechanisms of cation-regulated silicate gelation still has the following limitations: (1) the regulation patterns of cations on the early stage gelation kinetics remain unclear; (2) the differences in gel network structure induced by different cations and their effects on viscoelasticity are not well understood; (3) a systematic understanding of the long-term network structure evolution and stability mechanism during the later stage of gelation is lacking. Therefore, systematically elucidating the influence mechanisms of different cations is of great significance for optimizing the performance of gel systems for profile control and water shutoff in high-temperature and high-salinity reservoirs.

Regarding the effects of cations on silicate gelation, sresearchers have conducted some exploratory studies. Hamouda et al. confirmed through experiments that divalent cations accelerate gel polymerization through charge screening, and the modification effect of Ca2+ is better than that of Mg2+. Tang et al. found that dissolved Mg2+ can alter the microstructure of C–S–H gel, and under high concentration conditions, it can induce a phase transformation from C–S–H gel to M–S–H gel. These studies reveal the important influence of cation types on the gelation behavior of silicate gels. However, existing work mostly focuses on describing macroscopic gelation phenomena and static characterization of final products. In situ and dynamic studies on the microrheological evolution, network cross-linking kinetics, and long-term structural relaxation behavior during the gelation process under the regulation of different cations are still very limited. Furthermore, the types of ions studied have mostly been limited to Ca2+ and Mg2+, with relatively little attention given to other common cations such as Na+, K+, and H+. This leads to the current situation where performance optimization of silicate gel systems for profile control and water shutoff in high-temperature and high-salinity reservoirs still relies mainly on empirical formula adjustments, lacking theoretical guidance based on micromechanisms.

In view of this, this paper employs diffusing wave spectroscopy (DWS), a microrheological technique, to systematically investigate the effects of different cations (Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, and H+) on the microviscoelasticity and network structure evolution of C–S–H and M–S–H gels during gelation. DWS can obtain the mean square displacement (MSD) of tracer particles and in situ acquire the evolution of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) without disturbing the sample, , providing a powerful tool for revealing the microdynamic mechanisms during gelation. This study aims to elucidate the action patterns and micromechanisms of cations in the gelation process of silicate gels from a microrheological perspective, clarify the viscoelastic differences and structural evolution characteristics of C–S–H and M–S–H gels under the regulation of different cations, and provide a theoretical basis for the rational design and performance optimization of silicate gel systems for deep profile control and water shutoff in high-temperature and high-salinity reservoirs.

Experimental Apparatus

The experimental equipment included a DWS RheoLab diffusing wave spectroscopy instrument (Switzerland), which was used to obtain the MSD of tracer particles in C–S–H and M–S–H gels and subsequently calculate the microrheological parameters of the systems [storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″)]. Additional equipment, such as an HH-S2 digital thermostatic water bath, was also used.

Experimental Design

Microrheological Behavior of C–S–H and M–S–H Gels During Gelation

C–S–H and M–S–H gels were prepared by utilizing the salt sensitivity effect of Na2O·SiO2, with Na2O·SiO2 selected as the primary agent, and CaCl2 or MgCl2 as the additive agent, respectively. A 0.01 mol/L NaCl aqueous solution was prepared using distilled water. Na2O·SiO2, CaCl2 and MgCl2 solutions, both at a concentration of 10 wt %, were then prepared using this NaCl solution. Tracer particles (460 nm polystyrene) were uniformly predispersed in the additive agent solution. During the experiment, the primary agent solution and additive agent solution were mixed with 0.01 mol/L NaCl solution at a certain ratio to achieve final concentrations of 0.5 wt %. The DWS instrument and thermostatic water bath were preheated and stabilized at 65 °C. The prepared sample solutions described above were immediately injected into the DWS sample cell. The MSD, G′ and G″ were recorded using a DWS instrument at 65 °C at the initial state. Subsequently, the sample cell was transferred to a constant-temperature water bath at 65 °C for static aging. The sample cell was returned to the DWS instrument for measurements at various gelation times within 72 h, and the MSD, G′, and G″ data were recorded at each time point.

Effect of Ionic on the Microrheological Properties of C–S–H and M–S–H Gels

To ensure a constant chloride ion concentration (C Cl – = 0.01 mol/L), a series of aqueous solutions containing different cations were prepared using distilled water. The cations were derived from NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, MgCl2, and HCl. These solutions were used to prepare the primary agent (Na2O·SiO2, 10 wt % concentration), the additive agent (CaCl2 or MgCl2, each at 10 wt % concentration), with 500 nm polystyrene tracer particles predispersed uniformly in the additive agent solutions. During the experiment, predetermined amounts of the primary agent and additive agent solutions were mixed with the 0.01 mol/L cation solutions at a specific ratio to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 wt % for both the primary and additive agents. Experiments were conducted at 65 °C to investigate the effects of different cations on the microrheological properties of C–S–H and M–S–H gels. MSD, G′ and G″ were recorded at various time points.

Analysis of Experimental Results

Microrheological Behavior of C–S–H and M–S–H Gels During Gelation

MSD of Tracer Particles in C–S–H Gel Solution

Na2O·SiO2 and CaCl2 solutions were separately prepared using NaCl aqueous solution as the solvent. The two solutions were mixed to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 wt % for each component to form C–S–H gel. The samples were then placed in a thermostatic water bath at 65 °C. The MSD of tracer particles (460 nm polystyrene tracer particles) as a function of lag time at various gelation times is shown in Figure . As shown in Figure , in the early stage of C–S–H gel formation, the MSD curve of 460 nm polystyrene tracers exhibits significantly higher values and a steeper slope than in the later stage. This indicates that the gel network is not yet fully dense, the motion of tracer particles is weakly constrained, their diffusion capability is strong, and the system exhibits pronounced viscous fluid behavior. As the gelation time progresses to 2 h, the cross-linking density of the gel network gradually increases and the structure matures, leading to greater confinement of tracer particle motion. This is reflected by a substantial decrease in MSD and a notable flattening of the curve. With further increase in gelation time, the MSD exhibits an upward trend, suggesting that during the dynamic development stage of C–S–H gel, the network cross-linking density undergoes continuous adjustment, as gel particles persistently rearrange and aggregate. This results in the formation of more pores and channels within the local microstructure, which facilitate tracer particle diffusion and consequently reduce the spatial constraint on particle motion. Between 6 and 12 h, the MSD stabilizes, indicating that the C–S–H gel network has reached a relatively stable structure. During this period, structural rearrangement and localized densification within the gel are largely complete, and the constraint on tracer particle motion shows no significant further change. As gelation time increases from 12 to 72 h, the MSD exhibits a moderate increase, indicating that during prolonged gelation, the C–S–H gel network undergoes local relaxation or microphase separation. In some regions, the network structure experiences relaxation and reorganization, forming looser microscopic channels that provide greater space for tracer particle diffusion, resulting in an elevated MSD.

Viscoelastic Modulus of C–S–H Gel

The viscoelastic modulus of C–S–H gel samples at different gelation times is shown are in Figure .

Viscoelastic modulus curves of C–S–H gel at different gelation times.

It can be seen from Figure that gelation process of C–S–H gel can be divided into four stages. The first stage is the viscosity-dominated stage (initial gelation), characterized by low moduli and G″ > G′, indicating ineffective particle aggregation and predominantly viscous fluid behavior. The second stage is the rapid cross-linking and viscoelastic transition stage (1–2 h), during which both G′ and G″ increase significantly, reflecting particle aggregation and network formation, with elasticity beginning to dominate at low frequencies (G′ > G″). The third stage is the stable equilibrium stage (2–12 h), during which moduli stabilize after 6 h and the G′/G″ ratio becomes constant, with G″ slightly higher than G′ at high frequencies and G′ dominant at low frequencies. This indicates that the C–S–H gel network had entered a metastable equilibrium stage of viscoelastic response by 6 h. During the subsequent gelation time (6–12 h), no substantial cross-linking densification occurred in the network. Instead, network structural adjustment was achieved only through local breaking and reorganization of physical cross-linking points (interparticle van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding), thereby maintaining a dynamic balance of viscoelasticity. The essence of this local structural adjustment lies in the physical cross-linking characteristics of the C–S–H gel: the cross-linking points are dynamically reversible and can undergo continuous breaking and reorganization to achieve network restructuring, which results in a stable viscoelastic response of the system at the macroscopic level. The fourth stage is the long-term relaxation stage (12–72 h), during which both G′ and G″ gradually decrease synchronously due to local relaxation, microphase separation, and structural reorganization, which create loose microscopic channels within the dense network and reduce the system’s resistance to deformation.

MSD of Tracer Particles in M–S–H Gel Solution

Na2O·SiO2 and MgCl2 solutions were separately prepared using NaCl aqueous solution as the solvent. The two solutions were mixed to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 wt % for each component to form M–S–H gel. The samples were then placed in a thermostatic water bath at 65 °C. The MSD of tracer particles (460 nm polystyrene) as a function of lag time at various gelation times is shown in Figure . The figure reveals that the gelation process of M–S–H gel differs markedly from that of C–S–H gel. Throughout gelation, the MSD curves of tracer particles in M–S–H gel exhibit slopes near unity on a double-logarithmic scale, indicating free diffusion behavior and a typical viscous fluid response. During the initial stage (0–3 h), the MSD curves nearly overlap, reflecting the presence of independent particles with weak interactions and no continuous three-dimensional network, allowing essentially unconstrained particle motion. From 3 to 9 h, the MSD curves shift downward, indicating intensified cross-linking among M–S–H particles, with the formation of aggregates and localized networks that impose spatial constraints on probe motion and enhance system viscoelasticity. From 9 to 72 h, MSD shows an upward trend. This indicates that during the long-term gelation process, chain segment relaxation occurs within the M–S–H gel network. Local chain segments undergo conformational rearrangement through thermal motion, leading to dynamic rearrangement of cross-linking points or reversible breaking of some hydrogen/ionic bonds. Consequently, the decrease in effective cross-linking density leads to a prolonged relaxation time, a slower stress response, reduced stress transfer efficiency, increased local motion space, and weaker confinement of probe particles, ultimately resulting in a higher MSD.

Viscoelastic Modulus of M–S–H Gel

The viscoelastic modulus of M–S–H gel samples at different gelation times is shown in Figure .

Viscoelastic modulus curves of M–S–H gel at different gelation times.

As shown in Figure , the rheological behavior of M–S–H gel differs from that of C–S–H gel. Throughout the gelation process, M–S–H gel exhibits a modulus response only in the high-frequency region, while the low-frequency modulus approaches zero. This indicates that at long time scales, the system undergoes complete structural relaxation and cannot resist external deformation. Moreover, G″ consistently exceeds G′, confirming that the system remains viscosity-dominated throughout. The evolution of the moduli with gelation time can be divided into three stages: In the initial stage (0–3 h), both G′ and G″ remain at low levels with little variation over time, and the system exhibits typical viscous fluid behavior. From 3 to 9 h, both G′ and G″ increase significantly, indicating a marked enhancement in viscoelasticity, although the response remains viscosity-dominated. From 9 to 72 h, G′ and G″ show a gradual decrease over time, with the viscous contribution still dominant.

Effect of Cations on Microrheological Properties of C–S–H and M–S–H Gels

MSD of Tracer Particles in C–S–H Gel Solution

Under the condition that the molar concentration of Cl– was kept constant (C Cl‑ = 0.01 mol/L), Na2O·SiO2 and CaCl2 solutions were prepared using five types of solvent water: NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, MgCl2, and HCl, respectively. The two solutions were mixed to achieve a concentration of 0.5 wt % for each, forming C–S–H gel. Figure presents the MSD of tracer particles (500 nm polystyrene particles) as a function of lag time for these samples at the initial stage, as well as after 12 and 24 h of gelation.

MSD curves of C–S–H gel under different ions and gelation times.

It can be seen from Figure that the ionic environment had an influence on the MSD of tracer particles during the C–S–H gelation process. Among the tested systems, the MSD curve corresponding to CaCl2 is consistently the lowest, indicating that the divalent Ca2+ cation, with its higher charge density, promotes effective cross-linking and network densification among C–S–H particles through strong charge bridging. This significantly restricts 500 nm polystyrene tracer particle diffusion and markedly enhances the viscoelasticity of the system. In contrast, the monovalent cations Na+, K+, and H+, with lower charge densities, exhibit weaker cross-linking effects and are less effective in forming dense three-dimensional networks. Consequently, the confinement of tracer particle diffusion is reduced, resulting in relatively higher MSD values. Compared with the divalent Ca2+, although Mg2+ is also a divalent ion, its MSD value is similar to those of the monovalent ions. This is because Mg2+ can competitively adsorb onto the surface of C–S–H gel particles. Due to its smaller ionic radius and higher charge density, Mg2+ has a stronger coordination effect with the silica tetrahedron. It can gradually replace the Ca2+ in the interlayer structure of C–S–H, generating a local M–S–H gel phase. Consequently, the system exhibits composite C–S–H and M–S–H phases. As can be seen from the results in the previous section, compared with pure C–S–H, the cross-linking effect of M–S–H gel is weaker. The addition of Mg2+ leads to a looser network structure in the composite system, which increases the movement space for tracer particles and reduces the degree of confinement, directly manifested as an increase in the MSD value. This indicates that the effect of cations on MSD depends primarily on their charge density and interaction mode with the C–S–H surface, rather than on the valence state alone.

Viscoelastic Modulus of C–S–H Gel

The viscoelastic modulus of C–S–H gel samples under different ions and gelation times is shown in Figures and . It can be seen from Figures and that during the C–S–H gelation process, the modulus of the C–S–H gel prepared with CaCl2 solvent water was the largest, followed by that with KCl and NaCl, and then that with HCl and MgCl2. The influence mechanism has already been discussed in the aforementioned MSD results. Further analysis reveals that the five cations also have a certain influence on the gelation process of C–S–H gel. In the initial stage of C–S–H gelation, for the C–S–H gels prepared with the five cations, G″ was higher than G′ in the high frequency region. In the later stage of C–S–H gelation (12 h), the measured modulus in the low-frequency region showed that G′ was higher than G″. For the C–S–H gel prepared with CaCl2 solvent water, its G″ was comparable to that in the initial stage, while G′ increased. This indicates that, in the early stage of formation, the gel had already established a relatively stable relaxation and dissipation behavior through the strong ionic bridging effect of Ca2+, causing the viscous characteristics of the system to stabilize rapidly. Simultaneously, the continuous polycondensation between silica chains and structural rearrangement in the later stage further increased the cross-linking density and rigidity of the gel network, leading to a continuous increase of G′. The G″ of the gels prepared with solvent water containing the three cations (NaCl, KCl, HCl) was higher than that in the initial stage. This is because Na+ and K+, as monovalent cations, can only achieve charge neutralization and cannot build a stable rigid network through ionic bridging. The silica chains within the gel are prone to relative slippage and structural rearrangement. As time progresses, the dissipative interaction between chains increases, leading to a rise of G″. In the HCl system, the acidic environment inhibited the polycondensation reaction of silanol groups, resulting in a low cross-linking density and a looser structure. In the later stage, chain segment movement and relaxation behavior intensified, also manifested as an increase in G″. When the gelation time was extended to 24 h, the modulus of the gels prepared with CaCl2, KCl, and HCl solvent water remained almost stable, while the modulus of the gel prepared with NaCl solvent water showed a slight decrease. This is related to the stronger hydration capacity and osmotic effect of Na+. Na+ tends to invade the interior of the gel, causing slight swelling. This leads to loosening of the cross-linking points between silica chains and a weakening of the network structure, thus exhibiting a trend of a slight decrease in modulus. Compared with the divalent Ca2+ system, the modulus of the gel in the Mg2+ system, which is also divalent, was closer to the inherent characteristics of M–S–H gel. Although both Ca2+ and Mg2+ are divalent cations and can promote gel network formation through ionic bridging, they react with silicate to form two different phases: C–S–H and M–S–H, respectively. C–S–H gel possesses a high cross-linking density and structural stiffness, whereas M–S–H gel is predominantly layered, with weaker interlayer forces and a more flexible structure. There are significant differences in the intrinsic viscoelastic characteristics between the two.

Mean Square Displacement of Tracer Particles in M–S–H Gel Solution

Under the condition that the molar concentration of Cl– was kept constant (C Cl = 0.01 mol/L), Na2O·SiO2 and MgCl2 solutions were prepared using five types of solvent water: NaCl, KCl, CaCl2, MgCl2, and HCl, respectively. The two solutions were mixed to achieve a concentration of 0.5 wt % for each, forming M–S–H gel. Figure shows the MSD of tracer particles (500 nm polystyrene) as a function of lag time for these samples at the initial stage, and after 12 and 24 h of gelation.

MSD curves of M–S–H gel under different ions and gelation times.

It can be seen from Figure that in the initial stage of M–S–H gel formation, the MSD curves of M–S–H gels in different solvent systems almost overlapped. This indicates that in the early stage of the reaction, the system had not yet formed a continuous elastic network, and silicate ions mainly existed as monomers or short-chain oligomers. As the reaction proceeded, silicate ions began to polycondense and form a three-dimensional network. The MSD curves of M–S–H gels in different solvents showed differences, and the order of the MSD values was: CaCl2 < KCl < NaCl < HCl < MgCl2.

Viscoelastic Modulus of M–S–H Gel

It can be seen from Figures and that during the M–S–H gelation process, the influence of solvent water on the modulus was consistent with the aforementioned MSD results. The order of the modulus values was: MgCl2 < HCl < NaCl < KCl < CaCl2. Further analysis reveals that the M–S–H gels prepared with the five cations all exhibited a phenomenon where G″ was higher than G′ throughout the gelation process. Compared with MgCl2, HCl, and NaCl, the modulus of the M–S–H gel in CaCl2 and KCl solvents gradually increased during the gelation process (0 h → 2 h → 24 h). This is because, in the CaCl2 solvent, the divalent Ca2+ continuously promotes the polycondensation of silica chains and the densification of the network in the M–S–H gel through a strong ionic bridging effect, causing G′ to increase significantly with time. Simultaneously, Ca2+ can also react with the sodium silicate in the system, with a certain probability of generating a C–S–H gel phase and forming an M–S–H/C–S–H hybrid network. The C–S–H phase itself possesses a higher cross-linking density and structural rigidity. Its synergistic effect with the M–S–H phase further enhances the overall network’s strength and stability, thereby causing the modulus to continuously increase over time. In the KCl solvent, due to the large ionic radius of K+ and its strong steric hindrance, gel swelling was effectively inhibited and the structure was stabilized. Consequently, the modulus continuously and stably increased over time.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the gelation and viscoelastic evolution characteristics of C–S–H and M–S–H gels, and identified clear intrinsic differences between the two gel systems. During gelation, C–S–H gel undergoes a four-stage structural evolution, gradually transforming from a viscosity-controlled fluid state into a stable elastic network. In comparison, M–S–H gel remains dominated by viscous behavior throughout the entire curing process, with loss modulus consistently exceeding storage modulus. Ca2+ strengthens network connectivity through strong ionic bridging and triggers the secondary formation of C–S–H phase, thereby constructing a compact M–S–H/C–S–H hybrid network and substantially improving gel modulus and structural stability. Mg2+ preferentially promotes the formation of loosely stacked M–S–H layered structures, resulting in gel stiffness comparable to that of K+ and Na+ ions.

This study clarifies the fundamental regulatory differences between Ca2+ and Mg2+, as well as the size-dependent performance differentiation mechanism of K+ and Na+, providing basic theoretical support for the performance tuning of C–S–H gel and M–S–H gel. More practically, these findings provide valuable guidance for the application of silicate plugging agents in high-calcium and high-magnesium oil reservoirs, and are conducive to improving the long-term plugging performance and stability of profile control and water shutoff technologies in harsh reservoir conditions.
