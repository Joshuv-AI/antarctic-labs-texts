Susceptibility assessment of wildfire-induced transmission line tripping using a physical-Bayesian modeling approach
Authors: Hui Huang, Keyu Chen, Biao Song, Changmeng Chen, Linlin Li, Jianli Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The escalating frequency and intensity of wildfires, driven by extreme climate events and anthropogenic activities, pose severe threats to the security and reliability of electrical power systems. Wildfires in proximity to high-voltage transmission lines can lead to a critical reduction in air insulation strength, caused by elevated temperatures, smoke particulate matter, and highly conductive flames. These conditions often precipitate insulation breakdown, line tripping, and potentially widespread power outages. This paper proposes an integrated physical-Bayesian modeling framework for assessing the susceptibility of transmission lines to wildfire-induced tripping. The framework combines a Bayesian network model, which estimates wildfire ignition probability by incorporating geographic, meteorological, fuel, and anthropogenic variables, with a physics-based model that evaluates the risk of insulation failure under wildfire conditions, accounting for both flame bridging and smoke effects. Key influencing features are identified through the Relief algorithm for feature selection. Applied to the Guangdong Power Grid in China, the model identifies distance to settlements, vegetation type, and monthly rainfall as the dominant factors affecting ignition probability. Spatial analysis reveals strong agreement between high-risk zones and historical fire events, and predicted tripping risks are consistent with actual outage records. The approach supports differentiated wildfire prevention strategies and enhances grid resilience, offering a scientifically robust and practical tool for proactive risk management in power systems exposed to wildfire hazards.

Introduction

Global climate change has precipitated a surge in the frequency and severity of extreme weather events, among which wildfires have exhibited a pronounced upward trend in occurrence, duration, and spatial extent1–5. These fires inflict considerable ecological and economic damage and present a critical threat to essential infrastructure, particularly electric power systems6–9. Transmission lines, often traversing vast and remote vegetated areas, are highly vulnerable to wildfires. When fires approach transmission corridors, the combined effects of intense heat, smoke, and ionized gases substantially degrade the dielectric strength of the air insulation around conductors. Elevated temperatures reduce air density, smoke particles distort local electric fields, and flames—which are inherently conductive—can bridge air gaps, collectively increasing the probability of flashover and line tripping10–12. Such events can trigger cascading failures, leading to extensive blackouts with profound socio-economic repercussions13–15,. Consequently, the development of robust methodologies for assessing and mapping the susceptibility of transmission lines to wildfire-induced tripping is imperative for enhancing the resilience and operational security of power grids.

Existing research on wildfire-related transmission line failures has evolved along several trajectories, including fire spread modeling16,17, characterization of the electrical properties of flames and smoke18,19, and experimental investigation of insulation breakdown mechanisms20,21. For predicting fire behavior, semi-empirical and semi-physical models, such as the widely adopted Rothermel22 model, have been extensively applied23–25. These models integrate fuel properties, environmental conditions, and topographic features to estimate fireline intensity and rate of spread, forming the cornerstone of systems like the U.S. National Fire Danger Rating System (NFDRS) and the BEHAVE fire prediction toolkit26,27. Such models provide critical inputs for operational fire management but are often limited to fire propagation dynamics without explicit linkage to power system vulnerabilities.

In the realm of electrical engineering, significant experimental and simulation efforts have been devoted to understanding how flames and smoke compromise air insulation12,28. Studies have demonstrated that combustion products from vegetation- such as charged particles, ash, and ionized gases- can reduce the breakdown voltage of an air gap to less than 40% of its value under clean air conditions29,30. Factors including particle concentration, size distribution, and local temperature gradients have been shown to critically influence discharge initiation and propagation31,32. Furthermore, the phenomenon of flame bridging, where conductive flames physically connect energized conductors to ground or other phases, has been identified as a particularly acute risk factor33,34. Despite these advances, prevailing approaches to assessing wildfire-induced tripping risk often exhibit notable limitations. Many studies focus on isolated aspects- such as estimating ignition frequency from historical fire data or evaluating breakdown probability based solely on flame height- without integrating the full spectrum of causal factors30,35. This neglects the complex interdependencies among ignition likelihood, fire behavior, and the physical mechanisms of insulation failure. Moreover, the spatial heterogeneity of risk, influenced by varying geographical, meteorological, combustible, and anthropogenic conditions, is frequently oversimplified. Many existing models also lack sufficient granularity to support differentiated grid planning and targeted mitigation measures36,37.

To address these gaps, this paper introduces a comprehensive integrated framework for assessing the susceptibility of transmission lines to wildfire-induced tripping. The approach combines a Bayesian network model for predicting spatial wildfire occurrence probability with a physics-based model for evaluating insulation breakdown risk under fire conditions. The Bayesian model incorporates multi-source data—spanning geographic, meteorological, fuel, and anthropogenic factors—and employs the Relief algorithm for feature selection to identify dominant predictors. The physical model accounts for both flame bridging and smoke effects on withstand strength, enabling a scenario-based evaluation of breakdown risk. By coupling these components, the framework allows for a spatially explicit and mechanistically grounded calculation of tripping risk along transmission corridors. Unlike previous hybrid models that often focus solely on fire propagation or electrical breakdown in isolation, our framework integrates spatial ignition probability with dynamic physical modeling of insulation failure, enabling an end-to-end risk assessment that is both spatially explicit and mechanistically grounded.

Physical model for wildfire-induced transmission line tripping

Wildfire spread model

Accurately predicting wildfire spread through purely physical mechanistic models remains challenging. Predominant approaches therefore blend physical principles with empirical analysis, yielding semi-physical or semi-empirical models38–40. These models incorporate assumptions regarding heat flux and ignition energy requirements without rigorously delineating heat transfer modes or absorption mechanisms- key constants governing heat flux and absorption are typically derived from experimental data. Furthermore, they do not explicitly model the processes governing net energy transfer per unit area on the fuel bed surface, which is often estimated empirically as a function of reaction intensity. Among these, the model proposed by Rothermel22 has garnered widespread acceptance due to its validation through extensive laboratory experiments and field observations41–44.

Calculation of average smoke withstand field strength

The average smoke withstand field strength under wildfire conditions refers to the maximum electric field intensity that a high-voltage transmission line can endure within a smoke-laden environment caused by a spreading fire without experiencing flashover. The intense heat from vegetation combustion reduces air density and humidity, facilitating the development of electron avalanches and streamers. Additionally, ash and smoke particles, transported by electric field forces and thermal buoyancy, infiltrate the air gaps around conductors, causing electric field distortion and potentially forming conductive pathways, thereby degrading the gap’s withstand capability29,30. The withstand voltage of an air gap during a wildfire is highly dependent on the combustion characteristics. Firstly, chemical dissociation and thermal ionization of alkali and alkaline earth metals in vegetation generate significant charge carriers, conferring high electrical conductivity to the flame itself. If the flame bridges a significant portion of the gap, breakdown is highly probable. Secondly, the presence of burning particles and the heated, smoke-laden atmosphere above the flame significantly reduce the breakdown field strength compared to clean air. Evaluation of wildfire occurrence probability using bayesian model

Factors influencing wildfire occurrence

This study identifies and collects data for 16 factors influencing wildfire occurrence within the study area, categorized into four groups: geographical conditions, meteorological conditions, combustible conditions, and human activities, as listed in Table 2. The 16 factors are widely recognized as the primary drivers of wildfire ignition and spread in the literature50–52. While no model can encompass every possible factor, our selection represents a holistic set of predictors that capture the key physical, environmental, and anthropogenic processes influencing wildfire occurrence near transmission corridors. Geographical and meteorological factors represent regional natural environmental variables. Geographical factors include elevation, slope, aspect, and distance to water source. Meteorological factors comprise monthly average rainfall, monthly average temperature, monthly average air humidity, and monthly thunderstorm day count.

The presence of an ignition source and sufficient combustible material are necessary preconditions for wildfires. Research indicates that over 95% of wildfire ignitions are attributed to human activities53. Therefore, five factors were selected to quantify human influence: distance to roads, distance to residential areas, population density, regional GDP per capita, and historical fire point density. Combustible conditions are characterized by vegetation attributes under the transmission line corridor, specifically land use type, vegetation type, and fuel load. Note that the vegetation data are considered within a 30-meter buffer on either side of the transmission corridor to account for potential fire spread from adjacent areas, consistent with typical right-of-way management practices. Data for these 16 factors were sourced from open-access databases provided by the Resource and Environmental Science and Data Center (RESDC) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://www.resdc.cn/) and the National Meteorological Information Center of China (NMICC) (https://sci.cma.cn/).

To facilitate Bayesian probability modeling, continuous variables were discretized using frequency-based methods. The Relief algorithm54 is then employed to rank the importance of each influencing factor. The algorithm operates on a dataset comprising n samples (instances), each described by m features and a class label (fire or non-fire), following four steps below:

Initialization: Assign an initial weight (often zero) to each feature f.Iterative Sampling: For T iterations, Randomly select a reference sample R; Find the nearest sample from the same class as R (Near Hit, NH); Find the nearest sample from the opposite classes to R (Near Miss, NM – often one per class, then take the closest overall).Weight Update: For each feature f, decrease the weight proportionally to the difference between R and NH on feature f (penalizing features that separate instances of the same class); Increase the weight proportionally to the difference between R and NM on feature f (rewarding features that distinguish between classes).Result: After T iterations, each feature has a final weight. Features are ranked by these weights in descending order to indicate importance.

Susceptibility assessment of wildfire-induced transmission line tripping

Tripping risk

The occurrence of a wildfire-induced transmission line trip requires the conjunction of two conditions: firstly, a wildfire ignites sufficiently close to the line to degrade the air insulation integrity beneath the conductors; secondly, the reduced withstand strength of either the conductor-to-ground or phase-to-phase air gap falls below the prevailing operating voltage stress, leading to insulation breakdown and consequent tripping. Given the typical duration of wildfires, successful automatic reclosing is often unlikely; therefore, the potential mitigating effect of reclosing is not considered in this risk assessment. Case study and results

Study Area.This study focused on the power grid of Guangdong Province, China. The provincial distribution of wildfire occurrence probability and the subsequent tripping risk for transmission line sections were evaluated. Guangdong features a subtropical monsoon climate with hot summers, mild winters, distinct seasons, and abundant vegetation. Notably, 57.9% of the province’s terrain is mountainous or hilly. This complex topography can hinder satellite-based fire detection due to occlusion, increasing the challenge of maintaining grid stability. During the high-risk spring season of 2022, two wildfires occurred within the line’s corridor (marked on Fig. 7). The first fire (Feb. 16, 11:30) ignited in a location classified as being at the boundary between susceptibility Level III and Level IV. It burned until 13:00 without causing a line trip. The second fire (Mar. 24, 16:00) occurred squarely in a Level IV section. By 17:30, the fire led to a line trip, and reclosing attempts failed, causing significant grid disruption. The vegetation under the line in this section was primarily tall broadleaf forest (~ 18 m height), with a clearance of only ~ 5 m to the conductor. The normalized tripping risk probability reached 82.4%. This real event demonstrates the model’s accuracy and practical utility. Proactive measures informed by such risk maps, like targeted vegetation management within high-risk corridors, could effectively reduce the likelihood of wildfire-related trips.

Conclusion

This study addressed the challenge of assessing the risk of wildfire-induced transmission line tripping by developing an integrated methodology combining susceptibility zoning and risk quantification. The coupling of a Bayesian wildfire occurrence probability model with a physics-based insulation breakdown risk model enabled a spatially explicit evaluation of tripping risk. The key findings are:

Feature importance analysis using the Relief algorithm identified distance to residential areas, vegetation type, and monthly average rainfall as the predominant factors influencing wildfire occurrence within the study area, emphasizing the critical roles of human activity and fuel characteristics.

The optimized Bayesian network model effectively predicted spatial patterns of wildfire probability, validated by the strong agreement between high-probability zones and independent historical fire incident data.

A scenario-based approach for assessing insulation breakdown risk, incorporating flame height and modified smoke withstand strength, successfully captured the significant impact of flame bridging on the electrical withstand capability of air gaps.

The case study in Guangdong Province demonstrated that the resulting tripping risk distribution was logically consistent with geographical features, vegetation types, and crucially, aligned with actual recorded tripping events, confirming the model’s accuracy and operational relevance. The findings provide a scientifically grounded basis for implementing zonal and prioritized wildfire prevention strategies for power grids, thereby enhancing their resilience.

It should be noted that the current physical model employs area-averaged parameters for vegetation combustion, which is a simplification suitable for regional-scale assessment but may not capture hyper-local variability.

Future work could enhance the model’s temporal dynamics by integrating real-time meteorological data and time-varying vegetation parameters. Furthermore, coupling the assessment with advanced monitoring technologies, such as drones and satellite remote sensing, could facilitate dynamic risk monitoring and early warning systems. While the framework is developed for a subtropical region, it is transferable to other regions provided local data on vegetation, climate, and grid infrastructure are available. Future work should validate the model in diverse biogeographic and climatic settings.
