The Riemann Ξ \Xi -function from primitive Markovian cycles II: Strip rigidity and divisor identification
Douglas F. Watson

Abstract
. We compare the Riemann Ξ \Xi –function to a canonical real-entire reference family arising from the cycle Laplacian developed in Paper I ( [ 4 ] ). These spectral determinants have only real zeros by self-adjointness. Our main tool is a rigidity lemma for holomorphic functions on horizontal strips. Applied to a normalized seam ratio linking Ξ ( 2 ⋅ ) \Xi(2\cdot) to the reference family, this lemma shows that, under explicit holomorphy and boundary nonvanishing hypotheses verified in the forthcoming Paper III, the seam ratio extends to a zero-free holomorphic function of bounded type on each overlap strip. It follows that, on every admissible overlap strip, Ξ ( 2 ⋅ ) \Xi(2\cdot) and the reference family have the same zero divisor.

two very different canonical constructions, and that the hard step is to show that these
constructions in fact produce the same function (or the same divisor), up to an

1.1.
Review of the primitive dynamical model

For each integer
N\geq 1
we consider a continuous-time, reversible, nearest-neighbor Markov
process on the discrete cycle
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
. The dynamics are specified by a collection of strictly
positive conductances
\{a_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}}
, where
a_{j}
is associated with the undirected edge
between
j
and
j+1
(indices taken modulo
N
). The generator
\mathcal{L}_{N}
acts on functions

f:\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
by

(1)

(\mathcal{L}_{N}f)(j)=a_{j}\,\bigl(f(j+1)-f(j)\bigr)\;+a_{j-1}\,\bigl(f(j-1)-f(j)\bigr).

We write
p^{\mathrm{cyc}}_{t}(j,k)
for the associated heat kernel,

p^{\mathrm{cyc}}_{t}(j,k)=(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{N}}\mathbf{1}_{\{k\}})(j)
, and we denote by
D>0
the
macroscopic diffusion constant appearing in the Gaussian scaling limit established later.

As described above, the trace of the heat kernel associated with the finite dynamics captures
global information about the system but contains a universal singular contribution reflecting
diffusive behavior. This singular term is independent of the fine structure of the dynamics and
depends only on the macroscopic scaling. To isolate the genuinely structural content, we
subtract this term in a canonical way.

Definition 1.3

(Scaling-limit trace and completed trace kernel)
.

Fix a macroscopic length
L>0
and choose a scaling parameter
s\to\infty
with
N=N(s)
such
that
N/s\to L
. Define the scaling-limit trace

K_{L}(t):=\lim_{s\to\infty}N(s)\,p^{\mathrm{cyc}}_{s^{2}t}(0,0),\qquad t>0,

and set the (scaling-limit) completed trace kernel

\widetilde{K}_{L}(t):=K_{L}(t)-\frac{L}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}.

When
L
is fixed we suppress the subscript and write
\widetilde{K}
.

Definition 1.4

(Full centering and half-density normalization)
.

Define the fully centered completed kernel

\widetilde{K}_{L}^{\star}(t):=\widetilde{K}_{L}(t)-1=K_{L}(t)-1-\frac{L}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}},\qquad t>0,

and the associated half-density kernels

\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{sym}}(t):=t^{-1/2}\,\widetilde{K}(t),\qquad\widetilde{K}^{\star}_{\mathrm{sym}}(t):=t^{-1/2}\,\widetilde{K}^{\star}(t).

It is important for what follows that the scaling-limit trace kernel is obtained purely from the
Markov dynamics via a lift–periodize procedure and a controlled Gaussian scaling limit.
No Mellin transforms, functional equations, or arithmetic input enter at this stage; those appear only
after the scaling limit has produced the explicit theta-series kernel. This separation will be used
repeatedly below to keep the comparison argument noncircular.

The existence of the scaling limit in Definition
1.3
, together with the
domination estimates needed to justify termwise limits and integral interchanges, follows from
the standing assumptions recorded below; for convenience we collect the relevant analytic
justifications in Appendix
A
.

Remark 1.5

(Standing assumptions and existence of the scaling limit)
.

We work throughout in a strong near-homogeneity regime in which the scaling-limit trace
K_{L}(t)

exists for every
L>0
and
t>0
, with convergence locally uniform in
t
on compact subsets of

(0,\infty)
. Under these assumptions the limit admits the explicit theta-series form stated in
Theorem
3.1
. Analytic interchanges (limits, sums, differentiation,
and Laplace/Mellin integrals) are justified by the domination estimates recorded in
Appendix
A
.

3.
Self-dual Archimedean completion and forced normalization

The Mellin transform in later sections is taken in the same variable
t
in which the scaling-limit
trace is naturally expressed. For the strip comparison it is therefore important to choose the
macroscopic normalization so that Jacobi inversion acts as the involution
t\mapsto t^{-1}
in
that same variable, rather than only after an auxiliary change of variables. This section isolates
that normalization and records the corresponding completed Archimedean kernel.

3.1.
Theta-series form and the forced self-dual scale

Recall from
[
4
]
that the macroscopic length parameter
L>0
enters via the scaling relation

N(s)/s\to L
in Definition
1.3
. Under the standing assumptions, the
scaling-limit trace admits an explicit theta-series representation.

Theorem 3.1

(Theta-series form of the scaling-limit trace
[
4
, Theorem X]
)
.

For every
L>0
and
t>0
,

(2)

K_{L}(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{4\pi^{2}D}{L^{2}}\,n^{2}\,t\Bigr).

It is convenient to rewrite this in Jacobi’s standard form. Set

(3)

t^{\prime}:=\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}\,t.

Then (
2
) becomes

K_{L}(t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\pi n^{2}t^{\prime}}=:\vartheta(t^{\prime}),\qquad\vartheta(u):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\pi n^{2}u}.

The classical inversion formula is

(4)

\vartheta(u)=u^{-1/2}\,\vartheta(u^{-1}),\qquad u>0.

For our later Mellin transforms, the relevant notion of “self-duality” is that Jacobi inversion
corresponds to
t\mapsto t^{-1}
in the original
t
-variable.

Lemma 3.2

(Forced self-dual normalization)
.

The following are equivalent:

(a)

t^{\prime}(t^{-1})=(t^{\prime}(t))^{-1}
for all
t>0
.

(b)

K_{L}
satisfies an inversion symmetry in the
t
-variable,

(5)

K_{L}(t)=t^{-1/2}K_{L}(t^{-1}),\qquad t>0.

(c)

The macroscopic scale satisfies

(6)

L^{2}=4\pi D.

Proof.

By (
3
), one has
t^{\prime}(t^{-1})=\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}t^{-1}
and

(t^{\prime}(t))^{-1}=\bigl(\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}t\bigr)^{-1}=\frac{L^{2}}{4\pi D}\,t^{-1}
.
Thus (a) holds for all
t
if and only if
\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}=\frac{L^{2}}{4\pi D}
,
i.e.
L^{2}=4\pi D
, which is (c).

Assuming (a), we have
K_{L}(t)=\vartheta(t^{\prime}(t))
and therefore, by (
4
),

K_{L}(t)=\vartheta(t^{\prime}(t))=(t^{\prime}(t))^{-1/2}\vartheta\!\bigl((t^{\prime}(t))^{-1}\bigr)=(t^{\prime}(t))^{-1/2}\vartheta(t^{\prime}(t^{-1})).

Since
(t^{\prime}(t))^{-1/2}=\bigl(\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}}t\bigr)^{-1/2}=t^{-1/2}\cdot(\frac{4\pi D}{L^{2}})^{-1/2}

and the same constant rescales
\vartheta(t^{\prime})
back to
K_{L}
, this yields (
5
);
conversely, (
5
) is precisely Jacobi inversion transported back to
t
.
∎

Remark 3.3

(Interpretation)
.

Condition (
6
) is the unique choice for which Jacobi inversion acts as

t\mapsto t^{-1}
in the Mellin variable used later. This normalization is macroscopic: it fixes
the relationship between the continuum parameters
L
and
D
in the scaling limit and does not
require any special tuning of the microscopic conductances beyond the assumptions ensuring the
existence of
K_{L}
.

3.2.
The Archimedean completion operator

The theta-series trace contains two universal singular pieces at the ends
t\to 0
and
t\to\infty
:
a constant contribution (the zero mode) and a
t^{-1/2}
contribution (the diffusive singularity).
Following
[
4
]
, we remove these in a way compatible with Mellin analysis by applying a
second-order differential operator that annihilates both
1
and
t^{-1/2}
.

Definition 3.4

(Archimedean completion operator)
.

For a sufficiently smooth function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, define

(7)

(\mathcal{A}f)(t):=\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(t^{3/2}\frac{d}{dt}f(t)\Bigr),\qquad t>0.

Lemma 3.5

(The completion operator kills the singular pieces)
.

One has
\mathcal{A}(1)=0
and
\mathcal{A}(t^{-1/2})=0
.

Proof.

This is a direct calculation:
f\equiv 1
gives
f^{\prime}\equiv 0
. For
f(t)=t^{-1/2}
,
one has
f^{\prime}(t)=-(1/2)t^{-3/2}
so that
t^{3/2}f^{\prime}(t)\equiv-1/2
, whose derivative is
0
.
∎

Definition 3.6

(Archimedean-completed kernel)
.

Let
K_{L}(t)
be the scaling-limit trace. Define the Archimedean-completed kernel

\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t):=\bigl(\mathcal{A}(K_{L}-1)\bigr)(t),\qquad t>0.

3.3.
The classical
\Theta
-kernel at the self-dual scale

At the self-dual normalization (
6
), the theta-series form of
K_{L}
becomes the
standard Jacobi theta function in the variable
t
, and
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}
coincides with
a classical rapidly decaying theta-kernel.

Lemma 3.7

(Identification with the classical
\Theta
-kernel
[
4
, Lemma Y]
)
.

Assume the self-dual scale
L^{2}=4\pi D
. Then for all
t>0
,

\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)=\Theta(t),

where

\Theta(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(2\pi^{2}n^{4}t^{3/2}-3\pi n^{2}t^{1/2}\Bigr)e^{-\pi n^{2}t}.

Remark 3.8
.

The proof is a termwise differentiation of the absolutely convergent series for
K_{L}(t)
after
imposing
L^{2}=4\pi D
; see
[
4
]
for details and uniformity justifications.

Lemma 3.9

(Rapid decay of the completed
\Theta
-kernel)
.

Assume the self-dual scale
L^{2}=4\pi D
so that
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}=\Theta
.
Then
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)
decays rapidly as
t\to 0^{+}
and as
t\to\infty
.
In particular, for every
A>0
there exists
C_{A}>0
such that

|\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)|\leq C_{A}\,t^{A}\quad(0<t\leq 1),\qquad|\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)|\leq C_{A}\,t^{-A}\quad(t\geq 1).

Consequently, the Mellin integrals defining
F_{\mathrm{arch}}(z)
are absolutely convergent and
integration by parts produces no boundary terms on strict vertical strips.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
,
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)
is given by an absolutely
convergent series whose
n
th term is a finite linear combination of
t^{1/2}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}
and

t^{3/2}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}
. For
t\geq 1
these terms decay exponentially in
t
, uniformly in
n
, hence
faster than any power. For
0<t\leq 1
, apply Jacobi inversion to the underlying theta series (or,
equivalently, use the standard modular relation for
\vartheta(t)
) to rewrite
\Theta(t)
as a
rapidly decaying combination of terms
t^{-1/2}e^{-\pi n^{2}/t}
and their
t
-derivatives; since
n\geq 1

the factor
e^{-\pi n^{2}/t}
yields decay faster than any power as
t\to 0^{+}
. The stated bounds follow
by termwise estimation of the absolutely convergent series.
∎

Mellin transform and the Riemann
\Xi
-function

The point of introducing

Definition 3.10

(Completed zeta functions)

Define

\xi(w):=\frac{1}{2}\,w(w-1)\,\pi^{-w/2}\Gamma\!\Bigl(\frac{w}{2}\Bigr)\zeta(w),\qquad\Xi(z):=\xi\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}+iz\Bigr).

Theorem 3.11

(Archimedean Mellin identification
[

)
.

Assume the self-dual scale
L^{2}=4\pi D
so that
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}=\Theta
.
Define, for

,

F_{\mathrm{arch}}(z):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)\,t^{\frac{3}{4}+iz}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Then for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

(9)

F_{\mathrm{arch}}(z)=\Xi(2z).

Remark 3.12

(How this will be used)
.

Theorem
3.11
produces the Mellin-side analytic object to be compared

this paper does not revisit the derivation of (

4.
Cycle spectral determinants and the reference family

In the revised Paper I we do
not
invoke Pólya-frequency rigidity. Instead, from the same
primitive Markovian input (the reversible random walk on the size–
N
cycle) we extract a
self-adjoint cycle operator
L_{N}
(e.g. the unnormalized Laplacian) and use its spectrum as a
rigid reference for later divisor comparisons.

4.1.
Cycle spectrum

For the unnormalized Laplacian on the cycle one has the explicit eigenvalues

\lambda_{k}^{(N)}=2-2\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi k}{N}\Big)\in[0,4],\qquad k=0,1,\dots,N-1.

In particular
\mathrm{spec}(L_{N})\subset\mathbb{R}
.

4.2.
Rescaled spectral map and determinant

Following Paper I, introduce the rescaled spectral map

\widetilde{q}_{N}(w):=\Big(\frac{N}{2\pi}\Big)^{2}\Big(2-2\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi w}{N}\Big)\Big),

so that
\widetilde{q}_{N}(w)\to w^{2}
locally uniformly on
\mathbb{C}
as
N\to\infty
. Define the spectral
determinant reference function

P_{N}(w):=\det\!\big(\widetilde{q}_{N}(w)I-L_{N}\big)=\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\big(\widetilde{q}_{N}(w)-\lambda_{k}^{(N)}\big).

Since
L_{N}
is self-adjoint, the zeros of
P_{N}
are real in the axis variable
w
(up to the real
preimages under
\widetilde{q}_{N}
).

4.3.
How Paper II uses
P_{N}

In what follows
P_{N}
plays the role of a real-entire reference family against which we compare

X(w):=\Xi(2w)
on horizontal strips. The bridge mechanism is purely analytic: under boundary
separation on strip rectangles (Hypothesis
1.1
) and a bounded-type/rigidity
principle (Hypothesis
1.2
), the normalized seam ratio becomes a strip-unit, forcing
divisor identification on overlap strips. The verification of the required boundary bounds and the
choice of an admissible regime
N=N(T)
are carried out in Paper III.

5.
Left-strip continuation via modular splitting

In this section we extend the bilateral Laplace transform of the centered kernel

\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx,

initially defined on the strip
-\tfrac{1}{4}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{3}{4}

(Lemma
[
4
]
), to a larger strip on the left.
The continuation uses only the explicit theta-series form of
K_{L}
together with Jacobi inversion,
the exact centering built into
\Phi^{\star}
, and absolute convergence of explicitly split integrals.
No Mellin transforms, factorization results, or properties of
\Xi
enter here.

5.1.
Modular splitting of the Laplace integral

Fix
s\in\mathbb{C}
and split the defining integral at the origin:

(10)

\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx+\int_{-\infty}^{0}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx.

On the initial strip
-\tfrac{1}{4}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{3}{4}
both integrals converge
absolutely by Lemma
[
4
]
.

Changing variables
x\mapsto-x
in the second integral in (
10
) gives the elementary
split identity

(11)

\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(-x)e^{sx}\,dx.

To continue
\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}
to the left, we rewrite the second term on a left strip using
the twisted symmetry of the centered kernel (proved in
[
4
]
) together with absolute
convergence of the resulting split integrals.

5.2.
Absolute convergence on the left strip

We now record the absolute convergence needed for the left-strip defining representation.

Lemma 5.1

(Left-strip convergence)
.

Each integral in

(12)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(\frac{1}{2}-s)x}\,dx

converges absolutely provided

-\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}.

Proof.

We use the tail bounds from Lemma
[
4
]
.

First integral.

For
x\geq 0
, one has
|\Phi^{\star}(x)|\leq Ce^{-3x/4}
. Thus

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl|\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\bigr|\,dx\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\Re(s)+3/4)x}\,dx,

which converges whenever
\Re(s)>-\tfrac{3}{4}
, in particular for all

\Re(s)>-\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Second integral.

Again using
|\Phi^{\star}(x)|\leq Ce^{-3x/4}
for
x\geq 0
, we estimate

\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl|\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(\frac{1}{2}-s)x}\bigr|\,dx\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\frac{5}{4}-\Re(s))x}\,dx,

which converges for all
\Re(s)<\tfrac{5}{4}
, and in particular for

\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}
.

Combining the two bounds gives the stated strip.
∎

5.3.
Definition and agreement on the overlap

Lemma
5.1
allows us to use the left-strip split
(
12
) as a definition.

Definition 5.2

(Left-strip continuation)
.

For
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}
, define

(13)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(\frac{1}{2}-s)x}\,dx.

Lemma 5.3

(Consistency on the overlap)
.

On the intersection strip
-\tfrac{1}{4}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}
, one has

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s).

Proof.

Fix
s
with
-\tfrac{1}{4}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}
. The defining bilateral Laplace integral
for
\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx
converges absolutely,
so we may split at
0
and change variables on the negative half-line:

\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(-x)e^{sx}\,dx.

Using the twisted symmetry
\Phi^{\star}(-x)=e^{-x/2}\Phi^{\star}(x)
(proved in
[
4
]
),
the second term becomes
\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(\frac{1}{2}-s)x}\,dx
.
This is exactly (
13
).
∎

Proposition 5.4

(Analytic continuation to the left strip)
.

The function
\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}
admits an analytic continuation from

-\tfrac{1}{4}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{3}{4}
to the larger strip

-\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4},

given by
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s)
.

Proof.

Each integral in (
13
) depends analytically on
s
by dominated
convergence on compact subsets of the left strip (Lemma
5.1
).
Agreement on the overlap follows from Lemma
5.3
, so the two definitions glue to a
single holomorphic function.
∎

Remark 5.5

(Scope of the continuation)
.

At this stage we have extended
\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}
to the left strip using
only exact centering/twisted symmetry and absolute convergence. No boundary-value identities,
factorizations, or rigidity arguments are used here. These enter only in subsequent sections.

Remark 5.6

(Two potential failure modes)
.

The continuation above relies only on two inputs: the twisted symmetry

\Phi^{\star}(-x)=e^{-x/2}\Phi^{\star}(x)
and the one-sided decay of
\Phi^{\star}
on
(0,\infty)
.
Accordingly, the only points at which the argument could fail are:
(i) an incorrect self-dual normalization leading to an inexact twisted symmetry, and
(ii) insufficient decay to justify the split integrals as absolutely convergent.
Both are verified explicitly in Section
3
and in Appendix
A
,
and no other properties of
\Phi^{\star}
are used here.

6.
Boundary identity on the left line

In this section we evaluate the left-strip continuation of

\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}(s)
on the boundary line

\Re(s)=-\tfrac{1}{2}.

At this stage the role of the Mellin transform is purely identificational:
we compare two explicit integral representations on a region where both
sides converge absolutely. No analytic continuation of
\zeta
or
\Xi

beyond their classical Mellin definitions is used.

6.1.
Evaluation of the continued Laplace transform on the boundary

Recall from Definition
5.2
that for

-\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}
the continuation is given by

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-sx}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(\frac{1}{2}-s)x}\,dx.

We now specialize to the boundary line.

Lemma 6.1

(Boundary-value formula)
.

For every
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the boundary value

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz)
exists and is given by

(14)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{(\frac{1}{2}-iz)x}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(1-iz)x}\,dx.

Both integrals converge absolutely.

Proof.

Substitute
s=-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz
into Definition
5.2
. Absolute
convergence follows from Lemma
5.1
: the first
integral is controlled since
\Re(\tfrac{1}{2}-iz)=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and the second since

\Re(1-iz)=1
. No cancellation is used.
∎

6.2.
Reduction to the Archimedean kernel

We now express the right-hand side of (
14
) in terms of
the Archimedean-completed kernel from Section
3
.

Lemma 6.2

(Boundary identity with the Archimedean kernel)
.

For every
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

(15)

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi\left(z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{16}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)\,t^{\frac{3}{4}+iz}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Proof.

We start from (
14
). By construction of the cycle-spectral kernel
(from the half-density data in
[
4
]
), the centered logarithmic kernel satisfies

\Phi^{\star}(x)=e^{-x/4}\,\widetilde{K}^{\star}(e^{x}),\qquad\widetilde{K}^{\star}(t)=\widetilde{K}(t)-1,

with
\widetilde{K}(t)=K_{L}(t)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}
at the self-dual scale.
Substituting this into (
14
) and changing variables
t=e^{x}

(with
dx=dt/t
, justified by absolute convergence) gives the explicit Mellin forms

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{(\frac{1}{2}-iz)x}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}^{\star}(t)\,t^{\frac{1}{4}-iz}\,\frac{dt}{t},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\star}(x)e^{-(1-iz)x}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}^{\star}(t)\,t^{-\frac{5}{4}+iz}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

so
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz)
is a finite linear combination of Mellin-type
integrals of
\widetilde{K}^{\star}
with exponents shifted by
\pm iz
.

At the self-dual scale, the completion operator
\mathcal{A}
from (
7
)
removes the universal singular contributions and produces

\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}=\mathcal{A}(K_{L}-1)
(Lemma
3.5
).
To pass from Mellin integrals of
K_{L}-1
to Mellin integrals of
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}
, we use
the following integration-by-parts identity: for any sufficiently regular
f
with rapid decay at
0
and
\infty
,
and any
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

(16)

\int_{0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}f)(t)\,t^{s}\,\frac{dt}{t}=(s-1)\Bigl(s-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr)\int_{0}^{\infty}f(t)\,t^{s-\tfrac{1}{2}}\,\frac{dt}{t},

where the boundary terms vanish by rapid decay.
Taking
s=\tfrac{3}{4}+iz
and noting

(s-1)\Bigl(s-\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr)=\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{4}+iz\Bigr)\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{4}+iz\Bigr)=-(z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{16}),

we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}(K_{L}(t)-1)\,t^{\frac{1}{4}+iz}\,\frac{dt}{t}=-\frac{1}{z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{16}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)\,t^{\frac{3}{4}+iz}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Applying this relation (and the harmless symmetry
z\mapsto-z
) to the Mellin combination above yields
(
15
) after collecting the constant factors.

∎

6.3.
Identification with the Riemann
\Xi
-function

We can now invoke the Mellin identification proved earlier.

Theorem 6.3

(Left-boundary identity)
.

For every
z\in\mathbb{R}
one has

(17)

\Xi(2z)=2\pi\Bigl(z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{16}\Bigr)\,\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz\right).

Proof.

By Lemma
6.2
and Theorem
3.11
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}(t)\,t^{\frac{3}{4}+iz}\,\frac{dt}{t}=\Xi(2z).

Substituting into (
15
) yields (
17
). All steps take place on regions of absolute
convergence; no analytic continuation is invoked.
∎

Remark 6.4

(What this identity does and does not use)
.

The identity (
17
) is obtained by combining:
(i) modular splitting and exact centering/self-duality (Section
5
),
and (ii) the classical Mellin transform of the Archimedean theta kernel
(Section
3
). No spectral approximation, no real-entire (self-adjoint spectral)
factorization, and no Nevanlinna theory enter here. These will be used only in
the next section.

6.4.
Extending the bridge identity to a strip

Lemma 6.5

(Bridge identity extends to a strip)
.

Define

X(w):=\xi\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-iw\right)=\Xi(-w),\qquad F(w):=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{i}{2}w\Bigr),

and

U(w):=\frac{\pi}{8}(4w^{2}+1).

Then
F
admits a holomorphic extension to the open strip
\{|\Im w|<\tfrac{1}{2}\}
, and the
left-boundary bridge identity

(18)

X(w)=U(w)\,F(w)\qquad(w\in\mathbb{R})

upgrades to an identity of holomorphic functions on the full strip:

(19)

X(w)=U(w)\,F(w)\qquad(|\Im w|<\tfrac{1}{2}).

Proof.

We first obtain holomorphy of
F
on the upper half-strip
0<\Im w<\tfrac{1}{2}
directly from the
defining left-strip integral formula. Indeed, writing
w=u+iv
we have

-\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{i}{2}w=-\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{v}{2}-\tfrac{i}{2}u,

so for
0<v<\tfrac{1}{2}
the argument lies in the left strip
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\Re(s)<\tfrac{1}{4}
where

\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s)
is defined by an absolutely convergent sum of Laplace integrals.
Local uniform domination (on compact subsets of
0<v<\tfrac{1}{2}
) allows differentiation under the
integral sign, hence
F
is holomorphic on
0<\Im w<\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Next, since
\Phi^{\star}
is real-valued on
(0,\infty)
, the defining integrals imply the conjugation
symmetry

\overline{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(s)}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{LB}}\Phi^{\star}(\overline{s})

throughout the left strip. Consequently
\overline{F(w)}=F(\overline{w})
on
0<\Im w<\tfrac{1}{2}
, and
Schwarz reflection yields a holomorphic extension of
F
to the lower half-strip
-\tfrac{1}{2}<\Im w<0
,
hence to the full strip
|\Im w|<\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Finally,
X
is entire in
w
, and
U(w)
is holomorphic and nonvanishing on
|\Im w|<\tfrac{1}{2}

(since its zeros are at
w=\pm i/2
, on the boundary). Thus both sides of (
18
) are
holomorphic on
|\Im w|<\tfrac{1}{2}
, and they agree for all real
w
. By the identity theorem, the
equality extends to all
|\Im w|<\tfrac{1}{2}
, giving (
19
).
∎

7.
Strip-unit forcing on an overlap strip

In this section we isolate the analytic forcing principle that turns boundary separation on strip
rectangles into divisor identification on the interior. The reference family is the cycle spectral
determinant
P_{N}
from Section
4
and Hypothesis
1.1
supplies
a zero-free holomorphic unit
U_{\eta,N}
so that
\Xi(2\cdot)
is close to
U_{\eta,N}P_{N}
on

\partial R_{T}
.

Define the
normalized seam ratio

\widetilde{R}_{N}(w):=\frac{\Xi(2w)}{U_{\eta,N}(w)P_{N}(w)}.

On the boundary
\partial R_{T}
the separation inequality in Hypothesis
1.1
implies

|\widetilde{R}_{N}(w)-1|<1,\qquad w\in\partial R_{T},

so in particular
\widetilde{R}_{N}
is holomorphic and nonvanishing on
\partial R_{T}
and has winding
number
0
there.

Lemma 7.1

(Rouché forcing on strip rectangles)
.

Assume Hypothesis
1.1
. Then for all
T\geq T_{0}(\eta)
the functions
w\mapsto\Xi(2w)
and
w\mapsto U_{\eta,N}(w)P_{N}(w)
have the same number of zeros in
R_{T}
(counted with
multiplicity). Since
U_{\eta,N}
is zero-free,
\Xi(2\cdot)
and
P_{N}
have the same zero divisor in

R_{T}
.

Proof.

On
\partial R_{T}
we have
|\Xi(2w)-U_{\eta,N}(w)P_{N}(w)|<|U_{\eta,N}(w)P_{N}(w)|
by
Hypothesis
1.1
. Rouché’s theorem gives equality of zero counts in
R_{T}
. The
unit factor
U_{\eta,N}
does not contribute zeros.
∎

The remainder of the paper develops strip holomorphy and bounded-type control for the seam ratio
under mild additional hypotheses (e.g. sector control, Hypothesis
1.2
), so that the
divisor identification can be upgraded to a strip-unit statement for a suitably normalized ratio.
The required quantitative boundary bounds and an admissible choice of the regime
N=N(T)
are
established in Paper III.

Exhaustion of admissible overlap strips

The forcing mechanism above applies on any fixed strip
S_{\eta}
once the boundary separation
hypothesis is verified on a family of rectangles
R_{T}
exhausting the strip. Paper III provides the

\Xi(2\cdot)
together with an admissible choice of cycle size

so that Hypothesis

holds for each

(and, in

relevant to the critical strip in the

9.
Further remarks

9.1.
Logical separation and non-circularity

We briefly summarize the logical structure of the argument, emphasizing the strict separation
of inputs and the absence of circularity. The construction begins with finite reversible
Markov dynamics and produces a scaling-limit trace kernel via lift, periodization, and a
controlled Gaussian limit. The self-dual normalization and the Archimedean completion are
fixed entirely at the level of this scaling limit and do not depend on any number-theoretic
data.

Independently, the logarithmic kernel
\Phi^{\star}
is constructed and shown to satisfy exact
reflection symmetry and spectral approximation. The resulting real-entire (self-adjoint spectral) factorization of its
bilateral Laplace transform is therefore obtained without reference to Mellin transforms,
theta functions, or the Riemann zeta function.

The Mellin-side analysis enters only at a later stage, through the Archimedean-completed kernel

\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}
, whose Mellin transform is identified explicitly with the
classical
\Xi
-function. This identification is one-directional: it does not feed back into,
or constrain, the Laplace-side constructions.

Finally, the two sides are compared through a boundary identity obtained by modular splitting
and exact self-duality, followed by a strip-based rigidity argument. At no point is analytic
continuation of
\zeta
or
\Xi
assumed beyond what is already built into their classical
integral representations.

9.2.
Stress testing and potential failure modes

The argument was stress-tested against several natural potential failure modes.

First, the modular splitting used to continue
\mathcal{B}\Phi^{\star}
to the left strip could
fail if either the twisted symmetry were inexact or the decay of
\Phi^{\star}
were
insufficient to justify absolute convergence of the split integrals. Both points are verified
explicitly in Sections
3
and
5
.

Second, the boundary identity could introduce spurious poles or zeros if boundary terms failed
to vanish under integration by parts. This possibility is ruled out by the rapid decay of the
Archimedean kernel
\widetilde{K}_{\mathrm{arch}}
at both
0
and
\infty
, established
independently of any factorization or rigidity arguments.

Third, the strip-unit forcing mechanism relies on bounded-type control on strict substrips.
On the right-hand side this control follows from the Schoenberg–Edrei–Karlin factorization,
while on the left-hand side it follows from the explicit integral representation obtained by
modular splitting. No additional growth assumptions are required.

9.3.
Remarks on scope

The method developed here isolates a general mechanism by which exact self-duality, total
positivity, and analytic rigidity combine to force real-zero phenomena. While the present
paper focuses on the Riemann
\Xi
-function, the framework is not inherently tied to zeta
functions and may apply more broadly in settings where comparable kernel-level symmetries and
positivity properties can be established.

Throughout, we have attempted to make all analytic interchanges, normalizations, and limiting
procedures explicit, so that each step may be checked independently. We would welcome further
scrutiny, refinement, or simplification of any part of the argument.

Appendix A
Technical analytic justifications

This appendix collects several analytic estimates and justifications that are
used implicitly or explicitly in the main text. None of the results here
introduce new objects or alter the logical structure of the argument; they serve
only to justify standard limiting procedures, termwise operations, and boundary
manipulations already invoked.

A.1.
Uniform local central limit bounds

In Section
3
we appealed to a uniform local central
limit theorem (ULCLT) to pass from the discrete primitive dynamics to the
scaling-limit kernel
K_{L}
. We record here a representative bound sufficient for
all uses in the paper.

Lemma A.1

(Uniform local CLT estimate (translation-invariant cycle))
.

Assume the conductances are translation-invariant, so that the generator on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
is

(\mathcal{L}_{N}f)(j)=a\bigl(f(j+1)-f(j)\bigr)+a\bigl(f(j-1)-f(j)\bigr),

and the effective diffusion constant is
D=a
. Let
p_{t}^{(N)}(j)
denote the transition
kernel started from
0
and viewed on
\mathbb{Z}
via the canonical lift. Then for every compact
interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset(0,\infty)
there exists
C=C(t_{0},t_{1})
such that

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|p_{t}^{(N)}(j)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{j^{2}}{4Dt}\Bigr)\Bigr|\leq\frac{C}{N},

for all sufficiently large
N
.

Proof.

Because the chain is translation-invariant on
\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
, the Fourier modes

e_{k}(j):=e^{2\pi ikj/N}
diagonalize
\mathcal{L}_{N}
, and the corresponding eigenvalues are

\lambda_{k}=a\bigl(e^{2\pi ik/N}-1\bigr)+a\bigl(e^{-2\pi ik/N}-1\bigr)=-2a\Bigl(1-\cos\frac{2\pi k}{N}\Bigr)\qquad(k=0,1,\dots,N-1).

Hence the heat kernel on the cycle has the exact representation

(20)

p_{t}^{(N)}(j)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\exp\!\bigl(t\lambda_{k}\bigr)\,e^{2\pi ikj/N}.

Fix
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset(0,\infty)
. We compare (
20
) to the Gaussian
kernel on
\mathbb{R}
by isolating the small-frequency contribution and bounding the tail.

Step 1: quadratic approximation of the dispersion relation.

Write
\theta_{k}:=2\pi k/N\in[0,2\pi)
. For
|\theta|\leq\pi
one has the Taylor expansion

1-\cos\theta=\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}+O(\theta^{4}),

with an absolute implied constant. Therefore, for
|k|\leq N/4
(so that
|\theta_{k}|\leq\pi/2
),

(21)

t\lambda_{k}=-2at\Bigl(1-\cos\theta_{k}\Bigr)=-at\,\theta_{k}^{2}+O\!\Bigl(t\,\theta_{k}^{4}\Bigr)=-4\pi^{2}Dt\,\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}+O\!\Bigl(t\,\frac{k^{4}}{N^{4}}\Bigr),

uniformly for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

Step 2: tail bound for large frequencies.

For
|k|\geq N/4
(interpreting
k
modulo
N
and taking the representative in

\{-\lfloor N/2\rfloor,\dots,\lfloor(N-1)/2\rfloor\}
), we have
|\theta_{k}|\in[\pi/2,\pi]
and
hence
1-\cos\theta_{k}\geq c_{0}
for an absolute constant
c_{0}>0
. Thus

\Re(t\lambda_{k})\leq-2at\,c_{0}\leq-2at_{0}c_{0},

so the tail contribution satisfies, uniformly in
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(22)

\Bigl|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|\geq N/4}\exp(t\lambda_{k})\,e^{2\pi ikj/N}\Bigr|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|\geq N/4}e^{-2at_{0}c_{0}}\leq e^{-2at_{0}c_{0}}.

Since the right-hand side is a fixed number in
(0,1)
, it is in particular
O(1/N)
for
all sufficiently large
N
(absorbing the threshold into the phrase “sufficiently large”).

Step 3: replacing the low-frequency sum by an integral.

Restrict to
|k|<N/4
and use (
21
). Write

A_{t,N}(k):=\exp\!\Bigl(-4\pi^{2}Dt\,\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}\Bigr),\qquad B_{t,N}(k):=\exp(t\lambda_{k}).

Then (
21
) gives

B_{t,N}(k)=A_{t,N}(k)\,\exp\!\Bigl(O\!\bigl(t\,k^{4}/N^{4}\bigr)\Bigr)=A_{t,N}(k)\Bigl(1+O\!\bigl(k^{4}/N^{4}\bigr)\Bigr),

uniformly for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
|k|<N/4
. Hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|<N/4}\bigl|B_{t,N}(k)-A_{t,N}(k)\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N}\sum_{|k|<N/4}A_{t,N}(k)\,\frac{k^{4}}{N^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{N^{5}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{4}\exp\!\Bigl(-4\pi^{2}Dt_{0}\,\frac{k^{2}}{N^{2}}\Bigr).

Estimating the last sum by a Riemann integral with the change of variables
u=k/N
gives

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{4}e^{-ck^{2}/N^{2}}\leq C_{2}N^{5}\quad\text{for }c=4\pi^{2}Dt_{0},

so the preceding display is
\leq C_{3}/N
uniformly in
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

Therefore, uniformly in
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(23)

\Bigl|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|<N/4}e^{t\lambda_{k}}e^{2\pi ikj/N}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|<N/4}e^{-4\pi^{2}Dt\,k^{2}/N^{2}}\,e^{2\pi ikj/N}\Bigr|\leq\frac{C_{3}}{N}.

Step 4: Poisson summation and the Gaussian kernel.

Extend the truncated Gaussian sum to all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
at cost
O(1/N)
, since

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|\geq N/4}e^{-4\pi^{2}Dt\,k^{2}/N^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|\geq N/4}e^{-4\pi^{2}Dt_{0}\,k^{2}/N^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{4}}{N}.

Thus,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{|k|<N/4}e^{-4\pi^{2}Dt\,k^{2}/N^{2}}e^{2\pi ikj/N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-4\pi^{2}Dt\,k^{2}/N^{2}}e^{2\pi ikj/N}+O\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\Bigr).

By the Poisson summation formula (or the standard Jacobi theta identity),

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-4\pi^{2}Dt\,k^{2}/N^{2}}e^{2\pi ikj/N}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(j+mN)^{2}}{4Dt}\Bigr).

Since
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
is bounded away from
0
, the terms with
m\neq 0
are exponentially small in

N
uniformly in
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, so

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-(j+mN)^{2}/(4Dt)}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}e^{-j^{2}/(4Dt)}\Bigr|\leq e^{-c_{5}N^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{5}}{N}

for all sufficiently large
N
.

Conclusion.

Combining the tail estimate (
22
), the low-frequency replacement
(
23
), and the Poisson-summation identification above yields

\sup_{t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Bigl|p_{t}^{(N)}(j)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{j^{2}}{4Dt}\Bigr)\Bigr|\leq\frac{C}{N},

for a constant
C=C(t_{0},t_{1})
and all sufficiently large
N
, as claimed.
∎

A.2.
Dominated convergence and termwise operations

Several arguments in Sections
3
,

[
4
]
, and
3
rely on exchanging
limits, sums, derivatives, and integrals. We record here a generic domination
principle that applies uniformly to all such steps.

Lemma A.2

(Uniform domination for theta-series)
.

Let

\Theta(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}P(n,t)\,e^{-\pi n^{2}t},

where
P(n,t)
is a finite linear combination of monomials of the form

n^{\alpha}t^{\beta/2}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then:

(i)

for each
k\geq 0
, the series defining
\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta(t)
converges
absolutely and locally uniformly on
(0,\infty)
;

(ii)

for each
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
, the Mellin-weighted function

t\mapsto\Theta(t)\,t^{\sigma}
is integrable on
(0,\infty)
whenever the integral
is (formally) convergent at
0
and
\infty
.

Proof.

It suffices to treat a single monomial
P(n,t)=n^{\alpha}t^{\beta/2}
, since the general
case follows by linearity and the triangle inequality. Thus consider

\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}(t):=\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha}t^{\beta/2}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}.

Step 1: uniform bounds for Gaussian sums.

Fix
\alpha\geq 0
. For
t\geq 1
we have
e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\leq e^{-\pi n^{2}}
and hence

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha}e^{-\pi n^{2}}=:C_{\alpha}<\infty,

uniformly in
t\geq 1
.

For
0<t\leq 1
, monotonicity of
x\mapsto x^{\alpha}e^{-\pi tx^{2}}
on
[1,\infty)
and an
integral comparison give

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\leq 1+\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha}e^{-\pi tx^{2}}\,dx=1+\frac{1}{2}\,(\pi t)^{-(\alpha+1)/2}\Gamma\!\Big(\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\Big)\ll_{\alpha}t^{-(\alpha+1)/2}.

Combining the two regimes yields the uniform estimate

(24)

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\ll_{\alpha}\begin{cases}t^{-(\alpha+1)/2},&0<t\leq 1,\\[2.0pt]
1,&t\geq 1.\end{cases}

Step 2: termwise differentiation and local uniform convergence.

Differentiate the summand:

\partial_{t}^{k}\!\bigl(t^{\beta/2}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\bigr)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{k,m,\beta}\,t^{\beta/2-m}\,(\pi n^{2})^{k-m}e^{-\pi n^{2}t},

for explicit constants
c_{k,m,\beta}
. Hence

\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\sum_{m=0}^{k}c_{k,m,\beta}\,n^{\alpha+2(k-m)}\,t^{\beta/2-m}\,e^{-\pi n^{2}t}.

Fix a compact interval
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset(0,\infty)
. Then
t^{\beta/2-m}
is bounded on

[t_{0},t_{1}]
, and by (
24
) the series

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha+2(k-m)}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}

converges absolutely and uniformly on
[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Therefore the series for

\partial_{t}^{k}\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}(t)
converges absolutely and uniformly on
[t_{0},t_{1}]
,
which proves (i) by the Weierstrass
M
-test.

Step 3: Mellin integrability.

Using (
24
), we obtain for
0<t\leq 1
,

|\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}(t)|\leq t^{\beta/2}\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{\alpha}e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\ll_{\alpha,\beta}t^{\beta/2-(\alpha+1)/2},

and for
t\geq 1
,

|\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}(t)|\ll_{\alpha,\beta}t^{\beta/2}e^{-\pi t}\qquad(\text{since }e^{-\pi n^{2}t}\leq e^{-\pi t}\text{ for }n\geq 1).

Hence
|\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}(t)|t^{\sigma}
is integrable near
+\infty
for every
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}

by exponential decay, and it is integrable near
0
precisely when the power

\sigma+\frac{\beta}{2}-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}

is
>-1
, which is exactly the formal convergence condition at
0
.
This proves (ii) for
\Theta_{\alpha,\beta}
, and hence for
\Theta
by linearity.
∎

A.3.
Boundary terms for the Archimedean completion operator

In Section
6
we used an integration-by-parts identity for the
Archimedean completion operator
\mathcal{A}
. We record here a convenient
sufficient condition ensuring vanishing of boundary terms.

Lemma A.4

(Vanishing of boundary contributions)
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
be twice continuously differentiable. Assume there exist constants

\alpha,\beta>0
and constants
C_{0},C_{\infty}>0
such that, for
t\in(0,1]
and
t\geq 1
,

|f(t)|+t|f^{\prime}(t)|+t^{2}|f^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C_{0}\,t^{\beta},\qquad|f(t)|+t|f^{\prime}(t)|+t^{2}|f^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C_{\infty}\,t^{-\alpha}.

Fix real numbers
\sigma_{0}<\sigma_{1}
with

-\beta<\sigma_{0}\leq\sigma_{1}<\alpha.

Then for every
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(s)\in[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}]
, all boundary terms arising from
one or two integrations by parts in Mellin-type integrals of the form

\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}f(t)\,t^{s-1}\,dt

vanish as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and
R\uparrow\infty
, uniformly in
s
with

\Re(s)\in[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}]
.

Proof.

We record the two boundary expressions that occur after one and two integrations by parts.
For
s\in\mathbb{C}
and
0<\varepsilon<R
, one has

(25)

\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}f(t)\,t^{s-1}\,dt=\Bigl[\frac{f(t)t^{s}}{s}\Bigr]_{\varepsilon}^{R}-\frac{1}{s}\int_{\varepsilon}^{R}f^{\prime}(t)\,t^{s}\,dt,

and integrating by parts once more gives boundary terms involving

f(t)t^{s}
and
f^{\prime}(t)t^{s+1}
(with rational prefactors in
s
).

We show that each boundary term tends to
0
at both ends, uniformly for

\Re(s)\in[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}]
.

As
t\downarrow 0
.

Using the hypotheses for
t\in(0,1]
,

|f(t)t^{s}|\leq C_{0}\,t^{\beta+\Re(s)}\leq C_{0}\,t^{\beta+\sigma_{0}}\to 0\quad(t\downarrow 0),

since
\beta+\sigma_{0}>0
. Likewise,

|f^{\prime}(t)t^{s+1}|=|(tf^{\prime}(t))\,t^{s}|\leq C_{0}\,t^{\beta+\Re(s)}\leq C_{0}\,t^{\beta+\sigma_{0}}\to 0.

The same estimate applies to
f^{\prime\prime}(t)t^{s+2}
via
t^{2}|f^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C_{0}t^{\beta}
.
Thus all boundary expressions arising from one or two integrations by parts vanish at
0
,
uniformly for
\Re(s)\in[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}]
.

As
t\uparrow\infty
.

Using the hypotheses for
t\geq 1
,

|f(t)t^{s}|\leq C_{\infty}\,t^{-\alpha+\Re(s)}\leq C_{\infty}\,t^{-\alpha+\sigma_{1}}\to 0\quad(t\uparrow\infty),

since
-\alpha+\sigma_{1}<0
. Similarly,

|f^{\prime}(t)t^{s+1}|=|(tf^{\prime}(t))\,t^{s}|\leq C_{\infty}\,t^{-\alpha+\Re(s)}\leq C_{\infty}\,t^{-\alpha+\sigma_{1}}\to 0,

and likewise for
f^{\prime\prime}(t)t^{s+2}
. Hence all boundary terms vanish at
\infty
uniformly on the
strip
\Re(s)\in[\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}]
.

Combining the endpoint estimates proves the claim.
∎

A.4.
Analytic continuation by identity principles

Finally, we record the elementary identity principle used repeatedly to promote
local equalities to global ones.

Lemma A.6

(Identity principle for meromorphic functions)
.

Let
F
and
G
be meromorphic functions on a connected domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
.
Assume there exists a set
E\subset\Omega
with an accumulation point in
\Omega
such that

F(z)=G(z)
for all
z\in E
at which both sides are defined (i.e.
z
is not a pole of
F
or
G
).
Then
F=G
as meromorphic functions on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
P\subset\Omega
be the (discrete) union of the pole sets of
F
and
G
, and set

\Omega^{\prime}:=\Omega\setminus P
. Then
\Omega^{\prime}
is open and each connected component of
\Omega^{\prime}

is a domain on which both
F
and
G
are holomorphic. The hypothesis implies that on at least
one component
U
of
\Omega^{\prime}
, the set
E\cap U
has an accumulation point in
U
and

F=G
on
E\cap U
. Hence by the holomorphic identity theorem,
F=G
on
U
, i.e.
F-G\equiv 0

there.

Now consider the meromorphic function
H:=F-G
on
\Omega
. We have shown that
H
vanishes
identically on the nonempty open set
U\subset\Omega
. Since
\Omega
is connected, meromorphic
continuation forces
H\equiv 0
on all of
\Omega
: indeed, if
H
were not identically zero,
its zero set would be discrete away from poles, contradicting that it contains an open set.
Therefore
F=G
as meromorphic functions on
\Omega
.
∎

We record a convenient sufficient condition for strip-uniform decay of oscillatory integrals.

Lemma A.8

(Strip-uniform IBP/Riemann–Lebesgue)
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be absolutely continuous. Assume that for some
\eta_{0}>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\eta_{0}y}\bigl(|f(y)|+|f^{\prime}(y)|+y|f(y)|+y|f^{\prime}(y)|\bigr)\,dy<\infty.

Define

I(w):=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy.

Then
I
is holomorphic on
\{|\Im w|<\eta_{0}\}
. Moreover, for every
0<\tilde{\eta}<\eta_{0}
, as
|\Re w|\to\infty
,

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}|I(w)|=o_{\tilde{\eta}}(1/|w|),\qquad\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}|I^{\prime}(w)|=o_{\tilde{\eta}}(1/|w|),

and

I(w)=\frac{f(0)}{iw}+o_{\tilde{\eta}}(1/|w|)\qquad\text{uniformly for }|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}.

Proof.

Fix
0<\tilde{\eta}<\eta_{0}
. For
|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}
one has

|e^{-iwy}|=e^{(\Im w)y}\leq e^{\tilde{\eta}y}
, so the hypothesis implies

\int_{0}^{\infty}|f(y)|\,|e^{-iwy}|\,dy\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\tilde{\eta}y}|f(y)|\,dy<\infty,

uniformly for
|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}
. Hence
I(w)
is well-defined and bounded on the closed strip

\{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}\}
. By dominated convergence (applied to difference quotients),
I
is holomorphic
on
\{|\Im w|<\eta_{0}\}
.

Strip-uniform Riemann–Lebesgue.

Let
F_{\tilde{\eta}}(y):=e^{\tilde{\eta}y}f(y)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Then for
|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}
,

I(w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{\tilde{\eta}}(y)\,e^{-(\tilde{\eta}+iw)y}\,dy.

Writing
w=u+iv
, this is the Fourier transform (in
u
) of the
L^{1}
-function

y\mapsto e^{(\tilde{\eta}+v)y}f(y)
evaluated at frequency
u
. Since

\sup_{|v|\leq\tilde{\eta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\tilde{\eta}+v)y}|f(y)|\,dy<\infty
,
the usual
L^{1}
Riemann–Lebesgue lemma yields

(26)

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}|I(w)|\longrightarrow 0\qquad(|\Re w|\to\infty).

One integration by parts.

Because
e^{\eta_{0}y}(|f(y)|+|f^{\prime}(y)|)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, we have
e^{\tilde{\eta}y}f(y)\to 0
and

e^{\tilde{\eta}y}f^{\prime}(y)\to 0
as
y\to\infty
, and also
f
has a finite limit
f(0)
as
y\downarrow 0

(by absolute continuity). Thus the boundary terms below vanish uniformly for
|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}
.
Integrating by parts gives, for
w\neq 0
,

(27)

I(w)=\Bigl[\frac{f(y)e^{-iwy}}{-iw}\Bigr]_{0}^{\infty}+\frac{1}{iw}\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy=\frac{f(0)}{iw}+\frac{1}{iw}\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy.

By (
26
) applied to
f^{\prime}
(using
e^{\eta_{0}y}|f^{\prime}(y)|\in L^{1}
), we have

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy\Bigr|\to 0\qquad(|\Re w|\to\infty),

and hence from (
27
),

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}\Bigl|I(w)-\frac{f(0)}{iw}\Bigr|\leq\frac{1}{|w|}\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy\Bigr|=o(1/|w|).

In particular
\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}|I(w)|=o(1/|w|)
.

Derivative bound.

Since
e^{\eta_{0}y}y|f(y)|\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
, differentiation under the integral sign is justified and

I^{\prime}(w)=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}yf(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy.

Set
g(y):=yf(y)
. Then
g
is absolutely continuous,
g(0)=0
, and

g^{\prime}(y)=f(y)+yf^{\prime}(y),

so the hypothesis implies
e^{\eta_{0}y}(|g(y)|+|g^{\prime}(y)|)\in L^{1}(0,\infty)
. Integrating by parts as above,
for
w\neq 0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}g(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy=\Bigl[\frac{g(y)e^{-iwy}}{-iw}\Bigr]_{0}^{\infty}+\frac{1}{iw}\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy=\frac{1}{iw}\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy.

By the strip-uniform Riemann–Lebesgue lemma applied to
g^{\prime}
, we have

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{\infty}g^{\prime}(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy\Bigr|\to 0\qquad(|\Re w|\to\infty),

and therefore

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{\infty}yf(y)e^{-iwy}\,dy\Bigr|=o(1/|w|),

whence

\sup_{|\Im w|\leq\tilde{\eta}}|I^{\prime}(w)|=o(1/|w|).

A.6.
Quantitative sector control

This appendix records the analytic content required to upgrade
Hypothesis
1.2
to a proved lemma.
The main text isolates this point because it is the unique nontrivial
analytic “forcing” step needed for the rectangle/argument-principle mechanism.

•

A correct mechanism must control boundary arguments on long rectangles.

For rectangles
R_{T}=\{|\,\Re w|\leq T,\ |\Im w|\leq\eta_{0}\}
that are long in the
\Re w
direction,
standard harmonic-measure weights for interior points favor the horizontal sides, not the vertical
sides. Thus any proof must use additional structure (beyond “bounded type” and “vertical anchoring”)
to control
\arg\widetilde{R}
on the horizontal edges.

•

Global holomorphic logarithms cannot be assumed on excised domains.

If one removes small disks around zeros in order to define
\arg\widetilde{R}
, the resulting domain
is typically multiply connected. On such a domain, the existence of a single-valued holomorphic
logarithm of
\widetilde{R}
is obstructed by the nontrivial periods of
\widetilde{R}^{\prime}/\widetilde{R}

around the holes. Any use of
\Im\log\widetilde{R}
therefore requires an explicit device
(e.g. crosscuts to make the domain simply connected, or an argument not relying on a global log).

A complete proof of Hypothesis
1.2
would therefore need to supply:
(i) a quantitative estimate that forces
\widetilde{R}(\partial R_{T})
into a sector from the available
strip-uniform bounds, and (ii) a branch-management argument that is valid in the presence of interior
zeros and possible boundary zeros (handled by indentation as in Appendix
A.7
).

A.7.
Rectangle argument with possible boundary zeros

Lemma A.9

(Rectangle exclusion under sector control)
.

Let
\widetilde{R}
be holomorphic on a neighborhood of
\overline{R_{T}}
and assume that

\widetilde{R}
is nonvanishing on
\partial R_{T}
. If
\widetilde{R}(\partial R_{T})
is contained in some
open sector of angle
<\pi
(in particular, if Hypothesis
1.2
holds on
R_{T}
), then

\widetilde{R}
has no zeros in
R_{T}
.

Proof.

Since
\widetilde{R}
is nonvanishing on
\partial R_{T}
and
\widetilde{R}(\partial R_{T})
lies in a sector
of angle
<\pi
, we may choose a continuous branch of
\arg\widetilde{R}
on
\partial R_{T}
with total
variation strictly less than
\pi
. Hence the winding number of the loop
\widetilde{R}(\partial R_{T})

about the origin is zero. By the argument principle,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial R_{T}}\frac{\widetilde{R}^{\prime}(w)}{\widetilde{R}(w)}\,dw

equals the number of zeros of
\widetilde{R}
in
R_{T}
, counted with multiplicity. The left-hand side is
the winding number and therefore equals
0
, so
\widetilde{R}
has no zeros in
R_{T}
.
∎

We justify the argument-principle computation in Lemma
A.9
when

\widetilde{R}
has zeros on
\partial R_{T}
. Fix
T\geq T_{0}
and suppose
\widetilde{R}
is holomorphic on a
neighborhood of
\overline{R_{T}}
. Since zeros of a nontrivial holomorphic function are isolated, the set

Z_{T}:=\{w\in\partial R_{T}:\ \widetilde{R}(w)=0\}
is finite.

For each
w_{0}\in Z_{T}
, choose
\rho_{w_{0}}>0
so small that the disks
D(w_{0},\rho_{w_{0}})
are pairwise
disjoint and meet
\partial R_{T}
in a single connected arc. For
0<\rho<\min_{w_{0}}\rho_{w_{0}}
, define the
indented contour
\Gamma_{T,\rho}
by replacing each arc
\partial R_{T}\cap D(w_{0},\rho)
with the
corresponding circular arc in
\partial D(w_{0},\rho)
lying inside
R_{T}
.

Then
\widetilde{R}
is nonvanishing on
\Gamma_{T,\rho}
, so the argument principle applies:

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{T,\rho}}\frac{\widetilde{R}^{\prime}(w)}{\widetilde{R}(w)}\,dw=N_{T,\rho},

where
N_{T,\rho}
is the number of zeros of
\widetilde{R}
in the interior of the indented region,
counted with multiplicity. As
\rho\downarrow 0
, the indented region increases to the interior of
R_{T}
.

It remains to show that the contribution from each circular indentation tends to the correct boundary
limit. If
w_{0}
is a zero of order
m
, then locally

\widetilde{R}(w)=(w-w_{0})^{m}h(w)
with
h(w_{0})\neq 0
. Hence on the small circular arc,

\frac{\widetilde{R}^{\prime}(w)}{\widetilde{R}(w)}=\frac{m}{w-w_{0}}+O(1),

and therefore the integral over that arc tends to
m\pi i
if the arc is a semicircle (or the
corresponding fraction of
2\pi im
for other arc angles). In particular, the total change of argument
along
\Gamma_{T,\rho}
differs from that along
\partial R_{T}
by a sum of contributions that converge
as
\rho\downarrow 0
, and the winding-number computation remains valid. Thus letting
\rho\downarrow 0
,
we recover the same conclusion as in the nonvanishing-boundary case.
