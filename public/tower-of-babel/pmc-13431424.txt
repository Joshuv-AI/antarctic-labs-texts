Endovascular Stent-Assisted Intracranial Lysis for Acute Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion in a Young Woman with Primary Sjögren’s Syndrome: Case Report
Authors: Pao-Sheng Yen, Victor C Kok, Shang-Chang Ho, Yu-Hui Lin, Yu-Tzu Wu, Guo-Dung Hung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a systemic autoimmune disease characterized by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, with potential extra-glandular manifestations including central nervous system vasculitis. Ischemic stroke resulting from pSS-associated vasculitis is rare, and large vessel occlusion (LVO) in this setting poses a significant therapeutic challenge. The inflamed vessel wall is inherently fragile, rendering it susceptible to iatrogenic dissection or rupture when subjected to the mechanical forces of standard thrombectomy devices or permanent intracranial stenting. We report a case of pSS presenting with acute ischemic stroke secondary to right M1 large vessel occlusion. Successful revascularization was achieved using the Stent-Assisted Intracranial Lysis (SAIL) technique. We highlight the procedural nuances of employing a temporary stent-retriever scaffold to minimize mechanical trauma to an inflamed vessel wall and demonstrate that this approach may yield favorable functional outcomes even in settings where permanent intracranial stenting is contraindicated.Keywords: Sjögren’s syndrome, acute ischemic stroke, endovascular thrombectomy, EVT, SAIL technique, case report

Introduction

Primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, resulting in xerophthalmia and xerostomia. Beyond glandular involvement, pSS is a systemic disorder with potential multi-organ manifestations.1 Among its most severe extra-glandular complications, cerebral vasculitis represents a rare but recognized condition that may precipitate acute ischemic stroke.2–4 The rarity of cerebral vasculitis in pSS is underscored by the French Assessment of Systemic Signs and Evolution in Sjögren’s syndrome (ASSESS) cohort, in which only 5 of 392 patients (1.28%) had cerebral vasculitis during an approximately 3 years follow-up, highlighting the exceptional nature of vasculitis-related large vessel occlusion in pSS.5 To the best of our knowledge, we report the first case of successful application of the Stent-Assisted Intracranial Lysis (SAIL) technique6 in a patient with acute right M1 large vessel occlusion (LVO) secondary to pSS-associated vasculitis.

EVT has fundamentally transformed the management of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) caused by LVO. Despite high rates of successful recanalization in embolic cases, approximately 10–20% of procedures fail to achieve successful reperfusion. These refractory cases are frequently associated with underlying intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) or rare inflammatory arteriopathies, such as vasculitis.7,8 In these scenarios, traditional thrombectomy maneuvers—which rely on mechanical clot retrieval—often prove ineffective and may induce further vessel wall injury or immediate re-thrombosis.9–11 Furthermore, the pro-thrombotic milieu of an inflamed arterial segment increases the risk of acute in-stent thrombosis if a permanent intracranial implant is deployed. To address these challenges, the Stent-Assisted Intracranial Lysis (SAIL) technique has emerged as a promising bailout strategy. By deploying a stent retriever as a temporary endovascular scaffold, clinicians can establish a “bypass channel” to restore immediate flow while providing a conduit for targeted delivery of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, such as tirofiban. Recent evidence, including the SAIL bridging protocol and findings from the RESCUE BT randomized controlled trial, suggests that this hybrid approach may successfully stabilize unstable lesions and achieve sustained recanalization without the need for permanent intracranial implants.6

Case Description

A 46-year-old female presented to the emergency department with a 6-hour history of acute-onset left-sided hemiparesis (Medical Research Council grade 4) and left central facial palsy. She remained ambulatory upon presentation, with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score of 6. While she could lift her left arm and leg against gravity, her strength was compromised when encountering mild manual resistance from the examiner.

Upon admission, the patient reported a 4-year history of xerophthalmia, xerostomia, and bilateral small joint pain and stiffness of the hands. Serological workup revealed a positive anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern at a titer of 1:640, along with positive anti-SSA and anti-SSB antibodies. Rheumatoid factor and anti-phospholipid antibodies were negative. Anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibody was mildly elevated on initial testing but normalized within 2 months. Complement levels (C3 and C4) remained within normal limits, and no additional features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or other systemic autoimmune disorders were identified (Table 1).

Given the absence of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) eligibility, mechanical thrombectomy was pursued as the primary revascularization strategy. Endovascular thrombectomy was performed under general anesthesia. The onset-to-puncture time was 420 minutes (7 hours), reflecting the delayed presentation following transfer from a non-thrombectomy-capable referring hospital. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) via right internal carotid artery (ICA) injection confirmed complete occlusion of the right distal M1 segment (Figure 2A). As the initial revascularization strategy, a direct aspiration first-pass technique (ADAPT) was attempted using a 6F SOFIA™ Plus aspiration catheter (Terumo Neuro, USA) in conjunction with a 0.027-inch microcatheter (Phenom, Medtronic, USA). Following 90 seconds of sustained mechanical aspiration, the vessel remained occluded with no appreciable thrombus retrieved. Given the clinical suspicion for an underlying vasculitic arteriopathy, the Stent-Assisted Intracranial Lysis (SAIL) technique was thereafter employed.

Following microcatheter access across the lesion to distal M2 (Figure 2B), a 3×20 mm Solitaire™ X (Medtronic, USA) was deployed as a temporary scaffold across the occluded segment (Figure 2C). Intra-arterial tirofiban (Aggrastat, 0.5 mg) was next infused via the microcatheter for over 10 minutes. The stent retriever was subsequently resheathed by gently advancing the microcatheter distally. Post-procedural angiography confirmed complete recanalization of the right distal M1 segment, albeit with residual mild focal stenosis (Figure 2D). The patient was admitted to the ICU following the procedure and was initiated on a maintenance IV infusion of tirofiban at 0.1 μg/kg/min for 24 hours, with bridging to dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) at hour 18 of the infusion. Immediately after the SAIL procedure, her neurological deficit improved significantly to an NIHSS score of 2. Follow-up MRI on postoperative day 1 demonstrated an acute infarction in the right frontotemporal lobe (Figure 3A). MR angiogram (MRA) reveals multifocal segmental stenosis and irregular vessel margins at the left M1 segment and right MCA trifurcation, consistent with a vasculitic process (Figure 3B). The patient was stepped down to the general ward on postoperative day 2, who demonstrated sustaining neurological improvement at NIHSS score of 2. She underwent a comprehensive one-week inpatient rehabilitation and clinical observation course prior to safe discharge, with an NIHSS score of 0 at discharge. Following discharge, the patient entered a structured outpatient observation protocol consisting of weekly clinical visits for the first month, followed by standard longitudinal evaluations every three months. At the 90-day (3-month) follow-up milestone in her recovery journey, a repeat digital subtraction angiography was performed to objectively assess luminal changes and vessel stability. The patient achieved a complete and uneventful neurological recovery with both NIHSS and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores 0. No delayed iatrogenic or systemic complications were noted at any point during her short- or long-term observation windows.

Objective sicca gland evaluation was performed to fulfill the 2016 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for pSS. Schirmer’s test confirmed bilateral lacrimal hyposecretion (OD: 4 mm/5 min; OS: 5 mm/5 min), both falling below the diagnostic threshold of 5 mm/5 min. Salivary gland scintigraphy demonstrated impaired uptake and secretory function of the bilateral parotid and submandibular glands, consistent with sicca-related glandular involvement. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of pSS was established with a classification score of ≥ 4, derived from positive anti-SSA antibody (3 points) and abnormal Schirmer’s test (1 point). Given the organ-threatening nature of the CNS vasculitis, induction immunosuppressive therapy was initiated with high-dose glucocorticoids and azathioprine following confirmed diagnosis, with hydroxychloroquine sulfate (200 mg/day) maintained as background therapy.

Follow-up DSA at 3 months revealed mild segmental stenosis of the right proximal M2 segment (Figure 3C).

Patient’s Perspective

I never imagined that I could have a stroke at such a young age. When my symptoms first appeared, I did not realize they were signs of a stroke. I thought something was temporarily wrong, and I did not understand the seriousness of the situation at first. Before this experience, I was not aware that an autoimmune disease could increase the risk of stroke. Learning about the connection changed my understanding of my illness. Rather than feeling shocked by the diagnosis, I wanted to understand why this had happened and what I could do to prevent it from happening again.

I underwent mechanical thrombectomy, and my strongest memory of the procedure was the numb sensation associated with the treatment. Throughout my hospitalization, I felt that the medical team took very good care of me. Their prompt decisions and professional treatment gave me confidence and reassurance during a frightening time.

This experience has had an impact on my daily life. I have become more aware of my health and have made changes to my lifestyle. I also think more about the possibility of another stroke and the importance of ongoing follow-up and disease control. Looking back, I am grateful for the care I received and for the recovery I have achieved.

Discussion

As previously described, pSS is a systemic autoimmune disease with potential extra-glandular manifestations. Among its most severe complications, vasculitis represents a rare but recognized condition that may precipitate acute ischemic stroke.1–4,12 While Sjögren’s syndrome is traditionally recognized for its sicca symptoms, this case highlights its potential for life-threatening neurovascular complications. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of endovascular thrombolysis for LVO-associated acute ischemic stroke in a patient with pSS. This case underscores the importance of considering autoimmune vasculitis as a potential etiology of LVO in young or middle-aged patients without conventional vascular risk factors at the outset. Recent studies suggest that Asian females represent a particularly susceptible population for large-vessel cerebrovascular involvement in the context of pSS.12 In this case, IVT with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) was withheld as the patient presented beyond the 4.5-hour eligible time window. Nevertheless, the use of EVT in the setting of autoimmune vasculitis represents a complex clinical decision. Although autoimmune vasculitis does not constitute an absolute contraindication to mechanical thrombectomy, it introduces several unique procedural and pathophysiological challenges. Vasculitis-related transmural inflammation and structural weakening of the vessel wall heighten the risk of iatrogenic complications, including vessel perforation or dissection during catheter navigation or stent retriever deployment.7,8 Furthermore, the occlusion in vasculitis may not represent a conventional thromboembolic event but rather arises from inflammatory mural thickening and in situ thrombosis, rendering standard thrombectomy devices potentially less effective, with a risk of rapid re-occlusion in the absence of prompt and adequate immunosuppressive therapy.13 Hua et al reported two cases of AIS in young patients with SLE, a condition sharing overlapping vasculitic mechanisms with pSS, who underwent successful revascularization via EVT combined with IVT.14 While both patients achieved favorable outcomes at 1-year follow-up, case 2 was complicated by subarachnoid hemorrhage following intracranial balloon angioplasty and permanent stent placement for M1 occlusion. This hemorrhagic complication, potentially reflecting increased vascular fragility in the setting of active vasculitis, underscores the technical challenges inherent to EVT and the necessity for individualized intervention strategies in autoimmune-mediated vasculopathy. As demonstrated in the RESCUE BT randomized controlled trial, adjunctive tirofiban in refractory LVO cases was associated with improved functional outcomes, defined as mRS 0–2 at 90 days.6,15 In this case, we successfully leveraged the Stent-Assisted Intracranial Lysis (SAIL) technique as a bailout strategy to achieve complete recanalization in a setting of intracranial vasculitis-related large vessel occlusion (Figure 4). Unlike conventional rescue strategies that rely on permanent intracranial stenting, our approach utilized temporary stent-retriever scaffolding combined with intra-arterial glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibition to restore luminal patency while minimizing mechanical stress on an inflamed and structurally compromised vessel wall. This strategy is particularly relevant in autoimmune vasculopathies, where endothelial dysfunction, transmural inflammation, and fragility markedly increase the risk of iatrogenic dissection, perforation, or acute in-stent thrombosis. The SAIL approach allowed us to establish immediate flow restoration while avoiding the need for permanent implantation and prolonged dual antiplatelet therapy—an important consideration in patients who may subsequently require immunosuppressive treatment. Management of Sjögren’s syndrome is stratified by disease severity: hydroxychloroquine is reserved for mild systemic manifestations, whereas organ-threatening involvement such as CNS vasculitis warrants high-dose glucocorticoids with immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or rituximab, in accordance with EULAR recommendations.16 Accordingly, this patient received high-dose glucocorticoids with azathioprine as induction therapy for the vasculitic manifestation, with hydroxychloroquine continued as background treatment. Our findings align with emerging evidence supporting pharmacologically assisted recanalization strategies in refractory large vessel occlusions, particularly when the underlying pathology is non-atherosclerotic and inflammatory in nature. This case likely represents one of the first documented successful endovascular revascularization procedures using a stent-avoidant strategy in a young patient with pSS-associated intracranial vasculitis, resulting in an excellent functional outcome (mRS 0 at 90 days).

In the emergency and hyperacute stroke setting, several clues should raise suspicion for an underlying inflammatory arteriopathy: (1) young age without traditional vascular risk factors; (2) fluctuating or progressive neurological deficits; (3) angiographic findings of multifocal narrowing, beading, or segmental stenosis; and (4) laboratory evidence of systemic autoimmunity. Prompt recognition is crucial, as standard thrombectomy techniques may be less effective in the absence of a true embolic clot and may increase procedural risk. Current research directions increasingly focus on differentiating inflammatory from non-inflammatory intracranial vasculopathies using high-resolution vessel wall MRI, identifying circulating biomarkers of vascular inflammation, and optimizing tailored endovascular strategies that minimize endothelial injury. In addition, there is growing interest in hybrid approaches—such as SAIL or pharmacologic augmentation with glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors—to stabilize inflamed arterial segments while avoiding permanent implants. Prospective registries and multicenter studies are needed to better define optimal management paradigms in this rare but high-risk population.

Conclusion

Cerebrovascular involvement in pSS is uncommon, with cerebral vasculitis reported in only a small minority of patients. Furthermore, large-vessel occlusion is particularly rare, making the present case noteworthy. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for autoimmune-mediated intracranial vasculopathy in patients presenting with acute ischemic stroke, particularly in younger individuals without conventional risk factors. It underscores the potential hazards of routine stent deployment in inflamed and fragile vessels and emphasizes the value of individualized, pathology-driven intervention strategies. The successful use of the SAIL technique in this context demonstrates that effective recanalization can be achieved while avoiding permanent intracranial implantation and its associated risks. Ultimately, this report advocates for heightened awareness in the acute neurological care setting, careful diagnostic evaluation to prevent overtreatment, and consideration of stent-avoidant techniques such as SAIL as a safer and rational approach for this unique subgroup of patients.

To systematically implement pathology-driven intervention strategies like the SAIL technique in acute settings, a structured, three-tiered clinical action plan is required. First, real-time clinical screening protocols must be optimized to actively flag young or middle-aged stroke patients presenting with LVO who lack traditional atherosclerotic risk factors, ensuring that systemic autoimmune histories (eg, pSS or lupus) and active inflammatory biomarkers are immediately communicated to the neurointerventional team. Second, a standardized intraoperative diagnostic pivot must be established within the angiosuite workflow. If first-line mechanical aspiration (such as ADAPT) fails or a highly resistant, non-thromboembolic clot or a focal, irregular, or stenotic vessel segment is encountered, the intervention neuroradiologist should immediately halt aggressive pulling maneuvers to prevent iatrogenic dissection or rupture of an underlying fragile, inflamed vessel wall. Third, a dedicated low-trauma bailout toolkit must be readily operationalized, substituting high-force extraction with temporary endovascular scaffolding (via a stent retriever) paired with microcatheter-directed, weight-based glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor infusion. By formalizing these steps into institutional acute stroke pathways, multidisciplinary teams can rapidly transition from generic clot retrieval to individualized, low-trauma vascular stabilization, thereby maximizing recanalization rates while safeguarding structural vessel integrity in complex vasculitic pathologies.
