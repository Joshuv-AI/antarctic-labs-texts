Gradient-based Optimisation of Modulation Effects*
Alistair Carson, Alec Wright and Stefan Bilbao

Abstract
Modulation effects such as phasers, flangers and chorus effects are heavily used in conjunction with the electric guitar. Machine learning based emulation of analog modulation units has been investigated in recent years, but most methods have either been limited to one class of effect or suffer from a high computational cost or latency compared to canonical digital implementations. Here, we build on previous work and present a framework for modelling flanger, chorus and phaser effects based on differentiable digital signal processing. The model is trained in the time-frequency domain, but at inference operates in the time-domain, requiring zero latency. We investigate the challenges associated with gradient-based optimisation of such effects, and show that low-frequency weighting of loss functions avoids convergence to local minima when learning delay times. We show that when trained against analog effects units, sound output from the model is in some cases perceptually indistinguishable from the reference, but challenges still remain for effects with long delay times and feedback.

Introduction

Modulation effects including phasing, flanging, chorus and vibrato are widespread in music production and performance
[
2
]
. These effects involve phase modulation of an audio signal controlled by one or more low-frequency oscillators (LFOs)
[
7
]

Phasers typically use a cascade of first-order analog or digital all-pass filters and mix the dry and phase-modulated signals to produce a time-varying filter with a small number of peaks and notches in the spectrum
[
29
]
. The delays in flanger, chorus and vibrato effects are generally longer—on the order of several milliseconds—and implemented with interpolated digital delay lines
[
29
]
, analog bucket-brigade devices
[
26
]
or magnetic tape manipulations
[
1
,
28
]
.

The design and optimisation of digital modulation effects to emulate a reference analog effect unit, such as a guitar pedal, is an example of virtual analog modelling
[
31
]
. Such electronic circuit-based simulations can be accurate and lightweight
[
8
,
14
,
10
,
13
]
but are device specific. Neural network black-box models
[
20
]
offer more generality but at greater computational expense. Lighter-weight neural models have been presented in the form of recurrent neural networks conditioned on the LFO signal
[
34
]
, which must be either manually measured
[
34
]
or whose behaviour must be predicted using another neural network
[
22
]
. In both cases, the models struggled to accurately implement the longer delay times in flanger effects
[
34
,
22
]
. Recurrent neural networks have a strong inductive bias towards distortion and non-linear time-invariant effects
[
33
]
, but their application to modulation effects—that are most often classified as linear time-varying
[
7
]
—is perhaps less suitable.

Differentiable digital signal processing (DDSP)
[
9
,
12
]
offers a potential middle ground that exploits the benefits of automatic differentiation and well-known priors. Previous work
[
6
]
presented a method for optimizing a DDSP phaser topology to model an analog reference device, including a differentiable LFO learned during training
[
6
]
. Lee et al.
[
18
]
extended this method to a general time-varying filter and showed that this could emulate both phaser and flanger effects. Both methods
[
6
,
18
]
, however, operate in the time-frequency domain which imposes a minimum latency at inference. The DDSP phaser model
[
6
]
was later extended to a time-domain implementation
[
36

, but issues with numerical stability made training unpredictable. Other recent related work used a DDSP architecture to extract modulation signals from a target synthesiser sound
[
23
]
.

Here we build on the DDSP phaser model
[
6
]

[
6
,
18

II
Phase estimation with gradient descent

II-A

Estimating delay times

The accurate and robust prediction of time-varying phase shifts is central to the problem of modelling flanger, phaser and chorus effects. Let us first consider a target system that applies a delay of
D\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
samples (not necessarily integer) to a time series
x[n]
. This output is given by the convolution:

y[n]=\sum_{\tau=0}^{N-1}\textrm{sinc}(\tau-D)x[n-\tau],\quad n=0,\dots,N-1

(1)

where
\textrm{sinc}(\eta)=\sin(\pi\eta)/(\pi\eta)
. For integer
D\leq N-N^{\prime}
where
N^{\prime}
is the index of the last non-zero sample in
x[n]
, expression (
1
) reduces to
y[n]=x[n-D]
. If the delay exceeds this, the output signal will be truncated, so sufficient zero padding is essential to avoid this. For non-integer
D
, the sinc function provides ideal band-limited approximation to the fractional delay
[
17
]
. In this case the impulse response has infinite-support and truncation is inevitable, but its effects can be lessend by increasing
N
or using tapered window functions
[
17
]
.

In the frequency domain, the system (
1
) can be approximately expressed as:

Y(k)=\begin{cases}X(k)e^{-j2\pi kD/N},\quad&0\leq k\leq N/2\\
X(k)e^{j2\pi(N-k)D/N},\quad&N/2<k<N\end{cases}

(2)

where
X(k)
is the
N
-length (for even
N
) discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of
x[n]
. The time-domain output can then be taken as the inverse DFT of
Y(k)
, but sufficient zero-padding is again essential here: for
N\geq D+N^{\prime}
and integer
D
, the systems (
1
) and (
2
) are equivalent, but otherwise time-aliasing will occur.

In this work we explore estimating the delay
D
through gradient descent optimisation:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad D=\textrm{arg}\min_{\hat{D}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{D})

(3)

where
\hat{D}
is the optimization variable (the estimated value of the delay) and
\mathcal{L}(\hat{D})
is the objective or loss function. The loss function can be defined either in the time-domain (TD) or frequency-domain (FD):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{TD}}(\hat{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}|y[n]-\hat{y}[n]|^{2}

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{FD}}(\hat{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|Y(k)-\hat{Y}(k)|^{2}

(4b)

where
\hat{y}[n]
is the input signal delayed by
\hat{D}
samples and
\hat{Y}
is its spectrum. As
N\to\infty
(or integer
D\leq N-N^{\prime}
) the above expressions are equivalent due to Parseval’s theorem
[
24
]
. Here we focus on FD optimisation, which has been used in other DDSP applications
[
27
,
19
]
.
Taking only the first half of the DFT, it follows that a loss function and its gradient with respect to
\hat{D}
can be defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\hat{D})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}|X(k)|^{2}\cdot\Gamma(\hat{D},k),

(5a)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\hat{D},k)=1-\cos\left(2\pi k(\hat{D}-D)/{N}\right),

(5b)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{L}(\hat{D})}{d{\hat{D}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}|X(k)|^{2}k\sin\left(2\pi k(\hat{D}-D)/N\right).

(5c)

The loss function (
5a
) and its gradient (
5c
) are oscillatory in
\hat{D}
and the power spectrum
|X(k)|^{2}
acts as a frequency-dependent weighting function. For the special case of a spectrally flat input signal,.
|X(k)|=1
, there is a globally optimal solution at
D=\hat{D}
but the width of the global minimum is extremely narrow, as shown in Fig.
1
. The implication for gradient–based optimisation is that unless the initial delay estimation is within
\approx\pm 1
sample of the target delay
D
, the gradients will guide the optimizer to a local minimum and therefore the incorrect solution. The argument to the loss function summation,
\Gamma(\hat{D},k)
, is visualised in Fig.
1
. Oscillatory local minima are present in both
k
and
\hat{D}
directions, with the convex region
around the global minimum widest for small
k
. We can therefore deduce low-frequency weighting of the loss surface (or using an input signal with a low-passed spectrum) will improve the suitability to gradient-based optimisation.

Fig. 1:
Loss surface
\Gamma(\hat{D},k)
(top) and its mean over bin index
\mathcal{L}(\hat{D})
for a spectrally flat input signal (bottom) for DFT domain estimation of a delay of
D=100
samples.

A simple of choice of a such a signal is a low-pass filter kernel. Fig.
2
shows triangular kernels of varying lengths
N^{\prime}
, their spectra and the resulting loss surface when used as an input signal to the delay optimization system for a DFT length of
N
. The low-frequency weighting of the spectra for
N^{\prime}>1
results in a widening of the convex region around the global minimum to approximately
N^{\prime}
samples, making the loss function more suited to gradient-based optimisation.

We can also consider convolving these kernels with signals that have a flat spectrum to give the same resulting loss surface. Due to the linearity of the delay operator, this filtering could be applied before processing with the delay, or afterwards – in which case the kernel can be viewed as a pre-emphasis filter in the loss function
[
35
]
. We will consider two signals of this type: the impulse response of a cascade of all-pass filters (AP chirp)
[
32
]
; and a chirp with linearly increasing group delay (Lin chirp)
[
5
]
. These have a perfectly flat spectrum and are therefore suitable for estimating the frequency response of LTI systems
[
4
]
. Repeated short frames of these signals have also been used in previous works of modelling time-varying modulation effects
[
15
,
34
,
6
,
36
]
.

Fig. 2:
Triangular kernels of length
N^{\prime}
(top); their
N=256
point DFTs (middle); and the corresponding loss surface as a function of
\hat{D}
for a target delay of
D=100
samples as in Eq. (
5
) (bottom).

II-B

Estimating all-pass coefficients

Suppose now that instead of a linear-phase delay operator, the phase of the input signal is shifted by a cascade of
K
first-order all-pass filter (APF) sections, as found in phaser effects
[
7
]
. Assuming sufficient zero-padding, this can be expressed in the DFT domain as:

\displaystyle Y(k)=X(k)\cdot A_{p}(k)^{K}

(6)

where
A_{p}(k)
is the frequency response of the APF section with pole
p
, given by:

\displaystyle A_{p}(k)=\frac{p-e^{-j2\pi k/N}}{1-pe^{-j2\pi k/N}},\quad 0\leq k\leq N/2.

(7)

The prediction of the pole location
p
based on observation of
Y(k)
can formulated as the optimization:

\displaystyle p=\text{arg}\min_{\hat{p}}\mathcal{L}(\hat{p})

(8)

where
\hat{p}
is the optimization variable and:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\hat{p})=\sum_{k=0}^{N/2}|X(k)|^{2}\left|A_{p}(k)^{K}-A_{\hat{p}}(k)^{K}\right|^{2}.\end{aligned}

(9)

The loss function (
9
) is plotted in Fig.
3
for
K=4
APF sections, two different target pole locations, and two different input signals. Here the loss function is plotted against
1-\hat{p}
, which is approximately proportional to the break frequency of the APF. The loss function shape (not only its shift) depends both on the input signal and the target pole location. In the examples shown, low-frequency weighting does not necessarily improve the loss surface for gradient descent optimization: the local minima are diminished at certain locations but new local minima appear at others. Note however that given the target application of phaser effect modelling (where
K
is typically limited to
4
or
6
APFs), there are fewer ripples overall in the loss function than, for example, when learning a linear-phase delay of several milliseconds in the context of chorus or flanger modelling. Therefore, spectrally flat input signals may indeed be suitable for this application, and indeed previous works indicate that they are
[
6
,
36
]
.

The loss surface as a function of pole location of a cascade of
K=4
first order all-pass filer sections for a spectrally flat input signal (length
N^{\prime}=1
) and a triangular pulse of length
N^{\prime}=128
samples, for a DFT length of
N=256
.

III
Proposed modelling method

This section outlines the proposed methodology for modelling and sound matching digital and analog time-varying modulation audio effects.
The model as shown in Fig.
4
is trained in the time-frequency domain, using a frequency sampling method over short time frames, based on our previous work
[
6
]
. Here however, this framework is used only as a proxy during training, and at inference the learned parameters are employed by a purely time-domain model.

III-A

Frame-based frequency sampling

The model during training is constrained to process signals for fixed length
L
. An input signal of length
L
is first windowed into a set of non-overlapping frames
x_{m}[n],\>n=0,\dots,N-1
, where
N
is the frame length and
m
is the frame index. Here
L
and
N
are fixed to be powers of two so that there are exactly
M=L/N
frames centred at time instants
(m+0.5)\times N/F_{s}
seconds. The FFT of each frame is taken, keeping only the first half of the spectrum to give a set of complex vectors
\textbf{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N/2+1)\times 1}
. The equivalent STFT for the target audio signal
y[n]
is taken to give frames
\textbf{Y}_{m}
, and a linearly spaced frequency vector of length
N/2+1
samples is initialised,
\textbf{z}=[e^{2\pi j\frac{0}{N}},\dots,e^{2\pi j\frac{N/2}{N}}]^{T}
. The proposed model predicts a time-dependent frequency response (but time-invariant over the duration of one frame), which is multiplied with the input frame to produce an approximation to the target STFT at frame
m
:

\hat{\textbf{Y}}^{\textrm{(FS)}}_{m}=\textbf{X}_{m}\odot\textbf{h}_{m}

(10)

where
\textbf{h}_{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{(N/2+1)\times 1}
is the learned frequency response at frame
m
and
\odot
is the Hadamard product. The vector
\mathbf{h}_{m}
is produced by a function with learnable parameters, as detailed in Sec.
III-C
, with the time-varying behaviour controlled by an LFO module.

III-B

LFO generation

The model has a learnable control signal,
c_{m}
, that varies at the frame rate
F_{f}=F_{s}/N
. In our previous work
[
6
,
36
]
, the control signal was a learnable oscillator using the damped sinusoid method
[
11
]
. Here, however, an alternative method is presented in which it is sampled from a learnable look-up table (LUT) of length
M
. The sampled value is then passed through a small multi-layer perceptron (MLP) neural network with scalar input, a single hidden layer of width 16 and
\tanh
activations, and scalar output with linear activation. The control signal is therefore denoted:
c_{m}=\textrm{MLP}(\textrm{LUT}[m])

This method essentially over-fits the model parameters to the training data over a fixed duration and frame rate. At inference, however, this restriction is lifted by first estimating the fundamental frequency
f_{0}
of the learned control signal
c_{m}
from its magnitude spectrum, and extracting a single period (or integer multiple thereof) from the signal. This is then resampled to the audio sample rate
F_{s}
(here using cubic interpolation) and is treated as a wave-table which can be periodically sampled to generate an arbitrary length control signal. This also allows for controllable
f_{0}
at inference through the wave-table read rate. The
M
entries of the LUT are initialised from a normal distribution
\textrm{LUT}[m]\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with mean
\mu=0
and variance
\sigma^{2}=1/(2\pi)
.

III-C

Model frequency response

The proposed model at inference consists of a phase shifting filter, either an interpolated delay line or a cascade of first-order APF sections, arranged in the structure shown in Fig.
4
. During training the model predicts the parameters of a frame-dependent frequency response, given by:

\textbf{h}_{m}=\textbf{h}_{\textrm{SVF1}}\odot\left(b_{0}+b_{1}\textbf{h}_{\textrm{SVF2}}\textbf{s}_{m}\right)\dots{\\
}\odot\begin{cases}\left({1-a_{1}\textbf{s}_{m}}\right)^{-1}\qquad&\textrm{(i)}\\
\left(1-a_{1}\textbf{h}_{\textrm{SVF2}}\textbf{s}_{m}\right)^{-1}\qquad&\textrm{(ii)}\end{cases}

(11)

where
\{b_{0},b_{1},a_{1}\}
are the learnable comb filter coefficients,
\{\textbf{h}_{\textrm{SVF1}},\textbf{h}_{\textrm{SVF2}}\}
are the frame-invariant frequency responses of learnable biquad filters (defined Sec.
III-D
), and
\mathbf{s}_{m}
is the frame-dependent component which has two variants: one for flanger/chorus effects and one for phaser effects. The two cases in (
11
) correspond to the switch positions in Fig.
4
b): filter SVF1 is either outside (i) or inside (ii) the feedback loop.

Flanger/chorus variant
: the frame-dependent component for the flanger/chorus effect is a linear phase delay:

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{m}=\textbf{z}^{-d_{m}},\quad d_{m}=\frac{N}{4}\left(1-\cos(\pi c_{m})\right)

(12)

where
c_{m}
is the MLP output and the sinusoidal activation maps and bounds this to a delay between 0 and
N/2
samples, ensuring causality and that time-aliasing is avoided.
Feedback configuration (i) is used for all flanger/chorus modelling experiments.

Phaser variant
: in this case the vector
\mathbf{s}_{m}
is the frequency response of a cascade of
K
APF sections, given by:

\mathbf{s}_{m}=\left(\left(p_{m}-\textbf{z}^{-1}\right)\odot\left(1-p_{m}\textbf{z}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right)^{K}

(13)

The control signal is mapped to the the predicted pole location of the APFs with the following activation function:

\displaystyle p_{m}=\tanh\left(\pi c_{m}+0.5)\right).

(14)

This ensures the all-pass filter cascade remains stable, and maps the initialisation distribution towards lower break frequencies. By default we use feedback configuration (i) for phaser modelling experiments, but a comparison of the two configurations is given in Sec.
VII
.

III-D

Biquad filters

The frequency response of each biquad filter is given by:

\textbf{h}_{\textrm{SVF}}=\left({\beta}_{0}+{\beta}_{1}\textbf{z}^{-1}+{\beta}_{2}\textbf{z}^{-2}\right)\dots{\\
}\odot\left(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\textbf{z}^{-1}+\alpha_{2}\textbf{z}^{-2}\right)^{-1}.

(15)

Instead of directly optimizing the polynomial coefficients of the filter, we use the state-variable filter (SVF) parameters to express the coefficients as such:

\displaystyle\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=g^{2}m_{\textrm{L}}+gm_{\textrm{B}}+m_{\textrm{H}},\>\>

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}=g^{2}+2R+1

(16)

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=2g^{2}m_{\textrm{L}}-2m_{\textrm{H}},\>\>

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=2g^{2}-2

\displaystyle\beta_{2}

\displaystyle=g^{2}m_{\textrm{L}}-gm_{\textrm{B}}+m_{\textrm{H}},\>\>

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=g^{2}-2R+1,

where the pole angles depend on
g=\tan(\pi f)
for normaliesd frequency
f
in cycles per sample;
R\geq 0
controls the resonance with self-oscillation occurring at
R=0
, and
m_{\textrm{L}},m_{\textrm{B}},m_{\textrm{H}}
are the mixing coefficients
between the low-pass, band-pass and high-pass filters. This form been previously favoured over direct form biquad implementations in other differentiable DSP applications
[
16
,
19
]
. Here we use the FLAMO implementation
[
27
]
, which provides non-negativity constraints on
f
and
R
to ensure stability:

f:=\frac{1}{2(1+e^{-f^{\prime}})},\quad R:=\log(1+e^{R^{\prime}}).

(17)

where
f^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}
are the unconstrained parameters learned during training
[
27
]
. Note that time-aliasing can potentially still occur in our model due to the infinite impulse responses of these filters, but we assume the decay time of the biquad filter will be short relative to the delay or all-pass filters.
The initialisation of the filter parameters had a significant effect on model robustness with respect to different initial seeds.
The parameters were initialised as follows:
m_{\textrm{\lx@text@lbrace H, L, B\lx@text@rbrace}}\sim\mathcal{U}(l,u)
where
\mathcal{U}
is a uniform distribution with lower and upper bounds
l=0.5
,
u=1.5
;
R^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
; and
f^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\pi,1)
. The
-\pi
here shifts the distribution of
f
towards low frequencies.

III-E

Input signals and filters

The model as it appears during training is designed for a specific class of input signals that consist of a series of frames of length
N
, such that the input STFT frames
x_{m}[n]
(see Sec.
III-A
) are identical for all
m
.
Each frame contains a Lin chirp, AP chirp or a triangular kernel (Tri) of length
N^{\prime}
samples followed by
N-N^{\prime}
zeros. For the chirp inputs, the frequency response of the triangular kernel is considered as an optional pre-emphasis filter in the loss function, denoted
\textbf{h}_{\mathcal{L}}
. A naming convention of {input signal} + {pre-emphasis filter} is used to distinguish these different combinations.

The numbers
N
and
N^{\prime}
are key hyperparameters. The frame length,
N
, controls the trade-off between time and frequency resolution, and, for a given
N
, the non-zero signal length
N^{\prime}
controls the trade-off between loss function smoothness, as detailed in Sec.
II-A
, and time-aliasing mitigation both in the model processing and in the target signal measurement. For a target system that implements a delay of
D
samples, the pulse length must be
N^{\prime}<N-D
, otherwise the signal in frame
m
of the input will be delayed into frame
m+1
of the target. Here we set
N^{\prime}=N/2
as we found that this was a suitable trade-off between the competing factors.

III-F

Loss function, training details and validation

The loss function is computed directly in the frequency domain as follows:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}||\textbf{h}_{\mathcal{L}}\odot\left(\textbf{Y}_{m}-\hat{\textbf{Y}}^{\textrm{(FS)}}_{m}\right)||_{2}^{2}}{{\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}||\textbf{h}_{\mathcal{L}}\odot\textbf{Y}_{m}||_{2}^{2}}}

(18)

where
||\cdot||_{2}
is the L2-norm and
\textbf{h}_{\mathcal{L}}

is the frequency response of the optional pre-emphasis filter.
Models were trained for 15k iterations with an Adam optimizer and learning rate of 1e-3. To test the robustness of training with respect to different initialisations, 30 repetitions of each experiment were performed in parallel using multi-thread processing. Validation is conducted on the time-domain model using guitar and bass signals both of length
L
samples as inputs, and the ESR and MRSL are computed relative to the target output. The 95% confidence interval (CI) was computed for a given statistic of the validation metrics as
\pm 0.95\sigma/\sqrt{N_{\textrm{res}}}
where
\sigma
is the standard deviation and
N_{\textrm{res}}=30
. A sample rate of
F_{s}=

44.1\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
and an input signal length of
L=2^{18}
(
5.94\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
) is used throughout. Frame sizes of
N=\{1024,2048,4096\}
(approx. {23, 46, 93}
\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
) are considered, giving
M=\{256,128,64\}
frames respectively.

IV
Toy problem: in-domain parameter recovery

As an initial simplified problem, we consider optimizing the proposed model against two specific parametrizations of itself: a flanger configuration and a phaser configuration. The target audio outputs for both effects are generated through the time-domain model (Fig.
4
b) with the SVFs omitted and
K=6
all-pass filters in the phaser model. The target flanger effect used the sinusoidal LFO shown in Fig.
6
a). The shared break-frequency of the all-pass filters is modulated by an LFO that is sinusoidal in the log-frequency space (as is typical
[
25
]
) to give a pole modulation LFO as shown in Fig.
6
b). For both effects the feed-forward comb coefficients wet/dry mix is set equal (
b_{0}=b_{1}=1
) and the feedback coefficient is set to
a_{1}=0.5
. The starting phases of the LFOs are set to zero for all input signals, including the guitar and bass validation signals, so that there is no phase misalignment when evaluating the model outputs.

In this simplified problem we know there exists a globally optimal solution within the model’s parameter space. Note however that the loss metrics can never be exactly zero, because the target signal is generated with an LFO sampled at
F_{s}
whereas the trained model learns an approximation at the control rate
F_{f}
and then resamples this to
F_{s}
at inference with (non-ideal) interpolation.

The ESR results of the flanger experiment are shown in Fig.

The flanger results show that using the full-band signals (Lin/AP chirp) consistently gives poor results with the median ESR only a few dB below the trivial solution. The results are significantly improved when using the triangular kernel either as the input signal or low-pass pre-emphasis filter, which supports the previous analysis in Sec.

. The overall best results are achieved using a frame size of

The AP chirp signal (with no pre-emphasis filter) has been previously used to successfully train phaser models

BF-2 Flanger

Here the proposed method is used to model the
Boss BF-2 Flanger
pedal. This is a bucket-brigade delay device
[
26
]
with a delay time ranging from
1\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
to
13\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
and an LFO period ranging from
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
to
16\text{\,}\mathrm{s}

[
3
]
. Here we consider two configurations of the pedal with the “Resonance” (feedback) knob set to zero (BF-2-A) and to its maximum (BF-2-B). In both cases Rate=0.5 (half-way), Manual=0 and Depth=1, which generates an LFO with the largest possible bias and amplitude
[
3
]
.

We consider only the {Lin chirp + Tri, AP chirp + Tri, Tri + None} input/filter combinations as these gave the best results in the previous experiment, and the same choices of
N
as before. The training and validation signals (each of length
L=2^{18}
) were concatenated and processed through the pedal to record the target signals. A recording was also taken with the pedal in by-pass mode to compensate for any latency in the digital to analog conversion. Unlike the simplified experiment, the starting phase of the target LFO cannot be reset so will differ for each training and/or validation signal (unless the LFO period happened to be exactly an integer multiple of
L/F_{s}
s). To compensate for this, the
f_{0}
of the learned control signal
c_{m}
is estimated and used to compute its approximate periodic extension to length
2M
. Then the validation loss is computed
M
times for each starting index
m
, choosing
m
such that the validation loss is minimised.

VIII
Perceptual evaluation

The perceived quality of six selected models compared to the analog reference devices was investigated with a MUSHRA-style listening test
[
21
]
. The test consisted of 18 trials using three different guitar excerpts as input signals per effect. Participants were presented with the output from the analog reference, and asked to rate the model output, hidden reference and an anchor signal based on the perceived similarity to the reference. The anchor was the uneffected input signal — we acknowledge that this is a very basic baseline but wanted to keep test complexity low to avoid listener fatigue. Twelve participants took the test, with 10 remaining after standard post-screening
[
21
]
.

The results are shown in Table
II
. For the BF-2-A, SV-1-B, SS-A and SS-B models, there is no statistical significance between the Reference and Model ratings suggesting that the models are indistinguishable from the analog references. The SV-1-C model (the vibrato effect) was rated lower but still within the MUSHRA Exellent band
[
21
]
. The model trained on the flanger with feedback (BF-2-B) gives the worst results, with the mean rating in the Fair band, and this is consistent with the objective evaluation.

IX
Conclusion

This work has investigated the optimisation of phase shifting modulation effects including flangers, chorus and phasers to match digital and analog reference systems. For learning delay times (relevant for chorus and flanger effects), low-frequency weighting of the loss function using either a low-pass kernel input signal or pre-emphasis filter proved essential for model convergence. For cascaded all-pass filter coefficient estimation (relevant to phaser effects), the results were less conclusive and but in this case full-band optimisation yields good results on the analog phaser modelling experiment. A perceptual evaluation showed that the proposed model gave an excellent perceived similarity to analog references in most cases, but devices which exhibit long delay times with feedback proved more challenging to model.
