GradAlign: Gradient-Aligned Data Selection for LLM Reinforcement Learning
Ningyuan Yang, Weihua Du, Weiwei Sun, Sean Welleck, Yiming Yang

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central post-training paradigm for large language models (LLMs), but its performance is highly sensitive to the quality of training problems. This sensitivity stems from the non-stationarity of RL: rollouts are generated by an evolving policy, and learning is shaped by exploration and reward feedback, unlike supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with fixed trajectories. As a result, prior work often relies on manual curation or simple heuristic filters (e.g., accuracy), which can admit incorrect or low-utility problems. We propose GradAlign , a gradient-aligned data selection method for LLM reinforcement learning that uses a small, trusted validation set to prioritize training problems whose policy gradients align with validation gradients, yielding an adaptive curriculum. We evaluate GradAlign across three challenging data regimes: unreliable reward signals, distribution imbalance, and low-utility training corpus, showing that GradAlign consistently outperforms existing baselines, underscoring the importance of directional gradient signals in navigating non-stationary policy optimization and yielding more stable training and improved final performance. We release our implementation at https://github.com/StigLidu/GradAlign .

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become an important tool for enhancing the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) in complex tasks and environments
(
4
;
42
;
32
)
. However, the current success of LLM RL is critically dependent on high-quality human-labeled training corpora
(
12
;
11
)
, which are expensive to obtain. To support large-scale RL training, researchers increasingly turn to massive, automatically collected corpora
(
46
)
. These collections inevitably mix high-quality instances with trivial or misleading ones and noisy labels. This raises two key challenges: (1) how to identify instances that are suitable for a given downstream task, and (2) how to design mechanisms that prevent low-quality or misleading data from corrupting the RL training signal.

Existing RL data selection strategies for LLM training are relatively simple and primarily rely on rule-based heuristics. For example,
44
retains problems with intermediate accuracy to alleviate gradient vanishing, whereas
33
prioritizes samples with accuracy near 50%, analogous to selecting samples near the decision boundary in active learning. Although such partially solved problems can be informative, these methods rely on scalar difficulty signals and do not consider whether the resulting updates move the policy toward improved downstream performance.
Other approaches score training problems using gradient-based criteria, often emphasizing large parameter updates
(
19
)
, internal consistency within the training distribution
(
18
)
, or gradient diversity
(
20
)
. However, these strategies do not explicitly verify alignment with downstream optimization objectives and are typically static, failing to adapt the selection criterion as the policy evolves during RL training.

In this work, we propose
GradAlign
, an automated gradient-informed algorithm to select training problems for LLM reinforcement learning. GradAlign leverages a small, trusted validation set and uses the policy gradient as a first-order surrogate to approximate the expected improvement in validation performance induced by policy updates. For each candidate training problem, we compute an alignment score defined as the cosine similarity between its policy gradient and the aggregated validation gradient. Training problems are then ranked by this alignment score, favoring updates that are most consistent with improving validation performance.

To address RL non-stationarity, GradAlign adopts an adaptive curriculum that periodically recomputes validation gradients and re-scores candidate problems under the current policy, using the validation set as a directional probe rather than a direct scoring or retrieval signal.
GradAlign is particularly effective in challenging data regimes common in large-scale LLM RL, including unreliable reward signals
(
30
)
, distribution mismatch between training and downstream problems
(
14
)
, and datasets with low-utility training instances
(
5
)
. In these settings, scalar accuracy-based heuristics often fail to distinguish useful learning signals from misleading ones, whereas GradAlign consistently prioritizes updates that improve downstream performance.

In summary, our contributions include the following.

•

We propose
GradAlign
, an online RL data-selection method that selects training samples whose updates are aligned with downstream performance improvement.

•

We identify three challenging data regimes for RL data selection and conduct targeted experiments showing that GradAlign consistently outperforms prior baselines in these settings.

3
Problem Formulation

We study an online reinforcement learning (RL) setting in which, at each
data selection round
, the learner is presented with a large pool of candidate training problems that may be noisy, unlabeled, or even incorrect. The learner selects a subset of these problems for training, aiming to filter out low-quality candidates and maximize performance on a downstream evaluation task.

Formally, at round
r
, given the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
, we observe
M
candidate problems
\mathcal{P}_{r}=\{p_{r,1},p_{r,2},\ldots,p_{r,M}\}
, where
M
can be large (e.g.,
M=5120
). The data selector outputs a subset
\mathcal{S}_{r}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{r}
of size
|\mathcal{S}_{r}|=M/q
, where
q>1
controls the selection ratio. The selected set
\mathcal{S}_{r}
is then used to train the policy
\pi
through multiple RL optimization steps. Each problem is associated with a reference answer and a judge model
computes a binary reward indicating whether the generated output matches the reference answer.

Because the candidate pool may be highly noisy, naively training on all candidates can be inefficient and yield limited gains. We therefore assume that each pool contains a (typically small) subset of high-quality problems that are correct and informative for the downstream task, and that selecting such problems can lead to substantial improvements. We evaluate performance on a downstream benchmark split into a validation set
\mathcal{P}_{v}
and a held-out test set
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{v}
contains a limited number of representative or i.i.d. examples of the downstream task. During training, data selection algorithms can access
\mathcal{P}_{v}
to guide data selection, while
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is used only for the final evaluation. Having a validation set is common in real-world tasks, such as using few-shot examples in in-context learning or public test cases in code generation tasks.

3.1
GRPO as the Reinforcement Learning Objective

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
29
)

is a widely adopted reinforcement learning objective
for LLM post-training. In this work, we use GRPO as a unified
training objective across all experiments to isolate the effect
of data selection from differences in RL algorithms.

Given an input problem
x
, the policy
\pi_{\theta}
generates

k
responses
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
, each evaluated by a reward
function producing reward
r_{j}
. GRPO first constructs a normalized
advantage
\hat{A}_{j}
by comparing rewards within the sampled group:

\hat{A}_{j}=\frac{r_{j}-\bar{r}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j}(r_{j}-\bar{r})^{2}}+\epsilon},

where
\bar{r}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}r_{j}
.
GRPO optimizes a clipped policy objective with KL
regularization:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\min\!\left(\rho_{j}(\theta)\hat{A}_{j},\,\text{clip}(\rho_{j}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{j}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right],

where

\rho_{j}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{j}|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{j}|x)},

and
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
denotes the policy used for sampling.
While experiments use the standard GRPO training objective,
for theoretical analysis, we consider a surrogate
\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{\text{GRPO}}

obtained by ignoring clipping and KL regularization terms and
assuming on-policy sampling, yielding

\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\left[\hat{A}_{j}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{j}|x)\right].

Taking gradients yields the policy update

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\hat{A}_{j}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{j}|x)].

This objective provides a stable policy-gradient estimator
without requiring a learned value function and serves as the
shared optimization backbone for all methods compared in this paper.
While we instantiate GradAlign using GRPO, the framework only
requires on-policy gradient estimates and can be readily combined
with other on-policy RL objectives.

4
GradAlign

We propose
GradAlign
, an online data selection method for reinforcement learning. In realistic RL settings, training data is abundant but highly heterogeneous, and reward signals can be noisy or uninformative to learning. Accuracy-based filtering methods are commonly used in prior work
(
44
)
, based on the intuition that LLMs learn most from problems that are neither too easy nor too hard and lie near the decision boundary. However, accuracy alone does not distinguish which problems induce beneficial policy updates. GradAlign addresses this limitation by selecting high-quality samples through gradient alignment.

Our key insight is that a training problem is valuable only if updating the policy on it improves performance on a trustworthy validation set representing the downstream task. We approximate this improvement using first-order policy gradients: GradAlign treats the average policy gradient on the validation set as a target direction and ranks candidate problems by their alignment with this direction. As illustrated in Figure
1
, GradAlign operates iteratively by estimating validation gradients, selecting aligned training problems, and updating the policy, while resampling rollouts in each round to adapt to the evolving policy.

4.1
Validation Improvement as a Proxy

Unlike LLM fine-tuning, where detailed solutions are provided in training data, in RL settings, we only observe reward signals. This makes it difficult to estimate the utility of individual training problems directly.
We therefore use
validation improvement
as a proxy for problem utility: a training problem is useful if updating the policy on it improves performance on the validation set
\mathcal{P}_{v}
, which is more likely to transfer to the held-out test set. A direct approach would be to perform RL updates on each candidate problem and measure the resulting change on
\mathcal{P}_{v}
, but this is computationally prohibitive.

4.2
Validation Improvement via Policy Gradients

The accuracy change is a discrete signal, which makes a precise ranking of candidates for training expensive. We find that the gradient of the on-policy GRPO loss can serve as a surrogate for accuracy change, and, in fact, we have the following theorem:

Theorem 4.1
.

Under on-policy sampling, binary rewards, unbiased advantage estimation
without normalization, and ignoring KL regularization and clipping,
the GRPO gradient is an unbiased estimator of the gradient of expected accuracy.

Proof.

Let
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[R(x,y)]
be the expected reward, where
R(x,y)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[y=y^{*}(x)\right]
is the judge outcome indicating whether
y
matches the reference answer
y^{*}(x)
. Hence,
J(\theta)
equals the expected accuracy.
Under on-policy sampling, the policy gradient theorem
(
34
)
gives

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[Q^{\pi}(x,y)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\right].

Here
Q^{\pi}(x,y)
denotes the action-value function, i.e., the expected
return obtained by taking action
y
under policy
\pi
given input
x
.
Let
A^{\pi}(x,y)=Q^{\pi}(x,y)-b(x)
denote the advantage function,
where
b(x)
is any baseline independent of
y
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[b(x)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y|x)]=0.

Therefore

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[A^{\pi}(x,y)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y|x)\right].

If the advantage estimator
\hat{A}
is unbiased, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}\mid x,y]=A^{\pi}(x,y)
, then

\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\hat{A}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right]=\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta),

showing that the resulting update is an unbiased estimator of the policy gradient.
In particular, GRPO forms its advantage by subtracting the expected return baseline that is
independent of the sampled action under the stated assumptions,
and therefore satisfies this condition.
∎

We therefore approximate the expected validation accuracy change using the gradient of the GRPO objective. Based on Thm
4.1
, under a first-order approximation, the validation improvement induced by a candidate training problem is proportional to the inner product between the policy gradients computed on the validation set and on the candidate.

In real RL training practice, the advantage estimator term
\hat{A}
is normalized for stable training. As stated in the following theorem, the direction of the policy gradient is still aligned with the expected accuracy gradient:

Theorem 4.2
.

Based on
Theorem

4.1
, when advantage normalization is applied, the GRPO gradient becomes biased in magnitude but remains directionally aligned, in expectation, with the expected accuracy gradient.

Proof.

Advantage normalization applies an affine transformation with
a positive scaling factor to the raw advantage estimator

\hat{A}^{\text{raw}}
, producing the normalized advantage

\hat{A}=\frac{\hat{A}^{\text{raw}}-\mu}{\sigma+\epsilon},

where
\mu
and
\sigma
denote the mean and standard deviation
of
\hat{A}^{\text{raw}}
computed over the sampled batch,
and
\sigma+\epsilon>0
.
The quantity
\mu
is constant with respect to individual sampled actions.
This changes the magnitude of the gradient but preserves its direction.
∎

Because gradients are typically normalized in practice, their direction is more important than their magnitude. Therefore, we use cosine similarity as the alignment score in GradAlign instead of the inner product. This choice is further supported by our ablation studies (Section
7
).

4.3
Algorithm Details

In particular, for each problem
p_{i}
in the validation set
\mathcal{P}_{v}=\{p^{v}_{1},...,p^{v}_{|\mathcal{P}_{v}|}\}
, we roll out
k_{v}
samples
o_{i,1},...,o_{i,k_{v}}
with rewards
r_{i,1},...,r_{i,k_{v}}\in\{0,1\}
(correct or not) and compute the normalized advantage

\hat{A}_{i,j}
following the GRPO surrogate objective
defined in Section
3.1
. Then, the gradient
g_{i}
for problem
p_{i}
is calculated by:

\displaystyle g^{v}_{i}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{\tilde{L}}_{\text{GRPO}}(p^{v}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{v}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{v}}\hat{A}_{i,j}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}\!\left(o_{i,j}\mid p^{v}_{i}\right).

Averaging all gradients, we obtain the validation policy gradient
G_{v}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{v}|}\sum_{i=1}g^{v}_{i}
.

In each data selection round, for each candidate training problem
p_{r,i}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}
, similarly, we estimate the GRPO policy gradient
g_{r,i}
using sampled rollouts
k_{r}
. Since the training set is substantially larger than the validation set (
|\mathcal{P}_{r}|\gg|\mathcal{P}_{v}|
), we use fewer rollouts (
k_{r}<k_{v}
) for training candidates, as high-precision gradient estimates are less critical in this case. Finally, we rank the candidate training problems according to the cosine similarity between
g_{r,i}
and
G_{v}
, selecting the top-
|S_{r}|
candidates.

Note that our settings and design differ from previous methods based on the influence function that requires fully trained models and second-order information
(
16
)
. Second-order effects are unnecessary in our setting, as each candidate example is used for only a small number of updates, for which first-order approximations are sufficient.

4.4
On-Policy Resampling

Because the policy evolves during RL training, both the expected validation accuracy and the validation GRPO gradients change over time. To mitigate the bias introduced by stale rollouts in advantage estimation, we resample all validation problems and recompute the average validation gradient at the beginning of each data-selection round.

This design differs from
48
, which estimates gradients using offline rollouts with importance sampling. Although importance sampling can be unbiased, as policy evolves, newly emerging behaviors may not be covered by the offline rollout distribution, preventing reliable estimation of their gradients. Moreover, importance sampling typically incurs high variance when the behavior and target policies diverge, requiring substantially more samples for stable estimation and increasing computational cost.

5
Considerations

5.1
Applicable Scenarios

GradAlign is particularly well-suited for settings where the training rewards are noisy or unreliable, as the gradients are more dispersed. We highlight three common scenarios, with illustrations in Figure
2
.

Unreliable Reward Signals

In many RL settings, the reward signal can be noisy or unreliable, providing an imperfect proxy for true task performance. For example, in LLM RL, rewards are often obtained from model-based judges, and such rewards can be inaccurate on ambiguous problems or inputs beyond the judge’s capabilities. These cases often exhibit intermediate accuracy and are therefore difficult to remove with accuracy-based filtering, which can waste compute and destabilize training. GradAlign is well-suited to this setting: gradients induced by misjudged problems tend to be weakly aligned with the validation gradient (often near-orthogonal in high-dimensional space), so GradAlign naturally downweights them. In Section
6
, we show that GradAlign improves the stability of the training and the final performance under unreliable reward signals.

Distribution Imbalance

When the training data set contains a wide range of problems (e.g., math problems containing different categories
(
2
)
) and the target downstream task is more specific (e.g., one specific puzzle game), many training problems may be correct and of moderate difficulty, but uninformative for the target task, resulting in accuracy-based methods failing in filtering such problems. GradAlign addresses this mismatch by favoring problems whose gradients align with the validation gradient, while downweighting target-irrelevant ones with low alignment.

Low-Utility Training Data

As RL training scales up, practitioners increasingly rely on large web-mined datasets
(
46
;
6
)
. Unlike distribution imbalance, where useful data is underrepresented, the challenge here is that many training problems are on-distribution but overly simple, yielding valid rewards while providing limited learning signal for the target task. GradAlign addresses this setting by prioritizing problems whose gradients align with validation performance and downweighting low-utility samples.

5.2
Computational Overhead

GradAlign introduces additional computations for estimating gradient alignment, but the overhead is modest for three reasons.
First, the validation set is small, so computing the target gradient incurs a negligible cost relative to policy training.
Second, gradient computation for training candidates adds only a constant-factor overhead compared to accuracy-based filtering
(
13
)
.
Finally, by prioritizing more informative training problems, GradAlign improves sample efficiency, reducing the number of ineffective updates and leading to better final performance than training on the full dataset.

6
Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the effectiveness of GradAlign across diverse scenarios. Through these experiments, we aim to verify the capability of GradAlign to select high-quality training data targeting specific domains from a large and noisy training dataset.

6.1
Experimental Setup

RL Framework

All experiments are conducted using GRPO
(
29
)
. We use a sparse binary reward that indicates whether the final answer is correct, and all rewards are provided using Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as a judge. Detailed RL training hyperparameters can be found in Appendix
A
.

Scenario 1: Distinguishability Test with Noisy Rewards

To rigorously assess the selector’s capacity to discriminate valid learning signals from stochastic noise, we establish a controlled experiment involving systematic perturbation of the training set. Utilizing the DAPO training corpus
(
44
)
, we introduce controlled corruptions to 50% of the samples, whereby rewards are decoupled from model outputs and instead sampled from a Bernoulli distribution (
p=0.5
).
This procedure generates a subset of data that statistically mimics the difficulty of “complex” problems (50% pass rate) while devoid of helpful gradient information.

Scenario 2: Unbalanced Dataset

To evaluate alignment capabilities under distributional shift, we construct a heterogeneous dataset comprising 4k Countdown Game problems
(
8
)
, 20k WebInstruct problems, and 10k DAPO problems
(
44
)
. Given that the target domain (Countdown Game) constitutes merely
\approx
10% of the aggregate pool, this setting challenges methods to retrieve domain-specific knowledge from imbalanced distributions. We employ a selection ratio of
q=20
(selecting the top 5%), with validation and test sets consisting exclusively of Countdown Game problems.

Scenario 3: Low-Utility Training Data

In this setting, we utilize the WebInstruct-filtered-unverified dataset
(
24
)
as the training set, which is a large-scale collection (808k problems) gathered from internet sources that represents typical noisy, uncurated training data. The downstream tasks and validation/test splits are specified as follows:

•

MMLU-Pro:

We train Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math-Instruct on WebInstruct. The target domain is the Math category of MMLU-Pro
(
37
)
, which is partitioned into validation and test sets. This setting evaluates GradAlign’s ability to identify training problems that induce policy updates beneficial to multiple-choice mathematical reasoning.

•

SuperGPQA & TheoremQA:

We investigate scenarios where the training distribution is broad but the target domain is narrow. Specifically, we train Qwen3-8B-Base on
WebInstruct
. To improve computational efficiency, we apply a preliminary difficulty filter (pass rates
\in[0.2,0.8]
) before RL selection. We utilize SuperGPQA
(
35
)
and TheoremQA
(
3
)
as the downstream target domains.

•

Mathematical Reasoning Task:

We train Qwen3-8B-Base on the same pre-filtered WebInstruct subset described above. Data selection is guided by AMC22
(
25
)
as the validation set, while performance is evaluated on AMC23 and AIME24/25. This setting tests whether GradAlign can leverage a small, competition-level validation set to extract similarly challenging reasoning problems from a broad and noisy training distribution.

)
: A method that selects data based on gradient similarity within the training set without using external validation guidance.

8
Conclusion

We presented GradAlign, a gradient-aligned data selection approach for LLM reinforcement learning that uses a small validation set to identify training problems whose policy-gradient updates most consistently improve downstream performance. Across multiple challenging regimes, including noisy rewards, distribution imbalance, and low-utility web data, GradAlign produces more stable training and stronger final results than heuristic and prior gradient-similarity baselines, suggesting policy gradient direction as a reliable signal for the attribution of RL data and the construction of the curriculum, which can be broadly used to improve the efficiency of training downstream tasks in LLM reinforcement learning.
