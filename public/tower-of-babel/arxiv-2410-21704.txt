Stochastic Approximation with Unbounded Markovian Noise: A General-Purpose Theorem
Shaan Ul Haque, Siva Theja Maguluri *

Abstract
Motivated by engineering applications such as resource allocation in networks and inventory systems, we consider average-reward Reinforcement Learning with unbounded state space and reward function. Recent work [ 1 ] studied this problem in the actor-critic framework and established finite sample bounds assuming access to a critic with certain error guarantees. We complement their work by studying Temporal Difference (TD) learning with linear function approximation and establishing finite-time bounds with the optimal sample complexity. These results are obtained using the following general-purpose theorem for non-linear Stochastic Approximation (SA). Suppose that one constructs a Lyapunov function for a non-linear SA with certain drift condition. Then, our theorem establishes finite-time bounds when this SA is driven by unbounded Markovian noise under suitable conditions. It serves as a black box tool to generalize sample guarantees on SA from i.i.d. or martingale difference case to potentially unbounded Markovian noise. The generality and the mild assumptions of the setup enables broad applicability of our theorem. We illustrate its power by studying three more systems: (i) We analyze the least mean squares algorithm to solve regression problem for generalized linear models with unbounded and uncountable Markovian noise. (ii) We improve upon the finite-time bounds of Q-learning in [ 2 ] by tightening the error bounds and also allowing for a larger class of behavior policies. (iii) We establish the first ever finite-time bounds for distributed stochastic optimization of high-dimensional smooth strongly convex function using cyclic block coordinate descent.

The main contributions of the paper are as follows.

Finite-Time Convergence Guarantees for SA with Unbounded Markov noise:
TD learning is based on using SA to solve the underlying Bellman equation of the MDP. The aforementioned results on TD learning is obtained by studying a general class of non-linear SA corrupted by unbounded Markovian noise, and establishing the following general-purpose result.

Informal Theorem
.

Consider a nonlinear SA, and suppose that a Lyapunov function satisfying certain drift condition is constructed in the setting when the noise is i.i.d. or martingale difference. Then, we establish finite sample bounds when the same SA is driven by Markovian noise with unbounded state space under appropriate assumptions.

In other words, we decouple the challenge of handling Markovian noise from the issue of analyzing the SA itself.
Our result complements the existing literature by enabling one to generalize any SA result to the case of unbounded Markovian noise. Therefore, we believe that this powerful result is of independent interest due to its applicability in a wide variety of settings.

Performance of TD-Learning in Unbounded State Space and Rewards:
The problem of policy evaluation in MDPs corresponds to the setting of countable state space but unbounded noise, where both the feature vectors and rewards in the algorithm can grow arbitrarily large. We analyze average-reward TD

Due to the challenges in the average-reward setting, to the best of our knowledge, even the asymptotic convergence has not been formally established in the literature. By a careful projection of the iterates to an appropriate subspace, we also establish its almost-sure (a.s.) convergence.

Generalized Linear Model with Markovian Data:
The general setup of our theorem also allows us to consider settings beyond RL. In this instance, we address the fully general case of analyzing unbounded and uncountable Markovian noise in the algorithm. Problem Setting and Main Result

Consider a non-linear operator
\bar{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our objective is to find the solution
x^{*}
to the following equation:

\displaystyle\bar{F}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\mu}[F(x,Y)]=0,

(3.1)

where
Y
represents random noise sampled from a Markov chain with a unique stationary distribution
\mu
, and
F
is a general non-linear operator. The state space of the Markov chain is denoted by
\mathcal{Y}
.

Suppose
\bar{F}(\cdot)
is known, then Eq. (
3.1
) can be solved using the simple fixed-point iteration
x_{k+1}=\bar{F}(x_{k})
. The convergence of this iteration is guaranteed if one can construct a potential function—also known as Lyapunov function in stochastic approximation theory— that strictly decreases over time. However, when the distribution
\mu
is unknown, and thus
\bar{F}(x)
is unknown, we consider solving Eq. (
3.1
) using the stochastic approximation iteration proposed as follows.

Let
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be a Markov process with stationary distribution
\mu
. Then, the algorithm iteratively updates the estimate
x_{k}
by:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(F(x_{k},Y_{k})+M_{k})\right),

(3.2)

where
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence of step-sizes,
\{M_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is a random process representing the additive external noise, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)
is
\ell_{2}
-norm projection of the iterates to set
\mathcal{X}
. The projection on the set
\mathcal{X}
is included for generality, where
\mathcal{X}
can be either a compact set or the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, depending on the context. We emphasize the importance of projection operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}
to get meaningful mean square bounds here. In a recent study
[
34
]
, the authors constructed an SA with unbounded noise that operates without any projection step. It is shown that such an algorithm will converge to the stationary point a.s., however, the mean square error diverges (Proposition 4, Section 3.3,
[
34
]
). Thus, projecting the iterates to a bounded set is not a proof artifact, but rather a technical necessity. We present this example in Section
3.3
and also provide a new proof for the divergence of the mean square error.

We begin by outlining the set of assumptions for Algorithm
3.2
. These assumptions are motivated by practical applications of SA algorithms, such as those in RL and optimization algorithms, which will be studied in Sections
4

7

\|\cdot\|_{c}

Assumption 3.1
.

There exist functions

such that for all

and

satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\|F(x,y)\|_{c}\leq A_{1}(y)\|x-x^{*}\|_{c}+B_{1}(y).

Remark
.

Prior works such as

and

by their upper bounds. However, in contrast, we consider the case of unbounded state space where these functions can possibly be unbounded as well.

Next, we state the assumptions about the Markov process. Let

(3.4)

(d)

However, projecting the iterates is a more realistic solution in such cases, as it does not change the solution provided that the projecting set is taken to be large enough. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that even after projection, handling the noise is substantially challenging, and no previous work handles this.

SA with almost sure convergence but diverging mean square error

In this section, we argue for the necessity of the projection operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}
for the unbounded state space setting. Specifically, we will construct a counterexample where, without the projection, the iterations converge to
x^{*}
almost surely but the mean square error diverges. We again highlight that although the example is taken from
[
34
]
, we use a different proof technique to show divergence. In particular, instead of using a large deviation principle, we present a proof that is based on elementary arguments.

Consider a uniformized DTMC
\{Q_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
constructed from an
M/M/1
queue. The DTMC is defined on the state space
\mathbb{\mathcal{Y}}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. Then, the queue length

evolves according to the following relation:

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}=\max(0,Q_{k}+D_{k+1}).

(3.9)

where
\{D_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables satisfying
\mathbb{P}(D_{k}=1)=\lambda
and
\mathbb{P}(D_{k}=-1)=1-\lambda=\nu
. For simplicity, we will assume that
Q_{0}=0
, i.e., the queue was started with no jobs in the system. It is well known that if

then DTMC

is stable and admits a unique stationary distribution which we denote by
\mu

and define the following one-dimensional linear stochastic approximation:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}\left(\left(Q_{k+1}-\bar{q}-1\right)x_{k}+W_{k}\right)

where

is a i.i.d. sequence of random variables that are also independent of the
\{Q_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
process and the step-size
\alpha_{k}=\alpha/(k+1)^{\xi}
with

and

Note that the Markovian noise in the algorithm is given by
Y_{k}\equiv Q_{k+1}
. Thus, the noisy operator

and the stationary expectation is

. This implies that the root of equation

is simply

. Consider
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
as the Lyapunov function. Then, it is straightforward to verify that all Assumptions

-
3.5

Theorem 3.3
.

The SA recursion defined in Eq. (

(a)

For any

(b)

Remark
.

The two results presented above seem surprising since the mean square error diverges even when the iterates converge almost surely.
However, such seemingly contradictory behavior of mean-square and almost sure convergence is well-known in the literature. A classical example related to the gambling and St. Petersburg paradox involves a product of random variables that exhibits similar behavior, and we present it in Appendix
C.1

A natural question for future research is to obtain finite-time bounds that reconcile both the results in the above theorem. This can be done by establishing a concentration result of the following form. Suppose that we run the SA without projection, then for any

\displaystyle P(\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{c}<f(k,\delta))\geq 1-\delta.

To reconcile both the results in Theorem

has the following property. There exists a threshold

such that,

(i)

is summable, i.e.,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{0}(k)<\infty

(ii)

For any sequence

that is

(iii)

that is

satisfies

In other words, we get a concentration result, where if our high probability guarantee is smaller than

Policy Evaluation for Infinite State Space MDPs

In this section, we consider the infinite-horizon MDP which is specified by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{R},P)
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
is the state space which may be countably infinite,
\mathcal{A}
is the finite action space,
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
is the transition kernel. At each time step
k=0,1,2,\dots
, the agent in state
S_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
selects an action
A_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
sampled from a policy
\pi(\cdot|S_{k})
, receives a reward
\mathcal{R}(S_{k},A_{k})
, and transitions to the next state
S_{k+1}
sampled from
P(\cdot|S_{k},A_{k})
.

Consider the problem of evaluating the performance of a policy
\pi
from data generated by applying
\pi
to the MDP. Let us denote
P_{\pi}(s^{\prime}|s)=\sum_{a\in S}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\pi(a|s)
as the transition probabilities, and
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathcal{R}(s,a)\pi(a|s)
as the average reward for each state. For clarity, we will drop the subscript
\pi
from the notation wherever it is evident. We assume that the policy
\pi
has the following property:

Assumption 4.1
.

The Markov chain generated by policy
\pi
is irreducible, aperiodic and has a unique stationary distribution given by
\mu
. Furthermore, the rewards have a finite fourth moment under
\mu
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\mathcal{R}^{4}(S_{k},A_{k})]=\hat{r}^{2}<\infty,

where
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\cdot]
denotes the stationary expectation. Let
\bar{r}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(s)
. Then, Assumption
4.1
is sufficient for the existence of a differential value function

that satisfies the Bellman/Poisson equation
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}V^{*}=V^{*}

[
80
]
. Here
\mathcal{B}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\pi}V(s)=\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(s)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s)V(s^{\prime})-\bar{r},\penalty\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(4.1)

It should be noted that the average-reward Bellman equation has a unique solution only up to the additive constant. Thus, if there exists a

such that

, for all

, then

is also a solution to the Bellman equation.

Since the state space is infinite, directly estimating
V^{*}
from data samples is intractable. Hence, we will use linear function approximation (LFA) to approximate
V^{*}
. Denote
\psi(s)=(\psi_{1}(s),\psi_{2}(s),\dots,\psi_{d}(s))^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that

for all

into a feature matrix
\Psi

d

corresponds to

\mu(s)
. Then, we have the following assumption on

Assumption 4.2
.

(a)

The columns of matrix
\Psi

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}\psi_{j}(s)=0,\penalty\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}\implies a_{j}=0,\penalty\ 1\leq j\leq d.

\displaystyle\|\psi_{j}\|_{\Lambda}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(s_{i})\psi_{j}^{2}(s_{i})\leq\hat{\psi}^{2}<\infty,\penalty\ 1\leq j\leq d.

For any initial state

and

Remark
.

Note that these assumptions are always true for finite state space. For infinite-state space, they quantify the stability of the Markov chain. Overall, while these assumptions are technical, they are fairly mild in many practical applications of interest. For example, in stable queueing systems with downward drift, the stationary distribution is light-tailed and decreases geometrically fast with queue length. Moreover, one can show rapid convergence of

for these Markov chains

Remark
.

Although, these assumptions imply certain level of mixing in the Markov chain, it remains unclear if one can use the techniques in the existing literature for this setting. This is because these works use the finiteness of the noise to control the growth rate of the iterate by picking small enough step-size. However, this is not possible in the case of unbounded space since the noise can be arbitrarily large.

\mathbb{E}_{y_{0}}[\|T(Y_{k})\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\hat{T}(s_{0},s_{1})
and
\mathbb{E}_{y_{0}}[\|b(Y_{k})\|^{2}_{2}]\leq\hat{b}(s_{0},s_{1})
.

(2)

Define
\bar{F}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Y}\sim\mu}[F(x,\tilde{Y})]
. Then,
\bar{F}(x)
exists and is given by
\bar{F}(x)=\bar{T}x+\bar{b}
.

(3)

There exists a unique
\theta^{*}\in E_{\Psi}^{\perp}
such that
x^{*}=(\bar{r},\theta^{*T})^{T}
solves
\bar{T}x+\bar{b}=0
. Furthermore, it is also one of the solutions to the Projected-Bellman equation
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}^{(\lambda)}(\Psi\theta)=\Psi\theta
.

(b)

There exists a solution to the Poisson equation (
3.3
) for the Markov chain
\mathcal{M}_{Y}
which satisfies Assumption
3.2
with
\hat{A}_{2}^{2}(y_{0})=9\hat{g}(s_{0},s_{1})
and
\hat{B}_{2}^{2}(y_{0})=2(\|x^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+1)\hat{g}(s_{0},s_{1})+8c^{2}_{\alpha}\sqrt{f_{2}(s_{0})+f_{2}(s_{1})}/(1-\rho)^{2}
.

Refer to Appendix
D.2
for proof and the constants
\hat{T}(s_{0},s_{1})
,
\hat{b}(s_{0},s_{1})
, and
\hat{g}(s_{0},s_{1})
.

Finite Sample Bounds for TD
(\lambda)

We pick
\Phi(x_{k}-x^{*})=(\bar{r}_{k}-\bar{r})^{2}+\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
as our Lyapunov function. A key insight in
[
62
]
that established the negative drift was the observation that for any function in the set
\{V|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}V(s)=0,\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}V^{2}(s)=1\}
, there exists a
\Delta>0
such that

\displaystyle V^{T}\Lambda(I-P^{(\lambda)})V\geq\Delta.

However, in general such an inequality is not true for infinite state space, as explained in Appendix
D.7
.

To establish a similar drift condition in our setting, we leverage the fact that the matrix
\Psi
has a finite number of linearly independent columns. This effectively restricts the value function to a finite-dimensional subspace, allowing us to prove the following lemma. For proof please refer to Appendix
D.3
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Under Assumption
4.1
and
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta:=\min_{\theta\in E^{\perp}_{\Psi},\|\theta\|_{2}=1}\theta^{T}\Pi_{2,E^{\perp}_{\Psi}}\Psi^{T}\Lambda(I-P^{(\lambda)})\Psi\theta>0.

Furthermore, when
c_{\alpha}\geq\Delta+\sqrt{\frac{d^{2}\hat{\psi}^{4}}{\Delta^{2}(1-\lambda)^{4}}-\frac{d\hat{\psi}^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}}
, we have
-x^{T}\bar{T}x\geq\frac{\Delta}{2}\|x\|_{2}^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}\times E^{\perp}_{\Psi}
.

Since
\Phi(x)
is
\ell_{2}
-norm squared and has a negative drift, Assumption
3.4
is also verified. With all the assumptions now satisfied, we can apply Theorem
3.1
to get the following sample complexity for TD(
\lambda
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Consider the iterates
\{\theta_{k},\bar{r}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by Algorithm
1
under Assumption
4.1
-
4.3
and
c_{\alpha}\geq\Delta+\sqrt{\frac{d^{2}\hat{\psi}^{4}}{\Delta^{2}(1-\lambda)^{4}}-\frac{d\hat{\psi}^{2}}{(1-\lambda)^{2}}}
.

(a)

When
\alpha_{k}\equiv\alpha\leq 1
, then for all

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\bar{r}_{k+1}-\bar{r})^{2}+\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\varphi_{V,0}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta\alpha k}{2}\right)+3\hat{C}_{V}(s_{0},s_{1})\alpha+\frac{12\hat{C}_{V}(s_{0},s_{1})\alpha}{\Delta}.

(b)

When

,
\alpha>1/\Delta
and

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\bar{r}_{k+1}-\bar{r})^{2}+\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\varphi_{V,0}\left(\frac{K}{k+K}\right)^{\frac{\Delta\alpha}{2}}+\frac{\hat{C}_{V}(s_{0},s_{1})\alpha}{k+K}+\frac{4(6+2\Delta)\hat{C}_{V}(s_{0},s_{1})e\alpha^{2}}{\left(\frac{\Delta\alpha}{2}-1\right)(k+K)}.

Refer to Appendix
D.4
for rate of convergence for the constants

and
\hat{C}_{V}(s_{0},s_{1})
.

Remark
.

It is evident from the above bound that to find a pair
(r,\theta)
such that
\mathbb{E}[|r-\bar{r}|]\leq\epsilon
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\theta-\theta^{*}\|_{2}]\leq\epsilon
, one needs at most
\mathcal{O}\left(1/\epsilon^{2}\right)
number of samples.

Remarkably, one can show the convergence of Algorithm
1
even without using
\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot)
in the final step. Recall that due to projection of the iterates on

, the fixed point
\theta^{*}
is unique. Thus, with an additional assumption on Markov chain, we can apply the general result on SA from
[

]
(Theorem 17, Page 239) to show a.s. convergence.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that in addition to Assumptions
4.1
-

, we have
\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}}[f^{q}_{1}(S_{k})]\leq f_{1}^{q}(s_{0})
for all

. Then, the Algorithm

a.s. converges to
(\bar{r},\theta^{*})
.

Remark
.

Previous works showed a.s. convergence when
E_{\Psi}=\{0\}
since the limit point is unique in this case. However, by utilizing the uniqueness of solution in

and the ease of projection in such a space, we eliminate any such assumptions on

Approximation Error due to LFA

Now we will provide a bound on the approximation error of the limiting weights
\theta^{*}
. In contrast to discounted setting, which admits a unique solution, the average-reward Bellman equation has an infinite number of solutions since any constant function added to one of the solutions also satisfies the Bellman equation. To handle this anomaly, we will slightly modify the definition of error in the following sense. Fix
V^{*}
as the unique solution of Eq. (
4.1
) such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[V^{*}(S)]=0
. Then, the approximation error for any function in the feature space
\Psi\theta
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is obtained by adding a constant
c
to each element of
V^{*}
and choosing the least possible error from this shift. More specifically, with slight abuse of notation, let
e
be an infinite-dimensional vector with all the entries equal to 1. Then, the approximation error of
\theta
is defined as the following:

\displaystyle\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(s_{i})(\psi(s_{i})^{T}\theta-c-V^{*}(s_{i}))^{2}=\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\|\Psi\theta-ce-V^{*}\|^{2}_{\Lambda}=\|\Psi\theta-(\mu^{T}\Psi\theta)e-V^{*}\|^{2}_{\Lambda}.

where for the last inequality we used the fact that the minimum is obtained at
c^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\psi(S)^{T}\theta-V^{*}]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\psi(S)^{T}\theta]
. Note that for any function
x:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have
x-\mu^{T}xe=(I-e\mu^{T})x
. Thus, we can define
\bar{\Psi}=(I-e\mu^{T})\Psi
and write the above equation as

\displaystyle p(\Psi\theta-V^{*}):=\sqrt{\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu(s_{i})(\psi(s_{i})^{T}\theta-c-V^{*}(s_{i}))^{2}}=\|\bar{\Psi}\theta-V^{*}\|_{\Lambda}.

(4.4)

Define
\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}=\bar{\Psi}(\bar{\Psi}^{T}\Lambda\bar{\Psi})^{-1}\bar{\Psi}^{T}\Lambda
. Then, it can be verified that the solution
\theta^{*}\in E_{\Psi}^{\perp}
satisfies
\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}(\mathcal{B}_{\pi}^{(\lambda)}\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*})=\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}

[
61
, Section 4]
.
Since the approximation error is now expressed in terms of
\bar{\Psi}
, and we know that
\theta^{*}
also satisfies the modified Bellman equation with
\bar{\Psi}
as the new feature matrix, we will work with
\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}
instead of
\Pi_{\Lambda,\Psi}
for easier analysis.

Next, recall that in the discounted setting, the contraction property of the Bellman operator plays a crucial role in obtaining the approximation error
[
54
, Lemma 6]
. In contrast, no such discount factor exists in the average-reward case and as a result, we need to use the mixing property of the Markov kernel
P
to get the desired contraction and eventually obtain the upper bound on the approximation error. Recall that the Markov kernel associated with the
\lambda
-weighted projected Bellman equation is given by
P^{(\lambda)}
in Lemma
4.1
. Thus, we will assume the following about the mixing property of
P^{(\lambda)}
.

Assumption 4.4
.

There exists a constant
\nu_{\lambda}
such that for all
\lambda
the following relation holds true

\displaystyle\nu_{\lambda}=1-\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{T}x=0\end{subarray}}\frac{x^{T}\Lambda P^{(\lambda)}x}{x^{T}\Lambda x}>0.

Finally, following the idea in
[
61
]
, one can obtain a much tighter approximation bound by using the following simple trick. Let
\delta\in(0,1]
and define a lazy Markov chain as
P_{\delta}=(1-\delta)I+\delta P
. Define
\mathcal{R}^{(\delta)}_{\pi}(s)=\delta\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then note that
V^{*}
is also the solution to the following auxiliary Bellman equation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(\delta)}_{\pi}V(s)=\mathcal{R}^{(\delta)}_{\pi}(s)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P_{\delta}(s^{\prime}|s)V^{*}(s^{\prime})-\delta\bar{r}=V^{*}(s).

(4.5)

Furthermore, using the linearity of the projection operator, it is easy to show that
\theta^{*}
also satisfies the corresponding projected Bellman equation:

\displaystyle\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{B}^{(\delta)}_{\pi}\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{R}^{(\delta)}_{\pi}(s)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P_{\delta}(s^{\prime}|s)\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}-\delta\bar{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}\left(\delta\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(s)+\delta\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s)\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}-\delta\bar{r}\right)+(1-\delta)\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}\left(\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}\left(\mathcal{B}_{\pi}\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}\right)+(1-\delta)\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}.

To extend the above idea to the
\lambda
-weighted projected Bellman operator, we define
P_{\delta}^{(\lambda)}=(1-\delta)I+\delta P^{(\lambda)}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\pi}^{(\lambda,\delta)}=(1-\delta)+\delta\mathcal{B}_{\pi}^{(\lambda)}
, and use similar steps as before to show that
\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}(\mathcal{B}^{(\lambda,\delta)}_{\pi}\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*})=\bar{\Psi}\theta^{*}
. Clearly, now we can leverage the freedom of choosing
\delta
and get the tightest possible contraction factor by optimizing over it. Specifically, we define the contraction factor as

\displaystyle\gamma_{\lambda}=\inf_{\delta}\sup_{x}\frac{\|\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}(P_{\delta}^{(\lambda)}x)\|_{\Lambda}}{\|x\|_{\Lambda}}=\inf_{\delta}\|\bar{\Pi}_{\Lambda,\Psi}P_{\delta}^{(\lambda)}\|_{\Lambda}.

where
\|\cdot\|_{\Lambda}
is the operator norm. Now, we establish the approximation error for TD
(\lambda)
-Learning with LFA in the following whose proof is given in Appendix
D.5
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\theta^{*}\in E_{\Psi}^{\perp}
be the unique solution to the
\lambda
-weighted projected Bellman equation Eq. (
4.2
). Under Assumptions
4.1
-
4.4
, we have the following:

(a)

For each
\lambda
,
\gamma_{\lambda}\leq\sqrt{1-\nu_{\lambda}/2}<1
.

(b)

The approximation error is upper bounded by

\displaystyle p(\Psi\theta^{*}-V^{*})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma_{\lambda}^{2}}}\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(\Psi\theta-V^{*}).

Part (a) provides an upper bound on the contraction factor in terms of the mixing factor of
P^{(\lambda)}
and ensures that it is strictly less than 1. In part (b), we observe that the upper bound is a multiple of
\inf_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\bar{\Psi}\theta-V^{*}\|_{\Lambda}
which is the least error that can be achieved using the feature matrix
\bar{\Psi}
. Additionally, if the solution
V^{*}
lies in the range space of
\bar{\Psi}
, or in other words if
V^{*}=\Psi\theta+ce
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, then the limiting solution
\theta^{*}
to TD
(\lambda)
algorithm
1
solves the Bellman equation (
4.1
) exactly.

Note that we assumed mixing on
P^{(\lambda)}
which has no physical significance and only emerged in the analysis of our Algorithm
1
. Instead, it is more natural to assume the mixing property on the original underlying Markov kernel
P
and get bounds in terms of
P
. Unfortunately, to the best of our knowledge, there does not exist any closed form relationship between the mixing factor of
P
and
P^{(\lambda)}
even in finite-state setting for a general Markov chain. Nevertheless, for the special case of reversible Markov chains, we can obtain bounds in terms of the absolute spectral gap of
P
.

Special Case of Reversible Markov Chains

Many queueing systems, such as birth-death chains, exhibit rich structural properties including reversibility of the underlying Markov operator
P
. Thus, we will now specialize the results in Theorem
4.3
for the case of reversible Markov chains to give a more interpretable result. Specifically, we will characterize the contraction factor
\gamma_{\lambda}
in terms of the absolute spectral gap of the Markov operator
P
. To this end, we will assume the following:

Assumption 4.4
′
.

The absolute spectral gap of
P
satisfies:

\displaystyle\nu:=1-\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu^{T}x=0\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{x^{T}\Lambda Px}{x^{T}\Lambda x}\right|>0.

Remark
.

It is worth noting the relation between
\rho
in Assumption
4.3
and the absolute spectral gap of
P
for the reversible setting. By
[
84
, Proposition 22.2.8]
for reversible Markov chains, the assumption holds by setting
\rho=1-\nu
.

Now we present the following lemma that characterizes
\gamma_{\lambda}
in terms of
\nu
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Under Assumptions
4.1
-
4.3
and
4.4′
, for each
\lambda
,
\gamma_{\lambda}\leq((1-\lambda)(1-\nu))/(1-(1-\nu)\lambda)
and
\lim_{\lambda\uparrow 1}\gamma_{\lambda}=0
.

Remark
.

Our upper bound on
\gamma_{\lambda}
suggests that larger value of
\lambda
leads to lower approximation error. However, note that the constants
\phi_{V,0}
and
\hat{C}_{V}(s_{0},s_{1})
in Theorem
4.1
scale inversely with respect to
1-\lambda
(see Appendix
D.2
and
D.4
). Thus, when
\lambda
is very close to 1, these constants will become extremely large which might slow down the convergence. In summary, combining Theorem
4.1
and Theorem
4.3
, our bounds suggest choosing a moderate value for
\lambda
to balance the trade-off between faster rate of convergence and smaller approximation error.

Generalized Linear Model with Markovian data

In this section, we will study the statistical learning problem of estimating an unknown parameter
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
observed through the sequence of pairs
\{(z_{k},\phi_{k})\}_{\geq 0}
governed by the following generalized linear model:

\displaystyle z_{k}=g(\phi_{k}^{T}x^{*})+v_{k}

(5.1)

where
\{v_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is additive zero-mean i.i.d. noise such that
\mathbb{E}[v_{k}^{2}]=\sigma_{v}^{(2)}
,
\phi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the Markovian regressor, and
z_{k}
denotes the received signal.
Further,
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a known link/activation function (possibly non-linear) that satisfies the following properties.

Assumption 5.1
.

For all
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, we have:

\displaystyle|g(z)-g(z^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L_{1}|z-z^{\prime}|

(Lipschitz Continuity)

\displaystyle(g(z)-g(z^{\prime}))(z-z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\mu|z-z^{\prime}|^{2}

(Strong Monotonicity)

Furthermore, since
g(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous, it is differentiable almost everywhere by virtue of Rademacher theorem
[
85
]
. We assume that for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists a
L_{2}>0
such that the gradient of
g(\cdot)
, wherever defined, with respect to
\phi
satisfies:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\phi}g(\phi^{T}x_{1})-\nabla_{\phi}g(\phi^{T}x_{2})\|_{2}\leq L_{2}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}.

For notational simplicity, we define
L=\max\{L_{1},L_{2}\}
as the common Lipschitz constant for both
g(\cdot)
and
\nabla_{\phi}g(\cdot)
.

Remark
.

Some of the well-known examples of non-linear functions which satisfy the above assumption include activations functions used in neural networks such as Leaky ReLU, Maxout or the identity function. Other popular functions include ReLU, GeLU, sigmoid, etc. but they do not satisfy the strong monotonicity condition. Specifically, strong monotonicity implies that gradient of the function
g
is bounded below by
\mu>0
, however, the aforementioned functions have vanishing gradients. Nevertheless, we remark that if one can ensure, independent of the setup, that the input to these activation functions is from a bounded set, then the strong monotonicity condition holds and our results can easily be carried to these cases.

We suppose that the sequence of regressors
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is generated according to an auto-regressive process which satisfies following assumption.

Assumption 5.2
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
matrix whose eigenvalues lie strictly inside a unit circle. Then, the Markov process
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is given by:

\displaystyle\phi_{k+1}=M\phi_{k}+w_{k}

where
\{w_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is an i.i.d. sequence of zero-mean noise with
\mathbb{E}[\|w_{1}\|_{2}^{4}]=\sigma_{w}^{(4)}

and
\phi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the initial state.

Remark
.

Note that in contrast to previous work
[
40
]
, we do not impose the bounded support condition for
w_{k}
. As a result,
w_{k}
can even be a noise sample from unbounded distributions, provided the fourth moment is finite such as in the case of Gaussian or exponential distributions. Another related work in this setting is
[
41
]
that studies linear regression with Markovian data. However, their analysis is restricted to the special case where
w_{k}
is Gaussian noise.

Remark
.

The problem of filter design in signal processing can also be modeled using this framework by setting
g(\cdot)
as the identity function
[
33
,
47
]
. In signal processing,
\phi_{k}
denotes the received signal sequence where the dimension of
\phi_{k}
corresponds to the window length,
z_{k}
corresponds to the original signal sent from sender and
x^{*}
is the optimal filter at the receiver’s end which minimizes the mean-square error between original and processed signal.

Note that since the eigenvalues of
M
lie strictly inside the unit circle, there exists constants
D\in[1,\infty)
and
\rho\in(0,1)
such that
\|M^{k}\|_{2}\leq D\rho^{k}

[
86
]
. Using this property in conjunction with the martingale convergence theorem, it is easy to argue that the process
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
converges a.s. to
\phi_{\infty}
that has a unique stationary distribution which we denote by
\mu_{\phi}

[
33
, Part 2, Chapter 2]
. Furthermore, this implies that
\Sigma_{\phi}(k):=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{k}\phi_{k}^{T}]
converges to
\Sigma_{\phi}^{*}\succ 0
which is given by the solution to the following Lyapunov equation

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\phi}^{*}=M\Sigma_{\phi}^{*}M^{T}+\Sigma_{w}

where
\Sigma_{w}=\mathbb{E}[w_{k}w_{k}^{T}]
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a sufficiently large ball around the origin such that
x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then, given the above assumption, we will run the following algorithm to estimate
x^{*}
, also called Least Mean Squares (LMS) in the signal processing literature. In particular, we have the following algorithm

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}\phi_{k}(z_{k}-g(\phi_{k}^{T}x_{k}))\right)

(5.2)

where
\alpha_{k}
is chosen such that Assumption
3.5
is satisfied.

Properties of LMS algorithm

To reformulate the Algorithm (
5.2
) into the framework of Eq. (
3.2
), we define the Markovian noise as
Y_{k}=(\phi_{k},z_{k})
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{Y}=\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the Markov process that takes values in
\mathcal{Y}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. It is easy to argue using Bayes’ Rule and independence of
v_{k}
that if
\phi_{k}\sim\mu_{\phi}
, then
\text{Law}(Y_{k+1})=\text{Law}(Y_{k})
. In other words,
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
has a unique stationary distribution (dependent upon the distribution of
v_{k}
) which we denote by
\mu_{Y}
. Define the operator
F(\cdot,\cdot)
as follows

\displaystyle F(x_{k},Y_{k})=\phi_{k}(z_{k}-g(\phi_{k}^{T}x_{k})).

Then, the algorithm can be written as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{X}}\left(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}F(x_{k},Y_{k})\right).

(5.3)

Using Assumption
5.2
, we have the following proposition to verify the required assumptions on
F(\cdot,\cdot)
and the Markov process
\{Y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
. The proof is provided in Appendix
E.1
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Under Assumption
5.2
, the LMS algorithm satisfies the following:

(a)

The operator
F(x_{k},Y_{k})
defined in Eq. (
5.3
) has the following properties:

(1)

\|F(x_{k},Y_{k})\|_{2}\leq A_{1}(Y_{k})\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{2}+B_{1}(Y_{k})
, where
\hat{A}_{1}^{2}(y_{0})=L^{2}D^{4}(\|\phi_{0}\|_{2}^{4}+\sigma_{w}^{(4)})/(1-\rho)^{4}
and
\hat{B}_{1}^{2}(y_{0})=D^{2}\sigma_{v}^{(2)}\sqrt{(\|\phi_{0}\|_{2}^{4}+\sigma_{w}^{(4)})}/(1-\rho)^{2}
.

(2)

Define
\bar{F}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Y}\sim\mu_{Y}}[F(x,\tilde{Y})]
. Then,
\bar{F}(x)
exists and is given by
\bar{F}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Y}\sim\mu_{Y}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(g(\tilde{\phi}^{T}x)-g(\tilde{\phi}^{T}x^{*}))\right]
. Furthermore, let
\lambda_{min}^{\phi}
be the smallest eigenvalue of
\Sigma^{*}_{\phi}
, then
\langle x-x^{*},\bar{F}(x)\rangle<-\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi}\|x-x^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
.

(b)

There exists a solution to the Poisson equation (
3.3
) for the Markov chain
\mathcal{M}_{Y}
which satisfies Assumption
3.2
with
\hat{A}_{2}^{2}(y_{0})=32L^{2}D^{8}\left(\|\phi_{0}\|_{2}^{4}+\sqrt{\sigma_{w}^{(4)}}(\|\phi_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{(4)}\right)/(1-\rho)^{10}
and
\hat{B}_{2}^{2}(y_{0})=\hat{B}_{1}^{2}(y_{0})
.

Finite Sample Bounds for LMS algorithm

We again choose
\Phi(x-x^{*})=\|x-x^{*}\|_{2}^{2}/2
as our Lyapunov function. Then, using Proposition
5.1
, it is straightforward to see that
\eta=\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi}
and
L_{s}=1
. Now, we apply Theorem
3.2
to LMS to obtain the following finite-time sample complexity.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the iterates
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
generated by iteration (
5.2
) under Assumption
5.2
.

(a)

When

, then for all
k\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}

\displaystyle-x^{*}\|_{2}^{2}]\leq\varphi_{L,0}\exp\left(-\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi}\alpha k\right)+3\hat{C}_{L}(z_{0},\phi_{0})\alpha+\frac{6\hat{C}_{L}(z_{0},\phi_{0})\alpha}{\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi}}.

(b)

When
\xi=1
,
\alpha>1/(\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi})
and

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38109pt\mathbb{E}[\|x_{k+1}-x^{*}\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\varphi_{L,0}\left(\frac{K}{k+K}\right)^{\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi}\alpha}+\frac{\hat{C}_{L}(z_{0},\phi_{0})\alpha}{k+K}+\frac{4(6+4\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi})\hat{C}_{L}(z_{0},\phi_{0})e\alpha^{2}}{\left(\mu\lambda_{min}^{\phi}\alpha-1\right)(k+K)}.

Refer to Appendix
E.2
for the constants

Our bounds extend the performance analysis of the LMS algorithm in two notable ways compared to

Control Problem in Reinforcement Learning:
Q
-learning

In this section, we will consider the control problem in discounted-reward RL for finite state space MDPs. We will carry the same notation for MDP parameters as introduced in Section
4
for this section. The goal here is to maximize the expected cumulative discounted-reward. More formally, let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be the discount factor and
\pi
be a policy, define the state value function

as:

\displaystyle V_{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\mathcal{R}(S_{k},A_{k})|S_{0}=s\right]

Then the objective of the control problem is to directly find an optimal policy

such that

and any policy

. It can be shown that, under mild conditions, such a policy always exists
[
18
]
.

Q
-learning
[
87
]
is one of the most popular algorithms for finding the optimal policy by running the following iteration:

\displaystyle Q_{k+1}(s,a)=Q_{k}(s,a)+\alpha_{k}\mathbbm{1}\{S_{k}=s,A_{k}=a\}\left(\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q_{k}(S_{k+1},a^{\prime})-Q_{k}(s,a)\right).

(6.1)

where
\{(S_{k},A_{k})\}_{k\geq 0}
is a sample trajectory collected using a suitable behavior policy

and

is the indicator function.
It can be shown that the Algorithm (

which is the unique fixed point of the Bellman optimality operator

defined by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(Q)=\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}).

Since
Q^{*}
and the optimal policy

satisfy the following relation:
\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)\in\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}Q^{*}(s,a)

[
9
]

directly related to finding the optimal policy.
We make the following standard assumption on the Markov chain generated by

Assumption 6.1
.

The behavior policy

for all

and the Markov chain

Remark
.

The condition that

for all

and the irreducibility of the induced Markov chain

is a standard assumption which ensures that all state action pairs are visited infinitely often

9

implies that there exists a unique stationary distribution, which we denote as

Remark
.

Recent works on the finite-time analysis of

-learning often leverage the geometric mixing of Markov chain to handle Markovian noise
[

15

2

17

Application in Optimization: Cyclic Block Coordinate Descent

Consider an optimization problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
where the objective function
f(x)
is
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth. Denote
x^{*}
as the unique minimizer of
f(x)
. We assume that any vector
x
can be partitioned into
p
blocks as follows:

\displaystyle x=(x(1),x(2),\ldots,x(p)),

where
x(i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
with
d_{i}\geq 1
for all
1\leq i\leq p
and satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{p}d_{i}=d
. Furthermore,
\nabla_{i}f(x)
denotes the partial derivatives with respect to the
i
-th block. Suppose that we have access to the partial gradients only through a noisy oracle which for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and block
i
returns
\nabla_{i}f(x)+w
. Here
w
represents the noise with appropriate dimension which satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{x_{i},w_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq k-1}\cup\{x_{k}\}
. Then, there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}\geq 0
such that for all
k\geq 0
: (a)
\mathbb{E}[w_{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}]=0
, (b)
\|w_{k}\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\|x_{k}-x^{*}\|_{2}+C_{2}
.

Assumption
7.1
is a standard assumption in optimization and basically implies that
w_{k}
is a martingale difference sequence with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and grows linearly with the iterates. Then, we have Algorithm
2
to estimate
x^{*}
.

Algorithm 2

Stochastic Cyclic Block Coordinate Descent (SCBCD)

Initialize
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and step-size
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.

for

k=0,1,\dots

do

Set
i(k)=k\ \mathrm{mod}\ p+1

x_{k+1}(j)=\begin{cases}x_{k}(j)+\alpha_{k}(-\nabla_{j}f(x_{k})+w_{k}),\penalty\ \text{if $j=i(k)$}\\
x_{k}(j),\penalty\ \text{otherwise}\end{cases}

end for

Without loss of generality, we assume that at
k=0
we update the first block. At each time step
k
, we cyclically update a block, where the block index
i(k)
is determined through the modulo function. The oracle provides a noise gradient
-\nabla_{j}f(x_{k})+w_{k}
and the block corresponding to
i(k)
gets updated while the rest of the blocks remain unchanged.

Properties of SCBCD

To fit Algorithm
2
in the framework of (
3.2
), we will set up some notation. Define the matrices
U_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{i}},\penalty\ 1\leq i\leq p
that satisfy

\displaystyle\left(U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{p}\right)=I_{d}.

Note that
x(i)=U_{i}^{T}x
for any vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and similarly the partial derivatives with respect to the
i
-th block can be written as
\nabla_{i}f(x_{k})=U_{i}^{T}\nabla f(x_{k})
.
Thus, we rewrite the update equation as follows:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(-U_{i(k)}\nabla_{i(k)}f(x_{k})+U_{i(k)}w_{k})

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}(F(x_{k},i(k))+M_{k})

(7.1)

where
F(x_{k},i(k))=-U_{i(k)}\nabla_{i(k)}f(x_{k})
and
M_{k}=U_{i(k)}w_{k}
. Observe that
\mathcal{M}_{U}=\{i(k)\}_{k\geq 0}
can be viewed as a periodic Markov chain defined on the state space
\mathcal{S}=\{1,2,\dots,p\}
with transition probabilities given as
P(i\ \mathrm{mod}\ p+1|i)=1
,
\penalty\ \forall i\in\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore, it is easy to verify that
\mu(i)=1/p,\penalty\ \forall i\in\mathcal{S}
is the unique stationary distribution for this Markov chain. This implies
\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mu}\left[U_{i}\nabla_{i}f(x)\right]=\nabla f(x)/p
. Thus, solving for
\nabla f(x)=0
is equivalent to finding the root of
\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\mu}\left[U_{i}\nabla_{i}f(x)\right]=0
. It is now easy to verify from Eq. (
7.1
) that all the Assumptions
3.1
-
3.2
are satisfied as summarized in the following proposition. We provide the proof in Appendix
G.1

Proposition 7.1
.

The SCBCD algorithm has the following properties:

(a)

The operator
F(x,i)
, satisfies:
\|F(x,i)\|_{2}\leq L\|x-x^{*}\|_{2},\penalty\ \forall i\in\mathcal{S}
.

(b)

There exists a solution to the Poisson equation (

) for the Markov chain
\mathcal{M}_{U}
which satisfies Assumption

with

and

.

(c)

The noise sequence

.

Conclusion

In this work, we established a general-purpose theorem to obtain performance bounds for a general class of non-linear SA corrupted with unbounded Markovian noise. We handled the Markovian noise by using the solution of Poisson’s equation to decompose it into a martingale difference term and some other higher order manageable terms. This enabled us to extend the convergence analysis of SA with martingale difference or i.i.d. noise to Markovian noise. To illustrate the power of our theorem, we studied four different settings: TD learning with eligibility traces for policy evaluation in infinite state space MDPs, generalized linear regression with Markovian data,
Q
-Learning for discounted-reward RL in finite state settings, and Stochastic CBCD for stochastic distributed optimization.

Some interesting future directions are proving some concentration results without projecting the iterates for SA with unbounded Markovian noise. In CBCD, potential natural extensions could involve using the periodic Markov chain perspective to study non-smooth functions, exploring SCBCD with block dependent step-size, and reducing
p
dependence in specialized cases.

Expectation vs. Almost Sure Behavior

This example is inspired by Exercise 5.6 in
[
91
]
and St. Petersburg Paradox. Suppose that a gambler has initial wealth
W_{0}
and finds a casino where the probability of winning the game is
p=0.6
. The strategy he adopts is as follows: for each round, he bets
\alpha=50\%
of his wealth, and if he wins, he doubles his bet for the next round. He decides to keep betting until he goes bankrupt.

Denote
X_{i}
as the return in the
i
th round, then the wealth
W_{k}
at the end of round
k
is given by
W_{k}=W_{0}\Pi_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
. Note that for all
i\geq 1

\displaystyle X_{i}=\begin{cases}1+\alpha\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{w.p.}\penalty\ p\\
1-\alpha\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{w.p.}\penalty\ 1-p\end{cases}

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=p(1+\alpha)+(1-\alpha)(1-p)

\displaystyle=0.6\cdot 1.5+0.4\cdot 0.5=1.1

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}[W_{k}]

\displaystyle=W_{0}\Pi_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]

\displaystyle=W_{0}(1.1)^{k}.

Thus,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[W_{k}]=\infty
.

On the other hand, let us consider
\log W_{k}
.

\displaystyle\log\frac{W_{k}}{W_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log X_{i}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{1}{k}\log\frac{W_{k}}{W_{0}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log X_{i}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[|\log X_{i}|]<\infty
, using strong law of large numbers, we get

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log X_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[\log X_{1}]

\displaystyle=p\log(1+\alpha)+(1-p)\log(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle\approx-0.05.

The above implies
\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{k}\log\frac{W_{k}}{W_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{=}}0.6\log 1.5+0.4\log 0.5<0
. In other words,
W_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}0
. Hence, the gambler’s wealth blows up to infinity in expectation, while almost surely he goes bankrupt. This behavior is identical to the counterexample presented in Section
3.3
.

Concentration bound

Nevertheless, for this simple setting, we can still upper bound the expected wealth conditioned on a high probability set as follows. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}
be indicator random variables that denote the event of success in round
i
. Then, we can re-write
X_{i}=(1+\alpha)^{\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}}(1-\alpha)^{1-\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}}
and therefore, the wealth
W_{k}
is given by

\displaystyle W_{k}

\displaystyle=W_{0}(1+\alpha)^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}}(1-\alpha)^{k-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}}

\displaystyle=W_{0}\left((1+\alpha)^{p}(1-\alpha)^{1-p}\right)^{k}(1+\alpha)^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}-p)}(1-\alpha)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}-p)}

\displaystyle=W_{0}\rho^{k}(1+\alpha)^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}-p)}(1-\alpha)^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}-p)}

where
\rho=(1+\alpha)^{p}(1-\alpha)^{1-p}<1
. Note that
\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}
are i.i.d. random variables. Thus, for any
\delta^{\prime}>0
, Hoeffding’s inequality leads us to

\displaystyle P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{i}}-p)\geq\delta^{\prime}k\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}k}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow P\left(W_{k}\geq W_{0}\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\delta^{\prime}k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-2{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}k}.

Let
\delta=e^{-2{\delta^{\prime}}^{2}k}
. Then, the above concentration can be translated into

\displaystyle P\left(W_{k}<W_{0}\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta.

Note that
\rho<1
and
\lim_{\xi\to 0}((1+\alpha)/(1-\alpha))^{\xi}=1
. Thus, there exists a constant
\xi_{0}>0
such that
\rho((1+\alpha)/(1-\alpha))^{\xi}<1
for all
\xi\in(0,\xi_{0})
. If we set
\delta_{0}(k)=\exp(-2k\xi_{0}^{2})
, then for
\delta_{1}(k)=\omega(\delta_{0}(k))
, we get

\displaystyle P\left(W_{k}<W_{0}\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{1}(k)}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta_{1}(k).

By the definition of
\omega(\cdot)
, there exists a function
g_{1}(k)
such that
\delta_{1}(k)=g_{1}(k)\delta_{0}(k)
with
g_{1}(k)\to\infty
. Using the upper bound
\sqrt{1+x}\leq 1+x/2
for all
x\geq-1
, we get

\displaystyle\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{1}(k)}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{k^{2}\xi_{0}^{2}+\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{g_{1}(k)}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{k\xi_{0}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2k\xi_{0}^{2}}\log\left(g_{1}(k)\right)}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{k\xi_{0}-\frac{1}{4\xi_{0}}\log\left(g_{1}(k)\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\infty}}{{\to}}0.

Similarly, for
\delta_{2}(k)=o(\delta_{0}(k))
, we get

\displaystyle P\left(W_{k}\geq W_{0}\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{2}(k)}\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{2}(k).

By the definition of
o(\cdot)
, there exists a function
g_{2}(k)
such that
\delta_{2}(k)=g_{2}(k)\delta_{0}(k)
with
g_{2}(k)\to 0
. Using the lower bound
\sqrt{1+x}\geq 1+x/4
for all
x\in[0,1]
, we get

\displaystyle\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{2}(k)}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{\sqrt{k^{2}\xi_{0}^{2}+\frac{k}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{g_{2}(k)}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{k\xi_{0}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2k\xi_{0}^{2}}\log\left(g_{2}(k)\right)}}

\displaystyle\geq\rho^{k}\left(\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\right)^{k\xi_{0}-\frac{1}{8\xi_{0}}\log\left(g_{2}(k)\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\to\infty}}{{\to}}\infty.

Comparison with a simpler approach to show divergence

Now we discuss a “potentially simpler method” for proving divergence and show why it fails in doing so, thus highlighting the significance of our method. Previously, we considered the set
\{D_{l}=1\}
for

for a large enough

within the time window. Instead, pick any arbitrary constant

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x_{k}^{2}]

Challenges in the infinite state space

Consider a birth-death chain. Let the state space be given as
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
with the transition kernel
P(s_{i+1}|s_{i})=p
and
P(s_{i-1}|s_{i})=1-p
, where
p<1/2
. Furthermore,
P(s_{0}|s_{0})=1-p
. It is well known that for
p<1/2
, this chain is positive recurrent and the stationary distribution is given by
\mu(s_{i})=\frac{(1-2p)p^{i}}{(1-p)^{i+1}}
. For simplicity, we will consider the setting when
\lambda=0
, which implies
P^{(0)}=P
. Consider a sequence of functions
\{V_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
in the set
\{V|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}V(s)=0,\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}V^{2}(s)=1\}
that satisfy the following:

\displaystyle V_{j}(s_{i})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\penalty\ \penalty\ i=j\\
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\penalty\ \penalty\ i=j+1\\
0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then, we have the following:

\displaystyle V_{j}^{T}\Lambda(I-P)V_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[(V_{j}(S_{k+1})-V_{j}(S_{k}))^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mu(s_{j-1})p\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}+\mu(s_{j})p\left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}+\mu(s_{j+1})(1-p)\left(\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}+\mu(s_{j+2})(1-p)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-2p)p^{j}}{(1-p)^{j}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{4(1-2p)p}{(1-p)}+\frac{(1-2p)p^{2}}{2(1-p)^{2}}\right).

Note that
p<1/2
, therefore
\lim_{j\to\infty}V_{j}^{T}\Lambda(P-I)V_{j}\to 0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\inf_{V}V^{T}\Lambda(I-P)V=0.

Observe that in the above example the vector has infinite dimension, thus the point at which the function value has a variation for the first time can drift to infinity. However, by using linear function approximation with a finite number of columns, we are essentially restricting the function in a finite-dimensional setting. More concretely, in Lemma
D.3
we establish that for any function given by a linear combination of columns of
\Psi
