The influence of model orientation on achieved accuracy in multi jet fusion additive manufacturing
Authors: Alžbeta Briliaková, Richard Joch, Mário Drbúl, Jaromír Markovič, Jakub Měsíček, Dominik Krišák, Andrej Czán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study analyses the effect of model orientation on the geometric and mechanical properties of PA12 parts produced by Multi Jet Fusion (MJF). Model orientation represents a critical factor affecting dimensional accuracy and overall print quality in additive manufacturing. In this experimental study, samples of identical geometry were manufactured with variations limited solely to their orientation along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes. Circularity, diameter deviation, and maximum tensile stress were evaluated. The results demonstrate that sample orientation significantly influences the dimensional accuracy of the manufactured parts. The most substantial deviations were observed for rotations about the X- and Y-axes. In contrast, orientation along the Z-axis exhibited only a minimal influence on the monitored properties. Mechanical testing confirmed stable tensile strength values, with no significant differences detected among the various orientations. The obtained data were used to develop a predictive model for estimating the geometric accuracy of a part as a function of its orientation within the build chamber. This model serves as a practical tool for designing and optimizing parts produced by MJF technology. Furthermore, it enables the selection of the optimal orientation to achieve the required accuracy and quality.Keywords: Multi jet fusion, Orientation, Additive manufacturing, Dimensional properties, RoundnessSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Additive manufacturing (AM), commonly referred to as 3D printing, represents a transformative approach in production, characterized by the layer-by-layer fabrication of three-dimensional objects1. This innovative approach differs from conventional subtractive methods, offering greater design freedom, reduced material waste, and the capability to produce complex geometries with intricate internal features2. Additive manufacturing has gained increasing popularity because it enables rapid prototyping, the production of customized products, and the fabrication of geometrically complex parts without the need for molds or extensive pre-production processes. Consequently, it drives innovation across a broad range of industrial sectors3,4.

Among AM technologies, Multi Jet Fusion (MJF) is distinguished by its layer-by-layer production process. Unlike laser- or filament-based techniques, MJF employs ink arrays to selectively apply fusing and detailing agents onto a bed of powdered material5. The treated areas are then exposed to infrared radiation, which fuses the material and stabilizes the desired geometry. This process is repeated until the entire part is formed, producing parts with exceptional dimensional accuracy, high density, and isotropic mechanical properties6. Developed by HP and commercially available since 2016, MJF operates on a powder bed fusion (PBF) principle, combining selective material deposition with thermal energy to enable high-speed production7,8.

In a typical MJF process, a layer of polymer powder is spread across the build platform, followed by selective application of fusing and detailing agents. These agents facilitate targeted melting via thermal energy, primarily infrared radiation9,10, allowing the production of complex geometries with superior surface finish compared to other AM methods, such as selective laser sintering (SLS)11,12. MJF improves part performance while supporting rapid, cost-effective prototyping and small-scale production, making it an economically viable solution for a wide range of applications. Studies indicate notable advantages in tensile performance and surface smoothness, particularly for polyamide 12 (PA12)11,13, while higher productivity is a key factor in industrial adoption14. Ongoing development of multi-material capabilities further expands potential applications and future innovations15.

Extensive research has explored the relationships between processing parameters and mechanical properties, including the effects of cooling rate and layer orientation on dimensional accuracy and surface quality16–18. Such insights are critical for optimizing print settings and material selection, ultimately enhancing part functionality and performance in real-world applications9,19. Dimensional accuracy, surface finish, and mechanical strength are key indicators of suitability for specific applications20, as achieving required accuracy ensures parts meet functional demands, fit accurately within assemblies, and operate reliably under intended service conditions21,22.

Part orientation during MJF printing strongly affects final accuracy, surface finish, and mechanical performance23,24. Strategic orientation can optimize support structure use, minimize material consumption, and improve overall printing efficiency. Orientation also influences thermal gradients during printing, affecting residual stress and potential deformation25. Despite its significance, systematic studies quantifying these effects remain limited.

This study addresses this gap by investigating the impact of different orientations of PA12 samples within the MJF build chamber on their geometric and mechanical properties. By systematically varying sample orientation, the influence on dimensional accuracy, surface finish, and mechanical performance is quantitatively evaluated. Furthermore, a predictive model is developed to estimate dimensional accuracy based on orientation, providing a practical tool for optimizing part design and ensuring desired quality in MJF production. Integrating design-for-additive-manufacturing principles is essential for design optimization, accommodating geometric constraints, and reducing production costs related to energy consumption, build time, and post-processing26.

Despite the widespread industrial adoption of Multi Jet Fusion technology, a significant research gap persists regarding the simultaneous interaction of multi-axial orientations on dimensional stability. Current literature predominantly focuses on single-factor experiments, which fail to account for the non-linear thermal and fluid-dynamic interactions of the printing agents during complex spatial rotations. This study addresses this limitation by investigating the synergistic effects of simultaneous X, Y, and Z rotations using a Taguchi L25 experimental design. The motivation is to provide a robust predictive framework that replaces empirical best-practices with a statistically validated methodology, enabling a more rigorous approach to Design for Additive Manufacturing (DfAM).

Role of model orientation

Part orientation in additive manufacturing is critical for optimizing mechanical performance and structural integrity. MJF employs a powder bed fusion technique in which each layer is selectively melted using a fusing agent activated by infrared radiation. While this mature technology benefits from precise process control, it may exhibit anisotropic behaviour that is strongly influenced by the orientation of the printed part during production8,9. Part orientation affects not only the mechanical strength of the final part but also thermal behaviour and overall dimensional stability.

Studies have shown that build orientation plays an important role in determining the mechanical properties of additively manufactured parts. Tensile strength is orientation-dependent, and parts manufactured at different angles exhibit distinct performance characteristics. For example, parts printed at a 45° orientation often show improved resistance to external loading compared to those printed at 90°, which may exhibit increased brittleness27,28. The anisotropic behaviour of MJF-manufactured parts arises from layer-by-layer material deposition and bonding mechanisms, resulting in predictable, orientation-dependent strength patterns29.

Additionally, the quality of printed parts may depend on cooling rates associated with different orientations within the build chamber. Non-uniform cooling can induce thermal stresses, warping, and reduced print quality, particularly for horizontally oriented parts30. Careful consideration of part orientation is therefore essential not only for achieving the desired mechanical properties but also for minimizing defects arising from thermally induced deformation during the printing process31.

The advantages of MJF in producing complex geometries with tailored material properties are strongly dependent on orientation-related considerations. The capability to produce parts with programmed performance attributes offers significant benefits in functional applications, such as biomedical engineering, where anatomically customized models are essential for surgical planning and medical training32.

Methodology

The methodology of this study outlines the experimental procedures used to consider the influence of PA12 sample orientation on dimensional and mechanical properties in MJF manufacturing. It encompasses the design and preparation of test samples, the definition of processing parameters, and detailed methods for measuring mechanical and dimensional characteristics. This systematic approach enables a robust evaluation of orientation effects on part quality and facilitates comparison of sample behaviour across different build orientations. The primary aim is to advance understanding of the relationship between model orientation and accuracy, surface quality, and mechanical performance of the resulting parts.

Material and equipment

The HP Multi Jet Fusion 5210 is an advanced industrial 3D printer that uses MJF technology to produce functional parts from polymer powders. The system is optimized for processing materials such as polyamides (PA12, PA11) and thermoplastic elastomers (TPU), enabling the production of parts with high mechanical strength, dimensional accuracy, and repeatability. The printing process involves the successive deposition of powder layers, the selective application of fusing and detailing agents, and layer-by-layer sintering via infrared radiation, eliminating the need for support structures. The machine offers a build volume of 380 × 284 × 380 mm, with a layer resolution of up to 0.08 mm. Additionally, the build chamber temperature can reach 180 °C. The HP MJF 5210 offers both high throughput and process reliability, enabling its use in prototyping, medium-volume production, and a wide range of industrial applications, particularly in the automotive, aerospace, and medical industries.

HP 3D High Reusability PA 12, a thermoplastic polymer developed for MJF technology, was used for samples. Its combination of high tensile strength, impact resistance, and ductility makes it suitable for durable structural engineering applications. Despite its low density, PA12 maintains high mechanical strength, enabling the production of lightweight parts with excellent dimensional stability. The material also demonstrates strong chemical resistance to oils, greases, hydrocarbons, and other industrial chemicals, while retaining mechanical integrity over a wide temperature range and under cyclic loading conditions. PA12 was selected due to its reliable dimensional stability, well-balanced mechanical properties, and chemical resistance, which together ensure consistent and accurate evaluation of orientation-dependent effects within the MJF build volume. Sample description

Tensile strength represents the maximum stress that a material can withstand under tensile loading before macroscopic failure occurs. The static tensile test is rigorously defined by relevant technical standards, which specify the testing procedure, requirements for the testing apparatus, and the dimensions and geometries of samples for different material types. During testing, the applied force F on samples with circular or rectangular cross-sections is measured as a function of relative elongation, capturing the complete stress–strain response up to failure. The resulting tensile curve provides the basis for evaluating key mechanical and deformation properties of the material.

The mechanical and qualitative properties of additively manufactured materials are evaluated according to established standards that define both sample geometry and evaluation procedures. STN EN ISO 527-2 standard provides the baseline for defining the shape and dimensions of plastic samples used to determine strength characteristics through static tensile testing. Although the standard specifies a minimum sample length of 75 mm, a length of 95 mm was used in this study to provide sufficient space for labelling each sample during production. Each sample was labelled to ensure unambiguous identification of its build orientation and to prevent potential mix-ups during subsequent testing. Sample dimensions were also selected to optimize material and energy usage. To maximize efficiency in the build volume and minimize waste, a smaller-sized test sample was chosen. In accordance with the standard, a Type 1BA sample was used for the measurements (Fig. 1).

Test sample of type 1BA with its dimensions.

To ensure methodological clarity and reproducibility, the part orientation was defined according to ISO/ASTM 52921. The orientation of each sample in the build volume was determined by simultaneous rotations around the X-, Y-, and Z-axes of the device’s coordinate system. The testing direction (loading axis) was consistently aligned with the longitudinal axis of the tensile sample. Consequently, the geometric relationship between the layer-wise building direction (Z-axis) and the loading direction was determined by the vector resulting from the combined rotations defined in the Taguchi L25 orthogonal array. This multi-axial approach was chosen to identify potential non-linear interaction effects on both dimensional accuracy and mechanical isotropy, moving beyond simple single-axis orientation studies.

The standard STN EN ISO/ASTM 52902:2019 specifies test artifacts designed to evaluate the geometric capabilities of additive manufacturing systems. It defines a set of samples that enable the evaluation of various geometric parameters, including accuracy, repeatability, and the ability to reproduce detailed features.

To maximize utilization of the printer’s build volume, test samples were selected from STN EN ISO/ASTM 52902:2019 that enable evaluation of fundamental dimensional and geometric accuracy. Samples were specifically selected to evaluate circular artifacts, for which the ISO/ASTM 52902 standard provides three alternative geometric evaluation methods. Primarily, measuring the roundness of surfaces; alternatively, measuring the diameters of both external and internal circles; and, thirdly, measuring the wall thickness of each ring at multiple locations. This sample type was selected for its ability to provide comprehensive information on the accuracy of the additive process, as it allows multiple measurement approaches within a single part. Of the three available artifact sizes, the medium size was chosen (dimensions shown in Fig. 2) to ensure sufficient scale for coordinate measuring machine (CMM) inspection while optimizing material usage during production.

Design of experiment

Before sample production, orientations were defined using a Design of Experiments (DoE) approach. The experiment aimed to analyse the effect of model orientation on the dimensional and mechanical properties of parts produced via MJF. The experimental design was consistent across both sample types.

The experiment focused on three main factors: the orientation of samples along the X-, Y-, and Z- axes. Each factor had five levels, which in a full factorial design would require 125 samples with three repetitions. Such a large number of samples would significantly increase production and measurement time. To address this, the Taguchi method with an L25 orthogonal array was used. Using Minitab, an experiment was designed with three factors at five levels, resulting in 25 partial experiments that cover all necessary factor combinations and allow evaluation of individual factor effects. This approach saves time and material while enhancing experimental reliability. The method enables the identification of key factors most influencing dimensional and mechanical properties and minimizes the impact of process variability. Due to its orthogonality, the design is balanced, ensuring each factor has equal weight without mutual interference.

Each orientation combination was evenly represented. Samples were labelled A01 to A25 according to their X-, Y-, and Z-orientations, as detailed in Table 2. Measurement results will enable analysis of the influence of orientation on mechanical properties and dimensional accuracy. Based on these results, optimal orientation settings and a predictive model can be established to guide the production of high-quality parts using MJF technology (Fig. 3).

Manufacturing preparation

Before the experiment was conducted, samples were prepared to meet all measurement and testing requirements. This process involved modeling the samples, specifying their orientation within the build volume, and exporting them in a format suitable for production.

Sample models were created in Autodesk Fusion 360 in accordance with ISO 527-2 (tensile testing) and ISO/ASTM 52902 (geometric accuracy) standards. Each sample was labelled according to its orientation (A01 to A25) to ensure unambiguous identification throughout the experiment. The models were exported in STL format, enabling smooth import into the production software.

Sample orientation was set using Materialise Magics, a software platform for pre-production model preparation. In this step, orientations were configured according to the experimental design, and sample placement within the build volume was optimized. Optimization focused on uniform distribution to ensure consistent thermal exposure and minimize the risk of deformation. A spacing of 10 mm between samples was maintained to allow sufficient heat circulation and cooling. During production, the samples were positioned as shown in Fig. 4. The prepared samples were then fully ready for import into the printing software, with proper identification, orientation, and spatial arrangement ensuring reliable and comparable measurement results.

Distribution of samples in the workspace.

Sample production was carried out on the HP Multi Jet Fusion 5210, which operates using predefined production modes. Since the system does not permit manual adjustment of individual processing parameters, printing was carried out in Balance mode, which offers a compromise between dimensional accuracy, mechanical performance, and surface quality. Device calibration was performed following a seven-level procedure, addressing specific aspects of system setup: infrared camera calibration, heating calibration, printhead calibration, secondary printhead calibration, X- and Y-offset calibration, line width calibration, and fusing density and uniformity calibration. In this mode, the layer thickness is 80 μm. The Balance mode is manufacturer-defined to ensure consistent results across parts of varying geometries and sizes. It should be noted that the Balance mode prioritizes process stability and dimensional consistency rather than maximum mechanical performance, which may contribute to lower experimentally measured Young’s modulus values compared to the values reported in the supplier datasheet, typically obtained under more performance-optimized conditions.

Before the production, the prepared sample data were imported into the printer software. The build duration depended on the layer thickness and the total height of the samples. After printing, the build chamber was cooled to prevent sample deformation. Cooling was performed directly within the production cart using a slow-cooling protocol, aiming to achieve ambient temperature throughout the entire build volume. Once cooled, the majority of unfused powder was mechanically removed using automated air nozzles. The recovered powder was recycled for reuse in subsequent production cycles. Samples were then sandblasted in a closed, automated blasting chamber to remove residual powder and improve surface quality. This automated process, commonly used in series production, allows efficient processing of large quantities of samples with minimal manual labour. PA12 blasting media with a particle size of 70–110 µm and a 10 mm nozzle diameter were used. This post-processing step ensures dimensional accuracy and a clean surface, which are essential for precise measurement of the samples.

For geometric accuracy, roundness and deviation from nominal diameter were measured as quality indicators for MJF-produced parts. Measurements were performed using a Zeiss Eclipse coordinate measuring machine, providing high-precision and repeatable results. Measurement programming and evaluation were conducted using Zeiss Calypso software.

Roundness and diameter deviation were measured on all available circular diameters, specifically ϕ14 mm, ϕ16 mm, ϕ30 mm, and ϕ47 mm. Each diameter was measured at three different heights to capture the deviations from the intended geometry resulting from variations in sample orientation. Before the measurement, samples were carefully positioned and stabilized on the granite table of the CMM. Custom clamping and height-adjustment fixtures were used to ensure all measurement points were accessible. Proper fixturing was critical to eliminate vibrations and shifts that could negatively affect measurement accuracy.

In Zeiss Calypso software, the diameters for roundness measurement were defined. Since the depth of each hole fell within 1–3 times its diameter, three circles were scanned perpendicular to the hole axis. The angular measurement range for each circle was 380° to eliminate errors from the start and end paths. Scanning was performed counterclockwise at 3 mm s−1. For smaller diameters, a 50 UPR Gaussian filter was applied, while a 150 UPR Gaussian filter was used for larger diameters. The Gaussian filter separates the true surface form from roughness and waviness, ensuring more accurate evaluation of geometric deviations.

Before the measurement, the sensing system was calibrated to ensure accuracy, the base coordinate system was established, and safety planes were set. For roundness measurement on the CMM, the Lc 2.5 mm filter was applied to remove short-wavelength surface irregularities and emphasize longer-wavelength deviations related to overall geometry. The 2.5 mm value is appropriate for medium-sized parts, minimizing the influence of surface roughness on measurement results. A 1.5 mm diameter ruby stylus was used, chosen based on sample dimensions to safely measure features in the grooves between ϕ16 mm and ϕ30 mm.

Using the predefined settings and measurement program, all samples were measured automatically. The probe followed defined trajectories and recorded the coordinates of individual points. Results

The following section details the results of the experimental study, assessing the influence of build orientation on dimensional and mechanical properties. This analysis was performed in two stages: initially, tensile testing was used to determine the mechanical properties, followed by an evaluation of dimensional accuracy, with particular attention to roundness and diameter deviations.

Mechanical properties

Tensile tests were conducted in accordance with ISO 527-2. The aim was to experimentally verify claims by HP and various scientific studies that the tensile strength of PA12 is largely unaffected by changes in sample orientation. The obtained data were processed and analysed using the statistical software Minitab, with a focus on evaluating maximum tensile strength across different sample orientations. For clarity, the results were also presented graphically.

The results of tensile tests on samples with different orientations are presented in Fig. 5, including maximum stress values and their standard deviations for each sample. The graph indicates that maximum stress remained largely unchanged across orientations, ranging narrowly from 44.26 to 45.45 MPa. This consistency confirms that the selected orientations did not result in significant variations in the mechanical properties of the tested material.

Graph of maximum stresses and standard deviations for tested PA12 samples.

The graph also displays the standard deviations of maximum stress, providing insight into measurement variability. Most samples exhibited low deviations (from ± 0.004 MPa), indicating high reproducibility and stability of the results. Only a few samples, including A15, A16, A17, and A18, showed higher deviations (± 1.12 MPa), suggesting slight variability in measurements. Overall, these differences do not significantly affect the tensile strength of the material. The results corroborate previous studies and HP’s claims that PA12 strength remains stable regardless of build orientation in MJF. This finding simplifies part design and production, as orientation does not significantly impact tensile performance, and it enables more efficient part placement within the build volume and production optimization.

A comprehensive evaluation of the tensile properties was performed by analyzing the Young’s modulus and the nominal strain at yield across all 25 samples (Fig. 6 and Fig. 7). The Young’s modulus values (Fig. 5) ranged from 0.84 to 1.18 GPa. While these values are lower than the manufacturer’s nominal specification of 1.7 GPa, they demonstrate high internal consistency and confirm that the material stiffness remains remarkably stable across different printing angles. The study specifically focused on these parameters as they define the functional elastic limit of the parts. While the fracture strain was not explicitly quantified for every orientation, the specimens exhibited high ductility during testing, consistent with the typical behaviours of PA12.

Graph of Young’s module and standard deviations for tested PA12 samples.

Graph of Nominal strain at yield and standard deviations for tested PA12 samples.

Similarly, the nominal strain at yield (Fig. 7) exhibited minimal variation across the tested orientations, with values ranging from approximately 10.26% to 13.14%. The presence of error bars, representing the standard deviation for each orientation, confirms the high repeatability and stability of the MJF process. From a physical perspective, this high degree of isotropy in yielding behaviours suggests that the thermal fusion of PA12 particles is sufficiently robust to ensure strong bonding between layers. Unlike another processes, where the interface between filaments often acts as a point of failure, the MJF process maintains a more uniform thermal profile, effectively preserving the mechanical integrity of the bulk material throughout the entire build volume regardless of the specimen’s orientation.

The observed mechanical isotropy (independence of tensile strength from orientation) is a key feature of MJF technology, distinguishing it from SLS or FDM processes. This phenomenon is driven by the diffusion mechanism of polymer chains, known as reptation theory. Since the entire powder bed is maintained at a temperature just below the melting point of PA12 and energy is delivered across the entire surface rather than point-by-point, a thorough re-melting of the previous layer occurs. This provides sufficient time for the polymer chains to interdiffuse across the layer interface and form strong intermolecular bonds before solidification. This process eliminates critical planes of weakness, as confirmed by our experimental tensile tests, where strength values across different orientations varied only within the margin of statistical error.

Results of roundness measurement

In the MJF additive manufacturing process, every object is discretized into layers of constant thickness (80 μm). For inclined orientations, a characteristic stair-stepping effect occurs, which influences geometric tolerances. However, our results suggest that in MJF, this effect is partially suppressed due to high heat transfer within the powder bed, leading to a slight melting of the edges (referred to as surface smoothing). The higher stability observed in the Z-axis is because accuracy in this direction is dictated by the mechanical step of the build platform and homogeneous volumetric heating. Conversely, in the XY plane, the process is influenced by the movement dynamics of the printhead and microsecond variations in agent application.

Measurements of roundness on four diameters at three heights generated a dataset of 900 values, which were analysed statistically. This analysis aimed to determine the influence of individual factors on the resulting roundness, a key parameter of the tested artifact. Measurements were conducted in accordance with ISO/ASTM 52902, and the data were evaluated using a statistical model with a reliability of 71.59%, confirming its suitability and sufficient accuracy for analysing new data.

Measured roundness values ranged from 0.04 to 0.52 mm and exhibited a clear dependence on sample orientation. Figure 8 presents the roundness values for all 25 samples, enabling direct comparison. The lowest values were observed for samples A01 and A02 (up to 0.128 mm), while the highest roundness deviations were recorded for samples A21 and A25 (up to 0.519 mm). The height of the boxes in the graph represents the spread of measurements; shorter boxes (e.g., A01, A02, A07) indicate more consistent results, whereas taller boxes (e.g., A05, A21, A24) reflect greater variability in roundness.

The lowest standard deviations were observed for samples A01, A02, and A07, indicating higher dimensional stability and more accurate preservation of the circular shape. In contrast, samples A21, A24, and A25 showed higher standard deviations, suggesting greater variability and less consistent roundness.

The dominant influence of orientation along the X and Y axes on dimensional accuracy can be explained by the thermodynamic equilibrium at the interface between the part and the powder bed. The Fusing Agent (FA) locally increases infrared radiation absorption, generating the heat required for melting, while the Detailing Agent (DA) acts as a thermal barrier. Edge precision is defined by the latent heat of vaporization of the DA, which removes energy from the boundary zones and prevents undesired sintering of the surrounding powder (known as thermal bleed). Changes in orientation alter the projection area targeted by the printhead nozzles, leading to local variations in agent density and, consequently, to non-linear thermal gradients that directly correlate with the observed deviations in circularity and dimensions.

In the evaluation of cylindrical samples, distinctions between the outer cylinder and the inner hole are also considered. For the outer diameters (ϕ16 mm and ϕ47 mm), the circumscribed element method was applied, while for the inner hole diameters (ϕ14 mm and ϕ30 mm), the inscribed element method was used. This approach provides results that accurately reflect not only the individual diameter values but also their geometric relationship, ensuring proper part functionality during assembly.

The boxplot in Fig. 11 illustrates the distribution of roundness values for the four different sample diameters. Analysis shows that the ϕ14 mm and ϕ16 mm diameters exhibit the lowest roundness values with minimal spread, indicating high accuracy and minimal shape deviations. In contrast, the ϕ30 mm and ϕ47 mm diameters display a greater spread, suggesting higher measurement variability.

Roundness distribution for four different diameters.

Specifically, roundness for the ϕ14 mm diameter ranged from 0.50% to 0.91% of the nominal dimension, for ϕ16 mm from 0.50% to 0.80%, for ϕ30 mm from 0.47% to 1.00%, and for ϕ47 mm from 0.28% to 0.54%. These data indicate that, while absolute roundness values increase with diameter, the relative roundness as a percentage of the nominal dimension tends to decrease. Consequently, the largest percentage deviations occur in smaller diameters (ϕ14 and ϕ16), whereas larger diameters (ϕ30 and ϕ47) exhibit relatively more stable roundness.

The roundness of the samples was affected by variations in build orientation. To quantify the effect of individual factors on roundness, a Pareto chart was used to identify the contributions of each factor and its interactions to the resulting part roundness. As shown in Fig. 12, the most significant factor is orientation along the X-axis (factor A), with orientation along the Y-axis (factor B) also exerting a notable effect. Interactions between factor pairs AB and AD, as well as the influence of the cylinder or hole parameter (D), are also apparent. These factors and interactions significantly affect roundness, whereas orientation along the Z-axis (factor C) has a negligible impact. The Pareto chart indicates that optimizing rotations along the X and Y axes can effectively minimize roundness deviations.

Pareto diagram for roundness.

This setup follows previous findings indicating that Z-axis orientation has minimal influence on roundness. The plot shows that the lowest roundness deviations (dark blue regions) occur at small rotation angles along the X- and Y-axes. As the rotation angles increase, roundness gradually deteriorates, indicated by a colour transition from blue to green. The diagram thus enables prediction of the resulting roundness based on the set X- and Y-orientations.

The findings are consistent with the previous main-effects analysis and the Pareto chart, which identified X-axis orientation as the dominant factor influencing roundness, followed by Y-axis orientation. The contour plot confirms that to achieve minimal roundness deviations in MJF-manufactured parts, sample rotations along both X and Y axes should be kept as small as possible.

Deviation from the average of the measurements

Deviation from the average was measured on four diameters at three heights, analogous to the roundness evaluation. The experiment aimed to determine the influence of individual factors on the resulting deviation from the average at the corresponding locations on the artifact. Measurements were conducted in accordance with ISO/ASTM 52902, with deviation from the average serving as a secondary measurement parameter for this artifact. The collected data were statistically processed into a data model, which achieved a reliability of 73.89%, confirming its adequate accuracy and suitability for interpreting new data.

Unlike roundness, the deviation-from-average results include negative values, allowing a more precise evaluation of dimensional accuracy relative to the optimal shape. The results presented in Fig. 16 demonstrate that sample orientation influences both the range and variability of the measured values. Measured deviations ranged from ± 0.08 mm to ± 0.68 mm. The most accurate results were obtained for samples A01 and A02, where deviations did not exceed ± 0.137 mm; together with sample A06, these also exhibited the lowest variability and a median close to zero, indicating high manufacturing precision. In contrast, the largest dispersions were observed for samples A05, A10, and A21, reflecting higher variability in the measurements. Overall, the data confirm that while some samples achieved highly stable dimensional accuracy, others showed more significant deviations.

Boxplot of deviations from nominal diameter for individual samples.

The boxplot of standard deviations presented in Fig. 17 provides a complementary perspective on the stability and precision of the individual samples. The lowest variability in deviation from the mean was observed for samples A01, A02, and A06, whose orientations were only minimally altered during production. In contrast, the highest standard deviations were recorded for samples A05 and A10, for which the Y-axis orientation was set to 90°. These results confirm that sample orientation during production has a significant influence on dimensional consistency and accuracy. A comparison with the roundness measurements reveals that the standard deviations of deviation-from-average values are approximately twice those obtained for roundness. This difference can be attributed to the wider dispersion of measured values around the nominal diameter, which results in increased variability. It should be noted that, although deviations from the average may be either positive or negative, the standard deviation is insensitive to their sign, as it is calculated from the squared deviations and therefore reflects only the magnitude of dispersion.

Graph of standard deviations of deviations from nominal diameter for individual samples.

When evaluating deviation from the average on the artifacts, it is essential to distinguish between the outer cylinder diameter and the inner hole diameter, as each feature requires a specific measurement and analysis approach, similar to roundness. This distinction is crucial for proper part fitting and is based on the Maximum Material Condition (MMC) principle, commonly used in dimensional inspection. For the outer cylinder diameter, the least-squares (or described) element method is applied, focusing on the minimum measured diameter, which represents the smallest cylinder enclosing all measured surface points. Conversely, for the inner hole diameter, the inscribed element method is used, considering the maximum measured diameter, i.e., the largest cylinder that still fits within all points of the hole wall.

Analysis of deviations for individual diameters revealed that ϕ14 and ϕ30, measured using the maximum-diameter approach, predominantly exhibited positive deviation values. In contrast, ϕ16 and ϕ47, evaluated using the minimum-diameter method, showed deviations oriented toward negative values. This difference arises from the measurement methodology, which determines whether the maximum or minimum diameter is considered. Specifically, deviations for ϕ14 ranged approximately from 0.21% to 1.00%, for ϕ16 from 0.13 to 0.50%, and the highest deviations occurred for ϕ30, reaching 0.47% to 1.43% of the nominal diameter. Conversely, for ϕ47, deviations were in the negative range, approximately − 0.02% to − 0.36% relative to the nominal dimension. These data, shown in Fig. 18, indicate that larger nominal diameters do not automatically ensure higher accuracy or more stable deviations. Instead, the range of deviations depends more on the specific geometry and sample orientation than on the diameter size itself.

Deviations from nominal diameter at different measured diameters.

Individual factors and their interactions influenced deviations from the average. The Pareto chart in Fig. 19 illustrates the impact of each factor on the resulting deviation. The most significant factor is the sample orientation along the Y-axis (factor B), with a similar influence observed for X-axis orientation (factor A). Notable effects also arise from the two-factor interactions AD and BD, as well as from the evaluation method itself (factor D). These factors and their interactions substantially affect deviation from the average, whereas Z-axis orientation (factor C) and interactions CD, BC, AC, and AB have a negligible influence. Therefore, optimizing the X and Y orientations can reduce deviation from the average, achieve more precise results, and minimize undesired variability.

Discussion

Additive manufacturing has become a key technology in recent years, enabling the efficient production of functional parts with high dimensional accuracy. Multi Jet Fusion operates by applying specialized agents to a layer of powder and then uniformly heating it to fuse the layers. This process produces more homogeneous layers, resulting in parts with more stable and predictable mechanical properties compared to other powder-based additive technologies, such as SLS. This principle enables nearly isotropic behaviour of the PA12 material, as confirmed by the tensile test results obtained in this study.

The tensile test results confirmed that the orientation of the model along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes has no significant effect on the tensile strength of PA12 samples produced using MJF. The maximum stress values ranged between 44.26 and 45.45 MPa, indicating isotropic material behaviour. This finding is consistent with the study by O’Connor et al., who also observed minimal differences in the strength of PA12 samples produced in different orientations. The authors explain that the uniform heat distribution and consistent layer fusing in the MJF process reduce variations in material properties between directions. Consequently, the produced part behaves isotropically, exhibiting consistent properties throughout. The results of our measurements support these conclusions, indicating no significant differences even when considering a broader set of orientations. This supports the claim that, in terms of mechanical properties, model orientation in MJF is a negligible factor and does not substantially influence tensile strength.

On the other hand, the evaluation of roundness clearly showed that sample orientation, particularly along the X- and Y-axes, has a significant effect. The lowest roundness deviations were observed in samples oriented parallel to the build plane (A01 and A02), whereas samples with other orientation combinations exhibited higher deviations and greater variability. This trend is consistent with the findings of Adach et al., who studied spherical samples at different orientations. Their study confirmed that wall thickness has a negligible effect on accuracy, whereas model orientation is the primary determinant of geometric stability. Similarly, Mele et al. reported that changes in part orientation significantly affect dimensional errors, primarily due to the staircase effect. Our results corroborate these observations: circular features oriented in the XY plane exhibited the smallest dimensional deviations, whereas inclined orientations led to increased roundness deviations.

A similar trend was observed for diameter deviation, which behaved analogously to roundness. The lowest deviations occurred in samples A01 and A02, with the largest effects stemming from X- and Y-axis orientations and the type of measured surface (internal or external diameter). Orientation along the Z-axis and its interactions were negligible. These findings align with Sağbaş, who showed that model orientation significantly affects dimensional characteristics; perpendicular orientations reduce variability in vertical dimensions and improve measurement reproducibility. While Sağbaş considered only two orientations (45° and 90°), the present study analysed a broader range across all three axes, allowing detailed identification of factor interactions and their impact on dimensional accuracy.

Overall, the results indicate that, although the mechanical properties of PA12 samples produced via MJF are largely isotropic, dimensional and geometric accuracy is strongly influenced by XY-plane orientation. This effect arises from MJF’s layer-wise process, where horizontal layers promote uniform sintering and reduce stress accumulation. Consistent with Adach, Mele, and Sağbaş, it is therefore recommended that parts requiring high geometric accuracy be oriented with main functional surfaces as close as possible to the XY plane. Proper orientation thus serves as a key parameter for optimizing MJF production, minimizing roundness errors and diameter deviations without adjusting other process parameters.

Conclusion

The study focused on the additive manufacturing technology Multi Jet Fusion and the influence of sample orientation on the dimensional and mechanical properties of parts made from PA12. Understanding the impact of model orientation is crucial for properly setting production parameters and achieving the desired part quality.

Based on experimental measurements and obtained data, the following conclusions can be drawn:

The material’s maximum tensile stress is not significantly affected by model orientation, with all samples exhibiting similar tensile strength values ranging from 44.26 MPa to 45.45 MPa.

The results of this study provide practical recommendations for optimizing model orientation during MJF production. Correct orientation is key to achieving high part quality, especially for geometrically precise or functionally demanding features. Additionally, this study complements previous work by analysing a wider range of orientation combinations. This approach enables a more detailed understanding of the effects of individual factors and provides specific recommendations for the industrial application of MJF technology.

The significance of this research lies in the transition from descriptive empirical studies to a predictive control framework for MJF processes. The developed models, validated with a Predicted R-squared of over 70%, offer a reliable tool for designers to anticipate and mitigate geometric deviations before the production phase. By quantifying the dominance of X and Y interactions over Z-axis stability and providing a physical interpretation based on thermal gradients and polymer diffusion, this work contributes to the scientific foundation required for high-precision additive manufacturing in regulated industries such as aerospace and medical engineering.
