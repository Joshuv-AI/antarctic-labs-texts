CanvasMAR: Improving Masked Autoregressive Video Generation With Canvas
Zian Li, Muhan Zhang

Abstract
Masked autoregressive models (MAR) have recently emerged as a powerful paradigm for image and video generation, combining the flexibility of masked modeling with the potential of continuous tokenizer. However, video MAR models suffer from two major limitations: the slow-start problem , caused by the lack of a structured global prior at early sampling stages, and error accumulation across the autoregression in both spatial and temporal dimensions. In this work, we propose CanvasMAR , a novel video MAR model that mitigates these issues by introducing a canvas mechanism—a blurred, global prediction of the next frame, used as the starting point for masked generation. The canvas provides global structure early in sampling, enabling faster and more coherent frame synthesis. Furthermore, we introduce compositional classifier-free guidance that jointly enlarges spatial (canvas) and temporal conditioning, and employ noise-based canvas augmentation to enhance robustness. Experiments on the BAIR and Kinetics-600 benchmarks demonstrate that CanvasMAR produces high-quality videos with fewer autoregressive steps. Our approach achieves remarkable performance among autoregressive models on Kinetics-600 dataset and rivals diffusion-based methods.

Related Work

Diffusion-Based Video Generative Models.

Diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable success in image generation
(
Ho et al. 2020
;
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2020
)
, and have since been extended to video generation by treating the temporal axis as an additional dimension alongside the spatial ones
(
Ho et al. 2022b
;
Brooks et al. 2024
;
Kong et al. 2024
;
Wan et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
)
. While effective, this paradigm generates entire videos in a single pass, which leads to high latency and limited interactivity—two critical drawbacks for applications such as interactive simulators and game environments
(
Feng et al. 2024
;
Bruce et al. 2024
)
. To mitigate these issues, recent research has adopted an autoregressive approach, wherein videos are generated sequentially by conditioning on previously generated clean frames. This design enables interactivity and supports the generation of longer, temporally coherent videos
(
Ruhe et al. 2024
;
Song et al. 2025
;
Yin et al. 2025
;
Gupta et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
. Nevertheless, these methods remain diffusion-based, generating entire frames at once through iterative frame-level denoising. In contrast, token-based autoregressive models directly learn token-level distributions and produce image tokens, making them more natively compatible with language models and are thus more suitable for multi-modal model designs
(
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Jin et al. 2024
)
.

Autoregressive Video Generation.

An alternative line of work decomposes video frames into image tokens, shifting the generative task from producing full frames to predicting sequences of tokens. Many approaches in this direction adopt quantized tokens, typically following paradigms such as next-token prediction
(
Yan et al. 2021
;
Hong et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024d
;
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Jin et al. 2024
)
or masked generative modeling
(
Gupta et al. 2022
;
Yu et al. 2023a
;
Yu et al. 2023b
;
Bruce et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Villegas et al. 2022
)
. However, as observed by
Yu et al. 2023b
, these paradigms rely on carefully designed and trained VQ-VAEs
(
Van Den Oord et al. 2017
;
Esser et al. 2021
)
, which inevitably introduce quantization errors. Inspired by Masked Autoregressive Models (MAR)
(
Li et al. 2024
;
Fan et al. 2024
)
, a growing body of work—including NOVA
(
Deng et al. 2024
)
, VideoMAR
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
, and MAGI
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
—adapts MAR to video generation. These methods operate by masking image tokens within frames and training the model to recover them while leveraging historical frame context.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Factorizing Video Generation

In this paper, we focus on autoregressive video generation.
A video is denoted as
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times H\times W\times 3}
, which consists of
N
frames
[f^{(1)},f^{(2)},\ldots,f^{(N)}]
of height
H
and width
W
. We employ latent generation
(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
; however, for simplicity, we retain pixel-level notations.
Autoregressive video generation models the video distribution via the following
temporal
factorization:

p(V)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p\big(f^{(i)}\mid f^{(1)},f^{(2)},\ldots,f^{(i-1)}\big)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p\big(f^{(i)}\mid f^{(<i)}\big),

where
f^{(<i)}=\{f^{(1)},f^{(2)},\ldots,f^{(i-1)}\}
denotes all preceding frames.
Each frame
f^{(i)}
is further decomposed into a sequence of image tokens in raster order,
f^{(i)}=[x^{(i)}_{1},x^{(i)}_{2},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{n}]
.
In standard autoregressive generation, tokens are generated in raster order token-by-token
(
Yan et al. 2021
;
Hong et al. 2022
)
:

p(f^{(i)}\mid f^{(<i)})=\prod_{j=1}^{n}p\big(x^{(i)}_{j}\mid f^{(<i)},x^{(i)}_{<j}\big),

where
x^{(i)}_{<j}=\{x^{(i)}_{1},x^{(i)}_{2},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{j-1}\}
denotes the tokens already generated within frame
i
.

Following masked generative paradigms
Chang et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
, we generate tokens set by set in a random order.
Specifically, the
n
tokens are partitioned into
K
sets of sizes
[n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{K}]
, with cumulative index
s_{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}n_{m}
and
s_{0}=0
,
s_{K}=N
.
This factorization introduces
spatial
autoregression:

p(f^{(i)}\mid f^{(<i)})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p\big(X^{(i)}_{k}\mid f^{(<i)},X^{(i)}_{1},X^{(i)}_{2},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{k-1}\big),

(1)

where each token set is defined as

X^{(i)}_{k}=\{x^{(i)}_{\tau(s_{k-1}+1)},x^{(i)}_{\tau(s_{k-1}+2)},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{\tau(s_{k})}\},

and where
\tau\sim\mathcal{S}_{N}
denotes a random permutation sampled from the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{N}
, i.e., the set of all possible permutations of the index sequence
[1,2,\ldots,N]
. This sampling introduces stochasticity into the generation process.

3.2
Masked Autoregressive Models with Continuous Tokens

Unlike conventional autoregressive generative models that produce quantized image tokens
(
Hong et al. 2022
;
Yan et al. 2021
)
using categorical distributions, MAR
(
Li et al. 2024
)
directly generates continuous image tokens using a diffusion head, which models token distributions conditioned on token embeddings.
Formally, the distribution in
Equation

1
is further factorized as:

p\big(X^{(i)}_{k}\mid f^{(<i)},X^{(i)}_{1},X^{(i)}_{2},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{k-1}\big):=p(X^{(i)}_{k}\mid Z^{(i)}_{k})\,p\big(Z^{(i)}_{k}\mid f^{(<i)},X^{(i)}_{1},X^{(i)}_{2},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{k-1}\big),

where
Z^{(i)}_{k}=\{z^{(i)}_{\tau(s_{k-1}+1)},z^{(i)}_{\tau(s_{k-1}+2)},\ldots,z^{(i)}_{\tau(s_{k})}\}
denotes the token embeddings.
The conditional distribution
p(X^{(i)}_{k}\mid Z^{(i)}_{k})=\prod_{j=s_{k-1}+1}^{s_{k}}p(x^{(i)}_{j}\mid z^{(i)}_{j})
models per-token outputs given the embeddings, where the correlation between tokens are captured in the calculation of embeddings.
In practice,
p\big(Z^{(i)}_{k}\mid f^{(<i)},X^{(i)}_{1},X^{(i)}_{2},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{k-1}\big)
is parameterized by a Vision Transformer
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
, which learns token-wise embeddings, while
p(x^{(i)}_{j}\mid z^{(i)}_{j})
is modeled by a lightweight MLP-based diffusion head that conditions on
z^{(i)}_{j}
to generate
x^{(i)}_{j}
.

4.1
Factorized Temporal and Spatial Autoregression

We design our model to generate videos through a factorized temporal–spatial autoregressive framework, as illustrated in
Figure

2
.
Specifically, CanvasMAR proceeds in two stages:
1) a temporal embedding
zt^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where
n
denotes the number of frame tokens and
d
the hidden dimension, is autoregressively generated using a Temporal ViT
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2020
)
, taking all past frames
f^{(<i)}
as context;
2) this temporal embedding is then provided as a conditioning sequence to the spatial autoregressive model, implemented as a Masked Autoregressive Model
(
Li et al. 2024
)
, which generates image tokens in randomly ordered sets for the next frame
f^{(i)}
.

The attention mask of the Temporal ViT is designed as a
hybrid
structure
(
Deng et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
—causal across frames and bidirectional within each frame.
This design preserves temporal causality while maintaining comprehensive spatial perception within frames, which also enables
KV caching
, further accelerating the video generation process.

We note that this temporal-spatial factorization preserves both training and inference efficiency, in contrast to models that process temporal and spatial sequences uniformly
(
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
.
1) During training, tokens within each frame can be independently masked, and all masked tokens across all frames contribute to the loss in parallel, analogous to decoder-only language models.
In contrast, models without factorized autoregression can only mask tokens in the last frame of a sequence when computing the loss
(
Yu et al. 2025
)
, leading to inefficient training.
A potential remedy, as explored in MAGI
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
, is to duplicate “clean” frames for each frame in the sequence and design a corresponding attention mask, which doubles the sequence length and substantially increases memory usage.
2) During inference, the temporally summarized embedding
zt
captures motion information necessary to guide next-frame generation.
As a result, spatial token generation only needs to attend to this fixed-length temporal embedding rather than all previously generated image tokens, whose size grows with sequence length.

4.2
Bridging Fast Temporal Autoregression and Slow Spatial Autoregression with Canvas

As shown in
Figure

1
, MAR suffers from a slow-start problem: the absence of an initial global estimate of the target frame significantly limits the size of the initial token set.
In video generation, this issue further degrades both quality and speed.
To address this, we introduce the
canvas
between temporal autoregression and spatial autoregression, as illustrated in
Figure

2
.

After obtaining the temporal embedding
zt^{(i)}
, instead of using it solely as the temporal condition for the Spatial MAR to generate the next frame, we first pass it through the
Canvas ViT
module.
This module predicts a blurred, coarse version of the next frame
zs^{(i)}
, serving as an effective
spatial condition
.
The Spatial MAR then receives both the temporal condition
zt^{(i)}
and the spatial canvas embedding
zs^{(i)}
to predict the next frame, as illustrated on the right side of
Figure

2
.

Concretely, the Canvas ViT takes the temporal representation
zt^{(i)}
together with the most recent frame
f^{(i-1)}
as input and outputs a canvas embedding
zs^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, representing an initial guess of the next frame
f^{(i)}
.
To supervise this process, we introduce a canvas reconstruction loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{canvas}}=\mathbb{E}_{f^{(<i+1)}}\big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|f^{\text{prob}}_{\theta}(zs^{(i)}_{j})-x^{(i)}_{j}\big\|_{2}^{2}\big],

(2)

where
zs^{(i)}_{j}
denotes the
j
-th row of
zs^{(i)}
, and
f^{\text{prob}}_{\theta}
is a simple linear projection layer that supervises each patch embedding to match the corresponding patch in the ground-truth future frame.

We note that, unlike the Spatial MAR—which is inherently slower and stochastic—the Canvas ViT is fast and deterministic: given the same temporal embedding
zt^{(i)}
and previous frame
f^{(i-1)}
, it always produces the same canvas.

Theoretically, the optimal solution of Canvas ViT learns to approximate the expected future frame
\mathbb{E}_{f^{(i)}\sim p(f^{(i)}\mid f^{(<i)})}[f^{(i)}]
given previous frames
f^{(<i)}
, effectively producing a superposition of plausible outcomes. This provides the Spatial MAR with an efficient and informative initialization, enabling it to maintain global coherence early on and quickly “collapse” onto one of the plausible future frames through Spatial MAR.

4.3
Mitigating Error Accumulation in Two-Level Autoregression

Direct autoregression typically suffers from error accumulation
(
Song et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
)
.
In our setting, there are two levels of autoregression—temporal over frames and spatial over image tokens—which compounds the issue.
We introduce two techniques to alleviate this problem.

4.3.1
Canvas Augmentation

The canvas is predicted in one pass, unlike iterative generative models that refine outputs across multiple iterations.
This introduces two potential sources of error:
1) inaccuracies in the previously generated frame may mislead the Canvas ViT, compounding errors in the next canvas prediction; and
2) even with an accurate previous frame, the one-pass Canvas ViT may produce overly blurred or imprecise canvases, confusing the Spatial MAR and amplifying artifacts.

To mitigate these effects, we adopt a noise-based augmentation strategy inspired by cascaded generation methods
(
Zhang et al. 2025a
;
Ho et al. 2022a
)
.
Specifically, when feeding the most recent frame
f^{(i-1)}
into the Canvas ViT, we augment it with Gaussian noise:

f^{(i-1)}_{\text{aug}}=f^{(i-1)}\cdot(1-r)+\epsilon\cdot r,

(3)

where
\epsilon
is random Gaussian noise and
r
is an interpolation coefficient.
Similarly, we perturb the canvas embedding:

zs^{(i)}_{\text{aug}}=zs^{(i)}\cdot(1-r^{\prime})+\epsilon^{\prime}\cdot r^{\prime},

(4)

where
\epsilon^{\prime}
and
r^{\prime}
denote another Gaussian noise and interpolation coefficient.
These augmentations simulate instability in previously generated frames and canvases, forcing both the Canvas ViT and the Spatial MAR to maintain robustness to imperfect conditions.
During training, we sample
r,r^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,0.8)
, while during inference we fix
r
and
r^{\prime}
to a number between
[0.3,0.6]
.

4.3.2
Compositional Classifier-Free Guidance for Temporal and Spatial Conditions

For the Spatial MAR, the generative distribution is defined as
p(f^{(i)}\mid zs^{(i)},zt^{(i)})
, where
zs^{(i)}
denotes the spatial condition (the canvas) and
zt^{(i)}
denotes the temporal condition.
These two conditions naturally induce two forms of classifier-free guidance (CFG).

We decompose the distribution as:

p(f^{(i)}\mid zs^{(i)},zt^{(i)})\propto p(f^{(i)})\,p(zt^{(i)}\mid f^{(i)})\,p(zs^{(i)}\mid f^{(i)},zt^{(i)}),

and apply compositional CFG
(
Du & Kaelbling 2024
;
Brooks et al. 2023
)
to up-weight both the spatial and temporal posteriors:

p^{(w_{s},w_{t})}(f^{(i)}\mid zs^{(i)},zt^{(i)})=p(f^{(i)})\,p(zt^{(i)}\mid f^{(i)})^{w_{t}}\,p(zs^{(i)}\mid f^{(i)},zt^{(i)})^{w_{s}},

(5)

where
w_{s}
and
w_{t}
denote the guidance scales for the spatial and temporal conditions, respectively. Intuitively,
w_{s}
and
w_{t}
enhance the likelihood of the generated frame
f^{(i)}
conforming to the spatial prior
z_{s}^{(i)}
and the temporal consistency constraint
z_{t}^{(i)}
, respectively.

By Bayes’ theorem, the corresponding score of the guided log-probability can be expressed as

\displaystyle\nabla_{f^{(i)}}\log p^{(w_{s},w_{t})}\big(f^{(i)}\mid z_{s}^{(i)},z_{t}^{(i)}\big)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{f^{(i)}}\log p\big(f^{(i)}\big)

(6)

\displaystyle+w_{t}\Big(\nabla_{f^{(i)}}\log p\big(f^{(i)}\mid z_{t}^{(i)}\big)-\nabla_{f^{(i)}}\log p\big(f^{(i)}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle+w_{s}\Big(\nabla_{f^{(i)}}\log p\big(f^{(i)}\mid z_{s}^{(i)},z_{t}^{(i)}\big)-\nabla_{f^{(i)}}\log p\big(f^{(i)}\mid z_{t}^{(i)}\big)\Big),

where three (un)conditional score terms jointly contribute to the final gradient. To learn the classifier-free guidance
(
Ho & Salimans 2022
;
Brooks et al. 2023
)
, during training:
(1) with 5% probability, we replace the spatial condition with a uniform mask
(
Chang et al. 2022
)
;
(2) with 5% probability, we replace the temporal sequence condition with a learnable vector augmented by sinusoidal positional encoding.
and (3) with 5% probability, we remove both simultaneously.
This encourages the model to generalize effectively under varying conditional contexts.

5
Experiments

Model Implementation

We implement the model based on the NOVA
(
Deng et al. 2024
)
codebase with several modifications, in addition to those stated in previous sections: 1) using a flow-head trained with flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
instead of DDPM
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
; 2) for the Spatial DiT, adopting a design similar to MM-DiT
(
Esser et al. 2024
)
, which employs separate MLPs for canvas/mask and clean tokens; and 3) omitting the Scaling and Shift Layer for the temporal embedding
(
Deng et al. 2024
)
.

Tasks and Datasets

We primarily evaluate the proposed architecture on video prediction tasks, where one or multiple initial frames are provided and the task is to predict subsequent frames.
We use two datasets of different scales and complexity: 1)
BAIR.
The BAIR dataset
(
Unterthiner et al. 2018
;
Ebert et al. 2017
)
is a toy benchmark designed for single-frame conditional video generation.
It contains 43K training videos and 256 evaluation videos, depicting a robot arm pushing objects within a static camera view.
We train our models at a resolution of
64\times 64
. 2)
Kinetics-600.

The Kinetics-600 dataset
(
Carreira et al. 2018
)
is a large-scale collection of diverse real-world videos spanning 600 human action categories.
It contains approximately 400K training clips and serves as a challenging benchmark for large-scale video generation.
For this dataset, we train at
128\times 128
resolution. We evaluate all models using the Frechet Video Distance (FVD)
(
Carreira et al. 2018
)
metric on
64\times 64
resolution videos, following the conventional evaluation protocol
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
.
Generated videos larger than this resolution are bilinearly downsampled for evaluation.

5.2
System-Level Comparisons

We compare
CanvasMAR
with well-known models, including both diffusion-based and autoregressive video generation methods. We categorize each model as diffusion or autoregressive by its frame-level generation mechanism-generating frames as a whole, or decomposing it into image tokens. Such frame-level generation mechanism more directly determines visual quality characteristics than its temporal generation paradigm (i.e., generating an entire video at once versus rolling out frames sequentially).

As shown in
Table

1
,
CanvasMAR
achieves the second-best performance on BAIR among all autoregressive models. We note that MAGVIT is autoregressive only in the spatial domain (i.e., generating image tokens), but not in the temporal domain—it generates all frames simultaneously, without enforcing temporal causality. This naturally provides advantages in capturing temporal dependencies, especially in such simplified scenarios, yet with higher latency since users cannot see videos until all frames are generated.

In diffusion-based autoregressive models
(
Yin et al. 2025
;
Song et al. 2025
)
, predicting multiple upcoming frames jointly is common.
However, for token-based autoregressive models, next-group prediction often leads to performance degradation.
For example, NOVA
(
Deng et al. 2024
)
generates frames one at a time.

With the introduction of the canvas, we find next-group frame prediction to be stable, enabling faster generation with small quality loss. To achieve this, we slightly modify the architectures by appending
K

canvas heads
, each implemented as a two-layer MLP, to the output of the Canvas ViT. We also add lightweight temporal attention layers among spatial layers every 4 layers, following the decomposed attention design of
Gupta et al. 2024
, to ensure temporal consistency. These canvas heads provide the spatial conditioning canvases for generating the next
K
frames within the Spatial MAR.

Empirically, we finetune a next-group frame prediction model (with a group size of 2) from a well-trained next-frame prediction model on BAIR dataset, requiring only about
10\%
of the original training cost to converge, indicating that substantial prior knowledge can be transferred between next-frame and next-group settings.

As shown in
Figure

6(b)
,
the next-2-frame model exhibits substantial speedup at smaller batch sizes compared to the next-frame model and converges to similar throughput at higher batch sizes. This occurs because, when the batch size becomes large, both the next-frame and next-2-frame models fully utilize the available GPU resources.
Because of KV caching, they reach a similar level of parallel efficiency.
In practical scenarios, however, users typically need to generate only few videos and expect low latency, where the next-2-frame prediction model proves particularly advantageous.
Furthermore, we train a next-2-frame prediction model
without
using the canvas, whose performance is illustrated by the purple curve.
Our canvas-based model achieves significantly better performance when transferred to next-group prediction, as the canvas provides strong structural priors that guide future-frame prediction under such aggressive sampling settings.

6
Limitations

Despite its remarkable performance demonstrated in previous sections, we identify several limitations of CanvasMAR. First, when predicting videos with significant motion, CanvasMAR tends to produce severely distorted results, as shown in
Figure

7
. One reason for this is that the initially predicted canvas is overly blurred for high-dynamic videos, which misleads the Spatial MAR, as it still lacks the capability to correct such artifacts. Scaling up the model may improve its self-correction ability given the techniques introduced in
Section

4.3
, especially considering that the largest Spatial MAR model we currently use has only half the layers of NOVA
(
Deng et al. 2024
)
.

Failure care of CanvasMAR for high-dynamic videos. a) The predicted canvas. b) The generated frame.

Second, while enlarging temporal classifier-free guidance sometimes improves perceptual quality and aesthetic scores
(
Huang et al. 2024
)
in case studies, it typically negatively affects the FVD score of generated videos. This discrepancy partly arises from the unreliability of FVD
(
Skorokhodov et al. 2022
;
Yu et al. 2023b
;
Huang et al. 2024
)
, which is often biased and misaligned with human perception. More comprehensive evaluations using modern metrics like VBench
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2025
)
are needed, though current baselines on these metrics are very limited, and most competing methods remain closed-source
(
Yu et al. 2023a
)
.
Future research should extend CanvasMAR to text-to-video generation, where modern evaluation protocols are more prevalent.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented
CanvasMAR
, a masked autoregressive video generation framework that enhances both quality and efficiency through the introduction of a canvas-based conditioning mechanism. By generating a blurred global estimate of the next frame before spatial token generation, CanvasMAR bridges the gap between fast temporal and slow spatial autoregression, mitigating the slow-start problem that affects conventional MAR models. Combined with compositional classifier-free guidance and noise-based canvas augmentation, our approach effectively reduces error accumulation across frames and preserves global coherence throughout long sequences. Experiments on BAIR and Kinetics-600 confirm that CanvasMAR achieves competitive or superior FVD scores with fewer autoregressive steps, outperforming most of prior autoregressive baselines. Future work will explore scaling CanvasMAR to text-conditioned and multi-modal settings.

Appendix A
Experiment Details

Sampling Methods

As noted by
Skorokhodov et al. 2022
, the sampling procedure can significantly influence FVD scores. To ensure a fair comparison, we adhere closely to the sampling strategies used in prior work:

•

BAIR
: For each of the 256 test videos, we use the first frame as the conditioning input and the subsequent 16-frame segment as the real distribution. For each conditioning frame, we generate 100 synthetic 16-frame video clips, resulting in a total of
256\times 100
generated clips in the fake distribution.

Following
Yu et al. 2023a
, we also report the
debiased FVD
, which is computed by randomly sampling one frame from each test video as the conditioning input and defining the real distribution accordingly. This process is repeated 100 times per test video, resulting in also a total of
256\times 100
generated clips in the fake distribution. Since this approach mitigates bias, we report the debiased FVD in all cases except for system-level comparisons for fairness.

•

Kinetics-600
: We randomly sample 50K test videos from the test dataset and, for each, select a random 16-frame clips for defining the real-distribution and taking the 5-frame conditions for generating one 16-frame video clips in the fake distribution.
