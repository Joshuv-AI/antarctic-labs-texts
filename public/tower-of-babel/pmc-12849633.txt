Perceived need of information and education about conception preparedness (CP) among engaged and soon to be married couples: a qualitative survey
Authors: Richard Nsengiyumva, Olive Bazirete, Thiery Claudien Uhawenimana, Darius Gishoma, Ing-Marie Carlsson, Pamela Meharry, Haeun Lee, Lori Jody R
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreconception care, especially education, has been shown to improve maternal, neonatal, and child health outcomes. Although the journey to conception marks a significant milestone for many individuals and couples, there is a scarcity of studies on the needs of engaged and soon-to-be-married couples regarding conception preparedness (CP). This study aimed to explore the information and education needs related to conception preparedness among engaged couples on the verge of marriage.MethodsA qualitative, descriptive, and exploratory design was employed. Just one to two months away from getting married couples were recruited at premarital counseling sites, including churches and sector administration offices, between June and July 2024 and through purposive sampling 40 officially engaged couples across five provinces in Rwanda participated. With a discussion guide, focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted to gain in-depth insights from participants. Audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim data were analyzed using MAXQDA software and an inductive thematic analysis was done. Ethical clearance No. 337/CMHS-IRB/2024 was obtained from the University of Rwanda, and permissions from local administration in districts and sectors were secured.ResultsMost participants were aged 21 to 30 years and lived in rural areas. Five key themes emerged regarding conception preparedness: knowledge and understanding, motivators for seeking information, desired educational content, preferred information sources, and urgency of education. While all recognized its benefits for reducing pregnancy risks and promoting family health, many associated conception preparedness mainly with HIV testing and believed it concerned only women. Few were aware of the timing and full components of preconception care. Almost all expressed a strong interest in learning about lifestyle behaviors and medical care during this period. Health professionals were the preferred information source, with schools, churches, and social media also mentioned. Nearly all participants considered this education urgent before they get married.ConclusionA significant desire for information and education on conception preparedness was reported by most of participants with also many of them expressing various misconceptions and misbeliefs around pre-pregnancy preparedness. Therefore, targeted preconception education and awareness programs tailored specifically to engaged couples before marriage are needed.Keywords: Perceived needs, Conception, Preparedness, Information, Education, Engaged couples, Soon to be married couples, Verge of marriage

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that progress in maternal mortality has been stagnated since 2015, with 810 women dying every day from preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth worldwide [1]. Furthermore, the United Nations agency for children(UNICEF) also shows that 1.9 million stillbirth and 2.3 millions of newborn babies die within their first 28 days [2]. On the other side, despite maternal and neonatal mortality is still high in developing countries, evidence show that most of complications (75%) that develop during pregnancy and childbirth are preventable particularly if prevention starts earlier before pregnancy occurs [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for almost one third of world’s preventable maternal and newborn deaths [3].

To address the high rates of maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality, the World Health Organization and other literature emphasize that antenatal, perinatal, and postnatal care alone are insufficient to fully tackle this burden. Therefore, preconception care (PCC) is recommended as a vital intervention [4]. Preconception care, as defined in various studies, is a set of interventions aimed at identifying and modifying risks that could affect a woman’s health or pregnancy outcomes [5, 6]. Its primary goal is to improve the health of women and couples before conception by promoting behaviors and addressing factors that may lead to adverse health outcomes for both mother, neonate and child [7]. The PCC generally includes three key components: (1) risk assessment, which involves evaluating existing health conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and mental health issues that could impact pregnancy [8]; (2) lifestyle modifications, such as counseling on smoking cessation, alcohol avoidance, weight management, and healthy dietary practices to support a healthy pregnancy [9]; and (3) health education [10].

Preconception health promotion and education address topics such as nutrition, exercise, contraception, STI prevention, chronic disease management, alcohol reduction, smoking cessation, mental health and other important pre-pregnancy health related information [11]. Effective preconception education is crucial for safe motherhood and positive pregnancy outcomes by encouraging healthy behaviors, managing conditions, and promoting health-seeking actions [12],. Such interventions improve pregnancy outcomes, reduce complications, and increase maternal health service use [13]. For example, preconception education has been associated with a 57% reduction in maternal and child mortality and a 73% decrease in maternal and neonatal morbidity by addressing health issues and risky behaviors [11, 14]. It also supports higher smoking cessation rates [15], increases antenatal care attendance by 39% [11], and lowers neonatal mortality by 17% through better risk management [11, 16].

Although existing literature increasingly emphasizes the importance of assessing information and educational needs related to preconception care before designing interventions, there remains a notable gap in research on Couples’ Perceived Information and Education Needs (CPIEN), particularly in Sub-Saharan African countries. This study aimed to explore CPIEN among engaged couples approaching marriage a population often in a transitional life phase where they begin actively planning for children and family life.

Preconception health encompasses the well-being of all individuals of reproductive age, regardless of gender, sexual orientation, or intentions to conceive [17]. However, premarital couples are considered a key target group for preconception education [18]. Educating them during this period can support their reproductive and fertility goals while contributing to the reduction of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Understanding their perceived needs allows healthcare providers, reproductive and maternal health policymakers, and other stakeholders to tailor premarital education and counseling strategies to better align with couples’ expectations and realities, which ultimately, may contribute to improved reproductive, maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Methods

Study approach and design

A qualitative approach was used and focus group discussions (FGDs) design was applied to gather perceptions and insights to gain in-depth insights on soon to be married couples’ knowledge and informational gaps and needs related to preconception health and conception preparedness.

Study settings

Rwanda is made of five provinces including Kigali city, Northern, Southern, Eastern and Western province with 30 districts. This study was conducted in rural and urban areas of all five provinces in Rwanda. Each province is made of district and the subunits of district are sectors. When couples become engaged and need an official legal or religious marriage, they have to attend some premarital counseling sessions at either sector headquarters or churches. In those sessions, engaged couples are educated on how to navigate the journey of marriage, family’s economy and finances management, peaceful cohabitation, faith and spirituality, communication styles, roles in the marriage, and conflict resolution strategies and about faith and spirituality. These premarital gathering sites are scattered and located in all districts in Rwanda.

As we could not cover all premarital counseling sites in the whole country, with the aid of list of sectors published by the ministry of local governance in Rwanda (MINALOC), using a random number generator, we randomly selected one sector in each province for representativeness. The selected sites included Gisenyi in Western Province. Nyarusange (Muhanga district) in Southern Province. Ruli(Gakenke district) in Northern province. Muhura(Gatsibo district) in Eastern Province and Kinyinya(Gasabo district), in Kigali city.

Study population

The study involved individuals in committed relationships preparing for either formal legal or religious marriages in Rwanda. By law, the minimum legal age for marriage in Rwanda is 21 years [19].

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants were the following:

The inclusion criteria for participants were as follows: (1) Both male and female partners aged above 21 years, with males not exceeding 65 years and females not exceeding 48 years; (2) Participants must have been officially registered for either civil or religious marriage, with the marriage expected to take place within one to two months from the date of data collection; (3) The couple must self-identify as intending to have children after marriage. Then, exclusion included 1) old couples with male > 65 and female exceeding child bearing age. 2) Couples who do not intend to have children after marriage, 3) Individuals in re-marriages or subsequent marriages, 4) Couples where one or both partners were not willing to provide informed consent.

Sampling techniques, sample size

A total of 40 couples (8 per province) were recruited in the study. Using purposive sampling strategy 8 first couples meeting inclusion criteria at each study site were invited to participate in the study. The choice of 8 couples per province was based on qualitative research principles aiming to achieve data saturation within each region while ensuring manageable group sizes for effective focus group discussions. In addition, that sample size ensured geographic diversity, allowed for thematic saturation, and maintained feasibility within time and resource constraints enabling rich, meaningful insights into couples’ perspectives without compromising data quality.

In the recruitment process, participants were proactively recruited by the research team specifically for the focus group discussions (FGDs). Using the official lists of engaged couples registered for civil or religious marriage in selected sectors, the research assistants contacted potential participants through phone calls and community liaison officers. This purposive recruitment strategy ensured that only couples fulfilling the inclusion criteria were invited.

Data collection instrument

To obtain data, an interview guide questionnaire made of open questions was used as a tool for data collection. This questionnaire was developed drawing from a review of existing literature, along with insights gained from both professional and clinical experiences, and consultations with specialists in public health as well as in reproductive, maternal, neonatal, and child health.

In developing the questionnaire, considerations on local context as well as the connection to the study objectives were made. The initial version of the questionnaire was created in English and subsequently translated into the local language, Kinyarwanda, by a language expert. Following this, a third individual translated the questionnaire back into English to verify its internal consistency. ​Before its use, it was again reviewed by research experts to confirm its accuracy and content validity. Both language and research experts’ part of the study team to reduce bias, data misinterpretation, and communication barriers, thereby strengthening the overall quality, reliability.

The interview guide consisted of questions organized around five key discussion areas:1)Personal readiness for pregnancy: This section explored participants’ perceptions of their current level of preparedness for pregnancy, including emotional, physical, and practical aspects of getting ready to conceive.2)Knowledge and awareness of preconception care and conception preparedness: Participants were asked about what they know or have heard regarding preconception care, its importance, components, and general understanding of conception readiness.3)Information needs related to conception and preconception care: This part identified specific gaps in knowledge and the types of information participants felt they required to better prepare for pregnancy, such as nutrition, health screenings, lifestyle modifications, and risk factors.4)Current and preferred sources of information: Questions focused on where participants currently obtain information (e.g., family, healthcare providers, media) and their preferences for reliable and accessible sources for preconception care and conception preparedness education 5)Additional insights, suggestions, or concerns: This open-ended section invited participants to share any other thoughts, ideas, cultural beliefs, barriers, or concerns that might influence their engagement with preconception care and preparation for conception.

In addition to that, probes such as “give examples, clarify your answer etc. were also added in the questionnaire to enhance participants engagement, clarify vague answers, eliciting deeper and more comprehensive responses, and uncover additional insights during discussion. Furthermore, participants socio-demographic characteristics including age, marital status, education, residency, religion was annexed to help understanding the diversity and background of participants, and enhanced interpretation and depth of analysis by situating participants’ perspectives within their social and cultural contexts.

Prior data collection, a pilot interview was done on four couples in Kigali city and adjustments to the interview guide questionnaire were made prior data collection. Specifically, we refined unclear or ambiguous questions to improve clarity and ensure they were culturally appropriate and easily understood by participants. Probes were enhanced to better elicit detailed responses and minimize misinterpretation. Additionally, the sequence of questions was reorganized for more logical flow and to facilitate a smoother interview experience. Minor modifications were also made to better capture locally relevant concepts related to preconception care. During data analysis phase, the responses from pilot phase were not included as the pilot interviews served solely as a pretesting step to identify and address any limitations or challenges in the instrument and data collection procedures prior to the main study.

Data collection procedure

The data collection procedure involved meeting participants at premarital counseling sites usually sector headquarters or churches, where two separate rooms other than the usual meeting room were sought and arranged to provide safe, quiet, and comfortable settings. Two clusters per site were formed one male and one female cluster, each consisting of 8 participants to minimize power imbalances and increase participant comfort.

Two trained research assistants (TRAs) 1 male and 1 female per site facilitated the discussions based on the interview guide. They were individuals with prior experience in qualitative data collection, plus background in public health, reproductive health or maternal and child health areas. These TRAs had undergone a comprehensive training lasting 2 days, provided by the principal investigator and some of research team members experienced in qualitative research. The training covered ethical considerations, informed consent processes, facilitation skills, probing techniques, note-taking, managing group discussions, and use of audio recording equipment.

At the start of each session, eligible participants who met the inclusion criteria and consented to participate signed informed consent forms after a clear explanation of the study’s purpose and benefits. Separate permission was also obtained for audio recording, with participants informed that the discussions would be held in the local language, Kinyarwanda. Socio-demographic data were collected using a structured questionnaire, and participants were assigned codes used during the discussions to maintain confidentiality.

Then, one TRAs who served as moderator initiated the discussions by briefly reintroducing the study’s objectives, followed by questions organized according to the interview guide while the other TRAs who served as note taker ensured all planned discussion domains were addressed and documented observations on participants’ emotional expressions and group interactions, which enriched the data analysis and interpretation as such nuances were not always evident from audio recordings alone.

Focus group discussions (FGDs) lasted approximately 40 to 50 min. Probing techniques were strategically used throughout to clarify vague responses, elicit deeper insights, and enhance participant engagement. Examples of probes included requests for examples, reformulations, and clarifications.

Data analysis

The data analysis followed a six-step inductive thematic analysis process based on Naeem et al. [20], using MAXQDA version 2022 software for systematic coding and theme development.

First, audio recordings from the FGDs were transcribed verbatim in the local language (Kinyarwanda) by trained research assistants experienced in qualitative transcription. Then, professional bilingual translators with expertise in health research translated the transcripts into English to maintain accuracy and cultural relevance. Second, a qualitative research expert from the research team reviewed the transcripts for accuracy by thoroughly listening to all audio files and cross-checking them against the transcripts to ensure fidelity and completeness. Third, multiple authors including the principal investigator and co-authors independently read the entire set of English transcripts multiple times to familiarize themselves with the data, noting initial impressions and emerging themes to understand context and nuances. Fourth, field notes documenting observations, non-verbal cues, and emotions during FGDs were systematically integrated by linking them with relevant transcript excerpts, enriching the contextual understanding during analysis.

Fifth, the research team performed inductive open coding, identifying significant phrases and concepts related to preconception needs. In addition, in vivo codes using participants’ exact words were applied to retain original meaning. Sixth, similar codes were grouped into broader categories, and overarching themes were developed through iterative comparison and discussion among the research team to ensure coherence and accurate representation across the dataset. MAXQDA’s code maps tool was used to visualize relationships between codes and themes.

Finally, a comprehensive narrative report was prepared, presenting each theme with supporting excerpts from participants, enriched by contextual details from field notes.

Data quality control

The data quality control procedures implemented in this study were comprehensive and aimed at ensuring the credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability of the findings.

The key data quality control strategies implemented in this study included: Use of inductive approach where inductive qualitative analysis was done to derive themes from participants’ responses without imposing pre-existing codes. This bottom-up approach facilitated the emergence of insights from the data itself, enhancing the validity of findings. Triangulation: Methodological triangulation was employed exclusively within the focus group discussions (FGDs) using several complementary strategies. Mini-interviews were conducted within FGDs to probe individual perspectives more deeply, during discussion flexible moderation approach was applied to adapt questions and probing according to group dynamics and emerging themes, and post-FGD follow-ups with selected participants provided additional clarification and insights to enrich data. Additionally, analyst triangulation was conducted by having an independent qualitative researcher, not involved in data collection, analyze a randomly selected 20% subset of transcripts. This independent analyst held a master’s degree in public health with qualitative research expertise. The rationale for selecting 20% was to provide a representative sample for cross-validation without compromising resource efficiency. The independent review involved comparing codes generated via MAXQDA software with those created by the primary research team to identify and resolve coding discrepancies through consensus, thus enhancing coding rigor.

Participant Diversity: For this, the sampling purposive sampling approach we used to include engaged couples from diverse demographic and socio-cultural backgrounds helped exploring varying preconception education needs across different groups, strengthening the study’s transferability. Member Checking: Preliminary thematic findings and interpretations were shared with some study participants who consented to follow-up, as well as with a research team member specialized in reproductive and maternal health, to confirm that the themes accurately represented participants’ views. This iterative validation process enhanced the trustworthiness of the findings.

Training and Standardization: All data collectors and the research coordinator underwent intensive training focused on qualitative data collection techniques, including the conduct of FGDs, use of interview guides, probing strategies, and ethical considerations. Role-plays and rehearsals were integral to the training, ensuring consistency and quality across all data collection sessions.

Other strategies used for data quality control included: Documentation and Audit Trail where detailed records of research procedures, data collection, coding decisions, and analytical processes were maintained. This transparent audit trail facilitates external scrutiny and potential replication, bolstering dependability. Continuous Quality Assessment: Throughout data collection and analysis, the team regularly reviewed transcripts for accuracy and completeness by cross-checking against audio recordings. Consistency checks across transcripts and coding sets were performed to minimize errors and ambiguities. Discrepancies were addressed promptly in team meetings to preserve data integrity. Furthermore, the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) 32-item checklist for FGDs and the thematic analysis procedures we used align with qualitative research methodologies rigor as described by Braun & Clarke [21].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences, University of Rwanda (Reference No. 337/CMHS-IRB/2024). Prior to data collection, permission was also sought from the local administrative authorities at district and sector levels. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant after the study’s purpose and procedures were fully explained. Participants were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Confidentiality of information was maintained by using anonymized codes to protect participant identities. To further safeguard confidentiality, only the Principal Investigator had access to the identifiable raw data, while co-authors contributed to the data analysis by working with anonymized, coded datasets derived from the raw data.

The first theme, Basic Knowledge and Understanding of PCC/CP, highlights how participants recognize what conception preparedness entails, including its timing, benefits, and associated medical and behavioral interventions. The second theme centers on Perceived Motivators or Reasons for the desire to receive conception preparedness information, encompassing gaps in knowledge, early care needs, and concerns for protective measures toward pregnancy and family wellness.

Theme three reflects the specific content needs identified by participants concerning fertility, gender-based considerations, risk factors, and appropriate behaviors in preparation for pregnancy. The fourth theme addresses perceived preferred sources, channels, and places for conception preparedness information delivery, emphasizing health care providers and social networks as key supports. The fifth theme reflects the urgency of the need, discussing timing preferences prior to marriage and varying levels of urgency expressed by participants.

Theme no 1: Knowledge and understanding of preconception care (PCC) and conception preparedness (CP)

Participants from diverse regions and backgrounds exhibited a general understanding regarding preconception care (PCC) and conception preparedness (CP). Most participants defined CP primarily as medical check-ups conducted before pregnancy to promote a healthy mother and baby during pregnancy. For example, some participants described it as:

“It’s when a woman goes to the clinic for tests and check-ups before getting pregnant, so she can have a healthy baby later on.” Male, Southern Province FGDs N° 2; Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 6.

There was widespread awareness of the importance of screening for infections mainly HIV and syphilis before conception. Participants clearly understood the risks of not testing, especially for the baby’s health. For example, one participant noted, “If you don’t test for syphilis before pregnancy, when you get pregnant the baby may end up with serious health problems or even die inside the uterus.” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 8), while another stated, “If you don’t get checked for HIV before pregnancy and get treatment earlier, you may pass it on to your baby.” (Male, Western Province FGDs N° 5).

However, some participants especially men from rural areas were unsure about the need for testing when they felt healthy. A male participant explained, “I don’t see the point of getting tested if you feel okay and nothing seems wrong even if you are planning for pregnancy soon.” (Male, Western FGDs N° 5; Female, Northern FGDs N° 2).

Regarding lifestyle and self-care aspects of PCC, only a few participants mainly younger women from urban settings mentioned the importance of nutrition, rest, and reducing alcohol consumption. One female participant stated, “You need to eat healthy and cut down on drinking…. because what you eat and drink affects your body, and if your body isn’t ready, the pregnancy might not go well. You also need to be strong enough to carry the baby, so taking care of yourself early really matters.”” (Female, Western FGDs N° 3), and others remarked “It’s about looking after yourself getting enough rest and avoiding heavy work…….because if your body is tired or stressed, it can affect your chances of getting pregnant or giving birth a healthy baby. (Female, Southern FGDs N° 7; Female, Kigali FGDs N° 5.

Most participants saw conception preparedness (CP) as mainly a woman’s responsibility, with the focus on protecting the health of both mother and child. Many participants expressed this view clearly: “It’s what women do to get ready for pregnancy like looking after themselves and going for check-ups.” (Female, Eastern FGDs N° 2, Female, Western FGDs N° 7, Female, Northern FGDs N° 1, Male, Eastern FGDs N° 5, Male, Kigali FGDs N°8). On the other hand, only a few participants mostly from urban areas recognized that CP should involve both partners. A male participant commented, “Both the man and the woman need to get tested so the baby can be healthy.” (Male, Kigali FGDs N° 4; Female, Western FGDs N° 6).

Concerning the location and timing for seeking or starting conception preparedness, most participants mentioned public health centers and hospitals as the main places to go, although some preferred private clinics or pharmacies for convenience. Views on timing varied considerably. One participant noted, “You can start preparing for conception anytime even just two weeks before getting pregnant.” (Female, Kigali FGDs N° 6), while another suggested, “It’s better to start preparing for pregnancy about six months before, so you have enough time to get everything in order and prepare well.” (Female, Northern FGDs N° 1). No male participant at least guessed the time for seeking or starting conception preparedness.

Theme 2: Perceived motivators for conception preparedness information and education need

The majority of participants clearly expressed a strong need for accurate information about conception preparedness (CP), pointing to widespread misinformation, rumors, and traditional beliefs that often clash with modern preconception care. Many shared frustrations about not having trusted sources to help clear up confusion. For instance, a participant said, “I really want to know what’s true. I haven’t found anyone who can explain things properly. I have so many questions……. like someone told me that if a woman with negative rhesus marries a man with positive rhesus, she can’t have children…even if she gets pregnant, it ends in miscarriage. Is that even true? I need to understand this better.” (Female, Northern Province FGDs N° 1).

Traditional beliefs also caused confusion; some participants questioned whether customary preconception rituals were truly effective compared to modern medical care. For example, supported by others one participant expressed this concern as follow: “We really want to know if these traditional rituals for preparing to have children actually work. In our villages, people say that if a couple skips the customs and herbal medicines like amasubyo….they might never have kids. Some of the early marriage ceremonies focus a lot on these things. We just want to understand what’s true and what’s not.” (Female, Western Province FGDs N° 4).

Gendered misconceptions were also apparent. Several male participants believed infertility was mainly a woman’s issue, reflecting deep-rooted cultural assumptions. Emotionally supported by other participants One participant stated “I’ve always heard that if a man can perform sexually, then he can’t be infertile …….it’s only women who have fertility problems.” (Male, Eastern Province FGDs N° 6).

In addition to that, another participant shared a common myth about alcohol: “Some people say drinking alcohol boosts sexual energy and helps women have strong babies. I don’t drink myself, but I’ve heard that from friends…though I don’t really believe it.” (Male, Kigali City FGDs N° 8).

Nearly all participants across all FGDs expressed a strong desire to receive timely and accurate information about preconception care, highlighting the importance of early health checks and treatment when needed. For example, some participant remarked, “We really wish I had proper information about preconception care ….so I could get myself checked and treated early if needed.” (Female, Northern FGDs N° 1, Male, Southern FGDs N° 6). Many others echoed similar feelings, saying things like, “We want to understand how to take care of myself and what to avoid so I can get ready for pregnancy the right way.” (Male and Female participants, all FGDs).

A strong motivator for wanting information and education on conception preparedness (CP) across all FGDs was the desire to protect oneself and one’s future family from complications and poor pregnancy outcomes. Most of participants stated “When we get married, we know pregnancy may happen at some point, so we want to learn how to prepare ourself but we still haven’t found someone who can really guide us.” (Participants from multiple FGDs). Many female participants expressed pressure to avoid being blamed for fertility or pregnancy issues. For example, some stated “I really need to understand reproductive health so people don’t point fingers at me if something goes wrong. When pregnancy fails, everyone starts blaming each other.” (Female participants, Northern and Southern Provinces FGDs N° 3 and 4).

Furthermore, many participants expressed to have curiosity and gaps towards conception preparedness knowledge. They emphasized the need for clear, detailed information from qualified experts. Supported by others, one stated, “There’s probably a lot to know about preconception care, but since we’re not health workers, we really don’t know what’s important. If professionals could break it down and explain everything we need to do before getting pregnant, we’d trust them and follow their advice.” (Female, Western Province FGDs N° 6).

Theme 3: Perceived content needs for conception preparedness information and education

This theme captures the varied CP information and education content needs expressed by participants.

The majority of participants across all FGDs consistently emphasized the need for clear, comprehensive information to support healthy pregnancies and children, with fertility-related knowledge seen as the top priority. Many voiced concerns about infertility, often based on what they had observed in their communities. Most of participant stated “We really want to know how fertile we are. We’ve seen neighbors, friends, and even family members struggle to have kids for years, and it makes us worry…. what if the same thing happens to us after we get married?” (General consensus from all FGDs).

Some women expressed a strong interest in understanding how their menstrual cycle relates to fertility. For example, one participant shared, “I decided I want to get pregnant and thought it would happen right after sex. But then I heard from friends that there are only certain days when you can actually get pregnant. So now I’m confused, because it doesn’t happen every time.” (Female, Western Province FGDs N° 5). Additionally, a few women wanted more information about assisted reproductive technologies. As one stated, “I want to learn how artificial pregnancy works and what’s needed for that.” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 2; Eastern Province FGDs N° 5).

Moreover, many male participants also showed interest in understanding the best timing and approach for conception. One expressed “I want to know the right time and what I should do to help my wife get pregnant safely.” (Men, all FGDs).

Almost all male participants expressed curiosity about what men specifically should do to support a safe and healthy pregnancy beyond just HIV and syphilis testing. A common statement was: “We’d really like clear information on what a husband should do to get ready for pregnancy. So far, all we’ve been told is to test for HIV and syphilis to protect the baby….nothing more.” (Men, all FGDs). Women echoed this interest, wanting to understand both male-specific roles and shared responsibilities. For example, supported by fellows one participant stated “I want to know what a husband can do on his own, and what both of us should do together to prepare for pregnancy.” (Women, all FGDs).

Most participants requested clear information about health conditions or diseases that could negatively affect pregnancy outcomes. Some participants expressed, “We know everyone’s body is different, and we really want to understand what kinds of illnesses can make it hard to get pregnant or have a healthy baby. How do these things affect pregnancy?” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 1; Southern Province FGDs N° 4). Others were interested in learning about risky behaviors and when to begin taking preventive steps. As some stated “I want to know what health issues or habits could lead to problems during pregnancy and when is the right time to start getting ready for a healthy one.” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 4; Eastern Province FGDs N° 1).

Participants from all FGDs expressed a strong interest in learning more about specific lifestyle behaviors, diet, and medical care during conception preparedness. For instance, one participant said, “I’d really like to know which foods and drinks are good or bad when you’re getting ready to have a baby.” (Female, Eastern Province FGDs N° 6). Questions about alcohol were common, where some asked “We want to know if drinking alcohol is okay or not while preparing for pregnancy. Should I stop or is it fine to keep drinking like usual?” (Male, Southern Province FGDs N° 7; Female, Western Province FGDs N° 3). Many also wanted clear guidance on medicines and vaccines related to CP. As many participants stated “We’ve heard there are medicines and vaccines you’re supposed to get before pregnancy. We want full details what they are, when to take them, and why they matter. That kind of info would really help us.” (Participants from all FGDs).

Theme 4: Perceived and suggested sources, means and time for conception preparedness information and education

The theme four focuses on the participants ‘most preferred sources, channels, and places for conception preparedness information delivery.

All participants reported receiving some information about conception preparedness (CP) from friends and family members, but they strongly preferred getting education directly from trained health care professionals. Most viewed doctors, nurses, and midwives as the most reliable sources of information, even though accessing them could be challenging. As some of participants expressed, “I’d rather get information about conception preparedness from a doctor, nurse, or midwife than from friends or family. But honestly, it’s not always easy health workers are often busy, and waiting at the clinic can be exhausting.” (Female, Southern Province FGDs N° 1; Male, Northern Province FGDs N° 5).

While face-to-face communication with health professionals was the preferred method, many participants acknowledged that providers are often unavailable and expressed openness to receiving information through mass media channels. Some stated “I’d really prefer to get information directly from doctors or nurses, but since they’re usually busy, I’m okay with getting it from trusted radio or TV programs too.” (Female, Southern Province FGDs N° 1; Male, Northern Province FGDs N° 5). Then, a smaller number of participants pointed to social media as a fast and effective way to learn. For example, two participants stated “I wish we could get information about preconception and pregnancy through WhatsApp, Facebook, and other platforms. I actually first learned about testing before pregnancy from a WhatsApp group.” (Male, Kigali City FGDs N° 3; Female, Western Province FGDs N° 8).

There are participants who suggested that preconception education should be introduced in schools to help raise awareness early on. One participant expressed “Honestly, even though we learned about the reproductive system in school, we didn’t get real information about how to prepare for pregnancy like when to start or what steps to take even after finishing secondary school.” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 2). Others felt that premarital counseling venues, such as churches or local sector offices, could be good places to share this kind of information. As one woman explained, “If they gave us information about how to prepare for pregnancy at marriage centers like this one today, it would be really helpful especially since I came here with my partner.” (Female, Northern Province FGDs N° 8).

With regards to the timing of conception preparedness information and education (CPIE), the majority of participants recommended that it be provided as early as possible. Some felt it should begin as soon as a romantic relationship starts, given the possibility of unexpected pregnancy. As one participant stated, “I wish we could get information about how to prepare for pregnancy right when people start dating, because pregnancy can happen anytime. They should also give this education at the premarital registration office.” (Female, Southern Province FGDs N° 3). A few participants advocated for even earlier education, starting from the onset of menstruation. For example, one young woman explained, “I think girls should start learning about pregnancy preparation from the time they get their first period, because they can get pregnant from that point anything can happen.” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 5).

Theme 5: Urgency of conception preparedness information and education need

Almost all participants across focus groups discussions expressed an urgent need for conception preparedness education. This urgency was driven by a desire to understand their own health and reproductive options in order to make informed choices about family planning. For example, the most common statement was “I feel like I really need to learn about preconception right now. Knowing about my health and what options I have is super important because I want to make smart decisions about starting a family.” (General participant, multiple FGDs). Many others stressed the importance of receiving CPIE before marriage to ensure both partners are on the same page. Some other participants expressed, “I think it’s best to get preconception education before getting married… It’s important for me and my partner to understand the risks and prepare together early.” (Female, Kigali City FGDs N° 2; Male, Eastern Province FGDs N° 8).

Only a few participants saw conception preparedness information and education (CPIE) as something for the future, choosing to engage with it closer to when they plan to start a family. As one female participant from the Northern Province expressed “It’s not really urgent for me right now. I’ll think more about it when I’m actually ready to have kids.” (Female, Northern Province FGDs N° 4).

Many respondents expressed a strong desire for immediate access to conception preparedness information. One participant captured this urgency, saying, “I feel like I need this information right now. The choices I make today could affect my future, and I don’t want to wait to be ready.” (Female, Eastern Province FGDs N° 7). Others stated frustration over how hard it is to find reliable information, which only increased their eagerness. As some participants stated “It’s really frustrating that there’s so little information out there. We feel ready to start a family, and we wish we could get this education right away.” (Male, Southern Province FGDs N° 3, Female, Kigali city FGDs N° 2, Female, Northern Province FGDs N°1, Male, Western Province FGDs N° 7). On the other hand, a few participants recognized the value of CPIE but didn’t feel an immediate need. One male participant expressed “Talking about conception preparedness helped me see its long-term benefits, but I don’t think I need it just yet.” (Male, Western Province FGDs N° 3).

Discussion

This study explored the perceived need for information and education on conception preparedness (CP) among engaged couples on the verge of marriage.

With regards to socio-demographic patterns, our findings revealed that urban and younger participants demonstrated a broader understanding of the behavioral aspects of CP, while men tended to be less informed or skeptical about early preconception care (PCC) when asymptomatic. Predominantly, CP was viewed as a female-centered responsibility, with limited male involvement, highlighting the need for targeted, culturally sensitive education encouraging shared reproductive health responsibilities. In addition to that, as majority of our participants were Christian churches adherent, fertility and safe pregnancy were commonly perceived by some participants as a divine blessing.

In consistence with findings by Khan et al. [22], almost all of our study participants expressed openness and positive impressions of pre-pregnancy preparation but simultaneously reported insufficient knowledge and an urgent desire for timely preconception information and education (CPIE). This contrasts with Foussi and Mandura’s findings [23] among healthcare providers, who demonstrated higher knowledge levels likely influenced by health literacy. Our study underscores two key issues: recognition of PCC benefits and a notable information deficit stemming from community rumors, traditional rituals, and religious beliefs. Such misinformation fosters misconceptions about fertility and pregnancy, risking inadequate preparation and adverse outcomes. Similar trends of low knowledge and high demand for preconception information have been documented in Nigeria, Japan, and Malawi [23, 24]. Addressing these gaps can empower engaged couples to make informed reproductive health decisions.

We found that motivations for seeking CPIE were fueled by participants’ proactive desire to ensure safe pregnancies and healthy offspring, reflecting appreciation for advance planning benefits. Comparable findings have emerged from Australia, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa, where preconception education was seen as integral to pregnancy readiness [22, 23]. The imminent marital transition in our sample likely heightened sensitivity to fertility concerns, consistent with literature showing marriage often catalyzes reproductive reflections [25]. Additionally, in many African cultures, early procreation is socially emphasized post-marriage [26], potentially driving the expressed urgency.

Concerning information and education content, participants prioritized information on pre-pregnancy individual behaviors, attitudes, and lifestyle modifications over medical care aspects. This reflects eagerness to adopt beneficial changes for fertility and pregnancy well-being. Our results align with Schoenaker et al. [27], who highlighted strong interest in healthy pre-pregnancy behavior knowledge. However, behavior change alone is insufficient without awareness of the full spectrum of PCC, including laboratory testing and preventive treatments such as immunization and folic acid supplementation [28]. The knowledge gap regarding medical components is notable, contrasting with developed nations where comprehensive preconception education frequently includes medical screening and genetic counseling [29]. The disparity likely reflects resource limitations impacting healthcare system capacities and educational outreach in low-resource settings.

Alarmingly, our findings revealed that conception preparedness (CP) was predominantly perceived as a female-centered concern, with the majority of participants, including women themselves, defining CP primarily as preparatory activities undertaken by women before pregnancy. This perception aligns with Rwanda’s patriarchal social structure and mirrors observations in other low-income countries where cultural norms often marginalize men’s roles in reproductive health [30, 31]. Studies from Zimbabwe and Malawi similarly report limited male involvement in reproductive health programs [31, 32].In contrast, high-income countries tend to adopt more inclusive approaches that actively encourage male participation in preconception care and reproductive life planning [33, 34].

Nevertheless, low awareness and preparation levels among men have been documented even in developed contexts, such as Sweden [35]. Our findings underscore a critical policy and educational gap advocating for male engagement and challenging restrictive gender norms to foster shared fertility responsibilities.

Regarding information sources, we revealed that most of participants primarily trusted healthcare professionals, especially midwives and doctors, but faced access challenges. This mirrors reproductive health service gaps in many low- and middle-income countries [36]. Participants’ suggestions to use social media and schools as additional education avenues align with evidence supporting multimedia efficacy in sexual and reproductive health promotion [37]. Though few participants mentioned schools, integrating preconception health into school curricula is endorsed in the literature to build early awareness [27]. Exploration of feasibility and context-specific adaptation of these strategies is warranted to effectively enhance population-wide preconception knowledge and preparedness.

Strength and limitation of the study

We explored both male and female partner’s perceived needs which is different from other many studies that involved only females. This inclusiveness helps illuminate differences and similarities in the perceived needs of each partner. Also, insights gained from interviewing both partners allowed researchers to explore the complex interplay of shared and individual experiences regarding conception preparedness. These insights contribute to a holistic understanding of the challenges and needs that couples face in the context of their relationship dynamics, which is often overlooked in women-only studies.

Our study was conducted in a country where the comprehensive preconception care program does not exist. This might have affected the results of our study particularly on knowledge and awareness. It might have also lead participants to express varied and potentially misguided perceptions of their CP needs since they had limited information resources to recognize their actual reproductive health needs as argued by Lassi et al., [38]. Consequently, this can affect generalizability of the findings to populations in different contexts, especially that where comprehensive preconception care is a routine or standard practice. However, this study is also an opportunity as our results can be applied to other settings with similar characteristics to ours. Furthermore, the study offers valuable insights and serves as an opportunity for stakeholders to address the gaps in preconception care. The findings could serve as a catalyst for discussions regarding the establishment of comprehensive preconception care programs within the country. Highlighting the perceived needs identified by participants can inform policymakers and health practitioners about critical areas where intervention is necessary.

Also, only one site was selected in each province, the findings may not be generalized to all couples in other premarital counseling sites in the whole country. Moreover, majority of study participants lived in rural areas. This may have impacted the study results as rural areas undergo different information difficulties due to geographical patterns and low information and technology infrastructures. Also, rural couples often face unique socio-cultural dynamics that differ markedly from their urban counterparts. For instance, societal norms and family structures in rural settings may emphasize traditional values regarding marriage and parenting, which could skew the respondents’ perceptions toward more conservative or conventional views on conception. Thus, the insights garnered from a rural population may not accurately reflect the diverse attitudes and needs present in urban areas, where individuals often have more exposure to a variety of reproductive health educational opportunities, and cultural perspectives.

All these may affect preconception health literacy and cause risks of overlooking the nuanced needs of couples as found in other studies [39, 40].But, on the other side, this may be an opportunity that can help to shape educational intervention accordingly. Future research should aim to include a more balanced representation of both rural and urban couples.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study revealed that in overall, couples demonstrated limited knowledge, awareness, and held various misconceptions and misbeliefs around conception preparedness among soon to be married couples in Rwanda, and a significant desire for information and education on conception preparedness was reported by participants. To address these gaps, it is recommended to initiate and design targeted preconception education programs tailored specifically to engaged couples before marriage. Such programs may include individual preconception care counseling, the integration of preconception preparedness guidelines into premarital education, and the establishment of dedicated preconception healthcare services or clinics.

Furthermore, as our study focused solely on those in formal relationships, future research should also explore awareness and educational needs among individuals/couples in informal relationships to support inclusive reproductive health interventions in all reproductive aged population.
