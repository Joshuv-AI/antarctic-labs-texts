1.
Introduction

The class number of an algebraic number field plays a vital role in number theory.
An important problem concerning class number of a number field is to have an understanding of the size of the class number. Let
h_{K}
denote the class number of the number field
K.
Let
d
be the fundamental discriminant of the number field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
. Assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH), Littlewood
[
12
]
proves

(1.1)

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})}\leq\left(\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{\pi}+\text{o}(1)\right)\sqrt{|d|}\log\log(|d|),

where
\gamma
denotes the Euler-Mascheroni constant. Under the same hypothesis, Littlewood shows the existence of infinitely many fundamental
discriminants
d
such that

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})}\geq\left(\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{\pi}+\text{o}(1)\right)\sqrt{|d|}\log\log(|d|).

For positive discriminants, one can show that the bound

(1.2)

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})}\leq\left(4e^{\gamma}+\text{o}(1)\right)\sqrt{d}\frac{\log\log(d)}{\log d},

holds under GRH. Montgomery and Weinberger
[
13
]
show that this bound cannot be improved for real quadratic fields,
apart from the value of the constant and an unconditional result without GRH.
They prove that there exist infinitely
many real quadratic fields

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})

such that

(1.3)

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})}\gg\sqrt{d}\frac{\log\log(d)}{\log d}.

Lamzouri
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
improved the result by Montgomery and Weinberger by showing the following. Let
x
be large.
There are at least
x^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\log\log x}}
and at most
x^{\frac{1}{2}+\text{o}(1)}

real quadratic fields
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})
with discriminant

d\leq x
such that

(1.4)

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})}\geq\left(2e^{\gamma}+\text{o}(1)\right)\sqrt{d}\frac{\log\log(d)}{\log d}.

Assuming GRH and Artin’s conjecture for Artin L-functions, Duke
[
6
, Theorem 1]
proves an analogue lower bound like equation (
1.3
) for more general number fields. To be precise, Duke considers the set
\mathcal{K}_{n}
consisting of totally real number fields of degree
n
whose Galois closures have
S_{n}
as their Galois group.
[
6
, Theorem 1]
states that
there is a constant
c>0
depending only on
n
such that there exist
K\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
with
arbitrarily large discriminant
d
for which

h_{K}>c\sqrt{d}\left(\frac{\log\log d}{\log d}\right)^{n-1}.

Daileda
[
5
, Theorem 1]
proves
an unconditional version of Duke’s result for
\mathcal{K}_{3}.

He further
[
5
, Theorem 3]
shows that there is an absolute constant
c>0
such that

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d})}\geq c\sqrt{|d|}\frac{\log\log|d|}{\log|d|}.

In 2023, Cherubini, Fazzari, Granville, Kala and Yatsyna
[
4
]
prove that for a given positive integer
k,
there are at
least
x^{1/2-\text{o}(1)}
integers
1\leq d\leq x
such that for all
j=1,\dots,k,

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d+j})}\gg_{k}\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\log d}\log\log d.

To generalize the above, recently Byeon and Yhee
[
3
]
show the following: Given a positive integer
k,
there are at least
x^{1/3-\text{o}(1)}
integers
1\leq d\leq x
such that the consecutive pure cubic fields
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d+1})
, …,
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d+k})
have arbitrary large class number. To be precise, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
k
be a fixed positive integer. There are atleast
x^{1/3-\text{o}(1)}
integers
1\leq d\leq x
such that the class number

of the pure cubic field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d+j})
satisfy

h_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{d+j})}\gg_{k}\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\log d}\log\log d,

for all

Let

2.
A Proposition

Let
F
be a number field and
E
be a degree
n
extension of
F
with Galois closure
\hat{E}
. Let
G=\text{Gal}(\hat{E}/F)
and
\pi
be a finite dimensional complex representation of
G.
For a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
in
F

I_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
\sigma_{\mathfrak{p}}
denote the inertia group and Frobenius element for an ideal
\mathfrak{P}
of
\hat{E}
lying over
\mathfrak{p}.
Let the space corresponding to
\pi
is
V
and
V^{I_{\mathfrak{p}}}
denote the subspace of
V
fixed by
I_{\mathfrak{p}}.

We define
L_{\mathfrak{p}}(s,\hat{E}/F,\pi)=\det\left(I-\pi(\sigma_{\mathfrak{p}})|_{V^{I_{\mathfrak{p}}}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}\right)^{-1}.

The Artin L-function associated to
\pi
is given by

(2.1)

L(s,\hat{E}/F,\pi)=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}}L_{\mathfrak{p}}(s,\hat{E}/F,\pi).

If the degree of
\pi
is
{n^{\prime}},
then
L_{\mathfrak{p}}(s,\hat{E}/F,\pi)
takes the form

\prod_{i=1}^{{n^{\prime}}}\left(1-\alpha_{i}(\mathfrak{p})N(\mathfrak{p})^{-s}\right)^{-1}

where
\alpha_{i}(\mathfrak{p})
are either
|G|
-th roots of unity (see
[
15
, Chapter VII (10.1)]
or
0
and
N(\mathfrak{p})
denotes the absolute norm of
\mathfrak{p}.

In particular, for
F=\mathbb{Q},
on expanding the Euler product given by equation (
2.1
) as a Dirichlet series, we have

(2.2)

L(s,\hat{E}/F,\pi)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(m)}{m^{s}}.

For an unramified rational prime
p
in
\hat{E},

{\lambda^{\prime}}(p)=\text{Tr}(\pi(\sigma_{p})).

Hence if
p
splits completely in
\hat{E}
, then
\sigma_{p}
is trivial and

(2.3)

{\lambda^{\prime}}(p)={n^{\prime}}.

We have

(2.4)

\lambda^{\prime}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}\alpha_{i}(p)

for all rational prime
p
and
|\lambda^{\prime}(p)|\leq{n^{\prime}}.

Let
H=\text{Gal}(\hat{E}/E).
Action of
G
on the coset space
G/H
gives rise to an
n-
dimensional complex (permutation) representation
\rho
of
G.
This representation is induced from the trivial representation of
H
which implies

(2.5)

L(s,\hat{E}/F,\rho)=L(s,\hat{E}/E,1_{H})=\zeta_{E}(s).

Let us take

, where

-th root of unity. Using

On keeping equation (

.

2.1.
Upper bound for the regulator of
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{a}\right)

Let
L^{\prime}
be a number field with
[L^{\prime}:\mathbb{Q}]=n.
Let
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r_{1}}
denote the real embeddings of
L^{\prime}.
Let
\sigma_{r_{1}+1},\dots,\sigma_{r_{1}+r_{2}}
denote all pairwise complex non-conjugate embeddings of
L^{\prime}.
We have
r_{1}+2r_{2}=n
and let
r_{1}+r_{2}-1=r^{\prime}.
Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{r^{\prime}}
be a fundamental system of generators for the unit group of
L
modulo roots of unity.
Given
x\in L^{\prime\times},
define

l_{i^{\prime}}(x)=\begin{cases}|\sigma_{i^{\prime}}(x)|,\text{ if $1\leq i^{\prime}\leq r_{1}$}\\
|\sigma_{i^{\prime}}(x)|^{2},\text{ if $r_{1}+1\leq i^{\prime}\leq r_{1}+r_{2}$}.\end{cases}

Consider the matrix
A_{L^{\prime}}=(a_{i^{\prime},j})
of order
(r+1)\times r,
where
a_{i^{\prime},j}=\log(l_{i^{\prime}}(u_{j})).
The determinant of the matrix obtained by deleting any one of the row of
A
is defined to the regulator of
L^{\prime}
and denoted by
R_{L^{\prime}}.

One of the key ingredients of
[
3
, Proposition 2.2]
, which gives us a sharp bound for the regulator of the field of the form
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[3]{n^{3}+r}\right)
with
n,r\in\mathbb{N},

is the existence of units of certain form. To be precise, we see that in the proof of
[
3
, Proposition 2.2]

uses the following fact: If
r|3n^{3}
, then
\frac{r}{(\omega-n)^{3}}
is a unit in
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[3]{n^{3}+r}\right),
where
\omega=\sqrt[3]{n^{3}+r}.
Since
r_{1}+r_{2}-1=1
for both the cases
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt{n^{2}+r}\right)
and
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[3]{n^{3}+r}\right)
, it is sufficient to find a unit
u
in each field and bound the regulator (which is asymptotic to
\log u
) by bounding the unit. For our case of
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right),
there are
\frac{l-1}{2}
many fundamental units, all of which can not be written explicitly, making it difficult to get a sharp upper bound for the regulator like
[
3
, Proposition 2.2]
.
We do, however, have an explicit value of a unit for the field
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right).
Since
l
is a prime number and
r|ln^{l-1},
from
[
8
]
we see that
u=\frac{r}{(\tilde{\omega}-n)^{l}}
is a unit in
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)
where
\tilde{\omega}=\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}.
Following along
[
3
, Proposition 2.2]
, we see that
u=\text{O}\left((n^{l}+r)^{l-1}\right).

We do believe that the other remaining
\frac{l-3}{2}
units could hopefully be shown to
\text{O}\left((n^{l}+r)^{l-1}\right),
which would help to get a sharp bound of
\text{O}(n^{l}+r)
(an analogue of
[
3
, Proposition 2.2]
) for
R_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)}
and remove Hypothesis
1
.
However, at this point, we do not have much information about other
\frac{l-3}{2}
units; so we choose to
state the best known, i.e. Landau’s bound (see
[
16
]
) on regulators instead.

Proposition 2.2

(Landau)
.

Let
l
be a prime number and
n,r
be positive integers. Let
R_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)}
denote the regulator for the pure cubic field
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)
and
D_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)}
denote the absolute value of its discriminant. Then

R_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)}=\text{O}\left(\sqrt{D_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)}}\log^{l-1}D_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{n^{l}+r}\right)}\right).

3.
Two lemmas

In this section, we prove two main lemmas analogous to Lemma 3.1 and Lemma 3.2 in
[
3
]
.
These two lemmas are
crucial to prove Theorem
2
.
First, we consider some notation involving
k,l.
. Recall that
l
is a fixed odd prime number greater than or equal to
3
.
Let
k
be a fixed natural number. Let
P=\text{lcm}(1,\,\dots\,,k)
,
\Delta(m)=(mP)^{l},
and
P_{j}=\frac{P}{j}.
If
\Delta(m)\leq x,
then
m\leq\frac{x^{1/l}}{P}.
Let
M=\frac{x^{1/l}}{P}
and

q=\prod_{k<p\leq(\log M)^{\epsilon}}p,

where
p
is a rational prime.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
0<\epsilon<1
be given and
D_{j}
be the absolute value of the discriminant of the
pure field
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}\right)
. Then for all
j=1,\dots,k,
there exists
m_{0}(\text{mod}\,\,q)
such that if
m\equiv m_{0}
(mod

q)
, then for all primes
p\equiv 1(\text{mod}

l)
with
l^{2k}(lk+1)^{2}<p\leq(\log M)^{\epsilon},
we have

\left(\frac{D_{j}}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1

where
p=\pi\bar{\pi}
in
Q(\sqrt{-l})
and
(\frac{\cdot}{\cdot})_{l}
is the
l
th power residue symbol (see
[
7
, Chapter VI, Exercise 9]
). Moreover, we can
take
m_{0}\equiv 0
(mod
p)
for all primes
p
with
k<p\leq l^{2k}(lk+1)^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime
\equiv 1(\text{mod}

l)
with
k<p\leq(\log M)^{\epsilon}
. Note that
P
is invertible modulo
p>k.
Hence there are
l
times as many nonzero classes of
m_{0}
(mod
p
) satisfying

(3.1)

\left(\frac{(m_{0}P)^{l}+1}{\pi}\right)_{l}=\left(\frac{(m_{0}P)^{l}+2}{\pi}\right)_{l}=\dots=\left(\frac{(m_{0}P)^{l}+k}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1

as the number of
n\equiv(m_{o}P)^{l}
modulo
p
with

(3.2)

\left(\frac{n}{\pi}\right)_{l}=\left(\frac{n+1}{\pi}\right)_{l}=\dots=\left(\frac{n+k}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1.

For
j=0,\dots,k,
let
r_{j}\in\{0,1,\dots,l-1\},
and consider the polynomial

Q_{(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})}(X)=\prod_{j=0}^{k}(X+j)^{r_{j}}.

On applying
[
9
, Theorem 11.23]
for each
(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})\neq(0,\dots,0),
for the sum

S_{(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})}=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\left(\frac{Q_{(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})}(n)}{\pi}\right)_{l},

we obtain
|S_{(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})}|\leq k\sqrt{p}.
Hence the number of solutions modulo
p
to the equation (
3.2
) is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{l^{k+1}}\sum_{n=1}^{p-k-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(1+\left(\frac{n+j}{\pi}\right)_{l}+\left(\frac{n+j}{\pi}\right)^{2}_{l}+\dots\left(\frac{n+j}{\pi}\right)^{l-1}_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{l^{k+1}}\sum_{n=1}^{p}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{t=0}^{l-1}\left(\frac{n+j}{\pi}\right)^{t}_{l}+O(k+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{l^{k+1}}+\frac{1}{l^{k+1}}\sum_{(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})\neq(0,\dots,0)}S_{(r_{0},\dots,r_{k})}+O(k+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{l^{k+1}}+O\left(k\sqrt{p}+k+1\right).

Therefore there is atleast one
m_{0}
(mod
p
) satisfying equation (
3.1
) provided
p\geq l^{2k}(lk+1)^{2}
. Using the Chinese remainder theorem, we can obtain at least one
residue class (mod
q
).
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
m_{0}
(mod
q)
be as given by Lemma
3.1
. For
M^{1-o(1)}
integers with
1\leq m\leq M
and
m\equiv m_{0}
(mod

for all

.

Proof.

Let
S_{j}^{l}
and
s_{j}^{l}
be the largest

\Delta(m)+j
and
j
respectively. Using
[
10

D_{j}=l^{l-2}\prod_{r|(\Delta(m)+j)}r^{l-1}

or
l^{l}\prod_{r|(\Delta(m)+j)}r^{l-1}.

For the case
S_{j}=s_{j}

D_{j}\geq\frac{\Delta(m)+j}{S_{j}^{l}}=\frac{\Delta(m)+j}{s_{j}^{l}}\geq\frac{\Delta(m)}{k^{l}}.

Hence we will show that for

1\leq m\leq M
and

(mod

On letting

Following along the proof of

(mod

Using similar sieving argument as in the proof of

For a fixed

4.
Proof of Main theorem

We now give a proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
, there are
x^{\frac{1}{l}-\text{o}(1)}
integers
1\leq\Delta(m)\leq x
such that
D_{j}\gg_{k}\Delta(m)
and
\left(\frac{D_{j}}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1
, for all primes
p\equiv 1
(mod
l
) and
l^{2k}(lk+1)^{2}<p\leq(\log M)^{\epsilon}
and for all
j=1,\dots,k.

Using Proposition
2.1
,with at most
\text{O}(x^{\frac{1}{4}})
exceptions, we see that

\log L(1,\tilde{\pi}_{D_{j}})=\sum_{p\leq(\log D_{j})^{\epsilon}}\frac{\lambda(p)}{p}+\text{O}_{\epsilon}(1).

If
p\equiv 1(\text{mod}\,\,l)
and
\left(\frac{D_{j}}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1,
then
p
splits completely in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}).
From equation (
2.3
) we get
\lambda(p)=l-1
for complete splitting.
Using

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq(\log D_{j})^{\epsilon},\\
p\equiv 1(\text{mod}\,l)\\
\left(\frac{D_{j}}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1\end{subarray}}\frac{l-1}{p}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq(\log D_{j})^{\epsilon},\\
p\equiv 1(\text{mod}\,l)\\
\left(\frac{D_{j}}{\pi}\right)_{l}=1\end{subarray}}^{\prime}\ \frac{\lambda(p)}{p}\gg_{k}\log\log\log x,

gives us
\log L(1,\tilde{\pi}_{D_{j}})\gg_{k}\log\log\log x.

It now follows from the class number formula that

(4.1)

h_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}\right)}=\frac{\sqrt{D_{j}}L(1,\tilde{\pi}_{D_{j}})}{R_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}\right)}}\gg_{k}\frac{\sqrt{\Delta(m)}\log\log\Delta(m)}{R_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}\right)}},

for all
j=1,\dots,k.

The proof is now complete by using Hypothesis
1
on the regulator of
\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}\right)
for all
j
.
∎

Remark 5

(Necessity of Hypotheis
1
)
.

Using Proposition
2.2
in equation (
4.1
), we get

h_{\mathbb{Q}\left(\sqrt[l]{\Delta(m)+j}\right)}\gg_{k}\frac{\log\log\Delta(m)}{\log^{l-1}\Delta(m)},

which is a triviality since
\frac{\log\log\Delta(m)}{\log^{l-1}\Delta(m)}\rightarrow 0
as
\Delta(m)\rightarrow\infty.

This implies Proposition
2.2
(Landau’s bound) will not suffice to ensure an arbitrarily large class number.
Thus, assuming Hypothesis
1
is necessary. For completeness, we note that we need an upper bound that is at least a factor reduction of
\frac{\log\log D_{j}}{\log^{l-1}D_{j}}
in Landau bound.
