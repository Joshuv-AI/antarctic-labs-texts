Introduction

End-stage ankle osteoarthritis (OA) is often associated with prior trauma1, unlike the hip and knee, where primary idiopathic OA is more common2,3. The mechanical stability of the ankle joint, with the talus constrained by fibula, tibial plafond, medial malleolus, and strong ligamentous attachments, may partly explain the lower prevalence of primary OA4. Nevertheless, ankle joint mechanics can be greatly disrupted after trauma, what could explain why approximately 70% of end-stage ankle OA have a post-traumatic origin1, including tibial shaft fractures, pilon fractures, talus fractures, ankle fractures, ankle dislocations, and ankle sprains1,5.

Currently, there is limited knowledge regarding specific initial injuries and the onset of end-stage ankle post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA)4. This retrospective study aimed to explore the precedent causes of end-stage ankle OA, focusing on identifying specific injury patterns that lead to end-stage ankle PTOA. The study also aimed determining time between injury and need for definitive surgical treatment, such as ankle fusion (AF) or total ankle replacement (TAR). Our hypotheses were that higher energy and more severe injuries would be the primary factors contributing to ankle PTOA and that individuals with more severe injuries would require AF/TAR in a shorter timeframe after the injury.

Materials and Methods

In this retrospective cohort study approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB), we conducted an analysis of patients who underwent AF or TAR for end-stage OA at a single tertiary institution over a 20-year period (January 2000-January 2020). Electronic medical records were reviewed using current procedural terminology codes for AF or AR (27870, 27702). The study included adult patients (18 years or older) who underwent AF or TAR, with exclusion criteria consisting of prisoners/inmates, individuals with previous fusions in the foot and ankle, history of Charcot arthropathy, unclear injury type, and patients who underwent tibiotalocalcaneal fusion. Initially, clinical data from all included patients were collected and, similarly to the work of Valderrabano et al.5, patients were classified into 3 groups to determine the underlying cause of ankle OA: post-traumatic (documented limb injury), primary/idiopathic (no specific cause identified), or secondary (nontraumatic factors such as infection or avascular necrosis).

In cases of ankle PTOA, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of initial injury radiographs to identify the underlying causes. We classified the etiologies of PTOA into specific groups such as tibial shaft fractures, tibial pilon fractures, rotational ankle fractures, talar fractures, and single/recurrent ankle sprains. Secondary causes of ankle OA were further categorized into groups including infection/septic joint, systemic disorders (Charcot arthropathy, rheumatoid arthritis, hemophilic arthropathy), congenital deformities, and foot/ankle deformities (cavovarus or progressive collapsing foot deformity).

Three independent and blinded observers, 2 fellowship-trained orthopedic foot and ankle surgeons and one fifth year orthopedic resident, used established classification systems to subcategorize the traumatic injuries (appendix). All fractures were staged based on Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen (AO)/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) classification6. In addition, pilon fractures were staged with Ruedi and Allgower7, rotational ankle fractures were classified with Lauge-Hansen and Weber systems8,9, and talar fractures were staged using the anatomical classification system10. When there was any disagreement between the 3 readers regarding the staging of the traumatic ankle injuries, the 3 observers revised the disagreements together and defined the classification to be used by consensus.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and reported as mean with 95% confidence intervals (CI) or median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The primary outcome was the time interval from index injury to the need for ankle fusion or total ankle replacement (AF/TAR). A multivariate linear regression model evaluated the independent association between clinically relevant demographic, injury-related, and radiographic variables and degeneration time. Variables were selected for inclusion in the multivariate model based on clinical relevance and univariate association with the outcome. Model assumptions were assessed by evaluation of residual normality, linearity, and multicollinearity. In addition, a partition prediction model was used to explore interactions and identify potential predictive thresholds. Variables demonstrating significant association in the multivariate analysis were further examined using appropriate univariate methods (Student's t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, linear regression). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 491 limbs from 479 patients were included, 264 male (55%) and 215 female patients (45%). Ankle OA was observed in 258 right-sided (53%) and 233 left-sided limbs (47%). The mean age and body mass index (BMI) at the time of the TAR/AF were 57.8 years (95% CI 56.6-59; range 18-93) and 32.5 kg/m2 (95% CI 31.9-33.1; range 17.3-79.5), respectively. Ninety-three patients were found to be diabetic (19.4%), 178 patients had history of smoking (37.2%), 31 had systemic inflammatory disease (6.5%), and 60 patients had peripheral neuropathy (12.5%). In this cohort, 256 patients (52.1%) underwent TAR, and 235 (47.9%) underwent AF. Among these, 399 limbs (81.3%) were identified as PTOA, 47 ankles (9.6%) primary OA, and 45 ankles (9.1%) secondary OA.

The most common reason for ankle OA was rotational ankle fractures (n = 188, 38.3%), followed by ankle sprains (n = 110, 22.4%), pilon fractures (n = 61, 12.4%), idiopathic/primary (n = 47, 9.6%), secondary (n = 45, 9.2%), tibial shaft fractures (n = 23, 4.7%), and talar fractures (n = 12, 2.5%) (Fig. 2). Among the 399 PTOA, 188 were ankle fractures (47.1%), 87 multiple ankle sprains (21.8%), 61 pilon fractures (15.3%), 23 isolated ankle sprains (5.8%), 23 tibiofibular shaft fractures (5.8%), 12 talar neck/body fractures (3%), 4 combined ankle/talar fractures (1%), and 1 ankle dislocation (<1%) (Fig. 2).

For pilon fractures (n = 46) with AO/OTA system, common patterns were 43C3 (n = 19, 41.3%) and 43C1 (n = 12, 26.1%). Using Ruedi-Algower, type III was the most common (n = 34, 73.9%), followed by type II (n = 12, 26.1%). For tibiofibular/tibial shaft fractures (n = 22) using AO/OTA, the most common were 42A1 and 42A2 (n = 7, 32%, each). Regarding talar fractures (n = 11), same frequency for body and neck was found (n = 7, 50%, each) (Fig. 3). Ankle sprains were found to be mostly recurrent (n = 87, 79.1%), followed by isolated (n = 23, 20.9%).

Secondary etiologies accounted for approximately 9% (n = 45) of the total cases (Fig. 2), with rheumatoid arthritis being the most common cause (n = 17, 37.8%), followed by flatfoot/progressive collapsing foot deformity (n = 5, 5.1%), septic arthritis and talar osteochondral defect (n = 4, 8.9%, each), and cavovarus deformity and hemophilic arthropathy (n = 3 ankles, 6.7%, each).

Of the 399 ankles with PTOA, we were able to calculate time from initial injury to end-stage ankle OA (AF/TAR date) in 311 cases (77.9%). Combined ankle and talar fracture (4 cases) and a single case of ankle dislocation took 6 and 5.7 years, respectively, from injury to AF/TAR (Table I). Discussion

This study found that most patients (81%) who underwent treatment with either AF or TAR for end-stage ankle arthritis at a tertiary institution over a 20-year period had a post-traumatic etiology. Rotational ankle fractures and single/recurrent ankle sprains were the most common forms of trauma identified in this cohort, followed by pilon fractures. Although high-energy injuries, including pilon and talus fractures, lead to end-stage ankle arthritis more rapidly, these were relatively less common injuries. These findings suggest that low-energy ankle injuries not always have a benign long-term course, and, given their high incidence, account for most cases of ankle OA.

Our research findings closely mirrored those of Valderrabano et al.5 that included 390 patients (406 ankles) with a prevalence of PTOA at 78%. The most common causes of PTOA in both studies were ankle fractures (39% vs. 38.3% in ours), ankle ligamentous injuries/instability (16% vs. 22.4%), pilon fractures (14% vs. 12.4%), and tibial shaft fractures (5% vs. 4.7%). Interestingly, our study found a slightly higher proportion of primary OA cases (9% vs. 9.6% in ours) compared with secondary OA cases (13% vs. 9.1%). This study also supports data presented by Saltzman et al.1 who studied 639 patients between 1991 and 2004. They found that 70% of cases were due to prior trauma and 7.2% were primary ankle OA. Neither previous studies have examined the timeframe from injury to definitive surgery or identified the specific injuries associated with PTOA1,5. However, both previous cohorts and ours emphasized the significant prevalence of low-energy ankle injuries, including rotational ankle fractures and ankle sprains, as the leading causes of PTOA. While high-energy ankle injuries are recognized for their rapid induction of arthritis due to extensive chondrocyte death at the time of injury11-13, the specific contribution of initial cartilage damage versus abnormal tibiotalar contact pressures resulting from residual instability, malreduction, or deformity in low-energy ankle injuries remains uncertain and requires additional research.14-21

Previous research has sought to measure the incidence PTOA in patients with ankle fractures, but studying this phenomenon is difficult due to the extended period before symptoms manifest. A 1985 prospective study of 321 consecutive cases of ankle fracture dislocations reported a 14% PTOA rate22. A retrospective series by Lübbeke et al. in 2012 included 373 ankle fractures who underwent ORIF between 1988 and 1997 and found advanced radiographic OA in 31.3%, at a mean follow up of 17.9 years23. Significant risk factors included Weber C fractures, associated medial malleolar fracture, fracture-dislocation, increased BMI, and age >30 years. However, not all those patients required AF/TAR. This study is believed to be the first of its kind in the literature to determine the arthritic degeneration time following different traumatic ankle injuries (16.9 years; range 0.2-59.9). Although this was calculated only in the universe of patients requiring surgery, degenerative time was shorter after high-energy injuries such as pilon (8.1 years) and talar fractures (14 years) compared with low-energy rotational injuries such as ankle fractures (17.1 years) and ankle sprains (25.7 years). Type of injury, age, smoking status, and peripheral neuropathy were the 4 variables assessed that influenced the time from index injury (R2: 34%; p < 0.0001)

There are several limitations to this study. First, the retrospective design of the study introduces uncertainty regarding the progression to end-stage OA in patients with ankle fractures and sprains. The long latency period between initial injury and the development of PTOA may lead to inaccuracies in patient recall of date or the presence of an injury. A significant constraint was that only patients requiring a TAR or an AF were evaluated, potentially leading to an overestimation of the calculated degeneration time. In addition, the study did not consider reduction, which can affect the development of degenerative joint changes. Classification disagreements were resolved by consensus, reason why inter-rater reliability was not assessed, but potentially introducing evaluator bias. Furthermore, the study was conducted on a cohort of patients from a single tertiary care institution, which may limit its generalizability. As previously mentioned, the study population included only patients who required AF or TAR, which introduced a selection bias. Despite the limitations, this study is one of the few shedding lights on the causes of end-stage ankle OA. It is noteworthy for being the first to classify initial injury patterns using widely accepted classifications. All patients included in the study had symptomatic arthritis that required definitive treatment in the form of fusion or replacement. The study was conducted at the sole tertiary institution in the state, ensuring that most patients received both their initial trauma care and definitive treatment at the same hospital. This allowed for long-term follow-up, providing valuable insights into the development of end-stage ankle OA over time.

Conclusion

Rotational ankle fractures and ankle sprains were identified as the most common traumatic injuries leading to PTOA, with high-energy injuries, such as pilon and talar fractures, associated with a more rapid development of end-stage arthritis. These findings suggest that low-energy rotational injuries do not always carry a benign course. This study highlights key epidemiological features and risk factors for the development of post-traumatic end-stage ankle OA.
