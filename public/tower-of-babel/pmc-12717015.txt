Huge Saccular Ascending Aortic True and False Aneurysms—Chalk and Cheese
Authors: Hisato Takagi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhen ascending aortic aneurysm is saccular and the histories associated with false aneurysm do not exist, imaging differential diagnosis whether true or false aneurysm is generally difficult, and definitive diagnosis can be done merely with histopathological examinations of the aneurysm wall.

The wall of a true aneurysm consists of all three original layers (i.e., intima, media, and adventitia). In contrast, that of a false aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm) comprises merely one or two layers with the rest of the wall made of connective tissue. Despite the incontrovertibly distinct definitions, however, these two clinical entities may sometimes be indistinguishable in diagnostic images and intraoperative findings.

Three‐dimensional CT imaging in an asymptomatic 70‐year‐old woman revealed a huge saccular ascending aortic aneurysm just above (distal to) the ostium of the right coronary artery (with the ostium of the aneurysm located not in the Valsalva sinus but in the ascending aorta) (Figure 1A). Intraoperative findings (Figure 1B) confirmed the CT imaging, and aortic root and ascending aortic replacement were successfully performed. Histological examinations of the aneurysm wall indicated continuous medial structure (arrows, Figure 1C, Hematoxylin and Eosin stain), which definitively diagnosed true aneurysm. Aortogram in another asymptomatic 75‐year‐old man with no history of cardiac surgery or chest trauma suspected a brachiocephalic artery aneurysm (Figure 1D). Intraoperative findings, however, revealed a huge saccular ascending aortic aneurysm just below (proximal to) the origin of the brachiocephalic artery (with the ostium of the aneurysm situated not in the brachiocephalic artery but in the ascending aorta) (Figure 1E), and ascending and transverse aortic replacement was uneventfully performed. Pathological examinations of the aneurysm wall indicated disruption (white arrow) of elastic fibers (black arrow) in the media (Figure 1F, Verhoeff Van Gieson stain), which definitively diagnosed false aneurysm.

Causes of ascending aortic true aneurysm include degeneration, connective tissue disorder, congenital anomalies of the aortic valve, genetic causes, and aortitis [1]. Meanwhile, ascending aortic false aneurysm usually develops in patients with a history of cardiac surgery, and additionally traumatic, inflammatory, or infectious events [2]. Although most saccular ascending aortic aneurysm is probably considered to be false aneurysm, a few cases of saccular ascending aortic true aneurysm have been reported [3]. When ascending aortic aneurysm is saccular and the abovementioned histories associated with false aneurysm do not exist, imaging differential diagnosis whether true or false aneurysm is generally difficult, and definitive diagnosis can be done merely with histopathological examinations of the aneurysm wall.
