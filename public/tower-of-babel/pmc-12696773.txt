Enzyme‐Instructed Self‐Assembly for Cellular Supramolecular Chemistry
Authors: Yali Huang, Xingjie Hu, Zhimou Yang, Bing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnzyme‐instructed self‐assembly (EISA) uses endogenous enzymatic activity to convert soluble precursors into self‐assembling species, enabling the spatiotemporal formation of supramolecular nanostructures directly within cellular environments. Unlike other supramolecular strategies triggered by pH, redox, or light, EISA leverages the inherent spatial localization and dynamic kinetics of enzymes to achieve precise, context‐dependent control over where and when assembly occurs. While previous reviews have summarized EISA's mechanisms and biomedical applications, this perspective positions EISA as a conceptual framework for supramolecular chemical biology—emphasizing its role in mimicking higher‐order protein assemblies and in bridging molecular design with cellular function. We discuss how EISA enables programmable conformational and morphological switching, the creation of growth factor–mimicking assemblies, and the in situ formation of artificial supramolecular architectures inside or around cells. By highlighting EISA as a catalytic strategy for constructing functional supramolecular systems in vivo, this perspective outlines a new direction for integrating enzymatic control with nanoscale self‐organization in cellular supramolecular chemistry, generalizing EISA beyond alkaline phosphatases to programmable multi‐enzyme networks, and thereby advancing adaptive biomaterials, programmable therapeutics, and synthetic cellular machines.

Introduction

Enzyme‐instructed self‐assembly (EISA) occurs when enzymatic reactions trigger small molecules to spontaneously organize into ordered nanostructures through noncovalent interactions. In this process, the enzyme acts as a molecular switch: it converts soluble, non‐assembling precursors into self‐assembling building blocks. These building blocks form larger structures via hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, hydrophobic effects, and van der Waals forces, rather than covalent bonds. EISA exemplifies enzymatic noncovalent synthesis (ENS), a strategy widely used in nature, for example, in assembling complexes of proteins or nucleic acids [1]. EISA requires enzyme activity above a certain threshold to convert sufficient precursors into assembling species; this threshold is governed by both the thermodynamic (critical aggregation concentration) and the kinetic (rate of the enzymatic reaction) factors. Even for phosphorylation‐controlled EISA, the process can be made reversible or tunable by introducing kinases [2] or by modulating the rate of dephosphorylation [3], as previously demonstrated. Compared with classical enzyme inhibition, EISA offers distinct biological advantages, including enhanced selectivity via enzyme‐specific activation, spatial localization of assemblies at sites of high enzymatic activity, and reversible modulation of cellular functions without permanently blocking the catalytic pathways that sustain normal physiology.

Early studies established two strategies of EISA: enzymatic bond cleavage [4] and bond formation [5] (Figure 1A). In both, enzymes transform precursors into hydrogelators that self‐assemble in aqueous solution and produce gelation. Kinetic analyses [6] showed that enzymatic bond cleavage is generally faster than bond formation, while bond formation often remains reversible, a feature essential for dynamic combinatorial libraries [7] and systems chemistry [8]. Gelation provides a simple, visual assay for probing molecular self‐assembly in water. Compared with pH‐triggered self‐assembly, enzyme‐mediated approaches yield defect‐free supramolecular nanofibers [2], demonstrating the precision of enzyme catalysis. Moreover, using enzymes to control the self‐assembly of small molecules provides a new way to regulate reaction‐diffusion at nanoscale [9].

Researchers have advanced EISA by creating localized nanostructures [10], improving biocompatibility [11], and enabling dynamic control of assembly [12]. EISA can actively modulate cellular functions by disrupting protein‐protein interactions [13] or reorganizing cytoskeletal architecture [14]. Importantly, it operates selectively in disease‐associated microenvironments, such as tumors that overexpress enzymes like alkaline phosphatase (ALP) [15, 16, 17] or matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [18, 19]. In pathological tissues such as tumors or inflamed regions, distinct enzyme expression profiles, such as overexpression of ALP, MMPs, or esterases, can serve as biological cues that define local biochemical environments. By responding specifically to these enzyme signatures, EISA enables site‐selective self‐assembly, ensuring that nanostructures form only within disease‐associated microenvironments while minimizing off‐target effects in healthy tissues. In these settings, EISA produces functional intracellular nanostructures that trigger cell death, suppress tumor growth, or act as drug depots that release therapeutics in response to enzymatic activity [20, 21, 22]. By mimicking natural self‐assembly, such as cytoskeleton formation [23], EISA offers a biomimetic route for applications in imaging [24, 25, 26] and therapy [27].

Several excellent reviews have summarized the progress of EISA [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. In this perspective, we examine EISA within the context of supramolecular chemical biology. We will explore these possibilities in detail across the following sections. We first discuss its conceptual suitability for mimicking higher‐order protein structures in cells. Next, we highlight representative examples of conformational and morphological switching, growth factor‐mimicking assemblies, and artificial supramolecular nanostructures. Finally, we offer an outlook on opportunities and challenges for integrating EISA into adaptive therapeutics, intelligent biomaterials, and synthetic cellular systems.

EISA for Supramolecular Chemical Biology

Traditional chemical biology emphasizes small‐molecule inhibitors that block enzyme function [35, 36]. In contrast, supramolecular chemical biology considers how molecular assemblies influence cellular outcomes. In nature, higher‐order protein structures [37], such as cytoskeletons [38], inflammasomes [39], and gasdermin pores [40], demonstrate how enzyme‐controlled self‐assembly underpins essential cellular processes. Ongoing advances in molecular biology and imaging continue to reveal such elegant protein machineries. Because these higher‐order protein structures originate from enzymatic reactions followed by self‐assembly, designing substrates for enzymes, rather than inhibitors, offers a strategy to direct supramolecular assemblies and influence cell fate.

EISA displays several unique features for directing supramolecular constructions. First, enzymes catalyze bond‐breaking or bond‐forming reactions that convert soluble, non‐assembling precursors, or weakly assembling species, into strongly self‐assembling building blocks, such as the enzyme‐responsive rigid‐rod aromatics [41]. Second, the resulting products self‐assemble into nanostructures exclusively through noncovalent interactions, such as the self‐assembly of Nap−FFGEY [2], setting EISA apart from covalent polymer synthesis. Compared with other triggers such as pH, redox, or light, the inherent reversibility and substrate specificity of enzymatic catalysis provide higher fidelity in initiating self‐assembly, allowing precise spatiotemporal control and minimizing undesired aggregation. Third, enzymatic activity provides spatiotemporal control: localizing enzyme expressions in cells regulate when and where assembly occurs, and the resulting nanostructures remain non‐diffusive, like the pericellular nanofibers [42]. Finally, EISA responds to endogenous enzymatic signals in a context‐dependent manner [43], enabling applications in therapeutic delivery, tissue engineering, and other biomedical uses. Collectively, these attributes position EISA as a versatile platform for supramolecular construction in biological systems.

This substrate‐based approach provides immediate conceptual and therapeutic benefits, most notably the potential to overcome drug resistance. As illustrated in Figure 1B, elevated enzyme expression or activity typically diminishes the efficacy of inhibitors by requiring higher doses, thus contributing to drug resistance. In contrast, increased enzyme levels boost EISA, making substrate‐based strategies more effective. This distinction turns a mechanism of drug resistance into a therapeutic vulnerability, positioning EISA as a complementary, and in some cases superior, approach for cancer therapy [44, 45, 46]. Beyond its therapeutic potential, EISA opens new avenues in supramolecular biology, including conformational and morphological switching, growth factor–mimicking nanostructures, and artificial cellular assemblies, as discussed below.

Conformational and Morphological Switch

Through enzymatic reaction kinetics and molecular design, EISA enables dynamic and precise control of the conformation and morphology of supramolecular nanostructures. Its core principle relies on the specific catalytic action of enzymes (e.g., phosphatases) to activate or transform precursor molecules (e.g., phosphorylated peptides) in situ, thereby directing reorganization of intermolecular non‐covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic effects, electrostatic interactions). The precise control over conformational and morphological transformations endows EISA with unique advantages in constructing functional nanostructures with tailored properties, which is particularly important for modulating their intracellular distribution and functions.

Because enzymatic reactions are highly sensitive to temperature, this critical kinetic parameter precisely regulates the conformational and morphological evolution in EISA systems. With increasing temperature, the enzymatic reaction rate accelerates, strongly influencing precursor dephosphorylation and subsequent assembly kinetics. At low temperatures (e.g., 4°C), the enzymatic catalysis slows down, resulting in a uniform distribution of self‐assembling peptides that favors the formation of nanofibers and hydrogels. In contrast, at elevated temperatures (e.g., 37°C), accelerated catalysis produces localized high concentrations of self‐assembling peptides, driving rapid molecular aggregation into nanoparticles (Figure 2A). For example, during ALP‐catalyzed dephosphorylation of the peptide NBD‐GDFDFpDY, reactions at 4°C facilitate orderly molecular stacking, generating nanofibrous supramolecular hydrogels dominated by α‐helix peptide conformation. Conversely, reactions at 37°C induce rapid aggregation, resulting in aqueous nanoparticle dispersions dominated by random conformation (Figure 2B–D) [47]. This temperature‐dependent kinetic divergence fundamentally arises from altered rates in forming intermolecular hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, enabling a morphological transition from nanofibers to nanoparticles. These findings provide thermodynamic foundations for fabricating self‐assembled nanomaterials with controllable structures and functions.

The phosphorylation site directly determines the hydrophobicity and charge distribution of the precursor molecule, thereby specifically regulating the conformation and morphology of the preassembled and dephosphorylated assemblies. For instance, in the case of three phosphorylated peptides, Cy5.5‐1P, Cy5.5‐2P, and Cy5.5‐3P, which differ only in their single tyrosine phosphorylation sites, their preassembled conformations and morphologies varied significantly, leading to distinct cellular internalization rates and tumor tissue retention time (Figure 3A). Among them, Cy5.5‐3P exhibited faster cellular internalization and longer tumor retention compared with the others [48].

Another study demonstrated that three phosphorylated peptides (pY1, pY2, and pY3), each with a different phosphorylation site, also displayed distinct pre‐assembly states and dephosphorylation rates (Figure 3B) [49]. Upon dephosphorylation, these peptides formed nanofibers with varying widths, lengths, uniformity, and densities. The variation in pre‐assembly state, dephosphorylation rate, and nanostructure morphologies led to markedly different cellular distributions. After co‐incubation with HeLa cells, pY1 remained stably anchored to the cell membrane, pY2 was mostly endocytosed, and pY3 only showed minimal endocytosis.

These findings collectively demonstrate that variation in phosphorylation patterns on the same peptide scaffold can effectively program their self‐assembly behavior. This enables precise control over the conformation and morphology of both pre‐assembled and mature nanostructures, ultimately leading to distinct cellular distributions and biological effects.

Multi‐substrate co‐assembly can drive heterogeneous molecules to form supramolecular complexes with novel conformations. When the same enzyme catalyzes two substrates, their differing dephosphorylation rates enable them to form supramolecular structures that are difficult to reach by a single component. For example, Gao et al. synthesized the self‐assembling peptides Ada‐GDFDFpDYG‐DPPA‐1 (SA‐P) and Ada‐GDFDFpDYG‐DTBP‐3 (SA‐T) (Figure 4A).[51] SA‐P formed α‐helix‐based nanoparticles, while SA‐T generated β‐sheet‐based short nanofibers. Their co‐assembly (SA‐P&T) produced α‐helix‐based nanoribbon‐like structures. However, under ALP catalysis, SA‐P&T transform into β‐sheet‐based long nanofibers, indicting a complete inversion of both secondary structure and morphology (Figure 4B,C). The ALP‐treatmed SA‐T&P exhibited stronger binding affinity for both mPD‐L1 and mTIGIT targets and achieved optimal tumor‐suppressive effects (Figure 4D‐F). These studies demonstrate that EISA can regulate supramolecular conformation and morphological transformation through temperature control, phosphorylation site design, and co‐assembly strategy. These dynamic control approaches not only deepen the understanding of enzyme‐driven assembly mechanisms in living systems but also open new paths for intelligent drug delivery and adaptive biomaterial development. Developing scalable design rules that link enzyme–substrate interactions with assembly behavior remains an open challenge but will be essential for predictive, rather than empirical, control of EISA. Compared to the single component self‐assembly, multi‐substrate co‐assembly offers a dynamic and programmable platform that integrates functionally distinct modules, such as targeting ligands and therapeutic drugs, within a single supramolecular architecture to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects unattainable by individual components. By exploiting differences in enzymatic kinetics and intermolecular interactions, this strategy enables temporally cascaded and spatially controlled assemblies that mimic biological pathways. Simple adjustment of co‐blending composition enables convenient fine‐tuning of morphology, mechanical stiffness, and surface potential without synthesizing new covalent molecules. Overall, transitioning from single‐ to multi‐component EISA shifts the focus from building static structures to engineering adaptive, intelligent systems for precision biomedicine.

Growth Factor‐Mimicking Nanostructures

Growth factors play a pivotal role in tissue regeneration and repair, but their clinical application remains limited by short half‐life, high cost, and poor stability. In recent years, researchers have increasingly designed self‐assembling peptides to mimic growth factor functions, offering an innovative strategy to overcome these challenges. These peptides fold into ordered nano‐assemblies with well‐defined secondary structures, imposing conformational constraints similar to natural growth factors. In doing so, they mimic the topology and receptor activation patterns (e.g., multimerization [51]) of their natural counterparts while enabling sustained release and localized delivery.

Precise control of peptide folding is essential construction of growth factor‐mimicking nanostructures. Preparation methods strongly influence peptide self‐assembly and folding, with distinct fabrication approaches directing peptides toward different secondary conformations that yield markedly different biological outcomes [52]. In the study of constructing insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (IGF‐1)‐mimicking supramolecular nanofibers, Shang et al. systematically compared two assembly approaches (EISA and heating‐cooling) and evaluated their effects on peptide secondary structure and biological functions [53]. The EISA process induced peptide folding into an α‐helix conformation with a weak binding affinity to IGF‐1R, while the heating‐cooling process produced β‐sheet conformations that bound strongly to IGF‐1R and more effectively mimicked the function of IGF‐1 (Figure 5A). Specifically, the researchers combined the self‐assembling motif Nap‐FFG with the C‐domain peptide segment of IGF‐1 (IGF‐1C) to construct an opaque hydrogel (Gel 1) via the heating‐cooling process. They also synthesized a phosphorylated precursor and used ALP‐catalyzed EISA to form a transparent hydrogel (Gel 2) (Figure 5B). Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy confirmed that peptides in opaque Gel 1 adopted β‐sheet conformations, while those in transparent Gel 2 adopted α‐helix conformations (Figure 5C). Diluted peptides from Gel 1 significantly promoted HUVEC proliferation (Figure 5D) and demonstrated IGF‐1‐like functionality by enhancing the recovery of ischemic limbs, while Gel 2 showed no relevant bioactivity (Figure 5E). This work highlighted a versatile strategy for modulating the secondary structure of bioactive peptides through tailored preparation methods to mimic the functions of growth factors. Based on this, the research group further designed a novel self‐assembling IGF‐1‐mimetic peptide that successfully promoted hair regeneration [54].

Interestingly, the hydrogel formed via EISA demonstrated markedly lower biological activity compared to that prepared by the heating‐cooling approach, a discrepancy rooted in their fundamentally different final secondary structures. The EISA‐derived hydrogel predominantly adopted an α‐helical conformation while the heating‐cooling method yielded a β‐sheet architecture. The β‐sheet configuration enables critical receptor‐binding amino acid sequences to be properly positioned, forming a bioactive surface complementary to the IGF1R binding pocket. This structural advantage translates to a 15‐fold stronger IGF1R binding affinity and IGF‐1‐like biological activity. Conversely, the spatial arrangement of the α‐helical likely disrupts the precise geometry of key binding residues, making it difficult to achieve effective interaction with the IGF1R binding pocket. Consequently, the α‐helical hydrogel shows negligible biological activity.

By employing the peptide folding strategy, Shang et al. also developed a self‐assembling short peptide with platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF)‐like bioactivity [55]. This peptide, characterized by a β‐sheet secondary structure, successfully mimics the functional activity of PDGF and enhances the repair of radiation‐induced skin injuries (Figure 6A). Subsequently, Shi et al. adopted a similar design strategy to synthesize a novel self‐assembling PDGF‐mimetic peptide [56], which spontaneously forms nanofibrous hydrogels with a β‐sheet conformation and exhibits potent therapeutic efficacy in promoting cutaneous wound healing (Figure 6B). Together, these studies demonstrate the feasibility of mimicking growth factors through controlled peptide folding.

These biomimetic designs succeed by constructing β‐sheet assemblies. Cytokine receptor binding (e.g., IGF‐1R, PDGFR, EGFR) depends on the precise three‐dimensional positioning of key amino‐acid residues. Consequently, peptide mimetics must lock their active fragments into a conformation that complements the receptor pocket to trigger correct signal transduction. This conformation positions the key amino acid side chains in three‐dimensional space to form a surface complementary to the receptor's binding pocket. The surface embeds into the receptor's binding site, triggering the correct signal transduction. For instance, in PDGF mimicry, both mimicking peptides were designed by selecting an active fragment from the L3 loop of PDGF‐BB, which is crucial for dimer formation and high‐affinity binding. This fragment was combined with a self‐assembling peptide to regulate the secondary structure into a β‐sheet, thereby mimicking the active binding surface composed of β‐sheets and the L3 loop region in the native PDGF, and consequently emulating the structure and function of PDGF.

Overall, controlled peptide folding holds broad application prospects in the development of growth factor mimetics, owing to its biocompatibility, design flexibility, functional diversity, and ease of synthesis and modification. Although no successful applications of EISA have yet been reported for fabricating growth factor‐mimicking nanomaterials, this technique remains promising because of its unique ability to provide spatiotemporal control over peptide folding into defined secondary structures. Compared to traditional peptide folding strategies, EISA enables dynamic control of the assembly process through enzymatic reaction kinetics. In contrast to recombinant proteins, EISA systems exhibit reduced steric hindrance and enhanced tissue penetration capacity. One limitation of conventional EISA driven by ALP is its reliance on phosphate dephosphorylation, which may restrict substrate scope and fine‐tuned regulation. To overcome this limitation, emerging enzyme‐triggered systems can be employed. For instance, MMP‐responsive EISA systems can be selectively activated under specific pathological conditions, such as the bone injury microenvironment, enabling disease‐specific assembly at target sites. Conversely, esterase‐driven assembly strategies leverage intracellular esterase distribution heterogeneity to achieve more precise control over intracellular self‐assembly. These alternative enzyme systems trigger peptide self‐assembly in a more microenvironment‐dependent manner, not only expanding the substrate scope but also enhancing fine‐tuning capability over the assembly process, thereby opening new avenues for developing next‐generation intelligent growth factor‐mimetic materials.

Artificial Cellular Nanostructures

Cells are collections of molecular machines at nanoscale [40]. A large number of these nanomachines are made by enzymes. Thus, using EISA to mimic such enzymatic control offers a uniquely powerful route to generate artificial cellular nanostructures. In both extracellular and intracellular contexts, EISA couples an enzyme‐triggered chemical transformation with self‐assembly, enabling molecular precursors to undergo morphological transitions directly in the native cellular environment. This combination allows nanostructures to emerge with precise spatiotemporal control, dictated by local enzyme activity, rather than relying on pre‐formed assemblies that must diffuse through, and potentially be disrupted by, the crowded cytosol or extracellular milieu.

In the extracellular domain, EISA has been shown to produce fluorescent, non‐diffusive peptide nanofibrils that selectively form around cells overexpressing target ectoenzymes, such as ALP. Because the reaction and assembly occur locally where enzymes exhibit high activity, EISA achieves subcellular resolution, maintains compatibility with live cells, and reports dynamic enzyme behavior beyond what genomic or proteomic analyses can capture. Specifically, the use of proteolytically stable D‐peptides (Figure 7A) enhances selectivity and durability, allowing nanostructures to persist long enough for imaging (Figure 7B). Notably, correlated light and electron microscopy (CLEM) confirmed the formation of peptide nanofibers on the surface of the HeLa cells, which overexpress ALP as ectoenzymes. This work [42] not only demonstrated a molecular probe based on EISA for profiling the activity of enzymes of live cells, but also rigorously demonstrated the ability of EISA for generating extracellular nanostructures.

Inside cells, EISA can convert enzyme‐accessible nanoparticle precursors into well‐defined filamentous assemblies, even within the highly crowded and heterogeneous cytoplasm. For example, EISA can create self‐limiting intracellular filaments from a synthetic peptide. Through the integration of cryo‐electron microscopy (cryo‐EM), fluorescence imaging, electron tomography (ET), molecular dynamics simulations, and molecular design, this study reveals how a trimethylated tetrapeptide forms well‐defined filaments inside living cells (Figure 8A). Structural analysis by cryo‐EM shows that these filaments adopt two distinct cross‐β architectures with C7 or C2 symmetry, determined via helical reconstruction (Figure 8B). The flexibility of intracellular filaments likely affects cell signaling and cytoskeletal dynamics. In this case, the intracellular filaments are flexible, thus exerting minimal impact on cell migration. Within cells, these monodisperse filaments bundle into twisted networks spanning from the plasma membrane to the nuclear envelope. This enzymatic morphological transition strategy thus offers a means to recreate lost intermediate filament functions and to shed light on the biology of pathological protein or peptide filaments. The self‐limiting nature of these assemblies, arising from controlled filament bundling under macromolecular crowding, demonstrates how EISA can generate biomimetic architectures that maintain structural uniformity in complex environments. Intracellular filaments formed by EISA may modulate cytoskeletal organization, trafficking, and signaling, suggesting future opportunities to explore their roles in diseases associated with intermediate filament pathology.

Outlook

EISA enables supramolecular chemistry to operate within the complex and dynamic environment of living systems. Unlike conventional self‐assembly strategies that rely on external triggers (pH [57], redox [58], light [59], temperature [60], and biorthogonal reactions [61, 62, 63, 64, 65]) or occur in homogeneous environments [66], EISA uses endogenous enzymatic activities to regulate nanostructure formation with spatiotemporal precision, mimicking natural processes. By using enzymes both as triggers and spatial cues, EISA provides a powerful means to construct responsive supramolecular systems directly in biological settings. Future directions include expanding the functional scope of EISA assemblies beyond structural or localization roles toward catalytic, regulatory, or communicative functions. Such systems could modulate signaling, enable in situ diagnostics, or dynamically interact with biomolecules. Integration with photosensitive, redox‐active, or aptamer‐modified building blocks may further broaden inputs and outputs, leading to programmable “cellular plugins.”

Looking forward, several critical gaps define the next phase of EISA development. Most current systems rely on phosphatase‐ or esterase‐mediated cleavage. Expanding EISA to other enzyme classes—including kinases, proteases, transferases, and ligases—will broaden reaction diversity, enable cascade assemblies, and allow context‐dependent regulation across cellular environments and disease states. While off‐target aggregation remains a potential challenge, more precise substrate design can help manage this issue.

Achieving reversible, dynamic control is equally essential for adaptive supramolecular systems. Incorporating enzyme pairs such as kinase/phosphatase or ligase/protease may allow switchable transitions between assembled and disassembled states under cellular regulation [2]. Bond‐forming processes, such as peptide ligation, lipidation, or esterification, offer slower yet reversible kinetics that could support feedback‐regulated assemblies. Recent examples of intracellular lipidation as EISA triggers [67, 68] highlight the feasibility of this strategy.

Translating EISA into animal models remains a major frontier. Demonstrating selective in vivo assembly, controlled therapeutic release, or modulation of signaling pathways will be key to validating its biomedical potential. Integrating imaging, pharmacokinetic, and histological analyses can directly reveal enzymatic specificity and supramolecular behavior in physiological contexts.

Speculative avenues include co‐assembly with small gas molecules (O2, N2, CO2) to generate supramolecular sensors, scavengers, or catalysts. For example, CO2‐binding nanostructures may enable carbon capture or pH‐responsive systems, whereas O2‐related assemblies could function as redox catalysts or artificial enzymes [69]. Dye–peptide conjugates combined with EISA [70] may further yield in situ photocatalysts, merging light harvesting with biological responsiveness. Another, more daring, application is to use EISA to target loss‐of‐function processes. While the loss of esterase function has been addressed [12], restoring other targets—such as TP53—remains to be explored.

Collectively, addressing these challenges—expanding enzyme scope, enabling reversibility, and demonstrating function in vivo—will transform EISA from a conceptual framework into a practical platform for adaptive materials and programmable therapeutics. Ultimately, EISA is more than a tool for molecular assembly: it encodes spatiotemporal dynamics into synthetic systems, bridging chemistry and biology to advance intelligent biomaterials and synthetic cellular machines.
