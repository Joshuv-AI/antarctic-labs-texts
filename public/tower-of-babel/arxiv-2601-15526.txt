Introduction and results

Interacting particle systems that combine random motion with local activation rules—most prominently the
frog model
—offer a parsimonious framework to probe survival/extinction phenomena and front propagation in disordered media. Since its introduction by Alves, Machado and Popov
[
1
]
, the model has revealed genuine phase transitions on trees and higher–dimensional lattices
[
10
,
16
,
11
]
, with sharp or near–sharp criteria depending on geometry and initial densities
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
. On
\mathbb{Z}
, however, geometric lifetimes with a
fixed
survival parameter
p
often entail almost–sure extinction under mild assumptions, unless additional structure (e.g. drift or spatial heterogeneity) is introduced
[
3
,
12
]
. A key step forward was obtained by Carvalho and Machado
[
5
]
, who considered i.i.d.
random survival parameters

\pi\in(0,1)
and, for geometric lifetimes, identified a sharp
edge
threshold at
\beta=\tfrac{1}{2}
driven by the mass of
\pi
near
1
. A subsequent paper extends this threshold to broad classes of right–edge densities with slowly varying corrections; see
[
6
]
.

In the present paper we keep the same one–dimensional frog model with random survival parameter
\pi
, but we replace geometric lifetimes by discrete Weibull lifetimes. Here
\Xi
denotes the lifetime of each active frog, i.e., the number of steps it performs after activation before it dies. Conditionally on
\pi=p
, we assume

\mathbb{P}(\Xi\geq k\mid\pi=p)=p^{k^{\gamma}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},\ \gamma>0.

Under a standard regularity assumption on the distribution of
\pi
near its right endpoint
p=1
, we obtain a sharp extinction–survival transition at the critical exponent
\beta_{c}=1/(2\gamma)
: extinction holds for
\beta>\beta_{c}
, survival holds for
\beta<\beta_{c}
, and the boundary case
\beta=\beta_{c}
depends on the slowly varying factor. In particular, when
\gamma=1
we recover the geometric case and the known threshold
\beta_{c}=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Motivation: from geometric to discrete Weibull lifetimes

Why move beyond geometric lifetimes?
Geometric lifetimes are memoryless: the discrete hazard
h_{k}:=\mathbb{P}(\Xi=k\,|\,\Xi\geq k)
is constant in
k
, so past survival contains no information about future risk. In many contexts—reliability, epidemiological clearance, device aging—risk is age–dependent, exhibiting either
burn–in
(decreasing hazard after early failures) or
wear–out
(increasing hazard with age); see the classical reliability framework of Barlow–Proschan
[
2
]
.

To capture both behaviors in discrete time with a single, tractable family, we adopt the type–I
discrete Weibull
distribution
[
19
]
with paremeter
\gamma>0
, whose survival function is given by

S(k)\;=\;\mathbb{P}(\Xi\geq k\,|\,\pi=p)\;=\;p^{\,k^{\gamma}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \ 0<p<1,\ \ \gamma>0,

(1)

which is the integer–time analogue of the continuous Weibull tail
e^{-\lambda t^{\gamma}}
(via
p=e^{-\lambda}
). The shape parameter
\gamma
is the single knob for aging:

h_{k}\ =\ 1-\frac{S(k+1)}{S(k)}\ =\ 1-p^{(k+1)^{\gamma}-k^{\gamma}},

so the hazard is increasing when
\gamma>1
(wear–out), constant when
\gamma=1
(memoryless, i.e., geometric), and decreasing when
0<\gamma<1
(burn–in). Thus (
1
) preserves analytical simplicity while aligning the model with the two canonical hazard regimes.

Figure
1
depicts the discrete Weibull tail
k\mapsto p^{k^{\gamma}}
for representative
(p,\gamma)
: for
\gamma>1
long lifetimes are strongly suppressed relative to the geometric case, while for
0<\gamma<1
the tail is heavier.

\gamma=1

As in
[

, we quantify the availability of almost–immortal particles via the asymptotic behavior

f_{\pi}(u)\ \sim\ (1-u)^{\beta-1}\,L\!\Big(\frac{1}{1-u}\Big)\qquad(u\uparrow 1),

(2)

with
\beta>0
and
L
slowly varying at
\infty
. This class contains the Beta family and heterogeneous right edges modulated by
L
. An extensive list of examples satisfying (
2
) is given in
[
6
]
. Intuitively, a smaller
\beta
corresponds to a thicker right edge and hence to more long–lived particles.

The choice (
1
) reconciles discrete-time dynamics with the two canonical aging regimes while keeping the model analytically tractable. For
\gamma>1
the tail
k\mapsto p^{k^{\gamma}}
decays faster than the geometric tail (reflecting wear–out), for
0<\gamma<1
it decays more slowly in a stretched–exponential fashion (reflecting burn–in), and
\gamma=1
recovers the memoryless geometric case. When, in addition, the survival parameter has a density satisfying the right–edge condition (
2
), the mass near
\pi\simeq 1
—encoded by
(\beta,L)
—interacts with the random-walk hitting-time distribution in a way made precise by our one-particle estimates (Theorems
2.3
–
2.4
). As a consequence, the extinction–survival criterion exhibits the
\gamma
–dependent critical curve

\beta_{c}\;:=\;\frac{1}{2\gamma}.

In particular, for
E(\eta)<\infty
there is extinction when
\beta>\beta_{c}
, and for
\mathbb{P}(\eta=0)<1
there is survival with positive probability when
\beta<\beta_{c}
, with critical envelope conditions spelled out at
\beta=\beta_{c}
. The case
\gamma=1
recovers the geometric benchmark
\beta_{c}=\tfrac{1}{2}
from
[
5
]
and its regularly varying extensions in
[
6
]
.

1.1

Model and notation on
\mathbb{Z}

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
. For each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
\eta_{x}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the initial number of sleeping particles at
x
. Particles are indexed by
(x,i)
with
1\leq i\leq\eta_{x}
. The families

\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad\{\pi_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}},\quad\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}

are mutually independent, where
(S^{x,i}_{n})
is a simple symmetric random walk with
S^{x,i}_{0}=x
, and
\pi_{x,i}\in(0,1)
are i.i.d. with law
\pi
(density
f_{\pi}
when it exists). Given
\pi_{x,i}=p
and
\gamma>0
, the lifetime
\Xi_{x,i}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
has
discrete Weibull tail

\mathbb{P}(\Xi_{x,i}\geq k\mid\pi_{x,i}=p)\;=\;p^{\,k^{\gamma}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(3)

Conditional on
\{\pi_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}
, the lifetimes
\{\Xi_{x,i}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent of
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z},\,i\in\mathbb{N}}
(and of each other).

At time
0
all particles at the origin are
active
and all others are
inactive
. For every
(x,i)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}
, the
i
-th particle of vertex
x\in\mathbb{Z}
(considering
i\leq\eta_{x}
), if activated at some instant
t_{x,i}
, is considered
alive
at any instant
t\leq t_{x,i}+\Xi_{x,i}
and
dead
at any
t>t_{x,i}+\Xi_{x,i}
. At every instant of time
t\in\mathbb{N}
, each active and alive particle takes one step of its simple symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
. Whenever an active particle visits a site, all particles
present there become active and evolve independently by the same rules. We denote this system by
\mathrm{FM}(\mathbb{Z},\pi,\eta,\gamma)
, where
\pi
is the common law of the survival parameters,
\eta
is the law of the initial number of particles per vertex, and
\gamma>0
is the parameter of the discrete Weibull distribution.

Main results

Our main result is related to the survival of the frog model. We say that the frog model
survives
if, at every time instant
t>0
, there is at least one active and alive particle. Otherwise, we say that the model dies out.

We are now ready to state the main theorem of this paper.

(General extinction–survival dichotomy)
.

2

One–particle tail asymptotics

Under (
2
) we derive
two–sided
asymptotics for the one–particle displacement tail.

For a particle
i
initially at
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the maximal one–sided and two–sided displacements during its lifetime:

D^{\rightarrow}_{z,i}:=\max_{0\leq n\leq\Xi_{z,i}}\big(S^{z,i}_{n}-z\big),\,D^{\leftarrow}_{z,i}:=\max_{0\leq n\leq\Xi_{z,i}}\big(z-S^{z,i}_{n}\big),\,D^{*}_{z,i}:=\max_{0\leq n\leq\Xi_{z,i}}\big(|S^{z,i}_{n}-z|\big)=D^{\rightarrow}_{z,i}\vee D^{\leftarrow}_{z,i}.

By homogeneity, write
D^{\rightarrow}
,
D^{\leftarrow}
, and
D^{*}
for generic copies. By symmetry,

\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n)=\mathbb{P}(D^{\leftarrow}\geq n)\ \geq\ \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{P}(D^{*}\geq n),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4)

Since, conditional on the survival parameters
\{\pi_{x,i}\}
, the lifetimes
\{\Xi_{x,i}\}
are independent of the family of simple symmetric random walks
\{(S^{x,i}_{n})_{n\geq 0}\}

\{\pi_{x,i}\}
itself is independent of the family of simple symmetric random walks, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D^{\rightarrow}\geq n\mid\pi=p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\leq\Xi\mid\pi=p)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Xi\geq k,\ \tau_{n}=k\mid\pi=p)

(5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Xi\geq k\mid\pi=p)\,\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}=k)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p^{k^{\gamma}}\,\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}=k)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[p^{\tau_{n}^{\gamma}}\right],\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

so the random walk enters only through the law of the hitting time
\tau_{n}
, while the lifetime acts as an “age filter”.

[
5
]
and
[
6
]

Proofs

\log(1-u)=-u-\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2}+O(u^{3}).

As
u\downarrow 0
,

\begin{split}\log(f(e^{-u}))&=\log\Bigg(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-e^{-2u}}}{e^{-u}}\Bigg)=\log(1-\sqrt{y(u)})+u=-\sqrt{y(u)}-\frac{1}{2}y(u)+O(y(u)^{\frac{3}{2}})+u\\
&=-\sqrt{2u}(1+\frac{1}{2}O(u)+O(u^{2}))-u(1+O(u))+O(y(u)^{\frac{3}{2}})+u\end{split}

since
y(u)=O(u)
and
O(y(u)^{3/2})=O(u^{3/2})
. This proves (
26
). Exponentiating (
26
) yields

e^{-\sqrt{2u}}\,e^{-Cu^{3/2}}\ \leq\ f(e^{-u})\ \leq\ e^{-\sqrt{2u}}\,e^{Cu^{3/2}}.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and choose
u_{\varepsilon}
as in (
28
). If
0<u\leq u_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
, then
Cu^{3/2}\leq\varepsilon\sqrt{2u}
, and substituting into the last estimate gives (
27
).
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
0<\gamma<1
and
a>0
. Then there exists a
probability
measure
\nu_{\gamma,a}
on
(0,\infty)
such that, for all
t\geq 0
,

e^{-a\,t^{\gamma}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ut}\,\nu_{\gamma,a}(du).

(29)

Moreover, the family
(\nu_{\gamma,a})_{a>0}
satisfies the exact scaling

\nu_{\gamma,a}(du)=a^{-1/\gamma}\,\nu_{\gamma,1}\!\left(d\big(u/a^{1/\gamma}\big)\right),

(30)

and, for every
\theta>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{-\theta}\,\nu_{\gamma,1}(du)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{1}^{-\theta}\right]=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\frac{\Gamma(\theta/\gamma)}{\Gamma(\theta)}<\infty,

(31)

where
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes a (one–sided)
\gamma
–stable subordinator with Laplace transform
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda S_{t}}]=e^{-t\lambda^{\gamma}}
.

Proof.

Let
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a
\gamma
-stable subordinator
(nondecreasing Lévy process) in the standard normalization

\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\lambda S_{t}}\right]\;=\;\exp\!\big(-t\,\lambda^{\gamma}\big),\qquad\lambda,t>0,\ \gamma\in(0,1).

(For comparison,
[
14
]
writes
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda X(t)}]=\exp\{-\tilde{\gamma}\,t\,\lambda^{\alpha}\}
for some
\tilde{\gamma}\geq 0
and shows that with Lévy measure
\nu(dy)=y^{-1-\alpha}dy
one has
\tilde{\gamma}=\Gamma(1-\alpha)/\alpha
; rescaling time yields the unit-scale form above. See also
[
20
, Chs. 1, 3, 5]
.)

Fix
a>0
and rename the Laplace parameter by
t\geq 0
. Then

e^{-a\,t^{\gamma}}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\,t\,S_{a}}\right]\;=\;\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ut}\,\mathbb{P}(S_{a}\in du).

By the Bernstein–Widder theorem (uniqueness of Laplace transforms of positive measures on
[0,\infty)
) (e.g. see
[
20
, Thm. 1.4]
), we conclude (
29
) and, in addition, identifies
\nu_{\gamma,a}
as the law of
S_{a}
.

In addition, for every
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda S_{a}}\big]

\displaystyle=e^{-a\,\lambda^{\gamma}}=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\lambda\,a^{1/\gamma}S_{1}}\big]

By
uniqueness of Laplace transforms on
[0,\infty)
, we conclude

S_{a}\ \overset{d}{=}\ a^{1/\gamma}S_{1}.

Hence, for any bounded measurable test function
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{[0,\infty)}\varphi(u)\,\nu_{\gamma,a}(du)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\varphi(S_{a})\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\varphi\!\big(a^{1/\gamma}S_{1}\big)\big]=\int_{[0,\infty)}\varphi\!\big(a^{1/\gamma}s\big)\,\nu_{\gamma,1}(ds).

In particular,
\nu_{\gamma,a}(B)=\nu_{\gamma,1}\!\big(B/a^{1/\gamma}\big)
for all Borel sets
B\subset[0,\infty)
, which is precisely (
30
).

Let
\theta>0
. For every
x>0
, we have (see the Gamma distribution):

x^{-\theta}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\theta-1}e^{-sx}\,ds.

Apply this with
x=S_{1}
and use Tonelli’s theorem (the integrand is nonnegative) to justify the exchange of expectation and integral:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,\infty)}u^{-\theta}\,\nu_{\gamma,1}(du)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[S_{1}^{-\theta}\big]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\theta-1}e^{-sS_{1}}\,ds\right]=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\theta-1}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-sS_{1}}\big]\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\theta)}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\theta-1}e^{-s^{\gamma}}\,ds\qquad\text{since }\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-sS_{1}}\big]=e^{-s^{\gamma}}.

The integral is finite for all
\theta>0
: near
0
the integrand behaves like
s^{\theta-1}
, which is integrable on
(0,1]
, and for
s\to\infty
the factor
e^{-s^{\gamma}}
decays super-exponentially.
With the change of variables
v=s^{\gamma}
(i.e.,
s=v^{1/\gamma}
,
ds=(1/\gamma)\,v^{1/\gamma-1}dv
), we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{\theta-1}e^{-s^{\gamma}}\,ds=\frac{1}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{\theta/\gamma-1}e^{-v}\,dv=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\theta}{\gamma}\right).

Therefore,

\int_{(0,\infty)}u^{-\theta}\,\nu_{\gamma,1}(du)=\mathbb{E}\big[S_{1}^{-\theta}\big]=\frac{1}{\gamma}\,\frac{\Gamma(\theta/\gamma)}{\Gamma(\theta)}.

which is precisely (
31
).

∎

Lemma 4.3
.

If
L
is slowly varying, then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
C>1
there exists
X(\varepsilon,C)\geq 1
such that, for all
x\geq X(\varepsilon,C)
and
y>0
,

\frac{L(xy)}{L(x)}\ \leq\ C\,\max\{y^{\varepsilon},y^{-\varepsilon}\}.

(32)

Moreover, for every compact interval
0<a\leq y\leq b<\infty
,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\ \sup_{y\in[a,b]}\ \left|\frac{L(xy)}{L(x)}-1\right|=0.

(33)

Proof.

Since
L
is slowly varying at
\infty
, the Uniform Convergence Theorem
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 1.2.1]
) yields that for every compact

K\subset(0,\infty)
,

\sup_{\lambda\in K}\Bigg|\frac{L(\lambda x)}{L(x)}-1\Bigg|\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}0.

We now prove (
32
). We use Potter bounds for
regularly varying
functions (see
[
4
, Thm. 1.5.6]
): for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
C>1
there exists a constant
X_{0}(\varepsilon,C)
such that

\frac{L(xy)}{L(x)}\ \leq C\,y^{\varepsilon},\,y\geq 1,\qquad(x\geq X_{0}(\varepsilon,C)).

(34)

Moreover, let
g(x):=L(x)x^{\varepsilon}
; then
g
is regularly varying with index
\varepsilon
, and the Uniform Convergence Theorem
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 1.5.2]
) yields that for every
b>0

\sup_{\lambda\in(0,b]}\Bigg|\frac{g(\lambda x)}{g(x)}-\lambda^{\varepsilon}\Bigg|\xrightarrow[x\to\infty]{}0.

In particular, take
b=1
. Now, given
\delta>0
, there exists
X_{1}(\delta)>0
such that if
x\geq X_{1}(\delta)
, then

\frac{L(xy)}{L(x)}=\frac{g(xy)}{g(x)y^{\varepsilon}}\leq\delta y^{-\varepsilon}+1\leq(\delta+1)y^{-\varepsilon}

for all
y\in(0,1]
. Then, choosing
\delta>0
such that
1+\delta\leq C
, we obtain that (
32
) holds.

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(S_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the simple symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
with
S_{0}=0
and

\tau_{1}:=\inf\{k\geq 1:\ S_{k}=1\}.

Then

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>t)\ \sim\ \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\;t^{-1/2}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(35)

Proof.

By
[
15
, Prop. 5.1.2]
and by the spatial symmetry (translation by
1
and reflection) of the simple symmetric random walk, we have that:

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>2n)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\,n}}\qquad(n\to\infty).

Hence (
35
) follows.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\gamma>1
. Then for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exist
a_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
and positive constants
c_{1}(\varepsilon)\leq c_{2}(\varepsilon)
such that, for all
a\in(0,a_{\varepsilon}]
,

c_{1}(\varepsilon)\,a^{1/(2\gamma)}\ \leq\ 1-\mathbb{E}\!\big[e^{-a\,\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}\big]\ \leq\ c_{2}(\varepsilon)\,a^{1/(2\gamma)}.

(36)

Moreover,

\lim_{a\downarrow 0}\ \frac{1-\mathbb{E}(e^{-a\tau_{1}^{\gamma}})}{a^{1/(2\gamma)}}\ =\ \Gamma\!\Big(1-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\Big)\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}.

(37)

Proof.

Let
\gamma>1
and write
\overline{F}_{\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}(y):=\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}^{\gamma}>y)
. Define
\rho:=1/(2\gamma)\in(0,1/2)
and
c:=\sqrt{2/\pi}
. By Lemma
4.4
,
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{1}>t)\sim c\,t^{-1/2}
as
t\to\infty
. Hence

\overline{F}_{\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}(y)=\mathbb{P}\!\big(\tau_{1}>y^{1/\gamma}\big)\sim c\,y^{-\rho}\qquad(y\to\infty),

(38)

i.e., the tail of
\tau_{1}^{\gamma}
is regularly varying with index
-\rho
(standard terminology as in
[
4
, Ch. 1]
). For any nonnegative random variable,

1-\mathbb{E}\!\big(e^{-a\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}\big)\;=\;a\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-ay}\,\overline{F}_{\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}(y)\,dy,\qquad a>0,

(39)

see, e.g., the Laplace–Stieltjes integration-by-parts identity in equation (2.7) of
[
9
, Ch. XIII]
.

Since
F_{\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}(y)
is nonincreasing, by (
38
) and Karamata’s Tauberian theorem for Laplace transforms applied to the regularly varying tail in (
39
), we obtain

1-\mathbb{E}\!\big(e^{-a\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}\big)\ \sim\ c\,\Gamma(1-\rho)\,a^{\rho}\qquad(a\downarrow 0),

see
[
8
, Th.4,Ch. XIII.5]
. With
\rho=1/(2\gamma)
and
c=\sqrt{2/\pi}
, this gives the limit (
37
).
In particular, for every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
there exist
a_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)
and constants
c_{1}(\varepsilon),c_{2}(\varepsilon)>0
such that, for all
a\in(0,a_{\varepsilon}]
,

c_{1}(\varepsilon)\,a^{1/(2\gamma)}\ \leq\ 1-\mathbb{E}\!\big(e^{-a\tau_{1}^{\gamma}}\big)\ \leq\ c_{2}(\varepsilon)\,a^{1/(2\gamma)},

which is (
36
).
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

For
x_{i}\geq 0
and
\gamma>1
,

\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\Big)^{\gamma}\ \geq\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\gamma}.

(40)

Hence, if
T_{i}
are i.i.d. nonnegative random variables and
a\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\!\big[e^{-a(\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i})^{\gamma}}\big]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\!\big[e^{-a\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}^{\gamma}}\big]\ =\ \prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\!\big[e^{-aT_{i}^{\gamma}}\big].

(41)

Proof.

We first prove (
40
) for
n=2
. Fix
a\geq 0
and, for
b\geq 0
, set

g_{b}(a):=(a+b)^{\gamma}-a^{\gamma}-b^{\gamma}.

Since

g_{b}^{\prime}(a)=\gamma\big((a+b)^{\gamma-1}-a^{\gamma-1}\big)\geq 0

(because
\gamma\geq 1
and the map
a\mapsto a^{\gamma-1}
is nondecreasing), it follows that
g_{b}(a)\geq g_{b}(0)=b^{\gamma}-b^{\gamma}=0
. By symmetry, the same conclusion holds upon interchanging
a
and
b
. An induction on
n
then yields (
40
).

Applying (
40
) pointwise with
x_{i}=T_{i}(\omega)\geq 0
and multiplying by
-a\leq 0
,

-a\Big(\sum_{i}T_{i}\Big)^{\gamma}\leq-a\sum_{i}T_{i}^{\gamma}.

Since the exponential function preserves order,

e^{-a(\sum_{i}T_{i})^{\gamma}}\ \leq\ e^{-a\sum_{i}T_{i}^{\gamma}}\ =\ \prod_{i}e^{-aT_{i}^{\gamma}}.

Taking expectations and using independence to factor the product, we obtain (
41
).

∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(S_{k})_{k\geq 0}
be the simple symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
with
S_{0}=0
. For any
c_{0}>0
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\tau_{n}<c_{0}\,n^{2}\big)\ \geq\ \theta,

(42)

where
\theta:=\ \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\Phi(1/\sqrt{c_{0}})\big)\in(0,1)
and
\Phi
is the standard Gaussian cdf.

Proof.

Let
M_{m}:=\max_{0\leq k\leq m}S_{k}
. By definition of the hitting time,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}<m)=\mathbb{P}(M_{m}>n)\geq\mathbb{P}(S_{m}>n).

(43)

Write
S_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}
, where
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an i.i.d. collection of random variables with
X_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
,
\mathbb{E}X_{i}=0
,
\text{Var}(X_{i})=1
. By the Berry–Esseen theorem, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Big|\mathbb{P}\!\Big(\frac{S_{m}}{\sqrt{m}}\leq x\Big)-\Phi(x)\Big|\ \leq\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{m}},

see, e.g.,
[
7
, Thm. 3.4.17]
.

Set
x_{n}:=n/\sqrt{m}
with
m=\lfloor c_{0}n^{2}\rfloor
. The uniform Berry–Esseen bound
(see Durrett, Thm. 3.4.17) gives, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\!\Big(\frac{S_{m}}{\sqrt{m}}\leq x\Big)-\Phi(x)\Big|\;\leq\;\frac{3}{\sqrt{m}}.

Evaluating at
x=x_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}(S_{m}>n)=1-\mathbb{P}\!\Big(\frac{S_{m}}{\sqrt{m}}\leq x_{n}\Big)\ \geq\ 1-\Phi(x_{n})-\frac{3}{\sqrt{m}}.

Since
x_{n}\to 1/\sqrt{c_{0}}
and
\Phi
is globally Lipschitz with constant

1/\sqrt{2\pi}
, we have

\big|\Phi(x_{n})-\Phi(1/\sqrt{c_{0}})\big|\ \leq\ \frac{|x_{n}-1/\sqrt{c_{0}}|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.

Moreover, writing
m=\lfloor c_{0}n^{2}\rfloor=c_{0}n^{2}-r_{n}
with
r_{n}\in[0,1)
,

x_{n}=\frac{n}{\sqrt{c_{0}n^{2}-r_{n}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{0}}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-r_{n}/(c_{0}n^{2})}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{0}}}\Big(1+\frac{2\,r_{n}}{c_{0}n^{2}}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{0}}}+\frac{2}{c_{0}^{3/2}n^{2}},

for all
n
large enough (use
1/\sqrt{1-t}\leq 1+2t
for
t\in[0,1/2]
). Hence

\big|\Phi(x_{n})-\Phi(1/\sqrt{c_{0}})\big|\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{2}{c_{0}^{3/2}n^{2}}.

Combining the last two displays,

\mathbb{P}(S_{m}>n)\ \geq\ \big(1-\Phi(1/\sqrt{c_{0}})\big)\ -\ \frac{3}{\sqrt{m}}\ -\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,c_{0}^{3/2}n^{2}}.

Let

\theta\ :=\ \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\Phi(1/\sqrt{c_{0}})\big)\in(0,1).

Choose
n_{0}
so large that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
\frac{3}{\sqrt{m}}\ +\ \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,c_{0}^{3/2}n^{2}}\ \leq\ \theta
. Then, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}(S_{m}>n)\ \geq\ \theta
. Since
\{S_{m}>n\}\subseteq\{\tau_{n}<m\}
, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}<c_{0}n^{2})\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}(S_{m}>n)\ \geq\ \theta\ =\ \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\Phi(1/\sqrt{c_{0}})\big),

which is (
42
).

∎
