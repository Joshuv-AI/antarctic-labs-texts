EEG biomarkers for assessment, prognosis, and monitoring of natural upper limb recovery after stroke: a systematic review
Authors: Yifang Lin, Mingfen Li, Yajuan Su, Hewei Wang, Jie Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent upper limb deficits after stroke necessitate reliable candidate biomarkers to support precision rehabilitation. While electroencephalogram (EEG) provides a highly accessible tool to characterize post-stroke neurophysiology, its clinical translation is hindered by fragmented evidence. This systematical review critically synthesizes the directional associations between EEG biomarkers and upper limb outcomes, and introduces a novel functional framework to classify these biomarkers into assessment, prognostic, and monitoring roles for natural upper limb recovery under conventional rehabilitation.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE, SCOPUS, EMBASE, EBSCO CINAHL, and IEEE Xplore up to March 10, 2026. Studies investigating associations between quantitative EEG measures and upper limb motor outcomes in stroke adults were included. Two reviewers independently screened studies and assessed risk of bias. Data extraction classified EEG biomarkers by assessment, prognosis, and monitoring roles.ResultsForty-two studies were included, comprising 23 cross-sectional and 19 longitudinal designs. We categorized the evidence into three biomarker roles: (i) assessment, where measures like the brain symmetry index (BSI), β-band interhemispheric connectivity, and network efficiency correlated with impairment severity; (ii) prognostic, where baseline asymmetry and functional connectivity showed predictive potential; and (iii) monitoring, where longitudinal changes in oscillatory power, connectivity, and network topology paralleled functional gains. Across roles, the BSI emerged as one of the most frequently reported candidate metrics.ConclusionEEG-derived metrics, particularly the BSI, serve as frequently reported candidate biomarkers for potential clinical application in stroke rehabilitation. However, their immediate clinical translation is currently limited by the predominantly fair methodological quality of the underlying evidence. Our proposed framework helps to bridge the gap between current observational findings and future clinical utility. Future progress hinges on standardizing protocols and validating these biomarkers in large-scale rehabilitation trials to facilitate their transition toward potentially clinically useful tools.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-02028-0.Keywords: Stroke, Upper extremity, Biomarkers, Electroencephalography, Motor recovery, Neurophysiology

Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability, affecting over 93 million individuals globally [1]. Upper extremity motor deficits are among the most common and persistent impairments after stroke. Despite continuous advances in rehabilitation, large clinical trials have frequently reported neutral outcomes, with interventions providing little additional benefit over control conditions, underscoring the challenges in achieving meaningful upper limb recovery after stroke [2]. In this context, precision rehabilitation, which delivers individualized interventions at the ideal time, is crucial for maximizing upper limb recovery and functional independence [3]. This demand has led to a growing attention in reliable biomarkers of brain structure and function. Biomarkers are indicators of normal biological processes, pathogenic processes, or responses to an exposure, and in the context of stroke recovery, brain-based biomarkers have been further emphasized for their potential to guide patient stratification in clinical trials and to predict outcomes [4, 5]. Numerous brain-based biomarkers associated with upper limb function have been investigated. These include a range of neuroimaging and neurophysiological modalities, such as diffusion tensor imaging, transcranial magnetic stimulation, functional magnetic resonance imaging, functional near-infrared spectroscopy, and electroencephalogram (EEG) [6–8].

Among these, EEG has emerged as a promising tool to characterize stroke-related neurophysiological changes and cortical oscillatory dynamics, owing to its superior millisecond temporal resolution, low cost, and bedside accessibility [9]. Accumulating evidence suggests a promising role for EEG measures in predicting upper-limb motor outcomes and recovery. These findings may eventually inform precision rehabilitation if validated in larger and more rigorous studies [10]. Despite accumulating evidence, translating EEG biomarkers into clinical practice remains challenging. The literature is fragmented, featuring a wide variety of measures, paradigms, and frequency bands, which creates considerable heterogeneity and makes findings difficult for clinicians to interpret [11]. Crucially, while a prior scoping review mapped the landscape of available EEG measures [12], it did not provide a functional framework to guide their application. Without a clear distinction between biomarkers that assess current impairment, predict future outcomes, or monitor recovery, their clinical utility remains ambiguous.

To bridge this critical gap, this systematic review aims to critically synthesize the directional associations between EEG biomarkers and upper limb outcomes by: (i) characterize EEG biomarkers that reflect the severity of upper limb motor impairment; (ii) identify baseline EEG biomarkers capable of predicting motor outcomes or recovery trajectories; (iii) determine EEG biomarkers whose longitudinal changes parallel upper limb improvement, potentially serving as candidate indicators of recovery-related neurophysiological reorganization; (iv) Evaluate methodological limitations that may hinder the clinical translation of EEG biomarkers. By addressing these aims, this review may help establish a better understanding of EEG biomarkers in assessing upper limb function after stroke, and promote their clinical translation.

Methods

This systematic review was pre-registered on PROSPERO (CRD42022319841) and was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines [13]. After protocol registration, minor amendments were made to the initial protocol during the review process to refine eligibility criteria and classification strategy. All changes were updated in the PROSPERO record to ensure transparency. While this study follows a systematic search and screening protocol, its methodology is grounded in a scoping review perspective to map the broad functional landscape of EEG biomarkers. This approach was chosen to prioritize the categorization of evidence across assessment, prognostic, and monitoring roles, rather than estimating a singular aggregate effect size, which would be precluded by the high heterogeneity of current post-stroke EEG literature.

Literature search

A systematic search was conducted in five databases: MEDLINE, SCOPUS, EMBASE, EBSCO CINAHL, and IEEE Xplore. To ensure a comprehensive evidence mapping, the search time frame was open-ended, covering all records from database inception up to March 2026. The following search terms were used: ((stroke OR cerebrovascular accident OR brain infarction OR brain ischemia) AND (EEG OR electroencephalogram OR electroencephalograph OR electroencephalography) AND (upper extremity OR upper limb OR upper arm OR arm OR hand)). Search strings were adapted to meet the indexing and syntax requirements of each database. To optimize search precision for our specific clinical population while maintaining sensitivity, results were limited using standard database-specific filters to “Human” subjects, “Adults”, and peer-reviewed “Journals”. Preliminary testing confirmed these filters effectively excluded irrelevant animal models and pediatric cases without omitting eligible adult clinical studies. The full strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1. References of included studies and relevant systematic reviews were examined to identify additional eligible literature. We used NoteExpress software (version 4.0; Aegean Software Corp., Beijing, China) to manage and screen all retrieved studies.

Article selection

Two independent reviewers (LYF, LMF) screened the titles and abstracts retrieved based on the following eligibility criteria.

Inclusion criteria were: (a) non- interventional studies published in peer-reviewed journals, written in English, with full-text available; (b) studies involving adult participants diagnosed with upper extremity motor deficits resulting from stroke; and (c) studies investigating the association between EEG biomarkers and clinical assessments of upper limb motor function. Exclusion criteria were: (a) case reports, review articles, conference abstracts, or editorials; (b) studies involving animal models or participants with neurological conditions other than stroke; (c) studies primarily focused on evaluating the efficacy of specific therapeutic interventions; and (d) studies including fewer than 10 stroke participants. Studies evaluating specific therapeutic interventions were excluded to avoid the heterogeneity of treatment-induced neuroplasticity (e.g., targeted cortical modulation from specific neuromodulation devices or brain-computer interfaces), allowing us to strictly map the foundational neurophysiology of natural recovery. Additionally, a pragmatic exclusion threshold of fewer than 10 participants was established to filter out ultra-small case series incapable of reliable group-level correlational analysis, while still retaining early exploratory data whose statistical limitations were rigorously penalized during the risk of bias assessment.

A full-text screening was subsequently conducted to confirm the final selection of studies. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through re-examination of the source articles and discussion among all authors. Reasons for study exclusion at the full-text stage were documented. Additional relevant records were identified by manually screening the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews, which yielded 9 additional records for initial screening.

Data extraction and syntheses

The following data were extracted from the eligible studies and systematically organized: last name of first author and year of publication, study design, stroke recovery phase, timepoints, sample size, stroke type, EEG paradigm, EEG measures, frequency bands, upper limb motor assessments, analysis type and main findings. Timepoints refer to the time since stroke onset at the baseline EEG and/or motor assessment (T0), as well as follow-up timepoints (e.g., T1, T2) when available. Only frequency bands demonstrating a statistically significant association with upper limb motor outcomes were extracted.

While our primary design follows a systematic review framework, the profound methodological heterogeneity of the included studies precluded the calculation of an aggregate effect size. Therefore, we incorporated an evidence mapping approach and performed a narrative synthesis. Frequency counting was employed to quantify specific EEG metrics and map their distribution. Quality assessment

The quality of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (LYF and LMF) using National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies [14]. This tool comprises 14 items covering five domains of bias: study clarity and population selection, exposure assessment, outcome assessment, follow-up, and confounding control. Each item was evaluated as YES, NO, cannot determine, not applicable, or not reported. Overall quality rating of each study was assessed as either “Good”, “Fair” or “Poor” based on the answers to each item. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus among all authors.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Figure 2 illustrates the study selection process. A total of 2599 records were identified through database searching using the predefined strategy, and an additional 9 records were identified through manual screening of reference lists of relevant reviews. After removal of duplicates, 1613 articles remained. Title and abstract screening led to the exclusion of 1531 records, leaving 82 articles for full-text eligibility assessment. Of these, 40 were excluded for reasons detailed in Fig. 2, resulting in 42 studies being included in the final review for data extraction and quality assessment. The 42 included studies comprised 23 cross-sectional and 19 longitudinal designs. Sample sizes of participants with stroke varied considerably, ranging from 10 to 139. Based on the stroke recovery timeline proposed by the Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation Roundtable [57], the majority of studies focused on the subacute (n = 18, 42.9%) and chronic (n = 13, 31.0%) phases. Additionally, several studies (n = 9, 21.4%) enrolled mixed cohorts spanning multiple recovery stages, and only a small number of studies (n = 2, 4.7%) were conducted during the acute phase.

Upper limb outcome measures

Five studies (11.9%) generated a composite upper limb motor score by integrating two or more outcome measures [15, 16, 19, 31, 37]. To better characterize the clinical relevance of the remaining 37 studies (88.1%) that analyzed individual measures, we categorized these outcomes according to the domains of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF).

Body Structure and Function (Impairment domain): The vast majority of assessments targeted motor impairment. The Fugl-Meyer Assessment Upper Extremity scale (FMA-UE) was overwhelmingly the most frequent (n = 32), followed by the Modified Ashworth Scale (n = 2), grip force (n = 2), and the Medical Research Council scale (n = 2). Other measures, including pinch force, Brunnstrom motor recovery stages, and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), were each reported in 1 study.

Activity and Participation domain: Assessments evaluating functional activity were utilized far less frequently. These included the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT, n = 4), the Nine-Hole Peg Test (NHPT, n = 3), and the Box and Block Test (BBT, n = 1). Additionally, the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) and real-world actigraphy (Actiwatch) were each utilized in 1 study to evaluate daily functional activities.

These descriptive frequencies indicate a predominant reliance on impairment-level assessments, particularly the FMA-UE, and reveal a relative lack of activity-level outcomes across the current EEG biomarker literature.

EEG paradigms and frequency-domain features

Resting-state EEG was applied in 24 studies, while task-based paradigms (e.g., motor imagery, motor attempt, or motor execution) were used in 19 studies. The motor tasks varied but mainly included finger tapping, hand grasping and grip force tasks, reaching and grasping movements, wrist or forearm movements, and proximal upper limb movements.

Source analysis was performed in six studies [16, 26, 27, 31, 36, 40], while the remaining 36 studies relied on scalp-level signals without source localization. Regarding frequency bands, significant associations with upper limb motor function were most frequently reported in the β band, with 26 studies identifying relevant effects. The α band was reported in 10 studies, δ in 7 studies, θ in 6 studies, µ in 3 studies, and γ in 2 studies. Four studies investigated cross-frequency interactions, while two studies did not clearly specify the frequency band analyzed. Additionally, two studies applied the individual alpha frequency approach to define personalized frequency ranges [25, 40].

Methodological quality

Based on the NIH assessment, 11 studies (26.2%) were rated as good quality, 30 (71.4%) as fair, and 1 (2.4%) as poor. The most frequently identified limitations leading to a fair quality rating were the absence of sample size justification or power description, lack of repeated exposure assessment over time, lack of blinding of outcome assessors, and failure to measure or statistically adjust for potential confounding variables. A summary of the quality assessment is presented in Supplementary Table 3.

EEG as a biomarker of upper limb motor outcome

To better summarize the main findings, the role of EEG biomarkers in post-stroke upper limb function are reported separately in terms of assessment (Category A), prognostic (Categories B1 and B2), and monitoring (Category C).

Assessment

A total of twenty-nine studies have investigated Category A relationships, which refer to the associations between EEG biomarkers and the degree of motor impairment [15–18, 20, 22, 25, 27–34, 37, 39, 41–43, 46, 47, 49, 51–56]. The biomarkers examined in these studies can be broadly classified into six types: spectral power, event-related dynamics, interhemispheric asymmetry, microstates, functional connectivity, and graph theoretical metrics.

With respect to spectral power, seven studies focused on absolute or relative power, power spectral density (PSD), or task-related power [15, 22, 28, 33, 49, 51, 55]. All studies included patients in the subacute and chronic stage of stroke recovery, with the β band being the most frequently investigated. In the studies by Thibaut and Strens, increases in β power or PSD in the affected hemisphere tended to be associated with worse upper-limb motor performance, whereas increases in the unaffected hemisphere were generally associated with better outcomes [15, 22]. However, Zhang and Ding reported that PSD in both hemispheres was associated positively with FMA-UE and ARAT scores [49, 51]. Two additional study employed a convolutional neural network approach to analyze PSD, demonstrating predictive associations with motor performance without specifying the frequency band or hemisphere [28, 55]. Beyond the β band, Cassidy observed that increased δ power in the bilateral posterior cortex showed a positive correlation with FMA-UE [33].

Eight studies investigated event-related dynamics, including event-related desynchronization and synchronization (ERD/ERS) and inter-trial coherence (ITC) [17, 30, 31, 39, 42, 47, 52, 54]. Stronger ERD, reflected by more negative values in the α, µ, or β bands, was generally associated with better motor performance, with significant correlations reported between ERD in the affected or bilateral primary motor cortex (M1) and higher FMA-UE and Box and Block Test performances across subacute and chronic patients [30, 42, 47, 52, 54]. However, ERD was not exclusively indicative of preserved function. Chen et al. and Kaiser et al. found that stronger α ERD in the ipsilesional M1 was associated with greater wrist spasticity [17, 42]. Quandt et al. applied a task-based EEG design during movements of the affected upper limb in patients with severe corticospinal tract damage. They observed stronger β ERD in the supplementary motor area (SMA), which was associated with poorer motor outcomes [31]. Additionally, Chen et al. found that higher α and β ERS in the contralesional M1 during motor imagery was correlated positively with FMA-UE. Beyond ERD/ERS, Gyulai et al. reported that higher δ band ITC over the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex was associated with better NHPT performance [39].

Three studies analyzed interhemispheric asymmetry using the brain symmetry index (BSI) and laterality coefficient (LC) [17, 29, 51]. Both reported that greater asymmetry was associated with poorer motor outcomes, as indicated by higher BSI values correlated with lower FMA-UE scores [29, 51] and increased LC for ERS correlated with reduced muscle strength and greater spasticity [17].

One study investigated EEG microstates and demonstrated that specific microstate transition patterns were associated with upper-limb motor impairment, with transitions from microstate B to A related to better FMA-UE scores and transitions from microstate B to D related to poorer FMA-UE scores in acute stroke patients [56].

Ten studies used functional connectivity to assess the upper limb impairment, encompassing coherence, imaginary component of coherence (ICC), task-related coherence, phase synchrony index (PSI), weight phase lag index (wPLI), amplitude envelope correlation (AEC), partial directed coherence, and directed transfer function [15, 16, 20, 25, 32–34, 37, 41, 53]. Five studies investigated intra-hemispheric connectivity within the affected hemisphere. Strens et al. reported that increases in task-related coherence within the α and low β bands over the ipsilesional mesial frontal cortex were associated with poorer outcomes in chronic patients [15]. Dubovik et al. demonstrated that higher α band ICC of the ipsilesional M1, reflecting its greater integration within the residual cortical network, was associated with better motor outcomes in subacute patients [16]. Lee et al. observed that widespread alterations of intra-hemispheric ICC in δ, θ, and low-β bands were associated with more severe upper limb impairment in chronic patients [53]. Furthermore, two studies examined connectivity between the ipsilesional M1 and premotor cortex (PMC) using coherence and AEC, reporting positive and negative associations with FMA-UE, respectively [20, 34]. The remaining five studies investigated interhemispheric connectivity between homologous regions. Four of these consistently demonstrated that stronger β band connectivity was associated with better upper limb motor outcomes, including increased PSI, wPLI, and directed transfer function between interhemispheric M1 [32, 41], as well as debiased wPLI and partial directed coherence between interhemispheric sensorimotor areas [25, 37]. Additionally, one study reported that α band PSI between interhemispheric parietal lobes was positively correlated with motor outcomes [32]. In contrast, Cassidy et al. found that stronger δ band coherence between interhemispheric M1 was negatively associated with FMA-UE in subacute patients [33], whereas Kawano et al. reported that δ band PSI between interhemispheric M1 was positively associated with motor outcomes [32].

Finally, six studies investigated the associations between graph theoretical metrics and upper-limb motor performance [18, 27, 41, 43, 46, 49]. In the α and β bands, greater strength [46], clustering coefficient [46], small-worldness [27, 46], and intradensity [18], as well as shorter path length [46], all within the affected hemisphere, together with higher global efficiency across the whole brain [41, 43], were consistently correlated with better motor outcomes. In the δ band, Zhang et al. reported that strength in the affected hemisphere was correlated negatively with FMA-UE and ARAT, whereas strength in the unaffected hemisphere showed a positive correlation with motor performance [49].

Prognostic

Six studies examined Category B1 relationships, indicating the ability of EEG biomarkers to predict subsequent upper limb motor outcomes [21, 34, 38, 50, 51, 55]. Three studies focused on brain symmetry. Studies by Agius et al. and Saes et al. consistently reported that a higher BSI at baseline was associated with poorer FMA-UE scores at follow-up in subacute patients [21, 38, 51]. One study applied a deep learning approach to resting-state PSD [55]. One study investigated interregional functional connectivity. Hoshino’s team demonstrated that lower α and β band AEC between M1 and PMC, together with higher β band AEC between SMA and M1 within the affected hemisphere at baseline, predicted better FMA-UE scores at follow-up [34]. Additionally, one study applied a graph theoretical approach. Kim et al. developed a predictive model integrating multiple EEG features in one cohort and externally validated it in an independent cohort. They found that stronger ERD, higher degree centrality, and more preserved clustering coefficient in the β band within M1 of the unaffected hemisphere at baseline predicted better subsequent FMA-UE performance [50].

Ten studies addressed Category B2 relationships, highlighting the prognostic value of EEG biomarkers for motor recovery over time [19, 20, 23, 24, 32, 36, 45, 48, 54, 55]. Only one study investigated a spectral power ratio. Lacerda et al. reported that a higher theta/alpha ratio (TAR) in M1 of the affected hemisphere at baseline was associated with reduced improvements in dexterity and grip strength, whereas a higher TAR in the ipsilesional parietal cortex predicted poorer FMA-UE recovery [48]. One study explored event-related measures. Triccas et al. found that higher baseline somatosensory evoked potentials amplitude and stronger β ERS were associated with greater improvements in FMA-UE scores [54]. Seven studies examined functional connectivity and graph theoretical metrics. In the affected hemisphere, stronger β band coherence between M1 and parietal cortex and higher α band wPLI between SMA and M1 at baseline were predictive of poorer FMA-UE improvement [20, 36]. Notably, enhanced θ and δ band PSI in the contralesional M1 at baseline was associated with greater improvements in FMA-UE [32]. Furthermore, several studies demonstrated that, at baseline, higher β band weighted node degree (WND) within M1 [19] and PMC [24] of the affected hemisphere, greater local efficiency in the β band [23], greater strength in the low α band within the ipsilesional PMC [36], and lower small-worldness in the high β and γ bands [45] were all predictive of better upper-limb motor recovery.

Monitoring

Ten studies explored Category C relationships, characterizing co-occurring longitudinal correlations where EEG dynamics covary with concurrent changes in upper limb function. Two studies focused on spectral power and ratio [19, 20, 23, 26, 33, 35, 36, 40, 44, 48]. Bönstrup et al. applied a task-based paradigm and calculated low-frequency power (3–5 Hz) during the movement-preparatory window. They observed that low-frequency power in the ipsilesional sensorimotor cortex was reduced after stroke, and its re-emergence from the acute to the subacute stage was associated with greater recovery in FMA-UE, NHPT, and grip strength [26]. Lacerda’s study showed that increases in the TAR within ipsilesional M1 and parietal cortex over time were associated with greater gains in FMA-UE and dexterity, respectively [48]. Only one study assessed interhemispheric asymmetry, reporting that increases in δ band directional BSI were associated with poorer FMA-UE recovery [35]. Functional connectivity changes were region- and frequency-specific across five studies. Within the affected hemisphere, greater FMA-UE gains were associated with increased high β coherence between M1 and PMC, and with decreased coherence between M1 and parietal cortex [20]. Similarly, enhanced frontal and temporal connectivity within the affected hemisphere correlated with greater FMA-UE gains [44]. Studies on interhemispheric connectivity between homologous regions revealed a frequency-dependent pattern. Studies by Li et al. and Pirovano et al. reported that increases in α band connectivity, including wPLI between interhemispheric M1 and information flow from contralesional to ipsilesional PMC, were associated with better FMA-UE recovery [36, 40]. In contrast, increases in δ band coherence between interhemispheric M1 were associated with unfavorable recovery [33]. Finally, three studies applied graph theoretical metrics, each targeting distinct network dimensions and brain regions. In the β band, increases in WND within ipsilesional M1 were associated with less motor improvement [19], whereas increased strength within ipsilesional PMC was associated with better FMA-UE improvement [36]. Philips et al. reported that lower intradensity within the unaffected hemisphere was associated with greater FMA-UE gains [23].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized observational evidence on the application of EEG measures in relation to post-stroke upper limb function. We extend previous work by adopting a clinically-driven framework that classifies associations between EEG measures and upper limb function into assessment, prognostic, and monitoring roles, and additionally organizes them by EEG metric type and evidence consistency. Our study provides a structured perspective for understanding how different EEG measures might inform rehabilitation research and practice. These results suggest that multiple EEG features across frequency bands and metrics are associated with upper limb impairment, that some baseline measures may predict outcomes, and that longitudinal EEG changes can parallel recovery. Nevertheless, most evidence is correlational, based on small heterogeneous samples, and therefore insufficient to establish EEG as a validated surrogate endpoint for upper limb recovery after stroke. Advancing toward this goal will require larger and more rigorous studies, as well as effective integration of existing data, to enhance consistency and strengthen causal inference.

EEG biomarkers for assessment

Current findings indicate that EEG has been widely investigated as a tool for characterizing upper limb motor impairment after stroke. When applied for assessment, EEG measures can complement clinical measures by quantifying cortical oscillatory activity and network interactions that covary with motor deficits, thereby offering insights into co-occurring neurophysiological states. Moreover, they may provide a preliminary basis for patient stratification according to distinct neurophysiological profiles, which could in turn inform hypotheses about differential therapeutic needs, such as the use of excitatory versus inhibitory neuromodulation strategies [58, 59]. Based on current evidence, EEG assessment biomarkers can be organized into three aspects: frequently reported candidate measures, measures with promising but heterogeneous findings, and measures reported only in isolated studies with limited evidence.

Consistently supported biomarkers

Across included studies, three types of EEG measures demonstrated consistent associations with upper limb motor impairment. First, greater interhemispheric asymmetry, typically quantified by the BSI or LC, was invariably associated with poorer motor outcomes [17, 29]. This aligns with the well-established concept that stroke disrupts the interhemispheric balance, reducing ipsilesional activity while enhancing contralesional dominance, thereby impairing functional integration and recovery [60]. Second, stronger interhemispheric connectivity between homologous motor-related regions (i.e., M1, parietal, and sensorimotor areas), particularly in the β band, was associated with better motor performance [25, 32, 37, 41]. These findings are consistent with evidence from human neuroimaging and primate studies, suggesting that strengthened interhemispheric pathways facilitate ipsilesional recruitment and support functional reorganization [61, 62]. Third, graph theoretical analyses indicated that more efficient network topology, characterized by higher clustering coefficient, shorter path length, and greater small-worldness or global efficiency, was consistently related to superior motor performance [27, 41, 43, 46]. These measures are interrelated: clustering coefficient reflects extent of local cliquishness, path length reflects the capability for global information integration, and their balance determines small- worldness [63]. Global efficiency, defined as the average inverse shortest path length, provides an intuitive measure of network-wide information transfer [64]. These findings indicate that preserved or re-established small-world organization, enabling efficient communication at minimal cost, supports better motor capacity after stroke. Aligning with established mechanisms of post-stroke motor deficits, these consistent measures represent robust candidates for clinical stratification of impairment severity.

Promising but heterogeneous biomarkers

Several EEG measures show potential as assessment biomarkers but exhibit marked heterogeneity. Spectral power in ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres, particularly within the β band, have yielded divergent associations. Some studies correlate increased β power or PSD in the affected hemisphere with worse motor performance, while others report positive correlations in either hemisphere [15, 22, 49]. These discrepancies likely reflect the stage-dependent dynamics between ipsilesional and contralesional contributions during recovery, rather than inherent limitations of the measure itself [65, 66].

Similarly, stronger ERD in α, µ, or β bands, reflected by more negative value, is generally associated with better outcomes but has also been associated with greater spasticity [17, 30, 42, 47]. This may reflect (i) a threshold effect, where moderate ERD supports adaptive plasticity but excessive ERD indicates maladaptive hyperexcitability [60, 67, 68], or (ii) a pathway-dependent effect, where enhanced ERD is beneficial only when corticospinal pathways are preserved, but may otherwise drive maladaptive activity through alternative circuits, thereby contributing to spasticity [69]. Supporting the latter interpretation, one of the included studies found that in patients with greater corticospinal tract (CST) damage, stronger β band ERD in the SMA was associated with poorer motor function [31].

Findings for ipsilesional hemisphere connectivity are likewise inconsistent. For example, α and β band task-related coherence in frontal regions were negatively associated with motor outcomes, whereas α band ICC of M1 was positively associated [15, 16]. Connectivity between M1 and PMC within the affected hemisphere produced conflicting results [20, 34]. These inconsistencies likely stem from variation in electrode density, analytical methods, patient characteristics, and stage-specific reorganization strategies [70, 71]. Interhemispheric connectivity outside the β band has also shown mixed associations. For instance, δ band coherence between bilateral M1 was negatively related to motor outcomes, whereas θ band PSI showed positive associations [32, 33]. Such frequency-dependent effects may reflect distinct neurophysiological roles [72]. An increase in δ activity is widely considered a marker of pathological conditions and cortical dysfunction [73]. Thus, stronger δ coherence may indicate inefficient interhemispheric communication. In contrast, θ oscillations are correlated with cognitive processes such as attention, working memory, and sensorimotor integration [74]. Enhanced θ band interaction may therefore facilitate attentional resources and motor planning across hemisphere.

Overall, these findings suggest that local oscillatory and connectivity measures may capture complementary aspects of cortical reorganization. However, their clinical interpretability is highly context-dependent, varying with lesion stage, cortical region, and oscillatory frequency. Systematic validation across recovery stages and patient subgroups is required before these measures can be regarded as reliable assessment biomarkers.

Biomarkers reported in isolated studies

A few EEG biomarkers have been reported only in single studies and remain preliminary. These include higher β intradensity ratio and α network strength within the ipsilesional hemisphere, and enhanced δ band ITC over the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [18, 39, 46]. All were positively associated with motor performance, suggesting that preserved intrahemispheric coupling, engagement of prefrontal control processes, and greater network integration may each support recovery [75]. While these isolated findings raise hypotheses for novel assessment biomarkers, their clinical utility remains uncertain. Their interpretation should be particularly cautious, as they originate primarily from single studies with “Fair” methodological quality, and in some instances from evidence with even greater methodological limitations.

EEG biomarkers for prognostic

Current research suggests that EEG biomarkers hold promise for predicting post-stroke upper limb recovery. Growing evidence supports their role as prognostic biomarkers. Consistent with the FDA’s framework, they may support patient stratification by identifying individuals most likely to progress in clinical trials [2, 76]. The prognostic utility of EEG measures can be considered along two complementary dimensions.

Category B1 biomarkers estimate the likely absolute level of future upper limb motor outcome. Such measures are valuable for stratifying patients in rehabilitation trials and for enhancing statistical power by controlling for baseline prognostic heterogeneity. Evidence remains preliminary but points to several promising candidates. For instance, the BSI has consistently shown negative associations with subsequent motor outcomes [21, 38]. Notably, these longitudinal findings converge with cross-sectional evidence [17, 29], reinforcing interhemispheric imbalance as a robust correlate of motor impairment that may also carry prognostic value [77, 78]. Preliminary evidence from one study suggested that higher baseline α and β coupling between M1 and PMC predict poorer motor outcomes [34], though its generalizability remains uncertain. Additionally, multivariate modeling approaches that integrate ERD with graph theoretical metrics represent a methodological advance toward improved prognostic accuracy. However, current findings are limited by modest sample sizes and lack of external replication, and further work is needed to establish their reliability for clinical stratification.

Category B2 biomarkers predict the degree of functional improvement and provide insights into the brain’s intrinsic recovery potential. Three types of measure have been investigated. First, spectral power ratios have received limited attention, with only one study reporting that higher TAR in ipsilesional M1 and parietal cortex predicted poorer recovery [48]. This isolated finding requires validation before its prognostic value can be confirmed. Second, frequency-specific connectivity measures revealed divergent associations for contralesional versus ipsilesional intracortical circuitry. Enhanced contralesional θ and δ band connectivity centered on M1 predicted greater motor improvements [32], consistent with the supportive role of contralesional hemisphere in recovery [79]. Low-frequency synchronization is often interpreted as promoting large-scale coordination that may facilitate the recruitment of distributed networks [80]. In contrast, stronger α and β band connectivity within ipsilesional motor networks predicted poorer improvement [20, 36]. Such over-synchronization may reflect inefficient recruitment of damaged motor circuits. Neuroimaging studies provide converging evidence, showing that excessive ipsilesional activity in M1, PMC and SMA were associated with poorer recovery, whereas subsequent normalization accompanies functional improvement [81]. Third, graph-theoretical metrics have demonstrated heterogeneous associations. Small-scale network properties such as WND and nodal strength within M1 and PMC, as well as large-scale network properties including local efficiency and small-worldness, were variably related to upper limb recovery [19, 23, 24, 36, 45]. As these topological metrics are higher-order abstractions derived from functional connectivity rather than direct physiological interactions, their mechanistic interpretation remains challenging [79]. Accordingly, their prognostic value should be regarded as exploratory, with limited immediate translational relevance. In practice, EEG features are often most useful when integrated into multivariate models alongside established predictors such as CST damage and baseline motor impairment. In this complementary role, EEG may enhance prognostic accuracy rather than serve as a stand-alone predictor.

EEG biomarker for monitoring

Current evidence shows that several EEG metrics correlate longitudinally with functional recovery. However, a key conceptual distinction is necessary: without controlled interventions, it remains unclear whether these EEG shifts drive recovery, result from behavioral improvement, or are simply parallel epiphenomena [9]. Consequently, while these measures track neurophysiological trajectories, they currently serve as candidate rather than definitive monitoring biomarkers. Establishing true monitoring utility requires evidence that these biomarkers can dynamically and causally reflect changes induced by specific, targeted therapies.

Spectral power and ratio measures appear to reflect restoration of physiological rhythms within ipsilesional sensorimotor networks. For instance, the re-emergence of low-frequency activity and longitudinal increases in the TAR within ipsilesional motor areas were consistently correlated with greater motor gains [26, 48]. These findings suggest that normalization or adaptive up-regulation of oscillatory dynamics may accompany more favorable recovery trajectories.

Although evidence relating BSI change to recovery is limited to a single study [35], interhemispheric asymmetry is widely recognized as a pathological condition that hinders motor recovery. This is consistent with longitudinal transcranial magnetic stimulation findings showing that abnormal interhemispheric inhibition, emerging from the acute to the chronic stage, correlated inversely with motor improvement [82].

Reorganization of functional connectivity in parallel with motor improvement has been observed. Within the affected hemisphere, strengthened high-β coupling between M1 and PMC, reduced connectivity between M1 and parietal cortex, and enhanced frontal-temporal interactions were each associated with better recovery [20, 44]. These patterns may reflect selective reinforcement of premotor-motor pathways [83], reduced dependence on less efficient parietal-motor interaction [84, 85], and the supportive engagement of broader frontotemporal networks [86]. Interhemispheric connectivity between homologous motor cortices further revealed frequency-specific effects. Increases in α band coupling appeared to support recovery, consistent with the role of α oscillations in coordinating large-scale neural communication and facilitating sensorimotor integration [87, 88]. In contrast, increases in δ band coherence between bilateral M1 may reflect distressed attentional control and motivational processes, hindering motor recovery.

Graph theoretical analyses also highlighted changes in WND, nodal strength, and intradensity that accompanied upper limb improvement. These metrics reflect local information transfer and intrahemispheric integration, and may serve as candidate monitoring biomarkers by capturing network-level adaptations.

Overall, EEG-based longitudinal markers provide informative descriptions of post-stroke recovery trajectories. However, the current observational evidence remains insufficient to establish them as definitive monitoring biomarkers of rehabilitation effects. Their translational relevance will depend on standardized protocols, larger longitudinal cohorts, and controlled trials demonstrating that these EEG changes dynamically capture therapy-induced neurophysiological change.

Versatility and reliability of EEG biomarkers

Several EEG biomarkers demonstrate relevance across assessment, prognostic, and monitoring domains, underscoring their potential versatility. This cross-domain applicability aligns with the concept of biological gradients: (i) cross-sectional gradients; (ii) future (prediction) gradients; and (iii) parallel gradients [70]. The distribution of evidence across assessment, prognostic, and monitoring roles for all EEG biomarkers is shown in Fig. 3, which serves as a visual evidence map of the frequency and consistency of specific EEG metrics across different clinical roles. Among these, interhemispheric asymmetry provides the most consistent example. BSI not only correlates with baseline impairment severity but also predicts long-term outcomes, and its longitudinal modulation reflects recovery-related rebalancing of cortical activity. Similarly, interhemispheric connectivity between homologous motor cortices in the α and β bands shows convergent evidence of clinical utility, with associations reported for both baseline impairment and recovery-related changes. These features therefore represent the most frequently reported cross-domain candidate biomarkers identified to date.

In summary, while metrics like the BSI represent frequently characterized functional patterns across clinical roles, their underlying evidence is predominantly of “Fair” methodological quality. Consequently, they should currently be regarded as promising candidates rather than fully validated clinical tools. The transition of these metrics into clinical practice is heavily bottlenecked by variations in EEG paradigms and fundamental methodological limitations.

A second group of candidates involves ipsilesional M1 connectivity with other motor-related regions, including PMC, parietal cortex, SMA, and contralesional M1 areas. These measures also demonstrate cross-domain associations, but with lower consistency. For example, while interhemispheric M1 connectivity in α and β bands appears relatively consistently reported, associations involving θ and δ bands activity or broader M1-centered networks are more variable. Such findings suggest that M1-centered connectivity captures important but context-sensitive aspects of reorganization.

Finally, spectral power indices and graph theoretical metrics have also been reported across assessment, prognostic, and monitoring roles. However, their associations are strongly influenced by stroke stage, lesion profile, hemisphere-specific contributions, frequency band, and analytic approach. At present, these measures should be considered exploratory rather than established cross-domain biomarkers.

Notably, the relative consistency of reporting across these roles is not equivalent: assessment appears most reliable, monitoring shows promise but is highly sensitive to rehabilitation exposure and timing, whereas prognostic applications remain the most vulnerable to heterogeneity in baseline damage and CST injury. Clarifying these boundaries will be essential for advancing the translational utility of EEG biomarkers in stroke rehabilitation.

Influence of EEG paradigms: resting-state vs. task-based dynamics

The choice of EEG paradigm fundamentally influences the neurophysiological processes captured and is therefore a major contributor to heterogeneity across studies. In the context of biomarker development, resting-state and task-based paradigms offer complementary advantages and may reflect different aspects of post-stroke network reorganization [9, 12].

Resting-state EEG (rsEEG) primarily characterizes the intrinsic baseline functional architecture of the brain [9, 12]. Its major advantage is task-independence, as it places minimal cognitive or physical demands on the patient and is therefore feasible even in individuals with severe impairment. This reduces confounding related to task performance, effort, and sensory feedback. Consistent with this, several relatively frequently reported biomarkers in the current literature, particularly measures related to interhemispheric asymmetry and resting-state connectivity, have often been derived from rsEEG paradigms [21, 29, 32]. However, rsEEG is less informative about the motor network’s dynamic capacity to organize movement-related processes [9, 70].

By contrast, task-based EEG probes the active recruitment of motor-related networks, but its findings are strongly influenced by the specific task employed [12, 70]. A simple movement such as finger tapping may primarily engage residual primary motor cortex function, whereas more complex actions such as grasping may require broader compensatory recruitment involving the premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, and, in some cases, contralesional regions [31, 47]. This task dependence may partly explain the heterogeneity observed across studies, particularly for metrics such as ipsilesional connectivity and event-related dynamics [30, 42, 81].

Within task-based paradigms, an additional distinction should be made between motor execution and motor imagery. Motor execution reflects overt functional performance but is constrained by residual motor ability and influenced by somatosensory feedback. In contrast, motor imagery can probe covert motor planning and intention-related processes without requiring overt output, making it particularly valuable in severely impaired patients [17, 42]. For this reason, motor imagery may provide access to aspects of preserved or reorganized network function that are not readily captured through execution-based tasks alone. This distinction is especially relevant when interpreting findings such as stronger ERD in severely impaired patients, which may reflect compensatory or maladaptive recruitment rather than more preserved physiological motor function [17, 31, 69].

Taken together, these considerations suggest that EEG biomarker performance and interpretation are paradigm-dependent. Future studies should therefore align paradigm selection with the specific clinical dimension of interest, such as baseline network architecture, overt motor execution, or covert motor planning, and improve paradigm standardization to enhance cross-study comparability and biomarker development [9, 12, 70].

Methodological limitations hindering clinical translation

Several methodological challenges currently limit the clinical translation of EEG biomarkers for post-stroke upper limb recovery. First, most studies are based on small and heterogeneous samples, reducing generalizability. Notably, several included studies enrolled the bare minimum threshold of 10 participants. Such critically underpowered cohorts raise significant concerns regarding statistical power, overfitting, and selection bias, which is reflected in their “Fair” quality ratings. Based on our NIH assessment, more than 70% of the included studies were rated as “Fair” and one as “Poor”, further compounded by a widespread lack of blinding and failure to adjust for confounding variables. Although some subgroup analyses (e.g., cortical vs. subcortical lesions, subacute vs. chronic stage, or CST integrity) have been conducted, these remain sparse and typically underpowered, limiting robust conclusions across patient subgroups. Furthermore, psychometric properties such as test-retest reliability, validity, and minimal detectable change have rarely been examined. Consequently, large-scale studies with a priori sample size calculations are urgently needed to validate these preliminary findings.

Second, study protocols display extreme variability in experimental paradigms (e.g., resting-state vs. task-based conditions, simple vs. complex multi-joint movements), EEG acquisition parameters (e.g., varying electrode densities, differing reference schemes), and preprocessing and analytical pipelines (e.g., fixed vs. individualized frequency bands, sensor- vs. source-level analyses, distinct artifact rejection methods). This profound methodological heterogeneity makes direct cross-study comparability and quantitative evidence integration (such as meta-analysis) exceptionally challenging, thereby complicating the clinical interpretation of the isolated findings.

Third, the lack of standardization in how similar EEG biomarkers are defined and computed poses a severe barrier to clinical translation. For instance, the same physiological phenomenon is variably described as “ERD increase,” “β desynchronization,” or “relative power decrease,” occasionally with opposite directionality conventions. Furthermore, generic terms like “strength” are often overloaded. They are used in some studies to denote simple pairwise functional connectivity and in others to represent complex graph-theoretical nodal properties. This terminological and methodological heterogeneity introduces profound interpretive ambiguity, preventing the establishment of normative cut-off values. For these biomarkers to transition from experimental observations to reliable clinical tools, establishing international, consensus-based protocols for EEG acquisition, computational pipelines, and standardized nomenclature is a prerequisite.

Fourth, EEG inherently captures cortical dynamics but provides limited sensitivity to subcortical processes. Multimodal integration with structural or functional imaging is therefore needed to enhance mechanistic interpretability and clinical translation.

Fifth, based on our ICF framework categorization, the current literature exhibits a predominant reliance on impairment-level assessments, specifically the FMA-UE. Consequently, existing evidence primarily links EEG biomarkers to the body structure and function domain. Because changes in impairment scales (e.g., a reduction in MAS score) do not necessarily translate to clinically meaningful improvements in daily functional performance, a gap remains in mapping EEG biomarkers to activity-level outcomes. It remains to be determined whether these neurophysiological metrics can reliably reflect or predict real-world upper limb utilization. To address this, future studies should incorporate more activity and participation-level assessments (e.g., ARAT, NHPT) to better evaluate the clinical meaningfulness of these biomarker associations.

Finally, a notable limitation of this review is the explicit exclusion of interventional studies, which restricts our scope to natural recovery trajectories. Consequently, the evidence supporting ‘Monitoring’ (Category C) biomarkers is fundamentally correlational. The most compelling evidence for a monitoring biomarker typically requires demonstrating that it dynamically captures changes driven by a specific, known therapy. While our observational approach cannot establish this causal link, mapping the natural temporal covariance provides an essential ‘proof of concept’ before deploying these markers in complex interventional trials.

Future recommendations

Translating observational EEG findings into clinical tools remains challenging. To bridge this gap, future research must standardize acquisition protocols and promote data sharing to build normative datasets. Integrating EEG with multimodal neuroimaging and clinical assessments may help develop more reliable composite candidate biomarkers. Meanwhile, technological advances like dry electrodes, portable systems, and AI-driven pipelines will accelerate bedside application. Crucially, as the field transitions from observational to interventional paradigms, candidate markers must be validated within active trials to strengthen causal inference [70]. Specifically, evaluating EEG metrics alongside non-invasive brain stimulation and closed-loop neuromodulation will determine whether these biomarkers can dynamically and causally capture treatment-induced neuroplasticity. Ultimately, embedding biomarker-informed stratification into adaptive rehabilitation trials will facilitate the transition from experimental correlates to actionable tools, guiding precision neurorehabilitation.

Conclusion

EEG provides a valuable descriptive window into the neural dynamics of post-stroke recovery. This systematic review demonstrates that specific EEG features, particularly the BSI, emerge as frequently reported candidate biomarkers for upper limb function. However, the current evidence base is predominantly characterized by fair methodological quality, which limits the immediate clinical application of these findings. By introducing a functional framework for assessment, prognosis, and monitoring, we help bridge the gap between current observational findings and future clinical utility. The path forward requires a concerted effort toward standardization, multimodal integration, and validation in rehabilitation trials, ultimately paving the way for more precise neurorehabilitation for stroke survivors.
