Gingival mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomal miR-23a-3p targets IL-6R to attenuate autoimmune insulitis: a cell-free therapeutic strategy for type 1 diabetes
Authors: Qing Qu, Si-Yu Liu, Bin Fu, Qiang Ao, Yong Long, Zi-Yu Liu, Wu-Mei Wei, Wei-Jian Hou, Xiao-Li Liu, Xiao-Hong Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 1 diabetes (T1D) is characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells, yet current therapies fail to address immune dysregulation. Gingival mesenchymal stem cells (GMSC) exhibit immunomodulatory potential, but cell-based therapies face challenges in standardization and survival. Exosomes, nanosized vesicles delivering bioactive molecules like miRNAs, offer a cell-free alternative. This study investigates GMSC-derived EXO (GMSC-EXO) enriched with miR-23a-3p as a novel strategy to attenuate T1D progression.MethodsSpontaneous T1D in NOD/LtJ mice was treated with GMSC-EXO. Exosome secretion was inhibited using GW4869, and miR-23a-3p was knocked down via LV3-miR-23a-3p inhibitor. Blood glucose, pancreatic inflammation, and immune cell dynamics were monitored in vivo. CD4+ T-cell subsets (Th1/Th17, Tregs) and cytokine levels were analyzed via flow cytometry and ELISA. Dual-luciferase assays and Western blot validated miR-23a-3p targeting of IL-6R and downstream IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 signaling.ResultsGMSC-EXO delayed hyperglycemia progression and improved survival. Depleting exosomes or miR-23a-3p diminished therapeutic efficacy. Mechanistically, miR-23a-3p directly silenced IL-6R, suppressing IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 signaling, reducing Th1/Th17 infiltration, and expanding Tregs and IL-10 + cells. In vitro, GMSC-EXO reduced IFN-γ/IL-17 A and elevated IL-10, effects abolished by miR-23a-3p inhibition.ConclusionsGMSC-EXO deliver miR-23a-3p to target IL-6R, restoring immune balance and mitigating insulitis in T1D. This study highlights the miR-23a-3p/IL-6R axis as a therapeutic target and establishes GMSC-EXO as a safe, cell-free strategy for autoimmune disorders. These findings provide a foundation for engineering exosome-based therapies in diabetes and related conditions.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04744-1.

Background

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by immune-mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells, leading to insulin deficiency and lifelong dependence on exogenous insulin [1, 2]. Despite advances in glycemic control strategies, current therapies—including insulin replacement and islet transplantation—fail to address the root cause of immune dysregulation and are constrained by risks of immune rejection and donor scarcity [3]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as promising immunomodulators due to their ability to suppress pathogenic T cells and promote regulatory T cell (Treg) expansion [4, 5]. Among MSCs sources, gingival mesenchymal stem cells (GMSC) stand out for their accessibility, rapid proliferation, and potent immunosuppressive capacity [6, 7]. In addition, short- and long-term toxicity tests show that GMSC are safe in a variety of autoimmune animal models, and no genotoxicity or tumors have been detected [8]. However, their clinical translation faces challenges inherent to cell-based therapies, including batch-to-batch variability, limited long-term survival post-transplantation, and logistical hurdles in large-scale manufacturing and storage [9, 10]. Moreover, the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs primarily depends on paracrine signaling rather than direct cell replacement [11, 12], highlighting the need to identify the key mediators of their immunomodulatory effects.

Exosomes, nanosized extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm in diameter) secreted by cells, serve as pivotal mediators of intercellular communication by transferring bioactive molecules such as miRNAs, proteins, and lipids [13, 14]. These vesicles encapsulate the functional repertoire of their parental cells, enabling them to exert immunomodulatory and regenerative effects without the risks associated with whole-cell therapies (e.g., immunogenicity or tumorigenicity) [15, 16]. The therapeutic application of exosomes for diabetes and its complications has gained widespread utilization. For instance, Liang et al. [17] demonstrated that exosomes from adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells effectively improve diabetic wound healing and suppress skin fibrosis through the miR-128-1-5p/TGF-β1/Smad axis. Song et al. [18] utilized human umbilical cord stem cell-derived exosomes to enhance SIRT1/FoxO1/3a-mediated mitochondrial function, thereby counteracting diabetes-induced muscle atrophy. Fan et al. [19] employed hydrogel-loaded exosomes from hypoxia-preconditioned urine-derived stem cells, which significantly inhibited inflammation and accelerated wound healing in diabetes. Similarly, another research group combined photo-crosslinkable hydrogel with citrus-derived exosomes, confirming their efficacy in promoting diabetic wound repair [20]. Notably, GMSC-derived exosomes (GMSC-EXO) have demonstrated therapeutic potential in diverse inflammatory and degenerative conditions. For instance, they suppress collagen-induced arthritis by blocking the IL-17RA-NF-κB pathway [21], enhance M2 macrophage polarization to mitigate periodontal bone loss [22], and promote sensory nerve regeneration in taste bud injury models [23, 24]. Despite these advances, their role in autoimmune diabetes remains unexplored. Given the central involvement of immune dysregulation and β-cell destruction in T1D, GMSC-EXO may offer a cell-free strategy to modulate autoreactive T cells and protect pancreatic islets. To validate this hypothesis, we employed GW4869, a neutral sphingomyelinase inhibitor, to inhibit exosome biogenesis in GMSC [25], thereby dissecting the contribution of exosomal signaling to their therapeutic efficacy.

The rationale for focusing on miR-23a-3p stems from its unique combination of abundance, functional relevance, and exosomal specificity. As an important component of exosomes, miRNAs are the main contributors to the biological functions of exosomes [26–28]. Systematic miRNA profiling of MSCs-derived exosomes identified miR-23a-3p as the second most abundant miRNA in cargo, suggesting its potential dominance in mediating exosomal functions [29]. In parallel, our collaborators detected miR-23a-3p among the top 14 most abundant miRNAs (ranked 14th/738) in GMSC-EXO, further underscoring its exosomal prominence. Beyond quantitative significance, miR-23a-3p exhibits dual therapeutic properties critical for T1D: (1) it suppresses pro-apoptotic proteins in human pancreatic β-cells, enhancing β-cell survival [30]; and (2) it exerts broad anti-inflammatory effects by modulating immune cell polarization [31]. These attributes position miR-23a-3p as a prime candidate for mediating GMSC-EXO’s therapeutic effects.

NOD/LtJ mice are the core models for studying T1D pathogenesis, genetic-environmental interactions and treatment strategies due to their spontaneous disease progression, high human similarity between genetics and pathology, and strong plasticity. Therefore, we interrogate the role of miR-23a-3p-enriched GMSC-EXO in T1D using the NOD/LtJ murine model. By selectively inhibiting miR-23a-3p in GMSC-EXO, we demonstrate its indispensable role in attenuating hyperglycemia and pancreatic inflammation. Mechanistically, miR-23a-3p directly targets IL-6R, a key driver of autoimmune insulitis. Our study not only validates GMSC-EXO as a superior cell-free therapy but also establishes miR-23a-3p/IL-6R axis as a novel therapeutic target for T1D.

Methods

Animals

NOD/LtJ mice (female, 7-week-old, 20–25 g) were procured from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology (Beijing, China). C57BL/6J mice (male, 6–8-week-old, 20–25 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng biotechnology company (Shenyang, China). All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at China Medical University. The animals were kept in controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) at the specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility of the Department of Animal of China Medical University. All mice were acclimatized for one week prior to the start of experiments. The mice were humanely euthanized following the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition). Anesthesia was initially induced and maintained using 3–4% isoflurane in 100% oxygen delivered via a precision vaporizer (ISOFLURANE USP, Piramal Critical Care, Bethlehem, PA). Following loss of consciousness, terminal anesthesia was achieved through gradual displacement of 70% carbon dioxide in medical-grade air using a euthanasia chamber (Euthanex™ System, Braintree Scientific, Braintree, MA) over a 5-minute period. This method was immediately followed by exsanguination via thoracotomy under deep anesthesia as a secondary confirmation of death. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional IACUC approval (Protocol #CMU2020204) and included verification of cessation of corneal reflexes, respiratory arrest, and cardiac asystole confirmed by Doppler ultrasound (Model 8400, Parks Medical Electronics, Inc.). This research is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines Version 2.0.

Isolation, culturing and characterization of human GMSC

Human tissue samples were acquired from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, sourced from discarded tissues of periodontal patients undergoing routine dental surgeries (excluding those with autoimmune or inflammatory diseases). Informed consent was obtained from all participating human subjects, adhering to the guidelines recommended by the Clinical Research Ethics Review Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The isolation of human GMSC was conducted following previously established protocols [32, 33].

The cells were cultured in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 1% L-glutamine (Gibco) and 1% non-essential amino acids (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified tissue culture incubator with 5% CO2. Flow cytometry analysis was performed to confirm that cells were positive for MSC surface markers CD44, CD105, CD73 and CD90, and negative for hematopoietic stem cell surface markers CD45 and CD34 (Biolegend). Phenotypic identification of GMSC was confirmed by flow cytometry, while their multipotent differentiation capabilities were verified through adipogenic and osteogenic induction. For the specific details of this part of the experiment, please refer to the results of researchers in the same experimental group [21]. In this study, GMSC from the third to fourth passages were utilized.

Production and characterization of GMSC-EXO

GMSC-EXO was extracted and purified utilizing Exosome Extraction Kit (Umibio Science and Technology Group, China), with the operational procedures adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s instructions. Human third-generation GMSC were transferred into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris from the cell supernatant. To further purify the sample, Exosome concentration solution (ECS, UR52121, Umibio) was added to the centrifuged supernatant, with a ratio of 5 mL of ECS reagent per 20 mL of sample. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and then incubated at 2–8 °C for 2 h. Afterward, the centrifuge tube containing the mixture was then removed and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 60 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded and the precipitate was found to contain abundant exosome particles. To clean the centrifuged precipitate, 100 µL of 1×PBS was sprayed evenly over the precipitate. The suspension was then transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 g. The pellet was delicately aspirated and carefully transferred to the upper chamber of the exosome purification flter (EPF column), followed by centrifugation at 3,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, the purified exosome particles, located at the bottom of the EPF column, were collected. Finally, the concentration of exosomes was assayed, and their characteristics were evaluated using electron microscopy, particle size analysis, and specific markers. The collected exosome pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). The morphology and size of exosomes were assessed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi HT7700, JP), while their size distribution was analyzed by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA, Zetasizer Nano ZS90). The presence of specific exosome markers (CD9, CD63 and CD81) (ABclonal Technology) was confirmed by Western blot analysis.

Lentivirus construction and infection

Lentiviruses containing miR-23a-3p inhibitor and its negative control of inhibitor were purchased from GenePharma (Suzhou, China). Subsequently, GMSC were infected with either the miR-23a-3p inhibitor (GMSCmiR−23aI) or the negative control of inhibitor (GMSCINC). Following a 72-hour incubation period post-infection, exosomes secreted by GMSCmiR−23aI (GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO) and GMSCINC (GMSCINC-EXO) were isolated and purified.

Pretreatment of GMSC with GW4869

When cell fusion reached 80%, GW4869 (Sigma) was added to the culture medium at a concentration of 10 µM (GMSC-GW4869). The cells were then treated with GW4869 for 24 h.

Experimental groups for in vivo studies

Animals were randomly divided into 8 groups, namely PBS group (n = 6), GMSC-GW4869 group (n = 4), GMSC group (n = 4), GMSC-EXO group (n = 5), GMSCmiR−23aI group (n = 5), GMSCINC group (n = 4), GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO group (n = 5) and GMSCINC-EXO group (n = 4). Animals were assigned to experimental groups using stratified randomization, as follows: Stratified by weight (± 10 g interval) and sex; Random numbers were generated using the Excel RAND() function, sorted by stratification label and then sequentially assigned to the 8 experimental groups; Ensure that baseline parameters (weight, sex) are balanced across groups. Records of randomization sequence generation and allocation were saved in the experiment log.

At week 8, these animals were administered either stem cells (1 × 106/mouse) or exosomes (150 µg/mouse) via tail vein injection. In the control group, mice received equal PBS injection. The experimental unit was an individual mouse. Blood glucose levels were monitored weekly through tail bleeding, with diabetes diagnosed based on two consecutive readings exceeding 13.88 mmol/L.

The sample size was determined based on the following: Ethical compliance: following the minimization requirements of the 3R principles and approved by the Ethics Committee (approval number: CMU2020204); Statistical calculations: sample size required for ANOVA (≥ 4 per group) was calculated by G*Power based on pre-experimental effect sizes (d = 1.2), α = 0.05, Power = 0.8; Literature reference: refer to the range of sample sizes (n = 4–6/group) for similar designs in published studies (PMID: 12391400); Experimental feasibility: balancing resource constraints with the statistical needs of key groups (e.g., GMSC-EXO) to appropriately increase sample size.

The inclusion criteria are as follows. Health Status: only animals with no visible signs of disease (e.g., normal weight gain, active behavior) during acclimatization (7 days) were included. Age & Weight: Female NOD/LtJ mice, aged 7 weeks, body weight 20–25 g (to minimize metabolic variability). Baseline Measurements: animals with baseline physiological parameters (e.g., blood pressure, heart rate) within ± 10% of group mean were included.

The exclusion criteria (Predefined) are as follows. Health Issues: Animals showing signs of infection, injury, or abnormal behavior pre-intervention were excluded. Procedural Complications: Animals dying during intervention (e.g., anesthesia overdose, surgical complications) were excluded from analysis. Technical Failures: Data points from animals from which sample collection failed (e.g., due to hemolyzed blood or contaminated tissues) were excluded. Data Point Exclusion (Predefined): Outliers: Data points > 3 standard deviations from group mean (Grubbs’ test, α = 0.05) were excluded. Measurement Errors: Incomplete or invalid recordings (e.g., equipment malfunction) were removed.

Declaration of A Priori Criteria: all criteria were established before data collection and documented in the approved animal protocol (IACUC CMU2020204).

To minimize confounding bias, the following measures were taken: Treatment sequence randomization: daily operation sequence was randomized by Latin square design; Cage position randomized rotation: cage positions were randomly adjusted weekly to balance environmental differences; Blinded analysis: data collection and analysts were blinded to the group; Baseline balance validation: no difference between groups at baseline was confirmed by statistical tests (p > 0.05).

To minimize bias and ensure experimental integrity, group assignment awareness and blinding were tightly controlled at all stages of the study: treatment assignments were coded using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) to prevent bias in the handling of animals by researchers. Third-party researchers (who kept the identity of each group confidential) dispensed treatment solutions (e.g., exosomes, inhibitors) labeled only with unique identifiers. Researchers performing treatments (e.g., tail vein injections) are aware of group assignments to ensure accuracy of dosage (e.g., exosome concentration, inhibitor volume). All other experimental procedures (e.g., glucose monitoring, tissue collection) were performed by technicians and group identities were blinded using a standardized protocol. Data collection and preliminary analysis were performed by technicians and group identity was kept confidential. Sample identifiers were decoded only after data were exported to statistical software.

In vitro co-culture assay

There are five groups for the assay, namely CD4+ T cell group, GMSC group, GMSC-EXO group, GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO group, and GMSCINC-EXO group. CD4+ T cells were isolated from splenocytes of C57BL/6J mice utilizing the CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. CD4+ T cells were then cultured alone or co-cultured with GMSC, GMSC-EXO, GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO and GMSCINC-EXO, respectively. Flow cytometer (LSRFortessa, BD, USA) was employed to determine the proportions of Th1, Th17, CD4+IL-10+ and Treg cells; enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Mouse ELISA Kit, Biolegend) was used to detect the levels of IFN-γ, IL-17 A and IL-10 in culture supernatants.

H&E staining

The pancreas was sectioned along its longitudinal axis and divided equally into two parts. One segment was preserved with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently prepared for H&E staining. The other segment was immediately stored in a -80℃ refrigerator for Western blot analysis. The pancreatic tissues from the mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, underwent gradual dehydration, were embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned into 4-micron-thick slices. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). At least 12 islets from each group were randomly selected and scored blindly.

Flow cytometry analysis

Total spleen cells were collected from the spleen of NOD/LtJ mice. The cellular surface markers were labeled with fluorescently conjugated antibodies specific for CD4 and CD25 (Biolegend) and incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 20 min. For intracellular staining, the cells were stimulated with a cocktail of PMA (50 ng/ml), BFA (10 µg/ml), and ionomycin (1 µg/ml) (Biolegend) for 4 h, followed by permeabilization using either membrane-permeabilizing or nuclear-permeabilizing buffer. Subsequently, the cells were stained with fluorescent antibodies targeting IFN-γ, IL-17 A, IL-10, and Foxp3 (Biolegend) and incubated in the dark at room temperature for an additional 20 min. After thorough washing with buffer, the stained cells were analyzed using BD LSRFortessa flow cytometry, and the acquired data were processed with FlowJo software (version 10.0).

Western blot analysis

Total pancreatic protein was extracted from NOD/LtJ mice following standard procedures. For detection, the following antibodies were utilized: JAK1 Rabbit mAb at a concentration of 1:1000 (Cell Signaling Technology; 3344 S), STAT3 Rabbit mAb at a concentration of 1:2000 (Cell Signaling Technology; 12640 S), IL-6R Rabbit mAb at a concentration of 1:1500 (Bioss; bs-23659R), anti-beta Actin Mouse mAb at a concentration of 1:2000 (Servicebio; GB12001), Goat-anti rabbit HRP at a concentration of 1:3000 (Servicebio; GB23303), Goat-anti mouse HRP at a concentration of 1:5000 (Servicebio; GB23301). Protein bands were visualized using the Tanon high-sig ECL Western blotting Substrate and Tanon 5200 multifunction laser-scanning system (Tanon).

ELISA assays

Peripheral blood was collected from the eyelids of NOD/LtJ mice, allowed to stand for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min to obtain the serum. The levels of cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-17 A, IL-10) in co-culture supernatants or serum were quantified using a Mouse ELISA Kit (Biolegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO and GMSCINC-EXO using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of the total RNA were evaluated using the NanoDrop. cDNA was synthesized and amplified using the Bulge-Loop miRNA qRT-PCR Starter Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China). All cDNA samples were frozen at − 20℃ until use. PCR amplification was conducted as follows: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 2 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 70 °C for 10 s. U6 was treated as the internal control for miRNA expression. The results were recorded and analyzed using Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific, USA). The miRNA relative expression was calculated by 2–ΔΔCT method. Three independent experiments were conducted. The primers used were provided by Ribobio.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

293T cells were cultured in DMEM/HIGH GLUCOSE supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. When the cells reached 50%-60% confluence in 24-well plates, the cells were co-transfected with either 0.5 µg of pmirGLO-wt-IL-6R or pmirGLO-mut-IL-6R (Synbio Technologies, Suzhou, China) and 20 pmol of miR-NC or miR-23a-3p-mimics (GenePharma, Suzhou, China) using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (11668030, Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, the relative luciferase activity was measured. Data were analyzed and normalized to the Renilla luciferase vector using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The experiment was repeated three times.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Prior to analysis, data normality was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was verified using F test (for two groups) or Brown-Forsythe test (for three or more groups). The verification experiment of LV3-miR-23a-3p inhibitor transfection passed the Shapiro-Wilk test (P>0.05) and failed the F test (P < 0.05). Therefore, Welch’s t test was used for comparison between the two groups. All other data were analyzed by unpaired t-tests (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA (for three or more groups). Dual luciferase assay data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Error bars represent the mean ± SD. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Identification of GMSC and GMSC-EXO, and verification of LV3-miR-23a-3p inhibitor transfection

For the identification of GMSC, we followed the previous report [19]. Regarding the optimal conditions for transfecting GMSC with lentiviral vectors, we achieved the highest transfection rate when the virus stock solution to medium ratio was 1:50, with a transfection efficiency exceeding 90% (Fig. 1A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) indicated that the extracted GMSC-EXO had a mean particle size of approximately 44.81 nm (Fig. 1B), which fell within the typical range for exosome particle size. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the exosomes exhibited a characteristic cup-shaped morphology (Fig. 1C). Western blot analysis confirmed that the extracted exosomes expressed the exosomal markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 (Fig. 1D). Since miR-23a-3p was highly expressed in MSC-EXO, we transfected GMSC with the LV3-miR-23a-3p inhibitor. The results demonstrated that we successfully inhibited the expression of miR-23a-3p in GMSC-EXO (Fig. 1E).

Changes in blood glucose levels and survival curves of NOD/LtJ mice

We next aimed to determine whether GMSC-EXO could ameliorate T1D and to elucidate the role of miR-23a-3p in this process. We utilized NOD/LtJ mice as our in vivo model, as these mice spontaneously develop T1D. At 8 weeks of age, mice were injected with GMSC or exosomes. Blood glucose levels were monitored weekly, and mice with two consecutive readings above 13.88 mmol/L were deemed diabetic and sacrificed at 28 weeks of age (Fig. 2A).

As depicted in Fig. 2B, blood glucose levels increased over time in all mouse groups. Notably, the PBS group exhibited a rapid increase starting at week 13. In comparison to the PBS group, the blood glucose levels in the other treatment groups rose significantly more slowly. Specifically, the GMSC-EXO group demonstrated a slower rise in blood glucose levels and maintained them at a lower level compared to the GMSC group. In contrast, administration of GW4869 to GMSCs (GMSC-GW4869 group), which inhibits exosome secretion, abrogated this protective effect, resulting in a more rapid increase in blood glucose levels. When comparing the GMSCINC-EXO group to the GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO group, the latter exhibited a faster rise in blood glucose levels and higher overall levels.

Furthermore, the incidence of diabetes in the PBS group reached 100% by the 19th week of age, while in the GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO group, it reached 100% by the 27th week of age. Notably, in the GMSC-EXO or GMSCINC-EXO groups, the incidence rate was only 75% at 28 weeks of age (Fig. 2C). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GMSC-EXO has a beneficial effect on T1D, outperforming GMSC alone. Additionally, this therapeutic effect is attenuated when the expression of miR-23a-3p in GMSC-EXO is inhibited.

Inhibition of miR-23a-3p in GMSC-EXO aggravates pancreatic inflammation in NOD/LtJ mice

At 28 weeks of age, the pancreases of mice were harvested for H&E staining to assess the extent of islet inflammation in NOD/LtJ mice (Fig. 3A). The degree of islet inflammation was then scored based on the H&E staining results (Fig. 3B). H&E staining revealed extensive inflammatory cell infiltration in the islets of PBS-treated control mice, with a high proportion of islets receiving severe inflammation scores (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, the islets in the treatment group remained relatively intact, with fewer islets scoring high on inflammation. Notably, there was no significant inflammatory cell infiltration observed in the GMSC and GMSC-EXO groups, while the GMSC-GW4869 group showed minimal inflammatory cell infiltration. The abrogation of the protective effect in the GMSC-GW4869 group suggests that the anti-inflammatory properties of GMSCs are mediated primarily through the release of functional exosomes. Importantly, exosomes derived from GMSCs with inhibited miR-23a-3p (GMSCmiR-23aI-EXO) lost a significant portion of their protective capacity, as evidenced by a marked increase in inflammatory infiltration compared to the control exosome group (GMSCINC-EXO) (Fig. 3A, B). This demonstrates that the anti-inflammatory effect of GMSC-EXO is critically dependent on miR-23a-3p.

MiR-23a-3p mediates the immunosuppressive effect of GMSC-EXO in NOD/LtJ mice

CD4+ T cells play a crucial role in the progression of T1D. Based on the cytokines secreted by CD4+ T cells and their responses to self-antigens, these cells are classified into various subpopulations, including Th1, Th17, Tr1, and Treg cells, etc [34–37]. Tr1 cells primarily suppress the immune response by producing IL-10; they are characterized by secreting large amounts of IL-10 and varying levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, and TGF-β, depending on the microenvironment and disease context. However, none of these biomarkers are yet ideal for definitively identifying Tr1 cells [38]. Therefore, flow cytometry was employed in this study to assess the proportion of CD4+ T cell subsets, including Th1 (CD4+IFN-γ+), Th17 (CD4+IL-17+), CD4+IL-10+ and Treg cells (CD4+CD25+Foxp3+). The results are shown in Fig. 4 (A-H). Compared with the PBS group, the other treatment groups significantly reduced the proportion of pro-inflammatory cells (Th1, Th17) and increased the proportion of anti-inflammatory cells (CD4+IL-10+, Treg). Specifically, the GMSC-EXO group showed a more pronounced decrease in pro-inflammatory cells and a higher proportion of anti-inflammatory cells compared with the GMSC group. Furthermore, compared with the GMSC-GW4869 group, the proportion of pro-inflammatory cells (Th1, Th17) decreased and the proportion of anti-inflammatory cells (CD4+IL-10+, Treg) increased in the GMSC group. Lastly, compared with the GMSCINC-EXO group, the proportion of pro-inflammatory cells (Th1, Th17) in the GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO group increased, and the proportion of anti-inflammatory cells (CD4+IL-10+, Treg) decreased. Similarly, the GMSCmiR−23aI group showed the same trend compared with the GMSCINC group.

The results of ELISA of NOD/LtJ mouse serum are presented in Fig. 4 (I-K). The findings indicated that the levels of pro-inflammatory factors (IFN-γ, IL-17 A) were decreased, while the levels of anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10) were increased in all treatment group compared with the PBS group. Compared with the GMSC group, the GMSC-EXO group showed a more pronounced decrease in the levels of pro-inflammatory factors and higher levels of anti-inflammatory factors. Compared with the GMSC-GW4869 group, the levels of pro-inflammatory factors decreased and the levels of anti-inflammatory factors increased in GMSC group. Compared with the GMSCINC-EXO group, the GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO group had an increase in the level of pro-inflammatory factors and a decrease in the level of anti-inflammatory factors, and the GMSCmiR−23aI group also showed the same trend compared with the GMSCINC group. The above results are consistent with that of flow cytometry, which proved that (1) the anti-inflammatory effect of GMSC-EXO is better than that of GMSC; (2) the anti-inflammatory effect was decreased after inhibiting exosome production indicating that GMSC mainly exerted its anti-inflammatory effects through exosomes; and (3) miR-23a-3p was one of the key contents of the anti-inflammatory effect of GMSC-EXO.

MiR-23a-3p directly targets IL-6R to mediate the immunomodulatory effects of GMSC-EXO

In order to clarify the specific mechanisms through which GMSC-EXO improves blood glucose and suppresses islet inflammation in NOD/LtJ mice, we investigated the molecular targets of miR-23a-3p. Bioinformatic prediction using miRDB and TargetScan identified IL-6R as a top candidate target gene of miR-23a-3p. To substantiate this predicted relationship between miR-23a-3p and IL-6R, we conducted dual-luciferase reporter assays to determine whether IL-6R is directly targeted by miR-23a-3p. Figure 5A illustrates the sequence alignment of the IL-6R 3′-UTR with miR-23a-3p, along with the mutated version of the IL-6R 3′-UTR. The results indicated a significant inhibition of luciferase activity when the vector bound to the target sequences of miR-23a-3p. However, this inhibition was abolished when mutations were introduced at the binding site (Fig. 5B), confirming that IL-6R is indeed a target of miR-23a-3p.

MiR-23a-3p mediates the therapeutic effects of GMSC-EXO by targeting the IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 pathway

As shown in Fig. 5 (C-F), the expression levels of proteins involved in the IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 pathway were inhibited in all treatment groups when compared to the PBS control group. This finding indicates that both GMSC and GMSC-EXO can alleviate islet inflammation in NOD/LtJ mice by inhibiting the IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 pathway. Notably, the expression of IL-6R was significantly elevated in the GMSCmiR−23aI and GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO groups compared to the GMSCINC and GMSCINC-EXO groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D). Although JAK1 and STAT3 protein expression exhibited an upward trend, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5E, F). These results suggested that miR-23a-3p can ameliorate insulitis in NOD/LtJ mice by inhibiting IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 pathway, and multiple pathways may be involved in downstream of IL-6R.

GMSC-EXO exerts immunosuppressive effects via miR-23a-3p in vitro

In vitro experiments were conducted primarily on four CD4+ T cell subsets: Th1, Th17, CD4+IL-10+ and Treg. Figure 6 showed the outcomes of CD4+ T cells co-cultured with stem cells or exosomes for a duration of 3 days. Across all treatment groups, a significant suppression in the proportion of Th1 and Th17 cells, along with an increase in the proportion of CD4+IL-10+ and Treg cells, was observed when compared to untreated CD4+ T cells. Notably, the GMSC-EXO group exhibited a more pronounced decrease in the proportion of pro-inflammatory cells (Th1 and Th17) and a higher proportion of anti-inflammatory cells (CD4+IL-10+ and Treg) compared to the GMSC group. Furthermore, when treated with the miR-23a-3p inhibitor, the GMSCmiR-23aI-EXO group demonstrated an elevation in the proportion of pro-inflammatory cells (Th1 and Th17) and a reduction in the proportion of anti-inflammatory cells (CD4+IL-10+ and Treg) relative to the GMSCINC-EXO group.

We also measured the cytokines in the co-culture supernatant, and the observed trends aligned with the results of flow cytometry. In comparison to the control group (CD4+ T group), the levels of pro-inflammatory factors, IFN-γ and IL-17 A, were significantly reduced, while the level of anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 was elevated in treatment group. Furthermore, when comparing the GMSC-EXO group to the GMSC group, we noted a decrease in the levels of pro-inflammatory factors IFN-γ and IL-17 A, along with an increase in the level of anti-inflammatory factor IL-10. Additionally, the inhibition of miR-23a-3p expression in GMSC-EXO led to an upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors and a downregulation of anti-inflammatory factors. Collectively, the results obtained from both flow cytometry and ELISA indicate that GMSC-EXO exhibits a more potent immunosuppressive effect than GMSC in vitro, and that this effect is mediated, at least in part, through miR-23a-3p.

Discussion

While GMSC have demonstrated therapeutic potential in type 1 diabetes (T1D) [32], the precise mechanisms underlying their immunomodulatory effects remain incompletely understood. Our findings establish a novel role for GMSC-EXO enriched with miR-23a-3p in mitigating pancreatic islet inflammation and ameliorating T1D progression. Notably, NOD/LtJ mice treated with miR-23a-3p-depleted exosomes (GMSCmiR−23aI-EXO) exhibited exacerbated T1D symptoms compared to the GMSCINC-EXO group, characterized by heightened pro-inflammatory cell infiltration (Th1/Th17), elevated inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-17 A), and reduced anti-inflammatory mediators (IL-10, Treg populations). Crucially, pharmacological inhibition of exosome biogenesis via GW4869 significantly attenuated GMSC’s immunosuppressive capacity, underscoring exosomes as indispensable mediators of their therapeutic effects. Complementary in vitro experiments further corroborated that miR-23a-3p serves as a pivotal functional component of GMSC-EXO in suppressing inflammatory responses.

The pathogenesis of T1D involves a dynamic imbalance between autoreactive effector T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [39]. Earlier studies in NOD mice implicated Th1 dominance over Th2 responses in disease initiation [40], while human T1D patients exhibit β cell-specific CD4+ T cells secreting IFN-γ and/or IL-17 [41–44]. In this context, our data reveal that GMSC-EXO administration rectifies this immune disequilibrium by suppressing Th1/Th17 expansion while promoting Treg subsets—particularly CD4+CD25+Foxp3+ Tregs and IL-10-producing Tr1 cells. These observations align with the established role of Treg dysfunction in triggering autoimmune disorders [45, 46], suggesting that GMSC-EXO restores immune tolerance through multifaceted T cell modulation.

The immunomodulatory potential of GMSC-EXO likely involves important molecular mediators. The IL-6 signaling pathway serves as a significant driver in numerous autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [47]. Its dysregulation plays a critical role in synovial inflammation, bone erosion, and Th17/Treg imbalance in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [48], and IL-6R blockade therapy (e.g., tocilizumab) has been proven effective in targeting this pathology [49]. Notably, miR-23a-3p has demonstrated immunomodulatory functions across various disease models. For instance, upregulated miR-23a-3p expression correlates with methotrexate treatment response in RA patients [50]. Qin et al. demonstrated that miR-23a-3p within exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells promotes remyelination in central nervous system demyelinating diseases by targeting the Tbr1/Wnt pathway [51]. Furthermore, aberrant IL-6 activity contributes to the pathogenesis of IgA nephropathy by influencing the production of galactose-deficient IgA1 and mesangial cell proliferation [52]. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic relevance of both the IL-6 signaling pathway and miR-23a-3p as modulators in autoimmune pathogenesis.

Mechanistically, miR-23a-3p emerged as a central player in GMSC-EXO’s anti-inflammatory action. Bioinformatics and functional analyses identified IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) as a direct target of miR-23a-3p—a finding of particular relevance given the documented upregulation of IL-6R in T1D patients and its association with enhanced T cell autoreactivity [53, 54]. Our in vivo and in vitro experiments consistently demonstrated that GMSC-EXO suppresses IL-6R expression, an effect abolished upon miR-23a-3p inhibition. Given the critical role of IL-6 signaling in Th17 differentiation via the IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 axis [55–57], we further investigated this pathway. Western blot analyses revealed significant downregulation of IL-6R, JAK1, and STAT3 protein levels in GMSC/EXO-treated groups, with IL-6R suppression being miR-23a-3p-dependent (P < 0.05 vs. controls). While JAK1 and STAT3 exhibited similar downward trends, these changes lacked statistical significance. This discrepancy may stem from methodological limitations: our detection of total (rather than phosphorylated) JAK1/STAT3 proteins likely obscured activation status, as phosphorylation—not total protein levels—drives signaling cascade transmission [48]. Furthermore, compensatory signaling through alternative IL-6R downstream effectors (e.g., JAK2/TYK2, PI3K/AKT) [58, 59] or cross-talk with STAT3-independent pathways (e.g., MAPK) [33] could dilute observed effects. Future studies quantifying phospho-protein dynamics and parallel pathway engagement will be essential to fully delineate the mechanistic landscape.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, while miR-23a-3p is established as a key mediator, GMSC-EXO likely contains additional immunomodulatory miRNAs or bioactive molecules that may synergistically contribute to therapeutic outcomes. Second, our focus on the IL-6R/JAK1/STAT3 axis does not preclude involvement of other inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB, IRF1) previously linked to miR-23a-3p [35, 60]. Third, while our in vitro studies utilized C57BL/6J CD4⁺ T cells to establish the mechanistic role of miR-23a-3p/IL-6R axis under standardized conditions, we recognize that T cells from NOD/LtJ mice exhibit distinct autoreactive properties. Future work will isolate CD4⁺ T cells from prediabetic NOD/LtJ mice to validate these findings in a disease-specific microenvironment, particularly focusing on kinetics of T cell exhaustion (PD-1/CTLA-4 expression) and response to islet antigens. Fourth, translational challenges—including exosome production scalability and interspecies differences between murine models and human pathophysiology—must be addressed before clinical application. Notably, the absence of statistical significance in JAK1/STAT3 total protein levels, despite clear functional improvements, highlights the complexity of cytokine signaling networks and underscores the need for phosphoproteomic approaches in future investigations.

The GMSC-EXO-based therapeutic strategy demonstrated in this study represents one among many cell-free therapeutic approaches. Our previous work has shown that GMSC and their exosomes exhibit superior efficacy in suppressing arthritis [21], and this study further confirms their effectiveness in a T1D model. Compared to MSCs derived from bone marrow or adipose tissue, GMSC are more abundant and readily accessible [61]. Furthermore, the microenvironment in which GMSC reside is rich in food debris, microbial flora and saliva, providing a natural inflammatory microenvironment for GMSC, which is a unique advantage of GMSC [21]. However, systematic comparisons of exosomes from different sources regarding their miRNA profiles, proteomics, and functional potency still require further investigation. Compared to engineered exosomes [62], the “native” GMSC-EXO strategy employed in this study involves relatively straightforward procedures, avoiding the batch-to-batch variability and potential risks associated with complex engineering steps. Nevertheless, its efficiency in targeting specific sites or particular immune cell types may be inferior to that of engineered exosomes. Future research could explore simple surface modifications of GMSC-EXO to further enhance their targeted delivery efficiency. Finally, compared to traditional biologics such as monoclonal antibodies or fusion proteins, GMSC-EXO, as nano-scale vesicles, possess a more complex and multifaceted mechanism of action. They may offer broader immunomodulatory effects rather than single-pathway blockade. Additionally, exosomes theoretically carry a lower risk of inducing neutralizing antibodies. However, biologics currently hold significant advantages in terms of clinical maturity, standardized production, and established dosing regimens. In summary, GMSC-EXO, by virtue of their natural delivery capabilities, source accessibility, and pleiotropic immunomodulatory effects, hold very promising prospects within the field of cell-free therapy. They are particularly well-suited as therapeutic strategies for modulating complex immune networks.

Although this study confirms the potential of GMSC-EXO for T1D treatment via the miR-23a-3p/IL-6R axis, clinical translation of GMSC-EXO also faces challenges common to other exosomes. Studies have shown that 0.5 to 1.4 × 1011 exosomes are required per patient in clinical trials [63]. The kit-based extraction method currently employed in research, while suitable for laboratory scale, may have limitations in yield and safety that are difficult to meet for clinical-grade therapeutic needs [64]. Ultracentrifugation is considered the ‘gold standard’ for exosome isolation [65–67], but its low recovery rate, lengthy processing time, and high equipment costs render it unsuitable for scaled-up production [64]. Future translational research needs to adopt scalable, closed-system manufacturing processes compliant with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, such as size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) or tangential flow filtration (TFF) techniques. These methods are faster and easier to implement than traditional ones while yielding exosomes of comparable or higher concentration and purity. They are considered scalable and time-efficient, enabling a high degree of process standardization [62, 64]. Nonetheless there remain concerns such as exosome damage caused by shear stress, morphological changes when passing through the small membrane, and pore-clogging leading to exosome loss [64].

Simultaneously, it is imperative to establish a rigorous quality control system. This includes systematic characterization of particle size distribution (30–150 nm), expression levels of typical markers (e.g., CD9, CD63, CD81), purity, and biological function [65]. Secondly, optimizing storage stability and administration routes is central to clinical feasibility. Cryopreservation is currently the most common storage method for exosomes. However, the freezing process often causes vesicle rupture, cargo lost, aggregation, and precipitation [68]. Although the addition of cryoprotectants such as glycerol, trehalose and dimethyl sulfoxide stabilizes the protein to a certain extent, it also significantly improves the immunogenicity and biological risks in subsequent applications [68]. Lyophilization is another preservation method with recovery rates reaching approximately 85%. However, this process results in a broader size distribution compared to fresh vesicles [69]. More and more research is exploring new exosome storage methods. Jia et al. [70] use bionic mineralization strategy to package Zeolite Imidazolate Frameworks-8 as a protective shell on the exosome, protecting exosomes from interference from external environment and significantly extending their low-temperature storage time. The unshelled exosomes can maintain their complete structural morphology and cellular uptake, and can retain 76% of the original protein content even after storage for one month. Ji et al. [69] found that tetraspanin 4 (TSPAN4) play an essential role in protecting exosomes from cryodamage. They engineered TSPAN4-loaded exosomes, which demonstrated enhanced cryoprotection while maintaining a similar protein composition and uptake efficiency compared to wild-type exosomes. Considering the clinical transformation of exosomes, it is still necessary to further verify the long-term stability and clinical applicability of these strategies in the future, and explore optimized storage conditions to establish a low-damage and low-cost exosomes storage system [71]. Regarding administration, while this study utilized intravenous injection and observed therapeutic effects, future research should focus on developing efficient delivery systems to enhance bioavailability and lesion targeting. Examples include lipid-based or biomaterial-based encapsulation technologies, or exploring local/targeted delivery strategies. These could reduce dosing frequency, enhance efficacy, and minimize potential side effects [64].

Moreover, current research primarily focuses on short-term efficacy evaluation. Although exosomes are regarded as an ideal acellular therapeutic strategy due to their low immunogenicity, their long-term safety in chronic diseases still requires in-depth exploration. Existing literature indicates that most exosome therapy studies concentrate on acute pathological models, lacking systematic monitoring of long-term biodistribution, potential accumulation effects, and immunotoxicity under chronic pathological conditions [65]. Therefore, future studies should conduct multi-cycle administration trials and employ real-time imaging tracking technologies, such as MRI and fluorescence imaging, to monitor the distribution and metabolic pathways of exosomes in vivo, thereby enabling a comprehensive assessment of their long-term safety [65].

In conclusion, our study elucidates a miR-23a-3p/IL-6R axis through which GMSC-EXO alleviates T1D-associated insulitis. These findings not only advance our understanding of MSC-derived exosomes in autoimmune modulation but also identify miR-23a-3p as a potential therapeutic target. Future research should prioritize (1) phosphorylation-specific analyses of JAK/STAT signaling, (2) exploration of alternative IL-6R effector pathways, and (3) combinatorial strategies targeting multiple immune checkpoints to optimize T1D treatment efficacy.

Conclusions

Collectively, our findings have substantiated the following pivotal points: Firstly, exosomes play a crucial role in the functionality of GMSC; secondly, GMSC-EXO exhibit superior efficacy compared to GMSC in ameliorating Type 1 Diabetes (T1D); and thirdly, GMSC-EXO can inhibit the IL-6R related signaling pathway via miR-23a-3p, thereby achieving an improvement in T1D-associated insulitis. Our study furnishes both theoretical and experimental evidence to support the potential of cell-free therapy in the treatment of T1D.
