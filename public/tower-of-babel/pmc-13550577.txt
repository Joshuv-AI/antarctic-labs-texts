Latent profiles of social networks and their association with digital health readiness among rural older adults with hypertension
Authors: Ershan Xu, Cuijuan Lin, Jiangnan He, Yingzi Tang, Ying Xiong, Yan Pu, Tingting Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Person-centered approaches to social networks profiles and their association with digital health readiness (DHR) remain underexplored among rural older adults with hypertension. This study aimed to identify latent social networks profiles and examine their independent association with DHR in this population. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 576 rural older adults with hypertension by using the Lubben Social Network Scale-6 and the Digital Health Readiness Questionnaire (DHRQ) in Hunan Province, China. Latent profile analysis identified social networks profiles. Multivariable linear regression with forced entry examined the independent association between profiles and DHRQ after controlling for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related confounders. Three profiles emerged: socially restricted (16.5%), moderately connected (65.3%), and well-integrated (18.2%). Social networks profiles remained independently associated with DHRQ (F11, 564 = 20.362, p < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.270). The moderately connected profile was associated with a 4.536-point higher DHRQ score (95% CI: 1.346–7.726, p = 0.005) and the well-integrated profile with an 8.386-point higher score (95% CI: 4.447–12.326, p < 0.001), compared with the socially restricted profile. These findings highlight the potential of person-centered social networks to inform targeted digital health interventions for rural older adults with hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-67008-9.

Introduction

Ageing of the population poses substantial challenges to healthcare systems worldwide1. The Global Burden of Disease Study estimates that chronic noncommunicable diseases account for 74% of global mortality2, with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remaining the leading cause of death and disability3. Hypertension represents the single most important modifiable risk factor for CVD mortality4. Currently, China has approximately 245 million adults with hypertension, with prevalence rates in rural areas now exceeding those in urban settings; however, rates of awareness (51.6%), treatment (16.8%), and control (45.8%) remain suboptimal. Rapid urbanization has precipitated large-scale rural-to-urban migration of younger adults, resulting in a growing population of “left-behind” older adults. The Fifth National Sample Survey revealed that 61.9% of rural older adults were living alone or in empty-nest households7, dismantling traditional family-based hypertension management and eroding social networks8.

Digital health technologies (DHTs), including mobile health (mHealth) applications, remote monitoring systems, and wearable devices, offer transformative potential for hypertension self-management among geographically dispersed rural older adults9,10. However, successful implementation requires adequate digital health readiness (DHR). DHR is a multidimensional construct encompassing digital access, technical skills, digital literacy, eHealth literacy, and digital learnability11. Evidence demonstrates that rural older adults exhibit significantly lower DHR compared with their urban counterparts12,13.

Social networks refer to interpersonal structures that provide emotional, instrumental, and informational support and are strongly associated with health outcomes14. Individuals with limited social networks experience higher mortality, morbidity, and suicide rates15–18. Meanwhile, social isolation exacerbates individuals’ disease symptoms and precipitates adverse health outcomes19. Moreover, social networks are a key factor of healthy lifestyles20, subjective well-being21, life satisfaction22, and quality of life23. Berkman-Glass social network theory posits that social networks influence health behaviors through four mechanisms: social support, social influence, social engagement, and access to resources24. Therefore, we contend that social networks may facilitate digital health engagement by disseminating information and providing technology skills training.

Several critical gaps in the current literature warrant attention. First, while social networks have been linked to general health outcomes25, their specific association with multidimensional DHR remains largely unexplored in rural older adults with hypertension. Second, person-centered methods such as latent profile analysis (LPA), which identify social networks profiles within heterogeneous populations26, have rarely been applied to examine rural older adults with hypertension, although prior studies have used LPA in general older adult populations27,28. Third, the potential graded associations between social networks and DHR have not been examined using data-driven classification approaches.

Therefore, this study aimed to: (1) identify latent profiles of social networks among rural older adults with hypertension using LPA; and (2) examine the associations between these latent profiles and DHR. We hypothesized that distinct social network profiles would exhibit a graded association with DHR, where a higher - level social network corresponds to greater readiness. The findings will offer nuanced evidence for personalized interventions that can simultaneously alleviate social isolation and improve DHR, thus promoting equitable hypertension management in rural China.

Methods

Design and participants

A multicenter and cross-sectional study was conducted from September to October 2025. To ensure comprehensive and accurate reporting of the research, the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines29 were followed (Appendix 1). The research methodology framework, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1.We employed a convenience sampling to recruit rural older adults with hypertension registered in the chronic disease management systems of three rural township health centers across Hunan Province, China. These centers were purposively selected to represent diverse socioeconomic gradients: Liuyang (economically developed county, 3 villages), Zhuzhou (moderately developed region, 4 villages), and Loudi (less developed region, 3 villages), encompassing 10 villages in total. Eligible participants were identified through electronic medical records and community health records, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the target population within selected villages.

Inclusion criteria: age ≥ 60 years; confirmed diagnosis of essential hypertension; adequate cognitive and communication abilities; and voluntary informed consent.

Exclusion criteria: secondary hypertension; severe psychiatric or life-limiting physical illness, or significant hearing, language, or other communication barriers precluding participation.

Data collection procedures and instruments

Following administrative approval from the three township health centers, data collection began. At each site, two nurses identified potential participants from health management registries according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, 50 trained nursing student interviewers contacted eligible individuals through home visits or by telephone, explained the study purpose, and obtained informed consent. Using the mobile survey app “Wenjuanxing,” participants completed questionnaires either independently or with interviewer assistance. Mandatory completion of all scale items was enforced by the electronic system prior to final submission. Participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time. No personally identifiable information was collected. Data collection instruments included a general information questionnaire, the Lubben Social Network Scale, and the Digital Health Readiness Questionnaire.

Samples

Sample size was estimated using Kendall’s guideline of 5–10 participants per scale item33. With 34 items, which included the General Information Questionnaire (10 items), the LSNS-6 (6 items), and the DHRQ (18 items). The minimum required sample was 340 (10 × 34). Accounting for an anticipated 10–20% invalid response rate, the final target sample size was 374–408 participants. Given the availability of human and material resources, ultimately, we collected 701 questionnaires. The systematic exclusion of invalid questionnaires based on the following criteria: (a) logical inconsistencies between related items; (b) completion times below 3 min or exceeding 30 min without documented justification; and (c) response patterns indicative of careless responding, including straight-lining or systematic zigzag patterns. From these, 576 responses were deemed valid, resulting in an effective response rate of 82.2%.

Data analysis

Common method bias test

Initially, common method variance was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Exploratory factor analysis without rotation extracted eight factors with eigenvalues > 1; the first factor explained 33.3% of variance, well below the 40% threshold. CFA of a single-factor model demonstrated poor fit (CMIN/DF = 8.540, RMSEA = 0.114, CFI = 0.651, TLI = 0.628, SRMR = 0.090). These results indicate that common method bias was unlikely to confound our findings.

Descriptive statistics and univariable analysis

The SPSS 26.0 software was utilized to conduct statistical analysis. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics collected via the general information questionnaire were summarized using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Univariable analyses were conducted to examine the associations between sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and DHRQ, to characterize the identified social networks profiles, and to compare DHRQ total and subscale scores across social networks profiles. Since the dependent variables (DHRQ total and subscale scores) are continuous variables, one-way ANOVA was used for univariate analysis, with LSD post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons when variances were equal; when Levene’s test indicated unequal variances (p < 0.05), the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used instead. Spearman correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between DHRQ scores and LSNS-6 scores. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analyses were identified as candidate covariates for multivariable modeling.

Latent profile analysis (LPA)

LPA was conducted using Mplus 8.3 to identify distinct subgroups of rural older adults with hypertension based on their social network patterns34,35. The six LSNS-6 items, each scored on a six-point scale (0–5), were treated as continuous indicator variables for the LPA. This treatment is considered acceptable for ordinal variables with five or more response categories when distributions are approximately symmetric36; in the present sample, skewness(-0.500 to 0.299) and kurtosis(-0.890 to 0.523) for all six items were within ± 2. Model estimation employed the robust maximum likelihood estimator (ML), which yields standard errors and fit statistics robust to deviations from multivariate normality. Latent profile models with 1 to 5 classes were estimated sequentially. To ensure robust estimation and avoid local maxima, each model was specified with 500 random sets of starting values for the initial stage, retaining the 100 best solutions for final optimization (STARTS = 500 100). The initial stage used 20 iterations (STITERATIONS = 20), and a fixed random seed (123456) was set to ensure reproducibility. Model convergence was assessed via the default Mplus convergence criterion (0.0001), and the iteration history was monitored (TECH8). To verify that the final solution represented a global maximum rather than a local maximum, TECH11 and TECH14 outputs were examined. Model fit was evaluated using the following criteria: log-likelihood (LL), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy coefficient, Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT), and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT). Model selection criteria were defined as follows: superior fit is indicated by lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values coupled with higher entropy; LMRT and BLRT with significant p-values (p < 0.05) suggest the k-class model is statistically preferable to the (k − 1)-class alternative37. Additionally, to ensure the stability and generalizability of each identified profile, no latent profile with fewer than 5% of the total sample was retained. Following profile enumeration, participants were assigned to their most likely latent profile based on posterior probabilities, and the resulting categorical profile variable was used in subsequent analyses.

Regression analysis

Two sets of regression analyses were performed to address distinct research questions. First, since the latent profiles of social networks are categorical variables, univariate and multivariate logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with the latent profiles of social networks. The profile with the most restricted social network was designated as the reference group. Variables significant at p < 0.05 in univariable logistic regression analyses were entered into the multivariate logistic regression model using the forced entry method.

Second, multivariable linear regression was employed to examine the independent association between latent profiles of social networks and the DHRQ total score. This approach was selected because the dependent variable (DHRQ total score) is a continuous measure, and inspection of residuals supported the assumptions of ordinary least squares (OLS) estimation. The primary independent variable(social network profile) was a nominal category variable derived from LPA. Consequently, dummy-variable coding was applied, with most restricted social network profile designated as the reference group because it represents the theoretically most vulnerable subgroup, making the resulting coefficients most clinically interpretable as incremental advantages. Candidate covariates were first screened using one-way ANOVA, with entry into the multivariable model set at p < 0.05. All retained covariates and the primary factors were simultaneously entered into the multivariable model using the forced-entry (Enter) method. This approach was chosen over stepwise, forward, or backward selection procedures because stepwise algorithms rely on arbitrary statistical thresholds to eliminate variables, which can inappropriately exclude confounders that are non-significant but theoretically essential, thereby introducing residual confounding and inflating Type I error rates. The model was estimated using ordinary least squares with default convergence criteria (tolerance = 0.0001). Model diagnostics used multicollinearity (VIF < 10). p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

All procedures involving human participants in this study were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan University of Medicine (approval number: 2025(H11007)) (Appendix 2). Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, and the use of verbal consent was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan University of Medicine.

LPA of social networks

Based on model fit indices and interpretability, the 3-class solution was selected as optimal (Table 2). The 3-class model was selected based on: (a) significant LMRT for 3-versus 2-class (p = 0.0037) but non-significant LMRT for 4- versus 3-class (p = 0.3568) and 5- versus 4-class (p = 0.0549), indicating that the more conservative likelihood ratio test did not favor extraction beyond three classes; (b) acceptable entropy (0.829 > 0.800); and (c) adequate class sizes (minimum n = 95, 16.5%) without spurious small classes, whereas both the 4-class model and the 5-class model yielded an unstable class accounting for less than 5%. The parametric BLRT further supported the 3-class solution (p < 0.001). The 3-class model yielded the highest log-likelihood value (LL = -5166.718). Due to limited successful bootstrap draws (n = 5) in Mplus Version 8.3, the standard TECH14 replication rate was not available in the conventional format. However, model stability was ensured through 500 random starts and consistent convergence across optimization stages. The average probability of attribution for the three groups ranged from 90.0% to 93.3% (Supplementary Table S1), indicating that the LPA classification results in this study are credible.

Figure 1 presents the item-response profiles for the three social network classes across the six LSNS-6 items. Class 1 (16.5%, n = 95) was characterized by low scores on both family and friend network subscales and was labeled “socially restricted.” Class 2 (65.3%, n = 376) exhibited moderate scores across both dimensions and was labeled “moderately connected.” Class 3 (18.2%, n = 105) displayed high scores on all items and was labeled “well-integrated.”

The differences in total scores and scores on each dimension of the LSNS-6 scale among the three groups were statistically significant (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). The “well-integrated” profile consistently showed the highest item means, followed by “moderately connected” and “socially restricted,” with no overlap in 95% confidence intervals(CIs) between adjacent profiles, supporting the distinctiveness of the three profiles, as shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression results for the correlation with external features in the profiles of social networks

Variables significant in univariate analysis (p < 0.05) (Table 3) were retained in the multivariate model. Significant associated factors of latent class membership included number of children, per capita monthly household income, activities of daily living, and regular exercise (Table 4). Small family size (0–1 children) and low income (≤ 2000 RMB/month) were associated with socially restricted (OR = 0.241–0.391, p < 0.05). Regular exercise was associated with well-integrated (OR = 2.314, p < 0.05). Significant barriers in activities of daily living was associated with socially restricted (OR = 2.618–3.680, p < 0.05).

Association between latent profiles of social networks and DHR

Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive correlations between total social network scores, all subscale scores, and DHRQ dimensions (all p < 0.01) (Supplementary Table S3). Significant differences emerged in overall DHRQ and all subscales across social networks profiles (p < 0.001) (Table 5). Post-hoc LSD tests showed that the overall DHRQ and all subscales of socially restricted group scored significantly lower than those of both the moderately connected group and well-integrated group. Meanwhile, the overall DHRQ and all subscales of the moderately connected group scored significantly lower than those of the well-integrated group.

Table 6 presents the results of the multivariable linear regression examining the independent association between social networks profiles and DHR. DHRQ total score as the dependent variable, eleven predictors were entered simultaneously: the LPA-derived 3-class social networks profiles (two dummy codes with the restricted class as reference) and nine covariates. Discussion

LPA of social networks

This study identified three distinct social networks profiles among rural older adults with hypertension using LPA: socially restricted (16.5%), moderately connected (65.3%), and well-integrated (18.2%). Our LPA results are largely consistent with other person-centered studies on elderly social networks38,39, identifying three distinct categories ranging from limited to well-integrated. However, the social network well-being groups in these studies accounted for approximately 30% or more of participants, compared to 18.2% in our study. This difference may be interpreted within China’s rapid sociodemographic transformation. Large-scale rural-to-urban migration has dismantled traditional three-generation co-residence patterns6,7, leaving many older adults in “empty-nest” households. Unlike Western aging populations, where social isolation often reflects lifelong individualism, rural Chinese older adults frequently experience structural isolation precipitated by macroeconomic forces beyond their control.

Heterogeneity of social networks profiles

Multivariate logistic regression revealed that small family size or low household income was associated with an increased likelihood of restricted profile membership. This finding illuminates that small families, particularly those with 0–1 children, may lack sufficient caregiving capacity to maintain robust support networks. Concurrently, financial constraints may curtail social participation by limiting transportation access, communication costs, and community activity involvement. These results highlight that social network deficits in this population are not merely individual failures but vulnerabilities linked to structural conditions rooted in China’s rapid demographic and economic transformation. Interventions should extend beyond individual-level counseling to encompass community-based peer support groups40 and family member education.

Multivariate logistic regression revealed that significant barriers in activities of daily living were associated with an increased likelihood of restricted profile membership. For rural older adults with hypertension with limitations in activities of daily living, interventions should be carried out through technical support (e.g., digital tools facilitating remote social connection), environmental modifications (e.g., accessible transportation), and community-based in-home social activities that compensate for the lost participation capacity. Regular physical exercise emerged as a behavioral correlate of well-integrated profile membership, reflecting a synergistic relationship between individual health behaviors and social connectedness41. Exercise creates opportunities for social engagement, enabling patients to connect with like-minded peers and expand their social circles. Moreover, mutual encouragement during group exercise fosters sustained health behaviors, generating a virtuous cycle wherein social integration and physical activity reciprocally reinforce one another. These findings underscore the necessity of health education emphasizing regular exercise.

Latent profiles of social networks associated with DHR among rural older adults with hypertension

Main findings

The principal contribution of this study lies in demonstrating that social networks profiles are independently associated with DHR, even after comprehensive adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related confounders. In our sample, the mean DHRQ score was 46.87 ± 6.13 (range 18–90). The moderately connected group scored 4.536 points higher than the restricted group (β = 0.134, p = 0.005). This corresponds to 0.74 standard deviations of the sample distribution, approaching Cohen’s large-effect threshold (d = 0.80). Relative to the sample mean, this moves an average patient from the 50th to approximately the 77th percentile of digital readiness. The well-integrated group scored 8.386 points higher (β = 0.201, p < 0.001), equivalent to 1.37 standard deviations—a very large effect size. This shifts an average patient from the 50th to approximately the 91st percentile of digital readiness within this population.

In the multivariable linear regression model, an adjusted R² of 0.270 indicates that the full set of variables explains 27.0% of DHRQ variance after penalizing for model complexity. This is substantively meaningful for three reasons. First, in behavioral health research involving older adults, this exceeds the conventional medium-effect threshold (R² ≈ 0.13) and approaches Cohen’s large-effect benchmark (R² ≈ 0.26) for observational studies. Second, this explanatory power was achieved after adjustment for nine competing demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables, suggesting that the model captures substantial unique variance. Third, given the sample’s low baseline digital readiness (mean 46.87 on an 18–90 scale, indicating that participants, on average, operate below the midpoint), explaining 27.0% of the variation represents good predictive utility and identifies a modifiable psychosocial factor with intervention relevance. The remaining 73.0% of the unexplained variance likely reflects unmeasured structural factors (smartphone ownership, internet connectivity, eHealth literacy) and healthcare-system factors (physician digital encouragement). This indicates that while the social network is an independent associated factor, interventions that combine family network strengthening with device provision, connectivity subsidies, and skills training are needed to comprehensively improve the DHR of rural older adults with hypertension.

Sensitivity analyses was conducted using each of the five DHRQ subscales as separate dependent variables in the multivariable linear regression model, controlling for the same nine covariates as in Table 6. The results are presented in Supplementary Table S4. Compared with the restricted group, the well-integrated group scored significantly higher across all five subdimensions: digital usability (β = 0.238, 95% CI 0.976–2.369), digital skills (β = 0.221, 95% CI 1.762–4.461), digital literacy (β = 0.134, 95% CI 0.266–1.691), digital health literacy (β = 0.146, 95% CI 0.361–1.913), and digital learnability (β = 0.122, 95% CI 0.259–2.714). This confirms that well-integrated social networks confer a broad-spectrum advantage in digital readiness. However, the moderately connected group showed a selective pattern: significant associations were observed for digital skills (β = 0.159, 95% CI 0.720–2.905), digital health literacy (β = 0.123, 95% CI 0.149–1.406), and digital learnability (β = 0.102, 95% CI 0.017–2.004), but not for digital usability (β = 0.093, 95% CI -0.035–1.093) and digital literacy (β = 0.069, 95% CI -0.170-0.984). These findings further confirm that the social network is a significant associated factor of DHR of rural older adults with hypertension. Meanwhile, This dimension-specificity underscores the importance of multi-component interventions that pair social support with structural enablers for foundational competencies.

Analysis of the reasons for the main findings

Theoretically, these results align with Berkman-Glass social network theory, which posits that social networks influence health behaviors through four distinct yet interrelated mechanisms: social support, social influence, social engagement, and access to resources24.

First, Social support encompasses both instrumental support (tangible assistance, such as help with device acquisition and smartphone operation) and emotional support (encouragement and reassurance), which have been shown to facilitate technology adoption among older adults42,43.Individuals with well-integrated social networks are more likely to receive greater instrumental and emotional support from family members and friends, which in turn enhances their DHR.

Second, Individuals with well-integrated social networks have greater exposure to peers and family members who successfully engage with digital health technologies and are therefore more likely to be influenced by their example. Such social modeling shapes their attitudes toward technology, reduces perceived technological complexity, and alleviates anxiety during technology use, thereby improving health literacy and enhancing DHR among rural older adults with hypertension.

Third, social engagement refers to active participation in social activities and interpersonal interactions. Individuals with well-integrated social networks are more likely to participate in community health education activities and village health lectures, where digital skills and health information are disseminated. These health training and education programs can improve older adults’ digital literacy44,45.

Fourth, access to resources, extends beyond material assets to include informational and human capital resources diffused through social network ties. Social networks serve as channels through which health literacy and digital literacy resources are transmitted. Individuals with well-integrated social networks may gain access to shared devices, pooled financial resources for technology procurement, and collective problem-solving when technical difficulties arise. These resource-access pathways are particularly critical in rural settings where resources are limited.

Collectively, these four mechanisms help explain the association between social network profiles and DHR among rural older adults with hypertension. They also indicate that social support, digital literacy, and health literacy may function as interrelated mediating factors linking social network profiles to DHR in this population. It is important to note, however, that the cross-sectional design of the present study precludes formal empirical testing of these mediating pathways. Future longitudinal and intervention studies should examine whether enhancing social support, digital literacy, and health literacy can improve DHR among rural older adults with hypertension.

Practical implications

These findings suggest that DHR is not merely an individual deficit but a vulnerability linked to social context. China’s urban-rural digital divide extends beyond individual literacy to encompass infrastructural inequities12,13. Rural areas often lack stable broadband, accessible technical support, and age-appropriate device design. Social networks may function not merely as a supplementary influence but as a compensatory mechanism that addresses gaps left by deficient formal digital support systems, which have direct policy relevance for resource-constrained settings. Therefore, Healthcare providers should systematically assess social support needs, facilitate community engagement to strengthen networks, and deliver targeted DHR interventions based on social networks profiles. For the restricted subgroup, dyadic interventions pairing isolated individuals with volunteer “digital navigators” from their extended family or community may be necessary. For the moderately connected majority, leveraging existing family networks to create “digital health buddy systems,” where one knowledgeable family member mentors others, may efficiently improve DHR46. For the well-integrated minority, peer-to-peer training models within established social groups could transform high-readiness individuals into community digital health champions47.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; while we identified distinct latent profiles of social networks and their statistical associations with DHR, we cannot ascertain whether limited social networks precede low DHR or whether low DHR constrains social participation. Reverse causality and unmeasured confounding may partly explain the observed associations. Future research should employ prospective cohort designs or randomized controlled trials to examine whether social network interventions causally influence DHR over time, and to explore potential mediating pathways (digital literacy, social support, and health literacy) in this relationship.

Second, this study relied solely on self-reported questionnaire data, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Future studies should integrate objective behavioral data with self-reported measures to provide a more comprehensive and valid assessment of the relationship between social networks and DHR among rural older adults.

Third, the CFA of the DHRQ indicated suboptimal model fit (RMSEA = 0.099), which exceeded the conventional threshold of 0.08. This finding is consistent with the original scale development study (Xu et al.33: CMIN/DF = 4.897, RMSEA = 0.109, CFI = 0.914, TLI = 0.895, SRMR = 0.0765). This may be due to the fact that the cultural interpretation of “digital readiness” may blur inter-factor boundaries among older adults with limited technology exposure. Additionally, the strict independence assumptions of CFA may be overly stringent for applied health behavior instruments where dimensions are expected to correlate. Future studies should consider the contextual adaptation of the DHRQ for rural older adults with hypertension in China.

Fourth, model stability in the LPA was supported by 500 random starts and consistent convergence; however, due to limited successful bootstrap draws (n = 5) in Mplus Version 8.3, the standard TECH14 replication rate was not available in the conventional format.

Fifth, the multivariable linear regression model explained 27.0% of the variance in DHR (adjusted R² = 0.270), leaving a substantial proportion of variance unexplained. DHR is a complex, multidimensional construct influenced by numerous factors beyond the scope of this study, including digital infrastructure accessibility, personality traits, and cultural attitudes toward technology.

Finally, this study employed convenience sampling in Hunan Province, China. The sample may not be representative of rural older adults with hypertension in other Chinese provinces. Hunan province is a major labor-exporting province in central China. It has a high proportion of left-behind elderly, which may differ from other regions. Consequently, the social network profiles and their associations with DHR identified in this study should undergo further validation in geographically diverse samples.

Conclusions

This multicenter cross-sectional study examined the association between latent social networks profiles and DHR among rural older adults with hypertension in China. This study identified three distinct social networks profiles among rural older adults with hypertension in China: socially restricted(16.5%), moderately connected(65.3%), and well-integrated(18.2%). After adjusting for nine covariates, social networks profiles remained independently associated with DHR(F11, 564 = 20.362, p < 0.001, adjusted R² = 0.270). Specifically, rural older adults with hypertension in the well-integrated group scored 8.386 points higher on the DHRQ (β = 0.201, 95% CI 4.447–12.326, p < 0.001) and those in the moderately connected group scored 4.546 points higher (β = 0.134, 95% CI 1.346–7.726, p = 0.005) than those in the restricted group. These findings suggest that social network quality is a meaningful, modifiable psychosocial factor of digital readiness among rural older adults with hypertension. From a practical standpoint, integrating brief social network screening into routine hypertension management may help identify rural older adults with hypertension at elevated risk of digital exclusion. However, because social networks profiles explained 27.0% of the variance, the remaining 73% unexplained variance likely reflects unmeasured factors. For rural older adults with hypertension and restricted networks, dyadic interventions that enlist family members or community volunteers as digital navigators, combined with device provision and targeted skills training, may offer a feasible pathway to narrowing the rural digital health divide. Meanwhile, these findings require validation in broader geographic areas before being generalized to rural populations in other provinces in China. Moreover, validation through longitudinal or experimental studies is warranted to establish the causal pathways between social networks and DHR among rural older adults with hypertension.
