Index estimates for constant mean curvature surfaces in three-manifolds by energy comparison
Luca Seemungal, Ben Sharp

Abstract
We prove a linear upper bound on the Morse index of closed constant mean curvature (CMC) surfaces in orientable three-manifolds in terms of genus, number of branch points and a Willmore-type energy.



1
Introduction

Given
m\geq 3
and a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
the celebrated Cwikel-Lieb-Rozenbljum inequality gives a bound on the number
n({\alpha})
of eigenvalues of a Schrödinger operator
L=-\Delta-q
(with Dirichlet boundary conditions) which are less than or equal to
{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}

n({\alpha})\leq C\int_{\Omega}(q+{\alpha})_{+}^{\frac{m}{2}}\quad\text{where $(q+{\alpha})_{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}q+{\alpha}&\mbox{if }q+{\alpha}\geq 0\\
0&\mbox{if }\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.$}

(1.1)

P. Li and S.-T. Yau’s elegant proof of this fact
[
16
]
elucidates the role of the
W^{1,2}\hookrightarrow L^{\frac{2m}{m-2}}
Sobolev embedding, so that the constant in the above estimate (
1.1
) depends explicitly and only upon the associated Sobolev constant.

S.-Y. Cheng and J. Tysk
[
7
]
proved that if
\Sigma^{m}\subset N^{n}
is a minimal immersion (which we know admits a Michael-Simon Sobolev inequality) in a closed Riemannian manifold, and
L=-\nabla^{2}-Q
is a shifted rough Laplacian on a Riemannian vector bundle
\mathcal{V}
over
\Sigma
(here,
Q\in\textrm{End}(\mathcal{V})
is some symmetric endomorphism), then for
\Omega\subset\Sigma
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions

n(0)=\textrm{index}(L)+\textrm{nullity}(L)\leq C(m,N)(\textrm{rank}(\mathcal{V}))\int_{\Omega}(\max\{1,q\})^{\frac{m}{2}}\,\,{\rm d}V_{\Sigma},

where
q(p)
is the largest eigenvalue of
Q
at
p\in\Sigma
.
In particular, and importantly, if
\mathcal{V}
is the normal bundle of
\Sigma
, and

QX=\sum_{i,j}\left\langle A(E_{i},E_{j}),X\right\rangle A(E_{i},E_{j})+\sum_{i}\Rm^{N}(X,E_{i})E_{i}

where
E_{i}
is an orthonormal frame of
\Sigma
and
A
is its second fundamental form, then
L
is the Jacobi operator of the area functional
\mathcal{A}^{m}

{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{m}(\Sigma)[X,X]=\int_{\Sigma}\langle LX,X\rangle\,\,{\rm d}V_{\Sigma}

so that the above provides interesting spectral estimates on minimal hypersurfaces in terms of
q(p)^{m/2}\leq K_{m}(|A|^{m}+|\Rm^{N}|^{m/2})
.
Assuming a uniform curvature bound on
N
(for example, if
N
is closed), one has that the index plus nullity (=
n(0)
) of a minimal surface is bounded by its area and total curvature
\int_{\Sigma}|A|^{m}
.

A fortiori
, this
L
is also the Jacobi operator of area when restricted to volume preserving deformations along constant mean curvature hypersurfaces
\Sigma^{m}\subset N^{m+1}
, and so similar estimates hold for CMC hypersurfaces, at least when
m\geq 3
. See below for precise definitions and clarifications of the notion of CMC index that we use in this paper.

1.1
The case
m=2

If the inequality (
1.1
) were true for
m=2
then the Gauss equations
|A|^{2}=2\kappa^{N}({\rm T}\Sigma)-2K^{\Sigma}-\tfrac{1}{2}|H|^{2}
and the Gauss-Bonnet formula would allow one to prove that the index of a minimal or CMC surface is bounded affine-linearly from above by the genus and area of the domain, up to a uniform constant depending only on the ambient manifold
N
and the size of the mean curvature
|H|
.

An estimate like (
1.1
) is false when
m=2
for general Schrödinger operators as standard counter-examples show. One cannot expect such an inequality to hold for general
q
when the right-hand side is the
L^{1}
norm of
q
. The correct replacement space is that
q
be in the Zygmund class
L\log L
see e.g.
[
23
]
. When
q\sim|A|^{2}+Rm^{N}
one cannot use Gauss-Bonnet to relate the
L\log L
-norm of
q
to the genus of
\Sigma
.
Nevertheless, for branched minimal immersions, Norio Ejiri and Mario Micallef
[
11
]
used the relationship between Dirichlet energy and the area functional along immersions in order to prove that the analytically-expected result does indeed hold inside arbitrary closed ambient manifolds.

Given a Riemann surface
\Sigma
, let
E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|u_{x}|^{2}+|u_{y}|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y
and
\mathcal{A}(u)=\int_{\Sigma}|u_{x}\wedge u_{y}|\,\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y
be the Dirichlet energy and area functionals respectively, defined here using isothermal coordinates. It is well known and easy to see that
\mathcal{A}(u)\leq E(u)
with equality if and only if
u
is conformal. Equally well known is that they agree up to first order too when
u
is conformal i.e.
\mathcal{A}(u)=E(u)
and
{\rm d}\mathcal{A}(u)[v]={\rm d}E(u)[v]
for any
v\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}{\rm T}N)
. In particular conformal harmonic maps are the same as a branched minimal immersions. One of the key results of
[
11
]
is the comparison of second variations
at a branched minimal immersion
u
,

\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathcal{A}}(u)[v,v]=\mathrm{d}^{2}{E}(u)[v,v]-4\int_{\Sigma}|\mu|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma,

(1.2)

which holds for any variation
v\in\Gamma(u^{*}{\rm T}N)
, and where
\mu
is a quantity depending on
v
that measures the “infinitesimal conformal deficit” of the variation
v
. This
\mu
is zero for instance along a variation which does not infinitesimally move the induced conformal structure of the domain, see (
2.5
) and the surrounding discussion.

As a corollary of the above comparison of the second variations of area and Dirichlet energy, Ejiri-Micallef prove the following comparison between the index
i_{\mathcal{A}}
of the area functional and the index
i_{E}
of the energy functional at a conformal harmonic map
u
.

Theorem 1.1

(Ejiri-Micallef: Theorem 1.1 in
[
11
]
)
.

Let
u:\Sigma_{g}\to N
be a (possibly branched) minimal immersion of a closed Riemann surface of genus
g
into a Riemannian manifold
N^{n}
. Then

i_{E}\leq i_{\mathcal{A}}\leq i_{E}+r,\,\,\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,\,\,r=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}6g-6-2b&\mbox{if }b\leq 2g-3\\
4g-2+2\left[\frac{-b}{2}\right]&\mbox{if }2g-2\leq b\leq 4g-4\\
0&\mbox{if }b\geq 4g-3,\end{array}\right.

and
b
is the total number of branch points of
u
counted with multiplicity, and
[x]=\max_{\mathbb{Z}}\{k:k\leq x\}
.

In particular, even though there are no suitable analytical estimates on the index of
{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}
from above, the second variation of
E
along a conformal harmonic map
u
is of the form
-{\nabla}^{2}-Q
where
Q
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}
by the ambient curvatures of
N
and one may proceed to bound the
E
-index of
u
by the area of its image, at least when one restricts to a conformal harmonic map. The above theorem thus provides an affine-linear upper bound on the area-index of a minimal immersion by the genus and area of the image.

The above is underpinned by the following fundamental heuristic from Ejiri-Micallef: Given a branched minimal immersion, we have
\mathcal{A}=E
and in general
\mathcal{A}\leq E
. Any variation which decreases
E
must therefore also decrease
\mathcal{A}
. On the other hand if a variation of
u
continues to be a conformal map relative to the fixed Riemann surface
\Sigma
, the two functionals
\mathcal{A}
and
E
coincide along the variation, so the only possibility for a variation to decrease
\mathcal{A}
but not necessarily
E
is if the induced metrics along the variation are changing the conformal class. These variations infinitesimally form a vector space which Ejiri-Micallef show has dimension
r
, which is as expected the dimension of the moduli space of the underlying surface.

Terminology

For a constant
h
, we call
u:\Sigma\to N^{3}
a
branched CMC
h
-immersion
(or CMC
h
-immersion for short) if
u:\Sigma\to N^{3}
is some branched conformal immersion from a closed Riemann surface into some oriented Riemannian three-manifold
N
parametrising a CMC surface whose mean curvature vector is
H=h\nu
where, in isothermal coordinates
(u_{x},u_{y},\nu)
is positively oriented in
N
. We let
g
be the genus of
\Sigma
and
b
the total number of branch points counted with multiplicity.

In particular a CMC
h
-immersion
u:\Sigma\to N
is a critical point of the following functional

\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)=\mathcal{A}(u)+V_{h}(u)

and
V_{h}
is an enclosed volume with oriented weight
h
. There is, in general, no canonical form for an enclosed volume in
N^{3}
, but it is easy to see that there is a good choice if
u(\Sigma)
misses a small ball in
N
: for any
{\varepsilon}<inj_{N}
and
B_{\varepsilon}(x)\subset N
if we set

{\alpha}_{h}=h\ast{\rm d}f\in\Gamma(\wedge^{2}{\rm T}^{\ast}(N{\setminus}B_{\varepsilon}(x))\,\,\text{where}\,\,f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(N{\setminus}B_{\varepsilon}(x))\,\,\text{solves}\,\,{\Delta}f=1

(1.3)

then
{\rm d}{\alpha}_{h}=h{\rm d}V_{N}
.
Thus given any
u\in W^{1,2}(\Sigma,N{\setminus}B_{\varepsilon}(x))
we may define

V_{h}(u):=\int_{\Sigma}u^{\ast}{\alpha}_{h}.

In particular if
u
is a CMC
h
-immersion then it will certainly avoid some such
B_{\varepsilon}(x)\subset N
and we may consider it to be a critical point of
\mathcal{A}_{h}
above (see Appendix
A
).
When
N=\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have a globally-defined
{\alpha}_{h}=\tfrac{h}{3}(x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\wedge{\rm d}x_{3}-x_{2}{\rm d}x_{1}\wedge{\rm d}x_{3}+x_{3}{\rm d}x_{1}\wedge{\rm d}x_{2})
.

We could equally well consider a branched CMC
h
-immersion to be a conformal critical point of

E_{h}(u)=E(u)+V_{h}(u)

and in either case, given a CMC
h
-immersion we will implicitly assume that it is a critical point of both
\mathcal{A}_{h}
and
E_{h}
for an appropriate
V_{h}
.

As to be expected (see
[
4
]
for the Euclidean derivation and (
A.3
) in Appendix
A
) along a CMC
h
-immersion the second variation of
\mathcal{A}_{h}
only sees the normal part of the variation and in particular if
s=f\nu=v^{\bot}
,
f=\langle v,\nu\rangle
we have

{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[v,v]={\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[s,s]={\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[f,f]=\int_{\Sigma}fLf\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

(1.4)

where
L=-{\Delta}-(|A|^{2}+Ric_{N}(\nu,\nu))
. The ‘weak’ CMC-index
i_{h}
, is equivalent to the number of ways we can reduce the area
\mathcal{A}(u)

amongst volume preserving deformations
. This quantity is equivalent to

i_{h}(u):=\max_{V\subset C^{\infty}(\Sigma)}\{\dim V:{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[f,f]<0\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\int_{\Sigma}f\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma=0\,\,\,\forall f\in V\},

where
V
is a linear subspace. In this paper we will estimate

i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}:=\max_{V\subset C^{\infty}(\Sigma)}\{\dim V:{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[f,f]<0\,\,\,\forall f\in V\}=\textrm{index}(L)\quad\text{and}\quad n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}=\textrm{nullity}(L).

We recall that the index
i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}
of the operator
L
differs from
i_{h}
by at most
1
(see e.g.
[
5
]
).

One of the main auxiliary results of this paper, crucial to obtaining an index estimate for immersed CMC surfaces in arbitrary three-manifolds, is that the second-order comparison of area and Dirichlet energy remains true along conformal maps which do not necessarily parametrise minimal surfaces. In particular (
1.2
) holds along
all
conformal maps into arbitrary Riemannian manifolds
N^{n}
(i.e. there is no constraint required on the mean curvature), see Section
2
. We therefore have exactly a comparison for the energies
\mathcal{A}_{h}
and
E_{h}
. From this point we are able to utilise the approach of Ejiri-Micallef in order to show:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
u:\Sigma_{g}^{2}\to N^{3}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a branched CMC
h
-immersion (see terminology above) from a closed Riemann surface of genus
g
to an orientable
3
-manifold
N
, itself isometrically embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then there exists some uniform
C<\infty
so that

i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}+n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\leq C(4J^{2}+h^{2})\Area(\Sigma)+r

where
J
is the sup-norm of the largest eigenvalue of the second fundamental form
\mathrm{II}
of the embedding
N\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r
is a purely topological quantity, exactly as in Theorem
1.1
above: if
b
is the total number of branch points counted with multiplicity then

r=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}6g-6-2b&\mbox{if }b\leq 2g-3\\
4g-2+2\left[\frac{-b}{2}\right]&\mbox{if }2g-2\leq b\leq 4g-4\qquad[x]=\max_{\mathbb{Z}}\{k:k\leq x\}\\
0&\mbox{if }b\geq 4g-3.\end{array}\right.

If in addition
u
is totally umbilic then

i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}+n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\leq C(4J^{2}+h^{2})\Area(\Sigma).

Remark 1.3
.

C
can be estimated explicitly by
\tfrac{60}{\pi}
, but it is most likely far from optimal as demonstrated by considering round spheres in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. In the presence of non-negative ambient (sectional) curvatures the
order
of the estimate (linear in terms of Willmore energy and genus) is certainly optimal — see discussion and examples below.

In the case that
h=0
this of course reduces to estimates obtained by Ejiri-Micallef e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 4.3]
, where here we show the specific dependence on the embedding of
N
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Recall the unduloid and nodoid CMC surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which lie in the same one-parameter family of rotationally symmetric and periodic immersions known collectively as Delaunay surfaces. To get from the (embedded) unduloids to the (non-embedded) nodoids, the family passes through a singular configuration of an infinite family of spheres of the same radius with centres along the
x
-axis (the axis of rotation), touching tangentially. We thus refer to each sphere-like portion of a Delaunay surface as a bulge or lobe and the connecting region as a neck in the below. Close to the singular limit we note that the Willmore energy of a (given region of a) Delaunay surface is roughly equivalent to the number of bulges in that region.

In a three-torus
T^{3}=\frac{[0,1]^{3}}{\sim}
, one can produce a singly-lobed Delaunay torus
u_{1}:T^{2}\to T^{3}
by projecting (the fundamental piece of) an appropriately scaled Delaunay surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
onto
T^{3}
.
For Delaunay tori with more lobes, one proceeds in the obvious way by
taking
k
lengths of the fundamental piece of the Delaunay surface and scale by
1/k
, then immerse into the fundamental piece of
T^{3}
so that under the projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to T^{3}
, one has a
k
-lobed Delaunay torus (with
k
lobes and
k
necks). The
x
-component of the unit normal to these surfaces is a Jacobi field, so the Courant nodal domain theorem tells us that the index of these surfaces asymptotically grows at least affine-linearly with twice the number of lobes,
\mathrm{index}(u_{k})\geq 2k-2
. We also have
\Area(u_{k})=k^{-1}\Area(u_{1})
(the area of the fundamental piece, of which we have
k
copies, scales by
k^{-2}
), and the mean curvature
h_{k}=kh_{1}
.

In this way, for large
k
,
\mathrm{index}(u_{k})
and Willmore energy
h_{k}^{2}\Area(u_{k})
grow affine-linearly in
k
. In particular the fact that the Willmore energy appears linearly as an upper bound in our estimate is optimal for large index.

This optimality is also shown by considering families of Wente tori (resp. rotationally symmetric CMC tori) in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(resp.
\mathbb{S}^{3}
). Rossman
[
19
]
and Rossman-Sultana
[
20
]
have proven that the index of these CMC tori grows affine linearly with twice the number of “bulges”. In both cases these surfaces look like a piece of a Delaunay surface, bent around and with ends glued together. The terms bulges and necks are similarly interpreted as above in these papers (indeed this is why we use it here). See the descriptions in
[
19
,
20
]
and the references therein for more precise definitions. In both cases one may easily infer that the number of bulges is roughly equivalent to the Willmore energy of the surface, which in the case of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
takes the form
(4+h^{2}){\rm Area}(\Sigma)
.

There are several interesting general lower-bounds on the index of a CMC-surface in Riemannian
3
-manifolds in terms of genus see e.g.
[
1
,
6
]
most of which build on the works of Ros
[
18
]
, Savo
[
21
]
and more recently
[
3
]
. For instance in a space form it is shown
[
6
]
that the weak CMC index grows at least as fast as
\frac{g}{3+c}
where
c
is the curvature of the ambient space. Given the examples of arbitrary genus constructed by Kapouleas
[
15
]
in Euclidean space, and Heller-Heller-Traizet
[
13
]
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
there are certainly CMC surfaces whose index diverges at least linearly with genus and thus the order of the estimate is optimal in terms of genus.

Given the above discussion we pose the following conjecture, which should be compared with the conjecture of Schoen and Marques-Neves (see
[
3
]
)

Conjecture 1.4
.

Let
u:\Sigma^{2}\to N^{3}
be a branched CMC
h
-immersion and suppose
N^{3}
has strictly positive sectional curvature. Then there exists some
C=C(N)<\infty
so that

C\left((1+h^{2})\Area(\Sigma)+g\right)\leq i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}+n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}.

In the case that
N=\mathbb{R}^{3}
or
N=T^{3}
is a flat three torus, we expect

C\left(h^{2}\Area(\Sigma)+g\right)\leq i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}+n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}.

In the presence of strictly negative sectional curvatures the estimate given in Theorem
1.2
is seen to be highly sub-optimal by considering spheres of large radius in a hyperbolic three-manifold. All CMC spheres in a hyperbolic three manifold are round (and the boundaries of geodesic balls)
[
14
,
9
]
and satisfy
i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}+n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}=4
, but the Willmore energy grows exponentially with the radius: Given an arbitrary radius, there are closed hyperbolic three-manifolds admitting a CMC sphere of this radius and we always have that the mean curvature satisfies
h^{2}>4
and
h^{2}\to 4
as the radius diverges as can be checked directly.

In the presence of sufficiently negative ambient sectional curvatures relative to the size of the mean curvature, we are able to obtain an index bound purely in terms of genus. This bound still does not apply to CMC spheres in hyperbolic space, however, showing that the hypothesis
h^{2}\leq 4|\kappa_{0}|
in the below is indeed optimal:

Theorem 1.5

(cf. Corollary 4.1 in
[
11
]
)
.

Suppose that
u:\Sigma^{2}\to N^{3}
is a branched CMC
h
-immersion inside an orientable
3
-manifold
N
whose sectional curvatures satisfy
\kappa^{N}\leq\kappa_{0}<0
.
If
h^{2}\leq 4|\kappa_{0}|
then either

1.

i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}+n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\leq r,

where
r
is a purely topological quantity defined in Theorem
1.2
or

2.

u
is totally umbilic,
h^{2}\equiv 4|\kappa_{0}|
,
Ric_{N}(\nu,\nu)\equiv-2|\kappa_{0}|
,
i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}=0
and
n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}=1
.

2
Comparison of second variations of area and energy at a conformal map

Consider a smooth immersion
u:\Sigma\to N^{n}
from a closed manifold
\Sigma
into a Riemannian manifold
N
. We equip
\Sigma
with the pulled-back metric
u^{\ast}g
, which we still denote by
g
, making
u
an isometric immersion. The
g
-inner product is denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the induced volume form is denoted
{\rm d}\Sigma
.
If
v\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}{\rm T}N)
is an infinitesimal variation, which we decompose via
v=\sigma+s
with
s=v^{\bot}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{V}\Sigma)
and
\sigma=v-s\in\Gamma({\rm T}\Sigma)
, then (see
Appendix

A
) we show that the Hessian of area in direction
v
is as follows, noting that for us it is crucial both that
u
is not necessarily minimal and
v
is not necessarily normal:

\displaystyle{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u)[v,v]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}|{\nabla^{\bot}}s|^{2}-\sum_{i,j}\langle A(E_{i},E_{j}),s\rangle^{2}-\sum_{i}Rm^{N}(s,E_{i},E_{i},s)+\langle s,H\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),H\rangle+2\langle{\nabla}^{\bot}_{\sigma}s,H\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

where
A
is the second fundamental form,
H=\text{tr}A
is the mean curvature vector and
\{E_{i}\}
is an arbitrary orthonormal frame at each point.
Here we define the Hessian in the usual way: if
u_{t}:\Sigma\times(-{\varepsilon},{\varepsilon})\to N
is a one-parameter family of variations of
u_{0}=u
with
\partial_{t}u_{t}|_{t=0}=v
, then

{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u)[v,v]={\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}\vline_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{\,t=0}}}}}}}}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u(\cdot,t))-{\rm d}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u)\left[{{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}u(\cdot,0)}\right],

where (as in the Appendix) by
u_{tt}
we mean
\nabla_{u_{t}}u_{t}
.
For a derivation of the above under the additional assumption that
u
is minimal see
[
24
]
. For a derivation in full generality see
Appendix

A
.
We further remind the reader that if
E(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|{\rm d}u|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma
for an arbitrary (not necessarily harmonic) map
u:(\Sigma,h)\to(N,g)
between Riemannian manifolds, we have

\displaystyle{\rm d}^{2}E(u)[v,v]=\int_{\Sigma}|{\nabla}v|^{2}-\tr_{h}Rm^{N}(v,{\rm d}u,{\rm d}u,v)\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma.

In the above
{\nabla}
and
{\nabla}^{\bot}
denote the induced connections on
u^{\ast}{\rm T}N
and
\mathcal{V}\Sigma
respectively.

We now restrict to dimension
m=2
. Let
\Sigma
be a closed Riemann surface and
u:\Sigma\to N
a (possibly branched) smooth conformal immersion.
We will use the notation
\langle,\rangle
as the real inner product
g
on
N
, extended complex bi-linearly to
{\rm T}_{\mathbb{C}}N={\rm T}N\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}
. We use the same notation for
u^{\ast}g
the pulled-back metric on
u^{\ast}({\rm T}_{\mathbb{C}}N)
. In a local complex coordinate
z=x+iy
, we use the notation
\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})
and
u_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(u_{x}-iu_{y})
. Defining
\lambda
so that
e^{2\lambda}=2|{\partial_{z}}|_{g}^{2}=2|u_{z}|_{g}^{2}
we have
u^{\ast}g=e^{2\lambda}({\rm d}x^{2}+{\rm d}y^{2})
and
{\rm d}\Sigma=e^{2\lambda}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y
.
{\nabla}
and
{\nabla}^{\bot}
will be extended complex linearly as required.

As in Ejiri-Micallef
[
11
]
we let
\xi
denote the ramified tangent bundle so that
u^{\ast}({\rm T}N)=\xi\oplus\mathcal{V}\Sigma
and we split
v\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}{\rm T}N)
as above via
v=\sigma+s
for
\sigma\in\Gamma(\xi)
and
s\in\Gamma(\mathcal{V}\Sigma)
noting that the complex structure on
\Sigma
allows us to further decompose
\xi_{\mathbb{C}}=\xi\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}=\xi^{1,0}+\xi^{0,1}
.

Given
\sigma\in\Gamma(\xi)
we may decompose, away from the branch points,

\sigma=2e^{-2\lambda}\langle\sigma,u_{\overline{z}}\rangle u_{z}+2e^{-2\lambda}\langle\sigma,u_{z}\rangle u_{\overline{z}}=\sigma^{1,0}+\sigma^{0,1}.
Similarly we define the related
\sigma_{\Sigma}
below which we consider to be a section of
{\rm T}_{\mathbb{C}}\Sigma
away from the branch points:
\sigma_{\Sigma}:=2e^{-2\lambda}\langle\sigma,u_{\overline{z}}\rangle\partial_{z}+2e^{-2\lambda}\langle\sigma,u_{z}\rangle{\partial_{\overline{z}}}=\sigma_{\Sigma}^{1,0}+\sigma_{\Sigma}^{0,1}
.

In this setting, the second variation of area can be written (as may be checked directly):

Theorem 3.1
.

Let

Proof.

This is analogous to the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[

Let

be the non-positive eigenspace of

Let

Therefore the second variation of energy may be written

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{2}{E_{h}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}-\Rm(v,e^{-\lambda}u_{x},e^{-\lambda}u_{x},v)-\Rm(v,e^{-\lambda}u_{y},e^{-\lambda}u_{y},v)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+h\left({\omega(v,\nabla_{e^{-\lambda}\partial_{x}}v,e^{-\lambda}u_{y})+\omega(v,e^{-\lambda}u_{x},\nabla_{e^{-\lambda}\partial_{y}}v)}\right)\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|{\nabla}v|^{2}-2\Rm(v,e^{-\lambda}u_{x},e^{-\lambda}u_{x},v)-2\Rm(v,e^{-\lambda}u_{y},e^{-\lambda}u_{y},v)-2h^{2}|v|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

by (
3.2
) with
{\varepsilon}=1
.

We now note that by the Gauss equations:

\sup_{p\in\Sigma}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}X,Y\in T_{u(p)}N,\\
|X|=|Y|=1\end{subarray}}\Rm(X,Y,Y,X)\leq J^{2}

which leaves

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{2}{E_{h}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}|{\nabla}v|^{2}-(4J^{2}+2h^{2})|v|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\left\langle(-\overline{\Delta}-4J^{2}-2h^{2})v,v\right\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

where
\overline{\Delta}
is the rough Laplacian in
u^{\ast}{\rm T}N
.

This estimate immediately leads to

i_{E_{h}}+n_{E_{h}}\leq\mathrm{index}(-\overline{\Delta}-4J^{2}-2h^{2})+\mathrm{nullity}(-\overline{\Delta}-4J^{2}-2h^{2})\leq\#\left\{{\overline{\lambda}\leq 4J^{2}+2h^{2}}\right\},

(3.3)

where
\overline{\lambda}
are the eigenvalues of
-\overline{\Delta}
(counted with multiplicity).

Now, for all
t>0
, we have

\#\left\{{\overline{\lambda}\leq 4J^{2}+2h^{2}}\right\}\leq\sum_{i}e^{-t(\overline{\lambda}_{i}-(4J^{2}+2h^{2}))}=e^{(4J^{2}+2h^{2})t}\overline{k}(t),

(3.4)

where
\overline{k}(t)=\sum_{i}e^{-\overline{\lambda}_{i}t}
is the trace of the heat kernel of
-\overline{\Delta}
.

Similarly, the trace of the heat kernel related to the scalar Laplacian
-\Delta
on
\Sigma
, is
k(t)=\sum_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}
where
\{\lambda_{i}\}
are the eigenvalues of
-\Delta
.

Now, by Theorem 2.1 of
[
25
]
, we have that
\overline{k}(t)\leq 3k(t)
for all
t>0
, and so keeping in mind the estimates (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we have

i_{E_{h}}+n_{E_{h}}\leq 3\inf_{t>0}e^{(4J^{2}+2h^{2})t}k(t).

(3.5)

At this point we are required to estimate the heat kernel of
-\Delta
, and we do this
via
an argument of Cheng–Tysk
[
7
, pp. 991 – 993]
. We provide details since we wish to keep track of constants more precisely.

View
u
as a map
\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose mean curvature is
\vec{H}:=H+\mathcal{J}
, where
\mathcal{J}=\tr_{\Sigma}\mathrm{II}
(
\mathrm{II}
is the second fundamental form of
N
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) and
H
is of course the mean curvature of
\Sigma
in
N
: note that
|\vec{H}|=\sqrt{|H|^{2}+|\mathcal{J}|^{2}}\leq\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}
.

Applying the Michael-Simon-Sobolev inequality to a general
f\in W^{1,1}(\Sigma)
, we have

\left({\int_{\Sigma}f^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\Sigma}\left({|\nabla f|+\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}|f|}\right).

Replace
f
with
f^{2}
; an application of Hölder’s inequality and the Peter-Paul inequality yields

\left({2\pi\int_{\Sigma}f^{4}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}\left({|\nabla f^{2}|+\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}f^{2}}\right)\leq(1+{\delta})\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}f^{2}+\frac{1}{{\delta}\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f|^{2}.

An interpolation inequality (
\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{3}\leq\|f\|_{L^{4}}^{2}\|f\|_{L^{1}}
) yields, for any
{\delta}>0
:

\sqrt{2\pi}\frac{\left({\int_{\Sigma}f^{2}}\right)^{3/2}}{\int_{\Sigma}|f|}\leq(1+{\delta})\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}f^{2}+\frac{1}{{\delta}\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla f|^{2}.

(3.6)

Now, consider
K(x,y,t)
the heat kernel of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Sigma
.
From, for example,
[
22
, p. 103]
, we know that

K(x,y,t)=\sum_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}t}\phi_{i}(x)\phi_{i}(y),\qquad\lim_{t\downarrow 0}K(x,y,t)=\delta_{x}(y)

and that
K>0
(see, for instance,
[
22
, Lemma 1, p. 99]
), where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\Sigma
counted with multiplicity, and
\phi_{i}
are the corresponding
L^{2}
-orthonormal eigenfunctions.
Moreover, since
\partial_{t}\int_{\Sigma}\!K(x,y,t)\,\,{\rm d}{y}=\int_{\Sigma}\!\Delta K(x,y,t)\,\,{\rm d}{y}=0
, we have that
\int_{\Sigma}|K(x,y,t)|\,\,{\rm d}{y}=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\int_{\Sigma}K(x,y,t)\,\,{\rm d}{y}=1
.
Setting therefore
f(y):=K(x,y,t)
in the estimate (
3.6
) we have

\sqrt{2\pi}\left({\int_{\Sigma}K^{2}\mathrm{d}{y}}\right)^{3/2}\leq(1+{\delta})\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}\int_{\Sigma}K^{2}\mathrm{d}{y}+\frac{1}{{\delta}\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}}\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla_{y}K|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}{y}.

But
K(x,x,t)=\int_{\Sigma}K(x,y,t/2)^{2}\,\,{\rm d}{y}
, and so

\displaystyle\partial_{t}K(x,x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}K(x,y,t/2)\left({\partial_{t}K}\right)(x,y,t/2)\,\,{\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}K(x,y,t/2)\left({\Delta_{y}K}\right)(x,y,t/2)\,\,{\rm d}{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla_{y}K(x,y,t/2)|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}{y}.

Therefore,

\sqrt{2\pi}{\delta}\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}K(x,x,t)^{3/2}\leq{\delta}(1+{\delta})(h^{2}+4J^{2})K(x,x,t)-\partial_{t}K(x,x,t).

Setting
\phi(t)=K^{-1/2}(x,x,t)
yields the ordinary differential inequality

\sqrt{\pi}{\delta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left({h^{2}+4J^{2}}\right)}\leq{\delta}(1+{\delta})\frac{1}{2}(h^{2}+4J^{2})\phi+\phi^{\prime},

the solution of which can be obtained by integration (noting that
\phi(0)=0)
, yielding, with
{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}(h^{2}+4J^{2})

\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{(1+{\delta})\sqrt{h^{2}+4J^{2}}}\frac{e^{{\delta}(1+{\delta}){\alpha}t}-1}{e^{{\delta}(1+{\delta}){\alpha}t}}\leq\phi(t).

Therefore, replacing
\phi
with its expression in terms of the heat kernel, and noting that
k(t)=\int_{\Sigma}K(x,x,t)\,\,{\rm d}{x}
, gives

k(t)\leq\frac{(1+{\delta})^{2}}{2\pi}\Area(\Sigma)(h^{2}+4J^{2})\cdot\left({\frac{e^{{\delta}(1+{\delta}){\alpha}t}}{e^{{\delta}(1+{\delta}){\alpha}t}-1}}\right)^{2}.

Using this in the inequality (
3.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle i_{E_{h}}+n_{E_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3(1+{\delta})^{2}}{2\pi}\Area(\Sigma)(h^{2}+4J^{2})\inf_{t>0}\left({\frac{e^{({\delta}(1+{\delta})+2){\alpha}t}}{e^{{\delta}(1+{\delta}){\alpha}t}-1}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2\pi}\Area(\Sigma)(h^{2}+4J^{2})\frac{({\delta}(1+{\delta})+2)^{2+\frac{4}{{\delta}(1+{\delta})}}}{{\delta}^{2}2^{\frac{4}{{\delta}(1+{\delta})}}},

for any
{\delta}>0
.

The expression in
{\delta}
is less than
40
when
{\delta}\approx 2.3
and so we have an upper bound of the form:

i_{E_{h}}+n_{E_{h}}\leq\frac{60}{\pi}\Area(\Sigma)(h^{2}+4J^{2}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Again letting
{\rm d}V_{N}=\omega

\mathrm{d}^{2}{E_{h}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla v|^{2}-e^{-2\lambda}\left({\Rm(v,u_{x},u_{x},v)+\Rm(v,u_{y},u_{y},v)}\right)\\
+e^{-2\lambda}h\left({\omega(v,{\nabla}_{\partial_{x}}v,u_{y})+\omega(v,u_{x},{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}v)}\right)\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

(3.7)

Now, the fact that any sectional curvature
\kappa^{N}\leq\kappa_{0}<0
gives that

-e^{-2\lambda}\left({\Rm(v,u_{x},u_{x},v)+\Rm(v,u_{y},u_{y},v)}\right)\geq 2|\kappa_{0}||v|^{2}.

Setting
{\varepsilon}=1/2
in (
3.2
) yields the estimate

\mathrm{d}^{2}{E_{h}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]\geq\int_{\Sigma}\left({2|\kappa_{0}|-\frac{1}{2}h^{2}}\right)|v|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma,

whence if
h^{2}\leq 4|\kappa_{0}|
,
\mathrm{d}^{2}{E_{h}}(u)\geq 0
. If
{\rm d}^{2}E_{h}>0
we clearly have conclusion 1.

On the other hand if there exists
v
so that
{\rm d}^{2}E_{h}(u)[v,v]=0
, following the equality case back through the proof (and also in (
3.2
))
we see that
h^{2}=4|\kappa_{0}|
and up to constant
v=\nu
,
{\nabla}_{\partial_{x}}\nu=-|\kappa_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{x}
,
{\nabla}_{\partial_{y}}\nu=-|\kappa_{0}|^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{y}
and of course
Ric(\nu,\nu)\equiv-2|\kappa_{0}|
. This easily yields that
u
is totally umbilic (
A(u_{z},u_{z})\equiv 0
) and that
|A|^{2}\equiv 2|\kappa_{0}|
. In particular
{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[1,1]=0
and so we must have
n_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}=1
and
i_{\mathcal{A}_{h}}=0
.
∎

Appendix A
Area, conformally invariant variational problems, enclosed volume and their variations

We first derive the full second variation of area along a (potentially) non-minimal immersion and along arbitrary (non-normal) variations. Of course the result holds under far fewer assumptions on
u
and its variation
v
but for simplicity we state the results for smooth immersions and variations.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
u:\Sigma^{m}\to N^{n}
be a smooth immersion from a closed manifold into some Riemannian manifold
(N,g)
. Suppose that
u(x,t):\Sigma\times(-{\delta},{\delta})\to N
is a smooth one-parameter family of immersions and we denote
v=u_{t}(x,0)\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}{\rm T}N)
which we decompose via
v=\sigma+s
where
s=v^{\bot}\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}\mathcal{V}\Sigma)
and
\sigma\in\Gamma({\rm T}\Sigma)
, then

\displaystyle{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{m}(\Sigma)[v,v]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}|{\nabla^{\bot}}s|^{2}-\sum_{i,j}\langle A(E_{i},E_{j}),s\rangle^{2}-\sum_{i}Rm^{N}(s,E_{i},E_{i},s)+\langle s,H\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),H\rangle+2\langle{\nabla^{\bot}}_{\sigma}s,H\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma,

where
\{E_{i}\}
is an arbitrary orthonormal frame for
{\rm T}\Sigma
.

Proof.

We follow both
[
10
]
and
[
2
, Appendix A]
in order to derive the full Hessian of the area functional along such
u
. We equip
\Sigma
with the pulled-back metric
u^{\ast}g
, which we still denote by
g
, making
u
an isometric immersion. The
g
-inner product is denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the induced volume form is denoted
{\rm d}\Sigma
. First given any local coordinates
\{x^{i}\}
on
\Sigma
we may define

g_{ij}(t)=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle\quad\text{where $u_{i}=\partial_{x^{i}}u(x,t)$}

and

G(t)=\sqrt{\det g^{-1}(0)g(t)}

which we note is well-defined independently of the choice of local coordinates.

The area formula gives

\mathcal{A}^{m}(u(\cdot,t))=\int_{\Sigma}G(t)\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma.

We note the following useful facts for the proceeding computations, where now
{\nabla}
denotes the pulled back Levi-Civita connection by
u(x,t)
:
The
t
and
x^{i}
derivatives commute giving (here and throughout we use the short-hand
u_{tt}=\nabla_{u_{t}}u_{t}
),

\partial_{t}g_{ij}(t)=\langle{\nabla}_{u_{i}}u_{t},u_{j}\rangle+\langle u_{i},{\nabla}_{u_{j}}u_{t}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\langle{\nabla}_{u_{t}}{\nabla}_{u_{i}}u_{t},u_{j}\rangle=\langle{\nabla}_{u_{i}}u_{tt},u_{j}\rangle-Rm^{N}(u_{t},u_{i},u_{j},u_{t}),

which together imply that, taking a Taylor expansion about
t=0
,

g_{ij}(t)=g_{ij}(0)+tA+t^{2}B+O(t^{3})

where

A=\partial_{t}g_{ij}(0)=\langle{\nabla}_{u_{i}}v,u_{j}\rangle+\langle u_{i},{\nabla}_{u_{j}}v\rangle

and

B=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{tt}g_{ij}(0)=\langle{\nabla}_{u_{i}}v,{\nabla}_{u_{j}}v\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\nabla}_{u_{i}}u_{tt},u_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle u_{i},{\nabla}_{u_{j}}u_{tt}\rangle-Rm^{N}(v,u_{i},u_{j},v).

In particular, choosing coordinates
\{x^{i}\}
so that
\{u_{i}=E_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis at a point
p
when
t=0
, and using that

\sqrt{\det(I+tA+t^{2}B+O(t^{3}))}=1+\frac{t}{2}\textrm{tr}(A)+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(\textrm{tr}(B)+\frac{1}{4}(\textrm{tr}(A))^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\textrm{tr}(A^{2})\right)+O(t^{3})

we have that, at the point
p
:

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+t\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v)+

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left(|({\nabla}v)^{\bot}|^{2}+\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(u_{tt})-\sum_{i}Rm^{N}(v,E_{i},E_{i},v)+\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+(\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v))^{2}-\sum_{ij}\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}v,E_{j}\rangle\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}v,E_{j}\rangle\right)+O(t^{3}).

As is standard, setting
\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(w)=\sum_{i}\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}w,E_{i}\rangle
for any
w\in u^{\ast}{\rm T}N
and orthonormal frame
\{E_{i}\}
we have that
\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v)=\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)-\langle s,H\rangle
where now
\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)
can be interpreted as an intrinsic divergence along
\Sigma
itself.
Clearly

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u)[v]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\vline_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{\,t=0}}}}}}}}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u(\cdot,t))=\int_{\Sigma}\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v)\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle H,s\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma.

The full Hessian is therefore:

\displaystyle{\rm d}^{2}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u)[v,v]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}\vline_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{\,t=0}}}}}}}}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u(\cdot,t))-{\rm d}\mathcal{A}^{m}(u)[u_{tt}(\cdot,0)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}|({\nabla}v)^{\bot}|^{2}-\sum_{i}Rm^{N}(v,E_{i},E_{i},v)+(\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v))^{2}-\sum_{i,j}\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}v,E_{j}\rangle\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}v,E_{j}\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}|{\nabla}^{\bot}s|^{2}-\sum_{i,j}\langle A(E_{i},E_{j}),s\rangle^{2}-\sum_{i}Rm^{N}(s,E_{i},E_{i},s)+\langle s,H\rangle^{2}+\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

where

\mathcal{B}=\sum_{i}|A(E_{i},\sigma)|^{2}-Rm^{N}(\sigma,E_{i},E_{i},\sigma)+(\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma))^{2}-\sum_{i,j}\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}\sigma,E_{j}\rangle\langle{\nabla}_{E_{i}}\sigma,E_{j}\rangle

and

\mathcal{C}=2\sum_{i}\langle{\nabla}^{\bot}_{E_{i}}s,A(E_{i},\sigma)\rangle-2Rm^{N}(\sigma,E_{i},E_{i},v)-2\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)\langle s,H\rangle+2\sum_{i}\langle A({\nabla}^{\top}_{E_{i}}\sigma,E_{i}),s\rangle.

Directly from
[
2
, Appendix A]
we see that

\mathcal{C}=\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\omega)+2\langle{\nabla^{\bot}}_{\sigma}s,H\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{B}=\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),H\rangle+\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)\sigma-{\nabla}^{\top}_{\sigma}\sigma)

where
\omega
is a one-form defined along
\Sigma
by
\omega(\xi)=2\langle A(\sigma,\xi),s\rangle-2\langle s,H\rangle\langle\sigma,\xi\rangle
and we arrive at the desired formula.
∎

A.1
Conformally invariant variational problems and enclosed volume

Let
(N^{n+1},g)
be an arbitrary smooth Riemannian manifold equipped with a two-form
{\alpha}\in\Gamma(\wedge^{2}{\rm T}^{\ast}N)
. For
u:\Sigma\to N
and
\Sigma
a closed orientable surface, let

V_{\alpha}(u):=\int_{\Sigma}u^{\ast}{\alpha}.

(A.1)

If
u_{t}
is a one-parameter family of variations with
\partial_{t}u_{t}\vline_{t=0}=v\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}{\rm T}N)
, we have (see e.g.
[
17
]
)

{\rm d}V_{\alpha}(u)[v]={\frac{\partial}{\partial t}}\vline_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{{}_{\,t=0}}}}}}}}V_{\alpha}(u_{t})=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}{\alpha}(v,u_{x},u_{y})\,\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

where
\{x,y\}
is a local choice of oriented coordinates and one may straightforwardly check that the above integral is independent of coordinates and globally well-defined.

The second variation has been derived in
[
17
]
and may be written:

{\rm d}^{2}V_{\alpha}(u)[v,v]:=\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}{\alpha}(v,v_{x},u_{y})+{\rm d}{\alpha}(v,u_{x},v_{y})+\left({{\nabla}_{v}{\rm d}{\alpha}}\right)(v,u_{x},u_{y})\,\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

If
N
is an orientable three-manifold then
{\rm d}{\alpha}=\mathcal{H}{\rm d}V_{N}
for some smooth function
\mathcal{H}
on
N
. In this case, we may write
E_{\mathcal{H}}
and
V_{\mathcal{H}}
rather than
E_{\alpha}
and
V_{\alpha}
. In the case that
u
is an immersion we have

{\rm d}V_{\alpha}(u)[v]=\int_{\Sigma}\langle v,\mathcal{H}\nu\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

where
\nu
is a unit vector such that
(u_{x},u_{y},\nu)
is positively oriented in
N
and
{\rm d}\Sigma
is the induced area on
\Sigma
.

In particular if
u:\Sigma\to N
satisfies
u^{-1}(B_{\varepsilon}(x))=\emptyset
then defining
{\alpha}_{h}
via (
1.3
) we have:
u
is a CMC
h
-immersion if and only if

{\rm d}\mathcal{A}_{h}(u)[v]=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle v,H\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma+\int_{\Sigma}\langle v,h\nu\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma=0\quad\text{for all $v\in\Gamma(u^{\ast}{\rm T}N)$.}

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
N
is an orientable three-manifold equipped with a two-form
{\alpha}\in\Gamma(\wedge^{2}{\rm T}^{\ast}N)
.
Then, writing
\mathrm{d}{\alpha}=\mathcal{H}\mathrm{d}V_{N}
, we have that for any smooth possibly branched immersion

,

\mathrm{d}^{2}{V_{\alpha}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}-\left\langle s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle\left\langle s,H\right\rangle-2\left\langle{\nabla^{\bot}}_{\sigma}s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle-\left\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle+\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\nabla_{s}\mathcal{H}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma

(A.2)

where we employ the usual decomposition
v=\sigma+s
into the tangential and normal components,
\sigma
and
s
respectively, of
v
.

Corollary A.3
.

If
u:\Sigma\to N
is a CMC
h
immersion, i.e.
V_{\alpha}=V_{h}
and
\mathcal{H}\equiv h
, then the mean curvature of
u
is
H=h\nu
and

\mathrm{d}^{2}{V_{h}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}-\left\langle s,H\right\rangle^{2}-2\left\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{\sigma}s,H\right\rangle-\left\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),H\right\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma.

Remark A.4
.

Combining this with Theorem
A.1
we have, if
u
is a CMC
h
-immersion,

\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mathcal{A}_{h}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}|\nabla^{\perp}s|^{2}-\left({|A|^{2}+Ric(\nu,\nu)}\right)|s|^{2}\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma.

(A.3)

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

Setting for convenience
\omega={\rm d}V_{N}
so that
\mathrm{d}{\alpha}=\mathcal{H}\omega
, we have from
[
17
]
that

\mathrm{d}^{2}{V_{\alpha}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{H}\omega(v,v_{x},u_{y})+\mathcal{H}\omega(v,u_{x},v_{y})+\nabla_{v}\mathcal{H}\,\omega(v,u_{x},u_{y})\,\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Let
\nu
be a normal vector field such that
\omega(\nu,u_{x},u_{u})=e^{2\lambda}
.
Then we have that
\omega(v,u_{x},u_{y})=\omega(s,u_{x},u_{y})=\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\omega(\nu,u_{x},u_{y})=\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle e^{2\lambda}
, so that the third term above is

\nabla_{v}\mathcal{H}\,\omega(v,u_{x},u_{y})=\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle e^{2\lambda}\nabla_{v}\mathcal{H}.

Now, the first term in the above integral is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\omega(v,v_{x},u_{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\omega(\left\langle v,\nu\right\rangle\nu,v_{x},u_{y})+\mathcal{H}\omega(\sigma,v_{x},u_{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\omega(\nu,\left\langle v_{x},u_{x}\right\rangle e^{-2\lambda}u_{x},u_{y})+\mathcal{H}\omega(\sigma,\left\langle v_{x},u_{x}\right\rangle e^{-2\lambda}u_{x},u_{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\left\langle v_{x},u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\omega(\nu,\sigma,u_{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\left\langle v_{x},u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\omega(\nu,e^{-2\lambda}\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle u_{x},u_{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\left\langle v_{x},u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle.

(A.4)

Similarly, the second term is

\mathcal{H}\omega(v,u_{x},v_{y})=\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\left\langle v_{y},u_{y}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle.

(A.5)

Note now that

\displaystyle\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\langle\nabla_{e_{i}}v,e_{i}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-2\lambda}\left\langle\nabla_{u_{x}}v,u_{x}\right\rangle+e^{-2\lambda}\left\langle\nabla_{u_{y}}v,u_{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-2\lambda}\left({\left\langle v_{x},u_{x}\right\rangle+\left\langle v_{y},u_{y}\right\rangle}\right).

Thus combining (
A.4
) (
A.5
), one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\omega(v,v_{x},u_{y})+\mathcal{H}\omega(v,u_{x},v_{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle e^{2\lambda}\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(v)-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle e^{2\lambda}\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)-\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle H\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle

(A.6)

where
H
is the mean curvature along the immersion
u:\Sigma\to N
.
We focus now on the last two terms; indeed, we have

\displaystyle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{H}\left\langle\sigma_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle s_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\mathcal{H}\left\langle\sigma_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle s_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle.

The first and third terms combine to give
-e^{2\lambda}\mathcal{H}\left\langle\nabla_{\sigma}\sigma,\nu\right\rangle
, and the second and fourth terms combine to give
-e^{2\lambda}\mathcal{H}\left\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{\sigma}s,\nu\right\rangle
; thus we have

-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{x},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{x}\right\rangle-\mathcal{H}\left\langle v_{y},\nu\right\rangle\left\langle\sigma,u_{y}\right\rangle=-e^{2\lambda}\left\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle-e^{2\lambda}\left\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{\sigma}s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle.

(A.7)

Combining (
A.6
) and (
A.7
) we have

\mathrm{d}^{2}{V_{\alpha}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)-\left\langle s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle\left\langle s,H\nu\right\rangle-\left\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle\\
-\left\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{\sigma}s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle+\nabla_{\sigma}\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle+\nabla_{s}\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\,\,{\rm d}\Sigma.

(A.8)

Now, note that
\mathcal{H}\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\sigma)=\textrm{div}_{\Sigma}(\left\langle s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle\sigma)-\left\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{\sigma}s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle-\left\langle s,\left({\nabla_{\sigma}\mathcal{H}}\right)\nu\right\rangle
, and so

\mathrm{d}^{2}{V_{\alpha}}\left({u}\right)\left[{v,v}\right]=\int_{\Sigma}-\left\langle s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle\left\langle s,H\nu\right\rangle-2\left\langle\nabla^{\perp}_{\sigma}s,\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle-\left\langle A(\sigma,\sigma),\mathcal{H}\nu\right\rangle+\left\langle s,\nu\right\rangle\nabla_{s}\mathcal{H}\mathrm{d}\Sigma.

(A.9)
