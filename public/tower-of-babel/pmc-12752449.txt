Surgical interruptions and preventable delays in the operating rooms of academic teaching hospitals
Authors: Reza Kalantari, Mehdi Hasanshahi, Somayeh Gheysari, Anahid Geramshahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOperating rooms are complex environments prone to delays and interruptions, which are linked to human error and adverse patient outcomes. Since more than half of the hospitals in Iran are teaching hospitals, understanding these delays and interruptions is crucial to prevent them. This observational study aimed to assess the lost time and causes for surgical interruptions and delays in Iranian academic teaching hospitals.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study was conducted in five teaching hospitals in Iran during July and August of 2025. A total of 251 surgeries were randomly observed using full-case non-participant observation. Data were collected using a chronometer and standardized forms. Causes of delays and interruptions were categorized through directed content analysis. Quantitative data were analyzed with statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 24. The primary outcome was total lost time per surgery. The secondary outcomes were frequency and duration of interruptions and delays, as well as the distribution of causes.ResultsOut of 50,675 observed minutes, 7,920 min (16.83%, Confidence Interval 95%: 14.62–19.04) were lost due to interruptions and delays. A total of 281 delays with 319 causes were recorded and classified into five categories: operating room staff (172 times), staff outside the operating room (21), instruments and devices (60), patients (33), and managerial issues (33). Notably, 14.1% of causes were related to the educational nature of the hospitals, involving residents and trainees.ConclusionsSurgical interruptions and delays happen frequently and may reduce operating room efficiency and threaten patient safety. Delays related to residents and trainees require further investigation considering overall operating room efficiency. Educational, managerial, and instrument-related interventions may reduce lost operating room time. The findings may be generalizable to academic teaching hospitals.

Introduction

Operating room (OR) is a complex [1] fast-paced environment where time and resources are limited [2]. Delivering high-quality care while maintaining efficiency is challenging [3]. ORs are major cost centers in hospitals [4], contributing over 40% of hospital revenue [5]. However, they incur significant expenses, costing between $30 and over $100 per minute [6], making efficiency crucial for the hospital system. Efficient and exact scheduling improves staff and resource allocation, patient flow, and overall satisfaction [7, 8].

In the unpredictable OR environment, multiple factors contribute to inefficiency. The variety of patients, surgical procedures, expertise levels, instruments, and staff needs create complexities that disrupt surgical flow and cause delays [9]. ORs have substantial potential for interruptions that interfere with the surgical team’s work [10]. Such interruptions lead to extended operation times and increased errors [11], negatively impacting the surgical process, case completion [12], and patient outcomes [13]. Delays are also critical issues; for example, late starts of the first case delay subsequent surgeries, reducing overall efficiency [14]. In a research, delays were reported in nearly two-thirds of starting day surgeries [15]. These delays often stem from human errors and system deficiencies [16]. Additionally, case cancellations, which may result from prolonged delays [17], further challenge OR efficiency. Therefore, studying the causes and frequency of surgical delays and interruptions is essential. Identifying these issues enables the development of evidence-based strategies to enhance efficiency, reduce costs, and improve patient safety [18].

Surgical flow disruptions waste nearly 20% of OR time [13]. A comprehensive review reported interruptions ranging from 2.4 to 114 per hour [19]. In one study, surgical teams experienced about three interruptions per hour, mostly caused by conversations [2]. Various studies categorize causes of surgical delays and interruptions differently. Organizational constraints and malfunctions contribute significantly to disruptions [20]. Procedure-related deficiencies, such as surgical counts, also play an important role [21]. Other causes include slow patient registration, late anesthetist arrival, delayed surgical setup by nurses, equipment failures, and shortages of beds or personnel [22]. Additionally, equipment problems, procedural and environmental issues, telephones, pagers, and conversations are common distractions [23]. Factors like staff shortages, multitasking, and lack of instruments particularly affect laparoscopic surgeries [24]. A research showed that low resource settings may increase the percentage of case cancellations to more than 35% [25]. It seems that in every context the causes of interruptions and delays differ and may endanger patient safety. Poor condition of patient safety climate has already reported in several of Iranian hospitals [26].

Improving OR efficiency requires addressing multiple factors [22]. In teaching hospitals, the patterns of delays and interruptions may differ from non-teaching hospitals due to their educational role. The active presence of surgical and anesthesiology residents, as well as anesthesia and OR nursing trainees, introduces a wider range of delays and interruptions. While involving residents and trainees offers valuable learning opportunities and potentially lowers costs, their associated interruptions can jeopardize patient safety and impair surgical efficiency. Since approximately half of Iranian hospitals are teaching institutions [27], this study aims to determine the precise rates and causes of surgical interruptions and delays in such settings and to propose effective prevention strategies. Specifically, this research assessed surgical interruptions and delays in five Iranian teaching hospitals for the first time. We hypothesized that a substantial proportion of surgical time in teaching hospitals may lost due to preventable interruptions and delays and tried to find the causes.

Methods

This cross-sectional non-participant full-case observational study was conducted during July and August of 2025 in the ORs of five grand teaching hospitals affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Iran. A total of 251 surgeries were randomly selected for observation and analysis. The sample size of 250 was calculated using the Cochrane formula, with a type I error of 0.05, margin of error (d) of 0.05, and an estimated total of 700 surgeries conducted during two months. Inclusion criteria required the willingness of OR practitioners to be observed; surgeries performed during night shifts were excluded. The primary outcome was total lost time per surgery. The secondary outcome was frequency and duration of interruptions and delays, as well as the distribution of causes.

Data were collected through full case, non-participant observation, where the observer did not engage in the OR activities. An experienced OR technologist obtained necessary permissions and introduced herself to the surgical team before each surgery, explaining the study aims. Informed consent was obtained from the surgical team and patients. The observer positioned herself to avoid disruption and recorded data using a chronometer (Q & Q brand, CBM model, Japan) and a standardized data collection form. A “cause” was defined as any act or incident disrupting surgical flow; an “agent” was a person or mechanism causing the disruption; and “time” referred to the duration of the disruption. “Interruption” refers to an event that breaks the flow of primary surgical task. “Delay” was an avoidable or unexpected pause in surgical workflow. The planned pauses were excluded, so “lost time” refers to any period during the surgical process which halted due to an interruption or delay. Lost time is not refereeing planned pauses. Interruptions, delays, agents, and lost time were recorded. Figure 1 shows the interruption and delay record form that was used.

The informed consent was signed by the patient or relatives. Only patient who provided informed consent were included. No identifying patient information was recorded. The notes contained no clinical details that could link data to individuals and all data was anonymized. This way the protection of patient autonomy, confidentiality, and privacy was ensured.

In the observations, we tried to minimize the observer bias. The observer was an experienced operating room practitioner which is familiar to OR staff. This helped in decreasing behavioral reactivity. Maintaining in a fixed and unobtrusive position, avoiding eye contact and conversational engagements minimized the influence on staff behavior. The observer used a structured protocol that minimized subjective interpretation. Moreover, real-time documentation helped avoiding recall bias. Moreover, the inter-observer reliability was checked during pilot observations in ten surgeries. The consistency was 95% in causes for interruptions, 97% in agent, and 100% in time analysis.

The observation protocol was designed to minimize missing data. In the case of ambiguous or unclear interruption or event, the observer documented it and the case was discussed with research team members. The coding process was conducted later. As observations relied on human recording, it is possible that brief or spontaneous interruptions were not captured.

The interruptions and delays, the agents, and the lost time were recorded and listed. Causes were grouped via directed content analysis by two coders—an OR technologist and a human factors specialist with ten years’ experience—under supervision of two other team members. The coding reliability among coders for the first 10 surgeries was 90%. Codes were categorized based on similarities through member checking and group discussions, so the discrepancies were discussed and the code book was refined. The total lost time and its percentage per cause were calculated using SPSS version 24 software. Descriptive statistics including means, percentages, standard deviations, and 95% confidence interval were calculated for study outcomes.

The total observed time for 251 surgeries was 50,675 min (844 h and 35 min), with a mean surgery duration of 201 ± 89.23 min. The shortest surgery lasted 35 min, while the longest extended to 720 min. Among the observed surgeries, 179 cases (71.32%) experienced at least one interruption or delay. The total time lost due to interruptions and delays was 7,920 min (132 h), averaging 30 ± 38.22 min per surgery. This accounted for 16.83% (Confidence Interval 95%: 14.62–19.04) of the overall surgery duration.

There were 281 interruptions and delays recorded, averaging 1.12 delays per surgery. The shortest interruption lasted 3 min, and the longest 210 min. Most interruptions and delays (260 of 281, 92.52%) lasted less than 61 min. The surgery with the highest time lost was ultimately cancelled, with 105 min lost to delays. The greatest number of interruptions in a single surgery was six, while 111 surgeries (39.50%) had only one interruption or delay.

Content analysis categorized delay causes into five groups: OR staff (172 instances), staff outside the OR [21], instruments and devices (60), surgical patients [33], and managerial issues [33]. Multiple causes were identified in some cases, totaling 319 causes. Late arrival of staff was recorded for 73 times, consisting of 22.88% of all causes for interruptions and delays. The most frequent cause was the late arrival of the surgical attending (27 times), followed by late arrival of anesthesia attending or senior staff (20 times). Other notable causes included late arrival of patient transport staff [12], blocked ICU beds [10], late arrival of ward nurses after surgery [10], and failure of the set room to prepare surgical sets on time [9].

OR staff accounted for the majority of interruptions and delays (172 of 319, 53.91%). Of these, 45 interruptions (26.16%) were related to the educational nature of the hospital, with residents and trainees responsible for 14.10% of all interruptions and delays.

Discussion

This observational study assessed interruptions and delays in ORs of teaching hospitals. Findings showed that 16.83% of total surgery time was lost due to these factors, with an average rate of 1.12 interruptions or delays per surgery. Content analysis revealed 319 causes for these events, categorized primarily into five groups: OR staff (most frequent), staff outside the OR, instruments and devices, surgical patients, and managerial issues.

In this study, 7,920 min out of a total of 50,675 surgery minutes (16.83%) were lost due to interruptions and delays. This finding aligns with the narrative synthesis studies by Koch et al. and Al-Hakim, who reported that approximately one-fifth of OR time is spent on flow disruptions [13, 28]. Interruptions and delays appear to be an inherent part of many surgeries and may compromise patient safety and lead to poor outcomes. Other research has reported higher lost time, such as 25% in cardiovascular surgeries [29]. Lost time increases operation duration, anesthesia exposure, hospital costs, and staff fatigue. Surgical flow disruptions have been linked to procedural errors [11], which is consistent with our observation that only 28.68% of surgeries were free of such disruptions, making the majority vulnerable to errors. This is comparable to previous studies reporting delays in 40% to 96% of surgeries [30]. On average, each surgery in our study experienced 1.12 delays. Since over half of surgical interruptions are avoidable [31], identifying and preventing their causes is vital to reducing flow disruptions and minimizing adverse events in ORs. Ensuring patient and surgical team arrive promptly may improve operating room efficiency and enhances patient safety and health [15].

This study identified five main categories of causes for interruptions and delays: OR staff, staff outside the OR, instruments and devices, surgical patients, and managerial issues. This categorization was based on the nature of the causes, differing from some previous studies. OR staff were responsible for over half of the interruptions and delays, with late arrival of staff—especially surgeons and anesthesiologists—during the early stages of surgery being a major contributor. Late arrival may be recognized as a root cause of start-time delays. Consistent with our findings, unavailability of surgeons and anesthesiologists has been highlighted as a primary cause of delays in other research [32]. Late arrivals can frustrate patients and delay the surgical team already present in the OR. One study found that surgeon tardiness accounted for 27% of late surgery start times [33]. It is therefore essential to encourage punctuality among OR staff, particularly surgeons and anesthesiologists, and to supervise adherence. Targeted, multifaceted approaches, including organizational transformation programs, have demonstrated significant improvements in surgery start times [32].

In this study, trainees and residents accounted for 14.10% of interruptions and delays. Given that the surgeries were performed in educational hospitals; their inexperience is an understandable factor. Previous research has identified teaching among OR nurses as a procedural interruption that may sometimes be necessary [23], reflecting the educational role of teaching hospitals. While the involvement of residents and trainees provides valuable learning opportunities and can reduce hospital costs, it is essential to offer feedback and correct their performance. As surgical team leaders, surgeons can anticipate and prevent delays [34], by guiding less experienced team members. Additionally, other experienced staff can support minimizing interruptions and delays caused by residents and trainees in the OR. Patient safety depends on collective harmony of OR team members. With better rehearsal, communication, and support, the ORs will be safer places for patients [35].

All OR staff in this study caused at least one interruption or delay. Minimizing interruptions requires timely arrival, precise performance, avoiding unnecessary movement out of the OR, maintaining situational awareness, and enhancing coordination and cooperation among staff. Training and retraining programs emphasizing the consequences of interruptions and delays can be beneficial. Previous research has suggested the use of standardized teams with consistent members to improve efficiency [9]. Additionally, fostering personal accountability, streamlining procedures, and promoting interdisciplinary teamwork have proven effective in preventing OR inefficiencies [36].

Staff working outside the OR can also cause interruptions and delays. Common causes include delayed patient transfers and waiting for technicians [22]. Poor patient preparation and late arrivals from inpatient wards have been identified as key contributors, which may be mitigated by improving coordination and cooperation between wards and the OR. Calling patient transport staff and technicians earlier than the scheduled time could also help, considering the OR’s fast-paced and resource-limited environment [2], which incurs high costs for hospitals [37]. Staff in inpatient wards should adhere strictly to protocols for preparing surgical patients to facilitate smooth surgical flow and minimize time loss.

Insufficient or malfunctioning instruments and devices accounted for 18.80% of interruptions and delays in the observed surgeries. Equipment-related issues are among the most common causes of OR delays [38]. Additionally, a lack of available beds in recovery rooms and intensive care units (ICUs), previously reported as a significant factor [22] and responsible for one-fourth of equipment relate delays. This is critical because when beds are occupied, patients and several surgical team members may remain in the operating or recovery room, blocking the space and causing delayed starts for subsequent cases. Coordinating surgical admissions based on bed availability and increasing bed capacity may help alleviate this problem. Using fuzzy models to optimize surgical scheduling under uncertain bed availability has also shown promise [39]. Ensuring adequate numbers of functional instruments and devices is essential, given the high cost of OR time and its role as a major hospital revenue center [6]. Scheduled maintenance and servicing of equipment should be a hospital priority to prevent delays caused by device failures. As low resource settings face more number of case cancellation and late surgery start times [25], enough budget should be allocated to operating room to equip themselves and enhance patient safety.

Managerial issues, including poor planning, staff shortages, missing surgical items, and the physical layout of units, accounted for 10.34% of interruptions and delays. Poor planning examples include scheduling a surgeon to work simultaneously in two rooms, lacking contingency plans, and scheduling surgeries during shift change times. The adoption of newer technologies to facilitate OR scheduling and booking could improve planning and help prevent delays [40]. Staff shortages also contribute significantly, as surgical care is a team effort; when understaffed, personnel may be forced to multitask, which can disrupt workflow [2]. Therefore, increasing OR staff where needed can be beneficial. Additionally, monitoring and supervising staff performance is crucial to guide behavior and maintain efficiency. Long distances between ORs and essential units like the pharmacy or set room further contribute to time loss. Strategically locating OR components near each other could help reduce such delays.

Patient-related interruptions and delays may be considered more inevitable. These issues were mentioned in a former research [4]. When a patient’s condition is unstable, the OR team may need to wait for stabilization. Previous studies have classified several patient-related issues as random events [22] Calming patients and reducing their stress can better prepare them for surgery, while providing thorough explanations about the procedure helps set appropriate expectations. Patients may also feel anxious or need to use the restroom before surgery. Addressing patients’ special needs, ensuring maximum preparation, and informing their companions about necessary items and processes can help minimize patient-related interruptions and delays.

Like any research, this study had limitations. The potential under-recording of interruptions might be happened as the observer was human. The findings may not be easily generalizable to other contexts or hospitals, non-teaching hospitals, or healthcare settings in other regions and countries, as well as different surgical subspecialties. Future studies are warranted to examine interruption patterns across a broader range of hospitals We didn’t non-teaching hospitals as comparators, nor stratify by case urgency and staffing level. These factors may be considered in future studies. Additionally, observation bias may have influenced the behavior of participants during the study.

Conclusions

This observational study highlights that interruptions and delays are common in teaching hospital ORs, resulting in the loss of 16.83% of total surgery time, with an average of 1.12 delays per surgery. The majority of disruptions were attributed to OR staff, particularly late arrivals of surgeons and anesthesiologists, as well as inexperience among trainees and residents. Other contributing factors included insufficient or malfunctioning instruments, limited recovery room capacity, patient-related issues, and managerial and interdepartmental coordination challenges. These disruptions are consistent with previous findings indicating that surgical delays frequently occur, prolong operation and anesthesia times, increase hospital costs, and may compromise patient safety. Importantly, a significant proportion of interruptions are preventable. Strategies such as improving punctuality, providing structured training, enhancing multidisciplinary teamwork, optimizing patient preparation and transfer, ensuring equipment availability, and strengthening managerial oversight can reduce delays and improve workflow. While some patient-related delays are inevitable, proactive measures such as patient education and stress reduction can mitigate their impact. Addressing both human and systemic factors through a comprehensive, multifaceted approach can enhance OR efficiency, reduce costs, and improve clinical outcomes and patient safety, emphasizing the importance of continuous monitoring and targeted interventions in surgical settings.
