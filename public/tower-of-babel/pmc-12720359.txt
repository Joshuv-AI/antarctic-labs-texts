Fish blood flukes (digenea: Aporocotylidae) from marine fishes of South Africa, including proposal of a new genus☆
Authors: Russell Q-Y. Yong, Olena Kudlai, Anja Vermaak, Nico J. Smit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Novel records of marine fish-infecting blood flukes (Aporocotylidae Odhner, 1912) were obtained from several locations across the South African coastline. These constitute the first records of aporocotylid flukes from the region. A new genus, Paraskoulekia, is proposed for two new species of aporocotylids found in the hearts of Hottentot seabream, Pachymetopon blochii (Valenciennes) and bronze seabream, Pachymetopon grande Günther (Sparidae). The two species show close morphological affinities to the sparid-infecting genus Skoulekia Alama-Bermejo, Montero, Raga & Holzer, 2011 and are phylogenetically sister to species of that genus, but are genetically distant from them and differ in key aspects of morphology, namely in having a medially-positioned ovary, the uterine coils not lateral or anterior to the ovary and the cirrus-sac being posterior or at least lateral to the rest of the genitalia. Cardicola mediterraneus Palacios-Abella, Montero, Merella, Mele, Raga & Repullés-Albelda, 2021 is reported from the heart of zebra seabream, Diplodus hottentotus (Smith) (Sparidae), and Skoulekia meningialis Alama-Bermejo, Montero, Raga & Holzer, 2011 from the eyes of musselcracker seabream, Sparodon durbanensis (Castelnau) (Sparidae), marking the first time these species have been reported outside the western Mediterranean Sea. In addition, a species of Deontacylix Linton, 1910) is recorded from blue seachub, Kyphosus cinerascens (Forsskål) (Kyphosidae) and is contextualised within the global understanding of species of that genus.

Introduction

The marine teleost-infecting blood fluke family Aporocotylidae has rapidly grown in the number of recognised species, more than doubling in both number of genera and species described since the turn of the 21st century. This increase in knowledge has primarily been driven by a greater general awareness of their presence and better understanding of the biology of these worms, particularly in terms of their infection sites, thus facilitating better targeted searching (Cutmore et al., 2025). The significance of several aporocotylid species as disease agents in aquaculture has also catalysed interest in the group (Power et al., 2020). Interest in aporocotylids has primarily coalesced around four regions: the southeastern United States, the Spanish Mediterranean, Japan and eastern Australia, with 13 of 14 genus proposals and 70 of 82 species descriptions from this century authored by research groups from these regions. This regional bias in attention means large parts of the world are under-studied for fish blood flukes in both marine and freshwater systems; the entire continent of Africa, for example, has been the source of just two reports of marine aporocotylids (Lablack et al., 2022; Maillard and Ktari, 1978). Recent attention towards blood flukes in southern Africa has been confined to freshwater systems, involving species of the family Sanguinicolidae Poche, 1926; Outa and Avenant-Oldewage (2024); Warren et al. (2024a). This study provides the first documented evidence of aporocotylid infections in marine fishes from South Africa.

Materials and methods

A variety of fishes were sampled as part of long-term ongoing parasitological assessments of marine fishes off the South African coast, commencing in 2016 (e.g. Bruce et al., 2019; Lisitsyna et al., 2019; van der Spuy et al., 2023; Yong et al., 2025). These fishes were caught in a variety of ways including line fishing as well as cast and seine netting. Fishes were transported in containers of aerated seawater to research bases, where they were humanely euthanased and dissected using standard methods (Yong et al., 2013; Cutmore et al., 2025). Blood flukes found were heat-fixed in saline just off the boil and preserved in tubes of 70% or 80% ethanol. Specimens intended for morphological analysis were stained and mounted using the standard protocol for Mayer's hematoxylin stain and Canada balsam mounting medium (see Yong et al., 2019 for full protocol). Drawings were made using camera lucida attachments mounted on Nikon NiE light microscopes and digitised in Adobe Illustrator 6.0. Measurements were made using digital camera attachments mounted to the same light microscopes and their respective imaging software. All measurements are in micrometres (μm) and provided as ranges with means in parenthesis. Where breadth follows length, measurements are separated by “×”. Common names for fish host species follow those used on FishBase (www.fishbase.se; accessed July 2025).

For each taxon, one specimen had tissue excised and used for molecular sequencing, with the remainder of the specimen prepared for morphological analysis, per Pleijel et al. (2008). Total genomic DNA was extracted from representative specimens of each taxon, either using the KAPA Express Extract Kit (KAPA Biosystems, Cape Town, South Africa) following the manufacturer's protocols, or the PCRBiosystems Rapid DNA Extraction kit (PCRBiosystems) following the manufacturer's protocols, but using only 10 μL of lysis buffer and 5 μL proteinase K-containing buffer, and the final extracted product diluted to 300 μL or 450 μL. As recommended by Blasco-Costa et al. (2016), ribosomal and mitochondrial molecular data was generated and used in the present study. PCR amplifications for partial reads of 28S rDNA were performed using the primers LSU5 (Littlewood, 1994), DigI2 and 1500R (Snyder and Tkach, 2001), for ITS2 rDNA using 3S (Morgan and Blair, 1995) and ITS2.2 (Cribb et al., 1998), and for cox1 mtDNA using Dice1F (Moszczynska et al., 2009) and DICE14R (Van Steenkiste et al., 2015). The three target marker regions were conventionally amplified with 25 μL reaction volumes using cycle schedules per Snyder and Tkach (2001) and Yong et al. (2016b) for rDNA, and Vermaak et al. (2023) for cox1 mtDNA. These amplifications yielded full reads for ITS2 rDNA (with partial flanking of 5.8S and 28S regions) and partial reads for 28S rDNA (including the D1–D3 domains) and cox1 mtDNA. Purification and Sanger sequencing were performed by Inqaba Biotechnical Industries (Pretoria, South Africa). Forward and reverse DNA strands were sequenced using the amplification primers plus the internal primers 300F (Littlewood et al., 2000) and ECD2 (Littlewood et al., 1997) for the 28S rDNA marker. Contiguous sequences were assembled in Geneious™ v10.2.2 (Kearse et al., 2012). Final sequences generated by this study were accessioned in GenBank.

Although ITS2 rDNA and cox1 mtDNA sequences were generated, these were ultimately not fully analysed, though were accessioned in GenBank in order to contribute to growing rDNA and mtDNA libraries for trematodes. Although the ITS2 rDNA and cox1 mtDNA regions are frequently used as “barcode” regions (Nolan and Cribb, 2005), our alignments show that, for aporocotylids, the ITS2 rDNA region is highly affected by indels. As for cox1 mtDNA, there are insufficient sequences across the group to facilitate effective comparison, and previous studies (Yong et al., 2021) have shown that the cox1 mtDNA region of aporocotylids may be significantly affected by non-stationarity, making it unreliable for phylogenetic inference. Molecular phylogenetic analyses therefore focused on the partial 28S rDNA dataset, with ITS2 rDNA and cox1 mtDNA data only used in direct sequence comparisons to facilitate species identification. Novel partial 28S rDNA sequences were analysed against a dataset of partial 28S sequences of aporocotylid taxa available on GenBank (see Table 1). Alignments were performed using MUSCLE 3.7 (Edgar, 2004) with ClustalW sequence weighting and UPGMA clustering for iterations 1 and 2. Molecular sequence data included in the partial 28S rDNA analyses used in this study, including those sequences accessed via GenBank and novel data generated by this study and uploaded to GenBank. Additional ITS2 rDNA and cox1 mtDNA sequences not used in full molecular phylogenetic analyses are listed in the summaries of each respective taxon.

Bayesian inference and Maximum Likelihood phylogenetic analyses were performed upon the partial 28S rDNAalignment to explore affinities of the novel material using MrBayes v3.2.2 (Ronquist et al., 2012) and RAxML v7.2.8 (Stamatakis, 2014), respectively, via the CIPRES portal (Miller et al., 2010). The closest approximation to the GTR + I+Γ model of nucleotide substitution evolution was assumed in analyses, based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) in jModelTest v2.1.10 (Darriba et al., 2012). The Bayesian inference analysis was run over 10,000,000 generations with two runs each containing four simultaneous Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains (nchains = 4) and every 1,000th tree saved (samplefreq = 1000). Bayesian inference analyses used the following parameters: nst = 6, rates = invgamma, ngammacat = 4, and the priors parameters of the combined dataset were set to ratepr = variable. Samples of substitution model parameters, and tree and branch lengths were summarised using the parameters ‘sump burnin = 3000’ and ‘sumt burnin = 3000’. The Maximum Likelihood analysis ran 100 bootstrap pseudoreplicates. The clade formed by species of Aporocotyle was forced as the root node in presented trees, as this is consistent with the clade being the most basal in the Aporocotylidae, per molecular phylogenies presented by Yong et al. (2021) and Warren and Bullard (2023).

Pairwise genetic distance matrices for ITS2 and 28S rDNA datasets were constructed in MEGA 7 (Kumara et al., 2018) using the “Compare Pairwise Distances” function with the parameters “Model/method = No. of differences”, “Substitutions to include = d: Transitions + Transversions”, “Variance estimation method = none” and “Gaps/missing data treatment = pairwise deletion”.

Results

Four species of sparid fishes, the Hottentot seabream, Pachymetopon blochii (Valenciennes), the bronze seabream, Pachymetopon grande Günther, the zebra seabream, Diplodus hottentotus (Smith) and the musselcracker seabream, Sparodon durbanensis (Castelnau) were found to be infected by aporocotylids. All four fish species are endemic tosouthern Africa and represent the first records of marine fishes infected by aporocotylids in this region. Molecular sequence data were successfully generated for all four aporocotylid taxa. The alignment of our new ITS2 and partial 28S rDNA sequence data with those of other aporocotylids on GenBank, respectively produced 567 and 1,300 characters for analysis. Due to the issues pertaining to ITS2 rDNA mentioned above, only the molecular phylogeny and distance matrices resulting from the partial 28S rDNA analyses are presented (Fig. 1). A combination of morphological and molecular sequence data indicate that worms from the two species of Pachymetopon represent new species. These show affinities to the exclusively sparid-infecting genus Skoulekia Alama-Bermejo, Montero, Raga & Holzer, 2011; however, consistent and crucial differences in the arrangement of the terminal genitalia mean that the proposal of a new genus to accommodate them is warranted. Such a classification was supported by the molecular phylogenetic analyses. Of the others, the one infecting S. durbanensis was morphologically and genetically consistent with Skoulekia meningialis Alama-Bermejo, Montero, Raga & Holzer, 2011, and the one infecting D. hottentotus with Cardicola mediterraneus Palacios-Abella, Montero, Merella, Mele, Raga & Repullés-Albelda, 2021; both of these species were hitherto known from the Spanish Mediterranean coast. We also report an instance of infection of blue seachub, Kyphosus cinerascens (Forsskål) (Kyphosidae), by a presently unidentifiable species of the exclusively kyphosid-infecting genus Deontacylix Linton, 1910.

Remarks: Bar some minor morphological differences, our single specimen conforms well to the description of Cardicola mediterraneus. Our specimen is immediately identifiable as C. mediterraneus by its extremely unequal posterior caeca, the longer arm of which is highly sinuous and extends to the mid-level of the ovary. Our specimen differs in having a sinuous (not straight) oesophagus, more deeply diverticulated caecal arms and distinctly smaller tegumental spines (2 μm versus 9–12 μm long). Our specimen is distinctly smaller than those described by Palacios-Abella et al. (2021), being 1.369 μm long (the quoted length range for C. mediterraneus was 1,818–2,641 μm) and thus has smaller dimensions to many organs e.g. oral sucker; however, we are wary of interpreting these as species-discriminating differences due to the fact that we only have a single specimen. For the same reason, we interpret the other observed morphological differences as intraspecific variation, possibly influenced by fixation or ontogeny. In the partial 28S rDNA analyses, our specimen differs from Spanish C. mediterraneus by 6 bp (Fig. 1). This amount of difference is comparable to that seen between other aporocotylid congeners, e.g. between species of Braya (as few as 2 bp) and species of Paradeontacylix McIntosh (1934) (as little as 6–7 bp); however, in light of previous studies’ reluctance to distinguish and propose new species from closely-related Mediterranean forms in the absence of sampling from the wider Atlantic and west African coast, we are satisfied that this level of genetic difference is consistent with intraspecific variation.

In facilitating identification of this and other aporocotylid species, we revalidate the generic concepts proposed by Yong et al. (2021) and Cutmore and Cribb (2022) that were rejected by Warren and Bullard (2023): Balistidicola Cutmore & Cribb, 2022; Chanicola Yong, Cribb & Cutmore, 2021; and Spirocaecum Yong, Cribb & Cutmore, 2021. All comprehensive molecular phylogenetic analyses that incorporate all available aporocotylid taxa after Yong et al. (2021), i.e. Cutmore and Cribb (2021, 2022) and Cutmore et al. (2023), support the recognition of these genera: they form well-supported monophyletic clades that, in combination with their diagnostic morphological characters and host ranges, strongly indicate discrete radiations in particular host families, findings that have once again been strengthened by our current analyses. The rampant polyphyly of the genus Cardicola is not particularly satisfying, but it is not in doubt. Resolution of this conundrum is a work in progress, and it may be that most species of Cardicola ultimately relate to multiple, yet-to-be-recognised genera. The family Aporocotylidae now contains 28 genera.

Remarks: Deontacylix ovalis Linton, 1910 was described by Linton (1910) from the Dry Tortugas, Florida, infecting Bermuda seachub, Kyphosus sectatrix (L.). Manter recollected this species from the same locality and host [as well as from yellow seachub, K. incisor (Cuvier)], and elaborated upon Linton's description (Manter, 1940, 1947). Since then, the species has been reported by Overstreet (1969) from K. sectatrix from Biscayne Bay, Florida, USA, and by Léon-Regagnon et al. (1997) from Cortez seachub, K. elegans (Peters) from Chamela Bay, Jalisco, Mexico. By contrast, D. kyphosi Yamaguti, 1970 has not been reported since its original description by Yamaguti (1970), from blue seachub, K. cinerascens (Forsskål) from Hawaii.

Our lone specimen is an immature, non-gravid worm, much smaller than has been previously reported for species of this genus; some features and dimensions should therefore be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, our specimen is unmistakably that of a species of Deontacylix, the only aporocotylid genus known to infect kyphosid fishes. Of the two recognised species of this genus, Deontacylix ovalis differs from D. kyphosi in the position of the ovary, that of the former being distinctly sinistral and that of the latter being medial. The ovary and oötype complex of our specimen was lost when a portion of tissue was excised for molecular sequencing. Nevertheless, the distribution of the uterine coils, the dextral-ward direction of the vitelline duct and lack of any discernible ovarian tissue in the medial portion of the hindbody mean we can comfortably infer that the ovary and oötype are sinistrally located, indicating that our specimen is not D. kyphosi.

There is no publicly accessible molecular sequence data for Deontacylix spp. with which our data could be compared. Recent phylogenies of Warren and Bullard (2023, 2024b) include a sequence for an undetermined species of Deontacylix, but either do not provide a GenBank number (Warren and Bullard, 2023) or provide an erroneous one (Warren et al., 2024b); a search on GenBank does not return any results. We provide novel molecular data in the form of partial 28S rDNA and cox1 mtDNA sequences for our South African specimen. The molecular phylogenetic position of our species of Deontacylix in our analyses matches that of Warren and Bullard (2023) and Warren et al. (2024b), being positioned basal to species of Pearsonellum Overstreet and Køie, 1986, Psettarium Goto and Ozaki, 1929, Skoulekia, Cruoricola Herbert, Shaharom-Harrison & Overstreet, 1994, Ankistromeces Nolan & Cribb, 2004 and Phthinomita Nolan & Cribb, 2006.

Paraskoulekia n. gen.

Diagnosis: Body lanceolate, broadest at midbody level. Oral sucker present, frail and poorly delineated from body. Tegumental spines minute, straight-tipped, arranged in dorsolateral rows that commence almost immediately post-oral sucker and wrap all way around body margins, evenly spaced throughout, rows increasing in length toward midbody. Prominent rosethorn-shaped or fused spines absent. Tegumental bristles or papillae not detected. Nervous system with anterior nerve commissure well-defined; nerve cords obscured by vitelline follicles for much of length post-anterior nerve commissure. Mouth ventrally subterminal. Pharynx absent. Oesophagus slightly sinuous, to around one-third total body length, glandularised in mid-hind portion. Swelling or vesicle at oesophageal-caecal junction absent. Intestinal caeca form X-shape. Anterior caeca much shorter than posterior caeca; posterior caeca mostly straight, with some gentle bends and diverticulation in some specimens, parallel to one another, equal to subequal in length. Single testis oblong, with margins irregularly lobed, mostly inter-caecal, with anterior extremity either meeting or distant from caecal bifurcation, with lateral margins exceeding bounds of posterior caeca and posterior margin either exceeding or not exceeding posterior caeca extremities, lateral margins often impossible to discern. Auxiliary seminal vesicle or secondary testis absent. Vas deferens arises medially from posterior margin of testis, does not expand to form external seminal vesicle. Cirrus-sac distinct, retort-shaped, posterior or lateral to posterior extremity of rest of male and female genitalia; proximal region contains well-defined spherical to subspherical seminal vesicle; distal region conical, reflexed from proximal region, accommodates short pars prostatica. Male genital pore dorso-sinistral, distant from lateral body margin, near posterior end of body. Ovary immediately post-testicular, lateral margins lobate, distinctly bilobed with medial space between lobes accommodating vas deferens and start of oviduct. Oviduct commences from posterior margin of ovary within inter-lobular space, passes posteriorly parallel to dextral body margin, expands to form distinct oviducal seminal receptacle, then redirects anteriorly to meet oötype almost immediately. Oötype well-defined, medial, with distinct Mehlis' glandularisation. Uterine coils arise and pass anteriorly from oötype to meet posterior ovarian margin, then redirect posteriorly to meet female genital pore. Descending distal portion of uterus weakly muscularised, does not form distinct metraterm. Female genital pore opens dorsosinistrally, anterior and dextral to male pore, anterior or posterior to level of oötype and uterus. Laurer's canal not observed. Eggs thin-shelled, ovate spheroid. Vitelline follicles diffuse and evenly distributed, not clumped or in bunches, not confined by nerve cords, extending anteriorly to anterior nerve commissure and posteriorly to level of ovary, follicles often obscuring testis margins. Excretory pore terminal. In heart of marine teleosts (Sparidae). Type-species: Paraskoulekia faltynkovae n. sp.

Etymology: The name Paraskoulekia uses the Greek prefix “para”, meaning “beside” to emphasise the similarity and relationship to species of the genus Skoulekia.

Differential diagnosis: Species of Paraskoulekia n. gen. can be distinguished from all other aporocotylid taxa through a combination of a broadly lanceolate body, X-shaped caeca, single mostly intercaecal testis, bilobed ovary and a retort cirrus-sac bearing a spherical seminal vesicle and positioned posterior to or lateral to the posterior extremity of the rest of the terminal genitalia. In lacking multiple or secondary testes, or auxiliary seminal vesicles, species of the new genus are distinguished from species of Adelomyllos Nolan & Cribb, 2004, Aporocotyle Odhner, 1924, Chaulioleptos Nolan & Cribb, 2005, Cruoricola, Littorellicola Bullard, 2010, Mugilitrema Warren & Bullard in Warren, Ksepka, Truong, Curran, Dutton & Bullard, 2024, Neoparacardicola Yamaguti, 1970, Paradeontacylix McIntosh, 1934, Pearsonellum, Phthinomita, Plethorchis Martin, 1975, Psettarium (in part), Pseudocardicola Parukhin, 1985 and Rhaphidotrema Yong & Cribb, 2011. The broadly lanceolate body shape distinguishes it from species of Ankistromeces and Cardallagium Yong, Cutmore, Jones, Gauthier & Cribb, 2018, as well as Chaulioleptos, Mugilitrema and Phthinomita, which have highly elongate and/or threadlike body forms.

Among those aporocotylid taxa which have broadly lanceolate body forms and single testes, species of Paraskoulekia n. gen. can be differentiated from the sole species of Allocardicola Yong, Cribb & Cutmore, 2021 in having anterior caeca and an intercaecal (not entirely post-caecal) testis; from species of Deontacylix and Metaplehniella Lebedev and Parukhin, 1972 in possessing an oral sucker and a testis and ovary that are not highly dendritically lobate; from the sole species of Primisanguis Bullard, Williams & Bunkley-Williams, 2012 by the fact that the uterine coils do not extend into the posterior extremity of the body and the lack of a distinct male genital protuberance; from species of Braya Nolan & Cribb, 2006) by the ovary not being distinctly asymmetrically positioned and the male genital pore not being posteriorly terminal or nearly so; from species of Holocentricola Cutmore & Cribb, 2021 in lacking an elongate ejaculatory duct meeting a marginal male genital pore; and from species of Spirocaecum in not having spiralling posterior caeca that exceed the length of the testis or uterine coils that extend posterior to the cirrus-sac. It can be distinguished from those species of Psettarium that do not have multiple or secondary testes by the ovary not being distinctly asymmetrically-positioned and the cirrus-sac not being large and elongate-reniform. The anterior caeca not being distinctly elongate and a testis entirely bound by the intercaecal region differentiates species of our new genus from Chanicola; they can also be partly differentiated from this genus by the absence of robust, recurved tegumental spines. Paraskoulekia n. gen. lacks the large retort reniform cirrus-sac of species of Balistidicola, its uterine coils are distinctly more convoluted and the oötype is consistently medial, parallel (not posterior) to and deflexed from the oviducal seminal receptacle. The lack of an elongate or elongate-reniform cirrus-sac and possession of a distinctly spherical seminal vesicle also distinguishes species of Paraskoulekia n. gen. from species of Cardicola and Elaphrobates Bullard & Overstreet, 2003. Species of Paraskoulekia n. gen. have clear morphological affinities to those of Skoulekia, possessing small oral suckers, long and diverticulated posterior caeca, lobular and mostly intercaecal testis and a distinctly spherical seminal vesicle; however, they differ in having a medial (i.e. not asymmetrically-positioned) ovary, the uterine coils never passing lateral or anterior to the ovary and the cirrus-sac being well posterior to, or at least lateral to the posterior extremity of, the rest of the genitalia.

In all molecular phylogenetic analyses, species of Paraskoulekia formed a clade sister to one formed by species of Skoulekia (Fig. 1). These analyses also clearly distinguish the species of the new genus from those of 23 other sequenced aporocotylid genera. Species of Paraskoulekia n. gen. differed from those of Skoulekia by a minimum of 37 bp in the partial 28S rDNA dataset and 87 bp in the ITS2 rDNA dataset, a level of difference comparable to those seen between other closely-related aporocotylid genera, e.g. Ankistromeces and Phthinomita. In our opinion, the proposal of Paraskoulekia n. gen. as a distinct genus from Skoulekia is therefore justified.

We also noted a discontinuous distribution of the spine rows along the dextral body margin in all three of our specimens, with rows being absent from the level of the ovary to the posterior extremity; this was not observed in either report from Spain. The extent of the testis in our specimens varied somewhat in terms of distance from the caecal bifurcation; however, such variation was documented for S. meningialis by Palacios-Abella et al. (2017), who examined the paratype series in addition to their fresh collections and found the testis variously not reaching, meeting or exceeding the caecal bifurcation anteriorly. Newly generated molecular sequence data obtained from our specimens differed from those from the Spanish Mediterranean coast by 3 bp in the ITS2 rDNA region and 1 bp in the 28S rDNA region. Skoulekia meningialis was previously reported from two sparid species from the Spanish Mediterranean coast, the two-banded seabream Diplodus vulgaris (Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire) and the sheephead bream Diplodus puntazzo (Walbaum). Discussion

Studies of fish blood flukes in Africa have been infrequent and primarily focused on those species which infect freshwater fishes. Since the restructuring of the group by Warren and Bullard (2023), all but two reports of fish blood flukes from the continent have been from freshwater and of species of Sanguinicolidae. The first report of fish blood flukes of any kind from Africa were of sanguinicolids made by Woodland (1923), who observed, but did not describe, a species of Sanguinicola Plehn, 1905 from bubu catfish, Auchenoglanis occidentalis (Valenciennes) (Siluriformes: Auchenoglanididae) and wahrindi catfish, Synodontis schall (Bloch & Schneider) (Siluriformes: Mochokidae) from the Nile River at Khartoum, Sudan. Odhner (1924) examined this material, refined Woodland's observations and described it as Sanguinicola chalmersi Odhner, 1924. Imam et al. (1984) reported the same species from Cairo and Giza fish markets, further downstream on the Nile, and described Sanguinicola clarias Imam, Marzouk, Hassan & Itman, 1984 from North African walking catfish, Clarias gariepinus (Burchell) (as Clarias lazera) (Siluriformes: Clariidae) from the same localities. Warren and Bullard (2023) questioned the validity of the latter species, reclassifying it as Nomasanguinicola clarias (Imam, Marzouk, Hassan & Itman, 1984) while also designating it a species inquirendum. Outa and Avenant-Oldewage (2024) were the first to identify the presence of sanguinicolids in southern Africa, characterising four morpho-genotypes of intermediate stages consistent with this family infecting burnupiid snails from the Vaal River, South Africa. Warren et al. (2024a) subsequently reported adults of a species of Nomasanguinicola from C. gariepinus from the Kavango River, Namibia. The species was ascribed to Nomasanguinicola dentata (Paperna, 1964) and bears a close resemblance to it while sharing the same fish host, but molecular sequence data are lacking from the species' type-locality (Israel) and the type- and only known specimen is apparently lost (Truong and Bullard, 2013). Recollection and molecular characterisation of this species from the type-locality is desirable. The only marine blood flukes previously recorded from Africa are Hyperandrotrema cetorhini Maillard & Ktari, 1978 (Selachohemecidae), described from the basking shark, Cetorhinus maximus (Gunnerus) (Lamniformes: Cetorhinidae) from off Tunisia (Maillard and Ktari, 1978), and Skoulekia bogaraveo Palacios-Abella, Raga, Mele & Montero, 2018 (Aporocotylidae) from blackspot seabream, Pagellus bogaraveo (Brünnich) (Sparidae) sampled off Bou Zedjar, Algeria (Lablack et al., 2022). Given the geographic proximity (e.g. the southernmost point of Spain is only 13 km from Morocco) and the fact that many host species are found throughout the Mediterranean Sea, it is highly likely that most aporocotylid species recorded from Mediterranean Europe also occur off north Africa.

Of the five taxa reported in this paper, four infected fishes of the family Sparidae. This fish family is host to two major radiations of aporocotylids, one of species of Cardicola and one of species of Skoulekia + Paraskoulekia n. gen. The genus Skoulekia comprises just three species and, prior to this paper, all reports were restricted to the western Mediterranean. The first species to be described, Skoulekia meningialis, was reported infecting the ectomeningeal blood vessels of the optic lobes of the brain of Diplodus vulgaris obtained from off Valencia on the Spanish Mediterranean coast (Alama-Bermejo et al., 2011). This species was subsequently reported from sharpsnout seabream, Diplodus puntazzo by Palacios-Abella et al. (2017), also from blood vessels of the brain. Palacios-Abella et al. (2017) also described a second Skoulekia species, Skoulekia erythrini Palacios-Abella, Georgieva, Mele, Raga, Isbert, Kostadinova & Montero, 2017 from common pandora, Pagellus erythrinus (L.), also from the Spanish Mediterranean. Unlike S. meningialis, this species was reported infecting the heart, gills and cephalic kidney. The third Skoulekia species, S. bogaraveo, was described by Palacios-Abella et al. (2018) from Pagellus bogaraveo off Santa Pola, Spain, infecting the brain blood vessels. This species was subsequently recorded from P. bogaraveo from Bou Zedjar, Algeria, also in the western Mediterranean, by Lablack et al. (2022), who also recorded a single infection by an unknown Skoulekia species from the gills of red porgy, Pagrus pagrus (L.) (Sparidae) obtained from the same locality. All three species share a highly conserved morphology, differing mostly in fine details of body and organ length/width proportions (many of which overlap) and tegumental spine form. They are readily differentiated on the basis of molecular sequencing, with existing ITS2 and partial 28S rDNA data validating the concept of separate species. Species of our new genus, Paraskoulekia n. gen., show obvious morphological affinities to species of Skoulekia and form a clade sister to them. Species of this genus, so far, seem restricted to sparids of the genus Pachymetopon Günther, all three of which are endemic to southern Africa. Both species have been found only in the hearts of their hosts; in the case of P. umbungugazi n. sp. and in most cases for P. faltynkovae n. sp., infections were entirely localised in the atrium, though a minority of specimens of the latter were also recovered from the ventricle. The combination of morphological differences, molecular sequence distance and host- and infection site-specificity, are indicative of a discrete radiation likely endemic to southern Africa and, in our opinion, validate the recognition of the new genus.

The genus Cardicola was proposed by Short (1953) to accommodate two aporocotylid species, one, the sparid-infecting Cardicola cardiocolus (Manter, 1947) reassigned from the genus Psettarium and designated as the type-species; the other, C. laruei Short, 1953, infecting sciaenid fishes. Cardicola cardiocolus was originally described by Manter (1947) (as Psettarium cardiocolum) from the jolthead porgy, Calamus bajonado (Bloch & Schneider), from off the Dry Tortugas, Florida, USA. Warren et al. (2021) recollected the species from C. bajonado from off Boca Grande, western Florida. A record in a Bachelor's thesis (Villarreal-Lizárraga, 1995) of this species infecting the bullseye pufferfish Sphoeroides annulatus (Jenyns) (Tetraodontidae) from off La Paz, Pacific coast of Mexico, is almost certainly erroneous. With the present recognition of narrowly diagnosed genera related to Cardicola sensu lato, the genus includes 23 species infecting 13 fish families and one, the enigmatic Cardicola dhangali Hutson, Vaughan & Blair, 2019, infecting the dugong (Mammalia: Sirenidae) (Hutson et al., 2019). The 14 of these 23 species that have been sequenced do not form a monophyletic clade; four of the 23 species infect sparids. In addition to C. cardiocolus, Cardicola langeli Bullard, 2013 is also found in the Gulf of Mexico, having been described from off the coast of Mississippi, USA, infecting the sheepshead, Archosargus probatocephalus (Walbaum) (Bullard, 2013). Cardicola auratus Holzer, Montero, Repullés, Sitjà-Bobadilla, Alvarez-Pellitero, Zarza & Raga, 2008, and C. mediterraneus both infect the gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata L., off the Spanish and Italian Mediterranean coast (Holzer et al., 2008; Palacios-Abella et al., 2021). Molecular sequence data exist for all sparid-infecting species except C. cardiocolus. Analyses show the four sparid-infecting species form a clade basal to, and to the exclusion of, all other species of Cardicola; we predict that C. cardiocolus also forms part of this clade. If so, further future subdivision of Cardicola may lead to the genus being limited to this sparid-infecting radiation.

The apparently widespread distribution of our putative species of Deontacylix is noteworthy. Our specimen possesses a dextrally-positioned ovary, which is consistent with D. ovalis. However, given we are unable to molecularly verify D. ovalis from its type-locality in the Gulf of Mexico, nor compare with sequences which are not publicly accessible, we are unwilling to conclusively attribute our taxon to this species. Nevertheless, specimens of Deontacylix with sinistrally-positioned ovaries are now known circumglobally, from the southwest Indian Ocean (South Africa), the east Pacific (west Mexico) and the central Atlantic (Gulf of Mexico). Specimens have also been recovered from eastern Australia, but have not been studied (Cribb, pers. comm.). Should D. ovalis ultimately prove to have a circumglobal distribution, the only aporocotylid species with a comparable range would be Cardicola opisthorchis Ogawa, Ishimaru, Shirakashi, Takami & Grabner, 2011, which has been recorded infecting bluefin tunas (Thunnus spp.) from off southern Australia, Japan, the Pacific coast of Mexico and in the Mediterranean Sea off Spain and Italy (Aiken et al., 2007; Ogawa et al., 2011; Palacios-Abella et al., 2015). The other bluefin tuna-infecting Cardicola species have similarly large ranges: Cardicola forsteri Cribb, Daintith & Munday, 2000 has also been reported from off southern Australia, Japan and in the Mediterranean, as well as the USA Mid-Atlantic coast (Aiken et al., 2007; Bullard et al., 2004; Palacios-Abella et al., 2015; Shirakashi et al., 2016), and C. orientalis Ogawa, Tanaka, Sugihara & Takami, 2010 has been found off southern Australia, Japan and in the Mediterranean Sea (Ogawa et al., 2010; Palacios-Abella et al., 2015; Shirakashi et al., 2013). The type-host of D. ovalis, Kyphosus sectatrix, and the three other species reported as hosts, K. bigibbus, K. cinerascens and K. vaigiensis, also have circumglobal distributions and great long-distance dispersal ability, likely through a combination of rafting across open oceans in association with flotsam, as well as long-shore and trans-island movements while inshore (Knudsen and Clements, 2016; Sakihara et al., 2014). Their movements do not seem to be constrained by traditional biogeographic barriers such as the East Pacific Oceanic Barrier and the Benguela Barrier (Knudsen and Clements, 2016), although some localisation has also been observed (Eristhee and Oxenford, 2001). It is distinctly possible that D. ovalis, and indeed the second species of Deontacylix, D. kyphosi, genuinely have circumglobal distributions. Expanded and rigorous examination of these species using molecular sequencing, including of other gene regions like cox1 mtDNA and incorporating the type-localities of both species, is highly desirable.

The discoveries of Skoulekia meningialis and Cardicola mediterraneus from off South Africa are the latest evidence of the close biogeographic relationship between the fish parasite fauna of the Mediterranean Sea and southern Africa. This relationship has been apparent since the work of Bray in the 1980s, who identified several trematodes from South Africa as species known from the Mediterranean, e.g. Elstia stossichianum (Monticelli, 1892) (Mesometridae), Holorchis pycnoporus Stossich, 1901 (Aephnidiogenidae), Macvicaria obovata (Molin, 1859) (Opecoelidae) and Proctoeces maculatus (Looss, 1901) (Fellodistomidae) (Bray, 1984, 1985, 1987). It might be expected that taxa separated by an entire continent would show substantial differentiation, particularly if they or their hosts are less vagile (the example above of Deontacylix and Kyphosus spp. being the exception rather than the rule). However, the use of molecular sequencing techniques to test and quantify these differences has shown there to be a surprising amount of similarity. Vermaak et al. (2023) demonstrated that Proctoeces maculatus found off South Africa was indeed conspecific with those from the Mediterranean, differing by no more than 6 bp in the partial 28S rDNA region, the same amount of genetic difference between C. mediterraneus from South Africa and the Mediterranean. The affinities of the trematode fauna are mirrored by those of their hosts: southern Africa and the Mediterranean have a significant amount of overlap in ichthyofauna, with several species shared between both regions [e.g. the salema, Sarpa salpa (L.) (Sparidae) and the leerfish, Lichia amia (L.) (Carangidae)] and others that are closely related, e.g. the Cape white seabream, Diplodus capensis (Smith) (Sparidae) which was, until recently, considered conspecific with the Mediterranean Diplodus sargus (L.) (Heemstra and Heemstra, 2004). The different biogeographical factors which underpin these relationships (host vagility, oceanic current barriers, disjunctness in populations etc) create a highly complex dynamic which will require sustained and increased effort to disentangle. As helminthological interest in the region increases, the nature of more of these relationships will surely be elucidated.
