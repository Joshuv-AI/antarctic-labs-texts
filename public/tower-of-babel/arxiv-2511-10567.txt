Gravitational wave polarization modes and the kinematical tensors in general relativity and beyond
Cynthia Maldonado, Francisco Nettel, Pedro A. Sánchez

Abstract
Interrelationships between the expansion, the shear, and the vorticity—the kinematical tensors—on the one hand, and the polarization modes of gravitational waves on the other hand, are studied by considering freely falling test particles. After studying exact relations, we consider slowly moving particles under the influence of a weak gravitational field. Linearized plane waves of metric theories of gravity representative of those determined by a general second order Lagrangian, including General Relativity, are shown to exemplify the following interconnections: between the transverse components of the shear and the transverse tensor polarization mode; between the expansion, and both the transverse scalar and the longitudinal polarization modes; and between the longitudinal-transverse components of the shear and the vorticity, and the vector polarization mode.



I
Introduction

At the heart of General Relativity (GR) and other metric theories of gravity, lies Einstein’s equivalence principle (cf., e.g., chapter 2 of
Will 2018
). It establishes an equivalence between the outcomes of local experiments, thus making nonlocal experiments essential to probe the gravitational field. Simple but physically rich nonlocal experiments can be conceived with a cloud of freely falling test particles, for instance by studying the kinematics and dynamics of their relative separation. Within a metric theory of gravity, neighboring particles with separation vector
\xi
are predicted to have a relative velocity

\frac{D\xi^{a}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{\theta}{3}\xi^{a}+{\sigma_{b}}^{a}\xi^{b}+{\omega_{b}}^{a}\xi^{b},

(1)

where
\tau
is the proper time of the particles; and
\theta
,
\sigma
, and
\omega
are their expansion, shear, and vorticity, respectively. Moreover, these neighboring particles are predicted to experience a relative, or tidal, acceleration given by the equation of geodesic deviation

\frac{D^{2}\xi^{a}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}={K_{b}}^{a}\xi^{b},

(2)

written here in terms of the tidal tensor
K
. Then, measurements of the relative velocity between the particles reveals information about the kinematical tensors, namely
\theta
,
\sigma
, and
\omega
, from which some aspects about the gravitational field could be inferred. Similarly, measuring the relative acceleration reveals information about the part of the Riemann tensor encoded in the tidal tensor—its so-called electric part.

Some gravitational fields have a distinctive tidal tensor, as the following examples arising in GR illustrate (cf., e.g.,
Szekeres 1965
). A cloud of particles falling radially in a Schwarzschild spacetime, experiences a tidal acceleration that tends to elongate it in the radial direction and compress in the transverse directions. On the other hand, a cluster of galaxies in a spatially homogeneous and isotropic universe, tends to expand accelerating equally in all directions. Contrastingly, a cloud of test particles is affected by a plane gravitational wave, such that only particles separated in directions transverse to the wave spatial propagation direction accelerate while preserving the transverse area of the cloud.

For a general gravitational field, the six independent components of the tidal tensor have different effects on a cloud of test particles. This is particularly relevant to detect a gravitational wave. Given a wave with definite propagation direction, the tidal tensor is uniquely decomposed into its purely longitudinal, mixed longitudinal-transverse, and purely transverse parts, the latter being further decomposed into its tensor and scalar parts. These constitute the polarization modes of the wave
Eardley et al. 1973
;
Will 2018
. GR predicts that a vacuum propagating wave has only the two polarization modes of the transverse tensor part, but many other metric theories of gravity allow more polarization modes (cf. §11.4.2 of
Will 2018
, and
Dong et al. 2024
).

Likewise, the study of the relative velocity of neighboring particles has its own relevance for some types of gravitational fields, standing out cosmological models for which it has been a key element in their construction (cf. §5.1 of
Ellis 1971
;
*ellis2009republication
and §9.1 of
Ellis et al. 2012
). However, it is not so common in the description of gravitational waves, with notable exceptions
Herrera and Hernández Pastora 2000
;
Bini and de Felice 2000
;
Seraj and Oblak 2022
. Intuition leads one to expect that the kinematical tensors of a cloud of test particles subject to the effects of a gravitational wave are interrelated with the polarization modes of the wave. Concretely, one expects (at least) the following interrelationships: between the transverse components of the shear and the transverse tensor polarization modes; between the longitudinal component of the shear and the longitudinal polarization mode; and between the expansion and the transverse scalar polarization mode. In the present work we analyze this interrelationship for gravitational waves with a definite propagation direction, within the context of GR and other metric theories of gravity, both in the strong and in the weak field regime.

This paper is organized as follows. In §
II
we review the definitions and the physical meaning of the kinematical tensors, the tidal tensor, and the polarization modes for a cloud of freely falling test particles. Additionally, we write the polarization modes in terms of the kinematical tensors on the one hand, and in terms of the irreducible parts of the Riemann tensor on the other hand. Then, in §
III
we specialize the analysis to slowly moving particles in a weak gravitational field, obtaining expressions for both the kinematical tensors and the polarization modes at the considered order. Next, in §
IV
we evaluate the obtained expressions on linearized gravitational plane waves of a selection of metric theories representative of those determined by a Lagrangian that is a general function of the Riemann tensor, and compare them with GR. In appendix
A
we write exact expressions for the polarization modes in terms of the Newman-Penrose curvature scalars.

General gravitational field

(\mathcal{M}^{4},g)

Polarization modes

Now, consider a gravitational wave to which one can associate a definite spacetime propagation direction, for example vacuum GR plane waves, either linearized or exact (cf. §35.3 and §35.9 of
Misner et al. 1973
). Then, its spatial propagation direction, as seen in the instantaneous rest frame of the test particles, is given by a unit vector
n
orthogonal to
u
, namely

\displaystyle n_{a}n^{a}=1,

\displaystyle n_{a}u^{a}=0.

(11)

With it, and with the aid of its orthogonal projector

p_{ab}\coloneqq g_{ab}+u_{a}u_{b}-n_{a}n_{b},

(12)

one can decompose the tidal tensor into the following parts

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{l}}}\coloneqq K_{ab}n^{a}n^{b},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{s}}}\coloneqq p^{ab}K_{ab},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}\coloneqq K_{bc}{p_{a}}^{b}n^{c},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}\coloneqq\left({p_{a}}^{c}{p_{b}}^{d}-\frac{1}{2}p_{ab}p^{cd}\right)K_{cd},

(13)

which we shall call respectively, the longitudinal mode, the transverse scalar mode
2
2

2

Also called breathing mode.
, the vector mode, and the transverse tensor mode. From their definition, the vector and the transverse tensor modes satisfy
{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}u^{a}={K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}n^{a}=0
,
{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}u^{b}={K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}n^{b}=0
, and
g^{ab}{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}=0
; thus they have two independent components each. The longitudinal and transverse scalar modes comprise one polarization mode each; while the vector mode and transverse tensor mode comprise two polarization modes each, one for each independent component. In terms of the polarization modes, the tidal tensor gives

K_{ab}=\frac{{K^{\mathrm{s}}}}{2}p_{ab}+{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}+2{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{(a}n_{b)}+{K^{\mathrm{l}}}n_{a}n_{b}.

(14)

The physical effects of the polarization modes stem from the foregoing equation and from (
2
). A small cloud of test particles initially at rest, tends to: elongate longitudinally due to
{K^{\mathrm{l}}}
; expand or compress transversally due to a positive or negative
{K^{\mathrm{s}}}
, respectively; elongate along a longitudinal-transverse direction due to
{K^{\mathrm{v}}}
; and elongate along a transverse direction due to
{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}
. The modes defined here correspond with those defined in the seminal work
Eardley et al. 1973
(cf. their §V A) for linearized plane waves with null propagation as follows:

{K^{\mathrm{l}}}
corresponds to their
p_{1}

{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{I}
correspond to their

and

; two independent components
{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{IJ}
correspond to their
p_{4}
and
p_{5}
; and
{K^{\mathrm{s}}}
corresponds to their
p_{6}
.

Not all the parts of the Riemann tensor contribute equally to each of the polarization modes. In fact, the irreducible decomposition of the Riemann tensor (cf., e.g., §3.5 of
Stephani et al. 2003
) allows one to write

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{l}}}=\frac{R}{12}+\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{ab}n^{a}n^{b}-S_{ab}u^{a}u^{b}\right)-C_{abcd}n^{a}u^{b}n^{c}u^{d},

(15a)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{R}{6}-\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{ab}n^{a}n^{b}+S_{ab}u^{a}u^{b}\right)+C_{abcd}n^{a}u^{b}n^{c}u^{d},

(15b)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}=\frac{1}{2}S_{bc}{p_{a}}^{b}n^{c}-C_{bcde}{p_{a}}^{b}u^{c}n^{d}u^{e},

(15c)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}S_{cd}{p_{a}}^{c}{p_{b}}^{d}+\frac{1}{4}\left(S_{cd}n^{c}n^{d}-S_{cd}u^{c}u^{d}\right)p_{ab}-C_{cdef}{p_{a}}^{c}u^{d}{p_{b}}^{e}u^{f}-\frac{1}{2}C_{cdef}u^{c}n^{d}u^{e}n^{f}p_{ab},

(15d)

where
C_{abcd}
is the Weyl tensor,
S_{ab}=R_{ab}-(R/4)g_{ab}
is the traceless Ricci tensor, and
R
is the scalar curvature. It stands out that the scalar curvature does not contribute to the vector and transverse tensor modes.

Similarly, not all kinematical tensors contribute equally to the polarization modes. From the expression for the tidal tensor in terms of the kinematical tensors (
10
), it follows that

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{l}}}=\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{3}\right)+n^{a}n^{b}\left(\frac{2\theta}{3}\sigma_{ab}+\frac{D\sigma_{ab}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\sigma_{ac}{\sigma_{b}}^{c}-\omega_{ac}{\omega_{b}}^{c}\right),

(16a)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{2}{3}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\frac{\theta^{2}}{3}\right)+p^{ab}\left(\frac{2\theta}{3}\sigma_{ab}+\frac{D\sigma_{ab}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\sigma_{ac}{\sigma_{b}}^{c}-\omega_{ac}{\omega_{b}}^{c}\right),

(16b)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}={p_{a}}^{b}n^{c}\left(\frac{2\theta}{3}\sigma_{bc}+\frac{D\sigma_{bc}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\sigma_{bd}{\sigma_{c}}^{d}-\omega_{bd}{\omega_{c}}^{d}\right),

(16c)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}=\left({p_{a}}^{c}{p_{b}}^{d}-\frac{1}{2}p_{ab}p^{cd}\right)\left(\frac{2\theta}{3}\sigma_{cd}+\frac{D\sigma_{cd}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\sigma_{ce}{\sigma_{d}}^{e}-\omega_{ce}{\omega_{d}}^{e}\right).

(16d)

To gain some insight on the foregoing expressions, it is instructive to analyze the contributions of each kinematical tensor when the others vanish. For example, for a cloud with vanishing shear and vorticity, only the longitudinal and transverse scalar modes survive, determined completely by the combination
\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}\tau+\theta^{2}/3
; this reveals a contribution from the expansion to the transverse scalar mode, as expected, but also to the longitudinal mode.
On the other hand, for a cloud with vanishing expansion and vorticity, all the modes are nonvanishing and are determined by the following combination of the shear
D\sigma_{ab}/\mathrm{d}\tau+\sigma_{ac}{\sigma_{b}}^{c}
; this shows a contribution from some of its transverse components to the transverse tensor mode and a contribution from its longitudinal component to the longitudinal mode, as anticipated, but also shows a contribution from some of its transverse components to the scalar mode and from its longitudinal-transverse components to the vector mode. Lastly, a rigid cloud, namely one with vanishing expansion and shear, exhibits a similar behavior to that of the preceding example, but with all the modes determined by
\omega_{ac}{\omega_{b}}^{c}
.

III
Weak gravitational waves

1
Approximation order

Far away from a radiating system, the gravitational field is expected to be weak. Then, to certain precision, the metric can be approximated as

\displaystyle g_{ab}=\eta_{ab}+h_{ab},

\displaystyle|h_{ab}|\ll 1,

(17)

with respect to an almost inertial coordinate system
(x^{a})=(t,x^{i})
, say the laboratory frame. It is worth recalling that this coordinate system is determined up to Lorentz transformations and infinitesimal coordinate transformations. From now on, we shall consider expressions linear in the perturbation
h_{ab}
, and indices will be understood to be raised and lowered with the Minkowski metric. Linearized expressions for the purely geometric objects can be obtained, and are readily found in the literature (cf., e.g., §18.1 of
Misner et al. 1973
). In particular, the linearized Riemann tensor yields

R_{abcd}=-\partial_{a}\partial_{[c}h_{d]b}+\partial_{b}\partial_{[c}h_{d]a},

(18)

which is invariant upon infinitesimal coordinate transformations. Within linearized theory, the 4-velocity of a cloud of test particles is conveniently written in terms of their coordinate velocity

v^{i}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}x^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(19)

If the particles are freely falling, one expects them to be moving slowly,
|v^{i}|^{2}\ll 1
, in accordance with the weak field approximation. Concretely we shall consider that

\displaystyle|v^{i}|^{3}\ll|h_{ab}|,

\displaystyle|h_{ab}v^{i}|\ll|h_{ab}|,

(20)

thus keeping terms up to order
O(|v^{i}|^{2})
and
O(h_{ab})
.

Then, from the normalization of
u
one obtains

u=\left(1+\frac{1}{2}v_{k}v^{k}+\frac{1}{2}h_{tt}\right)\partial_{t}+v^{i}\partial_{i},

(21)

and, consequently, its orthogonal projector (
5

\displaystyle P=v_{k}v^{k}\mathrm{d}t\otimes\mathrm{d}t-2v_{j}\mathrm{d}t\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\mathrm{d}x^{j}+\left(\eta_{ij}+h_{ij}+v_{i}v_{j}\right)\mathrm{d}x^{i}\otimes\mathrm{d}x^{j},

(22)

where
\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}
was used to denote the symmetrized tensor product. Moreover, if the test particles fall freely, the geodesic equation implies

\frac{\mathrm{d}v^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\dot{h}_{ti}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}h_{tt},

(23)

where
\dot{h}_{ab}=\partial_{t}h_{ab}
. Incidentally, the foregoing expression reduces to that for the Newtonian limit if the gravitational field is additionally pseudo-stationary, namely if
\dot{h}_{ab}

Scalar curvature dependent Lagrangian

theories subject to the following restrictions: that admits Minkowski as a solution in the absence of matter, which via the trace of the field equations requires
f(0)=0
(cf., e.g., §II A of
Sotiriou and Faraoni 2010
); that is analytic at
R=0
; and that satisfies
f^{\prime}(0)/f^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
. The first restriction is compulsory if one is to consider weak gravitational waves propagating on Minkowski spacetime, while the last restriction is necessary for causal wave propagation [cf. (
41
)–(
42
) below] and to include GR as a special case.

From the assumed analyticity of
f(R)
, it follows that only terms up to
O(R^{2})
in the action will contribute to the linearized field equations. Then, two different
f(R)
will yield the same linearized field equations if their corresponding values of
f^{\prime}(0)
and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
coincide (cf., e.g., §II of
Şentürk et al. 2012
). In particular, those for which
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
vanishes yield the same vacuum linearized equations as those of GR, and thus to the gravitational waves of §
1
. Thus, let us now turn our attention to the nonvanishing
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
case.

For a general linearized field, it is not possible to impose the very same gauge as that of GR, in particular the conditions in (
34
).
However, one can impose a gauge such that the perturbation is decomposed as (cf. §III and §IV of
Berry and Gair 2011
)

\displaystyle h_{ab}=\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}+2\varphi\eta_{ab},

\displaystyle\varphi=-\frac{R}{6\mu^{2}},

\displaystyle\mu\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{f^{\prime}(0)}{3f^{\prime\prime}(0)}},

(41)

with
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
subject to the transverse-traceless conditions (
32
) and (
34
), yielding the linearized field equations

\displaystyle\Box_{\eta}\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}=0,

\displaystyle\Box_{\eta}\varphi=\mu^{2}\varphi.

(42)

One sees that the metric perturbation consists of a massless spin-2 field
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
, which is the same as that of GR since it is subject to identical equations, and a massive spin-0 field
\varphi
.

Consider monochromatic plane wave solutions for
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
and
\varphi
. Assuming both of them have the same wave propagation direction, allows one to write
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
exactly as the GR
wave, namely

\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}=h_{+}e^{+}_{ab}+h_{\times}e^{\times}_{ab},

(43)

supplemented with (
36
) and (
37
), and

\displaystyle\varphi=\real\left(\alpha\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\varpi t-qz)}\right),

\displaystyle\varpi^{2}-q^{2}=\mu^{2},

(44)

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\varpi,q\in\mathbb{R}
, constants.

For this
f(R)
gravitational wave, stationary particles in this “generalized” TT frame do not fall freely, since the right hand side of (
23
) does not vanish, in contrast with GR. The corresponding kinematical tensors give

\displaystyle\theta=\theta^{\mathrm{M}}+3\dot{\varphi},

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma^{\mathrm{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\tilde{h}}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}\mathrm{d}x^{I}\otimes\mathrm{d}x^{J},

\displaystyle\omega=\omega^{\mathrm{M}},

(45)

while the polarization modes give

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{l}}}=-\mu^{2}\varphi,

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{s}}}=2\ddot{\varphi},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\ddot{\tilde{h}}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ},

(46)

and
{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}=0
. In addition to the contribution from
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}
, which certainly is the same as that of GR, one observes that
\varphi
contributes to the expansion, and to the longitudinal and transverse scalar modes.

3
Einstein-Bach gravity

Finally, let us analyze Einstein-Bach gravity
4
4

4

Also called Einstein-Weyl gravity.
, determined by the action

S_{\gamma}[g_{ab}]=\frac{1}{16\pi G}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left(R-\gamma C_{abcd}C^{abcd}\right).

(47)

The contribution of the Weyl squared term to the field equations is the Bach tensor
Bach 1921
, that is traceless. Thus, the trace of the vacuum field equations yield
R=0
in general. This theory is in fact a particular class of the theories whose Lagrangians are at most quadratic in the Riemann tensor

\gamma>0
for causal wave propagation [cf. (

For a general linearized field,

allows one to impose a gauge such that

h_{ab}=\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}+\psi_{ab},

(48)

with
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
subject to the transverse-traceless conditions (

) and (

), and
\psi_{ab}
traceless and subject to the de Donder condition (
32

Bueno et al. 2017
, or §IV of
Alves et al. 2025
)

\displaystyle\Box_{\eta}\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}=0,

\displaystyle\Box_{\eta}\psi_{ab}=m^{2}\psi_{ab},

\displaystyle m\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}.

(49)

The metric perturbation now consists of a massless spin-2 field
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
, which is also the same as that of GR, and a massive spin-2 field
\psi_{ab}
(cf. §I of
Fierz and Pauli 1939
). The latter has five independent components in general. For other commonly used gauge, see
Teyssandier 1989
and §III of
Alves et al. 2025
.

Consider monochromatic plane wave solutions for
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
and
\psi_{ab}
. Assuming both of them have the same wave propagation direction, allows one to write again
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ab}
exactly as the GR wave [cf. (

) and (
37

\displaystyle\psi_{+}=\real\left(B_{+}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\varpi t-qz)}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{\times}=\real\left(B_{\times}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\varpi t-qz)}\right),

\displaystyle\psi_{zj}=\real\left(B_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\varpi t-qz)}\right),

\displaystyle\varpi^{2}-q^{2}=m^{2},

(50)

for
B_{+},B_{\times},B_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\varpi,q\in\mathbb{R}
, constants, and

\displaystyle\psi_{tt}=\left(\frac{q}{\varpi}\right)^{2}\psi_{zz},

\displaystyle\psi_{tj}=-\frac{q}{\varpi}\psi_{zj},

\displaystyle\psi_{IJ}=\psi^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{m}{\varpi}\right)^{2}\psi_{zz}\eta_{IJ},

\displaystyle\psi^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}=\psi_{+}e^{+}_{IJ}+\psi_{\times}e^{\times}_{IJ}.

(51)

As with the
f(R)
case, this gravitational wave does not allow stationary particles in this “generalized” TT frame to fall freely [cf. (
23
)]. The kinematical tensors give

\displaystyle\theta=\theta^{\mathrm{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{q}{\varpi}\right)^{2}\dot{\psi}_{zz},

(52)

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma^{\mathrm{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{\tilde{h}}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}+\dot{\psi}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}\right)\mathrm{d}x^{I}\otimes\mathrm{d}x^{J}+\frac{m^{2}+2\varpi^{2}}{12\varpi^{2}}\dot{\psi}_{zz}\left(-\eta_{IJ}\mathrm{d}x^{I}\otimes\mathrm{d}x^{J}+2\mathrm{d}z\otimes\mathrm{d}z\right)+\dot{\psi}_{zJ}\mathrm{d}z\otimes\mathrm{d}x^{J},

(53)

\displaystyle\omega=\omega^{\mathrm{M}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{q}{\varpi}\right)^{2}\dot{\psi}_{zJ}\mathrm{d}z\wedge\mathrm{d}x^{J},

(54)

and the polarization modes yield

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{l}}}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{m^{4}}{\varpi^{2}}\psi_{zz},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{m^{2}}{2}\psi_{zz},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{J}=-\frac{m^{2}}{2}\psi_{zJ},

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{IJ}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\ddot{\tilde{h}}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}+\ddot{\psi}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}\right).

(55)

Additionally to the GR contribution from
\tilde{h}^{\mathrm{TT}}_{IJ}
, the massive spin-2 field
\psi_{ab}
contributes to all the kinematical tensors and to all the polarization modes: its TT part has a contribution similar to that of GR; its longitudinal component contribute to the expansion, to the diagonal components of the shear, and to the longitudinal and transverse scalar modes; and its longitudinal-transverse components contribute to the longitudinal-transverse components of both the shear and the vorticity, and to the vector mode. It stands out that the contributions to the vorticity and to the vector mode are nonvanishing.

V
Discussion

We have considered a cloud of freely falling test particles acted upon a gravitational wave, and studied relations between the kinematical tensors of the cloud and the polarization modes of the wave, which, to our knowledge, had not been studied before. The polarization modes of a gravitational wave with a definite spacetime propagation direction were expressed in terms of the kinematical tensors of the cloud in §
2
.
In addition to the foreseen relations, the obtained expressions revealed that: the expansion contributes to the longitudinal mode; while both the shear and the vorticity contribute to the transverse scalar mode via some of their transverse components, and to the vector mode through some of their longitudinal-transverse components.
This analysis was general, and only assumed the existence of a wave propagation direction, having the advantage to be applied to a wide variety of gravitational wave phenomena. More concrete aspects of gravitational waves might be incorporated and would serve to obtain more particular and more relevant relations, which would be interesting to explore in the future.

Additionally, we have calculated the kinematical tensors and the polarization modes, for a cloud of slowly moving test particles that fall freely in an arbitrary linearized field. Being interested in studying gravitational waves beyond GR, made it necessary to consider nonstationary particles, since not all theories allow the particles to be stationary in the laboratory frame, as GR does [cf. (
23
)]. Linearized gravitational waves of certain metric theories were shown in §
IV
to exhibit most of the anticipated interrelationships between the kinematical tensors and the polarization modes.
The GR wave contributes to the transverse components of the shear and to the transverse tensor mode. The
f(R)
wave contributes additionally to the expansion, and to the longitudinal and transverse scalar modes. Finally, the Einstein-Bach wave contributes additionally to all the kinematical tensors and all the polarization modes, standing out nonvanishing contributions to the vorticity and to the vector mode.

It is reasonable to inquiry about the relevance of the considered metric theories of gravity. It turns out their linearized gravitational waves are representative of the larger class of metric theories determined by a Lagrangian that is a general function of the Riemann tensor. That this is so, comes from the fact that to obtain the linearized field equations, terms in the Lagrangian up to second degree in the Riemann tensor are the only ones that contribute (cf. §II of
Şentürk et al. 2012
), which can be chosen to be the quadratic term of an
f(R)
and the Weyl squared scalar considered in §
2
and §
3
, respectively. Moreover, the
f(R)
example turns out to be representative of scalar-tensor theories, since their gravitational waves are essentially the same (cf. §3 of
Hou et al. 2018
).

Our general expressions for the linearized kinematical tensors and the polarization modes were written in terms of the components of the metric perturbation, which facilitates their evaluation on linearized gravitational waves of metric theories of gravity found in the literature. Although it has the disadvantage of being written in terms of gauge-dependent variables, the gravitational waves of the examples we considered were expressed in a completely fixed gauge, thus excluding nonphysical coordinate effects. Rewriting our expressions in terms of gauge-independent metric potentials (cf., e.g., §2.2 of
Flanagan and Hughes 2005
or §III of
Dong et al. 2024
), specially for the kinematical tensors, would ease their interpretation for general weak gravitational waves, but this goes beyond the scope of this work.

Appendix A
Polarization modes in terms of the Newman-Penrose curvature scalars

To ease comparison of our results with others reported in the literature, we write here the polarization modes in terms of the Newman-Penrose curvature scalars
Newman and Penrose 1962
. In terms of the 4-velocity field
u
of a cloud of test particles and the spatial propagation direction
n
, as seen in the instantaneous rest frame of the particles, of a non-necessarily weak gravitational wave [cf. (
11
)], one can introduce a complex null tetrad
(k,l,m,\overline{m})
, namely one such that
g_{ab}=-2k_{(a}l_{b)}+2m_{(a}\overline{m}_{b)}
, defined by

\displaystyle k^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(u^{a}+n^{a}\right),

\displaystyle l^{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(u^{a}-n^{a}),

\displaystyle m_{a}u^{a}=0.

(56)

Then, the polarization modes (
13
) can be written as

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{l}}}=\frac{R}{12}-S_{ab}m^{a}\overline{m}^{b}-(\Psi_{2}+\overline{\Psi_{2}}),

(57a)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{s}}}=\frac{R}{6}-\frac{1}{2}S_{ab}k^{a}k^{b}-\frac{1}{2}S_{ab}l^{a}l^{b}+\Psi_{2}+\overline{\Psi_{2}},

(57b)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{v}}}_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\real\left(\left[S_{bc}k^{b}\overline{m}^{c}-S_{bc}l^{b}\overline{m}^{c}+2\left(\overline{\Psi_{1}}-\Psi_{3}\right)\right]m_{a}\right),

(57c)

\displaystyle{K^{\mathrm{tt}}}_{ab}=\real\left(\left[S_{cd}\overline{m}^{c}\overline{m}^{d}-\left(\overline{\Psi_{0}}+\Psi_{4}\right)\right]m_{a}m_{b}\right),

(57d)

where
\Psi_{0}=C_{abcd}k^{a}m^{b}k^{c}m^{d}
,
\Psi_{1}=C_{abcd}k^{a}l^{b}k^{c}m^{d}
,
\Psi_{2}=C_{abcd}k^{a}m^{b}\overline{m}^{c}l^{d}
,
\Psi_{3}=C_{abcd}k^{a}l^{b}\overline{m}^{c}l^{d}
and
\Psi_{4}=C_{abcd}l^{a}\overline{m}^{b}l^{c}\overline{m}^{d}
. These relations were first reported for the particular case of linearized plane waves with null propagation in
Eardley et al. 1973
[cf. their (28) supplemented with their (10)–(12)]. The general expressions given in this appendix have also been reported in the literature (cf., e.g., §III B of
Hyun et al. 2019
).
