Regularity aspects of Leray–Hopf solutions to the 2D inhomogeneous Navier–Stokes system and applications to weak-strong uniqueness
Timothée Crin-Barat, Nicola De Nitti, Stefan Škondrić, Alessandro Violini

Abstract
. We characterize the Leray–Hopf solutions of the 2D inhomogeneous Navier–Stokes system that become strong for positive times. This characterization relies on the strong energy inequality and the regularity properties of the pressure. As an application, we establish a weak–strong uniqueness result and provide a unified framework for several recent advances in the field.

76D03, 76D05.

1.1.
Presentation of the problem

We consider the
inhomogeneous incompressible Navier–Stokes system
, which describes incompressible flows with nonconstant densities (see
[
Lio96
, Chapter 1]
):

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}(\rho u)+\operatorname{div}(\rho u\otimes u)-\nu\Delta u+\nabla P=0,&(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho u)=0,&(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\operatorname{div}u=0,&(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
\rho(0,x)=\rho_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\\
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
\rho\geq 0
is the
density function
,
P
is the
pressure
,
u
is the
velocity field
and
\nu
is the
diffusivity
of the fluid. System
1.1
satisfies the following scaling invariance: if
(\rho,u,P)
is a solution of
1.1
with initial data
\left(\rho_{0},u_{0}\right)
then, for all
\lambda>0
, the rescaled triplet

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{\lambda},u_{\lambda},P_{\lambda}\right)\coloneqq\left(\rho\left(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x\right),\lambda u\left(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x\right),\lambda^{2}P\left(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda x\right)\right)

is a solution of
1.1
with initial data
(\rho_{0}(\lambda{{}\cdot{}}),\lambda u_{0}(\lambda{{}\cdot{}}))
. Furthermore, provided that a solution
(\rho,u,P)
is sufficiently regular, the solution also conserves the energy: for every
t\in(0,\infty)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(t)\left\lvert u(t,x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\nabla u(s,x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho_{0}(x)\left\lvert u_{0}(x)\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Scale invariance suggests that the critical requirements for well-posedness are

\rho_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
u_{0}\in\dot{H}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In this work, we focus on the two-dimensional case (
d=2
) with a strictly positive, bounded initial density and an
L^{2}

(1.3)

\displaystyle 0<c_{0}\leq\rho_{0}(x)\leq C_{0}<\infty\quad\text{for a.e.\penalty\ $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$},

\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\sigma}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

We recall that a Leray–Hopf weak solution with initial data satisfying

(1.4)

Compared to the homogeneous model, several problems arise in the inhomogeneous case when analyzing Leray–Hopf weak solutions:

•

No control of the pressure is guaranteed for Leray–Hopf weak solutions to
1.1
: it is not even clear whether the pressure is a distribution. Unlike the homogeneous case, the regularity of the pressure cannot be recovered using Calderón–Zygmund-type estimates (as done in
[
BV22
, Theorem 1.13]
). Indeed, the pressure
P
, at least for smooth triplet
(\rho,u,P)
satisfying
1.1

(1.5)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla P}{\rho}\right)=\operatorname{div}\operatorname{div}(u\otimes u)-\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nu\Delta u}{\rho}\right),

or, alternatively, the pressure can be recovered from the relation

(1.6)

\displaystyle-\Delta P=\operatorname{div}\operatorname{div}(\rho u\otimes u)+\operatorname{div}\left(\rho\partial_{t}u\right).

In both (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), the interaction between
P
and
\partial_{t}u
or
\Delta u
makes it difficult to deduce any kind of regularity for
P
when
\rho
is merely bounded.

•

Secondly, it remains challenging to establish a strong version of the energy inequality (
1.4
). In the homogeneous case, applying Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality,

As a result, it remains an open question whether the same conclusion holds for non-constant densities. In particular, it is not even known if every Leray–Hopf solution

(1.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(t)\left\lvert u(t)\right\rvert^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left\lvert\nabla u(\tau)\right\rvert^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x\,\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(s)\left\lvert u(s)\right\rvert^{2}\,\,\mathrm{d}x.

Due to the lack of a strong energy inequality, it is unclear whether there are any parabolic regularization effects. Given
u_{0}\in H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

, there exists a strong solution

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap L^{2}((0,\infty);\dot{H}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))

[

\tilde{u}_{t}
with initial data

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}((t,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap L^{2}((t,\infty);\dot{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})).

By the freedom in the choice of

\displaystyle u\in C^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})

(see
[

(\rho,u)
of
1.1

\displaystyle u(t)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{for almost every $t>0$,}

and that there exists (owing to

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}_{t}\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap L^{2}((0,\infty);\dot{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),

it is not clear how to conclude that

In our main result,

1.2.
Main results

In this paper, we introduce the class of
immediately strong solutions
which is the particular subclass of Leray–Hopf weak solutions that become strong for any positive time. Our goal is to characterize this class and derive a panoply of weak-strong uniqueness results. We say that a Leray–Hopf solution
(\rho,u)
is an
immediately strong solution
to
1.1
with initial data in
1.3
if

(1.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u,\,\nabla^{2}u\in L^{2}(({\varepsilon},\infty);\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\qquad\text{for every ${\varepsilon}>0$}

(see
Definition

2.11
).
In our first result, we establish a connection between the regularity properties, energy behavior, the existence of a suitable pressure, and the decay at infinity for this class of solutions.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let us assume that the initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
satisfy
1.3
and let
(\rho,u)
be a Leray–Hopf solution of
1.1
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

(\rho,u)
is immediately strong (in the sense of
Definition

2.11
);

(ii)

(\rho,u)
satisfies the strong energy inequality (in the sense of
Definition

2.3
);

(iii)

There exists a constant

C=C(\nu,\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}},c_{0},C_{0})
such that

\displaystyle A_{1}^{0}(u),\,A_{2}^{0}(u),\,A_{3}^{0}(u)\leq C,

where
A^{0}_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, is defined in
2.5
;

(iv)

There exists an associated pressure
P
(in the sense of
Definition

4.1
) such that

\displaystyle P\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((\varepsilon,+\infty);\,\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((\varepsilon,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})\qquad\text{for every $\varepsilon>0$}.

Remark 1.2

(BMO regularity for the pressure)
.

The pressure gradient of strong solutions belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and by the critical Sobolev embedding, see
Lemma

B.2
, this ensures that the pressure is in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Theorem
1.1
establishes the converse: if the pressure is in
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then the pressure gradient is in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, which makes it a natural regularity condition.

We then turn to the question of the existence of solutions satisfying one of the equivalent conditions in
Theorem

1.1
. Every Leray–Hopf weak solution produced by a smooth approximation argument is an immediately strong solution. In particular, for all initial data satisfying
1.3
, there exists an immediately strong solution of
1.1
.

Proposition 1.3

(Existence of immediately strong weak solution)
.

Let us assume that
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
satisfies
1.3
. Then there exists an immediately strong solution
(\rho,u)
of
1.1
.

Proposition

1.3
follows directly from the existence result established in
[
Dan24
, Theorem 1.1]
: indeed, the bounds derived therein imply (

).

Remark 1.4
.

The existence of solutions that fail to satisfy one (and, therefore, all) of the properties mentioned in

1.1
remains an open question.

Finally, as a corollary of
Theorem

1.1
, we observe that
u
actually satisfies the energy equality, provided that the pressure satisfies the regularity assumption in
(iv)
up to time

Corollary 1.5

(Energy equality)
.

Let us assume that the initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
satisfy
1.3
and let

(\rho,u)
be a Leray–Hopf solution of
1.1
. Let us assume, in addition, that

(1.10)

\displaystyle P\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty);\,\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}).

This corollary take inspiration from the result

1.2.1.
Application to weak-strong uniqueness

As mentioned above, in the case of constant densities, the uniqueness of Leray–Hopf solutions in two spatial dimensions has been known since the works by Ladyzhenskaya, Lions, and Prodi (see
[
Lad59
,
LP59
]
). However, this remains an open problem in the inhomogeneous framework. One of the primary reasons for this difficulty is the following observation. Let
(\rho_{1},u_{1},P_{1})
and
(\rho_{2},u_{2},P_{2})
be two sufficiently regular solutions of
1.1
with respect to the same initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0}).
A simple calculation shows that, for every
t>0
,

(1.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(t)}\delta u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\delta\rho\dot{u_{2}}\cdot\delta u-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho_{1}\delta u\otimes\delta u:\nabla u_{2},

where we used the notations

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{2}\coloneqq\partial_{t}u_{2}+(u_{2}\cdot\nabla)u_{2},\quad\delta\rho\coloneqq\rho_{1}-\rho_{2},\quad\text{and }\>\delta u\coloneqq u_{1}-u_{2}.

In the case of constant density, the first term on the right-hand side of (
1.11
) does not appear, and it is precisely this term that introduces the main technical difficulties. In order to get uniqueness, one usually relies on Grönwall’s inequality. To this end, it would be necessary find a function
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty))
such that the right-hand side of (
1.11
) is bounded by

(1.12)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f(s)\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(s)}\delta u(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Then, by Grönwall’s inequality, we would deduce that
\delta u\equiv 0
, which implies that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta\rho+\operatorname{div}(u_{2}\delta\rho)=0,\quad\delta\rho(0)=0.

Hence, by assuming, for instance, that
u_{2}
is Lipschitz continuous in space, we can conclude that
\delta\rho(t)=0
for almost every
t>0
.

However, the first term on the right-hand side of (
1.11
) is not quadratic in
\delta u
and it is therefore unclear how to rewrite it in the form (
1.12
). To capture the quadratic structure in terms of
\delta u
, one way is to perform a stability analysis of the continuity equation

(1.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta\rho+\operatorname{div}(\delta\rho u_{2})=\operatorname{div}(\rho_{1}\delta u)=-\nabla\rho_{1}\cdot\delta u,

so that the
\delta\rho
term can be bounded in term of
\delta u
. In doing so, the right-hand side of (
1.13
) causes a loss of one derivative, and, as discussed in
[
CŠV25
, Section 1.4]
, this requires estimating higher derivatives of the material derivative
\dot{u}_{2}
. Indeed, following this procedure, we obtain

(1.14)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\delta\rho\dot{u}_{2}\cdot\delta u\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s&\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\dive\left(\rho_{1}\delta u))(\tau,X(\tau,x)\right)\dot{u}_{2}\cdot\delta u\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\left\lVert(\rho_{1}\delta u)(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\left\lVert\nabla(\dot{u}_{2}(s)\cdot\delta u(s))\right\rVert_{L^{4/\penalty 3}}\,\mathrm{d}s,\end{aligned}

where
X
is the flow associated with the continuity equation (
1.13
); see
[
CŠV25
]
for more details.
If
(\rho_{2},u_{2})
is an immediately strong solution, the higher derivative estimates for the material derivative
\dot{u}_{2}
are ensured by
(iii)
and we are able to conclude the uniqueness argument with Gronwäll’s inequality provided that
u_{2}
is continuous in time at
t=0
. In this spirit, we establish the following weak-strong uniqueness result.

Theorem 1.6

(Critical weak-strong uniqueness)
.

Let us suppose that the initial data
(\rho_{0},u_{0})
satisfy
1.3
, and let
(\rho_{1},u_{1})
and
(\rho_{2},u_{2})
be two Leray–Hopf solutions of
1.1
. Let us assume, moreover, that
(\rho_{2},u_{2})
is immediately strong (or one of the other
equivalent conditions stated in
Theorem

1.1
) and that

(1.15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{2}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),\quad\nabla u_{2}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})).

Then,

\displaystyle(\rho_{1},u_{1})=(\rho_{2},u_{2})\quad\textrm{a.e in $[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}$}.

.

All the quantities appearing in

1.6

[

.

To motivate the assumptions

, we recall that, in

1.3.
Strategy of proof of
Theorem

1.1

Let us outline the key elements of the strategy used in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Immediately strong solutions satisfy the energy equality on
(0,\infty)
, see
Lemma

2.9
, and, together with the energy inequality, this implies the strong energy inequality. This shows that
(i)
implies
(ii)
. For the reverse implication, we demonstrate that the strong energy inequality grants a semi-flow property to the Leray–Hopf solutions; that is, any time translation of such a solution remains a Leray–Hopf solution with respect to the corresponding translated initial data, and, at the same time, the time translation is much more regular. More precisely, given a Leray–Hopf solution
(\rho,u)
which satisfies the strong energy inequality, and a
t>0
such that
u(t)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, then, by
Theorem

1.6
, the
t
-time translation of
(\rho,u)
coincides with the more regular solution
(\widetilde{\rho}_{t},\widetilde{u}_{t})
, see
1.8
, and, by repeating this procedure for arbitrarily small times, we conclude that
(\rho,u)
has the same regularity as
(\widetilde{\rho}_{t},\widetilde{u}_{t})
for every
t>0.
The crucial point is that the strong energy inequality guarantees that any
t
-time translation of
(\rho,u)
is a Leray–Hopf solution with respect to the initial data
(\rho(t),u(t))
, which allows us to use the
[
CŠV25
, Theorem 1.6]
.

The implication from
(iii)
to
(i)
is a direct consequence of the definition of
A^{0}_{1}
. For the reverse implication, assuming both
(i)
and
(ii)
, we make use of the result in
[
Dan24
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Let us describe how the equivalence between
(i)

First, we recall the definition of Leray–Hopf weak solutions.

Definition 2.1

(Leray–Hopf weak solution)

A pair

(i)

(ii)

\bullet

Definition 2.3

(Strong energy inequality and energy equality)

We say that a Leray–Hopf solution

Convolving in space the terms in
1.1
, we deduce that the following identities hold almost everywhere:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\gamma}+\dive((\rho u)_{\gamma})=0\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}(\rho u)_{\gamma}+\dive((\rho u\otimes u)_{\gamma})+\nabla P_{\gamma}=\nu\Delta u_{\gamma}.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
and fix compact sets
I\subset(0,\infty)
and
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\supp\varphi\subset I\times K
. Multiplying the regularized momentum equation by

\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi,

and integrating over space-time, we obtain

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\iint_{I\times K}\partial_{s}(\rho u)_{\gamma}\cdot\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&=\underbrace{-\iint_{I\times K}\left(\dive((\rho u\otimes u)_{\gamma})\cdot\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi-\nabla P_{\gamma}\cdot\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi+\nu\Delta u_{\gamma}\cdot\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\eqqcolon R_{\gamma}}.\end{aligned}

Our aim is to let
\gamma\to 0
in
4.2
.

Step 2.

The right-hand side.
Using integration by parts on the right-hand side of
4.2
, we get

\displaystyle R_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\iint_{I\times K}(\rho u\otimes u)_{\gamma}:\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\iint_{I\times K}P_{\gamma}\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad-\nu\iint_{I\times K}\nabla u_{\gamma}:\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

We claim that, as
\gamma\to 0
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)-\nabla u\ \right\rVert_{L^{2}(I\times K)}\longrightarrow 0.

We will prove (
4.3
) in Step 5. It is straightforward to check that, using
4.3
, we have that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\right)-\nabla(u\varphi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\varphi\right)-\operatorname{div}(u\varphi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\longrightarrow 0.

The pressure belongs to
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
by assumption and
\nabla u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
by the energy inequality. Then
u\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{4}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
due to Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality, and thus

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\rho u\otimes u)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{4}}\leq C.

By the properties of the mollification, we have that

(4.5)

\displaystyle(\rho u\otimes u)_{\gamma}\longrightarrow(\rho u\otimes u),\quad P_{\gamma}\longrightarrow P,\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla u_{\gamma}\longrightarrow\nabla u\qquad\text{in $L^{2}(I\times K)$}.

Combining
4.4
and
4.5
, we get that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}R_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\iint_{I\times K}\left(\rho u\otimes u:\nabla\left(u\varphi\right)+P\operatorname{div}\left(u\varphi\right)-\nu\nabla u:\nabla\left(u\varphi\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\iint_{I\times K}\left(\varphi\rho u\otimes u:\nabla u+\rho\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}u\cdot\nabla\varphi+Pu\cdot\nabla\varphi-\nu\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}\varphi+\nu\frac{\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s,

where we used the fact that

\displaystyle-\iint_{I\times K}\nabla u:\nabla\varphi\otimes u\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s=\iint_{I\times K}\frac{\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}}{2}\Delta\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

Step 3.

Time derivative.
Integrating by parts in time, we get

\displaystyle\iint_{I\times K}\partial_{s}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}\right)\frac{\varphi}{\rho_{\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{I\times K}\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}\partial_{t}\varphi\frac{1}{\rho_{\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\eqqcolon T_{1,\gamma}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\iint_{I\times K}\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}\varphi\frac{\partial_{s}\rho_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s}_{\eqqcolon T_{2,\gamma}}.

The convergence of
T_{1,\gamma}
is clear: as
\gamma\to 0
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle T_{1,\gamma}\longrightarrow-\frac{1}{2}\iint_{I\times K}\rho|u|^{2}\partial_{s}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

For
T_{2,\gamma}
, we first use the transport equation
\partial_{t}\rho_{\gamma}=-\operatorname{div}(\rho u)_{\gamma}
and integration by parts to write

(4.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}T_{2,\gamma}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\left(\iint_{I\times K}\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}\varphi\frac{\operatorname{div}((\rho u)_{\gamma})}{\rho_{\gamma}^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\left(\iint_{I\times K}\nabla\left(\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}\frac{\varphi}{\rho_{\gamma}^{2}}\right)\cdot(\rho u)_{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\left(\iint_{I\times K}\frac{\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\nabla\varphi\cdot u\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\right)+\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\left(\iint_{I\times K}\varphi\nabla\left(\frac{\left|(\rho u)_{\gamma}\right|^{2}}{\rho_{\gamma}^{2}}\right)\cdot(\rho u)_{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{I\times K}\rho\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\nabla\varphi\cdot u\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\left(\iint_{I\times K}\varphi(\rho u)_{\gamma}\otimes\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}:\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{I\times K}\rho\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}\nabla\varphi\cdot u\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\iint_{I\times K}\varphi\rho u\otimes u:\nabla u\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s.

To prove the last limit in
4.8
, we used the fact that

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho u\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\left\lVert\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}-u\right\rVert_{L^{4}}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{and}\quad\left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)-\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\longrightarrow 0,

where the first one follows from
\rho u\in L^{4}(I\times K)
, the last one by
4.3
, and, for the second one, we use again Ladyzhenskaya’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}-u\right\rVert_{L^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}-u\right\rVert^{1/\penalty 2}_{L^{2}}\ \left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)-\nabla u\right\rVert^{1/\penalty 2}_{L^{2}}

which converges to zero again by
4.3
.

Step 4.

Conclusion.
We let
\gamma\to 0
in
4.2
: using
4.6
,
4.7
, and
4.8
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left(\frac{\rho\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}}{2}\right)-\nu\Delta\left(\frac{\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}}{2}\right)+\nu\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}+\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho\left\lvert u\right\rvert^{2}u+Pu\right)=0

holds in the sense of distributions, which is the definition of suitability according to
Definition

4.2
.

Step 5.

Proof of
4.3
.
We apply the chain rule and use the fact that
\rho_{\gamma}
is bounded from below by a positive constant:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)-\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\nabla\left(\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{1}{\rho_{\gamma}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla\left((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert(\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\frac{1}{\rho_{\gamma}^{2}}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\left\lVert\nabla\rho_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert\nabla\left((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u}{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\gamma\nabla\rho_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}},

which converges to zero by
Lemmas

A.1
–
A.2
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(\rho,u)
be a Leray–Hopf solution of
1.1
, assume that
P\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}((0,\infty);\,\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
and that
(\rho,u,P)
is suitable. Then
(\rho,u)
satisfies the strong energy inequality.

Proof.

We will prove that for every
s,t\in I
, with
I
defined in
Lemma

4.3
, the energy equality holds.
Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
,
\supp\varphi\subset B(0,1)
and
\varphi\equiv 1
in
B(0,1/\penalty 2)
.
Then the sequence
\varphi_{n}(x)\coloneqq\varphi(x/\penalty n)
inherits the following properties:

(1)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
\varphi_{n}(x)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
;

(2)

\nabla\varphi_{n}
and
\Delta\varphi_{n}
are supported in the annulus
A_{n}\coloneqq\left\{x\in B(0,n):\;n/\penalty 2<\left\lvert x\right\rvert<n\right\}
and

(4.9)

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi_{n}|\lesssim\frac{1}{n},\qquad|\Delta\varphi_{n}|\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

Using
Lemma

4.3
with
\left\{\varphi_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we get that, for every
s,t\in I
(with
I
defined in
Lemma

4.3
),

(4.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\rho(t)

\displaystyle|u(t)|^{2}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\rho(s)|u(s)|^{2}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\nu\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}|u|^{2}\Delta\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\rho|u|^{2}u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Pu\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Before studying the convergence of
4.10
, we recall that, for every
p\in[2,4]
, we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}((s,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C,

where
C
is a constant depending on
p
,
c_{0}
,
(t-s)
and
\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{0}}u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{p}_{L^{p}((s,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{s}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p-2}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{c_{0}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}((s,t);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))}^{2}\int_{s}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p-2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{c_{0}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{0}}u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}((s,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p-2}(s-t)^{\frac{4-p}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{c_{0}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{0}}u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{0}}u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{p-2}(s-t)^{\frac{4-p}{2}},

where we used Gagliardo–Nirenberg’s inequality in the first inequality and the energy inequality in the second and fourth inequality.
The left hand side of
4.10
converges to

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\rho(t)|u(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\rho(s)|u(s)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\nu\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau

by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, since
\sqrt{\rho}u\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, we have
\nabla u\in L^{2}((0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
and
\varphi_{n}\to 1
pointwise.
Using
4.9
and Holder’s inequality we can control, up to a constant depending only on
\varphi
, also the first two terms of
4.10
by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}((s,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\frac{C_{0}}{2n}\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{3}_{L^{3}((s,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})}

which converges to zero by
4.11
.
We are left to prove that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Pu\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\to 0.

To this aim we control
4.12
as follows

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}Pu\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\int_{A_{n}}\left(P-\fint_{B(0,n)}P\right)u\cdot\nabla\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert P-\fint_{B(0,n)}P\right\rVert_{L^{2}((s,t)\times B(0,n))}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}((s,t)\times A_{n})}\left\lVert\nabla\varphi_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(A_{n})}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert P-\fint_{B(0,n)}P\right\rVert_{L^{2}((s,t)\times B(0,n))}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}((s,t)\times A_{n})},

where, in the first equality, we used
\operatorname{div}u=0
and, in the last inequality,
4.9
. Owing to
4.11
, we can use Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem to deduce
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}((s,t)\times A_{n})}\longrightarrow 0
. Moreover, using the definition of
\mathrm{BMO}
spaces, we have, for a fixed time,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left\lVert P-\fint_{B(0,n)}P\right\rVert_{L^{2}(B(0,n))}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\int_{B(0,n)}\left\lvert P-\fint_{B(0,n)}P\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\penalty 2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\fint_{B(0,n)}\left\lvert P-\fint_{B(0,n)}P\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/\penalty 2}

\displaystyle=C\left\lVert P\right\rVert_{\mathrm{BMO}_{2}},

which implies
4.12
and concludes the proof.

∎

Remark 4.6
.

In the homogeneous case, it is not necessary to assume the additional
\mathrm{BMO}
bound on the pressure, as the pressure can be recovered from the relation

\displaystyle\Delta P=\operatorname{div}\operatorname{div}(u\otimes u).

Since, for
d\in\{2,3\}
, every Leray–Hopf velocity
u
in
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies

\displaystyle u\otimes u\in L^{4}((0,\infty);L^{4/d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),

we obtain (using Calderón–Zygmund-type arguments; see
[
RRS16
, Lemma 5.1]
) that

\displaystyle P\in L^{2}((0,\infty);L^{2/d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})).

This suffices to show that

\displaystyle u\cdot P\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty);L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})),

and the last step of the proof can be performed without the additional
\mathrm{BMO}
bound. This is the main technical difference compared to
[
Kuk06
]
, where Kukavica demonstrated that the energy equality for Leray–Hopf solutions in three spatial dimensions holds under the weaker assumption

P\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).

We conclude this section by proving
Corollary

1.5
.

Proof of
Corollary

1.5
.

The proof follows from an adaptation of the proofs from this section. It is sufficient to use test functions that are not zero at the origin:

\displaystyle\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2}).

This adjustment is possible due to the increased rigidity of the assumption

\displaystyle P\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2})).

∎

Proposition 4.7

(Implication

Using the additional bounds of

(iii)

(4,4,2)
, we conclude

\displaystyle\left\lvert I_{1}(t)\right\rvert\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}s\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\sup_{\tau\leq s}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(\tau)}\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}s\left\lVert\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\sup_{\tau\leq s}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(\tau)}\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(s^{2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+s^{4}\left\lVert\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\sup_{\tau\leq s}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(\tau)}\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu}{8}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{4}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\nu}{8}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Using

\displaystyle s^{2}\left\lVert\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{s}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{4}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s

yields

\displaystyle\left\lvert I_{1}(t)\right\rvert\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}s^{2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\sup_{\tau\leq s}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(\tau)}\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\nu}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Step 4.

Conclusion.
Gathering the above estimates, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sup_{s\leq t}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(s)}\delta u(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert\nabla\delta u(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left\lVert\nabla u_{2}(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+s^{2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\sup_{\tau\leq s}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(\tau)}\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Defining

\displaystyle f(s)\coloneqq\sup_{\tau\leq s}\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\sqrt{\rho_{1}(\tau)}\delta u(\tau)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

we have

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left\lVert\nabla u_{2}(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+s^{2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Since
f\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty))
and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla u_{2}(s)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}}+s^{2}\left\lVert\nabla\dot{u}_{2}(s)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\in L^{1}((0,\infty)),

we can use Grönwall’s inequality to deduce that

\displaystyle\delta u\equiv 0.

Using that
\nabla u_{2}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,\infty);L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta\rho+\dive(u_{2}\delta\rho)=\rho_{1}\delta u=0,\quad\delta\rho(0)=0,

we deduce, for instance from
[
DL89
]
, that
\delta\rho\equiv 0.

∎

Appendix A
Commutator estimates

We provide a brief outline of the DiPerna–Lions’ commutator estimate from
[
DL89
]
. Let
K_{\gamma}
denote the
\gamma
-neighborhood
of a set
K
and let
f_{\gamma}
be the mollification of
f
in space,
i.e.
, the convolution of
f
, with respect to the space variable, with the standard mollifier
\eta_{\gamma}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
u\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty);H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\rho\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u),\quad\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u}{\gamma},\quad\nabla((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u)\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{in\> $L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})$ \ as $\gamma\to 0$}.

Proof.

Let
I\subset(0,\infty)
and
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact sets. Expanding the convolution and using Minkowski’s inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\operatorname{div}\left((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(I\times K)}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\int_{B}\rho(\cdot,\cdot+\gamma y)\frac{u(\cdot,\cdot+\gamma y)-u}{\gamma}\cdot\nabla\eta(y)\,\,\mathrm{d}y\right\rVert_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}\left\lVert\rho(\cdot,\cdot+\gamma y)\frac{u(\cdot,\cdot+\gamma y)-u}{\gamma}\cdot\nabla\eta(y)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

We recall that, by the properties of
L^{p}
and Sobolev functions, the following holds:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{u(t,\cdot+\gamma y)-u(t,\cdot)}{\gamma}-\nabla u(t,\cdot)y\right\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as $\gamma\to 0$}

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\rho\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C.

Applying Fatou’s lemma, we then obtain

(A.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\to 0}\left\lVert\operatorname{div}\left((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(I\times K)}\leq\int_{B}\left\lVert\rho\,[\nabla u\cdot y]\cdot\nabla\eta(y)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

Similarly, for the other terms, we obtain the following bounds:

(A.2)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\to 0}\left\lVert\frac{(\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u}{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(I\times K)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}\left\lVert\rho\,[\nabla u\cdot y]\ \eta(y)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\gamma\to 0}\left\lVert\nabla\left((\rho u)_{\gamma}-\rho_{\gamma}u\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(I\times K)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B}\left\lVert\rho\,[\nabla u\cdot y]\otimes\nabla\eta(y)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y.

The integrands on the right-hand sides of
A.1
and
A.2
exhibit odd symmetry (due to the radial symmetry of the convolution kernel in
y
), and since the unit ball has radial symmetry, we conclude that these terms are zero.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for
1\leq p\leq\infty
. For every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have:

\displaystyle\gamma\left\lVert\nabla f_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(K)}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(K_{\gamma})}.

Proof.

If
f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, by Young’s convolution inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla f_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\lVert\nabla\eta_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},

where
\eta
is the mollification kernel. If
f\in L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
K
is a compact set, we define the extended function
\bar{f}(x)\coloneqq\mathds{1}_{K_{\gamma}}(x)f(x)
and compute

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla f_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(K)}=\left\lVert\nabla\bar{f}_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(K)}\leq\left\lVert\nabla\bar{f}_{\gamma}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\lVert\bar{f}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(K_{\gamma})}.

∎

Appendix B
BMO functions

In this appendix, we recall some preliminary notions on functions of bounded mean oscillation. Let
\mathcal{Q}
denote the set of cubes of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with edges parallel to the coordinate axes, and let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of balls in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
We say that a locally integrable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has
bounded mean oscillation
,
i.e.
,
f\in\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, if

\displaystyle\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\fint_{Q}\left|f(x)-\fint_{Q}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

By identifying functions that differ only by constants,
\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
becomes a Banach space equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\fint_{Q}\left|f(x)-\fint_{Q}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\mathrm{d}x.

Equivalently, we could replace the cubes by balls; namely, the following result holds.

Lemma B.1
.

There are constants
0<c<C
such that

\displaystyle c\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\fint_{B}\left|f(x)-\fint_{B}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\mathrm{d}x\leq C\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

The following embedding is crucial in our computations.

Lemma B.2
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that, for every
f\in H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with

\nabla f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Proof.

According to
Lemma

B.1
, it suffices to show that, for every ball
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

\displaystyle\fint_{B}\left|f(x)-\fint_{B}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{B}\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\fint_{B}\left|f(x)-\fint_{B}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{C}{R}\left(\int_{B}\left|f(x)-\fint_{B}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

We now employ Poincaré’s inequality for balls,
i.e.
, for every ball
B=B(x_{0},R)
, there exists a constant
C
(independent of the center
x_{0}
and the radius
R
) such that, for every
f\in H^{1}(B)
,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{B}\left|f(x)-\fint_{B}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\leq CR\left(\int_{B}\left\lvert\nabla f\right\rvert^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

For
f\in L^{p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
1\leq p<\infty
, we define

\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}_{p}}\coloneqq\sup_{Q}\left(\fint_{Q}\left|f(x)-\fint_{Q}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}

and set

\mathrm{BMO}_{p}\coloneqq\left\{f\in L^{p}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}_{p}}<\infty\right\}.

We use the following result on the equivalence of these two spaces (for a proof, see
[
AB08
, Corollary 5.2.5]
).

Lemma B.3
.

For every
1<p<\infty
, we have
\mathrm{BMO}=\mathrm{BMO}_{p}
and
\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}_{p}}\approx\|f\|_{\mathrm{BMO}}
.

Appendix C
Anti-gradient operator

We start by recalling some notions on Hölder continuous functions.

Lemma C.1

(Hölder continuous functions)
.

With the
\alpha
-Hölder seminorm

\displaystyle\left\lvert u\right\rvert_{\alpha}\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\lvert u(x)-u(y)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{\alpha}}\quad\alpha\in(0,1),

the space
C^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\coloneqq\{u\in C(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\,u(0)=0,\ \left\lvert u\right\rvert_{\alpha}<\infty\}

is a Banach space.

Proof.

The fact that the semi-norm
\left\lvert u\right\rvert_{\alpha}
is a norm in
C^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is straightforward. To prove that
(C^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\left\lvert u\right\rvert_{\alpha})
is complete, consider a Cauchy sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert u_{n}(x)-u_{m}(x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\alpha}\frac{\left\lvert u_{n}(x)-u_{m}(x)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\alpha}\frac{\left\lvert(u_{n}-u_{m})(x)-(u_{n}-u_{m})(0)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert x\right\rvert^{\alpha}\left\lvert u_{n}-u_{m}\right\rvert_{\alpha},

where we used the fact that
u_{n}(0)=0
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the sequence
\{u_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}
is Cauchy, hence converging. Finally, we define

\displaystyle u(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}(x),&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\},\\
0,&\text{if }x=0.\end{cases}

We have that
u\in C^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lvert u(x)-u(y)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{\alpha}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left\lvert u_{n}(x)-u_{n}(y)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{\alpha}}\leq C<\infty,

where the uniform bound holds since
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence in
C^{\alpha}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. To conclude the proof, we have to show
\left\lvert u_{n}-u\right\rvert_{C^{\alpha}}\rightarrow 0
. For every
{\varepsilon}>0
, let
N
be large enough to have
\left\lvert u_{n}-u_{m}\right\rvert_{C^{\alpha}}<{\varepsilon}
for every
n,m>N
. For
n>N
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert u_{n}-u\right\rvert_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\left\lvert(u_{n}-u)(x)-(u_{n}-u)(y)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\neq y}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\left\lvert(u_{n}-u_{m})(x)-(u_{n}-u_{m})(y)\right\rvert}{\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\neq y}\limsup_{m\to\infty}\left\lvert u_{n}-u_{m}\right\rvert_{C^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle<{\varepsilon},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we define the Banach space of curl-free functions in
L^{p}\cap L^{2}
, with
p>2
, and prove the existence of a unique linear operator that serves as the inverse of the gradient (in a suitable sense).

Proposition C.2

(Anti-gradient operator)
.

Let
p>2
and
\alpha_{p}=1-\frac{2}{p}
. Let
G^{p}
be the Banach space of curl-free
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
functions:

\displaystyle G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\coloneqq\{g\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2}):\,\left\langle g,\nabla^{\perp}\psi\right\rangle=0\text{ for all }\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\}.

There exists a unique linear operator

\Phi\colon G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})\to C^{\alpha_{p}}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{BMO}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})

such that

\displaystyle\Phi(g)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\Phi(g)=g,\qquad\text{for all }g\in G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Furthermore, for every
g\in G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi(g),\operatorname{div}\psi\right\rangle=-\left\langle g,\psi\right\rangle,\qquad\text{for all }\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Proof.

We define
\Phi^{r}:G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by setting

\displaystyle\Phi^{r}(g)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}g(\tau x)\cdot x\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Since
\curl g=0
for every
g\in G^{p}\cap C^{\infty}
, we have
\nabla\Phi^{r}(g)=g
.
Furthermore, by Morrey’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Phi^{r}(g)\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha_{p}}}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla\Phi^{r}(g)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}=C\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

By
Lemma

B.2
, we can also estimate the
\mathrm{BMO}
norm:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Phi^{r}(g)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{BMO}}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla\Phi^{r}(g)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=C\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Then
\Phi^{r}:G^{p}\cap C^{\infty}\rightarrow C^{\alpha_{p}}_{0}\cap\mathrm{BMO}
is a linear and bounded operator between Banach spaces (see
Lemma

C.1
). Since
G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is dense in
G^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we can extend uniquely
\Phi^{r}
to an operator
\Phi:G^{p}\rightarrow C^{\alpha}_{0}\cap\mathrm{BMO}
which satisfies

\displaystyle\Phi(g)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi^{r}(g_{n})\quad\text{whenever }\left\lVert g_{n}-g\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\rightarrow 0.

Let
g\in G^{p}
and
\{g_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset G^{p}\cap C^{\infty}
such that
\left\lVert g_{n}-g\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\rightarrow 0.
Then, for every
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\Phi(g),\operatorname{div}\psi\right\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle\Phi^{r}(g_{n}),\operatorname{div}\psi\right\rangle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle\nabla\Phi^{r}(g_{n}),\psi\right\rangle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle g_{n},\psi\right\rangle=-\left\langle g,\psi\right\rangle.

It remains to prove the uniqueness of the operator. To this end, we let
\tilde{\Phi}\colon G^{p}\to C^{\alpha}_{0}\cap\mathrm{BMO}
be another operator with the same properties as
\Phi
. For every
g\in G^{p}\cap C^{\infty}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla(\tilde{\Phi}(g)-\Phi(g))=g-g=0.

Hence, there exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\tilde{\Phi}(g)=\Phi(g)+c
. From
this, we deduce

\displaystyle 0=\tilde{\Phi}(g)(0)=\Phi(g)(0)+c=c,

and, therefore, we have
\tilde{\Phi}(g)=\Phi(g)
for every
g\in G^{p}\cap C^{\infty}
.
Since the extension to
G^{p}
is unique, we conclude that
\Phi=\tilde{\Phi}
.
∎
