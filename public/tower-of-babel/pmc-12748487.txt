Bibliometric analysis reveals the current status and future trends of cancer associated fibroblasts research in breast cancer
Authors: Shixue Jing, Siping Zhou, Cui Jia, Yongfeng Wang, Hui Cao, Lushun Zhang, Xinlian Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer is one of the most common and lethal heterogeneous cancers among women worldwide, posing a significant threat to female health. Cancer associated fibroblasts (CAFs) play a critical role in the initiation and progression of breast cancer, and their behavior within the tumor microenvironment profoundly influences disease development. Systematic research on the functions of CAFs in breast cancer remains relatively limited. This study employs bibliometric theories and methods to comprehensively analyze the existing knowledge framework of breast cancer CAFs research, thereby identifying and examining research hotspots and future trends in this field.MethodsThis study retrieved literature related to CAFs in breast cancer from the Science Core Collection (WOSCC) database, covering publications from 2005 to 2025. After rigorous screening, 377 eligible academic papers were included as research subjects. Utilizing software tools such as VOSviewer, Pajek, Scimago Graphica, and CiteSpace, we conducted an in-depth analysis across multiple dimensions, including countries, research institutions, authors, journals, and keywords, to elucidate the knowledge structure of this field and identify research hotspots and developmental trends.ResultsThe findings indicate a general upward trend in annual publications, with particularly rapid growth between 2022 and 2024. China, the United States, and Italy were the most prolific countries, demonstrating strong academic influence. Among institutions, the University of Calabria, Chongqing Medical University, and the University of Manchester contributed the most research output. Notable researchers, including Marcello Maggiolini, Michael P. Lisanti, and Manran Liu, stood out in terms of publication volume and collaborative engagement. Regarding journals, Cancers published the highest number of articles, while Nature Communications had the greatest impact, with a 2023 impact factor of 14.7. This study systematically reviews the origin, activation mechanisms, and heterogeneity of CAFs in breast cancer. The research reveals that CAFs can be activated through various cytokines and signaling pathways and exhibit significant heterogeneity across different breast cancer subtypes. CAFs secrete multiple factors involved in angiogenesis and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, thereby promoting breast cancer cell metastasis and invasion. Additionally, CAFs may suppress the host’s anti-tumor immune response. Current therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs primarily focus on disrupting intercellular communication, degrading the ECM, and overcoming drug resistance. Future research may concentrate on exploring the mechanisms by which immunotherapy regulates CAF activity and the potential of combination therapies. This study provides a comprehensive overview of the current status, hotspots, and cutting-edge advancements in breast cancer CAF research.ConclusionOur study systematically examines research hotspots and trends in breast cancer CAF studies, establishing a solid literature-based foundation for defining future research directions and priorities. It highlights the significant potential and importance of targeting CAFs in the breast cancer stroma for therapeutic intervention and tumor progression inhibition. The findings are expected to offer scientific guidance for subsequent research and advance the development of breast cancer CAF studies. Although this study has certain limitations, it provides valuable references for future related research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03943-9.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy with high mortality rates among women worldwide, and its development and progression are closely linked to dynamic interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME). As a highly heterogeneous disease, breast cancer is widely distributed across the globe and represents a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women. Its incidence is strongly associated with age and a country’s level of economic development. Analysis based on the Human Development Index indicates that the incidence of breast cancer is significantly higher in developed countries compared to developing nations. Socioeconomic conditions influence key factors such as growth, nutritional status, age at menarche, reproductive history, age at menopause, and obesity rates in women. These factors, in conjunction with genetic determinants, collectively play a significant role in influencing the risk of breast cancer development [1]. Breast cancer can be classified into molecular subtypes including Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2 positive, and triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) [2]. Metastasis is the leading cause of death in breast cancer patients, with the TME playing a key role in the mechanisms of metastasis. Both immune and non-immune cells within the TME collaboratively regulate the process of cancer metastasis, and targeted inhibition of relevant signaling pathways can effectively reduce the vascular invasion ability and metastatic potential of tumor cells [3]. Early diagnosis is crucial for improving patient survival rates; however, more than half of patients are already at an advanced or metastatic stage by the time clinical symptoms appear [1].

In the TME, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), as the most predominant stromal cell component, regulate tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapy resistance through multiple mechanisms, making them a critical research target in breast cancer. CAFs are activated, heterogeneous stromal cells that are the most abundant stromal component in the TME and are closely associated with tumor progression. They drive tumor development by promoting ECM deposition and remodeling, mediating immunosuppression, enhancing cancer cell proliferation, inducing angiogenesis, and facilitating therapy resistance [4–6]. CAFs exhibit high heterogeneity and plasticity, with diverse cellular origins. These primarily include the activation of resident fibroblasts, as well as the transformation of various cell types such as mesenchymal stem cells, adipocytes, and bone marrow derived fibroblasts. Under the mediation of factors like TGF-β, CXCL12, Wnt7a, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), and specific miRNAs, these cells differentiate into CAF subsets expressing distinct markers. For instance, transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and CXCL12 can induce the formation of α-SMA⁺ CAFs, while mesenchymal stem cells can transform into CAFs through signals such as tumor-derived exosomes and TGF-β. Additionally, adipocytes can give rise to CAFs with an FSP1⁺/α-SMA⁻/FAP⁻ phenotype under the influence of Wnt3a. In contrast, bone marrow derived fibroblasts, which are often recruited into the TME with an α-SMA⁺/PDGFRα⁻ phenotype, contribute to tumor progression.

The activation mechanisms of CAFs are complex and multifaceted, involving cytokine receptor mediated signaling pathways, direct interactions between tumor cells and CAFs, as well as regulation by various physicochemical factors within the TME. Ligands of the TGF-β family are key signaling molecules regulating the transformation of normal fibroblasts (NFs) into CAFs [7–9]. In breast cancer, CD26⁺ NFs and CD26 NFs differentiate into inflammatory CAFs and myofibroblastic CAFs, respectively, collaboratively promoting tumor progression [10]. Breast cancer cells activate fibroblasts through autocrine TGF-β and CXCL12, further enhancing their activation and function via a positive feedback loop. The CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, TGF-β/Smad pathway, Wnt signaling, and osteopontin (OPN) are all involved in regulating marker expression and secretory functions of CAFs. Additionally, miRNAs and proteins carried by tumor cell derived exosomes play critical roles in CAF activation and functional remodeling.

CAFs exhibit significant heterogeneity in breast cancer and lack a unified specific marker. Their phenotypes and functions vary considerably depending on the molecular and pathological subtypes of the disease. Systematic analysis based on six CAF biomarkers (FAP, CD29, α-SMA, FSP1, PDGFRβ, and Caveolin) has led to the identification of four well-characterized CAF subtypes (CAF-S1 to CAF-S4) [10]. The application of single-cell RNA sequencing has greatly advanced the understanding of CAF subtypes, enabling researchers to identify multiple functional subpopulations, including vCAFs, mCAFs, and dCAFs. Among these, the CAF-S1 subtype significantly promotes the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and therapy resistance by expressing immune-modulatory ligands such as PD-L2 and OX40L. In contrast, FAP⁺ CAFs accelerate tumor progression by regulating extracellular matrix remodeling and metabolic reprogramming. These heterogeneous subpopulations demonstrate diversity in spatial distribution, marker expression, and clinical relevance, profoundly influencing breast cancer biology and patient prognosis. Furthermore, CAFs mediate immunosuppression by secreting immune modulatory cytokines and chemokines, thereby facilitating tumor progression [11]. It must be emphasized that CAFs are not a homogeneous cell population but rather a highly heterogeneous and plastic group of cells. They primarily originate from the activation of resident fibroblasts but may also derive from other cell types. Their activation is mediated by a complex network of cytokines, growth factors, and tumor cell-derived exosomes, leading to functional diversification into distinct subtypes. Through remodeling the extracellular matrix, modulating immune responses, and supporting cancer cell metabolism, CAFs play a key role in tumor progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance. The complex heterogeneity of CAFs is reflected in their diverse marker expression, which has become a major focus of current research.

To date, there have been no bibliometric studies reported on CAFs in breast cancer. This study employs VOSviewer and CiteSpace for visual analysis, aiming not only to map the knowledge structure and research hotspots in the field of breast cancer CAFs, but also to quantify geographical disparities in research output. Through qualitative and quantitative assessments across multiple dimensions including countries, authors, research institutions, and journals, we seek to elucidate current research trends, provide a theoretical foundation for promoting more inclusive and globally representative future research directions, and reveal the evolving knowledge structure and research frontiers within this complex and dynamic field.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategy

This study utilized the WOSCC database as the data source, with the index scope limited to SCI EXPANDED and SSCI. The search keywords were “breast cancer” and “CAFs,” and the retrieval strategy was as follows: TS=(“Breast Neoplasm” OR “Breast Tumor” OR “Breast Cancer” OR “Cancer of the Breast” OR “Cancer of Breast” OR “Breast Carcinoma”) AND TS=(“Cancer Associated Fibroblast” OR “Tumor Associated Fibroblast” OR “Cancer Related Fibroblast” OR “Tumor Related Fibroblast” OR “Tumour Associated Fibroblast” OR “Tumour Related Fibroblast”).

The search was conducted for publications from January 1, 2005, to March 13, 2025, with the document types limited to “Article” and “Review Article” and the language restricted to English. Based on the above search strategy, a total of 1,929 publications were retrieved. After evaluating the titles and abstracts, 377 articles highly relevant to the research topic were selected for further analysis.The complete records, including cited references, were exported in plain text format and imported into CiteSpace for bibliometric analysis. The final dataset comprised 377 publications, consisting of 334 research articles and 37 review articles .

Data analysis and visualization

This study employed bibliometric analysis on the screened 377 publications using tools including WPS Office, VOSviewer, CiteSpace (v6.3.R1), Pajek, and Scimago Graphica. Figure 1 illustrates the literature search and processing workflow.

Fig. 1Flow diagram

Flow diagram

Bibliometrics applies statistical and mathematical methods to quantitatively analyze literature within a specific field, thereby revealing research trends and developmental trajectories. By systematically organizing and analyzing information such as authors, keywords, countries, institutions, and journals, a comprehensive understanding of the knowledge structure and research hotspots in the field can be achieved.

VOSviewer, a freely available bibliometric visualization tool developed by Leiden University in the Netherlands, constructs network maps based on co-occurrence matrices to intuitively display the bibliometric structure of a research domain. In this study, VOSviewer was utilized to generate author collaboration networks, document co-citation networks, and keyword co-occurrence networks. In these network visualizations, node size represents element importance, line thickness indicates connection strength, and node colors distinguish different cluster modules.

CiteSpace, developed by Professor Chaomei Chen’s research team, specializes in collaboration networks and cluster analysis. By identifying and visualizing burst detection of key nodes, CiteSpace reveals pivotal turning points and critical advancements in a research field. Through centrality and burstness analyses, researchers can efficiently grasp the knowledge base, research hotspots, and cutting edge trends within the domain.

Literature co-citation analysis

Co-citation analysis is used to reveal the intrinsic relationships between publications. When two or more documents are simultaneously cited by another paper, a co-citation relationship is formed between them. Through co-citation analysis, researchers can gain in depth insights into the knowledge structure and developmental trajectory of a research field. Table 6 lists the top 10 most frequently co-cited references. The most highly cited publications are “The prognostic significance of tumor-associated stroma in invasive breast carcinoma” and “Stromal miR-200s contribute to breast cancer cell invasion through CAF activation and ECM remodeling”. The first study demonstrates that CAFs in tumor stroma can serve as a prognostic marker for breast cancer patients, with higher CAF density in TNBC being associated with chemotherapy resistance and reduced survival. The second study reveals, from a molecular perspective, that the miR-200s-ZEB1-MMP axis is a key mechanism through which CAFs promote metastasis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target. Together, these two studies underscore the critical role of stromal reprogramming in breast cancer progression.

Discussion

General information

This study employs bibliometric methods to evaluate the contributions and influence of research on CAFs in breast cancer across dimensions such as countries, institutions, authors, and journals. Since the first publication in this field in 2005, the number of articles has increased significantly from 2020 to 2024, marking a phase of rapid development. Although there was a decline in publications between 2021 and 2022, the upward trend resumed in 2022, reflecting dynamic shifts in research activity and interest. At the national level, China, the United States, and Italy have contributed the highest number of publications, highlighting their research maturity and academic impact in this field.

A total of 164 journals have published research on breast cancer CAFs, with Cancers being the most prolific, followed by Cell Cycle and Cancer Research. These journals provide scholars with the latest advancements and trends in the field. The top three most published authors are Marcello Maggiolini, Michael P. Lisanti, and Manran Liu, who have made significant contributions to breast cancer CAF research. For instance, Marcello Maggiolini uncovered the mechanism by which CAFs mediate therapy resistance in estrogen-rich microenvironments and proposed phytochemical based intervention strategies. Michael P. Lisanti and Diana Whitaker Menezes, long term research collaborators, jointly proposed a novel mechanism for the Warburg effect, demonstrating that stromal fibroblasts undergo aerobic glycolysis to produce and secrete more pyruvate/lactate, which fuels mitochondrial TCA cycles, oxidative phosphorylation, and metabolism in adjacent epithelial cancer cells. In 2014, they further elucidated how altered signaling pathways in cancer cells drive neighboring CAFs to generate reactive oxygen species and autophagy, thereby supporting cancer growth and metastasis. Their work revealed the critical role of CAF metabolism in breast tumor progression and introduced the “metabolic coupling” model, providing a theoretical foundation for targeting CAF metabolism to improve breast cancer treatment.

Among the top ten most published authors, Diana Whitaker Menezes has the highest average citation count. Her research focuses on the TME and CAFs in breast cancer, establishing the central role of CAFs in metabolic support, ECM stiffening, and therapy resistance. U. E. Martinez Outschoorn investigates the complexity of the TME, collaborating with Michael P. Lisanti and Federica Sotgia to explore metabolic interactions between cancer cells and CAFs. A. Orimo’s research demonstrated that fibroblasts constitute the majority of stromal cells in breast cancer and found that stromal fibroblasts in invasive breast cancer promote tumor growth and angiogenesis through high secretion of stromal cell derived factor1. These studies provide a fundamental understanding of CAF’s role in breast cancer and offer new directions for future research and therapeutic strategies.

Knowledge base

Based on the results of the literature co-citation network analysis, the research foundation of CAFs in breast cancer can be broadly delineated. Among the top 10 most cited publications, the 2nd, 7th, and 8th articles focus on the tumor-promoting role of CAFs, highlighting their facilitation of breast cancer invasion and growth through various signaling molecules. The 4th and 9th articles explore CAF heterogeneity and the functions of different subtypes, particularly the role of the CAF-S1 subset in breast cancer in modulating regulatory T lymphocytes (Tregs). The 3rd and 10th articles summarize CAF-mediated therapy resistance, including chemotherapy resistance induced by activated CAFs via HMGB1 and the protective role of CAF autophagy under hypoxic conditions in shielding tumor cells from stress induced damage. The 5th and 6th articles propose that CAFs influence breast cancer progression through ECM remodeling, while the 1st article mentions the prognostic value of tumor associated stroma, suggesting CAFs as potential prognostic markers for breast cancer.

Bibliometric co-citation analysis revealed that CAF heterogeneity constitutes the core of the knowledge base in this field (Table 6). Among the cited references, the study by Ana Costa et al. [12] received the highest citation frequency. This research used six protein markers, including FAP and α-SMA, in combination with flow cytometry sorting and population transcriptome analysis to classify human breast cancer CAFs into four functional subtypes, and clarified the immunosuppressive mechanism of the CAF-S1 subtype.

In the same year, Michael Bartoschek, Nikola Oskolkov et al. employed single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to perform unbiased clustering based on transcriptomic similarity, identifying three novel subtypes in a mouse model. Although this study did not rank among the top ten most co-cited works, it significantly advanced the understanding of CAF heterogeneity. These works mark a paradigm shift in research from population level analyses toward single-cell resolution, a transition that is now emerging as a new research frontier [13].

Ana Costa et al. further elucidated the association between breast cancer CAF subsets and immune suppression, demonstrating that CAF-S1 attracts CD4⁺CD25⁺ Tregs via CXCL12 expression and retains them on its surface through ligands TNFSF4/OX40L and PDCD1LG2/PD-L2, as well as the adhesion molecule JAM2. Moreover, compared to CAF-S4, CAF-S1 increases Treg numbers and enhances their ability to suppress effector T cell proliferation, thereby functionally augmenting Treg activity. These mechanisms enable CAF-S1 in breast cancer to mediate immune suppression [14].

Heather M. Brechbuhl’s research revealed that CAF subtypes influence the sensitivity of breast cancer receptors to estrogen, and their expression affects tumor cell responsiveness to chemotherapeutic agents. Understanding the composition of CAFs in breast cancer may aid in predicting treatment response and prognosis, positioning CAFs as potential targets for drug development [15].

Jan Kiefer et al. uncovered a positive feedback loop between immunosuppressive ECM-myCAF and TGF-β-myCAF (CAF-S1 clusters) and Tregs, which may contribute to immunotherapy resistance [13]. Erik Sahai et al. summarized the conceptual framework of CAFs, providing an overview of breast cancer CAF research: fibroblasts are defined as cells negative for epithelial, endothelial, and leukocyte markers, exhibiting an elongated morphology and lacking oncogenic mutations [5]. CAFs display phenotypic diversity, and their origin involves a process termed “stromagenesis”, wherein resident fibroblasts undergo some form of tissue dysfunction [16]. While stromal fibroblasts proliferate extensively in tumor bearing patients, this phenomenon is rarely observed [17]. The study also summarized key signals for fibroblast activation and mechanisms underlying CAF activation: heat shock factor 1 contributes to CAF generation, IL-1 promotes CAF phenotype formation, TGF-β family ligands and the lipid mediator lysophosphatidic acid activate signaling pathways, Notch signaling drives CAF phenotypes in breast cancer, and physical changes in the ECM can also activate CAFs. Additionally, other stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment participate in CAF generation. For example, macrophage derived granulin promotes the activation of fibrotic environments in liver metastasis [18, 19], illustrating the interplay between cancer and inflammatory regulation. The study further outlined CAF functions, including their association with tumor angiogenesis, interactions with other stromal cells, involvement in ECM deposition and remodeling within the tumor microenvironment [20, 21], and their role in promoting tumor growth.

Research trends and hotspots

By analyzing the 10 most frequently cited terms, the research frontiers and hotspots of CAFs in breast cancer were identified, including CAFs, tumor microenvironment, activation, expression, stromal fibroblasts, EMT, metastasis, and diagnostic/therapeutic strategies. Breast cancer CAFs are activated stromal cells, with research focus primarily on their regulatory mechanisms, exploring the origin, activation, and influencing factors of CAFs, as well as analyzing their functional characteristics and heterogeneity in breast cancer.

Additionally, studies emphasize the interaction between CAFs and cancer stem cells, their synergistic effects with other stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment, and their collective role in regulating tumor progression. Research also investigates the association between CAFs and immunosuppressive regulation, where CAFs secrete cytokines to inhibit immune cell function and promote tumor development. Through bibliometric keyword analysis, this study identified a strong citation burst of the term “stromal fibroblasts” and the central position of “heterogeneity” in the co-occurrence network. These findings indicate that utilizing specific biomarkers such as α-SMA, PDGFRβ, and FAP to characterize CAF heterogeneity and elucidate its functional mechanisms has become a major research focus in the field. The core of this direction lies in distinguishing functionally distinct subpopulations by identifying these biomarkers, thereby confirming the value of CAF biomarkers as potential therapeutic targets and providing a basis for developing precise treatment strategies targeting specific CAF subtypes.

Basic functions and characteristics of CAFs in breast cancer

Origin and activation of CAFs in breast cancer

In breast cancer, CAFs primarily originate from the activation of resident fibroblasts, but may also derive from the transformation of various cell types including mesenchymal stem cells, adipocytes, and bone marrow derived fibroblasts. Resident fibroblasts can differentiate into distinct CAF phenotypes under the influence of factors such as TGF-β, CXCL12, and co-culture with cancer cells. For instance, TGF-β, CXCL12, Wnt7a, miR-125b, miR-9, as well as chemotherapeutic agents, can induce their conversion into α-SMA⁺ CAFs. The combined action of TGF-β and PDGF promotes the formation of FSP1⁺ CAFs. Mesenchymal stem cells can also transform into CAFs under the influence of tumor-derived exosomes and TGF-β, among other factors. Adipocytes differentiate into FSP1⁺/α-SMA⁻/FAP⁻ CAFs upon Wnt3a stimulation. Bone marrow derived fibroblasts, which are often recruited into the TME with an α-SMA⁺/PDGFRα phenotype, contribute to tumor progression. Pericyte derived CAFs express genes associated with vascular regulation [22].

The activation mechanisms of CAFs encompass cytokine mediated signaling pathways, direct interactions between tumor cells and fibroblasts, and influences from the tumor microenvironment. Cytokines play a key role by binding to their respective receptors: breast cancer cells induce the transformation of normal fibroblasts into CAFs through autocrine TGF-β and CXCL12, thereby shaping a microenvironment conducive to tumor growth and invasion [23]. Following binding to CXCR4 on the fibroblast surface, CXCL12 not only activates the cells and upregulates α-SMA expression but also promotes the secretion of TGF-β and CXCL12, establishing a positive feedback loop that further enhances activation [23]. TGF-β binding to its receptors not only activates fibroblasts and induces α-SMA expression but also regulates metabolic processes such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation [23]. PDGF binding to its receptor, in synergy with TGF-β, downregulates isocitrate dehydrogenase 3α IDH3α, elevates levels of α-ketoglutarate and hypoxia-inducible factor 1α HIF-1α, thereby enhancing glycolysis and reducing oxidative phosphorylation. Wnt7a, a member of the Wnt family, participates in fibroblast activation by engaging TGF-β receptors. Osteopontin OPN binds to CD44 and integrin αvβ3, upregulating the expression of markers such as α-SMA, FSP-1, and FAP via the AKT and MAPK1 signaling pathways, and promotes the secretion of factors including CXCL1, CXCL2, COX-2, IL-6, OPN, and collagen.

Furthermore, breast cancer cells regulate fibroblast activation through exosome carried miRNAs such as miR-370-3p and proteins like survivin. miR-370-3p can enhance the stemness, migration, and invasion capabilities of cancer cells [24], while survivin transferred via exosomes upregulates SOD1 expression, thereby inducing CAF transformation [25].

Heterogeneity of CAFs in breast cancer

CAFs are highly heterogeneous and lack a unified marker, as their phenotype and function vary across different subsets and molecular subtypes of breast cancer. Studies have shown that CAFs in HER2-positive breast cancer differ significantly from those in triple-negative and ER-positive breast cancers in terms of genes related to cytoskeleton and integrin signaling [26]. For instance, invasive lobular carcinoma exhibits overexpression of FAP, FSP1, and PDGFRβ, whereas ductal carcinoma demonstrates higher expression of prolyl 4-hydroxylase, PDGFRα, and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan [27]. The application of single-cell RNA sequencing scRNA-seq has advanced research on CAF heterogeneity by enabling unbiased clustering based on whole transcriptome expression profiles. This approach has facilitated the identification of subpopulations such as vCAFs, mCAFs, and dCAFs [13], thereby refining the CAF classification framework.

CAFs exhibit phenotypic heterogeneity reflected by various biomarkers including PDGFRα/β, CD90/THY1, PDPN, α-SMA, FSP-1, FAP, FN1, VIM, CD29, CD10, and GPR77. The expression of these markers is unique to specific CAF subpopulations and demonstrates tissue-specific variation. Based on α-SMA and FAP expression levels, human breast cancer CAFs can be classified into four subtypes, designated CAF-S1 to CAF-S4. CAF-S1 and CAF-S4 are predominantly enriched within the tumor interior, CAF-S3 is more frequently observed in the peritumoral region, while CAF-S2 is uniformly distributed. The CAF-S1 subtype recruits Tregs and promotes their differentiation through the secretion of CXCL12. It also highly expresses immunomodulatory ligands such as PD-L2 and OX40L, mediating T cell exhaustion and enhanced Treg function, thereby driving immunosuppression and drug resistance [12]. In contrast, the CAF-S4 subtype lacks immunoregulatory capacity. scRNA-seq has further identified FSP-1-positive CAFs and PDPN positive CAFs, whose proportions are associated with BRCA mutations and clinical outcomes in triple-negative breast cancer [28].

The heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs profoundly influence the tumor microenvironment and tumor progression. Their differences from normal fibroblasts NFs are evident in the expression of specific protein markers. For instance, α-SMA-positive CAFs provide metabolic support for tumors by producing lactate and pyruvate [29]. VIM, a marker of cell structure and migration, contributes to advanced metastasis [30]. FAP accelerates tumor progression through ECM remodeling and fibrosis, and promotes the formation of an immunosuppressive TME [31–33]. Elevated levels of PDGFRα/β are also closely associated with CAF activation [34]. Research targeting these biomarkers provides a critical foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies for breast cancer.

CAFs promote breast cancer cell progression

TME is influenced by CAFs, which interact with the TME to modulate breast cancer cell progression. CAFs play a role in interfering with breast cancer cell metastasis, and the extensive proliferation of mammary connective tissue results in CAFs constituting up to 80% of the tumor mass, making them the most prevalent stromal cell component in the breast TME [33, 34]. The complexity of CAF-TME interactions is mutually influential [35], and the transformation and activation of CAFs are fundamental to cancer progression [36]. Upon activation, NFs promote cancer cell development through various mechanisms, including the secretion of growth factors, chemokines, and interleukins. Growth factors can directly activate fibroblasts, and CAFs also secrete a multitude of autocrine and paracrine cytokines, as well as other pro-tumor factors, to modulate the environment and create a microclimate conducive to tumor growth and metastasis [20, 37].

The most representative growth factor is transforming growth TGF-β, which plays a critical role in breast cancer progression by participating in ECM mechanical sensing and myofibroblast differentiation [38]. In the TME, CAFs secrete substantial amounts of TGF-β, activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway in breast cancer cells and driving tumor progression. The communication between CAFs and tumor cells is bidirectional: tumor cells can secrete TGF-β themselves or mediate the transformation of stromal fibroblasts into CAFs through paracrine signaling, altering the TME and promoting tumor development and dissemination.Studies have shown that Grem1, a bone morphogenetic protein antagonist produced by CAFs, promotes fibroblast activation and breast cancer cell infiltration and extravasation, driving the formation of micrometastases, which is the initial step in the invasion metastasis cascade [39]. Additionally, the lack of CAV-1 in fibroblasts leads to increased TGF-β secretion, activating the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway in breast cancer and promoting tumor metastasis [40]. Other growth factors also participate in remodeling the TME and facilitating cancer cell metastasis. For example, CAF secreted factors such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), nerve growth factor, connective tissue growth factor, and basic fibroblast growth factor are all involved in breast cancer metastasis and invasion.Among these, HGF secreted by CAFs exhibits a bidirectional interaction with breast cancer cells: breast cancer cells can reprogram surrounding NFs, transforming them into CAFs or key stage cells; meanwhile, HGF secreted by CAFs plays a significant role in breast cancer progression, with its secretion levels positively correlated with mammary tumorigenesis, cell migration, and invasive capacity [41], thereby driving the metastatic process. Breast cancer cells induce fibroblasts to secrete HGF, further enhancing the pro-tumor effects of CAFs and establishing a mutually reinforcing relationship.

Chemokines and their receptors mediate chemotaxis and are deeply involved in tumorigenesis and progression [42]. The association between CAFs and the chemokine CXCL12 is particularly significant: on one hand, chemokines secreted by CAFs regulate the cytoskeleton of mammary tumor cells, influencing their motility [43]; on the other hand, OPN secreted by cancer cells stimulates CAFs to secrete more CXCL12. The increased CXCL12 triggers EMT in tumor cells, enhancing their invasive and metastatic potential [44]. Additionally, CXCL12 produced by CAFs acts on endothelial cells, modulates the tumor microenvironment, and stimulates tumor cells through inflammatory mechanisms, activating the Notch1 signaling pathway and increasing CXCL8 production, thereby enhancing tumor cell metastatic activity [45] IL-6 is widely present in the tumor microenvironment. Studies have shown that IL-6 reduces the expression of the tumor suppressor HIC1 and promotes breast cancer progression through paracrine or autocrine signaling [46].

CAFs mediate breast cancer metastasis through exosomes, which are enriched with miRNAs that can be taken up by tumor cells to regulate target gene expression, promoting breast cancer progression and metastasis. Additionally, CAFs interact with stromal cells such as macrophages and TANs, participating in angiogenesis and ECM remodeling. Angiogenesis is critical for the formation of premetastatic niches, while ECM remodeling is a necessary step for tumor cell invasion and metastasis. CAFs primarily promote angiogenesis through the VEGF dependent classical pathway [47]. The binding of VEGF to VEGFR activates downstream signaling pathways, prompting vascular endothelial cells to develop into mature blood vessels, which supply nutrients to the tumor and remove metabolic waste, thereby facilitating tumor growth and metastasis.The ECM, composed of structural proteins, proteoglycans, and glycoproteins, has mechanical properties closely related to CAFs. Dynamic remodeling of the ECM leads to changes in tumor cell density, tissue stiffness, and structure, causing structural alterations in the TME due to force transmission, which creates conditions for directional cancer cell migration and invasion [48]. CAFs enhance tumor invasion and metastasis by promoting ECM protein deposition, secreting growth factors, and remodeling the ECM.

Targeting CAFs in breast cancer for therapeutic intervention

As shown in Fig. 11, synergistic therapeutic approaches targeting CAFs involve cellular reprogramming to deactivate their pro-tumor functions, combined with natural compounds and chemotherapeutic agents. In terms of immune regulation, the CAF-S1 subpopulation facilitates the recruitment and enhanced infiltration and survival of Tregs within the TME through the secretion of factors such as CXCL12, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive milieu. This process undermines antitumor immune responses and contributes to resistance to immunotherapy [12]. CAFs induce cytotoxic T cell death in an antigen independent manner by activating apoptotic ligands and LAG-3, further weakening anti-tumor immunity [49]. Studies have identified a senescent CAF population in breast cancer that promotes tumor progression by inhibiting the anti-tumor response of NK cells. senCAFs form physical or chemical barriers that impede NK cell infiltration into tumor cells and interfere with NK cell cytotoxicity through ECM secretion. Eliminating senCAFs significantly delays tumorigenesis, suggesting their potential as a therapeutic target in breast cancer [50]. As key cells in the TME, CAFs participate in tumor immune escape by communicating with other cells and secreting ECM components, modulating the functions of myeloid and lymphoid cells. Therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs may disrupt this process [49].

Synergistic Therapy for Breast Cancer via CAF-Targeting

TME in breast cancer influences cancer cell metastasis, with the transformation of normal stromal fibroblasts into CAFs being a critical step in the transition to a pre-cancerous microenvironment, as confirmed in studies [49]. Intervening in the activation state of CAFs is a key strategy to prevent breast cancer stroma formation, including inhibiting CAF activity to limit their functions or reprogramming active CAFs into quiescent fibroblasts. Both approaches may disrupt the formation of breast cancer stroma, offering new insights for early prevention and treatment of breast cancer [49].

Targeting CAFs for treatment can be achieved by disrupting the breast cancer tumor microenvironment. First, blocking communication between tumor cells and CAFs, such as targeting the TGF-β1/Smad or CXCL1/CXCR12 signaling axes, can inhibit tumor metastasis [49]. Disrupting the ECM, which acts as a protective barrier for tumors, is a potential strategy for anti-tumor therapy. Investigating the interactions between CAFs and immune cells, as well as the role of CAFs in anti-angiogenesis and estrogen related mechanisms particularly focusing on CAF hormone resistance in estrogen related breast cancer subtypes, may provide new insights for blocking CAF functions. CAF mediated chemotherapy resistance is a key mechanism by which they protect tumor cells. CAFs increase IFN expression through paracrine signaling, activating the IFN pathway to induce chemotherapy resistance, while IFN blocking antibodies can inhibit this effect. Chemotherapy-resistant breast cancer cells activate CAFs and induce resistance via the TGF-β/p44/42 MAPK signaling axis, and targeting this pathway may reverse CAF-mediated resistance. Additionally, radiotherapy resistance is associated with CAFs, and inhibiting Dll1-mediated Notch signaling post radiotherapy can reduce CAF numbers and enhance tumor cell radiosensitivity.

Emerging CAF-targeting strategies

In addition to traditional signaling pathway blockade strategies such as TGF-β and CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, various novel CAF-targeting approaches have emerged in recent years and gradually become research hotspots. The central role of FAP in heterogeneity and drug resistance-related clusters underscores its importance as a therapeutic target, a finding that naturally extends to cutting edge fields such as FAP-directed CAR-T therapy. Similarly, research foci on overcoming drug resistance (blue cluster) and enhancing immunotherapy (green cluster) have found technological solutions in nanoparticle delivery systems. By incorporating chemotherapy drugs into nanoparticle technology to improve targeting efficiency, nanoparticles can simultaneously inhibit CAF formation, enhance therapeutic efficacy, reduce toxicity, and activate the immune microenvironment [51, 52]. Current explorations are actively investigating the use of this technology to eliminate CAFs or disrupt their tumor promoting functions. Although emerging strategies like CAR-T therapy have not yet manifested in bibliometric data due to their novelty, they represent a natural progression of research trends. Specifically, FAP-specific chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (FAP-CAR-T) has demonstrated in breast cancer models the ability to selectively eliminate FAP⁺ CAFs, thereby disrupting the tumor stromal barrier and enhancing T-cell infiltration and anti-tumor immunity [31]. The strong co-occurrence of “metabolism” with tumor growth and stemness in the red cluster highlights the importance of targeting CAF metabolic reprogramming to cut off their energy supply to tumors.

Future

With advancements in medical technology, breast cancer treatment has developed mature strategies, including early screening, prevention, diagnosis, chemotherapy, and drug therapy. Targeting the immune regulation mediated by CAFs, the interaction signaling pathways between CAFs and tumor cells, and the metabolic mechanisms involved in breast cancer progression can lead to the development of CAF targeted drugs that block CAF driven tumor progression and overcome chemotherapy and radiotherapy resistance. Currently, CAFs have become a focal point in drug development as a therapeutic target for breast cancer. The heterogeneity of CAFs contributes to immune suppression, weakening anti-tumor immune responses and promoting breast cancer development.

However, the clinical translation of these targeting strategies still faces significant challenges, with most remaining in the exploratory stage: FAP targeted therapies such as FAP-CAR-T cells and antibody-drug conjugates are predominantly in the preclinical or early clinical trial phase .Their development is primarily limited by on-target/off-tumor toxicity, due to low-level FAP expression in bone marrow and healing tissues, as well as the high heterogeneity of the tumor stroma, leading to inconsistent treatment responses and considerable potential risks [31, 53]. PDGFRβ inhibitors, such as imatinib and nintedanib. Although evaluated in multiple clinical trials, their efficacy as monotherapies is generally limited and has not significantly improved patient outcomes. This highlights the redundancy and compensatory capacity of CAF signaling pathways, underscoring the need for future research to explore combination strategies with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy [32, 54]. α-SMA as a broadly expressed activation marker, directly targeting α-SMA itself is not feasible. Current strategies instead focus on modulating its upstream activation pathways, such as through the use of TGF-β inhibitors, or inhibiting its downstream functional outputs, for instance by interfering with ECM remodeling, thereby indirectly targeting myofibroblast like CAF subsets with high α-SMA expression [38, 55]. Bridging the gap between research findings and clinical application remains a major challenge and a key direction for future efforts. Moving forward, research should not only focus on refining immunotherapeutic strategies by modulating CAF activity to revert them to a quiescent fibroblast state but also prioritize: Developing patient stratification strategies based on CAF biomarker profiles to precisely identify populations likely to benefit from specific interventions; Designing innovative clinical trials that combine drugs targeting specific CAF subpopulations with existing therapies such as immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy to test synergistic effects; Leveraging novel delivery systems such as nanotechnology to enhance drug accumulation and specificity within the tumor stroma, overcome resistance mechanisms, and ultimately improve treatment outcomes for patients.

Advantages and disadvantages

The data for this study were sourced from the WOSCC, a comprehensive academic literature indexing database that covers renowned multidisciplinary journals and is updated daily. As a literature retrieval tool, WOSCC ensures the credibility and high quality of the literature data, providing a solid foundation for bibliometric analysis. In terms of analytical methods, three bibliometric tools, VOSviewer, CiteSpace, and WPS Office, were employed to conduct visual analysis of the relevant literature. This approach minimizes bias caused by subjective information filtering, ensuring the objectivity and accuracy of the research findings. Compared to traditional reviews, this study, through visual analysis and systematic review, presents a more intuitive and comprehensive overview of the hotspots and frontiers in the field of breast cancer related CAFs.

However, this study has several limitations. Owing to constraints in data sources, literature from non SCI journals or other databases may not have been included, which could affect the comprehensiveness of the findings. Additionally, the assessment of literature relies primarily on citation frequency and publication counts. While useful, these metrics may not fully capture the multidimensional value of research, such as its innovativeness or potential for practical application. Future studies should aim to incorporate broader data sources and additional evaluation metrics to provide a more holistic understanding.

It is also important to note that breast cancer represents a major global health challenge, yet significant geographical disparities exist in research resources and output. High income countries dominate the research agenda on CAFs, whereas regions with high incidence rates and poor prognoses are markedly underrepresented. This geographical bias may lead to a understanding of breast cancer biology that is derived from non-representative populations, thereby limiting the global applicability of research findings. Furthermore, it impedes the translation of knowledge into clinically effective strategies tailored to local contexts, ultimately exacerbating worldwide disparities in prognosis.To address these challenges, we propose the following recommendations: First, international research funding agencies should prioritize supporting substantive collaboration with scholars from high burden regions, rather than engaging in one way data or sample extraction. Second, investment in local research capacity building is essential to cultivate domestic talent and achieve a sustainable research ecosystem. Furthermore, future research designs should intentionally include more diverse populations to ensure the generalizability of findings.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the current research status of breast CAFs through bibliometric analysis, covering dimensions such as publication date, country, institution, author, and literature source, while also Summarizeing research trends, hotspots, and frontier directions. The results indicate that CAFs in breast cancer originate from diverse sources and play a crucial role in tumor development. CAFs participate in tumor microenvironment remodeling through multiple mechanisms, with angiogenesis and ECM remodeling being key prerequisites for tumor cell progression. In response to the tumor-promoting effects of CAFs, this study proposes strategies to target and block CAF related signaling pathways to inhibit cancer cell metastasis and invasion. The research also explores the interaction mechanisms between CAFs and breast cancer cells, suggesting potential methods to disrupt CAF mediated interference in the tumor microenvironment.

CAFs not only promote tumor cell progression but also protect tumor cells from the effects of anti-cancer drugs by secreting cytokines and growth factors, leading to drug resistance. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting CAFs hold promise for overcoming this protective effect and addressing the issue of anti-cancer drug resistance. However, research on CAF targeted therapies for breast cancer remains exploratory, requiring further in depth studies to elucidate their mechanisms of action and clinical application potential.
