Tumor-microenvironment-modulating microspheres to augment tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy against solid tumors
Authors: Mingkang Li, Shen Yan, Xiaoying Yan, Ziwei Nie, Haihan Wang, Qiaofeng Li, Haoqi Liu, Wenzhuo Yu, Shuai Zhang, Yanbin Liu, Hong Chen, Winston Duo Wu, Yu Chao, Zhuang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy is a T cell therapy approved for treating solid tumors. However, the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) result in limited numbers and activities of initially harvested TILs, further leading to time-consuming preparation processes and suboptimal treatment efficacy. Herein, we develop alginate-based hydrogel microspheres co-loaded with hyaluronidase (HAase) and chemokine CXCL9. With released HAase to degrade the extracellular matrix and CXCL9 to recruit lymphocyte infiltration, such microspheres after intratumoral injection allow effective TME modulation, leading to greatly enhanced TIL numbers in tumors. TILs collected from those pre-treated tumors exhibit not only accelerated ex vivo expansion but also markedly improved activities and anti-exhaustion capacities. Those TILs after re-infusion could significantly inhibit tumor growths and metastases in different tumor models. Our work demonstrates that modulating TME before tumor collection may be an effective and clinically practical approach to boost current TIL therapies.Keywords: tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, TILs, tumor microenvironment, TME, T cell infiltration, T cell exhaustion

Introduction

Cell-based immunotherapies against cancers have made tremendous progresses in recent years, displaying strong anti-tumor potential by using a patient’s own immune cells, particularly T cells, to recognize and attack cancer cells.1,2,3 In particular, several types of CAR-T therapy products have been approved for clinical use in recent years, achieving remarkable efficacy in treating hematological malignancies.4,5,6 However, the efficacy of CAR-T therapy against solid tumors remains to be unsatisfactory, owing to the inability of CAR-T cells to infiltrate into solid tumors with a dense extracellular matrix (ECM).7 Meanwhile, the high heterogeneity of solid tumors may prevent CAR-T cells from recognizing and targeting all tumor cell populations, leading to immune evasion and significantly impeded therapeutic outcomes.8,9 Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy is another type of T cell-based immunotherapy by isolating TILs from the patient’s tumor sample, expanding them ex vivo, and infusing them back into the same patient.10,11 This personalized therapy can target neoantigens that are tumor-specific, effectively reducing treatment resistance and recurrence caused by tumor heterogeneity.12 At the same time, these TILs, having naturally infiltrated the tumor microenvironment (TME), are equipped with chemokine receptors that enhance their chemotactic and infiltrative capabilities.13 Therefore, compared with other T cell-based therapies, TIL therapy shows significant advantages in treating solid tumors. In February 2024, lifileucel, developed by Iovance, received accelerated approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma.14 This is an approved TIL product and a T cell therapy for the treatment of solid tumors.15

However, current TIL therapy still faces many challenges. On the one hand, the number of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes that can be harvested from the dissected tumor sample is often quite limited, owing to the very low numbers of intrinsic TIL cells in patients’ solid tumors, especially those tumors with low immunogenicity.16 In particular, the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) of the tumor forms a physical barrier, severely restricting the infiltration of TILs into the tumor mass.17,18 The insufficient quantities of TILs collected from the tumor sample at the beginning would lead to a significant increase in the time that is required to expand these cells (e.g., 1 month) and even failure in achieving therapeutically sufficient quantities of TILs for the subsequent re-infusion.19 On the other hand, the immunosuppressive TME created by secretion of various immunosuppressive factors and metabolites, as well as interactions with tumor-associated immunosuppressive cells, would lead to metabolic reprogramming of TILs toward dysfunctional ones.20,21 Moreover, long-term exposure to tumor antigenic stimuli may cause exhaustion of TILs, affecting their effector functions and proliferative capacities.22,23,24 Therefore, TILs collected from the tumor are usually less active. Consequently, the survival time and effector functions of adoptively transferred TILs may not be optimal, resulting in weakened therapeutic efficacy. Those limitations of TIL therapy not only put serious constraints on its feasibility and effectiveness but also lead to its high cost.

Considering the critical role of TME in affecting the number of TILs in a solid tumor and their activities, in this work, we developed TME-modulating hydrogel microspheres to assist TIL therapy.25 In our design, hyaluronidase (HAase) and chemokine CXCL9 were loaded into calcium alginate hydrogel microspheres (HAase&CXCL9@ALG) by a spray freeze drying (SFD) technique.26,27 The microspheres prepared by this technology boast a high drug loading rate (∼100%) and are able to preserve the activities of biomolecules, while their porous spherical structure allows controlled release of the payloads. Those microspheres are then injected into mouse tumors. After being released from HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres, HAase by degrading hyaluronic acid, a key component of the tumoral ECM, could lead to loosening of the ECM to facilitate the tumor infiltration of immune cells,28,29,30 while CXCL9 by engaging with its receptor CXCR3 expressed on the surface of various immune cells could further attract the migration of effector T cells into the tumor.31,32 As a result, the infiltration of T cells in the tumor was remarkably increased, and more importantly, the proliferation and tumor-specific killing performance of those TILs were also significantly enhanced. Notably, TILs collected from HAase&CXCL9@ALG pre-treated tumors could proliferate to a quantity sufficient for adoptive transfer within only 10 days, and those cells exhibited enhanced effector functions, improved proliferation, and attenuated exhaustion. After intravenous reinfusion, TILs from the microsphere pre-treated group significantly inhibited tumor growth and metastasis and extended the survival time of mice, especially when used with immune checkpoint blockade therapy. This strategy effectively addresses the scarcity of TILs in solid tumors, greatly shortens the cell expansion and culture cycle, and significantly enhances the activities of TILs, promising for boosting clinically used TIL therapies.

Results

Preparation and characterization of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres

In our experiments, protein-loaded microspheres were prepared by SFD for large-scale production. A 4% sodium alginate solution was prepared, with or without the addition of CaCl2 (0%–0.1%). Functional proteins (HAase and CXCL9) were then mixed into the solution. Subsequently, the solution was atomized into monodisperse droplets using a microfluidic nozzle. The droplets were flash-frozen by spraying them into cold air and finally lyophilized into microspheres using a vacuum freeze dryer (Figure 1A).33 Scanning transmission microscopy (SEM) images revealed the porous structure of microspheres with 200–400 μm in diameter (Figure 1B). Different from microspheres prepared from pure sodium alginate that would rapidly dissociate in water, our ALG microspheres prepared by adding a low concentration of Ca2+ into sodium alginate solution (to induce partial crosslinking of alginate chains) before the SFD process showed greatly improved stability, maintaining their spherical structure for up to 7 days in aqueous solutions and under various physiological conditions (pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4 and enzymatic environments) (Figures 1C and 1D; Figures S1A–S1D). Tumor sections collected 7 days after intratumoral ALG-Ca2+ microsphere administration further corroborated the persistence of intact microsphere structures in vivo (Figure S1E). Moreover, the addition of Ca2+ solution modifies the pore size of the microspheres (Figures S1F and S1G). Higher concentrations of Ca2+ result in a more compact microsphere structure, which may be associated with slower drug release. However, excessively high concentrations of Ca2+ can cause direct gelation during solution preparation. To balance the needs in controlling drug release with the maintenance of fluidity in the ALG-Ca2+ solution for droplet ejection, we selected the formulation of 4% ALG and 0.1% Ca2+ for the preparation of ALG microspheres.

For loading of proteins, a protein solution was mixed with an ALG-Ca2+ solution (comprising 4% ALG and 0.1% Ca2+) and then processed into microspheres via the spray freeze-drying technique. In this system, sodium alginate functions both as a structural component of the microspheres and as a pharmaceutical excipient to stabilize the loaded proteins. Additionally, the spray freeze-drying technique, which involves rapid freezing and vacuum drying, plays a crucial role in maintaining the activity of the loaded proteins. Notably, this method allows 100% of added proteins to be encapsulated inside ALG-Ca2+ microspheres. Using Cy5.5-labeled bovine serum albumin (Cy5.5-BSA) as the model protein, we found that ALG-Ca2+ microspheres allowed the sustained release of BSA over a period of 7 days in the aqueous solution and under various physiological conditions (pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4 and enzymatic environments) (Figure 1E; Figure S1H). Similarly, HAase and CXCL9 could be co-loaded inside those microspheres (HAase&CXCL9@ALG) by the same method, and the encapsulated proteins also demonstrated sustained slow-release behaviors upon incubation in the aqueous solution (Figures 1F and 1G).

We then studied the in vivo release kinetics of loaded proteins from those microspheres. Free Cy5.5-BSA or Cy5.5-BSA@ALG microspheres were intratumorally (i.t.) injected into CT26 tumors on Balb/c mice. In vivo fluorescence imaging was employed to monitor BSA retention in the injected tumor. After 24 h of administration, the free protein diffused rapidly with less than 20% remaining in the tumor, whereas the ALG microspheres allowed 55% of BSA retention. The microspheres create a physical barrier that allows the loaded protein to persist in the tumor for up to 7 days (Figures 1H and 1I), favorable for extending the functions of loaded proteins. Furthermore, the ex vivo fluorescence imaging of major organs and time-dependent blood fluorescence signals collectively indicate that our microspheres could substantially reduce the systemic exposure of loaded proteins (Figures S1I–S1L).

Intratumorally injected HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres to recruit TILs

Next, we assessed the alterations in the TME following the administration of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (Figure 2A). It has been reported that HAase, by degrading hyaluronic acid, an important component of the ECM in the tumor tissues, can relieve tumor hypoxia, facilitate the tumor infiltration of various immune cells, and enhance their activities.28,29,30 Consistent with this mechanism, tumor sections from mice treated with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres demonstrated marked hyaluronic acid degradation, as shown by reduced Alcian blue staining (Figure S2A). In addition, CXCL9, also known as monokine induced by gamma-interferon (MIG), further attracts cells expressing the CXCR3 receptor, including activated T and NK cells.31,32 In order to verify whether the degradation of hyaluronic acid can reduce the interstitial pressure in tumors, we carried out blood perfusion experiments using lipid-coated ultrasound microbubbles. In our experiment, the CT26 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups and intratumorally injected with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; the untreated group), HAase@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg HAase), CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 μg/kg CXCL9), or HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg HAase, 2.5 μg/kg CXCL9). At 48 h post-injection, the ultrasound microbubbles were administered via the tail vein, and contrast-enhanced ultrasound imaging was employed to monitor the perfusion of microbubbles within the tumors of mice. The results showed that the intratumoral microbubble signals in the HAase&CXCL9@ALG and HAase@ALG groups were significantly higher than those in the other control groups (Figure 2B). This observation indicates that the hyaluronidase-mediated degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) can break down the physical barrier of tumor ECM, thereby enhancing the level of blood perfusion. As increased blood perfusion can deliver more oxygen to the tumor and thus alleviate tumor hypoxia, we performed immunofluorescence staining using hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) on tumor sections from mice in different treatment groups. The results demonstrated that the HIF-1α expression levels in tumors of the HAase&CXCL9@ALG and HAase@ALG groups were significantly lower than that in the untreated group, indicating substantial relief of tumor hypoxia. In contrast, CXCL9@ALG alone did not alter hypoxia levels, confirming that hypoxia relief was HAase-dependent (Figure 2C).

To further explore the impact of microspheres on the tumor immune microenvironment, we used flow cytometry to assess dynamic changes in tumor-infiltrating immune cell subsets post-microsphere treatment. CT26-tumor-bearing mice (Balb/c) were randomly divided into six groups (n = 4): (1) untreated, (2) ALG microspheres (0.1 g/kg, i.t.), (3) free HAase (2.5 mg/kg, i.t.) and CXCL9 (2.5 μg/kg, i.t.), (4) HAase@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.), (5) CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.), and (5) HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). Under the synergistic effect of HAase and CXCL9, the proportion of multiple immune cells infiltrated within the tumor increased, especially dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and T cells (Figure 2D; Figure S2B). Compared to the untreated group, HAase&CXCL9@ALG microsphere injection resulted in greatly increased proportions of DCs, NK cells, and T cells within the tumor, by 5- to 10-fold. Notably, compared to the free drug group (group 3), the microsphere delivery system (group 6) further boosted T cell infiltration by 3.3-fold, indicating that microspheres enhanced the synergistic effect of HAase and CXCL9 by prolonging their local action time.

Further analysis of the phenotype and functions of T cells revealed a significant increase in the proportions of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and Treg cells in the tumor, with the proportions of CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells increasing by more than 6-fold (Figure 2E; Figures S2B and S2C). In comparison, the percentage of Treg cells increased to a lesser extent. Meanwhile, we assessed alterations in multiple functions of T cells within tumors after microsphere treatment. Elevated expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 indicates enhanced T cell expansion within the tumor after HAase&CXCL9@ALG microsphere treatment (Figure 2F; Figure S2D), while higher levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2) indicated robust activation of T cells within the tumor, a critical step in the adaptive immune response (Figure 2G; Figure S2D). Notably, the effector-associated cytokines interferon gamma (IFN-γ) (Figure 2H; Figure S2D) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) (Figure 2I; Figure S2D) were also significantly upregulated, implying that treatment with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres resulted in enhanced cytotoxicity of intratumor T cells against tumor cells.

Under prolonged stimulation by tumor antigens, the effector function of T cells is gradually lost, with successive precursor exhaustion (PD-1+TIM-3−) and terminal exhaustion (PD-1+TIM-3+).34,35,36 Interestingly, we found that for tumors treated with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microsphere, the proportions of T cells at both precursor exhaustion and terminal exhaustion statuses were significantly reduced (Figures 2J and 2K; Figure S2D). The reduction in precursor depletion may be attributed to the fact that recruited neo-TILs have not yet been continuously stimulated by tumor antigens. The decrease in the degree of terminal depletion hints at the restoration of CD3+ T cell function within the tumor.

Moreover, to assess the T cell recruitment ability of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres, allogeneic luciferase-expressing T (Luc-T) cells were used for in vivo imaging. Luc-T cells were first intravenously injected into tumor-bearing nude mice, and bioluminescence signals of enriched T cells were detected at the tumor site 5 days post-injection of different microspheres (Figure S3A). Compared to the untreated group and HAase@ALG microsphere-treated group, significantly stronger and sustained luminescence signals were detected at the tumor sites injected with microspheres containing CXCL9 (CXCL9@ALG and HAase&CXLC9@ALG microspheres), demonstrating the chemotactic effect of CXCL9 in recruiting T cells to the tumor. Together, the above results confirmed that intratumoral treatment with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres can recruit more TILs to the tumor, and those TILs would show greatly enhanced activities with reversed exhaustion statues.

HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres trigger a powerful anti-tumor immune response

To elucidate the in vivo immune activation potential of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres, we conducted a sequenomics analysis. Mice bearing CT26 tumors were intratumorally injected with 0.1 g/kg HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca), and tumors were excised 7 days post-treatment for TIL isolation. Comprehensive analyses, including transcriptome sequencing (RNA sequencing [RNA-seq]) and immune repertoire sequencing (IR-seq), were conducted to assess both gene expression alterations in TILs and shifts in TCR clones following treatment. The sequencing results showed that 13 genes were up-regulated and 5 genes down-regulated (Figure 3A). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were analyzed to study the enrichment of regulated genes. The most significantly enriched 30 GO terms (Figure 3B) and pathway terms (Figure 3C) (Q value ≤0.05) were closely associated with antitumor immune responses. These differentially expressed genes were highly enriched in immune-activation-related genes, such as T cell activation and chemokine receptors, especially in core pathways directly regulating antitumor immune responses, including cytokine secretion pathways and T cell receptor signaling pathways.

The diversity of TCR clones, which serves as a pivotal biomarker for evaluating antitumor immune responses, allows T cells to recognize a wide range of tumor-specific antigens, thereby timely initiating anti-tumor immune responses and overcoming immune escape. Several studies have shown that the increased diversity of TCR clones is also associated with a favorable therapeutic prognosis.37,38 The complementary determining region 3 (CDR3) directly interacts with antigens, playing a key role in determining the antigen-binding specificity of the TCR and significantly influencing the diversity of the immune repertoire. Following treatment with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres, the CDR3 amino acid sequence feature of T cells in the tumor exhibited notable changes (Figures 3D, 3E, 3H, and 3I), showing significant increases in the diversity of V-J gene combinations (Figures 3F and 3J), the Shannon index (Figure 3G), and Simpson index (Figure 3K). Collectively, these findings indicate that treatment with microspheres resulted in an increased diversity of TCR clones in TILs, thus enhancing their specificity and recognition ability against tumor cells.

To more comprehensively evaluate the functions of TILs, we delved into the energy metabolism features of TILs in tumors post-microsphere treatment. Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (O2 consumption rate, OCR) are pivotal parameters for characterizing the metabolic status of cells, offering important insights into glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial functions. Following the addition of glucose and oligomycin, an ATP synthase inhibitor, the ECAR in TILs collected from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated tumors was markedly higher than that in the untreated control group (Figures 3L and 3M). This indicates a significant enhancement in the glycolytic capacity of TILs in microsphere-treated tumors, along with an increase in their metabolic activity, which is conducive to cell development and proliferation.39,40 Meanwhile, the increased oxygen consumption of TILs from microsphere-treated tumor further substantiates this observation (Figure 3N). Furthermore, analysis of mitochondrial function in TILs confirmed metabolic reprogramming after treatment, as evidenced by a higher mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure 3O), lower reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels (Figure 3P), and increased ATP production (Figure 3Q), collectively indicating a marked improvement in cellular metabolic fitness. Our results collectively evidence that HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres via modulating TME could activate anti-tumor immune-related signaling pathways of TILs, enhancing their ability to recognize tumor antigens and improving their metabolic activity.

HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres enhance TILs activity in multiple tumor models

Following the TILs preparation process, we dissociated surgically removed tumor tissues into single-cell suspensions, which were incubated with fluorescently labeled antibodies and then isolated TILs (CD3+T) using a sorting flow cytometer. Then, after ex vivo expansion, we examined their cytotoxicity (Figure 4A). Under standard T cell expansion conditions, the addition of anti-CD3 antibody and anti-CD28 antibody to the culture medium would provide stimulatory signals to activate those TILs, while continuous supplementation of IL-2 is necessary to support T cell expansion. CT26-tumor-bearing mice (∼100 mm3) were randomly divided into two groups including the untreated group and HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated group (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). TILs isolated from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated CT26 tumors not only possessed a much higher starting number but also demonstrated superior amplification rate, proliferating more than 80-fold ex vivo from 1.36 million to 113.1 million in 17 days, in marked contrast to only 18-fold of amplification, from 100,000 to 1.84 million, for TILs collected from the untreated tumors (Figure 4B). Carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dilution further confirmed the accelerated proliferative kinetics of TILs collected from tumors injected with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (Figure S3B). This is because TILs expansion is closely related to cell density41,42 and cell activity, and TILs collected from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated tumors showed a larger initial number, and stronger activity, providing more favorable conditions for cell expansion. Additionally, after the TME was improved, TIL exhaustion was alleviated and their metabolic activity was increased, supplying the necessary energy for cell proliferation.

In order to confirm the cytotoxic capacity of TILs on tumor cells, we co-cultured expanded TILs with CT26 tumor cells for 24 h at different effector-to-target cell ratios. Afterward, the suspended TILs were removed by washing, and the viabilities of the adherent tumor cells were assessed. As the TIL:tumor cell ratio increased, the survival rate of tumor cells significantly decreased. At a TIL-to-tumor-cell ratio of 1:5, the majority of tumor cells would be destructed (Figure 4C). It is noteworthy that the TILs collected from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-microsphere-treated tumors showed a stronger killing effect at the same ratio, mainly due to their enhanced cytokine secretion, higher metabolic activity, and lower exhaustion levels. We also demonstrated that the killing effect of TILs on tumor cells is specific. No cell killing effect was detected when TILs isolated from CT26 tumors were co-cultured with other types of tumor cells or normal tissue cells, guaranteeing the safety and tumor specificity of TIL therapy (Figure 4D).

To further assess the broad applicability of our therapeutic strategy, we selected two challenging tumor models: the highly aggressive 4T1 mouse breast cancer and the low-immunogenic H22 mouse liver cancer. In both models, treatment of tumors with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres similarly resulted in significant increases in the number of collected TILs and clear advantages in their ex vivo amplification rates (Figures 4E and 4H). As the TME of these two difficult-to-treat models would be much harsher compared to that in CT26 tumors, the numbers of TILs collected from untreated tumors were quite sparse. Notably, TILs isolated from untreated H22 tumors showed no visible ex vivo amplification. This likely reflects a more potent immunosuppressive microenvironment within the H22 tumor model, severely limiting TIL survival and expansion. Moreover, in the followed ex vivo cytotoxicity assays, TILs derived from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-microsphere-treated tumors exhibited significantly stronger killing abilities compared to those collected from untreated tumors (Figures 4F and 4I). As expected, the cell killing effects of those TILs also showed high tumor-cell-type specificity (Figures 4G and 4J). Those results demonstrate that HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres are broadly applicable to enhance the abundance and activities of TILs for different types of tumors.

HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres enhance TIL therapy

Next, we studied the ability of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microsphere to augment in vivo TIL therapy. First, we utilized the CT26 mouse colon cancer model as a representative case study (Figure 5A). CT26-tumor-bearing mice (∼100 mm3) without or with treatment of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.) were sacrificed 7 days later, with TILs isolated from those tumors and cultured for expansion. We found that HAase&CXCL9@ALG microsphere treatment, causing no significant systemic toxicity to the treated mice, slightly reduced the collected tumor weight, likely owing to the newly recruited TILs that contribute to partial tumor suppression (Figure S4). Notably, the number of total immune cells collected from tumors pre-treated with HAase&CXCL9@ALG showed 8-fold increase compared to those collected from untreated tumors (Figures S5A and S5B), and the proportions of total T cells, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells within the tumor all showed significant increase (Figures S5C–S5E).

In order to obtain sufficient numbers of TILs, we expanded isolated TILs ex vivo cultures under continuous IL-2 support after activation with anti-CD3 antibody and anti-CD28 antibody. The immunodeficient NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice lacking functional T cells, B cells, and NK cells were selected for treatment simulation of TILs therapy for avoiding the interference of mouse autoimmune system with exogenous therapeutic cells. In advance, 2 × 106 CT26 mouse colon cancer cells were inoculated into the dorsal subcutis of NSG mice for constructing subcutaneous tumor models. When the tumor size reached 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four groups: (1) blank, (2) untreated TILs (1 million), (3) activated TILs (1 million), and (4) activated TILs (10 million). TILs isolated from untreated CT26 tumors (untreated TILs) or HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated tumors (activated TILs) after ex vivo expansion were injected into the tumor-bearing mice via tail vein. During 2 weeks of monitoring, there was no significant change in the body weight of the mice, and the tumor growth was inhibited, especially on mice after the infusion of 10 million HAase&CXCL9@ALG-microsphere-activated TILs, which doubled the survival time of the tumor-bearing mice (Figures 5B–5D). More excitingly, compared to untreated TILs, HAase&CXCL9@ALG-activated TILs showed better tumor suppression even when infused with the same amount of cells (1 million), owing to the enhanced activities and modulated exhaustion status of TILs collected from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated tumors. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of tumor slices also demonstrated the potent efficacy of activated TILs in destructing tumor cells after infusion (Figure 5E).

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of TILs in vivo, we conducted a systematic analysis and evaluation of both the quantity and functional status of TILs within tumors of treated mice 10 days following ex vivo expansion and adoptive transfer (Figure 5A). After TILs were infused intravenously into tumor-bearing NSG mice, significant increases in the proportions of CD3+ T cells (Figure 5F; Figures S5F and S5G), CD4+ T cells (Figure 5G; Figures S5F and S5G), and CD8+ T cells (Figure 5H; Figures S5F and S5G) could be detected within their tumors, especially for those infused with HAase&CXCL9@ALG-activated TILs. The higher proportion of microsphere-activated TILs within the tumor relative to the same number of untreated TILs infused may be associated with their increased TCR clonal diversity, metabolic activity, and resistance to exhaustion. The functionality of TILs is also a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy. In tumors on mice infused with HAase&CXCL9@ALG-activated TILs, we observed a significant increase in the secretion of multiple cytokines by intratumoral T cells, including IFN-γ (Figure 5I; Figure S5I), TNF-α (Figure 5J; Figure S5I), and granzyme B (Figure 5K; Figure S5I).

In addition, anti-exhaustion capacity is also a significant factor. Tumor-reactive T cells would be progressively weakened under prolonged antigenic stimulation, and their PD-1 expression, a marker of T cell activation and an indicator of exhaustion, is elevated upon activation and continues to increase with the exhaustion process in the tumor microenvironment.43 T cell exhaustion is categorized into precursor exhaustion (PD-1+ TIM-3−) with their functions preserved and terminal exhaustion (PD-1+ TIM-3+) with their functions severely impaired.44 In our earlier experiment, we found that TILs collected from tumors pre-treated with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres predominantly displayed a PD-1− TIM-3− phenotype, suggesting that initially infiltrated TILs remained minimally activated (Figures 2J and 2K). Due to minimal exposure to the immunosuppressive TME, these microsphere-activated TILs more readily transitioned into effector states. After ex vivo expansion, microsphere-activated TILs had a higher percentage of precursor exhaustion and a lower percentage of terminal exhaustion. This observation indicates that these cells not only maintained functional integrity but were also more readily activated into effector states (Figure S6a). After infusing TILs into the body, some of the precursor-exhausted TILs would be transformed to the terminal exhaustion state after performing their effector functions, a transition more prominent in tumors of mice treated with untreated TILs. In contrast, activated TILs preserved a higher proportion of precursor-exhausted cells after reinfusion, which implies a longer lifespan and effector time (Figures 5L and 5M; Figure S5H).

Enhanced TIL therapy in combination with immune checkpoint blockade

In addition, we have also validated the effect of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres in a 4T1 mouse breast cancer model, which is highly invasive and tumorigenic. 4T1 tumors were orthotopically inoculated in the breast pads of Balb/c mice, and HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.) were intratumorally injected in each mouse when the tumor volume reached about 100 mm3 (Figure 6A). After 7 days of treatment, TILs were isolated. As expected, after HAase&CXCL9@ALG treatment, there was a significant increase in the number of TILs (Figures S6B–S6G) within the tumor by the synergistic effect of HAase and CXCL9.

To obtain sufficient TILs for treatment, the acquired TILs were stimulated with anti-CD3 antibody and anti-CD28 antibody and then amplified ex vivo under the maintenance of IL-2. Prolonged antigen exposure leads to upregulated expression of PD-1, which binds to PD-L1 and inhibits survival and metabolic signaling pathways, thereby attenuating T cell function.43 This process is further reinforced by transcription-factor-mediated epigenetic silencing of effector genes, promoting T cell exhaustion.45 This process occurs both during the ex vivo expansion process and within the tumor environment, as evidenced by our results. Across multiple models, TILs exhibit a progressive increase in PD-1 expression from initial isolation through ex vivo expansion and after adoptive transfer, consistent with exhaustion signatures (Figures 2J, 2K, 5L, 5M, and 6B–6E; Figures S5H and S6A).

Notably, the overexpression of PD-1 provides a mechanistic rationale for combining PD-1 blockade with TIL therapy. By blocking PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, immune checkpoint inhibitors may reverse epigenetic silencing and restore TIL functionality, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy.46,47 This synergistic approach aligns with current clinical TIL protocols and is supported by emerging clinical evidence.48 Therefore, to further optimize the antitumor efficacy of TIL therapy, we combined HAase&CXCL9@ALG-enhanced TIL therapy with anti-PD-1 therapy in a mouse model of breast cancer lung metastasis. In our experiments, we injected NSG mice via the tail vein with luciferase-expressing 4T1 cells (Luc-4T1, 5 × 105 per mice) to establish a lung metastasis model. After 7 days, mice were randomly divided into four groups: (1) blank, (2) anti-PD1(20 μg), (3) activated TILs (5 million), and (4) anti-PD1 (20 μg) + activated TILs (5 million). We administered anti-PD-1 antibody (20 μg per mice) via tail vein injection on days 1 and 5 post-TIL infusion.

We next monitored the treated mice by in vivo bioluminescence imaging (Figures 6F and 6G). It was found that neither anti-PD1 therapy nor TIL therapy alone showed satisfactory therapeutic effect in treating this rather aggressive tumor model, and tumor metastases in those groups were only slightly delayed compared to that in untreated mice. Excitingly, for mice receiving the combination of activated TILs and anti-PD1 antibody, their tumors were much more effectively suppressed and even eliminated. Compared to the transient prolongation of survival for mice treated with activated TILs only, the combination of TILs and anti-PD-1 antibody significantly extended the mouse survival, with four out of seven mice completely cured and survived for over 60 days (Figure 6H). These results demonstrate that combining immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as anti-PD-1 antibodies, with TIL infusion can further enhance the efficacy of TIL therapy. This combinatorial approach facilitates the effective eradication of metastatic lesions and significantly prolongs survival.

Discussion

In this work, we propose a strategy to boost the efficiency and efficacy of TIL therapy by modulating TME before harvesting TILs from tumors. In our design, ALG-based microspheres with loading of both HAase and CXCL9 are fabricated with an SFD method, which enables large-scale standardized production of protein-loaded microspheres with 100% loading efficiency and sustained release profiles. Notably, the lyophilized microspheres showed <5% moisture with good batch-to-batch reproducibility. Since the freeze-drying workflow follows established pharmaceutical protocols, this platform has clear feasibility for future GMP production. When HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres are injected into the tumor, the released HAase from the microspheres could degrade hyaluronic acid in the ECM of the tumor tissue to enhance blood perfusion, relieve hypoxia, and facilitate infiltration of TILs inside those tumors. Meanwhile, the sustained release of CXCL9 mediates the chemotactic recruitment of polyclonal effector T cells into the TME. This heterogeneous T cell population, characterized by diverse TCR complexes, significantly broadens the spectrum of tumor antigen recognition, thereby effectively counteracting immune evasion mechanisms. This creates a favorable environment that enhances T cell priming and trafficking, overcomes physical barriers, and rescues exhausted TILs, collectively broadening tumor antigen recognition and promoting stronger anti-tumor responses. As a result, we could harvest much more TILs from HAase&CXCL9@ALG-treated tumors, and those TILs exhibit much faster ex vivo expansion rates and greatly improved activities in cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion.

As verified in different mouse tumor models, such a strategy could greatly shorten the ex vivo expansion process of TILs. Importantly, HAase&CXCL9@ALG-activated TILs after infusing back into tumor-bearing mice offer significantly enhanced therapeutic performance in tumor suppression, compared to untreated TILs with the same cell number. Notably, in mice treated with HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres, the proportion of precursor exhausted T cell subsets (PD-1+TIM-3−) within TILs exhibited a rapid increase following ex vivo expansion and adoptive transfer. This phenotypic shift not only confirms the functional activation of reinfused TILs but also suggests potential synergistic therapeutic effects with immune checkpoint blockade. In the 4T1 metastatic lung cancer model, the combinatorial regimen integrating microsphere-enhanced TILs and PD-1 antibodies demonstrated a remarkable synergistic efficacy, significantly prolonging survival duration compared to monotherapy controls, and resulted in complete remission of tumors on four out of seven mice.

A major challenge in current TIL therapy lies in the inherent scarcity of intratumoral TILs and their functional exhaustion, which significantly prolongs the ex vivo expansion period, limits the clinical applicability, and compromises the therapeutic efficacy. Current advancements in TIL technology primarily focus on two strategic approaches: (1) optimizing ex vivo culture systems (e.g., refining cytokine cocktails and co-stimulatory signaling) to shorten manufacturing timelines and (2) enhancing T cell functionality through checkpoint blockade antibodies to delay exhaustion. Herein, we demonstrate that intratumoral injection of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres by remodeling the TME could effectively enhance the intratumoral infiltration of TILs, whose metabolic activities are also markedly enhanced. Notably, those newly recruited TILs, having undergone less chronic antigen exposure, retain a stronger resistance to exhaustion, preserving their proliferative and effector potential. With greatly accelerated ex vivo expansion kinetics, those activated TILs after expansion exhibit increased cytokine secretion, superior cytotoxic function, and improved persistence, which are critical for sustained antitumor immunity. The concurrent improvement in the quantity and quality of TILs not only streamlines the TIL expansion process to significantly shorten the manufacturing timeline but also largely improves the final therapeutic outcome of TIL therapy against solid tumors. In future clinical practices, intratumoral injection of such TME-modulating agent can be carried out ∼1 week before collecting the tumor sample from this particular tumor to harvest TILs. Therefore, our work provides a clinically feasible strategy to optimize the current TIL therapy, the only approved T cell therapy against solid tumors, showing advantages in shorter preparation time and better therapeutic performance.

Limitations of the study

Although this study systematically demonstrates the potential of TME pretreatment in enhancing TIL therapy, several aspects warrant further exploration. While this study provides important mechanistic insights based on multiple mouse tumor models, future integration of human tumor samples and more clinically relevant models will be helpful to evaluate the clinical translational potential of this strategy. On the other hand, the observed enrichment of pre-exhausted T cells (PD-1+TIM-3−) correlates with enhanced antitumor activity and responsiveness to PD-1 blockade. Further analysis of this phenotype’s role in T cell longevity and treatment durability will deepen our understanding of the mechanism.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Female Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks) and female NSG mice (6–8 weeks) were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Experimental Animals. All mouse studies were performed according to the protocols approved by the Soochow University Laboratory Animal Center.

In Balb/c mice, subcutaneous tumor models were established by injecting 2 × 106 CT26, 4T1, or H22 cells into the right flank, while an orthotopic breast cancer model was established by injecting 2 × 106 4T1 cells into the mammary fat pad. Tumor size was calculated as follows: volume = (tumor length) × (tumor width)2/2. Treatment started when the average mouse tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3.

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animals Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (approval ID: SUDA20250308A04).

Cell lines

Mouse Embryonic Fibroblast Cells (NIH/3T3), mouse melanoma cells (B16), mouse glioma cells (Gl261) were bought from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured in DMEM/High (Absin) medium containing 10% feral bovine serum (Inner Mongolia Jinyuankang Biotechnology Co., Ltd, JYK-FBS-301), 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin (Procell). Mouse colon cancer cells (CT26), mouse breast cancer cells (4T1) were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% feral bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin. Mouse liver cancer cells (H22) were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures and cultured with RPMI 1640 medium containing 20% feral bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin. Mouse TIL cells were cultured with TexMACS GMP Medium (Miltenyi) containing 5 μg/mL CD3, 5 μg/mL CD28, and 10 ng/mL IL-2 (PeproTech) at 37°C in 5% CO2. Culture dishes were purchased from NEST Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All cell lines were tested by short tandem repeat DNA profiling analysis and mycoplasma-free.

Method details

Preparation and characterization of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres

HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres were prepared by spray freeze-drying technique. 8% ALG (Qingdao Bright Moon Seaweed Group Co. Ltd) aqueous solution and 0.2% Ca2+ aqueous solution were mixed in equal volumes with stirring. Afterward, 1 mg/mL HAase and 1 μg/mL CXCL9 were added to the ALG-Ca2+ solution. The microspheres were prepared from this mixture solution by microfluidic atomization, −50°C cold air freezing, and vacuum freeze-drying. BSA and HAase were fluorescently labeled by reacting with Cy5.5-NHS sulfo-ester (Duofluor Inc) in PBS at 4°C under gentle stirring overnight. The quantification of protein concentration was carried out using a BCA assay. Various microspheres of ALG, HAase@ALG, CXCL9@ALG, Cy5.5-BSA@ALG, and Cy5.5-HAase@ALG were successfully prepared. The size of microspheres dispersed in water was analyzed by the laser diffraction method (LA960, HORIBALtd., Kyoto, Japan). Laser diffraction (HELOS-RODOS, SYMPATEC GmbH, Germany) was employed to determine the particle size distribution of dry microspheres at a dispersion pressure of 0.5 bar.

Drug release from ALG-based microspheres

Drug release assays were performed by placing 10 mg of Cy5.5-BSA@ALG microspheres or Cy5.5-HAase@ALG microspheres into Transwell in 1 mL of aqueous solution and under various physiological conditions (pH 5.5, 6.5, 7.4, and enzymatic environments). Released Cy5.5-BSA and Cy5.5-HAase were analyzed by a multi-functional microplate reader, and the total fluorescence value of equal mass of microspheres after complete cleavage by 100 mM EDTA solution was recorded as the initial dose. The drug release assay was performed by placing 1 mg of CXCL9@ALG microspheres into a Transwell in 1 mL of water, and released CXCL9 was determined by the MIG ELISA kit.

Ex vivo culture expansion of TILs in mice

Mouse TILs were isolated by flow cytometry sorting, activated with anti-CD3 (5 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (5 μg/mL) antibodies, and expanded ex vivo in TexMACS GMP Medium containing recombinant IL-2 (10 ng/mL). Every 2–3 days, cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in fresh TexMACS GMP Medium supplemented with IL-2 (10 ng/mL), and passaged at a 1:2 to 1:4 ratio to maintain an optimal density of 5 × 105 cells/mL.

Cytocidal toxicity of TILs

TILs derived from the CT26 and 4T1 mouse tumor model were respectively co-cultured with their corresponding tumor cell lines (CT26 or 4T1) as well as with unrelated cell lines (3T3, GL261, and B16) in well plates for 24 h, after removal of TILs, the viability of the remaining cells was assessed using an MTT assay to evaluate the tumor-specific cytotoxicity of TILs.

TILs derived from the H22 mouse tumor model were co-cultured for 72 h with their corresponding tumor cell line (H22) as well as unrelated cell lines (3T3, GL261, and B16) in well plates. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay after subtracting background values from TIL-only controls.

Intra-microsphere drug retention in tumors

CT26 tumor-bearing mice were intratumorally injected with either Cy5.5-BSA or Cy5.5-BSA@ALG, normalized to an equivalent Cy5.5-BSA dose. In vivo fluorescence imaging was carried out at different time points after injection (0, 5, 10, 24, 48, 72 and 120 h).

CT26 tumor-bearing mice were intratumorally injected with either Cy5.5-HAase or Cy5.5-HAase@ALG, normalized to an equivalent Cy5.5-HAase dose. In vivo fluorescence imaging was carried out at different time points after injection (0, 24 and 72 h). Ex vivo fluorescence imaging was carried out at 72 h. Peripheral blood was collected from mice at different time points (0, 24 and 72 h), and plasma was isolated. The fluorescence intensity of Cy5.5 was measured by a multi-functional microplate reader.

Assessment of tumor hypoxia status

For immunofluorescence staining, CT26 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups and intratumorally injected with PBS, HAase@ALG (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca), CXCL9@ALG (2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca), or HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca). After 48 h of treatment, tumors were excised, and tissues were processed into frozen sections for analysis. Sections were fixed in acetone for 15 min and then washed three times with PBS. The sections were then co-incubated with HIF-1 alpha monoclonal antibody (1:200 dilution) fluorescent primary antibodies for more than 4 h. The sections were washed 3 times with PBST-2 (1× PBS with tween 20) and then incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies FITC (APExBIO, Houston, USA, K1201) for 90 min. After washing with PBST-2 for 3 times, the sections were incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 15 min. After washing, the sections were photographed with a confocal microscope (Nikon).

Detection of intra-tumor perfusion status

For detecting intra-tumor perfusion status, CT26 tumor-bearing mice (Balb/c) were randomly divided into two groups: (i) untreated, (ii) HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). After 48 h of treatment, mice were intravenously injected with ultrasound contrast agent microbubbles USphere (ClinBridge Biotech) and imaged by the Vevo LAZR Small Animal Ultrasound Imaging System, with ultrasound gel applied to the skin surface at tumor before imaging.

In vivo flow cytometry

For analysis of immune cells in tumors, CT26 tumor-bearing mice (Balb/c) were randomly divided into six groups: (i) blank, (ii) ALG microspheres (0.1 g/kg, i.t.), (iii) free HAase (2.5 mg/kg, i.t.) and CXCL9 (2.5 μg/kg, i.t.), (iv) HAase@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.), (v) CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.), and (vi) HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.).

On day 7 after the treatment, the tumor tissues were collected and then homogenized in cold phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) buffer in the presence of digestive enzymes, forming single-cell suspensions. The cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 first and then stained with fluorophore-labelled antibodies (Biolegend) CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD11b, CD11c, CD49b, Ly6G, γδTCR, Foxp3, Ki-67, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, PD1, and TIM-3 for 30 min at room temperature before analysis by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria III). For markers whose antibody binding sites are intracellular, such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, Foxp3 and Ki67, cell membranes were fixed and permeabilized with Fixation Diluent and Permeabilization Diluent (eBioscience) before intracellular staining.

T cell tracking in vivo

To analyze the migration of T cells in vivo, lentivirus-transduced Luc-T cells were pre-infused into CT26 tumor-bearing nude mice, which were then randomly divided into four groups (n = 3): (i) blank, (ii) HAase@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.) (iii) CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.), (iv) HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). After drug administration, the in vivo migration of T cells was tracked by the PE small animal in vivo bioluminescence imaging system imaging at days 0, 2, 5, and 7.

RNA&TCR sequencing and analysis

Balb/c mice bearing CT26 tumors were randomly divided into two groups: untreated and HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). On day 7 after the treatment, tumors were removed and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were sent to Azenta Life Sciences for TIL isolation, and the TILs were subjected to RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing.

Analysis of TIL energy metabolism

To analyze the effect of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres on TIL metabolic activity, equal numbers of freshly isolated TILs were placed in a 24-well plate and examined by Seahorse XFe24 analysis meter (Agilent). Different drugs (glucose, Oligomycin, FCCP and antimycin A) were added into the dosing orifice around the plate and injected into the sample according to the preset procedure. The Seahorse would record the oxygen consumption rate (OCR, pMoles/min) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR, mpH/min) of cells in real-time.

Isolation of TILs

To obtain TIL from different tumor models, we randomly divided mice with tumor-bearing CT26, 4T1 and H22 into two groups: (i) untreated, (ii) HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). On day 7 after the treatment, the tumor tissues were collected and then homogenized in cold phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) buffer in the presence of digestive enzymes, forming single-cell suspensions. The cells were stained with fluorophore-labelled antibodies CD45, CD3, CD4, and CD8 for 30 min at room temperature before sorting by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria III).

Reinfusion therapy for TILs

For the CT26 mouse tumor model, 2×106 CT26 cells were transplanted into the right flanks of mice (NSG). Treatment started when the average mouse tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3. The CT26 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6): (i) blank, (ii) untreated TILs (1 million), (iii) activated TILs (1 million), and (iv) activated TILs (10 million). Expanded TILs were infused back into the mice through the tail vein.

For the 4T1 mouse lung metastasis model, 5× 105 Luc-4T1 cells were injected into mice (NSG) through the tail vein. Mouse tumor was status analyzed by the PE small animal in vivo optical imaging system. Treatment started when the average bioluminescence intensity reached ∼5 × 105p/sec/cm2/sr. The 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 7): (i) blank, (ii) anti-PD1 (20 μg), (iii) activated TILs (5 million), and (iv) anti-PD1 (20 μg) + activated TILs (5 million). Expanded TILs were infused back into the mice through the tail vein.

Immunologic evaluation after TILs reinfusion therapy

For analysis of immune cells in the tumor after TILs reinfusion, CT26 tumor-bearing mice (NSG) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6): (i) blank, (ii) untreated TILs (1 million), (iii) activated TILs (1 million), (iv) activated TILs (10 million).

On day 10 after the treatment, the tumor tissues were collected and then homogenized in cold phosphate-buffered solution (PBS) buffer in the presence of digestive enzymes, forming single-cell suspensions. The cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 first and then stained with fluorophore-labelled antibodies (Biolegend) CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2, Granzyme B, PD1 and TIM-3 for 30 min at room temperature before analysis by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria III). For markers whose antibody binding sites are intracellular, cell membranes were fixed and permeabilized with reagents Fixation Diluent and Permeabilization Diluent before intracellular staining.

Evaluation of TIL cell function

During the ex vivo expansion process, TILs were labeled with CFSE to monitor their proliferation. After ex vivo expansion, TILs were incubated with anti-CD16/32 first and then stained with fluorophore-labelled antibodies CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, PD1, and TIM-3 for 30 min at room temperature before analysis by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria III).

Analysis of TIL mitochondrial function

To evaluate the effect of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres on TIL mitochondrial function, TILs were stained with TMRE and MitoSOX to assess mitochondrial membrane potential and reactive oxygen species levels, respectively. Cellular ATP production was further quantified using an ATP assay kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Safety evaluation

For evaluating the in vivo safety of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres, CT26 tumor-bearing mice (Balb/c) were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5): (i) blank, (ii) HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres (2.5 mg/kg of HAase, 2.5 μg/kg of CXCL9, 0.1 g/kg ALG-Ca, i.t.). On day 7 after the treatment, venous blood was collected from mice for blood biochemistry and hematology tests. Then, the mice were euthanized, and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and skin) were collected for Hematoxylin Eosin staining (Shandong Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd) to determine the systemic safety of HAase&CXCL9@ALG microspheres.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All results are expressed as means ± Standard error of the mean (SEM) as indicated. Statistical analysis was conducted via GraphPad Prism software 6. One-way analysis of variance was used for multiple comparisons when more than two groups were compared, and Student’s t test was used for two-group comparisons. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001. Genes differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 software between two different groups (and by edgeR between two samples). The genes with the parameter of false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥2 were considered differentially expressed genes. Differentially expressed genes were then subjected to enrichment analysis of GO functions and KEGG pathways.
