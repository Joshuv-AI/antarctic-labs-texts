Exploring the indicative role of ACT score in improving lung function in asthmatic children undergoing dust mite immunotherapy
Authors: Peilin Zhang, Tao Ai, Li Wang, Ronghua Luo, Yaping Duan, Yinghong Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to explore the indicative role of Asthma Control Test (ACT) score in the use of dust mite immunotherapy for asthmatic children by comparing the recovery of lung function in these children who have different ACT scores with or without undergoing dust mite immunotherapy, and to provide a more accurate and applicable method to select the population for dust mite immunotherapy.MethodsA total of 200 children diagnosed with asthma who had tested positive for dust mite allergens from February 2019 to January 2021 were divided into the observation group and control group based on whether sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) for dust mite allergy was incorporated into their regular asthma treatment. These two groups were further subdivided into ACT > 19 and ACT ≤ 19 groups based on the ACT scores before treatment. After 1 year of follow-up, the lung function and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) levels of these four groups were compared before and after treatment.ResultsA total of 160 asthmatic children completed the study. We found that the observation group with ACT > 19 showed better improvement in forced vital capacity (FVC) (z = -2.338 P = 0.019), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) (t = 3.02 P = 0.007), forced expiratory flow at 50% of FVC (FEF50) (t = 2.807 P = 0.012), and forced expiratory flow at 25% of FVC (FEF25) (t = 3.032 P = 0.007) than the control group with ACT > 19. Moreover, the observation group with ACT ≤ 19 showed significantly better improvement in FVC (z = -3.554 P < 0.001), FEV1 (z = -3.71 P < 0.001),forced expiratory flow at 75% of FVC (FEF75) (z = -3.8 P < 0.001), FEF50 (z = -3.733 P < 0.001), FEF25 (z = -5.593 P < 0.001), maximal mid-expiratory flow (MMEF) (z = -3.930 P < 0.001), and FeNO (z = -2.218 P = 0.027) than the control group with ACT ≤ 19. We found that in the observation group, irrespective of whether ACT > 19 or ACT ≤ 19, there was some improvement in all indicators, but there was no significant difference between the two subgroups. In the control group, patients with ACT > 19 showed significantly less improvement in FEV1 and FEF75 than patients with ACT ≤ 19 (z = -2.567 P = 0.010, t = 2.624 P = 0.010, respectively).ConclusionsImmunotherapy for dust mites is effective in improving lung function in children with asthma, especially in those with ACT > 19 before treatment.Keywords: ACT score, Immunotherapy, Lung function, Asthma, Children

Background

Asthma is a chronic airway inflammatory disease. With the development of the economy and society and with changes in disease patterns, asthma in children is becoming an important challenge threatening the health of children [1]. For the long-term management of asthmatic children, a stepwise approach is currently recommended. Medications primarily include inhaled corticosteroids, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and the use of β-receptor agonists as needed, as well as emerging treatments, such as immunotherapy [2].

Dust mite immunotherapy, a form of specific immunotherapy, includes sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) and subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT). It is currently widely used in clinical settings and has achieved good therapeutic effects [3]. In particular, SLIT, being a non-invasive measure, is more readily accepted by children and their guardians. However, due to the long course of treatment and high economic costs, more targeted selection of treatment subjects has become a pressing issue.

The Asthma Control Test (ACT) score, a commonly used scoring method for asthma, is currently widely applied in clinical practice. Many studies have found that the ACT score is not only related to symptom control in children with asthma but also to lung function assessment [4]. Further, some researchers have studied the relationship between the ACT score and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), but no consensus has yet been reached [5, 6]. An increasing number of studies have shown that children who receive immunotherapy show better improvements in the ACT score and better lung function recovery compared to those who do not receive immunotherapy [7, 8].

A number of studies found SLIT could improve the lung function of children with asthma [9, 10].Also, In the last research, we have pointed to similar findings [11].However, we have not found any studies to explore the more targeted selection of treatment subjects for SLIT in improving lung function in children with asthma.

Thus, this study aims to investigate the role of ACT score in indicating the use of dust mite immunotherapy in children with asthma, by the following two goals: 1)investigate recovery of lung function in asthmatic children with or without undergoing dust mite immunotherapy;2)investigate recovery of lung function in these children who have different ACT scores before treatment.

Methods

Patients and study duration

From February 2019 to January 2021, 200 children were diagnosed with asthma and had tested positive for dust mite allergens.

Eligibility criteria and treatment

Children with asthma diagnosed by respiratory specialists in our hospital, whose skin prick tests indicated positive results for dust mite allergy, were included. Children with acute asthma attacks at the beginning of therapy were excluded. All of them were treated with inhalation of glucocorticoids regularly and β-receptor agonists as needed.

Patient grouping

Children were divided into the following 2 groups: (1) Observation group (100 cases): children received additional treatment with sublingual dust mite drops. (2) Control group (100 cases): children did not receive any additional treatment with other drugs. According to ACT scores, the children in the observation and control groups were further divided into the ACT>19 group and ACT ≤ 19 group.

ACT scoring

ACT scoring was conducted by the guardians of children when they were included, by referring to seven questions. The first four questions were asked to the children, and these questions enquired about how they thought their asthma was, whether it affected their exercise, whether they coughed, and whether they often woke up at night because of asthma; the last three questions were asked to their guardians, and these questions enquired about the number of days in the past 4 weeks when their children had asthma symptoms during the day, whether they heard wheezing sounds during the day, and whether their children woke up at night due to asthma.

Evaluation

Lung function and FeNO levels were assessed before and after treatment in the children included, conducted by specialists who didn’t know if the patients had accepted dust mite immunotherapy. The testing of lung function and the evaluation of FeNO were mentioned in our previous study [9].

Statistical analysis

SPSS software was used for data statistics. T-test was used for continuous variables with normal distribution. Mann-Whitney U-test was used for continuous variables with non-normal distribution. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Follow-up results

We followed up all 200 children with asthma. After one year of follow-up, 160 children were included in this study. Among the 40 children excluded, 8 experienced acute asthma attacks, 14 were lost to follow-up, and 18 withdrew from the study. In the end, there were 71 children in the observation group and 89 children in the control group.

Comparison of basic parameters among groups

There were no statistically significant differences in age or gender between the observation and control groups. In the observation group, 7 children had ACT > 19 and 64 children had ACT ≤ 19. In the control group, 13 children had ACT > 19 and 76 children had ACT ≤ 19. On pairwise comparison, the above four groups showed no statistically significant differences in age or gender.

Comparison of lung function-related parameters among groups

Our preliminary study [11] has already compared the lung function and FeNO levels of the observation group and the control group. Apart from forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory flow at 25% of FVC (FEF25), there were no statistically significant differences in other indexes. The observation group showed better improvements in FVC and FEF25 compared to the control group.

Discussion

The methods of treatment for asthma in children are gradually being updated, with immunotherapy becoming an important alternative treatment for asthmatic children. Whether it is desensitization therapy targeting allergens or monoclonal antibodies against IgE or cytokines, these treatments offer new hope for children with asthma, especially those with refractory asthma [12, 13]. Allergen immunotherapy (AIT) is considered a disease-modifying anti-asthmatic drug, which can prevent allergic asthma, reduce the frequency of asthma exacerbations, and enhance asthma control [14, 15]. AIT is always conducted using the following two approaches: SCIT and SLIT. However, both SCIT and SLIT have a modestly increased risk of adverse events, particularly SCIT [16]. Additionally, specific immunotherapy takes a long time to conduct [12] and is relatively expensive; thus, it is necessary to optimize the screening measures for children with asthma using this type of therapy.

Pulmonary function tests are of great significance for the diagnosis, efficacy evaluation, and prognosis assessment of asthma [1]. A systematic review of 45 articles found that in early life, asthma, wheezing, and allergy manifestations can affect the development of children’s lung function, both large and small airways [17]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis [9] found that SLIT along with normal anti-asthma therapy could significantly improve the upper and lower airway scores, including FEV1 and MEF25. Djuric-Filipovic I [10] included 59 children with asthma and followed them for two years. They found that SLIT combined with regular therapy effectively and consistently improved FEV1 in these children.

The ACT score, as a method for evaluating asthma control, has been increasingly associated with lung function in children with asthma. Ozoh OB [18] found that the ACT score is closely related to FEV1% predicted. Loman DG [19] also discovered this relationship, and when ACT was ≤ 19, the sensitivity and specificity for FEV1 at or below 80% predicted were 44.9% and 66.4%, respectively. The impact of specific immunotherapy on ACT is also significant [7, 20–22]. Zhang AZ [7] found that patients who did not receive specific immunotherapy had significantly higher ACT scores compared to those who used SLIT for 1, 2, or 3 years with single allergens or the 1-to 2-allergen group, and the scores were also significantly higher than those in patients who used SLIT for 3 years with the 3-or-more-allergen group. Puerto JA [20] found that among patients whose ACT scores indicated uncontrolled conditions before specific immunotherapy, 46.7% were well-controlled and 23.3% were completely controlled after receiving specific immunotherapy.

Our previous study [11] found that SLIT helps improve lung function and asthma control in children with asthma. In this study also, we discovered that whether the ACT of children with asthma was >19 or ≤ 19 before treatment, routine therapy combined with SLIT can better improve FVC, FEV1, and small airway resistance compared to the use of routine therapy alone. Additionally, we found that children with asthma who had ACT >19 before treatment need SLIT more urgently than those with ACT < 19, showing greater improvements in FEV1 and FEF75, thus allowing for the identification of the most suitable population for SLIT use.

FeNO, an objective indicator of type 2 asthma, represents the airway type 2 inflammatory response. A decrease in the FeNO level can, to some extent, indicate better control of type 2 inflammation [23]. Liao F [24] found that SLIT, used either in the morning or evening, improved the FeNO level in children after 1 year, and a lower FeNO level was reported when SLIT was used in the evening. Similarly, Djuric-Filipovic I [10] also demonstrated that SLIT in combination with regular therapy reduced the FeNO level in children with asthma. We found that for asthmatic children with ACT < 19 before treatment, SLIT in combination with regular therapy caused better improvement in the FeNO level compared to the no combination group, suggesting that even with low ACT scores, SLIT may depress airway type 2 inflammation in children with asthma. However, due to the limitations of conducting this test comprehensively in children, we could only assess the FeNO levels in the entire airway, which may have introduced confounding factors in children with concurrent allergic rhinitis. Thus, more detailed grouping and further assessment of concentration of nitric oxide in the alveolar or acinar region are needed.

Our study has certain limitations. First, the heterogeneity in our sample size may introduce deviation. In future research, we will expand the cohort to include more children with asthma. Second, as previously noted, FeNO detection results require further refinement due to limitations in pediatric testing, which is essential for detailed assessment of large airway and small airway.

In future, we believe the indication of SLIT in asthmatic children will be increasingly valued.We can further discuss the efficacy of SLIT in children with asthma, such as in different regions or under different educational levels of guardians, so as to establish a more accurate SLIT indication model.

Conclusions

We conclude that SLIT is effective for the improvement of lung function in asthmatic children, and those with ACT > 19 before treatment seem to need SLIT on a more urgent basis.
