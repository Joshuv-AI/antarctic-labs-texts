Comparing Microbial Communities of Diseased and Healthy Isopora palifera Corals and Adjacent Waters at the Cocos (Keeling) Islands
Authors: Sophie Preston, Jacquelyn Jones, Megan J. Huggett, Arne A. S. Adam, Nicole E. White, Kar‐Chun Tan, Zoe Richards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGrowth anomalies (GAs) are coral diseases characterised by tumour‐like skeletal lesions reported globally, yet their causes remain poorly understood. Microorganisms are integral to coral health, but the role of bacterial communities in GAs remains unclear. We investigated an outbreak of GAs in Isopora palifera at the Cocos (Keeling) Islands using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing to compare bacterial communities of GA‐affected and asymptomatic corals, surrounding water and potential pollution sources. Significant differences in bacterial beta diversity were observed across sites, with an interaction between location and coral health status. Coral and water samples hosted distinct microbial communities, but there was no evidence linking GA‐affected corals to local pollution. Moreover, no consistent bacterial taxa were associated with disease, suggesting that resident microbes may not be primary drivers of GAs. However, our study does not account for transient microbes that may have initiated GAs. Our findings challenge assumptions of single‐agent causality and microbial compositional homogeneity in coral diseases. This study advances understanding of microbial dynamics in coral disease ecology and underscores the importance of early‐stage investigation and functional metagenomics to identify viral, fungal and microbial functional shifts in disease emergence. Studying outbreaks in minimally impacted systems offers valuable baselines for disentangling natural disease processes.

Introduction

Globally, coral reefs are in decline as a result of natural and anthropogenic factors, thermal stress events (Eakin et al. 2019; Hoegh‐Guldberg et al. 2023), pollution (Sandin et al. 2008; Aguiar et al. 2023), infectious diseases (Harvell et al. 2007; Randazzo‐Eisemann et al. 2022), outbreaks of coral predators (Rice et al. 2019; Matthews et al. 2024) or compounded events (Madin et al. 2018; Richards et al. 2024; Tkachenko and Hoang 2022). Extensive coral reef declines contribute to the overall loss of biodiversity and degradation of marine ecosystems (Hughes et al. 2018; Reimer et al. 2024). Coral diseases are reported to be one of the most significant contributors to global coral reef degradation, occurring as both direct and indirect impacts of stress events (Bourne et al. 2015, 2022; Moriarty et al. 2020; Weil and Rogers 2011).

Corals and their symbionts are referred to as the ‘holobiont’, consisting of host, bacteria, fungi, dinoflagellates and other associated microbiota, for example, alga, protists, etc. The holobiont is partitioned into unique communities that are hosted in microhabitats (mucus, tissue, skeleton) across the coral colony (Pollock et al. 2018; Sweet et al. 2011). The bacterial microbiome, which forms a significant component of the holobiont, varies based on factors such as host age, host type, geographic location, environmental conditions and health status (Bourne et al. 2016; Carlos et al. 2013; Chu and Vollmer 2016; Thurber et al. 2009). The community structure of the coral microbiome can exhibit variability, transiency and stability in response to environmental stressors (Hernandez‐Agreda et al. 2018), reflecting the adaptive capacity of different bacterial species. One of the most dominant and consistently detected microbial groups associated with corals is Endozoicomonas, a genus commonly found in high abundance in healthy coral tissue (Tandon et al. 2022). Widespread presence suggests that Endozoicomonas may be a key member of the core microbiome, which could become disrupted during disease. However, mutualistic bacteria can also shift towards pathogenic behaviour under certain environmental conditions (Krishnaswamy et al. 2023; Pogoreutz and Ziegler 2024), complicating traditional approaches to disease diagnosis. The challenges of diagnosing coral disease include, but are not limited to, the presence of obligate microbes that cannot be cultured axenically (Richardson et al. 2015), the unknown functional roles of bacterial consortia (Cooney et al. 2002) and the occurrence of potentially pathogenic bacteria in both enzootic and epizootic states (Lozada‐Misa et al. 2015).

To address these challenges, it is recommended that complementary in situ visual and genetic identification methods be applied together to examine the complex interactions between host, symbiont and environment in the context of coral diseases (Bourne et al. 2009; Burge et al. 2016). Genetic‐based identification methods have advanced our understanding of the structure and function of the bacterial microbiome in both stressed and healthy coral hosts (Egan and Gardiner 2016; Glasl et al. 2019; Traylor‐Knowles et al. 2022; Thurber et al. 2009), enabling the identification of potential disease‐causing agents (Morrow et al. 2012; Roder et al. 2014; Ushijima et al. 2012). The increasing frequency of anomalous thermal stress events has highlighted the need to revisit the structure and function of the microbiome. This has led to the hypothesis that adaptive dysbiosis—an imbalance in bacterial communities—may be linked to decreased thermal tolerance in coral species (Boilard et al. 2020; Pratte and Richardson 2018). The concept of microbial imbalance has also been applied to investigate coral disease pathogens and shifts in associated bacterial communities, particularly in cases where outbreaks occur without clear environmental triggers (Egan and Gardiner 2016; Pollock et al. 2016; Rajasabapathy et al. 2020).

Growth anomalies (GAs), a pale tumour‐like disease, have been reported across the Indo‐Pacific and Caribbean (Aeby et al. 2011; Ricci et al. 2022; Weil 2004). Characterised by abnormal skeletal development, GAs result in reduced biological and reproductive functioning within lesion boundaries affecting current and future coral development (Domart‐Coulon et al. 2006; Preston and Richards 2021). While the physiological impacts of GAs have been well documented, the mechanisms behind initiation and transmission remain poorly understood. GA outbreaks have been linked to thermal stress events and proximity to anthropogenic activity (Aeby et al. 2011) and research suggests that waterborne microbial transmission or direct colony‐to‐colony contact may play a role (Kaczmarsky and Richardson 2007; Work et al. 2015). Despite investigations into the microbiomes of affected corals identifying potential pathogens for some coral diseases (Pollock et al. 2016; Richardson et al. 2014; Sweet et al. 2014), the complexity of the coral holobiont and other extenuating factors complicate efforts to pinpoint the causal agents of GAs (Gignoux‐Wolfsohn et al. 2017; Sussman et al. 2008).

Very little is known about the bacterial associations of GA‐affected scleractinian corals. One study found that microbial communities in GA tissues exhibited a higher growth rate compared to those in healthy colonies (Breitbart et al. 2005), while others have shown that bacterial community composition is influenced by coral health status (Ng et al. 2015; Rajasabapathy et al. 2020). Conversely, a different study suggested that changes in bacterial composition were more closely related to environmental variables than to health status (Chiu et al. 2012). Ricci et al. (2022) emphasised that GAs impact the entire coral holobiont. In this study, we test the hypothesis that bacterial community composition is influenced by coral health status by characterising the bacterial associations and diversity in GA‐affected (Preston and Richards 2021) and asymptomatic Isopora palifera colonies using amplicon sequencing of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene region from whole coral samples. Additionally, we investigate the bacterial composition of surrounding water and water from potential pollution sources as possible vectors for GA transmission, identifying any indicator taxa associated with the disease. This study offers new insights into the bacterial associations of GA‐affected corals at a unique, isolated oceanic reef that has experienced minimal anthropogenic impact and where no recent environmental stress events are known to have occurred (Preston and Richards 2021).

Materials and Methods

Ecological Surveys and Specimen Collection

Replicate whole branch specimens of GA‐affected and healthy (control) colonies of Isopora palifera were collected in May 2019 from four sites at the Cocos (Keeling) Islands (CKI). Three sites were located near Direction Island and one site was at the Blue Holes (Figure 1, Table S1). GA‐affected colonies were identified by the presence of pale, tumorous masses (Figure 2b). At each site, three replicate branches were collected from three separate GA‐affected colonies and one branch was collected from three separate control colonies (n = 48). Each branch from GA‐affected colonies included both symptomatic (GA‐affected) and asymptomatic polyps. Samples were stored in seawater in separately labelled bags to prevent cross‐contamination and kept cool in an esky until processed later that day. GA‐affected branches were subsampled into 3–5 cm sections representing symptomatic (GA‐affected) and asymptomatic polyps which were analysed separately. Each subsample was stored in separate vials of 100% ethanol for laboratory examination at the Trace and Environmental DNA (TrEnD) Laboratory, Curtin University, Western Australia.

Map of sample sites at Cocos (Keeling) Islands. For detailed site information and specimen collection, see Table S1.

Field photo of (a) asymptomatic Isopora palifera colony and (b) GA‐affected Isopora palifera colony.

Water samples were also collected from the same four sites, along with two additional sites identified as potential point sources of aquatic pollution (West Island Outfall and Dump, Figure 1). At each site, six replicate 1 L water samples were collected from the benthos, within 1 m of an affected colony (n = 24). Nalgene bottles were sterilised in a 10% bleach solution before use and filled with distilled freshwater to allow underwater collection. During sample collection, the Nalgene bottles were opened underwater and the less dense freshwater was allowed to exit until no observable halocline remained. Samples from the point sources were collected by hand (Dump) or using a Niskin bottle (Sewerage Outlet) (n = 12). Access to the West Island Outfall was arranged with the local Water Corporation office and collections coincided with a manual override of the outflow. Water samples were individually filtered using two Sentino peristaltic microbiology pumps (Pall Life Sciences, New York, USA) through sterile 47 mm cellulose filter membranes with 0.22um pore size (Pall Life Sciences, New York, USA). The filtering equipment was soaked in a 10% sodium hypochlorite solution for a minimum of 15 min before use and between each sample, then rinsed with deionised water. Sodium hypochlorite controls (1 L) were filtered daily post‐processing and 1 L of tap water from the West Island tap water supply was also collected as a control. Filter membranes were stored in individually labelled sterile zip‐lock bags and frozen at −20°C until DNA extraction in a separate pre‐PCR facility. Samples were transported on ice and imported under AQIS Permit No: 0002817498.

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

A small piece (3–5 mm) of whole coral branch (n = 84) was used to capture microbial communities from each microhabitat within the coral; each DNA extraction thus contains a mixture from mucus, tissue and skeletal components. DNA extraction from samples and negative extraction controls (containing no sample filters) was conducted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen; Venlo, Netherlands) on the QIAcube automated sample preparation platform (Qiagen; Venlo, Netherlands), following the manufacturer's protocol with the following modifications: 360 μL ATL Buffer and 40 μL proteinase K. The coral samples and controls were digested overnight at 56°C and 400 μL of digested supernatant was used for extraction. DNA extracts were then stored at −20°C until further analyses. Environmental DNA from water samples was extracted from the filter membranes using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen) with the following modifications: 540 μL of ATL lysis buffer and 60 μL of Proteinase K during the cell digestion phase.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR; Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus, Massachusetts, USA) was used to assess the efficiency of DNA in the extracts (n = 71) at three dilution points (Neat, 1/10, 1/100). Extracts at the optimal dilution point for low inhibition and procedural controls were carried through and amplified using Bact16S primers 341 (5′‐CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG‐3′) and Bact16S 785 (5′‐GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC‐3′) targeting the hypervariable V3‐V4 region (Klindworth et al. 2013) and each with a unique multiplex identifier tag (MID‐tag, 6–8 bp). All PCR reactions contained 1 × PCR Gold Buffer (Applied Biosystems), 2 mM MgCl2 (Applied Biosystems), 0.4 μM dNTPs (Astral Scientific, Australia), 0.1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA; Fisher Biotec, Australia), 0.4 μM forward and reverse primers, 0.12 × SYBR‐Green dye (Life Technologies, USA), 0.2 μL AmpliTaq Gold (Applied Biosystems) and 2 μL of DNA extract solution, made up to 25 μL with Ultrapure Distilled Water (Life Technologies). The PCR reaction was performed under the following conditions: denaturation for 5 min at 95°C, 45 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s then 72°C for 30 s, followed by 60°C for 1 min, 95°C for 15 s and a final 10 min at 72°C (Alexander et al. 2018).

Fusion tagged amplicons were pooled together and blended in equimolar ratios into a final library for DNA sequencing. Amplicons were size selected (< 160 bp) using Pippin Prep (Sage Sciences, USA) and the library was purified on silicate columns (Qiaquick Purification Kit (Qiagen)). The final library concentration was determined using a QuBit 4 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher, Australia) and sequenced at the TrEnD Laboratory, Curtin University on a MiSeq platform (Illumina, USA) as per Illumina protocols for amplicon sequencing, using a paired‐end 600 cycle V3 kit.

Bioinformatic Analyses

Raw paired end sequence data were visualised using the FastQC tool (Andrews 2018) to assess overall sequence quality. Sequences were then demultiplexed according to their unique MID‐tag combination, using shi7 (Al‐Ghalith et al. 2018) and primer sequences were removed using the cutadapt tool (v2.5) (Martin 2011) retaining only biological target sequences. Demultiplexed reads were filtered using the DADA2 pipeline (Callahan et al. 2016), with the following parameters: maximum of 2 errors allowed per read and R1 length truncated to 275 bp and R2 to 240 bp, a minimum quality score of 30, no ambiguous bases (N) allowed, with phix (bacterial phage) removed. Sequences were dereplicated and merged, with a minimum overlap of 30 bp and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Callahan et al. 2017) were inferred using the “pseudo pooled” method. Amplicons were cleaned according to the estimated amplicon length (399–429 bp) and chimeric sequences were removed. Taxonomy of the unique ASVs was assigned using the Genome Taxonomy reference database (release 207) formatted for use with DADA2 (https://zenodo.org/records/6655692). The AssignTaxonomy command followed the RDP naïve bays classifier (Wang et al. 2007) at a bootstrap confidence of 80%. Species assignments were then added at 100% identity and ASV annotations were collapsed to last common phylogenetic rank. ASVs unassigned at the level of Phyla were removed and the remaining reads were denoised to remove ASVs that appeared less than once in 5% of the samples. Samples with less than 500 reads (2 coral tissue samples and 4 water samples) were filtered out, as were 4 water samples that were determined to have been subject to procedural error.

Data Analysis

Beta‐diversity of the microbial communities of the whole coral (mucus, skeleton, tissue slurry) was assessed at the ASV level, using centre‐log ratio transformed data and dissimilarity matrix based on Euclidian distances. Significant differences in microbial beta‐diversity between sample types (water coral samples and laboratory controls), coral site and coral health status (health status × site) were identified using PERMANOVA performed based on 9999 unrestricted permutations of raw data and type 3 sum of squares. Significant results were further analysed using a pairwise PERMANOVA applying the same parameters.

Correlation of alpha diversity to read count at each sampling location for water and coral tissue was assessed separately using Pearson correlation. Alpha diversity (Shannon‐index and inverse‐Simpson) was then estimated for coral tissue samples. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality prior to analysis. Alpha diversity measures that were normally distributed were then compared among sites with a one‐way ANOVA and among health status by sites with a two‐way ANOVA and Tukey method for pairwise comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for those alpha‐diversity measures and proportional abundance of taxa not normally distributed and the Dunn test for pairwise comparisons. The Bonferroni method was used to adjust for multiple testing. Differences in microbial community composition of coral tissue and water samples were assessed at the genus and family levels. Differences between tissue sample types (GA‐affected, asymptomatic and control) and sampling location for both coral tissue and water were performed using MaAsLin2 (default parameters, Mallick et al. 2021) with data normalised using CLR transformation and Benjamini–Hochberg to correct for multiple testing. Lastly, phylosmith (Schuyler Smith 2019) was used to construct a heatmap from spearman correlations between bacterial and archaeal phyla abundance according to coral health status.

Results

Amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene generated 12,681 raw sequence reads, and a total of 1148 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were retained after quality filtering. These were derived from water samples (n = 67) and whole tissues (n = 62) collected from GA‐affected, asymptomatic and control samples of I. palifera (Table 1). The ASVs belonged to Bacteria (99.9%) and Archaea (0.1%). Coral tissue samples were composed primarily of ASVs assigned to Actinobacteria (48%), Cyanobacteria (25%), Proteobacteria (14%) and Firmicutes (12%) (Figure S1) and water samples were primarily composed of Proteobacteria (42%), Cyanobacteria (25%), Bacteroidota (20%) and Actinobacteria (9%). ASVs from the genus Endozoicomos were detected in all I. palifera samples, however the relative abundance at BH5 was significantly reduced in comparison to all other locations (Dunn test, adjusted p < 0.02). Abundance of Endozoicomos was not impacted by health status (Kruskal–Wallis, adjusted p < 0.63) (Figure 3). Beta diversity was compared among laboratory controls, water samples and tissue samples (Figure S2) showing a significant difference between all three sampling types (PERMANOVA, Pseudo‐F = 16.5, p < 0.001) and after pairwise comparisons (water–laboratory control, t = 2.4, p < 0.001; water–coral tissue, t = 5.3, p < 0.001; laboratory control t = 2.5, p < 0.001). Overall coral tissue samples were more similar than water samples from different sampling locations and clustered tightly compared to water samples when visualised with PCoA.

Coral Tissue and Water Samples by Locations and Health Status

Microbial alpha‐diversity was assessed among sampling locations, showing Shannon‐index and inverse‐Simpson index to be highest at location BH5 and lowest at KK (Figure 4). Significant differences in both inverse‐Simpson and Shannon‐index were seen between locations AH and BH5 (p = 0.022), BH5 and KK (p > 0.003) and between DIO and KK (p = 0.019) for Shannon‐index diversity only. Alpha diversity measures (Shannon‐index and inverse‐Simpson) were comparable between control, asymptomatic and GA‐affected samples of I. palifera colonies (Table S2), with no significant difference between health status. Coral samples were then grouped by sampling location to further assess within locational differences in alpha‐diversity. No significant differences were found among health status at locations AH, BH5 and KK (Kruskal–Wallis, p > 0.9) after correcting for multiple testing; however, at location DIO, significant differences were found prior to correcting for multiple testing (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.018), but not after (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.073).

Beta‐diversity was assessed between sampling location and health status among coral tissue samples (Figure 5). A multivariate PERMANOVA based on a CLR transformed counts and Euclidian distance similarity matrix revealed a significant difference between sampling locations (Pseudo‐F = 4.35, p > 0.001) and a significant interaction between site and health status (Pseudo‐F = 1.26, p = 0.007). Discussion

Characterising the complex and dynamic microbial associations of reef building corals in various health states is an important foundational step towards identifying causal agents or mechanisms of coral diseases. Here, the bacterial and archaeal communities associated with asymptomatic and GA‐affected I. palifera were found to be broadly overlapping with no clear evidence that GA‐affected samples possessed unique bacterial communities. While some genera were differentially abundant between asymptomatic, adjacent asymptomatic and GA‐affected I. palifera, they were not found to be associated with a particular health state. Similarly, alpha diversity (i.e., species diversity) measures were also found to be comparable across samples, despite a higher number of genera reported solely in GA‐affected samples. The findings of this study are consistent with other reports of bacterial associations of GA‐affected corals (Ng et al. 2015; Rajasabapathy et al. 2020), whereby no significant difference in bacterial diversity was detected between tissues. Furthermore, Chiu et al. (2012), reported alpha diversity was influenced by seasonal variations and location, rather than by growth anomalies.

Several genera associated with disease and infection in the marine environment were identified. For example, members of the Clostridium genus, known to be associated with Black Band disease (Cooney et al. 2002; Frias‐Lopez et al. 2002), were found in water samples from location BH5. While these potential pathogens of coral disease were detected exclusively in GA‐affected tissues, their proliferation is likely opportunistic, as GAs are known to compromise the immune systems of corals (Kelly et al. 2016; Palmer and Baird 2018; Zhang et al. 2017).

Given that no common indicator taxa could be attributed to the microbiome of GA‐affected tissues, it is unlikely that this disease is either the result of a single pathogen (as seen in other studies e.g., Luna et al. 2009; Ushijima et al. 2012, 2014) or that the causal agent is found within the unclassified consortium. Furthermore, there was no indication of a shift in diversity measures between asymptomatic and GA‐affected tissues, indicating stability in the communities of bacteria and archaea. Microbial diversity measurements are commonly used to monitor coral health (Zaneveld et al. 2017), and dysbiosis (the imbalance in communities) has been associated with both the onset (Egan and Gardiner 2016; Moriarty et al. 2020; Thurber et al. 2020) and result (Boilard et al. 2020; Silva‐Lima et al. 2020) of coral disease. The microbial community stability observed here supports the concept of compositional homogeneity (Sweet et al. 2019; Sweet and Bulling 2017). Given microbial species diversity was comparable across all coral sample types, microbiome structure is unlikely to play a key role in GAs of I. palifera. This is particularly interesting given the species diversity of scleractinian corals at CKI (Gilmour et al. 2019; Preston and Richards 2021) and the prevalence of GAs solely on I. palifera, with no recorded appearance on adjacent and susceptible Acopora and Porites species (Aeby et al. 2011; Preston and Richards 2021; Richards and Newman 2019).

Bacterial community composition differed significantly by site, a reflection of the dynamic nature of the coral microbiome and the influence of spatial variation (Zhang et al. 2015; Ziegler et al. 2019). Environmental variables, such as waterflow, temperature, salinity and nutrients, have all been reported to affect the structure and function of the coral microbiome (Carlos et al. 2013; McKew et al. 2012; Pantos et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2015). Furthermore, corals have demonstrated both intra‐ and inter‐colony variation, adapting their symbionts to the local environment (Chu and Vollmer 2016; Morrow et al. 2012). The difference in bacterial communities found at sites across the Cocos (Keeling) Islands (e.g., West Island Dump, WID, Figure 5D) may be reflective of their largely transient and environmentally responsive communities (Hernandez‐Agreda et al. 2018; Morrow et al. 2015) however, temporal trends have not been assessed in this study and it is recommended that future studies increase the temporal sampling footprint to track the response of these communities to fluctuations in environmental conditions.

Despite potential influences from nearby pollution sources, including the sewerage outlet, both GA‐affected and asymptomatic coral tissues displayed distinctive microbiomes compared to the surrounding water column. Notably, there were no apparent associations between the bacterial communities of corals and these point sources of pollution, including the sewerage outlet. This is an important finding, given that growth anomalies have previously been linked to sewerage and anthropogenic pressures (Yoshioka et al. 2016; Aeby et al. 2011). The bacterial communities associated with coral tissues included genera involved in endosymbiosis (e.g., Endozoicomonas), nutrient acquisition and transformation, including nitrogen cycling (e.g., Alteromonas, Pseudomonas, Vibrio; Chimetto et al. 2008), as well as antipathogenic activity (Sa'adah et al. 2018). In contrast, the surrounding water column was dominated by microbial plankton and algae, including Pelagibacter and Phaeocystidibacter, as well as bacteria involved in biogeochemical and atmospheric processes (Louca et al. 2016; Moran 2015) (Figure 6). Some separation in water sample microbiome was observed, particularly those associated with Pelagibacter‐associated ASVs and those taken from BH5. This may reflect hydrographic and anthropogenic influences, though further investigation would be needed to explore these links.

The unique conditions of this outbreak, characterised by low anthropogenic influence and no apparent environmental anomalies (Preston and Richards 2021), provide a rare opportunity to study the bacterial and archaeal associations of GA‐affected corals without significant contributing factors. This is a situation not often available when investigating coral diseases. The complexity of the coral microbiome presents a significant challenge to identifying the causative agents of many coral diseases (Glasl et al. 2019; Schellenberg and Clarke 2020). Beyond abiotic environmental factors, the coral microbiome is also influenced by interstitial fauna (Ainsworth et al. 2020), anthropogenic pressure (Kelly et al. 2014; Ziegler et al. 2016) and predation (van Oppen and Blackall 2016; Welsh et al. 2016). This study used the 16S rRNA gene as a marker for species identification and diversity. While this approach is useful, it is limited in its ability to discriminate strains that carry specific pathogenicity genes that may have been horizontally transferred (Diard and Hardi 2017). Alternatively, favourable mutations can render microbes more pathogenic or help them evade the host immune response. For GAs, understanding the dynamics of functional niches filled by bacteria may be more informative than community composition alone, as well as examining the interactions between bacteria, archaea and other microbes (Rypien et al. 2010; Thurber et al. 2017). Understanding the coral holobiont also holds promise for developing novel approaches to coral disease management, such as probiotics, which seek to enhance thermal tolerance and improve coral propagation (Rosado et al. 2019; van de Water et al. 2022; Voolstra et al. 2021). Globally, research underscores the need to understand the links between coral disease, the microbiome and environmental and anthropogenic pressures to better predict present and future threats to coral reefs (Harvell et al. 2007; Ricci et al. 2022; van Oppen and Blackall 2019).

Conclusion

This study advances knowledge of the microbiome composition of the CKI population of I. palifera and of bacterial and archaeal associations in GA‐affected corals. No anomalous environmental influences at the study location are known to have catalysed the outbreak, and the distinct microbiomes of coral samples and the water column showed no evidence of bacterial associations with the potential point sources of pollution. Moreover, the homogeneity identified in bacterial communities across both control, asymptomatic and GA‐affected samples indicates no clear trends indicative of a bacterial driver of the disease outbreak. However, it is possible GA initiation happened before the coral and water sampling, hence we cannot exclude the possibility that transient microbes initiated the GA development. Future research should focus on the functional role of bacterial and archaeal communities, temporal variation in the community composition of the microbiome and alternative microbial agents such as viruses which may be responsible for the outbreak of GAs. Furthermore, while this outbreak provided a unique opportunity to study the microbiome of GA‐affected corals, it offers limited insight into how this remote atoll became affected by a disease previously thought to be associated with environmental or anthropogenic stressors. To better understand causality, future studies should endeavour to focus on characterising the microbiome communities associated with coral diseases at their initiation stage.
