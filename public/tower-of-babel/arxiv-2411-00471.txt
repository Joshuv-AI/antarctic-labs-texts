Dirichlet process mixtures of block g g priors for model selection and prediction in linear models
Anupreet Porwal, Department of Statistics, University of Washington

Abstract
This paper introduces Dirichlet process mixtures of block g g priors for model selection and prediction in linear models. These priors are extensions of traditional mixtures of g g priors that allow for differential shrinkage for various (data-selected) blocks of parameters while fully accounting for the predictors’ correlation structure, providing a bridge between the literatures on model selection and continuous shrinkage priors. We show that Dirichlet process mixtures of block g g priors are consistent in various senses and, in particular, that they avoid the conditional Lindley “paradox” highlighted by Som et al. (2016) . Further, we develop a Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm for posterior inference that requires only minimal ad-hoc tuning. Finally, we investigate the empirical performance of the prior in various real and simulated datasets. In the presence of a small number of very large effects, Dirichlet process mixtures of block g g priors lead to higher power for detecting smaller but significant effects without only a minimal increase in the number of false discoveries.



Keywords:
linear model, model selection, conditional Lindley paradox,
g
prior, continuous shrinkage prior

Introduction

Model selection and model averaging are foundational tasks in statistics and machine learning. Associated Bayesian procedures typically rely on the computation of Bayes factors and posterior model probabilities, whose properties are heavily dependent of the choice of priors associated with the parameters of each model. This feature makes model selection and model averaging tasks difficult in situations where standard “objective” or “default” priors (such as the reference prior,
Berger et al., 2009
) are improper. This is true even in well-studied settings, such as Gaussian linear models.

The literature on noninformative priors and default Bayes factors for model selection for (generalized) linear models is extensive. Examples include
g
-priors
(
Zellner, 1986
)
, mixtures of
g
-priors
(
Zellner and Siow, 1980
;
Liang et al., 2008
)
, unit information priors
(
Kass and Wasserman, 1995
)
, intrinsic Bayes factors
(
Berger and Pericchi, 1996
)
, non-local priors
(
Johnson and Rossell, 2010
;
Johnson and Rossell, 2012
)
and power-expected-posterior priors
(
Fouskakis et al., 2015
;
Porwal and Rodríguez, 2023
)
, among other approaches. See
Forte et al. (2018)
and
Consonni et al. (2018)
for recent reviews.

Bayarri et al. (2012)
describes a series of desiderata for default priors used for model selection and model averaging, with a particular focus on problems involving multiple linear regression. These include various forms of consistency and invariance, as well as predictive matching. More recently,
Som (2014)
and
Som et al. (2016)
suggested additional criteria related to the behavior of the Bayes factor as a subset of the significant coefficients grow to infinity. This setting is important because it serves as a proxy for situations in which effects sizes vary dramatically across covariates. Such situations arise often in practice, and they are arguably the kind of problem in which well-designed statistical methods can make a real difference.

Som (2014)
and
Som et al. (2016)
show that mixtures of
g
priors
(
Liang et al., 2008
)
fail to satisfy their new criteria (a behavior they call
the conditional Lindley paradox
), and introduce mixtures of block
g
priors, which address the issue by assigning different shrinkage parameters to preselected groups of coefficients.

In the absence of prior information to drive the
a priori
selection of the blocks, the methodology of
Som (2014)
is difficult to implement in practice. Furthermore, the prior introduced in
Som (2014)
assumes that the blocks of coefficients are independent a priori. When there is strong colinearity between covariates associated with “large” and “small” coefficients, the independence assumption can lead to loss of efficiency. Our first contribution in this paper is to develop Dirichlet process (DP) mixtures of block
g
priors that allow for differential shrinkage across coefficients while fully accounting for the observed correlations among predictors and treating the blocks of covariates as an unknown parameter that must be inferred from the data. Similar approaches have been suggested in the literature at least as early as in
Liang et al. (2008)
but, to the best of our knowledge, they have not been pursued before, perhaps because of perceived computational challenges.

Because of our focus on differential shrinkage, the literature on continuous shrinkage priors is also relevant to our discussion.
Examples of continuous shrinkage priors include the Student
t
-prior
(
Tipping, 2001
)
, the Bayesian Lasso
(
Park and Casella, 2008
;
Hans, 2009
)
, the Horseshoe prior
(
Carvalho et al., 2010
)
, the Normal-Gamma prior
(
Griffin and Brown, 2005
;
Brown and Griffin, 2010
)
, semiparametric multiple-shrinkage priors
(
MacLehose and Dunson, 2010
)
, the Bayesian adaptive Lasso
(
Leng et al., 2014
)
, the Dirichlet-Laplace prior
(
Bhattacharya et al., 2015
)
, global-local shrinkage priors
(
Polson and Scott, 2012
)
, the Beta-prime prior
(
Bai and Ghosh, 2018
)
, the Horseshoe-pit prior
(
Denti et al., 2023
)
, the group Inverse-Gamma Gamma prior of
Boss et al. (2023)
, and global-local-tail priors
(
Lee et al., 2024
)
among others. Continuous shrinkage priors tend to have computational advantages, can be connected to penalized likelihood methods, and are very effective in predictive settings. However, because they place probability zero on any one value of the parameter space, variable selection can be performed only by either looking at the coverage of posterior credible intervals or by thresholding the posterior distributions of the coefficients (e.g., see
Li and Pati, 2017
). Both of these procedures tend to work best in settings where enough prior information is available to establish practical significance. For this reason, the literature on continuous shrinkage priors is often considered as distinct from that on priors for model selection. A second contribution of this paper is to show that DP mixtures of
g
priors provide a unifying framework for these two strands of the literature, with canonical methods in each of the two corresponding to special cases of ours.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces our notation and reviews the conditional Lindley paradox. Section
3
introduces our proposed methodology and reviews its connections with the broader literature. In Section
4
, we investigate the properties of the prior and the associated Bayes factors, with a particular emphasis on the criteria introduced in
Bayarri et al. (2012)
and
Som et al. (2016)
. Section
5
discusses the computational implementation of our model.
Section
6
and Section
7
illustrate the performance of our methodology in both simulated and real datasets. Finally, Section
8
discusses future directions for research.

Motivation: Bayesian variable selection and mixtures of

Consider a collection of linear models for the observed response vector

\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

where

The parameters

are usually assigned the reference prior

(e.g., see
Berger et al., 1998
). A common choice for

is the so-called mixture of

-priors
(

)

f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\int\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},g\sigma^{2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}\right)f(g\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g,

where

denotes the density of the multivariate normal distribution and

is a suitable hyperprior. When
f(g\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is chosen carefully (e.g., an appropriately scaled member of the Compound Confluent Hypergeometric distribution introduced in
Gordy, 1998
), and under mild regularity conditions, Bayes factors based on mixtures of
g
priors have various appealing theoretical properties like model selection consistency and information consistency
(
Liang et al., 2008
;
Bayarri et al., 2012
)
.

In spite of these strong theoretical guarantees, procedures based on mixtures of
g
priors do suffer from some undesirable properties. For example,
Som (2014)
and
Som et al. (2016)
showed that Bayes factor based on mixtures of
g
priors suffer from the

. Roughly speaking, this “paradox” states that, when comparing nested models, if at least one of the regression coefficients common to both models is large relative to other coefficients present only in the bigger model, the Bayes factor will place too much weight on the smaller model irrespective of the data generating model. Consider the two models,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{0}:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\quad\mathcal{M}_{a}:\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
are
n\times p_{1}
and
n\times p_{2}

\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{2}=\boldsymbol{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
are
p_{1}
and
p-p_{1}
dimensional vectors, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the observational noise. Further, for fixed

,
p_{1}
,
p
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
and

Som et al. (2016)
showed that, if

, then, for the Bayes Factor for comparing
\mathcal{M}_{a}
and
\mathcal{M}_{0}
,

,
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}

The conditional Lindley paradox is a consequence of the use of a common shrinkage factor
g
and cannot be solved through alternative choices of the prior on a single shrinkage coefficient
g
. This is because, as some coefficients grow, the estimate of the common

propose priors that allow for different shrinkage coefficients for various blocks of parameters. In the case of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
above, these are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\mid g,\sigma^{2},\mathcal{M}_{0}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}g\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\right\}^{-1}\right)

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mid g_{1},g_{2},\sigma^{2},\mathcal{M}_{a}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\end{pmatrix},\sigma^{2}\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\right\}^{-1}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&g_{2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{2}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\right\}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\right),

(2.3)

where
g
,
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), e.g., from a hyper-

distribution.
Som et al. (2016)
showed that, under this block

Note, however, that implementing this strategy requires that we specify up front which groups of parameters will be assigned a common shrinkage parameter. This is problematic because the structure of the blocks can have a very big impact on the performance of the methods. Indeed, if at least one of the components of

3
A new class of priors: Dirichlet process mixtures of block
g
priors

In this paper we consider mixtures of priors of the form

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right),

(3.1)

where
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\gamma_{j}
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is a known covariance matrix that might depend on model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
,
\boldsymbol{G}^{1/2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\diag\{g_{1}^{1/2},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{1/2}\}
, and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
are identically distributed. The associated marginal likelihood conditional on
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
is given by:

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\sqrt{n}}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}\left[\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-n\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}.

(3.2)

where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}
(see Section
A
of the supplementary materials).
The differential-shrinkage
g
prior can be obtained by setting
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}
. Indeed, note that the standard
g
prior is then obtained by further setting
g_{1}=g_{2}=\cdots=g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=g
.

A natural approach to modeling the
g_{j}
s is to assign them a parametric family that is flexible enough to encompass various tail behaviors. One example is

\displaystyle f(g\mid\tau^{2},a,b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(a+b+2)}{\tau^{2}\Gamma(a+1)\Gamma(b+1)}g^{b}\left(1+\frac{g}{\tau^{2}}\right)^{-a-b-2},

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle>0,

(3.3)

where
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\exp\left\{-t\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
denotes the standard Gamma function. Note that this family is defined for
a,b>-1
and
\tau^{2}>0
and corresponds to a Beta prior on
g/(\tau^{2}+g)
. It includes, for example, the hyper-
g/n
prior (which corresponds to
-1/2\leq a\leq 0
,
b=0
and
\tau^{2}=n
), as well as the half Cauchy distribution that underlies the Horseshoe prior
(
Carvalho et al., 2010
)
(which corresponds to
a=b=-1/2
and assigning
\tau^{2}
a half-Cauchy distribution). Hence, borrowing from the literature on continuous shrinkage priors, we call priors of this type “global-local”
g
priors, where
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\gamma}}
are “local” shrinkage parameter and
\tau^{2}
is a “global” shrinkage parameter (which can be either known or unknown).

One potential challenge of the approach just outlined is the need to estimate what is, potentially, a very large number of different shrinkage factors. This is not only computationally costly, but the data is likely to have limited information about each of them, especially in high-correlation settings. Another challenge is that the performance of the procedure can be affected by the choice of the parametric family used to model the
g_{j}
s. In particular, recent work by
Piironen et al. (2017)
and
Lee et al. (2024)
indicates that, in the context of continuous shrinkage priors, the optimal tail behavior of
p(g_{j}\mid\tau^{2})
might depend on the level of sparsity among coefficients.

Both of these challenges can be addressed through the use of a nonparametric specification for the distribution of the
g_{j}
s based on the Dirichlet process
(
Ferguson, 1973
)
. A random distribution
H
is said to follow a Dirichlet process prior with centering measure
H_{0}
and concentration parameter
\alpha
, denoted
H\mid H_{0},\alpha\sim\mathsf{DP}(\alpha,H_{0})
, if it admits a representation of the form

\displaystyle H(\cdot)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w_{k}\delta_{g^{*}_{k}}(\cdot),

(3.4)

where
\delta_{a}
denotes a point mass at
a
,
g^{*}_{1},g^{*}_{2},\ldots
is an i.i.d. sequence with
g^{*}_{k}\sim H_{0}
, and
w_{k}=v_{k}\prod_{l<k}(1-v_{l})
, with
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
another i.i.d. sequence with
v_{k}\sim\mathsf{beta}(1,\alpha)

(
Sethuraman, 1994
)
. Because the samples from a Dirichlet process are almost surely discrete distributions, if
g_{1},\ldots,g_{c}
is an i.i.d. sample from a random
H\mid H_{0},\alpha\sim\mathsf{DP}(\alpha,H_{0})
, there is a positive probability of ties among the
g_{i}
s. In fact, their joint distribution can be described through two sequences,
\tilde{g}_{1},\tilde{g}_{2},\ldots
i.i.d. such that
\tilde{g}_{k}\sim H_{0}
, and
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{c}
such that
\xi_{1}=1
and

\displaystyle\xi_{j}\mid\xi_{j-1},\ldots,\xi_{1},\alpha

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{k=1}^{K^{j-1}}\frac{m_{k}^{j}}{j+\alpha-1}\delta_{k}+\frac{\alpha}{j+\alpha-1}\delta_{K^{j-1}+1},

\displaystyle j\geq 2,

where
K^{j-1}=\max_{j^{\prime}<j}\{\xi_{j^{\prime}}\}
and
m_{k}^{j}=\sum_{j^{\prime}<j}\mathbb{I}(\xi_{j^{\prime}}=k)

(
Blackwell and MacQueen, 1973
)
. The value of
g_{j}
can then be recovered from those of
\tilde{g}_{1},\tilde{g}_{2},\ldots
and
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{c}
through the relationship
g_{j}=\tilde{g}_{\xi_{j}}
. The vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}
defines a partition
\rho=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{K}\}
of the set
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,c\}
such that
\cup_{k=1}^{K}S_{k}=\mathcal{I}
,
S_{k}\cap S_{k^{\prime}}=\emptyset
for
k\neq k^{\prime}
, and
|S_{k}|=m_{k}
is the number of elements in
S_{k}
, so that
i\in S_{k}
if and only if
\xi_{i}=k
and

f(\rho\mid\alpha)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+c)}\alpha^{K}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\Gamma(m_{k})
.

In our setting, we let
g_{j}\mid H
i.i.d. for
j=1,\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
H\mid\alpha\sim\mathsf{DP}(\alpha,H_{0})
, where
H_{0}
is the distribution associated with (
3.3
), and
\alpha
is assigned the parameterization-invariant prior introduced in
Rodríguez (2013)
, which has density
f(\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-1}\frac{j}{(\alpha+j)^{2}}}
. The use of this prior circumvents the need to elicit prior information about the number of groups into which the coefficients are expected to be clustered. The resulting prior,

p(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid a,b,\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\int\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)\\
f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\rho,\gamma,\tau^{2}=n,a,b\right)f(\rho\mid\gamma,\alpha)f(\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha,

(3.5)

is a
Dirichlet process mixture of block
g
priors
.

Because there might be ties among the
g_{j}
s, the model implicitly defines a partition of the coefficients in which those assigned to the same group share a common shrinkage factor. The concentration parameter
\alpha
controls the prior distribution on the partitions, with
\alpha\to 0
leading to the standard (mixture of)
g
priors, and
\alpha\to\infty
leading back to global-local
g
priors defined above where each coefficient is assigned its own shrinkage factor. Since the model treats both
\alpha
and
\rho
as unknown, the model is able to learn an appropriate partition of the coefficients as it performs model selection. Furthermore, the use of a nonparametric prior for
H
implies that the model is potentially capable of learning from the data the shape of the distribution of the shrinkage factors,which can alleviate concerns about the specific choice of the hyperparameters
a
,
b
and
\tau^{2}
.

3.1
Unifying continuous shrinkage and variable selection priors

Traditionally, frameworks based on continuous shrinkage priors have recognized that differential shrinkage might be needed to attain optimal performance, but until recently (e.g.,
Boss et al., 2023
) they have tended to downplay the need to account for co-linearity among covariates. On the other hand, the literature on priors for model selection has, from the very beginning, acknowledged the need to account for colinearity, but has been slower to recognize the need for differential shrinkage, perhaps because of the computational challenges involved. Our approach provide a unifying framework for thinking about these two strands of the literature. To see this, consider a slightly less general spike-and-slab version of our prior where
\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2},\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{p},\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}^{1/2}\left\{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\right\}^{-}\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}^{1/2}\right)
,

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\diag\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p}\}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{G}}=\diag\{\tilde{g}_{\xi_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{\xi_{p}}\}
, and
A^{-}
represents the Moore-Penrose inverse of
A
. Different choices of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
and of priors on
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\rho
lead to various well-known procedures. For example, as we noted before, the DP mixture of block
g
priors includes the standard
g
prior and the “global-local” block
g
prior as special (limit) cases. Furthermore, when
\boldsymbol{X}
is orthogonal and the grouping variable
\rho
is treated as known, it also includes the block
g
prior of
Som (2014)
as a special case.

On the other hand, if we fix the model to
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
, either
\boldsymbol{X}
is orthonormal or we take
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\boldsymbol{I}
, and
\tau^{2}
is given a hyperprior, the DP mixture of block
g
priors corresponds to the Horseshoe Pit mixture prior of
Denti et al. (2023)
. As a consequence, it also includes a number of traditional continuous shrinkage priors such as those in
Park and Casella (2008)
,
Brown and Griffin (2010)
,
Carvalho et al. (2010)
,
Leng et al. (2014)
,
Bhattacharya et al. (2015)
and
Bai and Ghosh (2018)
. Also, under the full model and block orthogonality and a known group structure, our framework also includes the Group Inverse Gamma Gamma shrinkage prior of

Boss et al. (2023)
.

4
Properties of DP mixtures of block
g
prior

4.1
Tail behavior

Since, marginally, samples from a distribution generated by a Dirichlet process follow the baseline measure (e.g., see
Blackwell and MacQueen, 1973
or
Antoniak, 1974
), the marginal distribution for the
l
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
under a DP mixture of block
g
priors is given by

f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},l}\mid\tau^{2},a,b,\gamma)=\int\mathsf{N}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},l}\mid 0,g\kappa_{\gamma,l,l}\sigma^{2})f(g\mid\tau^{2},a,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g
,
where
\kappa_{\gamma,l,l}
is the
l
-th diagonal entry of
\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}
. The tail behavior of this type of marginal distributions was studied in
Boss et al. (2023)
(see their Theorem 2.1). In particular, the index of regular variation of the marginal prior is
\omega=-2b-3
, i.e.,

\lim_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},l}\to\infty}\frac{f(t\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},l}\mid\tau^{2},a,b,\gamma)}{f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},l}\mid\tau^{2},a,b,\gamma)}=t^{-2b-3}.

This implies that the our prior has heavy (polynomial) tails and point estimators derived from our procedures is robust, in the sense of having bounded influence in the case of likelihood-prior conflict. However, note that the contour plots associated with the DP mixture of block
g
priors are not elliptical, unlike those of the standard
g
prior (please see Section
B
of the supplementary materials). These non-elliptical contours enable non-uniform shrinkage by the DP block
g
priors, which in turn is what enables them to skirt the conditional Lindley paradox.

4.2
Information consistency of Bayes factors

For fixed
n
,
p
and
\boldsymbol{X}
that is full rank, consider a sequence of observations
\boldsymbol{y}(1),\boldsymbol{y}(2),\ldots
such that
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(N)\|\to\infty
and
N\to\infty
, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(N)=\left[\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}(N)
is the maximum likelihood estimator or
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
based on
\boldsymbol{y}(N)
. The Bayes factor
BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{0}}
is information consistent if
BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{0}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\right)\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
.

Bayes factors under standard mixtures of
g
priors are known to be information consistent under appropriate conditions on the prior on
g
. The following theorem, which is analogous to Theorem 2 in
Liang et al. (2008)
, establishes general conditions on the joint prior on
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
that ensure information consistency for general mixtures of block
g
priors.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\nu_{+}
be the largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
\lambda_{-}(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
be the smallest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\left[\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right]^{-1}
. The mixing prior
f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
leads to Bayes factors that are information consistent if

\int\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{-1/2}\\
\left[1-\frac{\lambda_{-}(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}{\nu_{+}}\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{1}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=\infty.

(4.1)

for all
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq p
.

The proof is included in Section
C
of the supplementary materials. A slightly simpler condition that applies to DP mixtures of block
g
priors is the following:

Theorem 4.2
.

A sufficient condition for the DP mixture of block
g
priors to lead to Bayes factors that are information consistent is for the density of the centering measure,
f(g\mid\tau^{2},a,b)
, to satisfy

\int(1+g_{j})^{(n-1-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})/2}f(g_{j}\mid\tau^{2},a,b)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{j}=\infty

for all
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq p
.

See Section
C
of the supplementary materials. This is the same condition in Theorem 2 of
Liang et al. (2008)
. Hence, this result just indicates that any mixing distribution for
g
that leads to information-consistent Bayes factors under a standard mixture of
g
priors also leads to information consistent Bayes factors under the DP mixture of block
g
priors.

4.3
Information consistency of block structures

One of the key motivations to consider DP mixtures of block
g
priors is the desire to avoid having to decide a priori on an appropriate partition for the covariates. In this Section, we show that, when the design matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
is orthogonal and the true coefficients have very different sizes, our prior assigns coefficients of different sizes to separate clusters with high probability. Before proceeding with our main result, we need to introduce the concept of refinement of a partition (sometimes called a fragmentation, e.g., see
Bertoin, 2006
).

Definition 4.1

(Refinement of a partition)
.

Let
\rho=\{S_{1},\dots,S_{K}\}
and
\rho^{\prime}=\{S_{1}^{\prime},\dots,S_{K^{\prime}}^{{}^{\prime}}\}
denote two partition of a set
\mathcal{I}=\{1,\ldots,c\}
with
K
and
K^{\prime}
unique blocks respectively, such that
1\leq K^{\prime}\leq K\leq c
. Then,
\rho
is said to be a refinement of
\rho^{\prime}
, denoted by
\rho\prec\rho^{\prime}
, if and only if for every
S_{k}\in\rho
there exist a
S_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}\in\rho^{\prime}
such that
S_{k}\subseteq S_{j}^{{}^{\prime}}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a full rank, centered, orthogonal design matrix of size
n\times p
, and
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
be two non-overlapping submatrices of sizes
n\times p_{1}
and
n\times p_{2}
with
p_{1}>0
,
p_{2}>0
and
p_{1}+p_{2}\leq p
. Denote by
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{j^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(1)}_{p_{1}}\}
the set of indexes associated with the columns of
\boldsymbol{X}
included in
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{j^{(2)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(2)}_{p_{2}}\}
the columns associated with
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
, so that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{2}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}\cap\mathcal{I}_{2}=\emptyset
.
Consider now an asymptotic regime where, for fixed
n
,
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, a sequence of observations
\{\boldsymbol{y}(N):N\in\mathbb{N}\}
is generated as
\boldsymbol{y}(N)=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(N)+\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(N):N\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a sequence such that,
\beta_{j}^{2}(N)\sim\mathcal{O}(N)
for all
j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
. If
\rho_{0}=\{\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}\}
, then

1.

For
\rho\nprec\rho_{0}
,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho_{0}\right)}=0
, and

2.

For
\rho\prec\rho_{0}
,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho_{0}\right)}=c_{\rho}
, with
0<c_{\rho}<\infty
.

The proof can be seen in Section
D
of the supplementary materials.

4.4
Conditional Lindley paradox

In addition to being important on its own right, the previous result allows us to show that, when the design matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
is orthogonal, the Bayes factors based on DP mixtures of block
g
priors avoid the conditional Lindley paradox.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a full rank, centered, orthogonal design matrix of size
n\times p
, and
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
be two non-overlapping submatrices of sizes
n\times p_{1}
and
n\times p_{2}
with
p_{1}>0
,
p_{2}>0
and
p_{1}+p_{2}=p
, and consider the pair of models
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}}:\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a}}:\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}+\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. Denote by
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{j^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(1)}_{p_{1}}\}
the set of indexes associated with the columns of
\boldsymbol{X}
included in
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{j^{(2)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(2)}_{p_{2}}\}
the columns associated with
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
, so that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{2}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}\cap\mathcal{I}_{2}=\emptyset
.

For fixed
n
,
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
,
\beta_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, let
\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(N):N\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence such that
\beta_{j}^{2}(N)\sim\mathcal{O}(N)
as
N\to\infty
for all
j\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
,
and
\{\boldsymbol{y}(N):N\in\mathbb{N}\}
be the associated sequence generated by setting
\boldsymbol{y}(N)=\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}(N)+\boldsymbol{X}_{2}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. Then, for the Bayes factor based on a DP mixture of block
g
priors under the hyper-
g/n
mixture distribution we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\right)>0

for any any
\{\boldsymbol{y}(N):N\in\mathbb{N}\}
and any pair of models
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a}
.

The proof, which can be found in Section
E
of the supplementary materials, only requires that the prior on the partition
\rho
puts positive probability on at least one refinement of the true partition
\rho_{0}=\{\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}\}
. This is trivially true for the Dirichlet process, whether its concentration parameter is fixed or treated as unknown and given a prior distribution. In fact, this is a very mild requirement that is also satisfied by the Bayes factor constructed under the global-local
g
prior, and one that does not require that the partition model be able to identify the true partition
\rho_{0}
. On the other hand, orthogonality plays a key role in the proof of this theorem, just like block orthogonality is key to similar proofs in
Som (2014)
. Simulation studies suggest that the result hold in the non-orthogonal case.

4.5
Model selection consistency

Model selection consistency refers to the ability of the procedure to choose the correct model as the sample size grows. DP mixtures of block
g
priors are model selection consistent in the fixed
p
regime (see Section
F
of the supplementary materials).

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that a sequence of observations
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots
is generated from some model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\in\{0,1\}^{p}
(i.e., one of the models considered by our procedure), and that
p
is fixed. Also, assume the following regularity conditions:

(i)

the column space
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{X})
does not contain
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}
.

(ii)

The sequence of covariate vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots
are such that
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|^{2}
is bounded by a constant for all
i=1,2,\ldots
.

(iii)

The smallest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}/n
is lower bounded by a positive constant for all
n
.

Then, under the DP mixture block
g
priors with
\tau^{2}=n
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{Pr}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=1
, as long as the prior on models satisfies
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})>0
.

4.6
Intrinsic consistency

Under slightly more stringent regularity conditions than those required for model selection consistency, DP mixtures of block
g
priors are also intrinsically consistent.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume that, as
n
grows, the columns
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots
of the design matrix satisfy either of the following two conditions for a finite, positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
:

(i)

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots
forms a deterministic sequence, then
\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

(ii)

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots
are random, then they are independent and identically distributed from a distribution with mean
\boldsymbol{0}
and covariance
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.

Then, the DP mixture of block
g
priors with
\tau^{2}=n
converges to a proper, non-degenerate intrinsic prior of the form

f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid a,b,\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\int\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)\\
f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\rho,\gamma,\tau^{2}=n,a,b\right)f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\alpha)f(\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{g}_{1}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{g}_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\alpha.

5
Computation

It is possible to construct MCMC algorithms for model selection under the DP mixtures of block
g
prior that require very minimal tuning. To do so, we take advantage of the conditional conjugacy of the priors and, when possible, we integrate out the intercept
\beta_{0}
, the vector of regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and/or the variance
\sigma^{2}
when deriving conditional posteriors. Additionally, we represent the shrinkage coefficients
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
in terms of their unique values
\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
and the group indicators
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
(recall Section
3
). The resulting posterior takes the form

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi},\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{y})\propto f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi})f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)f(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\alpha)f(\alpha|\boldsymbol{\gamma})f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}),

where
f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
corresponds to (
3.2
) with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}
, and
p(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is an appropriate prior on the space of models, e.g., a Beta-Binomial prior

f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\frac{\Gamma(c+d)}{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(d)}\frac{\Gamma(c+p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\Gamma(d+p-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}{\Gamma(c+d+p)}
.

Our MCMC algorithm then alternates sampling from the full conditionals
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
,
f(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
,
f(\alpha\mid\cdots)
,
f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\cdots)
,
f(\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\cdots)
and
f(\sigma^{2}\mid\cdots)
. To sample from
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
, we use a random walk Metropolis algorithm in which, at each iteration, we propose to either add one variable, remove one variable, or swap one variable currently in the model with one that is not. If a variable is added to the model, the corresponding value of
\xi_{i}
and, if necessary, a new value of
\tilde{g}_{k}
, are proposed from the prior distributions on these parameters. This is technically a Reversible Jump MCMC step
(
Green, 1995
)
, albeit a very simple one. In spite of this simplicity, the algorithm seems to perform quite well. On the other hand, once we condition on the model, sampling from
f(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
can be accomplished using any of the collapsed samplers for non-conjugate Dirichlet process mixture models (e.g., see
Neal, 2000
). To sample
f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\cdots)
we resort to a slight variant of the slice sampler introduced in
Finegold and Drton (2014)
and
Liu et al. (2012)
. Finally, sampling from
f(\alpha\mid\cdots)
is accomplished through the use of a random walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm with log-Gaussian proposals. This is the only step of the algorithm that requires tuning of hyperparameters. Details are provided in Section
G
of the supplementary materials.

An implementation of the code is available from

https://github.com/Anupreet-Porwal/DP-mix-block-g-prior
.

8
Discussion

We introduced novel class of priors that that enable a parsimonious, data driven approach to model selection and prediction in linear models that is free from the so-called conditional Lindley “paradox” and that provides a bridge between two strands of the literature (model selection priors and continuous shrinkage priors) that have often been treated as distinct. The use of a Dirichlet process prior for the distribution of the local shrinkage coefficients enable us to interpret our model in terms of blocks of covariates that receive the same level of shrinkage. However, the method is not specially reliant to the clustering properties of Dirichlet process. In particular, it does not require that we are able to consistently estimate the “true” partition of the coefficients, only that the partitions that receive high probability a posteriori be those that do not mix both “small” and “large” coefficients.

While this paper focuses on linear regression models, DP mixtures of block
g
priors can be extended to generalized linear models, and perhaps even non-linear regression, by setting
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
to be an appropriate information matrix, e.g., see
Bové and Held (2011)
,
Li and Clyde (2018)
and
Porwal and Rodríguez (2023)
. The approach introduced here can also be used to generalize the class of priors introduced in
Carvalho and Scott (2009)
, leading to a new class of (mixtures of) hyper-inverse Wishart block
g
priors for model selection in Gaussian graphical models.

Recently,
Lee et al. (2024)
demonstrated that, in the context of continuous shrinkage priors, the behavior of the tail of the distribution of the local shrinkage parameters plays an important role in the performance of the prior, and that different types of tail behavior might be necessary in sparse vs. ultra-sparse settings. The use of a non-parametric specification for the distribution of
g_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}
through a Dirichlet process prior enables such tail adaptability in the context of model selection priors.

From a theoretical perspective, there are two additional aspects of our work that are open for extension. First, our results around the conditional Lindley paradox assume that the design matrix is orthogonal. The evidence from our simulations suggested that the results, and in particular the ability of the model to separate “large” and “small” coefficients into separate clusters, extends to the non-orthogonal case. We believe that theoretical progress in this area can be achieved by developing an asymptotic expansion for the multivariate integral defining the marginal likelihood. Similarly, our model consistency results assume that
p
is fixed. We believe that it possible to extend the result to the cases where
p
grows with
n
. Both of these directions will be explored elsewhere.

Appendix A
Derivation of Equation (
3.2
)

By definition,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})=\int f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\sigma^{2})f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})f(\beta_{0},\sigma^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}

The integral with respect to
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is trivial to compute using the properties of the multivariate normal distribution, resulting in
\boldsymbol{y}\mid\beta_{0},\sigma^{2},\boldsymbol{\gamma},g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\boldsymbol{1}\beta_{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{I}_{n}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}
. Integrating now with respect to
\beta_{0}

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\sigma^{2},g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})

\displaystyle=\int(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-n/2}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{1}\beta_{0}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{1}\beta_{0}\right)\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{-n/2}\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{2}}{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\int\exp\left\{-\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\beta_{0}-\frac{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}}{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}}\right)^{2}\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta_{0}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^{2}}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}}{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\gamma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{2}}{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}}\right)\right\}.

Finally,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\sigma^{2},g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}}{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\gamma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\int\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{2}}{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}}\right)\right\}\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}}{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\gamma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right)^{1/2}}\left[\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{2}}{\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}}\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}.

Note, however, that since the design matrix has been centered,
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{0}
. This implies
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\gamma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}=n
and
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}
, and yields the simplified form in (
3.2
).

Two results will be useful in what follows. First, the Woodbury matrix identity implies

\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}=\left[\mathbf{I}_{n}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\right]^{-1}=\\
\mathbf{I}_{n}-\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}.

Secondly, using the matrix determinant lemma

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|=\left|\mathbf{I}_{n}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\right|=\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|}{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}.

Now, on to the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
From equation (
3.2
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{G})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{0})}

\displaystyle=\left|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1/2}\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}-n\bar{y}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-n\bar{y}^{2}}\right]^{\frac{n-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\left[\frac{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}-n\bar{y}^{2}}{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}-n\bar{y}^{2}-\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}}\right]^{\frac{n-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{1/2}}{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\left[1-R^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\frac{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}}{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}}\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{1/2}}{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\left[1-R^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\left[\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right]^{-1}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is the maximum likelihood estimator under model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
R^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is the coefficient of determination. Now, let

\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\left[\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right]^{-1}\right)\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}.

Then, the condition required for the Bayes factor to be information consistent in this setting can be written as

\lim_{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|\to\infty}\int\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{-1/2}\\
\left[1-R^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{y})\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{1}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=\infty

which, because of dominated convergence, can be written as

\int\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{-1/2}\\
\left\{\lim_{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|\to\infty}\left[1-R^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{y})\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\right\}f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{1}\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=\infty

(C.1)

Recalling that
\lim_{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|\to\infty}R^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=1
, (
C.1
) reduces to

\int\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{-1/2}\\

where

.

Now, since
\boldsymbol{X}
is full rank and all

\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\left[\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right]^{-1}

0<\lambda_{-}(\boldsymbol{G})\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\|^{2}\leq\\

\lambda_{+}(\boldsymbol{G})\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\|^{2}<\infty,

and

\displaystyle 0<\nu_{-}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\|^{2}\leq\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\leq\nu_{+}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\|^{2}<\infty,

where

\lambda_{-}(\boldsymbol{G})
and
\lambda_{+}(\boldsymbol{G})

\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\left[\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right]^{-1}
and
\nu_{+}
and
\nu_{-}
are the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle 0<\frac{\lambda_{-}(\boldsymbol{G})}{\nu_{+}}\leq\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{y})\leq\frac{\lambda_{+}}{\nu_{-}}(\boldsymbol{G})<\infty.

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and therefore apply to
\Upsilon^{*}(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})

\int\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{-1/2}\left[1-\Upsilon^{*}(\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{1}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\geq\\
\int\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right|^{-1/2}\left[1-\frac{\lambda_{-}(\boldsymbol{G})}{\nu_{+}}\right]^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}f(g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{1}\ldots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}.

4.1
.

The proof of Theorem
4.2
relies on the fact that
BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{0}}(\boldsymbol{y})
under the Dirichlet mixtures of block
g
prior can be written as a weighted average of Bayes factors conditional on each of the possible partitions of the
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
coefficients,

\displaystyle BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{0}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{\rho}p(\rho)BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{0}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\rho)

where

. Note that one of the terms in the sum corresponds to

Hence, if the density of the base measure satisfies

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
4.3

We start by introducing some notation. Let
\rho=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{K}\}
be a partition of
\mathcal{I}
,
m_{k}=|S_{k}|
be the number of elements in
S_{k}
and, similarly,
m_{1,k}=|S_{k}\cap\mathcal{I}_{1}|
and
m_{2,k}=|S_{k}\cap\mathcal{I}_{2}|
. Clearly,
m_{1,k},m_{2,k}\geq 0
and
m_{1,k}+m_{2,k}=m_{k}
.

Consider first the case where
\sigma^{2}
is known. Because
\boldsymbol{X}
is orthogonal, it is easy to verify that, under (
3.3
),

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho,\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{n/2}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\int_{0}^{1}u_{k}^{b}(1-u_{k})^{a+\frac{m_{k}}{2}}\exp\left\{\frac{u_{k}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{S_{k}}(N)\|}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{k}

\displaystyle\propto\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{n/2}\prod_{k=1}^{K}M\left(b+1,a+b+\frac{m_{k}}{2}+2,\frac{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{S_{k}}(N)\|}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)

where
M
is Kummer’s function
(
Abramowitz et al., 1988
)
,
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{S_{k}}(N)\|=\sum_{j\in S_{k}}\hat{\beta}_{j}^{2}(N)
, and
\hat{\beta}_{j}(N)
is the maximum likelihood estimator of
\beta_{j}
based on
\boldsymbol{y}(N)
. A well known asymptotic expansion of Kummer’s function is
M(a,b,z)\approx\frac{\Gamma(b_{0})}{\Gamma(a_{0})}z^{a_{0}-b_{0}}\exp\{z\}
for large
z
(see Equation 13.5.1 in page 508 of
Abramowitz et al., 1988
). Hence, under the assumptions of the theorem,

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho,\sigma^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\exp\left\{\mathcal{O}(N)\right\}\mathcal{O}\left(N^{1-a-\sum_{\{k:m_{1,k}>0\}}m_{k}/2}\right)

for large
N
. Now, if
\rho\preceq\rho_{0}
, then
\sum_{\{k:m_{1,k}>0\}}|S_{k}|=p_{1}
and therefore

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho,\sigma^{2}\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho_{0},\sigma^{2}\right)}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\exp\left\{\mathcal{O}(N)\right\}\mathcal{O}\left(N^{1-a-p_{1}/2}\right)}{\exp\left\{\mathcal{O}(N)\right\}\mathcal{O}\left(N^{1-a-p_{1}/2}\right)}=c_{\rho}

for some
0<c_{\rho}<\infty
.

On the other hand, if
\rho\npreceq\rho_{0}
then there exists at least one
S_{k}
such that both
m_{1,k}>0
and
m_{2,k}>0
. Recall that
m_{1,k}+m_{2,k}=m_{k}
. Therefore, for a partition
\rho\npreceq\rho_{0}
, we have
p_{1}=\sum_{k}m_{1,k}=\sum_{\{k:m_{1,k}>0\}}m_{1,k}<\sum_{\{k:m_{1,k}>0\}}m_{k}
. Hence, in this case,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho,\sigma^{2}\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho_{0},\sigma^{2}\right)}=0.

When
\sigma^{2}
is unknown, note that
Som (2014)
shows that the limit as
N\to\infty
of the posterior distribution for
\sigma^{2}
conditional on the partition is a proper, non-degenerate distribution. Hence, we have the asymptotic expansion in this case is instead

\displaystyle f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho\right)

\displaystyle\approx\mathcal{O}\left(N^{1-a-\sum_{\{k:m_{1,k}>0\}}m_{k}/2}\right).

for large
N
. Hence, we again have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\rho_{0}\right)}=\begin{cases}0&\rho\nprec\rho_{0},\\
c_{\rho}&\rho\prec\rho_{0}\end{cases}

for come
0<c_{\rho}<\infty
.

Proof of Theorem

As in Theorem
4.3
, let
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{j^{(1)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(1)}_{p_{1}}\}
denote the set of indexes associated with the covariates included in the design matrix
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
,
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{j^{(2)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(2)}_{p_{2}}\}
be the set associated with
\boldsymbol{X}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{2}
. Also, recall that
\mathcal{I}_{1}\cap\mathcal{I}_{2}=\emptyset
.
Now, note that

\displaystyle BF_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0}}(\boldsymbol{y}(N))

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\rho}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a})f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho\right)}{\sum_{\rho}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0})f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho\right)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho_{a}\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho_{0}\right)}}_{A}\underbrace{\frac{f(\rho_{a}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a})+\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{a}}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a})\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho_{a})}}{f(\rho_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0})+\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{0}}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0})\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho_{0})}}}_{B}

where
\rho_{0}=\{\mathcal{I}_{1}\}
and
\rho_{a}=\{\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2}\}
.

First focus on the
A
term. Because of the orthogonality of the design matrix, from
Som (2014)
we know that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho_{0}\right)}{f\left(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho_{a}\right)}>0.

Focus now on the
B
term. From Theorem
4.3
, the numerator is clearly strictly positive as long as the prior distribution
f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a})
puts non-zero probability on at least one refinement of of
\rho_{a}
. The prior distributions on partitions induced by the Dirichlet process obviously satisfies this requirement, as it places positive probability on every possible partition of
\mathcal{I}
. Similarly, for the denominator, note that the partitions over which we are summing are, by definition, all refinements of
\rho_{0}
. Hence, again from Theorem
4.3
, we know that the limit of each of the terms in the sum is finite and strictly positive. Hence,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{0}}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0})\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho_{0})}
is also finite and therefore

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{f(\rho_{a}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a})+\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{a}}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a})\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{a},\rho_{a})}}{f(\rho_{0}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0})+\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{0}}f(\rho\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0})\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}(N)\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{0},\rho_{0})}}>0.

Appendix F
Proof of Theorem
4.5

Since

Pr(\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\neq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})}}

and
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})>0
, it is enough to show that

\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}0.

for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\neq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}
. Now

\displaystyle\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\rho}f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho)f(\rho)}{\sum_{\rho}f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T},\rho)f(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho_{0})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T},\rho_{0})}}_{A}\underbrace{\left[\frac{f(\rho_{0})+\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{0}}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho_{0})}f(\rho)}{f(\rho_{0})+\sum_{\rho\neq\rho_{0}}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T},\rho_{0})}f(\rho)}\right]}_{B}

where
\rho_{0}=\{\{1,2,3,\ldots,p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}\}
, i.e., the partition that assigns all covariates to a single block.

Note that
A
is the Bayes factor based on the standard
g
prior. Since our hyperprior
p(g\mid a,b,\tau^{2})
is a member of the Confluent Hypergeometric (CH) family of distributions and
\tau^{2}\sim\mathcal{O}(n)
, this Bayes factor is known to be consistent (e.g., see
Li and Clyde, 2018
). Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho_{0})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T},\rho_{0})}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}0

for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\neq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}
. On the other hand,
f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho)
and
f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho_{0})
share the same likelihood and differ only on their priors, which are both (approximately) unit information. Hence,

\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho)}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho_{0})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho},

for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, where
0<c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\rho}<\infty
. Hence,
B
converges to a finite constant, and the product of A and B converges to zero as desired.

Appendix G
Details of the MCMC algorithm

As mentioned in Section
5
of the main manuscript, to construct the MCMC algorithm for our model we take advantage of the conditional conjugacy of the priors and, when possible, we integrate out the intercept
\beta_{0}
, the vector of regression coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and/or the variance
\sigma^{2}
when deriving conditional posteriors. Additionally, we represent the shrinkage coefficients
g_{1},\ldots,g_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
in terms of their unique values
\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
and the group indicators
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
. The resulting algorithm alternates sampling from the full conditionals
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
,
f(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
,
f(\alpha\mid\cdots)
,
f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\cdots)
,
f(\beta_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\cdots)
and
f(\sigma^{2}\mid\cdots)
. Special cases of our model where either the partition defined by
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and/or the model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
have been fixed in advance can be handled through slight modifications of the algorithm. The steps that we use are as follows:

1.

We sample from the conditional posterior
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)
given by

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\cdots)\propto f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi})f\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)f(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\alpha)f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}),

where
f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{g}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{g}_{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}},\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}})
corresponds to (
3.2
) with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}
, and
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is an appropriate prior on the space of models, e.g., a Beta-Binomial prior. We generate samples from the above distribution using a random walk Metropolis Hastings algorithm using a symmetric random walk proposal for
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
similar to equation (46) of
George and McCulloch (1997)
as follows:

•

We define a probability vector
p_{1}=(0.7,0.3)
.

•

Each time, we decide on one of two types of moves according to the probability vector
p_{1}
.

–

If a move type 1 is selected, then the proposed new model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}
is generated by randomly flipping one component of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

–

If a move type 2 is selected, the proposed model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}
is generated by removing one variable currently included in the model and replacing it with a variable currently excluded, leaving the dimensionality of the model unchanged. The variables to be added and removed are chosen uniformly at random within each set.

If a new variable is included in the model
\{i:\boldsymbol{\gamma}=0,\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}=1\}
, draw
\xi_{i}
from the following distribution

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\xi_{i}=k)\propto\begin{cases}m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}&\text{for }k=1,2,\dots,K_{\gamma},\\
\alpha&\text{for }k=K_{\gamma}+1.\end{cases}

If necessary, draw
\tilde{g}_{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}
from the centering measure
f(\tilde{g}_{j}\mid\tau^{2},a,b)
.

Similarly, if a variable is removed from the model, remove the corresponding
\xi_{i}
and update the number of clusters and partition if a variable that was in a singleton cluster was removed. Update to get
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(prop)},K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}},\rho_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}^{(prop)}
accordingly. Then the proposed model is accepted with probability.

\min\left\{\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}^{(prop)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(prop)})p\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}^{(prop)}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}\right)p(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(prop)}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)},\alpha)p(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)})}{f(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}},\boldsymbol{\xi})p\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{g}}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\alpha)p(\boldsymbol{\gamma})},1\right\}.

2.

Once the model is sampled, we can update
\beta_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
by exploiting normal-normal conjugacy as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{0}\mid\cdots

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\bar{\boldsymbol{y}},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\cdots

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\xi}},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\xi}})

where

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\sigma^{2}\left\{\frac{{\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{-1/2}}{\tau^{2}}+\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1},\quad\quad{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\xi}}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{y}}{\sigma^{2}}.

3.

We can sample sample variance as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\mid\cdots\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{\boldsymbol{y}^{T}(\boldsymbol{I}+\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T})^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}-n\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}^{2}}{2}\right)

4.

Conditional on the current model and the observational variance
\sigma^{2}
, sequentially sample
\xi_{i}
for variables included in the model i.e.
\mathcal{I}=\{i:\gamma_{i}=1\}
similar to Algorithm 8 of
Neal (2000)
: Let
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}
be the number of distinct
\xi_{j}
for
j\neq i
and let
h=K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}+d
. We choose
d
to be 20, by default. Label these
\xi_{j}
with values
\{1,\dots,K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}\}
. If
\xi_{i}=\xi_{j}
, for some
j\neq i
, draw values independently from the base measure given by (
3.3
) for those
\tilde{g}_{k}
for which
1\leq k\leq K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}
. If
\xi\neq\xi_{j}
for all
j\neq i
, let
\xi_{i}
have the label
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}+1
and draw independently from the base measure for those
\tilde{g}_{k}
for which
K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}+1<k\leq h
. The, draw a new value of
\xi_{i}
from
\{1,\dots,h\}
with probabilities

\displaystyle\mathsf{Pr}(\xi_{i}=k\mid\cdot)\propto\begin{cases}m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{-i}\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{\star 1/2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{\star 1/2}\right)&\text{ for }1\leq k\leq K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}\\
\frac{\alpha}{d}\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{\star 1/2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{\star 1/2}\right)&\text{ for }K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}+1<k\leq h\end{cases}

where
m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{-i}
is the number of
\xi_{j}
for
j\neq i
that are equal to k and
\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}^{\star}
is same as
\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
except for the fact that
g_{\xi_{i}}
replaced by
g_{k}
.

5.

Using equation (2) and (3) of
Rodríguez (2013)
, the posterior distribution of the concentration parameter
\alpha
can be written as

\displaystyle f(\alpha\mid\cdots)

\displaystyle\propto f(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\alpha)f(\alpha\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha+p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}\alpha^{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\prod_{k=1}^{K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\Gamma(m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k})\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-1}\frac{j}{(\alpha+j)^{2}}}

To sample from the above density, we employ a random walk Metropolis-Hasting algorithm with Gaussian proposals for
\log\alpha
; the default variance was the proposal was 0.05 but this needs to be tuned, depending on the dataset to achieve an average acceptance rate of 40-50%.

6.

The conditional posterior distribution of
\tilde{g}_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,K_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is given by

\displaystyle f(\tilde{g}_{k}\mid\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)f(\tilde{g}_{k}\mid\tau,a,b)

\displaystyle=\phi\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{0},\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\left\{\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}^{-1}\boldsymbol{G}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{1/2}\right)f(\tilde{g}_{k}\mid\tau=1,a,b)

We can simplify the above conditional posterior as

\displaystyle f(\tilde{g}_{k}\mid.)\propto{(\tilde{g}_{k})}^{b-\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}}{2}}(1+\tilde{g}_{k})^{-a-b-2}\exp\left(-\frac{v_{k}}{\tilde{g}_{k}}-\frac{w_{k}}{\sqrt{\tilde{g}_{k}}}\right),

where

\displaystyle v_{k}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}\\
i\in S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},jj}^{-1}\beta_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}\beta_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}\quad\quad w_{k}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}\\
i\notin S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}\end{subarray}}\frac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},ji}^{-1}\beta_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}\beta_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}}{\sqrt{g_{\xi_{i}}}}.

Using the transformation
t_{k}=\frac{v_{k}}{\tilde{g}_{k}}
, we can re-parametrize this density as

\displaystyle f(t_{k}\mid.)\sim t_{k}^{a+\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}}{2}}\left(1+\frac{t_{k}}{v_{k}}\right)^{-a-b-2}\exp\left(-t_{k}-\frac{w_{k}}{\sqrt{v_{k}}}\sqrt{t_{k}}\right).

Introduce the auxiliary variable
u_{k}
. Then, we can use slice sampling in conjunction with a modification of rejection sampler developed by
Liu et al. (2012)
to sample
t_{k}
from a truncated extended gamma distribution to sample
t_{k}
as follows

\displaystyle u_{k}\mid t_{k}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{U}\left(0,\left(\frac{v_{k}}{v_{k}+t_{k}}\right)^{a+b+2}\right),

\displaystyle t_{k}\mid u_{k},.

\displaystyle\sim\text{Truncated-Extended-Gamma}\left(a+\frac{m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},k}}{2}+1,\frac{w_{k}}{2\sqrt{v_{k}}},v_{k}(u_{k}^{\frac{-1}{a+b+2}}-1)\right),

where Truncated-Extended-Gamma distribution is given by

\displaystyle f(t\mid a,b,c)\propto t^{a-1}\exp(-t-2\sqrt{t}b)\mathbbm{1}_{\{0<t<c\}},\quad t>0,

for
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that rejection sampler for untruncated extended gamma distributions developed by
Liu et al. (2012)
can be modified in a straightforward manner by using truncated proposals at the truncation level
c
. This can then be used to efficiently draw from truncated versions of extended Gamma distribution.
