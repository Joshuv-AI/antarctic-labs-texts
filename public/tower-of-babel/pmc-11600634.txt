Application of digital classification based on AO classification in floating knee injury
Authors: Nan Zhou, Xingkai Zhang, Hongzhi Liu, Gangqiang Du, Dongyang Zhao, Zeyue Geng, Mingliang Ma, Zhigang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveA digital classification system based on AO classification was adopted to study and summarize different types of floating knee injuries.MethodsOne hundred cases with a floating knee injury from March 2005 to December 2020 were collected, including 74 males and 26 females with an average age of 43.74 years. The distribution of the affected limbs in there were 51 cases (51.0%) on the left and 46 cases (46.0%) on the right, as well as 3 cases (3.0%) that got bilaterally affected limbs. The femora and tibiae in this system were divided into three parts separately based on the AO classification, which was finally composed of six types after pairwise combination. The images in this study would be analyzed by using the classification to summarize the distribution rules of the classification in a floating knee injury.ResultsThe proportion of I-VI type injury was 42.7%(44/103) in type I, 18.4%(19/103) in type II, 9.7%(10/103) in type III, 10.7%(11/103) in type IV, 11.7%(12/103) in type V, and 6.8%(7/103) in type VI.ConclusionsThe digital classification system of floating knee injury based on AO classification could classify most cases of floating knee injury and cases with open injury, which referred to a reference effectively for the selection of surgical treatment plan, and would be conducive to the future clinical classification.Keywords: Floating knee injury, Femoral fracture, Tibial fracture, Classification, AO classification

Introduction

The definition of floating knee injury refers to the type of fracture that occurs in the ipsilateral femora and tibiae [1]. It is rare and accounts for 0.4% of fractures in adults [2, 3]. 70% of patients usually combine injuries to soft tissues like skin, muscle, vascular, and nerve. Other injuries like thoracic and abdominal injuries or fractures at other sites can also be seen in this injury [4, 5].

The floating knee injury has been studied since it was first proposed. The characteristics of the injury have been summarized in the current common classifications such as the Fraser classification or the Letts classification, but deficiencies such as the inclusion of an incomplete range of this injury or the inadequate assessment of the injury still exist among them. Considering this, we proposed a classification system based on AO/OTA [6] classification into the research of patients with a floating knee injury to provide a new reference for further studies.

Classification

Imaging results of all cases were collected and classified. The first step was to divide the femora and tibiae into three parts from proximal to distal based on AO classification and marked them with No. 1–6 (Fig. 1). Among them, No. 1–3 referred to the femoral part, and No. 4–6 meant the tibia part. During the process of classification, we found that fractures of the proximal femoral or distal tibial fractures would be considered the single type due to the less occurrence of fractures in these parts. In addition, patients with two or more fractures in the tibia and or femoral were classified into the same type. Finally, we classified all the cases into six types. Type I, shaft fracture of both femoral and tibial (2 + 5). Type II, femoral shaft fracture and proximal tibia fracture (2 + 4). Type III, distal femoral fracture and tibial shaft fracture (3 + 5). Type IV, distal femoral fracture, and proximal tibia fracture (3 + 4). Type V, two or more fractures of ipsilateral femoral and or tibia. Among them, cases of femoral segmental fractures were type VA while that of the tibia segmental fractures were type VB. Type VI, fractures involved proximal femur or distal tibia (1 + 6). Among them, injuries with proximal femoral fractures were typed VI A, and type VI B was that with distal tibia fracture, and type VI C was the combination of fractures on both parts.

The femora and tibiae were divided into six parts in this classification

After that, the specific type of floating knee injury may also be explained according to the subtype description of AO classification. For example, Type I(2 + 5): 32B2/42A1 refers to the wedge fractures in the femoral shaft and the simple spiral fracture in the tibial shaft (Fig. 2).

The range of fractures of each type in this classification

Patients of this injury with open fractures could also be classified with the help of IO classification based on classification.

Something in this classification should be noticed that the Type B of proximal femoral fracture on AO classification was femoral neck fracture, which was the intracapsular fracture and did not involve the metaphysis, and the Type C of proximal femoral fracture on AO classification was the isolation or compression of the femoral head, which was also not involved in the metaphysis. Both of these two types were excluded from our classification because they did not destroy the overall continuity of the femoral, which meant that they could not form a floating knee with the fracture of the tibia. Moreover, type A1 fractures of the distal femur and proximal tibia were avulsion fractures and type B fractures of the distal femoral, proximal tibia and distal tibia were unilateral condylar fractures, which were excluded due to the same reason.

Results

From March 2005 to December 2020, 100 cases were collected in this study, including 74 cases of males and 26 cases of females. The fracture site among them were 51 cases on the left and 46 cases on the right. Data like injury, associated injuries and fixation methods were collected for all patients.

In this study, both AO/OTA classification and novel classification were taught to the reachers beforehand. Each case was categorized in the same period. A consistency test of the result was performed based on Cohen’s kappa statistic and the Kappa value of 0.949 with the sigificant level.

According to the result, patients with type I was the most (44 cases, 42.7%), followed by type II (19 cases, 18.4%) and type V (12 cases, 11.7%), while type VI were the least (7cases, 6.8%). Most of these cases resulted in high-energy injuries such as traffic accidents, which were often combined with fractures in other parts, closed craniocerebral or thoracoabdominal injuries, and even hemorrhagic shock. Information of patients in different types

The floating knee injuries need different fixations under different types. Patients with open injuries usually need debridement and then external fixation at the first stage or wound healing with the affected traction for internal fixation at the second stage would be considered according to the actual situation. Conclusion on the selection of internal fixations for all patients showed that patients with type I were treated with intramedullary nails fixed for both femoral and tibia, while with type VI, they were treated with dual plates. Besides, patients with type II, III, V, and VI usually needed intramedullary nails fixed for shaft fractures and plate fixed for fractures on metaphyseal and distal fractures. While fractures on the proximal femoral should be fixed with intramedullary nails. Discussion

The classification of floating knee injury was first proposed by Blake et al., which classified the injury by classifying the different parts of the fracture [1]. After that, Fraser classification was proposed according to whether the fracture involved the knee joint, and Ran et al. modified it by referring to whether the fracture was accompanied by a patellar fracture [7]. While both of these two types were simply classified according to the fracture site involved, which could not specifically express the degree of fracture injury.

The Letts classification was proposed in 1986, which included open fractures in the classification system [8]. However, it was mostly used in cases of children and could not explain the severity of the open injury. After that, a few kinds of literature put their classification of segmental fracture forward. The overall analysis found that the fracture site in most classifications was limited that could not explain the severity and specific situation of the injury. Studies by matching and comparing the collected cases with the commonly used classifications found that all of them were not appropriate completely.

The novel classification system adopted in this study is based on the AO classification, which can classify most of the fracture types that meet the definition of a floating knee injury. At the same time, surgeons will have a certain understanding to realize the location of fractures by different types and assess the degree of fracture displacement via different subtypes. In addition, open injury of the floating knee injury can also be described by adding IO classification to this classification, which can be used to evaluate the degree of soft tissue injury more comprehensively.

This classification can summarize information about the location of the fracture and the degree of comminution on the basis of the AO classification, which makes it simpler to assess the fracture's severity in the patient's clinical work. For instance, due to the location of the fracture around the knee joint, type II patients are more likely to suffer from neurovascular damage. The classification may also serve as a guide for selecting the surgical technique.

Patients with type I in this classification have both femoral and tibial shaft fractures, which comprise the majority of cases overall. These fractures are typically fixed with intramedullary nails, consistent with the current fixation strategy for simple shaft fractures in floating knee injuries.

The fixation treatment for Type II patients with femoral shaft fractures are typically intramedullary nails, but plates, and screws are used as necessary for proximal tibial fractures. The fixation method should be determined case-by-case by the descriptions of the various subtypes.In this study, two patients were fixed with plates, which was analyzed because the two fractures were close to the proximal and distal tibia, making intramedullary nailing more challenging.

The fixation strategy for type III fractures was similar to that for type II fractures, and the data showed that most of them were fixed with plates for distal femoral fractures and intramedullary nails for tibial shaft fractures. The two patients who were fixed with plates in this study had fractures that were near the proximal and distal tibia, making intramedullary nailing more challenging. Additionally, retrograde intramedullary nails could be used to repair individuals with supracondylar fractures.

Since all patients with type IV have epiphyseal fractures, plates, and screws are necessary to establish successful fixation and restore regular lower extremity force lines to prevent the development of inversion or valgus deformity of the knee, which can impair knee function.

Multiple fractures occur together in type V patients, the fracture sites are more significant and the plate is more severely injured by internal fixation. Therefore, intramedullary nailing is frequently used to safeguard the blood supply of the fracture site and reduce stress on the soft tissues surrounding the fracture. According to this study's data, 10 shaft fractures were treated with intramedullary nailing, and the condyle fractures were treated with a plate and intramedullary nailing.

In this study, there were few patients with type VI fractures, suggesting that this type is uncommon. In contrast to the distal tibia, which needs plates and screws for internal fixation to restore force lines and fracture stability, proximal femoral fractures are typically treated with intramedullary fixation.

Our study did not conduct an analysis of patient prognosis follow-up. The prognosis is better for type II patients than for type III patients since the knee extension device is linked to the distal femur. Due to severe knee involvement, patients with Type IV floating knee injuries may have a worse prognosis for joint function.

Although the internal fixation strategy for each type of patient has some similarities, it must be seen in the context of the particular type of fracture, the location of the fracture, the surgeon's experience, and surgical habits.

Patella fractures were not considered in this classification [9]. In addition, actual cases were missed for some subtypes in this study, which need a larger sample for the study.
