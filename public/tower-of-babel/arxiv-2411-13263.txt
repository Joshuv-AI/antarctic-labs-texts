Estimating the tails of the spectrum of the Hessian of the log-likelihood for
ab-initio

Abstract
Electron cryomicroscopy (cryo-EM) is a technique in structural biology used to reconstruct accurate volumetric maps of molecules. One step of the cryo-EM pipeline involves solving an inverse-problem. This inverse-problem, referred to as ab-initio single-particle reconstruction, takes as input a collection of 2d-images – each a projection of a molecule from an unknown viewing-angle – and attempts to reconstruct the 3d-volume representing the underlying molecular density. Most methods for solving this inverse-problem search for a solution which optimizes a posterior likelihood of generating the observed image-data, given the reconstructed volume. Within this framework, it is natural to study the Hessian of the log-likelihood: the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the Hessian determine how the likelihood changes with respect to perturbations in the solution, and can give insight into the sensitivity of the solution to aspects of the input. In this paper we describe a simple strategy for estimating the smallest eigenvalues and eigenvectors (i.e., the ‘softest modes’) of the Hessian of the log-likelihood for the ab-initio single-particle reconstruction problem. This strategy involves rewriting the log-likelihood as a 3d-integral. This interpretation holds in the low-noise limit, as well as in many practical scenarios which allow for noise-marginalization. Once we have estimated the softest modes, we can use them to perform many kinds of sensitivity analysis. For example, we can determine which parts of the reconstructed volume are trustworthy, and which are unreliable, and how this unreliability might depend on the data-set and the imaging parameters. We believe that this kind of analysis can be used alongside more traditional strategies for sensitivity analysis, as well as in other applications, such as free-energy estimation.

To provide some background, a standard single-particle reconstruction pipeline typically involves several stages:

As described in

The electron-beam interacts with the electron-density in the frozen film, and elastically scattered electrons in the beam are subject to a density-dependent phase-shift

This phase-shift can be measured at each point in the image-plane, producing a ‘micrograph’.
The values of the phase-shift can be related to the electron density within the sample.
The details of this transformation depends on imaging-parameters such as the defocus level, which determines the contrast transfer function (CTF).

Scanning the micrographs for ‘picked-particles’:

The goal for this stage is to produce a collection of picked-particle images, each corresponding to a well-centered projection of a single molecule.

More specifically, the solution is typically a ‘model’ that optimizes the log-likelihood
\log P(\text{\small data}\>|\>\text{\small model})
.
Because the solution lies at a critical-point of
\log P(\text{\small data}\>|\>\text{\small model})
, any perturbations to the solution will result in a shift to the overall likelihood which depends on the Hessian of
\log P
.

While it is natural to consider the Hessian of
\log P
, it is not immediately clear how to calculate the spectrum of this object.
For example, the posterior likelihood
P(\text{\small data}\>|\>\text{\small model})
is usually defined using a standard noise-model (e.g., independent gaussian noise applied to each pixel in each picked-particle image)
[
31
,
35
]
.
In this scenario the likelihood
P(\text{\small data}\>|\>\text{\small model})
is typically written as a product of the individual image-likelihoods
P({A}_{j}\>|\>\text{\small model})
, each of which involves an accumulation of many exponential terms.
Taking a second-derivative of the overall log-likelihood with respect to the model-parameters can be difficult, especially because the model can involve many degrees of freedom (e.g., the number of voxels in the volume
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and the number of viewing-angles in
\{\tau_{j}\}
can both be large).

In this paper we make a simple observation.
Namely, when the noise-level is sufficiently small, the log-likelihood
\log\left(P(\text{\small data}\>|\>\text{\small model})\right)
can be written as a single three-dimensional integral.
This simplification allows for efficient calculation of the log-likelihood, as well as its gradient and Hessian.
A similar simplification can often be made away from the small-noise limit by marginalizing over the noise-level.

Using this simple observation, we can approximate the spectrum of the Hessian of the log-likelhood.
In particular, we can estimate the extremal eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hessian.
The eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues are sometimes called the ‘softest modes’ in the molecular-dynamics literature
[
36
]
.
The softest-mode indicates the volumetric- and viewing-angle-perturbations which are most easily confused for the current solution.
The softest eigenvalue is a measure of the fisher-information restricted to the direction of this particular perturbation.

Calculation of the softest-modes allows for various kinds of sensitivity analysis.
For example, we can use the soft subspace to determine which parts of the reconstructed volume are trustworthy, and which are unreliable.
We can also use this approach to determine the benefit of adding more images to the image-pool; often the information gained will depend on how those new images are aligned to the volume, as well as other image properties such as the defocus-level.
Additionally, when using cryo-EM data to estimate free-energy landscapes, we can use the spectrum of the hessian to help guide our choice of collective-variables.
We believe that this kind of analysis can be used alongside more traditional strategies for sensitivity analysis, such as bootstrapping and crossvalidation.
For example, we believe the structure of the soft subspace is a useful complement to the fourier-shell-correlation, which is often used to characterize the robustness of an ab-initio reconstruction
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

This paper is structured as follows.
We first briefly review the mathematical details underlying our approach.
We then provide several examples illustrating different applications of this kind of analyis.
We conclude by discussing some extensions of this work.
Many of the technical details are explained in the Appendix.

2
Methods Summary

In this section we briefly summarize our methodology, with the details deferred to the Appendix.

As described above, our methodology predominantly applies to the single-volume reconstruction problem.
To set the stage, let’s assume that we are given a collection of
J
picked-particle images
\{{A}_{j}\}
.
To ease the presentation for now, we’ll ignore the image-specific displacements
\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}
and contrast-transfer-functions
\{CTF_{j}\}
.

For any particular image
{A}
and volume
{F}
, we can define the likelihood:
P({A}\>|\>{F},P(\tau);\sigma)
, which is the probability of observing the image
{A}
, given that the true signal is produced by the volume
{F}
with image-noise
\sigma
.
In this expression the term
P(\tau)
is the prior-distribution of viewing-angles
\tau\in SO3
associated with the data.

The setting we consider assumes that a single-particle reconstruction algorithm has already been run, and has produced a volume
{F}
which optimizes the posterior likelihood of observing the collection of images
\{{A}_{j}\}
:

\displaystyle P\left(\{{A}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{F},P(\tau);\sigma\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}P\left({A}_{j}\>\big|\>{F},P(\tau);\sigma\right)\text{.}

(1)

For our purposes we’ll perform most of our calculations in fourier-space, referring to
{\widehat{A}}_{j}
and
{\widehat{F}}
as the 2d- and 3d-fourier-transforms of
{A}_{j}
and
{F}
, respectively, and using
{\hat{\sigma}}
to denote the image-noise in fourier-space.

When the image-noise is relatively small, we can approximate the likelihood in Eq.
1
with the so-called ‘low-temperature’ limit:

P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\},{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\cdot{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}\right)\right)\text{,}

where the negative-log-likelihood
{\cal L}
takes the form of a sum of
2
-dimensional integrals:

\displaystyle{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}\right)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|{\widehat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})-{\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};{\widehat{F}}\right)\right|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{.}

(2)

In this last expression we use
{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\widehat{F}})
to denote the fourier-transform of a
2
-dimensional projection of any given volume
{\widehat{F}}
along viewing-angle
\tau
, and denote by
\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}
the ‘optimum’ viewing-angle for image-
j
.
As we’ll discuss below, the viewing-angle
\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}
implicitly depends on
{\widehat{F}}
, and is often referred to as the ‘optimal-alignment’ of
{\widehat{A}}_{j}
to
{\widehat{F}}
.

The expression in Eq.
2
can be rewritten as a single
3
-dimensional integral:

{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}\right)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{2}\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\left(M_{20}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\left|{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right|^{2}-2\Re\left(M_{11}^{{\dagger}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}){\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right)+M_{02}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right)\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\left|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\right|}\text{,}

(3)

where
M_{20}
,
M_{11}
,
M_{02}
are each
3
-dimensional functions corresponding to the various moments of the data, and the integral is performed over frequency-space
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Considering the image-pool
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}
to be fixed, we can consider the negative-log-likelihood
{\cal L}
to be a function of the volume
{\widehat{F}}
and optimal-alignments
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}
.
By using both Eq.
2
and
3
, we can take various derivatives of this log-likelihood.
For our purposes we will assume that, with the image-pool fixed, the volume and the optimal-alignments
{[{\widehat{F}},\{\tau_{j}\}]}^{\intercal}
are situated at a local minimum of
{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}\right)
.
Consequently, we expect that perturbations to these quantities will produce second-order perturbations to
{\cal L}
of the form:

\displaystyle{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}+\Delta{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}+\Delta\tau_{j}\}\right)={\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta{\widehat{F}}\\
\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}\\
\end{array}\right]^{\dagger}\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}H_{FF}&H_{F\tau}\\
H_{\tau F}&H_{\tau\tau}\end{array}\right]\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta{\widehat{F}}\\
\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}\\
\end{array}\right]\text{.}

In this formula we use ‘
\dagger
’ to denote the adjoint (i.e., conjugate-transpose).
We also use the terms
H_{FF}
,
H_{F\tau}
,
H_{\tau F}
and
H_{\tau\tau}
to refer to blocks of the second-derivative (i.e., Hessian) of
{\cal L}
.
The diagonal-blocks
H_{FF}
and
H_{\tau\tau}
are trivial to calculate: the former is diagonal in fourier-space while the latter decomposes into small block-diagonal elements for each image.
The off-diagonal-blocks
H_{F\tau}
and
H_{\tau F}
require slightly more care.
In our calculation we use the volumetric-representation in Eq.
3
to calculate
H_{F\tau}
for any particular choice of
\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}
, and we use the 2-dimensional representation in Eq.
2
to calculate
H_{\tau F}
for any particular choice of
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
.

In most practical scenarios of interest the volume
{\widehat{F}}
and optimal-alignments
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}
are implicitly related: the optimal-alignment
\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}
for any particular image
{\widehat{A}}_{j}
is the viewing-angle for which that image aligns best to
{\widehat{F}}
.
Thus, we can consider the
\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}
each to be functions of
{\widehat{F}}
, and correspondingly interpret
{\cal L}
as a function of
{\widehat{F}}
alone.

With this perspective the negative-log-likelihood takes the form:

\displaystyle{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}+\Delta{\widehat{F}}\right)={\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{\dagger}\cdot\left[H\right]\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}\text{,}

(11)

with
[H]
representing the Hessian of
{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
with respect to volumetric-perturbations of
{\widehat{F}}
.

With this formalism in place, we can immediately see that the influence of any perturbation
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
on the likelihood is determined by the rayleigh-quotient.
That is to say, the log-likelihood-ratio

\displaystyle\Delta{\cal L}:={\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}+\Delta{\widehat{F}}\right)-{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{\dagger}\cdot\left[H\right]\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}\text{,}

(12)

contrasting the original volume
{\widehat{F}}
with the perturbed volume
{\widehat{F}}+\Delta{\widehat{F}}
is given by the rayleigh-quotient
\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{\dagger}[H]\Delta{\widehat{F}}
.
Thus, given any volumetric-perturbation
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
, the rayleigh-quotient associated with that
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
can be thought of as a measure of ‘information’ in that direction.

More generally, the critical-points of the rayleigh-quotient correspond to the eigenvectors of
[H]
.
Eigenvectors corresponding to the largest eigenvalues correspond to volumetric-perturbations
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
for which the nearby volumes
{\widehat{F}}\pm\Delta{\widehat{F}}
are most easily distinguishable from the original volume
{\widehat{F}}
.
I.e., these ‘stiff modes’ correspond to volumetric-perturbations which produce a significant mismatch between the projections of the perturbed volume and the image-pool.
On the other hand, the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues indicate volumetric-perturbations for which the nearby volumes are
least
distinguishable from the original
{\widehat{F}}
.
I.e., these ‘soft modes’ correspond to volumetric-perturbations which produce only subtle shifts to the relevant projections of the perturbed volume; shifts that might still allow for plausible alignment to the images.

With this in mind, we are motivated to estimate the spectrum of
H
from Eq.
11
, specifically the lower-tail of the spectrum, comprising the smallest eigenvalues as well as their corresponding eigenvectors (i.e., the softest-modes).
In terms of computational details, the Hessian
H
involves an accumulation of terms combining the various blocks
H_{FF}
,
H_{F\tau}
,
H_{\tau F}
and
H_{\tau\tau}
from Eq.
2
with the volumetric-perturbations
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
and the (implicitly-determined) alignment-perturbations
\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}
.
As a result, the
H
from Eq.
11
is not quite as easy to diagonalize as the
H_{FF}
from Eq.
2
.
However, we can still compute quantities such as
H\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}
relatively easily, allowing us to use iterative strategies such as lanczos-iteration to estimate the extreme eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
H
.
During this calculation we make sure to focus only on true deformations, projecting away any combinations of volumetric- and alignment-perturbations
[\Delta{\widehat{F}},\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}]
which correspond to trivial rigid rotations.

As mentioned above, the details of this calculation are provided in the Appendix.
Importantly, for our calculation, the soft modes (as well as the rest of the spectrum of the Hessian) depend on the volume
{\widehat{F}}
and the image-pool
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}
, as well as on the optimal-alignments for the images (which are determined implicitly).
More generally, the soft modes also depend on the image-specific CTF-functions
\{CTF_{j}\}
(see applications below) as well as other image-parameters, such as the image-specific displacements
\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}
(mentioned in the discussion).

3
Applications

As a straightforward application, one can simply inspect the softest modes of a given single-particle reconstruction.
The softest modes can then be used to directly estimate how robust that reconstruction might be.
More specifically, the magnitude of the ‘softest eigenvalue’ associated with the softest mode determines the size of the largest volumetric-perturbation which can be ‘confused’ with the original reconstruction.
That is to say, the largest volumetric-perturbation that can’t easily be rejected in favor of the original reconstruction (under a standard hypothesis test).
The softest modes can also be used to identify (i) which regions of the original reconstruction are least trustworthy, and (ii) which images-alignments are least trustworthy.

3.5
case-study: paths in volume-space

As demonstrated above, the structure of the softest subspace depends on the viewing-angle distribution, as well as on imaging parameters such as the defocus.
The softest subspace also obviously depends on the particular volume which is provided as a solution to the single-particle-reconstruction problem.
This dependence can be of interest in certain scenarios, such as free-energy estimation
[
43
]
.

To illustrate this phenomenon, we consider a one-dimensional family of conformations for an RNA-molecule, shown in Fig
22
.
This family of conformations corresponds to a particular path of interest in volume-space, and can be parametrized by a ‘collective variable’
s
.
As described in
[
43
]
, it is in principle possible to use cryo-EM data to estimate the free-energy density
G(s)
along this path.
This kind of free-energy estimate involves calculating, for each volume
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
along the path, the likelihood that the image-pool might produce that particular volume.
Those values of
s
for which
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
is more likely will correspond to lower values of
G(s)
.

When performing this calculation, it is important to consider the interplay between the path-tangent
\partial_{s}{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
(i.e., the direction in volume-space corresponding to perturbations in the path-variable
s
) and the softest modes of the Hessian
H(s)
of the likelihood at each location along the path.
Locations
s
where the path-tangent
\partial_{s}{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
lies along a stiff-mode of the Hessian
H(s)
correspond, locally, to locations along the path that are highly discriminable from their neighbors (given the image-pool).
In other words, these are locations where projections of
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
are not easily confused for projections of nearby volumes
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s\pm\Delta s)
.
Conversely, locations where the path-tangent
\partial_{s}{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
lies parallel to a soft-mode of the Hessian
H(s)
correspond, locally, to locations along the path that are easily confused with their neighbors.
In other words, these are locations where projections of
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s)
are quite similar to projections of nearby volumes
{F}({\boldsymbol{x}};s\pm\Delta s)
.

Thus, the reliability of any free-energy estimate
G(s)
will be influenced by the rayleigh-quotient:

{\cal R}(s):=[\partial_{s}{\widehat{F}}(\cdot;s)]^{\dagger}[H(s)][\partial_{s}{\widehat{F}}(\cdot;s)]\text{.}

Locations along the path for which
{\cal R}(s)
is small will correspond to locations where the estimate of
G(s)
is inherently unreliable, simply due to the poor conditioning of the likelihood function.
Estimates of
{\cal R}(s)
are shown in Fig
22
.
This calculation was performed in the low-temperature limit, for which the likelihood can be approximated by Eq.
1
.
Similar calculations can be performed away from the low-temperature limit by marginalizing over the noise-parameter (see
[
44
]
and section
23
in the Appendix).

For the collective-variable shown in Fig
22
, the values of
{\cal R}(s)
are smallest when the RNA-molecule is ‘closed’; i.e., there are many projections along which neighboring closed configurations seem quite similar to one another.
By contrast, the values of
{\cal R}(s)
are largest when the RNA-molecule is ‘open’; i.e., there are comparatively fewer projections along which neighboring open configurations seem similar.
These differences in
{\cal R}(s)
can be important when interpreting free-energy estimates.
Moreover, it may be worth considering
{\cal R}(s)
when designing a collective variable.
By ensuring that the path-tangent travels along stiff-modes of the Hessian of the likelihood-function for each point in volume-space, it may be possible to mitigate one source of uncertainty in the final free-energy estimate.

4
Discussion

In cryo-EM, single-particle reconstruction algorithms typically search for volumes which maximize a likelihood function.
Consequently, the effect of any local perturbation depends on the Hessian of the log-likelihood in Eq.
1
.
In the context of
ab-initio
reconstruction, this log-likelihood can often be approximated with either a low-temperature limit or a noise-marginalization, yielding the simple accumulation of 2d-integrals shown in Eq.
2
.
This simple structure allows the log-likelihood to be represented as a volumetric integral akin to Eq.
3
.
Together, these 2d- and 3d-representations of the log-likelihood allow us to recover the extremal eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hessian, opening the door to several kinds of sensitivity analyses.
We believe that these analyses can complement more traditional strategies for sensitivity analysis, such as bootstrapping and crossvalidation (e.g., measuring the fourier-shell-correlation between reconstructions built using disjoint subsets of the image-pool)
[
37
,
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

In the applications described above, we have illustrated how the ‘softest modes’ of the hessian can be used to quantify how robust a particular single-particle reconstruction might be.
For example, one can examine the softest subspace to identify which regions of the molecule are most uncertain.
The same can be said for the image-alignments, and the softest modes can quantify which (implicitly determined) image-alignments are most uncertain.
The softest subspace of the Hessian can also be used to probe how the uncertainty depends on properties of the image-pool, such as the viewing-angle distribution and range of defocus-values.

We expect this kind of analysis to be useful in scenarios where the viewing-angle distribution is naturally nonuniform, and where experimentalists have a range of defocus values to choose from.
One such example might be the reconstruction of membrane proteins from picked-particle images of cellular lammelae
[
45
,
46
]
.
There are scenarios where the picked-particles can be biased towards regions where the cell membrane intersects the micrograph transversely.
In such scenarios, the membrane-proteins in any picked-particle images would typically be viewed from a ‘side’ (i.e., a direction tangent to the membrane) rather than from the ‘top’ (i.e., the direction orthogonal to the membrane).
As illustrated above, nonuniformity in the viewing-angle distribution can result in large uncertainties for particular regions of the molecule.
This can be particularly problematic when these large uncertainties occur in regions of interest (e.g., the pores of a membrane channel).
By analyzing how the softest modes of the Hessian depend on the viewing-angle distribution and the imaging parameters, one can determine which additional viewing-angles (and choice of defocus-values) would help reduce this uncertainty the most.

While most of our examples involve single-particle reconstruction, the structure of the Hessian can provide important information in other applications as well.
For example, when using cryo-EM data to estimate free-energy landscapes one often considers paths in conformation-space.
Each location along a conformational-path can be associated with a conformation-specific Hessian, which in turn produces conformation-specific soft modes.
In such a scenario, the relative orientation between the conformational-tangent-space and the softest subspace of the conformation-specific Hessian plays a role in determining the local sensitivity of any free-energy estimate.

The analysis of the Hessian can also shed light on some of the difficulties associated with multi-particle reconstruction.
As described in section
24
of the Appendix, multi-particle reconstruction can be ill-posed and/or yield spurious heterogeneity even in situations where the analogous single-particle reconstruction problem is well-posed.
While we do not have a generic solution to this ill-posedness, we suggest applying the analysis above – namely calculating and inspecting the softest modes – for each of the volumes produced in a multi-particle reconstruction.
These softest modes can reveal regions of high sensitivity which could indicate ill-posedness.

Finally, while our presentation above largely ignored image-specific translations
\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}
, one can easily incorporate these translations into the definitions of the likelihood and the corresponding Hessian (see section
12
in the Appendix).
In future work we will expand our computational strategy to account for such translations, and plan to use strategies from
[
47
]
to accelerate this computation.

5
Image notation

We use
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
to represent spatial position and frequency, respectively.
In polar coordinates these vectors are represented as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(x\cos\theta,x\sin\theta)

(13)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(k\cos\psi,k\sin\psi)\text{.}

(14)

The Fourier transform of a
2
-dimensional function
A\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is defined as

{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}A({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}~.

(15)

We recover
A
from
{\widehat{A}}
using the inverse Fourier transform:

A({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{k}}}\text{.}

(16)

The inner product between two functions
A,B\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is written as

\langle A,B\rangle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}A({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\dagger}B({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathop{d{\boldsymbol{x}}}~,

(17)

where
z^{\dagger}
is the complex conjugate of
z\in\mathbb{C}
.
We will also use Plancherel’s theorem
[
49
]
,

\langle A,B\rangle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\langle{\widehat{A}},{\widehat{B}}\rangle~,\qquad\forall A,B\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})~,

(18)

ignoring the prefactors of
2\pi
associated with the Fourier transform when they are not relevant.

We represent any given image as a function
A\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with values corresponding to the image intensity at each location.
Any inner product between
A
and
B
can be calculated equally well in either real or frequency space.
Because of recent image alignment tools developed in the context of cryo-EM molecular reconstruction
[
50
,
47
]
, we will typically refer to images in frequency space (i.e.,
{\widehat{A}}
, rather than
A
).

Abusing notation, we’ll refer to
A({\boldsymbol{k}})
and
{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
in polar coordinates as:

\displaystyle A(x,\theta)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{x}})=A(x\cos\theta,x\sin\theta)

(19)

\displaystyle{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\widehat{A}}(k\cos\psi,k\sin\psi)\text{.}

(20)

With this notation, each
{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)
for fixed
k
and
\psi\in[0,2\pi)
corresponds to a ‘ring’ in frequency space with radius
k
.

6
Rotating images: the Fourier–Bessel basis

Using the notation above, a rotation
{\cal R}_{\gamma}
by angle
\gamma
can be represented as:

{\cal R}_{\gamma}A(x,\theta):=A(x,\theta-\gamma)\text{.}

(21)

Since rotation commutes with the Fourier transform, we have:

\widehat{{\cal R}_{\gamma}A}(k,\psi)={\cal R}_{\gamma}\circ{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)={\widehat{A}}(k,\psi-\gamma)\text{.}

(22)

In this manner, a rotation of any image by
+\gamma
can be represented as an angular shift of each image ring by
\psi\rightarrow\psi-\gamma
.

The effect of a particular rotation can also be applied in a different basis: the Fourier–Bessel basis.
To define the Fourier–Bessel coefficients of an image we recall that, for each fixed
k
, the image ring
{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)
is a
2\pi
-periodic function of
\psi
.
Thus, we can represent each image ring
{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)
as a Fourier series in
\psi
, obtaining

{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)=\sum_{q=-\infty}^{+\infty}{\check{A}}(k,q)\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}q\psi}\text{,}

(23)

for
q\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The Fourier–Bessel coefficients
{\check{A}}(k,q)
of the image ring
{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)
are given by

{\check{A}}(k,q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}~.

(24)

These coefficients can be represented in a more traditional fashion by recalling that the Bessel function
J_{q}(kx)
can be written as:

\displaystyle J_{q}(kx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}kx\sin(\psi)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}~

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\cos(\psi+\pi/2)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}~,

(26)

which, when combined with the definition of the Fourier transform, immediately implies that:

\displaystyle{\check{A}}(k,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint A(x,\theta)\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\cos(\psi-\theta)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}xdx\mathop{d\theta}~

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint A(x,\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(\theta+\pi/2)}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kx\cos(\psi+\pi/2)-\mathrm{i}q\psi}\mathop{d\psi}xdx\mathop{d\theta}~

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint A(x,\theta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q(\theta+\pi/2)}J_{q}(kx)xdx\mathop{d\theta}~,

(29)

which is the inner product between the original image (in real space) and a ‘Fourier–Bessel’ function.

The rotation
{\cal R}_{\gamma}
can now be represented as:

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\gamma}{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi-\gamma)

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{+\infty}{\check{A}}(k,q)\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}q(\psi-\gamma)}

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q=-\infty}^{+\infty}{\check{A}}(k,q)\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\gamma}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}q\psi}\text{,}

(32)

such that the Fourier–Bessel coefficients of the rotated image ring
{\cal R}_{\gamma}\circ{\widehat{A}}(k,\cdot)
are given by the original Fourier–Bessel coefficients
{\check{A}}(k,q)
, each multiplied by the phase factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}q\gamma}
.
Note that (
32
) naturally allows for rotation by any
\gamma
, even values of
\gamma
that may not lie on the polar grid used to discretize the images.

In a typical discretization scheme (see below) the images
{\widehat{A}}
and
{\widehat{B}}
each require the storage of
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
values, as do the Fourier–Bessel representations
{\check{A}}
and
{\check{B}}
.
The calculation of the array
\widehat{{\cal X}}(q)
involves
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
operations and
\mathcal{O}(N)
storage.
Once
\widehat{{\cal X}}
is calculated, the inner products
{\cal X}(\gamma)
can be recovered on a uniform grid of
\mathcal{O}(N)
angles
\gamma\in[0,2\pi)
with an additional
\mathcal{O}(N\log(N))
operations using the FFT.
More details are given in
[
50
,
47
,
42
]
.

7
Image noise model

We assume that the images considered can be modeled as the sum of a ‘signal’ plus a ‘noise’.
We assume that the signal corresponds to a 2-dimensional projection of a (smooth) molecular electron density function, while the noise corresponds to detector noise
[
31
,
32
,
35
,
18
]
.
We do not consider more complicated sources of noise, such as structural noise associated with image preparation (see
[
51
]
).

Based on these simple assumptions, we’ll model the noise in real space as independent and identically distributed (iid), with a variance of
\sigma^{2}
on a unit scale in 2-dimensional real space.
Thus, the image
A
evaluated at pixel
(n_{1},n_{2})
has a value modeled by:

A_{n_{1},n_{2}}=A({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n_{1},n_{2}})=A^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}+A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}\text{,}

(33)

where the signal and noise are represented by the arrays
A^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
and
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
, respectively.
Because each entry in
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
corresponds to an average over the area element
{\Delta x}^{2}
associated with a single pixel, we expect the variance of each
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
to scale inversely with
{\Delta x}^{2}
.
That is to say, we’ll assume that each element of the noise array
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
is drawn from the standard normal distribution
{\cal N}\left(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{{\Delta x}^{2}}\right)
, with a mean of
0
and a variance of
\sigma^{2}/{\Delta x}^{2}
.

Given the assumptions above, we expect that
{\widehat{A}}
will be modeled by:

{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})+{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(34)

where
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
is now complex and iid.
Because the noise term
A^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
is real, the noise term
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
will be complex, with the conjugacy constraint that
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}(+{\boldsymbol{k}})={\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}(-{\boldsymbol{k}})^{\dagger}
.

If we were to sample
{\boldsymbol{k}}
on the uniform
N\times N
cartesian grid associated with the standard 2-dimensional fast Fourier transform, then
{\boldsymbol{k}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
will correspond to the frequency
{\Delta k}\cdot(n_{1},n_{2})
, with frequency spacing
{\Delta k}=\pi
and indices
(n_{1},n_{2})
considered periodically in the range
[-N/2,\ldots,+N/2-1]
.
In this case the transformation between
A_{n_{1},n_{2}}
and
{\widehat{A}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
will be unitary.
When
(n_{1},n_{2})
corresponds to an index pair that is periodically reflected onto itself (i.e., the four index pairs
(0,0)
,
(N/2,0)
,
(0,N/2)
and
(N/2,N/2)
) then
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}_{n_{1},n_{2}}
will be real and drawn from
{\cal N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{{\Delta x}^{2}})
.
When
(n_{1},n_{2})
corresponds to an index pair that is
not
periodically reflected onto itself, the real and imaginary components of
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}_{n_{1},n_{2}})
will each be drawn iid from
{\cal N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2{\Delta x}^{2}})
subject to the conjugacy constraint above.

Motivated by this observation, we define
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}=\frac{\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}}{{\Delta x}^{2}}
as the variance on a unit scale in 2-dimensional frequency space.
We expect that the noise term
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
integrated over any area element
{\Delta k}^{2}
in frequency space will have a variance of
\frac{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}{{\Delta k}^{2}}
.

8
Image-image similarity: likelihood

Given one ‘noisy’ image
{\widehat{A}}={\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}+{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
and another ‘noiseless’ image
{\widehat{B}}={\widehat{B}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}
, we can derive the standard likelihood of observing any value of
{\widehat{A}}
, given that the signals
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}
and
{\widehat{B}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}
are the same:

P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{A}})\cdot P({\widehat{A}})=P({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}})\cdot P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}})\text{,}

(35)

which can be rearranged into:

P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{A}})=\frac{P({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}})\cdot P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}})}{P({\widehat{A}})}\text{.}

(36)

Assuming a uniform prior
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}})
, the posterior probability
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}}|{\widehat{A}})
is proportional to the likelihood
P({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}={\widehat{B}})
.

\displaystyle P({\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\>\big|\>{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{k}})):=P({\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\>\big|\>{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})={\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{k}}))=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\ {\hat{\sigma}})^{{\text{dof}}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}}|{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})-{\widehat{B}}({\boldsymbol{k}})|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\text{,}

(37)

where
dof
refers to the number of independent degrees of freedom in the image (e.g.,
dof
could be the number of pixels in the image
A({\boldsymbol{x}})
, or the number of quadrature-nodes used to discretize
{\widehat{A}}(k,\psi)
).

When dealing with two noisy images
{\widehat{A}}={\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}+{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
and
{\widehat{B}}={\widehat{B}}^{{\text{\tiny signal}}}+{\widehat{B}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
, as long as
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
is independent from
{\widehat{B}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
the same general argument applies. The only difference in this scenario is that the effective variance involves both the variance of
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
and
{\widehat{B}}^{{\text{\tiny noise}}}
.

9
Volume notation

We use
{\boldsymbol{\chi}},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
to represent spatial position and frequency, respectively.
in spherical coordinates the vector
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
is represented as:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}},\text{\ \ with\ \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=(\cos\phi\sin\theta,\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\text{,}

(38)

with polar angle
\theta
and azimuthal angle
\phi
representing the unit vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
on the surface of the sphere
S^{2}
.

Using a right handed basis, a rotation about the third axis by angle
\alpha
is represented as:

{\cal R}_{\alpha}^{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha&0\\
+\sin\alpha&+\cos\alpha&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)\text{,}

(39)

and a rotation about the second axis by angle
\beta
is represented as:

{\cal R}_{\beta}^{y}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}+\cos\beta&0&+\sin\beta\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\beta&0&+\cos\beta\end{array}\right)\text{.}

(40)

A rotation
{\cal R}_{\tau}
of a vector
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be represented by the vector of Euler angles
\tau=(\gamma,\beta,\alpha)
:

{\cal R}_{\tau}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={\cal R}_{\alpha}^{z}\circ{\cal R}_{\beta}^{y}\circ{\cal R}_{\gamma}^{z}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\text{.}

(41)

We represent any given volume as a function
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, with values corresponding to the volume intensity at each location
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\in\Omega_{1}
.
We’ll refer to
{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
in spherical coordinates as
{\widehat{F}}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
.
With this notation, each
{\widehat{F}}(k,\cdot)
corresponds to a ‘shell’ in frequency space with radius
k
.
The rotation of any volume
{\cal R}_{\tau}{\widehat{F}}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
corresponds to the function
{\widehat{F}}(k,{\cal R}_{\tau}^{-1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
.

10
Rotation in the spherical-harmonic basis

Using the notation above, we can represent a volume
{\widehat{F}}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
as:

{\widehat{F}}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{m=+l}{\widehat{F}}_{l}^{m}(k)Y_{l}^{m}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\text{,}

(42)

where
Y_{l}^{m}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
represents the spherical-harmonic of degree
l
and order
m
:

Y_{l}^{m}(\theta,\phi)=Z_{l}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}m\phi}P_{l}^{m}(\cos\theta)\text{,}

(43)

with
P_{l}^{m}
representing the (unnormalized) associated Legendre polynomial, and
Z_{l}^{m}
the normalization constant:

Z_{l}^{m}=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\times\frac{\left(l-|m|\right)!}{\left(l+|m|\right)!}}\text{.}

(44)

The coefficients
{\widehat{F}}_{l}^{m}(k)
define the spherical-harmonic expansion of the
k
-shell
{\widehat{F}}(k,\cdot)
.

Using this spherical-harmonic basis allows us to efficiently apply rotations.
For example, given a rotation by
\tau=(\gamma,\beta,\alpha)
, we can represent the rotated volume
{\widehat{G}}:={\cal R}_{\tau}{\widehat{F}}
as:

{\widehat{G}}_{l}^{m_{1}}=\sum_{m_{2}=-l}^{m_{2}=+l}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{1}\alpha}d_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{l}(\beta)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}m_{2}\gamma}{\widehat{F}}_{l}^{m_{2}}\text{,}

(45)

where
d_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{l}(\beta)
represents the degree
l
Wigner d-matrix associated with the interior Euler angle
\beta
.

11
Volume projection in the spherical-harmonic basis

We denote the projection of a volume
F({\boldsymbol{\chi}})
(with
{\boldsymbol{\chi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
) along a particular viewing-angle
\tau
by the function
S({\boldsymbol{x}};\tau;F)
(with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
).
We’ll refer to this projection
S({\boldsymbol{x}};\tau;F)
as the ‘template’ associated with
F({\boldsymbol{\chi}})
and viewing-angle
\tau
.

By the Fourier-slice theorem we can decribe the Fourier-transformed template
{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau,{\widehat{F}})
via:

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\widehat{F}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\widehat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(46)

where
\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}
denotes taking the equatorial slice in the Fourier domain.

In terms of computation, the
\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}
operator can be described by restricting the unit-vector
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
in Eq.
38
to the equatorial-plane, corresponding to setting
\beta\equiv 0
and associating
\alpha=:\psi
.

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\widehat{F}}\right](k,\psi)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{m=+l}{\widehat{F}}_{l}^{m}(k)Z_{l}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{+\mathrm{i}m\psi}P_{l}^{m}(0)\text{.}

(47)

If we consider the effect of a contrast-transfer-function
CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})
, then the template associated with
F({\boldsymbol{\chi}})
and a viewing-angle
\tau
can be written as:

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;CTF;{\widehat{F}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\widehat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{.}

(48)

If we were also to consider the effect of an image-dependent displacement
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
, then the template associated with
F({\boldsymbol{\chi}})
and a viewing-angle
\tau
can be written as:

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;{\widehat{F}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\widehat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})\text{,}

(49)

where
\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\boldsymbol{k}})
is the fourier-signature corresponding to a 2-dimensional translation by
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\boldsymbol{k}}):=\exp\left(-i{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\text{.}

(50)

12
Image-volume similarity: likelihood

12.1
single-particle:

Referring back to section
8
and Eq.
37
, we can express the likelihood that a particular volume
{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
might produce a particular image
{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
with CTF-function
CTF({\boldsymbol{k}})
via projection along viewing-angle
\tau
and translation by a displacement-vector
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
:

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;{\widehat{F}})\right)\text{.}

(51)

The likelihood
P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}\right)
can be marginalized across
\tau
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
to calculate the likelihood that
{\widehat{F}}
might produce the image
{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
:

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\tau\in SO3}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}\right)\cdot P\left(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)d\tau d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\tau\in SO3}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}\right)\cdot P\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\widehat{F}}\right)\cdot P\left(\tau\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)d\tau d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\text{,}

(53)

where
P\left(\tau\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
is prior distribution attributed to the viewing-angle
\tau
,
P\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\widehat{F}}\right)
is the prior distribution attributed to
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
for a particular value of
\tau
, and
P\left(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
is the joint-prior over the alignments
\tau
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.
To emphasize the dependence of
P({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
on the prior distributions and noise-level, we will sometimes write:

P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)=:P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\text{.}

In this manuscript we will typically assume that
\delta\equiv 0
.
This corresponds to taking the limit
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}\rightarrow 0
, resulting in
P\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\widehat{F}}\right)
becoming sharply peaked at the origin.
Under this limit we’ll suppress the notation for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.
More generally, we can consider
P\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\widehat{F}}\right)
to be an isotropic gaussian centered at the origin:

P\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\widehat{F}}\right)\ =\ P({\boldsymbol{\delta}})\ =\ \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|{\boldsymbol{\delta}}|^{2}}{2\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}^{2}}\right)\text{,}

where
\sigma_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}}
is a fixed small distance (e.g., one tenth the diameter of each picture).

Given a pool of
J
images
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}
and their associated CTF-functions
\{CTF_{j}\}
(both indexed by
j\in 1,\ldots J
), we can accumulate terms from Eq.
53
to calculate the likelihood that
{\widehat{F}}_{i}
might produce this collection of images:

\displaystyle P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)=P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}P\left({\widehat{A}}_{j}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\text{.}

(54)

12.2
multi-particle:

In the context of multi-particle reconstruction, we might consider
I
different volumes
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
(indexed by
i\in 1,\ldots I
), each with their own prior distributions.
In this context we can calculate the likelihood that the collection
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
might produce the image
{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
:

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)\cdot P\left({\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\tau\in SO3}\sum_{i=1}^{I}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)\cdot P\left(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)\cdot P\left({\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)d\tau d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\tau\in SO3}\sum_{i=1}^{I}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)\cdot P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)d\tau d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\text{,}\text{.}

(57)

where
P\left({\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)
is the prior probability that the image
{\widehat{A}}
was drawn from the volume
{\widehat{F}}_{i}
, and
P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)
is the prior probability that the image
{\widehat{A}}
was drawn from the volume
{\widehat{F}}_{i}
with viewing-angle
\tau
and displacement
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.
Once again, to emphasize the dependence of
P({\widehat{A}}\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\})
on the prior distributions and noise-level, we will sometimes write:

P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)=:P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\>;\>P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\text{.}

Just as in Eq.
54
, we can accumulate terms from Eq.
57
to calculate the likelihood that
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
might produce a collection of images:

\displaystyle P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)=P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\>;\>P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{J}P\left({\widehat{A}}_{j}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\>;\>P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\text{.}

(58)

Image-volume similarity in the low-temperature limit

13.1
single-particle:

The likelihood in Eq.
54
depends via Eqs.
53
,
51
and
37
on the estimated noise-level
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}
, which is related to the signal-to-noise ratio, or ‘snr’, and is sometimes referred to as the estimated ‘temperature’ used in the bayesian framework.
In the low-temperature limit that
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}\rightarrow 0
, the integral in Eq.
53
becomes dominated by the contribution associated with the ‘maximum-likelihood’ alignment-angle for the image
{\widehat{A}}
.
This maximum-likelihood alignment occurs for the viewing-angle, displacement and volume which maximize the summand
P({\widehat{A}}\>|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}})\cdot P(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
in the integrand of Eq.
53
.
More specifically, given a particular volume
{\widehat{F}}
, the maximum-likelihood alignment
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\}
for an image
{\widehat{A}}
occurs at:

\displaystyle\{\ \tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\ ,\ {\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\ \}:=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\{\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\}}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}\right)\text{,}

(59)

where
\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
are restricted to lie in the support of
P\left(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
.
With this notation, each of the
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\}
can be thought of as functions of the image
{\widehat{A}}
, the volume
{\widehat{F}}
and the prior distribution
P(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
.

In the context of single-particle reconstruction, the negative-log-likelihood in the low-temperature limit is dominated by the following leading-order expansion:

\displaystyle-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)\right)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{2{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}}\left|{\widehat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})-{\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\widehat{F}}\right)\right|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{.}

(60)

This ‘template-wise’ expression represents the likelihood in terms of a collection of 2-dimensional integrals.
Later on below we’ll constrast this with an equivalent ‘volumetric’ expression which represents the likelihood in terms of a collection of 3-dimensional integrals.

13.2
multi-particle:

In the context of multi-particle reconstruction, the likelihood in Eq.
58
depends on Eq.
57
.
In the low-temperature limit that
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}\rightarrow 0
, the maximum-likelihood alignment occurs for the viewing-angle, displacement and volume-label which maximize the summand
P({\widehat{A}}\>|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}_{i})\cdot P(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\})
in the integrand of Eq.
57
.

Given a collection of volumes
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
, the maximum-likelihood alignment
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\}
and volume-label
i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
for an image
{\widehat{A}}
occurs at:

\displaystyle\{\ \tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\ ,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\ ,\ i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\ \}:=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\{\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\}}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}}_{i}\right)\text{,}

(61)

where
\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
and
i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
are restricted to lie in the support of
P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)
.
With this notation, each of the
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\}
can be thought of as functions of the image
{\widehat{A}}
, the volume-set
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
and the prior distribution
P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)
.

Thus, within the context of multi-particle reconstruction, the negative-log-likelihood in the low-temperature limit takes a form very similar to Eq.
60
:

\displaystyle-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)\right)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{2{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}}\left|{\widehat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})-{\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\widehat{F}}_{i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}}\right)\right|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{.}

(62)

14
Volume reconstruction in the low-temperature limit via least-squares

In the low-temperature limit each image
{\boldsymbol{A}}_{j}
is assigned to a particular viewing-angle
\tau_{j}
, displacement
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}
and volume-label
i
via Eq.
61
.
Given these maximum-likelihood assignments, each volume can be reconstructed independently.
For example, in the context of single-particle-reconstruction, the single volume
{\widehat{F}}
can be reconstructed by solving the following linear-system in a least-squares sense:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}CTF_{1}\odot\widehat{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1})\odot\left\{\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
CTF_{J}\odot\widehat{\cal T}(+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{J})\odot\left\{\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{J})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny lsq}}}\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\widehat{A}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\widehat{A}}_{J}\\
\end{array}\right]\text{.}

In this expression, the
J
viewing angles
\tau_{j}
and
displacements
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}
are assumed to be given as input, as are the
image- or micrograph-specific CTF functions
CTF_{j}
and
images
{\widehat{A}}_{j}
.
The unknown (to be determined) is the volume
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny lsq}}}
.
By a least-squares solution to Eq.
14
we specifically mean the volume
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny lsq}}}
for which the residual is proportional to the negative-log-likelihood described in Eq.
60
.

If it is more convenient, we can center the images on the right-hand-side and rewrite Eq.
14
as:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}CTF_{1}\odot\left\{\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\right\}\\
\vdots\\
CTF_{J}\odot\left\{\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{J})\circ\right\}\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny lsq}}}\approx\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1})\odot{\widehat{A}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{J})\odot{\widehat{A}}_{J}\\
\end{array}\right]\text{.}

(70)

In the context of multi-particle reconstruction the pool of images can be sorted by volume-label, and then the same strategy can be applied for each volume independently.

15
Volume reconstruction in the low-temperature limit via back-propagation

When the number of images
J
becomes large, the solution to Eq.
70
can be well approximated via a form of back-propagation
[
42
]
.
To motivate this strategy one can rewrite the likelihood as a volumetric-integral (see section
17
).
Alternatively, one may observe that, if there were no noise and no CTF-attenuation, then the volume and images would solve:

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{1})\circ\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{J})\circ\\
\end{array}\right]\cdot{\widehat{F}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\widehat{A}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{J},{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\widehat{A}}_{J}\\
\end{array}\right]

with each template
\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\widehat{F}}
corresponding to a cross-section of the volume
{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, with
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Each of these cross-sections can be viewed as a sample of the overall volume
{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, with values given by the appropriate entry on the right-hand-side of the equation above.
With sufficiently many cross-sectional samples, a functional representation of the volume
{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
for any point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be reconstructed simply by collecting and averaging the images that correspond to that point.
That is, for each
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have the formula:

\displaystyle{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}):=\frac{1}{\left|{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right|}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\widehat{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j^{\prime}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\text{,}\text{\ where:\ }{\boldsymbol{k}}:={\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}};j^{\prime})={\cal R}(\tau_{j^{\prime}})\circ{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\text{.}

(71)

In this formula the set
{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
includes only those image-indices
j^{\prime}
for which
{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}
contributes to the point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
That is to say, for each
{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}
there must exist a point
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
corresponding to a location in the equatorial-plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
onto which the point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is rotated by
{\cal R}(\tau_{j^{\prime}})
.
This point
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
will implicitly depend on
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
as well as the chosen image-index
j^{\prime}
.
The normalizing factor
1/|{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})|
ensures that
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
equals the average of all the image-values associated with
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.

The above formula can easily be generalized to deal with image-specific CTF-values.
Indeed, by interpreting each image-value
{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
as a noisy sample of
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, we can think of Eq.
71
as a local maximum-likelihood estimate of
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
.
More specifically, one can imagine sampling the image-values
a_{j}
associated with a particular
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
from the corresponding volume-value
f
:

a_{j}=f+\epsilon_{j}\text{,}\quad\text{\ with each $\epsilon_{j}$ drawn independently from\ }{\cal N}(0,{\hat{\sigma}}^{2})\text{.}

Given a collection of
J
such observations, the local-maximum-likelihood estimate for
f
is:

f^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}a_{j}\text{.}

If each image-value
a_{j}
is weighted by an image-specific CTF-value
c_{j}
, then we can adjust our model and local maximum-likelihood estimate accordingly:

a_{j}=f\cdot c_{j}+\epsilon_{j}\quad\implies\quad f^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}a_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}^{2}}\text{.}

Applying this logic to Eq.
71
yields:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}):=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\left|CTF_{j^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\right|^{2}\text{,}\text{\ and}

(72)

\displaystyle{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}):=\frac{1}{\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}CTF_{j^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\cdot\widehat{\cal T}(-{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j^{\prime}},{\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}}\right)\text{,}

(73)

where once again the
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is implicitly related to
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
and
j^{\prime}
.
This strategy coincides with the CTF-weighting of Bayesian inference described in
[
16
,
33
]
.

The term
\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
can be interpreted as the accumulated evidence at each point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
in frequency-space.
Similarly, the term
\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\cdot{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
can be thought of as the accumulated image-data at
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
The quotient of these two terms (i.e.,
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
) can be thought of as a first-moment.
Later on below we’ll see a similarly constructed second-moment within the likelihood.

16
Numerical details for back-propagation

Our algorithm attempts to solve the back-propagation problem exactly up to a given spherical-harmonic degree
{L}
(which is itself related to the maximum frequency
K
determining the resolution).

To describe our approach in more detail, let’s consider the following summation
\Sigma(M)
, originally represented as a 2-dimensional ‘template-wise’ integral:

\Sigma(M)=\sum_{j}\int_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega({K})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}}M_{j}^{{\dagger}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\widehat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{,}

(74)

for some collection of data
\{M_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\}
.

Let’s first consider the case where
J=1
, and we have only a single nonzero data-value of
M=1
associated with the viewing-angle
\tau
at a point
{\boldsymbol{k}}
for which
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau)\circ{\boldsymbol{k}}
is positioned at the north-pole
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}={[0,0,1]}^{\intercal}
.
Instead of calculating the sum
\Sigma(M)
by integrating
M^{{\dagger}}
against the template
\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau)\circ{\widehat{F}}
in 2-dimensions, we can instead just integrate a delta-function ‘monopole impulse’ concentrated at the north-pole against the original volume
{\widehat{F}}
in 3-dimensions.
Put another way, our single nonzero data-value can be associated with the back-propagated volume
\delta({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0})
, again referring to a delta-function impulse concentrated at the north-pole
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}
.

When restricted to the spherical-shell of radius
1
and projected onto the spherical-harmonics of degree
l\leq{L}
, the ideal impulse
\delta({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0})
(itself fully concentrated at
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}
) produces a band-limited impulse which is a spherical-sinc that is now centered around (but no longer fully concentrated at) its source at the north-pole:

\tilde{\delta}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\sqrt{4\pi}\cdot\sqrt{1+2l}\cdot P_{l}(\cos(\beta))=\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}4\pi\cdot Y_{l}^{0}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\text{,}

where
P_{l}
is a legendre-polynomial of degree
l
and, by construction, the spherical-harmonic coefficients
\tilde{\delta}_{l}^{m}
are given by
4\pi
when
m=0
.

Similarly, a single data-value of
M=1
positioned at the equatorial-point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{1}={[\cos(\psi),\sin(\psi),0]}^{\intercal}
will (given our conventions) produce the back-propagated impulse
\left[{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi])\circ\tilde{\delta}\right](\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
(i.e., a spherical-sinc with a source positioned at the appropriate point on the equator).

Due to linearity, we can associate the more general data-pool
\{M_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\}
with the back-propagated shell (for any radius
k
):

\tilde{M}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\sum_{j}\int_{\psi\in[0,2\pi]}M_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\left[{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi])\circ\tilde{\delta}\right](\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})d\psi\text{,}

(75)

which specifies
\tilde{M}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
at magnitude
k
.
In this expression we implicitly relate
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\Omega({K})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
to the amplitude and unit-vector
(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
. Similarly, we relate
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega({K})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
to the amplitude and angle
(k,\psi)
. Finally, we relate
{\boldsymbol{k}}={\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
In words, this simply amounts to walking through each piece of data
M_{j}
and, for each point
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, placing an impulse of the appropriate magnitude on the associated point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\boldsymbol{k}}
in 3-dimensional space.

With this reorganization we can now represent the sum
\Sigma(M)
as a 3-dimensional ‘volumetric’ integral:

\Sigma(M)=\int_{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\Omega({K})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{M}^{{\dagger}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\cdot{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\cdot\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}|}\text{.}

(76)

To compute
\tilde{M}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
efficiently, we employ the following strategy:

1.

First, we apply a linear-transformation
\tilde{D}
to mollify the impulse
\tilde{\delta}
and ensure that the support of
\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is not too large on the surface of the shell.

2.

Alongside
\tilde{D}
, we construct a quadrature-mesh on the surface of the shell which can both (i) resolve the mollified impulse
\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}
and (ii) accurately integrate the spherical-harmonics
Y_{l}^{m}
up to degree
{L}
.

3.

Now, for each
j
and
q
, the image-value
{\widehat{A}}_{j}(k,\psi_{q})
is associated with the compactly-supported mollified impulse
{\widehat{A}}_{j}(k,\psi_{q})\cdot{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi_{q}])\circ\tilde{\delta}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
, which is simply a spherical-sinc with a source at
{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{[\cos(\psi),\sin(\psi),0]}^{\intercal}
which we sample on the quadrature-mesh.

4.

After accumulating all such values for all
j
and
q
on the quadrature-mesh, we use quadrature to recover the spherical-harmonic coefficients of
\tilde{D}\tilde{M}
.

5.

Finally, we ‘undo’ the effect of
\tilde{D}
and calculate
\tilde{M}:=[\tilde{D}]^{-1}\circ[\tilde{D}\tilde{M}]
.

This recipe provides a template to calculate
\tilde{J}
and
\tilde{J}\cdot{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int_{\psi\in[0,2\pi]}|CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})|^{2}\cdot\left[{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi])\circ\tilde{\delta}\right](\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\text{,}

(77)

\displaystyle\left[\tilde{J}\cdot{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}\right]({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int_{\psi\in[0,2\pi]}CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\widehat{\cal T}(-\delta_{j},{\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot{\widehat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\left[{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi])\circ\tilde{\delta}\right](\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\text{,}

(78)

where once again
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k,\psi)
and
{\boldsymbol{k}}={\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.

16.1
designing
\tilde{D}
:

In this recipe we have the luxury of designing the mollifier
\tilde{D}
. We choose
\tilde{D}
so that:

1.

\tilde{D}
is diagonalized by the spherical-harmonic basis. That is to say: the spherical-harmonic coefficients
[\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}]_{l}
are given by
\tilde{D}_{l}\cdot\tilde{\delta}_{l}
, for a collection of coefficients
\tilde{D}_{l}
. This allows for
\tilde{D}
and
\tilde{D}^{-1}
to be quickly applied in the spherical-harmonic basis.

2.

The majority of the amplitude of
\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}
is close to the north- and south-pole. Put another way, we would like:

I^{2}(\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}};\tilde{D}):=\int_{\beta\in[\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}}]}\int_{\alpha\in[0,2\pi]}\left|\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}(\beta,\alpha)\right|^{2}d\alpha\sin(\beta)d\beta\leq\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny pole}}}^{2}\text{,}

for parameters including cutoff polar-cap angles
\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}}
and
\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}}
and an error-tolerance of
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny pole}}}^{2}
, where
\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}}
and
\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}}
are as close to
0
and
\pi
as possible, while
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny pole}}}^{2}
is as small as possible. This constraint allows
\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}
to be compactly supported on each spherical shell.

3.

The coefficients
\tilde{D}_{l}
are not too small for
l\leq{L}
. That is, we would like:

\min(\tilde{D}_{l})\geq\tilde{D}_{\min}>0\text{,}

for some lower-bound
\tilde{D}_{\min}
. This constraint bounds the operator-norm of
\tilde{D}^{-1}
, allowing for both
\tilde{D}
and
\tilde{D}^{-1}
to be well-conditioned.

Note that the latter two criteria for
\tilde{D}
are in conflict with one another: compressing the support of the mollified impulse
\tilde{D}\circ\tilde{\delta}
can only be done if some of the
\tilde{D}_{l}
are small.
Thus, while in principle we could use a standard surface-diffusion (i.e.,
\tilde{D}_{l}\sim\exp(l(l+1)t)
for some fixed diffusion-time
t
), we find that more efficient mollifiers can be constructed by solving a quadratic program which balances the constraints placed on
\tilde{D}
.

For example, given a set of coefficients
\tilde{D}_{l}
, we can write
I^{2}(\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}};\tilde{D})
as the quadratic-form:

I^{2}(\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}};\tilde{D})=\sum_{l=0}^{{L}}\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{{L}}\tilde{D}_{l}\cdot\tilde{H}(l,l^{\prime};\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}})\cdot\tilde{D}_{l^{\prime}}\text{,}

with a kernel:

\tilde{H}(l,l^{\prime};\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}})=8\pi^{2}\int_{\beta\in[\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}}]}\sqrt{(1+2l)(1+2l^{\prime})}P_{l}(\cos(\beta))P_{l^{\prime}}(\cos(\beta))\sin(\beta)d\beta\text{.}

By fixing
\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}}
and
\tilde{D}_{\min}
and running a quadratic-program with a constraint
\tilde{D}_{l}\geq\tilde{D}_{\min}
, we can quickly find the optimal values of
\tilde{D}_{l}
and the associated error
\epsilon_{{\text{\tiny pole}}}^{2}
. This procedure can be run as a precomputation, scanning through values of
\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}}
and
\tilde{D}_{\min}
to find a mollifier which best satisfies the sparsity and error-tolerance constraints provided by the user.

Note that the quadratic-program described above only controls the amplitude of the mollified sinc away from the poles, but does
not
control the amplitude of the first- or second-derivatives of the mollified sinc.
Controlling these derivatives can be easily accomplished by adding to
\tilde{H}(l,l^{\prime};\beta_{{\text{\tiny north}}},\beta_{{\text{\tiny south}}})
additional kernels that integrate higher-order derivatives of the
P_{l}(\cos(\beta))
.

Note also that, in the discussion above, we have restricted ourselves to mollifiers
\tilde{D}
that were diagonal in the spherical-harmonic basis.
This is certainly not necessary, as our main design constraint involved being able to apply
\tilde{D}
‘quickly’ in the spherical-harmonic basis.
This constraint implies that
\tilde{D}
could be block-diagonal (e.g., like the wigner-d matrix), or more generally sparse, or even factorizable.
With these extra degrees of freedom available to
\tilde{D}
one can design mollifiers that accurately capture
\tilde{\delta}
and its derivatives, all while having sparse support.

16.2
accommodating derivatives:

We can extend the notion of back-propagation to deal with certain differential quantities.
To illustrate this idea, let’s consider the following template-wise summation
\Sigma_{\iota}(M)
:

\Sigma_{\iota}(M)=\sum_{j}\int_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega({K})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}}M_{j}^{{\dagger}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\partial_{\tau_{j,\iota}}\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\widehat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{,}

(79)

where
\partial_{\tau_{j,\iota}}
refers to the
\iota
-component of
\tau_{j}
(i.e., one of
\{\gamma_{j},\beta_{j},\alpha_{j}\}
).

Again, due to linearity, we can associate the data-pool
\{M_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\}
with the back-propagated shell:

\displaystyle\tilde{M_{\iota}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int_{\psi\in[0,2\pi]}M_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\left[\partial_{\tau_{j,\iota}}{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi])\circ\tilde{\delta}\right](\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})d\psi\text{,}

(80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\int_{\psi\in[0,2\pi]}M_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\cdot\left[{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ\tilde{\delta}^{{\text{\tiny dipole}}}_{j,\iota,\psi}\right](\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})d\psi\text{,}

(81)

where the dipole-impulse function
\tilde{\delta}^{{\text{\tiny dipole}}}_{j,\iota,\psi}
is given by:

\tilde{\delta}^{{\text{\tiny dipole}}}_{j,\iota,\psi}={\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ\partial_{\tau_{j,\iota}}{{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\cal R}([0,\pi/2,\psi])\circ\tilde{\delta}\text{.}

(82)

This dipole-impulse corresponds to placing two monopole impulses of opposite signs infinitesimally close to one another near the position
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}={{\cal R}}^{-1}(\tau_{j})\circ{\boldsymbol{k}}
, with the dipole oriented in a direction (along the surface of the sphere) determined by
\tau_{j}
,
\psi
and
\iota
.

With this reorganization we can once again represent the sum
\Sigma(M)
as a volumetric integral:

\Sigma_{\iota}(M)=\int_{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\Omega({K})\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{M_{\iota}}^{{\dagger}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\cdot{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\cdot\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}|}\text{.}

(83)

Volumetric representation of the likelihood in the low-temperature limit

The single-particle negative-log-likelihood shown in Eq.
60
can be written as a volumetric-integral.
To see this more clearly, let’s write:

-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)\right)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{2{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|{\widehat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})-CTF_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot\left[\widehat{\text{\small Sl}}\circ{\cal R}(\tau_{j})\circ{\widehat{F}}\right]({\boldsymbol{k}})\right|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{.}

(84)

By interchanging the integral and the sum, we can write:

-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)\right)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{2{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\iiint_{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\Omega_{{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\left\{\left|{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})-{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right|^{2}+{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny var}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right\}\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\left|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\right|}\text{,}

(85)

with
\tilde{J}
and
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}
given by Eq.
72
and
73
, and the second-moment
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny var}}}
given by:

\displaystyle{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny var}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})+\left|{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\left|\widehat{\cal T}({\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\boldsymbol{k}})\odot{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in{\cal J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})}\left|{\widehat{A}}_{j^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right|^{2}\quad\text{\ with:\ }{\boldsymbol{k}}={\cal R}(\tau_{j^{\prime}})\circ{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\text{.}

(86)

This ‘volumetric’ expression represents the likelihood as a collection of 3-dimensional integrals; this can be compared with the template-wise expression from Eq.
60
.
Note also that, as mentioned earlier, in the low-temperature-limit the likelihood is maximized when
{\widehat{F}}
matches the first-moment
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}
.
This first-moment
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}
itself depends on the maximum-likelihood-alignments
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\},\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}
, the
\{CTF_{j}\}
and the images
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}
.

Using a very similar strategy, we can rewrite the likelihood in Eq.
54
as:

\displaystyle P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\idotsint\limits_{\{\tau_{j}\},\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}}

(87)

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009pt\times\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\ {\hat{\sigma}})^{{\text{dof}}\cdot J}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\iiint_{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\Omega_{{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}}\tilde{J}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\left\{\left|{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})-{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right|^{2}+{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny var}}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\right\}\frac{d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}{\left|{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\right|}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt\times\hskip 10.00002ptP\left(\{\tau_{j}\},\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\times\hskip 10.00002ptd\{\tau_{j}\}d\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}\text{,}

where the outer integral is over all possible sets of image-alignments
\{\tau_{j}\},\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}
.
The terms
\tilde{J}
,
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny bp}}}
and
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny var}}}
within the integrand are each functions of the alignments
\{\tau_{j}\},\{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\}

The probability in the integrand is:

18
Synthetic-images

18.1
single-particle:

Given a volume
{\widehat{F}}
, we can create a distribution of synthetic-images by adding the appropriate amount of noise to the templates associated with
{\widehat{F}}
.
To construct this distribution we must choose the prior joint-distribution of alignments and CTFs, denoted by
P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},CTF\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
, as well as the temperature
{\hat{\sigma}}
.
Given these ingredients, we can create a synthetic-image
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}({\boldsymbol{k}})
by randomly drawing
\{\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},CTF\}
from
P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},CTF\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
, then producing the template
{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;{\widehat{F}})
, and then finally adding noise in a manner consistent with Eq.
34
.
In the low-temperature limit we do the same, except in this case the synthetic-images are simply the templates
{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};CTF;{\widehat{F}})
with no additional noise added.
With this construction, each synthetic-image
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})
is associated with a particular viewing-angle
\tau_{j}
, displacement
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}
and
CTF_{j}
, and we can express this dependence by writing
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau_{j};{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\widehat{F}}\right)
.

18.2
consistency between the likelihood and the synthetic-image distribution:

Note that the likelihood in Eq.
54
is consistent with the synthetic-images described above.
To step through the details, let’s start by considering the simpler case where we ignore displacements and CTFs (i.e., we assume each
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{j}\equiv 0
and each
CTF_{j}\equiv 1
).
In this simple case the volume
{\widehat{F}}
is associated with the family of templates
\{{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\widehat{F}})\}
, indexed by the viewing-angle
\tau
.
Given a specific distribution of viewing-angles
P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
, the family of templates
\{{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\widehat{F}})\}
produces a distribution
P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
of templates in image-space (here
{\widehat{S}}
is a dummy-variable indicating the template’s location in image-space).

Assuming isotropic noise with variance
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}
, the distribution of synthetic-images
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
associated with
P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
is simply the convolution of
P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
with a gaussian:

P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})=P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\star{\cal N}(0,{\hat{\sigma}}^{2})\text{.}

In this formula
{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}
is a dummy variable indicating location in image-space, and
{\cal N}(0,{\hat{\sigma}})
represents the gaussian in image-space associated with the isotropic variance of
{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}
per unit-area in
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-space.

With this perspective, the likelihood
P\left(\{A_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)
in Eq.
54
is equal to the probability of drawing the image-set
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}
from the distribution of synthetic-images
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
, provided that the likelihood is calculated using the same prior distribution
P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
and temperature
{\hat{\sigma}}
used to generate the distribution of synthetic-images.

18.3
consistency between
{\widehat{F}}
and the synthetic-images:

Following directly from the construction of the synthetic-image distribution
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
above, we can see that the function
{\widehat{F}}
along with the prior distribution
P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
are a global maximum of the likelihood:

\displaystyle\left\{\ {\widehat{F}}\ ,\ P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\ \right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\{{\widehat{G}},P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{G}})\}}\lim_{J\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\{A^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{G}}\>;\>P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{G}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\text{,}

(94)

where we have emphasized the dependence of the likelihood
P\left(\{A^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{G}}\right)
on the prior distribution and temperature.
Note that in Eq.
94
we have assumed (via
J\rightarrow\infty
) that
\{A^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\}
is a pool of synthetic-images which is sufficiently large to sample
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
.

To see why Eq.
94
might be true, we can consider any other candidate template-distribution
P^{\prime}({\widehat{S}})
.
Given any temperature
{\hat{\sigma}}^{\prime}
, the associated distribution of synthetic-images
P^{\prime}({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}})
is:

P^{\prime}({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}})=P^{\prime}({\widehat{S}})\star{\cal N}(0,[{\hat{\sigma}}^{\prime}]^{2})\text{.}

Now, given an infinite number of synthetic-images
\{{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\}
sampled from
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
, we can ask the following questions: (i) what is the probability of drawing this image-set from
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
, and (ii) what is the probability of drawing this same image-set from
P^{\prime}({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}})
.
The logarithm of the ratio of the probabilities from (i) and (ii) is proportional to the Kullback-Leibler divergence:

KL(P||P^{\prime}):=\int_{{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}}P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\ \log\left(\frac{P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})}{P^{\prime}({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}})}\right)d{\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\text{,}

where the integral is carried out over image-space.

The KL-divergence is always nonnegative, and is only equal to
0
when
P^{\prime}\equiv P
.
This directly implies that the log-probability-ratio is highest when
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\equiv P^{\prime}({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}})
.
Put more simply: given any distribution
P
, no other distribution
P^{\prime}
can possibly produce a collection of samples that better represents
P
.
Consequently, assuming the approximate temperature
{\hat{\sigma}}^{\prime}
matches the true temperature
{\hat{\sigma}}
used to generate the synthetic-images, no template-distribution
P^{\prime}({\widehat{S}})
can produce a higher likelihood than the template-distribution
P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
.
Thus, no other pair
\{{\widehat{G}},P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{G}})\}
can generate a higher likelihood than the true
\{{\widehat{F}},P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\}
.

Note that it is still possible for other volumes to produce the
same
likelihood as the true volume
{\widehat{F}}
(i.e.,
{\widehat{F}}
is not guaranteed to be the only global maximum of Eq.
94
).
Specifically, another volume
{\widehat{G}}
might look different from
{\widehat{F}}
, but produce a family of templates
\{{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\widehat{G}})\}
that overlaps (in image-space) the family of templates
\{{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;{\widehat{F}})\}
produced by
{\widehat{F}}
.
In this scenario there exist viewing-angle distributions
P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{G}})
and
P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
such that the induced distribution
P^{\prime}({\widehat{S}}):=P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{G}})
is the same as
P({\widehat{S}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
.
Examples along these lines are discussed below.

18.4
multi-particle:

We can also use a similar strategy to create a pool of synthetic-images associated with multiple volumes
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
.
In this case we draw alignments, CTFs and volume-labels from the prior joint-distribution
P\left(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},CTF,i\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\right)
.

In this multi-volume scenario a statement similar to Eq.
94
holds.
Suppressing the notation for
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
and the CTF momentarily, we can write:

\displaystyle\left\{\ \{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}\ ,\ P(\tau,i\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\})\ \right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\{\{{\widehat{G}}_{i}\},P(\tau,i\>|\>\{{\widehat{G}}_{i}\})\}}\lim_{J\rightarrow\infty}P\left(\{A^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{G}}_{i}\}\>;\>P(\tau,i\>|\>\{{\widehat{G}}_{i}\})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\text{,}

(95)

where again we have emphasized the dependence of the likelihood
P\left(\{A^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{G}}_{i}\}\right)
on the prior distribution and temperature.

Once again, is is possible for other volume-collections to produce the same likelihood as the true volume-collection
\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\}
.
This phenomenon is much more pronounced in the multi-volume setting than in the single-volume setting, and it is rather straightforward to construct alternative volume-sets
\{{\widehat{G}}_{i}\}
, each with their own viewing-angle distribution
P(\tau\>|\>{\widehat{G}}_{i})
, which collectively produce the same distribution of synthetic-images as
P({\widehat{A}}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\>|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{i}\})
.
Examples along these lines are discussed below.

19
Local well-posedness of single-particle reconstruction

For a fixed data-set
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}
, the likelihood
P(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}})
from Eq.
54
is usually a nonconvex function of
{\widehat{F}}
,
P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
and
{\hat{\sigma}}
.
Even if we assume that the alignment-distribution
P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
and temperature
{\hat{\sigma}}
are known, finding the ‘optimal’
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
which maximizes the likelihood is often not easy
[
31
,
32
,
35
,
16
]
.
In general, the iterative strategies used to search for
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
produce an approximate
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}
which has a local (but not global) maximum of the likelihood
P(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})
, which we’ll denote by
P^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}
.

One standard strategy for measuring the quality of any particular configuration
\{{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\}
is to search for nearby configurations that might have a similar likelihood.
More specifically, we can consider nontrivial infinitesimal perturbations of the form:

\displaystyle\left\{{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\right\}\rightarrow\left\{\ {\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}+\epsilon\Delta{\widehat{F}}\ \>;\>\ P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})+\epsilon\Delta P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}})\ \right\}\text{.}

(96)

In Eq.
96
the function
\Delta{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is the Fourier-transform of a function
\Delta F({\boldsymbol{\chi}})\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
representing a perturbation of the volume
F^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}({\boldsymbol{\chi}})
.
Similarly, the function
\Delta P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}})
represents a perturbation of the prior distribution
P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})
.
We’ll assume that both
\Delta{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
and
\Delta P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}})
have unit norm, and that
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
is small.
When considering ‘nontrival’ perturbations, we’ll restrict our attention only to infinitesimal deformations of the solution, and implicitly exclude those perturbations that correspond to infinitesimal rigid rotations or translations of the original volume
{\widehat{F}}
and prior-distribution
P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
.

Assuming that the configuration
\{{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\}
is indeed a local maximum of the likelihood, then the perturbed negative-log-likelihood should be approximately quadratic to leading-order in
\epsilon
for a typical choice of
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
and
\Delta P
:

\displaystyle-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}+\epsilon\Delta{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\>;\>P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})+\epsilon\Delta P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\right)=-\log(P^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)\epsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})\text{.}

(97)

In the expression above,
\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)
is the concavity of the negative-log-likelihood in the direction of
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P\}
.
The second-order term in Eq.
97
is equivalent to a rayleigh-quotient of the quadratic kernel
H
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\idotsint\Delta P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}})\ \Delta{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}})^{\dagger}\cdot H({\boldsymbol{\kappa}},\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime})\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime})\ \Delta P(\tau^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime})\ d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}d\tau^{\prime}d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime}d{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}d\tau d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\text{,}

(98)

where
H
is the hessian of the negative-log-likelihood with respect to perturbations of
{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}
and
P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})
:

\displaystyle H({\boldsymbol{\kappa}},\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime})=\partial_{\{{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}});P(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\}}\partial_{\{{\widehat{F}}({\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime});P(\tau^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{\prime}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})\}}\left\{-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}\>;\>P(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\>;\>{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\right)\right\}\text{,}

(99)

which is directly related to a kind of Fisher information.

If the magnitude
\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)
is large for every nontrivial
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P\}
, then
P^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}
indicates a sharp minimum of the negative-log-likelihood-function.
In this scenario we can say that the single-particle reconstruction problem is well-posed within a local neighborhood of
\{{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}};P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\}
.
Conversely, if there exist nontrivial directions
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P\}
for which
\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)
is small, then these directions correspond to nontrivial perturbations (i.e., deformations) along which the likelihood diminishes only gradually.
Put another way, there will be ‘nearby’ configurations that will have likelihoods similar to the original
P^{{\text{\tiny loc}}}
.
Thus, in this latter scenario the single-particle reconstruction problem is poorly-posed near
\{{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}};P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\}
.

One could in principle use Eq.
98
to characterize the well-posedness of the reconstruction near any given
\{{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}};P(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny loc}}})\}
by finding the nontrivial direction
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}\}
which minimizes
\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)
:

\displaystyle\left\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}\right\}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P\}}\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P)\quad\text{\ with\ }\quad\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}=\sigma_{H}(\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny crit}}})\text{.}

(100)

Note that
\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
H
, and
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}\}
is the associated eigenvector.

We are not aware of an efficient general strategy for calculating
\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}
and
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}\}
.
The main impasse is that we do not know how to apply the hessian
H
(or its inverse) in a computationally efficient manner.
However, any given nontrival direction
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\Delta P\}
can be used to give an upper-bound for
\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}
.
Because volume-space is high-dimensional, we use the low-temperature limit (described in the next section) to guide our search.

Estimating the hessian in the low-temperature limit

As mentioned in the previous section, we are not aware of any efficient strategies for calculating the smallest eigenvalue of the hessian of the negative-log-likelihood from Eq.
99
.
However, there are strategies that are useful in the low-temperature limit.

To ease the notation, let’s ignore the displacements for now, and let’s assume for the moment that the viewing-angles
\{\tau_{j}\}
for each image are given.
Let’s use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
to represent the standard inner product over
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

\int_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
to represent the integral over
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\Omega_{{K}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

{\cal L}

from Eq.

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}};\{\tau_{j}\}\right)\right)\text{,}

(101)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\left|{\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau_{j};CTF_{j};{\widehat{F}}\right)-{\widehat{A}}_{j}({\boldsymbol{k}})\right|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{,}

where we have suppressed reference to
{\boldsymbol{k}}
in the integrand, and emphazised the dependence on
\tau_{j}
and
{\widehat{F}}

from Eq.

\displaystyle{\cal L}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}};\{\tau_{j}\}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-80.00012pt\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}}\left(M_{20}\left(\{\tau_{j}\};\{CTF_{j}\}\right)\cdot\left|{\widehat{F}}\right|^{2}-2\Re\left(M_{11}^{{\dagger}}\left(\{\tau_{j}\};\{CTF_{j}\};\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\right)\cdot{\widehat{F}}\right)+M_{02}\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\right)\right)\text{,}

To describe this in more detail, let’s refer to the perturbation

\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}

\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{{\widehat{F}}}\partial_{{\widehat{F}}}{\cal L}\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}+\sum_{j^{\prime}}\partial_{{\widehat{F}}}\partial_{\tau_{j^{\prime}}}{\cal L}\cdot\Delta\tau_{j^{\prime}}\\

(114)

refers to the volumetric-component, which is diagonalized in
{\boldsymbol{k}}
-space as shown in Eq.
111
.
The second term
H_{F\tau}
refers to the volumetric-perturbation induced by any given alignment-perturbation, as shown in Eq.
112
.
The third term
H_{\tau F}
refers to the alignment-perturbation induced by any given volumetric-perturbation, as shown in Eq.
107
.
Finally, the block
H_{\tau\tau}
refers to the alignment-component, which is diagonalized in template-space as shown in Eq.
108
.

20.1
Finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
H
:

Given the ability to apply
H
to any perturbation
\Delta:=\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}\}
, we can use lanczos-iteration to estimate the extremal eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
H
.
One technical detail that comes into play is that we want to avoid the ‘trival’ eigenvectors of
H
that correspond to perturbations
\Delta
which are infinitesimal rigid-rotations of
\{{\widehat{F}},\{\tau_{j}\}\}
.
Thus, when applying lanczos-iteration we project away the 3-dimensional space associated with these trivial rigid-rotations.
The remaining subspace will include those perturbations which correspond to infinitesimal deformations.
An example illustrating the convergence of lanczos-iteration is shown in Fig
23
.

Figure 23:

In this figure we illustrate the properties of the hessian
H
, as well as its lanczos-iterates.
For this example we use the TRPV1 molecule at ultra-low resolution:
2\pi{K}=8
.
This ultra-low resolution allows us to take
{L}=2\pi{K}=8
, implying only
(1+{L})^{2}=81
degrees of freedom per frequency-shell.
We also project onto the dominant 3 volumetric-principal-modes (capturing over
99.9\%
of the alignment-information, as described in
[
48
]
) and determine the alignment-perturbations implicitly (using the first-order scheme described in section

).
Thus, the total number of degrees-of-freedom for this simple case-study is
3\times 81=243
.
The first subplot shows the lanczos-iterates along the vertical, with the iteration-number plotted along the horizontal.
The color ranges from cyan (early iterates) to magenta (final iterates).
Note that the outliers in the spectrum are captured quite early on.
The second subplot shows the cumulative-distribution function for the lanczos-iterates, with the different colors corresponding to different iterations (using the same color-scale shown in the top-left).
The third subplot shows the estimated probability-density-function for the lanczos-iterates, while the fourth subplot shows the cumulative-distribution function for the log of the lanczos-iterates.

\log\det(H)
is the average of the log of the eigenvalues of

, which is proportional to the area under the CDF shown in the bottom-right subplot.

in the low-temperature limit, we don’t necessarily expect such an estimate to hold for moderate temperature.
Neverthless, the same strategies used above can still be applied when the temperature is not small after marginalization (see section

).

21
Dealing with implicitly defined
\tau_{j}

In the construction above we have assumed that the
\{\tau_{j}\}
are given as input.
Thus, the gradient in Eq.
113
and the hessian in Eq.
114
both assume that the overall perturbation
\Delta
is given as an (independent) combination of a volumetric-perturbation
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
and an alignment-perturbation
\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}
.
However, in many scenarios it is more appropriate to assume that the volumetric- and alignment-perturbations are implicitly related to preserve the maximum-likelihood alignment (see Eq.
59
).
In this section we’ll describe how we account for this implicit constraint.

To ease notation, we’ll start by considering a single image-index
j
, setting
{\widehat{A}}:={\widehat{A}}_{j}
and
\tau:=\tau_{j}
, and suppressing the notation for the
CTF
.
Furthermore, we’ll assume that
\tau=\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}
is determined implicitly by the volume
{\widehat{F}}
.
This implicit constraint minimizes the negative-log-likelihood
{\ell}
for that image:

\displaystyle{\ell}({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)-{\widehat{A}},{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)-{\widehat{A}}\rangle\text{,}

(115)

where
{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)
is shorthand for the template
{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau;CTF;{\widehat{F}})
.
Thus,
\tau
should be a critical-point of
{\ell}
, implying that:

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau_{\iota}}{\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\partial_{\tau_{\iota}}{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau),{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)-{\widehat{A}}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)-{\widehat{A}},\partial_{\tau_{\iota}}{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)\rangle=\Re\langle{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)-{\widehat{A}},\partial_{\tau_{\iota}}{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)\rangle={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{,}

(116)

where
\tau_{\iota}
runs over each of the three components
{[\gamma,\beta,\alpha]}^{\intercal}
of
\tau
, and each of the 3 components of the gradient

are set

.
We expand
{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}},\tau)
as follows:

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}}+\Delta{\widehat{F}},\tau+\Delta\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}+\partial_{F}{\widehat{S}}\cdot\Delta{\widehat{F}}+\partial_{\tau}{\widehat{S}}\cdot\Delta\tau

(118)

\displaystyle\hskip-80.00012pt+\frac{1}{2}[\Delta{\widehat{F}}]^{{\dagger}}\cdot[\partial_{FF}{\widehat{S}}]\cdot[\Delta{\widehat{F}}]+\frac{1}{2}[\Delta{\widehat{F}}]^{{\dagger}}\cdot[\partial_{F\tau}{\widehat{S}}]\cdot[\Delta\tau]+\frac{1}{2}{[\Delta\tau]}^{\intercal}\cdot[\partial_{\tau F}{\widehat{S}}]\cdot[\Delta{\widehat{F}}]+\frac{1}{2}{[\Delta\tau]}^{\intercal}\cdot[\partial_{\tau\tau}{\widehat{S}}]\cdot[\Delta\tau]\text{,}

Noting that

is linear in

, we can ignore second-order contributions of

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}({\widehat{F}}+\Delta{\widehat{F}},\tau+\Delta\tau)

\displaystyle{\widehat{S}}+\partial_{\tau}{\widehat{S}}\cdot\Delta\tau+\frac{1}{2}{[\Delta\tau]}^{\intercal}\cdot[\partial_{\tau\tau}{\widehat{S}}]\cdot[\Delta\tau]

\displaystyle\ +\Delta{\widehat{S}}+\partial_{\tau}\Delta{\widehat{S}}\cdot\Delta\tau+\frac{1}{2}{[\Delta\tau]}^{\intercal}\cdot[\partial_{\tau\tau}\Delta{\widehat{S}}]\cdot[\Delta\tau]\text{,}

Probing the spectrum of the hessian: case studies

In this section we’ll describe one approach that allows us to crudely bound
\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}
(see Eq.
100
) by appealing to the low-temperature limit of the likelihood.
Specifically, we’ll assume that the noise
{\hat{\sigma}}
is sufficiently small that Eq.
60
is a good approximation to Eq.
54
.
We’ll then try and find perturbations
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny zero}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny zero}}}\}
for which the leading-order term
\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny zero}}}(\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny zero}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny zero}}})
is small (in this low-temperature limit).
These perturbations
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny zero}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny zero}}}\}
will then serve as candidates for
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}},\Delta P^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}\}
, from which we can bound
\sigma_{H}^{{\text{\tiny crit}}}

By using this approach, we’ll be able to investigate certain relationships between the viewing-angle distribution and the stability/robustness of single-particle reconstruction.
By examining a few case-studies we’ll see that, even if the data is sufficient for a single-particle reconstruction, non-uniformity in the viewing-angle distribution can compromise robustness.
Ultimately, we’ll recapitulate a well-known phenomenon: images from a variety of viewing-angles are required for a robust reconstruction.

To simplify the discussion, we’ll first make several additional assumptions which can be relaxed later on:

Assume a large pool of synthetic-images:

We’ll assume that the pool of images is well modeled by the distribution of synthetic-images described in section

Ignore translations and CTF:

We’ll also suppress the notation for the coordinate

22.1
Rotation Blur:

One of the most straightforward perturbations to analyze is when
\Psi(\tau)
corresponds to a rotation-blur.
To describe this scenario, let’s first assume that
P(\tau)
is uniform.
Now let’s consider a particular
\tau^{[0]}
, for which the template
{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau^{0})
has a pole
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}:={{\cal R}}^{-1}{\tau^{[0]}}\circ{[0,0,1]}^{\intercal}
.
Now let’s define
{\cal N}(\tau^{[0]},\epsilon^{2})
to be an isotropic gaussian-distribution on the surface of the sphere with a mean at
\tau^{[0]}
and a variance of
\epsilon^{2}
.
Finally, let’s define
\Psi(\tau^{[0]})
so that
\tau^{[1]}:=\tau^{[0]}+\epsilon\Psi(\tau^{[0]})
is drawn independently from the distribution
{\cal N}(\tau^{[0]},\epsilon^{2})
.

In this setting the back-propagated solution
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
on any
k
-shell can be constructed by simply convolving the values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
on that shell with
{\cal N}({\boldsymbol{0}},\epsilon^{2})
.
That is to say, the volume
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
can be constructed by ‘rotationally blurring’ the volume
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
: i.e.:

\displaystyle{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}}{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}){\cal N}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime},\epsilon^{2})d\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}\text{.}

(151)

This rotational-blur corresponds directly to an isotropic surface-diffusion of the volume
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
:

\left[{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}\right]_{l}^{m}=\exp(-l(l+1)\epsilon^{2}/2)\cdot\left[{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}\right]_{l}^{m}\text{.}

(152)

22.2
Latitudinal perturbation:

Another relatively simple perturbation involves a fully latitudinal perturbation:
\Psi(\tau)=\left(0,\Theta\left(\beta\right),0\right)=\left(0,\sin\left(2\beta\right),0\right)
.
If
\epsilon>0
, this perturbation pushes each
\tau
in the northern-hemisphere south (towards the equator), while pushing each
\tau
in the southern-hemisphere north (again towards the equator).

If
P(\tau)
is uniform, then in the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
each value of
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is determined by averaging the values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime})
on a small ‘circle’ (on the surface of the sphere).
This circle can be described in terms of the polar-angle
a
associated with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
the circle is centered at a polar-angle of
a_{c}(a)
with a radius (extending on the surface of the sphere of radius-
1
) of
r(a)
.

a_{c}(a)=a+0.5\epsilon\Theta(a)\quad\text{\ and:\ }r(a)=0.5\epsilon\Theta(a)\text{.}

(153)

Note that the radius of this circle vanishes as
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
approaches either the poles or the equator, and is largest in the temperate zones.

As a result of Eq.
153
the values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
can each be obtained by integrating the nearby values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
against a small local gaussian:

\displaystyle{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}}{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}){\cal N}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime},\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}))d\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}\quad\text{\ with:\ }\sigma^{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})=\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Theta(a)^{2}\text{.}

(154)

By looking at this case-study we see that a latitudinal alignment-perturbation
\Theta(\beta)
results in a volumetric-perturbation
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
which is well approximated by a
a
-dependent local-diffusion about each point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
This local diffusion-coefficient drops to
0
at the poles and equator (i.e.,
a=0
,
\pi/2
and
\pi
).
Furthermore, the latitudinal alignment-perturbation also shifts each value of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
in the latitudinal-direction, allowing the templates values to be unchanged to first-order if the viewing-angle is also perturbed latitudinally.
Thus, the template-perturbation

\Delta{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau)={\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};{\widehat{F}}^{[1]};\tau+0.5\epsilon\Theta(\beta)\right)-{\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};{\widehat{F}}^{[0]};\tau\right)

will vanish as
\tau
approaches the poles.
Consequently, any image with an initial viewing-angle near the poles will align almost as well to
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
(at the latitudinally-perturbed
\tau^{[1]}
) as it did to
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
(at its inital
\tau^{[0]}
).

Taking these considerations into account, we see that a latitudinal alignment-perturbation like
\Theta(\beta)
will introduce a likelihood-penalty to images that are oriented far from the poles, but will not introduce a likelihood-penalty to images that are oriented close to the poles.
As a result, any volume constructed from a distribution of viewing-angles concentrated near the poles will be especially susceptible to latitudinal-perturbations of this form.
To illustrate this phenomenon we can consider a non-uniform
P(\tau)
which is concentrated around the polar-caps.
As
P(\tau)
becomes more and more concentrated around the polar-caps, the likelihood of
\{{\widehat{F}}^{[1]},\{\tau^{[1]}_{j}\}\}
becomes more and more similar to
\{{\widehat{F}}^{[0]},\{\tau^{[0]}_{j}\}\}
, even though
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
may not be similar to
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
.
Equivalently, the average fisher-information per image with-respect to the perturbation
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}\}
vanishes as
P(\tau)
becomes more concentrated near the poles.

Put another way, if the image-pool doesn’t have many images that are oriented away from the poles, no amount of near-polar images will help improve the robustness of the reconstructed volume.
As mentioned above, this intuitive result aligns with many current methodologies: many images from a variety of viewing-angles are required for a robust reconstruction.

22.3
Longitudinal perturbation:

A slightly more elaborate perturbation involves
\Psi(\tau):=\Phi(\alpha)=\sin(2\alpha)
.
If
\epsilon>0
, this perturbation pushes each
\tau
either east- or west-ward away from the great-circle corresponding to the prime-meridian.

If
P(\tau)
is uniform, then in the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
each value of
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})
is determined by averaging the values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime})
on a small ‘collapsed circle’ (on the surface of the sphere) projected onto (i.e., extending only in) the azimuthal-direction.
This collapsed circle can be described in terms of the polar-angle and azimuthal-angle
(a,b)
associated with the point
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
The collapsed circle is centered at an azimuthal-angle of
b_{c}(a,b)
and has an azimuthal-radius of
r(a)
.

\displaystyle b_{c}(a,b)=b+\epsilon u(a)\Phi(b)\quad\text{\ and:\ }r^{2}(a)=\epsilon^{2}\left[1+u(a)\right]\text{,}

(155)

\displaystyle u(a)=\frac{1-|\cos(a)|}{1+|\cos(a)|}\quad\text{\ and:\ }\Phi(b)=\sin(2b)\text{.}

(156)

Note that the azimuthal-radius of this collapsed-circle vanishes as
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
approaches the equator, and is largest near the poles.

As a result of Eq.
156
the values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
can each be obtained by integrating the nearby values of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
against a small local gaussian extending in the azimuthal-direction:

\displaystyle{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{b}{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}(k,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}){\cal N}(b-b^{\prime},\epsilon^{2}\sigma^{2}(a))d\widehat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\prime}\quad\text{\ with:\ }\sigma^{2}(a)=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}(a)\text{.}

(157)

By looking at this case-study we see that a longitudinal alignment-perturbation
\Phi(\alpha)
results in a volumetric-perturbation
\Delta{\widehat{F}}
which is well approximated by a
b
-dependent local azimuthal-diffusion about each point
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
This local azimuthal-diffusion-coefficient drops to
0
at the equator (i.e.,
a=\pi/2
).
However, the longitudinal-perturbation induces an azimuthal-shift in each value of
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
that is both
a
- and
b
-dependent.
Thus, the original synthetic-images (i.e., templates from
{\widehat{F}}^{[0]}
) won’t typically align well to the volume
{\widehat{F}}^{[1]}
.
Taking these considerations into account, we see that a longitudinal alignment-perturbation like
\Phi(\alpha)
will introduce likelihood-penalties for all the images.

However, the results above are strongly dependent on the (near) uniformity of the viewing-angle distrubution
P(\tau)
.
If
P(\tau)
were to be concentrated near the equatorial-belt, then the results change substantially.
In fact, as
P(\tau)
becomes more concentrated around the equatorial-belt there are two major changes:

1.

The azimuthal-displacement
b_{c}(a,b)
becomes dependent mainly on the azimuthal-angle
b
of
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
(becoming proportional to
\Phi(b)
) and becomes more and more independent of the polar-angle
a
.

2.

the azimuthal-variance
r^{2}(a)/2
drops to zero away from the poles.

In this scenario (i.e., an equatorially-concentrated
P(\tau)
), the situation is quite similar to the poorly-posed scenario involving
only
equatorial viewing-angles.
That is to say, any volume constructed from a distribution of viewing-angles concentrated near the equator will be especially susceptible to longitudinal-perturbations.

To illustrate this phenomenon we can consider a non-uniform
P(\tau)
which is concentrated around the equator.
Similar to the latitudinal-perturbation described above, as
P(\tau)
becomes more and more concentrated around the equator, the likelihood of
\{{\widehat{F}}^{[1]},\{\tau^{[1]}_{j}\}\}
becomes more and more similar to
\{{\widehat{F}}^{[0]},\{\tau^{[0]}_{j}\}\}
, and the average fisher-information per image with-respect to the perturbation
\{\Delta{\widehat{F}},\{\Delta\tau_{j}\}\}
vanishes.
Put another way, if the image-pool only has images that have viewing-angles near the equator, their relative orientation cannot be pinned down.
Once again, this result echoes conventional-wisdom: many images from a variety of viewing-angles are required for a robust reconstruction.

22.4
Relating the laplacian to a local average:

In the above calculations we’ve used the following fact.
Given a twice-differentiable function
u({\boldsymbol{x}}):\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we can use the laplacian
\Delta u
to approximate the average of that function along the perimeter
\partial B_{r}
of a small disk
B_{r}
of radius
r
.

I(r)=\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{B_{r}}u({\boldsymbol{x}})d\Gamma.

\partial_{r}I(r)=\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{B_{r}}\partial_{x_{1}}^{2}u({\boldsymbol{x}})+\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}u({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}=\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\int_{B_{r}}\Delta u({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}.

\partial_{r}I(r)\approx\frac{1}{|\partial B_{r}|}\pi r^{2}\Delta u(0)\approx\frac{1}{2}\ r\ \Delta u(0).

I(r)=I(0)+\int_{0}^{r}\partial_{r}I(s)ds\approx u(0)+\frac{1}{4}\ r^{2}\ \Delta u(0).

We can also describe a similar relationship: if we were to define

to be the projection of

onto the

which is what one would expect if

The Noise-marginalized limit

In section
13
we discussed the low-temperature limit
{\hat{\sigma}}\rightarrow 0
, for which the likelihood in Eq.
54
can be greatly simplified, producing Eq.
60
.
We leverage this simplified expression to produce Eq.
85
, which affords certain computational and conceptual advantages.

A similar simplification can occur when the likelihood is marginalized over the noise
{\hat{\sigma}}
.
To demonstrate this, we can return to Eq.
37
and rewrite the likelihood of observing an image
{\widehat{A}}
, given a particular template
{\widehat{S}}
and noise-level
{\hat{\sigma}}
:

\displaystyle P({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},{\hat{\sigma}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\ {\hat{\sigma}})^{{\text{dof}}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}})\right)\text{,}

(176)

where
\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}})
takes the form:

\displaystyle\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\Omega_{{K}}}|{\widehat{A}}({\boldsymbol{k}})-{\widehat{S}}({\boldsymbol{k}})|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{k}}\text{,}

(177)

and
dof
refers to the number of independent degrees of freedom in the image.

Now we can use Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 3.326.2:

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{N}}\exp\left(-\frac{\ell}{{\lambda}^{2}}\right)d{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{N-1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\ell^{(N-1)/2}}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{N-1}{2}\right)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{N-1}{2}\log\ell\right)\text{,}

and marginalize over the noise
{\hat{\sigma}}
to produce:

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},{\hat{\sigma}}\right)\ d{\hat{\sigma}}

(178)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{{\text{dof}}/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\left({\text{dof}}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\exp\left(-{\text{dof}}^{\prime}\log\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}})\right)\text{,}

(179)

where we have denoted
{\hat{\sigma}}
-marginalization via the strikethrough
{\hat{\sigma}}
, and set
{\text{dof}}^{\prime}=({\text{dof}}-1)/2
.

Note that
{\text{dof}}^{\prime}
is often quite large in practice, and thus this marginalized likelihood is typically very well approximated using standard asymptotics (e.g., laplace’s approximation).
In this regime we can marginalize Eq.
53
over
{\hat{\sigma}}
and write:

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\tau\in SO3}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}};\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}}\right)\cdot P\left({\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\widehat{F}}\right)\cdot P\left(\tau\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}\right)d\tau d{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\text{,}

(180)

where
P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}};\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}}\right)
now represents a marginalized likelihood similar to Eq.
179
.
In the large-
dof
limit we now have a statement very similar to Eq.
59
.
Namely, as
{\text{dof}}\rightarrow\infty
, the integral in Eq.
180
becomes dominated by the contribution associated with the ‘maximum-likelihood’ alignment-angle for the image
{\widehat{A}}
.
Just as before, this maximum-likelihood alignment occurs for the viewing-angle, displacement and volume which maximize the summand
P({\widehat{A}}\>|\>\tau;{\boldsymbol{\delta}};{\widehat{F}};\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}})\cdot P(\tau,{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\>|\>{\widehat{F}})
in the integrand of Eq.
180
.

In the context of single-particle reconstruction, the negative-log-likelihood for a collection of images in the large-
dof
limit is now dominated by the following leading-order expansion:

\displaystyle-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}}\right)\right)\ \sim\ +{\text{dof}}^{\prime}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left(\ell\left({\widehat{A}}_{j}\>|\>{\widehat{S}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}\right)\right)\text{,}

(181)

with:

{\widehat{S}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}({\boldsymbol{k}}):={\widehat{S}}\left({\boldsymbol{k}};\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j};CTF_{j};{\widehat{F}}\right)\text{.}

23.1
marginalizing with respect to offset
\mu
:

In practice it is often convenient to take into consideration an image-specific real-space offset
\mu
:

\displaystyle P({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},{\hat{\sigma}},\mu)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2\pi}\ {\hat{\sigma}})^{{\text{dof}}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}},\mu)\right)\text{,}

(182)

where
\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}},\mu)
takes the form:

\displaystyle\ell({\widehat{A}}\>|\>{\widehat{S}},\mu)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega_{{X}}}|{A}({\boldsymbol{x}})+\mu-{S}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{x}}\text{.}

(183)

To marginalize this likelihood with respect to the image-offset
\mu
we can use Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 2.33:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{ax^{2}\pm 2bx+c}{\lambda}\right)dx=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda\pi}{a}}\exp\left(-\frac{ac-b^{2}}{\lambda a}\right)\text{,}

which holds for any normalizing factor
\lambda
.

Using this relationship, we can write:

\displaystyle P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},{\hat{\sigma}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}P\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},{\hat{\sigma}},\mu\right)d\mu

(184)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{{\text{dof}}/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{{\text{dof}}}}\cdot(2\pi{\hat{\sigma}}^{2})\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{1}{{\hat{\sigma}}^{2}}\ell\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}}\right)\right)\text{,}

(185)

\text{with:}\ \ell\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}}\right):=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega_{{X}}}\left|\left({A}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\bar{A}\right)-\left({S}({\boldsymbol{x}})-\bar{S}\right)\right|^{2}d{\boldsymbol{x}}\text{,}

(186)

where we have used
\bar{A}
and
\bar{S}
to denote the average image- and template-value.

Given the structure of
\ell\left({\widehat{A}}\>\big|\>{\widehat{S}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}}\right)
, we can assume without loss of generality that the images are all ‘centered’ (i.e., with mean
0
).
Similarly, we can assume that the volume
{F}
is also mean
0
, and hence every template
{S}
is centered as well.

After performing this offset-marginalization we can once again marginalize over the noise to obtain the negative-log-likelihood:

\displaystyle-\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}},\text{\sout{${\hat{\sigma}}$}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}}\right)\right)\ \sim\ +{\text{dof}}^{\prime}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log\left(\ell\left({\widehat{A}}_{j}\>|\>{\widehat{S}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}}\right)\right)\text{.}

(187)

In many practical scenarios the values of
\ell({\widehat{A}}_{j}\>|\>{\widehat{S}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}}},\text{\sout{$\mu$}})
are rather tightly distributed across the image-pool
j
, and it is not unreasonable to approximate the function
\log(\ell)
as an affine function
\log(\ell)\approx a\ell+b
for some fixed
a
and
b
(i.e., constants across the image-pool).
One such example is shown in Fig

.

24
Ill-posedness: Case studies

24.1
Equatorially-distributed viewing-angles

As described in section
22.3
, the single-particle reconstruction problem is generically poorly posed when the viewing angles are concentrated around the equator.
More specifically, assume we are given a volume
{\widehat{F}}
and a collection of templates taken from a viewing-angle-distribution
\mu(\tau)
supported on the equator.
Now any equatorial-perturbation
\Delta\tau(\tau)
of these viewing-angles can be applied to the original viewing-angles.
These perturbed viewing-angles can then be used to reconstruct a volume
{\widehat{G}}
.

As long as the map
\tau\leftarrow\tau+\Delta\tau(\tau)
is one-to-one, the reconstructed
{\widehat{G}}
will then produce a collection of templates that perfectly match those templates used to reconstruct it.
In other words, the collection of templates of
{\widehat{G}}
will include the original collection of templates from
{\widehat{F}}
.

This issue is a result of the fact that, when
\mu(\tau)
is restricted to the equator, different templates only overlap at the poles.
Any invertible equatorial-perturbation simply reorganizes the azimuthal-angle associated with each template, without disturbing this polar overlap.
Examples of this phenomenon are shown in Figs
27
-
34
.

24.2

On this frequency-shell each projection corresponds to a great-circle.
Most such great-circles do not intersect
D
or
D^{\prime}
, and thus correspond to projections
{\widehat{S}}
which cut through the background-values of
b
and
b^{\dagger}
(i.e., for which
{\widehat{S}}(\psi)\equiv b
or
b^{\dagger}
for all
\psi
).
However, great-circles that intersect
D
and
D^{\prime}
will produce projections
{\widehat{S}}
that have intervals of
\psi
for which
{\widehat{S}}(\psi)=d
and
{\widehat{S}}(\psi+\pi)=d^{\dagger}
.

Now consider another frequency-shell
{\widehat{F}}_{B}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
which is similar to
{\widehat{F}}_{A}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
, except that the localized feature now lies on an elliptical zone ‘
E
’.
The zone
E
has a major-axis that matches that of
D
, but a minor-axis which is smaller.
Let’s assume that
{\widehat{F}}_{B}
takes on the values
b
on most of the sphere, and the values
d
and
d^{\dagger}
on the elliptical-regions
E
and
E^{\prime}
(where
E^{\prime}
is antipodal to
E
).

Now each great-circle from
{\widehat{F}}_{A}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
corresponds to at least one great-circle from
{\widehat{F}}_{B}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
, and vice-versa.
A collection of templates corresponding to a uniform distribution of viewing-angles from
{\widehat{F}}_{A}
can now be associated with a collection of templates corresponding to a non-uniform distribution of viewing-angles from
{\widehat{F}}_{B}
.
Of course the same story holds if we reduce the minor-axis of the elliptical-region
E
to produce a shell
{\widehat{F}}_{C}(k,\hat{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
Thus, in this case the single-particle reconstruction problem is poorly posed.
Give only the collection of templates from
{F}_{A}
, but without knowledge of the viewing-angles nor of the viewing-angle-distribution
\mu(\tau)
, one cannot determine if the true underlying volume is
{F}_{A}
,
{F}_{B}
or any other admissible function (e.g.,
{F}_{C}
).

The scenario described above is just one example of the kinds of degeneracies that can occur.
Figs
37
and
38
illustrate another similar degenerate scenario.
In this case each projection of the volume
{F}_{A}
corresponds to at least one projection of
{F}_{B}
or of
{F}_{C}
.
Thus, given only an image-pool comprising the collection of projections from
{F}_{A}
, one cannot determine if the image-pool was produced by the single volume
{F}_{A}
, or a combination of other volumes (e.g.,
{F}_{B}
and
{F}_{C}
).

24.3
Noisy images

The previous section discussed degenerate scenarios exhibiting spurious heterogeneity: a collection of projections from one volume can be indistinguishable from a subset of projections collected from a pair of other volumes.

A similar scenario can manifest in real data, particularly when the number of images
J
is relatively small and the viewing-angle distribution
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny emp}}}
of the data is non-uniform.
As an example, consider the trpv1-molecule and empirical viewing-angle distribution shown in Fig
8
.

Now let’s consider the multi-particle reconstruction problem, given only the first
J=1024
images from the image-stack at
EMPIAR_10005
.
For simplicity, let’s ignore translations for now (assuming that each image is appropriately centered).
As described in section
13.2
, one might approximately solve this problem by constructing a collection of volumes
\{{F}_{i}\}
and, for each image
{A}_{j}
, determining the maximum-likelihood alignments
\{\tau^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}\}
and associated volume-label
i^{{\text{\tiny opt}}}_{j}
.

There is a trivial (and useless) solution to this problem involving a number of volumes equal to the number of images, where volume
j
is fit to image
{A}_{j}
.
Clearly, this trivial solution is overfit to the data, and one might hope to prevent such overfitting by restricting the total number of volumes.
However, as we’ll see in a moment, such a strategy is not guaranteed to succeed, and in this scenario we still observe spurious heterogeneity even if the number of volumes is restricted to only
2
.

Spurious heterogeneity with synthetic images:

To see why this might be the case, recall that the empirical viewing-angle distribution from
EMPIAR_10005
is partially concentrated around the equator, with the remainder concentrated near the poles.
Let’s now consider performing ‘surgery’ on
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny emp}}}
, and dividing this into two disjoint distributions, one
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
supported in a band around the equator, and the second
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
supported on a pair of antipodal polar caps.

As discussed in section
22.3
, the equatorial viewing-angles in
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
can, by themselves, give rise to a single-particle reconstruction which is not very robust.
That is to say, there are volumes that are not close to the true volume, but which can produce a collection of equatorial-templates which are a very close match to the equatorial-templates of the true volume.
Generally speaking, the quality of this match, as measured by the likelihood in Eq.
60
will increase as the viewing-angle distribution
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
becomes concentrated around the equator (see Fig
25
).

Similarly, as discussed in section
22.2
, the polar viewing-angles in
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
can, by themselves, also produce a reconstruction which is not very robust.
Once again, there are volumes that are not close to the true volume, but which can produce nearly identical polar-templates.
Generally speaking, the quality of this match will increase as the viewing-angle distribution
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
becomes concentrated around the poles (see Fig
24
).

For this particular case-study we’ll simply chop
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny emp}}}
into two parts, as indicated in Fig
39
.
Note that, while the data from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
technically covers the surface of the sphere, the data from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
does not.
As show on the right of Fig
39
, there is a ‘blind spot’ at each pole where the data from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
does not constrain the values of
{\widehat{F}}
near the poles.

We’ll return to these blind-spots in a moment, but for now we’ll illustrate in Figs
40
-
43
two volumes
{F}_{B}
and
{F}_{C}
.
These two volumes correspond to reconstructions using the distributions
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
and
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
, respectively.
These volumes are illustrated alongside the original volume
{F}_{A}
, which corresponds to the true volume
EMD-5778
.

The volume
{F}_{B}
has been crafted so that, for each template
{S}(\cdot;\tau^{A},{F}_{A})
with a
\tau^{A}
drawn from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
, there exists a template
{S}(\cdot;\tau^{B},{F}_{B})
which is very close to
{S}(\cdot;\tau^{A},{F}_{A})
.
Similarly, the volume
{F}_{C}
has been crafted so that, for each template
{S}(\cdot;\tau^{A},{F}_{A})
with a
\tau^{A}
drawn from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
, there exists a template
{S}(\cdot;\tau^{C},{F}_{C})
which is very close to
{S}(\cdot;\tau^{C},{F}_{C})
.

To be more explicit, consider taking a sample of
J
synthetic-images
\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\}
, each produced using the true volume
{\widehat{F}}_{A}
and a viewing-angle
\tau^{A}_{j}
drawn from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny emp}}}
.
If we were to align
{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}
to
{\widehat{F}}_{A}
, then the optimal viewing-angle would be
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},A}\equiv\tau^{A}_{j}
, by construction.
This collection of synthetic-images forms our ‘sythetic-image-pool’, and is the data we will use immediately below.

Let’s first align each
{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}
to
{\widehat{F}}_{B}
, producing an optimal viewing-angle
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B}
.
This optimal viewing-angle will only correspond to a close match if
{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}
corresponds to an polar-template (i.e., if
\tau^{A}_{j}
lies in the support of
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
).
To indicate this, let’s define
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}:=\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B}
and set the volume-assignment
i_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}
to be ‘
B
’ for these images.

In a similar fashion, we can align each
{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}
to
{\widehat{F}}_{C}
, producing an optimal viewing-angle
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},C}
.
This optimal viewing-angle will only correspond to a close match if
{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}
corresponds to a equatorial-template (i.e., if
\tau^{A}_{j}
lies in the support of
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny equa}}}
).
To indicate this, let’s define define
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}:=\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},C}
and set the volume-assignment
i_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}
to be ‘
C
’ for these images.

Now we can use the optimal-alignments
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},A}\}
to calculate the log-likelihood
{\cal L}_{A}:=\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}^{{\text{\tiny syn}}}\}\>\big|\>{\widehat{F}}_{A}\right)\right)
, as described in Eq.
60
.
Similarly, we can can use the optimal-alignments
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}\}
and optimal-assignments
\{i_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}\}
to calculate the log-likelihood
{\cal L}_{B+C}:=\log\left(P\left(\{{\widehat{A}}_{j}\}\>\big|\>\{{\widehat{F}}_{B},{\widehat{F}}_{C}\}\right)\right)
as described in Eq.
62
.

Now we can compare two different hypothesis.
The first hypothesis, denoted by ‘
\text{\tt Hyp}_{A}
’, is that the synthetic-image-pool was generated by the single volume
xF_{A}
and the alignments
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},A}\}
.
The second hypothesis, denoted by ‘
\text{\tt Hyp}_{B+C}
’ is that the synthetic-image-pool was generated by the pair of volumes
\{{F}_{B},{F}_{C}\}
along with the alignments
\{\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}\}
and the volume-assignments
\{i_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B+C}\}
.
Because we are using synthetic-images, we are guaranteed that the ‘true’ hypothesis
\text{\tt Hyp}_{A}
is more likely than the ‘spurious’ hypothesis
\text{\tt Hyp}_{B+C}
(see section
18
).
However, for this case-study we have prepared the volumes
{F}_{B}
and
{F}_{C}
so that, given the empirical noise-level (estimated from
EMPIAR_10005
), the log-likelihood ratio
{\cal L}_{A}-{\cal L}_{B+C}
is actually slightly less than
\log(20)
.
This means that, under standard-practice, the spurious hypothesis
\text{\tt Hyp}_{B+C}
would not be rejected in favor of the true hypothesis
\text{\tt Hyp}_{A}
.

Spurious heterogeneity with experimental-images:

Above we considered a somewhat ideal setting where the image-pool was constructed using only sythetic images drawn from the true volume
{F}_{A}
.
If the size
J
of the image-pool is not too large and the empirical viewing-angle distribution
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny emp}}}
is nonuniform, then we can encounter spurious heterogeneity.
Notably, if we revisit the same setting and use actual experimental-images, we see that spurious heterogeneity can be even more pernicious.

Recall that the distribution
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
was quite concentrated near the poles, yielding a ‘blind-spot’ near each pole shown in Fig
39
.
That is to say, the data from the synthetic-images drawn from
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
do not constrain the values of
{\widehat{F}}_{B}
in these blind-spots.
Thus, these blind-spots represents a collection of degrees-of-freedom that are unconstrained by the true projections of the volume.

Above, to produce the polar-aligned volume
{F}_{B}
shown in Figs
40
-
43
, we merely set the values of
{\widehat{F}}_{B}
to be constant in each blind-spot (subject to the conjugacy conditions).
However, in practice these blind-spots are not likely to be treated so simply.

One such example is shown on the right of Fig
44
.
This shows a volume
{F}_{B^{\prime}}
which has the same polar-templates as
{F}_{B}
from Fig
41
, but has different data in its blind spot, corresponding to ‘overfitting’ to noise.

To explain this scenario more carefully, first recall the original pool of
J=1024
synthetic-images described above.
As described above, these
J
synthetic-images were constructed using projections of the true molecule
{F}_{A}
.
When aligning these synthetic-images to the spurious-volumes
{F}_{B}
and
{F}_{C}
, those synthetic-images corresponding to polar-templates fit best to
{F}_{B}
, while those corresponding to equatorial-templates fit best to
{F}_{C}
.

Now let’s add
32
additional noisy experimental-images to the image-pool.
These additional noisy images have viewing-angles that are not too far from the poles, and naturally align best to
{F}_{B}
.
However, due to the noise, their optimal alignment-angle
\tau_{j}^{{\text{\tiny opt}},B}
is somewhat far from the poles, so that the images themselves correspond to values of
\hat{\boldsymbol{k}}
that pass through the polar ‘blind-spots’ associated with
\mu_{\tau}^{{\text{\tiny pole}}}
.

Now, by using these optimally-aligned noisy images (along with the optimally-aligned sythetic-images corresponding to polar-templates), we can reconstruct
{F}_{B^{\prime}}
.
Unlike the volume
{F}_{B}
shown in the middle of Fig
44
, the volume
{F}_{B^{\prime}}
shown on the right of Fig
44
has its polar-data fit to the additional noisy experimental-images.
This additional polar-information distorts the volume
{F}_{B^{\prime}}
, but does not appreciably change the polar-projections.
Thus while the volumes
{F}_{B}
and
{F}_{B^{\prime}}
provides equally good matches to the polar-templates, the volume
{F}_{B^{\prime}}
provides a better match than the ‘cleaner’ volume
{F}_{B}
to the noisy experimental-images.

This results in a classical instance of ‘overfitting’: When considering the expanded image-pool comprising both the idealized synthetic-images and the additional
32
noisy experimental-images, the hypothesis
\text{\tt Hyp}_{B^{\prime}+C}
is actually
more
likely than
\text{\tt Hyp}_{A}
.
A similar scenario is shown in Fig
45
, using a different collection of
64
noisy experimental-images.

Finally, we remark that spurious heterogeneity of this kind can be difficult to correct for a-posteriori.
In many cases it might not be clear which images are ‘good’ images of particles, and which are too strongly polluted by noise.
It also might be unclear which features of the reconstructed volume (or volumes) are a result of overfitting to noise, or if such a distinction can even be made.
Moreover, some commonly used techniques for validation, such as aligning the images to an ‘averaged’ volume of the form
0.5\times\left({F}_{B}+{F}_{C}\right)
, could erroneously provide support for the spurious hypothesis.

In these situations we would recommend calculating the softest modes of the Hessian associated with each reconstructed volume, as described in the main text.
While such a calculation won’t immediately identify a heterogeneous reconstruction as spurious, it can certainly provide a warning.
For example, in the case-studies shown above the spurious volume
{F}_{B}
is susceptible to latitudinal perturbations, while the spurious volume
{F}_{C}
is susceptible to longitudinal perturbations.
Both of these spurious volumes are less robust to perturbations than the volume
{F}_{A}
produced via single-particle reconstruction.
This kind of analysis can help indicate possible errors associated with a heterogeneous reconstruction.
