Real-world challenges of 4-month rifapentine-moxifloxacin TB regimen: High hyperbilirubinemia in Thailand
Authors: Thanyanuch Sanchat, Charoen Chuchottaworn, Piamlarp Sangsayunh, Sunsanee Pungtaway, Sirijit Rattanawai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe four-month rifapentine-moxifloxacin (HPMZ) regimen is an alternative to the standard six-month tuberculosis treatment. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health challenge. The standard treatment for drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) consists of a four-drug regimen for six months. However, long treatment duration, drug toxicity, and poor adherence contribute to unfavorable outcomes, including relapse and the emergence of drug resistance, leading to ongoing transmission. To enhance efficacy and adherence, recent efforts have focused on shortening therapy while maintaining high cure rates The key study, TBTC Study 31/A5349 [1], has demonstrated that a four-month regimen of daily rifapentine (P), moxifloxacin (M), isoniazid (H), and pyrazinamide (Z) (HPMZ) is non-inferior to the standard six-month HRZE regimen for drug-susceptible tuberculosis (DS-TB), with cure rates of 84.6% and 85.4%, respectively. In terms of safety, 18% of participants experienced adverse events (AEs). The rate of unfavorable outcomes was comparable to the standard six-month regimen, occurring in 15.5% of patients in the rifapentine-moxifloxacin group and 14.6% in the control group. The adjusted difference was 1.0 percentage point (95% CI, −2.6 to 4.5), confirming non-inferiority. Additionally, Grade 3 or higher adverse events were more frequent in the control group (19.3%) compared to the rifapentine-moxifloxacin group (18.8%) and the rifapentine-only group (14.3%). Hepatotoxicity was a concern, with higher rates of hyperbilirubinemia observed in the rifapentine-based regimens.

In 2021, the WHO endorsed the HPMZ regimen as an alternative to standard TB treatment [2], recognizing its potential to improve adherence and reduce healthcare burdens. Similarly, the 2024 An Official ATS/CDC/ERS/IDSA Clinical Practice Guideline IDSA/ATS [3] supports the use of rifapentine-based regimens, further reinforcing their role in optimizing TB management. While clinical trials have demonstrated the feasibility of shorter TB regimens, their real-world safety and effectiveness remain uncertain, particularly in high-burden settings such as Thailand. Before implementing the four-month HPMZ regimen in routine practice, it is crucial to evaluate its performance beyond controlled trial conditions.

This study examines the real-world effectiveness and safety of the HPMZ regimen. The fixed 1200 mg dose of rifapentine, as established in clinical trials, may not be suitable for Asian populations with lower average body weight, potentially resulting in higher drug exposure and an increased risk of toxicity. By evaluating these factors, this study aims to provide valuable insights into the challenges of implementing shorter TB regimens, ultimately contributing to the optimization of treatment strategies and improved patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This study was conduct as a pilot, prospective study at TB clinic, The Central Chest Institute of Thailand (CCIT) September 2023 – June 2025, This study was conducted under the approval of Ethics Committee of Central Chest Institute of Thailand with approval number REC 044/2566.

Screening and enrollment

During the study period, a total of 437 patients with tuberculosis were diagnosed and treated at our institution. Of these, 52 patients were approached for study participation. Twenty-six patients met the inclusion criteria, and 14 provided written informed consent and were enrolled.

Rifapentine was unavailable at our center from September 2024 to February 2025, which further limited recruitment during that period.

Participant and follow-up

Eligible participants were newly diagnosed drug-susceptible tuberculosis (DS-TB) patients aged 12 years or older with a positive acid-fast bacilli (AFB) sputum smear or a molecular TB test, such as Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (semiquantitative ‘medium’ or ‘high’) or the First-line Line Probe Assay (LPA) using Genotype® MTBDR plus Ver2.0. To be included in the study, patients needed to have normal liver function tests (LFTs), serum creatinine ≤2× the upper normal limit (UNL), potassium (K) ≥ 3.5 mmol/L, hemoglobin (Hb) ≥ 7 g/dL, and a platelet count of at least 100,000/mm3. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Patients were excluded if they had received more than five days of anti-TB treatment within the past six months, had a history of QTc prolongation, or were diagnosed with central nervous system TB, skeletal TB, miliary TB, or tuberculous pericarditis. Other exclusion criteria included HIV co-infection, a body weight below 40 kg, or resistance to rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, or fluoroquinolones. Patients who were unable to complete the follow-up process or who declined to provide informed consent were also excluded.

Physicians were also able to assess patients' clinical stability and could exclude those who were clinically unstable, such as individuals at high risk of hospitalization, clinically unstable was defined as hemodynamic instability, respiratory failure, or complications likely to require hospital admission. Additionally, patients who had difficulty understanding the importance of adherence could also be considered for exclusion.

Treatment and Baseline Assessment

Before initiating treatment, all patients received detailed counseling on their available treatment options. This discussion included a comparison of treatment efficacy, pill burden, duration, and potential AEs to ensure that patients could make informed decisions. Those who opted for the HPMZ regimen provided additional written informed consent before starting therapy.

The HPMZ regimen was administered under video directly observed therapy (VDOT). Patients received isoniazid at a fixed dose of 300 mg, rifapentine at 1200 mg, and moxifloxacin at 400 mg. Pyrazinamide was dosed according to body weight, with patients weighing less than 55 kg receiving 1000 mg, those between 55 and 75 kg receiving 1500 mg, and those over 75 kg receiving 2000 mg. This intensive phase lasted for eight weeks, after which patients continued treatment for an additional nine weeks with isoniazid 300 mg, rifapentine 1200 mg, and moxifloxacin 400 mg.

At baseline, all patients underwent a comprehensive medical history review, physical examination, and chest radiography. Microbiological evaluation included sputum acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear and culture from three consecutive specimens, or a single sputum sample tested using the Xpert MTB/RIF assay.

Baseline laboratory assessments included liver function tests—total protein, albumin, globulin, aspartate transaminase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), total and direct bilirubin—as well as renal function tests, complete blood count (CBC), blood glucose, and serum electrolyte levels. HIV testing and hepatitis B and C serology were routinely performed, with HBV and HCV serology assessed using chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) on an automated Architect Plus i1000 analyzer (Abbott Diagnostics). Participants with positive HBV or HCV serologic results were evaluated clinically and managed according to liver function status. Additional evaluations included pregnancy testing (when applicable) and a 12‑lead electrocardiogram (ECG).

Follow-up and Safety Monitoring

Patients attended scheduled follow-up visits at weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, and 17. At each visit, we carefully monitored clinical symptoms, adverse events, and liver function tests, along with sputum AFB smears and mycobacterial cultures to evaluate treatment response and safety. If a patient developed a Grade 3 or 4 AEs, HPMZ treatment was immediately discontinued, and they were transitioned to standard HRZE therapy. Regardless of treatment changes, all patients remained under close observation for symptoms and underwent chest X-rays (CXR) at 3, 6, 12, 15, and 18 months after completing treatment to assess disease recurrence. The severity of AEs was graded according to the Division of AIDS (DAIDS) Table for Grading the Severity of Adult and Pediatric Adverse Events, Version 2.1 (July 2017) [4]. Some patients were asked to provide additional blood samples to assess rifapentine and isoniazid levels to evaluate the correlation between adverse events and the dosage of anti-tuberculosis treatment.

In some individuals, blood samples were collected two weeks after treatment initiation to measure rifapentine and isoniazid concentrations, corresponding to the steady-state period. Samples were obtained at 1 h and again at 5 h after medication intake.

Result

Between September 2023 and June 2025, a total of 14 participants were enrolled and treated with the HPMZ regimen. The median age was 54 years (IQR: 42–61), and most were male (78.57%). The majority had normal body weight, with a median weight of 55.35 kg (IQR: 49.2–64.6) and a median BMI of 21.83 kg/m2 (IQR: 19.36–23.73). Based on body weight, the rifapentine dose was relatively high, averaging 21.26 mg/kg. Baseline characteristics and adverse events are summarized in Table 1. Overall, 78.57% of patients discontinued the HPZM regimen for various reasons (Table 2). The most common cause was Grade 3 hyperbilirubinemia, with a mean peak total bilirubin of 3.03 mg/dL and a mean peak direct bilirubin of 2.12 mg/dL.

Discussion

The implementation of the rifapentine–moxifloxacin regimen (HPMZ) for drug-susceptible tuberculosis (DS-TB) in real-world programmatic settings presents significant challenges, particularly in terms of safety outcomes. In this cohort, a high proportion of patients discontinued treatment before completion, with hyperbilirubinemia being the most frequently observed laboratory abnormality. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of the study design and clinical context.

Compared with TBTC Study 31/A5349 [1], the proportion of patients experiencing Grade 3 or higher adverse events and treatment discontinuation in our cohort appears substantially higher, with 78.57% of patients discontinuing treatment. Similar patterns have also been observed in real-world programmatic settings, including reports from San Francisco, USA, where 81.8% of patients experienced at least one adverse event and 50% discontinued HPMZ prematurely. Additionally, 63.6% developed hyperbilirubinemia after initiating HPMZ, with bilirubin levels reaching as high as 5.8 mg/dL [5]. Similarly, in New York City, 64% of patients discontinued the regimen, and 42% discontinued specifically due to adverse events [6]. Taken together, these findings suggest that tolerability in routine clinical practice may differ from that observed in randomized controlled trials.

Several factors may contribute to this discrepancy. First, the high rate of treatment discontinuation observed in this cohort was largely influenced by protocol-defined discontinuation criteria, which required permanent discontinuation for Grade 3–4 laboratory abnormalities, including isolated hyperbilirubinemia. Importantly, most bilirubin elevations occurred without substantial increases in aminotransferases and were not accompanied by clinical features of hepatitis. Rifamycins are known to increase bilirubin levels through inhibition of hepatic transporters, a mechanism that does not necessarily reflect hepatocellular injury. In routine clinical practice, such isolated abnormalities are often managed with continued monitoring rather than immediate discontinuation. However, in the absence of clear guideline recommendations for the management of these laboratory abnormalities, clinical decision-making in this study was more conservative, with a lower threshold for treatment discontinuation to prioritize patient safety. Consequently, the observed discontinuation rate likely reflects the impact of these conservative, protocol-defined safety thresholds.

Second, differences in baseline patient characteristics may also have contributed to the observed safety profile. The median age of participants in our cohort was substantially higher than that reported in TBTC Study 31/ A5349 [1], and older age is a well-recognized risk factor for hepatotoxicity, particularly in regimens containing pyrazinamide. This older age distribution may have increased susceptibility to bilirubin elevations and treatment intolerance.

Rifapentine overexposure has also been proposed as a potential contributing factor. In clinical trials [1], rifapentine was administered at a fixed daily dose of 1200 mg irrespective of body weight. Pharmacokinetic studies [7] have demonstrated dose-dependent increases in peak plasma concentration (Cmax) and overall drug exposure (AUC), suggesting that higher systemic exposure may be associated with an increased likelihood of bilirubin elevation. Supporting this, a randomized controlled trial [8] evaluating daily rifapentine at 1200 mg reported mean Cmax values of 31.97 ± 8.7 μg/mL in participants with diabetes and 33.10 ± 8.7 μg/mL in those without diabetes. In addition, the ORIENT study [9] (2024, China), which examined weight-based rifapentine dosing, reported a higher incidence of hyperbilirubinemia in patients receiving 20 mg/kg compared with lower-dose groups, suggesting a possible dose–response relationship.

In our cohort, the median body weight was 55.35 kg, and 57.14% of participants received rifapentine at doses exceeding 20 mg/kg under the fixed 1200 mg regimen. This relatively higher weight-adjusted dosing may have contributed to the observed increase in bilirubin levels. Furthermore, the mean Cmax among participants who developed Grade 3 hyperbilirubinemia (41.89 ± 10.42 μg/mL) appeared to be higher than values reported in prior studies, suggesting a potential association between rifapentine exposure and the severity of bilirubin elevation. However, pharmacokinetic sampling in this study was limited and not systematically conducted across all participants. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution and regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a causal relationship.

Third, the absence of a comparator group represents an important limitation. Without comparison to patients treated with standard regimens under similar programmatic conditions, it is not possible to determine whether the observed rates of hyperbilirubinemia and treatment discontinuation are specific to HPMZ or reflect background hepatotoxicity in this population. This limitation is particularly relevant given the older age profile of the cohort, which may independently increase the risk of adverse events.

In addition, the relatively small sample size and the limited enrollment—only 52 out of 437 eligible patients—may further restrict the generalizability of these findings, and the results may not be fully representative of the broader TB population.

Beyond drug exposure, interindividual variability in drug metabolism may also contribute to the observed findings. TBTC Study 31/A5349 [1] included a predominantly Black population (approximately 70%), whereas the present study was conducted in an Asian population. Genetic variation may influence drug metabolism and systemic exposure. Previous study [10] has demonstrated that Black participants carrying the AADAC rs1803155 G allele had 10.2% lower rifapentine AUC 0–24 compared with those carrying the A allele. In addition, Black participants exhibited significantly lower median rifapentine AUC 0–24 than non-Black participants (283 vs. 369 μg/mL, P < 0.001). However, pharmacogenetic testing was not performed in this study; therefore, the contribution of genetic factors to the observed variability remains speculative. These findings highlight the importance of considering population-specific pharmacokinetics when implementing fixed-dose regimens. While alternative dosing strategies, including weight-based approaches, may help optimize drug exposure, further studies are required to evaluate their safety and effectiveness.

Importantly, treatment discontinuation in this study was driven by protocol-defined safety thresholds, including isolated hyperbilirubinemia. In the absence of a standardized management protocol at the time of study implementation, the stopping criteria were more conservative than those used in TBTC Study 31/A5349 [1] and likely contributed to the higher discontinuation rate. Therefore, regimen completion rates should be interpreted in the context of these protocol-defined criteria. Before broader programmatic implementation, standardized guidance for the management of adverse events should be developed, including clear criteria for treatment continuation, modification, or switching to standard regimens. In addition, randomized controlled trials evaluating weight-based rifapentine dosing are needed to better define the balance between efficacy and tolerability.

Conclusion

This real-world study observed a high rate of treatment discontinuation among Thai patients receiving the HPMZ regimen, with hyperbilirubinemia being the most frequently reported laboratory abnormality. However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of protocol-defined discontinuation criteria, which may have contributed to the observed discontinuation rates. Potential contributing factors, including higher weight-adjusted rifapentine exposure and interindividual variability in drug metabolism, warrant consideration. Nevertheless, given the observational nature of this study and the absence of a comparator group, these associations should be interpreted with caution.

Overall, these findings highlight the need for further studies to better characterize the relationship between rifapentine exposure and safety outcomes, and to inform optimized dosing strategies and standardized approaches to adverse event management before broader programmatic implementation.
