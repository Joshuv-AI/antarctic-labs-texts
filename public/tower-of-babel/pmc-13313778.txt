Assessing the role of vitamins in dental caries occurrence: A retrospective study using NHANES data 2011 to 2018
Authors: Jingna Huang, Ye Zhang, Bo Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the relationship between vitamin intake and the occurrence of dental caries. This study was a retrospective study that collected data from the National Health And Nutrition Examination Survey database from 2011 to 2018. The number of dental caries teeth was determined using the Radike AW. Criteria for diagnosis of caries (1968), and vitamin consumption data were obtained through face-to-face interviews. Logistic regression analysis was used to explore the association between dental caries and vitamins, and variables with P < .1 in the univariate analysis were selected for multivariate logistic regression analysis. Clinical prediction models were constructed for variables with P < .05 and were internally and externally validated to assess the predictive ability of the models. This study included 4221 subjects (2215 training, 2006 validation), with vitamin C (47.6%), vitamin K (19.4%), vitamin B12 (14.2%), and vitamin D (11.3%) demonstrating the highest intake proportions. Logistic regression identified age, education, vitamin D, and vitamin K as predictors of dental caries, while the clinical model exhibited consistent moderate predictive performance across both datasets. Results remained stable after variable incorporation, confirming model reliability. Our findings suggest an association between vitamin D intake and vitamin K deficiency with dental caries; however, further prospective studies are warranted to confirm causal mechanisms.

1. Introduction

Dental caries is a prevalent chronic oral disease characterized by the localized destruction of tooth hard tissues due to acidic byproducts produced by bacteria fermenting dietary carbohydrates.[1] According to the World Health Organization, approximately 2 billion people worldwide suffer from permanent tooth caries, while 514 million children are affected by primary tooth caries.[2] The occurrence of this disease is influenced by various factors, including salivary flow, saliva composition, fluoride exposure, and individual preventive behaviors, with dietary habits and nutritional components playing a significant role.[3]

Vitamins, as essential nutrients for the human body, are crucial for numerous physiological processes, including growth, metabolism, and development, and they may also impact the progression of dental caries. Previous studies have indicated associations between dental caries and vitamins such as B6, B12, C, and D.[4,5] However, findings from existing literature remain inconsistent: while several investigations have reported that adequate vitamin D, B6, and C intake is associated with a lower risk of dental caries, others have observed no significant correlation or even conflicting trends. These discrepancies may stem from variations in population characteristics, dietary assessment methods, and confounding control. Adequate vitamin D intake is linked to a lower incidence of caries,[6] while vitamin B6 may inhibit the growth of cariogenic bacteria by promoting the proliferation of non-cariogenic microorganisms.[7] Additionally, vitamin C has been shown to inhibit dental plaque formation, and a deficiency in vitamin B12 is associated with an increased prevalence of caries and periodontal diseases in children.[8–10]

Vitamins, as important coenzymes or components of coenzymes, participate in numerous metabolic reactions within the body. Interactions among vitamins also significantly affect physiological processes. Particularly, the fat-soluble vitamins D and K play a central role in calcium metabolism. The combined action of vitamins D and K is more effective for skeletal health than either alone, potentially by influencing the carboxylation status of vitamin K-dependent proteins and the metabolic pathways of vitamin D.[11] However, the mechanisms underlying the multiple effects of these vitamins on bone are not fully understood.

Despite existing research on the relationship between vitamin intake and dental caries, the interactions among different vitamins and their specific effects on the occurrence of caries remain unclear. This study aims to utilize data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database to analyze the relationship between vitamin intake and the occurrence of dental caries, providing new insights for caries prevention.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study utilizes legally available data obtained from public databases and does not involve biomedical research in human subjects; therefore, it is not subject to Instituitional Review Board approval. The NHANES is a research project conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention using a multistage probability sampling method.[12] The project protocol is approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and each participant is required to sign a written informed consent. Approximately 5000 individuals from a nationally representative sample across 15 different counties (second-level administrative divisions in the United States, below the state level) are examined annually (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/?CDC_AAref_Val=https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). The methodology of this study is illustrated in Figure 1. This retrospective study selected 42,725 participants from the NHANES database spanning from 2011 to 2018. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: participants with hypertension; participants with diabetes mellitus (defined as a fasting blood glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours, according to the 1999 World Health Organization criteria); any missing demographic data, vitamin data, or caries information. Ultimately, a total of 4221 participants were included in this study. All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (Declaration of Helsinki).

2.2. Data collection

In this study, the collected variables included demographic data such as age (years), poverty-income ratio, sex, education level, and race. Vitamins comprised vitamin B1 (milligrams), vitamin B2 (milligrams), vitamin B6 (milligrams), vitamin B12 (micrograms), vitamin C (milligrams), vitamin K (micrograms), and vitamin D (micrograms), with all vitamins totaling the intake over a 30-day period for each participant. The number of decayed teeth (D) was examined by dentists licensed in the state of the United States and recorded by technicians and entered into the system, with diagnoses based on the caries diagnostic criteria (1968, Radike AW).[13] Those with caries were divided into the disease group, and those without caries were placed into the healthy group. All oral health examinations were conducted within rooms of the mobile examination center, equipped with portable dental units, lights, and compressed air. Information on vitamin intake was obtained through home interviews and calculated accordingly. During the interview, participants were shown a card listing dietary supplements and asked to select which supplements they had taken within the past 30 days, providing all packaging boxes/bottles of dietary supplements used to the interviewer.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were described using the mean ± standard deviation, while categorical data were described using frequencies and proportions (n [%]).

Firstly, we conducted univariate logistic regression analysis for all variables and calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF). Variables with a VIF > 5 were excluded due to high collinearity. Based on the results of the univariate logistic regression analysis, variables with a P value < .1 were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent predictors of dental caries occurrence.

Next, variables with a P value < .05 in the multivariate logistic regression analysis were used to build clinical prediction models. Internal validation of each model was performed using calibration plots and bootstrap resampling involving 500 iterations. Additionally, an external validation set was used to evaluate the predictive ability of the models.

To assess the predictive performance of each model, we calculated the concordance index (C-index). The C-index ranges from 0.5 (indicating completely random prediction) to 1 (indicating perfect prediction). We also assessed the consistency between actual outcomes and predicted probabilities using calibration curves and the area under the curve (AUC).

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R version 4.3.1, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

3.3. Construction and validation of dental caries risk prediction model

To improve the accuracy of dental caries risk prediction by dental practitioners, we integrated the identified independent predictors into a clinical prediction model and constructed a nomogram (Fig. 2B). The analysis results showed that individuals with a younger age, lower education level, lower vitamin K intake, and higher vitamin D intake had a higher risk of dental caries occurrence. The C-index and AUC of the model were both 0.65, P < .05 (Fig. 2C). The calibration curve demonstrated good agreement between the predicted probabilities and the observed probabilities (Fig. 2D).

To validate the predictive ability of the model, we tested it in an independent validation set. The test results showed that the C-index and AUC of the model were 0.62, P < .05 (Fig. 2E), and the predicted probabilities were consistent with the actual probabilities (Fig. 2F). These results indicate that the prediction model has moderate predictive performance and good stability.

4. Discussion

This study suggested a potential association between vitamin D and vitamin K intake and the occurrence of dental caries, highlighting the critical roles of these 2 vitamins in maintaining oral health. Specifically, we found that both excessive intake of vitamin D and insufficient intake of vitamin K may increase the risk of dental caries. These findings provide new perspectives for the prevention of dental caries, particularly in the context of public health policy-making and clinical practice.

Vitamin D plays a crucial role in calcium and phosphorus metabolism and is an essential nutrient for maintaining dental and skeletal health.[14–16] Previous studies have shown an association between vitamin D deficiency and increased dental caries rates.[17–20] Vitamin D promotes calcium absorption in the intestines and enhances tooth mineralization, thereby reducing the occurrence of dental caries.[21,22] However, this study unexpectedly found that excessive vitamin D intake was also associated with an increased risk of dental caries, which may be related to the complex metabolic mechanisms of vitamin D in the body. Excessive vitamin D can lead to excessive deposition of calcium,[23] which can subsequently affect the physiological structure of teeth. Therefore, it is crucial to determine the appropriate level of vitamin D intake to prevent dental caries. Importantly, because vitamin D intake in NHANES is self-reported and does not permit a reliable dose–response assessment, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence that excessive vitamin D increases caries risk. In addition, key factors such as oral hygiene, fluoride exposure, and dietary sugar intake were not available for full adjustment and may have influenced the observed relationship.

Similarly, vitamin K also plays an important role in bone and dental health. Research has shown that vitamin K promotes the synthesis of bone proteins, especially osteocalcin, which is crucial for tooth mineralization.[24] Vitamin K deficiency may lead to poor bone mineralization, consequently affecting dental health.[25] The results of this study further indicate the significant correlation between insufficient vitamin K intake and the occurrence of dental caries, emphasizing the potential role of vitamin K in oral health. Supplementation of vitamin K can improve oral health and reduce the incidence of dental caries.[26]

The combined action of vitamin D and vitamin K in calcium metabolism is complementary. Vitamin D promotes calcium absorption, while vitamin K ensures the effective deposition of calcium in teeth and bones. This synergy not only helps maintain the structure and strength of teeth but also reduces the incidence of dental caries. Therefore, balanced intake of vitamin D and vitamin K is crucial for oral health. Further studies have shown that combined supplementation of vitamin D and vitamin K significantly improves bone density and dental health while reducing the risk of dental caries.[27] The effectiveness of this combined supplementation may be closely related to their synergistic interaction in calcium metabolism. It should be noted that other unmeasured factors (such as fluoride exposure, high sugar intake, and oral hygiene practices) may also influence dental caries risk and could partly explain the observed associations.

Additionally, this study found age and education level to be independent predictive factors, reflecting the influence of socioeconomic factors on oral health. Lower education levels may be associated with poor dietary habits and a lack of oral health knowledge, further increasing the risk of dental caries.[28] Therefore, considering socioeconomic factors is crucial when formulating oral health interventions.

Despite providing valuable insights into the relationship between vitamin intake and dental caries, this study has several limitations.[29] First, as a retrospective cross-sectional analysis using the NHANES dataset, selection and recall bias may exist due to self-reported vitamin intake. Second, participants with systemic metabolic disorders were excluded to improve internal validity, which may limit generalizability.[30] Third, some potential confounders, such as fluoride exposure, oral hygiene, and sugar intake, were not consistently available and thus could not be controlled. Finally, only vitamins D and K were analyzed, and future studies should include a broader range of nutrients and prospective validation to confirm these associations. Given the limited availability/consistency of key covariates (e.g., sugar intake, fluoride exposure, and oral hygiene) and the potential for selection bias introduced by excluding participants with systemic diseases, we did not perform additional sensitivity analyses or apply NHANES survey weights; therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associative.

In conclusion, the findings of this study emphasize the importance of vitamin D and vitamin K in the prevention of dental caries, providing a basis for future research and public health policy-making. Prospective clinical trials should be conducted to validate the causal relationship between vitamin intake and dental caries occurrence and explore their underlying mechanisms.[31]

5. Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that lower vitamin K intake and higher vitamin D intake are associated with a higher risk of dental caries occurrence. These results provide preliminary evidence that vitamins could play a role in oral health through complex metabolic interactions. However, as this study was retrospective and observational, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these associations and clarify the underlying biological mechanisms before any clinical application.
