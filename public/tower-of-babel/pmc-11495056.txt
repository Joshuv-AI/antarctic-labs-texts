Development of a behaviour change intervention for improving physical activity amongst stroke survivors with physical disabilities: a co-design approach
Authors: Li Khim Kwah, Kinjal Doshi, Everlyn Wai, Jenna Hollis, Marie-Louise Bird, Yong Hao Pua, Julian Thumboo, Lian Leng Low, Hong-Gu He, Deidre Anne De Silva, Susan Niam, Irene Toh, Yook Cing Lui, Silvana Choo, Juliana Wang, Shamala Thilarajah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke survivors face many barriers to physical activity (PA). Solving physical inactivity after stroke requires a “systems-based” approach. We aimed to develop a complex intervention targeted at improving PA after stroke in Singapore using behaviour change theory and a co-design approach involving multiple stakeholders.MethodsWe carried out the intervention development in three phases: i. preparation phase, ii. co-design phase, and iii. intervention refinement phase. During the preparation phase, we conducted surveys (n = 38 stroke survivors, 71 physiotherapists and 35 exercise professionals) and interviews (n = 19 stroke survivors) to understand the factors influencing PA after stroke. The co-design phase consisted of two-co-design workshops held in August 2022 and were attended by 13 stroke survivors and 4 caregivers. Relevant domains of the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) and items in the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist guided the discussion topics in the first co-design workshop. Solution prototypes such as exercise videos, arm and leg straps and information resources were shown in the second co-design workshop to gather feedback. In the intervention refinement phase, eight healthcare professionals from various sectors participated in two virtual Zoom meetings in August 2023, and used the Acceptability, Practicability, Effectiveness, Affordability, Spillover effects and Equity (APEASE) grid to rate the active ingredients in the complex intervention by considering the current healthcare landscape in terms of resources and manpower.ResultsStroke survivors and caregivers want a personalised PA program, stroke-specific PA opportunities and information resources, medical clearance, advice and help from healthcare professionals skilled in stroke care, face-to-face sessions at preferred exercise spaces, and access to adaptive equipment. A complex intervention consisting of 21 behaviour change techniques, 6 intervention functions and 8 options was developed.ConclusionsUsing behaviour change theory and a co-design approach involving multiple stakeholders, a complex intervention was developed to target physical inactivity after stroke. The intervention titled MOTIVATE is currently being tested in a type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20403-1.Keywords: Behaviour, Co-design, Participatory action research, Patient and public involvement, Physical activity

• Using theoretical models of behaviour change and a co-design approach involving multiple stakeholders, we developed a complex behaviour change intervention targeted at improving PA after stroke.

• The complex intervention consists of 21 behaviour change techniques, 6 intervention functions and 8 interventions options (4 core and 4 optional add-ons), and allows for personalization by enabling stroke survivors to choose their preferred exercise spaces and optional add-ons.

• To our knowledge, our study is the first to report such a rigorous and systematic method in developing a complex PA intervention personalised to the needs and preferences of people after stroke.

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of disability in Southeast Asia, and the fourth leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. Up to a third of strokes occur in people with a prior stroke or transient ischemic attack [2]; these recurrent strokes are often more fatal, more disabling and more costly than the first stroke [2–4]. To reduce the risk of a recurrent stroke, stroke guidelines recommend medical and lifestyle interventions such as medications to control hypertension and atrial fibrillation, and adoption of a healthy diet, smoking cessation and regular physical activity (PA) [5, 6]. However, the implementation of secondary stroke prevention activities remain poor worldwide [7, 8] and no formal secondary stroke prevention programme has been developed and tested for efficacy in Singapore.

Of the ten modifiable risk factors of stroke [9], physical inactivity has been shown to be the strongest predictor of a recurrent stroke, myocardial infarction, or vascular death [10]. Regular PA has also been shown to have ripple effects on the other modifiable risk factors of stroke, including the reduction of blood pressure [11, 12], reduction of fasting glucose and insulin levels [12], increase in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and reduction in total cholesterol [11, 12], prevention of obesity and improvement of mental health and well-being [13]. Despite the known benefits of PA, most stroke survivors do not meet recommended PA guidelines for people with disabilities or chronic diseases (i.e., 150–300 min per week of moderate-intensity PA [14], or 6500–8500 steps/day [15]). Published in 2017, a systematic review of 103 studies including 5306 stroke survivors found that stroke survivors achieved an average of 5535 steps in the subacute phase and 4078 steps in the chronic phase [16]. Similarly in Singapore, stroke survivors clocked a median of 4870 steps in the subacute phase [17], highlighting the prevalent issue of physical inactivity after stroke.

To date, we do not have evidence-based strategies to target low levels of physical activity. While the effectiveness of wearable activity trackers to increase PA and improve health outcomes has been shown in healthy children, adults, older adults and clinical populations (e.g., type 2 diabetes, Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease, overweight or obesity) in a recent umbrella review of 39 systematic reviews and meta-analyses [18], its effectiveness is yet to be proven in the stroke population [19]. Systematic reviews looking at the efficacy of PA interventions after stroke have also found a paucity of well-designed studies and a lack of benefit to support current lifestyle interventions to promote PA [20, 21].

Physical inactivity after stroke is a complex problem to solve, as stroke survivors worldwide and in Singapore have reported many barriers to PA ranging from personal factors (e.g., beliefs about capabilities, fatigue and physical impairments) to environmental factors beyond their control (e.g., lack of accessible classes/programmes at fitness centres, lack of assistance from fitness centre staff and transport to accessible exercise spaces) [22–25]. Complex interventions that target the behaviour at multiple levels (i.e., individual, organisation and population levels) are therefore needed. To move forward in light of the lack of evidence on effective strategies to improve PA after stroke, there is a need to rely on theoretical frameworks (for behaviour change) and multisectoral partnerships to develop and implement a complex intervention that is effective, implementable and scalable on a national level [7, 13, 26, 27]. We chose to use the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) as it was comprehensive in covering cognitive, affective, social and environmental influences on behaviour, and allowed us to map our findings back to the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) to consider the full range of intervention functions and select the most appropriate ones [28, 29].

Using behaviour change theory and a co-design approach involving multiple stakeholders, the aim of this study was to develop a complex intervention to increase PA amongst stroke survivors in Singapore. This study follows our prior work on exploring the determinants of PA for stroke survivors in Singapore [24, 25] and precedes the conduct of a type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial to test the efficacy of our complex intervention titled MOTIVATE (Multi-mOdal Training program to promote physical actIVity After sTrokE).

Materials and methods

To develop and report our complex intervention, we follow the recommendations from the World Health Organisation, the Medical Research Council and the Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation roundtable [13, 26, 27, 30] by engaging people and communities in co-designing the intervention, using a structured framework for intervention development and ensuring a behaviour change theory underpinning the intervention. We also report this study as per the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public 2—GRIPP 2 short form reporting checklist [31]. Our intervention development process can be described in three main phases: 2.1 Preparation phase, 2.2 Co-design phase, and 2.3 Intervention refinement phase (Fig. 1).

Preparation phase

Three studies were conducted from June 2019 to January 2022 to understand the factors influencing PA in stroke survivors with physical disabilities, and the factors influencing the promotion of PA in health and exercise professionals. These studies included i. a face-to-face survey with 38 stroke survivors using the Barriers to Physical Activity Questionnaire for people with mobility impairments (BPAQ-MI) [32], ii. a semi-structured interview with 19 stroke survivors using the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [28], and iii. an online survey with 71 physiotherapists and 35 exercise professionals using adapted versions of previously published questionnaires [33, 34]. For the studies, stroke survivors were included if they were 21 years or over, English-speaking, community-dwelling, and had an affected arm and/or leg from the stroke. Stroke survivors learnt about the studies via recruitment advertisements distributed via web-based methods on the Singapore National Stroke Association (SNSA) platforms (e.g., mailing list, Facebook page, WhatsApp group chats), announcements at SNSA events, word-of-mouth and snowball sampling. Physiotherapists were included if they had treated at least one stroke survivor in the last three months, while exercise professionals (i.e., gym/fitness instructors, personal trainers and exercise physiologists/scientists) were included if they had seen at least one client in the last six months. Physiotherapists learnt about the study via a recruitment advertisement distributed via the mailing lists of the Singapore Physiotherapy Association and contacts of the research team, while exercise professionals learnt about the study via a recruitment advertisement distributed to public and private gyms, and contacts of the research team. As the survey is web-based, participants received the web-link via email and/or other social media platforms such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Twitter and Instagram. This background preparatory work guided the next phase of the intervention development process (i.e., the co-design phase), and helped us to identify small group discussion topics, and get feedback on solution prototypes during the co-design workshops in the next phase.

Co-design phase

We held two co-design workshops at the Singapore National Stroke Association (SNSA) office in August 2022 (Fig. 1). The workshops were attended by 13 stroke survivors, 4 caregivers, 3 facilitators (KLK, physiotherapist; ST, physiotherapist; and KD, neuropsychologist), treasurer of SNSA and a User Experience (UX) designer. All stroke survivors had an affected arm/leg but were able to walk independently with an aid for short distances, with some using an electric wheelchair for outdoor mobility. All facilitators had more than 15 years of clinical experience in the area of stroke and were trained in implementation science and behaviour change. Workshops were held on two separate days, with each workshop lasting for two hours in duration with refreshments served in-between. During the first workshop, stroke survivors and caregivers were divided into three small groups. Each group was tasked to discuss one of the following three topics: i. Knowledge and skills, ii. Reinforcements and reminders, and iii. Environment. We selected the topics based on the relevant TDF domains (i.e., theoretical constructs that influence behaviour) identified in our prior work [25], and the need for more details on the TDF domains to aid better selection of the active ingredients in our intervention. For example, with the TDF domains of “Knowledge” and “Skills”, we needed more specific information on what stroke survivors lacked knowledge and skills in, and how they wanted this knowledge and skills communicated/taught. Facilitators led the discussion in each of the small groups with guiding questions to help develop the intervention. At the end of the small group discussion, each facilitator presented the group’s discussion points back to the larger group, and more feedback/input was sought from other stroke survivors and caregivers (Table 1). The first workshop ended with a big group discussion on exercise preferences where items in the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist were covered such as the What, Who, How, Where, When and How much [35]. Unfinished discussions on TIDieR items were brought over to the second co-design workshop. During the second co-design workshop, stroke survivors and caregivers were also shown solution prototypes, which included videos of stroke survivors exercising in gyms, outdoor fitness zones and homes, arm and leg strap prototypes, educational infographics (e.g., related to how long and how hard one should be working) and the mock layout of a website. Solutions were generated from our prior work [24, 25] and the first workshop. Both workshops were audio-recorded, and a notetaker summarised the points of each workshop. Intervention refinement phase

Using the results from the preparation and co-design phases, the TDF guide and the BCW [28, 29], KLK, ST and KD set out to identify the behaviour change techniques (BCTs), intervention functions, and options that are likely to be effective in improving PA after stroke. This step was necessary so that we could be explicit about what the active ingredients in our complex intervention are and how they might work. We described our active ingredients in terms of BCTs, intervention functions and options, where BCTs refer to the “smallest components of behaviour change interventions that on their own have the potential to change behaviour” [36], intervention functions refer to “broad categories of means by which an intervention can change behaviour” (p.109) [29], and options refer to the modes of intervention delivery [29]. We refined the BCTs, intervention functions, and options using the following three steps. First, we listed out the relevant TDF domains identified from our interviews and mapped the intervention functions that are likely to be effective to these relevant domains [25, 29]. To further narrow down the intervention functions, we followed the recommended process of using the APEASE (Affordability, Practicability, Effectiveness and cost-effectiveness, Acceptability, Side-effects/safety, Equity) criteria to determine intervention functions that are likely to be affordable, practicable and acceptable for us to test and implement [29, 37]. Second, we used the Theory and Techniques tool (https://theoryandtechniquetool.humanbehaviourchange.org/tool) to prioritise the BCTs that have shown a strong link with a mechanism of action for relevant TDF domains identified from our interviews [25, 38]. (A mechanism of action is the process through which a BCT affects behaviour and helps to explain how a BCT works [38].) To ensure we did not miss out important BCTs that might influence PA after stroke, we also reviewed the BCTs that have either shown a strong link with a mechanism of action for non-relevant TDF domains from our interviews and the remaining BCTs that have not shown a strong link [38]. Here, we relied on co-design workshop findings to select BCTs that might be relevant in improving PA after stroke but were not initially prioritised due to inconsistent links, non-links and absence of evidence to a mechanism of action [38], or shown a strong link but belonged to a non-relevant TDF domain from our interviews [25, 38]. In doing so, we developed a system of prioritisation for the 93 BCTs [39] to specify in our intervention (Fig. 2). Third, we brainstormed on the different options/modes of delivery and ran our options by a panel of eight experts in chronic disease, population health and allied health landscape. Experts included health researchers in population health and chronic diseases (two doctors, one nurse and one physiotherapist), rehabilitation managers (two physiotherapists), stroke neurologist (one doctor). Additionally, six of the experts have background experience in national or health cluster level policymaking. Experts then used the APEASE grid [37] to rate each of the options identified in MOTIVATE. This was done via two virtual Zoom meetings facilitated by KLK and ST in August 2023. Experts discussed each option and filled in their ratings on the grid via a Qualtrics survey link. For each rating, experts also provided their justifications in a text box. These ratings help to ensure the successful implementation and scalability of the MOTIVATE intervention.

Results

Results from the preparation phase

Eight of the 14 TDF domains were also found to be relevant (i.e., likely to improve PA if targeted in interventions), and included environmental context and resources, knowledge, social influences, emotion, reinforcement, behavioural regulation, skills and beliefs about capabilities [25]. These formed the background of the discussions in the co-design workshops and helped us to prioritise BCTs in the intervention refinement phase.

Results from the co-design phase

Results from the co-design workshops are presented in Tables 1 and 2. Overall, stroke survivors and caregivers want a personalised PA program, stroke-specific PA opportunities and information resources, medical clearance, advice and help from healthcare professionals skilled in stroke care, face-to-face sessions at preferred exercise spaces, and access to adaptive equipment that will help them to use exercise machines fully at public spaces such as outdoor fitness zones and gyms. In terms of knowledge and skills, most stroke survivors were aware of the recommended 10,000 steps/day for the healthy population due to the National Steps Challenge programme by the Singapore Health Promotions Board [40], though most found this an unachievable goal. Most were not aware of the recommended 150 min of moderate-intensity exercise/week required to gain health benefits [14], and queried the safety of exercising at moderate intensity. They suggested that educational materials should cover these pointers and include signposting to PA opportunities that are appropriate for stroke survivors. When it came to reinforcements and reminders, all agreed that seeing one’s own recovery in terms of achieving goals and others’ recovery were the strongest motivator, followed by social opportunities to get out and about and meet friends though a few were happy to exercise on their own/with their partners. Other reinforcements like financial incentives and additional bonuses though were not absolutely essential to helping them increase their PA. Reminders via trackers or messages can become white noise if done too frequently or are not personalised (e.g., sent via a robot rather than a person they know). The type, timing and frequency of reminders needed to be personalised to the individual as stroke survivors expressed different preferences. Many environmental barriers were raised. Some were beyond their control (e.g., insufficient slots in gyms, unhelpful staff, lack of ramp and rail at swimming pools) while others could be overcome with the help from healthcare/exercise professionals and volunteers (e.g., advice from physiotherapists on how to adapt exercises and use equipment in gyms and outdoor fitness zones, manual assistance from volunteers to get on/off exercise machines or getting in/out of the pool). Visting a new environment/new exercise space with other stroke survivors increased the likelihood of them engaging in PA and became a less daunting task. If prior information about the exercise space and activity can be provided to indicate a level of "stroke survivor friendliness", that would help alleviate the fear and concerns they have about a new space and activity.

When asked to refine the TIDieR elements for MOTIVATE (i.e., WHO, HOW, WHERE, WHEN and HOW MUCH), participants had strong views about WHO should be delivering the intervention. They were clear that a physiotherapist with expertise in stroke was essential for the first few sessions of exercise prescription as the physiotherapist would be aware of the complications that might limit the stroke survivor in engaging in PA (e.g., spasticity) and would be able to give the right advice on management (e.g., referral for Botox or prescription of an ankle–foot orthosis). Subsequently after the exercise prescription by the physiotherapist, the participants were supportive of receiving help from other professionals such as exercise professionals, or volunteers who can physically assist them at the gyms or swimming pools. In terms of HOW, all participants agreed that face-to-face sessions with the physiotherapist at preferred exercise spaces were essential for the effective transfer of knowledge and skills. These can be supplemented with remote sessions if cost or limited manpower becomes an issue. In terms of WHERE and WHEN, participants had different preferences for exercise spaces and frequency and thus wanted a personalised approach towards where they wanted to be physically active and when they should engage with the physiotherapist. In terms of HOW MUCH, all wanted this support and help for as long as possible but acknowledged that it might not be possible with limited healthcare workforce/manpower resources. Participants also shared that a small nominal fee should be charged for such a programme, or individuals would not take the behaviour change seriously. (The WHAT of MOTIVATE was captured from the earlier discussions during the workshop, see Table 1.)

Feedback on solution prototypes is presented in Table 2. For the most part, participants supported the representation of stroke survivors as models in the videos but wanted more exercise routines from stroke survivors. Participants provided feedback that the arm and leg straps might be hard to put on and take off without an instructional manual or video. It would be more convenient if the leg strap could be removed with just one hand. Participants also commented that the information in the educational infographics was important and crucial for them to know how to exercise at recommended levels of PA but requested more representation of stroke survivors in the infographics as the current stock images showed people with other disabilities such as people with visual impairments and limb amputations. Participants also requested that the website be simpler so that when they are looking for PA opportunities, the information can be easily found with one to two clicks on the website.

Results from the intervention refinement phase

As all nine intervention functions (i.e., education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, training, enablement, modelling, environmental restructuring and restrictions) [29] were mapped to relevant TDF domains from our prior work [25], we used the APEASE criteria and prioritised 6 intervention functions (i.e., education, persuasion, training, enablement, modelling and environmental restructuring) that are likely to be affordable, practicable and acceptable for us to test and implement [29]. Using the Theory and Techniques tool [38], we identified 34 BCTs that showed a strong link with a mechanism of action for relevant TDF domains identified from our prior work [25]. Of the 34 BCTs, we only included 11 BCTs and selected them based on pragmatic reasons and feedback from the co-design workshops. We used the same justification to add 4 BCTs that showed a strong link with a mechanism of action for non-relevant TDF domains, and another 6 BCTs that did not show a strong link with a mechanism of action (Fig. 2). We provide two examples of our judgement/decision-making on BCTs below:

For example, the BCTs "10.1 material incentive (behaviour)" and "10.2 material reward (behaviour)" (BCTs with evidence of a strong link to a mechanism of action for relevant TDF domains) were not selected as these were readily available on a national level to everyone in Singapore as part of the National Steps Challenge™, an initiative in Singapore that incentivizes citizens and permanent residents to stay active daily [41]. This meant that both control and treatment groups would have access to the BCTs, but this would not enable us to test the efficacy of this aspect of MOTIVATE. In addition, incentives received mixed reviews during the co-design workshops. While some participants from the workshop shared that incentives would motivate them to exercise, some did not share the same views. But all agreed that incentives were added bonuses to staying active and what would motivate them the most was seeing themselves recover. Due to this feedback, the BCTs "1.1 Goal setting (behaviour)" and "1.5 Review behaviour goal(s)" (BCTs with evidence of a strong link to a mechanism of action for a non-relevant TDF domain “Goals”) were included as part of our complex intervention instead. Although “Goals” was not identified as a relevant TDF domain from our interviews, stroke survivors shared during the co-design workshops that seeing themselves improve/achieve goals (such as walking more independently, being able to do a new sport) would encourage them to keep going in terms of improving PA. Thus it is highly likely that if a stroke survivor was engaged in setting and reviewing PA goals on a regular basis, this can further affirm the mental representation of the outcome or end state that he/she wishes to achieve, leading to an increase in PA levels. Another example can be seen in the inclusion of BCT "9.1 Credible source". Although no evidence of a strong link was found between BCT “9.1 Credible source” and the relevant TDF domain “Skills” (based on the Theory and Techniques tool) [38], participants had emphasised the importance of a physiotherapist for the initial advice and exercise prescription, with the options to refer to exercise professionals and volunteers for physical assistance when moving around in public spaces or getting on/off exercise machines. How the credible source (i.e., a physiotherapist) might work to influence the TDF domain “Skills” is likely due to the fact that most stroke survivors have worked with physiotherapists in the past during their stint of physical rehabilitation in the hospitals, and are likely to trust physiotherapists in helping them to acquire the skills to do the exercises, or use the gym machines safely and effectively. This will in turn increase the confidence of stroke survivors to exercise independently and improve their PA levels.

Overall, we finalised a list of 21 BCTs that were packaged into 8 options/modes of intervention delivery (Table 3). Of the 8 options, 4 were core options meaning that all participants in the intervention group would receive them, and 4 were optional add-ons meaning that only some participants in the intervention group would receive them depending on their personal preferences (based on the feedback gained from the co-design workshops on the need to personalise the complex intervention). Of the 21 BCTs, 15 belonged to the core options, and 11 belonged to the optional add-ons (with 5 repeat BCTs from the core options) (Table 3). We provided a summary of the APEASE grid score in Table 4. Experts’ individual scores for each criterion of the APEASE grid were computed into an average score for each criterion, alongside a sample of justifications provided by experts for their own individual scores for that criterion. The justifications and discussions during the Zoom meetings led to further refinements of the complex intervention, specifically the reduction of sessions from 12 to 6, and allowing a telephone option for virtual sessions. Experts shared that 12 sessions were too many; it would likely be hard for stroke survivors with limited mobility to get to and hard for day rehabilitation centres to deliver 12 one-on-one physiotherapy sessions with stroke survivors. Six sessions were deemed more reasonable and acceptable for day rehabilitation centres to deliver. As some older patients might not be familiar with Zoom or have the resources for internet connectivity, a telephone option was proposed to meet their needs. We report the final details of the MOTIVATE intervention as per the TIDieR checklist [35] in Table 5. A visual representation of the programme theory (i.e., a logic model) is also presented in Fig. 3.

Discussion

In line with recommendations from the World Health Organisation, the Medical Research Council and the Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation roundtable [13, 26, 27, 30], we have used a co-design approach and a structured framework to develop and report our complex intervention referencing the TDF, BCW, TIDieR and APEASE [28, 29, 35, 37]. First of its kind in Singapore, our complex intervention consist of 21 BCTs, 8 options and 6 intervention functions developed with stroke survivors, caregivers and health care professionals across a period of six years. Instead of a “one size fits all” approach, we have allowed personalisation of the complex PA intervention where stroke survivors can select his/her preferred exercise spaces and the optional add-ons to include in the complex intervention. To our knowledge, our study is the first to report such a personalised approach in a complex intervention aimed at improving PA after stroke.

To date, there are five studies published in the United Kingdom and Australia that have used a co-design approach to develop interventions to improve outcomes associated with PA after stroke [42–46]. These outcomes include improving PA [43–46] and reducing sedentary behaviour [42]. Similar to the five studies, our study included at least two or more different stakeholders in the process of intervention development (e.g., stroke survivors, caregivers, healthcare professionals), engaged these stakeholders in co-design workshops and/or group meetings, and intended to test the interventions in randomised controlled trials. However, there were some differences between our studies in terms of the methods and results. Three of the five studies had a pre-conceived idea of delivering the PA intervention [43, 45, 46], and sought feedback from stakeholders on an outdoor walking intervention [43], a text message intervention [45] and a telehealth-delivered exercise intervention [46]. Although these interventions can potentially be effective and feasible to implement, having a pre-conceived idea of what might work may result in some solutions being overlooked. Certainly, in our study, none of these interventions would have worked as stroke survivors wanted a variety of exercises other than walking, preferred a range of reinforcements and reminders (e.g., telephone calls, text messages and diaries), and also preferred face-to-face sessions particularly for the initial sessions with the physiotherapist (Table 1).

Four of the five studies used theoretical models of behaviour change (i.e., TDF, BCW, Health Action Process Approach) [42–45] and three of the five studies listed the specific BCTs [43–45] which allowed for direct comparison with our study. We had 21 BCTs in our study, which was within the range of 17 to 34 BCTs reported in the three studies [43–45]. Most of our BCTs (86%; 18/21) were included in at least one of the three studies [43–45], except “2.6 Biofeedback”, “2.7 Feedback on outcome(s) of behaviour” and “12.5 Adding objects to the environment”. These BCTs were unique to our study and belonged to the optional add-ons of using a fitness tracker and prescribing arm and leg straps. Not all stroke survivors found these options useful and hence they were optional add-ons and not core options (Table 3). There was certainly value in doing this co-design work as the options/solutions selected were specific to what stroke survivors in Singapore deemed useful in improving their PA. It remains uncertain whether the number, type and certain combination of BCTs increase the likelihood of improving PA levels after stroke as the current evidence in stroke is limited and considered to be at high or unclear risk of bias [20]. In systematic reviews of trials evaluating behaviour change techniques in PA interventions for older adults and people with physical disabilities [47, 48], interventions that used theory or included one or more of the following BCTs (“1.2 Problem-solving”, “1.4 Action planning”, “2.3 Self-monitoring of behaviour”, “4.1 Instruction on how to perform behaviour”, “6.1 Demonstration of the behaviour”, “8.1 Behaviour practice/rehearsal” and “8.7 Graded tasks”) were shown to have larger effects on PA than interventions that did not. This was promising as our complex intervention was based on the TDF and BCW and included all of the aforementioned BCTs in our complex intervention. Whether our complex intervention (and combination of BCTs) is effective in improving PA after stroke remains to be tested in our type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial.

Ideally, a feasibility or pilot study should be done prior to a randomised controlled trial to assess the feasibility of running the trial successfully and highlighting any issues with the conduct of the trial in terms of recruitment, data collection and execution of intervention [30, 49]. We proceeded with a type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial instead for pragmatic reasons associated with grant funding, resource optimization and time efficiency. Grant funders typically fund trials and do not fund feasibility or pilot studies (small-scale version of the trial) [30, 49–51]. To ensure sufficient funding for our work, we needed to move to the stage of running the full-fledged trial. Although our prior development work put us in good stead of understanding the needs of stroke survivors and viewpoints of service providers (which increase the likelihood of an effective intervention), we might not be privy to the barriers and facilitators to implementation of the intervention. This can be addressed by the type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial design as it has a dual focus with its primary aim to determine effectiveness of an intervention, and its secondary aim to understand potential barriers and facilitators to implementation of the intervention through process evaluation [52, 53]. In doing so, research resources can be optimised and the time taken for research to translate to clinical practice can be shortened.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include the use of a rigorous and comprehensive approach in developing a complex PA intervention that is personalised to the specific needs and preferences of stroke survivors. Our methods of using theory and a co-design approach align with recommendations made by the World Health Organisation, the Medical Research Council and the Stroke Recovery and Rehabilitation roundtable [13, 26, 27, 30] when developing complex interventions in the areas of PA and stroke. We involved 47 stroke survivors, 4 caregivers, 71 physiotherapists, 35 exercise professionals and 8 experts in our development process. This gave us rich and in-depth perspectives on the problem of physical inactivity, ensuring a high likelihood of an effective and implementable intervention that is acceptable to stroke survivors and healthcare professionals. The most novel aspect of our study is the personalisation of the complex intervention in the form of optional add-ons (Table 3). These factors add to the robustness of an intervention that is personalised to the individual.

Despite the strengths of our study, we acknowledged three limitations. First, physiotherapists and other healthcare professionals were not included in our co-design workshops with stroke survivors. We chose to do this as a power imbalance often exists between people who are receiving healthcare services and people who are delivering healthcare services [46], and we wanted stroke survivors and caregivers to share freely during the sessions. However, in doing so, we might have missed out opportunities to make collaborative decisions with all stakeholders on each aspect of MOTIVATE. Second, we had not sought the perspectives of general practitioners in our development process. As part of Healthier SG (a national initiative by Singapore’s Ministry of Health to transform the country’s healthcare system by focusing on a proactive preventive approach), Singapore residents are encouraged to enrol with a family doctor/general practitioner for personalised care [54, 55]. Residents can develop an individualized health plan together with their general practitioner, and also gain access to free health screenings and vaccinations, and subsidies for chronic disease management [54, 55]. It is therefore likely that general practitioners will play a big role in promoting PA to stroke survivors in their community-based clinics, and future work can focus on gaining their perspectives to promotion of PA after stroke. Third, in terms of study design and reporting, we used GRIPP2 as the reporting guidelines, in the absence of specific reporting guidelines for co-design studies. Co-design is a type of participatory action research and while there are overarching elements of patients being co-researchers, it is not the same level of engagement as Public Patient Involvement methodology. While new reporting guidelines for co-design methodology are currently being developed, they were not ready for use at the time of writing this manuscript [56, 57].

Conclusions

Using behaviour change theory and a co-design approach involving multiple stakeholders, a complex intervention consisting of 21 BCTs, 6 intervention functions and 8 options was developed to target physical inactivity after stroke. The intervention titled MOTIVATE is currently being tested in a type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial.
