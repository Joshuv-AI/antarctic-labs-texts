Carbon Dots–Functionalized Biosilica from Bamboo Leaves for Adsorption and Optical Detection of Cu(II) Ions
Authors: Nuttapoj Uahchinkul, Kittirat Phooplub, Chittanon Buranachai, Kriangsak Songsrirote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biosilica extracted from bamboo leaves was functionalized with nitrogen-doped carbon dots to produce a CDs/Si composite and evaluated as both an adsorbent and an optical probe for Cu­(II) ion adsorption and detection. The development of multifunctional and sustainable materials for simultaneous Cu­(II) remediation and monitoring remains an important challenge, as many reported carbon-dot systems are used as dispersed nanoparticles that are difficult to recover after use. The integration of bamboo leaf-derived biosilica with nitrogen-doped carbon dots provides a recoverable biomass-derived platform that combines adsorption and optical sensing functionalities within a single material. The adsorption performance of CDs/Si was optimized, revealing an optimum pH of 5 and a contact time of 60 min, with a maximum Cu­(II) adsorption capacity of 15.15 mg/g. The adsorption behavior was well described by both Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models, while the adsorption kinetics followed a pseudo-second-order model, indicating that chemisorption was the dominant adsorption mechanism. The CDs/Si composite exhibited distinctive optical properties, with strong fluorescence emission at 550.0 nm upon excitation at 497.5 nm. A linear relationship between Cu­(II) ion concentration and fluorescence quenching was obtained according to the Stern–Volmer equation. In addition, smartphone-based colorimetric detection using the PhotoMetrix PRO application enabled both visual and quantitative analysis of Cu­(II) ions. The fluorescence and smartphone-based approaches showed linear detection ranges of 0.1–50.0 mg/L and 0.2–50.0 mg/L, with limits of detection of 0.03 and 0.05 mg/L, respectively. The optical sensing methods were successfully validated by standard spiking experiments in real water samples, showing excellent accuracy and no statistically significant difference compared with flame atomic absorption spectrometry. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses provided insight into the Cu­(II) binding mechanisms on the CDs/Si surface. Overall, this study demonstrates that the developed CDs/Si composite is a sustainable, simple, and high-performance analytical platform for Cu­(II) ion monitoring and remediation of water.

Introduction

With increasing concern about sustainable development for future generations, agricultural residues and biomass have been identified as promising raw materials for the production of high-value products. At the same time, green chemistry and green technologies that aim to reduce waste and protect the environment, while minimizing adverse environmental impacts, have become major driving forces in scientific and technological innovation. Recently, considerable attention has been devoted to the development of sustainable biobased materials for the removal and detection of toxic metal ions. Biopolymers such as cellulose, chitosan, alginate, and lignin have been extensively investigated for metal ion adsorption and sensing. Their biodegradability, abundance, and rich surface functionalities make them attractive materials for environmental applications. , Various biopolymer-based composites have demonstrated promising performance for heavy metal adsorption, separation, and sensing applications. However, limitations such as mechanical stability, long-term durability, and multifunctional integration still restrict their practical use. Therefore, the development of biomass-derived materials with improved structural robustness and multifunctionality remains an active area of research. A high silica (SiO2) content has been reported in various agricultural wastes, including rice and corn husks, , sugar cane bagasse, and bamboo leaves. , These crops are capable of converting monosilicic acid from soil into silica through an oxolation process. As a result, SiO2 has been widely used in numerous applications, such as chemical and moisture adsorbents, , catalyst supports, pharmaceuticals and medicine, , electronics, and composite materials.

At present, industrial silica production predominantly relies on sandstone, a nonrenewable raw material that requires substantial energy input and poses significant environmental concerns. Moreover, silica obtained from mineral sources is typically crystalline and has been classified as a Group 1 carcinogenic agent by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). Occupational exposure to crystalline silica dust can cause silicosis, a serious lung disease. In contrast, amorphous silica derived from agricultural residues is considered nontoxic. Furthermore, amorphous silica has been reported to exhibit higher reactivity than crystalline silica because of its abundant surface hydroxyl or silanol (Si–OH) groups, which facilitate strong interactions with other substances. Owing to these advantages, biosilica obtained from biomass, a renewable and sustainable resource, shows great potential as an economical raw material. Among various agricultural wastes, bamboo leaves have received relatively limited attention for material applications. Nevertheless, they represent an excellent source of biosilica, as the ash content of bamboo leaves is approximately 25.86 wt % with a silica content exceeding 90 wt % in the ash. , In comparison, rice husk contains about 16.1 wt % ash with a silica content of around 90 wt %, while sugar cane bagasse exhibits an ash content of 8–10 wt % with approximately 54.0 wt % silica in the ash. ,

One of the well-known applications of silica is as an adsorbent, owing to its porous structure and the abundance of silanol groups that enable effective adsorption of various substances, including toxic contaminants, from water and soil. , Surface functionalization and modification of silica have been shown to significantly enhance its selectivity and adsorption performance compared with unmodified silica. Carbon dots (CDs) are carbon-based nanoparticles characterized by distinctive optical and electronic properties as well as excellent biocompatibility. CDs have attracted considerable attention for metal ion adsorption and optical detection because of their tunable fluorescence, abundant surface functional groups, low toxicity, and facile synthesis. , Although CDs provide a high surface area and excellent analytical performance, they are difficult to separate and recover after use. This limitation restricts their reusability and practical implementation on a larger scale. Therefore, the development of immobilized CD-based materials that retain the functional advantages of CDs while improving handling and recovery remains an important challenge. Previous studies have reported the immobilization of CDs onto silica surfaces to improve the adsorption efficiency of dyes, heavy metals, gases, food additives, and drugs. Despite the promising performance of previously reported CDs–silica composites, several challenges remain. Most reported systems employ silica derived from synthetic precursors such as tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) or commercial silica, which increases production costs and limits sustainability. In addition, many carbon-dot-based adsorption and sensing systems rely on freely dispersed nanoparticles that are difficult to recover from aqueous media after use, limiting their practical application and reusability. Numerous studies have investigated either adsorption or sensing applications. However, sustainable biomass-derived materials capable of simultaneously providing efficient metal ion adsorption and optical detection remain limited. Consequently, the fabrication of multifunctional, recoverable, and environmentally sustainable CD-based composites represents an important research need. Bamboo leaf-derived biosilica represents an attractive alternative because it is a renewable agricultural waste resource with a high silica content and abundant surface silanol groups. Compared with synthetic silica supports, biosilica offers improved sustainability while maintaining excellent surface reactivity. Moreover, the incorporation of nitrogen-doped carbon dots introduces fluorescence properties and additional metal-binding functionalities, enabling simultaneous adsorption and optical detection of metal ions. The immobilization of CDs onto the biosilica matrix also facilitates material handling and recovery compared with dispersed CD systems.

In this work, a carbon dots–functionalized biosilica composite (CDs/Si) was fabricated using silica extracted from bamboo leaves and nitrogen-doped carbon dots. This study aimed to develop sustainable dual-function material capable of Cu­(II) adsorption and optical detection. The optical detection strategy integrates both fluorescence sensing and smartphone-assisted colorimetric analysis within a single material platform. The adsorption behavior, photoluminescence characteristics, sensing performance, and interaction mechanisms toward Cu­(II) ions were systematically investigated. The developed CDs/Si composite provides a multifunctional platform for water-quality monitoring and remediation while promoting the valorization of agricultural waste through the production of high-value functional materials.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of the Synthesized CDs/Si

Biosilica was successfully extracted from bamboo leaves with a yield of 18.4 wt %. The surface morphology and topography of the extracted silica were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Figure (A). The biosilica particles exhibited a nonuniform size distribution with irregular shapes and rough surfaces, which are typical characteristics of amorphous silica derived from biomass sources. Such surface roughness is advantageous for subsequent surface functionalization and adsorption applications. The elemental composition of the extracted silica was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and the corresponding spectrum is presented in Figure (B). The results revealed that the silica particles consisted predominantly of oxygen and silicon, accounting for 47.5 and 43.6 wt %, respectively. A minor carbon signal (8.9 wt %) was also detected, which was attributed to residual organic matter originating from the bamboo leaves.

SEM images and EDX spectra of biosilica (A–B) and CDs/Si (C–D). Inset in (C): HRTEM image of the synthesized N-doped CDs.

After grafting N-doped CDs onto the silica surface via APTMS functionalization, a visible color change from white to light brown was observed, indicating successful modification. The SEM image of CDs/Si (Figure (C)) revealed a markedly altered surface morphology compared with that of the pristine silica. The surface of CDs/Si appeared more compact and textured, suggesting effective coverage of the silica surface by the immobilized CDs. To confirm the successful synthesis of the CDs, the inset of Figure (C) shows a high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) image of the synthesized CDs. Clusters of CDs with well-defined lattice fringes were observed, exhibiting an interplanar spacing of 0.21 nm, which corresponds to the (100) plane of graphitic carbon. This result confirms the formation of partially graphitic N-doped CDs. EDX analysis of CDs/Si (Figure (D)) further confirmed the successful immobilization of N-doped CDs on the silica surface. The mass percentages of carbon and nitrogen increased substantially to 37.9 and 14.5 wt %, respectively, providing strong evidence for the presence of nitrogen-containing carbonaceous species.

The XRD patterns of pristine biosilica and CDs/Si (Figure S1) exhibited a broad diffraction halo centered at approximately 22–24°, confirming the predominantly amorphous nature of the silica matrix. No distinct diffraction peaks attributable to crystalline carbon were observed after CD immobilization, indicating that the grafting process did not alter the amorphous structure of the silica support. The absence of distinct crystalline carbon reflections is attributed to the small size and low degree of graphitization of the CDs, resulting in broad diffraction features that overlap with the amorphous silica background. The graphitic domains were nevertheless confirmed by HRTEM analysis. These results collectively demonstrate the successful fabrication of CDs-functionalized biosilica.

The photoluminescence behavior of CDs/Si was investigated to evaluate its optical properties. Figure (A) shows the fluorescence emission spectra of CDs/Si upon excitation at wavelengths ranging from 350 to 510 nm. Upon excitation at shorter wavelengths, a broad emission band spanning the blue to green region was observed, indicating the coexistence of multiple emissive states. This excitation-dependent emission behavior is characteristic of carbon dots and is commonly attributed to the coexistence of different surface states and emissive centers. The favorable optical properties of the synthesized CDs may also be associated with the use of DMF during synthesis. Owing to its high boiling point and strong polarity, DMF facilitates the dehydration and carbonization processes involved in carbon-dot formation. Moreover, DMF has been reported to promote the development of conjugated sp2 carbon domains and assist nitrogen incorporation, resulting in enhanced fluorescence properties of nitrogen-doped carbon dots. In the present system, the blue emission is likely associated with the silica matrix, whereas the green emission originates primarily from the surface states of the N-doped CDs. Interestingly, when the excitation wavelengths exceeded 490 nm, an up-conversion luminescence phenomenon was observed, in which the emission wavelength was shorter than the excitation wavelength. The corresponding up-conversion luminescence spectra are presented in the inset of Figure (A). A strong emission centered at 469 nm was consistently observed when excited between 495 and 510 nm. Although the emission intensity varied with excitation wavelength, the emission peak position remained nearly unchanged, suggesting the presence of a stable emissive center within the CDs/Si composite.

(A) Fluorescence emission spectra of the CDs/Si composite recorded at excitation wavelengths ranging from 350 to 510 nm. Inset: Up-conversion luminescence spectra. (B) FTIR spectra of biosilica (Si) and CDs/Si.

To further investigate the surface chemistry of CDs/Si, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed. The FTIR spectra of pristine biosilica (Si) and CDs/Si are compared in Figure (B). The extracted biosilica exhibited a strong absorption band at 1068.86 cm–1, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si bonds. A broad band in the range of 3000–3500 cm–1 was assigned to O–H stretching vibrations of surface silanol groups. After functionalization with CDs, the Si–O–Si band shifted to 1041.82 cm–1 and overlapped with C–O-related vibrations, indicating interactions between the silica surface and the grafted carbon dots. Additional absorption bands at 1495.77 and 1654.81 cm–1 were assigned to aromatic CC stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of graphitic domains in the CDs. The vibration band at 1570.72 cm–1 was attributed to the −CONH– group, indicating the incorporation of nitrogen-containing amide functionalities during CD synthesis. Furthermore, the C–H stretching vibrations at 2949.78 cm–1, together with a broad N–H/O–H absorption band, support the successful incorporation of N-doped CDs on the silica surface. Collectively, the FTIR results, together with the SEM, EDX, and HRTEM analyses, confirm the successful functionalization of bamboo leaf-derived biosilica with N-doped carbon dots, providing abundant surface functional groups that are beneficial for both Cu­(II) adsorption and fluorescence-based detection.

Activity and Optimum Conditions of CDs/Si for Metal Ion Adsorption

The selectivity of the CDs/Si composite toward different metal ions was preliminarily evaluated by monitoring changes in fluorescence intensity after exposure to different metal ion solutions (50 mg/L, 50.0 mL). After 30 min of contact, the CDs/Si particles were separated by filtration, oven-dried, coated onto filter paper, and subsequently analyzed by fluorescence spectroscopy. An excitation wavelength of 490.0 nm was selected for all measurements, as it provided strong fluorescence intensity with a symmetrical emission peak centered at 550.0 nm, as shown in Figure (A). Compared with the reagent blank, CDs/Si exposed to Cu­(II) ions exhibited a pronounced decrease in fluorescence intensity (data not shown), accompanied by a distinct color change from light brown to green. In contrast, other tested metal ions produced negligible fluorescence quenching. These observations indicate a strong and selective interaction between Cu­(II) ions and the CDs/Si composite. Therefore, Cu­(II) ions were selected as the target analyte for subsequent adsorption efficiency and optical detection studies.

The adsorption conditions, including solution pH and contact time, were optimized to achieve high removal efficiency. In the pH optimization study, 20 mg of CDs/Si was immersed in 50.0 mL of a 50 mg/L Cu­(II) ion solution with pH values ranging from 3 to 8, adjusted using Britton–Robinson buffer. Adsorption performance was evaluated in terms of removal efficiency (R m%), calculated using eq . As shown in Figure (A), Rm % increased steadily with increasing pH. Although the removal efficiency continued to increase at pH 6–8, visible precipitation of Cu­(OH)2 was also observed under these conditions. Therefore, pH 5 was selected as the optimum pH, because it provided high removal efficiency (51.2%) while avoiding interference from copper hydroxide precipitation. The enhanced adsorption at higher pH values can be attributed to the deprotonation of oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups on the CDs/Si surface, resulting in increased availability of coordination sites for Cu­(II) ions. To further elucidate the surface charge behavior of the adsorbent, the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of CDs/Si was determined. As shown in Figure (C), the pHpzc was found to be 8.7, indicating that the CDs/Si surface carries a net positive charge at pH values below 8.7.

Optimization of Cu­(II) adsorption by CDs/Si. Effect of (A) solution pH and (B) contact time on the removal efficiency of 50 mg/L Cu­(II). (C) Determination of the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of the CDs/Si composite.

Although the adsorption experiments were performed at pH 5, which is below the pHpzc (8.7), substantial Cu­(II) removal was still achieved. This observation suggests that electrostatic attraction is not the dominant adsorption mechanism. Instead, Cu­(II) adsorption is likely governed primarily by specific surface complexation (inner-sphere complexation) involving hydroxyl, carboxyl, amide, and silanol groups on the CDs/Si surface. These interactions occur through ligand exchange with surface hydroxyl groups and are characteristic of chemisorption rather than simple electrostatic adsorption. The effect of contact time on Cu­(II) adsorption was also investigated, as illustrated in Figure (B). The removal efficiency increased rapidly with time and reached equilibrium after approximately 60 min, corresponding to a removal efficiency of about 82.4%. A slight decrease in removal efficiency was observed upon further increasing the contact times. The reduction in adsorption efficiency at prolonged contact times may be attributed to partial desorption resulting from adsorption–desorption equilibrium or the partial destabilization of surface Cu­(II) complexes under continuous agitation. Based on these results, a contact time of 60 min was selected as the optimum condition for subsequent experiments.

Adsorption Isotherms and Kinetics of Cu­(II) Ions onto CDs/Si

To investigate the equilibrium adsorption behavior of Cu­(II) ions on CDs/Si, adsorption isotherm analyses were conducted under the optimized pH and contact time conditions. The equilibrium adsorption data were analyzed using the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models to evaluate the adsorption characteristics of the composite. The corresponding linearized plots are presented in Figures S2 and S3, and the calculated isotherm parameters are summarized in Table . The Langmuir isotherm model describes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface with finite adsorption sites, whereas the Freundlich model assumes adsorption on a heterogeneous surface with multilayer formation. The experimental adsorption data were well described by both models, as indicated by comparable correlation coefficients (R 2). This finding suggests that Cu­(II) adsorption on the CDs/Si composite involves heterogeneous adsorption sites while approaching monolayer coverage at high adsorbate loading. According to the Langmuir isotherm, the maximum adsorption capacity (q m) for Cu­(II) ions was 15.15 mg/g. The corresponding Langmuir constant (k L) of 0.259 L/mg indicates a favorable interaction between Cu­(II) ions and the active adsorption sites of the composite. The Freundlich constant (n) value for Cu­(II) ion adsorption was 2.571, indicating favorable adsorption on a heterogeneous surface. In addition, the k f value reflects the adsorption affinity of the composite, which can be attributed to the abundant oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups introduced by the immobilized CDs. The good agreement with both Langmuir and Freundlich models indicates that the CDs/Si surface contains adsorption sites with different affinities toward Cu­(II) ions while exhibiting a finite adsorption capacity.

Adsorption kinetics were analyzed to further investigate the rate and mechanism of Cu­(II) ion uptake by CDs/Si. As discussed in Section , the adsorption efficiency increased with increasing contact time and reached equilibrium within 60 min. The kinetic data were evaluated using the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models, which are commonly used to describe adsorption rate behavior and to provide insight into the possible adsorption mechanism. The corresponding linearized plots are shown in Figures S4 and S5, and the fitted parameters are summarized in Table . The correlation coefficients (R 2) indicate that the pseudo-second-order model provided a better fit to the experimental data than the pseudo-first-order model. Moreover, the equilibrium adsorption capacity calculated from the pseudo-second-order model (16.64 mg/g) was in excellent agreement with the experimental value (16.71 mg/g). These results suggest that the adsorption process is predominantly governed by chemisorption, in which Cu­(II) ions interact strongly with active functional groups on the CDs/Si surface through electron sharing or electron transfer. By contrast, the pseudo-first-order model yielded a lower correlation coefficient and a calculated equilibrium adsorption capacity (8.08 mg/g) that deviated considerably from the experimental value. This poorer agreement indicates that physisorption alone cannot adequately describe the adsorption behavior of Cu­(II) ions on CDs/Si. Overall, the adsorption isotherm and kinetic analyses indicate that Cu­(II) adsorption on CDs/Si involves heterogeneous adsorption sites and is dominated by chemisorption. This interpretation is further supported by the pHpzc results, the FTIR analysis, and the fluorescence quenching behavior observed after Cu­(II) binding.

Interaction between Cu­(II) Ions and Binding Sites of CDs/Si

To investigate the surface chemical composition of CDs/Si and identify the functional groups involved in Cu­(II) ion adsorption, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed before and after Cu­(II) adsorption. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum of pristine CDs/Si (Figure (A)) exhibits four characteristic binding energy (B.E.) components at 285.000, 286.322, 287.929, and 288.982 eV, which are assigned to C–C/C–H, C–O/C–N, OC–N, and OC–O functional groups, respectively. These signals confirm the successful immobilization of carbon dots onto the silica surface extracted from bamboo leaves. The N 1s spectrum of CDs/Si (Figure (B)) reveals three dominant peaks at 399.093, 400.287, and 401.403 eV, corresponding to pyridinic N, pyrrolic N/OC–N, and graphitic N species, respectively. A weak peak at higher binding energy (406.952 eV) was also observed, which can be attributed to highly oxidized nitrogen species, possibly originating from trace nitrate contamination. The O 1s spectrum (Figure (C)) shows three components at 531.222, 532.351, and 533.789 eV, assigned to N–CO, CO/C–O, and Si–O bonds, respectively. In addition, the Si 2p spectrum (Figure (D)) exhibits two characteristic peaks at 102.485 and 103.332 eV, corresponding to Si–O and SiO2 species within the silica network.

XPS spectra of C 1s (A, E), N 1s (B, F), O 1s (C, G), and Si 2p (D, H) of CDs/Si before and after Cu­(II) ion adsorption, Cu 2p spectrum (I), and schematic illustration of nitrogen configurations in N-doped CDs (J).

After Cu­(II) ion adsorption, notable changes in binding energies and peak intensities were observed, indicating strong interactions between Cu­(II) ions and surface functional groups. The C 1s spectrum after adsorption (Figure (E)) still displays peaks corresponding to the same carbon functionalities; however, the binding energies associated with OC–N and OC–O groups shifted negatively by approximately 0.90 and 0.45 eV, respectively. These shifts suggest changes in the local electronic environment due to coordination interactions with Cu­(II) ions. More pronounced changes were observed in the N 1s spectrum after Cu­(II) adsorption (Figure (F)). The peaks at 399.872, 401.035, and 402.198 eV shifted positively by approximately 0.75–0.80 eV compared to the pristine CDs/Si, indicating electron donation from nitrogen-containing functional groups to Cu­(II) ions. This positive shift strongly supports the involvement of nitrogen species in coordination-driven chemisorption processes. In contrast, the O 1s peaks (Figure (G)) showed only minor shifts (<0.25 eV), suggesting that oxygen-containing groups contribute mainly through weak interactions or secondary coordination rather than serving as primary binding sites. For the Si 2p spectrum (Figure (H)), the original Si–O and SiO2 peaks slightly shifted to lower binding energies (102.280 and 103.107 eV, respectively) after Cu­(II) adsorption. Additionally, new peaks emerged at higher binding energies of 104.036 and 105.079 eV, which are attributed to chemisorbed species formed on the silica surface and highly oxidized Si4+ species, respectively. These observations suggest that Cu­(II) ions not only interact with the surface of CDs but can also diffuse into the porous silica matrix and induce changes in the silicon oxidation environment.

The presence and chemical state of Cu species adsorbed on CDs/Si were further confirmed by the Cu 2p spectrum (Figure (I)). The doublet peaks at binding energies of 933.127/953.316 eV are characteristic of CuO species, while additional peaks at 934.741/954.798 eV and 936.058/956.082 eV correspond to octahedral and tetrahedral Cu­(II) coordination complexes, respectively. These multiple coordination environments reflect the heterogeneous nature of the adsorption sites on CDs/Si. Based on the XPS results, Cu­(II) ion adsorption onto CDs/Si proceeds predominantly through coordination interactions with nitrogen-containing functional groups of the CDs, as well as partial incorporation into the silica framework. Among the nitrogen species, pyridinic and pyrrolic N play dominant roles due to their available lone pairs, which can strongly coordinate with electron-deficient Cu­(II) ions. This is supported by the substantial increase in the relative area of pyridinic N from 25.9% to 61.6% after Cu­(II) adsorption, accompanied by a decrease in graphitic N, from 39.3% to 15.6%. The lower apparent involvement of graphitic N is consistent with its electronic structure, in which the lone-pair electrons are delocalized within the graphitic carbon framework and are therefore less available for direct coordination with Cu­(II) ions. These changes suggest a redistribution of electron density and possible structural transformation induced by Cu­(II) complexation within the carbon-dot framework. In contrast, pyrrolic N exhibited only minor changes in peak area, indicating a less pronounced role. Although graphitic N does not possess readily available lone pairs for direct coordination, its noticeable positive binding energy shift may arise from indirect electronic effects or water-assisted interactions, as previously reported. Overall, the XPS results are consistent with the adsorption mechanism proposed from the adsorption isotherm, kinetic, pHpzc, FTIR, and fluorescence studies. Together, these findings indicate that Cu­(II) adsorption on CDs/Si is dominated by coordination-driven chemisorption at heterogeneous nitrogen- and oxygen-containing surface sites, while the porous silica framework also contributes to Cu­(II) uptake. Optical Detection of Cu­(II) Ions Using a Smartphone and a Spectrofluorometer

The distinct color change of CDs/Si from light brown to dark green following Cu­(II) ion adsorption, which was clearly observable with the naked eye during adsorption studies, motivated the development of a smartphone-based colorimetric detection approach. After adsorption, the Cu­(II)-loaded CDs/Si particles were coated onto paper strips and imaged for color analysis using the PhotoMetrix PRO application, as illustrated in Figure (A). The effect of solution pH on optical detection performance was first investigated. The results demonstrated that pH 5, identical to the optimum pH for Cu­(II) adsorption, produced the highest fluorescence quenching response (F 0/F), as shown in Figure S5. This observation further confirms that the adsorption and optical sensing processes are governed by the same interaction mechanisms between Cu­(II) ions and the surface functional groups of the CDs/Si composite.

(A) Workflow for smartphone-based colorimetric determination of Cu­(II) ions using PhotoMetrix PRO and CDs/Si-coated paper strips; (B) calibration curve relating the smartphone colorimetric response to Cu­(II) ion concentration; and (C) effect of coexisting metal ions (1.0 mg/L each) on the smartphone-based colorimetric response toward 1.0 mg/L Cu­(II).

Using the multichannel univariate analysis feature of the PhotoMetrix PRO application, a good linear correlation between Cu­(II) ion concentration and the colorimetric signal was obtained over the concentration range of 0.2–50.0 mg/L, with a regression coefficient (R 2 ) of 0.9941 (Figure (B)). The limit of detection (LOD) and limit of quantification (LOQ) for smartphone-based colorimetric detection were calculated according to the 3.3σ/S and 10σ/S, respectively, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank signal and S is the slope of the calibration curve. The resulting LOD and LOQ values were 0.05 mg/L and 0.18 mg/L, respectively, indicating satisfactory sensitivity for practical water monitoring applications. To evaluate the selectivity of the smartphone-based colorimetric sensing system, interference studies were carried out by measuring the detection signal of Cu­(II) ions in the presence of various coexisting metal ions. As shown in Figure (C), the presence of interfering cations at a concentration of 1.0 mg/L caused signal variations of less than 5% relative to that of Cu­(II) ions alone (indicated by the red dashed line). These results demonstrate that the smartphone-based colorimetric approach exhibits good tolerance toward common coexisting metal ions, confirming its suitability for Cu­(II) ion determination in complex aqueous matrices.

In parallel, quantitative detection of Cu­(II) ions using a spectrofluorometer was investigated by exploiting the intrinsic fluorescence of CDs immobilized on the silica surface. The excitation wavelength of 497.5 nm was selected because it produced the maximum fluorescence intensity at 550.0 nm. Although an up-conversion luminescence signal at approximately 460.0 nm was also observed (Figure (A)), its response showed poor linearity with Cu­(II) ion concentration and was therefore unsuitable for quantitative analysis. Consequently, fluorescence detection was performed at an emission wavelength of 550.0 nm. The fluorescence emission spectra of CDs/Si after exposure to Cu­(II) ion concentrations ranging from 0.0 to 50.0 mg/L are presented in Figure (B). A progressive decrease in fluorescence intensity with increasing Cu­(II) ion concentration was observed, demonstrating efficient fluorescence quenching by Cu­(II) ions. The corresponding Stern–Volmer plot exhibited excellent linearity over the concentration range of 0.1–50.0 mg/L, with an R 2 value of 0.9957 (Figure (C)), confirming that the fluorescence quenching was concentration-dependent. The calculated LOD and LOQ values for fluorescence-based detection were 0.03 mg/L and 0.09 mg/L, respectively, indicating higher analytical sensitivity than the smartphone-based colorimetric approach. The influence of coexisting metal ions on fluorescence detection was also evaluated. While fluorescence detection provided higher sensitivity, slight signal variations were observed in the presence of several coexisting ions. Specifically, Fe­(II), Hg­(II), Na­(I), and K­(I) (each at 1.0 mg/L) increased the fluorescence response by more than 5% relative to that of Cu­(II) ions alone (red dashed line in Figure (D)). These results suggest that certain coexisting ions can moderately affect the fluorescence response, possibly through competitive interactions with the surface functional groups of the CDs/Si composite.

(A) Excitation and fluorescence emission spectra of CDs/Si; (B) fluorescence emission spectra of CDs/Si after exposure to different Cu­(II) ion concentrations; (C) Stern–Volmer calibration curve for fluorescence quenching by Cu­(II) ions; and (D) effect of coexisting metal ions (1.0 mg/L each) on the fluorescence response toward 1.0 mg/L Cu­(II).

Nevertheless, both detection approaches exhibited sufficient sensitivity for practical Cu­(II) ion determination in water samples. Notably, the limits of detection obtained by both methods are substantially lower than the maximum allowable Cu­(II) concentrations in drinking water established by the World Health Organization (2.0 mg/L) and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (1.3 mg/L). Therefore, the proposed dual-mode sensing strategy remains highly applicable. Overall, the combination of the high sensitivity of fluorescence detection with the simplicity, portability, and robustness of smartphone-based colorimetry demonstrates the complementary advantages of the developed CDs/Si composite for practical water-quality monitoring.

The optical properties of CDs/Si, including both color and fluorescence, changed noticeably after Cu­(II) ion adsorption. In aqueous solutions at sufficiently high concentrations, Cu­(II) ions typically exhibit a blue color due to the formation of octahedral [Cu­(H2O)6]2+ complexes. Interestingly, the originally light brown CDs/Si particles turned green after Cu­(II) ion adsorption. This color transformation can be explained by ligand field theory and changes in the coordination environment of Cu­(II) ions upon complex formation with surface functional groups. The observed color shift from pale blue (in aqueous solution) to green (after adsorption onto CDs/Si) indicates a decrease in the crystal-field splitting energy of the Cu­(II) d-orbitals, resulting from changes in ligand identity, coordination geometry, or coordination number. Although the Cu­(II) ion concentrations employed in this study were relatively low and appeared colorless in bulk solution, the ions were effectively adsorbed and concentrated on the CDs/Si surface through coordination interactions. The resulting localized accumulation of Cu­(II) ions produced a visible color change, enabling simple colorimetric detection.

In addition to the colorimetric response, Cu­(II) ion adsorption induced pronounced fluorescence quenching of CDs/Si. The quenching behavior followed the Stern–Volmer relationship, indicating strong interactions between Cu­(II) ions and the carbon dots immobilized on the silica surface. This behavior is attributed to surface coordination and electron transfer processes. The fluorescence quenching mechanism is consistent with static quenching, in which Cu­(II) ions form nonfluorescent ground-state complexes with electron-rich functional groups, particularly nitrogen-containing moieties, on the CDs. These coordination interactions facilitate photoinduced electron transfer (PET) from the excited CDs to Cu­(II) ions, suppressing radiative recombination and resulting in fluorescence quenching without an obvious shift in the emission wavelength, a characteristic feature of static quenching. Previous studies have shown that nitrogen doping in carbon dots introduces electron-rich active sites that enhance radiative recombination efficiency while suppressing nonradiative decay pathways. Consequently, nitrogen-doped carbon dots (N-CDs) have been widely employed as fluorescent probes for metal ion detection, as metal ions readily form coordination complexes with surface functional groups such as carboxyl, amino, and hydroxyl groups, leading to fluorescence quenching. Among various metal ions, Cu­(II) is recognized as one of the most effective quenchers of N-CD fluorescence due to its strong electron-accepting ability and high propensity to promote electron transfer processes. ,

Overall, the combined colorimetric and fluorescence responses originate from the strong coordination of Cu­(II) ions with the nitrogen- and oxygen-containing functional groups of CDs/Si, highlighting the potential of this multifunctional composite for practical Cu­(II) monitoring in aqueous systems.

Determination of Cu­(II) Ions in Water Samples and Method Validation

To evaluate the practical applicability of the proposed CDs/Si platform, Cu­(II) ions were determined in real water samples. The analysis also assessed the influence of sample matrices on the performance of the proposed optical detection methods. The concentrations of Cu­(II) ions in drinking water and groundwater, together with the recoveries obtained after spiking the samples with 0.50, 2.50, and 5.00 mg/L Cu­(II), were determined using the proposed smartphone-based colorimetric and fluorescence methods and validated against flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS). The analytical results are summarized in Table . No detectable Cu­(II) was found in either drinking water or groundwater using both proposed methods or FAAS. The results obtained using smartphone-based colorimetry and fluorescence spectrometry were in good agreement with those obtained by FAAS, with no statistically significant differences observed (95% confidence level, p > 0.05). These findings confirm the reliability and accuracy of the CDs/Si-based detection methods for real water analysis. The recoveries ranged from 102.0–116.6% for the smartphone-based method and from 89.9–114.3% for fluorescence detection, falling within acceptable analytical criteria for trace metal analysis. This indicates that the adsorption and detection of Cu­(II) ions by CDs/Si are not significantly affected by common matrix components present in real water samples. Consequently, Cu­(II) ions adsorbed onto the CDs/Si composite can be quantitatively determined using both the smartphone-based colorimetric method with PhotoMetrix PRO and fluorescence-based detection, demonstrating the suitability of the proposed approach for practical environmental applications.

Biomass-derived adsorbents prepared from agricultural residues have been widely investigated for Cu­(II) removal because of their abundance, renewability, and diverse metal-binding functional groups. , Their adsorption performance depends largely on the chemical composition of the biomass and the surface modification strategy employed. Conclusion

Nitrogen-doped carbon dots were successfully immobilized onto bamboo leaf-derived amorphous silica to produce a sustainable multifunctional material capable of both Cu­(II) ion adsorption and optical detection. The synergistic combination of biosilica and surface-functionalized carbon dots provides abundant active sites that promote strong Cu­(II) coordination through surface complexation, as supported by spectroscopic evidence. The adsorption behavior is consistent with adsorption occurring on heterogeneous surface sites and being dominated by chemisorption. Importantly, Cu­(II) binding induces distinct color and fluorescence responses, enabling both smartphone-based colorimetric and fluorescence detection without the need for sophisticated instrumentation. The proposed CDs/Si platform showed reliable analytical performance in real water samples and demonstrated its potential as a recoverable material for simultaneous Cu­(II) removal and monitoring. Overall, the CDs/Si composite combines renewable biomass-derived silica with the unique optical properties of nitrogen-doped carbon dots, providing a simple, sustainable, and practical approach for water-quality monitoring and remediation.

Experimental Section

Materials

3-Aminopropyltrimethoxysilane (APTMS), citric acid, urea, and metal ion salts were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrochloric acid, sodium hydroxide, ethanol, and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were obtained from Fluka. Sulfuric acid was supplied by Fisher Chemicals. Dried bamboo leaves were collected from a bamboo farm in Roi Et Province, Thailand. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification. Deionized (DI) water was used throughout the experiments.

Procedure for the Synthesis of CDs/Si

Extraction of Silica from Bamboo Leaves

Silica was extracted from bamboo leaves using a modified method reported by Joshi et al. Briefly, dried bamboo leaves were rinsed thoroughly with DI water and air-dried, followed by grinding into fine particles. The powdered leaves were calcined in an electric muffle furnace at 850 °C for 5 h. The resulting ash was soaked in 10% (v/v) HCl­(aq) for 2 h. After filtration, the residue was washed repeatedly with DI water until neutral pH was achieved. The acid-treated residue was then dispersed in 5 M NaOH­(aq) and stirred continuously at 70 °C for 12 h to dissolve the silica. The mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. Silica was precipitated by the dropwise addition of 5 M H2SO4 under constant stirring. The precipitated SiO2 was washed thoroughly with DI water to remove excess sulfate ions and subsequently dried in an oven at 50 °C.

Synthesis of Carbon Dots (CDs)

Carbon dots were synthesized using a modified procedure reported by Phimmasone et al. In brief, 0.8 g of citric acid and 1.6 g of urea were dissolved in 20 mL of DMF. The solution was sonicated for 5 min and then subjected to microwave irradiation at 1000 W for 5 min under ambient atmospheric pressure. After heating, a black solid product was obtained and washed with three successive portions of 50.0 mL of 80% (v/v) ethanol to remove unreacted precursors and impurities. The purified product was redispersed in 20 mL of DMF and sonicated for an additional 5 min. Large particles were removed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant containing CDs was collected and stored at 4 °C for further use.

Synthesis of CDs/Si

Carbon dots-modified silica was prepared by mixing 15 mL of the CDs solution (prepared according to Section ) with 10 mL of APTMS and 25 mL of DMF under continuous stirring for 5 min. An excess amount of CDs suspension was employed to promote sufficient surface functionalization of the biosilica. Subsequently, 3.0 g of extracted SiO2 was added to the mixture and stirred at room temperature for 3 h. After reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting CDs/Si precipitate was filtered and washed thoroughly with DI water to remove excess reagents. The final product was dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h prior to characterization and application.

Adsorption Studies of Cu­(II) Ion onto CDs/Si

Optimization of Adsorption Conditions

Optimization experiments were performed to determine the appropriate adsorption conditions for Cu­(II) ions. Approximately 20 mg of CDs/Si was added to a beaker containing 50.0 mL of a 50 mg/L Cu­(II) ion solution and stirred at a constant speed of 250 rpm. The effects of key parameters on adsorption performance were investigated, including solution pH (ranging from 2 to 9, adjusted using Britton–Robinson buffer) and contact time (up to 180 min). After adsorption, the CDs/Si particles were separated by centrifugation, and the residual Cu­(II) ion concentration in the supernatant was quantified using a flame atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS, PerkinElmer AAnalyst 300). All adsorption experiments were conducted in triplicate. The adsorption performance, expressed as removal efficiency (R m, %), was calculated using eq 1 Rm%=(C0−Ce)C0×100 where C 0 and C e represent the initial and equilibrium concentrations (in mg/L) of Cu­(II) ions, respectively.

Characterization of CDs/Si: Physical, Chemical, and Optical Properties

The morphology of silica (SiO2) extracted from bamboo leaves and the CDs/Si composite was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JEOL JSM-6510LV, Japan). Prior to SEM analysis, the samples were dried under vacuum using a lyophilizer and subsequently coated with a thin platinum layer to enhance conductivity. Their crystal structure and phase composition were determined using X-ray Diffraction (XRD) technique. The lattice fringe of CDs attached on the surface of SiO2 was characterized using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (FEI Tecnai G2 20, 200 kV TEM/STEM/EDX/Tomography).

To elucidate the binding sites involved in Cu­(II) ion adsorption, the surface functional groups of CDs/Si were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) using an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode (Spectrum GX FTIR System, PerkinElmer, USA). FTIR spectra were recorded over the wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm–1 with a resolution of 2 cm–1, averaging 64 scans per sample. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to further investigate the interaction mechanisms between Cu­(II) ions and functional groups on the CDs/Si surface. XPS measurements were carried out using an AXIS Ultra DLD spectrometer equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). All binding energies were calibrated using the C 1s peak at 285.0 eV as an internal reference.

The optical properties of CDs/Si were evaluated by fluorescence spectroscopy using a spectrofluorometer (Shimadzu RF-5301PC). Fluorescence excitation and emission spectra were recorded, and changes in fluorescence intensity upon adsorption of metal ions were systematically monitored.

Optical Detection of Cu­(II) Ions: Fluorescence and Smartphone-Based Detection

Optimization of Optical Detection Conditions

Optimization experiments were performed to evaluate the fluorescence emission conditions for Cu­(II) ion detection using CDs/Si. The investigated parameters included solution pH in the range of 2–9 (adjusted using Britton–Robinson buffer) and potential interference from coexisting metal ions, namely Cd­(II), Cr­(VI), Hg­(II), Mn­(II), Ni­(II), Pb­(II), and Zn­(II). Approximately 20 mg of CDs/Si was added to a beaker containing 50.0 mL of a 50 mg/L Cu­(II) ion solution and stirred at 250 rpm. After adsorption, the CDs/Si particles were separated by centrifugation. The recovered particles were dried, and approximately 0.03 g of CDs/Si was uniformly coated onto a screen-printed filter paper with a diameter of 0.8 mm. The CDs/Si-coated paper strip was then mounted onto the sample holder of the spectrofluorometer for fluorescence intensity measurements. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Fluorescence quenching was quantified in terms of the quenching degree (Q f) using eq 6 Qf=F0F where F 0 and F represent the fluorescence intensities of CDs/Si in the reagent blank and in the presence of Cu­(II) ions, respectively.

Application of CDs/Si as an Optical Probe for Cu­(II) Ion Detection in Water Samples

The applicability of CDs/Si as an optical probe was evaluated using real water samples, including drinking water and groundwater with standard spiking of Cu­(II) ions under the optimized detection conditions. Prior to analysis, all water samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm nylon syringe filter to remove suspended particulates. Following the adsorption process, the CDs/Si particles were oven-dried and coated onto screen-printed paper strips for fluorescence intensity measurement and colorimetric analysis using the PhotoMetrix PRO smartphone application. The analytical results obtained from fluorescence and smartphone-based colorimetric detection were compared with those acquired using the conventional flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS) method.
