Beyond the Scale: Health-Related Quality of Life and Weight Loss in a 12-Month Digital Lifestyle Intervention
Authors: Aila J. Ahola, Anu Joki, Mikko S. Venäläinen, Sakris K.E. Kupila, Laura-Unnukka Suojanen, Kirsi H. Pietiläinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObesity is associated with reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL). We examined the associations between HRQoL and weight loss in a 12-month real-world digital lifestyle intervention, the Healthy Weight Coaching (HWC) program.MethodsAt baseline, participants self-reported their weight, height, and waist circumference, followed by weekly weight and quarterly waist tracking. HRQoL was assessed at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months using the RAND-36, which covers eight domains (physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health) where higher scores indicate better HRQoL.ResultsData from 1,848 participants (82.9% women, median age 52 years, median body mass index 39.1 kg/m2) showed that higher baseline body mass index correlated with lower HRQoL in physical functioning, bodily pain, general health, and social functioning. Using K-means clustering, we identified three distinct clusters: high HRQoL, physical health burden, and globally low HRQoL. Lower baseline HRQoL in vitality, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health, as well as membership in the globally low HRQoL cluster, predicted smaller weight loss. Participation in HWC was associated with improvements across all HRQoL domains. Each 5% weight loss corresponded to increases of 3.8 points in general health, 3.3 points in physical functioning, 2.3 points in vitality, 2.1 points in bodily pain, 1.2 points in social functioning, and 1 point in mental health. Participants who lost ≥5% of their weight experienced greater improvements in physical functioning and general health compared with those who lost <5%. Membership in the globally low HRQoL cluster and below-median baseline scores in role limitations due to physical health, general health, and vitality predicted higher dropout rate.ConclusionDigital lifestyle programs may improve both weight outcomes and the overall lived experience of individuals seeking weight management.

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health challenge that, beyond its well-documented links to increased morbidity and mortality [1], profoundly affects individual’s everyday life. It can limit mobility [2], increase physical discomfort [3], and contribute to psychological distress [4]. These multiple harmful effects substantially reduce health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [5], which refers to an individual’s perception of how their health influences daily functioning and well-being [6]. As a result, people with obesity typically report lower HRQoL compared to those with normal weight [7].

HRQoL is increasingly recognized as a key endpoint, and robust evidence on its responsiveness to weight loss could support decision-making around obesity prevention, treatment, and resource allocation [8, 9]. Indeed, weight loss has been associated with improvements in HRQoL, with the most pronounced benefits typically observed in the physical components [10, 11]. Even modest reductions in body weight can yield meaningful improvements in mobility, vitality, and the ability to perform daily tasks [12, 13]. However, most research in this field has been conducted within face-to-face treatment settings. With the growing demand for obesity care, digital interventions are increasingly being implemented, as they can be delivered at scale and at lower costs compared to traditional in-person programs. Evaluating the impact of the digital treatment modalities, beyond weight loss as the sole indicator of effectiveness, is needed to capture the full scope of the health and economic impact of obesity and to inform the design of interventions that not only reduce costs but also enhance patients´ lived experiences. Furthermore, it remains to be established which specific domains of HRQoL benefit most from digital weight management programs. The present study was therefore undertaken to address these gaps in the literature.

Using data collected from the Healthy Weight Coaching (HWC) program, a 12-month real-world digital lifestyle intervention for individuals with overweight or obesity, our primary aims were to assess whether participation in the HWC was associated with improvements in HRQoL and whether weight loss outcomes were associated with these improvements. In secondary analyses, we examined whether baseline measures of obesity were cross-sectionally associated with HRQoL, whether baseline HRQoL were associated with weight loss outcomes, weight loss outcomes by HRQoL profiles, and whether HRQoL influenced program attrition.

Materials and Methods

The Healthy Weight Coaching

The HWC is an ongoing, referral-based, real-world digital lifestyle intervention delivered through the HealthyWeightHub.fi platform, offered as part of Finland’s national Health Village service [14]. Detailed descriptions of the program have been published elsewhere [15, 16]. Briefly, the HWC provides a year-long, evidence-based digital program grounded in behavioral therapy principles. The program emphasizes coping strategies, self-reflection, self-compassion, and practical lifestyle changes. Participants engage in weekly online sessions of approximately 1 h, covering key topics such as nutrition, physical activity, stress management, sleep, and recovery. The intervention incorporates elements shown to improve the effectiveness of digital weight loss programs, including personalized goal setting, self-tracking, peer support, and tailored educational content. Each participant is paired with a designated coach, a healthcare professional who provides individualized guidance based on a standardized protocol. For this study, we considered data from individuals who enrolled in the HWC program between October 2016 and September 2020 and completed an initial 2-week trial phase. Participants were excluded if they reported using additional weight loss methods or lacked baseline data on HRQoL. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District (327/13/03/00/2015; GDPR update 587/2019). All participants provided web-based informed consent for the use of their data in research.

Clinical Data

Except for age and sex, which were retrieved from a national registry, all data were collected through self-administered online questionnaires. At program entry, participants reported their height, weight, and waist circumference on a digital form. They were advised to use their home scales to measure body weight in the morning, immediately after using the restroom. Baseline measurements were used to calculate body mass index (BMI, kg/m2). Participants were encouraged to continue submitting weekly weight data throughout the intervention, with daily reporting optional. Body weights at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months were estimated using linear interpolation and subsequently used to determine follow-up BMI values. This interpolation strategy was chosen to avoid artificially smoothing the weight trajectories and to provide weight estimates at the exact dates of time points of interest, which were not always aligned with the self-paced weight diary entries (on average 2.9 weight entries per month). For waist circumference, patients were instructed to measure at the narrowest point between the lower ribs and the iliac crest and were provided with a visual aid to guide correct measuring tape placement. Waist circumference was reassessed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. For analyses, we calculated percentage changes for weight and waist circumference at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months using the interpolated values. Moreover, participants were classified according to whether they achieved ≥5% weight reduction by the 12-month time point.

Health-Related Quality of Life

Participants completed the validated Finnish version of the RAND-36 at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months [17]. The questionnaire asks respondents to assess their health status over the previous 4 weeks. Following standard scoring guidelines, responses were converted into scores across eight domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health. Each domain is scored from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better perceived health. To investigate attrition in relation to HRQoL, participants were dichotomized into two groups (above vs. below the median) for each of the eight domain scores. In the primary analyses, we examined each domain separately, and in the secondary analyses, we additionally derived HRQoL profiles as described in the statistical analysis paragraph.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline characteristics are presented as percentages for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges and full ranges for continuous variables. Group comparisons of categorical variables were performed using Pearson chi-squared test, while Mann-Whitney U test (two groups) and Kruskal-Wallis test (three groups) were applied to continuous variables. To assess changes in HRQoL over the 12-month program, repeated measures ANOVA was used, and estimated marginal means were calculated for each HRQoL domain at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. We used K-means clustering on baseline domain scores to derive distinct HRQoL profiles to be used in the analyses. Solutions with 2–4 clusters were explored. Cluster stability and separation were assessed by examining within-cluster variance and between-cluster difference in HRQoL domain scores (one-way ANOVA). Logistic regression was employed to analyze associations between baseline HRQoL and the odds of achieving ≥5% weight loss. Generalized linear regression was applied to assess associations between baseline measures of obesity (independent variable) and baseline HRQoL (dependent variable). Generalized linear regression for repeated measures was used to explore associations between baseline HRQoL (independent variable) and continuous weight loss outcomes (dependent variable), as well as associations between weight loss outcomes (independent variable) and changes occurring in HRQoL over the program (dependent variable). All multivariable models were adjusted for age and sex. In addition, analyses with weight change percentage or waist circumference change percentage as outcomes were further adjusted for baseline BMI and waist circumference, respectively, and HRQoL change analyses were adjusted for baseline HRQoL. Finally, longitudinal analyses were adjusted for time variable. Given that men were underrepresented in the sample, we conducted sensitivity analyses examining (1) the associations between baseline HRQoL and weight loss outcomes and (2) the associations between weight loss and changes in HRQoL separately in men and women. To assess potential effect modification by sex, interaction terms between sex and HRQoL variables (in analyses with weight change-% as the outcome) and sex and weight loss (in analyses with HRQoL change as the outcome) were included in the generalized linear models. In longitudinal analyses, a three-way interaction term (time × sex × HRQoL or time × sex × weight change-%) was additionally tested to examine whether associations differed by sex over time. Analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Secondary outcomes were considered exploratory; therefore, no adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of the 2,157 participants, we excluded 182 with additional weight loss methods such as very low energy diet or medications and 127 without data on HRQoL (online supplementary Fig. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552284). Individuals who were excluded from the analyses (n = 309, 14.3%) had higher median body weight (114 kg vs. 110 kg, p = 0.005) and higher median BMI (40.3 kg/m2 vs. 39.1 kg/m2, p < 0.001) compared to those included (n = 1,848, 85.7%) (online suppl. Table 1). Overall, 82.9% of the final sample were women, with a median age of 52 years (Table 1).

HRQoL at Baseline and Its Association with Baseline Measures of Obesity

At baseline, the highest median HRQoL scores, reflecting better quality of life, were observed for role limitations due to emotional problems, followed by social functioning, physical functioning, mental health, bodily pain, vitality, role limitations due to physical health, and general health (Table 1). Adjusted for age and sex, both baseline BMI and waist circumference were associated with lower HRQoL related to physical functioning (BMI: B = −1.330 [95% confidence interval, CI, −1.470 to −1.190], p < 0.001; waist circumference: B = −0.573 [95% CI, −0.645 to −0.501], p < 0.001), bodily pain (BMI: B = −0.488 [95% CI, −0.671 to −0.306], p < 0.001; waist circumference: B = −0.264 [95% CI, −0.359 to −0.169], p < 0.001), general health (BMI: B = −0.441 [95% CI, −0.581 to −0.300], p < 0.001; waist circumference: B = −0.258 [95% CI, −0.330 to −0.185], p < 0.001), and social functioning (BMI: B = −0.370 [95% CI, −0.554 to −0.186], p < 0.001; waist circumference: B = −0.171 [95% CI, −0.266 to −0.076], p < 0.001) (Table 2). In addition, greater baseline waist circumference was associated with lower vitality (B = −0.082 [95% CI, −0.154 to −0.010], p = 0.026).

HRQoL Clusters

The three-cluster solution was considered optimal in the K-means cluster analysis, as it provided clinically meaningful and distinct profiles while maintaining adequate sample sizes in each group. The clusters were clearly separated, with all HRQoL domains showing statistically significant differences among clusters (online suppl. Fig. 2). Overall, within-cluster standard deviations were small (typically 12–25 points on a 0–100 scale), indicating that the clusters were generally homogeneous. Based on the eight HRQoL scores, the clusters were named high HRQoL, physical health burden, and globally low HRQoL. Individuals in the physical health burden cluster had the highest proportion of women (87.9%) and were on average the eldest (53 years) and the heaviest (39.9 kg/m2), while those in the high HRQoL cluster had the lowest proportion of women (80.5%) and were the leanest (38.4 kg/m2) (online suppl. Table 2).

Associations between Baseline HRQoL and Continuous Weight Loss Outcomes

Of the eight HRQoL domains, higher baseline scores in vitality (B = −0.013 [95% CI, −0.020 to −0.006], p < 0.001), role limitations due to emotional problems (B = −0.005 [95% CI, −0.009 to −0.001], p = 0.006), and mental health (B = −0.011 [95% CI, −0.018 to −0.003], p = 0.006) were associated with greater weight loss (Table 3). Similarly, higher scores in physical functioning (B = −0.023 [95% CI, −0.034 to −0.011], p < 0.001), general health (B = −0.019 [95% CI, −0.030 to −0.008], p < 0.001), social functioning (B = −0.012 [95% CI, −0.021 to −0.003], p = 0.007), role limitations due to emotional problems (B = −0.008 [95% CI, −0.014 to −0.002], p = 0.008), and mental health (B = −0.013 [95% CI, −0.025 to −0.001], p = 0.044) were associated with greater reduction in waist circumference. Compared with the high HRQoL cluster, individuals in the globally low HRQoL cluster achieved less weight loss (B = 0.777 [95% CI, 0.435–1.120], p < 0.001) (Table 3). For waist circumference reduction, both the physical health burden cluster (B = 0.567 [95% CI, 0.003–1.131], p = 0.049) and the globally low HRQoL cluster (B = 0.982 [95% CI, 0.436–1.528], p < 0.001) were less successful compared with the high HRQoL cluster.

The sex-specific analyses showed some differences between men and women (online suppl. Table 3). However, examination of sex × HRQoL domain interactions over time revealed no consistent evidence of effect modification by sex (online suppl. Table 4). Specifically, for physical functioning, vitality, social functioning, and mental health, there was no evidence of interaction. For role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health, and role limitations due to emotional problems, weak interactions were observed at single time points only (p = 0.027–0.037), but these did not form a consistent pattern across follow-up.

Associations between Baseline HRQoL and Odds of Reaching ≥5% Weight Loss

Higher baseline HRQoL related to vitality was associated with increased odds of achieving ≥5% weight loss (odds ratio [OR] = 1.011 [95% CI, 1.001–1.020], p = 0.024) (online suppl. Table 5). With the high HRQoL cluster as a reference, individuals in the globally low HRQoL cluster were less likely to achieve ≥5% weight loss (OR = 0.604 [95% CI, 0.381–0.956], p = 0.031). Individuals achieving and not achieving ≥5% weight loss by 12 months had comparable sex distribution (83.3% vs. 87.0% women, respectively, p = 0.208) and age (55 vs. 54 years, respectively, p = 0.146). However, at baseline, those achieving ≥5% weight loss were heavier compared to those not achieving (weight, 110 kg vs. 105 kg, p = 0.009; BMI, 39.4 kg/m2 vs. 37.7 kg/m2, p = 0.012; waist circumference, 118 cm vs. 115 cm, p = 0.005).

In the sex-specific analyses, greater scores in role limitations due to physical health and vitality were associated with greater odds of achieving ≥5% weight loss in women (online suppl. Table 6). No significant associations were observed in men. However, sex and baseline HRQoL score interactions were not significant (all p > 0.05, data not shown), indicating that the influence of baseline HRQoL on odds of achieving at least 5% weight loss did not differ by sex.

Changes in HRQoL across the 12-Month Intervention and Its Association with Weight Loss Outcomes

Significant improvements were observed across all eight HRQoL domains over the 12-month intervention (Fig. 1). The average improvement was the greatest for physical functioning (6.9 points), followed by vitality (6.6 points), general health (6.6 points), social functioning (6.3 points), role limitations due to physical health (6.0 points), bodily pain (5.6 points), role limitations due to emotional problems (5.3 points), and mental health (3.6 points). In six of these domains, the improvements were associated with concurrent weight loss. Specifically, a 5% reduction in body weight was associated with increases in HRQoL related to general health (3.8 points), physical functioning (3.3 points), vitality (2.3 points), bodily pain (2.1 points), social functioning (1.2 points), and mental health (1.0 point). Table 4 shows results per 1-unit change. Furthermore, individuals who lost ≥5% of their baseline weight experienced greater increases in HRQoL related to physical functioning and general health compared to those with <5% weight loss (Fig. 2). Moreover, each 5% reduction in waist circumference was associated with improvements of 2.9 points in general health, 2.5 points in physical functioning, 2.0 points in vitality, 1.7 points in bodily pain, and 1.5 points in social functioning (Table 4).

HRQoL and Attrition

When participants were stratified by the median HRQoL scores, compared with individuals with below-median scores, those with above-median scores in role limitations due to physical health (69.1% vs. 61.7%, p = 0.001), general health (69.6% vs. 61.2%, p < 0.001), and vitality (68.1% vs. 61.9%, p = 0.007) were more likely to complete the program (online suppl. Fig. 3). No significant differences were observed for the remaining HRQoL domains. Of the HRQoL clusters, those in the globally low HRQoL cluster more frequently dropped out of the program compared to those in the high HRQoL cluster (61.3% vs. 67.5%, p = 0.029).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the relationships between HRQoL and weight loss outcomes in 1,848 adults enrolled in a 12-month real-world digital lifestyle intervention. Participation in the HWC program was associated with improvements across all HRQoL domains, with greater weight loss consistently associated with larger improvements in six of the eight HRQoL domains, including physical functioning, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, and mental health. Furthermore, individuals who achieved ≥5% weight loss experienced greater improvements in physical functioning and general health compared to those with less weight loss. Sensitivity analyses stratified by sex suggested some differences in associations between HRQoL and weight loss. However, formal interaction analyses showed no consistent evidence of effect modification by sex, indicating that observed relationships were largely similar in men and women.

Although the observed improvements in HRQoL were statistically significant and consistent across all domains, the magnitude of change on the 0–100 scale may seem relatively modest. Therefore, the clinical relevance of such changes is an important consideration. Importantly, it has been previously suggested that minimally important differences in HRQoL, referring to the smallest change in a score that patients perceive as beneficial or that would indicate a meaningful improvement in health status, may vary by domain, with estimates ranging from two to four points [18]. In the current study, the observed improvements ranged from four to seven points, exceeding the proposed minimally important differences, thus indicating clinically meaningful improvements in perceived health status. Importantly, these improvements also followed a dose-response pattern, as greater reductions in body weight and waist circumference were consistently associated with larger improvements across multiple HRQoL domains. In addition to these absolute changes, the generalized linear models indicated that the effect sizes per unit of weight change were modest but consistent across domains. Specifically, each 5% reduction in body weight was associated with approximately 1.0–3.8 point improvements in HRQoL, with the largest effects observed for general health and physical functioning. Furthermore, at baseline, HRQoL in our sample was lower than the age- and sex-adjusted reference values from the Finnish general population [17], particularly in domains related to general health (20 points), bodily pain (18 points), physical functioning (10 points), vitality (9 points), and mental health (7 points). This indicates that participants entered the intervention with impaired perceived health and well-being. Taken together, participation in the HWC program was associated with consistent and clinically relevant improvements in perceived health and functioning, and even moderate weight loss may translate into tangible, meaningful benefits across multiple aspects of perceived well-being.

While we are not aware of other studies specifically examining HRQoL outcomes in the context of digital weight management interventions, research on other treatment modalities have been published. Among these, a systematic review of reviews concluded that weight loss, particularly following bariatric surgery, improves HRQoL, whereas results for nonsurgical interventions are less consistent [7]. Nevertheless, in line with our observations, several studies have shown that weight loss, even beyond surgical interventions, is frequently linked to improvements in HRQoL, especially in physical domains. For example, using large cohort data from the Nurses’ Health Study, Pan et al. [10] demonstrated that weight loss was associated with improvements in physical functioning, role limitations, bodily pain, general health, and vitality, although effects on social and emotional domains were weaker. Similarly, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 53 randomized controlled trials found that weight loss produced modest but significant improvements in physical HRQoL, while effects on mental and overall HRQoL were inconsistent [19]. Using an obesity-specific quality of life questionnaire, Kolotkin et al. [11] reported significant gains across all dimensions after 1 year of outpatient weight management, with physical function and self-esteem most strongly affected by weight loss. In another study, following a 13-week weight management program, a mean weight loss of 8.6 kg was associated with improvements in physical functioning, general health, vitality, and mental health [20], with gains in general health and vitality persisting at 1-year follow-up [21]. Finally, pharmacological interventions have also demonstrated improvements in HRQoL that are superior to those in placebo groups, particularly among individuals achieving greater weight reductions or reporting baseline limitations in physical functioning [22]. Taken together, our results corroborate previous evidence that weight reduction is consistently associated with improvements in the physical aspects of HRQoL and suggest that real-world digital interventions may also positively affect domains such as social functioning and emotional well-being.

We also observed that higher baseline scores in physical functioning, general health, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health predicted either greater weight loss or larger reductions in waist circumference across the 12-month program. Moreover, a profile characterized by low HRQoL scores across several domains was associated with reduced weight loss success. Although we could not identify prior studies specifically examining the relationship between baseline HRQoL and weight loss outcomes, it is plausible that low HRQoL may reflect additional barriers to effective weight management. Poor physical functioning or compromised general health, for example, may limit participation in physical activity, reduce energy expenditure, and make adherence to exercise programs more challenging [23, 24]. Similarly, lower vitality and mental health could diminish motivation, persistence, or confidence in managing lifestyle changes, while deficits in social functioning and increased role limitations due to emotional problems may reduce access to social support, accountability, or reinforcement that facilitate sustained behavior change [25, 26]. Collectively, these factors may contribute to slower progress or smaller overall weight reductions, suggestive of the need for additional support for individuals facing physical, emotional, or social challenges.

In prior studies investigating the role of HRQoL in attrition in weight management programs, Rothberg et al. [27] found that neither a generic nor an obesity-specific HRQoL measure predicted retention in a 2-year physician-led program. Similarly, a pooled analysis of three randomized controlled trials of behavioral weight loss interventions reported no uniform association between baseline HRQoL and attrition [28]. However, reduced physical HRQoL was linked to attrition in a trial-dependent manner, suggesting its influence may vary by intervention context. Instead, mental HRQoL was not associated with attrition. In the current study, individuals with below-median HRQoL in the domains of role limitations due to physical health, general health, and vitality more frequently discontinued the program compared with those with higher scores. Similarly, individuals in the globally low HRQoL cluster more frequently discontinued the program, compared to those in the high HRQoL cluster. Several mechanisms may help explain these associations. Reduced perceived health and vitality may diminish the energy, motivation, and psychological resources needed to sustain long-term lifestyle changes. Similarly, the presence of physical limitations and health problems may hinder participants’ ability to fully engage in the program, potentially leading to frustration if they are unable to meet self-set expectations. This mismatch between intentions and perceived capacity can erode self-efficacy and increase the likelihood of attrition. These results highlight the importance of early identification of individuals with low HRQoL in these domains, so that tailored support can be provided to mitigate the risk of dropout.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample of real-world patients, a prospective design, and the use of the validated RAND-36 questionnaire to assess HRQoL. Data were derived from a nationwide digital lifestyle intervention, adding novelty, particularly with respect to HRQoL outcomes. The sample was diverse, spanning a broad age and BMI range, which further supports the generalizability of the results. Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. Most participants were women which, while common in weight loss studies, may limit the applicability of findings to men. To address this, we conducted sensitivity analyses stratifying the analyses by sex, which showed no consistent evidence that sex modified the observed relationships. Reliance on self-reported data may have introduced bias, particularly for sensitive measures such as weight. However, the anonymity of data collection and frequent weight reporting, with values interpolated at quarterly time points, may have mitigated social desirability bias and reduced the impact of occasional misreporting. The absence of a control group precludes causal inference. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than effects attributable to the intervention itself. The multivariable models were adjusted for available covariates, including age, sex, and baseline anthropometric measurements. Although baseline HRQoL preceded weight change, supporting the temporal order of associations, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as socioeconomic, clinical, and behavioral variables, potentially influencing both HRQoL and weight loss outcomes, were not accounted for. The logistic regression analyses examined associations rather than predictive performance. Finally, as improvements in HRQoL were based on participants with available follow-up data, and those with lower baseline scores in certain domains were more likely to discontinue, the observed changes may overestimate improvements for the full study population. Despite these limitations, this study offers valuable insights into the associations between HRQoL and weight loss outcomes in a real-world digital lifestyle intervention and may inform the design of future programs aimed at promoting long-term engagement and success.

In conclusion, participation in the HWC program was associated with improvements across all eight HRQoL domains, with weight loss particularly associated with beneficial changes related to physical functioning, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, and mental health. Our observations highlight the bidirectional relationship between HRQoL and weight, as lower baseline HRQoL in most of these same domains were linked to less successful weight loss outcomes. Overall, the results suggest that digital weight management programs may provide benefits not only for weight loss but also for the overall lived experience of individuals seeking weight management.
