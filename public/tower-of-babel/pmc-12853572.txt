Investigating the relationship between influenza vaccination and COVID-19 infection: a cohort study in Tehran
Authors: Ali Abbasian, Neda SoleimanvandiAzar, Hamid R Baradaran, Seyyed Amir Yasin Ahmadi, Babak Eshrati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe effect of vaccination on unrelated diseases, like the effect of influenza vaccination on COVID-19, has been shown in various studies. but the results of such studies are different and contradict each other. In Iran, not many studies have been done. The present study examines the relationship between Influenza vaccination and infecting with COVID-19.MethodsThe present study was conducted on a population of 3,000 individuals covered by the Iran University of Medical Sciences who had received COVID-19 vaccine before the start of study. This study was a retrospective cohort study. SPSS software (version 26) was used for data analysis. In order to estimate Hazard Ratio (HR), survival analysis and Cox regression model were used and, the relationship between Influenza vaccination and infecting with COVID-19 was investigated.ResultsThe results of the study show that different levels of demographic variables such as occupation, education, underlying diseases, gender, age, exercise, and socio-economic status do not significantly increase or decrease the likelihood of COVID-19 infection. After adjusting for confounding variables, the calculated Hazard Ratio indicated that influenza vaccination has no significant effect on the outcome variable and is neither a risk factor nor a protective factor in the speed of COVID-19 infection (HR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.819–1.859).ConclusionAccording to the results of study, Influenza vaccination is not a risk factor for infection with COVID-19. In a cohort study, it is not possible to adjust unknown and unexamined confounders, so in order to resolve possible ambiguities, more studies should be conducted in the future to adjust for such confounders.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12392-2.Keywords: COVID-19, Vaccination, Influenza

Introduction

In December 2019, for the first time in the city of Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, a new type of coronavirus was identified, which was known as the cause of a new disease, COVID-19. This infectious disease causes acute respiratory syndrome, hyper-inflammatory responses and even vascular damage. This disease can include a wide range of symptoms from a simple cold to severe respiratory infections. Most individuals get a mild disease without complications. Transmission of infection usually occurs by respiratory droplets. In severe cases, this infection can lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome, sepsis and septic shock, multiple organ failure, including acute kidney injury and heart damage [1–8]. In recent months, various vaccines have been developed to control the infection of COVID-19 and have received an emergency license. Studies have also shown the possibility of vaccination having an effect on unrelated diseases [9]. Among these vaccines, the role of influenza vaccination in reducing the incidence and death from COVID-19 can be mentioned [10–14]. Several theorized mechanisms of the potential protective effect of influenza vaccination have been proposed. The first hypothesis centers around the presence of MF59 in the influenza vaccine: an oil-in-water squalene emulsion that has been shown to assist in potentiating an immune response to SARS-CoV variants. Alternatively, influenza vaccination’s potential protective effect may be explained by its ability to stimulate the activation of natural killer cells, the levels of which have been found to be considerably decreased in moderate and severe SARS-CoV-2 cases. Another proposed mechanism is that both coronaviruses and influenza viruses engage with the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE-2) and tetraspanin antibodies. Thus, there is belief that ACE-2 and tetraspanin antibodies may inhibit both coronavirus and low-pathogenic influenza A virus infections [15]. According to the report of World Health Organization, the number of individuals infected with COVID-19 has reached 615,930,000 and the number of deaths due to this infection has reached 6,525,000. Of these, 595 million and 46 thousand individuals have recovered. The five countries with the highest reported number of infected individuals include the United States (97 million and 430 thousand), India (44 million and 522 thousand), France (34 million and 831 thousand), Brazil (34 million and 610 thousand) and Germany (32 million and 643 thousand). The countries with the highest number of reported deaths due to COVID-19 include the United States (1 million and 76 thousand), Brazil (685 thousand), India (528 thousand), Russia (385 thousand) and Mexico (329 thousand) [16]. In Iran, since the beginning of Covid-19 prevalence, the number of infected people has reached 7 million and 542 thousand and the number of deaths due to infection has reached 144 thousand people. It should be noted that during this period, the number of people who have recovered from the infection in Iran has reached 7 million and 319 thousand people [17].

COVID-19 is transmitted very quickly and easily from person to person. Measures such as quarantine may be used to reduce the burden of the disease, but such measures, especially in countries like Iran where the people’s situation is economically fragile, may cause political, social and economic problems and may make it more difficult to control the disease, so other methods of reducing cases and deaths from disease, such as vaccination, should also be considered. Today, various number of domestic and foreign vaccines have received authorization for emergency use from domestic and international health institutions. It should be noted that there may be conditions that lead to vaccine shortage. Even now in low-income countries like Iran, probably they face a lack of effective vaccines. A part from that, manufacturing and distributing vaccines to all members of society, especially in developing countries like Iran, imposes enormous costs on the country’s economy. It should also be noted that the COVID-19 vaccines available in society have not yet passed their final stages (phase four) and various complications have been observed in people after the injection of these vaccines. Therefore, if influenza vaccination has an effect on the course of COVID-19, the issue of combining influenza and COVID-19 vaccines or the current use of influenza vaccines instead of COVID-19 vaccines could also be considered. In Iran, the epidemy of COVID-19 has imposed enormous economic and social costs. It caused physical and psychological complications on infected individuals and their families, and it is necessary to further investigate various creative and effective methods, such as vaccination, to reduce the burden of infection and mortality caused by disease. It should be noted that various infections, including Influenza, increase the risk of infecting with the COVID-19.

Studies conducted in different countries have shown the effect of vaccination against unrelated diseases, including influenza vaccination on COVID-19; but these studies have not reached a single conclusion and their results are noticeably different and contradictory. The results of these studies are also not visible at the executive levels and health service delivery systems. Such a study has not been conducted in Iran either. Therefore, it seems that conducting such studies can help clarify the issue further and reach a valid and reliable result that can be implemented in society. If influenza vaccination is effective against COVID-19, it could save millions of dollars in infectious disease control and prevention costs, especially in developing countries like Iran, which have weaker economies. The results of such studies would also be useful and applicable for systematic reviews and trials. In light of the above, the research group members decided to take a positive step towards reducing the burden of the disease and its harmful effects by further investigating this issue. The group is trying to examine the impact of influenza vaccination in different gender and occupational groups, so health care organizations and institutions, as well as subsequent research groups, can benefit from the results. Therefore, the aim of the present study is to investigate the relationship between influenza vaccination and COVID-19 infection.

Methods

Study design

The present study was a historical cohort study to investigate the relationship between Influenza vaccination and COVID-19.

Study samples

The research population in this study is a population of 3000 residents of Tehran and covered by the Iran University of Medical Sciences (database of the Ministry of Health) who had been injected with the COVID-19 vaccine before the start of this study. After preparing a suitable sample from the said population (using a simple random sampling method), the vaccination status of the people (injection or non-injection of the Influenza vaccine and the type and frequency of the injected COVID-19 vaccine, which were recorded based on self-reporting by the study subjects) as well as the status of their infection with COVID-19 (The individual was asked whether or not they had the disease, and given the country’s epidemiological conditions, the diagnostic criteria were based on PCR and radiology.) was asked. Samples were selected using simple random sampling method. Considering the probability of type 1 error (∝=0.05) and type 2 error (𝐵=0.1) (Power = 90%) and according to previous studies [18] that P1 = 4% and P2 = 48% and that The current study is a cohort study, and in previous studies, the effect of vaccines on people infected by COVID-19 was calculated to be about 50% [19] We Used the formula to calculate the minimum sample size for comparing two ratios and the number of 236 was obtained. It indicated that to conduct this study, we should consider at least 236 exposed individuals and 236 non-exposed individuals, so that the total number of the sample was 472 individuals.

Data collection

After selecting the individuals, the necessary information was collected through a telephone interview and using a pre-designed checklist, and then it was compiled and uploaded in an Excel form. In this study, the status of individuals’ underlying diseases and their relationship with COVID-19 were also examined. Underlying diseases are diseases that weaken the immune system, cause inflammation, coagulation and metabolic disorders, and dysfunction of other body systems, leading to increased complications or mortality in patients with secondary diseases. Examples of these types of diseases that we examined in this study include: cardiovascular diseases, blood pressure, blood lipids, types of cancers, liver diseases, and diabetes. The inclusion criteria in this study are being present in database of health department and population covered by the Iran University of Medical Sciences who had been injected with COVID-19 vaccine before the start of study, access to the Influenza vaccine of 2020, Informed consent and willingness to participate in the study, Having proper identification information including the correct phone number, having Iranian citizenship and mastering the Persian language, alertness and the ability to communicate and answer the checklist questions. The criteria for exclusion from the study included individuals who have at least one of the following conditions: they are less than twenty years of old, they are forbidden to inject Influenza vaccine. In this study, the exposure variable is the injection of Influenza vaccine between the beginning of September 2020 and the end of November 2020, and the outcome variable is COVID-19 infection between the beginning of September 2020 and the end of December 2021.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26 software (IBM Corp., NY, US) was used for data analysis. First, the relationship between the Influenza vaccination and infecting with COVID-19 was investigated in a simple way and through univariate Cox regression. In the following, independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used to investigate the relationship between the confounding variables and the variable of infecting with COVID-19, as well as to investigate the relationship between the Influenza vaccine injection and the severity of COVID-19. Finally, confounding variables were adjusted and multivariable Cox regression was used to investigate the relationship between Influenza vaccination and COVID-19 infection (first, Cox model assumptions, including proportional hazards, were examined). We had a model selection strategy and according to this model, we first identified confounding variables. According to the definition of confounding variable, we considered three criteria: first, the confounding variable must have a known causal relationship with the outcome variable so that it is in causal priority. The second condition is that the confounding variable must have a correlation with the exposure variable and the third condition is that the confounding variable is not in the middle of the causal chain. The significance threshold for determining confounding variables and entering them into model was set at 0.2. In fact, probability values between 0.05 and 0.2 are not statistically significant, but they indicate a large association and were used for modeling based on this strong association. After running the model, the significance threshold for influenza vaccine, which is considered an exposure variable, was set at 0.05. As a result, after performing the above tests, the variables of socioeconomic status, occupation, immune status, education, age, exercise, and underlying diseases were identified as confounding variables and entered the model.

Results

Based on the results of this study and the significance of variables, there is no significant difference between female and male patients in terms of COVID-19 infection. Occupation has not been a significant factor affecting COVID-19 infection; individuals who were healthcare providers, housewives, unemployed, retired, or freelancers have experienced similar infection rates. Similarly, education level does not appear to influence COVID-19 infection rates. In the studied population, university-educated individuals, those without a diploma, illiterates, and individuals with a diploma have experienced comparable levels of infection. The results also indicate that underlying diseases (including cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, blood lipid disorders, various cancers, liver diseases, and diabetes) do not significantly affect the likelihood of COVID-19 infection. Age showed no significant relationship with infection status (Table-1). Furthermore, exposure status was not significantly associated with disease severity (Table-2).

Discussion

According to the results of study, the Influenza vaccination does not have a statistically significant relationship with infecting by COVID-19 and visiting a doctor due to this infection. The results of study are inconsistent with the study conducted by Anakanlen et al., that the probability of a positive test for COVID-19 is reduced in patients who received Influenza vaccine compared to those who did not receive the vaccine [20] but it was in line with the results of the systematic review conducted by George V. Coppola and colleagues based on the results of thirty-eight articles that there was no relationship between Influenza vaccination and the risk of infecting with COVID-19, hospitalization and death due to COVID-19 [21]. Also, according to a study by Yang et al. titled Influenza Vaccination and Hospitalization Among Adults with COVID-19; influenza vaccination potentially protects against moderate and severe cases of COVID-19 infection [22]. Based on the results of study conducted under title of the incidence and severity of COVID-19 among hospital workers of Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences and its relationship with seasonal Influenza vaccination by Malekzadeh et al., which was applied research with a descriptive-analytical approach in 2020, It was shown that the Influenza vaccine does not have a protective role against the infection of COVID-19. According to these observations, Influenza vaccination is not related to the severity of disease, and it is not possible to comment on prognosis of COVID-19 in patients based on their Influenza vaccination status [23].

The relationship between Influenza vaccination and hospitalization due to COVID-19 does not have statistical significance. Many studies have been conducted regarding the mentioned relationship between Influenza vaccination and hospitalization due to COVID-19, some of which have the same results as the present study, and some have obtained opposite results. In study by Pietroragni et al., which was conducted on seventeen thousand and six hundred and eight individuals, it was shown that individuals vaccinated with Influenza have a lower chance of infecting with COVID-19, but there is no relationship between the Influenza vaccination and hospitalization and death due to COVID_19. However, in the case of individuals older than or equal to 65 years, the results were different, and Influenza vaccination reduced hospitalizations and deaths due to COVID-19 [24]. According to a study conducted by Bojani et al., titled The Effect of Influenza Vaccination on Complications, Severity, and Mortality of COVID-19: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, influenza vaccination can reduce the likelihood of infection, hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality due to COVID-19 [25]. Also, according to a study conducted by Jiang et al. titled Association between influenza vaccination and SARS-CoV-2 infection and its results: A systematic review and meta-analysis; a total of 36 studies including 55,996,841 people were investigated. Influenza vaccination limits severe COVID-19 infection, ICU admission, and ventilator use, but it has no effect on hospitalization [26].

Examining the relationship between Influenza vaccination and hospitalization in the intensive care unit shows that these two variables do not have a statistically significant relationship. In a large-scale multi-database cohort study conducted in Italy by Marcomassari on 115,945 individuals, it was shown that Influenza vaccination was directly associated with a reduction in hospitalizations, although there was no association between Influenza vaccination and hospitalization in intensive care unit (ICU) and death due to COVID-19 [27].

Investigating the relationship between Influenza vaccination and using a ventilator due to COVID-19 shows that these two variables are not statistically significant. In a study conducted by Anakanlen et al., the probability of a positive test for COVID-19 was reduced in patients who received the Influenza vaccine compared to those who did not receive the vaccine. Also, the probability of hospitalization, mechanical ventilation and length of stay in the hospital in vaccinated individuals was shorter [18]. A study by Pontiroli et al. titled Vaccination against influenza viruses reduces infection, but not hospitalization or death from respiratory COVID-19: A systematic review and meta-analysis; which examined 51 studies, showed that influenza vaccination reduces the number of infections caused by COVID-19. The results of this meta-analysis show that influenza vaccination reduced the frequency of ICU admissions in 11 studies, had no effect on hospitalizations in 14 studies, and had no effect on COVID-19 mortality in 19 studies [28]. According to the study conducted by Hosseini Moghadam et al. titled “Association of influenza vaccination with SARS-CoV-2 infection, hospitalization, and mortality among patients aged 66 years and older,” which was conducted on 2,279,805 Ontario residents, it was shown that influenza vaccination causes lower prevalence, hospitalization, and mortality from COVID-19 [29].

In general, there is no statistically significant relationship between Influenza vaccination and severe state of COVID-19. Another systematic review study conducted by Hessam Waleed Almadhon et al. based on the results of sixteen articles and one hundred and ninety-one thousand and four hundred and ninety-six patients with COVID-19. The results showed a significant superiority in terms of mechanical ventilation in the vaccinated group compared to the non-vaccinated group. Also, no statistically significant relationship was observed between Influenza vaccination and the rate of death due to COVID-19, hospital admission and intensive care unit admission [30].

What is obtained from investigating the results of studies conducted in other parts of the world shows that the mentioned relationship between Influenza vaccination and the incidence and severity of the COVID-19 has many consistent and inconsistent results. This emphasizes the point that more studies are needed to clarify the different dimensions of this issue. The conditions of each society differ from other societies in terms of individuals’ inherent immunity, the type and number of vaccines administered, circulating viruses, the level of compliance with health protocols, individuals’ age and gender, underlying diseases, level of welfare, employment, and other demographic and epidemiological variables. Therefore, research results will vary across different populations and samples, and to reach a single conclusion, more diverse and larger studies must be conducted with maximum adjustment for unknown variables that may be confounding.

Limitations

One of the limitations of study was non-cooperation or inappropriate cooperation of some people participating in the study. Also, due to the collection information using the self-reporting method, there was a possibility of recall bias in taking and inserting information. Only people vaccinated with Covid-19 vaccine participated in this study, which causes selection bias. However, it should be noted that the variable of COVID-19 vaccination was a confounding variable, which we adjusted for in this way. If we had included people who were not vaccinated with COVID-19 in the study, we would not have been able to determine whether people vaccinated with COVID-19 were less likely to get sick due to the COVID vaccine or whether there was an effect of the flu vaccine. This study was conducted at the beginning of the pandemic and may be less generalizable than later studies. According to the results of study, influenza vaccination has no effect on COVID-19 infection, and vaccinated people have infected with COVID_19 as much as unvaccinated people. However, it should be noted that these results alone cannot be relied upon and, more studies are needed to clarify the issue. It is possible that people who receive the flu vaccine every year are the same vulnerable members of society, including the elderly, who are consequently more vulnerable to other diseases like Covid-19. Therefore, vaccination may not adequately protect them and they may still get COVID-19 despite the flu vaccination.

Conclusion

The present study was conducted to show the relationship between Influenza vaccination and the severity of the COVID-19. Based on the results of study, the variables of gender, occupation, age, education, and having underlying disease were not influencing factors for individuals to be infected with COVID-19. Also, after adjusting confounding variables in multivariable Cox model, it was shown that Influenza vaccination is not a risk factor for infecting with COVID-19. In a cohort study, it is not possible to adjust unknown and unexamined confounders, so in order to resolve possible ambiguities, more studies should be conducted in the future to adjust for such confounders.
