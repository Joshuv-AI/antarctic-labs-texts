Morbidity Among the Aging Population in Northern Bangladesh: An Epidemiological Study
Authors: Md. Abu Sayem, Md. Golam Hossain, Md. Mothashin, Md. Nurul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveMorbidity is a prevalent public health issue especially among the aging population worldwide. As the risks of morbidity increase with age, aging populations increasingly become a socioeconomic burden on their families. However, studies on morbidity among aging populations in Bangladesh remain limited and under‐documented. This study aims to determine the prevalence of morbidity and identify the associated factors among aging populations in Northern Bangladesh.MethodsThe cross‐sectional study was conducted in the northern part of Bangladesh. Data were collected from 540 aging people; they were selected using multistage random sampling. Face‐to‐face interviews were conducted by 12 trained interviewers for collecting data, and it was done from June 5, 2022 to August 18, 2022. The outcome variable morbidity was measured by a question, “are you suffering from chronic diseases?” Based on the answer, the respondents were classified into three classes; (i) did not have any disease (0 = No), (ii) having only one disease (1 = single), and (iii) having more than one disease (2 = morbidity). Finally, (iii) was considered as morbidity (Yes, code 1), and (i) and (ii) together were considered did not have morbidity (No, code 0). According to the objectives of the study, we used descriptive statistics, the χ 2 test, and multivariate logistic regression to determine the prevalence and to assess the factors influencing morbidity respectively.ResultsThe study revealed that the prevalence of morbidity was found 75.74% [95% CI: 0.719–0.793] among people. The significantly associated factors of morbidity included being male [AOR = 1.717; 95% CI = 1.109–2.657; p = 0.015], living in a rural area [AOR = 1.758; 95% CI = 1.082–2.856; p = 0.023], age above 70 [AOR = 1.926; 95% CI = 1.178–3.150; p = 0.009], having prior disability [AOR = 1.932; 95% CI = 1.126–3.315; p = 0.017], being underweight [AOR = 4.045; 95% CI = 1.568–10.438; p = 0.004], and overweight [AOR = 1.957; 95% CI = 1.223–3.132; p = 0.005].ConclusionsMorbidity is highly prevalent among aging populations, with over nourished individuals aged 70 and above, particularly those living in rural areas, being the most significantly affected. Targeted interventions should focus on improving nutritional status and raising awareness, particularly in rural areas, to help reduce the burden of morbidity among aging populations.

Introduction

Improved public healthcare services have increased life expectancy worldwide [1]. As individuals progress through different stages of life, their exposure to chronic diseases increases. These chronic conditions often lead to morbidity, which adversely affects quality of life and undermines the socio‐economic stability of families due to the high cost of treatment [2]. As life expectancy has exceeded 75 years in nearly 60 countries, the global aging population has grown substantially [3]. However, aging populations frequently face significant health challenges due to chronic diseases, with many individuals being diagnosed with more than one condition, a phenomenon defined as morbidity [4]. This has emerged as a major global public health concern [5, 6] as it is closely associated with socioeconomic deprivation [7].

At present, approximately 1.2 billion people worldwide are aged 60 and older. This number is projected to rise to 1.4 billion by 2030 and further increase to 2.1 billion by 2050 [8]. Globally, most aging individuals have at least one chronic disease, while nearly one‐third suffer from two or more chronic conditions [9]. The worldwide prevalence of morbidity is estimated at 37.20%, with the highest rates of morbidity observed in South America (45.70%), followed by North America (43.10%), Europe (39.20%), and Asia (35.00%) [10].

South Asia is home to approximately 216.40 million aging individuals, a figure expected to increase to 1.3 billion by 2050 [11]. Bangladesh, a South Asian country with a population exceeding 171 million, currently has approximately 12 million people aged 60 and over [12]. This number is projected to increase to 21.53 million by 2030 and 43.49 million by 2050 [13]. Consequently Bangladesh has experienced a significant demographic and social transition in recent decades [14]. Morbidity among aging individuals is linked to a higher risk of disability, frailty and hospitalizations [9].

In Northern Bangladesh, health‐seeking behavior is strongly influenced by poverty, local customs, religious beliefs, agrarian livelihoods, seasonal unemployment, and traditional practices. In this region, individuals often rely initially on home remedies, village doctors, drug sellers, or traditional healers and seek formal medical care only when symptoms become severe [13, 15]. Additionally, geographic barriers to healthcare facilities, weak infrastructure, limited service availability, and low awareness of preventive care further increase the risk of morbidity among aging populations in Bangladesh [13, 16].

The Northern region of Bangladesh has historically experienced high poverty rates and seasonal food insecurity, and its health infrastructure is less developed compared with major urban centers such as Dhaka and Chattogram. Although several studies have examined the prevalence and associated risk factors of morbidity in Bangladesh, three hospital‐based studies [17, 18, 19] were limited to six or nine chronic diseases, while another study [20] focused on individuals aged 50 years and over in only three divisions (Dhaka, Chattogram, and Khulna). In contrast, the present study considers 16 chronic diseases and focuses on individuals aged 60 years and above in two divisions of Northern Bangladesh. Most national aging research aggregate data without regional specificity, and localized research is needed for understanding targeted communities and targeted interventions.

Despite the growing burden of morbidity, limited research has explored its underlying socio‐economic and health‐related determinants in Northern Bangladesh. Therefore, this study aims to determine the prevalence of morbidity and identify the factors associated with morbidity among the aging population in Northern Bangladesh.

Methods

Study Area

Northern Bangladesh, comprising the Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions, has an estimated population of around 33 million—about 23.1% of the country's total. The region is mostly flat and low‐lying, shaped by the Ganges‐Brahmaputra‐Meghna delta. Some areas, particularly in Rangpur, face greater economic challenges compared to the rest of the country [21].

Study Population and Sample Size Determination

All aging people aged 60 years and above were the population of our present study. The sample size was determined by using the Cochran's [22] sample size formula: n=Z2pqd2=1.962×0.64×0.360.052=354.04≈355 where p is the proportion of morbidity, it (0.64) had been taken from a previous study [23], q = 1–p = 1–0.64 = 0.36, Z is the Type I error at 5% level of significance = 1.96 and d is the marginal error = 0.05. The formula provided that 355 samples were required for the present study; however, we collected data from 540 participants for getting more reliable results with greater precision and power.

Sampling Technique

Multistage random sampling was used for selecting our sample from the population. In the first stage, one district was randomly selected from Rajshahi and Rangpur divisions. Rajshahi and Rangpur city corporations were also selected as urban settings. In the second stage, two Upazilas were randomly selected from each selected district. Also, one ward was randomly selected from each Rajshahi and Rangpur city corporation. In the third stage, four unions and two Mohallas were randomly selected from each selected Upazila and each selected ward respectively. In the fourth stage, 47 and 41 aging people were randomly selected from each union and each Mohalla respectively, the total number of participants (8 × 47) = 376 from rural, and from urban (4 × 41) = 164. All necessary information about elderly people was gathered from the respective Union Parishad office and ward council office.

Data Collection Procedure

We contacted our selected subjects and discussed the objectives of our present study, and took their written consent. A standard questionnaire was used to collect information from 540 selected aging people. First, we prepared a draft questionnaire in English and sent it to five experts in health sciences for their suggestions. We finalized the questionnaire through experts' suggestions. Since the mother tongue of the participants was Bangla, the English questionnaire was translated into Bangla, and its meaning was checked by a bilingual expert. Face‐to‐face interviews were conducted by 12 trained interviewers for collecting data, and it was done from June 5, 2022 to August 18, 2022.

Outcome Variable

Morbidity is the outcome variable in this study, and it was measured by a question, “are you suffering from chronic diseases?” Based on the answer, the respondents were classified into three classes; (i) did not have any disease (0 = No), (ii) having only one disease (1 = single), and (iii) having more than one disease (2 = morbidity). Finally, (iii) was considered as morbidity (Yes, code 1), and (i) and (ii) together were considered did not have morbidity (No, code 0).

Independent Variables

The survey questionnaire included gender, place of residence, religion, age, marital status, schooling years, current occupation, current family income (Tk.), living arrangements, family members, smoking habit, disability, self‐rated health status, and body mass index (BMI) as independent variables. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate frequency distribution for categorical variables. χ 2 test was applied to find the association between independent (categorical) variables and outcome variable. Finally, binary logistic regression model was selected to determine the impact of independent factors on morbidity among aging people. The significant associated factors provided by χ 2 test were used as independent variables in the logistic model. The multicollinearity problems among the independent variables of the model were checked by variance inflation factors (VIF), and VIF showed that there was no evidence of multicollinearity problems among independent variables. The goodness of fit and accuracy of the model were checked by Hosmer and Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke R 2, and ROC curve respectively. The adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% Confidence Interval (CI) and p‐value were used to interpret the logistic results. Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software (version IBM 25).

Results

General Characteristics of Aging People

Around 57.00% of the aging population was male, and most of them had a monthly family income of less than Tk. 40,000. Around 70.00% of participants lived in rural areas, and 38.90% were living with their spouse. Approximately 86.00% of the aging populations were Muslim, and more than 64.00% were aged 70 or above. The majority (65.70%) had five or more family members, and more than half were not currently working. About 31.00% of the aging population had a smoking habit, and 25.40% were considered unhealthy. Approximately 59.00% of the aging population was overweight (Table 1).

χ 2 Test

The χ 2 test showed a significant association between morbidity and general characteristics among the aging population. It was observed that gender, place of residence, age, family income, number of family members, disability status, self‐rated health status, and body mass index (BMI) were significantly associated with morbidity at the 5% level of significance (Figure 2).

Regression Analysis

The multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that morbidity was significantly higher among individuals aged 70 years and above [AOR = 1.926; 95% CI = 1.178–3.150; p = 0.009], males [AOR = 1.717; 95% CI = 1.109–2.657; p = 0.015], those living in rural areas [AOR = 1.758; 95% CI = 1.082–2.856; p = 0.023], individuals with a prior disability [AOR = 0.658; 95% CI = 1.126–3.315; p = 0.017]. In addition, morbidity was significantly associated with being underweight [AOR = 4.045; 95% CI = 1.568–10.438; p = 0.004] and overweight [AOR = 1.957; 95% CI = 1.223–3.132; p = 0.005]. Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis indicated no evidence of multicollinearity among the independent variables. The Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test [χ 2 ‐value = 14.039; p = 0.081] suggested that the model was well‐fitted. Additionally, the Nagelkerke R 2 value indicated that the model explained 13.60% of the variation in the outcome variable (Table 2).

Discussion

With advancements in health care and medicine extending life expectancy, morbidity has emerged as a major health burden among the aging population. The findings indicate that more than three‐quarters (75.74%) of the aging population were affected by morbidity. Previous studies conducted in Bangladesh have also reported a high burden of morbidity among aging populations, with prevalence rates of 56.40% [18], 53.80% [19], and 39.00% [20], supporting the consistency of these findings. Similar findings have been reported in other low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including South Africa (69.40%), Ghana (62.10%), and India (51.60%) [5]. However, notable cross‐country variations were observed, with lower prevalence estimates reported in Sub‐Saharan Africa (43.00%) [6] and Scotland (42.20%) [7]. These variations in morbidity prevalence across countries may be attributed to differences in population aging, socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle behavior, access to healthcare services, health system capacity, and variations in disease definitions and measurement methods. The high morbidity burden among older adults in Bangladesh reflects overlapping structural, socioeconomic, and health system constraints distinct from high‐income settings. Limited access to geriatric and chronic care [24] particularly in under‐resourced rural areas combined with inadequate social protection [25], high out‐of‐pocket costs [26, 27], and lifelong exposure to poverty, undernutrition, and physically demanding work increases vulnerability to chronic morbidity in later life [28]. These cumulative disadvantages likely explain the substantially higher morbidity prevalence observed among the aging population in Bangladesh compared with many global contexts.

It was observed that males were at a higher risk of experiencing morbidity. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in China [29], Spain [30], Thailand [31], the Netherlands [32], and India [33]. However, it contrasts with earlier research from Spain [34] and West Asia [35]. This may be because males are more likely to engage in physically demanding work for longer periods and delayed healthcare‐seeking, potentially affecting their long‐term health [24, 36, 37]. Evidence from several European countries and other high‐income settings consistently reports a greater prevalence of morbidity among women [4, 38, 39, 40, 41], largely attributed to longer female life expectancy and higher prevalence of non‐fatal chronic conditions such as musculoskeletal disorders and depression [42]. The divergence observed in our study highlights the importance of contextual and population‐specific factors shaping morbidity patterns.

This research also revealed that aging populations living in rural areas were at a higher risk of morbidity, a finding that aligns with prior evidence from China [43] and Mongolia [44] showing greater morbidity prevalence among rural aging populations, likely due to lower socioeconomic status, poorer access to quality healthcare, and fewer social services in rural settings compared with urban aging populations. However, this rural–urban pattern is not universal; other research has found higher morbidity in urban areas of India [40, 41, 45, 46], with differences potentially arising from urban lifestyle risk factors such as sedentary behavior and better diagnostic access in urban settings, which can elevate reported morbidity rates.

Age was a strong determinant of morbidity, with individuals aged ≥ 70 years experiencing a substantially higher risk. Similar patterns have been reported in Bangladesh [17, 18, 19, 20], where community‐based studies show significantly greater odds of morbidity among middle‐aged and aging populations compared with younger populations, highlighting the pronounced effect of aging in low‐resource settings. It is also seen that over half of populations aged ≥ 60 years had high morbidity globally [10, 38]. Additionally, another global study indicates that adults aged ≥ 70 years face a markedly increased risk of morbidity in Eastern Nepal [47] and Malaysia [48], reflecting biological aging processes and cumulative exposure to chronic disease risk factors. However, another study conducted in China [49] showed that there is no significant difference in overall survival among aged groups of aging populations.

Furthermore, individuals with disabilities were found to be at greater risk of experiencing morbidity, as well documented in Scotland [7], Bangladesh [20], China [43], Malaysia [48] and Italy [50]. The progression of disability often accelerates as the number of chronic conditions increases.

This study also found that individuals with abnormal body mass index (BMI) had a higher likelihood of morbidity, which is consistent with previous research conducted in Malaysia [48], Italy [50] and India [51]. This association may be linked to limited public awareness regarding proper nutrition, regular physical activity, and weight management.

Conclusions

The findings of this study provide insights into the high prevalence of morbidity in Bangladesh, highlighting variations between rural and urban areas. This research identified various socioeconomic, demographic, and health‐related factors that contribute to this phenomenon. Factors such as gender, place of residence, age, disability, and body mass index (BMI) appear to influence morbidity. Morbidity findings will assist in guiding focused healthcare policies and actions in order to better meet the demands of the aging population and achieve better health outcomes and sustainable healthcare systems.

Strengths and Limitations

One of the major strengths of this study was that there was no prior research that had specifically focused on morbidity among aging populations in Northern Bangladesh. However, the study had several limitations. First, there may be respondent bias, as the findings were based on self‐reported data. Second, the cross‐sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish causality; a longitudinal approach could yield more accurate and robust results. Third, the assessment of chronic diseases relied on self‐reports, along with available prescriptions and medications, which may affect the accuracy of the findings. Finally, the study was limited to quantitative analysis and did not explore the qualitative aspects of aging populations' health‐related quality of life.
