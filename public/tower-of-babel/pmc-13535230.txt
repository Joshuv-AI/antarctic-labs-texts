Medicaid payment rates for substance use disorder treatment—design, data, and lack of transparency
Authors: Kayla N Tormohlen, Erika L Crable, Emma E McGinty
Published: 2026

Introduction

As the largest payer of substance use disorder (SUD) services, Medicaid plays a key role in SUD treatment delivery in the United States. Low Medicaid provider payment contributes to undertreatment of SUD.1 Insurers reimburse SUD services at lower rates than many other health care services, and rates are lowest in Medicaid relative to Medicare or commercial insurance.1 Low payment disincentivizes clinicians from specializing in addiction and limits the volume of Medicaid patients treated.1 Growing interest in implementing and evaluating improved Medicaid payments is stymied by complex payment methodologies and limited data. This study aimed to understand how states set Medicaid payment rates for SUD providers and explore the utility of publicly available data for characterizing rates.

Methods

We conducted semistructured qualitative interviews with state leaders with Medicaid expertise in 7 states (AK, CO, MA, ME, MI, WA, WV). The SUD provider payment rate setting and transparency vary due to state Medicaid program characteristics: (1) whether the state operates a fee-for-service (FFS) program or contracts with managed care organizations (MCOs) to administer Medicaid and (2) in MCO arrangements, whether SUD benefits are “carved out” into FFS or a specialized health plan. We selected states with variation on these factors (Supplement 1). State Medicaid experts were identified via state websites and purposively recruited via email. Additional experts were identified through snowball sampling. Further methodological details are reported in the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) checklist (Supplement 2).

Semistructured interviews were conducted via videoconference from March to May 2025, with a median length of 44 minutes. Interview questions (Supplement 3) focused on how state Medicaid programs set and structure SUD payment rates, and how published fee schedules should be interpreted. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed for analysis, with identifying information redacted. Interviews were conducted until no new themes emerged and were analyzed using a conventional content analysis approach (see Supplement 4 for coding tree). This research was approved by the Weill Cornell Medicine Institutional Review Board.

Results

We conducted interviews with 17 experts in selected states (Supplement 1). Three key themes, summarized below, were identified. Box 1 includes illustrative quotes.

States have heterogeneous and opaque processes for setting Medicaid SUD provider payment rates

“It starts off with a process to establish which services we are going to address each year because Medicaid covers a lot of services, and we have to decide each year how many of these services are we going to do a rate-setting exercise, or rate determination for. Once something is about to go into a rate determination, we review, how other payers are paying for the same service, Medicare and other state Medicaid agencies or other payers, if that's relevant to the service. We review whether there are alternative payment models that might allow us to incentivize value within the service models, and not just looking at pay-per-service rates, but adding elements of value-based payment to those rates. We always look at the service itself to see if it's well-defined. It's hard to set a rate for a service if you don’t know what the service is, so that comes into play with some services more than others. We look at whether there's a Medicare or other appropriate benchmark from another payer that we could just base our payments off of, instead of doing our own exercise to set the rates, like if someone's already done it and it's widely accepted.” (Expert 5-1)

“Different fees are set with different methodologies. For most substance use treatment services, the rates are recommended by the Office of Rate Review and based on provider reported costs. These recommendations are accepted and implemented by the Division of Behavioral Health.” (Expert 7-1)

State Medicaid fee schedules often do not reflect actual provider payments

Medicaid managed care program: “There's huge variation. I think some MCOs use their own internal fee for service fee schedule and other MCOs have different sorts of arrangements with providers. It could be something like a budget-based arrangement where they pay a portion of the provider's costs or it could be based on some kind of capitated amount they’ve developed. I think in some instances there's a bit of a tangential connection to what we pay in the fee for service side, but for behavioral health, I would say for the most part there's not a direct connection between what we do on our fee for service side and how the MCOs choose a contract with their providers. There is a lot of variation in how they structure those contracts.” (Expert 3-1)

Medicaid fee-for-service (FFS) program: “…for example, the opioid health homes and the maternal model have performance-based payments, which put 4% of the payment at risk pending performance on a set of performance measures. I actually don’t know, what's posted, whether it's with the 4% or without the 4%, and which dollar amount is on the fee schedule; that wouldn’t be captured. For hospitals and hospital-based professionals, in addition to getting paid off the fee schedule, we will cost settle their services to a percentage of cost that they report through a cost reporting process with us.” (Expert 5-1)

Medicaid provider payment has SUD-specific nuances

“Our 1115 Waiver Program bundles services into a daily rate (per diem) for residential and partial hospital program services. If the full array of services is not met for the per diem, providers can bill the component services that were provided. Under the 1115 Waivers (behavioral health and SUD) some rates are higher than comparable services allowed under the State Plan for providers who do not enroll as an 1115 Waiver Provider.” (Expert 7-1)

“High level, what's carved in are services where the need is of higher intensity. The low intensity services would be typically something that's outpatient therapy. For therapy, what we call more of the mild to moderate behavioral health, is not included.” (Expert 4-1)

States have heterogenous and opaque processes for setting Medicaid SUD provider payment rates

Rate-setting methodologies varied within and across states. Experts discussed using formulas developed for Medicare, including a resource-based relative value scale for clinician payment or diagnosis-related groups for hospital payment, benchmarking to Medicare or commercial rates, using provider-reported costs, or combined approaches.

State Medicaid fee schedules often do not reflect actual provider payments

State Medicaid agencies establish payment methodologies for covered services and publish FFS rates on state websites. Experts emphasized that fees in published schedules often do not reflect actual provider payment. In Medicaid FFS programs, payment may be adjusted to account for geographic needs, site of service, or patient mix. States may provide supplemental payments, often as a lump sum that makes up the difference between the rate listed in the fee schedule and the Medicare or average commercial rate. A state may have a Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS)–approved waiver to use an alternative payment model that alters payment structure (eg, bundled or capitated rather than FFS) or uses value-based approaches with incentive payments tied to performance metrics.

In managed care programs, states contract with MCOs to administer Medicaid. States develop capitation rates that are actuarially sound, meaning they should cover all relevant contractual and operational costs. States can leverage several mechanisms to influence MCO arrangements and payment. For example, state-directed payments require contracted MCOs to pay providers specific rates, often by setting a minimum. Medicaid MCOs then negotiate rates with network providers. Experts emphasized the significance of the MCO negotiation process in determining actual provider payment. They noted that fees in the FFS schedule may serve as a floor for negotiated rates, but not necessarily, and that MCO contracts are the most informative data source for understanding payment but are often not publicly available.

Medicaid provider payment has SUD-specific nuances

Medicaid covers some unique SUD services not covered by Medicare or commercial insurance precluding benchmarking rates against other payers. SUD services are commonly “carved out” from Medicaid MCOs into FFS or a specialized health plan. Experts discussed the significant impact of section 1115 waivers focusing on SUD, many of which include payment innovations.

Discussion

Results from interviewers with state Medicaid experts showed variation in state payment rate-setting processes for SUD providers and exposed limited utility of published fee schedules for interpretation of actual provider payment. While some of the rate-setting complexities discussed by experts have been described elsewhere,2 to our knowledge a comprehensive overview in the context of Medicaid SUD provider payment does not exist, a gap this study addresses. Information needed to accurately characterize Medicaid SUD payment rates is not publicly available. Improved transparency is needed to understand how changing Medicaid provider payments can improve care access, quality, and outcomes among patients with SUD.

The CMS Transparency in Coverage initiative3 requires commercial payers to disclose detailed payment data but does not apply to Medicaid. New CMS rules4,5 effective July 2026 make incremental improvements. First, states are required to publish Medicaid fee schedules that denote FFS adjustments by factors such as geography and provider type. Second, states are required to report Medicaid rates comparisons—for FFS and managed care—for behavioral health, primary care, and obstetrics and gynecology services relative to Medicare FFS rates. While states will directly compare Medicaid FFS with Medicare rates, Medicaid MCOs are not required to disclose negotiated rates for these services. Rather, MCOs will report how their total Medicaid payments for each service type compares with Medicare FFS.

Limitations

Interviews were conducted with a convenience sample, and findings could be limited by self-selection of experts willing to participate. To ensure rigor, increase reproducibility, and avoid researcher bias in results interpretation, we used a common interview guide and standard analysis protocol.

Conclusion

State processes for SUD payment rate setting vary widely and published fee schedules have limited utility for interpretation of actual SUD provider payment. Improved transparency is needed to study whether and how changing Medicaid provider payments can improve SUD treatment access and patient outcomes.
