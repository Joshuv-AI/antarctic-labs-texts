Implicit Bias and Convergence of Matrix Stochastic Mirror Descent
Danil Akhtiamov ∗, Reza Ghane ∗, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
We investigate Stochastic Mirror Descent (SMD) with matrix parameters and vector-valued predictions, a framework relevant to multi-class classification and matrix completion problems. Focusing on the overparameterized regime, where the total number of parameters exceeds the number of training samples, we prove that SMD with matrix mirror functions ψ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \psi(\cdot) converges exponentially to a global interpolator. Furthermore, we generalize classical implicit bias results of vector SMD by demonstrating that the matrix SMD algorithm converges to the unique solution minimizing the Bregman divergence induced by ψ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \psi(\cdot) from initialization subject to interpolating the data. These findings reveal how matrix mirror maps dictate inductive bias in high-dimensional, multi-output problems.

I
Introduction

The choice of optimization algorithm plays a crucial role in determining not only convergence speed but also the properties of learned models in overparameterized machine learning. While gradient descent and its variants have become the main workhorse in large-scale optimization, recent theoretical insights reveal that the geometry induced by different optimizers leads to fundamentally different solutions. This phenomenon, known as implicit bias, has sparked renewed interest in understanding how algorithmic choices shape the learning process beyond mere convergence guarantees.

Stochastic mirror descent (SMD) generalizes standard gradient descent by performing updates in a dual space induced by a mirror map
\nabla\psi
, where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is a strongly convex potential function. Specifically, for a given training loss
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\mathbf{w}_{t})
computed with respect to the batch sampled at the time step
t
, step size
\eta
, and parameters
\mathbf{w}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
at iteration
t
, SMD performs the following update:

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(\mathbf{w}_{t+1})=\nabla\psi(\mathbf{w}_{t})-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\mathbf{w}_{t})

(1)

Setting
\psi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
recovers standard SGD. The power of this framework lies in its flexibility: different potential functions
\psi
encode different geometries into the optimization dynamics. For overparameterized problems where multiple solutions interpolate the training data perfectly, SMD exhibits an implicit bias property - it converges to the solution minimizing the Bregman divergence
D_{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{0})
to the initialization
\mathbf{w}_{0}
among all global minimizers, where

D_{\psi}(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}_{0})=\psi(\mathbf{w})-\psi(\mathbf{w}_{0})-\nabla\psi(\mathbf{w}_{0})^{T}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}_{0})

In particular, when initialized near zero
\mathbf{w}_{0}\approx 0
, SMD converges to the interpolator minimizing
\psi(\mathbf{w})
among all interpolating solutions. This
implicit bias property
of stochastic mirror descent implies that SGD finds the minimal
\ell_{2}
-norm solution among interpolators.

Various works have established convergence and implicit bias for linear models with vector parameters and scalar labels, capturing the linear regression and linear classification tasks

. In particular,

[

proved the convergence of vector Stochastic Mirror Descent (SMD). More recently,
[

]
established an exponential convergence rate for this setting. Furthermore,
[

]
extended the analysis of
[

]

[

]
regarding classification margins from gradient descent to the mirror descent framework. However, these works treat parameters as unstructured vectors, potentially missing geometric properties encoded in their matrix representation. We therefore extend this framework to matrix weights and vector outputs, motivated by the observation that a plethora of problems in modern signal processing and data science, such as the matrix completion problem, are naturally formulated as problems of finding matrices satisfying certain structural properties. Namely, we consider the following update rule for
\mathbf{W}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
, which we refer to as Matrix SMD:

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{t})=\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})

After establishing convergence and implicit bias guarantees, we demonstrate a practical application of the matrix SMD. Using the mirror function
\psi(\mathbf{W})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{W})^{p}
, where
\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{W})
denotes the
i
-th singular value of
\mathbf{W}
, we fit a linear model to solve the matrix completion problem. Setting
p\approx 1
to approximate the nuclear norm yields a low-rank solution, which is a standard hypothesis for the matrix completion task. We demonstrate empirically that Matrix SMD leads to a lower error than standard singular value thresholding methods, which are usually used for minimizing the nuclear norm in practice.

II
Notation and Problem Formulation

II-A

The problem

We consider the problem of recovering a matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
subject to linear constraints.

Definition 1

(Linear Constraint System)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a linear operator with matrix representation
\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{a}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{p})^{T}
where each
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is treated as a vector. The constraint system is:

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{b}\quad\text{equivalently}\quad\mathbf{A}\textbf{vec}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{b}

where
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a known vector. In the current exposition, we assume that
d\times k>p
, a regime commonly referred to as the overparameterized regime.

Example 1

(Matrix Completion)
.

In matrix recovery, we observe a subset
\Omega=\{(i_{1},j_{1}),\dots,(i_{p},j_{p})\}\subset[d]\times[k]
of matrix entries. Here:

•

p=|\Omega|
is the number of observed entries

•

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})_{q}=\mathbf{W}_{i_{q},j_{q}}
extracts the
(i_{q},j_{q})
-th entry

•

\mathbf{b}
contains the observed values at positions
\Omega

As this problem is overparameterized (there is a linear space of valid solutions), we need additional hypotheses regarding
\mathbf{W}
. A common assumption is that
\mathbf{W}
is low-rank
[
9
]
.

Example 2

(Multi-class Linear Classification)
.

Given
n
data points
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with one-hot labels
\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

•

\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{c(i)}
where
c(i)\in[k]
is the class of point
i
.

•

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{W}^{(j)}
computes the prediction for class
j
.

•

The constraint
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})=(\mathbf{Y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Y}_{n})
ensures perfect interpolation.

II-B

Optimization Framework

We propose to minimize the empirical risk with a novel algorithm, which we call Matrix Stochastic Mirror Descent, that can be described as follows:

Definition 2

(Training Objective)
.

The loss function takes the form:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\ell_{i}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)

where each
\ell_{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a convex loss function.

Definition 3

(Matrix Stochastic Mirror Descent)
.

Given a strongly convex mirror potential
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\to\mathbb{R}
, the SMD update rule is:

\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{t})=\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})-\eta\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\mathcal{L}_{t}(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})

where
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is the loss on a random batch sampled at iteration
t
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{t}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\ell_{i_{j}}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})_{i_{j}}-\mathbf{b}_{i_{j}}\right)

(2)

II-C

Mathematical Preliminaries

Before stating our main results, we remind the key definitions on convexity:

Definition 4

(Matrix Convexity Properties)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is:

1.

Convex
if
f(\theta\mathbf{U}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{V})\leq\theta f(\mathbf{U})+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf{V})
for all
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}
and
\theta\in[0,1]

2.

Strictly convex
if the inequality is strict for
\theta\in(0,1)

3.

\mu
-strongly convex
if

f(\mathbf{V})\geq f(\mathbf{U})+\text{Tr}(\nabla f(\mathbf{U})^{T}(\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{U}))+\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{V}\|_{F}^{2}

We will also make extensive use of the following definition of Bregman Divergence:

Definition 5

(Matrix Bregman Divergence)
.

For a strictly convex differentiable mirror function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, the Bregman divergence is:

D_{\psi}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})=\psi(\mathbf{U})-\psi(\mathbf{V})-\text{Tr}(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{V})^{T}(\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{V}))

The Schatten
p
-norms will serve as the main illustrating example of the matrix mirrors for us:

Definition 6

(Schatten Norm)
.

The Schatten
p
-norm of
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
is:

\|\mathbf{W}\|_{\text{Schatten},p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\min(k,d)}\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{W})^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

where
\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{W})\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\min(k,d)}(\mathbf{W})\geq 0
are the singular values.

III
Main Results and Applications

We will require the following assumptions. Note that, contrary to most other works in the literature, we do not require the
L
-smoothness condition, thus relaxing the common assumptions even for the case of vector weights.

Assumptions 1
.

1.

The mirror
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and
\nu
-strongly convex for a
\nu>0
.

2.

The training loss is of the form

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\ell_{i}\left({\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})}_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)

Moreover,
\ell_{i}
is non-negative, has minimum
\ell_{i}(0)=0
, has a derivative
\ell_{i}^{\prime}
which is continuous at
0
and is
\mu
-strictly convex for a
\mu>0
.

3.

The batches chosen for (
2
) are chosen in such a way that
\mathbb{E}\mathcal{L}_{t}=\mathcal{L}
, where the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness in the choice of the batch.

4.

\eta>0
is small enough, so that
\psi-\eta\mathcal{L}_{t}
is convex.

5.

The matrix
\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{a}_{p}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d\times k)\times p}
satisfies
\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})>0
. Note that, in particular, this implies that we are in the overparameterized regime, i.e. that
p<dk
.

6.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{\ast}
be the unique minimizer of the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{W}}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}_{0})\text{, s.t. }\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{b}

(3)

Denote

\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{W}:D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W})\leq D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{0})\}

Then there exists a
C>0
, such that the following holds for all
\mathbf{W}\in\mathcal{B}
:

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\psi(\mathbf{W})\|_{op}\leq C

We are now ready to state our main result characterizing the implicit bias and the convergence rate of Matrix SMD:

Theorem 1

(Convergence Rate and the Implicit Bias)
.

Assume that the linear operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the mirror
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\to\mathbb{R}
and the training losses
\mathcal{L}_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy assumptions 1-4 from the list of Assumptions
1
, whose notation is also embraced below. Introduce
\mathbf{W}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
as the unique minimizer of the following objective:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{W}}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}_{0})\text{, s.t. }\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{b}

(4)

Denote the
t
-th iteration of the SMD algorithm defined via (
2
) with mirror
\psi
trained to minimize
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})
and initialized at
\mathbf{W}_{0}
by
\mathbf{W}_{t}
.
Then
\mathbf{W}_{t}
converges to
\mathbf{W}_{*}
as
t\to\infty
. Assume, in addition, that assumption 5 and 6 from the list of Assumptions
1
holds as well and let

L:=\max_{\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{D_{\psi}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})}{\|\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{V}\|_{F}^{2}}

Then
\mathbf{W}_{t}
converges to
\mathbf{W}_{*}
exponentially, namely the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{W}_{*}-\mathbf{W}_{t}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\nu}\left(1-\frac{\eta\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2pL}\right)^{t}D_{\psi}\left(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{0}\right)

(5)

Here, the expectation is taken with respect to the randomness in the batch choice at the step
i
for all
i=1,\dots,t
.

Remark 1
.

To ensure (
5
) implies exponential convergence, one must verify that the value of
L
is finite. This is true as
\mathcal{B}
is compact and
\frac{D_{\psi}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V})}{\|\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{V}\|_{F}^{2}}
remains bounded as
\mathbf{U}\to\mathbf{V}
because of the assumption 6) from the list of Assumptions
1
.

Remark 2
.

Assumption 5 always holds for the matrix completion problem from Example
1
. For the multi-class linear classification from Example
2
, we have
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{X}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{k}
and assumption 5 holds if and only if
\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{X})>0
.

Example 3
.

\psi(\mathbf{W})=\|\mathbf{W}\|^{p}_{\text{Schatten},p}+\nu\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}
satisfies all of the Assumptions
1
if
p\geq 2
. Hence, we can deduce exponential convergence in this case.

Example 4
.

As for
2>p>1
,
\psi(\mathbf{W})=\|\mathbf{W}\|^{p}_{\text{Schatten},p}+\nu\|\mathbf{W}\|_{F}^{2}
satisfies assumptions 1-5 from the list of the Assumptions
1
. It also satisfies assumption 6 if and only if
\mathcal{B}
does not contain singular matrices. Thus, in general, we can deduce convergence for
2>p>1
but cannot specify the rate.

IV-B

Implicit Bias

Summing up the SMD iterations, we note that

\displaystyle\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{t})-\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{0})=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathbf{W}_{s})

Consider the following optimization problem, whose solution

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}_{0})

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}}\max_{\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}_{0})+\bm{\lambda}^{T}(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})-\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W})-\nabla\psi(\mathbf{W}_{0})=\mathbf{A}^{T}\bm{\lambda}\\
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{b}\end{cases}

where

Lemma 5
.

The following holds:

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\eta\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2pL}\right)\mathbb{E}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})\geq\mathbb{E}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t})

Proof.

It suffices to show the following due to Lemma
3
:

\displaystyle\eta\mathbb{E}D_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})\geq\frac{\eta\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2pL}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})

Since the expectation is taken over the randomness in the SMD batch, the latter is equivalent to:

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})\geq\frac{\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2pL}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})

By strong convexity of
\ell_{i}
,
D_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}D_{\ell_{i}}(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W}_{*})_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{i})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\mu}{2}(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W}_{*})_{i}-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2p}\|\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W}_{*})-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W}_{t-1})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu}{2p}\|\mathbf{A}\textbf{vec}(\mathbf{P})\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2p}\|\mathbf{P}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2p}\|\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{W}_{t-1}-\mathbf{W}_{t-1}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2pL}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{W}_{t-1},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu\sigma_{p}(\mathbf{A})^{2}}{2pL}D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W}_{*},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})

Note that in the last line above we used that
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{W}_{t-1})=\mathbf{b}
and that
\mathbf{W}_{*}
minimizes
D_{\psi}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{W}_{t-1})
with respect to the constraint
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{W})=\mathbf{b}
.
∎

VI
Conclusion

This paper extends the theory of stochastic mirror descent to matrix parameters and vector-valued outputs, providing both theoretical guarantees and practical benefits.

Our experiments on matrix completion validate the practical value of this framework. Schatten-
p
mirror descent with
p\approx 1
outperforms standard proximal methods based on singular value thresholding ran with the same number of epochs, particularly in challenging low-sampling regimes, by naturally inducing low-rank structure through the geometry of the mirror map rather than explicit constraints.

While our analysis establishes convergence for SMD with
1<p<2
, proving exponential rates in this regime requires relaxing assumption 6 in the list of Assumptions
1
. This remains an important direction for future work.
