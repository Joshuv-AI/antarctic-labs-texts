Prevalence, clinical features, and seasonal patterns of SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and HRSV in patients with a history of kidney transplantation in Iran
Authors: Sara Heydari Sirat, Somayeh Shatizadeh Malekshahi, Mohammad Reza Jabbari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory infections pose a significant threat to immunocompromised individuals, particularly kidney transplant recipients, whose immunosuppressive therapy compromises their immune response and increases their susceptibility to various pathogens. The most common symptoms were weakness (n = 87, 62%, 95% CI: 54%–70%), fever (n = 75, 54%, 95% CI: 45%–62%), and chills. (n = 63, 45%, 95% CI: 37%–54%) Prednisone and mycophenolate mofetil were the most commonly used immunosuppressive drugs (n = 125, 89%, 95% CI: 82%–93% each). Introduction

Respiratory viruses are among the most prevalent human pathogens, accounting for a significant proportion of global mortality. These viruses spread rapidly within populations, causing acute infections and, in vulnerable groups, severe or chronic disease [1, 2]. They are characterized by relatively short incubation periods and share a common mode of transmission from person to person [3]. A major challenge for the medical community is the similarity of clinical symptoms among individuals infected with different respiratory viruses, which complicates accurate diagnosis based solely on clinical presentation [4]. Common symptoms include fever, chills, myalgia, dry cough, malaise, dyspnea, and headache, while less frequent symptoms comprise sore throat, rhinorrhea, nausea, vomiting, and dizziness [5]. Respiratory infections pose a significant threat to immunocompromised individuals, particularly kidney transplant recipients. These patients are typically maintained on immunosuppressive therapy to prevent organ rejection, which substantially impairs their immune response. This increases their susceptibility to infections, including respiratory illnesses caused by diverse pathogens [6]. Among various pathogens, human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV), SARS-CoV-2, and influenza virus are of particular concern due to their potential to cause severe respiratory illness. Timely detection of these viruses is essential to support healthcare providers and public health officials in effective disease management. HRSV, also known as human orthopneumovirus (HOPV), is a common cause of pulmonary infection in children and has increasingly been recognized as a significant respiratory pathogen in older adults and immunocompromised individuals. HRSV is a negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the Pneumoviridae family [7]. It is a leading cause of hospitalization and mortality in infants under one year of age worldwide. While most HRSV infections present as upper respiratory tract illness, some cases progress to lower respiratory tract disease, primarily manifesting as bronchiolitis [8]. Globally, HRSV is estimated to cause approximately 33 million cases of lower respiratory tract illnesses, three million hospitalizations, and up to 199,000 deaths in children annually [9]. SARS-CoV-2, first identified in China in December 2019, rapidly spread worldwide, prompting the WHO to declare COVID-19 a global pandemic on March 11, 2020 [10]. To date, the virus has caused over 704 million confirmed cases and more than 7 million deaths globally, including over 7 million cases and 140,000 deaths reported in Iran (https://www.worldometers.info/coronavirus/). SARS-CoV-2 is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the Coronaviridae family and is responsible for 5% to 10% of acute respiratory infections [11, 12]. The clinical spectrum of COVID-19 ranges from asymptomatic infections to severe manifestations such as respiratory failure, septic shock, and multiorgan failure [13]. During the early stages of the pandemic, coinfections with influenza were common, with an average rate of 16.3% [4]. Public health measures implemented to limit the spread of SARS-CoV-2 effectively suppressed influenza circulation during the 2020–2021 season; however, influenza has since reemerged as COVID-19 restrictions have been relaxed. Influenza viruses belong to the Orthomyxoviridae family and possess a genome composed of single-stranded, negative-sense, segmented RNA. A key characteristic of influenza viruses is their rapid evolution, resulting in significant genetic variability [14, 15]. The severity and duration of illness, affected age groups, and rates of complications such as hospitalization and mortality vary between influenza seasons [16]. Kidney transplantation is performed to extend and improve the lives of people with end-stage kidney disease [17]. However, these patients are at increased risk of infections due to the immunosuppressive therapies required for managing health risks [18]. Monitoring specific populations, such as transplant recipients, is essential for managing health risks and optimizing treatment outcomes. Preparedness, vaccination, and data collection are critical for preventing and controlling influenza and other viral infections in these vulnerable groups.

Seasonal trend analysis of respiratory viruses is important in Iran due to its diverse climate zones, from temperate to arid regions. These climatic variations are key factors affecting the transmission dynamics and epidemiology of respiratory viruses. Studies in Iran have shown associations between climate factors and respiratory infection incidence, including influenza-like illness and SARS-CoV-2. Understanding these seasonal patterns enables better prediction of epidemic peaks, informs timely public health interventions, and clinical management strategies. This is especially critical for vulnerable populations, and frequent healthcare interactions increase their risk of severe respiratory infections [19]. Limited comprehensive data exist on respiratory viral infections among kidney transplant recipients in Iran, a population vulnerable due to immunosuppression. This study aims to fill crucial knowledge gaps to inform public health interventions and improve clinical management in this setting.

This study aims to investigate the epidemiology, clinical presentation, and seasonal trends of SARS-CoV-2, HRSV, and influenza in samples obtained from patients with a history of kidney transplantation who were clinically diagnosed with respiratory infections. Understanding the prevalence and impact of these infections will facilitate improved clinical management and the development of more effective preventive strategies for this high-risk population.

Methods

Study design and population

In this cross-sectional study, throat and nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected from hospitalized patients referring to Labbafinejad Hospital in Tehran between November 2023 to February 2025. The study samples included respiratory samples from patients with a history of kidney transplantation who were 18 years of age or older and had been diagnosed by a physician with acute respiratory infections (ARIs). Eligible patients presented with an acute onset of respiratory symptoms and at least two of the following: fever (> 38 °C), cough, sore throat, fatigue, dyspnea, nausea, diarrhea, and myalgia. Individuals under 18 years of age were excluded to maintain a more uniform adult cohort. The entire available patient population during the study period was included. Demographic data, including age, gender, medications used, and clinical symptoms, were recorded. Demographic characteristics, medical history, laboratory results, and symptoms of all enrolled patients were extracted from hospital medical records. This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University (IR.TMU. REC.1403.131). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

RNA extraction

Viral ribonucleic acid (RNA) extraction was performed using the RNJiA kit (ROJE, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The protocol involves lysing the sample with a buffer to release RNA and inactivate RNases, adding ethanol to facilitate RNA binding to the silica membrane, loading the mixture onto the spin column, washing the column to eliminate impurities, and finally eluting pure RNA with nuclease-free water or elution buffer.

Molecular identification

Molecular identification and detection of SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and HRSV were performed using the GA SARSFlu and HRSV OneStep RT-PCR kits (Ben Da Faravar Co., Iran; Cat. No. GA-SARSFluRSV.100). This kit enables simultaneous detection of SARS-CoV-2 (FAM), influenza (Texas Red), and HRSV (Cy5) with RNase P (HEX) as the internal control via the TaqMan Real-Time PCR method. The assay used in this study does not differentiate between influenza A and B subtypes nor HRSV A and B subtypes. The primer and probe mix targets conserved regions of the N and ORF10 genes of SARS-CoV-2, the M gene region in the influenza virus, and the L gene region in HRSV. An internal control targeting the endogenous RNase P gene RNA is included to verify sample collection, RNA extraction, and the quality of the extracted RNA, thereby minimizing false-negative results. For each reaction, 10 µl of RNA template was added to 10 µl of GA SARSFlu & RSV Mastermix (labeled with FAM, HEX, Texas Red, CY5). Positive and negative controls were included in separate PCR tubes. Real-time PCR was carried out using QIAGEN Rotor-Gene® Q Real-Time PCR System (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of reverse transcription at 53 °C for 15 min, initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 46 amplification cycles of 5 s at 94 °C and 25 s at 60 °C. The assay’s limits of detection (LoD) are 10 copies for SARS-CoV-2, 50 copies for influenza virus, and 200 copies for RSV. A sample was considered positive when the virus-specific fluorescence channel yielded a Cq < 40. The internal control was acceptable when RNase P Cq < 37. Samples showing borderline amplification (Cq 37–40) were re-tested to confirm results. The assay demonstrated a diagnostic sensitivity exceeding 98% in repeated tests with a 20 µL reaction volume, and maintained stable performance across a dynamic and linear range of 10¹ to 10¹⁰ genomic copies. The reaction efficiency across target channels ranged from 92% to 97%, confirming the precision, accuracy, and reproducibility of the kit in detecting the target respiratory viruses.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and clinical data. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages with 95% confidence interval (95% CI) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, with the choice of test determined by expected cell counts (Fisher’s exact was used when expected counts were less than five). Normality of the variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the Welch two-sample t-test. For all hypothesis tests, exact p-values are reported. Given the large number of symptom by virus comparisons, these analyses are considered exploratory, and no formal correction for multiple testing was applied. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.1. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients with a history of kidney transplantation clinically diagnosed with respiratory infection

In this study, throat and nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected from 141 kidney transplant recipients who were clinically diagnosed with respiratory infections. The mean age of participants was 45.16 years (SD ± 14.33), with the largest proportion belonging to the 35–49 year age group (n = 48, 34%, 95% CI: 26%–43%). Regarding gender distribution, 58 patients (41%, 95% CI: 33%–50%) were female and 83 (59%, 95% CI: 50%–67%) were male. A broad spectrum of clinical symptoms was observed. The most frequently reported symptom was weakness (n = 87, 62%, 95% CI: 54%–70%), followed by fever (n = 75, 54%, 95% CI: 45%–62%), chills (n = 63, 45%, 95% CI: 37%–54%), and nausea (n = 42, 30%, 95% CI: 23%–38%). Comparison of demographic and clinical characteristics in patients with a history of kidney transplantation with and without transplant rejection

Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between 6 patients with transplant rejection and 135 patients without rejection. The mean age of patients without kidney transplant rejection was 45 years, compared to 47 years in those with transplant rejection (p = 0.9). Among patients without rejection, 58% were male and 42% female, whereas in the rejection group, males comprised 83% and females 17% (p = 0.4). The prevalence of SARS-CoV-2 infection was 13% in patients without transplant rejection and 17% in those with rejection (p = 0.6). Influenza infection was detected in 8.1% of patients without rejection and 33% of those with rejection (p = 0.1). Regarding immunosuppressive therapy, prednisone was used by 88% of patients without rejection and 100% of those with rejection (p > 0.9). Similarly, Prograf was administered to 84% of patients without rejection and 100% of patients with rejection (p > 0.9). Clinically, weakness was the most common symptom in both groups, reported in 61% of patients without rejection and 83% of those with rejection (p = 0.4). Myalgia and nausea were observed in 24% and 30% of patients without rejection, and 50% and 17% of patients with rejection, respectively (p = 0.2 for myalgia; p = 0.7 for nausea). Among underlying conditions, end-stage renal disease (ESRD) was present in 34% of patients without rejection and 50% of those with rejection (p = 0.4), while hypertension was observed in 36% and 67%, respectively (p = 0.2). Diabetes was reported in 27% of patients without rejection but in none of the patients with rejection (p = 0.3). Additionally, 22% of patients without rejection and 17% of patients with rejection had no underlying disease (p > 0.9) (Table 6).

Monthly trends of SARS-CoV-2 and influenza positive cases from November 2023 to February 2025

The study analyzed the monthly distribution of viral infections based on sample collection dates. SARS-CoV-2 positive cases showed a sharp increase in February and March 2024, accounting for 30.4% and 35.7% of total cases in those months, respectively. However, it declined significantly in April 2024 and reached zero by January 2025. Influenza cases demonstrated a relatively stable trend throughout the study period, with noticeable increases observed in November 2023 and December 2024. Notably, no cases of either SARS-CoV-2 were reported in January 2025 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The current study found SARS-CoV-2 to be the most prevalent respiratory virus (13%), followed by influenza (9.2%) and a very low prevalence of HRSV (0.7%) among patients with a history of kidney transplantation. This aligns with findings from a multicenter study involving 377 solid organ transplant recipients, where SARS-CoV-2 and influenza were also the predominant viruses detected, with HRSV less common in adult transplant populations [20]. The low detection of HRSV is consistent with the known epidemiology that HRSV primarily affects pediatric and certain immunocompromised groups more severely, while adult transplant recipients show lower incidence rates [21]. Studies comparing COVID-19 and influenza in transplant and immunocompromised populations have similarly noted that systemic symptoms such as myalgia and chills are more pronounced in COVID-19, while respiratory symptoms like cough are more typical of influenza [22, 23].

Odongo F et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study in São Paulo to examine the characteristics and outcomes of influenza infection among kidney transplant recipients. During the study period, thirty-one patients developed influenza A infection, corresponding to an incidence of 26.5 cases per 1,000 person-years. Rates of hospitalization (68%), secondary bacterial pneumonia (68%), intensive care unit admission (14%), and mortality (14%) were higher than those observed in immunocompetent patients. These findings suggest that kidney transplant recipients are more susceptible to viral infections and are more likely to experience severe outcomes [24]. Monfared et al. (2020) studied 22 kidney transplant recipients hospitalized with SARS-CoV-2 pneumonia in Rasht, Iran. Patients commonly presented with cough, shortness of breath, fever, and chills. Treatment included immunosuppression, high-dose corticosteroids, and antiviral therapy. More than half developed acute kidney injury (AKI) during hospitalization, and 27% died after a median of 12 days. The study highlighted a high prevalence of AKI and mortality among kidney transplant recipients with COVID-19 [25]. In a study by Elias et al. (2020), 66 (5%) of 1,216 kidney transplant recipients developed COVID-19 during an 8-week follow-up at two transplant centers in Paris, a rate significantly higher than that of the general French population (0.3%) [26]. Rahimzadeh et al. conducted a retrospective study in Iran to investigate the incidence of COVID-19 and associated risk factors among kidney transplant recipients. The study population included individuals who underwent kidney transplant surgery at Sina Hospital in Tehran between March 1996 and January 2021. Patient information was collected via telephone interviews and recorded using a questionnaire. Data from 523 kidney transplant recipients were analyzed, of whom 108 were diagnosed with COVID-19. Among those who tested positive, a higher proportion were women (43% vs. 32%), they had a higher mean age (46.9 ± 13.6 years vs. 43.7 ± 13.4 years), and a greater prevalence of diabetes was observed. Malaise, cough, and fever were the most commonly reported symptoms in this group [27]. In our study, the most common underlying conditions were hypertension (37%), chronic renal failure (35%), and diabetes (26%) (Table 3). Regarding clinical symptoms, fever and chills (72%), myalgia (67%), cough (33%), and shortness of breath (28%) were the most frequently observed (Fig. 1). Additionally, we found that the incidence of SARS-CoV-2 and influenza was higher in patients with kidney transplant failure, suggesting that this group may have an increased susceptibility to viral infections. Studies by Cheng Y et al. (2021) [28] and Bernardo Marques da Silva et al. (2025) [29] demonstrated that some COVID-19 patients were simultaneously infected with influenza. However, no cases of concurrent infection were observed in the present study. Overall, these findings underscore the need for surveillance of respiratory viruses in immunocompromised populations, use of multiplex viral diagnostic kits, adjustment of immunosuppressive medications, and therapeutic management.

The mean age of the patients studied was 45.16 years, ranging from 18 to 81 years (Table 1). The frequency of SARS-CoV-2 and influenza infections was higher in the 35–49 age group and lower in those aged 65 and older (Table 7). This difference may be attributed to variations in social activity levels, virus exposure, and lifestyle across different age groups. 41% of the patients were female and 59% were male. Influenza infection was reported more frequently in women than in men (62% vs. 38%), whereas no significant gender difference was observed for SARS-CoV-2. These differences are likely related to immunological factors, behavioral patterns, and varying levels of exposure to viral infections.

Pourmohammad et al. conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the clinical symptoms of patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection worldwide. Among 61,742 patients, the most common symptoms were fever (87%), cough (68%), malaise (39%), sputum (31%), and dry cough (14%) [30]. Clinical symptoms in patients with SARS-CoV-2 included chills (72.2%), myalgia (66.7%), fever (55.6%), decreased appetite (44.4%), and weakness (44.4%) (Fig. 1). In patients with influenza, fever (84.6%), cough and chills (76.9%), weakness (61.5%), sputum (53.8%), and vomiting (46.2%) were commonly observed (Fig. 2).

In a study by Monfared et al., 24 kidney transplant recipients were compared with 40 individuals from the general population regarding COVID-19 clinical symptoms, laboratory and imaging features, disease severity, and outcomes. Fever and cough were the most common clinical symptoms observed in patients with chronic kidney failure [31] which is consistent with symptoms reported in immunocompromised patients in a recent systematic review [32]. However, in some other studies, fever was a less common symptom among patients with chronic kidney failure [33].

The results of this study showed that hypertension (37%) was the most common comorbidity, followed by end-stage renal failure (35%) and diabetes (26%). In the study by Elhadedy et al., hypertension and diabetes were the most common comorbidities among kidney transplant recipients with COVID-19 [34]. Also, in the study by Monfared et al., hypertension was significantly higher in kidney patients with chronic kidney failure compared to the control group [31]. The Benotmane et al. cohort study showed that the most common underlying disease among kidney patients with COVID-19 was hypertension [35], and a study from Turkey found hypertension and diabetes to be the most common underlying diseases among kidney patients [36].

Since the clinical symptoms of SARS-CoV-2, influenza virus, and HRSV are similar and infect the same tissues, accurate diagnosis and simultaneous management of all infecting viruses are critically important. A critical aspect of managing respiratory infections in kidney transplant patients is the use of multiplex viral detection assays. Diagnosing SARS-CoV-2 infection without testing for other respiratory viruses may result in inappropriate treatment, as therapeutic approaches differ among these pathogens. When only SARS-CoV-2 testing is performed, co-infections or other viral etiologies may be overlooked, potentially leading to suboptimal clinical management. For instance, influenza requires antiviral agents such as oseltamivir, whereas COVID-19 treatment often involves specific antiviral and anti-inflammatory therapies. Therefore, employing multiplex molecular diagnostic tests to identify all relevant respiratory viruses can enhance treatment accuracy. According to reports from the Iranian Ministry of Health, in the year 1403 (March 2024–March 2025), the prevalence of the influenza virus began in October and peaked during December and January, which aligns with the findings of our study. In contrast to SARS-CoV-2, which was among the most prevalent viruses early in the year, its frequency declined concurrently with the influenza outbreak. Comparison between the Ministry of Health data and our study reveals similar trends for SARS-CoV-2; specifically, the virus’s frequency decreased from April 2024 and reached zero by January 2025 (Fig. 3). Although only one case of HRSV was detected in the study samples, official Ministry of Health statistics indicate a higher community prevalence of HRSV, suggesting that the actual number of cases is underestimated in our sample. Although false negatives cannot be entirely excluded, the low HRSV detection rate observed is biologically plausible. Two main factors likely contributed: first, the adult-only inclusion criteria (≥ 18 years); and second, reduced social exposure and infection-prevention behaviors among kidney transplant recipients during the post-COVID-19 period, which likely limited opportunities for transmission.

Monitoring the epidemiological status of specific populations, including transplant recipients, is crucial due to their increased vulnerability to respiratory viruses and the associated risks to health outcomes and treatment efficacy. Public health preparedness, optimal vaccination strategies, reduction of healthcare burden, and mitigation of the social and economic impacts of influenza and other viral infections in these groups are essential. Collecting prevalence data enhances preparedness and informs prevention and control efforts, thereby protecting public health. Additionally, identifying the circulation patterns of respiratory viruses, particularly influenza and SARS-CoV-2, in diverse patient populations facilitates improved clinical management, reduces unnecessary antibiotic use, and lowers healthcare costs.

Limitations of this study include its single-center design and modest sample size, which reflect the specificity of the target population, and may restrict the generalizability of the results. The analysis was restricted to three respiratory viruses, excluding other potential pathogens such as parainfluenza and adenovirus, whose inclusion was constrained by limited access to broader multiplex testing panels at the time of study implementation. Furthermore, the lack of longitudinal follow-up prevented evaluation of long-term outcomes. Future research should involve larger, multicenter cohorts and compare different transplant types to strengthen statistical power and enable subgroup analyses. Comprehensive evaluation of infection-related outcomes, such as ICU admission, mortality, and graft function, along with assessment of vaccination status, is also recommended to better define immune protection and susceptibility in transplant recipients.

In conclusion, this study represents the first investigation in Iran to assess the prevalence of influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and HRSV in patients with a history of kidney transplantation who had been clinically diagnosed with respiratory infection. This study provides valuable insights into the epidemiology and clinical features of respiratory viral infections in kidney transplant recipients. These findings underscore the importance of simultaneous monitoring and diagnosis of multiple respiratory viruses in transplant patients. Regular screening and vaccination programs in this population can effectively reduce complications, improve clinical outcomes, prevent unnecessary antibiotic use, and lower healthcare costs.
