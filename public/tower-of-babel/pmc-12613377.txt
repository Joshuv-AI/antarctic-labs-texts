Harnessing genotype-by-environment interaction and stability analysis of taro (Colocasia esculenta (L.) Schott) genotypes for resilient breeding and food security in Southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Ketema Balcha Debela, Derbew Belew Yohannes, Tewodros Mulualem Beyene, Kassahun Bantte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTaro (Colocasia esculenta (L.) Schott) is an important staple in southwestern Ethiopia, contributing to food security and adapting to marginal environments. Understanding genotype-by-environment interaction (G × E) is essential for identifying stable, high-yielding taro genotypes for resilient breeding and food security in Southwest Ethiopia. However, its G × E interaction and stability remain under-researched in Southwest Ethiopia.MethodsTen taro genotypes were evaluated across six environments using a randomized complete block design with three replications. Yield and related traits were assessed using Additive Main effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI), GGE biplot models, and Genotype Stability Index (GSI) analyses.ResultsAMMI analysis showed environment was the main source of variation for all traits, except cormel weight, where genotype had a greater influence. The genotypes x environment interaction (GEI) effects contributed less to the total variation but were still notable. Based on the Genotype Stability Index (GSI), Kiyaq and SM/Ce65 were the most stable genotypes with a high total yield, whereas JD/Ce17 and KD/Ce50 were unstable and low-yielding genotypes in all environments. According to the Which-Won-Where analysis, JS/Ce06 and JD/Ce23 outperformed all other genotypes across environments, with the exception of Jimma. JD/Ce23 had the highest mean yield (37.56 t/ha) and high stability, making it a promising genotype for wide adaptation. The GGE biplot identified JD/Ce23 as the ideal genotype due to its high yield and broad adaptability and recommended it for further evaluation under farmers’ conditions prior to potential release. The GGE biplot analysis identified two distinct mega-environments and delineated that Tercha and Gera were the most discriminating, while Jimma was the least. Tercha was identified as the ideal site for evaluating taro genotypes for yield traits.ConclusionThe study provides insight into taro G × E interaction and stability, supporting genotype selection for resilient breeding and enhanced food security in southwestern Ethiopia. These findings contribute to expanding taro genetic resources and inform breeding strategies aimed at enhancing both crop resilience and regional food security.

Introduction

Taro (Colocasia esculenta L. Schott) is a vital root crop cultivated in various tropical and subtropical regions, including Ethiopia [1]. In Africa, taro is cultivated primarily by resource-constrained smallholder farmers, the majority of whom are women [2]. After cassava and yam, taro is the third most important root and tuber crop cultivated and consumed in most African countries [3]. Despite its potential, taro is underused in Africa for export, nutrition, industrial purposes, and health benefits. Taro is rich in resistant starch and hypoallergenic properties. It offers potential health benefits and versatile applications in food industries due to its unique starch granules and high starch content in corms [4].

In Ethiopia, taro is primarily grown in the south, southwest, and western parts of the country. Here, it serves as a staple food and a source of income for smallholder farmers. In southwestern Ethiopia, particularly in the Bench-Sheko, Kafa, Sheka, and West-Omo zones, taro ranks second in area coverage and production after enset (Ensete ventricosum (Welw.) Cheesman), with Irish potato following next [5]. Despite its socioeconomic and nutritional importance, taro production in Ethiopia faces multiple constraints. These limit its potential contribution to food security and income generation. One of the foremost challenges is the lack of improved, high-yielding, and broadly adaptable genotypes. This restricts farmers to traditional varieties with variable performance. Furthermore, poor agronomic practices and limited access to quality planting materials exacerbate production problems. Environmental conditions strongly influence the crop’s yield, causing inconsistent performance across locations and seasons. These constraints collectively hinder the expansion and commercialization of taro cultivation in Ethiopia, despite its proven ability to produce reasonable yields under harsh conditions where many other crops fail [6, 7].

Various statistical methods have been used to analyze genotype × environment interactions (GEIs), including AMMI, PCA, linear regression, ANOVA, and GGE biplot analysis [8]. The G × E interaction is crucial for describing adaptation patterns and selecting specifically adapted genotypes. This interaction is essential for understanding how different genotypes respond to specific environmental conditions, which makes it valuable for targeted agricultural practices [9]. Multi-environment yield trials should focus on G × E interactions and key factors like climate, pests, crops, and soil management [10].

Research on G × E interactions in taro remains limited. For example, Donkor et al. [11] assessed the yield stability of 25 taro genotypes across three locations using the AMMI and GGE biplot models. Similarly, Singh et al. [12] applied ANOVA in multi-location trials to identify superior genotypes among six elite taro lines developed through the third cycle of the Papua New Guinea breeding program.

There is a critical need for multi-environment evaluations to understand G × E in taro under Ethiopia’s diverse agro-ecological conditions. These analyses help identify stable, high-performing genotypes suitable for cultivation across different environments. Although statistical tools like AMMI and GGE biplot analyses are widely employed elsewhere to dissect G × E effects, their application in taro research in Ethiopia remains limited. Few studies have systematically assessed G × E interactions in taro. Degefa and Anbessa [13] highlighted the underexplored taro diversity in Ethiopia and emphasized the need for scientific research to support local communities in improving taro production.

This study aims to fill that gap by evaluating the performance and stability of taro (C. esculenta (L.) Schott) genotypes across multiple environments in Ethiopia. Given taro’s resilience in marginal conditions and its role in food security, especially in Southwest Ethiopia, this research seeks to generate robust data to support sustainable production and breeding efforts. Specifically, the objectives are to (i) assess the effect of G × E interaction on yield and yield-related traits, (ii) identify the most discriminating and representative environments for these traits, and (iii) select stable and high-yielding genotypes suitable for major taro-growing areas in the region, thereby providing a foundation for genetic improvement and food security initiatives.

This study is among the first comprehensive multi-environment evaluations of taro genotypes in Ethiopia, covering six ecologically diverse locations in the southwest. By integrating AMMI, GGE biplot, and GSI analyses, it offers a holistic assessment of genotype performance and stability. Unlike previous studies that were limited in scope or focused solely on yield, this research combines broad environmental representation with multiple stability indices to identify genotypes that excel in both productivity and adaptability. The findings provide actionable insights for breeding programs, farmer participatory trials, and food security initiatives in under-researched taro-growing regions.

Experimental design and field layout

The experiment was laid out in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications at each location. Each observation plot measured 5 m in length and 3 m in width (15 m2), consisting of four rows. The spacing between plants was 30 cm within rows and 70 cm between rows, giving a total of 64 plants per plot. Data were recorded and harvested from the middle ten plants in the two central rows to reduce border effects and provide representative samples for each replication. The trial management practices were conducted in accordance with recommended guidelines for taro cultivation and experimental trials [15].

Plant materials

A total of 64 taro (C. esculenta (L.) Schott) accessions were collected during the 2021/22 cropping season from farmers’ fields across three administrative zones in southwest Ethiopia: Jimma, Kefa, and Sheka. The sampling covered 20 kebeles distributed across six districts, and the geographic coordinates of the collection sites are presented in Table 2. Each accession was treated as a unique sample based on its collection site and observable morphological traits. Efforts were made to minimize duplication through farmer interviews and field observations; however, some accessions may share genetic similarities due to informal seed exchange among farming communities. During collection, farmers participated in evaluating each accession based on tuber size, yield, taste, maturity, and suitability for processing. From these evaluations, seven genotypes were preliminarily selected during the first season for superior performance and farmer preference. In addition, one widely grown local cultivar was included as farmer-preferred material. Data collection

In this study, data were collected on the following traits: plant height, number of leaves, leaf length and width, petiole length, number of cormels, cormel weight (g), corm length (cm), corm diameter (cm), and total yield (t/ha). Plant height was measured from the base to the tip of the tallest leaf; leaf length, width, and petiole length were measured using a measuring tape; corm length and diameter were measured using a caliper; the number of leaves and cormels were counted per plant; and corm and cormel weight were recorded per plant and extrapolated to t/ha [16]. Foliar traits were measured five months after planting, while underground traits were recorded at harvest (at nine months). Data analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the quantitative traits using SAS version 9.4 [18] to assess the effects of genotype, location, and their interaction. In this study, location refers to each experimental site, whereas environment represents the combined effect of location and year-specific growing conditions. Before performing the combined ANOVA, the assumption of normality was assessed separately for each environment. Homogeneity of the variances was tested using Bartlett's test before the combined ANOVA across locations. The environments were considered random variables, while the genotypes were treated as fixed. To assess the stability of the genotypes across environments, further statistical analyses were conducted following the detection of a significant G × E interaction using statistical packages, such as GenStat 17th Edition [19] and GEA-R [20].

Mixed-model analysis for variance components and BLUP estimation

In addition to the conventional ANOVA, a linear mixed model (LMM) was fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method in R version 4.3.3 [22] with the lme4 package [23] to estimate variance components and predict genotypic performance across environments. Where Yijk is the observed yield of genotype i in environment j and replication k; μ is the overall mean; Gi ~ N (0, σ2g), Ej ~ N(0, σ2e), (G x E)ij ~ N(0, σ2g × e), and Eijk ~ N(0, σ2error).

Variance components (σ2g, σ2e, σ2g × e, σ2error) were extracted using the VarCorr() function, and standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were obtained via profile likelihood. The ranef() function gave us the best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) for each genotype. These values were adjusted for environmental and G × E effects, providing us adjusted genotypic values across environments. To complement the random model, a second model was fitted, treating genotype as a fixed effect [Yijk = µ + Gi + E + (GxE)ij + Eijk]. Estimated marginal means (EMMeans) and their standard errors were calculated using the emmeans package (Lenth, 2023). The REML approach provided unbiased estimates of variance components and more reliable genotype ranking based on BLUPs, facilitating inference across multiple environments (Piepho et al., 2008; Resende & Thompson, 2016).

The AMMI model accounts for multiplicative interaction effects between genotypes and environments. These interactions capture how specific genotypes perform differently across various environments [24]. AMMI stability value (ASV) was calculated using the following formula, as suggested by Purchase [26]. The ASV measures the distance from the origin in a two-dimensional plot, with IPCA1 on the x-axis and IPCA2 on the y-axis. Since the IPCA 1 score contributes more to the G x E sum of squares, it has to be weighted by the proportional difference between IPCA 1 and IPCA 2 scores to compensate for the relative contribution of IPCA 1 and IPCA 2 to the total G x E sum of squares. The distance from zero is then determined by using the theorem of Pythagoras.

Genotype Stability Index (GSI)

According to Farshadfar [27], a stability statistic was formed based on the rank of mean yield across environments and the rank of ASV. Based on this criterion, a genotype with the lowest value of GSI would be more stable. Variance estimates and G × E interaction

Leaf width was the only trait showing non-significant GEI, indicating its relative stability across environments. Genotypes exhibited varied responses to environmental changes, highlighting the presence of GEI. The AMMI stability value (ASV)

ASV was used to measure how stable taro genotypes are across different environments. It calculates how far each genotype or environment is from the center of the AMMI2 biplot, using their IPCA1 and IPCA2 scores [37, 38]. Genotypes that were located closer to the origin on the AMMI2 biplot were considered more stable, while those positioned farther away exhibited greater instability. To reflect how much IPCA1 and IPCA2 each contribute to genotype stability, their scores were treated as perpendicular components and combined using the Pythagorean Theorem. It was possible to calculate the ASV, which ranks genotypes by how far they are from the center. Although ASV did not directly quantify stability, Purchase [26] emphasized that it allowed genotypes to be ranked according to the consistency of their yield across environments.

Genotype stability index (GSI)

The GSI is a valuable tool in plant breeding, combining mean yield and stability into a single index. It helps breeders identify genotypes that yield well and remain stable across environments [41]. This balance is important because the most stable genotypes may not always produce the highest yields. Various selection criteria, such as rank-sum and modified rank-sum, allow simultaneous consideration of yield and stability [42]. GSI was calculated using IPCA1 and IPCA2 scores from the AMMI model, capturing most of the GEI. Kiyaq and SM/Ce65 had the highest total tuber yield and stability (GSI = 5), followed by JS/Ce06 [8], KS/Ce28 [9], and JD/Ce23 [10]. JD/Ce17 and KD/Ce50 were the least stable (Table 7).

Genotypes clustering together indicated similar performance patterns across environments. The angle between environment vectors in the biplot provides insight into the correlation among environments [44]. An acute angle indicated a positive correlation, a right angle suggested no correlation, and an obtuse angle reflected a negative correlation [45]. For total yield, environments such as Tercha and Shisho Ende, Mattu, Jimma, and Chida showed acute angles, indicating strong positive correlations. Similar patterns were observed for cormel weight, corm diameter, and corm weight across various environment pairs. Conversely, obtuse angles, such as those between Tercha and Jimma or Gera and Jimma, signed negative correlations, suggesting crossover G × E interactions (Fig. 1, patterns A–D). The largest angles slightly exceeded 90°, implying moderately large interaction effects [46].

In general, genotypes located near the origin of the biplot were less sensitive to environmental interactions and considered broadly adapted. In contrast, genotypes positioned far from the origin are more sensitive to environmental variation, indicated specific adaptation. Similarly, environments close to the origin, represented by short vectors, exert weak interactive forces, while environments farther from the origin exert strong interactive forces [47]. Based on the AMMI bi-plot (Fig. 1 A-D), genotype Kiyaq was positioned close to the origin, suggesting it was less influenced by environmental variation and exhibited stable performance for total tuber yield (t/ha). Likewise, genotype Danu showed broad adaptability for cormel weight per plant, JS/Ce06 for corm diameter, and KD/Ce50 for corm weight. In contrast, JD/Ce17 and SM/Ce65 were positioned farther from the origin, indicating high sensitivity to environmental variation and specific adaptation for total tuber yield and corm diameter, respectively. The JD/Ce18 genotype also showed specific adaptation for corm weight due to its distant position from the origin. Regarding environments, Mattu exhibited a short vector near the origin for total yield, cormel weight, and corm diameter, suggesting minimal environmental influence, while Gera and Cormel showed long vectors, indicating strong interactive forces for total yield and corm weight, respectively.

These patterns are consistent with findings by Gerrano et al. [48], who reported that 29 taro accessions located near the biplot origin displayed broader adaptability and lower sensitivity to environmental fluctuations across five test environments in South Africa. This consistency across studies highlights that taro genotypes with positions near the biplot origin tend to exhibit greater stability and wider adaptability under diverse growing conditions. Similarly, Waki and Birte [39] observed comparable trends among 13 taro genotypes evaluated across five locations, where genotypes with shorter distances from the origin were identified as more stable and widely adapted. The agreement of our results with these studies reinforces the robustness of the AMMI model in capturing genotype by environment relationships and supports the conclusion that certain taro genotypes, such as Kiyaq and Danu, possess wide adaptability suitable for diverse growing environments in southwestern Ethiopia.

In this study, the interaction principal component axes (IPCAs) explained 60.36%, 71.03%, 80.06%, and 68.37% of the genotype × environment interaction for total yield (t/ha), cormel weight (g/plant), corm diameter (cm), and corm weight (g), respectively. These values were relatively lower than those reported by Donkor et al. [11], who found that the IPCAs explained 99.03% of the variation in the corm yield of taro genotypes in Ghana. Nonetheless, AMMI biplots remain valuable for identifying redundant environments, reducing testing costs, and enhancing breeding efficiency. By understanding crossover interactions and stability patterns, breeders can make informed decisions about genotype selection and environmental targeting.

Evaluation of genotypes based on the ideal genotype

The GGE biplot model was an insightful application for evaluating genotypes relative to an ideal genotype. Several authors, including Daba et al. [64], Artur et al. [65], Fantaw [66], and Gebreselassie et al. [67], had defined an ideal genotype as having both high mean performance and consistent stability across environments. According to Fekadu et al. [68], the ‘ideal’ location (the center of the convex circle) was used to identify the genotype with the highest mean performance and stability. An ideal genotype combines high mean yield (large PC1 scores) with stability (small PC2 scores). Breeders aim to identify such genotypes to develop improved crop varieties. An ideal genotype has large first principal component scores (high mean yield) and small (absolute) second principal component scores (high stability). The concept of an “ideal” genotype is aspirational, but it guides research and helps drive progress [69]. According to Naık et al. [70], to better show the gap between genotypes and the ideal genotype, concentric circles were drawn on a GGE biplot graph using genotype-focused scaling. In the early stages of breeding, researchers can eliminate genotypes that fall far from the ideal. Those that lie closer to it are worth keeping for further evaluation and testing [71]. A genotype is more desirable if it is closer to the "ideal" genotype, which is located in the first concentric circle of the GGE biplot graphic [72].

Study limitation

This study has two main limitations. First, only ten genotypes were evaluated in the multi-location trial, including seven selected from the initial 64 accessions, so generalizability may be limited. However, this stepwise approach mainly targeted accessions that showed the greatest promise and diversity. Second, trials were conducted within a single year, so temporal stability across seasons was not assessed, although six diverse environments captured spatial variability. Despite these limitations, the findings provide a foundation for future multi-year trials, molecular characterization, and larger-scale evaluations of taro genotypes in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated that taro yield and yield-related traits were significantly influenced by both genotype and environment, with GEI contributing substantially to the observed variation. Genotype JD/Ce23 was identified as the most promising, exhibiting the highest average total tuber yield and broad adaptability across environments, while KD/Ce50 was the lowest-yielding genotype. Other high-performing genotypes, including Kiyaq and SM/Ce65, also showed desirable yield and stability, making them suitable candidates for integration into breeding pipelines and farmer participatory trials. Accordingly, JD/Ce23 could be recommended for further farmer-managed trials in Southwest Ethiopia to assess performance under real farm conditions and evaluate farmer preferences. Tercha was identified as the ideal environment for evaluating taro genotypes, and the study confirmed the presence of two mega-environments, highlighting the need for environment-specific selection strategies.

It is important to note that the current multi-location evaluation was conducted within a single cropping season; therefore, results should be interpreted with caution regarding long-term stability. Future research will focus on multi-year trials to validate these promising genotypes, assess their consistency and resilience under variable seasonal conditions, and support their incorporation into sustainable taro breeding programs in southwest Ethiopia.
