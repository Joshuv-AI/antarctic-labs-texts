Harnessing endogenous carbon monoxide for cancer-selective drug synthesis
Authors: Masayuki Kawai, Tsung-Che Chang, Ambara R Pradipta, Katsunori Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynthetic chemistry within living systems offers a transformative strategy for precision medicine by enabling the targeted generation of therapeutic agents directly at disease sites. Carbon monoxide (CO), an endogenous gasotransmitter upregulated in many cancer phenotypes, represents a unique yet underexploited chemical feedstock for such intracellular synthesis. However, the intrinsically low reactivity of CO necessitates transition-metal mediation, which is typically compromised by rapid deactivation by intracellular thiols such as glutathione (GSH). Here, we present a biocompatible albumin-palladacycle complex (APC) platform that harnesses endogenous CO for two distinct functions: the detection of CO levels and the in situ synthesis of cytotoxic phenanthridinone derivatives—a privileged scaffold found in potent poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) and topoisomerase inhibitors. Central to this design is a coumarin anchor that directs the palladium center into the hydrophobic binding pocket of albumin, shielding it from thiol-mediated poisoning while facilitating cellular uptake via endocytosis. We demonstrate that this system effectively detects elevated CO in cancer cells and, in a parallel application, enables the in situ synthesis of pharmacologically active agents. Although the associated cytotoxicity is modest, the approach nonetheless suppresses cancer cell growth. This study considerably advances the proof of concept for “therapeutic in vivo synthetic chemistry,” in which disease-associated metabolites are repurposed as structural building blocks for both diagnostic and therapeutic applications.Subject terms: Chemical biology, Chemical synthesis, Medicinal chemistry

Introduction

The clinical utility of systemic chemotherapy is frequently severely constrained by adverse drug reactions and off-target toxicity1,2. While prodrug strategies—typically activated by tumor-associated enzymes or microenvironments—have improved selectivity, a paradigm shift could be achieved by moving beyond simple activation to the de novo synthesis of pharmacologically active agents directly within cancer cells3–7. However, performing intermolecular C–C bond-forming reactions within a living cell is particularly challenging8. While intramolecular cyclization can circumvent some of these barriers, traditional intermolecular approaches often require a subcellular colocalization of multiple precursors. A transformative solution to this hurdle is to use a biogenic compound that is already endogenously present in the tumor as a precursor9–11. By administering a single, inert precursor designed to react selectively with such an oncometabolite, one could, in principle, convert a cancer-specific metabolic signature into a therapeutic bullet.

Among cancer-associated metabolites, carbon monoxide (CO) represents an exceptionally promising yet underutilized chemical feedstock12–14. In the high-oxidative-stress environment of a tumor, upregulation of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) catalyzes the degradation of heme, releasing elevated levels of ferrous ions (Fe2+), biliverdin, and CO15–17. Although harnessing this endogenous CO for drug synthesis is conceptually appealing, it presents formidable chemical challenges. Recent advances in bioorthogonal chemistry have enabled the use of transition metals to catalyze non-natural transformations within living systems18,19. However, although the inertness of CO can be overcome by transition-metal catalysts, particularly palladium, these metal catalysts are quickly poisoned by high levels of intracellular thiols, such as glutathione (GSH)20. Consequently, previous efforts to exploit biological CO have focused mainly on detection and imaging rather than therapeutic synthesis.

The existing CO-responsive fluorogenic chemical probes typically rely on Tsuji–Trost allylic cleavage21–25, or heavy-atom quenching mechanisms to release or activate a fluorophore (Supplementary Table S1)26–28. While innovative, these methods serve primarily as diagnostic tools and often depend on exogenously supplied CO from metal-based CO-releasing molecules (CORMs) rather than authentic endogenous gas. Furthermore, the specificity of non-palladium approaches (e.g., nitro reduction or Cu(II)/Fe(III) redox switches) has recently come under scrutiny, with reports of false-positive signals arising from interactions with CORM metal centers or intracellular reductants rather than CO itself29. As recently highlighted by comprehensive analyses30, many reported CO-detection systems suffer from false-positive responses to transition-metal-based CORMs.

More recently, Wang and co-workers have demonstrated CO-mediated de novo synthesis of a phthalimide fluorophore using HO-1 inducers to artificially increase intracellular CO31. On the other hand, while non-metallic CORMs have emerged as a therapeutic strategy32–35, the direct use of endogenous CO as a carbonyl synthon for the intracellular synthesis of bioactive therapeutic scaffolds—without reliance on exogenous donors or artificial induction—remains unrealized.

To bridge this gap, we focused on the phenanthridinone scaffold—a privileged pharmacophore found in numerous alkaloids (e.g., oxynitidine, oxoassoanine) and synthetic drugs (e.g., ARC-111, PJ34) with bioactivities ranging from antiviral effects to potent acetylcholinesterase (AChE), poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), and topoisomerase inhibition (Fig. 1a)36–38. The chemical basis for this approach lies in the reactivity of specific palladacycles (e.g., compound 1a)39–41, which can sequester CO to form the phenanthridin-6(5H)-one core (Fig. 1b)42. However, translating this reaction into the cytosol requires shielding the palladium center from thiol deactivation. Human serum albumin is a widely used drug carrier that accommodates small molecules within its hydrophobic pockets. With an isoelectric point (pI) of approximately 5, albumin is negatively charged under physiological conditions. Leveraging these features, we previously demonstrated that embedding ruthenium or gold complexes within defined hydrophobic regions of albumin maintains their reactivity under baseline thiol conditions in aqueous media and blood43–47.

Based on this strategy, we aimed to achieve two goals: detect endogenous CO concentrations and selectively synthesize bioactive drugs within cancer cells. Drawing on our expertise in albumin-based catalysis43–52, we propose a biocompatible albumin–palladacycle complex (APC) that is designed to minimize premature activation in normal tissues but effectively harvests endogenous CO in cancer cells to drive drug synthesis (Fig. 1c). This platform enables efficient carbonylation followed by spontaneous lactamization. We first demonstrate the utility of this system for detecting endogenous CO in live cells, and then its application to drug synthesis. This study considerably advances the concept of “therapeutic in vivo synthetic chemistry,” a strategy in which disease-associated metabolites are not merely biomarkers but are repurposed as structural building blocks for the on-site manufacturing of antitumor drugs.

Results and discussion

Design and screening of albumin-stabilized palladium complexes

To establish a robust platform for harnessing endogenous CO, we initiated our study by synthesizing stable binuclear palladacycle precursors 1a and 1b (Fig. 2a)40. The reaction of 2-phenylaniline with palladium (II) acetate in toluene (room temperature, 24 h) afforded the acetate-bridged dimer 1a in 57% yield. Alternatively, treatment with a PdCl2/NaCl/NaOAc system in methanol (room temperature, 41 h) yielded the chloride-bridged dimer 1b in 76% yield. From these precursors, we generated a diverse library of mononuclear palladacycles (2aa–2ag and 2ba–2bg) via bridge-splitting reactions in acetone40,53,54. To probe the steric and electronic factors governing stability and reactivity, we selected a panel of Lewis base ligands ranging from electron-deficient phosphines (e.g., P(C6F5)3) and electron-rich alkyl phosphines (e.g., P(n-Bu)3) to bulky N-heterocyclic carbenes (SIMes) and bipyridyls (Fig. 2a). The structural integrity of representative library members, such as the tributylphosphine derivative 2ad and the triphenylphosphine derivative 2bf, was unambiguously confirmed by X-ray crystallography (Fig. 2b, see also Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2).

A critical requirement for intracellular applications is resistance to deactivation by intracellular thiols. To evaluate this, we subjected the library to carbonylation in water supplemented with glutathione (GSH; 1.0 mM) and determined the yield by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). As illustrated in Fig. 2c–i (Condition a), the “naked” complexes displayed negligible reactivity (Fig. 2d, red and blue bars); the vast majority, including both binuclear precursors (1a, 1b) and mononuclear derivatives bearing electron-deficient ligands (e.g., P(C6F5)3; 2aa, 2ba), succumbed to rapid thiol poisoning, yielding less than 7% of the phenanthridin-6(5H)-one product 3a. Even complexes with bulky ligands such as XPhos (2ab, 2bb) or 2-(PPh2)biphenyl (2ae, 2be) generally showed low reaction efficiency (less than 10% yield) under these conditions.

To address this limitation, palladacycles were initially encapsulated within the hydrophobic regions of human serum albumin via hydrophobic adsorption, forming albumin–palladacycle complexes (APCs). Based on our previous findings43–47, the protein’s hydrophobic pockets and anionic surface are known to sequester small molecules while repelling anionic nucleophiles such as GSH55,56. Upon encapsulation (Fig. 2c-ii, Condition b), we observed a marked restoration of reactivity, although this effect was highly ligand dependent. The binuclear precursors (1a, 1b), as well as mononuclear derivatives bearing P(C6F5)3 (2aa, 2ba), XPhos (2ab, 2bb), bipyridyl (2ag), SIMes (2bg), and the P(t-Bu)3 (2bh) ligands, remained inactive or displayed negligible recovery even in the presence of albumin.

By contrast, only selected mononuclear species showed appreciable recovery: complexes bearing P(2-furyl)3 (2ac, 2bc) or 2-(PPh2)biphenyl (2ae, 2be) ligands exhibited modest improvements, whereas complexes coordinated with triphenylphosphine (PPh3) displayed superior performance. As shown in Fig. 2d (green and yellow bars), the albumin-stabilized complexes derived from acetate (2af) and chloride (2bf) precursors gave the highest product yields (24% and 23%, respectively) in the presence of 1.0 mM GSH. These results suggest that the PPh3 ligand provides an optimal steric and electronic balance to retain the reactivity of the palladium center within the albumin hydrophobic regions. Consequently, the PPh3-ligated motif was selected as the lead candidate for intracellular synthesis. While “naked” palladacycles bearing lipophilic ligands such as PPh3 exhibit poor aqueous solubility, formulation into the APC overcomes this limitation. The hydrophobic binding pockets of albumin effectively encapsulate the transition-metal complex, improving the aqueous compatibility of the APC under physiological conditions while protecting the palladium complex from premature deactivation.

Intracellular uptake and detection of endogenous CO

Having identified the PPh3-ligated motif as the optimal core, we next sought to direct its localization within a defined hydrophobic cavity of albumin. We strategically incorporated a coumarin moiety into the ligand framework44,45,47,48, as coumarin derivatives exhibit a specific, high-affinity interaction with the hydrophobic drug-binding site I (Sudlow’s site I) of human serum albumin57–59. To validate this “anchoring” strategy, we synthesized a probe molecule, palladacycle 9, featuring a coumarin fluorophore covalently tethered to the phenylaniline scaffold (Fig. 3a). The design of the ligand precursor 7 was based on product 10 (Fig. 3c; see also Supplementary Fig. S3 for the optical properties), which is a derivative of PJ34 (see Fig. 1a-ii) extended by a coumarin moiety.

The synthesis commenced with a Suzuki–Miyaura coupling between 2-bromo-4-nitroaniline and phenylboronic acid, followed by tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc) protection of the resulting biaryl amine to afford compound 4 (92% yield over two steps). Subsequent nitro reduction generated an aniline intermediate, which underwent amide condensation with the coumarin-carboxylic acid 5 to furnish precursor 6 (25% yield over two steps). Acidic deprotection then afforded the ligand 7 (87% yield). Cyclopalladation of 7 with palladium (II) acetate yielded the stable binuclear complex 8 (96%), which was converted to the desired mononuclear palladacycle 9 (69%) via bridge-splitting with PPh3. Finally, the biocompatible assembly APC-9 was prepared by incubating 9 with an equimolar amount of albumin in water containing 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at 37 °C for 30 min.

We characterized the host–guest interaction via fluorescence-quenching titrations, determining a dissociation constant (KD) of 40.4 ± 9.3 µM. Competitive binding assays showed that warfarin, a canonical Site I ligand, significantly engaged in competitive displacement, whereas ibuprofen, a Site II ligand, had a negligible effect. These results confirm that the coumarin anchor directs the palladium complex to drug-binding Site I (Fig. 3b). The functional consequence of this encapsulation was demonstrated by challenging compounds 8 and 9, or APC-9, with glutathione (0.2 mM) prior to 4-h CO exposure under identical substrate concentrations. Although the “naked” palladacycles 8 and 9 were substantially deactivated, affording product 10 in 24% and 43% yields, respectively, APC-9 retained high carbonylation activity, affording 10 in 88% yield. This result indicates that the albumin scaffold effectively shields the metal center from nucleophilic attack (Fig. 3c).

To evaluate the system in a biological context, we used APC-9 to assess the capacity of endogenous CO flux to drive intracellular product formation across a panel of cell lines (Fig. 3d). Although utilizing endogenous CO for intracellular reactions is inherently challenging due to its low concentration and inertness, our albumin–palladacycle complex (APC) platform—designed to protect the palladium center within the albumin hydrophobic pocket—successfully facilitated this transformation. Human cancer cell lines—HepG2 (liver), HeLa S3 (cervix), MCF7 (breast), and A549 (lung)—and the normal fibroblast cell line TIG3 were incubated with APC-9 (50 µM) for 48 h. Subsequent high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS) analysis of cell lysates revealed the distinct phenanthridin-6(5H)-one product 10 (m/z 533.18 [M + Na]+), as shown by the representative HepG2 chromatogram in Fig. 3e (see also Supplementary Fig. S4 for other cancer cell lines).

Quantitative analysis calibrated against the authentic standard (see Supplementary Fig. S27) showed a clear distinction: product formation was detected in all cancer cell lines (e.g., 77 pmol/106 cells after 48 h of incubation in HepG2 cells), whereas normal cells showed minimal conversion (e.g., 15 pmol/106 cells after 48 h of incubation in TIG3 cells) (Fig. 3f). These results demonstrate that minute endogenous CO flux can serve as a structural carbonyl source for cellular in situ synthesis. These findings are consistent with the reported upregulation of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and enhanced CO flux in malignant tissues60–63.

Although the steady-state concentration of freely available intracellular CO is expected to be low, upregulated HO-1 in cancer cells can provide a continuous local flux of this gas. Because APC-9 consumes CO during carbonylation, in situ product formation is likely governed by both local CO availability and the dynamic balance between CO production and consumption, rather than by the static concentration of solvated CO alone. Finally, to clarify the uptake mechanism, we treated HepG2 cells with APC-9 in the presence of dynasore, a dynamin-dependent endocytosis inhibitor64. Co-incubation significantly reduced intracellular fluorescence (Fig. 3g), indicating that albumin functions as a carrier and mediates cellular uptake of the palladium cargo via endocytic pathways65–67. These results indicate that the APC platform successfully captures endogenous CO to trigger an intramolecular reaction, serving as a robust tool for detecting cancer-specific metabolic signatures.

Intracellular synthesis of functional drugs

Having demonstrated that the albumin scaffold can deliver palladium complexes for endogenous CO-mediated reactions, we next turned to the direct intracellular synthesis of pharmacologically active compounds. Although the APC-9 system effectively detected intracellular CO, the covalent attachment of the coumarin fluorophore to the substrate limited its therapeutic potential because the final product remained tethered to the anchor. To overcome this, we engineered a “release-type” system (Fig. 4a) in which the coumarin unit is appended to the phosphine ligand 12 rather than to the phenylaniline substrate. In this design, the albumin-bound ligand stabilizes the palladium center during uptake and transport. Upon reaction with CO, carbonylation, followed by reductive elimination, releases the free, pharmacologically active phenanthridinone, while the spent ligand remains associated with the protein scaffold.

As shown in Fig. 4a-i, we synthesized the coumarin-functionalized phosphine ligand 12 in three steps from the carboxylic acid precursor 11 (75% overall yield) and coordinated it to the binuclear precursor 1a to obtain palladacycle 13a (71% yield). Binding studies showed that 13a retains high affinity for albumin (KD = 28.5 ± 4.3 µM, Fig. 4a-i) and localizes to drug-binding Site I (Fig. 4a-ii), consistent with the behavior of the APC-9 probe. We then assessed the protective capacity of this assembly by monitoring reaction efficiency over the 4-h reaction period across the 0.1–5.0 mM GSH concentration range and found that APC-13a retained its reactivity (Fig. 4b, left graph). Similarly, under oxidative conditions (0.1–5.0 mM H2O2), APC-13a maintained its reaction efficiency over the same period (Fig. 4b, right graph). Furthermore, under limited CO availability (74.2 ppm; estimated dissolved CO concentration, approximately 76 nM), APC-13a afforded comparable yields (approximately 7%) after 4 h, both in the absence and presence of 0.2 mM GSH (Supplementary Fig. S5). These results indicate that the albumin scaffold provides enduring protection for the palladium–triphenylphosphine complex and that the APC system can operate under limited CO availability.

To confirm that the system is not compromised by solvent-induced redox artifacts, we evaluated the chemical stability of the phosphine ligand. Time-dependent 31P NMR analysis indicated that PPh3 remains stable toward DMSO-mediated oxidation; even in 95% DMSO-d6 / 5% aqueous H3PO4 in H2O (v/v), conversion to triphenylphosphine oxide was minimal (approximately 4%) over the standard 4-h reaction period (Supplementary Fig. S6). We additionally monitored the potential solvent-driven depletion of thiols, specifically the DMSO-induced oxidation of GSH to disulfides68. In a separate assay, 1H NMR monitoring of a mixture of GSH and PPh3 in a 5% DMSO-d6 / 95% D2O (v/v) solution showed that 60% of the initial reduced GSH remained after 4 h, corresponding to 40% conversion to oxidized glutathione (GSSG; Supplementary Fig. S7). These results indicate that DMSO-induced GSH oxidation occurs under these conditions, but it does not fully deplete the GSH pool over the 4-h in vitro reaction period.

We next evaluated the substrate scope to demonstrate the versatility of the albumin-protected system. A diverse panel of phenanthridinone derivatives (3a, 3c–3o) was synthesized from the corresponding precursors (1a, 1c–1o) under Condition b (Fig. 4c-ii). The platform tolerated substantial electronic diversity (Fig. 4c-iii, blue bars), efficiently converting substrates bearing electron-donating groups (e.g., N-alkyl 3c, 93%; dimethoxy 3g, 44%) as well as electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., trifluoromethyl 3d, 90%; ester 3i, 40%). Fused-ring systems, including a benzothiophene analog (3e, 63%), were also accessible. Notably, we achieved the synthesis of the functionalized derivative 3j (22%), corresponding to the clinical PARP inhibitor PJ34, and the methylenedioxy-substituted scaffold 3l (16%), which represents the core structure of oxynitidine derivatives.

In all cases, the albumin–palladacycle complexes (Fig. 4c-ii, Condition b; and Fig. 4c-iii, blue bars) markedly outperformed the naked palladacycles (Fig. 4c-i, Condition a; and Fig. 4c-iii, orange bars), which generally afforded <11% product (with the exception of the N-alkyl derivative 3c) under identical baseline thiol conditions (0.2 mM GSH). Moreover, robust substrates such as 3a and 3c–3g provided appreciable yields (8–61%) even in the presence of 1.0 mM GSH (Fig. 4c-ii, Condition b; and Fig. 4c-iii, yellow bars). For a physiological model of the cancer cell cytosol, a representative panel of palladacycles (1a, 1c, and 1g) was further challenged with 5.0 mM GSH, which better reflects physiologically relevant intracellular GSH levels69. While unprotected palladacycles are strongly deactivated under such stringent reducing conditions, we observed that albumin encapsulation effectively shielded the metal center, enabling the albumin–palladacycle complexes (APCs) to sustain carbonylation activity (affording products 3a, 3c, and 3g in 13%, 8%, and 5% yields, respectively; Supplementary Fig. S8).

This pronounced difference underscores the critical role of the hydrophobic microenvironment of the albumin scaffold in preserving the integrity of the palladacycle during the synthesis of structurally complex functional drug molecules. To determine whether differential albumin encapsulation influenced variable carbonylation yields, we measured the binding affinities of four representative palladacycles (13a, 13d, 13f, and 13o; Supplementary Fig. S9), corresponding to high, medium, and low product conversions (see Fig. 4c-iii). All tested palladacycles exhibited comparable affinities for the albumin scaffold (KD = 28.5–103.3 μM; see Supplementary Fig. S9). Therefore, the observed variations in carbonylation efficiency are not dictated by binding affinity but are predominantly driven by the intrinsic electronic and steric properties of the individual palladacycles.

Therapeutic application and selective cytotoxicity

Finally, to demonstrate the bio-application potential of our platform, we targeted the in situ synthesis of the oxynitidine-derived phenanthridinone 14 that exhibits potent cytotoxic activity through topoisomerase I inhibition (Fig. 5a)37,70. A major impediment to the clinical translation of phenanthridinone-based therapeutics is their lack of intrinsic targeting capability and poor aqueous solubility. This limitation is overcome by the in situ synthesis of 14 within cancer cells using our albumin–palladacycle complex (APC) platform, which exploits albumin’s dual functionality to solubilize the hydrophobic organometallic precursor 15 and protect the palladium center from deactivation by intracellular thiols. Moreover, encapsulation within albumin ensures that the active drug is generated selectively in response to the cancer-associated metabolite carbon monoxide (CO), thereby minimizing off-target effects.

To enable the intracellular production of this therapeutic, we developed an engineered release-type precursor, APC-17. In this design (Fig. 5b), ligand 16—which features a PEGylated linker between the coumarin anchor and the phosphine moiety—was coordinated to the binuclear precursor 15 to generate complex 17, thereby facilitating its anchoring to albumin. Ligand 16 was synthesized in three steps from the coumarin precursor 11 (62% overall yield) and then quantitatively coordinated to the binuclear precursor 15 (see Supplementary Information for synthesis details) to afford the active palladacycle 17, which was encapsulated in albumin to yield APC-17 (Fig. 5b).

Upon administration of APC-17 (50 µM) to HepG2 liver cancer cells (see procedure details in the “Methods” section), we observed detectable intracellular generation of the active drug 14. HPLC–MS analysis of cell lysates confirmed the intracellular generation of 14 (m/z 421.18 [M + H]+, Fig. 5c). Based on the calibration with the authentic standard (Supplementary Fig. S28)37, the intracellular concentration of the synthesized drug 14 was estimated to be approximately 36.4 nM (see Supplementary Note 2 for calculation details).

We next assessed the therapeutic efficacy of this in situ synthesis using cell viability assays. APC-17 induced cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells with an IC50 of 13.99 µM (Fig. 5d). While this potency is modest, APC-17 is designed to be activated by endogenous CO under standard in cellulo conditions. To assess the contribution of endogenous CO, we co-treated cells with zinc protoporphyrin-9 (ZnPPIX, 10 µM), a selective inhibitor of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1)71. HO-1 inhibition reduced APC-17 toxicity, increasing the IC50 to 34.18 µM, thereby supporting a mechanistic link between HO-1 activity and drug activation.

Although the in situ generation of the cytotoxic agent by APC-17 inherently consumes endogenous CO, CO depletion alone is unlikely to account for the observed cell death. ZnPPIX alone showed negligible cytotoxicity up to the highest soluble concentration (Fig. 5d). These results support the conclusion that the antiproliferative response induced by APC-17 arises primarily from the in situ synthesis of the phenanthridinone derivative rather than from depletion of endogenous CO.

Regarding selectivity, APC-17 exhibited an IC50 of 37.54 µM in TIG3 normal fibroblasts, which naturally exhibit low endogenous CO (see Fig. 3f), approximately threefold higher than that observed in HepG2 liver cancer cells (Fig. 5d). While this difference represents a modest therapeutic window, it provides proof of concept that the system can be actuated by endogenous CO levels in cancer cells. To further provide functional evidence for CO-dependent activation, we pre-exposed complex 17 to exogenous CO gas to generate the active product ex situ. When this CO-activated assembly was applied to TIG3 cells, cytotoxicity was amplified, reducing the IC50 from 37.54 to 14.79 µM (Fig. 5d). These results support CO as the key activation trigger and indicate that the lower toxicity in normal fibroblasts is largely attributable to limited CO-dependent activation.

Importantly, control experiments showed that neither the free binuclear palladacycle compound 15 nor the albumin–compound 16 assembly elicited appreciable toxicity (IC50 > 150 µM; Fig. 5d), supporting the conclusion that cell death arises from CO-dependent generation of compound 14 rather than from the individual platform components. Future engineering of the ligand framework or drug payload may further widen this safety margin.

Most notably, the cytotoxic effect of APC-17 became more pronounced in assays of long-term proliferative capacity. In clonogenic 12-day survival assays72, APC-17 suppressed colony formation by MCF7 breast cancer cells in a dose-dependent manner, with significant inhibition observed at concentrations as low as 5 µM. In contrast, the control treatments [DMSO (0.5%), compound 15 (10 µM), and albumin–compound 16 assembly (10 µM)] showed no comparable suppression of clonogenic growth (Fig. 5e; see also Supplementary Fig. S10). The clonogenic survival profile exhibited a particularly steep dose-response between 5 µM and 10 µM. This non-linear, threshold-like cytotoxicity may reflect cumulative intracellular formation of the phenanthridinone derivative to levels above those required to suppress long-term proliferative capacity.

Together, these findings support the potential of the albumin–palladacycle complex (APC) platform to exploit cancer-associated metabolic features for targeted therapeutic synthesis. For CO–driven in vivo applications, however, the feasibility of this strategy must be considered in the context of systemic CO distribution and premature activation in the circulation. Although total blood CO can reach high micromolar levels, most circulating CO is tightly sequestered as carboxyhemoglobin (COHb)73,74. Because activation of the APC system requires free CO, premature drug formation in the circulation is expected to be kinetically disfavored. Instead, carbonylation is likely to occur preferentially within cancer cells, where upregulated heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) provides a continuous flux of free CO.

To experimentally assess this systemic stability, we conducted an ex vivo blood stability assay using APC-9 as a representative APC. Incubation of APC-9 (50 µM) in freshly obtained whole mouse blood at 37 °C for 4 h resulted in no detectable formation of compound 10 (Supplementary Fig. S11). This result suggests that, although CO is present in blood, it is likely sequestered by hemoglobin and therefore not readily available to react with the APC under these conditions. Taken together, these findings do not provide evidence for substantial premature activation of this APC design in blood. For future in vivo applications, surface modification of the APC with a glycocluster, an approach developed by our group, may further enhance tumor accumulation43–52.

This study advances strategies to harness a defining metabolic feature of cancer, namely the overproduction of endogenous carbon monoxide (CO), for the in situ synthesis of therapeutic agents. By designing a biocompatible albumin–palladacycle complex (APC), we overcome the longstanding challenge of transition-metal deactivation by intracellular thiols. The albumin scaffold serves as a hydrophobic “reaction vessel” that shields the palladacycle from glutathione-mediated poisoning. This platform enables two distinct applications: detection of endogenous CO and targeted construction of bioactive phenanthridinone scaffolds, including the PARP inhibitor PJ34 (compound 3j) and oxynitidine-derived topoisomerase I inhibitor (compound 14), directly within the cytoplasm of cancer cells. Notably, this localized synthesis elicits selective cytotoxicity in HepG2 cancer cells while exhibiting reduced toxicity toward normal fibroblasts. Although the therapeutic window observed in the assays is modest, the system nonetheless exhibits a clear cancer-selective effect intrinsically driven by the tumor’s metabolic output. To our knowledge, this is one of the first examples of incorporating a native gaseous oncometabolite into a structurally complex drug molecule within a living biological system. Together, these findings support and advance the concept of “therapeutic in vivo synthetic chemistry,” a paradigm in which disease-associated metabolites are repurposed from passive diagnostic biomarkers into essential structural feedstocks for precision medicine.

Methods

Ethical compliance

This research complied with all relevant ethical regulations. Fresh whole blood used for the ex vivo stability assay was collected from two female, 21-week-old BALB/cByJJcl mice under a protocol approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of RIKEN (W2019-2-049) and in accordance with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of RIKEN. Blood from the two animals was pooled before use. Sex was not considered an experimental variable because the experiment was designed solely to assess the ex vivo chemical stability of the APC system in whole blood. This study used established human cell lines and did not involve human participants or identifiable human-derived material.

General Information

All commercially available reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Gibco, Promega, Fisher Scientific, Funakoshi, Kanto Chemical, TCI, or Wako Chemicals and used without further purification. Human serum albumin (Prod#A1653-5G) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Preparative separation was performed using column chromatography with Silica Gel 60 (particle size 40–50 μm; Kanto Chemical Co., Inc.). 1H, 13C, and 31P NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Ascend 400 NMR spectrometer and analyzed using MestReNova (version 14.2.0). Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to solvent residual peaks (CDCl3: δH 7.26, δC 77.16; DMSO-d6: δH 2.50, δC 39.52; D2O: δH 4.79; CD3OD: δH 3.31, δC 49.00) or an internal standard (aqueous H3PO4 in DMSO-d6: δP 0.00). High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was performed on a micrOTOF-QIII spectrometer using electrospray ionization time-of-flight (ESI-TOF-MS) detection; data were acquired using Bruker Daltonics Hystar (version 3.2 SR4) and analyzed using Compass DataAnalysis (version 4.2, Build 383.1). HPLC and LC–MS data were acquired and analyzed using Shimadzu LabSolutions (version 5.118). Absorbance spectra were recorded on a JASCO V-630 spectrophotometer and analyzed using Spectra Manager Version 2 (version 2.09.01, Build 1). Fluorescence spectra were obtained on a Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD3 spectrofluorometer and analyzed using SoftMax Pro (version 7.0.3 SP1). Microscopy images were acquired using a KEYENCE BZ-X810 microscope and analyzed using BZ-X800 Viewer (version 01.02.03.02) and BZ-X800 Analyzer (version 1.1.2.4). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.1). No custom code or algorithms were used. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Pairwise comparisons were performed using two-sided Welch’s t-tests, and multiple comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test, as specified in the figure legends.

Preparation of Albumin Palladacycle Complex (APC) for thiol tolerance, ROS tolerance, and reactivity assay

Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO for the binuclear palladacycles 1a, 1b, and 8 (0.6 mM) and the mononuclear palladacycles 2aa–2ag, 2ba–2bh, and 9 (1.2 mM). Synthesis and isolation details for the synthesized complexes, along with source information for commercial 2bh, are provided in the SI. A stock solution of human serum albumin (HSA) was prepared in water (126.3 µM).

For the GSH tolerance screening of isolated complexes (Fig. 2c, Condition b; Fig. 3c, APC-9; Fig. 4b), an aliquot (41.6 µL) of the appropriate palladacycle stock—containing either 25 nmol of binuclear complex (1a, 1b, 8) or 50 nmol of mononuclear complex (2aa–2ag, 2ba–2bh, 9)—was combined directly with the HSA stock solution (395.8 µL, 50 nmol) and water (395.8 µL).

For control experiments using “naked” palladium complexes (Fig. 2c, Condition a; Fig. 3c, 8 or 9; Fig. 4c, Condition a), the same aliquots (41.6 µL) of the palladacycle stocks [1a, 1b, 8 (25 nmol); 2aa–2ag, 2ba–2bh, 9 (50 nmol)] were diluted with water (791.7 µL) in the absence of albumin.

In contrast, for the substrate scope evaluation (Fig. 4c, Condition b), the corresponding mononuclear complexes were generated in situ. The binuclear precursor 1a or 1c–1o (1.2 mM in DMSO, 20.8 µL, 25 nmol) was mixed with the phosphine ligand 12 (2.4 mM in DMSO, 20.8 µL, 50 nmol) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h (synthesis details for 1a, 1c–1o, and 12 are provided in the Supplementary Information). The resulting solution (41.6 µL, theoretically containing 50 nmol of the mononuclear complex) was then combined with the HSA stock solution (395.8 µL, 50 nmol) and water (395.8 µL).

In all APC preparations, the mixture was gently agitated and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to ensure encapsulation. To assess stability, aqueous glutathione (166.7 µL in 5% DMSO) or H2O2 (166.7 µL in 5% DMSO) was added to the APC solutions to achieve final GSH concentrations of 0.1–5.0 mM. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min and then purged with argon. Subsequently, the headspace was filled with carbon monoxide (CO), and the reaction mixtures were stirred under a CO atmosphere (via a balloon) at room temperature for 4 h.

Under these pre-incubation conditions, the concentration was 60 µM in palladacycle units; after the addition of GSH or H2O2 solution, the final reaction concentration was 50 µM in palladacycle units. For binuclear complexes such as 1a, 1b, 8, and 1c–1o, this corresponds to 25 µM of the binuclear complex in the final reaction mixture.

For in vitro assays using a CO balloon, the aqueous reaction mixture was maintained under a continuous 1 atm CO atmosphere, ensuring a saturating dissolved CO concentration of approximately 1.03 mM (see Supplementary Note 1 for calculation details). Alternatively, for the low-concentration CO assay, the headspace was filled with a certified CO calibration gas (74.2 ppm CO, Takachiho Chemical Industrial Co., Ltd.) following the argon purge, and the mixture was similarly stirred for 4 h. Reactions were quenched with methanol, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged (14,800 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) to precipitate proteins. The supernatants were collected and analyzed by HPLC.

Optical characterization of the “Naked” palladacycle 9 and phenanthridin-6(5H)-one product 10

To characterize the intrinsic optical properties of the “naked” palladacycle 9 and phenanthridin-6(5H)-one product 10, their absorbance and fluorescence spectra were measured. Stock solutions of compound 9 and compound 10 (500 µM in DMSO, 15 µL) were diluted with distilled water (1485 µL) to yield a final concentration of 5 µM in 1% DMSO (v/v). Absorbance spectra were recorded using a JASCO V-630 spectrophotometer (BRAND plastic cuvette format). Fluorescence spectra (λex = 420 nm) were recorded using a Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD3 spectrofluorometer (Falcon 96-well plate format).

Chromatographic analysis

Analytical reversed-phase (RP) HPLC was performed on a Shimadzu system (LC-20AP pumps, SPD-20AV detector) equipped with a COSMOSIL 5C18-Ar-300 column (4.6 mm I.D. × 250 mm; Nacalai Tesque). For most compounds, elution was performed at 1 mL/min using a gradient of H2O (0.1% TFA) and MeCN (0.1% TFA), with detection at 214 and 254 nm. LC-MS analysis was conducted on a Shimadzu LCMS-2050 system. For compound 10, H2O and MeCN (without acid) were used as the mobile phase. For compound 14, a TSKgel ODS-100Z column (4.6 mm I.D. × 250 mm; Tosoh) was used with a mobile phase gradient of H2O (0.01% formic acid) and MeCN (0.01% formic acid). HPLC gradient conditions, calibration curves and chromatograms are provided in the Supplementary Table S2 and Figs. S12–S28.

Albumin binding and competition assay

Binding affinities were determined by monitoring the quenching of the intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence of albumin (λex = 280 nm; λem = 320 nm). For the direct binding assay, a solution of the palladacycle substrate (0–1200 µM in DMSO, 20 µL) was mixed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4; 360 µL) and albumin solution (200 µM in PBS, 20 µL). The resulting mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluorescence was then measured in 96-well microplates (100 µL per well) using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader.

For the competitive binding assays, albumin–warfarin or albumin–ibuprofen assemblies were prepared in situ. A solution of warfarin or ibuprofen (50 µM in PBS, pH 7.4; 360 µL) was mixed with albumin solution (200 µM in PBS, 20 µL), and the mixture was preincubated at 37 °C for 1 h. A solution of the palladacycle substrate (0–1200 µM in DMSO, 20 µL) was subsequently added, and the resulting mixture was incubated at 37 °C for an additional 1 h. Fluorescence was measured in 96-well microplates (100 µL per well) using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader under the same excitation and emission conditions described above.

Cell culture

All cell lines were obtained from the RIKEN BRC Cell Bank: HepG2 (RCB1648), HeLa S3 (RCB1525), A549 (RCB0098), MCF7 (RCB1904) and TIG3 (RCB4468). The cells were supplied with repository quality-control information, and no additional authentication was performed. All cultures tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. HepG2, HeLa S3 and A549 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with L-glutamine, phenol red, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. MCF7 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 with L-glutamine and phenol red, supplemented with 10% FBS. TIG3 cells were cultured in MEM with Earle’s salts, L-glutamine, and sodium bicarbonate, supplemented with 10% FBS. For the clonogenic assay, MCF7 cells were maintained in MEME supplemented with 1% nonessential amino acids (NEAA), 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 10% FBS.

Preparation of Albumin-palladacycle Complex (APC) for intracellular reactivity assay

Stock solutions of palladacycle 9 (Fig. 3d) or 17 (Fig. 5b) (2 mM in DMSO) and human serum albumin (200 µM in water) were prepared. To assemble the albumin palladacycle complexes (APC-9 and APC-17), the corresponding palladacycle stock (50 µL, 100 nmol) and albumin stock (500 µL, 100 nmol) were combined in water (450 µL). The mixture was gently agitated and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to ensure encapsulation. The solutions were subjected to ultrafiltration (Amicon Ultra-0.5 mL, 30 kDa cutoff; Merck) at 14,800 × g for 5 min to remove debris. The resulting APC-9 or APC-17 was then used for the intracellular reaction with endogenous CO.

Intracellular phenanthridinone synthesis and quantification

Cells (2.5 × 105 for experiments with APC-9 and 1 × 106 for experiments with APC-17) were seeded in 6-cm dishes and incubated for 48 h. The culture medium was then replaced with 2 mL of fresh medium containing the pre-synthesized APC-9 or APC-17 (50 µM). After 24 h of incubation, DMSO (50 µL) and fresh medium were added to restore the volume to 2 mL, and the culture was incubated for an additional 24 h.

Cells were harvested by washing with cold PBS (1 mL), followed by trypsinization (0.5 mL), and resuspension in fresh medium (1.5 mL). Cell numbers were determined using an automated cell counter (Countess 3, Invitrogen). The cell suspension was pelleted, washed with cold PBS, and then centrifuged (600 × g, 3 min, 4 °C). The final pellet was resuspended in methanol (200 µL), vortexed for 2 min, and lysed by ultrasonication on ice (0 °C, 3 × 5 s; Ultrasonic Homogenizer UH-50, SMT Co.). The lysate was centrifuged (14,800 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) to remove debris, and the supernatant was analyzed by LC-MS to quantify the synthesized product (compound 10 or compound 14). Absolute quantification of the intracellularly synthesized product was performed using standard calibration curves (Supplementary Fig. S27, generated from purified compound 10, and Supplementary Fig. S28, generated from purified compound 14).

Assuming a mean cell diameter of 15 µm for HepG2 cells75, the total cellular volume was estimated to be 1.767 µL per 1.0 × 106 cells. Based on this value, the accumulated intracellular concentration of compound 10 in HepG2 cells (77 pmol per 1.0 × 106 cells) was calculated to be 43.5 µM. Using the same cellular volume approximation, the accumulated intracellular concentration of compound 14 was estimated at 36.4 nM, based on a total recovered amount of 0.220 pmol from 3.42 × 106 cells. Detailed mathematical derivations are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

In Cellulo assays of APC

Except for the ex situ CO-activated control (Fig. 5d), all in cellulo assays for the APCs were conducted in standard incubators without exogenous CO gas mixtures. The in situ carbonylation observed in these primary assays relies entirely on the endogenous CO flux natively generated by the cells.

Endocytosis inhibition and cellular uptake assay

HepG2 cells (2 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 52 h. The adherent cells were pre-incubated in medium containing 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 80 μM dynasore, and 50 μM albumin for 30 min at 37 °C. Following pre-incubation, the cells were treated with medium containing APC-9 (50 μM), dynasore (80 μM), and DMSO (5%) for 2 h. After the cellular uptake step, the cells were rinsed with cold medium (4 °C) and washed twice with acid buffer (0.1 M glycine, 150 mM sodium chloride, adjusted to pH 2.5 with 1 M aqueous hydrochloric acid) for 2 min each to remove surface-bound compounds. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min in the dark, washed with PBS, and imaged using a BZ-X810 fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, Japan). Fluorescence intensity was quantified (λex = 420 nm/λem = 460 nm) using a SpectraMax iD3 plate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS; Promega). HepG2 or TIG3 cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of the tested compounds (0–327.7 µM) in medium containing 1% DMSO and 9% PBS and incubated for 96 h. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm following a 2-h incubation with the MTS reagent.

Preparation of exogenous CO-activated complex for cell viability assays

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the system upon activation by exogenous CO, a stock solution of complex 17 (0–4 mM in DMSO) was placed in a reaction vessel, evacuated, and backfilled with CO gas (from a balloon). The mixture was stirred for 2 h at room temperature to ensure complete CO-mediated activation. To assemble the albumin-encapsulated products, an aliquot of this reaction mixture (10 µL, 0–4 mM) and an albumin stock solution (90 µL, 0–455 µM in PBS) were combined in cell culture medium (900 µL). The mixture was gently agitated and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate encapsulation. The resulting solution was immediately utilized for the MTS cell viability assays.

Clonogenic assays

MCF7 cells were seeded in 6-cm dishes at a density of 500 cells per dish. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the test compounds at the indicated concentrations (1, 5, 10 µM) in 0.5% DMSO. To minimize evaporation, the dishes were sealed, and the cultures were maintained for 12 days. The resulting colonies were fixed and stained with a solution containing 0.5% crystal violet and 6.0% glutaraldehyde for 30 min, rinsed with tap water, air-dried, and counted.
