Factors Associated With Self‐Perceived Burden and Its Relationship With Quality of Life Among Patients With Bladder Cancer Undergoing Ileal Conduit Urinary Diversion: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Jia‐lu Xu, Chan‐qing Fan, Ying‐qiong Peng, Xin‐ting Yang, Zuo Tan, Shi‐lin Gao, Ya‐li Tian, Deng‐bin Liao, Hong‐fei Ren, Chang‐qing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo determine the prevalence of self‐perceived burden (SPB), identify factors associated with SPB, and examine the associations of SPB with anxiety, depression, and quality of life (QOL) among patients with bladder cancer undergoing ileal conduit urinary diversion.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included 121 patients recruited from the Department of Urology at a tertiary hospital in China between January 2024 and June 2025. SPB was assessed as the primary outcome using the Self‐Perceived Burden Scale. Anxiety, depression, and QOL were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and the WHOQOL‐BREF, respectively. Correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between SPB and psychological symptoms and QOL, and multiple linear regression was used to identify factors independently associated with SPB.ResultsOverall, 66.94% of participants experienced SPB, including 36.36% with mild SPB, 27.27% with moderate SPB, and 3.31% with severe SPB. The impaired physical mobility (t = 3.266, p = 0.003), self‐care ability (F = 4.890, p = 0.009), monthly household income (F = 2.825, p = 0.028), and caregiver health status (F = 3.791, p = 0.025) were significantly associated with SPB. SPB was positively correlated with anxiety (r = 0.410, p < 0.01) and depression (r = 0.426, p < 0.01) but negatively correlated with all QOL domains (physiological, psychological, environmental and social relationships) (p < 0.01). In the final multivariable model, anxiety, depression and monthly household income were independently associated with SPB scores, collectively explaining 28.6% of the variance.ConclusionsSPB was common among patients with bladder cancer undergoing ileal conduit urinary diversion. Anxiety, depression, and lower monthly household income were independently associated with higher SPB, while SPB was negatively associated with QOL. These findings support the potential value of psychological assessment and targeted supportive care, although longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine causal relationships.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a common malignant tumor with high incidence and mortality rates worldwide. According to Global Cancer Statistics 2020, bladder cancer ranks 10th in global incidence and 13th in mortality [1]. Bladder cancer, which occurs more commonly in men than women, ranks fourth in cancer incidence and eighth in cancer‐related deaths among men [2]. Radical cystectomy (RC) combined with urinary diversion is the standard treatment option for muscle‐invasive bladder cancer and high‐risk nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer [3]. Ileal conduits, a common form of urinary diversion, effectively prolong survival but lead to long‐term challenges in physical, psychological, and social adjustment, such as altered body image, sexual dysfunction, and self‐care difficulties [4].

Notably, the patients after ileal conduits generally have a low quality of life (QOL), which is particularly significant in the domains of physical functioning, psychological status, and social adaptation [5]. Self‐perceived burden (SPB) in postoperative patients, defined as feelings of guilt, anxiety, and responsibility toward others due to their illness and care needs [6], has emerged as a critical psychological factor affecting recovery. Higher SPB has been associated with greater anxiety and depressive symptoms and poorer QOL; however, the direction and mechanisms of these relationships remain uncertain [7].

Although domestic and international studies have focused on the relationship between SPB and QOL, there is still a significant lack of research on patients after ileal conduit urinary diversion. For example, McPherson et al. [6] reported significant associations between SPB and anxiety and depression. Other studies have also demonstrated relationships among psychological distress, social support, self‐efficacy, and QOL, whereas a previous study revealed the key roles of social support and self‐efficacy in alleviating SPB [8]. Although existing studies have demonstrated a significant association between SPB and QOL, the mechanisms underlying the interactions between SPB, anxiety, and depression, such as the current status of SPB in bladder cancer patients after ileal conduit surgery, its influencing factors, and its relationships with anxiety, depression, and QOL, remain unclear. In addition, interventions for this group still focus on physical rehabilitation, and psychological and social adaptation support needs to be strengthened; further exploration is warranted.

Methods

Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study was conducted on postoperative patients admitted to the Department of Urology of our Hospital from January 2024 to June 2025.

A convenience sampling method was employed. The target sample size was estimated using a participant‐to‐variable ratio of 10:1 for 11 candidate independent variables in the primary SPB regression model [9]. The minimum required sample was therefore 110. Allowing for approximately 10% nonresponse or unusable questionnaires, the target sample was 121, and 121 participants were included in the final analysis (see Figure 1 for the participant flow diagram).

Inclusion process of the participants.

Of 150 patients assessed for eligibility, 13 were not enrolled (10 declined participation and 3 had communication barriers or were not competent to consent). Among the 137 consented patients, 7 were excluded because of serious postoperative complications, and 4 were lost to follow‐up. After excluding 5 patients with severe data deficiency, the final analytic sample comprised 121 participants.

The inclusion criteria: (1) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of bladder cancer and underwent ileal conduit surgery; (2) ≥ 1 month after surgery and stable condition; (3) aged ≥ 18 years, with basic communication skills; and (4) voluntarily signed the informed consent form and cooperated with the study.

The exclusion criteria: (1) patients with serious postoperative complications (e.g., enterocutaneous fistula, infectious shock); (2) patients with comorbid psychiatric diseases or cognitive disorders; and (3) patients who withdrew from the study halfway.

Research Tools

General Information Questionnaire

It includes: (1) demographic information, such as gender, age, marital status, and education level; (2) disease‐related characteristics, such as the presence of physical mobility impairments, course of the disease, self‐care ability, and knowledge of the disease; (3) caregiver information, such as the caregivers' health status; and (4) socioeconomic indicators, such as monthly household income and medical expense payment methods. The questionnaire has a content validity index (CVI) of 0.92 and a pretest Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.85.

Self‐Perceived Burden Scale (SPBS)

The SPBS with 10 entries was used to assess patients' perceived burden on carers and families. The items were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = “never” to 5 = “always”), higher scores indicated greater burden. SPBS < 20 was classified as not significant; 20 ≤ SPBS < 30 was classified as mild; a score of 30 ≤ SPBS < 40 was considered moderate; and SPBS ≥ 40 was considered severe [10]. The scale had a Cronbach's α of 0.909 in this study.

World Health Organization Quality of Life Scale (WHOQOL‐BREF)

A simplified Chinese version was used, containing 26 items in four domains: physiological, psychological, social, and environmental [11]. Entries 1–2 assessed overall QOL and health satisfaction, and entries 3–26 were rated on a 5‐point scale, with higher scores resulting in better QOL [12]. The Cronbach's α for the total scale in this study was greater than 0.825.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [13], was used to assess patients' anxiety and depressive symptoms; it contains 2 subscales for anxiety (HADS‐A) and depression (HADS‐D), each with 7 items, on a 4‐point scale (0–3) with a total score of 0–21. Critical values of anxiety ≥ 8 points and depression ≥ 9 points suggest abnormalities. The scale Cronbach's α values in this study were 0.729 (anxiety) and 0.746 (depression).

Data Collection

Data collection were carried out by uniformly trained research team members. Patients completed the questionnaires independently after receiving detailed instructions, with a completion time of approximately 20–30 min. After the questionnaires were completed, the researchers collected the questionnaires on the spot and conducted a preliminary quality check.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations (X¯± S) when normally distributed and as medians and interquartile ranges when non‐normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using [the Shapiro–Wilk test and inspection of histograms/Q–Q plots]. Differences in SPBS scores between participant subgroups were examined using independent‐samples t‐tests or one‐way analysis of variance, as appropriate. Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated according to variable distribution and measurement level to examine the associations of SPBS scores with anxiety, depression, and QOL scores (Figures 2 and 3).

Histogram of standardized residuals showing a normal distribution (Mean = 2.68E‐16, SD = 0.987).

Normal P–P plot demonstrating that observed residuals closely aligned with the expected normal line, confirming the normality assumption.

SPBS score was specified as the dependent variable in the multiple linear stepwise regression analysis. Candidate independent variables included presence of physical mobility impairments, self‐care ability, monthly household income, health status of caregivers, anxiety (HADS‐A), depression (HADS‐D), the four domains of the WHOQOL‐BREF (physiological, psychological, social relationship, and environmental) and overall QOL. Variables associated with SPBS scores at p < 0.05 in the univariable analyses, together with variables considered clinically relevant, were entered into the multivariable model. Categorical variables were coded as follows: monthly household income (1 = ≤ 1000 CNY, 2 = 1001–3000 CNY, 3 = 3001–6000 CNY, 4 = 6001–10,000 CNY, 5 = ≥ 10,001 CNY), presence of physical mobility impairments (1 = yes, 2 = no), self‐care ability (1 = able, 2 = partial, 3 = unable), and health status of caregivers (1 = good, 2 = general, 3 = poor).

A multiple linear stepwise regression procedure was applied using entry and removal criteria of PIN ≤ 0.05 and POUT ≥ 0.10. Linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, influential observations, and multicollinearity were evaluated using residual plots, the Durbin‐Watson statistic, tolerance, and variance inflation factors (VIF). The final model showed a Durbin‐Watson statistic of 1.774, and multicollinearity was not a concern (tolerance range: 0.520–0.985; VIF range: 1.015–1.924). All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0, and two‐sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

A stepwise procedure was used as an exploratory variable‐reduction approach because the study aimed to identify a parsimonious set of factors associated with SPB in a modest sample (N = 121). Given the known limitations of data‐driven selection, including coefficient instability and optimistic model fit, the resulting model should be considered exploratory and requires external validation.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital. All participants provided written informed consent prior to questionnaire completion.

Results

General Information of the Patients

A total of 121 patients who underwent ileal conduit urinary diversion were included (Figure 1). The majority of patients were male (76.0%), with a mean age of 63.45 ± 10.88 years old (range: 19–83 years), and were primarily elderly individuals. A total of 57.0% of patients had a disease course exceeding 3 months, 54.5% demonstrated complete self‐care ability, and 80.2% had knowledge of the disease. The monthly household income was predominantly distributed in the ranges of CNY 1001–3000 (38.0%) and CNY 3001–6000 (33.1%). The primary caregivers were spouses (48.8%) and other relatives (47.1%), and 66.1% of the caregivers were in good health.

The physical mobility impairments (t = 3.266, p = 0.003), self‐care ability (F = 4.890, p = 0.009), monthly household income (F = 2.825, p = 0.028), and caregiver health status (F = 3.791, p = 0.025) were significantly associated with SPBS scores in the univariable analyses. (Table 1).

The addition of anxiety, monthly household income, and depression at successive steps increased the explained variance by 21.3%, 4.3%, and 3.0%, respectively.

Discussion

Prevalence of SPB in Patients After Ileal Conduit Urinary Diversion

The investigation revealed that 66.94% patients experienced varying degrees of SPB, which indicated a high prevalence of SPB among patients who had undergone ileal conduit urinary diversion. A previous study revealed that the main reasons for SPB among bladder cancer patients included the following: (1) concern about the impact of their own care needs on the caregivers' physical health; (2) apprehension about the financial burden on the family caused by high treatment costs; (3) worry over the emotional burden imposed on the caregivers by the caregiving process; (4) fear of the emotional impact their illness or potential death would have on family members; and (5) concern that caregiving responsibilities would interfere with family members' work and social activities [14]. Studies have also indicated that greater SPB is associated with poorer physical, emotional, and functional health status; lower QOL; adverse effects on treatment decisions and prognosis; and an increased risk of suicidal tendencies among patients [15, 16, 17]. Therefore, early identification of risk factors for SPB in the postoperative period and the implementation of proactive, preventive interventions are of significant clinical importance for alleviating this psychological burden in patients.

Factors Associated With SPB

Presence of Physical Mobility Impairments

The present study revealed that patients with physical mobility impairment had significantly higher SPBS scores than patients without impairments. Physical mobility impairment may be related to patients' self‐care ability and increases their dependence on caregivers. Greater dependence on caregivers may be accompanied by feelings of guilt and self‐blame, which could partly explain the higher SPB scores observed among patients with mobility impairment. However, the cross‐sectional design does not permit determination of the temporal or causal direction of this relationship. These findings suggest that patients with mobility impairment may benefit from assessment of rehabilitation needs, self‐care support, and psychological well‐being. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of such interventions in reducing SPB should be evaluated in prospective interventional studies.

Self‐Care Ability

In exploratory analyses, patients who were completely dependent (n = 5) appeared to have higher SPBS scores than those who were partially dependent (n = 50) or fully independent (n = 66); however, because this subgroup contained very few participants, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. This observation is broadly consistent with the findings reported by Luo et al. [18]. A previous study [6] has reported associations between impaired physical function, lower self‐care ability, and greater perceived burden among patients and their families. Mechanistically, a decline in self‐care ability not only gradually diminishes patients' work capacity and restricts their social activities but also results in high dependence on caregivers, fostering a sense of frustration and guilt in patients. In addition, patients with limited self‐care ability require caregivers to invest more time and energy, which patients may perceive as disrupting caregivers' work and social activities. This perception could contribute to feelings of guilt and burden [19, 20, 21]. Given the small number of completely dependent patients in our sample, these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative and require confirmation in larger studies.

Monthly Household Income

This study demonstrated that lower monthly household income is a key factor influencing SPB, with a significant negative correlation observed (i.e., lower income associated with higher SPB). This finding aligns with those reported by Bazilainsky S. et al. [22] and Chang et al. [23]. Compared with patients with higher monthly household income, those with lower income experience a more pronounced psychological burden stemming from treatment costs. These combined pressures foster greater susceptibility to feelings of helplessness, self‐blame, and the perception of being a family burden, ultimately leading to elevated SPB [24, 25]. On the basis of these findings, the association between lower household income and higher SPB suggests that financial strain should be considered during psychosocial assessment. Referral to available social welfare, insurance consultation, or financial assistance services may be appropriate for patients reporting substantial financial difficulties. At the policy level, the findings may provide preliminary support for further research on the potential value of financial protection programs, rather than establishing the effectiveness of any specific policy intervention.

Health Status of Caregivers

In exploratory subgroup analyses, patients whose caregivers were in poor health (n = 3) had higher SPBS scores than those with caregivers in good or general health. However, because this estimate is based on a very small number of participants, this finding should not be overinterpreted. This phenomenon can be understood as the effect of empathic concern [10]: the physical fatigue experienced by caregivers (due to caregiving demands) may adversely affect their own health, prompting care recipients to feel intense guilt and perceive themselves as a burden, thereby increasing SPB. Nevertheless, prospective studies with larger samples are needed to confirm this association.

Anxiety and Depression

We found that the SPBS score was positively correlated with both anxiety and depression. Furthermore, both anxiety and depression scores were independently associated with SPBS scores in the multivariable model. One possible explanation is that depressive symptoms may be accompanied by reduced social engagement, while anxiety may be associated with lower confidence in coping with daily care needs. Conversely, greater SPB may also contribute to psychological distress. Given the cross‐sectional design, the direction of these relationships cannot be determined. The deterioration of these psychological states further weakens patients' self‐efficacy, making them more susceptible to a negative cycle of feeling powerless to change their situation, thereby exacerbating SPB. The following steps are recommended: (1) implement early psychological screening and intervention; (2) incorporate the HADS for regular assessment during postoperative follow‐up; and (3) provide cognitive–behavioral therapy (CBT) or mindfulness‐based stress reduction (MBSR) to patients with abnormal anxiety or depression scores to correct negative thinking.

Quality of Life

This study revealed a negative correlation between the SPBS score and the percentile scores of overall quality of life (QOL), the physiological domain of QOL, the psychological domain of QOL, the environmental domain, and the social relationship domain of QOL. Wu Dongmei et al. [26] reported that reduced QOL in patients after bladder cancer surgery is closely associated with factors such as medical‐economic stress, physical symptoms, and inadequate social support. Diminished work capacity postsurgery and the lifelong need for a urostomy pouch not only reduce expected income and increase financial pressure but also trigger negative emotions. Financial strain, physical symptoms, psychological distress, reduced social participation, SPB, and QOL may be interrelated. However, the present cross‐sectional analysis cannot determine the temporal sequence or causal pathways among these factors. Studies by Zhang WJ et al. [27], Grobet‐Jeandin E et al. [28], and Wang W et al. [29] consistently demonstrated that patients actively participating in rehabilitation training have a significantly lower risk of a decrease in QOL. Therefore, clinicians and nurses should focus on (1) addressing patients' psychological state and nonstoma‐related symptoms; (2) strengthening the prevention and management of postoperative complications; and (3) encouraging regular follow‐up visits and enhancing self‐management abilities. These findings highlight areas that may be considered in supportive care, although prospective studies are required to determine whether interventions targeting these factors improve QOL.

Study Limitations

This study also has several limitations. First, this study was conducted in a tertiary hospital in China; thus, the extrapolation of its conclusions is limited. Second, since this work is a cross‐sectional study, the use of a convenience sampling method and a sample predominantly composed of elderly patients with insufficient coverage of younger patient groups. Additionally, several subgroup categories in the univariable analyses contained very few participants, including those with poor caregiver health (n = 3) and those who were completely dependent for self‐care (n = 5). Consequently, estimates derived from these subgroups may be unstable, and these exploratory findings should be interpreted with caution. We have therefore avoided overemphasizing results from these small categories, and we recommend that future studies with larger, more diverse samples examine these associations more robustly. Third, potential influencing factors (e.g., social support, comorbidities) were excluded from the analysis. Future research should include more patients, particularly younger individuals and those from diverse cultural backgrounds, employ improved sampling methods, utilize longitudinal studies to explore the long‐term trajectory of SPB and its interactions with psychological symptoms, analyze multidimensional influencing factors, and design intervention programs targeting high‐risk factors to evaluate their effectiveness.

Clinical Implications

The findings suggest that healthcare professionals may consider incorporating assessment of SPB, anxiety, depression, self‐care ability, mobility limitations, caregiver health, and financial difficulties into postoperative follow‐up for patients undergoing ileal conduit urinary diversion. Anxiety, depression, and lower household income may help identify patients with higher SPB scores who could benefit from further psychosocial assessment and supportive services. However, because this study was cross‐sectional, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that interventions targeting these factors will necessarily reduce SPB or improve QOL. Prospective and interventional studies are required to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted supportive care strategies.

Conclusion

SPB was common among patients with bladder cancer undergoing ileal conduit urinary diversion. In univariable analyses, SPB scores were associated with physical mobility impairment, self‐care ability, monthly household income, and caregiver health status. Anxiety, depression, and monthly household income remained independently associated with SPB scores in the multivariable model. Higher SPB scores were also associated with poorer QOL. These findings identify potential areas for psychosocial assessment, but longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal relationships.
