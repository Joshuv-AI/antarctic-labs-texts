Organization of the first national workshop for families in Switzerland concerned with hereditary cancer syndromes
Authors: Sarah Glick, Monica Aceti, Claire M Barton, Bettina M Zimmermann, Lisa Schiess, Muriel Fluri, Andrea Kaiser-Grolimund, Carla Pedrazzani, Elissa Azzi, Arianna Maria Elena Bonfanti, Barbara Peters, Benjamin Thiele, Pierre O Chappuis, Maria Caiata-Zufferey, Maria C Katapodi, The CASCADE Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) workshops with families affected by hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes can benefit both affected individuals and their support networks, PPI workshops are rarely being organized. This article presents the planning and implementation of a one-day PPI workshop for families affected by Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC) or Lynch Syndrome (LS) in Switzerland.Workshop design & implementationThe event was funded by the Swiss National Science Foundation and planned over a 12-month period. The program included expert sessions presented by clinicians and researchers; moderated, small-group participatory exchanges exploring patients’ and families’ unmet health, psychosocial and educational needs; and interactions with patient advocates. Participant satisfaction surveys were analyzed using descriptive statistics.ResultsOne hundred and five adults attended the workshop in Bern in May 2025, with representation from the three major linguistic regions of Switzerland. Almost 75% of participants reported a personal genetic predisposition to cancer. Most respondents were very satisfied or satisfied with the workshop (92%), and nearly all (96%) found the presentations engaging, with the topic of immunotherapy as an emerging treatment rated highest. The participatory exchange sessions were the most valued component of the workshop overall (97% positive feedback).ConclusionsWe provide proof of concept for the feasibility and positive impact of organizing a national workshop for families affected by HBOC and LS. The participatory nature of the workshop allowed affected individuals and their support networks to communicate unmet needs related to their personal experiences and suggest future avenues for research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12687-026-00960-1.Keywords: Patient and public involvement, Participatory research, Family workshop, Patient advocacy, Support networks, Patient education, Patient unmet needs

Introduction

Genetic and clinical consultations remain central to the management of Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer syndrome (HBOC) and Lynch syndrome (LS), although families often express unmet needs that extend beyond these healthcare encounters, such as barriers to accessing genetic services, conflicting recommendations for surveillance and risk management, psychological well-being, and lack of support regarding family planning and family communication about cancer risk (Caiata-Zufferey et al. 2025, Sarki et al. 2022, Pedrazzani et al. 2022, O’Neill 2018). Workshops incorporating a Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) component can provide insights into unmet needs that are not always captured during routine clinical interactions (Arumugam et al. 2023, Duffett 2017). This is especially important for HBOC and LS, where the impact reaches far beyond individual medical decision-making to affect entire families across generations (Corines et al. 2017, Bannon et al. 2014, Espenschied et al. 2012).

To our knowledge, a PPI workshop specifically addressing hereditary cancer predisposition had never been hosted in Switzerland. To address this gap, we organized a one-day, in-person workshop dedicated to families affected by HBOC or LS in May 2025. The purpose was to inform attendees about updated risk management guidelines and emerging treatments and offer a community-building opportunity for expressing unmet needs. We report insights from the year-long planning process, event structure, publicity, and practical considerations involved in organizing this community genetics initiative.

Organization

Funding and venue

The workshop was organized over 12 months by the Swiss CASCADE Consortium (NCT03124212), which has led the family-based CASCADE cohort since 2017 (Katapodi et al. 2017). Funding was provided by the Swiss National Science Foundation Agora program (LAARP0_229708). This enabled free participation, including meals, parking, childcare, museum tour, and the closing reception. The workshop was held at the Paul Klee Museum in Bern, Switzerland, which was selected due to its central location, accessibility by public transportation, facilities for plenary and small-group sessions, experience with hosting multilingual events with simultaneous translation, and national cultural relevance.

Intended audience

The workshop targeted families affected by HBOC or LS, their support networks (partners, friends, caregivers) and interested members of the public, along with clinicians who provide genetic services in Switzerland, and patient support organizations. No prior knowledge of cancer genetics was required. The event was open to individuals with or without a personal history of cancer.

Publicity and registration

The workshop was nationally promoted for over 6 months using multiple strategies. CASCADE cohort participants were invited by direct mail and through the CASCADE and University of Basel websites and annual newsletter. To reach non-CASCADE participants, flyers were distributed in 14 onco-genetic clinics across Switzerland, while invitations were sent to 89 Swiss genetic testing centers and cantonal cancer societies and to 40 patient support groups, for dissemination through their networks and social media. Google advertisements were also used. Digital and printed materials were available in German, French, Italian, and English. Promotional materials included a QR code and a web link for registration. Online registration remained open until 5 days before the workshop, with on-site registration available. The registration form collected information on participants’ spoken language(s), canton of residence, topic of interest (HBOC, LS, or no preference), and number of accompanying family or support persons. Gender and age data were not collected.

Program

The morning program consisted of six 15–20-min plenary sessions delivered by clinical and research experts. Topics included cancer surveillance in hereditary cancer over the lifetime; family communication and decision-making; immunotherapy as an emerging treatment option; using digital tools to enhance surveillance and family communication of genetic cancer risk; and responsible use of research data (Online Resource 1). Two patient advocates from patient support organizations and two representatives from participatory research organizations each delivered 10-min oral presentations and were available for informal exchanges during the coffee breaks, lunch, and concluding reception. Presentations were simultaneously translated by six translators to cover different language combinations. The German, French, and Italian versions of the slide decks were simultaneously displayed on a large screen at the front of the auditorium. Participants received all slide decks in their preferred language, both as a paper copy for notetaking and as an electronic copy to share with family members and friends who could not attend the event.

The afternoon program included 1-h facilitated discussions to identify unmet needs in information, communication, access to healthcare, and psychosocial support at diagnosis and across the care continuum. Eight groups of 10–15 participants were organized by language (French, German, Italian), topic preference (HBOC, LS, or none), and participant role (affected individual or other). Each group was led by a health-related professional moderator and supported by an assistant. Further details about the organization of the facilitated participatory exchange sessions and collated results will be presented elsewhere.

Evaluation

An anonymous evaluation form was developed collaboratively by clinicians, researchers, and the organizing team. Completion required 5–10 min and was voluntary. Forms were available in German, French, Italian, and English and were collected at workshop completion or venue exit (Online Resource 2). Evaluations assessed overall satisfaction, expert presentations, facilitated discussions, and logistics (duration, pace, location, and translation services), using 7-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree). Open-text questions evaluated strengths, areas for improvement, and future recommendations. Feedback was summarized using descriptive statistics.

Participant consent

Given the anonymous data collection, participant written consent and ethics approval were not required. Facilitated discussions were neither audio- nor video-recorded. Participants received written information about photography during registration, with an option to opt out.

Outcomes

Participants

A total of 105 adults from 17 of 26 Swiss cantons attended the workshop. Seventy-five evaluation forms were returned (71%), with some completed jointly by couples or families. Most participants (72%) self-identified as having a genetic cancer predisposition; the remaining self-identified as family members, friends, or members of support organizations. About 50% learned about the event directly from the organizers and 25% through word-of-mouth communication from friends or family; social media was not a major avenue for reaching participants (Table 1).

Feedback

Satisfaction with the workshop was similar across languages, with 92% of responders reporting being satisfied or very satisfied, with no reports of dissatisfaction. The educational presentations met expectations and were rated as relevant, easy to understand, and engaging. The variety of topics, multilingual format, and opportunity to share experiences and connect with others were highly valued. The presentation about immunotherapy was rated highest (88% positive). Participation in small group discussions was valued by 90% of responders, while 93% felt comfortable expressing unmet needs. Respondents valued the opportunity to interact with patient support groups and to get involved in research. Most found the workshop length and pace of presentations appropriate (75% and 93%, respectively). About one in three would have liked more time to ask questions and one in five would have liked more opportunities to network (Table 2).

Feedback collected from the evaluation forms

Free-text comments described the workshop as well-organized and informative. There were relatively few comments addressing the least satisfying aspects of the workshop, most often noting a desire for time to ask personal questions to experts, more time on specific topics, or interest in topics not covered during the workshop, for example, sexuality after cancer treatment. There were also conflicting comments, for example, one participant requesting more detailed information about life expectancy after cancer diagnosis, while another participant found it distressing to hear about “years of life”.

Discussion

The successful organization of Switzerland’s first national workshop for families affected by HBOC or LS demonstrated the feasibility of such community-based initiatives. The workshop served simultaneously as an educational intervention, a mechanism for PPI involvement in research, and a community-building activity. We showed that family-oriented workshops can address broader needs in community genetics, affirming the importance of two-way interactions between research groups and the wider hereditary cancer community. Information gathered through formal evaluations and participatory exchanges can better inform the organization of support services, communication strategies, and research aims (Duffett 2017, Brett et al. 2014).

Strengths included diverse topics and engaging, knowledgeable speakers. Future workshops could offer parallel expert presentations and additional time for questions. Participants particularly valued the group discussions, which enabled open exchange on sensitive topics and unmet needs. Group discussions were rated even higher than expert presentations, underscoring the importance of “active listening.” The centrally located venue promoted equitable access, while simultaneous multilingual translation supported linguistic inclusion and national participation. Free offerings aimed to increase participation.

However, travel time, active cancer treatment, and privacy concerns may have limited attendance. A hybrid format could increase participation, although interpersonal exchanges would most likely be hindered. We also acknowledge potential self-selection bias, as attendees may have been more engaged than non-participants. Vulnerable or marginalized individuals may be underrepresented due to socioeconomic, cultural, or health barriers. Workshop outreach was also challenging, as many support groups and genetic testing centers did not respond.

Conclusion

Our experience affirms the feasibility of organizing PPI workshops for families affected by genetic predisposition to cancer, supports the importance of family-based programs as a tool for information dissemination, and affirms the importance of participatory research and peer support. Future workshops can build upon this organizational framework and lessons learned to deepen family-researcher-clinician-community partnerships.

Declarations

Compliance with ethics guidelines

The implementation of the workshop did not require approval from an ethics committee. Participation in the workshop was voluntary, evaluation and sticky note data was anonymous, and explicit consent was not requested except for whether participants were willing to be photographed (opt-out available). Only minimal data were recorded for participants, namely their spoken language(s), their canton of residence, and their preferred discussion topic (HBOC, LS or no preference). The protocol of the workshop was reviewed and approved by the Swiss National Science Foundation, and the workshop did not fall under the Swiss Human Research Act (art. 2).
