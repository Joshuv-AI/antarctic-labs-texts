A mechanically compliant SrTiO3 nanofilm interface promotes diabetic osseointegration by modulating Piezo1-associated macrophage osteoimmunomodulation
Authors: Shuo Guo, Chao-Yu Lan, Lu Ren, Dan-Ting Shang, Sha-Sha Zhao, Xiao-Yan Qu, Yao Tong, Ze-Dong Yan, Juan Liu, Yu-Lan Tian, Min-Yang Wang, Tian Chai, Xiao-Qin Li, Song-Yue Li, Er-Ping Luo, Zhi-Gang Wu, Wei Li, Wen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a widely applied material for bone repair in clinical practice, titanium alloy showed multiple excellent characteristics including mechanical properties and biocompatibility. However, under the state of diabetes mellitus (DM), insufficient osseointegration and impaired bone repair occurred driven by the tendency of macrophage pro-inflammatory polarization. To alleviate diabetes-associated macrophage inflammation, we fabricated a SrTiO3 nanofilm-engineered titanium surface (RTS) with a mechanically compliant film–stiff substrate interface and sustained Sr ion release. Under diabetic-mimicking conditions, RTS provided an integrated osteoimmune interface and suppressed pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization and osteoclast-like differentiation tendency, combined with reduced Piezo1-associated Ca2+ signaling and CaMKII activation. Macrophages regulated by RTS further created a favorable immune microenvironment for osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3-e1 cells, followed by the superior osteogenic differentiation, as verified in vitro and in vivo. Notably, in diabetic rats, collagen formation and osteopontin (OPN) expression surrounding the RTS implants combined with superior osseointegration were observed, indicating improved peri-implant bone formation. Yoda1 partially reversed the protective effect of RTS, supporting the involvement of Piezo1-associated mechanotransduction in RTS-mediated osteoimmunomodulation. These findings suggest that RTS may serve as a promising candidate for improving osseointegration under diabetic conditions.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Osseointegration, Macrophage, Piezo1, Stiffness

Introduction

As a widely used repair material in clinical practice, titanium and its alloys are widely applied in orthopedics, stomatology and neurosurgery because of its good mechanical properties, biocompatibility and corrosion resistance [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In the process of bone repair, osseointegration, a complex physiological behavior occurring at the interface between the implant and bone tissue, determines the firmness of bone-implant bonding [5]. However, the persistent chronic inflammatory microenvironment induced by various factors after implantation often leads to the poor integration between the bone and the implants, eventually leading to prosthesis loosening or displacement, and ultimately, implantation failure. This risk is significantly elevated in patients with chronic metabolic diseases, particularly diabetes mellitus (DM) [6,7].

DM, a metabolic disease that systemically alters the physiological microenvironment, seriously disrupts the repair and healing of various tissues in the body, including osseointegration [8,9]. Multiple studies have elucidated the complex internal pathophysiological mechanisms, confirming that the osseointegration process is disrupted by factors such as macrophage polarization disorder and the imbalance in osteoblast-osteoclast dynamic regulation [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Several research have made pioneering explorations on tissue repair in diabetic environments [4,14,15]. Monocyte/macrophage-lineage cells can acquire pre-osteoclastic features and further fuse into multinucleated osteoclasts under RANKL-dependent stimulation [[16], [17], [18]]. The polarization balance of macrophages is intricately linked to the inflammatory response, regulating osteogenic behavior by secreting cytokines such as BMP-2, PDGF-BB and IL-4. Among these, BMP-2 is deeply involved in the bone repair process by activating the RunX2-Sp7 pathway [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Macrophages can polarize into multiple subtypes, primarily M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory), which govern the tissue repair outcomes [23,24]. Under physiological conditions, macrophages are rapidly recruited around the injured tissue and polarize toward M1 subtype, producing pro-inflammatory factors to clear tissue debris and foreign pathogens [25,26]. However, in diabetic patients, this function is over-activated. The continuous polarization of M1 macrophages impairs osseointegration around the prosthesis, and even leads to osteolysis of the formed bone tissue by promoting osteoclast differentiation and maturation [27]. Cellular debris resulting from the inflammatory response further aggravates the inflammatory response, creating a feedback loop [28,29]. Therefore, correcting the disordered polarization and hyperactive osteoclast-like differentiation tendency of macrophages in the diabetic environment is essential for achieving robust implant-bone contact.

Recent studies have proposed constructing nano-topography (titanium oxide nanotubes, e.g.) on the surface of titanium alloy to regulate macrophage polarization and promote vascularized bone repair around the prosthesis based on multiple strategies, including the construction of hydrogel complexes, accelerating the healing of diabetic wounds effectively [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. Furthermore, the role of matrix stiffness/hardness on the behavior of macrophages, including polarization and function, has recently garnered significant attention [36]. Previous studies have identified Piezo1 as a crucial mechanosensor involved in activating inflammatory responses [[37], [38], [39]], making it a promising target for regulating various macrophage functions. Nano-morphology and hydrophilicity have the potential to regulate cell behavior, including cell adhesion, inflammation regulation and osteogenesis. The underlying mechanism involves changes in intracellular physiological processes involved in piezo1, such as calcium influx [[40], [41], [42]]. Additionally, numerous studies have highlighted the vital role of specific ions in bone repair [43]. In our previous study, we developed Sr/Mg-incorporated titanate coatings and the ability to regulate macrophage polarization was confirmed, promoting vascularized and lymphatic bone repair under normal conditions [44]. However, whether the SrTiO3 nanofilm can restore osseointegration under diabetic state and whether stiffness-associated mechanotransductive cues regulate macrophage inflammation, osteoclast-like differentiation and osteogenesis remains unclear. Therefore, we focus on the diabetic osteoimmune microenvironment and investigates the Piezo1-associated inflammation–osteoclastogenesis–osteogenesis axis regulated by RTS in this study.

Methods and materials

Construction of strontium titanate nanofilms

The specimens were prepared as described in our previous research [44]. Briefly, commercial titanium alloys (TC4: Ti6Al4V) were machined into discs (∅14 × 2 mm) and screws (M1.6 × 4 mm) (abbr. TC4). Then the specimens were treated with sandblasting and then etched, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in order (abbr. RT). Hereafter, the samples were subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180°C for 4 h to synthesize Sr-incorporated nanogranular coatings (abbr. RTS).

The sustained Sr ion release behavior of RTS has been systematically demonstrated in our previous work mentioned above [44]. Therefore, in the present study, the RTS coating served as an established ion-releasing SrTiO3 nanofilm platform. This research mainly focused on its biological performance and Piezo1-associated osteoimmune regulation under diabetic conditions.

Structure and property characterization of the coatings

Surface and cross-sectional morphological images were caught through a scanning electron microscope (SEM, SU6600, Japan) and transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL-JEM 2100 F, Japan). Nanoparticles scraped from the coatings were analyzed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEOL-JEM 2100 F, Japan) equipped with EDX (JEOL, Japan). Surface hydrophilicity was evaluated by measuring the water contact angle using a contact angle goniometer (DSA30, Kruss, Germany). The surface roughness was examined via atomic force microscopy (AFM; BRUKER Dimension Icon, Germany). Nano-indenter (Brucker, Hysitron TI980) was applied to detect the modulus of RTS nanofilm. RTS was immersed in 10 mL of PBS (containing glucose (25 mmol/L) and palmitate-conjugated BSA (500 μmol/L)) for different predetermined time at 37°C, and then the target ion concentrations of extract liquid abovementioned were detected using an inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800, USA). Then the morphology, roughness, and mechanical modulus of the coating were detected to assess the long-term service stability of the coating under the diabetic pathological environment.

Cell culture

Macrophage cell line Raw264.7 and osteoblast precursor cell line MC3T3-e1 were utilized in this research. Raw264.7 cells were cultured with DMEM complete medium (Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (InCellGene, IC-2206-S) mixed with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, InCellGene, IC-1907) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, U.S.A.)) containing high glucose (25 mmol/L) and palmitate-conjugated BSA (500 μmol/L) to mimics the culture atmosphere of type 2 diabetes mellitus (denoted as DM), palmitate was conjugated with fatty-acid-free BSA before use.

To thoroughly investigate the effect of matrix hardness on Piezo1 under DM conditions, the Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 (AmBeed, A1552284; supplemented to macrophages cultured with DM on TC4, denoted as DM@Gs, 4 μM) and the agonist Yoda1 (AmBeed, A458265; supplemented to macrophages cultured with DM on RTS, denoted as DM@RTS/Y, 5 μM) were applied. GsMTx4 was used to determine whether pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 mimics the immunomodulatory effect of RTS, whereas Yoda1 was used to determine whether Piezo1 activation reverses the protective effect of RTS. Cells cultured with the extracts from RTS (prepared by immersing RTS specimens in DM for corresponding time) were noted as DM@Sr. MC3T3-e1 cells were cultured with α-MEM complete medium. All cells were maintained in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and 95% humidity. Cell passaging was performed using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. Co-culture conditions involving DM media and TC4, RT or RTS substrates were denoted as DM@TC4, DM@RT and DM@RTS respectively.

Evaluation of the regulatory effect of RTS nanofilm on cell viability, macrophage polarization, morphology and secretion of cytokines

Raw264.7 cells were subjected to different treatments (DM@Gs, DM@TC4, DM@RTS/Y, DM@RTS, DM@RT) at the density of 2 × 104/mL per well in 24-well plates. CCK-8 was used to assess the overall viability of macrophages under DMEM complete medium (Control) and simulated diabetic conditions (DM). The polarization markers were evaluated together with the cell morphology and cytokine secretion profile. We selected iNOS (as the marker for M1 macrophages) and CD206 (as the marker for M2 macrophages) to determine polarization trends. For immunofluorescence staining, macrophages from each group were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and washed with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized and blocked in sequence. Finally, the specimens were subjected to primary antibodies (CD206, Abcam, ab64693; iNOS, Abcam, ab49999). After being washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) and incubated in secondary antibodies labelled with fluorescent probes, the samples were stained with DAPI. Images were acquired with laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM). Morphological observations were conducted according to operational specification. To assess the pro-osteogenic potential of macrophages, the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA, MultiSciences Biotech Co. Ltd, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) for BMP-2 and IL-6 was conducted. The procedures were conducted strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each experiment was repeated three times.

RNA-seq analysis of macrophage and MC3T3-e1 transcription treated under different conditions

To clarify the transcriptional alterations in Raw264.7 and MC3T3-e1 cells, we used transcriptome sequencing to detect the activation of related genes and the pathways involved. Raw264.7 cells seeded on DM@RTS were cultured for 3 days, the supernatant was harvested and applied for culturing MC3T3-e1. Collection of total RNA, mRNA and following steps (fragmentation, inverse transcription etc.) were performed by Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The functions and related signal pathways of DETs (Differentially expressed transcripts) were verified through KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and GO (Gene Ontology) databases. Differentially expressed transcripts were identified using |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and FDR <0.05 as thresholds. The multiple-testing correction method (Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjustment).

Detection of pre-osteoclastic activation of macrophages and exploration of related mechanisms

To evaluate the osteoclast-like differentiation tendency of macrophages, the mRNA and protein expression levels of pre-osteoclastic related markers (c-Fos, OCSTAMP, DCSTAMP, and NFATC1) were determined via RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence staining (NFATC1, Abcam, ab25916). The relevant primer sequences applied in this research are listed in Table S1. GAPDH served as the house-keeping gene. After culturing on specimens for 5 days, macrophages treated with each group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. RT-qPCR and immunofluorescence staining were conducted following standard steps. Tartrate Resistant Acid Phosphatase Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0332) and cytoskeleton staining (phalloidin labelled with FITC, InCellGene, IC-6134) were utilized to assess the fusion status of macrophages and evaluate the osteoclast-like differentiation tendency, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each experiment was repeated three times.

To explore the relationship between RTS, inflammation, pre-osteoclastic activation and Piezo1, immunofluorescence staining (Piezo1, Proteintech, 28511-1-AP), calcium influx and western blot (Piezo1, Proteintech, 28511-1-AP; p-CaMKII, Cell Signaling Technology, 12716; CaMKII, Abcam, ab52476, total CaMKII pan antibody; iNOS, Abcam, ab178945; Arg-1, Proteintech, 66129-1-Ig; NFATC1, Abcam, ab25916) were applied. For calcium imaging, cells were seeded at an equal density in all groups and imaged under identical acquisition settings. Fluorescence quantification was performed using the same analysis workflow for all samples. Data were obtained from 3 independent experiments. For detection of Piezo1, p-CaMKII and CaMKII, cells were lysed after treatment for 6 h, others were lysed after treatment for 48 h.

Evaluation of osteogenesis in vitro

Macrophage-conditioned medium-induced osteogenesis

To clarify the effect of RTS on osteogenesis via regulating macrophages polarization, MC3T3-e1 cells (2 × 104/mL) were cultured with the supernatant harvested from the macrophage mentioned in Section 2.5, noted as DM@RTS/Y-R, DM@T-R, DM@Gs-R, DM@R-R and DM@S-R respectively.

Direct osteogenic effect of RTS and Sr extract

Additionally, MC3T3-e1 cells were incubated on DM@TC4, DM@RTS and DM@Sr, serving as the comparison baseline. ALP staining (C3206, Beyotime, China) and alizarin red staining (C0148S, Beyotime, China) were operated to detect the osteogenic ability. The expression levels of Runx-2 (Primary antibody: ab192256, abcam) and Sp7 (Primary antibody: AF7080, Affinity) were determind via immunofluorescence staining. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Estimation of macrophage inflammatory response, pre-osteoclastic activation and osteogenesis in diabetic rats

For in vivo research, Sprague Dawley rats (SD rats, 6-7 weeks, male) were provided by the Fourth Military Medical University and. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a randomization procedure before intervention. Outcome assessment, including histological evaluation, imaging analysis, and quantitative measurements, was performed by investigators blinded to group allocation whenever applicable. To induce a diabetic microenvironment, the rats were fed with a high-fat diet (HFD) for 4 weeks. Then the rats were intraperitoneally injected with STZ (30 mg/kg, 2 wt%, dissolved in fresh cold citrate buffer (pH 4.5)), with the HFD persisting for another week. The blood glucose level (tail-vein blood) was measured 1 week after STZ administration via portable glucometer and the rats with glucose levels persistently exceeding 16.7 mmol/L (300 mg/dL) were considered as diabetic rats. Body weight and blood glucose were monitored weekly during the establishment period. The study has been approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Air Force Medical University (No. IACUC-20241491) and the whole process was based on animal welfare requirements. Unilateral femoral condyle implantation model was conducted for the whole study in vivo. The rat was considered the independent biological replicate for all statistical analyses. Briefly, DM rats mentioned above were subjected with the intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (10 mg/100 g). A incision (1.5–2 cm) along the long axis of the femur was made on the skin of the lateral femoral condyle. After blunt dissection, the lateral femoral condyle was exposed and constructed a channel (φ: 1.5 mm, length: 4 mm) perpendicular to the long axis via electric drill (ASOYOGA, China). Finally, the implants (TC4 and RTS, noted as DM@TC4 and DM@RTS) were gently screwed in. DM@TC4 was treated with inhibitor GsMTx4 (dosage of 100 μL/kg with a concentration of 10 μM, every other day, injected intraperitoneally, noted as DM@Gs) while DM@RTS was treated with agonist Yoda1 (injected intraperitoneally with 5 μmol/kg, 40 mM dissolved in DMSO then diluted with 5% ethanol, noted as DM@RTS/Y, served as RTS rescue) daily for five consecutive days per week until time of sacrifice. All non-Yoda1 groups received the same vehicle as applied in DM@RTS/Y. Each rat served as an independent replicate. Representative immunofluorescence and Masson staining images are presented as high-magnification views of the peri-implant region adjacent to the implant threads, which was the predefined region of interest for histological observation and quantitative analysis.

Evaluation of inflammatory cell infiltration, macrophage polarization marker detection and osteoclast-like differentiation tendency surrounding the implants

7 days after operation, the rats were sacrificed. The samples were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation and 10% EDTA for decalcification (21-28 d) in sequence. Then the distal femurs were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. The specimens were cut into slices (∼10 μm) and treated with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. Immunofluorescence staining for macrophage polarization markers (iNOS and Arg-1) and Piezo1 was performed using primary antibodies (iNOS, Abcam, ab178945; Arg-1, Proteintech, 66129-1-Ig; Piezo1, Proteintech, 28511-1-AP). Trap staining was conducted consistent with the above steps. LSCM was applied for images acquisition and Image-Pro Plus software was applied for analyses.

Osteogenic assessment around the implants

To preliminarily clarify the osteogenic property of RTS under the diabetic environment, Micro-CT, Van Gieson (VG) staining and Masson's trichrome staining were applied. For Van Gieson (VG) staining and Masson's trichrome staining, rats were sacrificed 2/4/6 weeks after implantation. VG staining was carried out following standard process. Generally, the specimens obtained were encapsulated in methyl methacrylate resin, followed by slicing (∼50 μm in thickness), ground, polishing staining and images acquisition. For Micro-CT, rats were sacrificed 2/4 weeks after operation, 100 μm around the implant was set as the region of interest (ROI). To assess dynamic bone formation, rats were intraperitoneally injected with calcein and alizarin red at 16 and 23 days post-implantation, respectively.

Synthesis of Col-1 and OPN was determined to evaluate the osteogenic differentiation via immunofluorescent staining, corresponding primary antibodies (Col-1: BF8424, Affinity; OPN: AF0227, Affinity) 3/6 weeks after implantation. The femurs containing screws were obtained and managed with immunofluorescent staining as described.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test, as appropriate. Differences between groups are considered to exist when the p-values were <0.05.

Results and discussion

Structure and property of the coatings

SEM surface images revealed that compared with RTS, the TC4 surface was relatively flat and smooth (Fig. 1A). In contrast, prominent macro/micro-roughness topography was observed on the RTS surfaces. Cross-sectional SEM images depicted the gradual structural evolution of the gradient-roughness surface and the integrated nanoparticle film (Fig. 1B).

Morphology characterization of RTS. (A) SEM surface pictures of TC4, RT and RTS. (B) Cross-sectional SEM pictures. (C) TEM panoramic bright-field images of RTS. HRTEM images (c1, c2), SAED patterns (c3) and EDS pictures (D).

TEM, SAED, and HRTEM analyses of the nanoparticles scraped from the RTS surface further confirmed that the nanoparticles were composed entirely of polycrystalline SrTiO3.

The hydrophilicity of TC4, RT and RTS was characterized via water contact angle (Fig. 2A). The results indicated that the macro/microscale roughness induced by sandblasting and acid etching on the RT surface enhanced the hydrophilicity compared to the machined titanium alloy (TC4). Compared with RT, the hydrophilicity was significantly increased, contributed from its chemical composition and nano-topography. Due to the high specific surface area from nanoscale roughness, RTS presented favorable hydrophilicity. To elucidate the mechanical cues contributed by the gradient surface, nanoindentation was performed. As listed in Fig. 2B, the commercially polished TC4 exhibited a high elastic modulus (∼110 GPa), which is mechanically mismatched with that of natural bone tissue. In contrast, RTS exhibited a reduced effective surface modulus of approximately 6–9 GPa, which closely matches the stiffness of natural cancellous bone. Recent studies in mechanobiology reveal that substrate stiffness profoundly dictates macrophage behavior. The engineered mechanically compliant microenvironment (low modulus) combined with the Sr ions released rapidly at the initial stage followed by a continued release phase, is expected to synergistically reduce the skeletal tension of macrophages, thereby regulating polarization. AFM results (Fig. 2C) revealed that low roughness value was observed on TC4 surface, indicating that the nanogranules play a crucial role in creating nanoscale roughness, which is highly beneficial for cell adhesion. Fig. S3 revealed that the RTS can stably release strontium ions under simulated diabetic conditions. Additionally, as indicated in Fig. S4, no significant changes were observed in the surface morphology, hydrophilicity and mechanical modulus of the material in the simulated diabetic environment, suggesting that the samples are certain stable under diabetic-mimicked environment.

Effects of RTS on macrophage incubated under DM condition

As revealed in Fig. S6, Diabetes-mimicking medium had no significant effect on macrophage activity. To further elucidate the underlying mechanisms responsible for these differences, we investigated the effect of RTS on macrophages in vitro. As shown in Fig. 4A, macrophages seeded on DM@RTS exhibited a relatively elliptical morphology. Additionally, consistent with its morphology, the expression of iNOS decreased, while the synthesis of CD206 increased, indicating a tendency toward M2 polarization (Fig. 4B–D). Concomitant with these phenotypic shifts in macrophages, the secretion of the osteogenic cytokine BMP-2 was upregulated, whereas the inflammatory factor IL-6 was suppressed on RTS (Fig. 4E).

Alongside the shift in polarization, macrophages cultured on the RTS surface exhibited a downward trend in Piezo1 expression (Fig. 4C–F). This downregulation may play a pivotal role in mediating the macrophage polarization balance from M1 to M2, thereby directly inhibiting the differentiation and maturation of osteoclasts [45,46]. Additionally, compared to DM@Sr and DM@RT, macrophage seeded on DM@RTS polarized towards M2 subtype more profoundly, which may regulated pre-osteoclastic activation indirectly, attributed by the hardness of strontium titanate nanofilms. To verify this hypothesis, we used RT-qPCR to detect the transcription levels of pre-osteoclast related genes (NAFTC, c-FOS, DCSTAMP, OCSTAMP), revealing that the expression of pre-osteoclastic related genes on the surface of RTS showed a downward trend (Fig. 4G). Generally, in this study, RTS achieves the regulation of macrophages from osteoclastic tendency to osteogenic direction in diabetic state through improved hydrophilicity, nanotopological morphology, released strontium ions and film stiffness. In the whole process, superior hydrophilicity promotes cell adhesion and recruitment firstly, providing the basis for repair; secondly, surface nanostructures, released strontium ions and mechanical compliance reduces the pro-inflammatory differentiation and osteoclast precursor differentiation of macrophages, in which the calcium influx suppression regulated by Piezo1 was involved. In addition, the strontium ions released from the film also provide long-term conditions for subsequent bone repair [38,[47], [48], [49]].

As reported previously, the expression change of Piezo1 is deeply involved in inflammatory response and the differentiation of osteoclast precursor cells from macrophages [39]. Piezo1-associated calcium influx promotes macrophage polarization to M1 phenotype, which is intimately linked to the pre-osteoclastic activation. Fig. S2 implies its possible internal mechanism: RTS attenuates the degree of calcium influx compared to other groups (Fig. S2A and B), accompanied with the decreased p-CaMKII Thr286/total CaMKII. The corresponding anti-inflammatory polarization trend was observed to decrease and pre-osteoclastic activation was inhibited as well, suggesting that under the action of RTS, the expression of Piezo1 was inhibited, which attenuated the influx of calcium ions and participated in the regulation of immune-osteoclast-like differentiation. It should be noted that comparing to DM@RTS, DM@RTS/Y promoted pro-inflammatory differentiation and resulted in corresponding functional change, indicating Piezo1 was involved in regulating polarization by RTS.

RNA-seq analysis of RTS effects on macrophages under DM conditions

As depicted in Fig. 5, RNA-seq analysis for DM@TC4 vs DM@RTS is applied to profile the differential expression of genes in macrophages, signaling pathways and related biological functions, serving as the guidance for further research. Compared with the DM@TC4 group, the genes involved in wound healing (Arg-1/Arg-2/Cxcl3) and bone remodeling (Mmp23/Fosl1) showed upregulated transcriptional levels in macrophages cultured on the RTS surface (Fig. 5a1-a3). The transcriptional upregulation of these genes indicated a beneficial role in the repair and remodeling of bone defects under diabetic conditions, primarily through the suppression of inflammation and the promotion of collagen remodeling.

RTS inhibits pre-osteoclastic differentiation of macrophages in diabetic state

As displayed in Fig. 6A, phalloidin staining results indicated that the macrophages cultured with DM@RTS/Y, DM@TC4 and DM@Gs tended to fuse into multinucleated cells, indicating a potential to differentiate into pre-osteoclasts. Reversely, no significant fusion tendency was observed for macrophages subjected to DM@RTS and DM@Sr, which demonstrates that pre-osteoclastic activation induced by DM condition was synergistically inhibited by strontium ions and nanofilm-titanium alloy gradient strength. To clarify the underlying mechanism, results listed in Fig. 6B, D indicated that the synthesis of osteoclast-like related key protein NFATC1 was inhibited on DM@RTS and DM@Sr, an effect likely co-contributed by Sr ions and nanofilm stiffness. Additionally, Fig. 6C and E indicated the reduced distribution of tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase in DM@RTS compared with others.

Macrophage-associated osteo-immunomodulatory effect of RTS

RTS regulates osteogenic differentiation by modulating the osteogenic/osteoclast-like differentiation potential of macrophages. The results shown in Fig. 7A, D indicated that the osteogenic ability of MC3T3-e1 was enhanced by supernatant collected from macrophages subjected with RTS. Considering that the morphology of osteogenic precursor cells plays an important role in the degree of osteogenic function, the cytoskeleton was visualized and the results are shown in Fig. 7A. Furthermore, differences observed among DM@RTS/Y, DM@RTS and DM@Gs confirmed that Piezo1 was involved in the synthesis function of macrophages (Fig. 4). Given the Runx-2/Sp7 presented the consistent tendency as that of ALP activity and mineralization capacity (Fig. 7B, C, E), we hypothesis that the osteogenic ability was regulated via Piezo1(macrophage)-Runx2/Sp7(MC3T3-e1) axis, synergistically activated by nanofilm stiffness and Sr ions released. Additionally, as displayed in Fig. S1A and D, ALP activity and mineralization capacity of MC3T3-e1cultured directly on RTS under diabetes state were enhanced. Consistent with this manifestation, the expression profiles of Runx2 and Sp7 validated the internal mechanisms driving this phenomenon (Figure. S1B, C and E).

RTS modulates in vivo macrophage polarization and osteoclast-like differentiation tendency under diabetic conditions

To clarify the in vivo regulatory effects of RTS on macrophages, HE staining, immunofluorescent staining for polarization markers and Trap staining were applied. As stated in Fig. 8A and B, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and more collagen fiber formation around RTS were observed, suggesting a superior early bone formation effect. Consistent with this trend, Piezo1 expression and Trap activity were suppressed around RTS (Fig. 8C and D), intimating the intrinsic regulatory effect of the surface on macrophage polarization and pre-osteoclastic activation.

RTS regulates the osteogenic capacity under diabetic condition

To intuitively evaluate the bone repair around RTS under diabetic conditions, immunofluorescent staining of Col-1 and OPN was conducted. As displayed in Fig. 9A–C, the expression of both Col-1 and OPN was enhanced adjacent to RTS implants. This is consistent of the in vitro results listed in Fig. 3, confirming that RTS promoted two critical processes of bone tissue formation: collagen formation and mineralization. In this study, the RTS surface exhibited a lower effective surface modulus, indicating a mechanically compliant interface that may be favorable for osteoimmune regulation. However, this parameter alone does not represent a full assessment of the coating's clinical mechanical durability, and additional studies on scratch resistance, adhesion stability, and loading endurance are needed in future work. It should be noted that RTS does not represent a purely stiffness-controlled surface. Combined with the reduced effective surface modulus, RTS also provides nanoscale topography, enhanced hydrophilicity and sustained Sr ion release. These physicochemical and mechanical factors may synergistically contribute to macrophage regulation and diabetic osseointegration. Therefore, this study emphasizes the involvement of stiffness-associated Piezo1 mechanotransduction rather than excluding the contribution of Sr ions or surface chemistry. Stiffness-matched Sr-free coatings are needed to further dissect the relative contribution of mechanical and biochemical cues in future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we further elucidated the internal mechanism by which RTS regulates macrophage polarization found in previous studies and applied this strategy to the treatment of diabetic bone defects. Compared with DM@RTS/Y and DM@TC4, DM@RTS regulates the Piezo1 pathway through the combined effects of Sr ion release and mechanically compliant SrTiO3 nanofilm on stiff titanium substrate. This synergistic action inhibits pro-inflammatory polarization and osteoclast-like differentiation tendency in macrophages simultaneously. Coupled with this immunomodulation and Sr2+ released, significantly enhanced osteogenesis was observed, facilitated by both the direct and indirect effects of the RTS surfaces.

Notablely, this study still has some limitations: Although GsMTx4 and Yoda1 have been widely used to modulate Piezo1 activity, pharmacological approaches cannot fully exclude potential off-target effects. Future studies using Piezo1 knockdown or macrophage-specific Piezo1-deficient models are required to further validate the specific involvement of Piezo1.
