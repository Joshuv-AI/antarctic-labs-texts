Human Exposure Levels of Volatile Organic Compounds in E-waste Recycling Area: Get Insight into Impacts of Manipulation Mode and Associations with Oxidative Stress Markers
Authors: Mengqi Yan, Zhipeng Cheng, Qiang Zou, Hongzhi Zhao, Luhan Yang, Hongkai Zhu, Tao Zhang, Hongwen Sun
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is currently a dearth of evidence regarding the impact of the manipulation mode on human occupational exposure to volatile organic compounds (VOCs) originating from e-waste dismantling. Therefore, 11 VOC metabolites (mVOCs) and four oxidative stress biomarkers (OSBs; i.e., 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine, 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-guanosine, 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine, and dityrosine) were analyzed in urine collected from two disassembly regions. Urinary ∑mVOC concentrations were significantly higher for e-waste-related participants than controls (geometric mean: 2500 vs 1360 ng/mL; p < 0.05). Notably, adults from formal dismantling site (3020 ng/mL) had equivalent ∑mVOC levels as those from informal area (2830 ng/mL) but with varying composition features, indicating that elevated exposure VOC levels still remained even e-waste is properly recycled. Majority of mVOCs were significantly correlated with OSB levels occurring in nucleotides and proteins, and weighted quantile sum regression analysis further manifested that urinary metabolites of 1,3-butadiene and N,N-dimethylformamide occupied the highest contributions to the positive responses of measured OSBs. The hazard quotients of 1,3-butadiene and benzene were greater than one for more than 80% of participants, implying the potential health risk associated with e-waste dismantling. Thus, this study provides crucial information on e-waste-related priority VOCs of various recycling practices for management to mitigate their health risks.Keywords: Volatile organic compounds, Oxidative stress biomarkers, Internal exposure, E-waste dismantling, Recycling mode

Introduction

Electronic waste (e-waste) generation is growing rapidly with an increase in consumer demand for electronic products. The global production of e-waste was approximately 54 million tons in 2019 and is expected to reach a record high of 75 million tons in 2030.1,2 A substantial amount of e-waste flows to developing countries, such as Ghana, India, and China, to recycle valuable waste resources,3,4 and 82.6% of e-waste was most likely not formally collected and recycled.1 Formal e-waste recycles valuable materials and manages hazardous materials in a pollution-controlled and environmentally sound manner, with waste being disposed of in a controlled manner.1 Since 1989, China has exerted considerable efforts to advocate better e-waste collection and recycling in both the public and private sectors.5 Even so, the contamination levels of functional additives, such as brominated flame retardants, in environmental matrices collected close to formal dismantling workshops were up to six times higher than those far from the workshops.6 These evidence suggest that informal and formal recycling merit our continued attention, as both can burden the local environment and population.7−9

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are the type of representative e-waste dismantling related contaminants. On the one hand, VOCs are widely used in the production of household appliances, automotive interior parts, and other electrical appliances; thus, VOCs are released during the recycling process as their high vapor pressure.10 For example, acrylonitrile and acrylamide are used to synthesize polymers, which may be discharged from waste.11 On the other hand, combustion and heating are common disassembly processes that may generate various VOCs.12 Polystyrene is a common raw material for producing printed circuit board assemblies; studies have demonstrated that benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, and xylene can be produced through the pyrolysis of polystyrene.13 Therefore, the centralized dismantling area of e-waste, whether informal or formal recycling, has been verified as a VOC pollution hotspot,14−16 but occupational exposure risks of VOCs to workers and nearby residents have received little attention.

VOCs can enter the human body through ingestion, inhalation and skin contact, and then be excreted as metabolites following a series of biochemical reactions.17 Health assessments through an external exposure approach can be challenging since of the difficulty in quantifying or covering all exposure routes. The noninvasive detection of metabolites in conventional diagnostic matrices, such as urine, could help address this issue.18 Compared with their parent compounds, VOC metabolites (mVOCs) have long physiological half-lives and are regarded as specific biomarkers used for assessing people’s VOC exposure in various environments.17,19,20 Nevertheless, only two studies have explored the urinary metabolic biomarkers of exposure to VOCs from e-waste disassembly sites; one in adolescents and one in adult workers.21,22 Under the impact of the manipulation mode, data on levels of multiple urinary mVOCs are limited.

Continuous exposure to VOCs is devastating, as these substances were classified as carcinogenic by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) with adverse effects on human health.23 The most common diseases potentially associated with VOCs mainly involve the respiratory, blood, and immune systems.24 Damage of nucleic acids is one of the primary mechanisms induced by VOC exposure in humans, leading to oxidative stress, genotoxicity, and inflammation.25,26 The excess radical oxygen species can react with guanine on nucleotides to generate some extracellular oxidized guanine derivatives, such as 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-guanosine (8-OHG), and 8-oxo-7,8-dihydroguanine (8-oxoGua), which are determinable in body fluids.27 Furthermore, dityrosine (diY) is produced when proteins are damaged by oxidative stress.28 These substances are called oxidative stress biomarkers (OSBs), which are claimed as indicators for predicting progressing diseases or human health status. Previous studies have demonstrated that some mVOCs are strongly correlated with 8-OHdG, one of the most widely used OSBs.20,29,30 Therefore, OSBs can be used to assess the health damage caused by VOCs from e-waste dismantling activities.

To bridge the gaps in our current understanding of VOCs, we therefore determined and compared the occurrence, residue levels, and composition profiles of 11 mVOCs and 4 oxidative lesions in urine samples collected from individuals in China’s informal and formal e-waste dismantling and control areas. The occurrences of urinary mVOCs were compared between people in the informal recycling area and those in formal recycling enterprises to determine the effect of the operation mode. The associations between mVOCs and OSBs were examined, and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression analysis was further performed to identify the individual contribution of each mVOC to the combined effect on OSBs. The results of the present preliminary study could promote our understanding of human exposure risks to VOCs in e-waste areas and provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of diseases associated with VOC exposure from an oxidative stress perspective.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

VOC-specific biomarkers for those parent compounds with high toxicity and detection rates were selected. Native standards included 11 mVOCs namely N-acetyl-S-(2-carboxyethyl)-l-cysteine (CEMA), N-acetyl-S-(2-carbamoylethyl)-l-cysteine (AAMA), N-acetyl-S-(3,4-dihydroxybutyl)-l-cysteine (DHBMA), N-acetyl-S-(3-hydroxypropyl-1-methyl)-l-cysteine (HPMMA), N-acetyl-S-(N-methylcarbamoyl)-l-cysteine (AMCC), N-acetyl-S-(3-carboxy-2-propyl)-l-cysteine (CMEMA), N-acetyl-S-(4-hydroxy)-2-methyl-2-trans-buten-1-yl)-l-cysteine (IPM3), N-acetyl-S-methyl-l-cysteine (MMA), 2-methylhippuric acid (2MHA), 3/4-methylhippuric acid (3 + 4MHA), and trans, trans-muconic acid (t,t-MA), as well as four OSBs, namely, 8-OHdG, 8-OHG, 8-oxoGua, and diY were purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Ontario, Canada), Medbio (Shanghai, China), J&K Scientific (Beijing, China), Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and Cayman chemical (Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA). The detailed information on the standards of target analytes, their isotope-labeled internal standards, relevant parent compounds, and main toxicological effects is listed in Tables S1–3 in the Supporting Information (SI).

High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade methanol, acetonitrile, acetic acid, and ammonium acetate were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) and CNW Technologies (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water was produced by a Water Purification System (Sartorius, Germany).

Sample Collection

Two representative e-waste dismantling sites were selected in the present study; one located in Qingyuan (denoted as informal e-waste dismantling site), Guangdong province, and another in Tianjin city (denoted as formal e-waste dismantling site). Qingyuan city, one of the most studied e-waste recycling regions in China, has a long history of dismantling activities and a large number of family run workshops, resulting in serious pollution of hazardous substances in surroundings, such as chlorinated paraffins, polybrominated diphenyl ethers, organophosphorus flame retardants, etc.31−33 The selected formal e-waste dismantling enterprise has 16 industrial parks across China and recycles more than one million tons of electronic waste with qualified operating facilities and pollution control means, which accounts for 10% of the total amount in China.

A total of 103 first morning urine voids were collected from participants (gender: 62 males, 41 females; age range: 0.4–87 years) who were living near the informal e-waste dismantling site where existing family based e-waste dismantling workshops. Another sample set included 38 first morning urine samples obtained from people (gender: 25 males, 13 females; age range: 25–53 years) who working in an e-waste dismantling industrial park with standardized management (i.e., formal e-waste dismantling site). These volunteers in the formal e-waste dismantling site were directly involved in the dismantling activities. For comparison, 34 first morning urine samples were collected as control from participants (gender: 18 males, 16 females; age range: 3–78 years) who lived approximately 80 km northwest of the informal dismantling area (hereafter referred to control site #1). Further, 40 first morning urine samples were collected from participants (gender: 21 males, 19 females; age range: 24–59 years) living in nonoccupational exposure area, which located about 50 km east of the informal dismantling area in Tianjin (hereafter referred to control site #2).

Current smoking status was self-reported and further confirmed by urinary cotinine measurements which enabled differentiation of the participants into subgroups of nonsmoker (<50 ng/mL) and smoker (>50 ng/mL).34 Demographic information including gender, age, and smoking status for the participants from e-waste and reference sites is summarized in Table S4. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Nankai University, China and signed informed consents were obtained from all participants or their statutory guardians. All samples were stored in 50 mL polypropylene tubes at −80 °C until further analysis. Specific gravity (SG) was measured using a digital refractometer (Atago PAL-10S; Atago, Tokyo, Japan).

Analytical Methods

A dilute-and-shoot assay was used to analyze urinary mVOCs, cotinine, and OSBs, which was described elsewhere with slight modification.19,30 Briefly, 50 μL of urine was diluted to 0.5 mL with 15 mM ammonium acetate solution containing 5 ng (for OSBs) or 25 ng (for mVOCs and cotinine) of each surrogate standards. The mixture was transferred into a 2 mL HPLC vial for instrumental analysis after spinning through a nylon filter.

Identification and quantification of mVOCs, cotinine, and OSBs were performed by an electrospray ionization triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (MS/MS; SCIEX 6500+, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) coupled to a liquid chromatography system (ExionLC, SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). The HPLC-MS/MS instrument is equipped with multiple reaction monitoring, which determines mVOCs in negative ionization mode, cotinine and OSBs in positive ionization mode simultaneously (Tables S5–6). An Ultra AQ C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 3.0 μm, Restek Corporation, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was used to separate the analytes. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% acetic acid in water (A) and 0.1% acetic acid in methanol: acetonitrile (70:30, v/v; B) (Table S7). Typical HPLC-MS/MS chromatograms for mVOCs and OSBs in solvent solution and sample are displayed in Figures S1–4.

Quality Assurance and Quality Control

A procedural blank (HPLC-grade water used in place of urine) and matrix-spiked sample were obtained in each analytical batch. Matrix spiking with 2.5 ng, 10 ng, and 25 ng, to represent low, medium, and high concentrations, for each of the target were used to determine the fortified recoveries as following: the recoveries for mVOCs, OSBs, and cotinine under different fortified levels were 73 ± 12–126 ± 5.7%, 71 ± 6.6–102 ± 14%, and 82 ± 5.1–96 ± 4.5%, respectively (Table S8), except 8-oxoGua (65 ± 2.1–76 ± 3.5%) had relative low recoveries. Matrix effect for the optimized analytical method was evaluated by the absolute recovery of internal standards, which was expressed as the peak area of internal standard in urine sample compared to that in solvent working solution. The matrix effects ranged from 65 ± 3.7–90 ± 8.5% for all analytes, falling within a satisfactory range (Table S9). The method limits of quantification (MLOQs) were estimated based on the lowest concentrations of the calibration standard with a signal-to-noise ratio of ≥10 and a dilution factor of 10, and the values were 1.0–20, 0.5–1.0, and 0.6 ng/mL for mVOCs, OSBs, and cotinine, respectively (Table S5). No target analyte was found in any procedural blanks at concentrations above the MLOQs.

Log10-transformed urinary concentrations were used for analyzing the associations between mVOCs and OSBs. The value of p ≤ 0.05 was set for statistical significance. Statistical analyses were done using SPSS 20.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), origin 2021b (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA), and GraphPad Prism 9.1.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The WQS regression analysis was carried out with the R version 4.1.0 using the package “gWQS” (R Development Core Team).

Residual Profiles of mVOCS in Urine

Target mVOCs were frequently detected in urine samples, regardless of the examined population. Seven of 11 selected mVOCs, including 3 + 4MHA, 2MHA, AAMA, CEMA, HPMMA, DHBMA, and CMEMA, were detected in greater than 80% of urine samples. IPM3 detection rates were significantly higher in the disassembly areas (informal site: 68.9%, formal site: 86.8%) than in the control areas (control site #1:44.1%, control site #2:57.5%). Notably, the geometric mean (GM) urinary concentration of ΣmVOCs (sum of 11 mVOCs) was approximately twice as high among people in the e-waste dismantling sites (2500 ng/mL) compared with those in the reference areas (1360 ng/mL), indicating that people residing in the e-waste areas are at a higher risk of exposure to VOCs compared with those living in control sites.

Among the analyzed mVOCs, more than half of the total mVOCs in both sites were metabolites of crotonaldehyde and acrolein such as CMEMA, HPMMA, and CEMA (Table 1). Crotonaldehyde is used in the production of sorbic acid, pyridines, dyes, pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and rubber antioxidants.42,43 Acrolein is a chemical with a high yield, and China is one of the major producers. It is primarily utilized in the production of dl-methionine feed additives for livestock and pesticides.42 These two VOCs can be produced during incomplete combustion from cigarettes, polymers, and wood.44 Indoor cooking and combustion of diesel gasoline are also major sources of crotonaldehyde and acrolein.42 It suggests that humans are exposed to crotonaldehyde by inhaling it in the air or ingesting it through food. Furthermore, endogenously metabolism, such as lipid oxidation, in the human body can also produce crotonaldehyde and acrolein.45

Concentrations of individual mVOCs were higher in residents exposed to informal e-waste than those residing in control site #1. The same results were observed in the formal dismantling of e-waste area, except for AMCC and AAMA (Table 1). Kuang also found that 10 mVOCs in children from informal dismantling e-waste were approximately 11.6% to 57.3% higher than those in control children.21 These findings are consistent with previous monitoring studies that reported VOC contamination in the air phase around the e-waste site and reiterate that residents of areas containing e-waste appear highly susceptible to VOC exposure through breathing.14,15 Thermal degradation of waste printed circuit boards, a complex constituent of electrical and electronic equipment, has been observed to increase VOC emissions (e.g., benzene and xylene).46 Polymers account for approximately 15–30% of electrical and electronic equipment, so we could not neglect certain VOC metabolites related to plastic composites, such as 2MHA, 3 + 4MHA, CEMA, AMCC, and DHBMA (the metabolites of xylene, acrolein, N,N-dimethylformamide, and 1,3-butadiene).47−50 Plastic recycling has been identified as a significant source of VOCs, with most VOC air phase levels exceeding 10 μg/m3 nearby plastic recycling plants.49 Therefore, greater emphasis should be placed on the plastic recycling process and controlling air VOC pollution in e-waste recycling.

The urinary concentrations of mVOCs were compared with those reported previously (Table S13). In most previous studies, crotonaldehyde metabolites—CMEMA and HPMMA—were among the most abundant substances in the urine samples.29,30,51−53 The t,t-MA, HPMMA, and DHBMA levels of the adults in the two control sites were lower than those reported in the rural adults of Wuhan, but people in the control sites exhibited high concentrations of 3 + 4MHA, 2MHA, AMCC, and CEMA in the present study.30 These findings imply a difference in VOC exposure patterns among the cities. Notably, the GM concentration of DHBMA in the two e-waste dismantling areas (informal e-waste site: 208 ng/mL, formal e-waste site: 229 ng/mL) was higher than that reported in Wuhan (165 ng/mL).30 Except for t,t-MA and HPMMA, other mVOCs in children exposed to informal e-waste dismantling were higher than those in Wuhan and Shenzhen.29 Urinary CEMA levels of people in the USA (126 ng/mL) were similar to those found in control site (∼120 ng/mL) but lower than those in the e-waste areas (∼180 ng/mL).19

Urinary mVOC Levels Under Different Operating Modes

Urinary mVOC levels were then emphatically discussed between adults from informal (i.e., family workshop) and formal (i.e., industrial park) e-waste disassembly sites. The GM urinary concentration of ∑mVOCs in people in the formal site was 3020 ng/mL, almost identical with those in the informal site (2830 ng/mL) (Table S14). Moreover, two e-waste sites have their respective composition features. For instance, the AMCC concentration in participants living in the informal site (accounting for 14.6%) was significantly (p < 0.05) higher than those in the formal site (accounting for 3.9%) (Figure 1A). By contrast, CMEMA exhibited the opposite trend and was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the formal site (accounting for 47.5%) than CMEMA in the informal site (accounting for 26.7%) (Figure 1A).

In plot A, informal e-waste dismantling site mainly contained family based dismantling workshops, and formal e-waste recycling site was located in industrial park; in plot B, control site #1 represents the reference area for informal e-waste dismantling area, and control site #2 represents the reference area for formal e-waste recycling area.

Individual mVOC levels of people in the informal dismantling site were 1.38 to 4.40-fold higher than those in control site #1 (group 1), and the gap narrowed to 1.44 to 2.69-fold for participants between the formal dismantling site and control site #2 (group 2). Generally, urinary AMCC, AAMA, and DHBMA—metabolites of N,N-dimethylformamide, acrylamide, and 1,3-butadiene, respectively—varied significantly among group 1 subjects, whereas urinary t,t-MA and CMEMA—metabolites of benzene and crotonaldehyde, respectively—varied significantly among group 2 participants (p < 0.01 and fold >2). Smoking is a crucial effect variable that may influence human exposure pattern to VOCs.53,54 However, our earlier conclusion that the patterns of VOC exposure varied between informal and formal dismantling sites remained unchanged when we excluded those smokers from the comparison (Table S15).

These pattern differences might not be attributed to the backgrounds of cities because of the similar composition pattern observed between the two control sites (Figure 1B). Volunteers at the formal dismantling factory originated primarily from machinery or hand-broken workshops. The operation mode of the informal dismantling plant consisted of manual dismantling and heating with an electric furnace. Nitrogen-containing compounds are mainly produced by heating;12 therefore, high concentration levels of AMCC and AAMA have been detected in the urine samples of people from the informal dismantling site. Whereas aromatic and aliphatic hydrocarbons are the main VOC types generated from mechanical breakage. The dismantling processes, such as crushing and heating, can contribute to VOCs release. It is worth noting that some common plastics, such as poly(vinyl chloride), poly(ethylene terephthalate) polyethylene, etc., can produce high concentrations of 1,3-butadiene during incineration.55 People could intake VOCs through inhalation and skin permeation because clothing like cotton-made does not reduce skin contact effectively.42 Furthermore, it is inevitable to ingest contaminants in high-exposure environments, even when wearing adequate protective gear such as rubber gloves.43 Moreover, once contaminated, personal protective equipment is no longer effective, and its protective ability is decreased.44 These workers are poorly educated and receive few protection trainings, which might increase exposure to pollutants.

Although no studies of VOC air contamination between formal and informal e-waste recycling facilities have been conducted, other pollutant concentrations in informal dust, such as metal, were statistically comparable to those in formal dust, with differences in specific contaminants.56 Therefore, more rigorous measures may be adopted to protect workers against pollutants such as VOCs in formal e-waste recycling industrial parks. To the best of our knowledge, this present study is the first to report the urinary mVOC levels in e-waste dismantling sites and to distinguish the effect of the operation mode.

Age- and Gender-Related Differences in Urinary mVOCs

The age-related differences were discussed only in the family workshop area because of the wide age range of the volunteers. The correlation between age and the urinary concentrations of 3 + 4MHA (r = 0.210, p < 0.05), AMCC (r = 0.267, p < 0.01), IPM3 (r = 0.315, p < 0.01), and CMEMA (r = 0.249, p < 0.01) was weak but significant. These samples were further divided into four age groups (0–17, 18–39, 40–59, and ≥60 years old) (Figure S5). The volunteers aged 18 to 59 comprised the main participants in the dismantlement activity. As expected, these groups exhibited the highest urinary concentrations of mVOCs. Other pollutant metabolites in the urine of residents living in e-waste disposal regions, such as organophosphate flame retardants and phthalate esters, decreased with age.57,58 The difference could be attributed to the primary form of pollutants. The VOCs are predominantly gaseous at room temperature and migrate more easily in well-ventilated circumstances. Adults who recycle e-waste are exposed to VOCs for longer durations than children. The main exposure routes for VOCs are inhalation and dermal absorption.59,60 Because of their larger body size, adults require more alveolar ventilation and inhale more VOCs than children.61 By contrast, organophosphate flame retardants and phthalate esters are more likely to leave residues on furniture surfaces; children are exposed to significant amounts of pollutants through frequent hand-to-mouth contact. In elder people, metabolic depression reduces the body’s ability to eliminate VOCs.62 Furthermore, the elderly frequently use medications that may affect VOC metabolism.25

Gender-related patterns of urinary mVOC concentrations are also discussed. However, no significant gender-related differences were observed between formal and informal e-waste sites, except that male exhibited significantly higher concentrations of t,t-MA than female in both e-waste dismantling areas (p < 0.05).

Associations between VOCs Exposure and Oxidative Stress

All four analyzed OSBs (i.e., 8-OHdG, 8-OHG, 8-oxoGua, and diY) were identified in >70% of the samples from both e-waste and control areas (Table S16). The concentrations of 8-OHdG and 8-OHG in the two e-waste dismantling areas were significantly higher than those in the corresponding control area (p < 0.05), indicating that DNA and RNA are more likely to be damaged by oxidative stress in participants who reside or work in e-waste areas. Significantly high concentrations of diY in the informal e-waste dismantling areas (p < 0.05) indicate that protein oxidative damage is also present in residents near informal e-waste sites.

According to multiple linear regression analysis, the majority of mVOCs was significantly and positively correlated with 8-OHdG and diY in group 1 (Figures 2 and S6). With regard to 8-OHG, significant positive correlations were found with 9 of 11 target mVOCs in the urine samples in the informal e-waste dismantling area, whereas such associations were obtained only with 4 of 11 target mVOCs in control site #1. These results indicated that the oxidative stress including DNA, protein, and RNA damages, especially the latter was associated with human VOCs exposure that related to informal e-waste dismantling activities. In formal e-waste dismantling area, furthermore, the majority of mVOCs were significantly positively correlated with 8-OHG (for 7 of 11 mVOCs) and diY (for 8 of 11 mVOCs); however, only DHBMA was positively correlated to 8-OHdG and diY in its control site. Our results indicated that RNA and protein oxidative were also associated with elevated levels of human exposure to VOCs released during formal e-waste recycling activities under standardized management. We may conclude that VOCs produced during disassembly activities can lead to oxidative stress, and these damages cannot be completely eliminated even via formal recycling mode.

This finding means that benzene generated by e-waste recycling should not be ignored. In a human myeloid cell line (HL-60) culture, benzene was found to increase ROS levels.65 Rats treated orally with 400 and 800 mg/kg/day benzene for 4 weeks exhibited high concentrations of ROS and 8-OHdG in the islets.66 Other pollutants related to e-waste dismantling, such as organophosphate, heavy metals, and bisphenols, have been confirmed to be associated with oxidative stress damage.21,58,67 Future studies may consider multiple pollutants in the assessment of mixed effects.

Multiple linear models have revealed that a few mVOCs in the urine collected from e-waste sites have demonstrated a significant association with 8-oxoGua, whereas WQS failed to investigate the association between mVOCs and OSBs in the family workshop (p > 0.05). Other studies have found no correlation between t,t-MA and 8-oxoGua in children.68 The radical oxygen species in excess can react with guanine on nucleotides to generate 8-oxoGua. Free 8-oxoGua can be produced through the excision repair pathway of oxidative DNA and RNA damage.69,70 VOCs caused oxidative damage to DNA and RNA, and damaged enzymes were associated with the repair process.71 This finding may account for the lack of a significant correlation between mVOCs and 8-oxoGua in the e-waste site. In this case, 8-oxoGua may not be a suitable biomarker for evaluating the association between mVOCs and human oxidative damage.

Estimated Daily Intakes and Risk Assessment

Table S17 displays the results of the EDIs for the parent VOCs in the four target areas. The EDIs of benzene, xylene, N,N-dimethyl formamide, and 1,3-butadiene were predicted to be >1.0 μg/kg-bw/day of participants in all sites. The VOC intake of people living near e-waste dismantling sites was 2-fold higher than that of those in unpolluted areas. Exposure models predicted that people aged 18 to 59 had the highest VOC intake levels. These findings demonstrate that e-waste recycling activities have a solid potential to generate VOCs, thereby contributing to VOC exposure risk among local people.

The health risks of VOC exposure were quantified using hazard quotients (HQs, as shown in Figure 4), which are calculated as the ratio of exposure doses to their corresponding reference doses (RfDs, shown in Table S17). Approximately 90% of participants in control regions while 95% of those in e-waste dismantling sites exhibited HQ values for 1,3-butadiene greater than 1.0. Similarly, Qian et al. reported that 97% of the general population revealed high HQs (surpassing one) of 1,3-butadiene,30 indicating that people may be experiencing potential detrimental consequences because of their exposure to 1,3-butadiene during their daily routines and such risks may be further amplified in the context of e-waste dismantling activities. Our findings suggest that benzene is another VOC of concern as more than 80% of volunteers had HQ above 1.0 in e-waste areas. According to the IARC, benzene is carcinogenic to humans even though its usage has decreased;37 this VOC is still used in the production of certain electronic products, including glue and dye.72 Benzene can also be produced through the pyrolysis of polystyrene.13 It should be noted that this result should be extrapolated with caution because t,t-MA as biomarkers of ambient exposure could be difficult to attribute only to exposure to benzene as t,t-MA is also a metabolite of sorbic acid, which was not controlled in the present study.73 Nevertheless, benzene is a kind of VOC commonly found in e-waste disassembly regions, and the health risk caused by benzene should be acknowledged in these areas, regardless of operation mode. The HQ value of other VOCs being less than 1 does not necessarily indicate a low health risk, as the combined effects with other pollutants may exacerbate harm to the human body.

Implications and Conclusions

Currently, >1000 hazardous substances such as emerging contaminants and heavy metals have been identified as the primary pollutants generated during e-waste dismantling processes.74 As the largest consumer of electrical products and importer of waste electrical and electronic equipment worldwide, China plays a key role in the global life cycle of electronics and naturally encounters severe environmental pollution issues. In recent years, Chinese government has made great efforts to implement strict legislation related to e-waste management and the exposure levels of toxic pollutants such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon in populations of the e-waste region significantly decline.75 Nevertheless, our results indicate that the current standardized e-waste dismantling actions do not protect workers, as expected against VOC-induced health effects such as oxidative stress. It has been reported that the removal efficiencies of different VOCs varied greatly and may depend on the physicochemical properties of VOCs.76 In this study, the urinary levels of VOC metabolites derived from exposure to acrylamide, 1,3-butadiene, and N,N-dimethylformamide were higher in participants of the informal e-waste disassembly region, while those of crotonaldehyde and benzene were increased for participants working in the formal workshops. That may be why we observed equivalent ∑mVOC levels between these two groups of people. Furthermore, it is imperative for dismantling plants to establish stringent protective measures and enhance workers’ awareness regarding health preservation. A diverse range of VOC protective personal equipment is readily available in the current market. These include but are not limited to gloves, protective garments, gas masks equipped with activated carbon adsorption, and filter boxes. The optimal choice of personal protective equipment is contingent upon the specific nature and extent of VOCs exposure, as well as the presence of other contaminants within the dismantling setting. Beyond the regular utilization of personal protective equipment, careful consideration should be given to its efficacy, prompt replacement, and adherence to scientific protocols for proper usage. Moreover, as an integral component of e-waste, the recycling process of plastics necessitates the implementation of effective measures to control VOCs pollution.

Furthermore, we reported statistically significant increases in the levels of oxidative stress biomarkers associated with urinary VOC metabolites. These associations with occupational exposure to VOCs may help interpret adverse health end points that are mediated by oxidative stress mechanisms. We calculated the daily intakes of six VOCs based on biomonitoring data and then performed a risk assessment for exposure to a single VOC. Our findings indicate that 1,3-butadiene and benzene are hazardous VOCs, as more than 80% of volunteers in the e-waste areas exhibited HQ values above 1.0. Taken together, human biomonitoring in occupational exposure to VOCs should be taken seriously, even in formal e-waste dismantling workshops.
