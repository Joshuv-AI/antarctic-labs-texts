Global Weak Solutions to a Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard System with Chemotaxis and Mass Transport: Cross Diffusion versus Logistic Degradation
Andrea Giorgini, Jingning He, Hao Wu

Abstract
We analyze a diffuse interface model that describes the dynamics of incompressible two-phase flows influenced by interactions with a soluble chemical substance, encompassing the chemotaxis effect, mass transport, and reactions. In the resulting coupled evolutionary system, the macroscopic fluid velocity field 𝒗 \bm{v} satisfies a Navier–Stokes system driven by a capillary force, the phase field variable φ \varphi is governed by a convective Cahn–Hilliard equation incorporating a mass source and a singular potential (e.g., the Flory–Huggins type), and the chemical concentration σ \sigma obeys an advection-reaction-diffusion equation with logistic degradation, exhibiting a cross-diffusion structure akin to the Keller–Segel model for chemotaxis. Under general structural assumptions, we establish the existence of global weak solutions to the initial boundary value problem within a bounded smooth domain Ω ⊂ ℝ d \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} , d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 . The proof hinges on a novel semi-Galerkin scheme for a suitably regularized system, featuring a non-standard approximation of the singular potential. Moreover, with more restrictive assumptions on coefficients and data, we establish regularity properties and uniqueness of global weak solutions in the two-dimensional case. Our analysis contributes to a further understanding of phase separation processes under the interplay of fluid dynamics and chemotaxis, in particular, the influence of cross diffusion and logistic degradation. Keywords: Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system, global weak solution, chemotaxis, mass transport, singular potential, cross diffusion, logistic degradation. MSC 2010: 35A01, 35A02, 35K35, 35Q92, 76D05.

Introduction

The diffuse interface method has attracted significant attention as an efficient and versatile methodology for moving interface problems arising from Materials Science
[
9
]
, fluid dynamics
[
3
,
33
]
, and mathematical biology, e.g., the tumor growth process
[
20
,
38
]
. Within this framework, the classical hypersurface description of free interfaces in the so-called sharp interface models is replaced by a thin layer that permits microscopic mixing of macroscopically distinct components. The diffuse interface approach avoids explicit tracking of free interfaces in both mathematical formulations and numerical computations, and enables handling large deformations and topological changes of the interfaces in a natural way
[
3
,
14
]
.

In this work, we consider the following Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system for viscous incompressible two-phase flows incorporating the effects of chemotaxis, mass transport and reaction:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{v}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}-\mathrm{div}\big(2\eta(\varphi)D\bm{v}\big)+\nabla p=(\mu+\chi\sigma)\nabla\varphi,

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,\bm{v}=0,

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),\

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\mathrm{div}\big(m(\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)+S(\varphi,\sigma),

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta\varphi+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Psi^{\prime}(\varphi)-\chi\sigma,

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\sigma-\mathrm{div}\big[n(\varphi)\sigma\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))\big]=R(\varphi,\sigma),

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,T),

(1.1e)

subject to the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\mathbf{0},\quad\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi=m(\varphi)\nabla\mu\cdot\bm{n}=\big[n(\varphi)\sigma\nabla(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))\big]\cdot\bm{n}=0,\quad\textrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(1.2)

as well as the initial conditions

\displaystyle\bm{v}|_{t=0}=\bm{v}_{0},\quad\varphi|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0},\quad\sigma|_{t=0}=\sigma_{0},\qquad

in

\displaystyle\ \Omega.

(1.3)

Here, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with spatial dimension
d=2,3
) is a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
, and
T>0
is a given final time of arbitrary magnitude. The notation
\partial_{\bm{n}}f
denotes the normal derivative of a function
f
on the boundary
with outer unit normal
\bm{n}
. The scalar function
\varphi:\Omega\times(0,T)\to[-1,1]
is the so-called order parameter (or phase field) that describes the difference in volume fractions of the binary mixture. The level sets
\{\varphi=1\}
and
\{\varphi=-1\}
represent regions occupied by the pure phases of fluid 1 and fluid 2, respectively, while the free interface corresponds to a narrow transition layer of thickness scaling as
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, in which
\{-1<\varphi<1\}
. The fluid velocity
\bm{v}:\Omega\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is taken as the volume-averaged velocity with
D\bm{v}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\bm{v}+(\nabla\bm{v})^{\mathrm{T}})
being its symmetrized gradient, and
p:\Omega\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
is the (modified) pressure. The unknown variable
\sigma:\Omega\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the concentration of an unspecific chemical substance and
\mu:\Omega\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
stands for the chemical potential of the phase separation process.
In the coupled system (
1.1
),
equations (
1.1a
) and (
1.1b
) represent the momentum balance for the viscous incompressible two-phase flow, equations (
1.1c
) and (
1.1d
) constitute a convective Cahn–Hilliard system for the order parameter
\varphi
, and equation (
1.1e
) yields an advection-diffusion-reaction equation for the chemical concentration
\sigma
.

Throughout this paper, we assume that the density of the binary fluid mixture as well as densities of the individual constituents is constant (all set to one), while the fluid viscosities are allowed to be unmatched. Let
\eta_{1}
,
\eta_{2}>0
be viscosities of the two homogeneous components, respectively. The averaged viscosity is modeled by a concentration dependent function
\eta=\eta(\varphi)
, for instance,

\eta(r)=\eta_{1}\frac{1+r}{2}+\eta_{2}\frac{1-r}{2},\quad\forall\,r\in[-1,1].

(1.4)

In (
1.1d
), the nonlinear function
\Psi^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of a potential
\Psi
that has a double-well structure, with two minima and a local unstable maximum in between. The physically significant example is the logarithmic type (also called the Flory–Huggins potential):

\Psi(r)=\frac{\theta}{2}[(1-r)\ln(1-r)+(1+r)\ln(1+r)]+\frac{\theta_{c}}{2}(1-r^{2}),\quad\forall\,r\in[-1,1],

(1.5)

with
0<\theta<\theta_{c}
(see, e.g.,
[
9
,
34
]
). It is referred to as a singular potential since its derivative
\Psi^{\prime}
blows up at the pure phases
\pm 1
. In the literature, the singular potential
\Psi
is often approximated by a fourth-order polynomial

\Psi(r)=\frac{1}{4}(1-r^{2})^{2},\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

(1.6)

or some more general polynomial functions
[
34
]
. The functions
m(\varphi)
and
n(\varphi)
in (
1.1c
) and (
1.1e
) are nonnegative mobility functions related to the phase field and the chemical concentration, respectively.

The coupled system (
1.1
) is thermodynamical consistent due to its variational structure, that is, solutions to (
1.1
) (under the boundary conditions (
1.2
)) (formally) satisfy the basic energy law:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}|^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Psi(\varphi)+\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+\chi\sigma(1-\varphi)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}\Big(2\eta(\varphi)|D\bm{v}|^{2}+m(\varphi)|\nabla\mu|^{2}+n(\varphi)|\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega}\big[S(\varphi,\sigma)\mu+R(\varphi,\sigma)(\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))\big]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1.7)

As seen in (
1.7
), the total energy density is the sum of the kinetic energy density
\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}|^{2}
for the macroscopic fluid, the Ginzburg–Landau energy density
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Psi(\varphi)
for mixing of the binary mixture, and the chemical free energy density given by

N(\varphi,\sigma)=\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)+\chi\sigma(1-\varphi).

(1.8)

In the context of tumor growth modeling (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
19
]
), the constant coefficient
\chi
is related to certain key transport mechanisms, such as chemotaxis and active transport (when
\chi>0
). The source term
S(\varphi,\sigma)
in (
1.1c
) corresponds to biological mechanisms like proliferation, apoptosis of cells (see
[
34
]
for further discussions on biologically relevant mass source terms). Besides, the function
R(\varphi,\sigma)
in (
1.1e
) models certain reaction for the chemical substance. The presence of mass source/reaction terms not only affects the mass dynamics for the mixture and the chemical substance, but also contributes to changes in energy, such that the total energy of (
1.1
) may increase with over time.

Our system (
1.1
) contains the well-known Navier–Stokes-Cahn–Hilliard system as a subsystem
(i.e., the “Model H”
[
27
,
25
]
), which has been extensively analyzed in the literature, see
[
1
,
8
,
17
,
23
,
35
,
52
]
and the references cited therein.
In the Navier–Stokes part (
1.1a
)–(
1.1b
), the coupling structure is reflected in terms of the capillary force
(\mu+\chi\sigma)\nabla\varphi
(only depending on
\varphi
in view of (
1.1d
)) and the viscous stress tensor with a concentration dependent viscosity
\eta(\varphi)
. Besides, the macroscopic velocity
\bm{v}
influences the dynamics of
\varphi
and
\sigma
via
the advection terms
\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi
and
\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\sigma
. Concerning the interaction between the phase field
\varphi
and the chemical concentration
\sigma
, we reformulate (
1.1c
) and (
1.1e
) as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}_{\varphi}=S(\varphi,\sigma),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\sigma+\mathrm{div}\,\bm{q}_{\sigma}=R(\varphi,\sigma),

with mass fluxes

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{\varphi}:=-m(\varphi)\nabla\mu=-m(\varphi)\nabla(-\varepsilon\Delta\varphi+\varepsilon^{-1}\Psi^{\prime}(\varphi)+N_{\varphi}),\quad\bm{q}_{\sigma}:=-n(\varphi)\sigma\nabla N_{\sigma},

where
N_{\varphi}=-\chi\sigma
and
N_{\sigma}=\ln\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi)
denote the variational derivatives of
N(\varphi,\sigma)
with respect to
\varphi
and
\sigma
, respectively.
The term
\chi\nabla\sigma
in
\bm{q}_{\varphi}
represents the chemotactic response of the mixture to the chemical substance, while the other term
\chi\sigma\nabla(1-\varphi)
in
\bm{q}_{\sigma}
propels the chemical
substance via the concentration gradient of the mixture, which differs from the conventional diffusion mechanism.

In comparison with the general thermodynamically consistent diffuse interface model derived in
[
30
]
for a mixture of two viscous incompressible fluids interacting with a chemical substance (see also
[
42
]
in the context of tumor growth modeling), a distinct feature of equation (
1.1e
) is the nonlinear cross-diffusion term
\chi\mathrm{div}(n(\varphi)\sigma\nabla\varphi)
. In
[
30
]
and related works for tumor growth, this is usually replaced by a simpler one with linear mass transport (see e.g.,
[
15
,
19
,
20
]
):

\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\chi\Delta\varphi=R(\varphi,\sigma),

(1.9)

where for simplicity, we have taken a constant mobility coefficient
n(\varphi)=1
and neglected the advection term
\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\sigma
. Since
\sigma
plays the role of a concentration, its nonnegativity is an expected property. However, the term
\chi\Delta\varphi
in (
1.9
) has no definite sign, preventing the application of the minimum principle to guarantee that
\sigma
will stay non-negative during its evolution. This unsatisfactory issue was avoided in
[
16
]
by considering a quasi-steady-state equation
\Delta\sigma-\sigma h(\varphi)=0
for
\sigma
. On the other hand, inspired by the Keller–Segel system for chemotaxis (see e.g.,
[
5
,
28
]
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}u=\mathrm{div}(\gamma(v)\nabla u-u\chi(v)\nabla v),\\
\tau\partial_{t}v=\Delta v+u-v,\end{array}\right.

(1.10)

the authors of
[
39
]
proposed an alternative of (
1.9
) such that

\partial_{t}\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\chi\mathrm{div}(\sigma\nabla\varphi)=R(\varphi,\sigma).

(1.11)

With a properly chosen reaction term
R(\varphi,\sigma)
, they were able to show that
\sigma
satisfies the required non-negativity preserving property, provided that its initial value
\sigma_{0}
is non-negative. See also
[
2
]
for a similar consideration in a multi-species tumor growth model with chemotaxis and angiogenesis.
From a modeling perspective, equation (
1.11
) is more natural for the mass transfer process of tumor growth, since the mass flux
\chi\sigma\nabla\varphi
depends on both the chemical substance concentration
\sigma
and the gradient of mixture distribution
\nabla\varphi
. On the other hand, a new mathematical structure emerges in (
1.1
) with the choice of (
1.11
): the equation (
1.1e
) for the chemical concentration
\sigma
is similar to the equation for the cell density
u
in the Keller–Segel system, while the fourth-order Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.1c
)–(
1.1d
) for the order parameter
\varphi
replaces the linear, second-order reaction-diffusion equation for the chemical signal concentration
v
in (
1.10
). This requires a rather different treatment for
\varphi
comparing with that for
v
, which introduces challenges in the mathematical analysis.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no analytic result concerning the initial boundary value problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) in the literature. A fluid-free version has been analyzed in
[
39
]
, that is, the Navier–Stokes equations for the fluid velocity
\bm{v}
were neglected.
Assuming a logistic type reaction term like
R(\varphi,\sigma)=\beta(\varphi)\big(\kappa_{0}\sigma-\kappa_{\infty}\sigma^{p}\big)
for some
p\in(1,2]
, the authors proved the existence of global weak solutions in two and three dimensional cases when
\chi>0
. Further regularity properties were established under more restrictive assumptions on structural coefficients and data. The proof therein is mainly based on
a priori
estimates and compactness methods. At the level of weak solutions, one crucial issue is about the coercivity of the energy functional (more precisely, (
1.8
)), which can be handled with the uniform boundedness of
\varphi
, hence on the choice of a singular configuration potential like (
1.5
). The authors of
[
39
]
outlined a possible regularization scheme in which both the singular potential
\Psi
and the chemical concentration
\sigma
are properly truncated. They were able to control the crossing term
\chi\sigma(1-\varphi)
in (
1.8
) and obtained uniform estimates for approximate solutions, which allowed them to pass to the limit. In particular, the logistic growth with respect to
\sigma
helped the derivation of adequate
a priori
estimates. We also mention the recent work
[
40
]
on tumor growth processes influenced by chemotaxis and mass transport, in which a Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system with a singular potential was analyzed. Using a different approximating scheme, the author demonstrated the existence of global weak solutions in two and three dimensions, provided that the chemotactic sensitivity function exhibits a
nonlinear
dependence on the chemical concentration like
\frac{\sigma}{1+\sigma^{q-1}}
for some
q\in(1,2]
(the so-called “degenerate sensitivity” characterized by a slower growth for large values of
\sigma
), but without incorporating the logistic degradation.

Our aim is to establish the existence of global weak solutions to the initial boundary value problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) in both two and three dimensions, under the choice of a singular potential like (
1.5
) and a reaction term
R(\varphi,\sigma)
involving logistic degradation. As shown in Theorem
2.1
, problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) admits a global weak solution
(\bm{v},\varphi,\sigma)
on
[0,T]
, provided that the initial total energy is finite, that is,
\bm{v}_{0}\in\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
with
\mathrm{div}\,\bm{v}_{0}=0
,
\varphi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with
\|\varphi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1
,
|\overline{\varphi}_{0}|<1
, and
\sigma_{0}\ln\sigma_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
with
\sigma_{0}\geq 0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.
When the spatial dimension is two, further conclusions can be achieved: the global weak solution satisfies better regularity properties under a more regular initial datum
\sigma_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
(see Theorem
2.1
); moreover, assuming constant mobility functions and a constant mass source, the aforementioned global weak solution is unique (see Theorem
2.2
).

Let us highlight the main novelties of our study.
Firstly, we have proposed a new approximation of the singular potential
\Psi
. The existence of global weak solutions to problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) is primarily based on the energy balance (
1.7
). To handle the Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.1c
)–(
1.1d
) with a singular potential like (
1.5
), a conventional strategy is to smooth out the singular term
\Psi(\varphi)
and subsequently apply some local existence theorem, for instance, an appropriate Faedo–Galerkin scheme. This can be accomplished through a polynomial-type regularization (see e.g.,
[
35
,
22
]
), or a Yosida approximation (see e.g.,
[
7
]
). Nonetheless, the uniform boundedness of the approximate solution for
\varphi
cannot be guaranteed, which is essential to manage the crossing term
\chi\sigma(1-\varphi)
. Furthermore, neither the growth of the polynomial regularization nor the Yosida approximation is sufficient to handle the product
\sigma\varphi
in conjunction with the mixing entropy
\sigma\ln\sigma
. In light of the generalized Young’s inequality (see Lemma
2.1
), we find that the aforementioned crossing term can be controlled by the mixing entropy for
\sigma
combined with a proper exponential function of
\varphi
(depending on the strength of chemotactic effect characterized by
|\chi|
). This insight motives us to introduce a new approximate potential
\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi)
that is regular on
\mathbb{R}
and exhibits exponential growth for large values of
\varphi
.
This enables us to maintain the uniform coercivity of the free energy at the level of approximate solutions, without involving any truncation for
\sigma
as was done in
[
39
]
. We note that the degenerate sensitivity considered in
[
40
]
leads to an additional contribution
\frac{1}{q(q-1)}\sigma^{q}
in the chemical free energy density
N(\varphi,\sigma)
. This gives a stronger coercivity on
\sigma
so that a sufficiently higher order polynomial-type regularization of
\Psi(\varphi)
can be adopted.

Secondly, we have devised a new semi-Galerkin approximation scheme that incorporates a
p
-Laplace regularization. Due to the nonlinear interaction with the Navier–Stokes system, it is unclear whether the approximating scheme outlined in
[
39
]
can be extended to our system (
1.1
). Besides, it remains uncertain how to apply the approximation schemes presented in
[
31
,
49
,
50
]
for the Keller–Segel–Navier–Stokes system with or without a logistic source, owing to the current coupling between the Cahn–Hilliard and Keller–Segel equations as well as the singular character of the potential
\Psi
.
A feasible approach for the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system with a singular potential and chemotactic effects is the so-called semi-Galerkin scheme. For instance, in
[
26
]
, the author proved the existence of global weak solutions to a system analogous to (
1.1
) in a simpler scenario with linear mass transport akin to (
1.9
). Roughly speaking, the author performed a Galerkin approximation solely for the Navier–Stokes system, but solved the coupled system for
(\varphi,\sigma)
independently. This approach ensures that the approximate solution for
\varphi
takes its values in
[-1,1]
and facilitates the completion of a fixed point argument.
Unfortunately, the semi-Galerkin scheme used in
[
26
]
is not applicable here, due to the cross-diffusion structure inherent in (
1.1e
) (cf.
[
39
]
). Indeed, it yields weaker energy for the chemical substance (that is,
\sigma(\ln\sigma-1)
versus
\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}
as in
[
20
,
30
]
) and weaker energy dissipation in the basic energy law (
1.7
) (that is,
n(\varphi)|\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\nabla(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))|^{2}
versus

n(\varphi)|\nabla(\sigma+\chi(1-\varphi))|^{2}
as in
[
20
,
30
]
).
To overcome these difficulties, we propose a new semi-Galerkin scheme. This involves performing a Faedo–Galerkin approximation to the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system (
1.1a
)–(
1.1d
) with the aforementioned regularization of the singular potential
\Psi
, while solving the advection-diffusion-reaction equation (
1.1e
) for
\sigma
independently. In particular, the approximate solution of
\sigma
is solved in the classical sense such that it is strictly positive in
\Omega\times(0,T)
thanks to the strong maximum principle. This key property allows us to use
\ln\sigma
as a test function in the derivation of uniform estimates for approximate solutions.

It is worth mentioning that a (regular) potential function with exponential growth for the Cahn–Hilliard equation is critical in two dimensions and super-critical in three dimensions in view of the Sobolev embedding theorem for
H^{1}(\Omega)
. To overcome this difficulty and the lack of maximum principle, we include an additional
p
-Laplace regularization (with
p=4
) in the chemical potential
\mu
, which yields an
L^{\infty}
-bound for the approximate solution of
\varphi
(not necessarily in the physical interval
[-1,1]
), since
W^{1,4}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
for
d=2,3
. This method has been applied in
[
6
]
within a periodic setting, where a diffuse interface model describing the complex rheology and interfacial dynamics during phase separation in a polar liquid-crystalline emulsion was analyzed. Taking advantage of recent works
[
10
,
11
]
on the
p
-Laplace system, we successfully extend this regularization technique to our problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) in a general bounded smooth domain, which may have its independent interest. Comparing with the regularization via a bi-Laplacian as described in
[
40
]
, the
p
-Laplace regularization appears to be more manageable.

In this study, we illustrate the approximating scheme in full detail and rigorously justify the associated uniform estimates. The presented method has the potential for application to scenarios when the Navier–Stokes system (
1.1a
)–(
1.1b
) for the fluid velocity is substituted with either a Brinkman’s system
[
15
,
13
]
, or a Darcy’s system
[
20
,
22
]
. It is well-known that logistic degradation exerts a regularizing effect on chemotaxis models
[
47
,
45
,
32
]
, and this phenomenon has also been observed in chemotaxis-fluid systems
[
44
,
50
,
31
]
. Our analysis explicitly indicates the role of the logistic term in the highly nonlinear system (
1.1
) within the framework of global weak solutions. It will be interesting to investigate the problem of global regularity and boundedness of weak solutions as well as their long-time behavior. These issues will be addressed in future works.

2
, we introduce the functional settings and state the main results (i.e., Theorems

,

).
In Section

Main Results

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
X
be a real Banach space. We denote its norm by
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, its dual space by
X^{\prime}
, and the duality pairing by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}
. The bold letter
\bm{X}
denotes the generic space of vectors or matrices, with each component belonging to
X
. Given a measurable set
J\subset\mathbb{R}
,
L^{q}(J;X)
with
q\in[1,+\infty]
denotes the space of Bochner measurable
q
-integrable/essentially bounded functions with values in the Banach space
X
. If
J=(a,b)
is an interval, we write for simplicity
L^{q}(a,b;X)
. For
q\in[1,+\infty]
,
W^{1,q}(J;X)
denotes the space of functions
f
such that
f\in L^{q}(J;X)
with
\partial_{t}f\in L^{q}(J;X)
, where
\partial_{t}
means the vector-valued distributional derivative of
f
. When
q=2
, we set
H^{1}(J;X):=W^{1,2}(J;X)
. Besides, we denote by
C(J;X)
(or
C_{w}(J;X)
) the space of functions that are strong (or weak) continuous from
J
to
X
.

We assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
) is a bounded domain with sufficiently smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
such that
\partial\Omega
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional compact and connected hypersurface without boundary.
For any
q\in[1,+\infty]
,
L^{q}(\Omega)
and
L^{q}(\partial\Omega)
denote the Lebesgue spaces on
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
, respectively.
For
s\geq 0
and
q\in[1,\infty)
,
we denote by
H^{s,q}(\Omega)
the Bessel-potential spaces and by
W^{s,q}(\Omega)
the Slobodeckij spaces. It holds that
H^{s,2}(\Omega)=W^{s,2}(\Omega)
for all
s
, but for
q\neq 2
the identity
H^{s,q}(\Omega)=W^{s,q}(\Omega)
is only true if
s\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
H^{s,q}(\Omega)
and
W^{s,q}(\Omega)

over the boundary

If

For simplicity, the norm and inner product in the basic space

(as well as

For every

\overline{f}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\langle f,1\rangle_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime},H^{1}(\Omega)}

Define the linear subspaces

\displaystyle L^{2}_{0}(\Omega):=\big\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\ |\ \overline{f}=0\big\},\qquad V_{0}:=H^{1}(\Omega)\cap L_{0}^{2}(\Omega),

\displaystyle V_{0}^{-1}:=\big\{f\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}\ |\ \overline{f}=0\big\}\subset(V_{0})^{\prime}.

In view of the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, we also introduce the space

H^{2}_{N}(\Omega):=\big\{f\in H^{2}(\Omega)\ |\ \partial_{\bm{n}}f=0\ \textrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\big\}.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{N}\in\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(\Omega),(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime})
be the realization of minus Laplacian

subject to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition such that

\langle\mathcal{A}_{N}u,v\rangle_{(H^{1})^{\prime},H^{1}}:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\forall\,u,v\in H^{1}(\Omega).

The restriction of
\mathcal{A}_{N}
from

onto

is an isomorphism. Besides,
\mathcal{A}_{N}
is positively defined on
V_{0}
and self-adjoint. We denote the inverse map by
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{A}_{N}^{-1}:V_{0}^{-1}\to V_{0}
. For every

,
u=\mathcal{N}f\in V_{0}
is the unique weak solution of the Neumann problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=f,\quad\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
\partial_{\bm{n}}u=0,\quad\ \ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

For every

, we set
\|f\|_{V_{0}^{-1}}=\|\nabla\mathcal{N}f\|
.
It is well-known that

and
f\to\big(\|f-\overline{f}\|_{V_{0}^{-1}}^{2}+|\overline{f}|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

are norms on
V_{0}^{-1}
and
(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}
,
respectively, which are equivalent to the standard ones (see e.g.,
[
36
]
). From the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality:

\|f-\overline{f}\|\leq C\|\nabla f\|,\qquad\forall\,f\in H^{1}(\Omega),

where
C>0
only depends on
\Omega
, we find that
f\to\|\nabla f\|
and
f\to\big(\|\nabla f\|^{2}+|\overline{f}|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}
are norms on
V_{0}
and
H^{1}(\Omega)
, respectively, which are equivalent to the standard ones.
Moreover, we report the following standard Hilbert interpolation inequality

\displaystyle\|f\|\leq\|f\|_{V_{0}^{-1}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla f\|^{\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\forall\,f\in V_{0}.

For later use, we also consider the elliptic operator
\mathcal{A}_{1}:=-\Delta+I

with domain

with a compact inverse denoted by
\mathcal{N}_{1}:=\mathcal{A}^{-1}_{1}

for

.

2.2
Statement of main result

We make the following hypotheses, which will be kept for the remainder of this paper.

(H1)

The singular potential
\Psi
belongs to the class of functions
C([-1,1])\cap C^{2}(-1,1)
. It can be written into the following form

\Psi(r)=\Psi_{0}(r)-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}r^{2},

such that

\lim_{r\to\pm 1}\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(r)=\pm\infty,\quad\text{and}\ \ \Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(r)\geq\theta,\quad\forall\,r\in(-1,1),

where
\theta
is a strictly positive constant and
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, there exists
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
such that
\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}
is nondecreasing in
[1-\epsilon_{0},1)
and nonincreasing in
(-1,-1+\epsilon_{0}]
. We make the extension
\Psi_{0}(r)=+\infty
for any
r\notin[-1,1]
. Without loss of generality, we also set
\Psi_{0}(0)=\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0
.

(H2)

The viscosity function
\eta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is globally Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}
and

\eta_{*}\leq\eta(r)\leq\eta^{*},\quad\forall\,r\in\mathbb{R},

where
\eta_{*}<\eta^{*}
are given positive constants.

(H3)

The mobility function
m\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is globally Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}
and

m_{*}\leq m(r)\leq m^{*},\quad\forall\,r\in\mathbb{R},

where
m_{*}<m^{*}
are given positive constants. Moreover, we set the mobility function
n(r)\equiv 1
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
.

(H4)

The mass source term
S
satisfies

S(\varphi,\sigma)=-\alpha\varphi+h(\varphi,\sigma),\quad\forall\,(\varphi,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},

where
\alpha
is a given positive constant. The function
h
is uniformly bounded and global Lipschitz continuous with respect to its variables. Moreover, the following compatibility condition holds

\alpha>\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})}=:h^{*}\geq 0.

(H5)

The reaction term
R
satisfies

R(\varphi,\sigma)=\beta(\varphi)\sigma-\kappa\sigma^{2},\quad\forall\,(\varphi,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},

where
\kappa
is a given positive constant.
The function
\beta\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\displaystyle|\beta(r)|\leq b^{*},

\displaystyle\forall\,r\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\beta(r)=0,

\displaystyle\forall\,r\in(-\infty,-2]\cup[2,+\infty),

where
b^{*}
is a given positive constant.

(H6)

The coefficients
\varepsilon
,
\chi
are prescribed constants such that

\varepsilon=1,\quad\chi\in\mathbb{R}.

Remark 2.1
.

The physically relevant logarithmic potential (
1.5
) fulfills the assumption (H1). Besides, as noted in
[
23
, Remark 2.1]
, one can easily extend the linear viscosity function (
1.4
) to
\mathbb{R}
in such a way to comply (H2). Since the singular potential guarantees that
\varphi\in[-1,1]
, the value of
\eta
outside of
[-1,1]
is not important and can be appropriately chosen as in (H2). Similarly, the only significant values of
m
,
n
,
\beta
will be those for
r\in[-1,1]
.
We extend them outside
[-1,1]
due to the Galerkin approximation for
\varphi
. To focus on the difficulty from the cross-diffusion structure and the regularizing effect due to logistic degradation, we keep a simpler form of the
\sigma
-equation by assuming
n(\varphi)\equiv 1
and
\kappa
a positive constant.

Remark 2.2
.

Concerning the mass source term
S
, only the behavior of

in the physical reference set
[-1,1]\times[0,+\infty)
is significant. If
h
is a constant belonging to
(-\alpha,\alpha)
,

corresponds to the classical Oono’s interaction
[
21

35

The magnitude of the chemotaxis sensitivity

is somewhat trivial since the effects of chemotaxis and mass transport are switched off. Comparing with
[
39

. Since we do not consider the so-called sharp interface limit as

has no influence on the subsequent analysis. For the sake of simplicity, we set

3
A Regularized System and its Semi-Galerkin Scheme

In this section, we introduce an appropriate regularized system for problem (
2.2
)–(
2.4
), with a non-standard approximation for the singular potential
\Psi
and a
p
-Laplace regularization in the chemical potential
\mu
. Then we solve the regularized system by a suitable semi-Galerkin scheme, specifically designed to handle the nonlinear coupling structure of the system. In summary, we proceed as follows: first, given a finite dimensional Galerkin ansatz
(\bm{u}^{k},\psi^{k})
for the fluid velocity and the phase variable, we solve the advection-diffusion-reaction equation for the chemical concentration
\sigma^{k}
; next, with the sufficiently smooth solution
\sigma^{k}
, we solve the Faedo–Galerkin approximation of the regularized Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system and obtain a finite dimensional solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k})
; finally, we prove the existence of a fixed point for the nonlinear mapping
\Phi:\Phi(\bm{u}^{k},\psi^{k})=(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k})
by Schauder’s fixed point theorem, which yields a local solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k},\sigma^{k})
of the semi-Galerkin scheme.

3.1
The regularized system

Regularization of the singular potential
\Psi
.
Let
\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)
be the constant given in (H1). There exists
\epsilon_{1}\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
such that

\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(-1+\epsilon)\leq-1,\quad\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(1-\epsilon)\geq 1,\quad\forall\,\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{1}).

Given
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{1})
, we introduce the following approximation of the singular nonlinearity
\Psi^{\prime}_{0}
:

\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(r)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(-1+\epsilon)-\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(-1+\epsilon)\left(\frac{4|\chi|+3}{4(|\chi|+1)}+\epsilon\right)&&\\
&\quad-\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(-1+\epsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-4(|\chi|+1)r-8(|\chi|+1)-\ln 4(|\chi|+1)},&&\quad r\leq-2,\\
&\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(-1+\epsilon)+\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(-1+\epsilon)(r+1-\epsilon),&&\quad-2<r<-1+\epsilon,\\
&\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(r),&&\quad\ |r|\leq 1-\epsilon,\\
\\[2.0pt]
&\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(1-\epsilon)+\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1-\epsilon)(r-1+\epsilon),&&\quad 1-\epsilon<r<2,\\
&\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(1-\epsilon)+\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1-\epsilon)\left(\frac{4|\chi|+3}{4(|\chi|+1)}+\epsilon\right)&&\\
&\quad+\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1-\epsilon)\mathrm{e}^{4(|\chi|+1)r-8(|\chi|+1)-\ln 4(|\chi|+1)},&&\quad\,r\geq 2.\end{aligned}\right.

Set

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Psi_{\epsilon}(r)=\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}r^{2}.

It is straightforward to check that
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and thus
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. According to (H1), it holds

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime\prime}(r)\geq\theta,\qquad r\Psi_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime}(r)\geq 0,\qquad\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)\geq-L,\qquad\forall\,r\in\mathbb{R},

where
L>0
is a constant independent of
\epsilon
and
r
.
Similar to
[
22
]
, we also find

\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)\leq\Psi_{0}(r),\quad\forall\,r\in[-1,1].

Remark 3.1
.

The approximate potential function
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)
depends on the chemical sensitivity
\chi
. For any given
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{1})
,
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)
provides a sufficiently fast growth for large values of
r
, while for
r\in[-1,1]
, it provides sufficient coercivity for small
\epsilon
. The former will be helpful to derive necessary estimates for approximate solutions, since the Galerkin ansatz of
\varphi
does not have a uniform
L^{\infty}
-bound. The latter enables us to recover the physical constraint
\varphi\in[-1,1]
after passing to the limit as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Approximation of the initial data.

For any given
\varphi_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with
\|\varphi_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1
and
|\overline{\varphi_{0}}|<1
,
we define
\varphi_{0,\gamma}
as the unique solution to the Neumann problem

\begin{cases}\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\gamma\Delta\varphi_{0,\gamma}=(1-\gamma)\varphi_{0},\,\ \quad\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}=0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ \ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
.
The classical elliptic theory yields

\displaystyle\varphi_{0,\gamma}\in H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\cap H^{3}(\Omega),\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{\varphi_{0,\gamma}}=(1-\gamma)\overline{\varphi_{0}}\in\big[-|\overline{\varphi_{0}}|,\,|\overline{\varphi_{0}}|\big].

(3.1)

Thanks to the maximum principle, we also find

\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1-\gamma.

(3.2)

It is straightforward to check that

\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|\leq\|\varphi_{0}\|,\quad\|\nabla\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|\leq\|\nabla\varphi_{0}\|,\quad\gamma\|\Delta\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|\leq 2\|\varphi_{0}\|.

(3.3)

Then by the elliptic estimate, we get

\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|\Delta\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|+\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|)\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}+1\right)\|\varphi_{0}\|,

(3.4)

where the constant
C>0
depends only on
\Omega
. From (
3.3
) and Young’s inequality, we infer that

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma\int_{\Omega}(\Delta\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0})(\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\|\nabla\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|\|\nabla(\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0})\|+\gamma\|\varphi_{0}\|\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma\|\nabla\varphi_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\|\varphi_{0}\|^{2},

which implies
\varphi_{0,\gamma}
strongly converges to
\varphi_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
\gamma\to 0
. This fact combined with (
3.3
) further yields that
\varphi_{0,\gamma}
weakly converges to
\varphi_{0}
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
as
\gamma\to 0
. Since
H^{1}(\Omega)
is uniformly convex, we apply
[
7
, Proposition 3.32]
and (
3.3
) to conclude

\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=0.

Next, for any
\sigma_{0}
satisfying
\sigma_{0}\ln\sigma_{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\sigma_{0}\geq 0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
, we consider a family of approximations
\{\sigma_{0,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
, with the following properties (see, e.g.,
[
49
, Section 2.2]
)

\sigma_{0,n}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega),\quad\sigma_{0,n}\geq 0\ \text{ in }\Omega,\quad\sigma_{0,n}\not\equiv 0,\quad\sigma_{0,n}\rightarrow\sigma_{0}\text{ in }\ L\log L(\Omega)\ \text{ as }n\to+\infty.

Here,
L\log L(\Omega)
denotes the standard Orlicz space associated with the Young function
(0,\infty)\ni z\mapsto

z\ln(1+z)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that

\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0,n}\ln\sigma_{0,n}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0}\ln\sigma_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x+1,\quad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.

The regularized problem.
For any given
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
,
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{1}]
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we introduce the following regularized problem
(\bm{S}_{\gamma,\epsilon,n})
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{v}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\bm{v}-\mathrm{div}\big(2\eta(\varphi)D\bm{v}\big)+\nabla p=(\mu+\chi\sigma)\nabla\varphi,

(3.5a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\,\bm{v}=0,

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\mathrm{div}\big(m(\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)-\alpha\varphi+h(\varphi,\sigma),

(3.5c)

\displaystyle\mu=-\gamma^{8}\mathrm{div}(|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\nabla\varphi)-\Delta\varphi+\Psi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi)-\chi\sigma,

(3.5d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla\sigma-\Delta\sigma+\chi\mathrm{div}(\sigma\nabla\varphi)=\beta(\varphi)\sigma-\kappa\sigma^{2},

(3.5e)

in
\Omega\times(0,T)
, subject to the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\bm{v}=\mathbf{0},\quad\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi=\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu=\partial_{\bm{n}}\sigma=0,\quad\textrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.6)

and the initial conditions

\displaystyle\bm{v}|_{t=0}=\bm{v}_{0},\quad\varphi|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0,\gamma},\quad\sigma|_{t=0}=\sigma_{0,n},\qquad

in

\displaystyle\ \Omega.

(3.7)

Remark 3.2
.

The (exponential) growth of
\Psi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}
is critical in two dimensions and super critical in three dimensions in view of the Sobolev embedding for
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
In order to overcome this difficulty, we introduce an additional regularizing term
-\gamma^{8}\mathrm{div}(|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\nabla\varphi)
in (
3.5d
). Together with the regularized initial datum
\varphi_{0,\gamma}
, the
p
-Laplace term yields a stronger estimate for the approximate solution
\varphi^{k}
in the Faedo–Galerkin scheme presented below such that
\varphi^{k}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,4}(\Omega))
. By the Sobolev embedding theorem, we have that
\varphi^{k}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
(
d=2,3
), which is crucial to control the nonlinear term
\Psi_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi^{k})
.

3.2
The semi-Galerkin scheme

We shall solve the regularized system (
3.5
)–(
3.7
) by a suitable semi-Galerkin scheme. Consider the family of eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and corresponding eigenfunctions
\{\bm{y}_{i}(x)\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of the Stokes problem

(\nabla\bm{y}_{i},\nabla\bm{\zeta})=\lambda_{i}(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{\zeta}),\quad\forall\,\bm{\zeta}\in{\bm{H}^{1}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)},\quad\textrm{with}\ \|\bm{y}_{i}\|=1.

It is well-known that
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\to+\infty
,
\{\bm{y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms a complete orthonormal basis in
\bm{L}^{2}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
and it is orthogonal in
\bm{H}^{1}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
.
Next, we consider the family of eigenvalues
\{\ell_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and corresponding eigenfunctions
\{z_{i}(x)\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of the Laplace operator with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions

(\nabla z_{i},\nabla w)=\ell_{i}(z_{i},w),\quad\forall\,w\in{H^{1}(\Omega)},\quad\textrm{ with }\|z_{i}\|=1.

Then
0=\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}\leq\cdots\to+\infty
,
z_{1}=1
,
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forms a complete orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and it is orthogonal in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.
For every integer
k\geq 1
, we denote the finite-dimensional subspace of
\bm{L}^{2}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
by

\bm{Y}_{k}:=\textrm{span}\{\bm{y}_{1}(x),\cdots,\bm{y}_{k}(x)\}.

The orthogonal projection on
\bm{Y}_{k}
with respect to the inner product in
\bm{L}^{2}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
is denoted by
\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}
. Similarly, we denote the finite-dimensional subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega)
by

Z_{k}:=\textrm{span}\{z_{1}(x),\cdots,z_{k}(x)\}.

The orthogonal projection on
Z_{k}
with respect to the inner product in
L^{2}(\Omega)
is denoted by
\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}
.
We note that
\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\bm{Y}_{k}
is dense in both
\bm{L}^{2}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
,
\bm{H}^{1}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
and
D(\bm{S})
, while
\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}Z_{k}
is dense in both
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H_{N}^{2}(\Omega)
. Recalling that
\Omega
is assumed to be sufficiently smooth, for instance, a
C^{4}
-domain, we have
\bm{y}_{i}\in\bm{H}^{1}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)\cap\bm{H}^{4}(\Omega)
as well as
z_{i}\in H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)\cap H^{4}(\Omega)
for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Moreover, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the following inverse inequalities hold

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}\|_{\bm{H}^{j}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}\|\bm{v}\|,\quad\forall\,\bm{v}\in\bm{Y}_{k},\quad j=1,2,3,4,

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{H^{j}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}\|\varphi\|,\quad\forall\,\varphi\in Z_{k},\quad j=1,2,3,4.

The symbol
C_{k}
denotes a generic positive constant that depends on the approximating parameter
k
.

Finite dimensional approximation of the initial data.

Let
k
be an arbitrary positive integer.
For the initial velocity field
\bm{v}_{0}\in\bm{L}^{2}_{0,\sigma}(\Omega)
, we consider its finite dimensional approximation
\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}\bm{v}_{0}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}\bm{v}_{0}-\bm{v}_{0}\|=0.

Next, we consider the finite dimensional approximation for the regularized initial datum
\varphi_{0,\gamma}
of the phase variable, that is,
\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}
. We have
\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}\in H^{2}_{N}(\Omega)
and

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

From the Sobolev embedding theorem
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
that is valid for
d=2,3
, for every given
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
, there exists an (sufficiently large) integer
\widehat{k}
such that

\|\bm{P}_{Z_{\widehat{k}}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{C(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\|\bm{P}_{Z_{\widehat{k}}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}-\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{\gamma}{2},

where the constant
C>0
only depends on
\Omega
. Thus, for all integers
k\geq\widehat{k}
, it holds

\|\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq 1-\frac{\gamma}{2},\quad\|\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|\varphi_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(3.8)

The semi-Galerkin scheme.

Let
T>0
be an arbitrarily fixed final time.
For every integer
k\geq\widehat{k}
, we consider the approximate solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k},\mu^{k},\sigma^{k})
to the regularized system (
3.5
)–(
3.7
) such that the Galerkin ansatz

\bm{v}^{k}(x,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{k}(t)\bm{y}_{i}(x),\quad\varphi^{k}(x,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}^{k}(t)z_{i}(x),\quad\mu^{k}(x,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}^{k}(t)z_{i}(x),

satisfy

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\bm{v}^{k},\bm{\zeta})+\big((\bm{v}^{k}\cdot\nabla)\bm{v}^{k},\bm{\zeta}\big)+\big(2\eta(\varphi^{k})D\bm{v}^{k},D\bm{\zeta}\big)=\big((\mu^{k}+\chi\sigma^{k})\nabla\varphi^{k},\bm{\zeta}\big),

(3.9)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}\varphi^{k},\xi)+(\bm{v}^{k}\cdot\nabla\varphi^{k},\xi)=-\big(m(\varphi^{k})\nabla\mu^{k},\nabla\xi\big)+\big(-\alpha\varphi^{k}+h(\varphi^{k},\sigma^{k}),\xi\big),

(3.10)

and

(\mu^{k},\xi)=\big(-\gamma^{8}\mathrm{div}(|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}\nabla\varphi^{k})-\Delta\varphi^{k}+\Psi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})-\chi\sigma^{k},\xi\big),

(3.11)

in
(0,T)
and for all
\bm{\zeta}\in\bm{Y}_{k}
,
\xi\in Z_{k}
, while the unknown function
\sigma^{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma^{k}+\bm{v}^{k}\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}-\Delta\sigma^{k}+\chi\mathrm{div}(\sigma^{k}\nabla\varphi^{k})=\beta(\varphi^{k})\sigma^{k}-\kappa(\sigma^{k})^{2},

(3.12)

in
\Omega\times(0,T)
. The approximate solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k},\mu^{k},\sigma^{k})

fulfills the boundary and initial conditions

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{k}=\bm{0},\quad\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi^{k}=\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu^{k}=\partial_{\bm{n}}\sigma^{k}=0,

\displaystyle\quad\textrm{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(3.13)

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{k}|_{t=0}=\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}\bm{v}_{0},\quad\varphi^{k}|_{t=0}=\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma},\quad\sigma^{k}|_{t=0}=\sigma_{0,n},

\displaystyle\quad\text{in}\ \Omega.

(3.14)

The following proposition states that, given
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
,
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{1}]
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, for every integer

(Local solvability of the semi-Galerkin scheme)

Let

, and the initial data

\displaystyle\varphi^{k}\in H^{1}(0,T_{k};Z_{k}),\quad\mu^{k}\in C([0,T_{k}];Z_{k}),

\displaystyle\sigma^{k}\in C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{k}]),\quad\sigma^{k}(x,t)>0\ \ \text{in }\ \Omega\times(0,T_{k}].

\Omega
,

,
\gamma
,
\epsilon
,

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
is based on a fixed point argument through Schauder’s theorem.

Step 1
. Let
\widetilde{M}
be a sufficiently large constant satisfying
\widetilde{M}\geq 2(\|\bm{v}_{0}\|^{2}+\|\varphi_{0}\|^{2}+1)
. The exact value of
\widetilde{M}
will be determined later. Consider two given functions

\bm{u}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{a}_{i}^{k}(t)\bm{y}_{i}(x)\in C^{\delta}([0,T];\bm{Y}_{k}),\quad\psi^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\widetilde{b}_{i}^{k}(t)z_{i}(x)\in C^{\delta}([0,T];Z_{k})

for some
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right)
, which fulfill

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{i}^{k}(0)=(\bm{v}_{0},\bm{y}_{i}),\quad i=1,\cdots,k,\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\widetilde{a}_{i}^{k}(t)|^{2}\leq\widetilde{M},

\displaystyle\widetilde{b}_{i}^{k}(0)=(\varphi_{0,\gamma},z_{i}),\quad i=1,\cdots,k,\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\widetilde{b}_{i}^{k}(t)|^{2}\leq\widetilde{M}.

Then it holds
\bm{u}^{k}(0)=\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}\bm{v}_{0}
,
\psi^{k}(0)=\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\bm{u}^{k}(t)\|^{2}\leq\widetilde{M},\quad\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\psi^{k}(t)\|^{2}\leq\widetilde{M}.

(3.15)

Let us first consider the following auxiliary equation for the chemical concentration

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma^{k}+\bm{u}^{k}\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}-\Delta\sigma^{k}+\chi\mathrm{div}(\sigma^{k}\nabla\psi^{k})=\beta(\psi^{k})\sigma^{k}-\kappa(\sigma^{k})^{2},

(3.16)

in
\Omega\times(0,T)
,
equipped with the boundary and initial conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{n}}\sigma^{k}=0\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega\times(0,T),\qquad\sigma^{k}|_{t=0}=\sigma_{0,n}\quad\text{in}\ \Omega.

(3.17)

Then we have

Lemma 3.1
.

Given
(\bm{u}^{k},\psi^{k})
, problem (
3.16
)–(
3.17
) admits a unique classical solution
\sigma^{k}
on
[0,T]
such that

\sigma^{k}\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])\cap C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T)),\quad\sigma^{k}(x,t)>0\ \ \text{in }\ \overline{\Omega}\times(0,T].

According to Lemma
3.1
, we find that the following mapping is well defined

\displaystyle\Phi^{k}_{1}:\ C^{\delta}([0,T];\bm{Y}_{k})\times C^{\delta}([0,T];Z_{k})\

\displaystyle\to\ \ \widehat{X},

\displaystyle(\bm{u}^{k},\psi^{k})\

,

is a bounded operator from

to

Next, we show that
\Phi^{k}_{1}
is continuous with respect to the given data
(\bm{u}^{k},\psi^{k})
in the topology of

, with

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

To this end, let
\bm{u}^{k}_{1}
,

,

. Both

3.15
).
Let
\sigma^{k}_{i}=\Phi^{k}_{1}(\bm{u}^{k}_{i},\psi^{k}_{i})
,

which fulfill

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\sigma^{k}+{\bm{u}^{k}_{1}\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}}+{\bm{u}^{k}\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}_{2}}-\Delta\sigma^{k}

\displaystyle\quad=-\chi\mathrm{div}\big(\sigma^{k}\nabla\psi_{1}^{k}+\sigma_{2}^{k}\nabla\psi^{k}\big)+\big[\beta(\psi^{k}_{1})-\kappa(\sigma_{1}^{k}+\sigma_{2}^{k})\big]\sigma^{k}+\big(\beta(\psi^{k}_{1})-\beta(\psi^{k}_{2})\big)\sigma^{k}_{2},

in
\Omega\times(0,T)

\sigma^{k}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\sigma^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\|\psi^{k}\|^{2}+C\|\sigma^{k}_{2}\|^{2}\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2}.

Combining the above estimates and integrating (

\displaystyle\|\sigma^{k}(t)\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\sigma^{k}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\|\sigma^{k}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s+C_{k}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\|\bm{u}^{k}(s)\|^{2}+\|\psi^{k}(s)\|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(3.21)

where the estimate
\|\sigma_{2}^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C
has been used.
An application of Gronwall’s lemma to (

Remark 4.1

Step 2. Basic energy law.
Thanks to Proposition

, we are allowed to test (

) by
\bm{v}^{k}
, (

) by
\mu^{k}
, (

, and (

) by

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\bm{v}^{k}|^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{8}}{4}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{4}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}+\Psi_{k}(\varphi^{k})+\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)-\chi\sigma^{k}\varphi^{k}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(2\eta(\varphi^{k})|D\bm{v}^{k}|^{2}+m(\varphi^{k})|\nabla\mu^{k}|^{2}+\sigma^{k}|\nabla(\ln\sigma^{k}-\chi\varphi^{k})|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\big[-\alpha\varphi^{k}+h(\varphi^{k},\sigma^{k})\big]\mu^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\beta(\varphi^{k})\sigma^{k}\ln\sigma^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\chi\int_{\Omega}\big[\beta(\varphi^{k})\sigma^{k}-\kappa(\sigma^{k})^{2}\big]\varphi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Noticing that

\|\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{W^{1,4}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\varphi_{0,\gamma}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)},

where
C>0
only depends on
\Omega
, thanks to the construction of the initial data (see (

), (

)) and Lemma

, we control the initial approximate energy as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{k}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}\bm{v}_{0}|^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{8}}{4}|\nabla\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}|^{4}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\big(\Psi_{\epsilon}(\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma})+\sigma_{0,n}(\ln\sigma_{0,n}-1)-\chi\sigma_{0,n}\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}\big)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+2(1+|\chi|)\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0}\ln\sigma_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x+C(1+|\chi|)|\Omega|

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\bm{v}_{0}\|,\|\varphi_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\max_{r\in[-1,1]}|\Psi_{0}(r)|,\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{0}\ln\sigma_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x,\chi,\Omega\Big)

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{E}_{0},

(4.3)

where the upper bound
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is independent of the parameters
k
,
\gamma
,
\epsilon
,
n

Step 3. Lower bound of the approximate total energy
\mathcal{E}^{k}
.

To obtain uniform estimates for the approximate solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k},\mu^{k}\,\sigma^{k})
, it is crucial to show that the total energy
\mathcal{E}^{k}(t)
is uniformly semi-coercive from below with respect to
k
,
\gamma
,
\epsilon
and
n
. The key point is to deal with the crossing term

Recalling the construction of
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}
, we find

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1-\epsilon}\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int^{2}_{1-\epsilon}\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{2}^{r}\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(1-\epsilon)+(r-1+\epsilon)\Psi_{0}^{\prime}(1-\epsilon)

\displaystyle\quad+\left[\frac{1}{2}(1+\epsilon)^{2}+\left(\frac{4|\chi|+3}{4(|\chi|+1)}+\epsilon\right)(r-2)\right]\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1-\epsilon)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{16(|\chi|+1)^{2}}\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}(1-\epsilon)\left(\mathrm{e}^{4(|\chi|+1)r-8(|\chi|+1)}-1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\theta}{16(|\chi|+1)^{2}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{4(|\chi|+1)r-8(|\chi|+1)}-1\right),\qquad\forall\,r\geq 2.

(4.4)

Hence, there exists
r^{*}>2
depending on
\theta
,
\theta_{0}
and
\chi
, but not on

, such that for any

, it holds

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(r)\geq\mathrm{e}^{(2|\chi|+1)r}+\theta_{0}r^{2}+2|\chi|r+1,\qquad\forall\,r\in[r^{*},+\infty).

(4.5)

We claim that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}(\varphi^{k})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)-|\chi|\sigma^{k}\varphi^{k}\geq 0,

for all
\sigma^{k}\geq 0
and
\varphi^{k}\geq r^{*}
.
Indeed, if

, it follows from (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}(\varphi^{k})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)-|\chi|\sigma^{k}\varphi^{k}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{(2|\chi|+1)\varphi^{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)-|\chi|(\sigma^{k}-1)\varphi^{k}.

Taking

and

in the generalized Young inequality (

|\chi|(\sigma^{k}-1)\varphi^{k}\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{2|\chi|\varphi^{k}}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1),

which together with (

By a similar procedure, we can find some constant

depending on
\theta
,
\theta_{0}
and
\chi
, but not on

, such that for any

, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Psi_{0,k}(\varphi^{k})-\frac{\theta_{0}}{2}(\varphi^{k})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)+|\chi|\sigma^{k}\varphi^{k}\geq 0,

and

.

\displaystyle|\chi\sigma^{k}\varphi^{k}|

\displaystyle\leq(1+|\chi|)\max\{-r_{*},r^{*}\}\sigma^{k}

In conclusion, there exists a sufficiently large constant

depending on
\theta

,
\chi
and
\Omega
, but not on

,
\gamma
,
\epsilon
and

, such that

\displaystyle|\chi|\int_{\Omega}|\sigma^{k}\varphi^{k}|\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left[\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})+\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)\right]\,\mathrm{d}x+C_{*},

which yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{k}(t)\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left[|\bm{v}^{k}|^{2}+|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}+\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})+\sigma^{k}(\ln\sigma^{k}-1)\right]\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\gamma^{8}}{4}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x-C_{*},

,
\gamma
,
\epsilon
,
n
.

Step 4. Estimate of the remainder term

Similar to

, we deduce from (H4) and the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality that

where

only depends on
\Omega
. The last term on the right-hand side of (

,

,

and

Next, testing (

A direct calculation yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}(|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}\nabla\varphi^{k})\Delta\varphi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}(|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}\partial_{i}\varphi^{k})\partial_{j}\varphi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x+\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{i}(|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}\partial_{i}\varphi^{k})\partial_{\bm{n}}\varphi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}S}_{=0}

\displaystyle\qquad-\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{j}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}(\partial_{i}\varphi^{k}\bm{n}_{i})\partial_{j}\varphi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x}_{=0}-\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\varphi^{k}\bm{n}_{i}\partial_{j}\varphi^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x,

. Hereafter, we use Einstein’s summation convention for repeated indices.
It remains to control the last term on the right-hand side of (

, (4.17)]
(which is a consequence of

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\bm{H}^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\mu^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|\varphi^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C,

(4.56)

\displaystyle\gamma^{4}\big\||\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{2}\big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C,

(4.57)

\displaystyle\|\sigma^{n}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\sigma^{n}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;W^{1,\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega))}+\|(\sigma^{n})^{2}\ln\sigma^{n}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C,

(4.58)

\|\partial_{t}\bm{v}^{k}\|_{L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;(\bm{H}^{1}_{0,\sigma})^{\prime})}+\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime})}\leq C.

(4.59)

Using the fact
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow W^{1,6}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we find

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\sigma^{n}\|_{(H^{2}(\Omega))^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bm{v}^{n}\|_{\bm{L}^{3}(\Omega)}\|\sigma^{n}\|+C\|\sigma^{n}\|\|\nabla\varphi^{n}\|_{\bm{L}^{3}(\Omega)}+C(\|\sigma^{n}\|+\|\sigma^{n}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\bm{v}^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\bm{v}^{n}\|_{\bm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\sigma^{n}\|\big)+C\|\sigma^{n}\|\|\nabla\varphi^{n}\|_{\bm{L}^{3}(\Omega)}+C\big(\|\sigma^{n}\|^{2}+1\big),

which combined with (
4.56
), (
4.58
) yields

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\sigma^{n}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;(H^{2}(\Omega))^{\prime})}\leq C.

(4.60)

In order to pass to the limit, we also need to handle the nonlinear term
\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(\varphi^{n})
. Since for every
n
,
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}(\varphi^{n})\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
(though the corresponding estimate depends on
n
), then we are allowed to test (
4.54c
) by
\Psi^{\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(\varphi^{n})
. After integration by parts, we find

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\big(\gamma^{8}|\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{4}+|\nabla\varphi^{n}|^{2}\big)\Psi^{\prime\prime}_{0,\epsilon}(\varphi^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x}_{\geq 0}+\|\Psi_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi^{n})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\Omega}\big(\mu^{n}+\chi\sigma^{n}+\theta_{0}\varphi^{n}\big)\Psi_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\Psi_{0,\epsilon}^{\prime}(\varphi^{n})\|^{2}+C\big(\|\mu^{n}\|^{2}+\|\sigma^{n}\|^{2}+\|\varphi^{n}\|^{2}\big).

Integrating in time over
[0,T]
, we conclude from (
4.56
) and (
4.58
) that

\displaystyle\|\Psi^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{n})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C.

(4.61)

Collecting the above uniform estimates that are independent of
n
, by classical compactness results, we are able to pass to the limit as
n\to+\infty
(in the sense of a suitable non-relabelled subsequence). We denote the limit by
(\bm{v},\varphi,\mu,\sigma)
, which is a global weak solution to the original problem (
2.2
)–(
2.4
) on
[0,T]
. Comparing with
[
39
]
, no truncation was used in our approximating scheme for the chemical concentration
\sigma
, thus the procedure to pass to the limit turns out to be simpler. The limiting procedure for the subsystem of
(\bm{v}^{n},\varphi^{n})
is similar to that for the Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
35
]
, while the limiting procedure for the equation of
\sigma^{n}
can be done as in
[
31
]
. In particular, thanks to
(
4.56
) and (
4.59
), the Aubin–Lions–Simon theorem entails that (up to a subsequence)
\varphi^{n}\to\varphi
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,q}(\Omega))
, for any
q\in[2,6)
. Owing to the weak convergence
\sigma^{n}\rightharpoonup\sigma\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, the latter allows the passage to the limit in the nonlinear terms involving the product
\sigma^{n}\nabla\varphi^{n}
.
Besides, due to the singularity of
\Psi
, we can apply the classical method for the Cahn–Hilliard equation to conclude that (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
34
]
)

\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))\ \textrm{with}\ \ |\varphi(x,t)|<1\ \ \textrm{a.e.\ in}\ \Omega\times(0,T).

If in addition,
\sigma_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, improved regularity of the weak solution
(\varphi,\sigma)
can be derived from the uniform estimates obtained in Step 8 in Section
4.1
in two dimensions. In particular, we can apply the classical result for the Cahn–Hilliard equation to obtain (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
21
,
22
]
)

\displaystyle\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{2,q}(\Omega))}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+\|\Psi^{\prime}(\varphi)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))}}\leq C,\quad\forall\,q\in[2,+\infty).

This concludes the proof of Theorem

and we omit the details.

\square

5
Uniqueness of Weak Solution in Two Dimensions

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.2
on the uniqueness of global weak solutions in two dimensions. The proof mainly follows the idea in
[
23
]
for the two dimensional Navier–Stokes–Cahn–Hilliard system with non-constant viscosity. Additional efforts are required to handle the
\sigma
-equation, cf.
[
26
]
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
(\bm{v}_{1},\varphi_{1},\mu_{1},\sigma_{1})
and
(\bm{v}_{2},\varphi_{2},\mu_{2},\sigma_{2})
be two weak solutions to problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) given by Theorem
2.1
-(2) subject to the initial data
(\bm{v}_{01},\sigma_{01},\varphi_{01})
and
(\bm{v}_{02},\sigma_{02},\varphi_{02})
, respectively.
Denote the differences by

(\bm{v},\varphi,\mu,\sigma)=(\bm{v}_{1}-\bm{v}_{2},\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2},\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}).

Following the detailed calculations performed in
[
26
, Section 4]
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}W(t)+\frac{\eta_{*}}{2}\|\bm{v}\|^{2}+\frac{\widehat{m}}{2}\|\nabla\varphi\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma\|^{2}\leq\widehat{C}Z(t)W(t)\ln\left(\frac{\widetilde{C}}{W(t)}\right),

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle W(t):=\|\nabla\bm{S}^{-1}\bm{v}(t)\|^{2}+\|\varphi(t)\|_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}}^{2}+\|\sigma(t)\|_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}}^{2}+|\overline{\varphi}(t)|,

and

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla\bm{v}_{1}(t)\|^{2}+\|\nabla\bm{v}_{2}(t)\|^{2}+\|\varphi_{1}(t)\|_{W^{2,3}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\varphi_{2}(t)\|_{W^{2,3}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\varphi_{1}(t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{4}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\Psi^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}(t))\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\Psi^{\prime}(\varphi_{2}(t))\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\sigma_{2}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\sigma_{2}(t)\|^{4}+\|\sigma_{1}(t)\|^{2}\|\sigma_{1}(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+1.

Here,
\widehat{C}
,
\widetilde{C}
are positive constants depending on norms of the initial data,
\Omega
, and the coefficients of the system,
\widetilde{C}
may also depend on
T
. In the derivation of (
5.1
), we only need modifications due to the new cross diffusion term as well as the logistic term, that is,

\displaystyle|\chi(\sigma_{1}\nabla\varphi_{1},\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma)-\chi(\sigma_{2}\nabla\varphi_{2},\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma)|

\displaystyle\quad\leq|\chi(\sigma_{1}\nabla\varphi,\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma)|+|\chi(\sigma\nabla\varphi_{2},\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma)|

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\|\sigma_{1}\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\varphi\|\|\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\|_{\bm{L}^{4}(\Omega)}+C\|\sigma\|\|\nabla\varphi_{2}\|_{\bm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\|\sigma_{1}\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\sigma_{1}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla\varphi\|\|\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\sigma\|^{\frac{1}{2}}+C\|\sigma\|\|\varphi_{2}\|_{W^{2,3}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{\widehat{m}}{4}\|\nabla\varphi\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8}\|\sigma\|^{2}+C\|\sigma_{1}\|^{2}\|\sigma_{1}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\sigma\|_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}}^{2}+C\|\varphi_{2}\|_{W^{2,3}(\Omega)}^{2}\|\sigma\|_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}}^{2},

and by Hölder’s inequality together with Agmon’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(\kappa\sigma_{1}^{2}-\kappa\sigma_{2}^{2})\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\sigma_{1}\|+\|\sigma_{2}\|)\|\sigma\|\|\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\sigma_{1}\|+\|\sigma_{2}\|)\|\sigma\|\|\mathcal{N}_{1}\sigma\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\sigma\|^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{8}\|\sigma\|^{2}+C\big(\|\sigma_{1}\|^{4}+\|\sigma_{2}\|^{4})\|\sigma\|_{(H^{1}(\Omega))^{\prime}}^{2}.

Integrating (
5.1
) on
[0,t]\subset[0,T]
, we get

\displaystyle W(t)\leq W(0)+C_{1}\int_{0}^{t}Z(s)W(s)\ln\left(\frac{\widetilde{C}}{W(s)}\right)ds,\quad\text{for a.e.}\,t\in[0,T].

(5.2)

From the regularity of weak solutions, it holds

Z(t)\in L^{1}(0,T).

Then we are in a position to apply the Osgood lemma (see
[
4
, Lemma 3.4]
): if
W(0)=0
, we find
W(t)=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
, which yields the uniqueness of global weak solutions to problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
);
if
W(0)>0
, we can derive a continuous dependence estimate like in
[
23
,
26
]
, that is,

W(t)\leq\widetilde{C}\left(\frac{W(0)}{\widetilde{C}}\right)^{\exp\left(-\widehat{C}\int_{0}^{t}Z(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right)},\quad\ \forall\,t\in[0,T].

(5.3)

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is complete.

\square

Proof of Lemma

The existence of a classical solution
\sigma^{k}

[
47

48

Assume
\|\sigma_{0,n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq K
for some
K>0
. Consider
T_{K}\in(0,1)
to be specified below and the closed set

\Sigma_{K}=\big\{\sigma^{k}\in C([0,T_{K}];C(\overline{\Omega}))\,|\,\|\sigma^{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T_{K};L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\leq K+1\big\}.

For
\sigma^{k}\in\Sigma_{K}
and
t\in[0,T_{K}]
, we define the nonlinear mapping
\mathcal{G}
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\sigma^{k})(t)

\displaystyle=e^{t\Delta}\sigma_{0,n}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\big[\bm{u}^{k}(s)\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}(s)+\chi\mathrm{div}(\sigma^{k}(s)\nabla\psi^{k}(s))\big]\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\big[\beta(\psi^{k}(s))\sigma^{k}(s)-\kappa(\sigma^{k}(s))^{2}\big]\,\mathrm{d}s.

(A.1)

Here,
(e^{t\Delta})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the Neumann heat semigroup in
\Omega
. By the maximum principle, we find

\|e^{t\Delta}\sigma_{0,n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|\sigma_{0,n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq K,\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T_{K}],

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\big\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\big[\beta(\psi^{k}(s))\sigma^{k}(s)-\kappa(\psi^{k}(s))(\sigma^{k}(s))^{2}\big]\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq(b^{*}+\kappa)\int_{0}^{t}\big(\|\sigma^{k}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|\sigma^{k}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq(b^{*}+\kappa)(K+2)^{2}T_{K},\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T_{K}].

We recall that
\mathcal{A}_{1}=-\Delta+I
subject to the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition is a sectorial operator in
L^{4}(\Omega)
. In two and three dimensions, it holds
D(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\frac{7}{16}})\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
. Applying
[
46
, Lemma 1.3]
, the incompressibility condition and the fact
T_{K}\in(0,1)
, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\big[\bm{u}^{k}(s)\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}(s)+\chi\mathrm{div}(\sigma^{k}(s)\nabla\psi^{k}(s))\big]\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\frac{7}{16}}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathrm{div}\big(\bm{u}^{k}(s)\sigma^{k}(s)+\chi\sigma^{k}(s)\nabla\psi^{k}(s)\big)\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\frac{15}{16}}\|\bm{u}^{k}(s)\sigma^{k}(s)+\chi\sigma^{k}(s)\nabla\psi^{k}(s)\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\max\{1,\,|\chi|\}T_{K}^{\frac{1}{16}}\sup_{s\in[0,T_{K}]}\big(\|\bm{u}^{k}(s)\|_{\bm{L}^{4}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\psi^{k}(s)\|_{\bm{L}^{4}(\Omega)}\big)\|\sigma^{k}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{k}\widetilde{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(K+1)T_{K}^{\frac{1}{16}}.

Combining the above estimates, we infer from (
A.1
) that

\|\mathcal{G}(\sigma^{k})(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq K+(b^{*}+\kappa)(K+2)^{2}T_{K}+C_{k}\widetilde{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(K+1)T_{K}^{\frac{1}{16}},\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T_{K}].

Using the same idea, given
\sigma^{k}_{1},\,\sigma^{k}_{2}\in\Sigma_{K}
, we can estimate the difference

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}(\sigma^{k}_{1})(t)-\mathcal{G}(\sigma^{k}_{2})(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{0}^{t}\big\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\big[\beta(\psi^{k}(s))(\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)-\sigma_{2}^{k}(s))\big]\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\big\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\big[\kappa(\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)-\sigma_{2}^{k}(s))(\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)+\sigma_{2}^{k}(s))\big]\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\big\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\mathrm{div}[\bm{u}^{k}(s)(\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)-\sigma_{2}^{k}(s))]\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\big\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\chi\mathrm{div}\big[(\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)-\sigma_{2}^{k}(s))\nabla\psi^{k}(s)\big]\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\leq(b^{*}+\kappa^{*})(2K+3)T_{K}\sup_{s\in[0,T_{K}]}\|\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)-\sigma_{2}^{k}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{k}\widetilde{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}T_{K}^{\frac{1}{16}}\sup_{s\in[0,T_{K}]}\|\sigma_{1}^{k}(s)-\sigma_{2}^{k}(s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)},\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T_{K}].

Hence, taking
T_{K}\in(0,1)
sufficiently small, we find that
\mathcal{G}
maps
\Sigma_{K}
into itself and is indeed a contraction on
\Sigma_{K}
. Applying the contraction mapping principle, we obtain a unique
\sigma^{k}\in\Sigma_{K}
such that

\mathcal{G}(\sigma^{k})=\sigma^{k}.

Since
\bm{u}^{k}
,
\psi^{k}
are sufficiently regular, according to standard bootstrap

\sigma^{k}
is a classical solution to problem (
3.16

3.17

[0,T_{K}]

such that problem (
3.16
)–(
3.17
) admits a classical solution
\sigma^{k}
on

T_{\max}<T
, then
\|\sigma^{k}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\to+\infty

.

Since
\sigma_{0,n}\geq 0
, the weak maximum principle for parabolic equations (or the Stampacchia truncation argument) easily yields that
\sigma^{k}(x,t)\geq 0

\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T_{\max})

\sigma^{k}(x,t)>0
for
\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T_{\max})

\sigma^{k}\equiv 0

3.16

\sigma^{k}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2}+\|\nabla\sigma^{k}\|^{2}=\chi\int_{\Omega}\sigma^{k}\nabla\psi^{k}\cdot\nabla\sigma^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\big[\beta(\psi^{k})(\sigma^{k})^{2}-\kappa(\sigma^{k})^{3}\big]\,\mathrm{d}x.

From (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\nabla\sigma^{k}\|^{2}+\|\Delta\sigma^{k}\|^{2}

Then it follows from Gronwall’s lemma and (

\displaystyle\|\sigma^{k}(t)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

where

) can be reduced to a system with
2k
nonlinear ordinary differential equations for the time-dependent coefficients
\{a_{i}^{k}\}_{i=1}^{k}
(by taking
\bm{\zeta}=\bm{y}_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,k
) and
\{b_{i}^{k}\}_{i=1}^{k}
(by taking
w=z_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,k
). From the assumptions (H2)–(H4), the construction of
\Psi_{\epsilon}
and the regularity property of
\sigma^{k}
, we can apply the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem for nonlinear ODE systems to conclude the existence and uniqueness of local solutions
\{a_{i}^{k}\}_{i=1}^{k}
,
\{b_{i}^{k}\}_{i=1}^{k}\subset C^{1}([0,T_{k}])
, which satisfy the above mentioned ODE system on a certain time interval
[0,T_{k}]\subset[0,T]
.
Therefore, we obtain a unique local solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k},\mu^{k})
to problem (
3.23
)–(
3.27
) that satisfies

\bm{v}^{k}\in C^{1}([0,T_{k}];\bm{Y}_{k}),\quad\varphi^{k}\in C^{1}([0,T_{k}];Z_{k}),\quad\mu^{k}\in C([0,T_{k}];Z_{k}).

We now show that the existence time
T_{k}
can be extended to
T
.
Testing (
3.23
) by
\bm{v}^{k}
, using the incompressibility condition and integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|\bm{v}^{k}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}2\eta(\varphi^{k})|D\bm{v}^{k}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\big(\mu^{k}\nabla\varphi^{k},\bm{v}^{k}\big)+\big(\chi\sigma^{k}\nabla\varphi^{k},\bm{v}^{k}\big),

(A.9)

for any
t\in(0,T_{k})
.
By Hölder’s and Poincaré’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\big(\chi\sigma^{k}\nabla\varphi^{k},\bm{v}^{k}\big)

\displaystyle\leq|\chi|\|\sigma^{k}\|\|\nabla\varphi^{k}\|_{\bm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\bm{v}^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq C\|\sigma^{k}\|\|\varphi^{k}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega)}\|\bm{v}^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\|\sigma^{k}\|\left(\|\nabla\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\|\bm{v}^{k}\|^{2}+1\right).

Testing (
3.24
) by
\mu^{k}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma^{8}}{4}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+\int_{\Omega}m(\varphi^{k})|\nabla\mu^{k}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=-\big(\bm{v}^{k}\cdot\nabla\varphi^{k},\mu^{k}\big)+\int_{\Omega}\big(-\alpha\varphi^{k}+h(\varphi^{k},\sigma^{k})\big)\mu^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x+\chi\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\sigma^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(A.10)

for any
t\in(0,T_{k})
. We note that the first terms on the right-hand side of (
A.9
) and (
A.10
) cancel each other.
Next, we infer from (
4.16
) that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big(-\alpha\varphi^{k}+h(\varphi^{k},\sigma^{k})\big)\mu^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{m_{*}}{2}\|\nabla\mu^{k}\|^{2}+C\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{2}+\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})\right)\mathrm{d}x+C\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2}+C.

(A.11)

Concerning the last term on the right-hand side of (
A.10
), we take

\xi=\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}
in (
3.24
) and apply (H3), (H4) obtaining

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(\bm{v}^{k}\varphi^{k},\nabla\partial_{t}\varphi^{k})-\big(m(\varphi^{k})\nabla\mu^{k},\nabla\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\big)+\big(-\alpha\varphi^{k}+h(\varphi^{k},\sigma^{k}),\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{v}^{k}\|_{\bm{L}^{6}(\Omega)}\|\varphi^{k}\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|+m^{*}\|\nabla\mu^{k}\|\|\nabla\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|+\big(\alpha\|\varphi^{k}\|+h^{*}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}(\|\bm{v}^{k}\|\|\varphi^{k}\|+\|\nabla\mu^{k}\|+\|\varphi^{k}\|+1)\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|,

which yields

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|\leq C_{k}(\|\bm{v}^{k}\|\|\varphi^{k}\|+\|\nabla\mu^{k}\|+\|\varphi^{k}\|+1).

Then, by Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\chi\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\sigma^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq|\chi|\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}\|\|\sigma^{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m_{*}}{4}\|\nabla\mu^{k}\|^{2}+C_{k}(1+\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2})+C_{k}(1+\|\bm{v}^{k}\|)\|\varphi^{k}\|\|\sigma^{k}\|.

(A.12)

Adding (
A.9
) with (
A.10
), we infer from (
A.11
), (
A.12
), (
4.1
) and the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{v}^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{\gamma^{8}}{4}|\nabla\varphi^{k}|^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\eta_{*}\int_{\Omega}|D\bm{v}^{k}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{m_{*}}{4}\|\nabla\mu^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{1,k}(1+\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{v}^{k}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)+C_{1,k}(1+\|\sigma^{k}\|^{2}).

(A.13)

Moreover, in light of (
3.8
) (cf. (
4.3
)), the initial value of the energy can be controlled as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}|\bm{P}_{\bm{Y}_{k}}\bm{v}_{0}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}|^{2}+\Psi_{\epsilon}(\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma})+\frac{\gamma^{8}}{4}|\nabla\bm{P}_{Z_{k}}\varphi_{0,\gamma}|^{4}\right]\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad\leq C\Big(\|\bm{v}_{0}\|,\|\varphi_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)},\max_{r\in[-1,1]}|\Psi_{0}(r)|,\Omega\Big)=:C_{0}.

(A.14)

Exploiting the convexity of
\Psi_{0,\epsilon}
, we have

\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\varphi^{k}\|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\Psi_{\epsilon}(\varphi^{k})\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{1}{2}\|\varphi^{k}\|^{2}-C_{0,k},

(A.15)

for some
C_{0,k}\geq 0
.
Hence, applying Gronwall’s lemma to (
A.13
), we deduce from (
A.15
) that

\displaystyle\|\bm{v}^{k}(t)\|^{2}+\|\varphi^{k}(t)\|^{2}\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{N_{1}(t)}\big(C_{0}+N_{1}(t)\big)+2C_{0,k}=:N_{2}(t),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T_{k}],

(A.16)

with

\displaystyle N_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=tC_{1,k}\Big(1+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\sigma^{k}(t)\|^{2}\Big).

Thanks to (
A.16
), a further integration in time of (
A.13
) gives

\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla\mu^{k}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{0}+C_{0,k}+N_{1}(t)N_{2}(t)+N_{1}(t)=:N_{3}(t),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T_{k}].

Based on the above estimates and (
A.4
),
we can extend the unique local solution
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k})
to the whole interval
[0,T]
,
with the same estimate as (
A.16
).
The boundedness of
(\bm{v}^{k},\varphi^{k})
in
H^{1}(0,T;\bm{Y}_{k})\times H^{1}(0,T;Z_{k})

can be easily derived from (
3.23
) and (
3.24
) by using the energy method.
Indeed, it is easily seen that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}\bm{v}^{k}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|\bm{v}^{k}(s)\|^{4}+\|\bm{v}^{k}(s)\|^{2}+\|\nabla\mu^{k}(s)\|^{2}\|\varphi^{k}(s)\|^{2}+\|\sigma^{k}(s)\|^{2}\|\varphi^{k}(s)\|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\left(TN^{2}_{2}(T)+TN_{2}(T)+N_{3}(T)N_{2}(T)+TN_{2}(T)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\sigma^{k}(t)\|^{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}\varphi^{k}(s)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|\bm{v}^{k}(s)\|^{2}\|\varphi^{k}(s)\|^{2}+\|\nabla\mu^{k}(s)\|^{2}+\|\varphi^{k}(s)\|^{2}+C\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\big(TN^{2}_{2}(T)+N_{3}(T)+TN_{2}(T)+CT\big).

The proof of Lemma
3.2
is complete.

\square
