Insights into the proportion of consultations for musculoskeletal disorders in Dutch general practice: a register-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Patrick Krastman, Kimberley Heijneman, Angeline Bosman, Marcel de Wilde, Johan van der Lei, Jos Runhaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral practice care is facing increasing pressure due to a sustained rise in healthcare utilization, partly driven by population ageing and the growing prevalence of chronic conditions, such as musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs). Existing evidence reports wide variation in the proportion of GP consultations related to MSDs, while data specific to Dutch primary care remain scarce.The purpose of the study is to estimate the proportion of consultations for MSDs in Dutch general practice, stratified by age categories, sex, and anatomical region.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was performed using data from the Integrated Primary Care Information database, comprising routinely collected electronic health records of over 2.5 million patients. The study included individuals of all ages between 2017 and 2023. The proportion of consultations attributed to each International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC) chapter was determined, with MSDs identified using ICPC L chapter codes.ResultsAcross the study period, annual consultations ranged from 7.5 to 9.3 million. MSDs accounted for 11.3–12.6% of all consultations, representing the leading reason for consultation in Dutch general practice. Lower limb disorders were most frequent (31.0 –33.1%), followed by upper limb (17,9 – 20,3%) and back (21,3 – 22,9%) disorders. Females accounted for approximately 60% of MSD consultations, with the highest proportions observed among individuals aged 55–64 years (18.6–19.0%) and ≥75 years (17.6–20.3%).ConclusionsMSDs is the overall leading contributor to consultation volume in Dutch general practice, particularly among females and older adults. As population ageing continues, MSD-related consultations are likely to increase further, underscoring the need for efficient workforce planning and sustainable models of GP care delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03390-7.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Consultation, General practice

Background

With the aging population and increased life expectancy, people become more susceptible to health problems, particularly musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) [1]. Patients with MSDs experience a loss of mobility, independence, and increased mortality rates [2]. MSDs are the fourth leading contributor to disease burden in older adults, following cardiovascular diseases, malignant neoplasms, and chronic respiratory diseases [3]. In this context, it is essential not only to consider the older population or mortality, but also to evaluate the extent and severity with which individuals experience disease or disability, in order to accurately monitor trends in the burden of disease [4]. According to a 2022 report by the Dutch institute NIVEL, which details the number of disease episodes across all age groups in general practice, MSDs are the second most common contributing factor, following skin disorders [5].

There are various types of MSDs, including overuse injuries, osteoarthritis, fractures, ligament injuries, joint inflammations, and congenital abnormalities. In terms of disability, low back pain has been the leading MSD and the primary contributor to non-fatal health loss for nearly three decades [4]. Correspondingly, low back pain–related MSDs represent the most prevalent conditions in general practice [5]. In the Netherlands, individuals with musculoskeletal complaints typically first consult their general practitioner (GP). Given the wide variety of MSDs presented to GPs, it is crucial that they possess adequate knowledge and skills to assist patients with these complaints. Several studies have questioned whether GPs have the expertise to manage these patients effectively, partly due to difficulties in applying clinical practice guidelines [6]. Reported concerns include overuse of radiological imaging [7, 8], limited diagnostic and therapeutic skills [9, 10], perceived inability to implement guideline-recommended interventions [10], inadequate patient education and advice [11], and a tendency to refer patients to unnecessarily costly secondary care [7, 9].

Current international research on musculoskeletal consultations (MSCs) in general practice care primarily relies on paper and electronic patient records, with limited utilization of centralized databases [12–15]. Moreover, the studies differ in terms of the research population’s structure (age and size), wide variation in the proportion of GP consultations related to MSDs, and the duration of the study periods [12–15]. A comprehensive, long-term study using a national centralized database can provide more reliable estimates of the burden of MSCs, offering greater representativeness, consistent data standards, and increased statistical power compared with regional databases. This is particularly relevant in the Netherlands, where GPs act as mandatory gatekeepers, regulating access to secondary care, including referrals for MSDs. In the Netherlands, the Integrated Primary Care Information (IPCI) database serves as a longitudinal observational resource comprising routinely collected data from computer-based patient records—covering over 2.5 million individuals—maintained by a selected cohort of Dutch GPs [16].

The primary aim of the current study was to gain insight into the proportion of consultations for MSDs in Dutch general practice, using the IPCI data base. In addition to this, we aimed to determine which MSDs were most commonly in general practice, as well as the specific anatomical region, age groups, and sexes in which these MSDs are most prevalent.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted without prior registration of a formal protocol. The study is reported in accordance with the RECORD (REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data) statement [17]. A completed RECORD checklist is provided as Additional file 1. A retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Integrated Primary Care Information (IPCI) database. The IPCI database contains routinely collected data from electronic health records (EHRs) of more than 2.5 million patients, making it representative of the general Dutch population [18]. Participation in this centralized database is voluntary, with general practices able to join on their own initiative. In the Netherlands, all citizens are required to register with a GP under a fee-for-service system, with GPs acting as mandatory gatekeepers who regulate access to secondary care, including referrals for MSDs [19, 20]. Relevant information gathered during consultations is documented in the patient’s EHRs by the GPs. For each consultation, the GP assigns a symptom/complaint, or diagnosis code, based on the International Classification of Primary Care-1 (ICPC-1) coding system [16]. The International Classification of Primary Care (ICPC) is a coding system developed to classify patient data and clinical activity in primary care settings [21]. It allows GPs to systematically record symptoms, diagnoses, and processes of care using standardized codes, facilitating data aggregation, research, and comparison across practices and countries. Each ICPC code consists of a letter representing a body system or chapter (e.g., “L” for musculoskeletal) and a number specifying a symptom or diagnosis within that chapter [21]. The ICPC is widely used in Europe, including the Netherlands, to support epidemiological research and healthcare planning. Other data included in the EHRs consists of demographic information, laboratory results, lifestyle factors, referral notes from healthcare professionals, hospital admissions, correspondence with secondary care, and drug prescriptions [18].

Patients were included in the database starting from the date their practice began participating in IPCI, or from the date they registered at a participating practice. Patient data was used only after a minimum of one year of registration. Data availability may have ended earlier if patients died, or deregistered.

Data set

The study population consisted of patients of all ages from the IPCI database who consulted their GP in the period from 2017 to 2023. Follow-up began once patients had been registered for at least one year, but no earlier than January 1st, 2017. Follow-up was terminated at the earliest occurrence of one of the following events: patient death, transfer to another GP, or December 31st, 2023.

A consultation with a GP was defined as a registered patient contact with a GP, where the documented data are assigned to a new or existing ICPC code. For consultations not assigned to ICPC-code, ‘care service code,’ and/or ‘integrated care code were used. ‘Care service codes’ are used in GP information systems to indicate the type and/or the duration of the consultation. An ‘integrated care code’ is used to indicate that a patient is receiving integrated care. Integrated care refers to coordinated approach to healthcare where various services and providers work together to deliver comprehensive and seamless care, especially for patients with chronic health conditions. In case multiple new or existing ICPC-codes were assigned during a single contact with a GP, these were interpreted as multiple consultations.

Over the period 2017–2023, we determined the total number of consultations and the proportion attributed to each ICPC code, categorized by chapter (broad categories of health problems based on body systems or thematic groups) and by calendar year. L Chapter codes represent musculoskeletal consultations, which were compared with those in the other chapters. The proportion for MSCs in Dutch general practice was shown as a percentage of the total number of consultations at the general practice per calendar year. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the proportion for MSCs were calculated using the normal approximation formula , where p is the observed proportion and n is the total number of consultations.

Sample

Given the current definition of a consultation, we assumed that not all actual patient-GP contacts were captured, which may lead to an underestimation of the true number of registrations. To estimate the percentage of missed contacts, we reviewed a random sample of 150 patient contacts, generated using PostgreSQL’s built-in RANDOM() function. Each selected contact involved a GP entry that contained narrative information in the free-text field but lacked an ICPC code, a ‘care service code’, or an ‘integrated care code’. For each record, we assessed whether it represented an actual consultation or not. We also reviewed whether these contacts were MSCs.

Anatomical clustering of ICPC-coded symptoms and diagnoses

Clustering conditions by body region aligns closely with clinical practice, where adjacent joints are jointly considered during the assessment of a specific joint, as the musculoskeletal system functions as a kinetic chain. Additionally, this approach increases statistical power for subgroup analyses and improves comparability across studies [22, 23]. Based on ICPC-1 codes from the L chapter and/or relevant keywords, multiple algorithms were developed, allowing MSCs to be assigned to a final ICPC code and subsequently clustered into the following categories: ‘spine’, ‘trunk’, ‘upper limb’, ‘lower limb’ and ‘undifferentiated anatomical region’ (Supplementary Table S1). The most common clusters were identified by calculating the percentage of consultations for each cluster in relation to the total number of consultations with an ICPC code from Chapter L, per calendar year. ICPC-1 codes were clustered according to general practitioners’ assessments and coding decisions, and no conditions from the ICPC chapters were excluded or reassigned to other chapters by the authors.

Sex and age determination and distribution

To present the age distribution and the percentage of males/females per calendar year, for each MSC related to an ICPC-code from the Chapter L, patient’s age and sex were determined. In addition, the different ages were clustered into different age categories (0–14, 15–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75+), to determine in which age category MSCs are most common. The ratio between the total number of consultations and MSCs per age were also determined. R Studio was used for Poisson distributions.

Results

Number of consultations and contributing conditions

The total number of consultations per year in the Dutch general practices varied from 7.5 million to 9.3 million consultations during the study period, with an average of 8.2 ± 0.6 million consultations per year. For 1.6% to 2.0% of eligible consultations, we were unable to determine the corresponding complaint chapter due to missing ICPC coding, even though these contacts were identified by a ‘care service code’ or ‘integrated care code (Supplementary Table S2). Of the 150 randomly selected patient’s contacts, seven contacts (4.7%) were ultimately considered consultations. Of these seven missed consultations, only one consultation was related to the musculoskeletal system.

Discussion

Summary

In the study period, with the exception of 2023, MSDs were the main contributor to consultations in Dutch general practice. The proportion of MSCs varied between 11.3% (95% CI = 11.3 to 11.3) and 12.6% (95% CI = 12.6 to 12.6). Lower limb disorders (31–33%) and females (60%) had the highest proportion of MSCs, while consultations for the trunk (8.2–9.2%) occurred least frequent. Present study showed that the majority of MSCs occurred in the 45–54 age category and in subsequent older age categories. Finally, this study showed that the proportion of MSCs compared with total consultations from all other ICPC chapters by age was highest around the ages of 10–15 years (13.5–12.9%) and 42–61 years (13.3–13.9%).

Comparison with existing literature

The usefulness of studies on the proportion of MSCs encountered by GPs is influenced by the age composition, size of the study population, as well as the duration of the study periods. In general, consultation rates may be influenced by whether patients with specific complaints (e.g. low back or neck pain) seek care from allied health professionals. Interpretation of our findings requires consideration of the healthcare context, as the Dutch system is characterised by mandatory GP registration and a gatekeeping role for access to secondary care, while patients also have the option of consulting paramedical professionals without referral through direct access [19, 20]. This organizational context suggests that the accessibility of allied health services may influence the number of musculoskeletal consultations in general practice. To date, no systematic reviews were found with an overview of prevalence- and incidence figures of MSCs in generale practice. Current international research on musculoskeletal care in general practice primarily relies on short study periods, small sample sizes, and limited use of centralized databases [12–14].

In comparison with the findings of the NIVEL study [5], the results of the present study indicate that musculoskeletal and dermatological disorders constitute prominent contributors to the overall burden of disease. Multiple international studies conducted in general practice settings, using loco-regional databases, have reported that musculoskeletal conditions comprise a variable share of consultations, with prevalence estimates ranging from 8.7% to 25% [24–28]. It should be noted that consultation prevalence per ICPC chapter may vary considerably due to factors including geographic variation, demographic composition of the patient population, and structural differences in healthcare systems [29].

The study by Jordan et al. [24], using data from an English regional database over the course of one year (2006), found that the back was the most common individual region recorded (591 people consulting per 10,000 registered persons), followed by the knee (324/10,000). In children, the foot was the most common region. Consistent with our study, Jordan et al. utilized a centralized database. However, theirs was regional rather than national in scope. Furthermore, their study covered a one-year period, whereas our research spanned seven years.

In a Swedish study involving individuals aged 18 to 65 years who sought care at primary health care centers, 2,000 patient records were examined over a three-year period [14]. Nearly 60% presented MSDs, with upper limb (34%) and lower limb (28%) issues being the most commonly reported. These symptoms were especially prevalent among younger and middle-aged adults, and more frequently observed in women (56%). The substantial discrepancy in the proportion of consultations for MSDs (12% versus 60%) is primarily attributable to differences in the study populations, specifically the inclusion of individuals of all ages in our study, compared to working-age individuals in the study of Wiitavaara et al. [14]. During a one-week study period, Keavy et al. found that MSDs among patients aged 12 to 92 years accounted for 21% of consultations at a general practice in an urban community in England, United Kingdom [15]. The mean age of patients presenting with an MSDs was 59 years, with the lumbosacral spine (18%) and the knee (17%) being the most commonly affected anatomical regions.

Goff et al. [13] conducted a prospective, diary-based study documenting MSDs encountered by general practice registrars over a one-month period. MSDs accounted for 17% of all consultations, with back disorders representing the most frequently recorded category (29%), followed by disorders of the lower limb (27%) and upper limb (15%). Consistent with the study by Jordan et al. [24] and Wiitavaara et al. [14], the back and lower limb were identified as the most frequently recorded MSDs regions. In comparison to our study, lower limb disorders were documented more frequently than back disorders. The overrepresentation of women in consultations for MSDs is likely attributable to their higher frequency of general practice visits relative to men [30–32].

Our findings indicated that the proportion of MSCs relative to the total number of consultations was the highest, among other age groups, in the 10–15-year age category. A possible explanation is provided by Kamath et al., who described musculoskeletal injuries as a frequent reason for primary care consultations in the paediatric population, attributing their prevalence in part to the high levels of physical activity, including sports and exercise, among children and adolescents [33]. Our study showed that within the consultations for MSDs, the most consultations were for patients between 55 and 64 years and 75 + years. Since other studies included different age groups in their populations, their results regarding age distribution are not directly comparable to those of our study. Rekola et al. [34] showed that the rate of people visiting a primary healthcare doctor for musculoskeletal symptoms was highest in men aged 45–54 years and in women aged 55–64 years. Lutz et al. [35] showed that the median age of the global population would be 37.3 years in 2050 and 45.6 years in 2100. Since the proportion of MSD mainly increases with age, it can be expected that there will be more consultations for MSD, as the life expectancy of the world population increases.

With the aging population and increased life expectancy, people become more susceptible to health problems, particularly MSDs [1]. Jacobse et al. reported a relatively high proportion of diagnostic errors related to MSDs, which may be partially explained by the high prevalence of MSCs in general practice and insights from studies on professional error [36, 37]. These demographic changes, diagnostic errors, and the associated increase in MSCs emphasize the need for efficient deployment of healthcare professionals and rethinking care models to ensure accessibility and quality of care. Based on these findings, it can be argued that adequate attention to the teaching of MSDs is essential during medical school, general practice training, and ongoing postgraduate education for GPs. To ensure and optimize the delivery of care for patients with MSDs, effective collaboration is essential among GPs (trainee), GPwSI in musculoskeletal system, allied health professionals, and hospital-based specialists.

Strengths and limitations

This study has both strengths and limitations. To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective cohort study measuring proportions of MSCs, that used the data of more than 2.5 million patients who were representative of the general Dutch population. The use of centralized registration data can be seen as a strength of this study, as almost all Dutch citizens are registered with a general practice. The majority of patients with MSDs first report to and are assessed by their GP (gatekeeper), since going directly to an emergency department for MSDs is discouraged. Another strength of this study was the long-term study period. This allowed us to compare the results across the different years and gives us the opportunity to provide more reliable estimations of the burden of MSDs.

A limitation of current study is that it only included information registered by the GP in the EHRs. It is plausible that patients consulted their GP without the consultation being recorded by the GPs. We assume that not registered consultations are evenly distributed over MSCs and the total number of consultations. Another limitation of present study is that the results of this study depend on the accuracy of the registration in the information systems by the GP, where the documented data were assigned to a new or existing ICPC code, ‘care service code,’ and/or ‘integrated care code [38, 39]. This may have led to certain consultations not being adequately documented by the GP. Given our definition of a consultation, it was assumed that not all actual patient-GP contacts were recorded, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true number of registrations. To address both limitation within time constraints, we reviewed a small sample that included narrative data from the free-text fields but lacked an ICPC code, ‘care service code’, or ‘integrated care code’ and determined whether it was a (musculoskeletal related) consultation. These consultations were included in the total number of consultations, but we were unable to determine to which chapter the complaints belonged. As a result, the percentage of MSCs reported in this study may have been lower than it is in the real world. The clustering by body region remains, to date, an arbitrary process, for which no clear international consensus has yet been reached, which limit comparison with existing literature.

Conclusions and implications of this study

MSDs accounted for a substantial number of consultations in (Dutch) general practice and most frequently involve consultations for the lower limb and spine. Females accounted for more MSCs, and the highest proportion of consultations involved people in the older age categories. With population aging and increased life expectancy, MSCs may constitute a larger proportion of total consultations in (Dutch) general practice.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the IPCI scientific and ethical advisory board (nr 9/2024). GPs whose data are linked to IPCI are required to inform their patients about the use of their de-identified data for medical research. Patients can object to the use of their data and if this is the case, the data of these patients are not included in the research datasets.
