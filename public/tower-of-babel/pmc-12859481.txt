Real-time 4D MRI reconstruction using DVR-NeMF, a framework for dynamic volumetric reconstruction
Authors: Ruoxi Wang, Sijie Zhong, Jincheng Li, Weifeng Zhang, Shunwen Zheng, Ziyong Hao, Shuyu Liu, Xin Fang, Rushi Jiao, Yizhe Yuan, Bingsen Xue, Ning Ding, Yanfeng Wang, Ya Zhang, Hongjiang Wei, Zhiyong Zhang, Cheng Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn this study, four dynamic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences were first developed to collect data. On this basis, we presented DVR-NeMF, a neural magnetic field framework that enables real-time, high-dimensional (4D) MRI reconstruction from synchronized dynamic 2D image slices and physiological signals. By embedding spatiotemporal priors into an implicit representation of the imaging space, DVR-NeMF reconstructs temporally consistent 3D volumes over time with high anatomical fidelity. Comprehensive evaluations across a cardiovascular phantom, cardiac dynamic bio-simulators, and living human hearts, including external out-of-distribution validation on the Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC) dataset, demonstrated that DVR-NeMF outperforms both autoencoder- and generative adversarial network (GAN)-based baselines in terms of reconstruction accuracy and computational efficiency. Comparative analysis with paired cardiac ultrasound data in terms of key left ventricular function parameters further confirmed its reliability. This work offers a promising paradigm for extending MRI to dynamic, high-dimensional imaging, with potential for real-time functional assessment in clinical settings.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive imaging modality that plays a crucial role in image-guided intervention due to its excellent soft tissue contrast and absence of ionizing radiation.1,2,3 Dynamic MRI provides critical temporal information on organ motion and physiological function, making it indispensable in areas such as cardiac imaging, where both anatomical structure and motion characteristics are essential for accurate observation.4,5,6,7 However, despite its potential, MRI is often limited by slow acquisition speed and restricted spatiotemporal resolution, which makes it challenging to capture high-dimensional, time-resolved MRI images at real-time level.8,9,10,11 This limitation is particularly evident in the imaging of periodic dynamic structures, such as the beating heart with respiratory motion, where capturing volumetric changes over time (i.e., 4D imaging, 3D volumes + time) is essential.12

Many approaches have been investigated enhancing the imaging speed of MRI, including techniques such as undersampling k-space, compressed sensing, and motion correction.13,14,15 While these methods accelerate image acquisition and improve quality, they are typically limited to 2D imaging and fail to capture the full temporal and volumetric details required for dynamic clinical applications. To achieve high temporal fidelity, physiological signals, such as cardiac and respiratory signals, are frequently employed to guide image reconstruction.16,17,18,19 Deep learning-based methods like autoencoders (AEs)20 and generative adversarial networks (GANs)21 have also been explored to offer improved spatial resolution and faster reconstruction.22,23,24,25,26,27,28,29,30 However, these methods still rely on offline processing, incur high computational costs, or exhibit limited performance in fine-grained reconstruction, rendering them unsuitable for real-time clinical applications.21,31,32,33,34,35,36,37

To address these challenges, we propose dynamic volumetric reconstruction neural magnetic field, DVR-NeMF, a deep learning-based framework for real-time 4D MRI reconstruction in dynamic imaging. The method is specifically designed to handle steady-state dynamic physiological processes, such as cardiac cycles, by leveraging real-time dynamic 2D slices and synchronized physiological signals to reconstruct temporally consistent 4D MRI volumes. To achieve the development of DVR-NeMF, a spectrum of dynamic MRI sequences with specifically designed image reconstruction methods were first implemented to obtain training data, which also form a systematic and extensible MRI reconstruction system with DVR-NeMF, as illustrated in Figure 1. The MRI sequences include real-time dynamic 2D MRI (speed +++, quality +), retrospective dynamic 2D MRI (speed ++, quality ++), retrospective dynamic 2D MRI with physiological signal correction (speed +, quality +++), and retrospective dynamic 3D MRI with physiological signal correction. The symbol “+” represents the strength of the corresponding property. Then we developed DVR-NeMF, which simulates the imaging principles of MRI for implicit 4D modeling of the scan space, thus enabling real-time dynamic 3D MRI reconstruction. Figure 2 illustrates the detailed architecture of the development of MRI sequences and DVR-NeMF. This method incorporates subject-specific prior knowledge as a geometric constraint for fine-grained target modeling. Specifically, the 2D slices (spatial input) and physiological signals (temporal input) are embedded as conditions within the 4D neural magnetic field, with spatial and temporal encoders discerning the correlation between the conditions and the modeled 4D scene. A decoder then integrates these spatiotemporal features and propagates them into a field rendering module for image reconstruction. To evaluate the robustness, accuracy, and adaptability of DVR-NeMF, a multi-stage experimental strategy spanning three types of studies was conducted: a cardiovascular phantom study, dynamic cardiac bio-simulators39 study, and in vivo human hearts study, as illustrated in Figure 1C. The first study intended to validate the spatial reconstruction performance of the model for small targets under foreground-background imbalance conditions. The second study focused on the model’s ability to reconstruct the intricate details of dynamic biological tissues, capturing nuances such as soft tissue contrast and minute motions. The third study employed human subject data to validate the model’s capacity in the spatiotemporal reconstruction of actual cardiac structures. The reconstructed outputs were further leveraged for cardiac functional parameter assessment, juxtaposing these results with those derived from corresponding ultrasound (US) data to substantiate the model’s reliability and clinical utility. Complementary to these, additional validation was performed using the external public dataset Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC),40 thereby reinforcing the robustness of DVR-NeMF. To comprehensively demonstrate the advantages of DVR-NeMF, we conducted quantitative and qualitative comparisons with two representative baseline methods, the AE-based20 and GAN-based21 volumetric reconstruction methods, across all experimental studies. Given the scarcity of publicly available datasets and code in this research field, all private data and code used in this study will be fully disclosed to support and accelerate progress in related research.

Results

This section presents the key results in the following order: (1) spatial performance evaluation, (2) temporal performance evaluation, (3) performance evaluation on out-of-distribution (OOD) data, and (4) ablation study.

Spatial performance evaluation of DVR-NeMF

Figure 3A demonstrates that DVR-NeMF achieves high-fidelity reconstructions on cardiovascular phantom data, accurately capturing contrast differences between foreground and background, clearly delineating thin coronary-like vessels, and even visualizing intraluminal guidewires. In contrast, the GAN-based model only approximates the coarse shape of the aorta, while the AE-based model fails to generate meaningful anatomical detail. Quantitatively, as shown in Figure 4A, both the teacher and student models of DVR-NeMF achieved superior metrics, with mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.0015 and 0.0018, peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of 47.1740 and 45.5295, and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) of 0.9938 and 0.9906, respectively. These values substantially exceed those of the AE model (MAE 0.0281, PSNR 21.8878, and SSIM 0.8349) and GAN model (MAE 0.0215, PSNR 25.0230, and SSIM 0.7618). On the dynamic cardiac bio-simulator data, DVR-NeMF again exhibits superior spatial fidelity (Figures 3B and 4B). The method precisely reconstructs complex tissue boundaries in the ex vivo porcine heart, capturing anatomical detail and subtle passive motion, which are either oversmoothed or completely missed by the AE and GAN models. The teacher DVR-NeMF achieves an average MAE of 0.0159, PSNR of 33.1435, and SSIM of 0.8508, slightly outperforming the student model (MAE 0.0172, PSNR 32.3210, and SSIM 0.8209) and markedly outperforming the AE (MAE 0.0375, PSNR 24.6701, and SSIM 0.6626) and GAN (MAE 0.0452, PSNR 23.7605, and SSIM 0.5896) baselines. For a target volume size of 128 × 128×128, reconstruction time is reduced from 1.62 s (teacher) to 0.39 s (student), while the AE and GAN models take 0.08 and 0.39 s, respectively.

Figures 3C and 4C further demonstrate DVR-NeMF’s applicability in clinical human cardiac imaging. It reconstructs clear and anatomically faithful representations of the heart, including myocardium, ventricles, fat, and blood vessels. In contrast, AE and GAN results appear blurred with poor contrast and indistinct structural boundaries. Notably, DVR-NeMF successfully captures myocardial motion over time, a feature absent from AE results and only faintly present in those from the GAN model. Despite variability in human anatomy and imaging conditions, both the teacher and student DVR-NeMF models maintain strong and stable performance, with average MAE of 0.0122 and 0.0172, PSNR of 33.5491 and 31.0731, and SSIM of 0.9522 and 0.8710, respectively. The student model also demonstrates smaller variance across test cases compared with the teacher model. In contrast, the GAN model shows unstable performance, with MAE ranging from 0.1375 to 0.0234, PSNR from 15.5694 to 28.8804, and SSIM from 0 to 0.7689. For a volume size of 64 × 64×64, the student model reconstructs a 3D frame in just 0.04 s, significantly faster than the teacher model (0.17 s per volume).

Temporal performance evaluation of DVR-NeMF

Building on the spatial reconstruction strengths of DVR-NeMF, this section explores its temporal modeling capabilities. The evaluation is conducted on the in vivo human heart dataset containing paired cardiac MRI and US images, enabling cross-modality comparison across the full cardiac cycle. Given that DVR-NeMF is designed to reconstruct temporally resolved 3D volumes from highly undersampled or implicit observations, this experiment assesses its ability to preserve both temporal coherence and physiological motion.

Figure 5 illustrates DVR-NeMF’s temporal performance through comparison with US imaging, regarded as the clinical gold standard for real-time cardiac function evaluation. Four left ventricular (LV) functional parameters, end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), ejection fraction (EF), and global longitudinal strain (GLS), were extracted from both DVR-NeMF-reconstructed images and measured US images for each subject. In Figure 5A, bar graphs represent MRI-derived measurements, overlaid with line plots denoting corresponding US values. The curves from both MRI and US modalities closely match, demonstrating synchronized motion trajectories and consistent phase transitions. Visualizations of reconstructed four-chamber and two-chamber views at end-diastole and end-systole reveal DVR-NeMF’s ability to preserve structural consistency throughout the cardiac cycle. Case 1 and case 2 (23- and 29-year-old healthy males) exhibit vigorous LV motion, with case 1 displaying a greater contraction amplitude. In contrast, case 3 (a 55-year-old healthy female) and case 4 (a 55-year-old female with mild arrhythmia) demonstrate reduced motion patterns and diminished systolic function. Notably, while most cases demonstrated strong agreement between MR- and US-derived measurements, Case 4 revealed discrepancies on LVSD, suggesting that the myocardial contrast in DVR-NeMF-reconstructed MR images could be further refined in specific scenarios. Performance evaluation on OOD data

Although DVR-NeMF is inherently designed as a subject-specific reconstruction framework, requiring model training tailored to each individual subject and thereby precluding conventional cross-subject generalization tests, we still evaluate its robustness under partially OOD conditions. Specifically, we employ the ACDC dataset as a surrogate OOD benchmark, since its data structure, composed of 2D multi-slice acquisitions rather than conventional 3D volumes, deviates from the DVR-NeMF design assumptions. The results on ACDC data, as shown in Figures 3D and 4D, confirm DVR-NeMF’s fine-grained spatial modeling capability, even when applied to multi-slice data. DVR-NeMF accurately reconstructs complex tissue structures and restores tissue contrast, whereas AE and GAN fail to replicate the original high signal-to-noise ratio images and detailed targets. However, DVR-NeMF’s temporal reconstruction performance on multi-slice data exhibits some degradation due to the absence of strict spatial constraints, compared to the results on 3D CINE MRI data.

However, this simplification resulted in a moderate decrease in image reconstruction quality (MAE: 0.01 to 0.02, PSNR: 33.55 to 31.07, and SSIM: 0.95 to 0.87).

The study further explored the reconstruction performance of DVR-NeMF, AE-based method, and GAN-based method across subjects with varying heart rates (HRs). The metrics of AE-based method and GAN-based method are significantly lower than that of DVR-NeMF.

Discussion

To facilitate the use of MRI in real-time clinical applications, we have developed an advanced MRI reconstruction method grounded in conventional MRI technology. DVR-NeMF is a real-time 4D magnetic resonance volumetric reconstruction approach, leveraging the capabilities of deep learning. Initially, we employed a radial sampling technique coupled with a sliding window reconstruction strategy to devise a real-time 2D MRI sequence capable of achieving a reconstruction rate of 20 frames per second (with image size of 256 × 256). However, this approach to data reuse inadvertently leads to varying degrees of motion blurring. The more frequent the reconstruction and the greater the volume of data utilized, the more pronounced the motion blurring becomes. In scenarios demanding precise temporal resolution, such as cardiac reconstruction, this blurring can significantly impair the clinical value of the images. To address this, we capture both k-space and ECG signals from subjects to conduct dynamic 2D image reconstruction via post-processing, which incurs a delay of several minutes. Although this method forgoes the immediacy of real-time imaging, it effectively mitigates motion blurring and yields high-fidelity dynamic 2D images. Building on this, we expand the sampling domain and concurrently collect ECG and respiratory signals to serve as phase separation criteria in post-processing, facilitating the acquisition of superior dynamic 3D images through iterative reconstruction. Finally, the dynamic 3D MRI images of cardiovascular phantom, cardiac bio-simulator, and heart of living human were collected and used to develop DVR-NeMF, which could transform dynamic 2D images into dynamic 3D representations with exceptionally low latency. In all the three experimental settings, DVR-NeMF consistently outperformed baseline models based on AE and GAN, particularly in scenarios characterized by severe foreground-background imbalance. Its fine-grained spatial modeling enables DVR-NeMF to accurately capture anatomical distributions and reconstruct detailed structural contrasts, yielding high-fidelity results across varying target complexities. Results from the ACDC dataset underscore the model’s dependence on rigorous spatial constraints to ensure accurate spatiotemporal modeling. In summary, the series of methodically designed experiments comprehensively demonstrates the robustness, accuracy, and clinical utility of DVR-NeMF.

From an architectural perspective, the compared AE model20 processes single or multiple 2D images to extract spatiotemporal features within the 2D domain before dimensionality expansion and subsequent feature aggregation and reconstruction in 3D space. Similarly, the compared GAN model,21 accepting single or multiple 2D images, dedicates each view to a feature extraction pathway in 2D space, followed by feature interaction and aggregation during the up-sampling phase, culminating in image reconstruction. These methods first extract spatial features from individual or multiple 2D images, then perform dimensionality expansion and feature aggregation through deconvolution layers to synthesize 3D volumes. However, such deconvolution operations are known to induce checkerboard artifacts, resulting from uneven kernel-stride alignment during transposed convolution. As shown in Figure S3, these artifacts were especially pronounced in 3D reconstructions, leading to blurred boundaries and distorted anatomical continuity. Moreover, both AE and GAN approaches compute loss over the entire volumetric space, without spatially adaptive or fine-grained supervision. This global supervision strategy can result in suboptimal reconstructions, especially when the foreground object occupies a small portion of the overall volume, frequently falling into local minimum and failing to recover fine structural details. In contrast, DVR-NeMF introduces a neural magnetic field, inspired by Neural Radiance Fields35 but tailored for the MRI reconstruction domain. Instead of performing deconvolution for spatial expansion, DVR-NeMF learns a continuous signal field representation via a neural field rendering function. This function implicitly models the spatial signal distribution over a high-dimensional latent space, which serves as a proxy for k-space, enabling physically meaningful simulation of the Fourier transform process. The rendering function aggregates both local and global contextual signals and maps them back to the image domain, thereby facilitating accurate volumetric reconstruction without introducing checkerboard artifacts. By directly modeling the reconstruction process over the continuous spatial domain, DVR-NeMF enables voxel-level precision and preserves fine anatomical structures. Moreover, its design allows it to flexibly incorporate temporal information (e.g., ECG signals) into the reconstruction process in a physically coherent way. This principled modeling, grounded in the physics of MRI acquisition, enhances both the realism and interpretability of the reconstructed outputs.

In dynamic scenes, we assume that object motion is expected to be either predictable or to follow discernible patterns in this study. In this research, we have leveraged ECG signals as primary temporal guides for cardiac motion, which informs the dynamic reconstruction of the in vivo human heart by DVR-NeMF, ensuring that the temporal evolution of the scene is accurately captured and rendered. Specifically, in traditional cardiac MRI, the ECG signal is typically used for gating or retrospective phase sorting, serving to coordinate the timing of data acquisition to specific cardiac phases. This usage ensures temporal consistency across images but does not directly influence the image reconstruction model. Here, DVR-NeMF leverages the ECG signal as a continuous temporal input to the neural field model, encoding the temporal progression of anatomical changes across the cardiac cycle. This allows DVR-NeMF to model the dynamic behavior of anatomy directly within the reconstruction framework, rather than relying on discrete phase alignment during acquisition.

The ablation experiments demonstrated that DVR-NeMF benefits most from the design of field rendering and positional encoding. In addition, the design of parallel training enhances the attention of DVR-NeMF to the temporal dimension. The design of multiple objective functions and orthometric inputs improves DVR-NeMF’s accuracy in modeling 3D space. Applying the Gaussian noise implantation and adaptive sampling strategy enables more stable model training. Subgroup analyses verified the applicability of the model to reconstruct targets with different motion patterns. More samples will be collected for further evaluation in the future.

Utilizing a feature-level knowledge distillation strategy, we improve the reconstruction speed of DVR-NeMF. The reconstruction speed is critical for cardiac reconstruction. A single cardiac cycle in an adult is typically 0.6 to 1.0 s, in which the duration of the QRS complex wave reflecting ventricular contraction is typically 0.06 to 0.1 s. The accurate reconstruction of the QRS signal is critical since it has been shown to be a prognostic marker in coronary artery disease.41 Capturing the nuanced motion throughout the cardiac cycle demands high temporal resolution. Our method ensures that each phase of the cardiac cycle is captured with precision, providing a comprehensive view of the heart’s dynamics. By processing real-time ECG signals with a maximum information-preserving downsampling method, our approach ensures no valuable temporal information is lost. Consequently, this facilitates the reconstruction of cardiac 4D MRI with a maximum delay of 0.04 s (64 × 64×64), aligning perfectly with the average of 20 phases per cardiac cycle. This synchronization is crucial as it allows for a significant reduction in motion artifacts, which are particularly problematic during rapid ventricular contractions, thereby preserving the integrity of the cardiac imaging and ensuring clinical utility.

Limitations of the study

There are several limitations remaining. First, the current study focuses primarily on steady-state dynamic targets, such as the beating heart, where motion patterns are relatively periodic and can be captured through auxiliary physiological signals (e.g., ECG and respiratory signals). For non-stationary dynamic targets, such as organs undergoing irregular or unpredictable motion, the absence of consistent temporal signals poses a challenge for accurate spatiotemporal reconstruction. In such scenarios, relying solely on spatial input (dynamic 2D images) may result in inaccurate reconstruction for the motion of the target, since DVR-NeMF relies on reliable temporal information to guide the reconstruction for the motions. Future work will explore methods to infer temporal information directly from spatial observations, aiming to improve the reconstruction quality for targets exhibiting irregular or non-cyclic motion.42,43 Second, DVR-NeMF is currently implemented as a subject-specific model that requires separate training for each subject. This is because the model learns subject-specific anatomical priors during training to compensate for missing spatial information in the 2D inputs. While this approach ensures high accuracy for individual cases, it limits scalability and generalization. In future work, we plan to expand our dataset across a broader range of anatomies and motion patterns and investigate strategies such as population-based pretraining, to enhance the generalization capability of DVR-NeMF. Third, in the in vivo human heart experiments, the reliability of the reconstructed results was validated by comparing cardiac functional parameters computed from the reconstructed MRI images with those obtained from matched echocardiographic measurements. While multiple investigators independently calculated the parameters from different cardiac cycles to reduce variability, the measurement process still contains an element of subjectivity. In the future, we aim to develop automated algorithms to extract cardiac function metrics, which could improve the objectivity and reproducibility of the evaluation. Finally, although we have validated the proposed framework across three different target types (phantom, bio-simulator, and in vivo human heart), additional experiments involving more subjects and diverse anatomical targets will be conducted to further assess the robustness and generalizability of DVR-NeMF.

Experimental model and study participant details

To comprehensively develop and validate the DVR-NeMF framework, we designed a multi-stage experimental strategy spanning three types of studies: (1) cardiovascular phantom study, (2) dynamic cardiac bio-simulator study, and (3) in vivo human heart study. These experiments were structured to assess the performance of DVR-NeMF under progressively more complex and physiologically realistic conditions. The phantom experiment simulates cardiovascular structures with controllable pulse dynamics, offering a controlled setting for evaluating reconstruction fidelity under foreground-background imbalance. The bio-simulator study, based on isolated porcine hearts, allows assessment of the model’s capacity to reconstruct soft-tissue motion and intricate anatomical details. The in vivo human heart study provides realistic physiological variation and serves as the basis for evaluating clinical utility through comparisons with corresponding US measurements. Detailed technical descriptions of each platform were provided in the Figure S4.

For cardiovascular phantom experiment, we collected 8 cases of dynamic 3D data, each comprising 20 phases of the cardiovascular phantom, by varying the pulsating pump’s frequency (40, 80 Hz), flow rate (50, 100 mL/min), pressure (30, 60 mmHg). Simulated ECG signals with zero-filling were generated as temporal inputs. Given that the dynamic phantom lacks motion capabilities and only a single static 3D MRI volume was available, this volume was used both for model training and as the reference for evaluating reconstruction performance. While this setup prevents a strict train–test split and limits assessment of cross-sample generalization, it offers a controlled means to study model behavior under severe foreground–background imbalance.

For dynamic cardiac bio-simulator experiment, 10 fresh porcine hearts were obtained from a licensed commercial slaughterhouse in Zhejiang, China. The hearts were collected from commercially sourced hybrid pigs used for food production (typically Duroc x Landrace x Large White crosses), all male and aged approximately 6–7 months. As these tissues were acquired postmortem from animals processed for food production, no live-animal experimentation was conducted, and therefore no specific ethical approval was required. The hearts, varying in size (10–20 cm3) and weight (2–5 kg), were artificially actuated by a pulsatile pump at frequencies of 40 and 80 Hz, yielding 20 cases of 3D + t data with 20 phases. The physiological signals for phasing were synthetically generated to match the pulsatile system’s settings, resulting in discernible but subtle heart motion within the images. A focused sub-region measuring 128 × 128×128 around the isolated heart is extracted. The dynamic 3D data from both the cardiovascular phantom and the cardiac bio-simulator encompass 20 phases, with first third of the phases data allocated for training and final quarter phase data reserved for testing.

For in vivo human heart experiment, a cohort of 20 subjects with diverse demographic profiles, including 14 males and 6 females, aged between 20 and 60 years, were enrolled to underwent dynamic 3D MRI scans in a resting state, concurrently recording synchronized physiological ECG and respiration signals. This process resulted in 20 cases of dynamic 3D data, each with 4 respiration phases and 20 cardiac phases. Complementary cardiac US images were also obtained for all subjects, capturing both ventricular long-axis and apical four-chamber views. Given that the motion of the heartbeat exhibits minimal variation across different respiratory phases, we consider each of the four respiratory phases as distinct cardiac cycles for our analysis. A sub-region surrounding the heart, sized 64 × 64×64, is isolated, with three cardiac cycles dedicated to training and one cycle for testing. The original ECG signal, captured at a frequency of 400 Hz, undergoes downsampling to 20 Hz through a method that preserves maximum information.

For external and out-of-distribution validation, we incorporated the Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC) dataset, which comprises 100 dynamic multi-slice MRI images (not typical 3D volumetric images). These multi-slice images, while not constituting traditional 3D volumes, exhibit a lack of consistent geometric scaling along the z axis. From this dataset, 20 cases were randomly selected for analysis.

Approvals from the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee were secured from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and all other collaborating entities. Informed consent was procured from all volunteers engaged in the study.

Method details

The development of the four dynamic MRI sequences

To obtain qualified training and evaluation data to develop DVR-NeMF, we have progressively refined our sequence designs to enhance image dimensionality and quality. Initially, we employed a balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence with a 2D free-breathing golden-angle radial estimation, as shown in Figure 2A. This was combined with non-uniform fast Fourier transform and sliding window techniques during continuous scanning to produce navigation-capable images with minimal latency. The parameters for this method were as follows: repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) of 3.28/1.58 ms, a field of view (FOV) of 340 mm2, a matrix size of 192 × 192, a thickness of 8 mm, a bandwidth of 930 Hz/pixel, a flip angle of 62°, and a total acquisition time of approximately 15 s, encompassing several cardiac cycles. This initial approach, while rapid, resulted in images with significant motion blur and undersampling artifacts.

Subsequent retrospective reconstruction using the iGRASP14 technique, after 3 to 5 min of data acquisition, yielded images with enhanced temporal resolution, reduced motion blur, and virtually no undersampling artifacts, as presented in Figure 2B. Concurrently, physiological signals such as electrocardiograms and respiration were recorded, facilitating the segmentation and immobilization of the subject’s movements. With identical sampling parameters and similar reconstruction times, we were able to obtain superior 2D images for motion characterization in Figure 2C.

Maintaining the acquisition of physiological signals, we progressed to a bSSFP sequence with a 3D golden-angle radial trajectory to capture 5-dimensional data (3D morphology, 1D cardiac motion phase, and 1D respiratory motion phase), as illustrated in Figure 2D. The imaging parameters for this advanced sequence included a repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) of 3.82/1.91 ms, a field of view (FOV) of 220 mm3, a matrix size of 192 × 192×192, a bandwidth of 814 Hz/pixel, a flip angle of 55°, a total of 200,000 views, and a total acquisition time of approximately 12 min. The average specific absorption rate (SAR) for these scans was 67% of the “first-level” mode limit. To ensure the consistency of the 3D results with the 2D images, we refrained from incorporating T2 preparation, fat suppression, and other techniques that could potentially improve image contrast, focusing instead on maintaining a consistent methodology across dimensions. The reconstruction results and statistical analysis of the self-developed dynamic MRI sequences were presented in Figure S5.

DVR-NeMF: Dynamic volumetric reconstruction neural magnetic field

Problem formulation

The challenge of dynamic volumetric reconstruction is to establish a rational mapping function from a temporal sequence of 2D images to a corresponding sequence of 3D volumetric images. This mapping must adhere to several critical conditions: First, the reconstruction process must incorporate subject-specific priors, acknowledging that volumetric reconstruction is inherently an ill-posed problem that necessitates substantial prior knowledge of the target for accurate reconstruction. Consequently, the process is inherently non-generalizable. Second, the input 2D slices should contain as much information as possible that can be used to guide reconstruction. Third, the target’s motion within the dynamic scene must be coherent, with an explicit indicator that captures this motion. The reconstruction of non-stationary or irregularly moving targets falls outside the scope of this study.

Formally, given a temporal sequence of 2D slice images X1,X2, …,XT, where each Xi∈RH2D×W2D corresponds to a discrete time point t∈1, …,T, and a concurrent sequence of downsampled one-dimensional (1D) ECG signals P1,P2, …,PT, with each Pi∈R, the objective is to reconstruct a sequence of volumetric 3D images Y1,Y2, …,YT, where each Yi∈RD3D×H3D×W3D. The proposed DVR-NeMF framework conceptualizes this as a mapping function ΨΘ, which accepts a 2D image Xt and an ECG signal Pt as inputs and produces the corresponding 3D image Yt at moment t. Here, Xt provides mainly spatial constraints, while Pt provides the temporal cue. This relationship is encapsulated in the following equation:(Equation 1)ΨΘ((X1,P1),(X2,P2),…,(XT,PT))=Y1,Y2,…,YT

Determine the optimal scan plane

Volumetric reconstruction of MRI images introduces an additional issue compared to that of CT images,20 i.e., which layer of the 2D slice should be employed as the input to guide the reconstruction. As mentioned earlier, the input 2D slice should be the most informative to yield the best reconstruction results, which is referred to as the Optimal Scan Plane (OSP). A plane with the minimum sum of distances to all informative vertices was determined as the optimal scan plane in shape instantiation.44,45 As for the dynamic volumetric reconstruction, the target is to recover the entire anatomy of individual tissues. There are no predefined informative vertices in volumetric reconstruction. Hence, a novel optimal scan plane determination method was proposed as shown in Figure 2E. Specifically, a self-supervised index-prediction task was designed to search the latent feature space for the determination of the optimal scan plane.

For all the 3D volumes in the training set, we shuffle the slices of each volume and record the original index of each slice. For instance, a sequentially arranged volume Vaxial = {s1,s2,s3, …,sk} is shuffled into Vaxial′={s2,sk,s1,…,s3}, and the original slice index idx = {2,k, 1, …, 3} is recorded, where Vaxial indicates that the volume is presented by axial plane. Then a neural network is constructed to take the shuffled volume Vaxial′ as input and predict its original index idx. The underlying hypothesis is that the anatomical features and the information content vary across different layers, which should manifest as a discernible pattern in the prediction outcomes. Network architecture

Figure 2F illustrates the network architecture of DVR-NeMF, which is conditioned on specific inputs to perform the dynamic volumetric reconstruction. Single or orthogonal views of 2D slices on the optimal scan plane were spatially embedded within a three-dimensional coordinate system at their respective original locations on the optimal scan plane. To ensure that the spatial information is propagated throughout the entire volume, we introduce Gaussian noise at varying levels across the coordinate system. This noise gradient is centered at the optimal scan plane, with intensity diminishing toward the periphery. The introduction of Gaussian noise not only facilitates a more robust reconstruction by mitigating overfitting but also enhances the network’s ability to generalize across different noise distributions. Each point within this coordinate system is characterized by four attributes: the spatial coordinates x, y, z, and a condition value c. The condition value c is computed as the sum of the spatial input at that point and the corresponding Gaussian noise. The output of θd was then passed to the field rendering module θfr to generate the pixel value σ of each sample point. Field rendering

We proposed an innovative volume rendering approach termed field rendering, as illustrated in Figure 2F. This method was conceptually inspired by the principles governing signal aggregation within a magnetic field, which is the foundational mechanism of MRI. In MRI, each point in k-space reflects the integrated signals from the entire spatial domain. Analogously, field rendering can be conceptualized as a function that aggregates global information at each discrete coordinate in a high-dimensional space, similar to k-space. Importantly, the signal value at any given point in this space both influences its own representation and contributes to all other points. Let K represent the high-dimensional space and s(p) denote the signal value at a point p within K. The field rendering function, F(p), at a specific point p is defined as:(Equation 6)F(p)=s(p)+∑q∈K,q≠pw(p,q)·s(q)where F(p) is the field-rendered signal value at point p. s(p) is the inherent signal value at point p. w(p,q) denotes the weighting function that quantifies the influence of the signal at point q on point p. The summation is taken over all q∈K, with the exception of p itself. This formulation encapsulates the comprehensive signal aggregation process, mirroring the k-space interactions in MRI. This dual consideration in the modeling of signal values ensures the clarity and distinctiveness of the signal at each point, thereby averting any potential signal ambiguity or confusion in the reconstructed image.

Adaptive sampling and parallel forward training

When training DVR-NeMF, repeated sampling of free space or uninteresting regions that do not contribute to the rendering can reduce the speed and effectiveness of reconstruction. The four-dimensional field will further lead to a decrease in reconstruction efficiency. To enhance training efficiency, an adaptive sampling strategy that incorporates the subject-specific manner was proposed. This approach was designed to allocate a higher sampling probability to foreground regions, thereby improving the overall performance. Parallel training was performed to improve the temporal feature retrieval and temporal reconstruction capabilities of DVR-NeMF. In the initial stage, a foreground region with the greatest differences between the two phases in parallel training was extracted, and a higher sampling probability was assigned to this region. Then in the training process, the adaptive sampling strategy worked by introducing a modulating factor to the sampling probability, which reduced the contribution of easy-to-reconstruct points and focused on hard-to-reconstruct points. The modulating factor was calculated based on the difference between the predicted results and the ground truth, with a focus on those points that tend to be mis-predicted. The adaptive sampling strategy improved the stability of the training, while also allowing for faster overall convergence.

Distillation-based real-time rendering

The distillation-based real-time rendering approach offered a significant speed advantage for DVR-NeMF over traditional NeRF, facilitated by the streamlined field rendering process. Utilizing a standard network configuration, comprising eight linear layers with a feature dimensionality of 256 per layer, the model achieves an average rendering time of 0.17 s for a 64 × 64×64 volume. While augmenting the number of layers and feature dimensions can enhance model performance, it inversely affects the inference speed. To balance speed with performance, we adopt a feature-level knowledge distillation strategy. This strategy enables a lightweight student model to rapidly infer while retaining robust performance by transferring knowledge from a sophisticated pre-trained teacher model. This process is depicted in Figure 2F.

Similar distillation-based work distills knowledge by training the student model with the pseudo data generated by the teacher model,46 which transforms the neural radiance field into the neural light field. Here we kept the design principle of the neural magnetic field and distilled the raw signal of the whole field as knowledge. Specifically, a teacher model was first trained, and the inner feature maps on each module, including spatial encoder, temporal encoder, decoder, and field render module were used as the supervision for training the student model with simpler network complexity, while the teacher model was frozen during distillation.

Statistical analysis and implementation

The evaluation of the performance of DVR-NeMF involves two aspects: image quality and image rationality. For image quality, we employed three widely used metrics to provide a comprehensive quantitative analysis. The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) quantifies the average magnitude of errors between the reconstructed images and the ground truth, offering a straightforward measure of prediction accuracy on a per-pixel basis. The Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) evaluates the quality of the reconstructed image by comparing the maximum potential power of a signal (in this case, the pixel intensity of the image) to the power of corrupting noise that affects its fidelity. The Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) is a more nuanced metric that evaluates image similarity by considering changes in texture, contrast, and luminance, which is particularly sensitive to structural information. These metrics collectively provide a robust framework for evaluating the fidelity of the reconstructed images.

The fidelity of temporal reconstruction by DVR-NeMF was validated through the precise measurement of left ventricular (LV) function parameters, which were then compared with corresponding calculations derived from US imaging to ascertain both accuracy and rationality. For each reconstructed cardiac sample, we meticulously quantified changes in cardiac volume and myocardial movement to determine key functional parameters: end-systolic volumes (ESV), end-diastolic volumes (EDV), and myocardial longitudinal projection lengths during systole (MLs) and diastole (MLd). These parameters are instrumental in calculating the ejection fraction (EF) and global longitudinal strain (GLS), pivotal indicators of cardiac function, as delineated by the following equations.

The ejection fraction (EF), a critical measure of cardiac output efficiency, is calculated as the percentage of blood volume pumped out of the ventricles with each heartbeat, relative to the total ventricular filling volume. It is computed as follows:(Equation 11)EF=(EDV−ESV)EDV×100%where EDV represents the volume of blood within the ventricle at the end of diastole (heart’s relaxation phase), and ESV denotes the volume of blood remaining in the ventricle at the end of systole (heart’s contraction phase).

The global longitudinal strain (GLS), a measure of myocardial deformation, reflects the percentage change in myocardial longitudinal length between the diastolic and systolic phases. It is indicative of the myocardial contractility and is calculated as:(Equation 12)GLS=(MLs−MLd)MLd×100%where MLs is the myocardial longitudinal projection length during systole, and MLd is the length during diastole.

For US imaging, EDV, ESV, and EF are derived using M-mode echocardiography, which captures motion over time in a single dimension, typically along the ventricular long-axis view. The longitudinal projection length of the LV myocardium is measured in the apical four-chamber cardiac view, extending from the LV apex to the midpoint of the mitral valve. To ensure the reliability of the US reference measurements, all US image acquisitions were performed by three experienced US specialists to ensure standardized and high-quality data collection. Then, for the calculation of cardiac functional parameters based on the US images, the three specialists performed measurements on at least twice per subject. This setup enabled us to assess both inter-reader and intra-reader variation. The detailed measurements were presented in Table S1. The final reference value for each parameter was obtained by averaging all the measurements across readers and cycles. Conversely, for MRI images, the volumetric data facilitated the extraction of EDV, ESV, and EF from a three-dimensional perspective. The longest longitudinal projection of the LV myocardium was identified within the volumetric data, and the myocardial longitudinal projection length was calculated from the LV apex to the mitral valve midpoint.

DVR-NeMF was implemented using PyTorch on an NVIDIA V100 GPU with 32GB of memory. In the experiments, we focused on the chest, especially the heart. Cropping localized heart areas from full-size images not only eliminated irrelevant information but also saved GPU memory usage. The saved memory can be utilized to design a more complicated network to boost its performance. We followed the same positional encoding strategy as NeRF to map the spatial input Ispatial and Itemporal from R into a higher dimensional space R2L to obtain a better representation of high-frequency variation.48 We used the Adam optimizer49 with a learning rate that begins at 5 × 10−4 and decays exponentially by 10−1 every 10000 epochs. The optimization for each case takes 20k iterations on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU.
