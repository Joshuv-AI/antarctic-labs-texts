Efficacy and safety of platelet-rich plasma injection for hemophilic chronic synovitis and arthropathy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shaoyu Yin, Yijia Lu, Qi He, Hui Bi, Zeping Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlatelet-rich plasma (PRP) has emerged as a potential therapeutic approach for hemophilia. Systematically summarize the evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of PRP therapy in hemophilia. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched. Risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) and the RoB for Non-Randomized Studies of interventions (ROBINS-I V2). Analyses were conducted separately for hemophilic synovitis and arthropathy given their distinct pathophysiology. A random-effects model was applied for meta-analysis. Evidence certainty was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE). Six studies were included: 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT) and 5 non-randomized trials. For chronic synovitis, pooled analyses demonstrated significant reductions in Visual Analogue Scale (mean difference: −3.36, − 4.42, and − 2.60 at 3, 6, and 12 months), Hemophilia Joint Health Score (mean difference: −3.00, − 4.72, and − 3.65 at 3, 6, and 12 months); however, all outcomes were rated as very low certainty. For arthropathy, the single high-quality RCT showed no clinically meaningful improvements in Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index, Hemophilia Joint Health Score, Visual Analogue Scale, or magnetic resonance imaging compared with placebo over 24 months (high certainty). Adverse events were minimal, with injection site discomfort reported in 8.4% of patients (high certainty). The evidence for PRP in hemophilic chronic synovitis is of very low certainty, and the results should be interpreted with caution. High-certainty evidence does not support the routine use of PRP in hemophilic arthropathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-07055-4.Keywords: Hemophilia, Hemophilic chronic synovitis, Hemophilic arthropathy, Platelet-rich plasma, Meta-analysis, GRADE

Introduction

Hemophilia is characterized by frequent and lifelong bleeding episodes affecting joints and muscles [1]. Recurrent hemarthrosis leads to chronic synovitis and hemophilic arthropathy, which profoundly compromise patients’ quality of life (QoL) through persistent pain, restricted range of motion, muscle atrophy, and functional impairment [2].

Existing therapeutic strategies for hemophilic synovitis and arthropathy, including analgesics, corticosteroids, non-steroidal and steroidal anti-inflammatory agents, and intra-articular hyaluronic acid (HA) administration, offer symptomatic relief of pain and inflammation but demonstrate limited effectiveness in preventing cartilage degeneration. Surgical interventions such as total joint arthroplasty remain effective for end-stage hemophilic arthropathy; however, these procedures are highly invasive and associated with considerable hemorrhagic and infectious complications [3].

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is an autologous platelet concentrate derived from peripheral blood. It is enriched with multiple bioactive molecules, including platelet-derived growth factor, insulin-like growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor, and transforming growth factor β-1, which exert pivotal regulatory effects on tissue repair and regeneration by modulating cell proliferation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis [4]. Owing to its regenerative properties, PRP has gained widespread application in the management of musculoskeletal injuries and osteoarthritis [5]. Moreover, preclinical evidence from animal models suggests that PRP may attenuate hemophilic knee arthropathy by inhibiting synovial inflammation and mitigating cartilage matrix degradation [6]. These findings suggest that PRP may represent a potential therapeutic approach for hemophilic synovitis and arthropathy.

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy and safety profile of PRP therapy in the treatment of hemophilic synovitis and arthropathy.

Methods

Registration and protocol

This systematic review follows the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for design and reporting [7]. The study protocol was prospectively registered and published in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251244223).

Study inclusion criteria

Included studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis included all peer-reviewed studies published as complete papers, encompassing randomized controlled trials (RCT), cohort studies, case-control studies, and cross-sectional studies. There were no publication date restrictions, and only English-language studies were included.

Participants

Patients with inherited hemophilia of any type, severity, and age who received PRP therapy for hemophilic synovitis and arthropathy.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was PRP therapy efficacy, assessed through pain (e.g., Visual Analogue Scale [VAS]), functional status (e.g., Hemophilia Joint Health Score [HJHS], Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index [WOMAC]), bleeding episodes (BE), and structural changes (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], ultrasound). VAS, HJHS, total WOMAC scores, and BE were evaluated at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months post-treatment. VAS measured pain on a 0–10 cm scale (0 = no pain; 10 = worst pain) [8]. HJHS evaluates six joints (bilateral elbows, knees, ankles) and gait (joint score: 0–20; gait score: 0–4; range: 0-124; higher scores = worse condition) [9]. WOMAC comprises 5 pain, 2 stiffness, and 17 function items, each scored 0–4 (range: 0-100; lower scores = better outcomes) [10]. MRI was scored using the International Prophylaxis Study Group (IPSG) system (range: 0–17; higher scores = worse pathology), assessing soft tissue (effusion/hemarthrosis, synovial hypertrophy, hemosiderin) and osteochondral components (erosion, cysts, cartilage degradation) [11]. Minimum clinically important differences (MCID) were 1.5 cm for VAS and 10 points for WOMAC [12, 13]. Synovial hyperemia was scored 0–3 based on color signal intensity [14].

Secondary outcomes included PRP therapy safety and health-related QoL. Safety was assessed through adverse events (wound infection, septic arthritis, fever, injection site pain or bleeding). QoL was measured using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36; range: 0-100; higher scores = better QoL) [15].

Information sources

Relevant studies were identified by searching four electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library) and one clinical trial registry (ClinicalTrials.gov). Additionally, we manually reviewed references of all primary studies and review articles to identify any potentially eligible studies.

Search strategy

The search strategy focused on the terms “hemophilia” and “platelet-rich plasma”. Complete search strategies for each database are presented in Supplementary Tables S1-S5.

Study selection

Two authors (Shaoyu Yin and Yijia Lu) independently screened titles and abstracts of retrieved studies, obtaining full texts when information was insufficient. Both researchers applied predetermined inclusion criteria to determine study eligibility, resolving disagreements through discussion. When consensus could not be reached, Qi He served as a third reviewer.

Data collection and management

Duplicate publications were identified and excluded by examining author names, affiliated institutions, and study content. For studies with overlapping patient cohorts, priority was given to the most recent publication, the largest sample size, or the highest methodological quality (Fig. 1).

Data items

Descriptive characteristics of included studies and participants as detailed in the Supplementary Table S6 were extracted by 2 review authors independently.

Assessment of risk of bias in studies

For assessment of risk of bias in RCTs, we used the revised Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (R0B 2) [16]. For evaluating risk of bias of non-randomized studies, we relied on the Risk of bias in non-randomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I V2) tool [17]. Assessment criteria are shown in Fig. 2.

Risk of bias. Distributional summary of risk of bias assessments per domain of included studies. The risk of bias in randomized clinical trials (1 study) and non-randomized studies (5 studies) was assessed using Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0) and Risk of bias in non-randomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I V2), respectively. The ROB 2.0 bias risk judgment criteria are as follows: if all domains are rated as “low risk”, the overall risk of bias of the study is classified as “low risk of bias”; if some domains are rated as “some concerns” and no domain is rated as “high risk”, the overall risk of bias is classified as “some concerns of bias”; if at least one domain is rated as “high risk”, the overall risk of bias of the study is classified as “high risk of bias”. The ROBINS-I V2 bias risk judgment criteria are as follows: if all domains are rated as low risk, the overall risk of bias is classified as “Low risk of bias”; if all domains are rated as low or moderate risk, the overall risk of bias is classified as “Moderate risk of bias”; if at least one domain is rated as high risk with no domain reaching critical risk, the overall risk of bias is classified as “High risk of bias”; if at least one domain is rated as critical risk, the overall risk of bias is classified as “Critical risk of bias”

Effect measures

Continuous outcomes were reported as means with SD or quartiles. Dichotomous outcomes were reported as frequencies or proportions. We measured the treatment effects on VAS, HJHS, WOMAC, BE, and imaging scores as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs).

Synthesis method

Handling missing data

For studies with missing data, we contacted corresponding authors via email to request supplementary information. However, we received no responses from the authors.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was assessed using multiple approaches. Visual evaluation of 95% CI overlap in forest plots was performed, alongside the Chi-square test (Chi²), with P ≤ 0.1 indicating significant heterogeneity. The I² statistic was used to quantify heterogeneity [18]: 0%–50% indicates no significant heterogeneity, 50%–70% moderate heterogeneity, and > 70% high heterogeneity. Clinical heterogeneity was further evaluated based on differences in study design, participant characteristics, and interventions.

Data analysis and pooled effect measures

The analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3. Hemophilic synovitis, characterized by active synovial inflammation and recurrent bleeding, represents an inflammatory response to intra-articular hemorrhage, whereas hemophilic arthropathy is the chronic end-stage consequence, manifesting as cartilage destruction, structural joint damage, and fibrosis-induced range-of-motion limitation. Given their distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and outcome measures, analyses were performed separately for the two conditions.

For hemophilic synovitis, considering variations in PRP preparation protocols, characteristics, and injection dosage, heterogeneity in true effect sizes was anticipated. Therefore, a random-effects model was employed to estimate the treatment effect.

For hemophilic arthropathy, only one RCT was identified, which represented high-quality Level 1 evidence with a low risk of bias. Two remaining non-randomized studies exhibited at least a moderate risk of bias. Incorporating these non-randomized studies into a quantitative synthesis alongside the high-quality RCT would undermine the reliability and certainty of the resulting evidence. Consequently, a qualitative analysis alone was conducted.

Sensitivity analyses and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses were performed using the leave-one-out method. As fewer than 10 studies were included, funnel plots were considered uninformative and therefore not used to assess publication bias.

Certainty assessment

We used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach to assess the quality of evidence [19].

Excluded studies are listed in Supplementary Table S7.

Study subjects

These 6 studies included 221 patients with hemophilia A and 15 patients with hemophilia B. The number of participants in each study ranged from 6 to 95. Two studies included only patients with severe hemophilia [20, 24], 3 studies included patients with moderate or severe hemophilia [21, 22, 25], and 1 study included patients with mild, moderate, or severe hemophilia [23]. Three studies enrolled only adults [23–25], and 3 studies included both adults and children [20–22]. Only 2 studies reported patients’ regular treatment regimens (both prophylactic therapy) [23, 25]. The RCT employed a placebo control [25], whereas the 2 non-randomized controlled trials used HA and PRP plus HA as controls, respectively [23, 24]. Three studies examined PRP treatment for hemophilic chronic synovitis [20–22], while three others addressed hemophilic arthropathy [23–25].

PRP treatment characteristics

Five studies reported PRP preparation protocols [21–25], and 4 studies reported platelet counts in PRP [21, 23–25]. Five studies reported a total of 184 joints treated with PRP [20–24], including 124 knees (67.4%), 33 ankles (17.9%), and 27 elbows (14.7%). Except for one study that administered PRP injections once weekly for 3 weeks [25], all other studies used a single PRP injection. Injection doses ranged from 2 to 7 mL per injection (see Table 2 for details.).

Risk of bias

For the RCT, Duan et al. 2022 was rated as low overall risk of bias [25]. Among non-randomized studies, Li et al. 2019 [23], despite lacking randomization, had moderate overall risk due to adjustment for confounding through baseline demographic comparisons. Landro et al. 2021 had critical overall risk owing to critical risk regarding missing data, as only means were reported [24]. The remaining non-randomized trials had serious risk of bias, mainly from residual confounding inherent to before-after designs and inadequate reporting of routine treatment regimens. See Fig. 2 for additional details.

Results of individual studies

Primary outcomes

Summary estimates of primary outcomes for PRP treatment of hemophilia from all included studies are shown in Table 2. All studies reported VAS, most (5/6) reported HJHS, 2 reported WOMAC, and 4 reported BE. Three studies reported MRI findings; only 1 used IPSG scoring [25], while others provided qualitative descriptions. Two studies reported joint ultrasound findings, with 1 providing only qualitative descriptions [20].

Three studies — Duan et al. (2022), Li et al. (2019), and Landro et al. (2021) — investigated PRP treatment for hemophilic arthropathy [23–25], while another three — Caviglia et al. (2024), Caviglia et al. (2017), and Teyssler et al. (2014) — focused on PRP treatment for chronic hemophilic synovitis [20–22].

In the RCT by Duan et al. (2022) [25], participants were randomly allocated to PRP or placebo groups with 24-month follow-up. HJHS and WOMAC showed no significant changes compared to placebo. While VAS decreased compared to placebo, the reduction did not reach the MCID, indicating no clinically meaningful improvement. Both soft tissue and osteochondral subscores remained unchanged at 24 months.

In Li et al.‘s (2019) study [23], patients were non-randomly assigned to PRP or HA groups with 6-month follow-up. VAS, WOMAC, and BE all improved from baseline but showed no significant differences versus controls. Ultrasound-detected hyperemia and synovial thickness showed no significant differences versus controls.

In Landro et al.‘s (2021) study [24], patients received PRP in the right knee and PRP + HA in the left knee with 6-month follow-up. VAS, HJHS, and BE all decreased from baseline in both groups, with no significant differences between groups.

In Caviglia et al.‘s (2024) study [22], HJHS, VAS, and BE (reported as a proportion) all decreased following PRP treatment compared to baseline.

In Caviglia et al.‘s (2017) study [21], HJHS, VAS, and BE all decreased following PRP treatment compared to baseline. MRI examination showed a reduction in synovial thickness.

In Teyssler et al.‘s (2014) case series [20], HJHS and VAS decreased following PRP treatment. Among 8 treated joints, MRI showed no changes in 4, improvement in joint effusion and synovial thickness in 3, and ultrasound showed no changes in 1.

Secondary outcomes

Four studies reported adverse events following PRP treatment. Two studies reported changes in SF-36. The main adverse events reported in 2 studies were pain and swelling at the injection site [23, 25], while 2 studies reported no adverse events [20, 21]. Duan et al. reported physical component summary and mental component summary scores, both showing no changes compared to placebo. Li et al. reported total SF-36 scores, which showed no changes compared to controls.

Pooled results

A random-effects model was applied to quantitatively synthesize the three studies on PRP treatment for chronic hemophilic synovitis. The MDs for VAS at 3, 6, and 12 months post-PRP treatment were − 3.36 (95% CI: -4.65 to -2.07), -4.42 (95% CI: -5.38 to -3.46), and − 2.60 (95% CI: -3.40 to -1.80), respectively. The MDs for VAS reached the MCID threshold. The MDs for HJHS at 3, 6, and 12 months were − 3.00 (95% CI: -4.45 to -1.55), -4.72 (95% CI: -6.81 to -2.63), and − 3.65 (95% CI: -4.72 to -2.58), respectively (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized 1 RCT and 5 non-randomized clinical trials [20–25]— 3 on chronic synovitis and 3 on arthropathy — providing the most comprehensive evidence to date on the potential efficacy and safety of intra-articular PRP injection in patients with hemophilia.

For hemophilic chronic synovitis, PRP treatment was associated with reductions in VAS (MD: −3.36, − 4.42, and − 2.60 at 3, 6, and 12 months) and HJHS (MD: −3.00, − 4.72, and − 3.65 at 3, 6, and 12 months) in the included studies; VAS reductions exceeded the MCID. BE was observed to decrease from baseline, and MRI suggested reduced synovial thickening with resolution or stabilization of joint effusion. Adverse events were reported in only one study. Nevertheless, the certainty of evidence for all outcomes was rated as very low.

The single high-quality RCT found no statistically significant differences between intra-articular PRP and placebo in total WOMAC score or HJHS over a 24-month follow-up. Although VAS showed a statistically significant difference, the mean difference did not reach the MCID, indicating limited clinical relevance. MRI assessments similarly revealed no significant between-group differences. Current high-quality evidence does not support the use routine of PRP in patients with hemophilic arthropathy. However, as this conclusion is derived from a single-center trial, its generalizability across broader clinical contexts remains to be established through multicenter RCTs enrolling larger and ethnically diverse populations.

Among the five non-randomized clinical studies, assessment using the ROBINS-I V2 tool revealed that four studies exhibited at least serious risk of bias, with the exception of Li et al.‘s study [23]. Three studies on chronic synovitis compared changes in VAS, HJHS, and BE from baseline only, demonstrating varying degrees of decline in these parameters during follow-up [20–22]. Li et al. reported that at 6 months post-treatment, the PRP group showed significantly lower VAS, total WOMAC, pain scores, and physical function scores compared with the HA group. Landro et al. found no statistically significant differences in VAS, HJHS, or BE between the PRP group and the combined HA plus PRP group over a 6-month follow-up period, suggesting no clear difference in outcomes between the two treatment regimens [24]. Based on these findings, all five studies reported findings in favor of PRP, but do not provide reliable evidence supporting its effectiveness in patients with hemophilic chronic synovitis or arthropathy.

These studies have several methodological limitations that may lead to substantial overestimation of PRP efficacy. First, all five studies lacked placebo controls, making it impossible to exclude the influence of placebo effects and the natural disease course. Subjective outcome measures such as VAS and HJHS are particularly susceptible to placebo effects. Second, the assessment of PRP efficacy was based solely on statistical significance without comparing the mean difference to the MCID; yet statistical significance does not necessarily translate into meaningful clinical benefit. The study populations showed considerable heterogeneity in joint damage severity and bleeding frequency, and the optimal PRP dosage and injection frequency remained unstandardized, potentially affecting treatment consistency. Additionally, these studies were generally limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up durations. Notably, four studies failed to report patients’ clotting factor replacement therapy regimens (on-demand versus prophylactic treatment), and this omission of a critical patient characteristic further undermines the reliability of PRP efficacy assessments. These limitations in study design may account for the differences in findings and conclusions between the non-randomized trials and the high-quality RCT.

Therefore, although multiple studies have supported the use of PRP for hemophilic chronic synovitis, the significant methodological limitations of the included studies resulted in very low certainty of evidence in the meta-analysis. The results should be interpreted with caution. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that PRP exerts multiple protective mechanisms in hemophilic chronic synovitis: it inhibits neutrophil extracellular trap formation, reducing direct damage to joint tissues [22]; suppresses hemoglobin oxidation and Fenton reactions, thereby decreasing reactive oxygen species generation and attenuating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [26]; and promotes macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory reparative M2 phenotypes [27]. The lack of high-quality clinical evidence, together with supportive findings from mechanistic preclinical studies, underscores the urgent need for rigorous research to clarify the clinical utility of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in hemophilic chronic synovitis. Future efforts in this field should focus on well-designed, multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that include populations from diverse geographic regions and ethnic backgrounds and employ standardized PRP preparation and administration protocols. Such studies are essential to provide robust evidence on the efficacy and safety of PRP for hemophilic chronic synovitis and to ensure the generalizability of findings across different clinical settings.

Although both osteoarthritis and hemophilic arthropathy are characterized by articular cartilage and bone destruction, their pathophysiological mechanisms differ fundamentally. The core mechanisms of osteoarthritis include chondrocyte apoptosis, autophagic dysfunction, and senescence leading to extracellular matrix imbalance; synovial hyperplasia, fibrosis, and inflammatory infiltration; and aberrant subchondral bone remodeling driven by dysregulated osteoclast and osteoblast activity [5]. In contrast, hemophilic arthropathy is driven by recurrent joint hemorrhage: hemosiderin deposition directly damages chondrocytes by dysregulating FGF23 and SOX9 expression; blood components stimulate synovial proliferation and pathological neovascularization, with fragile new vessels prone to rupture, establishing a “bleeding-inflammation-rebleeding” cycle; activated synoviocytes and immune cells release pro-inflammatory mediators and matrix metalloproteinases that degrade cartilage matrix, ultimately causing irreversible osteochondral damage [28]. The distinct etiologies and pathophysiological mechanisms underlying osteoarthritis and hemophilic arthropathy may explain why PRP therapy has demonstrated efficacy in some osteoarthritis studies but has not shown a clear therapeutic benefit in hemophilic arthropathy.

Among the three studies on hemophilic chronic synovitis, none reported QoL, and only one small-sample study reported adverse events. Injection site swelling was reported by 8.4% (8/95) of patients in Duan et al.’s high-quality study. Regarding QoL, PRP conferred no benefit compared with placebo for either the physical component summary score or mental component summary score of the SF-36.

This study has several strengths that enhance its reliability. This study was prospectively registered and conducted in accordance with the 2020 PRISMA reporting guidelines, ensuring methodological rigor and transparency. A comprehensive search across five databases, supplemented by manual reference screening, minimized the risk of missing eligible studies. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2 and ROBINS-I V2, and evidence certainty was evaluated using the GRADE framework. Analyses were conducted separately for hemophilic synovitis and hemophilic arthropathy given their distinct pathophysiological mechanisms. The inclusion of a double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT with low risk of bias and a 24-month follow-up provided high-certainty evidence for PRP in hemophilic arthropathy. Treatment effects were further interpreted against the MCID, ensuring conclusions reflected clinical rather than merely statistical significance.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Restricting inclusion to English-language publications may have introduced language bias, potentially leading to the omission of relevant evidence from non-English-speaking regions. The overall evidence base was small, comprising only six studies (236 patients), which limited statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. For hemophilic arthropathy, only one high-quality RCT was available, precluding meta-analysis and restricting conclusions to qualitative assessment. Regarding hemophilic chronic synovitis, most studies had small sample sizes, were single-center in design, and were predominantly single-arm, increasing the risk of selection bias and confounding. The lack of placebo controls made it difficult to distinguish true treatment effects from placebo responses or the natural course of the disease. Key clinical variables—including coagulation factor regimens, disease severity, and baseline joint status—were incompletely reported, limiting subgroup analyses and adjustment for confounders. Some outcomes (e.g., imaging findings) were reported qualitatively, reducing the robustness of the evidence. Finally, given the limited number of included studies, publication bias could not be reliably assessed.

Conclusion

The current evidence regarding PRP for hemophilic chronic synovitis remains of very low certainty, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should prioritize multicenter RCTs across diverse ethnic populations with standardized PRP protocols to generate more definitive evidence. Based on a single high-quality RCT, intra-articular PRP injections did not demonstrate improvement in pain, function, or QoL compared with placebo over 24 months in hemophilic arthropathy, and its use in this condition is currently not supported for routine clinical practice; nonetheless, multicenter trials are equally needed to confirm the generalizability of these findings.
