Traumatic Isolated Palsy of the Inferior Division of the Third Cranial Nerve: A Case Report
Authors: Oinam Somapika Devi, Benjamin Nongrum, Subhankar Paul
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Orbit is a small anatomical space, and so are its fissures. The superior orbital fissure (SOF) measures 10.90 ± 1.20 mm in length and 6.14 ± 0.95 mm in width [1]. It is fascinating to know that two divisions of the third cranial nerve (CN III) pass through the narrow middle portion of the SOF, but only one division got affected in the present case.

We present a case of a young man in his 30s who sustained a penetrating injury to his right eye lower lid while unloading bamboos from a truck. He came to the outpatient department after 5 days of the injury, when the pain and redness did not subside, and he developed diplopia. Upon examination of the right eye, a healed wound was noted in the mid-lower lid, 5 mm below the lid margin. The visual acuity was 20 / 20 (far) and N8 (near). The pupil measured 6 mm in size and was sluggishly reacting to light. The extraocular movements (EOM) were restricted in adduction (–4), depression (–4) (Fig. 1A), and extorsion (Supplementary Video 1). There was 10° of incyclotorsion measured by the double Maddox rod test. The right eye was in exotropic and hypertropic position in primary gaze. There was no ptosis, and abduction, elevation and intortion were normal. Anterior segment and fundus examination were within normal limits. The right eye corneal and eyelid skin sensations were normal. In the left eye, visual acuity was 20 / 20 (far) and N6 (near). The left eye examination was within normal limits with a normal response to direct and indirect pupillary light reflex. Written informed consent for publication of the research details and clinical images was obtained from the patient’s guardian.

Noncontrast computed tomography of the orbit revealed a hyperdense foreign body (FB) on the right orbital floor adjacent to the lateral orbital wall near the orbital apex at the level of the SOF. It was positioned 13.04 mm anterior and 1.6 mm lateral to the SOF (Fig. 1B–1D). The FB (Fig. 1E) was surgically removed through a circumciliary incision over the right lower lid. The FB was located deep, close to the apex; there was no direct visualization of the nerve.

At 1 week postoperatively, the pain and redness had subsided; however, the diplopia persisted. The right eye exotropia with hypertropia in primary gaze did not improve (Fig. 1F). The pupil size remained 6 mm dilated with sluggish reaction. The strabismus measured 30 prism diopters hypertropia with 25 prism diopters exotropia. The incyclotorsion of 10° persisted (measured by the double Maddox rod test). The near vision improved to N6 with a +1.50 diopter spherical lens. The forced duction test was negative. These clinical findings were consistent with injury to the inferior division of the CN III in the right eye. He was advised to patch one eye to alleviate the diplopia and counseled for the possibility of strabismus surgery at a later date. The patient was followed up at 1, 6, and 12 months. There was no improvement in strabismus at the end of 12 months.

The penetrating injury with an intraorbital FB close to the orbital apex can damage the structures passing through the SOF and the optic canal. SOF syndrome involves CN III (both divisions), IV, V (ophthalmic division), and VI, which presents as total ophthalmoplegia with loss of corneal and eyelid skin sensations. Orbital apex syndrome includes features of SOF syndrome along with optic neuropathy, as it involves both the SOF and the optic canal.

Orbital FB can mechanically restrict EOM by its sheer presence or impingement on the extraocular muscle, which improves after removal of FB, unless any nerve is injured. There can be direct physical damage to the nerve or compression of the nerve by the FB. Direct physical damage can cause disruption of axoplasmic flow, and compression can cause anterograde and retrograde flow stasis and loss of myelin sheath [2]. Both mechanisms ultimately lead to loss of function of the extraocular muscles, and as a result, the affected eye develops strabismus. In a case of mechanical restriction by orbital FB, the EOM restriction is toward the direction of the FB. The forced duction test is positive, and a tug is felt in the force generation test. The saccade is normal with sudden cessation of movement toward the direction of FB. In a paralytic case, the EOM limitation will be in the direction of the paralyzed extraocular muscle. The forced duction test is negative, and no tug is felt in the force generation test. The saccade is slow in the direction of the paralytic muscle.

The CN III leaves its nucleus at the midbrain, bypasses the basilar artery and its branches, and travels in the cavernous sinus to ultimately reach the orbit via the SOF. At the SOF, it divides into two divisions. The inferior division supplies the extrinsic muscles—medial rectus (primary adductor), inferior rectus (primary depressor), inferior oblique (primary extorter), and the intrinsic muscles—the ciliary muscle and sphincter pupillae (involved in accommodation, which can affect near vision). The superior division supplies the levator palpebrae superioris (upper eyelid retractor) and superior rectus (primary elevator) [3]. This anatomical division of nerve fibers results in distinct clinical presentations, which can aid in determining the involved division as well as localizing the site of lesion. Inferior division palsy typically presents with exotropia, hypertropia and a dilated, sluggish pupil, whereas superior division palsy presents with ptosis and hypotropia. A similar functional division of the CN III into superior and inferior divisions also occurs at its fascicular portion in the midbrain [4]. Therefore, lesions of the midbrain can also present as isolated superior or inferior division palsy of the CN III.

Midbrain lesions can be associated with headache, pyramidal tract signs such as increased muscle tone, brisk reflexes and positive Babinski sign, and additional cranial nerve involvement. Ksiazek et al. [5] have reported one case of inferior division palsy of CN III due to hemorrhage from a cryptic arteriovenous malformation in the midbrain and two cases of superior division palsy due to ischemia and demyelination in the midbrain. These cases presented with diplopia, headache and pyramidal tract signs, and recovered spontaneously over time. Cunningham and Good [6] have described inferior division palsy due to presumed vasculitis or demyelinating disease in a patient who initially presented with diplopia, which resolved within 3 months, but developed new onset numbness and dysesthesia in the upper extremities after 4 months. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), as well as relevant blood investigations, were unremarkable.

Orbital lesions may present with proptosis, ocular pain, tenderness, congestion and chemosis, without other neurological deficits. Gray et al. [7] described a case of inferior division palsy of the CN III due to orbital dural arteriovenous malformation, which was managed conservatively, although the patient did not recover at the end of 1 year. Notably, the carotid angiogram detected the causative malformation, which was initially missed on MRI. Lee et al. [4] reported another case of inferior division palsy due to a small metastatic lesion on the inferior branch of the CN III in the orbit, from a diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, emphasizing the diagnostic value of understanding the anatomical division of the CN III. Therefore, a thorough clinical history and examination, supported by appropriate radio imaging, can help to identify the level and cause of injury. Treatment targets the primary cause when identified. Ischemic, hemorrhagic and demyelination-associated palsies often recover spontaneously. When an initial MRI or computed tomography fails to detect any lesion, MRA or computed tomography angiography can be considered. When dealing with a divisional palsy of the CN III, focus should be on the fascicular portion of the nerve at the midbrain and around the SOF in the orbit. In cases where a cause cannot be found, close monitoring is prudent, as other neurological deficits and signs may appear during follow-up, guiding toward a particular etiology. If no improvement is seen in the strabismus over the course of a year, considering the nature of the etiology, strabismus surgery can be planned wherever appropriate.

In the present case, the orbital FB has likely injured the inferior division of the CN III in the right eye and caused paralysis of the medial rectus, inferior rectus, inferior oblique, ciliary muscle and sphincter pupillae as evident by the clinical presentation. This paralysis did not recover during the follow-up period after the FB removal. Injury to extraocular muscles by the FB would not cause a dilated, sluggishly reacting pupil and loss of accommodation. Such injuries would generally recover following FB removal. The diagnosis of SOF syndrome is unlikely, as abduction, elevation and intortion were normal; corneal and eyelid skin sensations were normal. The clinical presentation is also inconsistent with the diagnosis of orbital apex syndrome, as the visual acuity (far) in the right eye was normal, and the left eye showed a normal indirect pupillary light reflex.

Restrictions of EOM and diplopia are the common presentations of orbital FB, which generally improve after its removal. This case highlights the importance of detailed discussion with patients about the possibility of permanent EOM limitation caused by preexisting injury to any nerve in the orbit, as in this case.
