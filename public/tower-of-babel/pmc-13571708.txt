From screens to live interactions: Considerations for naturalistic EEG paradigms in social and affective neuroscience
Authors: Ruohan Xia, Tahl I. Frenkel, Lindsay C. Bowman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Live, naturalistic, interactive electroencephalographic (EEG) paradigms have increased over the last 20 years, reflecting a movement towards ecologically valid measurement of the actual stimuli and responses theorized to support human brain and cognitive development. A challenge to this growing field is that many analytic decisions are at researchers’ discretion, and there is a lack of clear standards for reporting and justifying decision-making processes, and a lack of understanding how different analytic decisions may affect results. These circumstances threaten study replicability, transparency, and accuracy. Thus, necessary objectives are to bring awareness to this existing analytic variability, empirically evaluate its effects, and ultimately develop guidelines for optimally applying different analytic approaches. The present study takes an important first step toward these objectives. We review studies using live, interactive EEG paradigms published between 2006 and 2026, and identify several analytic approaches that vary widely: 1) analyzing EEG across longer conditions versus shorter, discrete events, 2) considering influence of amount of usable EEG, 3) inclusion of covariates, 4) whether to examine all electrodes across the scalp versus targeted electrodes, and 5) determining appropriate baselines for comparison to target EEG activity. We discuss considerations for these different analytic approaches, and evaluate their effects in 4- to 6-month-old (N = 35) infant EEG data from a novel, live, naturalistic mother-infant interactive task. Results reveal that different, often arbitrarily selected analytic approaches can change EEG results. We recommend standards and best practices in reporting analytic decisions to increase transparency and replicability, and suggest strategies for determining optimal analytic approaches.

Introduction

A recent advance in developmental cognitive neuroscience is the ability to examine infants’ and young children’s brain activity during live naturalistic social interactions. These types of tasks generally involve the infant sitting across from a caregiver or an experimenter who is interacting with the infant in naturalistic ways such as talking, showing toys, and/or emoting. Gaining insights into how infant brains process such live, naturalistic interactive social stimuli is critical to advance the field of social cognitive and affective neuroscience and socioemotional development more generally. Much of what we currently know about infants’ socioemotional brain activity is based on paradigms initially designed for adults (e.g., event-related potentials in response to passively viewing canonical emotional facial expressions in an isolated, quiet environment for an extended period of time), which already poses unique challenges for developmental cognitive neuroscientists due to infants’ shorter attention span and lower tolerance of neuroimaging equipment (Bell and Cuevas, 2012, Hoehl and Wahl, 2012). Moreover, these computer-administered paradigms lack critical social and contextual nuances such as body language or tone of voice that are central to infants’ everyday social experiences, and thus may be core to the development of the neural activity supporting infants’ socioemotional perception and understanding. Given a historical reliance on such computer-based stimuli, it is possible that the field’s current insight into how the infant brain perceives and responds to socioemotional stimuli may not represent how the infant brain perceives and responds to social partners in real life.

Theories and empirical research underscore the importance of ecological validity and use of live, naturalistic stimuli for social and affective neuroscience investigations. To elaborate, infant brain development is posited to be experience-dependent (Tierney and Nelson, 2009). Individual differences in the functional and structural organization of the brain are related to the development of social (e.g., Martin and Weisberg, 2003), cognitive (e.g., Bowman et al., 2018), and affective (e.g., Leppänen and Nelson, 2009) functioning from infancy to adulthood. Critically, researchers have attributed such individual differences in the brain to variability in the social environment (Atzil et al., 2018), particularly through interactions with other social agents such as caregivers. Indeed, there is existing empirical evidence of caregiving relating to both infant brain structure and function (Ilyka et al., 2021, Tan et al., 2020), as revealed in associations between caregiver characteristics and infants’ neural responses to computer stimuli, ‘resting’ brain activity, or brain structure. However, these experimental set-ups are not equipped to examine how infants respond to specific social input in all of its important subtlety and complexity as it occurs within a naturalistic interaction. Further, existing paradigms that rely on computer-administered presentation of static or dynamic stimuli (e.g., Holland et al., 2019, LoBue and Thrasher, 2015, Tottenham et al., 2009) are limited in their ability to assess constructs that depend on live social interaction that adapts in real-time to infants’ interactive behavior such as joint attention, caregiver responsiveness, or any other aspect of a social interaction that may only be measurable in a live, fluid, actually interactive experience (e.g., a social partners’ multifaceted emotional displays that are contingent on infant behavior). Thus, alternative, live, interactive set-ups are critical to test the existing foundational theory on infant brain development, because it is only these set-ups that measure the precise stimuli hypothesized to shape the developing brain.

Therefore, to directly answer the question of how variability in infants’ social experiences is related to infant brain development, two scientific approaches are needed: (1) development of tasks that can assess the natural variability in infants’ real-world social experiences (e.g., variability in how caregivers interact with their infant), given that a critical aspect of existing theory is that such variability in the infants’ every-day social-interactive experience will be related to variability in infant brain structure and function, and (2) direct examinations of infants’ neural responses to such natural variability in a live, interactive paradigm in order to investigate how infants’ brains respond to the precise stimuli posited to influence their brain development–these ecologically valid neural responses may be most meaningful in predicting developmental outcomes. As such, live, naturalistic, interactive paradigms offer unique perspectives in the field of developmental neuroscience particularly in social and affective domains (see also Jones et al., 2015).

An especially useful modality of cognitive neuroscience for investigating infants’ and young children’s neural responses to such live, naturalistic social stimuli is event-related electroencephalography (EEG). EEG is a child-friendly neuroimaging method that enables quick, quiet recording of awake infants’ brain activity while maintaining proximity to their caregivers (Bell and Cuevas, 2012). Moreover, given that EEG measures the electrical activity generated by neurons firing, it can directly reflect change in neural activity and cognition in real time (Buzsáki et al., 2012). This flexibility thus enables researchers to investigate neural responses to social or emotional events as they occur in rapid succession in live, naturalistic settings. Indeed, there has been a steady increase over the past 20 years in the number of studies adopting this live, naturalistic, interactive EEG approach from infancy to adulthood, with paradigms studying a wide range of social cognition such as joint attention, emotion perception, and action observation/execution (see Figure SI in Supplemental Appendix I). This increase in adopting live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms to examine social cognition echoes the recognition of necessity of naturalism, and the call towards ecological validity in the broader neuroscience field (e.g., Zaki and Ochsner, 2009; Fan et al., 2021). These studies also highlight the feasibility of studying neural responses to live social input during naturalistic interaction. Thus, the study of EEG activity during live, interactive paradigms represents an important current and future direction in the field.

However, there are many challenges to the implementation of live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms that should be considered in order to help foster a productive future of the field in which studies are maximally efficient and accurate. For example, the inclusion of an observational procedure (e.g., empathy or prosocial behavior tasks, Salerni and Caprin, 2022, Zahn-Waxler et al., 1992; joint attention tasks, Mundy et al., 2003) requires labor-intensive behavioral coding to characterize the precise stimuli delivered to or experienced by participants. Additionally, many analytic decisions are at researchers’ discretion which, without transparent reporting, can result in difficulty in replication. Moreover, without systematic empirical evaluation of the different effects of these different analytic decisions, it is possible that findings may be biased or inaccurate. Thus, necessary current objectives are to bring awareness to the existing variability in analysis of live interactive EEG, begin empirical evaluation of the effects of such variability, and ultimately develop careful considerations for how and under which circumstances different analytic approaches may be best.

This paper takes important first steps towards these objectives. To our knowledge, there has been no systematic evaluation or empirical comparison of how different designs or analytic approaches impact results in live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms (although for guidelines and best practices in designing interactive hyperscanning, see Norton et al., 2022 and Turk et al., 2022). As a first aim of this paper, we highlight existing variability in analytic decisions for live, interactive EEG in the field by reviewing studies of live interactive EEG published over the past 20 years. We conducted this review by using Google Scholar search terms of “live naturalistic EEG” or “social interactive EEG” or “live social EEG” or “live interactive EEG” and selecting any study conducted in the last 20 years that measured EEG activity during a live social interaction. Studies were excluded if they did not use any live naturalistic interactive tasks (e.g., participants viewed movies; participants viewed live, non-interactive stimuli such as an experimenter conducting prescribed actions but not adjusting activity in response to a participant), did not record EEG during live naturalistic interactive tasks (e.g., studying resting-state EEG with a behaviorally coded live interaction), or did not use EEG as the neural index (e.g., used EEG equipment to measure heart rate, used fNIRs). For more information regarding the review, see Supplemental Appendix I. Our review reveals that details of the decision-making that led to reported analytic approaches were rarely explicitly stated or discussed. For example, only 8.6% of papers describe any specific justification for trial cutoff, 13% for selecting condition- or event-based analysis, and 30% described preliminary analysis or consideration of covariates. While such variation in analytic decisions and the lack of reporting or explicit justification reflects a universal challenge across all EEG studies, it could be especially impactful for investigations using live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms due to the smaller sample sizes associated with live-interactive EEG (see Table 1), and the likely greater variability in analyzable EEG trials due to variability in the naturalistic interaction itself. Importantly, the presence of variability in analytic approaches combined with the lack of transparent decision-making that led to those approaches makes it difficult for future researchers to know what approach may be most appropriate for their target live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms, and prevents objective evaluation of the existing studies’ paradigms, analyses, and results.

Thus, as the field continues to move towards live, interactive neuroscience paradigms in the long term, we need more rigorous empirical research that establishes a validated set of standards to help elucidate our understanding of what affects data input and interpretation. Such research will also importantly guide how to choose the most appropriate approaches for different questions. The second aim of this paper takes a first step towards this objective by evaluating our own data collected from a live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigm, and highlighting how different analytic decisions can yield different results. The task we use for these empirical analyses is described in Fig. 1 below with additional details in Supplemental Appendix II. In brief, in the absence of an experimenter, infants’ EEG was recorded as they sat across from their mothers while mothers showed infants wordless pictures in a booklet. Mothers were instructed to play with their infant as they normally would and talk about each picture. All empirical analyses in which we examine effects of researcher decisions and analytic approaches are conducted with data from this task using a sample of 35 typically developing 4- to 6-month-old infants (18 infant males and 17 infant females, Mage = 159.11 days, SD = 16.75 days, range = 126–184 days; additional demographic information see Supplemental Appendix III). Importantly, our live, naturalistic, interactive EEG task includes many features exemplified in the existing naturalistic interactive EEG tasks in the literature. Specifically, our task includes multiple experimental conditions that elicit different behavioral input from the social agent (e.g., Wass et al., 2018; Nguyen et al., 2023), minimal experimenter presence (e.g., Wass et al., 2018), and a baseline procedure (e.g., Hoehl et al., 2014; Jones et al., 2015). In addition, the targeted age group is widely used as revealed by our review, and the sample size is also in line with published work (e.g., Hirotani et al., 2009; Hoehl et al., 2014; Nguyen et al., 2023; see Table 1).Fig. 1Task schematic and description of the live interactive EEG task (Emotion Interaction Task; EIT) used in the empirical exploration of the current study. In the sections that follow, we discuss 5 analytic considerations, informed by our review of existing studies of live, naturalistic, interactive EEG (Table 1). For each consideration, we briefly discuss theoretical background and practical impact. Then, where possible, we showcase how specific analytic decisions can yield different results in the real infant data collected during our live, naturalistic, interactive EEG task administered in our lab (Fig. 1), and we include recommendations for future research with respect to each analytic consideration. In a final section, we highlight additional recommendations and next steps for future research to continue ecologically valid investigation of the development and specialization of the neural circuitry supporting social and affective processing in the field of developmental EEG.

Considerations and Empirical Explorations of Live, Interactive EEG

Consideration 1: Condition- versus Event-Based Analytic Approach and The Stimulus Experience

An important consideration in live, naturalistic, interactive EEG research concerns the main analytic approach of analyzing long, uninterrupted conditions (condition-based analyses) versus discrete short-duration events (event-based analyses).

Condition-based Analyses

According to our review, the most common analytic approach is to analyze neural activity during a live interaction across different prescribed or manipulated conditions of many consecutive seconds or minutes (see Fig. 2; e.g., neural activity for 2.5-minute social condition versus 2.5-minute non-social condition; St. John et al., 2016). As shown in Table 1, 78% of the existing studies we reviewed used a condition-based approach. This approach can be particularly advantageous for several reasons. First, it could reduce the burden of labor-intensive behavioral coding. To elaborate, assuming that the condition manipulations were implemented correctly and yield intended differences in the target experiences, and assuming that infants were attentive during the manipulated conditions, researchers may only need to identify the start and end points of each condition to effectively compare neural responses across the conditions (see examples such as Hoehl et al., 2014, St. John et al., 2016). Second, these longer condition-based approaches are necessary to examine target cognitive processes or experiences that can only be measured through sustained exposure or prolonged experience, rather than as elicited by a discrete, short-duration event (see examples such as Liao et al., 2015). The lower time-consuming coding burden and the focus on global or cumulative experiences may in part explain why the majority of studies in our review adopted this condition-based analytic approach.Fig. 2Condition-based EEG epochs example. However, important assumptions must be met to fully harness the advantages of a condition-based approach and avoid inaccurate interpretation of results.

Verifying Infant Attentiveness

First, researchers must confirm infants’ attentiveness or general state of engagement during the experiment to ensure the accuracy in extraction and interpretation of neural signals. While this practice is important across all developmental EEG investigations including computer-administered standardized paradigms, this verification is particularly crucial in live naturalistic, interactive paradigms because of the increased variability and interactive nature in the task design. Different conditions could be inherently more or less engaging, resulting in systematic differences in infants’ attentiveness or fussiness (e.g., infants may be more engaged in a social condition where another social partner is actively interacting with them, and may become upset or distracted in a non-social condition). These condition-elicited changes in infants’ attentiveness or fussiness may be of interest in some tasks, but they may become unwanted confounds in tasks where the goals are unrelated to infants’ attentiveness. Based on our review, 90% of the infant or toddler studies reported a procedure of verifying infants’ attentiveness during the experiment, demonstrating that the field has adopted this process of verifying infant attention as near-standard practice. However, the procedure of infant attention verification varied, and details of these procedures were sparse. Some studies reported not analyzing trials in which infants were inattentive, but only one study reported statistics verifying that attentiveness was equivalent across condition manipulations or target event types.

Characterizing the Stimulus Experience and/or Participant Response

Second, it is important to verify and evaluate the actual stimulus experience of the participant in the interaction. For interactions led by trained experimenters meant to deliver prescribed and systematic experiences across a condition or task, researchers should still check for any deviations in the task administration and consider whether level of experimenter expertise has an impact on the delivery of the task (e.g., novice experimenter versus an experienced experimenter). Interactions led by other untrained social agents such as caregivers will likely be more variable. Such variability may be systematically influenced by the social partner’s or infant’s own characteristics, and in some cases may even be the target variable itself (e.g., researchers may be interested in seeing how infants’ brains respond to different caregiving experiences as influenced by temperamental tendencies or psychopathology; Todd and Dixon, 2010; Beebe et al., 2011). Notably, despite the critical importance of verifying the actual experience of participants during the interaction to ensure appropriate analysis and interpretation of results, there is still great variability in the reporting of such information in published studies. Specifically, 69.5% of the condition-based studies used a trained experimenter or actor as the social partner, but no study reported explicitly whether they verified that the experimenter/actor delivered the tasks as planned. For the remaining studies in which an untrained social partner was asked to interact with the participant (i.e., caregiver, another participant, a lecturer), only one study quantified the social agent’s behaviors prior to conducting the analyses. Thus, verification of the actual stimulus experience of the participant in the interaction is not currently a standard practice in the field. We recommend it be adopted.

From the audio-video recordings of the live, interactive EEG sessions, we coded maternal emotional expressions of different emotion categories (e.g., happy; sad; see Supplemental Appendix II for coding scheme details) as they naturally occurred within each prescribed condition (e.g., mother talks about the frolicky puppy picture; mother talks about the caged dogs picture). Specifically, Fig. 3A shows that caregivers exhibited high consistency aligned with our intended condition manipulations (e.g., concern/sadness only in the caged dogs picture condition, disgust only in the moldy fruit picture condition). We encourage future researchers to adopt a standard practice of characterizing the stimulus experience for their participants in a live, naturalistic interaction, and to report their procedures and results of this verification for evaluation by reviewers and readers.

Counterbalancing Condition Order

A third important consideration for condition-based analyses is counterbalancing the order of conditions presented in the overall interactive task. Counterbalancing is important for addressing potential carry-over or priming effects from one condition to the next. For example, if a social-interaction condition always precedes a non-interaction condition, then the transition to the non-interactive condition may be especially jarring, which could confound interpretation of neural activity differences across conditions. Counterbalancing can also help equate the temporal distance from any pre-task baselines (i.e., points of reference for task-related EEG) and the position of the condition relative to the start of the task, when participants may be most attentive and when stimuli are most novel. Counterbalancing is especially important if the target neural signal attenuates over time (e.g., due to habituation). Differences in the strength of neural signal that occur as a function of when in the temporal order they were measured create confounds that can lead to inaccurate interpretation of results if temporal order is not counterbalanced and if order effects are not tested (e.g., the difference between condition A and condition D may be due to A occurring first and D occurring last rather than to differences in the conditions’ stimulus experience). Despite the importance of counterbalancing and of verifying a lack of order effects, in our review, only 37.5% of the studies mentioned counterbalancing across experimental conditions, or provided justification for not including a counterbalancing procedure.

Empirical Analysis of Order Effects Across Conditions

For our interactive EEG task, we counterbalanced the presentation of the picture conditions across two preset orders to diminish priming or carry-over effects. Our final sample included 43% participants receiving one order, and 57% receiving the other. Analysis of order effects confirmed that there was no order effect for the conditions (Fs < .62, ps > .63), Therefore, we collapsed across all participant orders and performed all subsequent analyses with the full sample together. Additionally, after collecting a portion of the sample using 4 emotional picture conditions, we added a neutral picture condition (parked car) that always occurred last for all participants (43% of the final sample received this condition). This fixed presentation order of having the parked car condition last allowed us to analyze the full sample altogether as the experiences in the first four picture conditions would be the same across all participants, even those that had the parked car condition introduced. However, the inclusion of the parked car condition at the end of the task introduced a potential temporal confound in any analyses that included EEG data during the parked car condition as compared to conditions that always appeared earlier in the task. To address this potential order effect, we conducted supplementary analyses which confirmed that our target EEG signal (6–9 Hz suppression) did not systematically attenuate over time. This confirmation provides confidence that analyses revealing significant differences between EEG in the car versus other picture conditions are likely not due to temporal order effects, supporting our approach to collapse across the full sample. We encourage researchers to consider including and reporting counterbalancing procedures, analyses of potential order effects, and/or explicit justification for using specific fixed-order designs.

Event-based Analyses

Based on our review, another common analytic approach is to analyze differences in neural responses to discrete, short-duration events targeted within the broader interaction. These events may occur both within and across different conditions or at different points in the interaction, and may be more or less frequent or of different types throughout (see Fig. 4). The events are coded typically in audio and/or video recordings of the live interaction as trained coders pinpoint a series of target events-of-interest (e.g., looking behavior, Wass et al., 2018; attention to social stimuli such as experimenter’s face or non-social stimuli such as the toy; Jones et al., 2017). Researchers then epoch the EEG tied to these target events for analyses.Fig. 4Event-based EEG epochs example. Panels A-C represent different stimulus event distributions that can occur across conditions or portions within a task. In all cases, EEG is extracted and analyzed as tied to a specific event (e.g., EEG extracted for all events X and compared to EEG extracted for all events Y).

In all cases, EEG is extracted and analyzed as tied to a specific event (e.g., EEG extracted for all events X and compared to EEG extracted for all events Y).

There are several advantages to this event-based analytic approach. First, an event-based approach could help alleviate critical confounds inherent to a global, condition-based analysis. Within a broad experimental condition wherein multiple distinct events could co-occur (e.g., social vs. non-social events), extracting neural activity at the broader condition level could reflect neural responses to different stimuli, including both target and non-target. By contrast, an event-based approach relies on discrete, identified events-of-interest, and isolates neural responses to such discrete events, enabling researchers to more precisely target the neural activity that is elicited by a specific stimulus or response event. This approach can thus provide more clarity to the interpretations of the results. Furthermore, an event-based approach may also mitigate the temporal confounds associated with the lack of counterbalancing. Specifically, distinct target events can occur distributed across the entirety of a naturalistic task, and the neural signal would thus be less susceptible to condition-level order effects, such as habituation or fatigue. Despite the relative advantages of this event-based approach, in our review, only 21.7% of the studies employed it (see Table 1).

As with condition-based analyses, there are also challenges associated with the event-based analytic approach.

Coding for and Checking the Distribution of Events

First, unlike condition-based approaches wherein behavioral coding can be reasonably constrained to start/end timestamps of each condition, event-based approaches require more labor intensive behavioral coding. And unlike computer-administered tasks wherein specific events are typically pre-coded and flagged on the continuous EEG recording, the process of identifying specific events-of-interest in a live, naturalistic, interactive task requires iterative efforts to develop a reliable coding scheme, train reliable coders, and code all events across every interaction for all participants in a sufficiently powered sample. Moreover, procedures for accurately synching video/audio recorded events with their corresponding EEG epochs are essential, and require both planning in the design and equipment set-up phase as well as verification post recording. Second, once videos are all coded reliably, the number of target events may not be evenly distributed across event types, creating imbalances in event comparisons. This issue may be addressed if the number of events is included as a covariate in analyses, as discussed further in 2.2 below. However, covarying the number of contributed events will not help if some target events are missing altogether in some participants. Issues become further complicated if event numbers are related to characteristics of the participant (e.g., infant) or social partner. For example, the number of infant vocalization events may depend on infants’ temperament, or the number of maternal emotional displays may depend on the mothers’ psychopathology. This variability in target events within a live, naturalistic interaction may be of specific interest to experimenters (e.g., studying how infants’ neural activity supporting social vocalizations differs by infant temperament, or how infants’ neural responses to maternal displays differs for babies of depressed or non-depressed mothers), but it can create challenges in event-comparisons nonetheless.

We thus recommend that researchers conduct preliminary checks and explore the distribution and number of different events-of-interest prior to running focal analyses. Doing so can help researchers determine appropriate next-step analytic decisions (e.g., collapse across more nuanced event categories into a composite to balance the number of events for more equal signal-to-noise ratios). In our review, 40% of event-based studies reported procedures to verify distributions and balance of events, and 60% used events contributed as a covariate in analyses or explored how trial contribution may affect results. Thus, these practices are gaining prevalence in the field, and we encourage researchers to adopt them as standard.

Counterbalancing Considerations for Events

Third, although as noted above there are potential advantages to the event-based approach that may diminish potential order or carry-over effects across conditions, counterbalancing may still be beneficial for event-based analyses. For example, if different event types are elicited by different conditions, then some events may always occur earlier or later in an experiment. For neural signals that tend to attenuate, this imbalanced temporal ordering could confound the event-related contrasts. However, if different event types are evenly distributed across the task duration, then counterbalancing may be less critical.

Position and Length of Event Epochs

Finally, for event-based analyses, researchers must decide the position and length of the EEG epoch in relation to the targeted events-of-interest.

For EEG epoch positioning (see Fig. 6), researchers must decide whether to extract the EEG data prior to the event onset, post the event onset, or around the event onset (capturing both pre and post-event EEG). Importantly, different epoch positions relative to an event may capture different underlying neural and cognitive processes. The case of capturing the neural activity associated with infants’ joint attention illustrates these issues. For example, if the event of interest in a joint-attention interaction between an infant and a social partner is defined as an infant gaze shift between the social partner and common referent, then examining the epoch of EEG pre -shift may capture the infants’ neural activity associated with a decision to engage in joint attention. In contrast, examining an EEG epoch post-shift may capture neural activity associated with a different cognitive process, such as an active state of jointly attending. Both a pre- and post-shift event avoids targeting an EEG epoch that will likely contain a saccade (a known artifact in EEG data; Thickbroom and Mastaglia, 1985; Becker and Fuchs, 1988; for a review of challenges associated with gaze-related EEG see Plöchl et al., 2012). Researchers may therefore wish to avoid epoching the EEG around the shift (i.e., capturing EEG pre, during, and post gaze-shift) as this epoch positioning will contain the saccade artifact. In all cases, we encourage researchers to plan epoch positions relative to their events-of-interest prior to data analysis, and to include justification of their reasoning for targeting a particular position over another. If multiple epoch positions are explored, all should be reported and corrections for multiple comparisons may be necessary.Fig. 6Epoch position example (with example analytic challenges illustrated). For EEG epoch length, researchers must decide the duration of EEG to analyze relative to the event. As with epoch positioning, the epoch length should also ideally be determined a priori, and matched across event types, to best capture the neural signal associated with a target process of interest. Different epoch lengths may be optimal depending on the target process. For example, longer epochs may be needed to capture underlying neural activity that is associated with a more elaborated social stimulus (e.g., infants’ neural activity to a spoken phrase using infant-direct speech), whereas shorter epochs may be more appropriate for more discrete, transient neural events (e.g., infant gaze shift). Epoch lengths that are mismatched to the cognitive/neural process of interest create challenges for analysis and interpretation of results. For example, if an epoch length is too long relative to its underlying target cognitive or neural process, then this longer epoch is more likely to be ‘contaminated’ by non-target activity, including motion artifacts or other non-target cognitive or neural processes. If an epoch length is too short relative to its underlying target process, then the epoch may not capture the complete neural signal being targeted.

In novel, live, interactive EEG paradigms in which events occur in close succession and may be highly variable, it could be difficult to determine appropriate epoch position or length a priori. One approach to address this issue is to analyze a pilot sample, and compare different epoch lengths and positions. This pilot sample can then inform decisions of epoch length and positioning as applied to a new target sample for analysis, and can even be pre-registered on open science platforms. In all cases, the field will benefit from the inclusion of justification of epoch position and length. In our review, only 20% of the studies provided justification for the specific epoch position or length. To enhance replicability and transparency, we encourage researchers to report their process of determining these important analytic parameters, and as noted above, if multiple epoch positions or lengths were tested, these results should also be reported and potentially corrected for multiple comparisons.

Empirical Comparison Between Condition- and Event-based Analytic Approaches

We compared both condition-based and event-based analyses of infant EEG activity during our live, naturalistic mother-infant interaction as mothers talked to their infants about emotionally evocative wordless pictures in a booklet. Condition-based analyses examined infants’ neural responses to maternal emotional displays occurring during 20-second picture conditions. We compared the infant neural activity during conditions designed to elicit positive maternal emotional displays (positive picture condition) versus conditions designed to elicit negative maternal emotional displays (negative picture condition). Event-based analyses compared infants’ neural responses during 1-sec events in which EEG was epoched post the onset of a discrete maternal emotional facial and/or vocal expression, coded as either a positive maternal emotional event (mother facial or vocal displays of joy, excitement, happiness) or negative maternal emotional event (mother facial or vocal displays of fear, sadness, concern, shock, disgust, anger) (see coding scheme details in Supplemental Appendix II). For event-related analyses, the post-event-onset position was chosen because we aimed to capture infants’ neural activity associated with the perception of a live, naturalistic maternal emotional display. The 1-sec epoch length was chosen because it was long enough to capture the maternal emotional displays which lasted an average of 1.3 s (SD=1.08 s, range=1–2.5 s), and short enough that a single epoch did not contain neural responses from multiple maternal emotional displays of different types/valences as confirmed in our coding scheme. We then compared results from condition-based versus event-based analyses for (1) a group-level research question examining whether infants’ neural responses differed for positive versus negative maternal emotional displays; and (2) an individual differences research question examining whether individual differences in infants’ neural responses to positive versus negative emotions (calculated as a difference score between neural activity for positive versus negative events or conditions) was related to individual differences in mothers’ self-reported emotion reactivity (a characteristic related emotion perception in previous research, see Xia et al., 2024). Preliminary analyses revealed that infant age and amount of usable EEG epochs contributed were related to our target EEG signal at several channels (i.e., of infant 6–9 hz alpha suppression relative to a pre-task baseline of infants passively viewing silent moving colors on a screen) (significant rs > -.63, ps < .05, uncorrected). Thus, all analyses for both condition- and event-based approaches were conducted using infant EEG activity that had been residualized to control for the influence of infant age and EEG contributed. To equate samples for comparison, we conducted analyses on the sample of 20 children who had usable event- and condition-based EEG.

As shown in Fig. 7A, for the group-level research question, both condition- and event-based analytic approaches revealed similar significant differences in infants’ neural activity to positive versus negative maternal emotions at the right temporal parietal scalp. However, the topographical distribution was not identical across approaches, with the event-based approach revealing more amplified neural differences between positive and negative maternal emotional valences. For the individual differences research question, the condition-based and event-based approach revealed more dissimilarity. The differing results across condition-based and event-based analytic approaches in the infant EEG data from our live, interactive task highlight that the patterns of neural activity within the same task could differ depending on the analytic approach, for both group-level and individual differences research questions. Importantly, while we discussed specific advantages and disadvantages for both approaches and our specific set of analyses was guided by empirical exploration of the stimulus experience as well as inspections of the frequency distribution of different events-of-interest, it was still not clear that one approach would be superior over the other for our novel task. To elaborate, our task did elicit different maternal emotional events for different conditions. In this case, we reasoned that a condition-based approach allowed us to average across different events within the condition to reveal global neural differences across conditions. Conversely, an event-based approach allowed us to more closely target the neural responses to specific emotional expressions. However, challenges arose for both approaches: while our picture conditions did elicit target emotional expressions, they also elicited many non-target emotional expressions. Similarly, even though differently valenced events occurred throughout the interaction across picture conditions, there was a strong imbalance between the positive and negative emotional events. Nevertheless, both approaches yielded statistically significant results. Critically, if we had selectively reported only the approach that produced more significant results (i.e., condition-based approach), we would have artificially inflated estimated effect size. This outcome reporting bias would subsequently mislead future researchers into calculating smaller-than-needed sample sizes for similar constructs. Thus, given that methodological ambiguity is inherent to exploratory research – and especially prevalent in novel naturalistic interactive EEG paradigms – transparency in reporting of analytic decisions and results is critical. We recommend that future researchers transparently report all conducted analyses and carefully weigh the consequences of foundational task design and analytic decisions to ensure accurate effect size estimation and robust replicability in this growing field of live naturalistic EEG research.

Consideration 2: Amount of Usable EEG Contributed

A relevant consideration in EEG research is the amount of usable EEG contributed. The amount of usable EEG contributed is determined by a number of factors including the amount of data that is removed due to presence of noise artifact, and the amount of data available in the raw recording (e.g., the number of target events-of-interest produced in the interaction). The amount of usable EEG data contributed can be quantified in different ways such as duration of EEG in seconds or milliseconds, or number of discrete events or trials. Importantly, the amount of usable EEG can differ across conditions, by event type, and with participant and social partner characteristics.

Challenges associated with variability in the amount of usable EEG epochs and their distribution across the interactions, conditions, and event types have already been discussed in 2.1 above (e.g., in terms of case-wise deletion or imbalanced comparisons across different conditions/events). Here, we discuss other ways that variability in the amount of usable EEG contributed across participants can be considered: with the creation of exclusion criteria based on a minimum threshold of useable EEG data contributed, and through covarying effects of amount of useable EEG contributed on target EEG signals.

Exclusionary Criteria: Trial Cutoffs for Amount of EEG Contributed

In existing studies, researchers commonly address issues of the amount of usable EEG contributed by enforcing an exclusionary criterion based on a minimum threshold of usable EEG data. Typically, this is referred to as a ‘trial’ cutoff threshold, wherein participants who fail to contribute a specific number of usable EEG epochs are excluded from analyses. Trial cutoffs are common in both adult and pediatric EEG analyses, and especially prevalent in event-related potential (ERP) analyses in which higher trial counts have been shown to relate to higher signal-to-noise ratio. Yet, in the broader field, these cutoffs are increasingly scrutinized because they are often arbitrary, making it difficult to objectively quantify an ‘acceptable’ number of trials. This is particularly problematic in developmental research. Specifically, because researchers already have to constrain the maximum number of trials administered to accommodate infants’ and young children’s reduced attention spans and lower tolerance for EEG equipment (Bell and Cuevas, 2012), enforcing arbitrary cutoffs (often based on published adult studies) further leads to case-wise deletion and sample reduction, reducing power to detect real effects. In live, naturalistic paradigms, this issue is even more exacerbated: As discussed above, the amount of usable EEG is not only dependent on the task design, but potentially also on the participant or social partners’ characteristics. In such investigations, having an arbitrary, high trial cutoff may significantly reduce the sample size while systematically excluding participants that have inherently different social experiences to begin with, posing additional threat to external validity. Most critically, the use of different trial cutoffs can change study results.

Empirical Comparison of Different Trial Cutoff Thresholds

Using the most common trial cutoffs revealed in our review that are applicable to our dataset, we examined how infant EEG data during our naturalistic mother-infant interaction differed across different trial cutoff thresholds: excluding infants who had fewer than 5 useable EEG data points (5-trial cutoff) versus 10 usable EEG data points (10-trial cutoff) versus no cutoff (all participants with at least 1 usable EEG data point were included). We compared these different trial cutoffs in a condition-based analysis, wherein cutoffs were implemented in each of the picture conditions separately. Any participants who did not meet the criteria in any single picture condition were excluded from analyses. Thus, for our comparisons, sample sizes varied due to the exclusion of children who did not meet the minimum usable EEG threshold cutoffs.

We examined how these three different trial cutoffs (none, 5, and 10) affected results when examining two research questions: 1) a group-level research question of how infants’ EEG during the positive maternal display picture condition (frolicking puppy) differed from EEG during the negative maternal display picture conditions (average of breaching shark, moldy fruit, and caged dogs); and 2) an individual differences research question of how infants’ positive-negative EEG contrast related to mothers’ self-reported emotional reactivity (a variable that has been shown to relate to children’s ability to discern emotional valence and intensity; Xia et al., 2024). Power analyses revealed that, for the group-level research question, a sample size of 34 was needed to achieve 80% percent power to detect a medium size effect. For the individual differences research question, a sample size of 32 was needed to achieve 80% percent power and detect a medium-large size effect (more details about the power analysis see Supplemental Appendix IV).

Taken together, our data demonstrate how the use of different arbitrarily selected trial cutoffs can yield different results. Critically, it is not clear in these cases which trial cutoffs yield the most accurate results, nor is it clear whether results are changing due to differences in amount of usable EEG data contributed versus differences in the sample of participants examined: trial-cutoff and sample are inherently confounded. Given our small sample and results of our power analyses, it is likely that a sample size of 25 participants is underpowered to detect effects, making interpretations of the 10-trial cutoff results difficult. Results from an underpower sample may be more susceptible to false positives (Christley, 2010) and may overestimate the effect size, leading to additional challenges with replication (Jaksic et al., 2026).

Controlling for EEG Contributed in the Full Sample

As an alternative to determining appropriate trial cutoffs, recent work in the broader EEG and ERP literature has begun to avoid using arbitrary trial cutoffs, and instead include all participants who have at least one usable EEG data point (i.e., trial). For example, researchers can use more advanced statistical approaches (i.e., linear mixed effects models; Heise et al., 2022, Heise et al., 2025) to allow for retention of more participants and all available trials by adjusting the weight of each participant depending on the number of usable EEG trials contributed through partial pooling. More generally, researchers can check whether variability in the amount of usable EEG contributed across participants is related to target EEG signals. If the EEG contributed is found to significantly influence target EEG signal (e.g., in preliminary correlations), researchers should control for the effect of contributed EEG for example by including EEG contributed as a covariate in analyses, or using EEG variables that have been residualized for the effect of EEG contributed as discussed further in the next 2.3. This practice is discussed in the broader field of EEG and ERP research (Boudewyn et al., 2018, Meyer et al., 2017) and is being adopted in existing studies (e.g., Xia et al., 2024). Critically, the addition of controlling for the amount of usable EEG contributed can change study results.

Insights and Recommendations for Considering the Amount of Usable EEG

Broadly, the issue of how to address effects of EEG contributed is largely undetermined in the field. In our review, only 4% of studies examined whether variability in the amount of usable EEG affected target EEG signals. Regarding trial cut-offs, 48% of the studies reported some exclusion criteria based on minimum trial contribution, but of these studies, only 2 studies provided justification for such a decision. Moreover, only 9% of the studies we reviewed conducted power analyses to determine whether their collected sample was sufficiently powered to detect effects in their conducted analysis.

In future research, we recommend the field give serious attention to developing protocols and standards for empirically determining how to best address effects of usable EEG contributed on target EEG signals, and whether and which trial cut-offs are appropriate. The field has begun this endeavor in ERP research (e.g., with the use of aSME; Luck et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2024; Pestana et al., 2025; see also Heise et al., 2022). We encourage researchers to continue to assess data quality objectively, to include justification for the use of specific trial cutoffs in analyses, and/or to report analyses conducted using multiple different trial cutoffs for full transparency. In all cases, researchers must consider their sample size and available power to conduct analyses that include additional covariates or residualization processes (Streiner, 2016, Cohen et al., 2003).

To aid justification and decision-making, researchers could analyze pilot samples and compare effects across different trial cutoffs to determine which cutoff is better for the target signal in the target task. Researchers could also conduct simulation analyses to empirically and objectively evaluate the number of trials necessary to accurately estimate the underlying target signal when it is known (see e.g., Heise et al., 2022, Heise et al., 2025). We recommend conducting preliminary analyses to determine if amount of useable EEG contributed affects target EEG signals, and if it does, we recommend controlling for amount of useable EEG contributed in focal analyses to statistically adjust the effect of trial contribution on the magnitude of the EEG signal of interest (see e.g., Jones et al., 2015). Researchers could also use more robust statistical analyses where appropriate such as linear mixed effect models that allow for analysis of all trials and adjust for the amount of EEG contributed via partial pooling (e.g., Heise et al., 2022, Heise et al., 2025).

Consideration 3: Covariates

Inclusion of covariates allows researchers to more accurately model the target variables, and reduces unexplained error variances to isolate the effect of interest between target variables (Cohen et al., 2003, Gelman and Hill, 2007). In developmental EEG studies, researchers should not only consider controlling for common demographic variables such as infant age, sex or race/ethnicity, but also variables that help control confounds related to EEG data quality, such as the amount of usable EEG data points or trials contributed (see 2.2 above). For live, interactive EEG studies in particular, researchers should also consider variables that may affect the participant and/or social partners’ behavior in the interaction. We elaborate below.

First, participant age should be considered as a covariate. While age may not always be the primary predictor in a given research question, it is still crucial to examine age-related effects, as rapid developmental changes can introduce variability in brain function, even when the age range is limited. To elaborate, infant age should be considered in all developmental EEG studies (not just live, interactive EEG) because younger infants tend to contribute fewer task-related or baseline epochs due to lower tolerance for the experimental procedures (see Heise et al., 2022). Fewer artifact-free epochs result in overall higher variance and effectively reduce the signal-to-noise ratio of the estimated power, given there is less data for conventional power estimation methods such as the Welch’s method (Welch, 1967). In addition, there have been additional documented links between age and physiological anatomy such as skull thickness that can also affect strength of the signal regardless of the length of the recording (Lillie et al., 2016). Further, age also has a unique impact in live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms. Specifically, because social partners who interact with infant participants naturally adapt their own behaviors to the infants’ age-related characteristics, the infants’ resulting stimulus experience and behavioral responses to such stimuli thus also depend on infants’ age. To mitigate these confounds within naturalistic interactive paradigms, researchers should include age as a covariate while explicitly controlling for the frequency of target events available for analysis. Several published studies in the broader field of EEG research have adopted the practice of controlling for age and EEG data contributed (e.g., Bowman et al., 2019, Jones et al., 2015, Jones et al., 2017, Pestana et al., 2025, Sabbagh et al., 2009, Xia et al., 2024). In our review, of the studies in which age was not a primary variable of interest, only 16% included age as a covariate. As noted above, only 4% of studies checked whether the amount of EEG contributed influenced target EEG signals. These percentages reveal that in the field of live interactive EEG, examining potential effects of age and amount of usable EEG contributed is not yet standard practice. We recommend it be adopted.

Other covariates beyond age and EEG contributed could also impact the EEG results and mask target comparisons or effects. In live, naturalistic interactive paradigms, the dyadic interactive nature of the task can be influenced by individual differences in the infant or the social partner, such as infant temperament or language skills, or caregivers’ stress or psychopathology. When such constructs are not the primary variables of interest, they act as potential confounds. For example, an infant’s temperament may influence caregivers’ engagement style, just as a caregiver’s underlying psychopathology may independently change their interactive behavior. Consequently, researchers should carefully consider including measures of relevant participant and social partner characteristics where applicable, and check whether they influence target neural signals and may thus need to be included as covariates to better isolate the neural signals of interest. In our review, among studies that incorporated any covariates, no study using caregiver-infant dyads examined the potential influence of any caregiver characteristics.

We recommend that researchers conduct preliminary analyses to determine whether any measured covariates significantly contribute to the variances of the target variables (Gelman and Hill, 2007) (and that researchers thus consider in the study design phase what potential covariates to measure during data collection). Should significant influence from covariates occur, there are several ways to account for them prior to subsequent analyses. First, given a sufficiently large sample, researchers could include all significant covariates in a final model. However, doing so may artificially decrease degrees of freedom and reduce statistical power especially if the included covariate only contributes to the variances in one of the target variables. Alternatively, researchers could consider residualizing the target variables to the covariates prior to conducting focal analyses to isolate the effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable while controlling for the unique contributions of the covariates on the independent or dependent variables (Wurm and Fisicaro, 2014; see Klawohn et al., 2020; see also Bowman et al., 2017, Meyer et al., 2017, Sabbagh et al., 2009). While the residualization of variables prior to focal analyses can ‘save’ degrees of freedom and potentially increase statistical power, underpowered models that include residualized variables may underestimate standard errors (Streiner, 2016, Cohen et al., 2003). Therefore, researchers should take care to obtain appropriate sample sizes for sufficiently powered analyses regardless of the method for handling covariates.

Consideration 4: Whole-scalp versus Target Electrode Analyses

Another important analytic decision researchers should consider in analysis of live, interactive EEG data is whether to conduct more exploratory channel-wise analyses across the whole scalp, versus analyze selected channels/clusters of channels determined a priori based on prior literature (e.g., as informed by relevant interactive tasks or computer-administered tasks).

Cluster-based analysis wherein selected channels are targeted for analysis based on a priori justification can be confirmatory in nature because it involves empirical guidance in identifying specific patterns of scalp activity that are involved in the same or relevant constructs of interest. Similar to region-of-interest analyses in other neuroscience research (e.g., see Bedny et al., 2015 for an example using fMRI), this approach can reduce the risk of spurious results by focusing analyses on a subset of channels where, based on prior research, effects are more likely to be found. Researchers thus can reduce the number of comparisons that need to be corrected for (e.g., in a false discovery rate correction, Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) by only correcting across the number of clusters tested. However, infants’ neural correlates of naturalistic interactions are largely uncharted, and the reliance on results from computer-administered paradigms that lack ecological validity may be misleading. Further, researchers may risk missing a true effect that is present in other, unselected channels because the effect was not indexed by the a priori selected channels or was reduced by the averaging of multiple channels together in a cluster.

Whole-scalp, channel-wise analyses in which researchers examine effects in all channels across the entirety of the scalp can address some of the issues of cluster-based analyses. Specifically, rather than constraining analyses to selected channels, a channel-wise analysis reveals all effects across the scalp, and avoids the risk of ‘missing’ an effect that is there. This exploratory approach can be particularly advantageous in novel, live-interactive EEG tasks in which identifying relevant scalp regions a priori can be difficult due to lack of relevant literature to inform channel selection. Moreover, presentation of the whole scalp topography (versus only illustrating EEG data at selected clusters) reveals to reviewers and readers the complete picture of results, allowing for better evaluation of the study results and better informing future research. Thus there are advantages to showing channel-wise analyses and plots even when there is good justification for cluster-based approaches. However, increasing the search space in a channel-wise analysis also increases the possibility of type I error, and researchers must implement corrections for multiple comparisons to avoid reporting on spurious results. Selecting the appropriate correction can be challenging, and many options exist.

Indeed, pertinent to both channel-wise and cluster-based analytic approaches, researchers should select an appropriate inference criterion to balance the risks of Type I and Type II errors (Anderson et al., 2001). Several approaches exist to adjust the significance thresholds when multiple statistical tests are performed, each offering unique trade-offs between error control and statistical power. Traditional methods for correcting multiple comparisons include the Bonferroni-type correction (e.g., Dunn, 1961; Holm, 1979), which control the family-wise error rate by comparing the significance threshold (ɑ) against all performed tests. While straightforward and robust, these methods can become overly conservative for studies with exploratory designs involving large numbers of statistical tests (e.g., tests at 32 + channels, 12 clusters), therefore increasing the risk of Type II errors and potentially overlooking meaningful effects (Verhoeven et al., 2005). Alternatively, False Discovery Rate correction (FDR; Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) offers a less stringent and more flexible approach by controlling the expected proportion of false positives among the set of significant results, improving statistical power when detecting moderate or small effects.

Beyond these parametric methods, permutation- and Monte Carlo–based approaches can also be used to adjust inference criteria and control for inflated Type I error rates in exploratory analysis. These nonparametric approaches estimate the null distribution by repeatedly shuffling and resampling the data (Nichols and Holmes, 2002, Maris and Oostenveld, 2007). By comparing the observed statistics to this empirically derived distribution, researchers can obtain corrected significance thresholds that appropriately account for multiple comparisons (Groppe et al., 2011). In addition, cluster-based permutation testing further enhances the sensitivity by identifying clusters of temporally or spatially adjacent data points that exceed a predefined threshold, thereby leveraging the data’s inherent structure while controlling for the family-wise error rate (Maris and Oostenveld, 2007). Compared to traditional corrections such as Bonferroni corrections or FDR, these methods provide greater statistical power, particularly when parametric assumptions may not hold or when dealing with large numbers of correlated comparisons (i.e., parallel channel-level analysis on the whole scalp).

Based on our review, 52% of the studies used a cluster-based analysis, and 35% used a whole-scalp analysis. Of those that used a cluster-based analysis, 57% provided justification for a priori channel selection, highlighting this justification step as a more common practice in the field that should be continued in future research. However, only 30.8% of the cluster-based studies and 33% of the whole-scalp studies reported inclusion of statistical correction methods to adjust for multiple comparisons. We recommend researchers adopt as standard the practice of correcting for multiple comparisons across channels or clusters, or specifically justifying a lack of correction.

When conducting exploratory analyses of novel live, naturalistic interactive EEG data, one approach to address this issue is to evaluate the whole-scalp, channel-wise distribution on a pilot sample, prior to determining whether to take a whole-scalp or cluster-based approach. The pilot visualization can help guide decisions regarding which specific channels or clusters to target in the separate full sample. Researchers can then include descriptions of the pilot sample results as justification for their selection of specific channels.

Consideration 5: Selecting a Baseline to Compare to EEG in a Target Condition or Event

A common approach in EEG research more broadly is to examine how power spectral density (PSD) in a condition- or event-of-interest compares to PSD in a ‘baseline’ state that is meant to capture an ‘idling’ or ‘resting’ brain or other non-target activity. Researchers then examine the change in PSD between baseline and condition- or event-of-interest to assess how responsive the brain is during the target condition or event. This approach is used in adult and developmental EEG research (Pfurtscheller and Da Silva, 1999) and in both live, interactive and computer-based EEG paradigms (e.g., Jones et al., 2015, Liao et al., 2015, Zhao et al., 2023). In our review, 30% of studies compared neural activity in a target condition- or event-of-interest to a baseline state. In one especially common form of this type of analysis, researchers compare alpha PSD (i.e., ∼6–9 hz in infants and young children, ∼8–13 hz in adults) during an event- or condition-of interest to alpha PSD in a baseline, and interpret a relative decrease in alpha PSD during the event or condition relative to baseline as a reflection of the magnitude of underlying brain activation during the target event/condition (Pfurtscheller and Da Silva, 1999, Nunez et al., 2005). This approach is often referred to as event-related suppression or event-related desynchronization (ERD) and can be calculated as: 10⨉log10(PSDevent/PSDbaseline)) (e.g., Filippi et al., 2016; Thorpe et al., 2016).

Importantly, given that EEG activity during the baseline is part of the calculation of target EEG, different baselines can impact the target EEG, and consequently shape study results and interpretation.

Fig. 12 illustrates three common types of baseline used in event-/ condition-related EEG designs: 1) long, pre-task baseline (e.g., 80-second EEG recording while sitting quietly in front of a screen showing silent abstract moving colors); 2) within-trial baseline (e.g., 2-second EEG immediately preceding the presentation of target stimuli), and 3) between-trial baseline (e.g., 8-second EEG during short, resting or non-target trials between presentations of target stimuli). Empirical research has already documented different baseline effects on computer-administered paradigms (Hobson and Bishop, 2016, Meyer et al., 2017). Specifically, EEG in an event- or condition-of-interest is better targeted when using baselines that are temporally close to the event of interest, and when baseline content matches the content of the event-of-interest in as many ways as possible for the specific constructs studied (e.g., both baseline and event-of-interest constitute colorful motion stimuli, but the event of interest constitutes biological motion–the target process). Depending on the design and research question, baselines that are temporally more distant from events of interest and/or that are less matched on stimulus features may still be appropriate.Fig. 12Illustration of different baseline methods for EEG spectral power studies.

Illustration of different baseline methods for EEG spectral power studies.

In all cases, researchers should evaluate which baseline position and content best serves their study design and research questions, as well as consider what may be feasible. For example, while it is rather straightforward to select as baseline a non-target event that directly precedes an event-of-interest in a standardized computer-based task (e.g., 20 s non-biological motion video clip always precedes biological motion video clip), it can be challenging to identify a uniformed baseline event when infants naturalistically interact with another social agent; reasonable periods of non-target activity that could serve as baselines may not occur prior to each event-of-interest, nor with equivalent frequency across conditions designed to elicit different events (Fig. 13, top panel). This variability in naturalistic within-trial baseline may create issues for analyzing target EEG (e.g., Should the researcher select a single non-target event within a trial as the representative baseline for each event-of-interest? Is it ok to have some baselines be temporally closer to some events-of-interest than others?) Likewise, in naturalistic interactions, there may be carry-over effects from infants’ neural activity in response to a prior event-of-interest that may ‘contaminate’ a baseline period occurring prior to a subsequent event of interest (Fig. 13, bottom panel). All of these issues may make the use of a within-trial baseline less optimal for live, naturalistic EEG power tasks.Fig. 13Challenges associated with within-task baseline for EEG analyses.

There are also considerations for pre-task and between-trial baselines for live, naturalistic interactive EEG analyses. For a pre-task baseline, researchers should consider whether the close proximity to one condition versus another may influence the accuracy of the data especially if the neural signal of interest shows signs of attenuation with time (e.g., alpha, Xie et al., 2018) – this may result in less change in the condition that is temporally furthest away from the pre-task baseline. Counterbalancing order of condition could address this issue such that all conditions are experienced at all temporal distances from the pre-task baseline across the sample. Similarly, researchers could design a pre-, mid-, and post-task baseline, and counterbalance the baseline position across participants in the sample (e.g., some participants receive pre-task baseline, some mid-task, some post-task). Between-trial baselines may offer a balance of proximity and equidistance from target events/conditions. However, researchers should consider whether the inclusion of several baseline periods that constitute a forced rest or non-target activity may interrupt the sought-after naturalistic flow of the interaction, and seek to balance the duration and timing of such between-trial baselines.

In all cases, piloting different baselines and analyzing results in an initial pilot sample to inform full-sample studies may offer the best insight as to which baselines best reveal change in a target processes of interest. Researchers may also consider forgoing a ‘baseline’, and opt to compare PSD across differing conditions- or events-of-interest (see e.g., St. John et al., 2016). Depending on the target frequency band, interpretation of raw PSD results will differ. Additional considerations include verifying participants were attending to the baseline stimulus, in line with our recommendations to verify infants’ attendance to target stimuli (as discussed in 2.1 above).

From our review, 39% of the studies used a baseline to isolate neural activity associated with target stimuli. However, only 4 studies justified the use of the specific baseline, and only 1 study verified the balanced distribution of non-target pre-task baseline events across experimental conditions.

Empirical Analysis of the Effects of Different Baselines

Using data from our live, interactive EEG task, for both condition- and event-based approaches, we examined how different baselines may impact the resulting infant neural activity as examined in both a contrast EEG variable (i.e., EEG difference between positive and negative valenced conditions or events) and in raw EEG variables in each of the positive or negative conditions or events separately. We tested for a baseline effect in analysis of both a group-level question (i.e., does infants’ EEG differ across different maternal emotional displays) and an individual-differences question (i.e., does infants’ EEG to maternal emotional displays relate to maternal self-reported emotion reactivity). We compared how the topographical distribution of condition-based and event-based infant neural signals differed between a long, pre-task baseline versus a within-task baseline. Preliminary analyses revealed that infant age and amount of useable EEG epochs contributed were related to our target EEG signal (i.e., of infant 6–9 hz alpha suppression relative to a pre-task baseline of infants passively viewing silent moving colors on a screen) (significant rs > -.63, ps < .05, uncorrected). Thus, all analyses for both condition- and event-based approaches across baselines were conducted using infant EEG activity that had been residualized to control for the influence of infant age and EEG contributed. For the condition-based approach, given that the neutral picture condition was added after data collection has started, we restricted our sample size to participants with all conditions for this comparison. For the event-based approach, we restricted our sample size to participants with all target events (i.e., positive, negative, neutral) for this comparison. Thus, across all analyses we equated samples for comparison across baselines to best reveal the presence of any baseline effects.

Results of our empirical analyses importantly illustrate that different baselines paired with different conditions/events-of-interest can yield different results for group-level or individual differences research. The observed robustness of the calculated contrasts (i.e., difference scores) to baseline effects likely occurs because shared baseline-related variability is mathematically subtracted out, effectively isolating the neural responses specific to processing emotion-related displays of different valences. These results thus suggest that there are advantages to employing difference scores to isolate target cognitive functions (as discussed in the broader field of EEG/ERP research; Luck et al., 2021). Further, the robustness of the event-based approach in individual differences analyses may be attributable to signal specificity. To elaborate, the condition-based approach relies on a global estimation of infants’ EEG that contains different targeted neural activity (e.g., positive and negative maternal displays may occur within the same picture condition, but we bypass the event-level variability in condition-based analyses). When this mixed signal is paired with a baseline that is also more variable (e.g., Moving Colors), it may further obscure the underlying cognitive process. Conversely, the event-based approach isolates specific behavioral events, constraining stimulus variability and ‘protecting’ the signal of interest from a messy baseline.

Ultimately, when determining the most appropriate baselines for EEG suppression studies, researchers should consider using baselines that more closely match the stimulus feature of the target cognitive process. Based on our review of the studies that calculated EEG activity relative to a baseline, no study explicitly discussed the rationale for selecting their specific baseline method. Therefore, to ensure the transparency and replicability of the research in our field, especially when employing novel, live, naturalistic interactive paradigms to understand the neurodevelopmental mechanisms that support the developing cognition, researchers should clearly justify the selection of baseline method.

We also recommend that researchers consider the benefits of using baselines that more closely match their conditions-of-interest or events-of-interest to achieve more precision in targeting the neural activity associated with an underlying process of interest. Additionally, data from our study also highlight that while matched versus unmatched baselines can affect infant neural responses, condition- or event-related contrasts are more robust to these baseline effects. As such, researchers could consider creating condition- or event-related contrasts to more precisely examine the differentiation between conditions- or events-of-interest.

General Discussion

The central questions of the present study concern whether and how live, naturalistic, interactive EEG research varies in its analytic approaches and parameters, and whether such variability affects results and interpretation of the data. As the field of developmental cognitive neuroscience moves towards more naturalistic approaches in understanding how the brain responds to the actual real-world social stimuli hypothesized to influence its development (Atzil et al., 2018), the answers to these questions are essential to move toward consistency, transparency, and replicability of the field. In the following sections, we 1) summarize findings from our literature review and empirical exploration, 2) reiterate some immediate study design and planning recommendations for researchers using live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms, and 3) share additional suggestions to achieve the long-term goals of promoting transparency, reducing analytic inconsistencies, and fostering replication in the field of live, naturalistic, interactive EEG.

The Literature Varies in Analytic Approach and Parameters, and Different Analytic Decisions Change Results

As revealed by our literature review, much variability exists in the reporting and justification of the use of novel tasks and analytic decisions for live, naturalistic, interactive EEG (see Table 1). In brief, we observed wide variability in the reporting and justifications of 1) whether researchers chose to analyze data using condition-based versus event-based approaches, 2) whether and how researchers dealt with different amounts of useable EEG data contributed across participants (e.g., in the form of trial cutoffs or including useable trials as a covariate in analyses), 3) whether and how researchers considered effects of additional covariates that may likely influence target effects (e.g., participant age, participant temperament, other characteristics of the participant and social partner), 4) whether researchers analyzed data from a priori selected channels/clusters versus whole-scalp analyses and how corrections for multiple comparisons were employed, and 5) the selection of different baselines for analytic comparison to target conditions or events. Critically, this wide variability poses challenges for interpretability of the findings across research teams and impacts researchers’ ability to replicate results.

Our empirical analyses of infant data collected from our own novel, live, naturalistic, interactive EEG task further demonstrated that different analytic approaches and parameters indeed can affect results and interpretation. To elaborate, we found that the magnitude and topographic distribution of infants’ 6–9 Hz power, and the magnitude, topographic distribution, and statistical significance of effects when comparing infants’ 6–9 Hz power across conditions, events, and in relation to a maternal self-report measure changed across different parameters and approaches we tested. That is, we found differences in results when analyzing across condition-based vs. event-based approaches, when using different trial cutoffs and whether the amount of usable EEG was statistically accounted for, whether we included covariates (e.g., infant age), whether we analyzed a priori selected channel clusters or whole-scalp channel-wise analysis, and depending on which baseline we used to isolate the neural response specific to target conditions-and events-of-interest. These differences persisted across group-level research questions (e.g., comparing infants’ EEG 6–9 Hz power across positive vs. negative maternal emotional displays) and individual differences research questions (e.g., relating infants’ EEG 6–9 Hz power to maternal self-reported emotion reactivity). These findings thus showcase that patterns of results differed when we arbitrarily changed the analytic approaches or parameters.

Taken together, along with the variability observed in the literature in reporting and justifying analytic approaches or parameters, the differences in results within the same dataset revealed that, without careful consideration and transparent reporting, results from novel, naturalistic, interactive EEG investigations may not be replicable or even interpretable. While several of the analytic approaches we highlighted and tested are applicable to broader EEG/ERP research, they are of particular importance to researchers adopting a live, naturalistic, interactive approach because the novelty and general ‘uncharted territory’ of such tasks make it challenging for researchers to determine a priori whether one analytic option will be superior to another, and resulting patterns of neural activity do not yet benefit from a broader literature in which to contextualize the results.

It is important to note that the present study cannot provide precise prescriptions for which parameters or analytic approaches to choose for different live, naturalistic, interactive EEG studies or EEG studies in general. However, at the broadest level, the considerations we highlighted and discussed in this paper represent a critical first step in guiding researchers towards more standardized and transparent reporting, equipped with the knowledge that seemingly arbitrarily determined analytic practices could have important implications on the results.

An important next step in the field is to establish standards in developing and planning analyses for novel tasks (such as those assessing live, naturalistic, interactive EEG correlates) and in reporting and justifying each analytic decision. A critical long-term goal is to elucidate true, best parameters and analytic approaches given different research questions and datasets. In the sections that follow, we discuss both short- and long-term actions towards these goals.

Implementation guide.

As noted, the above considerations and recommended practices may be informed by analysis of a pilot sample of data in the planned live, naturalistic, interactive study. As we have demonstrated, novel naturalistic, interactive tasks often involve highly variable stimulus experiences and participant responses, creating multiple important analytic decision points that can affect study results and interpretation. Therefore, if researchers conduct preliminary checks and analyses using a pilot sample, they can then use results from these pilot analyses to inform analytic approaches of the separate, powered, target sample. This proactive step guides selection of the most-fitting analytic parameters and approaches (e.g., condition- vs. event-based analysis, minimum trial contributions, epoch lengths etc.) and provides empirical justification for their use. For example, visualizing the topographic distributions of data within a separate pilot sample can objectively inform whether to target specific channels or channel clusters in a different, powered, target sample, alleviating methodological and statistical issues such as hypothesizing-after-results-known (e.g., HARKing, Kerr, 1998).

Additionally, the piloting or preliminary checks outlined in the summary table are more powerful when paired with pre-registrations. Specifically, once analytic decisions have been made after pilot testing, pre-registering the analytic plans will foster transparency in live, naturalistic, interactive EEG paradigms. As our empirical exploration demonstrated, seemingly small processing decisions can yield fundamentally different outcomes (e.g., baseline selection), with some analytic approaches yielding more statistically significant results than others. In an exploratory context (as is often true of novel, live, interactive EEG tasks), if multiple analytic approaches are tested but left unreported, this selective reporting inflates Type I error by masking the multiple comparisons actually performed. Further, it can also skew researchers’ ability to find the accurate effect size to inform for sample size estimations or power calculations for future studies. Importantly, pre-registration does not preclude exploratory analyses. Instead, it provides a transparent framework that explicitly separates confirmatory hypothesis testing from post-hoc exploratory analyses. Ultimately, combining proactive pilot testing with rigorous pre-registration moves the field forward towards more transparent and rigorous investigations. Some existing studies in developmental EEG research have adopted the practice of pre-registration (e.g., Meyer et al., 2024).

Broader Considerations & Future Directions

Grounding Novelty in Established Metrics

To navigate the inherent ambiguity of novel, live, interactive paradigms that capture naturalistic social interactions, researchers should aim to balance exploratory analytic approaches or neural signals with established metrics. Specifically, the interpretability of new tasks can be greatly strengthened by anchoring analyses to well-characterized neural signals (e.g., alpha band activity) that have been validated in adjacent paradigms or constructs of interest. Further, incorporating a behavioral anchor known to correlate with these established neural markers can help elucidate complex variables and confirm the validity of the novel task design. For example, in the current study, we included caregivers’ self-reported emotionality as a behavioral anchor, building on prior event-related potential (ERP) research linking this construct to children’s neural responses to emotional faces (i.e., N170; Xia et al., 2024). Consequently, observing that infants’ neural activity during naturalistic maternal displays of emotions also correlated with caregiver emotionality increased our confidence that we have accurately elicited a target neural signal within the novel task. Whenever possible, future investigations should continue to pair ecologically valid novel tasks with established behavioral or neuroscientific anchors. Several studies in the broader field of developmental EEG have already adopted these practices (e.g., see examples of Striano et al., 2006 and Parise et al., 2008 for pairing live interactive stimuli with the Nc component; Jones et al., 2017 for pairing live interactive stimuli with P400 component).

Additionally, it is also important to consider test-retest reliability when validating novel paradigms. Given that live interactions are inherently fluid and variable (sometimes by design), it is important to establish stability of the elicited naturalistic behaviors as well as the neural signals associated with them. To address these issues, researchers could use longitudinal designs to track behavioral/signal stability within the same individual. Additionally, the field could also benefit from large-scale, team science efforts (e.g., HBCD, Volkow et al., 2024) to implement newly developed tasks and replicate task reliability across diverse samples and research sites to ensure that novel naturalistic paradigms are capturing more representative developmental phenomena rather than lab-specific idiosyncrasies.

We have argued that the use of naturalistic, live stimuli in developmental neuroscience is necessary to examine how infants respond to specific social input with important subtlety and complexity that computer-based stimuli are not equipped to elicit, or to explore the naturalistic interactions between infants and another social partner. Indeed, in some cases, there are no alternatives to a live, interactive design because a computer-based design cannot provide the real time adjustment between participant and social partner that may be the variable of interest (e.g., imitative loops). However, in other cases, neural correlates of live, naturalistic stimuli can be compared to a computer-based counterpart (e.g., neural responses to live emotional faces versus computer-present images). In these cases, it would be interesting to examine similarities and differences between live and computer-based EEG activity. For example, researchers could employ a two-visit experiment where participants complete both an established, highly controlled computer-administered task (e.g., ERP faces with NimStim set), and a live, naturalistic interactive paradigm (e.g., infant interact with caregivers that show variety of facial expressions) aimed to assess the same construct (e.g., emotion perception). By comparing the neural responses from both tasks against a separate, established behavioral outcome (e.g., Infant Behavior Questionnaire), researchers can determine whether the naturalistic paradigms or the computer-based paradigm yield similar or different results. However, it is also important to note that a method that yielded more statistically significant results may not necessarily suggest superiority in that method. Researchers should consider the theoretical alignment between the research question and the research method (e.g., computer-based or naturalistic interactive task), along with the results to evaluate the utility of the novel paradigms.

Utility of Simulations

The variability with pediatric EEG data already raises significant questions regarding the appropriateness of adult-derived processing pipelines (Bell and Cuevas, 2012). When paired with the fluid, variable stimuli of novel naturalistic interactive tasks, the field lacks clear guidelines for basic processing parameters, leaving researchers to rely on subjective heuristics for important analytic decisions such as epoch lengths, trial contributions, and/or baseline selection. Thus, beyond implementing immediate practices to improve the interpretability, consistency and transparency in investigations with novel live naturalistic interactive EEG paradigms, we also recommend that the field move towards establishing processing and analytical standards. One approach to establishing such standards is through the use of simulation, particularly simulations based on empirical infant data. Specifically, researchers can use simulations to generate datasets with a known “ground truth” signal and quantifiable artifacts. Several platforms for data simulation exist (e.g., SEREEGA toolbox within MATLAB, Krol et al., 2018; EEGg function within MATLAB, Vaziri et al., 2023 etc.). In these stimulated data sets, researchers can systematically manipulate variables such as noise level or trial numbers to objectively determine the minimum or maximum thresholds required for reliable signal extraction. Furthermore, conducting multiverse analyses on these simulated datasets will reveal which analytic approach or processing pipelines are most robust, allowing the field to establish empirically grounded, data-driven recommendations that replace the current analytic ambiguities. These kinds of simulation analyses are already being conducted in the broader EEG/ERP research field (e.g., Heise et al., 2022, Heise et al., 2025; Sujatha Ravindran and Contreras-Vidal, 2023).

Summary

By discussing several important analytic decisions and empirically illustrating how different analytic decisions could impact the results of a novel interactive task, this manuscript takes the important first step to shed light on the pathway to a more transparent, replicable future of developmental cognitive neuroscience, particularly in investigations with live, naturalistic, interactive paradigms.
