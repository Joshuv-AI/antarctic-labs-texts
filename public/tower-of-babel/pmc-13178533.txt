Functional targeting of PTTG1 in pericytes restores vascular integrity in diabetic retina
Authors: Linyu Zhang, Ling Ren, Jingyue Zhang, Ya Zhao, Min Xia, Xiaosa Li, Mudi Yao, Fanfei Ma, Chang Jiang, Wan Mu, Jiao Xia, Jin Yao, Biao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pericyte loss is an early and critical event in the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy (DR), yet the molecular mechanisms underlying pericyte dysfunction remain incompletely understood. Using single-cell RNA sequencing, we generated a retinal cellular overview comprising 37,982 cells from diabetic and nondiabetic mice. We identified a previously unrecognized pericyte subpopulation defined by high expression of pituitary tumor–transforming gene 1 (Pttg1), which was enriched in diabetic retina. Functional studies demonstrated that CRISPR-Cas9– or small interfering RNA–mediated silencing of PTTG1 restored pericyte stability and barrier-supporting function under high-glucose stress. In vivo, Pttg1 silencing via viral or pericyte-specific adeno-associated virus delivery improved retinal vascular integrity and reduced retinal vascular dysfunction in diabetic mice. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling revealed that PTTG1 silencing reprogrammed metabolism, modulating glycolytic flux and attenuating oxidative stress. Furthermore, a therapeutic strategy using spherical nucleic acid–based siPttg1 nanocarriers (sTDN-siPttg1) substantially ameliorated retinal vascular dysfunction in DR model. These findings suggest that PTTG1 is a critical regulator of pericyte metabolic homeostasis and microvascular function in DR, highlighting its translational potential as a therapeutic target for diabetic microvascular complications.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common microvascular complication of diabetes and a leading cause of vision loss. It progresses from early vascular leakage and microaneurysms to abnormal neovascularization and retinal detachment (1, 2). A hallmark of early retinal microvascular injury is pericyte loss and apoptosis (3). Pericytes are specialized mural cells that envelop endothelial cells (ECs) within the microvessel basement membrane. They connect closely with ECs through junctions, using tiny filaments to maintain vessel stability and proper blood flow (4). In the retina, pericytes are abundant, with a pericyte-to-EC ratio of roughly 1:1 (5). They support EC stability and differentiation, maintain blood-retinal barrier, and mediate communication via platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and angiopoietin-1 signaling (6, 7). Under diabetic conditions, chronic hyperglycemia and metabolic stress impair pericyte-EC interactions, leading to pericyte detachment and vascular dysfunction. Although oxidative stress and advanced glycation end products have been implicated in pericyte loss (8, 9), the metabolic mechanisms linking hyperglycemia to pericyte dysfunction remain poorly understood. Understanding pericyte responses to diabetic stress is crucial for elucidating systemic vascular injury and identifying therapeutic targets.

Pericytes exhibit considerable heterogeneity across tissues, reflecting diverse origins and context-dependent functions. In the kidney, pericytes give rise to mesangial cells, which provide structural support to glomerular capillaries and regulate filtration dynamics (10). In the central nervous system, pericytes are indispensable components of blood-brain barrier, regulating endothelial tight junctions, maintaining vascular permeability, and supporting neurovascular unit integrity (11). In pulmonary hypertension, pericytes maintain vascular stability but can transform into contractile, smooth muscle–like cells under hypoxia-inducible factor 2α signaling, driving vascular remodeling (12). In the retina, where pericyte coverage is high, heterogeneous subtypes likely coordinate angiogenesis, vascular stabilization, and neurovascular coupling (13). However, the molecular features and specific roles of these subtypes in DR remain largely undefined.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables high-resolution profiling of transcriptomes across cell populations, facilitating the identification of disease-associated subtypes and gene regulatory programs (14). Applying this approach to diabetic retina allows precise delineation of pericyte heterogeneity and the discovery of subtype-specific mechanisms underlying vascular dysfunction. In this study, we combined scRNA-seq with functional assays to investigate pericyte heterogeneity in DR and identify genes linking metabolic stress to vascular dysfunction. We focused on pituitary tumor–transforming gene 1 (Pttg1), which was up-regulated in a distinct pericyte subcluster under diabetic conditions, and examined its role in metabolic reprogramming and vascular stability. We also developed a nanocarrier (sTDN-siPttg1) for efficient delivery of Pttg1 small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), which substantially alleviated diabetes-induced vascular dysfunction in vivo. Collectively, this study identifies PTTG1 as a critical regulator of pericyte dysfunction and establishes a translationally relevant therapeutic strategy for restoring microvascular integrity in DR.

RESULTS

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of diabetic retinas identifies a Pttg1-driven angiogenic pericyte subcluster

To characterize cellular changes associated with DR, we established a streptozotocin (STZ)–induced diabetic mouse model (fig. S1A and table S1) and performed scRNA-seq on retinas from nondiabetic and diabetic mice (Fig. 1A). After stringent quality control and batch correction based on detected gene number, unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, and mitochondrial gene proportion (fig. S1B), a total of 18,926 cells from nondiabetic retinas and 19,056 cells from diabetic retinas were retained, with strong concordance between nFeature_RNA and nCount_RNA in both groups (fig. S1C). Unsupervised clustering identified 34 distinct cell clusters visualized by Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (Fig. 1B), which were annotated into 10 major retinal cell types using canonical markers, including photoreceptors, Müller glia, bipolar cells, astrocytes, amacrine cells, retinal pigment epithelium, microglia, pericytes, ECs, and retinal ganglion cells (Fig. 1, C and D). Comparative analysis revealed disease-associated alterations in retinal cellular composition under diabetic conditions (Fig. 1E).

Given the critical role of pericytes in retinal vascular homeostasis (15), we investigated pericyte heterogeneity by UMAP-based subclustering. Three transcriptionally distinct pericyte populations were identified (Fig. 1F and fig. S1D): an angiogenic-like subcluster enriched for extracellular matrix interaction, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling, and angiogenesis-related genes; a stress response subcluster characterized by high expression of inflammatory and oxidative stress–related genes; and a contractile subcluster marked by high expression of smooth muscle–related genes, including Tagln, Acta2, and Tpm2 (Fig. 1G and fig. S1E).

To characterize the functional properties of angiogenic-like pericyte subcluster, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the top 200 highly expressed genes revealed significant enrichment in angiogenesis-related biological processes, including angiogenesis, blood vessel branching, vasculogenesis, and cell-cell adhesion (fig. S1F). KEGG and REACTOME analyses highlighted pathways involved in cell adhesion, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase–Akt signaling, and cell surface interactions (fig. S1G), supporting a role for this subcluster in angiogenesis. Notably, differential expression analysis identified Pttg1 as the most up-regulated gene in diabetic pericytes, with predominant expression in angiogenic-like subcluster and marked induction under diabetic conditions (Fig. 1, H to J).

PTTG1 expression is elevated in diabetic retina and mainly localized to pericytes

We next determined the expression pattern of PTTG1 in nondiabetic and diabetic retinas. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blots revealed a significant up-regulation of PTTG1 expression in diabetic retinas (Fig. 2, A and B). Longitudinal analysis at 2, 4, and 6 months after STZ injection showed a gradual increase in PTTG1 levels by Western blots (fig. S2A). Three-dimensional confocal imaging revealed that PTTG1 was largely confined to retinal pericytes, as indicated by colocalization with pericyte marker NG2 (Fig. 2C).

To further determine PTTG1 localization, immunostaining was performed for multiple retinal cell markers. PTTG1 showed minimal overlap with endothelial marker CD31, astrocyte marker glial fibrillary acidic protein, retinal ganglion cell marker RBPMS, or Müller glia marker GS, indicating that it is not substantially expressed in these cell types (fig. S2, B to E). In contrast, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated clear colocalization of PTTG1 with the pericyte markers NG2 and PDGFRβ (fig. S2, F and G). In line with its angiogenic role, PTTG1 expression was increased in the angiogenic regions of oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) model (fig. S3).

PTTG1 suppression improves pericyte function and mitochondrial health under high-glucose conditions

We next investigated the effects of PTTG1 silencing on pericyte function in vitro. PTTG1-specific siRNAs were designed and validated for efficient knockdown, which was confirmed by qRT-PCRs and Western blots (Fig. 3, A and B). Given the importance of pericyte recruitment in angiogenic process, Matrigel coculture assays were performed. Pericytes transfected with PTTG1 siRNA showed enhanced recruitment to ECs compared with control siRNA (Fig. 3C). High glucose can induce pericyte apoptosis, compromising vessel integrity (7, 16). Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) staining indicated that PTTG1 silencing reduced apoptosis under high-glucose conditions, as shown by fewer PI-positive cells (Fig. 3D). Since early pericyte apoptosis involves mitochondrial dysfunction and loss of membrane potential, we detected mitochondrial membrane potential using Rhodamine 123 and JC-1 staining. PTTG1 silencing reduced high glucose–induced mitochondrial membrane potential collapse, helping maintain mitochondrial polarization (Fig. 3, E and F).

To further validate these results, PTTG1 was knocked out in pericytes using CRISPR-Cas9–mediated genome editing (fig. S4, A and B). The outcomes were consistent with those observed in siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments (fig. Pttg1 silencing alleviates retinal vascular dysfunction and pericyte loss

To determine the role of PTTG1 in retinal vascular dysfunction, we established an STZ-induced diabetic mouse model. Three short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting Pttg1 were packaged into viral vectors and delivered via intravitreal injection. Seven days after injection, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed effective PTTG1 silencing, with shRNA3 achieving the highest suppression and selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 4, A and B).

Hyperglycemia can alter retinal blood flow and increase vascular permeability, leading to endothelial and pericyte detachment (17). Evans blue assays showed that Pttg1 shRNA substantially reduced vascular leakage in diabetic retinas (Fig. 4C). Retinal trypsin digestion revealed a marked decrease in acellular capillary formation following Pttg1 silencing (Fig. 4D). Immunofluorescent staining of IB4 and NG2 showed that Pttg1 silencing rescued pericyte loss compared to the controls (Fig. 4E).

On the basis of single-cell sequencing data linking Pttg1 up-regulation in pericyte subclusters to DR, we next used an adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying a Pttg1-targeting construct for cell-specific knockdown. Four weeks post–intravitreal injection, green fluorescent protein fluorescence from the AAV vector colocalized with pericyte marker NG2, confirming pericyte-specific delivery (fig. S5A). Diabetic mice treated with Pttg1 AAV showed reduced PTTG1 expression in pericytes (fig. S5B), accompanied by alleviation of pericyte loss and improvement in retinal vascular integrity (fig. S5, C to E). These results suggest that PTTG1 contributes to diabetes-induced retinal vascular dysfunction and pericyte loss in vivo and that its targeted knockdown can reduce these pathological changes.

Transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling reveals altered pathways following PTTG1 silencing

We performed transcriptomic and untargeted metabolomic analyses by RNA-seq and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (MS) to identify the changes in gene expression and metabolic pathways following PTTG1 silencing in pericytes. Pericytes transfected with PTTG1 siRNA were compared with those transfected with Scr siRNA (Fig. 5A). Principal components analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering between the two groups across three major components (Fig. 5B). A total of 974 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 525 up-regulated and 449 down-regulated genes in the PTTG1 silencing group (Fig. 5C). GO enrichment analysis showed that the top altered terms were primarily associated with cell division, apoptosis, and angiogenesis regulation (Fig. 5D), while pathway analysis highlighted notable effects on angiogenesis, apoptosis, and glycolysis (Fig. 5E), indicating that both angiogenic functions and key metabolic pathways were affected by PTTG1 silencing.

Metabolomic analysis using MS and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) identified 419 significantly altered metabolites, with 206 up-regulated and 213 down-regulated (vasoactive intestinal peptide, >1.0, P < 0.05; fig. S6, A and B). Integrative pathway enrichment of both DEGs and differential metabolites revealed glycolysis or gluconeogenesis as the most significantly altered pathway. Genes involved in glycolysis were down-regulated after PTTG1 silencing, and the pathway map highlights coordinated changes in both genes and metabolites (fig. S6, C to F).

PTTG1 silencing alleviates high glucose–induced mitochondrial dysfunction and glycolytic impairment in pericytes

Prolonged high glucose enhances pericyte glycolysis, leading to oxidative stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, antioxidant depletion, and mitochondrial damage (18). To assess the role of PTTG1 in glucose metabolism, we detected the expression of key glycolytic enzymes by Western blots and qRT-PCRs. Transfection of PTTG1 siRNA reduced HK1, ENO2, and LDHA expression, indicating a decreased glycolytic capacity in pericytes (Fig. 6, A and B). Seahorse assays further confirmed a decrease in basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve capacity following PTTG1 silencing (Fig. 6, C to F). In addition, the levels of lactate and pyruvate, key glycolytic metabolites, were reduced, further confirming impaired glycolytic activity (Fig. 6, G and H).

High glucose also triggers mitochondrial oxidative stress, causing energy deficits, mitochondria-dependent apoptosis, and pericyte loss (19). Since PTTG1 silencing alleviated changes in mitochondrial membrane potential, we examined its effect on oxidative stress and biosynthesis. High glucose reduced intracellular GSH and NADH, whereas PTTG1 silencing restored their levels, enhancing antioxidant capacity. Consistently, ROS levels measured by 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) were decreased following PTTG1 silencing (fig. S7, A to F). In addition, Seahorse assays showed that PTTG1 silencing did not affect basal respiration but increased maximal respiration and mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate production, while reducing proton leakage, indicating improved mitochondrial function (fig. S7, G to K). Collectively, these results indicate that PTTG1 silencing reduces high glucose–induced mitochondrial oxidative stress and preserves mitochondrial function in pericytes.

Clinical samples confirm aberrant PTTG1 expression in retinal diseases

To evaluate PTTG1 protein dysregulation in retinal diseases, we examined epiretinal membranes (ERMs) collected from patients with idiopathic ERM (iERM) and fibrovascular membranes (FVMs) from patients with proliferative DR (PDR) or retinopathy of prematurity (ROP). ERMs were used as nonneovascular controls, while FVMs exhibited typical fibrovascular features, including pathological neovascularization and inflammatory cell infiltration. Immunostaining showed minimal PTTG1 signal in iERM samples, whereas PDR- and ROP-derived FVMs exhibited markedly elevated PTTG1 expression. Notably, PTTG1 immunoreactivity was localized to NG2-positive pericytes within neovascular regions, indicating preferential enrichment in these cells (Fig. 7, A to C). This pericyte-associated localization is consistent with our single-cell transcriptomic findings and supports that aberrant PTTG1 expression in pericytes is closely linked to retinal vascular dysfunction.

Targeted silencing of Pttg1 via DNA nanocarrier–based siRNA platform alleviates retinal vascular dysfunction

The eye is an ideal target for RNA-based therapeutics because of its low dosing requirement. Since PTTG1 up-regulation drives pericyte impairment and retinal vascular dysfunction, we developed a therapeutic strategy targeting Pttg1. To overcome siRNA’s instability and poor cellular uptake (20), we used a stable, membrane-permeable nanocarrier (sTDN) to deliver siPttg1, forming sTDN-siPttg1 and improving both stability and retinal delivery efficiency. We evaluated the preclinical efficacy of intraocular sTDN-siPttg1 delivery in STZ-induced diabetic mice. siPttg1 was loaded onto tetrahedral framework nucleic acid (tFNA) to form sTDN-siPttg1 (Fig. 8A). The mice injected with sTDN-siPttg1 exhibited reduced retinal levels of PTTG1 and a marked reduction of retinal vascular leakage (Fig. 8, B and C). Trypsin digestion and immunofluorescence assays further demonstrated that sTDN-siPttg1 alleviated capillary degeneration in diabetic retinas, as shown by decreased number of acellular capillaries and reduced pericyte degeneration (Fig. 8, D and E). Electroretinography revealed improved scotopic b-wave amplitudes in sTDN-siPttg1–treated mice compared with the sTDN controls (Fig. 8, F and G), indicating that sTDN-siPttg1 not only reduces vascular dysfunction but also restores retinal function.

We next evaluated the in vivo safety of sTDN-siPttg1. Mice received intravitreal injections of either sTDN or sTDN-siPttg1 over a 1-month period (fig. S8A). Immunofluorescence analysis showed no significant loss of NeuN-positive retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) compared with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) controls, indicating no overt neurotoxicity (fig. S8B). RBPMS staining further confirmed that RGC density was unaffected by intravitreal administration of sTDN or sTDN-siPttg1 (fig. S8C). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated deoxyuridine triphosphate nick end labeling assays detected no apoptotic cells in retinal tissues at 1 week, 1 month, or 3 months postinjection (fig. S8D). These results indicate that intravitreal administration of sTDN or sTDN-siPttg1 is well tolerated and does not induce cytotoxic effects in retinal tissues.

DISCUSSION

DR is a major microvascular complication of diabetes, with early pericyte dysfunction serving as a key initiating event that precedes vascular leakage, ischemia, and pathological neovascularization (21, 22). Although anti-VEGF therapies can control advanced lesions, they do not reverse early microvascular dysfunction and are limited by the requirement for repeated dosing and the development of resistance (23, 24). By combining single-cell transcriptomics, metabolomics, and functional studies, we identify PTTG1 as a critical driver of pericyte dysfunction. Under diabetic stress, a Pttg1+ pro-angiogenic–like pericyte subset expands, and PTTG1 orchestrates metabolic reprogramming toward excessive glycolysis, causing redox imbalance and mitochondrial stress. Silencing Pttg1 using a tFNA-based nanocarrier protects retinal vessels in diabetic mice.

A major advance of this study is the redefinition of pericyte heterogeneity in the diabetic retina. Although pericytes are often considered a uniform population (25), single-cell analysis reveals three distinct subsets: inflammatory-like, contractile, and pro-angiogenic–like pericytes. Among these, only the Pttg1+ subset shows significant expansion and disease-associated transcriptional changes in diabetes, with activation of angiogenesis-, adhesion-, and growth factor–related pathways. Pericytes show pronounced inter- and intratissue heterogeneity due to their dual neuroectodermal and mesodermal origin (26): dense NG2+/CD13+ networks in the central nervous system and retina, smooth muscle cell (SMC)-like alpha-smooth muscle actin–positive (α-SMA+) pericytes in cardiac and skeletal muscle (27), and loosely associated hepatic stellate cells storing vitamin A (28). scRNA-seq studies have revealed pericyte diversity across tissues, including fibroblast-pericyte heterogeneity (29) and metastasis-promoting Tcf21+ subsets (30), yet retinal pericyte heterogeneity remains underexplored. Our findings emphasize that understanding pericyte subpopulations is critical for elucidating DR pathogenesis and defining precise therapeutic targets.

PTTG1, a well-established oncogene regulating cell cycle, DNA repair, and metabolism, is overexpressed in multiple malignancies, including pituitary adenomas, hepatocellular carcinoma, gliomas, and gastrointestinal tumors. Its silencing suppresses glioma proliferation, migration, and invasion (31); reduces inflammatory cytokine expression via MAPK signaling (32); and alleviates hepatic fibrosis (33). In addition, PTTG1 overexpression enhances fibroblast growth factor and VEGF secretion, thereby promoting angiogenesis (34). Consistent with these roles, Pttg1 silencing in diabetic pericytes reduces high glucose–induced apoptosis and mitochondrial depolarization and restores pericyte-endothelial interactions, suggesting that PTTG1 is a potential target for early DR intervention, potentially complementing or surpassing anti-VEGF therapy.

Mechanistically, PTTG1 acts as an upstream transcriptional regulator that coordinates metabolic reprogramming in pericytes. It up-regulates HK1 and LDHA, leading to increased lactate accumulation, redox imbalance, and mitochondrial oxidative stress. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses reveal a glycolytic shift in diabetic pericytes, whereas PTTG1 silencing normalizes glycolysis, restores mitochondrial function, reduces ROS, and improves redox homeostasis. The modest increase in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) following silencing likely reflects compensatory mitochondrial activity rather than a shift to oxidative phosphorylation, as tricarboxylic acid cycle metabolites remain unchanged. These findings position PTTG1 as a central metabolic node linking hyperglycemia to pericyte loss and impaired endothelial interactions, consistent with its established role in cancer metabolism (35) and previous evidence that glycolysis shapes pericyte function across tissues (18, 36). Our results extend recent studies highlighting the importance of pericyte metabolism in microvascular disease: pericyte senescence driven by MAT2A down-regulation and impaired mevalonate-CoQ biosynthesis delays wound healing in diabetes, while glycolytic activation in ECs via PFKFB3 contributes to pathological angiogenesis in DR (37, 38). Together, these studies support the concept that maintaining metabolic balance is essential for proper pericyte function and vascular stability.

Therapeutically, this study proposes a paradigm shift beyond conventional anti-VEGF strategy by targeting PTTG1 to restore vascular support through correction of intrinsic pericyte dysfunction, particularly relevant in early-stage DR. Using a tFNA-based nanocomplex (sTDN-siPttg1), we achieved preferential siRNA delivery to retinal vessels, and intravitreal administration in diabetic mice improved vascular structure and function without detectable toxicity. PTTG1 effects are context dependent: While it can facilitate vascular repair in acute injury, chronic hyperglycemia drives pathological remodeling, highlighting the necessity for spatiotemporally precise, pericyte-targeted interventions to maximize therapeutic benefit and minimize off-target effects (39, 40). Although PTTG1 inhibition improves vascular function, the progressive nature of DR requires long-term evaluation to determine its effects on advanced pathology, including fibrosis and neovascularization. As a well-established oncogene involved in cell proliferation, PTTG1 suppression may carry risks in other cell types. Excessive or nonspecific inhibition could impair vascular-repair cells or disrupt retinal neuronal organization, potentially causing mitotic defects and compromising tissue regeneration (41, 42). These safety concerns are particularly relevant when considering clinical translation. Our pericyte-targeted delivery strategy is designed to reduce such risks, highlighting the need for precision in both target selection and therapeutic delivery.

Despite these advances, several limitations still remain. First, pericytes constitute only a small fraction of retinal vascular cells and progressively decline in DR, which limits their capture in whole-retina scRNA-seq dataset (26) and reduced the resolution for identifying rare or functionally important subpopulations. Future studies using pericyte-enrichment strategies, such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting of pericyte-specific reporter mice (43) or microvascular fraction isolation (44) combined with deeper sequencing, will help improve coverage. Second, our transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses relied largely on high glucose in vitro systems and STZ-induced diabetic mice, which do not fully recapitulate the metabolic complexity or chronic progression of human type 2 diabetes. Validation in metabolically relevant models, including db/db mice or human retinal organoids, will be important. In addition, although the nanocarrier preferentially targeted pericytes, absolute cell-type specificity remains to be optimized, and the use of pericyte-specific Cre lines may help minimize off-target effects (45). Last, given the role of PTTG1 in fundamental cellular processes, long-term safety and potential impacts on physiological retinal repair should be carefully evaluated in extended in vivo studies.

In summary, this study identifies a disease-driving Pttg1+ pericyte subpopulation in DR and reveals PTTG1-mediated metabolic reprogramming as a key link between hyperglycemia and vascular instability. By focusing on pericyte metabolism rather than endothelial angiogenic signals, we propose a precise, potentially disease-modifying intervention for early DR, and our pericyte-targeted therapy demonstrates encouraging translational potential.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Single-cell library construction and sequencing

Retinal tissues were collected from diabetic and age-matched nondiabetic mice for scRNA-seq. Following intraperitoneal anesthesia, the mice were transcardially perfused with precooled 0.9% saline. The eyes were immediately enucleated into ice-cold PBS. Under ribonuclease-free conditions, the whole retinas, including both central and peripheral regions, were carefully isolated on a chilled platform under a stereomicroscope. The isolated retinas were then minced and enzymatically dissociated with papain (1 mg/ml) in digestion buffer containing l-cysteine, EDTA, and 2-mercaptoethanol at 37°C for 30 min. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70-μm strainer, followed by red blood cell depletion. Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue staining, and samples with ≥80% viability were used for single-cell library preparation.

Single-cell transcriptomic libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10× Genomics) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, single-cell suspensions were loaded onto the Chromium Controller to generate gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs). Cells were lysed within GEMs, and polyadenylated mRNA was captured and barcoded during reverse transcription. After breaking the emulsions, cDNA was purified and subsequently amplified. The libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a paired-end configuration.

Single-cell sequencing data analysis

scRNA-seq data were analyzed using the R package Seurat (v4.3.0). UMIs were used to remove PCR duplicates. Cells were filtered out if they expressed fewer than 200 or more than 8000 genes, or if mitochondrial transcripts accounted for more than 20% of total UMIs. In addition, cells with fewer than 500 or more than 100,000 total UMI counts were excluded. After quality control, 37,982 cells were retained for downstream analyses.

Dimensionality reduction was performed by PCA using the top 2000 highly variable genes. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. Cell clustering was performed on the basis of a shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization approach. Cluster identities were manually annotated according to DEGs identified by FindAllMarkers, in combination with published literature and the CellMarker database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker).

Pericyte subclusters were identified using the FindClusters function in Seurat. The distribution of cell populations was visualized in two-dimensional space using the UMAP algorithm. To characterize the functional features of each subcluster, the top 200 DEGs were subjected to GO enrichment analysis. Pathway enrichment analyses were further performed using KEGG and REACTOME databases through the DAVID Bioinformatics Resources platform (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/home.jsp).

Animal experiment

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (approval no. 2410049) and conformed to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) and maintained under standard conditions (12-hour light/dark cycle, 20° to 25°C, 40 to 60% humidity) with free access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted with the experimenters blinded to group allocation during treatment administration and outcome assessment to minimize potential bias.

STZ-induced diabetic model

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (20 to 25 g) were randomly assigned to diabetic or control groups before treatment. Diabetes was induced via intraperitoneal injection of STZ (50 mg/kg) for five consecutive days following a 6-hour fast, while control mice received an equivalent volume of citrate buffer. Hyperglycemia was confirmed by measuring random tail-vein blood glucose levels using a OneTouch Ultra glucometer. Urinary glucose levels were monitored monthly in both groups using a mouse urine glucose assay kit (Meiao Biotech Co. Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Body weight was recorded at each time point using an analytical balance. Inclusion criteria required sustained hyperglycemia (≥16.7 mM) and detectable urinary glucose, and body weight loss within a physiologically expected range. Exclusion criteria included failure to develop hyperglycemia or detectable urinary glucose, severe weight loss (>20% of baseline), signs of systemic illness, or death unrelated to experimental procedures. The relevant data are shown in table S1.

Optical coherence tomography

High-resolution cross-sectional imaging of the mouse retina was performed before and after modeling using an optical coherence tomography system (YG-20 W MAX, TowardPi Medical, China). Scans were centered on the optic nerve head, with vertical B scans acquired to enable detailed layer segmentation into three compartments: the inner retinal composite, the middle retinal layer, and the photoreceptor-retinal pigment epithelium complex. This stratified quantification scheme enabled accurate morphometric evaluation of treatment effects across all major retinal substructures.

OIR model

The mouse pups and their nursing dams were exposed to 75% oxygen from postnatal day 7 (P7) to P12, followed by a return to room air from P12 to P17. Retinal tissues were harvested for analysis at P17. Age-matched littermates maintained in room air throughout the experiment served as normoxic controls.

Cell culture and transfection

Human retinal vascular ECs (ACBRI-181) were cultured in EC medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (penicillin-streptomycin). Pericytes (ACBRI-183) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution at 37°C under 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity. Cells between passages 3 and 7 were used for all experiments. PTTG1-targeting siRNA and scrambled control siRNA (Scr siRNA) duplexes were obtained from Guangzhou RiboBio Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequences of all siRNAs are listed in table S2.

CRISPR-Cas9–mediated PTTG1 knockout in pericytes

PTTG1 was knocked out in pericytes using a CRISPR-Cas9 system. A recombinant viral vector delivering Cas9 protein and a single guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting exon 2 of PTTG1 (sgRNA sequence: 5′-AGAAAAGTCTGTAAAGCCAAGG-3′) was generated. The sgRNA was chosen for high on-target efficiency and minimal off-target potential, directing Cas9 to induce a double-strand break (DSB) downstream of PAM sequence. DSBs were repaired by the endogenous nonhomologous end joining pathway, introducing small indels predicted to disrupt PTTG1 reading frame. Seventy-two hours posttransduction, genomic DNA was extracted, and the target region was PCR-amplified for Sanger sequencing to confirm indel formation. Successful PTTG1 knockout was further verified by Western blots.

Clinical sample collection

This study was conducted according to the principle of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the ARVO Statement on Human Subjects. All participants in this study gave written informed consent for the protocol that was approved by Eye Hospital’s Institutional Review Board (Ethics approval number AF/47/03.0). Control ERMs were collected from patients with iERM (mean age 52.48 ± 9.40 years; three males and two females) who had no history of diabetes or other retinal vascular disorders. FVMs were obtained from patients with PDR (mean age 67.64 ± 6.23 years; two males and three females), characterized by neovascularization requiring surgical intervention, and from preterm infants with stages 4 and 5 ROP (mean age 1.71 ± 0.95 years; two males and three females) requiring vitrectomy. Exclusion criteria included a history of ocular trauma, prior pars plana vitrectomy, or other intraocular surgery that could affect retinal architecture, any form of glaucoma, and coexisting retinal diseases potentially affecting vascular or macular integrity. All membranes were excised under sterile conditions, immediately placed in ice-cold PBS, and embedded for cryosectioning. Frozen sections were prepared, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, washed, and processed for immunofluorescence using identical protocols for both iERM and PDR/ROP membranes. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, and high-resolution images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Synthesis of sTDN-siPttg1

tFNA carrying siRNAs were synthesized via a programmable self-assembly method. Four custom-designed single-stranded DNAs were dissolved in TM buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 50 mM MgCl2·6H2O] and subjected to thermal treatment at 95°C for 10 min, followed by rapid cooling to 4°C for 20 min to form a stable tetrahedral structure. siRNA sequences targeting Pttg1 were then mixed with the assembled tFNA at a 1:1 molar ratio and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour, allowing efficient siRNA loading via electrostatic interactions or sequence complementarity. The resulting sTDN-siPttg1 complexes were stored at 4°C. Detailed sequences are shown in table S3.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism was used for statistical analysis. Data were expressed as means ± SD. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Bartlett’s test. For normally distributed data with equal variances, an unpaired Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. For in vivo data, each n value represents one mouse with one eye analyzed per animal. For in vitro data, each n represents an independent biological replicate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
