Determinants of cardiopulmonary fitness in individuals poststroke: based on quantile regression analysis
Authors: Zeyan Zhang, Zhixuan Duan, Huiqun Wu, Guodong Wang, Hua Ling, Pengkun Li, Sijia Wu, Xiaoxia Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo understand the factors influencing cardiorespiratory fitness after stroke and the differential effects at varying levels of cardiorespiratory fitness.MethodsThis prospective study analyzed the factors affecting cardiorespiratory fitness via quantile regression with weighted least squares regression.ResultsThe study included 80 subjects. Both univariate and multivariate quantile regression analyses indicated that higher Fugl-Meyer assessment scores were associated with better cardiorespiratory fitness. The impact of the Fugl-Meyer assessment score on peak oxygen uptake increased with increasing quantiles.ConclusionThe FMA score was positively associated with peak oxygen uptake, and quantile regression suggested that this association may vary across the peak oxygen uptake distribution. These findings improve the understanding of the relationship between motor function and cardiorespiratory fitness after stroke and may inform future studies on stratified rehabilitation approaches after further validation.Trial registrationBeijing Bo’ a.i. Introduction

For able-bodied individuals, cardiopulmonary fitness (CRF) is a significant predictor of chronic diseases [1]. After a stroke, CRF decreases markedly, leading to cardiovascular complications that further diminish CRF and impair glycolipid metabolism, creating a vicious cycle of reduced physical activity and functional decline [2, 3]. Research has shown that aerobic exercise can increase peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) and walking endurance in stroke patients, thereby improving cardiorespiratory fitness and functional recovery after stroke [4, 5]. Higher levels of CRF have also been associated with lower levels of poststroke fatigue and better functional outcomes, including improved motor function and daily living ability [6]. Consequently, a thorough understanding of CRF in poststroke patients and the factors influencing it is vital for assessing and mitigating the potential risk of disability following a stroke.

CRF represents the capacity of the circulatory and respiratory systems to supply oxygen to skeletal muscles during sustained, moderate to vigorous physical activity [7]. In stroke patients, the prevalence of impaired neuromotor function, reduced functional muscle volume, diminished oxidative capacity in paralyzed muscles, and the presence of comorbidities make VO2peak a commonly used clinical measure to describe the highest level of CRF.

There is limited information on the patient characteristics that influence CRF after stroke. In a study of 113 individuals with a recent transient ischemic attack or minor ischemic stroke, lower VO2peak was associated with lower physical activity level, cardiovascular or pulmonary disease history, and vascular risk factors, whereas previous stroke history and stroke-related characteristics, such as stroke type, stroke severity, and time since the index event, were not significantly associated with VO2peak [8]. Another study demonstrated that CRF was related to activity limitations and beta-blocker medication use [9]. However, these studies did not specifically address CRF in patients during the subacute phase of stroke and were broad concerning the duration of the patient’s illness. In this study, we focused on CRF in patients during the subacute phase of stroke and explored the clinical and functional factors associated with it, in order to improve the understanding of physical health determinants of CRF after stroke and provide a basis for future rehabilitation research.

After reviewing the published studies, it was observed that most scholars utilized logistic regression and linear regression models to examine the factors influencing CRF [9, 10]. However, these methods do not adequately capture the relationships between the influencing factors and the distribution of CRF. To address this limitation, this study employed both weighted least squares (WLS) regression to analyze correlations and quantile regression to assess the influencing factors across different distributions of CRF. This approach allows for a more detailed and comprehensive exploration of the relationships between independent and dependent variables, enabling tailored recommendations for patients with varying levels of CRF. Additionally, this analysis yields more robust results [11].

Materials and methods

Participants

This prospective study involved stroke patients who underwent rehabilitation in the Department of Neurological Rehabilitation at Beijing Bo’ a.i. Hospital. A total of 80 patients were recruited, and the sample size met the required rule of having 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables, as suggested by the regression model. The study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the China Rehabilitation Research Centre (2022–135–01). Participants are permitted to include their data by signing the informed consent form or afterward by a no-objection letter.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria outlined in the 2018 edition of the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke or the 2019 edition of the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Cerebral Hemorrhage; (2) first-ever stroke with a single lesion confirmed by CT or MRI; (3) duration of the disease between 1 and 6 months, presence of hemiparesis, Brunnstrom stage ≥ 3 in the lower limbs on the affected side, functional ambulation category (FAC) score ≥ 2, and ability to walk short distances without significant deep or superficial sensory deficits; (4) good subjective initiative with the ability to understand and cooperate with the tests; (5) no severe organic lesions such as severe heart disease, severe hypertension, significant hyperglycemia, severe vascular malformations, or mental disorders; and (6) informed consent.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) unstable conditions, such as postural hypotension, hypoglycemia, or severe dizziness; (2) significant cognitive impairment, depression, etc.; (3) serious cardiopulmonary dysfunction, such as arrhythmia, heart failure, pulmonary edema, pulmonary embolism, or related infectious diseases; and (4) severe functional disorders of the hips, knees, or ankles or significant sequelae.

These criteria were determined through routine clinical evaluation before exercise testing. Vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, and blood glucose levels, were assessed to identify unstable medical conditions. Cognitive and psychological status were evaluated by experienced rehabilitation physicians based on clinical assessment and medical records to determine whether patients were able to understand and cooperate with the testing procedures. Cardiopulmonary dysfunction was determined according to physician diagnosis documented in the medical records. Musculoskeletal limitations were assessed through physical examination to ensure that patients were able to safely perform the cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) and the six-minute walking test (6MWT).

Therefore, the study population mainly represented a relatively mildly to moderately impaired subgroup of medically stable patients in the subacute phase of stroke, rather than the entire subacute stroke population.

To evaluate the feasibility of exercise testing in subacute stroke patients, we recorded the completion rate and tolerance of both the 6MWT and CPET. (The flowchart is shown in Fig. 1)

Data collection

(1) Baseline data collection: General clinical information, including demographic details (name, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI)), type of illness, and time since stroke, was collected from patients. (2) Exercise function assessment: This assessment included the Fugl-Meyer assessment (FMA) score, the FAC scale score, the modified Ashworth scale(MAS) score, the results from the 6MWT (walking distance, changes in diastolic and systolic blood pressure before and after exercise, changes in oxygen saturation before and after exercise, changes in the Borg score before and after exercise, and resting heart rate), and the results from the CPET. (3) Assessment of Activities of Daily Living: The modified Barthel Index Scale(MBI) was used to assess the ability to perform activities of daily living.

Assessment

Six-minute walking test

The 6MWT is a widely used assessment method for measuring the distance an individual can walk in six minutes, providing a valid and reliable measure of motor endurance and functional ability [12]. Patients are instructed to walk for six minutes in a 30-meter-long corridor. The tester encourages the patient every minute via standardized phrases and records the patient’s vital signs, walking distance, and Borg score [13].

Cardiopulmonary exercise test

The CPET was conducted via a Type Master Screen-CPX cardiopulmonary exercise tester, which was administered by a professional. After 3 min of rest and 3 min of warm-up at 0 watts, a ramp-up protocol was implemented on the basis of the subject’s estimated maximal exercise capacity, aiming for the exercise phase to last approximately 8–12 min. Patients were instructed to cycle at 60–65 revolutions per minute when possible. The test was terminated if the patient’s cycling speed dropped below 50 revolutions per minute due to fatigue and weakness or if the physician deemed it inappropriate to continue. A 3-minute cooling phase was then performed at 10% of the maximum power. Respiratory gas exchange, electrocardiogram, and arterial oxygen saturation were continuously monitored throughout the test [14].

Following clinical CPET guidance, attainment of a near-maximal effort was considered supported when either one strong indicator (Respiratory Exchange Ratio(RER) ≥ 1.0) was present [5], or when two moderate indicators (%HRmax ≥ 85% and Rating of Perceived Exertion(RPE) ≥ 17) were achieved concurrently [15, 16].

Observation indicators

(1)Type of disease: Two types of disease were considered: cerebral infarction and cerebral hemorrhage. (2) Time since stroke: The time since stroke onset was defined as the time from the onset of the disease to when the patient underwent the cardiopulmonary exercise test. (3) FMA score: Motor function was assessed by an experienced clinician using the simplified FMA scale. In the present study, the total FMA motor score, calculated as the sum of the upper-extremity and lower-extremity subscores, was used for analysis. (4) FAC scale: The score is as follows: the patient walks with continuous or intermittent support from one person: 2 points; the patient walks without the need for direct physical support but under supervision: 3 points; the patient can walk independently on flat surfaces but requires assistance on stairs, slopes, or uneven surfaces: 4 points; and the patient can walk independently: 5 points. (5) MAS: Patients were categorized as having either present or absent dystonia. (6) 6MWT results: Monitored variables included diastolic and systolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation, heart rate, and the Borg score, both before and after the test. (7) VO2peak: The average value obtained during the last 30 s of the CPET [17].

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted via Stata 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). For descriptive statistical analysis, categorical variables are presented as proportions (%), whereas continuous variables are expressed as the means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges), depending on the distribution of values. The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. To compare differences between the two groups, an independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed data, the Mann‒Whitney U test was used for nonnormally distributed data, and the chi-square test was used for categorical data.

Quantile regression

WLS regression was used to estimate the mean effect of influencing factors on CRF. Additionally, univariate and multivariate quantile regression analyses were conducted after 20 iterations via the bootstrap-intensive algorithm to investigate the factors significantly associated with CRF and its distribution. The dependent variable was VO2peak, while the independent variables were various patient characteristics. Separate univariate and multivariate models were constructed for each selected VO2peak quartile (25th, 50th, and 75th) to determine whether different covariate associations existed across distributions. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General information

A total of 82 patients were initially recruited for this study. Among them, 80 patients successfully completed both the 6MWT and CPET. One patient was unable to participate because of a fracture sustained from a fall before the test, and another patient declined participation for unknown reasons. Therefore, the final analysis included 80 participants. No serious adverse events occurred during the exercise testing procedures, indicating that CPET and the 6MWT were generally feasible and well tolerated in this subacute stroke cohort. Among the 80 participants who completed CPET, 64 reached RER ≥ 1.0. Of the remaining 16 participants who did not reach RER ≥ 1.0, all achieved both %HRmax ≥ 85% and RPE ≥ 17. Therefore, all participants met the predefined criteria for near-maximal effort during CPET. The study population had a mean age of 52.7 ± 13.4 years and consisted predominantly of male participants (81.2%). The median VO2peak was 14.75 ml/kg/min (12.48–16.78), with no significant difference between males and females (P > 0.05). WLS regression and quantile regression

Given the positively skewed distribution of the dependent variable, the traditional mean regression model is inadequate for fully capturing its distribution. In contrast, quantile regression provides a more comprehensive analysis by examining the relationships between independent and dependent variables across different quantiles. This method is more robust, as it is less affected by unequal data distributions and extreme outliers [18]. Therefore, this study employed univariate and multivariate quantile regression analyses at various quantile levels to identify the independent factors influencing CRF, with the results presented in Tables 2 and 3. Additionally, considering the heteroskedasticity of the cross-sectional data, WLS regression was used to estimate the mean effect of influencing factors on peak oxygen uptake.

Univariate quantile regression

Univariate quantile regression analyses revealed that time since stroke, FMA score, modified Barthel Index score, FAC scale score, and 6MWT distance were correlated with VO2peak across different quartiles. The time since stroke was negatively correlated with VO2peak, whereas the FMA score, MBI score, FAC scale score, and 6MWT distance were positively correlated with VO2peak.

Multivariate quantile regression

When these five independent variables were incorporated into the multivariate quantile regression model, only the FMA score was significantly associated with VO2peak at the 25th percentile (P = 0.005), 50th percentile (P = 0.006), and 75th percentile (P = 0.016). These percentiles corresponded to VO2peak values of 9.870, 11.044, and 12.407 ml/kg/min, respectively. To further illustrate the quantile regression results, the fitted coefficients across quantiles were plotted (Fig. 2). In Fig. 2, the gray shaded area represents the 95% confidence interval of the quantile regression coefficients, the blue solid line represents the quantile regression coefficients, the black solid line represents the WLS regression coefficients, and the black dashed lines represent the 95% confidence interval of the WLS regression coefficients.

The regression coefficients for the FMA score were consistently greater than 0 across the examined quantiles, indicating a positive association between motor function and VO2peak. The fitted quantile regression curve suggested that this association tended to become stronger at higher quantiles of VO2peak. However, the confidence intervals widened at the extreme quantiles, indicating lower precision in these regions; therefore, the estimates at the tails of the distribution should be interpreted cautiously. The coefficients were estimated using bootstrap resampling, which improves the stability of quantile regression estimates.

Discussion

The results of this study revealed reduced CRF in stroke patients, with no statistically significant difference between sexes. The median VO2peak in our cohort was 14.75 ml/kg/min (12.48–16.78). This value falls within the range of 8–22 ml/kg/min reported in a systematic review of VO2peak in individuals after stroke [19] and is comparable to the mean VO2peak of 16.7 ml/kg/min reported in an Austrian chronic stroke cohort. However, the VO2peak observed in our cohort was substantially lower than that reported in able-bodied individuals. For example, a previous report indicated that the average CRF of individuals after stroke was approximately 53% of that of healthy individuals [20]. Previous studies have suggested that functional independence in the general population may be compromised when VO2max falls below approximately 18 ml/kg/min in men and 15 ml/kg/min in women [19]. Given that the median VO2peak in the present study was below these thresholds, our findings further highlight the substantial reduction in CRF among patients in the subacute phase of stroke.

Multivariate quantile regression analysis showed that the FMA score was the only variable significantly associated with VO2peak across the 25th, 50th, and 75th quantiles, whereas time since stroke, MBI, FAC, and 6MWD were not independently associated with VO2peak in the multivariate model. These findings suggest that motor impairment, as assessed by the FMA, is closely related to CRF in patients during the subacute phase of stroke.

However, this association should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of causality. One possible explanation is that reduced motor function may be associated with impaired skeletal muscle oxygen utilization and lower physical activity levels after stroke, which could contribute to lower CRF. Conversely, impaired motor function may also limit the ability to achieve a true peak effort during CPET, such that the measured VO2peak may partly reflect motor limitation rather than pure cardiorespiratory capacity. Therefore, the relationship between motor function and VO2peak in stroke patients is likely complex and potentially bidirectional.

In the present study, CPET completion was high, and all participants who completed CPET met the predefined criteria for near-maximal effort based on RER, %HRmax, and RPE. These findings support the interpretability of the measured VO2peak in this cohort.

In this study, we further examined the association between FMA score and VO2peak across different quantiles of VO2peak using quantile regression. The results suggested that the positive association between FMA score and VO2peak tended to be stronger at higher VO2peak quantiles. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly at the extreme quantiles, and should not be taken as evidence for specific rehabilitation strategies. Rather, they indicate that the relationship between motor function and CRF may vary across the VO2peak distribution. From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that motor function assessment may provide useful complementary information when interpreting CRF in patients during the subacute phase of stroke, although further validation is needed before these observations can inform stratified rehabilitation approaches.

Strengths and limitations

While most previous studies did not focus on the duration of stroke patients or subdivide them by phase, making their findings more general, the present study specifically explored the CRF of stroke patients in the subacute phase and the factors affecting it. This approach facilitates earlier identification of CRF issues in stroke patients and allows timely interventions. Additionally, by using quantile regression analysis, this study further examined the influence of the FMA score on VO2peak across different levels of CRF, building on previous research into factors affecting CRF to enable more precise interventions. However, this study included only 80 patients, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to further validate the present results. In addition, the inclusion and exclusion criteria selected a relatively specific subgroup of patients in the subacute phase of stroke. Participants were required to be medically stable and to have at least limited walking ability, while patients with unstable conditions, severe cardiopulmonary disease, marked musculoskeletal limitations, or inability to cooperate with testing were excluded. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all patients in the subacute phase of stroke, particularly those with more severe neurological deficits, severe medical comorbidities, or substantial mobility limitations.

Conclusions

This study showed that Chinese patients in the subacute phase of stroke had reduced CRF, with a median VO2peak comparable to that reported in previous stroke cohorts. The FMA score was positively associated with VO2peak, and the quantile regression analysis suggested that this association may vary across the VO2peak distribution. These findings add to the current understanding of the relationship between motor function and CRF after stroke, but do not yet support specific rehabilitation recommendations. Further studies in larger and independent cohorts are needed to validate these findings and to explore their implications for future rehabilitation research.
