Optimum anastomosis angle of end-to-side microsurgical anastomosis for preventing future shear-related risk of thrombosis by computational modeling
Authors: Patcharaporn Wongchadakul, Apram Jyot, Kongkrit Chaiyasate, Suphalerk Lohasammakul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnd-to-side (ETS) microsurgical anastomosis is a powerful technique in microsurgery. It can overcome vessel’s diameter discrepancy and preserve the distal blood flow. Optimal angle of the ETS anastomosis has been debated and studied, and is currently limited to animal models. Computational model using Finite Element Analysis (FEA) was used to simulate the models with 45 (model45), 90 (model90), and 135 (model135) ETS anastomosis angles. Flow dynamics including flow velocity and wall shear rate were studied and compared. Maximum flow velocity ranged from the highest to the lowest in model135, model90, and model45, respectively. The velocity in model135 showed fluctuation flow. The maximum wall shear rate and distribution were highest in model45 and lowest in model90. ETS anastomosis angle of 90 degrees provided the most favourable flow dynamics to prevent shear related thrombosis in comparison to ETS anastomosis at 45 and 135 degrees.Subject terms: Medical research, Computational science, Risk factors

Introduction

In light of the paradigm shift from the reconstructive ladder to the reconstructive elevator in plastic surgery, free tissue transfer is now emerging as a common procedure1,2. This procedure involves 2 key vessels: donor and recipient. The donor vessel is transferred with the tissue to anastomose with the recipient vessel that resides in the area to be reconstructed. End-to-end (ETE) anastomosis is preferred in the vessels with comparable diameters. End-to-side (ETS) anastomosis, on the other hand, is performed to overcome the diameter discrepancy or to preserve the distal blood flow in an extremity with impaired vascularity3. Clinically, anastomotic angle is a factor that defines anastomotic success. In the field of vascular surgery, there are various studies regarding the optimal anastomotic angle4–8. However, the anastomotic angle is created based on surgeon’s preferences and experience in the field of reconstructive microsurgery. The common angles of ETS anastomosis are 45 and 90 degrees. As far as we know, the evidence regarding ETS anastomotic angle in extremely small vessels is scarce and is usually limited to animal models6,9. This may be due to the existence of numerous limitations for conducting a clinical study in a human model. Further understanding of the physiologic changes within the anastomosis from the human model would be beneficial and would help in intraoperative decision making. These include velocity of blood flow and shear force disturbing in the vessel wall, as bothaffect thrombosis risk - the most concerning cause of free flap failure. Finite element analysis (FEA) is widely used and commonly accepted method in clinical applications, such as anatomic implant placement10. In this study, it was therefore constructed to evaluate the physiologic changes of blood flow within the various angles of ETS anastomosis and todeduce the optimal ETS angle to mitigate intravascular shearing forces and the subsequent vascular thrombosis.

Methods

Formulation of the problem

Shear rate is a critical factor in vascular wellness and pathology. It is related to the conformational change of Von Willebrand factor (vWF)11. In a small vessel, the process is typically initiated and stepped up to critical activation when the shear rates close to the vessel wall are around 1,000 s−1 and 5,000 s−1, respectively11. The extreme rate that exceeds 10,000 s−1 will efficiently capture platelets near the vessel wall. Therefore, high shear rate is considered a key factor for platelet aggregation, thrombus formation, arterial stenosis and wall injury. As a vicious cycle, arterial stenosis leads to the formation of high shear rate zones that can trigger explosive platelet aggregation and thrombosis12.

This study employs fluid-structure interaction (FSI) to analyse 3D blood flow within an elastic fistula. The blood and arteries were considered within the fluid and structural domains, respectively. The model utilised FEA through COMSOL Multiphysics for this investigation.

Verification and validation of the model

For representing an ETS anastomosis in a patient, vessel characteristics were obtained from the Department of Anatomy Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University. In adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines, the data were collected from 10 fresh-frozen cadavers (20 specimens of each vessel) that were donated to Siriraj hospital for studying purpose and informed consent was obtained from their legal guardians. The collection was performed after approval of the Ethics Committee of Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Thailand (protocol number MU-MOU 297/2566(Exempt). These data points including average vessel wall thickness of 320 m, average diameter of 3 mm in ATA and 2 mm in SCIA (Fig. 1). These data and blood flow were verified with the data from previous studies13,30–34.

Accuracy of the numerical model was validated by comparing simulation results with relevant literature35. Due to limitations in obtaining numerical data for ETS anastomosis under perfectly matched conditions, the results were compared with different geometries and conditions of blood vessels using the similar procedure and numerical methodology. The corresponding outcomes assured result accuracy and reliability (Fig. 2).

Part I: Flow patterns and velocity (Fig. 3(a)-(c) and Fig. 4)

Under the similar vascular conditions and inflow, the different anastomosis angles yielded different progression of the blood flow patterns into the SCIA. At the right-handed side of the anastomoses in model45 and model90, the central streamline within the ATA became a winding bundle before flowing into the SCIA (Fig. 3(a)-(b)). This pattern was not observed in the model135 (Fig. 3f(c)).

Exhibition of the flow velocity was different between the models. The highest flow velocity was seen in model135, followed by model90, and model45. The points of maximum velocity occurred at the anastomosis site in model45 and model90, while it was located distally into the SCIA in the model135 (Fig. 4(a), (b), (c-1)). These maximum velocity points were also associated with the point of maximum shear rate in each model (Fig. 3(d)-(f)). The relationship between velocity and shear rate will be emphasised in the part II section underneath. The maximum velocity point locations with respect to the distance from the anastomosis were depicted in the right column of Fig. 3. Comprehensive flow within the cross-sectional area of these locations (as viewed from the ATA side) was shown in Fig. 4. This view displayed that the pattern of the flow velocity was different. The points of maximum velocity were eccentric, located towards the SCIA in model45, slightly concentric in model90, and concentric in model135. At the anastomosis however, the point of maximum velocity, like model45, was eccentrically located towards the direction of SCIA in model135 (Fig. 4(c-2)).

For the flow velocity, it was different between each anastomosis angle. Given the size of the arrow is associated with the flow velocity (the larger arrow, the greater velocity; Fig. 4), the flow velocities at the similar area with the maximum velocity were the highest in model135 and the lowest in model45 within the cross-sectional view. The point of maximum velocity of the model135 located distal to the anastomosis (C-1 location in Fig. 4) while this points of model45 and model90 located at a point within the anastomosis level. At the anastomosis level, the flow velocity in model135 was the lowest. In sum, the order of the highest-to-lowest flow velocity at the anastomosis was model90, model45, and model135, respectively (Fig. 4). It should be noted that the cross-sectional areas of model45 and model135 (obliquely cut surface of the SCIA) were greater than that of model90 (2.2 mm2 VS 1.47mm2) to allow angled anastomosis while maintaining similar diameter of the SCIA in each model. Averages flow volume (velocity x area; unit of mm3/s) at the level of maximum velocity were 133.39 mm3/s, 108.16 mm3/s, and 167.88 mm3/s for model45, model90, and model135, respectively. At the anastomosis level the average flow volume of model135 was 117.70 mm3/s.

Part II: Shear rate (Fig. 3(d)-(f) and Fig. 4)

The shear rate primarily arises at a location where the vessel wall is exposed to the force exerted by the flowing blood. Faster flow results in greater shear rate. For the maximum shear rate, its order from the highest to the lowest was in model45, model135, and model90, respectively (Fig. 3(d)-(f)). This order was different from the order of the blood flow velocity (model135 > model90 > model45; Fig. 4). For the scrutiny of its distribution, the location of maximum shear rate at the anastomosis site correlated with deviation of the point of maximum flow velocity and the direction of the flow in the ATA (Figs. 1 and 4). Briefly, its maximum area deviated to the left-handed side of the figures in model135, the superior side in model45, and the right-handed side in model90. In contrast, the deviation was deviated to the right-handed side in model135 at where the true maximum shear rate occurred. This location is possible where the flow hit the vessel wall. Regarding the true maximum shear rate of each model, the sequence of extent involving highest-to-lowest area of the vessel wall was model135, model45, and model90, respectively.

Discussion

In microsurgical ETS anastomosis, the optimal angle has been debated and usually performed based on the surgeon’s experience. Understanding the physiologic changes between the different angles, including blood flow velocity, volume, and wall shear rate and its distribution, are important for achieving higher anastomotic patency and the free flap success.

Understanding flow physiology involves two critical principles. The principle of fluid flow (Q) dictates that the mass entering (A1V1) equals the mass exiting (A2V2) in a continuous flow. As fluid encounters a decrease in cross sectional area (CSA) of the lumen, it necessitates an increase in velocity (V) to maintain a constant flow rate (Q). This adjustment ensures that the identical volume passes through the narrowed tube within the similar timeframe. The second theory, the Hagen-Poiseuille equation36 (, explains the connection between pressure gradient (∆P), viscosity (µ), flow rate (Q), vessel radius (r), and length (L) in a pipe. Given the flow is maintained, a change in diameter impacts other factors.

The wall shear rate arises at a location where the vessel wall is exposed to the force exerted by the flow and are affected by any change in flow rate.Since blood is a non-Newtonian fluid, an increase in flow velocity decreases blood viscosity. Both lead to a surge in shear rate and vice versa. The increased shear rate subsequently changes vWF conformation that potentially leads to platelet aggregation and subsequent thrombosis11. Again, shear rate reflects the shearing force generated by blood flow velocity against the vessel wall. Therefore, its magnitude is additionally influenced by variations in pressure gradient, viscosity, and vessel radius, as explained by the aforementioned Hagen-Poiseuille equation.

In the ETS anastomosis, the change in diameter and the anastomosis angle substantially shape blood flow velocity and pattern, affect shear rate, and impact the overall flow scenario.

According to the Hagen–Poiseuille equation and fluid flow principles36, the flow velocity, including the maximum rate, becomes higher in a vessel with narrower diameter (e.g. at the anastomosis). This explained why the flow velocity was higher in the model90 than the model45 and model135. Since blood flow possesses non-Newtonian characteristics that behave in a more complex way than a simple fluid21,22,24,26, a different flow pattern may additionally affect its flow velocity (e.g. the winding bundle slows the flow down). In the model135, the larger anastomotic angle results in greater change in inertia and subsequent turbulence yielding flow diminution9. However, the larger anastomotic angle offered greater curvature that favorably support the direction change of the blood flow (like turning cars in the traffic) through the anastomosis and resulted in less flow bunching and overlapping at the anastomotic site. Without the winding bundle, the flow can be re-established and surged. This explanation may relate to the distally surged velocity within the SCIA of the model135. Additionally, the winding pattern may affect the other models, but, without the greater change in inertia, the velocity is more stable and less noticeable. An improvement in inner anastomosis curvature may minimise the development of winding bundle and improve the flow, but further studies are required to affirm this hypothesis. Compared to previous study9, Zhang et al. reported highest flow volume in the 45-degree model at the 30 min after surgery and it was concordant with our results that the flow volume was higher in 45-model model (Table 3). However, the difference of flow volume became insignificantly different after 2 h. This finding possibly suggests that the flow volume between the 2 models may not be different once the homeostasis state is re-established. However, higher shearing rate, an additional information from our study, within the 45-degree model poses a higher risk for changing vWF conformation that potentially leads to platelet aggregation and subsequent thrombosis compared to the 90-degree model11.

Since the flow dynamics involve the interplay between flow velocity and shear rate, the value of the shear rate is usually associated with the velocity. Given the shear rate is related to the force exerted by the blood flow to the vessel wall, the velocity value that generates the shear rate is one that damages the vessel wall. Therefore, a flow with central-located maximum velocity results in lower shear rate. This explained why the shear rate of the model90 was the lower than the model45, although its velocity was higher. Also, it can be applied to the comparison between the model45 (more eccentric location) and model135, that demonstrated lower shear rate in the latter, although their maximum velocity values were almost identical. Even though the shear rate of the model135 potentiated once the velocity stepped up distally within the SCIA, its concentric location resulted in lower shear rate than that of the model45. Regarding the impact of shear rate on vascular thrombosis, the value of > 1,000 S−1 is the critical value since the change in vWF conformation is initiated at a rate > 1,000 S−1. Subsequently, the activation of the vWF leads to activation of the coagulation cascade. This implies that the higher the value and extent of the area with high shear rate distribution, the higher risk of thrombosis11,12. The higher values of the maximum shear rate and the area of involvement of the high shear rate in model45 and model135 (Fig. 4) emphasised this concern and hence were considered less favourable. Another possible factor that associates with the highest shear rate in model45 may be the inner geometry of the anastomosis. The shear rate distribution is defined by the following equation: , where F is a force exerting to a certain area (A). Its noticeable sharp angle may concentrate the force to a smaller area. This factor together with the eccentrically-located maximum velocity resulted in shear rate potentiation at that area. Again, a better inner anastomosis curvature may improve this profile, but further studies are required.

There were animal studies conducted to compare the results between different angles of ETS anastomosis6,9. The study by Zoubos et al.6 reported the best outcomes (100% anastomosis patency and 20% aneurysm formation) in the use of 90-degree elliptical arteriotomy of the recipient vessel compared other models. Slit arteriotomy showed 100% aneurysm formation. Zhang et al. performed experiment in 45-degree, 90-degree, and 135-degree ETS anastomosis using elliptical arteriotomy9. The initial flow was significantly higher in the 45-degree model and became insignificant between 45-degree and 90-degree model after 2 h. The flow was measured at the donor vessel distal to the anastomosis and rate of aneurysm formation was not reported. Like the previous studies6,9, our study supported the use of 90-degree angle for ETS anastomosis if feasible. This model not only provided favourable flow dynamics, but also decreased the aneurysm formation as reported in a previous study6. The model45 is acceptable, especially when compared to the model135 that had fluctuant flow velocity and shear rate around the anastomosis. Its substantial shear rate and its distribution may relate to the aneurysm formation in the 45-degree anastomosis in the animal study6, but prediction of the aneurysm formation is beyond the scope of our finite element analysis models due to its complex biological processes. In comparison to the previously reported studies6,9, our experiment provided additional information regarding the flow dynamics within the different ETS anastomotic angles and controlled the angle accuracy and vessel diameter that both affect the flow dynamics. Also, the flow in our study was measured at the areas of maximum value that located close to the anastomosis where complications (e.g. thrombosis) commonly occurs. In the nutshell, the results of this study showed the most favourable flow dynamics (velocity and shear rate) in model90 for ETS anastomosis similar to previous studies6,9.

The limitation of this work lies in validating the model across various sizes of blood vessels and flow conditions due to constraints in available databases, as it introduces the first numerical simulations of microsurgical vessel models for the SCIA and ATA. However, this challenge was addressed by validating similar vascular geometries of varying sizes and blood flow conditions using the same numerical approach, which is a widely accepted and practical method. Accordingly, the numerical approach, simulation methodology, and modeling assumptions used in this model have been validated using this strategy. Therefore, the accuracy and reliability of our findings can be ensured through this validation approach. In addition, the application of the simulation to surgical practice requires further studies to verify its favorable outcomes as the success of microsurgery involves not only the flow dynamics inside the vessels, but also anatomical characteristics of the anastomosis that can tolerate compression from adjacent tissue weight and subsequent tissue swelling that may develop later in the early healing process.

Conclusion

The 90-degree ETS anastomosis model showed the most favourable flow dynamics (blood flow velocity, blood flow pattern, wall shear rate, and the extent of the maximum wall shear rate) in comparison to the others in our report.
