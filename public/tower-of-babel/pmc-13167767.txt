Trade-offs between light absorption and energy transfer in a marine light-harvesting complex 2
Authors: Graham P. Schmidt, Dihao Wang, Amala Phadkule, Chern Chuang, Mike Reppert, Gabriela S. Schlau-Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe light-harvesting complex 2 (LH2) antenna of purple bacteria is a model system for photosynthetic light harvesting. The discovery of its delocalized excited states established a picture in which delocalization and associated rapid energy transfer is responsible for the high quantum efficiency of photosynthesis. In marine purple sulfur bacteria, the LH2 structure is modified to accommodate an additional carotenoid. We report that these changes enhance light absorption, yet also reduce delocalization and slow energy transfer. Increased heterogeneity of the excited-state energies likely contributes to the slowdown without perturbing the typical energy transfer pathways. Our results highlight the importance of light absorption over rapid energy transfer within LH2, as well as its flexibility in altering excited-state dynamics for different niches.

Purple bacteria are a widely distributed and ancient group of photosynthetic bacteria (1). They share a common photosynthetic architecture, consisting of an intracellular membrane that contains an array of antenna proteins, chiefly light-harvesting complex 2 (LH2) (2). The antenna array absorbs light and transfers energy to the light-harvesting complex 1–reaction center (LH1-RC), where charge separation takes place (2–6). Purple bacteria can be divided into two groups: the purple sulfur bacteria, which depend on the oxidation of sulfide to grow, and the purple nonsulfur bacteria, which do not (1, 2, 7). The purple nonsulfur bacteria are a diverse, polyphyletic group that are often capable of growing under varied light conditions, or even in the dark. Their acclimation processes typically include modifications to the properties of their LH2s, which have been thoroughly characterized, with extensive benchmarking and knowledge of the energy transfer dynamics and structural organization of this important antenna protein (8–16). The purple sulfur bacteria inhabit a variety of photosynthetic niches, especially those in high salt environments, such as the ocean, and those in anoxic and sulfidic environments, where they serve as key primary producers and nitrogen fixers (1, 7, 17–19). The extent to which they adapt or acclimate to these environments is largely unknown, as are the properties of their LH2s more generally (1). Understanding how such properties are tailored across a wide range of conditions identifies the constraints on the optimization of solar energy capture and conversion.

Recently, developments in cryogenic electron microscopy have led to a proliferation of high-resolution structures of LH2 from purple sulfur bacteria. Some LH2s from purple sulfur bacteria have shown organizations similar to LH2s from purple nonsulfur bacteria, which consist of eight or nine alpha-helical α/β heterodimeric subunits arranged in a circularly symmetric complex such as the prototypical LH2 from Rhodoblastus (Rbl.) acidophilus 10050 (Fig. 1A, Left) (20, 21). In contrast, LH2 from Marichromatium (Mch.) purpuratum exhibited a unique heptameric arrangement of the subunits (Fig. 1A, Right) (22). Each heterodimeric subunit binds three bacteriochlorophyll a (BChla) pigments (2, 23) organized into a monomer and dimer of BChla. The subunits assemble to generate two concentric rings from the monomer and dimer, B800 and B850, respectively, named after their typical absorption maxima. The separation between the B800 BChla is >20 Å, leading to weak coupling, whereas the separation between the B850 BChla is only ∼10 Å, leading to strong coupling. In most LH2s, each subunit binds a single carotenoid pigment. LH2 from Mch. purpuratum uniquely binds a second okenone carotenoid in each subunit (Fig. 1A, Right) (22). The two unique features of this LH2 appear to be related: the additional carotenoid is secured by a 15 amino acid extension of the α-subunit N-terminal domain that protrudes radially outward. This bulky domain creates steric clashes with adjacent heterodimers, forcing the protein into the lower symmetry heptameric arrangement. This heptameric arrangement increases the separation of the B800 and B850 BChla on adjacent subunits from 21.2 Å and 8.8 Å in Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 to 24.1 Å and 10.7 Å in Mch. purpuratum LH2. Additionally, the angle between BChla on adjacent subunits increases from 40° to 52° (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (22).

The presence of the extra carotenoid per subunit can be clearly seen in the visible portion of the absorption spectrum (Fig. 1B). In the 500 to 600 nm range, Mch. purpuratum LH2 absorbs roughly twice as much light per subunit as Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2. Although purple nonsulfur bacteria usually occupy surface niches, purple sulfur bacteria such as Mch. purpuratum are typically found in the anoxic zones of stratified lakes and in marine communities, often below several meters of water (1, 17, 24). Water absorption at these depths means that Mch. purpuratum relies on a solar spectrum with little intensity in the near-infrared (Fig. 1B), suggesting that the additional carotenoid may be required for blue light absorption (25, 26). Such a role is further indicated by the close proximity (<4Å) of the carotenoid to the B800 BChla, and by transient absorption spectroscopy of the carotenoid, which shows highly efficient energy transfer to the B800 Qy band (27). While these contributions to light harvesting have been reported, the impacts of the carotenoid on the overall energy landscape and dynamics of the complex, and the potential trade-offs associated with its presence, have not yet been established.

Here, we report the excited-state properties and energy transfer dynamics of LH2 from the purple sulfur bacteria Mch. purpuratum. Our spectroscopic measurements revealed slower energy transfer within LH2, with up to a threefold reduction in rate as compared to previously studied variants from purple nonsulfur bacteria. Steady-state analysis and accompanying simulations show that the slower energy transfer can be ascribed to the reduced symmetry and the impact of the additional carotenoid on the B800 BChla. These slower rates can therefore be understood as a tradeoff for the enhanced absorption of Mch. purpuratum to match the underwater solar spectrum. While most studies of LH2 have focused on the implications of its electronic properties for energy transfer, our findings point to the pre-eminence of light absorption in driving these properties. LH2 from the purple sulfur bacteria Mch. purpuratum has responded to the spectral availability of light in its environment with an adaptation strategy distinct from the acclimation strategies seen in the well-studied LH2s from purple nonsulfur bacteria. These different strategies highlight the versatility of LH2, and our findings demonstrate that further study of LH2s with unusual properties can uncover photophysical insights.

Results and Discussion

B800 and B850 Excited-State Manifold.

The position, maxima, and lineshapes provide an initial characterization of the excited state manifold. The absorption spectra for Mch. purpuratum LH2 and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 are shown in Fig. 1B. In the carotenoid region, the well-documented increase in absorbance was observed for Mch. purpuratum LH2 due to the second carotenoid in each subunit (22, 28). In the BChla Qy region, the prototypical B800 and B850 peaks were observed for Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2. In contrast, the B800 peak appeared only as a shoulder to the substantially larger B850 peak for Mch. purpuratum LH2 due to a reduced extinction coefficient of the B800 band maximum. The wavelength of the B850 band maximum was also blue-shifted to 828 nm similar to low-light variants of LH2 (23, 29–31), which is thought to enhance the efficiency of light harvesting for the photosynthetic network (32).

To more clearly resolve both BChla Qy bands, the absorption spectra for Mch. purpuratum LH2 and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 were also collected at 77 K (Fig. 1C) and showed similar features as in previous reports (28, 33). The B800 peak was significantly broader and shallower for Mch. purpuratum LH2 than for Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2.

While the position and amplitudes of the two peaks were observable in both the room temperature and cryogenic absorption spectra, even at cryogenic temperatures there was overlap between the two peaks for Mch. purpuratum LH2, preventing a direct extraction of linewidths. To determine the linewidths, each absorption spectrum was fit to a two-component model representing the B800 and B850 peaks. The arPLS (34, 35) algorithm, a baseline fitting method, was also applied to remove the broad vibronic and excitonic background. The peak profiles were fixed as the empirical profile from Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 for the B850 peak and a Voigt profile for the B800 peak with the maxima and linewidths as fit parameters. The two components extracted from the fit are also plotted in Fig. 1C.

The full-width half maxima (FWHM) for the Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 B850 and B800 bands were 258 and 140 cm−1, consistent with previous experimental and theoretical work (36). The FWHM for Mch. purpuratum LH2 B850 band was 196 cm−1, close to the value for Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2. In contrast, the FWHM for the B800 band was 376 cm−1, which is two- to three-fold larger than for LH2 from other species (36). Although it is more difficult to fit the room temperature spectrum due to spectral overlap, the extracted FWHM for the B800 band was 510 cm−1. This comparative insensitivity to temperature, resulting in a broad B800 band even at 77 K, suggests that static disorder is the primary broadening mechanism for the B800 band (36–39).

The larger static disorder in the Mch. purpuratum LH2 B800 band has two potential origins. Both possibilities involve the second carotenoid, and the extended N-terminal domain that binds it, which sits in close proximity to the B800 BChla. First, analysis of the cryo-EM structure revealed both flexibility and heterogeneity in this domain. Three different peptide sequences were also observed in the structures (22). Consistent with this picture, previous single-molecule studies have shown that heterogeneous polypeptide compositions in LH2 from Rhodopseudomonas palustris can lead to substantial modulations of the BChla energies (40). Second, electronic interactions between the B800 BChla and the carotenoid may also play a role in the broadening of the band. Studies on LH2 have found fast energy transfer from the carotenoid to the B800 states (0.56 ps for Mch. purpuratum LH2), consistent with their close proximity and suggestive of significant coupling between the states. Notably, the efficiency of this pathway is significantly higher in Mch. purpuratum LH2 than Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2, and those of other previously studied species (27, 41–45). Furthermore, the S1 state of the Mch. purpuratum LH2 carotenoid, okenone, has an energy of 12,750 cm−1 (46), which is nearly isoenergetic with the B800 Qy band and may facilitate mixing between the states. The mobility and heterogeneity of the N-terminal domain to which the carotenoid is bound could then give rise to a large spread in the magnitude of the coupling, potentially broadening the B800 band via state mixing. An analogous mechanism has recently been suggested as a source of broadening of the B850 band due to short-range coupling to optically forbidden charge-transfer states (47).

B800-to-B850 Energy Transfer.

In LH2, absorbed energy rapidly relaxes from the B800 to the B850 ring before being transferred to neighboring complexes, and ultimately to the reaction center (11, 12, 16, 32, 49, 50). To identify the effects of the unique structural features on energy transfer within and between the rings, we performed spectrally resolved transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy at room temperature on Mch. purpuratum LH2 and the well-studied LH2 from Rbl. acidophilus 10050 to compare their dynamics (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Upon initial excitation at 800 nm, a ground-state bleach/stimulated emission signal from the B800 band and the blue edge of the B850 band was present with a maximum at 805 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The B800 band signal decayed concomitantly with a rise in stimulated emission and excited-state absorption from the B850 band, reflecting B800-to-B850 energy transfer. This energy transfer step is clearly visible in the single-wavelength transients at the peak of the B850 stimulated emission (Fig. 2B). A sharp rise was present in the Mch. purpuratum LH2 data from direct excitation of the B850 band, which was a minor component due to the blue-shift of this band in this LH2. The overall growth of the transient was much slower for Mch. purpuratum LH2 than for Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2, reflecting the slower B800-to-B850 energy transfer. The rates of B800-to-B850 energy transfer were extracted by globally fitting the data across the probe wavelength range using a two-component sequential model (51). A rate of 1.14 ± 0.11 ps−1 (0.88 ± 0.08 ps) was extracted for B800-to-B850 energy transfer Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2, in line with previous results, and consistent with rates in similar LH2s, whereas for Mch. purpuratum LH2 a much slower rate of 0.36 ± 0.02 ps−1 (2.75±0.15 ps) was extracted. These rates are plotted alongside previously reported rates for other LH2s. Notably, the B800-to-B850 energy transfer rate for Mch. purpuratum LH2 is, to our knowledge, the slowest yet reported.

Context for the unusually slow B800-to-B850 relaxation can be provided in part by the differences in arrangements of BChlas in Mch. purpuratum and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2s (Fig. 2 D and E). The B800 BChlas in Mch. purpuratum LH2 are tilted slightly out of the plane of symmetry and rotated nearly 180 degrees relative to those in Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2, similar to those found in the structure of LH2 from the purple sulfur bacteria Ectothiorhodospira haloalkaliphila (21). A recent study calculated the electronic couplings for Mch. purpuratum and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 (Fig. 2D) (48). The geometric differences mean that the largest B800 to B850 BChla couplings were 22 and −22 cm−1 for Mch. purpuratum LH2 and −8 and 38 cm−1 for Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2.

To understand the effects of these couplings, we calculated the rate of B800-to-B850 energy transfer for both complexes using generalized Förster theory (52–55). The calculated B800-to-B850 energy transfer rates were 0.40 ps−1 and 0.50 ps−1 for Mch. purpuratum and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). In the calculations, the rate in Mch. purpuratum LH2 is reduced due to a decrease in electronic coupling from the change in orientation of the B800 BChla (Fig. 2D). While the calculated values are in agreement with the experimental trend, they fail to reproduce the dramatic threefold difference we observed in our TA spectroscopy.

There are multiple other factors that can influence the rate of energy transfer in Mch. purpuratum LH2. The unusual properties of the B800 band provide the most likely explanations for the slow B800-to-B850 energy transfer. First, the static disorder that broadens the B800 band also affects energy transfer. Although static disorder is known in some cases to increase the rate of B800-to-B850 energy transfer due to increased spectral overlap (11), further broadening of the B800 band can decrease this overlap and thus the rate of energy transfer (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Supporting this picture, the species-associated spectra extracted from global fitting show only a very weak B800 ground-state bleach for Mch. purpuratum LH2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). This suggests either a substantial broadening or a reduction in the oscillator strength of the B800 band as compared to other LH2 variants (32), in agreement with the fitting of the cryogenic absorption spectra (Fig. 1C). Second, a reduction in oscillator strength, possibly due to a change in the local polarizability of the binding pocket, could contribute to the slow B800-to-B850 energy transfer which, in the generalized Förster theory model, scales with the oscillator strength of the BChlas in both bands (54, 55). Finally, the S1 state of okenone is energetically close enough to the B800 band to allow reverse energy transfer; at room temperature, the energy gap of 250 cm−1 implies a reverse rate of 0.54 ps−1 (1.85 ps), large enough to provide kinetic competition to B800-to-B850 relaxation and potentially reduce this energy transfer rate. These characteristics of the B800 band suggest a unique role in Mch. purpuratum, in which its light-harvesting capabilities in the IR are de-emphasized, consistent with the reduced solar intensity in this region (Fig. 1B). Instead, it may simply serve as a conduit, transferring energy from the neighboring second carotenoid to the B850 band.

B850 Relaxation.

To characterize energy relaxation within each band, we also performed TA anisotropy studies, which can resolve transfer between spectrally overlapping transitions. TA anisotropy measures the orientation of the transition dipole relative to the initial excitation, and its decay reflects energy migration around the ring. The TA anisotropy transients for the B850 bands of both Mch. purpuratum LH2 and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 are shown in Fig. 2C. Both transients were well fit with a monoexponential function. A timescale of 104 ± 10 fs was extracted for Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2, in agreement with previous findings for this complex and similar LH2s (10, 12, 49). However, a timescale of 203 ± 13 fs was extracted for Mch. purpuratum LH2, once again reflecting substantially slower dynamics than in other LH2s. Consistent with these timescales, couplings within the B850 ring were smaller for Mch. purpuratum LH2 than for Rbl. acidophilus LH2 at 166 cm−1 and 298 cm−1, respectively (48), due to steeper angles and larger separations between the BChla on adjacent subunits. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1, the lower symmetry of Mch. purpuratum LH2 positions the pair of B850 BChla from each subunit approximately 2 Å further away from the pair on the adjacent subunits, with steeper pairwise angles between them as compared to Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 (22). In a simple Förster theory model with a square dependence of the energy transfer rate on the intramolecular coupling (56), these coupling values provide approximate agreement with the relative rates of energy relaxation around the B850 ring from the anisotropy decay.

The TA anisotropy transient for the B800 band of Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S5C. The transient was also well fit with monoexponential function. A timescale of 860 ± 40 fs was extracted, again in agreement with previous findings for this complex and similar LH2s (10, 12, 49). However, the anisotropy decay of the B800 band of Mch. purpuratum LH2 was hidden by the grow-in of the overlapping spectral signal of the B850 excited-state absorption feature, as was previously observed for LH3 (32). Notably, in contrast to previous observations for LH3, the relative perpendicular polarization response of the B800 band was a third of the corresponding value typical of LH2s (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), even at short time delays. The smaller anisotropy suggests minimal intraband B800 relaxation. Consistently, there is weaker coupling between the B800 BChla in Mch. purpuratum than in Rbl. acidophilus at 8 cm−1 and 30 cm−1, respectively, which can be attributed to a 3 Å larger separation in Mch. purpuratum.

Superradiance.

In addition to the reduction in energy transfer rates, the changes in BChla geometry due to the heptameric organization of LH2 from Mch. purpuratum also reduce the degree of delocalization, which influences energy transfer within and between LH2. In delocalized states, coupling extends the coherence of an excitation across multiple chromophores. The delocalization of the B850 band, induced by the strong coupling within this ring, is in large part responsible for the substantial redshift of its transitions. The low-energy excitonic states of this band are primarily responsible for transporting energy through the network of LH2 complexes (13, 57, 58). For the BChla arrangement of the B850 ring, one characteristic of its delocalized states is superradiance, in which the radiative emission is enhanced over that of an excitation on an individual BChla (15, 59, 60). Quantification of the superradiance provides a metric for the number of BChla over which the delocalized state extends (61, 62). The superradiance is calculated from the radiative rate of LH2 as compared to isolated BChla, and the radiative rate is determined from the ratio of the fluorescence lifetime and quantum yield (15, 63).

To determine how superradiance depends on LH2 oligomerization, we measured the fluorescence lifetimes (Fig. 3A) and quantum yields (Fig. 3B) of the LH2 complexes of Mch. purpuratum, Rbl. acidophilus 10050, and Phaeospirillum (Phs.) molischianum. From the fluorescence lifetimes and quantum yields, the relative radiative rates were calculated and compared to previously reported values for the model species Rhodobacter (Rba.) sphaeroides (15). In Fig. 3C, the radiative rates are plotted per BChla, where values greater than one indicate superradiance. The degree of superradiance observed in Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 and Rba. sphaeroides LH2 are similar, consistent with their common nonameric geometry. Both the heptamer, Mch. purpuratum LH2, and the octamer, Phs. molischianum LH2, exhibited lower radiative rates than the two nonamers. The smaller delocalization lengths implied by these values agrees with our observations of slower energy relaxation in Mch. purpuratum LH2 and similar observations previously reported for Phs. molischianum LH2 (49). These differences in radiative rates for the lower-symmetry LH2s can be explained by decreased coupling in their B850 bands due to increased separations and larger angles between B850 BChla on adjacent subunits. The especially low radiative rate of Phs. molischianum LH2 may also be related to the relatively large static disorder of its B850 band (36). These findings show that the symmetry of the complex and the degree of intraband B850 coupling can have a substantial impact on exciton delocalization in LH2.

Overall energy transport and trapping in photosynthetic membranes is a multistep process, involving transfer between LH2s, to LH1, and ultimately to the RC on timescales of 3 to 60 ps (13, 64–66). In many organisms and growth conditions, the formation of arrays of LH2 means that multiple LH2-to-LH2 steps are required (67, 68). Reducing the rate of this step could, therefore, impact the timescales of transport and trapping. LH2-to-LH2 energy transfer depends on both the properties of LH2 and their organization in the membrane. In terms of LH2 properties, delocalization of the lowest-lying excitonic states of the B850 band is thought to enhance energy transfer between LH2 due to their increased oscillator strength (69, 70). The decreased delocalization length associated with lower symmetry is likely to, therefore, slow energy transfer between these LH2s. In terms of LH2 organization, previous research has suggested that optimal packing geometries are achieved with octameric and nonameric LH2. Thus, the packing geometry generated by heptameric LH2 may also slow energy transfer between LH2 (71). Both LH2 properties and organization, therefore, point to slower energy transport in Mch. purpuratum, although in vivo the complexity of the system can give rise to unexpected effects.

Conclusion

In this work, we report the excited states and energy relaxation within LH2 from the purple sulfur bacteria Mch. purpuratum. Our results show that the inclusion of an additional carotenoid to enhance underwater light absorption leads to dramatic consequences for energy transfer within LH2, slowing relaxation steps two- to threefold. The carotenoid imposes a symmetry reduction on LH2 that also decreases exciton delocalization, long thought to be a hallmark of the function of this complex. Extensive previous research on LH2 from purple nonsulfur bacteria focused on the ways in which it is optimized for rapid energy transfer within the complex; our results show that the changes to the electronic structure of Mch. purpuratum LH2 enhance underwater light absorption while slowing energy transfer. This adaptation suggests that, at least in certain environments, the absorption profile may be a more important adaptation criterion than the energy transfer rates within LH2, which may even simply be a byproduct of the high BChla densities required for photon capture. Indeed, although the energy transfer rates we have reported for Mch. purpuratum LH2 are substantially slower than those found in the LH2 of other species, they are still much faster than the energy transfer steps between complexes, including LH1, the reaction center, and other LH2s, that limit the overall rates for energy transport and conversion.

Our results also highlight the flexibility of the functional roles played by carotenoids and BChla. In the marine habitats of Mch. purpuratum, in which near-IR light is relatively unavailable, carotenoids become critical for light absorption. In the presence of the additional carotenoid, the role of the B800 BChla in Mch. purpuratum LH2 is altered: they serve primarily as a conduit from the carotenoid to the B850 band. Notably, this conduit appears to be robust to accompanying changes in excited-state properties of the B800 BChla. As observed here, the differences in light absorption and their effect on energy transfer underscore the flexibility of LH2 and its versatility across environments. Further investigation of other LH2s from underexplored taxa can yield functional and foundational insights into solar energy systems.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation.

The LH2 proteins were isolated from the purple bacteria Mch. purpuratum strain BN5500 and Rbl. acidophilus strain 10050 with the standard protocols as described in SI Appendix and were stored in a Tris buffer containing 20 mM Tris HCl, 0.1% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) at pH 8.0.

Steady-State Spectroscopy.

A full description of steady-state measurements can be found in SI Appendix. Room temperature absorption spectra were recorded using the Cary 5000 UV–Vis-NIR (Agilent) and the Epoch microplate (BioTek) spectrophotometers. Room temperature emission spectra were measured using a home-built set-up, with 405 and 532 diode lasers (Thor Labs) as excitation sources. Cryogenic absorption spectra were measured on a home-built set-up at 77 K; samples were mixed with glycerol to 40% (v/v), flash frozen, and held in an optically transparent dewar (Horiba Instruments FL-1013).

Time-Resolved Fluorescence Spectroscopy.

The fluorescence lifetime measurements were carried out using time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) and fit as described previously (12, 49). A MaiTai Ti:Sapphire oscillator (80 MHz, Spectra-Physics) was used to pump a nonlinear photonic crystal fiber (FemtoWhite 800, NKT photonics), generating a white light supercontinuum that could be used to choose the excitation frequency. Further details can be found in SI Appendix.

TA Spectroscopy.

Details of the TA apparatus are provided in SI Appendix. In brief, all the protein samples were pumped at 800 nm with a femtosecond Ti:Sapphire laser (Coherent Libra). The pump pulse energy was attenuated to 4 to 6 nJ/pulse and focused on the protein samples in a flow cuvette. The sample was flowed using a peristaltic pump and stored on ice during data acquisition. The white-light continuum probe pulse was generated by focusing the 800-nm pulses through a tube of argon gas. The polarization between the pump and the probe pulses was set to be the magic angle (54.7°) with ∼110 fs instrument response. The probe was detected with a CCD array (AViiVA EM2) on a shot-to-shot basis for data acquisition and analysis.

Theoretical Calculations.

Frenkel exciton models were employed to describe the excitonic structure of LH2. On-site energies and couplings for Mch. purpuratum and Rbl. acidophilus 10050 LH2 were taken from ref. 48. Near-field effects in energy transfer was accounted for using the generalized FRET formalism (72, 73). Details of the theoretical calculation are described in SI Appendix.
