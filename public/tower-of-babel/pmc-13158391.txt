A vitronectin-functionalized ECM-mimetic hydrogel platform for modelling integrin-dependent tumour responses to cilengitide
Authors: Isaac Vieco-Martí, Sofia Granados-Aparici, Amparo López-Carrasco, Miguel F. Segura, Manuel Salmerón-Sánchez, Inmaculada Noguera, Samuel Navarro, Rosa Noguera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The extracellular matrix (ECM) critically regulates tumour cell adhesion, mechanotransduction and therapeutic response, yet there is a lack of in vitro models that faithfully reproduce ECM–integrin signalling while remaining compatible with histological and molecular workflows. Here, we present a biomimetic hydrogel platform based on gelatin–tyramine and silk fibroin, engineered to recreate defined vitronectin microenvironments and integrin-targeting conditions, including pharmacological inhibition with the αvβ3-integrin antagonist cilengitide. The platform enables control over matrix composition and bioactivity while preserving tissue-like architecture. We establish robust protocols for hydrogel synthesis under distinct vitronectin conditions, cell embedding, pharmacological treatment and downstream processing, including genomic DNA isolation. In addition, the platform supports standard histological processing and immunostaining, enabling digital pathology-based assessment of morphological features, vitronectin expression patterns, and adhesion-related markers such as vinculin and β3 integrin. Importantly, the system enables recovery of viable cells, allowing subsequent orthotopic implantation in vivo. Together, these workflows provide a reproducible and scalable framework to investigate ECM-mediated regulation of neuroblastoma cell behaviour and drug response, with scope for quantitative image analysis of integrin-associated signals and ECM deposition. This ECM-mimetic hydrogel system offers a versatile and histology-compatible experimental platform for studying integrin-dependent tumour–matrix interactions and developing physiologically relevant in vitro–in vivo translational pipelines.

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a dynamic compartment of the tumour microenvironment composed of adhesion glycoproteins and fibrous proteins that provides structural support and mechanical cues [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Alterations in ECM architecture and stiffness, generally measured by Young's modulus, can promote aggressive tumour phenotypes through mechanotransduction at the cell–ECM interface [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. A major component of this signalling involves integrins, including αvβ3, which binds RGD motifs present in multiple ECM proteins and has been implicated in tumour progression, angiogenesis, invasion, and therapy resistance, making it a relevant mechanobiology-linked therapeutic target [5,6]. These processes involve the integration of mechanical signals into transcriptional responses through mechanosensitive pathways such as YAP/TAZ, which regulate proliferation, survival and differentiation in response to matrix stiffness and adhesion cues. In several neoplasms, ECM stiffness has been directly linked to YAP-driven transcriptional programmes associated with tumour progression [9]. However, mechanotherapy strategies that target mechanotransduction nodes such as integrins face important translational limitations, including ligand and receptor redundancy, and compensatory adhesion pathways that can reduce efficacy [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Clinical implementation of these drugs has also been hindered by the lack of robust biomarkers to identify responsive contexts and unexpected activation of mechanisms [[14], [15], [16]]. Tuneable 3D models can mimic tissue-like organisation and enable controlled matrix composition, thereby supporting the study of cell–ECM-dependent drug responses and helping identify potential biomarkers of activity. In contrast, conventional 2D culture systems fail to recapitulate these biomechanical and spatial constraints, often leading to altered cell morphology, polarity and signalling, including dysregulated integrin engagement and mechanotransduction pathways [17]. This limitation is particularly relevant when studying integrin-targeted therapies, where ligand availability and spatial organisation critically determine functional outcomes [5]. Among the available 3D platforms, gelatin–tyramine (GTA) and silk fibroin (sf) offer a practical balance of biological relevance and experimental simplicity. GTA adds cell-interactive cues and sf is a biocompatible, biodegradable biomaterial widely used for 3D culture [[18], [19], [20]]. Enzymatic dityrosine crosslinking using horseradish peroxidase and low concentrations of H2O2 enables gelation at 37 °C under mild conditions that do not compromise cell viability, with Young's modulus in the range relevant, among other tissues, for nervous tissue [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. The same chemistry also allows straightforward incorporation of additional tyrosine-containing ECM glycoproteins without modification steps [20]. Our focus is to study GTA-sf HGs in the specific setting of neuroblastoma, the most common extracranial solid tumour of childhood which accounts for 15% of childhood cancer-related deaths [24]. Despite major therapeutic advances, high-risk neuroblastoma (HR-NB) disease still carries poor long-term survival and frequent relapse, highlighting the need for new biomarkers and more effective targeted strategies, including approaches that consider the tumour microenvironment [25]. In this context, vitronectin (VN) is an RGD-containing ECM glycoprotein that binds integrins such as αvβ3 and αvβ5 and has been implicated in the aggressiveness of multiple and different tumoral pathologies [[26], [27], [28]]. Our group has shown that VN is expressed in HR-NB patient samples, murine models and multiple 3D models. VN is associated with aggressive clinicopathological and genomic features by digital pathology and is detectable as a secreted factor in culture media and patient plasma, supporting its relevance as a biomarker and a candidate therapeutic target in HR-NB [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. In this system, therefore, GTA-sf provides a permissive structural matrix, while VN serves as the main instructive cue examined in relation to integrin-dependent behaviour. Using the GTA–sf platform with variable VN microenvironments, we have already demonstrated HR-NB compatibility and applied short-term readouts including real-time holographic microscopy, as well as ELISA-based detection of VN secretion into culture media [32,33]. Here, we show new applications of this work by detailing GTA–sf HG fabrication and presenting a set of workflows for multi-level characterisation, including mechanical testing, formalin-fixed paraffin embedded compatible histopathology and imaging, genomic profiling, and downstream cell recovery for in vivo implantation, while also illustrating its qualitative potential for drug testing in defined VN microenvironments. Cilengitide (CLG) is a cyclic RGD peptide targeting αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrins which has been clinically evaluated in a phase III clinical trial for glioblastoma but has shown limited efficacy and a lack of response biomarkers [7,34,35]. Our prior data nonetheless suggest that CLG remains of interest in HR-NB, showing cytotoxicity in vitro, synergy with etoposide (chemotherapy used in rapid COJEC regime treatment for HR-NB patients [36]), signals of reduced αvβ3 expression by immunohistochemistry assay and growth restraint in a preliminary in vivo setting [31,37]. These observations form the rationale for proof-of-concept testing of CLG in a 3D context with controllable VN availability. In this context, the non-VN-added condition models exposure to endogenous VN, whereas the VN-rich condition represents increased extracellular VN availability. The platform does not aim to replicate full tissue complexity, but rather to provide mechanistic resolution of specific tumour–matrix interactions within a controllable experimental framework. This is biologically relevant because VN-rich patterns in neuroblastoma are linked to aggressive disease, and host VN status can influence tumour selection in orthotopic models [30,38]. Thus, the VN-functionalized GTA–sf HG platform provides a 3D system for studying integrin-dependent morphology-based tumour phenotypes and their modulation by CLG using HR-NB as a representative tumour example.

Materials and methods

2D culture and PDX suspension culture of commercial cell lines

We used MYCN-amplified SK-N-BE(2) and ALK-mutated SH-SY5Y human cell lines as models of HR-NB [39,40]. Both SK-N-BE(2) and SH-SY5Y were acquired from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), expanded on Iscove's Modified Dulbecco's Medium (IMDM, Gibco, Thermofisher), supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermofisher), 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium G Supplement (Thermofisher), 0.2% Plasmocin (InvivoGen) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermofisher). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere. Media was replaced every two days.

In addition to commercial cell lines, we included two previously established and published patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models from patients at Hospital Clínico Universitario de València (PDX1 and PDX2) [41,42]. Both PDX cell lines (MYCN-amplified) were expanded in suspension on DEMEM-Glutamax without pyruvate (Gibco, Thermofisher), F12 Nutrimix Glutamax (Gibco, Thermofisher), 2% B27 without vitamin A (Thermofisher), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermofisher), 40 ng/ml bFGF (Peprotech) and 20 ng/ml EGF (Peprotech). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere. Media was replaced every two days.

3D hydrogel fabrication protocol and CLG treatment

Dityrosine bond formation is a key mechanism used for HG polymerisation at 37 °C. Phenol groups were introduced into gelatin to enhance its ability to form dityrosine bonds, resulting in gelatin–tyramine (GTA), as previously described [43]. Briefly, gelatin (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was melted in 50 mL of 0.05 M 2-(N-morpholino) ethane sulfonic acid buffer at 60 °C and then cooled to 25 °C. Subsequently, 500 mg of tyramine (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added together with 184 mg of carbodiimide hydrochloride and 57 mg of N-hydroxysuccinimide to facilitate the carbodiimide-mediated reaction. The mixture was agitated for 18 h at room temperature, followed by dialysis against distilled water using a 3.5 molecular weight cut-off membrane and lyophilisation. The GTA was stored at −20 °C until use.

HGs were prepared with a final volume of 65 μL. SK-N-BE(2) and SH-SY5Y cells were embedded at a target density of 2 × 106 viable cells mL (approximately 120,000 cells per HG), while PDX1 and PDX2 cells were embedded at 4 × 106 viable cells mL (approximately 240,000 cells per HG). Fabrication was performed following a previously reported protocol [20]. Lyophilized GTA was weighed, UV sterilised for 10 min and melted in serum-free IMDM in a 37 °C water bath to obtain a stock solution of 50 mg/mL. GTA was mixed with sf solution (50 mg mL, Sigma Aldrich, Merck, USA) at a ratio of 25:75. Following trypsinization, viable SK-N-BE(2) and SH-SY5Y cells were counted with trypan blue assay and resuspended in the GTA–sf mixture to achieve the target density. For PDX1 and PDX2, spheroids were dissociated using Accutase (Sigma Aldrich, Merck, USA), and after 12 h in culture medium, viable cells were counted with trypan blue assay and resuspended in the GTA–sf mixture to achieve the target density.

For VN-rich HGs, VN (PrepoTech, London, UK) was resuspended in dPBS to a stock concentration of 2 mg/mL. The required volume to achieve the final concentration indicated in Table 1 was placed in a tube; for non-VN-added HGs we used an equivalent volume of dPBS. The corresponding volume of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) stock solution (type VI, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was then added to the VN or dPBS tube to obtain a final concentration of 20 U mL (double that of the original protocol to accelerate enzymatic crosslinking kinetics without altering structural properties [20,44,45]). The GTA–sf–cell suspension was subsequently added to the VN/dPBS–HRP tube and gently mixed. All components except hydrogen peroxide were therefore combined prior to polymerisation. Polymerisation was initiated by placing 2 μL of hydrogen peroxide stock solution (0.3%) in the centre of a 24-well plate, followed by gentle addition of 63 μL of the GTA–sf–VN/dPBS–cell to avoid premature polymerisation during handling. HGs were incubated for 45 min at 37 °C to allow complete enzymatic polymerisation and subsequently overlaid with 2 mL of the corresponding culture medium. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for two or three weeks, with medium changes every three days.

Cilengitide trifluoroacetic acid (CLG) [salt ≥98% (HPLC), SML1594-25 MG, Merck] was dissolved in dPBS. A subset of HGs was treated with six CLG doses of 100 μM. Addition of CLG started after the first change of media, and subsequent dosages were applied at every change of media for up to three weeks.

Measurement of hydrogels elastic moduli

Elastic moduli of non-treated HGs for all cell lines were measured using a mechanical compression test performed with a Zwick Roell Z0.5 system. Following two or three weeks of culture, HGs were placed on a microscope slide, and their width, height and depth were measured with a ruler. A subset of HGs of both VN conditions was crafted without any cells to use as reference for mechanical compression tests. HG morphology was approximated as a cube. The elastomer was set to a pre-charge of 3 mN and compressive force was applied to a speed of 20% of deformation/min, until reaching 30% of deformation. The device recorded the force deformation curve. Measurements showing non-continuous curves were discarded. When possible, three measurements per HG were performed. Elastic moduli, expressed in kPa, were calculated using testXpert III software (Zwick Roell) by linear regression between 5% and 15% deformation, corresponding to the linear region of the force-deformation curve. Data was exported from testXpert III (Zwick Roell software) to R. The curve plots were grouped by time of culture (two and three weeks) and VN addition to the matrix (VN-rich and non-VN-added). To test differences between VN-rich vs. non-VN-added conditions, the two-tailed non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. A p-value threshold of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparisons were predefined based on biological rationale; no multiple testing correction was applied.

Hydrogel histological processing and staining

At the 2- and 3-week time points HGs were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE), then cut into 3 μm sections. HG sections were stained with Haematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) and immunoassayed against VN (EP873Y, Clone; ab45139, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) diluted at 1:100, using OptiView Amplification Kit (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, U.S.A.) in the BenchMark XT automated slide staining system (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, USA).

Two tissue microarrays (TMA) were built for super-resolution microscopy. TMA cores of 1.2 mm were extracted from FFPE blocks including tissue from SK-N-BE(2), SH-SY5Y, PDX1 and PDX2 for both VN conditions. TMA was cut in into 3 μm sections for fluorescence staining. Samples were deparaffined using a battery of decreasing alcohol concentrations, and antigen retrieval was performed by heating samples for 20 min in the microwave in citrate buffer 10 mM PH 6.0. The slides were then rinsed in water and incubated in 20 mM glycine solution for 5 min to reduce quenching. After three 5-min washes in PBS, the slides were incubated three times for 5 min with 0.3% PBS-TritonX100 and then blocked with 1% BSA solution for 1 h. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against vinculin (V9131 Mouse, Merck) and integrin β3 (D7X3P Rabbit, Cell Signaling) diluted to 1:200 in 1% BSA solution. After three washes with PBST 0.5%, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies with TexasRed at 1:100 dilution (Horse anti-mouse IgG Antibody [H + L], TI-2000 Vector Laboratories) and Alexa488 at 1:250 dilution (Donkey anti-rabbit IgG Antibody [H + L], Invitrogen) for 1 h. After three 5-min washes with PBST 0.5%, the samples were incubated with DAPI (Invitrogen™ D1306,1 μg/mL) for 5 min, rinsed with PBS and mounted with antifading media (Fluoromount-G™ Mounting Medium).

Slide digitisation, hydrogel area determination and fluorescence image post-processing

H&E and anti-VN slides were scanned with Ventana iScanHT (Roche) at 20x using the HT strategy, resulting in TIF format RGB images at 0.465 μm/pixel resolution. A subset of slides was scanned with 3D Histech PANNORAMIC 250 Flash III 40x, obtaining TIF format RGB images at a resolution of 0.25 μm/pixel. Digital slides were reviewed in QuPath (version 0.6) [46] by an experienced histologist and pathologist to confirm the technical quality of the HG sections and HR-NB-compatible histomorphology. HG area assessment on H&E-stained sections was performed in QuPath, where segmentation was achieved using pixel classifiers adapted for each image. For descriptive purposes, cell clusters in 3-week H&E sections were additionally segmented using our DANEELpath toolkit for QuPath [47] and validated. Qualitative inspection of both H&E and anti-VN staining was performed at low magnification to assess overall section integrity and cell cluster density/distribution. At higher magnification, cellular organisation and peri-cluster regions were examined to identify features varying across VN microenvironments and CLG treatment in both staining techniques. These observations guided the selection of descriptors later prioritised for digital pathology quantification.

Super-resolution imaging of vinculin and β3 integrin was performed using the ZEISS LSM 980 with Airyscan 2, equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 63x objective and a scan zoom of 1.9. Following image acquisition, the Airyscan 2 deconvolution algorithm was applied using the ZEN Microscopy Software (ZEISS) to each of the samples, resulting in a resolution of 0.0353 μm/pixel for all samples. Vinculin and β3 integrin signals appeared as bright punctate structures, but background fluorescence was present due to the FFPE processing. Background removal was performed with a custom pipeline using FIJI [48] and Python. Briefly, vinculin and β3 channels were isolated using a custom FIJI macro. Next, a Python pipeline was generated by using pyClesperanto package [49]. The process involved applying a Difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) filter (σ = 2 vs. σ = 10). The resulting DoG image was duplicated, and a sum of oriented openings was applied using linear kernels (20 pixels long) at angular increments of 5° (from 0° to 180°). Finally, the sum of oriented openings was subtracted from the DoG image, enhancing the dot-like structures characteristic of vinculin and β3 integrin. A second custom FIJI macro was used to generate a composite image combining DAPI, the original vinculin/β3 channel, and the enhanced channels. Composites were visually inspected in QuPath as in brightfield images. For one representative figure, vinculin and β3 integrin signals were segmented using QuPath's built-in watershed detection to aid visualization.

Next Generation Sequencing of hydrogels

We applied Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) panel to discern the effect of HG composition, culture time and CLG treatment on tumour genomics in the four NB cell lines. DNA was extracted from the cells cultured in the HGs using the Maxwell(R) RSC FFPE Plus DNA Kit (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, U.S.A.) following the manufacturer's instructions. The previously described customized NGS mechanopanel was applied to identify copy number variations (CNVs), small insertions and deletions (indels), and single nucleotide variants (SNVs) [38]. We employed the SeqCap EZ HyperPlus kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) and the NextSeq 550 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, California, U.S.A.) to obtain at least 100x coverage. Raw data were filtered for quality with PRINSEQ-lite v0.20.4 and mapped against human genome GRCh38 with BWA-MEM v0.7.17. Variant calling was performed with a combination of GATK v4.2.0.0.0 and LoFreq v2.1.3.1. of SNV and indel annotation was performed with Variant Effect Predictor software and the Ensembl v105 database. CNV detection was performed using CopywriteR v2.29.0 and processed with CGHcall v2.58.0.

Isolation of hydrogel cells, orthotopic inoculation and in vivo imaging

To assess whether cells grown within the HGs could be inoculated orthotopically, we developed a pipeline for cell isolation. Cells were transfected with a lentiviral FLUC vector expressing luciferase and mCherry [50]. Transduced cells from all cell lines were cultured in non-VN-added HGs for three weeks. HGs were transferred to six well plates and washed with dPBS. To disrupt the silk fibroin structure, HGs were incubated with Liberase (0.05 mg mL, Liberase HT, Merck) for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by gentle mechanical disaggregation and an additional 10-min incubation. Liberase activity was reduced by adding FBS to a final concentration of 10%. Cells were centrifuged to remove Liberase and resuspended in culture medium. A second centrifugation step was followed by trypsinization for 5 min at 37 °C to achieve cell individualisation. Trypsin activity was stopped by adding 10% FBS, and viable cells were counted. The cell pellet was washed and resuspended in dPBS.

For orthotopic inoculation, Matrigel (Corning, 356234) was mixed with the cell suspension in dPBS at a 1:1 ratio, and one million cells were injected into the left adrenal gland of a total of 52 RAG1−/−/VN−/− mice (RAG1−/− B6.129S7 Rag1tm1Mom J; VN−/− B6.129S2 Vtntm1Dgi J) [28]. Animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled 12 h light/dark cycles and ad libitum access to food and water.

Tumour progression was monitored using bioluminescence (luciferase) assay every 10 days at the University of Valencia Animal Care core facilities. Briefly, 10 min before image capture mice were submitted to intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg body weight of luciferin (D-Luciferin potassium salt, AAT Bioquest). Induction with isoflurane and capture of bioluminescence was achieved using the IVIS system (IVIS Lumina X5 Imaging System). If no tumour was detected after two months, assay frequency was reduced to once per month.

Once tumour presence was confirmed, animals were enrolled in a parallel experimental protocol involving repeated intraperitoneal administrations of either CLG (n = 24) or vehicle control (n = 28). For animal experimentation CLG was dissolved in saline solution and administered at a dose of 20 mg/kg body weight every two days for a total of six doses, as previously described [31]. Control animals received six saline injections. At the experimental endpoint, animals were euthanised by cervical dislocation. When macroscopically detectable, the left adrenal gland with tumour mass was collected with other organs and fixed in FFPE for histological analysis.

The elastic modulus of VN-rich 3D models tends to be higher than non-VN-added matrices

Mechanical properties of GTA–sf HGs were assessed by elastic modulus measurements after two and three weeks of culture under non-VN-added and VN-rich conditions (Fig. 2). Force–deformation curves (Supplementary Materials 1) revealed median elastic modulus values ranging from 0.71 to 2.55 kPa across all conditions, falling within the 0.17-8.45 kPa range previously reported for HR-NB tissues [21]. Acellular HGs showed that VN incorporation did not significantly change stiffness, suggesting that VN functions primarily as a biochemical rather than structural component of the hydrogel network. No statistically significant differences were detected either within individual cell lines when comparing VN-rich and non-VN-added conditions or at two versus three weeks of culture. Despite this, consistent trends towards increased stiffness on VN-rich matrices were observed. When comparing VN conditions, VN-rich matrices generally exhibited higher elastic moduli than non-VN-added HGs at the 2-week time point in all cell lines, suggesting an early stiffening effect associated with VN incorporation. Although the temporal differences were not statistically significant, a trend toward higher stiffness at early time points and slightly lower values at later stages was observed. Early mechanical increases have previously been reported in enzymatically crosslinked silk-based hydrogels, likely reflecting ongoing post-gelation structural maturation [51]. In parallel, the known susceptibility of these HGs to enzymatic degradation suggests that the later reduction in stiffness may be consistent with progressive matrix remodelling by the embedded cells. Overall, these results indicate that VN incorporation modulates the mechanical properties of GTA–sf HGs in a time- and model-dependent manner, while maintaining stiffness values within physiologically relevant ranges. In addition, GTA–sf HGs were compatible with immunohistochemical staining of Ki67, BCL2, Caspase 3, and YAP (Fig. 3B). In representative SK-N-BE(2) VN-rich HGs, qualitative evaluation suggest CLG-treated samples showed reduced Ki67 and BCL2 staining together with increased Caspase 3 signal compared with controls. Caspase 3 positivity in CLG-treated samples also tended to localize at the cluster periphery. In contrast, YAP signal was low in both conditions.

Anti-VN staining revealed a strong DAB signal throughout the matrix in VN-rich conditions, indicating effective incorporation of exogenous VN (Fig. 3C). Within and inside cell clusters, VN was also similarly expressed under both matrix conditions, as we have previously described in malignant neuroblastic cell niches in human biopsies. Notably, anti-VN staining revealed two distinctive peri-cell cluster features. In non-VN-added conditions, some cell clusters were surrounded by an area of VN deposition, referred to as a VN-corona (Fig. 3C). In VN-rich conditions, a lightly stained region was observed around certain cell clusters, which we call a pale-halo.

In addition, FFPE sections supported fluorescence staining and super-resolution imaging. Super-resolution microscopy combined with digital image post-processing enabled visualization of adhesion-related proteins, vinculin and integrin β3 at subcellular resolution (Fig. 3D).

Qualitative histopathology reveals model-specific morphological and spatial responses to VN microenvironments and CLG treatment

H&E staining showed that all four HR-NB models formed multicellular structures within both VN microenvironments and treatment conditions at the 3-week time point. Section area was quantified in QuPath across 44 HG slides cultured for three weeks, yielding a mean ± SD of 11.84 ± 4.52 mm2. For descriptive purposes, DANEELpath-based segmentation of the same H&E sections identified a total of 35,895 detectable cellular structures (including cell clusters and individual cells), corresponding to a mean ± SD of 815.79 ± 667.40 structures per HG. These quantitative descriptors provide a basis for systematic morphometric analysis, including cluster size, shape, density, and spatial distribution, enabling objective comparison across experimental conditions. Such analyses are essential to move beyond qualitative assessment and establish reproducible morphology-based response metrics, and represent a key direction for future studies using this platform. Qualitative evaluation revealed several changes in spatial and morphological features of cell clusters according to VN and CLG environments. Under non-VN-added conditions, SK-N-BE(2) showed the highest cell cluster density among models, and under CLG treatment clusters frequently appeared partially detached from the surrounding matrix; in PDX1, cluster organisation appeared markedly disrupted (Fig. 4A–Di–iv). Under VN-rich conditions, cluster density increased relative to non-VN-added matrices, and larger clusters were more frequent; following CLG treatment, density appeared reduced and clusters tended to be more widely spaced (Fig. 5A–Di–iv). Our study of SNVs or indels revealed no new variants or changes in allele frequencies in any of the four NB cell lines when cultured in HGs. Only those already found in the 2D cultures were detected, notably the pathogenic mutations p.C135F in TP53 of SK-N-BE (2), p.F1174L in ALK and p.G12V in KRAS of SH-SY5Y.

Discussion

The use of 3D HGs has been widely adopted in cancer research, with notable applications affording a deeper understanding of the biology of multiple pathologies such as glioblastoma tumour and prostate tumour carcinoma. The range of strategies has broadened from organoid systems to the diverse selection of biomaterials used in these studies, including gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), alginate, hyaluronan, polyethylene glycol (PEG), cellulose-based scaffolds, and Matrigel [22,23,[52], [53], [54]]. While Matrigel-based systems provide a biologically relevant ECM environment, they are complex and incompletely defined basement membrane extracts enriched in laminin and collagen IV, and therefore offer limited control over matrix composition. Similarly, although organoid systems are highly valuable, they often rely on complex and variable extracellular matrices that can limit precise control over individual matrix cues [55]. Importantly, beyond general biomaterial diversity, a key limitation of several synthetic platforms such as PEG-based systems lies in their reduced intrinsic bioactivity and dependence on chemical functionalisation to introduce adhesion ligands [56]. In contrast, the GTA–sf system combines inherent bioactivity with enzymatically driven crosslinking, enabling direct incorporation of ECM proteins such as VN without additional modification steps. This platform therefore enables controlled modulation of VN as a defined adhesion cue in a reproducible and experimentally accessible setting [20].

In HR-NB research, 3D models have been developed using diverse approaches, including bioprinting, microfluidic platforms, and HG cultures of commercial cell lines, PDX, and patient derived organoid (PDO) models and co-culture systems [22,23,[57], [58], [59], [60]]. While these strategies provided valuable insights, most reported systems are limited to short culture durations (typically ≤15 days), which constrains long-term studies and cumulative drug testing. Our group has previously explored several 3D modelling strategies, including GelMA-based bioprinted systems that enable long-term culture of commercial HR-NB cell lines for up to 12 weeks and support downstream genetic and histological analyses. However, these models relied on specialised 3D bioprinting equipment and did not incorporate VN as a matrix component [22]. A PEG–maleimide HG system was also evaluated, allowing controlled stiffness, degradability and VN incorporation via the chemical addition of PEG arms, but it involved complex chemical handling, was studied in shorter culture times (up to 14 days), and was not compatible with standard FFPE histological processing [23]. Thus, whereas the previous VN/PEG platform provided a more chemically defined proof of concept for matrix-bound VN under short-term conditions, the present GTA–sf/VN system offers a complementary platform for longer-term, histology-compatible analysis of VN-dependent phenotypes. In this context, the GTA–sf HG platform offers a simplified and versatile 3D system, enabling VN incorporation via enzymatic dityrosine crosslinking. The biological relevance of the system arises from its ability to combine controlled VN availability with a protein-based 3D environment that supports cell–matrix remodelling. In the present study, VN-rich conditions were associated with measurable differences in cluster organisation and treatment-associated phenotypes relative to non-VN-added conditions, supporting the functional relevance of VN within this matrix setting. Previous research has demonstrated its suitability for advanced analysis such as holographic microscopy and detection of VN secretion by ELISA [32,33]. This earlier work showed no detectable VN in the culture media of cell-free HGs by ELISA, indicating a stable incorporation and retention of VN within the HG matrix [32]. In the present study, we have shown that HGs mechanical properties fall within HR-NB values [21], enable long-term culture of both commercial and PDX cell lines, integrin-targeted drug response, and are fully compatible with FFPE histology and advanced imaging. This range of histological and imaging approaches enabled qualitative examination of hundreds of cell clusters per tissue section. The histological description focused primarily on the 3-week time point, when differences in tissue organisation and cell–matrix interactions are more readily observable across experimental conditions. Nonetheless, the models were successfully analysed at various time points during the cultivation process. The VN-rich matrix platform has enabled greater cluster density and proximity between clusters, as well as increased cell–cell and cell–ECM interactions that may promote proliferation, autocrine chemokine gradients, and collective migratory events, as described in other 3D systems [[61], [62], [63]]. Anti-VN staining demonstrated that VN expression appears in both non-VN-added and VN-rich matrices with variations between cell lines. These peri-cluster structures likely reflect dynamic ECM remodelling and integrin-mediated adhesion processes. From a mechanobiological perspective, these observations can be interpreted within a framework in which tumour cell behaviour arises from the integration of ligand presentation and matrix mechanics. This concept is consistent with established materiomechanobiology principles, where cell fate emerges from the coordinated integration of ECM composition and bulk mechanical cues through multiscale mechanotransduction processes [64]. VN incorporation provides adhesive ligands that can promote αv-integrin engagement, leading to integrin clustering and adhesion reinforcement at the cell-matrix interface. This process is expected to enhance focal adhesion assembly and cytoskeletal tension, thereby activating downstream mechanotransduction pathways such as FAK/Src signalling and RhoA-mediated contractility, with potential convergence on transcriptional regulators including YAP/TAZ. While these observations are consistent with integrin engagement, it is important to note that the present study does not include direct quantification of downstream signalling activity, and these pathways remain to be functionally validated in future work. Importantly, these ligand-driven effects act in concert with bulk matrix stiffness, which modulates force transmission and cellular mechanosensing, ultimately shaping tumour phenotype. The formation of VN-corona and pale-halo regions may indicate localised secretion, degradation, or redistribution of VN driven by tumour cells, consistent with the adaptive adhesion mechanisms and matrix remodelling observed in other tumour systems. Within this context, these features may also reflect spatial he biological relevance of the system arises fromorganisation of adhesion forces and integrin-mediated signalling, emerging from biochemical–mechanical coupled regulation of the microenvironment [65]. Such features further support the relevance of this platform to study spatially resolved cell–ECM interactions and enable the study of specific regulatory mechanisms for each cell line, which may involve feedback mechanisms or differential use of the matrix [[66], [67], [68]]. In addition, we observed distinct peri-cluster VN structures in HR-NB models, like the ones observed on Ewing Sarcoma cells cultured on the same HGs [69]. VN-corona features were mainly associated with MYCN-amplified models, whereas pale-halo structures were most prominent in the ALK-mutated SH-SY5Y, suggesting genotype-dependent responses to VN availability that should be further explored using more cell lines in this platform. Systematic quantification of such features could therefore help identify morphology-based treatment response indicators, in line with morphological profile approaches [70,71]. It is important to highlight that this study focuses primarily on developing the GTA–sf HG platform as a potential predictive and drug-screening platform rather than comprehensive biological endpoint analysis. The morphological features illustrated in Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11 serve as representative examples of platform performance. Notably, these features can be systematically quantified using our DANEELpath toolkit, enabling robust morphometric and spatial analysis of cell clusters and peri-cluster regions. This quantitative capability underscores the platform's potential for future high-content studies of cell–ECM interactions, integrin-dependent signalling, and drug-response profiling. In this context, digital pathology and automation are essential. Existing tools such as BEHAV3D [72] and SpheroidJ [73] are primarily optimised for live imaging, fluorescence microscopy, or single-spheroid segmentation, rather than for FFPE whole-slide analysis of engineered 3D HGs. To address this gap, our group developed the DANEELpath toolkit for QuPath [47] to enable scalable cell cluster segmentation and extract morphological and spatial features from cell clusters, individual cells, and super-resolution images in these specific 3D HG models. Combined 3D HG culture and digital pathology may complement large-scale HR-NB drug platforms [74] by enabling phenotypic assessment of integrin- and matrix-targeted therapies in long-term cell culture, multi-dose settings. The observed spatial and morphological changes following CLG treatment suggest context-dependent integrin inhibition effects that may not be captured in conventional systems.

Genetic alterations are central to tumour biology and therapeutic stratification, with both inter- and intra-tumour heterogeneity influencing tumour behaviour and responses to targeted therapies [[75], [76], [77], [78]]. Given that long-term 2D culture and microenvironmental perturbations can promote clonal selection and genetic drift [79,80], preserving the original genetic landscape is essential for meaningful drug testing. In this study, HR-NB cells cultured within GTA–sf HGs retained their core genomic features across VN microenvironments and CLG treatment. Notably, loss of the +2p(ter 16.3) gain in SH-SY5Y has previously been reported between 2D and 3D culture in other HG systems, and the gain was present in only 31% of cells in 2D culture [38]. While our analyses focused on genomic copy number, SNVs, and indels, integrating transcriptomic profiling would provide complementary insights into microenvironment-driven gene expression changes, pathway activation, and cell-state heterogeneity. Such analyses could reveal ECM- and VN-dependent transcriptional programs, identify mechanotransduction-responsive genes, and uncover early molecular indicators of integrin-targeted therapy response that are not apparent from DNA-level analyses. In combination with spatial and morphological features derived from digital pathology, transcriptomics could therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of how long-term 3D culture and VN-rich microenvironments shape tumour behaviour and therapeutic susceptibility [81,82].

Together, these findings support that GTA-sf HGs maintain core genomic features while enabling the emergence of microenvironment-driven phenotypes, including multicellular organisation, integrin redistribution, and context-dependent responses to VN and CLG, highlighting the importance of decoupling genetic stability from functional tumour behaviour in 3D culture systems.

This is particularly relevant for phenotypic drug screening approaches that integrate genetic context, as demonstrated by recent large-scale HR-NB drug screening platforms combining genomics with drug response profiling [74].

Another strength of this GTA–sf HG platform is its ability to recover viable cells after long-term 3D culture for downstream applications, including orthotopic implantation. Orthotopic xenografts are a gold standard in HR-NB and PDX models show variable engraftment kinetics while preserving tumour-specific phenotypes [79,83]. This experimental workflow, integrating tuneable 3D culture with downstream orthotopic implantation of recovered cells, enables a new research framework in which ECM composition and stiffness can be systematically varied in vitro and their effects tracked in vivo. It is expected that sustained microenvironmental conditioning activates mechanotransduction pathways that persist after extraction, as a form of mechanical memory, thereby influencing tumour cells behaviour after implanation. Future studies comparing cells preconditioned in VN-rich versus VN-poor matrices, or previously exposed to Cilengitide, will be important to assess how microenvironmental and pharmacological preconditioning influence engraftment efficiency, tumour growth kinetics, and histological characteristics. Moreover, this recovery can facilitate downstream single-cell analyses to investigate microenvironment-driven adaptation and differentiation trajectories [84]. Future work should incorporate orthotopic transplantation features, quantitative assessment of mechanotransduction and cell fate markers, including YAP/TAZ localisation, proliferation markers such as Ki67, and apoptosis markers such as cleaved caspase-3. Integrating these markers with spatial and morphometric features derived from digital pathology could provide a more comprehensive framework to link ECM mechanics with tumour cell behaviour and therapeutic response.

In summary, we present a versatile 3D GTA–sf HG platform with tuneable VN microenvironments, demonstrating that matrix engineering, long-term culture, drug testing, histopathological assays, molecular analysis, and in vivo translation can be integrated within a single 3D system. Its applicability is illustrated in HR-NB, with potential across other childhood cancer models.
