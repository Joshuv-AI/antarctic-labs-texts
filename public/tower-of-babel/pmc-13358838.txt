Connecting the dots: historical perspective on lung volume reduction
Authors: Avantika Nathani, Jean Luis Reinoso, Ashutosh Sachdeva, Tudor P Toma, Atul C Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung volume reduction (LVR) has evolved over nearly a century as a therapeutic strategy for advanced emphysema, reflecting cycles of innovation, skepticism, and renewed acceptance informed by advances in physiology, imaging, and patient selection. Early surgical approaches—including bullectomy, intracavitary drainage techniques, and nonselective lung resection—laid the conceptual groundwork but were limited by high morbidity and inconsistent outcomes. The modern era of LVR was catalyzed by refined surgical techniques and culminated in the National Emphysema Treatment Trial, which established the critical importance of phenotype-based patient selection. Parallel advances in bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR) expanded treatment options, particularly through the development of endobronchial valves (EBVs), coils, thermal vapor ablation, and biologic approaches. Among these, EBVs have achieved the strongest evidence base and regulatory approval, supported by improved understanding of collateral ventilation and fissure integrity. More recently, minimally invasive surgical approaches, robotic-assisted techniques, hybrid procedures, and expanded indications—including lower-lobe disease, very severe airflow limitation, and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD)—have further broadened the therapeutic landscape. LVR also plays an evolving role as an adjunct to lung transplantation, both as a bridge and for management of native lung hyperinflation (NLH). This narrative review traces the historical arc of LVR, highlighting how past successes and failures inform contemporary practice. As technology advances, multidisciplinary evaluation and increasingly personalized, data-driven decision-making will be central to optimizing outcomes and guiding the future of emphysema management.Keywords: Emphysema, lung volume reduction (LVR), endobronchial valves (EBVs), lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

Introduction

Lung volume reduction (LVR) is a well-established intervention for managing advanced emphysema in select patients. By surgically removing or bronchoscopically isolating damaged lung areas, it optimizes respiratory function, enhances airflow, and improves quality of life (QoL). LVR alleviates dyspnea, increases exercise tolerance, and may delay the need for lung transplantation.

The concept of lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) emerged nearly a century ago. This review is intended to connect the historical pivotal milestones in the evolution of LVR rather than a head-to-head procedural comparison or clinical practice guideline. Our focus is on how innovations in patient selection, technique, and risk understanding have shaped current paradigms. Connecting the dots helps inform current practice and guides future innovations in emphysema management (Figure 1).

The beginning

Bullectomy

The surgical resection of bullae, or bullectomy, has been in practice for many decades to treat giant bullous emphysema that causes compression of surrounding lung tissue, resulting in dyspnea. This was historically performed using an open technique via a thoracotomy or median sternotomy. In the earlier part of the 20th century, it was considered a last resort for palliative management in patients with advanced emphysema.

Monaldi procedure [1938]

In the 1930s, Monaldi (Figure 2), an Italian surgeon, developed a technique for draining apically located tuberculous cavities, lung abscesses, and giant bullae. His method involved intracavitary suction and drainage, particularly for relieving tension in tuberculous abscess cavities (1,2). This was a two-stage procedure, by first inducing pleural adhesions using iodine packs to reduce the risk of pneumothorax and then draining the bulla. It was also used as a salvage procedure for palliative relief in advanced bilateral cases or as a preparatory step for more definitive surgery, like thoracoplasty (3).

Head and Avery [1949]

In 1949, Head and Avery applied the Monaldi technique to treat emphysematous bullae in critically ill patients who could not tolerate thoracotomy (4). Chest X-rays guided optimal drainage by identifying the bleb’s proximity to the chest wall. The procedure involved detecting or inducing pleural adhesions, inserting a catheter into the cavity, and connecting it to a water-seal chamber (Figure 3). This technique promoted lung re-expansion via gentle negative pressure and obliterated the bleb through catheter-induced inflammation. Patients experienced symptomatic improvement, but despite this technique being advocated by the authors, most surgeons still preferred bullectomy, unless it was an inoperable case (5).

Crenshaw and Rowles [1952]

The shift from resecting bullous disease to removing non-bullous emphysematous lung laid the foundation for modern LVRS. This concept was first introduced in the early 1950s by Drs. Gerald Crenshaw and Donald Rowles of Oakland, California (6). They performed thoracotomies in 11 patients with resection (segmental or lobar) of emphysematous lung and bullectomies to allow for mechanical relief from increased lung volumes and stimulate collateral circulation between the systemic and pulmonary vasculature. These surgeries also involved complete autonomic denervation of the lung and talc pleurodesis. Most patients had subjective improvement in symptoms and the ability to return to daily activities. Despite being an innovative technique, it was ahead of its time, with limited acceptance into clinical practice.

Otto Brantigan [1959]

In 1959, Dr. Otto Brantigan of the University of Maryland hypothesized that removing non-functional pulmonary tissue could restore lung elasticity and maintain bronchiolar patency (Figure 4A: Brantigan’s hypothesis) (7). In 1961, he introduced a staged thoracotomy technique using a clamp-and-sew method for LVR, radical hilar stripping, and parasympathetic lung denervation (Figure 4B) (8). Despite reporting improvement in 75% of patients, the procedure had a high 16% mortality rate and lacked objective validation, leading to limited acceptance (9). Respected pulmonary physiologists of the time, Knudson and Gaensler, criticized this approach commenting that “although the idea of restoration of lung tension and a more negative intrathoracic pressure is attractive, it is difficult to believe that a disease characterized by extensive loss of lung parenchyma can be effectively treated by further resection of lung” (10).

Laser photocoagulation

In 1991, Wakabayashi et al. (11) introduced thoracoscopic ablation of pulmonary bullae using low-energy carbon dioxide (CO2) laser photocoagulation. While it showed subjective improvement in dyspnea, a 9% mortality rate and high incidence of lung injury led to its replacement by the sapphire contact neodymium: yttrium-aluminum-garnet (Nd-YAG) laser.

A retrospective review of over 500 Nd-YAG laser procedures reported 87% subjective improvement and enhanced pulmonary function (12). Little et al. further demonstrated improvements in Borg’s scores, modified medical research council scores, and pulmonary function tests (PFTs), with a 30-day mortality rate of 5.5% (13). However, despite initial reports of success, However, a randomized study comparing stapled resection and laser ablation found similar morbidity but an 18% higher incidence of delayed pneumothorax in the laser group (14). Additionally, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) improvement was greater in the stapled resection group, with fewer reports of dyspnea. Due to these inferior outcomes and safety concerns, laser photocoagulation was eventually abandoned in favor of stapled resection.

Brompton procedure

In 1994, the Brompton technique (Figure 5) was introduced as a modification of the Monaldi procedure for cavities (15). This one-stage procedure involved using computed tomography (CT) guidance, performing a limited thoracotomy, resection of the underlying rib, opening of the bulla using purse string sutures, instilling iodized talc followed by closure of the purse string sutures with a Foley catheter in place which was then removed on postoperative day 8. They reported subjective and PFT improvement in the majority of patients. This was further modified by Froeschle et al. in 2012 where they converted the open-access Brompton procedure to video-assisted thoracoscopy surgery (VATS) (16).

Early years

Joel Cooper

The resurgence of LVRS for the treatment of emphysema occurred over 30 years later in 1995, when Cooper (Figure 6A) et al. observed improved chest wall compliance and diaphragmatic position in emphysema patients undergoing lung transplantation. With strict selection criteria (only 20% of referred patients) and mandatory pulmonary rehabilitation, they refined the procedure to excise 20–30% of lung volume, primarily in the upper lobes, using pericardial strip buttressing via median sternotomy (Figure 6B) (9,17). Patients with centrilobular emphysema and upper lobe involvement showed the most benefit. At 6 months, LVRS improved pulmonary function, dyspnea, and QoL, with a 90-day mortality of 4%. These benefits persisted at 1- and 2-year follow-ups (92% survival at 2 years). This reappraisal led to multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) confirming LVRS as an effective treatment for select patients (18-21).

The first trial

National emphysema treatment trial

Following Joel Cooper’s success with LVRS, the procedure gained popularity despite a lack of long-term data and standardized selection criteria. This led to a great variability in results from beneficial effects to unforeseen complications. To address this, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services the National Institute of Health and its lead agency for healthcare quality, the Agency for Health Research and Quality, in a cooperative effort, conducted the National Emphysema Treatment Trial (NETT). This was a multicenter RCT to assess LVRS outcomes, risks, and patient selection. A 2001 interim analysis identified a high-risk cohort [FEV1 <20%, diffusion capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) <20%, homogenous emphysema] with a 16% mortality rate (22). By 2003, NETT results showed that in all-comers, LVRS did not improve overall survival but enhanced exercise capacity Patients with non-upper-lobe emphysema and high exercise tolerance had increased mortality and minimal functional gain. However, those with predominant upper-lobe emphysema and low exercise capacity experienced significant survival benefits. Long-term follow-up confirmed durable benefits in this subgroup (23). Incidentally, Joel Cooper, the pioneer of the revival of this procedure did not participate in the trial based on the design of the study.

Despite NETT’s landmark status in providing evidence that LVRS benefits carefully selected patients, relatively few patients received the procedure after the trial (24). LVRS utilization remained low due to procedural restrictions to NETT centers, regulatory barriers, clinician knowledge gaps, and perceptions of high cost and complexity (25). The strict inclusion criteria and exclusion of high-risk phenotypes along with the reimbursement restrictions limited the generalizability of the procedure to a wider range of emphysema patients.

Other techniques

Trans-pleural fenestration

In the 1970s, Macklem proposed extrapulmonary fenestration to enhance expiratory flow in emphysema by creating direct chest wall openings to bypass airway obstruction, similar to insect spiracles (Figure 7A) (26). The concept was that air would take the path of least resistance and in patients with high expiratory airway resistance as in patients with emphysema, expiratory flow would occur preferentially through these collateral channels. Chahla et al. later observed three lung transplant patients with inadvertent trans-pleural ventilation (27). The expiratory flow through these channels was 100% in two patients while one had 75%. There was also a significant improvement in arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide at the same ventilator settings suggesting improved alveolar ventilation despite potential benefits, further research has been limited over the past 15 years due to safety concerns and infection risks.

Bronchial fenestration

Bronchial fenestration, creating a connection between emphysematous lungs and larger airways, was first tested ex vivo in 2003 using bronchoscopy and radiofrequency catheter stent placement, showing FEV1 improvement proportional to stent number (Figure 7B) (28). Subsequent ex vivo studies confirmed enhanced lung mechanics (29). In 2007, Cardoso et al. demonstrated in a feasibility study that paclitaxel-coated airway bypass stents reduced hyperinflation and improved lung function in severe emphysema (30). The Exhale Airway Stents for Emphysema (EASE) trial found initial improvements in forced vital capacity post-procedure, but benefits disappeared by three months, likely due to stent expectoration, mucus occlusion, or granulation tissue formation (31). Most recently, the BREATHE group developed a novel, helix shaped, self-expanding ninotol airway scaffold that connected emphysematous segments to central airways. They reported safety at 6 months with 21.7% patients experiencing adverse events like pneumonias or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation (32). Patients reported improvement in symptoms, QoL and exercise capacity with PFTs showing a mean decrease in residual volume at 6 months. These initial trial results are promising and warrant further investigation.

Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR)

BLVR emerged as an alternative to LVRS due to its associated morbidity, prolonged hospitalization, air leaks, and mortality. A retrospective review of LVRS patients reported a 10.5% rate of inpatient mortality or tracheostomy (33). BLVR primarily involves placing an endobronchial blocker or one-way valve to induce functional collapse of distal lung segments. First described in 2001 by Tudor Toma as “endoscopic shut down”, the approach was inspired by the collapse of lung tissue distal to an aspirated foreign body (34). In 2003, Sabanathan (Figure 8) et al. introduced detachable silicone balloons, later modified into stainless steel wire stents with biocompatible sponge (35). Another approach involves extrapulmonary pathways to enhance expiratory flow (described above). Among various BLVR techniques, endobronchial valves (EBVs) remain the most widely used (36). The evolution of these techniques has paralleled advances in imaging and physiologic assessment of patients with selection guided by emphysema distribution, fissure completeness and collateral ventilation assessment to predict the success of the procedure.

Of the below mentioned BLVR modalities, only EBV has received both United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval [2018] and the Conformité Européenne (CE) mark [2003]. Endobronchial coils and thermal vapor ablation have been CE marked in 2010 and 2011 respectively.

Endobronchial Vapor

Bronchoscopic Thermal Vapor Ablation (BTVA) is a BLVR technique using 100 °C water vapor through the InterVapor system (Uptake Medical Technology, Inc., San Jose, CA) to emphysematous lung to induce localized inflammation, fibrosis, and LVR (Figure 9A) (37). A pre-procedure CT helps calculate the time needed to achieve 8.5 calories of energy per gram of lung tissue (38,39). It does not require fissure completeness or collateral ventilation assessment.

Initial studies showed feasibility and LVR, with improvements in pulmonary function and quality-of-life scores (40,41). The STEP-UP trial demonstrated significant FEV1 and St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) score improvements, though adverse events like pneumonia and COPD exacerbations were noted (42). Current expert recommendations are to limit use of BTVA to clinical trials or commercial registries given sparse data but we anticipate that it’s role will expanding with more recent data and newer delivery systems (37,43).

Endobronchial coils

Lung volume reduction coils (LVRC) are a reversible, non-blocking BLVR device made of memory-shape nitinol, placed bronchoscopically to improve lung mechanics. However, safety concerns include higher rates of infections, hemoptysis, and inflammatory responses. The ELEVATE trial [2021] showed improvements in FEV1 and SGRQ but was terminated early due to discontinuation of the coil system, with a higher mortality rate in the coil group.

Herth et al. proposed LVRC as a reversible non-blocking BLVR device made of memory-shape nitinol, placed bronchoscopically (using a proprietary system: PneumRx, Inc., Mountain View, Calif., USA) to improve lung mechanics (Figure 9B) (44,45). Between 2010 and 2020, three RCTs have evaluated their efficacy, showing improvements in FEV1, six-minute walk test (6MWT), and QoL (46-49). A systematic review of the three RCTs found significant between-group mean differences in FEV1 and SGRQ with no differences in mortality but more adverse events in the treatment group (50). There were no significant differences in mortality but more adverse events in the treatment group. Most recently, the ELEVATE trial [2021] showed improvements in FEV1 and SGRQ but was terminated early due to discontinuation of the coil system (ELEVAIRTM endobronchial coil system), with a higher mortality rate in the coil group (51).

At this time, experts emphasize careful patient selection, particularly for those with severe hyperinflation and upper-lobe heterogeneous emphysema. Caution is advised against coil-associated inflammatory response that can occur in some patients (52).

EBVs

EBV for bronchoscopic BLVR has evolved significantly since their first pilot studies in 2003 by Tudor Toma (Figure 10A) (53). Interestingly, Snell concluded there was significant reduction in ventilation and perfusion of the target lobes by nuclear scans (54). In 2006, the then-largest multicenter study showed improvement in PFTs in patients treated unilaterally (55). Results up to this date, although promising, were criticized due to lack of control arms. Notably, William Lunn in his editorial, called BLVR a “cart before the horse” (56).

In 2010, Sterman et al. reported the first results of Spiration (Intrabronchial Valve: Redmond, Washington, USA) valves with improvement in QoL outcomes (57). In the same year, Scurbia published the VENT study showed improvement in FEV1 and 6MWT but with higher complication rates in the valve group compared to medical therapy alone (58). They identified a subgroup of patients (high heterogeneity and complete fissures) to have better outcomes. Based on this, in 2015, the BeLieVer-HIFi study was designed with patients with heterogeneous emphysema and intact interlobar fissures with promising results (59). Collateral ventilation was assessed with the use of the Chartis system (PulmonX, Redwood City, CA, USA), a balloon catheter designed to occlude the target lobe and measure airflow at the distal tip (Figure 11A). The STELVIO trial also showed similar results (60).

This led to the two pivotal trials, first, the LIBERATE trial was pivotal, leading to FDA approval of the Zephyr valve (PulmonX, Redwood City, CA, USA) in June 2018 (Figure 11B) (61). This was followed by the FDA approval for the Spiration Valve System (Figure 11C) (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) in December 2018 after the EMPROVE trial (62). A 2022 meta-analysis confirmed long-term benefits in PFTs, exercise tolerance, and QoL, particularly for patients with heterogeneous emphysema and no collateral ventilation (63).

Biological LVR

Biological sealants are locally delivered adhesives that trigger an inflammatory and fibrotic response within the bronchi, leading to irreversible LVR (64). The first-generation bioLVR gel, composed of chondroitin sulfate and poly-L-lysine within a fibrin vehicle, showed early promise in lung function improvement (65,66), but was eventually replaced by AeriSeal® (PulmonX, CA, USA), a polymeric foam (Figure 12). Initial studies with AeriSeal® demonstrated improvements in lung function, exercise capacity, and QoL. A 2015 RCT confirmed these benefits, though the study was terminated early due to financial constraints. Despite positive outcomes, increased adverse events like pneumonia and COPD exacerbations were noted (67). More recently, polymer foam has been explored to eliminate collateral ventilation in patients with incomplete fissures before EBV placement, showing outcomes similar to those receiving EBV therapy alone. (68). Ongoing studies [Mind The Gap—Crossing Borders Study (69) and CONVERT (70)] are investigating the potential of this approach to expand EBV use to patients with collateral ventilation.

LVRS versus BLVR

The 2023 CELEB trial was the first randomized controlled study comparing LVRS to BLVR in patients eligible for both treatments (71). Using the i-BODE score (which evaluates body mass index, airflow obstruction, dyspnea grade, and shuttle walk test performance), the study found comparable improvements in overall scores, individual components, and air trapping between the two groups. The decision between LVRS and BLVR remains complex, requiring individualized patient assessment. Table 1 outlines the differences between the two procedures; while our purpose is not to recommend one procedure over the other, we strongly believe in a multidisciplinary approach to assess individual patients. This aligns with other expert guidelines, emphasizing patient-specific evaluation when determining the most appropriate treatment pathway (72,73).

Technological advancements

Bullectomy

Bullectomy has undergone significant advancement with the introduction of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (74-76). Recent developments include performing this technique in non-intubated patients using locoregional anesthesia to reduce hospital length of stay, operating time, and complication rate while maintaining safety and efficacy (77,78). Case reports are now describing a uniportal approach for bullectomy to reduce hospital length of stay and improve aesthetic outcomes without increasing the rate of complications (79,80).

Robotic surgery

The latest advancement in minimally invasive LVRS is the introduction of robot-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) (81). Initially described as a technique utilizing three 8-mm incisions, RATS aims to reduce scarring, lower infection risk, and potentially shorten hospital stays. A retrospective analysis by Lee et al. compared hospital resource utilization between BLVR and RATS (82). Their findings suggest that while an uneventful BLVR procedure requires fewer resources than RATS, this advantage diminishes when complications such as pneumothorax or the need for revisional procedures arise. Additionally, RATS has been explored as a method to complete fissures in patients with positive collateral ventilation, thereby facilitating the successful placement of EBVs (83).

Hybrid procedures

Hybrid procedures utilize surgically completing the interlobar fissure (using thoracoscopic or robotic techniques) to eliminate collateral ventilation before placement of EBV for LVR. First studied in animal models in 2020 (84), this technique has since been reported (both thoracoscopic as well as robot-assisted) in human subjects with improved symptoms and PFTs (83,85). Ongoing clinical trials will hopefully shed light on this novel technique in the upcoming years (86,87).

LVR & lung transplant

LVR before lung transplant

Lung transplantation remains the definitive therapeutic option for patients with end-stage COPD. However, LVR is often utilized as an alternative or as a bridge to transplantation and can sometimes delay or even eliminate the need for transplant by improving lung function and physical conditioning (88). Studies have shown no significant difference in short- and long-term survival between patients who have undergone bilateral lung transplantation after LVR compared to those who received transplants alone (89). However, Krishnan et al. reported worse outcomes in patients who had undergone LVR before transplantation, including lower survival rates and an increased risk of graft failure (90). Despite these concerns, the International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation advises that patients be evaluated simultaneously for both lung transplantation and LVR when clinically appropriate (91).

LVR during or after lung transplant

LVR has been explored as a treatment option for native lung hyperinflation (NLH) (Figure 13), a common complication in post-single lung transplant patients. This intervention can be performed prophylactically during the transplant surgery or as a treatment afterward.

In 2012, Wilson et al. performed LVRS via multiple wedge resections and bilobectomy on patients with NLH (92). Their study reported symptomatic improvement in all patients, with a mean increase in FEV1 and an acceptable surgical risk. In 2015, a multicenter study evaluated the use of EBV for BLVR in NLH patients, finding that most participants experienced symptom relief and improved PFTs (93). Presently, there are no guidelines to treat NLH but expert recommendations include placement of bronchial blocker (in intubated patients) and differential lung ventilation (with low minute ventilation and lower I:E ratio in the native lung) (94). Should this fail, valves are preferred over LVRS.

Newer indications

With better understanding of physiology and improved procedural safety, LVR indications have now expanded beyond the original population defined by NETT, thus broadening the therapeutic reach of the procedure. Some newer indications are:

Patients with very severe disease

The NETT trial reported significantly higher mortality in high-risk patients defined as FEV1 or DLCO <20% as compared to other patients (22). This led to a significant number of subsequent studies to exclude this patient population from receiving LVR interventions. Despite this, single-center retrospective studies showed benefits in these patient groups (95,96). A recent review looked at 6 studies of patients with very low ≤20% FEV1 and/or DLCO for BLVR (97). They found an improvement in PFTs and SGRQ scores in both groups. No deaths were reported in either of these groups and the rates of complications were similar when compared to patients with FEV1 and/or DLCO >20%. This raises the question of perhaps expanding BLVR criteria to include these patients with end-stage lung disease to provide improvement in symptoms, PFTs and QoL.

Lower lobe disease

The NETT trial demonstrated a lack of mortality or functional benefit of LVRS in patients with non-upper lobe predominant disease. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified non-upper lobe predominant emphysema as an independent risk factor for operative mortality (98). These observations led to the practice of LVRS to be primarily aimed at patients with upper lobe predominant disease. However, trial studies for valves often included patients with both upper and lower lobe disease. A retrospective analysis of patients from the VENT study compared outcomes of those with upper lobe versus lower lobe disease (58). At baseline, the upper lobe disease group had higher destruction scores and heterogeneity index but at 6 months, both groups improved clinically with no significant difference in responder rates of FEV1, 6MWT, target lobe volume reduction with a similar safety profile (99). Yang and colleagues found that in patients undergoing lower lobe targeted BLVR, clinical and PFT improvements were like those that underwent upper lobe targeted procedures. They also noted improved ventilation-perfusion matching in the contralateral upper zone (100).

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD)

LVR has historically not been considered for patients with AATD. In 2007, Stoller et al. studied a subgroup of patients in the NETT population with AATD, and while the sample size was small, there was a higher mortality in the surgical arm than the medical treatment arm in patients with AATD (101). For those who underwent LVRS, the benefit of improved exercise tolerance and improvement FEV1 was greater in those who were alpha-1 antitrypsin replete than those with AATD. In their 2016 statement, the Alpha-1 Foundation did not recommend LVRS for patients with COPD related to AATD (102). However, recommendations for this have now evolved with advances in both procedural techniques as well as patient selection and with the introduction of BLVR. A retrospective analysis of EBV treatment done for emphysema, which included patients both with deficiency and low level (without deficiency) showed an improvement in PFTs, exercise tolerance as well as QoL with an acceptable safety profile in both groups with EBV treatment (103). The European Respiratory Society statement now recommends both LVRS and BLVR as a consideration in the treatment of selected patients with emphysema related to AATD (104).

Future directions

The journey of LVR over the last century exemplifies a cyclical pattern in medical innovation—initial enthusiasm, critical reappraisal and eventual integration of physiological understanding into clinical practice. Looking ahead, the future of LVR will be defined by increasingly personalized approaches. Quantitative chest CT and novel imaging platforms allow for granular assessment of disease distribution, destruction scores, and fissure completeness. These imaging tools, along with artificial intelligence (AI)-based algorithms, in complement with clinician experience will help refine patient selection as well as predict outcomes, especially in borderline cases. In addition to device and imaging advances, future strategies will also incorporate composite patient phenotypes like functional metrics, comorbidities and possibly genetic or other biomarker profiles. This, along with radiographic and spirometry data will help tailor individual patient-level decisions.

A multidisciplinary team approach to treat emphysema is the future, especially as procedural options expand and the data available becomes more complex. In some settings, virtual or AI-supported multidisciplinary teams may play a supportive role—offering consistency and access in resource-limited environments. However, such systems should be implemented cautiously, with safeguards to ensure transparency and clinical oversight.

Conclusions

This narrative offers a comprehensive overview of the evolution of LVR in thoracic surgery and bronchoscopy over the last century. Understanding this historical arc helps recognize the missteps and insights of those that preceded us. As we embrace emerging technologies, anchoring them in experience and evidence will be key in avoiding redundancy and accelerating meaningful progress in the future.
