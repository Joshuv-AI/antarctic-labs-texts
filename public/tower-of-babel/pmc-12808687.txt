A Multi-phase CT Dataset for Automated Differential Diagnosis of Liver Tumors
Authors: Xiang´an Wu, Haoyang Su, Yiwei Hua, Yali Xu, Lilong Wang, Xiaosong Wang, Shitian Wang, Bao Jin, Xiao Liu, Xueshuai Wan, Qiang Sun, Xuan Wang, Shunda Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver tumors exhibit significant heterogeneity in etiology, pathology, and treatment response, making accurate differential diagnosis critical for diagnosis and management. While multi-phase contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) provides valuable imaging patterns for differentiation, visual assessment alone is often limited by overlapping features. To address this, we present MCT-LTDiag, a comprehensive Multi-phase CT dataset for Liver Tumor Diagnosis, comprising 517 cases with four-phase contrast-enhanced CT scans (non-contrast, arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases) and five tumor subtypes: hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), colorectal liver metastasis (CRLM), breast cancer liver metastasis (BCLM), and hepatic hemangioma (HH). The dataset features standardized preprocessing, rigorous quality control, and expert-annotated tumor masks. Baseline experiments using radiomics-based machine learning and deep learning models demonstrate the dataset’s utility, with multi-phase integration significantly improving diagnostic performance. MCT-LTDiag serves as a benchmark for advancing automated liver tumor subtype classification and is publicly available to support future research.Subject terms: Cancer, Diagnosis, Cancer imaging

Background & Summary

Liver tumors are highly heterogeneous, including primary liver cancers [e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC)], metastatic liver cancers [e.g., breast cancer liver metastases (BCLM) and colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM)], and benign liver tumors [e.g., hepatic hemangiomas (HH)]1–4.These tumors differ significantly in etiology, pathology, clinical presentation, and prognosis2. Treatment strategies vary: while surgical resection is the mainstay, ICC requires lymph node dissection, HH seldom necessitates intervention, HCC benefits from systemic targeted/immunotherapy, ICC responds better to chemotherapy, and metastatic tumors are managed based on their primary origin1,3–5. Accurate classification is thus critical for diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis—yet misdiagnosis remains common, leading to overtreatment or delays6,7.

Although liver biopsy is the diagnostic gold standard, its invasiveness carries risks like bleeding and tumor seeding8. Non-invasive alternatives, such as multi-phase contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), provide high-resolution dynamic imaging to assess tumor enhancement patterns2,9. These patterns are often diagnostic: HCC typically exhibits “wash-in and wash-out” dynamics, ICC shows delayed enhancement, metastases demonstrate ring-like enhancement, and HH display “fast-in and slow-out” kinetics2,4,7. However, visual assessment alone is limited by overlapping imaging features, leading to diagnostic uncertainty7,10.

To address these challenges, radiomics extracts high-dimensional quantitative features (e.g., texture, shape, intensity) from CT scans, revealing subtle patterns imperceptible to the human eye. Coupled with machine learning, these features enable automated, objective tumor classification11–14. Deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), further improves accuracy by learning discriminative features directly from images15–17. Together, these artificial intelligence (AI) tools offer robust decision-support systems for liver tumor characterization.

Current AI-based liver tumor differentiation studies face limitations due to inconsistent data scales and heterogeneous sources, stemming from variations in scanner protocols and collection settings. Existing public datasets (Table 1)—such as the Liver Tumor Segmentation (LiTS) benchmark18—are limited in scope, offering only 131 portal venous phase CT scans for segmentation tasks. The two available multiphase CT datasets19,20 each focus on a single tumor type.

Here, we introduce MCT-LTDiag, a novel Multi-phase CT dataset for Liver Tumor Diagnosis, comprising 517 cases from Peking Union Medical College Hospital. The dataset includes five liver tumor categories (HCC, ICC, CRLM, BCLM and HH), complete four-phase CT scans (non-contrast, arterial, portal venous and delayed phases), and rigorous quality control with standardized formatting, image registration, and precise tumor annotation (Fig. 1). We conducted baseline experiments using radiomics-based machine learning and end-to-end deep learning, demonstrating the dataset’s utility for algorithm development. MCT-LTDiag serves as a benchmark to advance automated liver tumor differential diagnosis.

Methods

Sample selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Approval No. K23N0520), with waived informed consent due to the use of de-identified retrospective data. All patient data were securely managed to protect privacy. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) a pathological or clinical diagnosis of HCC, ICC, BCLM, CRLM, or HH; and (2) availability of complete contrast-enhanced CT imaging data. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) poor image quality (e.g., severe motion or metal artifacts, excessive noise); (2) unclear tumor margins; and (3) tumors with a maximum diameter < 1 cm on axial images, as smaller lesions are less suitable for robust quantitative imaging analysis due to limited spatial extent and potential variability across imaging devices. This size threshold aligns with established guidelines for liver lesion assessment, including those from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD)21 and contemporary literature on liver tumor imaging22. The final cohort included 517 patients with liver tumors (August 2014–January 2024): 203 primary liver cancers (103 HCC, 100 ICC), 218 metastases (103 CRLM, 115 BCLM), and 96 HH cases (Fig. 2). Patient demographics, including age, cirrhosis status, hepatitis history, and chemotherapy history, along with key imaging features, were stratified and are systematically presented in Tables 2, 3.

This study utilized six CT scanners: Siemens, Canon, GE, Philips, Toshiba and Neusoft medical systems. Four-phase liver CT protocols were performed, including: (1) non-contrast (NC); (2) arterial phase (AP, 20–30 s post-injection); (3) portal venous phase (PVP, 60 s post-injection); and (4) delayed phase (DP, 180 s post-injection). Iohexol or Iopamiron contrast agents were administered intravenously at 3 mL/s (dose: 1 mL/kg, maximum 100 mL). Standardized scanning parameters included: 120 kV tube voltage, 512 × 512 matrix size, 1.0 pitch factor, and automatic tube current modulation. All images were reconstructed at 5 mm slice thickness, archived in DICOM format, and analyzed via PACS to ensure data integrity and clinical utility.

Data processing

Consistent with clinical guidelines21,22, tumors <1 cm were excluded to ensure the benchmark’s focus on analytically robust and clinically relevant lesions. The standardized data processing pipeline included data format conversion and multi-phase registration (Fig. 3). All qualified cases were converted from DICOM to NIfTI format using SimpleITK v2.2.1 while preserving original spatial metadata. For multi-phase alignment, all the NC, AP, and DP images were registered to PVP for each case using B-spline interpolation method (ITKElastix v0.20.0).

The sequential data processing workflow, encompassing format conversion, data processing, and semi-automatic segmentation.

We developed a U-Net-based liver tumor segmentation model23 based on the LiTS18 dataset to automatically delineate tumors in our study’s PVP images. A senior radiologist (W.X.) with over 10 years of abdominal image reading experience manually reviewed and corrected all the model-predicted tumor masks using ITK-SNAP v4.0.2 (Fig. 3).

Data Records

The MCT-LTDiag dataset generated in this study has been deposited in the Harvard Dataverse repository24, and is publicly accessible for research purposes. The dataset was constructed by systematically organizing original DICOM files by scan sequence and converting them to NIfTI format. Each patient received a unique anonymized identifier based on conversion timestamp and sequence order, replacing all hospital identifiers. The final dataset comprises 2068 imaging sequences (4 phases per patient) across 517 cases. The dataset is organized within a primary directory containing a meta-information CSV file alongside compressed archives for individual cases. Each case archive comprises two principal subdirectories. The first subdirectory stores the raw images for the four phases containing NC, AP, PVP, and DP phases according to a study–series–file hierarchy. The second subdirectory contains the four-phase NIfTI images after registration, tumor segmentation masks validated by expert radiologists, and liver masks generated by an automated segmentation model (Fig. 4).

Technical Validation

Implementation details

Based on the labeled tumor masks, we selected tumors with 3D long-axis diameters ranging from 10–150 mm for technical validation. From the multi-phase images, we extracted tumor-centered sub-volumes to generate volumetric regions of interest (VOIs) as input for the classification model. In the liver tumor differential diagnosis experiments, we treated both BCLM and CRLM as liver metastases (LM). To rigorously evaluate model performance, we implemented five-fold cross-validation on the MCT-LTDiag dataset, ensuring each sample was used exactly once for testing.

Radiomics baseline

We extracted tumor-level radiomics features from multi-phase CT images using PyRadiomics v3.1.0, with calculations performed within the defined tumor masks. Then two radiomics-based machine learning approaches were adopted: (1) a Random Forest classifier with ReliefF-based feature selection on colorectal CT (Park et al.11), and (2) an SVM model using radiomics features from unenhanced CT (Zhao et al.12). We adapted these frameworks by implementing our own standardized radiomic feature extraction pipeline while maintaining their original classifier architectures, enabling direct performance comparison and reproducibility in liver tumor classification.

Deep learning baseline

We established the deep learning baseline by implementing two representative CNN-based feature extraction models, following methodologies from two published studies. First, based on Yasaka et al.15, we replicated a 2D CNN model using cropped dynamic contrast-enhanced CT images across four phases. The architecture consists of six convolutional, three pooling, and three fully connected layers. Second, inspired by Ying et al.16, we developed a 3D CNN-based model mirroring their large-scale multicenter AI system (LiAIDS). Additionally, we incorporated Uniformer25 as a robust spatiotemporal visual backbone to enable comparative experiments across different phase combinations. As demonstrated in Table 4, this approach validated the advantage of multi-phase CT integration in liver tumor discrimination tasks using our dataset.

Evaluation metrics

We assessed model performance using six metrics: specificity, sensitivity, F1-score, AUC, accuracy, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ). For cases with multiple tumor VOIs, all metrics were computed at the VOI level using the scikit-learn package. Patient-level metrics were then derived as the expected value of the VOI-level results for each case.

Benchmark results

As shown in Table 4, image-based deep learning models, particularly Uniformer, outperformed traditional CNNs (2D/3D) by combining convolutional locality with transformer-based global attention. This hybrid approach enhanced discriminative capability for heterogeneous tumors, yielding a 6.09% relative improvement in HCC specificity compared to 2D CNN. In contrast, radiomics-based machine learning demonstrated superior specificity (e.g., 91.11% for ICC using Random Forest) and AUC performance in localized classification tasks (e.g., 89.63% for BCLM + CRLM differentiation with SVM). This advantage stems from engineered features that explicitly capture texture and statistical patterns.

As demonstrated in Table 5, multi-phase integration significantly enhanced diagnostic performance, with the full-phase combination achieving optimal metrics: 65.67% HCC specificity (vs 59.26% without DP), 90.32% LM sensitivity (vs 77.88% PVP-only), and 85.88% HCC AUC (vs 74.08% without DP). The DP proved particularly impactful, driving an 11.18% F1-score improvement for HCC and improving the average AUC, accuracy, and κ by approximately 5.04%, 4.57%, and 7.96%, respectively, thereby demonstrating its essential role in the diagnostic synergy of multi-phase CT for liver tumor differentiation.

Usage Notes

The dataset is organized in a hierarchical directory structure to facilitate straightforward access (Fig. 4). At the top level, cases are grouped by tumor category, including primary liver tumors, benign liver tumors, and liver metastases. Within each tumor category folder, individual case folders are provided. Each case folder contains multi-phase contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans, along with the corresponding segmentation masks for PVP phase.

All imaging data and segmentation files are stored in NIfTI (.nii.gz) format. Users can visualize and analyze the data using open-source tools such as ITK-SNAP or 3D Slicer, which allow for interactive 3D exploration of the images and segmentation masks.
