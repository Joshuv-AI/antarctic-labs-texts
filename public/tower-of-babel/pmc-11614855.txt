Impact of chest pain on mortality in patients with acute pulmonary embolism
Authors: Jiarui Zhang, Haitao Zou, Yongjiang Tang, Lige Peng, Jiaqi Pu, Jiaxin Zeng, Xueqing Chen, Jianlin Yuan, Qun Yi, Haixia Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Evidence for the prognostic impact of chest pain in acute pulmonary embolism (APE) is limited. This study aimed to assess the prognostic value of chest pain in a Chinese cohort of patients with APE. Consecutive hospitalized patients diagnosed with APE between January 2016 and December 2019 were retrospectively enrolled and followed prospectively for 2 years at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. The primary outcome was in-hospital all- cause mortality, while the secondary outcomes included 3-month, 6-month, and 2-year all-cause mortality, APE recurrence, mechanical ventilation, and length of hospital stay (LOS). A total of 737 APE patients met the study criteria, with 254 patients (34.5%) presented with chest pain at admission. Patients with chest pain had significantly lower in-hospital (3.1% vs. 11.2%), 3-month (3.7% vs. 7.5%), 6-month (5.3% vs. 10.0%), and 2-year (9.8% vs. 15.4%) all-cause mortality compared to patients without chest pain (all P < 0.05). A lower rate of mechanical ventilation was observed in APE patients with chest pain, and no significant differences were identified in terms of APE recurrence and LOS between APE patients with and without chest pain. Chest pain was an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in three separate multivariable models (range of odds ratios 0.390–0.423, all P < 0.05). APE patients with chest pain had a lower short-term and long-term all-cause mortality compared to those without chest pain. Chest pain may be considered a strong, favorable prognostic marker in acute pulmonary embolism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81520-w.

Introduction

Acute pulmonary embolism (APE) is a common, life-threatening cardiovascular disease, with a reported incidence of 60 to 120 cases per 100 000 population per year in Western countries1,2. Despite remarkable advances in diagnosis and management, APE remains the third most common cause of death from cardiovascular disease, only behind myocardial infarction and stroke3. In the United States, approximately 60 per 100 000 patients die of APE each year2,4. In China, 343 deaths were reported among 8789 patients hospitalized for APE, corresponding to an in-hospital mortality of 3.9%5. The hospitalization rate of APE is increasing annually, which imposes a substantial burden on the healthcare system5,6. Current guidelines recommend APE patients with low risk of death may be discharged early from hospital and managed in an outpatient setting7,8. It is therefore essential to identify APE patients at low risk of mortality.

Chest pain is one of the most frequent symptoms of patients with APE, with a reported prevalence varies from 19–59%9–12. In the EMPEROR study, a prospective and multicenter registry in the United States, the proportion of patients with chest pain was 39.4% among those with confirmed pulmonary embolism11. According to an Italian registry, 321(19%) patients with APE reported pleuritic chest pain12. Several studies have found that clinical symptoms were linked to the severity and prognosis of APE13–16. However, the relationship between chest pain and mortality has not been established precisely. Wong et al. found that chest pain occurred in 771 (59%) patients and chest pain was an advantageous prognostic marker for APE patients in Australia9. It remains unclear whether the presence of chest pain has any impact on mortality in Asian patients with APE.

The aim of this study is to identify the prognostic value of chest pain in a cohort of Chinese patients with APE.

Methods

Study design and patients

Consecutive hospitalized patients diagnosed with APE between January 2016 to December 2019 were retrospectively enrolled and followed prospectively at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. Patients were selected based on the following criteria: (1) a definitive APE diagnosis by imaging, which consisted of CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA), ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan, and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA); (2) APE patients were aged 18 years and over. Patients were excluded from this analysis if the chest pain caused by other diseases (including acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pneumothorax and pericarditis) and they had missing records of symptoms on admission. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the West China Hospital of Sichuan University. An informed waiver from included patients was granted because of the retrospective study design.

Data collection

Baseline demographics, signs and symptoms on admission, comorbidities, laboratory tests, radiologic findings, treatments and outcomes were collected using standardized case record forms. The simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index (sPESI) score is widely used tool for identifying patients with low-risk pulmonary embolism17. Variables included in the sPESI score were recorded and a total score was calculated for each patient. Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is considered the gold standard for comorbidity measure and is recommended in clinical research18. We computed the CCI score for each patient on admission. Further details of sPESI and CCI were summarised in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Table S2.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality. The secondary outcomes included 3-month, 6-month, and 2-year all-cause mortality, APE recurrence, mechanical ventilation, and length of hospital stay (LOS).

Statistical analysis

APE patients with chest pain were compared to those without chest pain at admission. Continuous variables were reported as mean (SD) when the data were normally distributed and group comparisons were conducted using paired t test. Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) when the data were skewed. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies(percentages) and compared using the chi-square test. Logistic regression models were performed to identify the predictors of chest pain and assess the association between chest pain and all-cause mortality in patients with APE. Survival curves were plotted with the method of Kaplan–Meier, and comparison among groups was assessed by the log-rank test. All data were analyzed with SPSS (Version 25.0), and a value of P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

In total, 803 consecutive patients with APE were initially enrolled in the study, of which 66 patients were excluded, leaving a total of 737 patients for analysis. Among these, 254 patients (34.5%) presented with chest pain at admission (Fig. 1). Fig. 1Flow chart of the study.

Flow chart of the study.

APE patients with chest pain, compared to those without chest pain, were younger, and tended to be current smokers. Hemoptysis and dyspnea were more common in patients with chest pain, while syncope was more common in patients without chest pain. The systolic blood pressure of patients with chest pain was lower than those without chest pain. According to sPESI risk stratification, more patients with chest pain were categorized as low risk. APE patients with chest pain had lower CCI scores. Antiphospholipid syndrome was more frequently seen in patients with chest pain, whereas hypertension, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cancer, chronic renal dysfunction, and diabetes were more frequently seen in patients without chest pain (all P < 0.05). In terms of laboratory tests, patients with chest pain had higher level of hemoglobin (Hb) and platelet (PLT). Regarding radiologic findings, pleural effusion and pulmonary infarction were more common in APE patients with chest pain. The vast majority of patients with APE received standard anticoagulant therapy, and there was no significant difference between the chest pain group and the non-chest pain group (Table 1). Outcomes

Table 2 summarized clinical outcomes for the two groups (chest pain group and non-chest pain group) in patients with APE. Patients with chest pain had significantly reduced in-hospital all-cause mortality (3.1% vs. 11.2%, P < 0.001) compared to patients without chest pain. The 3-month (3.7% vs. 7.5%), 6-month (5.3% vs. 10.0%), and 2-year (9.8% vs. 15.4%) all-cause mortality in APE patients with chest pain were significantly lower than those without chest pain (all P < 0.05). The presence of chest pain was associated with a lower rate of mechanical ventilation (1.2% vs. 4.1%, P = 0.027). However, there were no significant differences between groups in terms of APE recurrence and LOS (all P > 0.05). Predictors of chest pain in patients with APE

Candidate variables with P < 0.05, including age, current smoking, antiphospholipid syndrome, hemoglobin levels, platelet counts, pleural effusion and pulmonary infarction, were integrated into multivariate analysis for predictors of chest pain in patients with APE. We found that age (OR = 0.978; 95% CI: 0.969–0.988, P < 0.001), hemoglobin levels (OR = 1.098; 95% CI: 1.032–1.168, P = 0.003), platelet counts (OR = 1.028; 95% CI: 1.011–1.046, P = 0.002), and pulmonary infarction (OR = 1.804; 95% CI: 1.231–2.643, P = 0.002) were independent predictors of chest pain (Table 3). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, chest pain was present in 34.5% of patients with APE. The prognostic impact of chest pain on clinical outcomes in APE patients was evaluated. We found that chest pain was independently associated with a lower risk of in-hospital mortality after adjusting for other factors.

In clinical practice, chest pain can occur in a variety of conditions such as acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, pericarditis, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and herpes zoster19. Notably, the characteristics of chest pain differ in various diseases. For example, chest pain that is localized behind the sternum and pain radiating to the neck, shoulder, and back are related to myocardial ischemia20. Sudden onset of tearing chest pain is suspicious of an acute aortic syndrome20. GERD-related chest pain is typically a squeezing or burning sensation in the lower sternum that may radiate to the neck, arms, or back21. The clinical symptoms of APE are non-specific and chest pain is one of the most common presenting symptoms. The prevalence of chest pain has been reported to range from 19 to 59% in patients with APE9–12. Consistent with previous reports in China, the prevalence of chest pain in our study was 34.5%22. Chest pain caused by several conditions (e.g. myocardial infarction, aortic coarctation, acute pulmonary embolism) can be life-threatening. Therefore, clinicians should focus on chest pain and enhance their capacity for differential diagnosis, so as to take effective measures and improve outcomes.

Chest pain associated with APE is typically pleuritic, related to breathing. Some patients experience pain resembling that of myocardial infarction in the early stages of the disease. The presence of chest pain in APE is likely multifactorial and complex, two possible explanations should be considered. First, local thrombi or emboli blocked the pulmonary artery or its branches, preventing blood flow to the lungs and causing pulmonary infarction. This results in chest pain due to large-area infarction23. Second, central pulmonary embolism causes right ventricular ischemia, leading to typical angina-like chest pain24.

In the present study, age, hemoglobin levels, platelet counts, and pulmonary infarction were found to be independent predictors of chest pain in patients with APE. Young patients with APE were more likely to experience chest pain in our study. We hypothesize that younger patients may have fewer complications and chest pain is not easily masked by other disease related symptoms. This phenomenon is intriguing, but the underlying cause remains unclear and further research is needed. Elevated hemoglobin levels and platelet counts result in a hypercoagulable state, accelerating the formation and enlargement of blood clots, which further exacerbate infarction and ischemia25–27. Pulmonary infarction is characterized by ischemia, hemorrhage and necrosis of the lung parenchyma, and can lead to chest pain in APE patients28.

We evaluated the prognostic impact of chest pain in Chinese patients with APE and found that chest pain could serve as a favorable prognostic marker in APE. Patients with chest pain had a significantly lower proportion of severe pulmonary embolism (sPESI ≥ 1) and exhibited higher survival rates in our study, which was in accordance with the findings by a large cohort study conducted in Australia9. The exact mechanisms underlying the association between chest pain and reduced mortality in APE patients remain unclear, and our data suggest two possible explanations. First, age and comorbidities are important prognostic factors for acute pulmonary embolism29,30, patients with chest pain were younger and had lower CCI scores, which contributed to explain their better prognosis. Secondly, it is well known that chest pain is a warning sign in clinical practice, and physician attention may improve the diagnosis of acute pulmonary embolism, thereby reducing mortality rates.

The findings of our study have several implications for the management of APE. Chest pain is relatively common in patients with APE, suggesting that it may serve as a key symptom in guiding clinical diagnosis. For patients presenting with unexplained chest pain, particularly when accompanied by symptoms such as dyspnea, hemoptysis, and syncope, healthcare providers should remain vigilant for the possibility of pulmonary embolism and promptly conduct appropriate assessments and interventions. In addition, chest pain, serving as a protective prognostic factor for APE, highlights the multitude of prognostic factors and the intricacies involved in prognostic assessment. Consequently, a comprehensive assessment, including clinical manifestations, imaging examinations, laboratory tests, and other relevant factors, is essential for accurate prognosis determination in APE patients.

This study involved three principal limitations. First, this was a retrospective analysis of a single-center cohort of hospitalized patients with APE, some data were missing for key variables, such as troponin T, NT-pro-BNP, which may affect the accuracy and generalizability of the study results. Second, the endpoint of our study was all-cause mortality, and the prognostic values of chest pain in pulmonary embolism-related death and cardiovascular (heart-related) death were not been determined. Third, the data on the types of chest pain experienced by APE patients was not reported, which prevented us from further elucidating the potential relationship between chest pain characteristics and clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

APE patients with chest pain had a lower short-term and long-term all-cause mortality compared to those without chest pain. Chest pain could be considered as a powerful, favorable prognostic marker in acute pulmonary embolism.
