Patient-reported outcomes during pregnancy and delivery in epidermolysis bullosa
Authors: Anna Zott, Anja Diem, Christine Prodinger, Katharina Ude-Schoder, Johann W Bauer, Tobias Welponer, Martin Laimer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch on pregnancy in women with epidermolysis bullosa (EB) and its effect on maternal disease activity is limited. Lack of evidence poses significant challenges for patient management.ObjectiveThis study aimed to retrospectively characterise the spectrum and course of patient-reported symptoms and complications during pregnancy referring to the EB House Austria patient cohort.MethodsData on pregnancy outcomes, clinical symptoms, and EB disease activity before, during, and after pregnancy were collected using a structured questionnaire. To specify the impact of disease severity, the responders were classified into three groups based on the individual overall EB phenotype (mild, moderate, severe) prior to pregnancy (baseline). The pseudonymised data were analysed descriptively and complemented by a comprehensive literature review.ResultsThis study assessed 33 pregnancies in 19 women encompassing all major EB subtypes. Most reported parameters, including EB lesional burden, remained stable during pregnancy. A trend towards symptom exacerbation was reported by patients with moderate EB, while those with severe EB more often recalled some symptomatic improvement. Anaemia was documented in 30% of pregnancies, predominantly among moderate to severe EB patients. Vaginal delivery constituted the predominant mode of birth (67%). Caesarean section rates increased with EB severity (0% in mild, 38% in moderate, and 50% in severe EB groups). Among the caesarean deliveries, antenatal vulvovaginal lesions were recalled in 70% of cases. Severe perineal tears were the most common perinatal complication, occurring in 15% of pregnancies and primarily in the moderate and severe cohorts. Due to sample size limitations, no formal statistical hypothesis testing was conducted.ConclusionReferring to a limited cohort size, our findings suggest that pregnancy management in women with EB is feasible, though antenatal and perinatal complication rates rise with disease severity. Vaginal delivery appears to be a viable option and was the preferred method in most cases. In planned caesarean sections, neuraxial anaesthesia was not associated with adverse events. Background

The term epidermolysis bullosa (EB) describes a heterogeneous group of rare inherited genodermatoses that are caused by variants in components mediating the structural and functional integrity of the interface between superficial epithelial layers [1–3].

Clinical hallmarks include an increased mechanical fragility with blistering, erosions and non-healing wounds, particularly in skin and mucous membranes. The phenotypic spectrum ranges from milder variants with localised symptoms mainly restricted to trauma-exposed acral sites, to severe subtypes with generalised skin involvement and numerous extracutaneous manifestations accounting for significant morbidity and mortality [4, 5].

EB-associated cutaneous, genitourinary and vulvovaginal findings may pose unique challenges to patients during pregnancy and delivery. Therefore, multidisciplinary care, tailored antenatal monitoring and individualised delivery plans are essential to mitigate risks for this vulnerable patient group [6].

While the first international guideline on pregnancy in women with EB was recently published, the recommendations therein are largely derived from single case reports and small case series, underscoring the absence of systematic data on maternal outcomes [6]. Moreover, literature on impact of pregnancy on maternal EB disease activity is not only sparse but also heterogeneous, with reported improvement, worsening or no change of clinical symptoms [7–13]. This limited evidence also reflects the broad phenotypic spectrum and heterogeneity of EB in addition to its rareness, which all pose significant challenges to clinical research.

Consequently, prevalence of pregnancy-related complications, the effect of pregnancy on EB disease activity, and optimal management strategies remain poorly defined. Against this background, this survey study sought to retrospectively characterise the spectrum and course of patient-reported EB symptoms and pregnancy-related complications, with the aim to inform anticipatory guidance, optimise care strategies, and support clinical decision-making for this high-risk patient population.

Methods

Literature review

To complement the content of the patient survey and the inherently limited sample size in our study, a comprehensive literature review was conducted to investigate potential changes in clinical parameters during pregnancy in women with EB (Table 1). The search was performed in the PubMed database between September 1, 2024, and November 1, 2024, using the keywords "(pregnancy) AND (epidermolysis bullosa)". All types of articles reporting on the course of clinical parameters during pregnancy in women with EB were included for analysis.

Patient survey

A questionnaire (Appendix A, Questionnaire on Epidermolysis Bullosa in Pregnancy) was developed to collect patient-reported data on pregnancy outcomes, clinical symptoms, EB disease activity and course, each before, during, and after pregnancy. A distinction was made between EB-associated symptoms (i.e. symptoms within the phenotypic scope of the individual EB subtype) and pregnancy-associated symptoms (i.e. onset of symptoms/complications related to pregnancy that are common also in non-EB cohorts). After approval by the Ethics Committee of the State of Salzburg (Approval No: 1170/2021), potential participants were identified from the medical records within the patient registry of the EB House Austria, a designated national center of expertise for EB care, and contacted by email.

Study population

The study population comprised women over 18 years of age with molecularly confirmed EB and a history of pregnancy. To specify the impact of clinical EB severity, participants were further categorised into three groups based on the retrospective assessment of individual overall disease course and clinical phenotype prior to pregnancy. Referring to medical records, patients were thus grouped according to the instrument for scoring clinical outcomes of research for EB [19] as suffering from mild (i.e. ≤ 5% of affected body surface area, no extracutaneous complications), moderate (> 5% affected body surface area, up to two extracutaneous manifestations/complications) or severe disease (> 5% affected body surface area, ≥ 3 extracutaneous manifestations or complications).

Questionnaires were anonymized by designated staff members of the EB House Austria and assigned a unique participant number. In the Excel sheet used for data analysis, each participant number was listed alongside the corresponding EB severity as assigned by the attending or an EB physician. No additional identifying information was included, ensuring that disease severity could be linked to survey responses while maintaining participant confidentiality.

This classification, reflecting each patient’s usual baseline severity, was recorded at study enrolment and applied to all pregnancies contributed by that individual. As all pregnancies occured within a limited time frame, a reassessment for each pregnancy was not performed. While EB disease activity can fluctuate and cumulative tissue damage may progress with age, major changes in overall disease severity within the interval of reported pregnancies are unlikely but cannot be excluded.

Data analysis

Following pseudonymisation, the data from returned questionnaires were transferred to an Excel spreadsheet. Descriptive analyses and graphical visualisations were performed by Excel. Due to sample size limitations, no formal statistical analysis was conducted.

Participants were instructed to complete one questionnaire per pregnancy. When a respondent had experienced more than one pregnancy, each was documented through a separate questionnaire and handled as an independent observation. Multiple pregnancies from the same individual were thus not formally linked at the patient level and analysed separately without adjustment for intraindividual correlation.

Results

Literature review

Literature search identified 10 articles (9 case reports, 1 retrospective survey study) reporting on the course of clinical symptoms during pregnancy in patients with EB (Table 1). No significant alterations in EB-related symptoms had been detected in the majority of patients (56/74; 76%) [7–9, 11, 12, 19]. Occasionally, improvement as well as worsening of symptoms had been observed, including skin blistering [18, 19], vulvovaginal erosions [20], skin ulcers on lower abdomen [17], skin infections [9, 15] and wound healing [18].

Survey

Participant characteristics

Questionnaires were completed by 19 patients (Epidermolysis bullosa simplex, EBS: n = 6; Junctional epidermolysis bullosa JEB: n = 6; Dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa, DEB: n = 6; Kindler epidermolysis bullosa, KEB: n = 1). A total of 33 questionnaires were submitted by these patients, with each representing a single pregnancy. The numbers of childbirths per participant ranged from one to four (Table 2).

Scope of patient reported pregnancy-associated complications

Fatigue (19/33; 58%), heartburn (15/33; 45%) and nausea (12/33; 36%) were the most frequently reported symptoms of pregnancy across all severity groups. In contrast, only patients of the moderate and severe EB group indicated to have had been diagnosed with other complications specifically queried in the survey, with anaemia (10/33; 30%) being most the commonly reported (Table 3). There were no reports of proteinuria, arterial hypertension or preeclampsia.

The mean number of reported antenatal complications per pregnancy increased with clinical EB severity. Patients categorised as having mild EB reported a total of 12 complications across 7 pregnancies, corresponding to an average of 1.71 complications per pregnancy. In the moderate EB group, 25 complications occurred in 16 pregnancies (1.56 per pregnancy), whereas the severe EB group experienced the highest complication burden with 30 complications in 10 pregnancies, averaging 3.00 complications per pregnancy.

Mild disease group

Changes in EB symptoms were rarely reported in this cohort. While symptomatic improvement was not recalled, one patient each reported an increase in abdominal itching, whole body skin blistering, and gastrointestinal complaints (diarrhoea, constipation) (Fig. 1).

Moderate disease group

Those patients most frequently reported worsening of EB symptoms, especially heartburn, fatigue, skin infections, blistering, pain, gastrointestinal complaints (e.g. diarrhoea, constipation), and itch (Fig. 2).

Severe disease group

Patients tended to report improvement of EB symptoms during pregnancy, including reduction of blistering (also on the stretched skin of the abdomen), itch, pain, and gastrointestinal symptoms (Fig. 3).

Mode of delivery and type of anaesthesia

In accordance with the literature, vaginal delivery was performed in most cases (22/33;67%), including 4 out of 11 (36%) patients with preexisting vulvovaginal involvement. Additionally, 10 cases of planned caesarean section under epidural or spinal anaesthesia were reported. Caesarean rates correlated positively with EB severity: 0% in the mild group (0/7 deliveries), 38% in the moderate group (6/16 deliveries) and 50% in the severe group (5/10 deliveries). Among the caesarean deliveries, antenatal vulvovaginal lesions (blisters, scarring, vaginal stenosis) were reported in 70% (7/10 cases) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our survey study retrospectively assesses the spectrum and course of patient-reported symptoms during pregnancy and postpartal outcomes in 19 EB patients with a total of 33 pregnancies. With the exception of KEB diagnosed in only one participant, the cohort was equally distributed with regard to the main EB types. The study group was further categorised into 3 EB severity groups, revealing a higher proportion of moderately (42%) and severely (37%) affected patients. These categories accounted for 16 (48%) and 10 (30%) of the 33 pregnancies, respectively.

Fatigue, nausea and heartburn were the most frequently reported antenatal complications in our cohort, irrespective of EB type and severity group. This observation is in line with data for the general population, in which fatigue (> 90%) [21], nausea (50–80%) [22] and heartburn (30–80%) [23] represent common symptoms of pregnancy. All other pregnancy complications were reported exclusively in patients with either moderate or severe EB, with anaemia being the most frequent symptom (Table 3). While pregnancy associated anaemia is a prevalent condition affecting a significant proportion of pregnant women worldwide [16, 24], it is also a common and chronic symptom particularly in the more severe EB variants. New onset or worsening of preexisting anaemia antenatally has also been repeatedly observed in EB [7, 13, 15, 17, 18, 25, 26]. Given its high prevalence, prompt identification and management of both preexisting and pregnancy-exacerbated anaemia should be prioritised in patients with EB to prevent antenatal complications [27]. Notably, maternal haemoglobin levels below 6 g/dL have been associated with an increased risk of premature delivery, spontaneous abortions, low birth weight and fetal deaths [28–30].

Additional complications during pregnancy reported for EB in the literature include spontaneous rupture of membranes [8, 14, 27], fetal growth restriction [8, 9, 13, 18, 20], preeclampsia [14], gestational diabetes [7, 15], polyhydramnios [7], and anhydramnios [20]. Of these, however, only oligo- or anhydramnios and gestational diabetes were observed in our cohort.

As for EB symptoms, most of these were recalled to have had remained overall unchanged during pregnancy, particularly in patients with mild EB (Fig. 1). However, in patients with moderate EB, a trend toward worsening of clinical symptoms during pregnancy was observed (Fig. 2), while patients with severe EB reported a subjective improvement in most symptoms (except for heartburn) (Fig. 3). The latter observation may be attributable to the typically higher baseline inflammation in severe EB, on which the immunomodulatory impact of pregnancy could exert relevant anti-inflammatory and antipruritic effects. A similar favourable modulation of the phenotype has been described for psoriasis [3, 31–34]. Hormonal changes during pregnancy, particularly the increase of immunomodulatory estrogen, progesterone, and glucocorticoids level, are implicated to be modifying factors [35]. These hormones play a key role in reducing inflammatory immune responses that otherwise could compromise fetal survival and were shown to contribute to the downregulation of natural killer cells, macrophages and Th1 cells, while increasing the activity of regulatory T cells and the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines [36].

Labor complications were found to occur exclusively in patients with moderate to severe EB and included uterine inertia, severe hemorrhage, pelvic end/breech position, and umbilical cord wrapping. Severe (third and fourth degree) perineal tears, involving the vaginal mucosa, perineal body and anal sphincter complex, was revealed as the most frequent perinatal complication (15%) in this study, which again was predominantly reported by patients with moderate to severe EB phenotypes. While severe perineal tears (third and fourth degree) occur in 0.1 to 10% of vaginal births in the normal population [37], the distribution pattern observed in our cohort likely represents the impact of high skin fragility and vulnerability associated with severe EB subtypes. This assumption is in line with data from several published cases of vaginal tears in EB patients [9, 11, 27].

Most patients had reported a vaginal delivery, while caesarean sections were exclusively performed in patients with moderate to severe EB. A higher frequency of planned caesarean sections was observed in patients with vulvovaginal involvement. The frequency of preexisting lesions in the vulva or vagina, as well as vaginal stenosis, appeared to increase with the EB severity grade. The findings are corroborated by literature data reporting on caesarean sections necessitated by vulvovaginal involvement [8, 18, 20] or vaginal stenosis [12, 15]. Notably, vaginal delivery was still feasible in a relevant proportion (36%, 4/11) of our patients with vulvovaginal involvement including stenosis, blisters, or scars. Thus, even in the presence of vulvovaginal lesions, vaginal birth may be a viable and safe option when accompanied by appropriate monitoring and care.

Neuraxial anaesthesia was used in all patients who underwent planned caesarean section (n = 10) – spinal anaesthesia in 9 cases (90%) and epidural anaesthesia in 1 case (10%). General anaesthesia was not required in this subgroup and no anaesthesia-related complications were reported. Although the number of cases is limited, these findings suggest neuraxial techniques to be a feasible and well-tolerated option for patients with moderate to severe EB undergoing planned caesarean delivery.

Abdominal delivery in EB patients may also be indicated due to breech presentation [14, 18], primary cephalopelvic disproportion [38], or patient preference [10, 26, 27]. In this context, three pregnancies with moderate to severe EB reported a pelvic end or breech position in our cohort, with two requiring caesarean sections and one having had a vaginal delivery.

A postpartal increase in vulvovaginal complaints was predominantly reported by patients with moderate to severe EB. No significant association was observed between the mode of delivery and the occurrence of these complaints. Thus, vaginal delivery appears to be safe for patients with EB. Consistently, it is often considered the preferred approach by both patients and health care professionals [6].

Breastfeeding was avoided or discontinued after a short period of time mainly in moderate and severe EB cohorts. This could be due to a higher skin vulnerability and thus risk of blistering of the nipple and periareolar region. Given that breastfeeding provides immunological, nutritional and psychological benefits for both infants and mothers [39, 40] (such as faster uterine involution and lower postpartum bleeding [41, 42]), individualised support, including early lactation counseling, optimising latch technique and positioning or use of breast shields, is advised [12, 15, 17, 18, 27].

Limitations

In addition to the small sample size recruited from a single center, this study’s retrospective design and reliance on patient recall may introduce bias.

Moreover, queried symptoms were defined in plain language (layman’s terms) which may limit their medical specificity.

Finally, while the majority of study participants were classified as having moderate and severe EB disease, sexual activity, fertility and reproductive health may be critically impaired in many individuals at childbearing age with severe EB subtypes. Therefore, our data may be insufficiently representative of most severely affected EB patients, whose disease burden restrains them from pregnancy, and provides no information on EB-inherent infertility.

Conclusions

This study is, to date, the largest retrospective analysis on pregnancy in women with EB. Our data demonstrate that most patients with mild EB report no or only modest changes of EB symptoms during pregnancy. Symptomatic improvement was experienced by some EB patients with moderate or severe disease. Reported antenatal and perinatal complications as well as caesarean rate showed a tendency to increase with EB severity, likely reflecting the higher disease burden.

Vaginal delivery is feasible even in patients with vulvovaginal involvement, and caesarean section does not appear to offer an advantage in preventing postnatal vulvovaginal complications. For planned caesarean sections, neuraxial anaesthesia, including epidural and spinal techniques, was not associated with complications.

Identification and management of anaemia should be prioritised in patients with EB to prevent antenatal complications.

In addition, patients with severe EB may face challenges with breastfeeding, prompting accurate advise and implementation of preventive measures, such as proper nipple positioning and the use of breast shields, to mitigate the risk of blistering [26, 27].

Finally, our findings underscore the necessity of an individualised, multidisciplinary approach to reproductive care in EB, ensuring that patients’ needs and risks are balanced to optimise pregnancy outcomes.
