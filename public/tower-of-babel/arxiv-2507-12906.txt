Thabit and Williams Numbers Base b b as a Sum or Difference of Two g g -Repdigits Thanks: 1 Corresponding author.
Kouèssi Norbert Adédji, Marija Bliznac Trebješanin 1, Jelena Pleština

Abstract
. We investigate cases where Thabit and Williams numbers in base b b can be expressed as the sum or difference of two g g -repdigits. For specific values of b b and g g , we describe parametric solutions yielding infinitely many solutions for some equations and establish upper bounds for the parameters of the remaining finitely many solutions. As an illustration, we also provide a complete solution for some equations.

1.
Introduction

A Thabit number, or a Thâbit ibn Qurra number, or
321

number is an integer of the form
3\cdot 2^{n}-1
for a non-negative integer
n
. The sequence of Thabit numbers begins with

2,5,11,23,47,95,191,383,767,1535,3071,6143,\dots

We can generalize this concept in multiple ways. Let
b\geq 2
be an integer. A Thabit number base
b
is a number of the form

(b+1)b^{n}-1
for a non-negative integer
n
. A second kind Thabit number base
b
is a number of the form
(b+1)b^{n}+1
for a non-negative integer
n
. Another natural generalization are the Williams numbers.
For an integer
b\geq 2
, a Williams number base
b
is a number of the form
(b-1)b^{n}\pm 1
for a non-negative integer
n
. The question of which Thabit or Williams number is prime is a widely studied problem. In this paper, we will consider another property of Thabit and Williams numbers.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
g\geq 2
be an integer. A positive integer
N
is called a repdigit in base
g
or simply a
g
-repdigit if all of the digits in its base
g
expansion are equal.

If
N
is a
g
-repdigit then it has the form

N=a\left(\frac{g^{m}-1}{g-1}\right),\quad\textit{for }m\geq 1,\ a\in\{1,2,\dots,g-1\}.

Taking
g=10
, a positive integer
N
is simply called a repdigit.

The aim of this work is to prove when a Thabit number base
b
or a Williams number base
b
is a sum or a difference of two
g
-repdigits. More precisely, let
b\geq 2
and
g\geq 2
be integers. We are searching for solutions
(d_{1},d_{2},l,m,n)
of each Diophantine equation

(1.1)

(b\pm 1)b^{n}\pm 1=d_{1}\left(\frac{g^{l}-1}{g-1}\right)+d_{2}\left(\frac{g^{m}-1}{g-1}\right),

and

(1.2)

(b\pm 1)b^{n}\pm 1=d_{1}\left(\frac{g^{l}-1}{g-1}\right)-d_{2}\left(\frac{g^{m}-1}{g-1}\right),

where
1\leq d_{1},d_{2}\leq g-1
,
n\geq 0
and
l,m\geq 1
are integers.

For some choices
b
and
g
, some of the equations from (
1.1
) have infinitely many solutions
(d_{1},d_{2},l,m,n)
. The statement of this proposition is easily verified.

Proposition 1.2
.

a)

Let
b=2
and
g=2^{k}
for an integer
k\geq 2
. Then
(d_{1},d_{2},l,m,n)=(2,g-1,1,m,mk)
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, represent infinitely many solutions of the equation

(1.3)

(b-1)b^{n}+1=d_{1}\left(\frac{g^{l}-1}{g-1}\right)+d_{2}\left(\frac{g^{m}-1}{g-1}\right).

b)

Let
b=2
and
g=2^{k}
for an integer
k\geq 2
. Then
(d_{1},d_{2},l,m,n)=(d_{1},g-1-d_{1},m,m,mk)
,
d_{1}\in\{1,2,\dots,g-1\}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, represent infinitely many solutions of the equation

(1.4)

(b-1)b^{n}-1=d_{1}\left(\frac{g^{l}-1}{g-1}\right)+d_{2}\left(\frac{g^{m}-1}{g-1}\right).

c)

Let
b=g
. Then
(d_{1},d_{2},l,m,n)=(1,g-2,n,n+1,n)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, represent infinitely many solutions of the equation (
1.4
).

The main result about (
1.1
) is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
b,g\geq 2
be integers. Then, except for the cases described in Proposition
1.2
, the Diophantine equations (
1.1
) have finitely many solutions in integers
1\leq d_{1},d_{2}\leq g-1,\,n\geq 0
and
l,m\geq 1
. Moreover,

l,m<2.71\cdot 10^{29}\log^{2}{g}\log^{3}{b}\log^{2}{\left(\max{\{b,g\}}\right)}

and

n<2.08\cdot 10^{29}\log^{3}{g}\log^{2}{b}\log^{2}{\left(\max{\{b,g\}}\right)}.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
b,g\geq 2
be integers. Then the Diophantine equations (
1.2
) have finitely many solutions in integers
1\leq d_{1},d_{2}\leq g-1,\,n\geq 0
and
l,m\geq 1
. Moreover,

m\leq l<2.85\cdot 10^{29}\log^{2}{g}\log^{3}{b}\log^{2}{\left(\max{\{b,g\}}\right)}

and

n<2.19\cdot 10^{29}\log^{3}{g}\log^{2}{b}\log^{2}{\left(\max{\{b,g\}}\right)}.

The outline of the paper is as follows. We begin by presenting some preliminary lemmas and results that will be used throughout the paper. Additionally, we provide a complete solution in the case
g=2
, as it is straightforward.

In Section 3, we examine equations involving the sum of two
g
-repdigits by deriving several inequalities between the parameters
l
,
m
, and

. We then analyze two linear forms in logarithms to obtain upper bounds for the same indices in terms of

and

In particular, it involves solving an additional Diophantine equation and developing tailored arguments to identify all possible solutions. This makes the proof significantly more intricate compared to similar results. In contrast, in Section 4, where we study the difference of two
g
-repdigits, more direct arguments suffice to prove an analogous result.

To fully resolve certain equations, we describe all possible solutions in the decimal case
g=10
, and for
2\leq b\leq 12
, in Subsections 3.1 and 4.1. Note that the approach is based on the reduction method by Bravo, Gómez, and Luca
[
6
]
using some auxiliary tools from
[

]
.

The motivation for this paper arises from recent work on repdigits, such as
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]
, and
[

]
. Our methods and overall approach are similar to those used in other studies on number sequences and their interesting representations, such as
[

,

[

2.
Preliminaries

First, we will list some supporting definitions and results.

Let
\eta
be an algebraic number of degree
t,
let
a_{0}\neq 0
be the leading coefficient of its minimal polynomial over
\mathbb{Z}
and let
\eta=\eta^{(1)},\ldots,\eta^{(t)}
denote its conjugates. The logarithmic height of
\eta
is defined by

h(\eta)=\frac{1}{t}\left(\log|a_{0}|+\sum_{j=1}^{t}\log\max\left(1,\left|\eta^{(j)}\right|\right)\right).

If
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
are algebraic numbers and
j\in\mathbb{Z},
then we have

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}\pm\eta_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta_{1})+h(\eta_{2})+\log 2,

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\eta_{1})+h(\eta_{2}),

\displaystyle h(\eta_{1}^{j})

\displaystyle=|j|h(\eta_{1}).

If
p
and
q
are integers such that
q>0
and
\gcd(p,q)=1,
then for
\eta=p/q
the above definition reduces to
h(\eta)=\log(\max\{|p|,q\}).

(Theorem 9.4 of

Assume that

If

|\Lambda|\geq\exp(-1.4\cdot 30^{s+3}\cdot s^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{s}).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

be a convergent of the continued fraction of the irrational

be some real numbers with

and

. Let

\varepsilon=\|\mu q\|-M\cdot\|\tau q\|.

a)

If
\varepsilon>0
, then there is no solution to the inequality

(2.2)

0<|m\tau-n+\mu|<AB^{-w},

in positive integers
m,n
and
k
with

m\leq M\quad\textrm{and}\quad w\geq\frac{\log(Aq/\varepsilon)}{\log B}.

b)

Let

. Then either there is no solution to the inequality (
2.2
) in positive integers

and
k
with

m\leq M\quad\textrm{and}\quad w>\frac{\log(3Aq)}{\log B},

or
w>\frac{\log(3Aq)}{\log B}
holds and
m=m_{0}
where

is a unique solution to a linear congruence equation

mp\equiv-r\ (\bmod\ q),

such that
m_{0}<q
. If

then there is no solution to the inequality in this case since

.

Proof.

Let

. Then
|\mu q-r|\leq 1/2
. Since

is a convergent of
\tau
then
|p-q\tau|<1/q
.

After multiplying (

) with

and rearranging, we have

(2.3)

|q\mu+mp-qn|<qAB^{-w}+m|p-q\tau|.

Now observe,

\displaystyle|mp-qn+r|

\displaystyle=|mp-qn+q\mu-q\mu+r|\leq|mp-qn+q\mu|+|q\mu-r|

\displaystyle<qAB^{-w}+m|p-q\tau|+1/2<qAB^{-w}+m/q+1/2

\displaystyle<qAB^{-w}+2/3

where we have used the fact that

.

If

then
w\leq(\log(3Aq))/(\log B).

If
qAB^{-w}<1/3
, then

. Consequently,
mp-r\equiv 0\ (\bmod\ q)
must hold, and there is a unique integer solution

. If

then there is no solution to (

) in this case. If

.
∎

Lemma 2.4

If

3.
The sum of two
g
-repdigits

This section is devoted to proving Theorem
1.3
, so we consider equations (
1.1

2.4

g\geq 3

Note that for the equations (

Lemma 3.1
.

All solutions to the equations (
1.1
) satisfy

n<2.5m\log{g}.

Proof.

From equations (
1.1
) and
d_{1},d_{2}\leq g-1,\,l\leq m
, we obtain

(3.1)

\left(b\pm 1\right)b^{n}<2g^{m}.

Given that
2\leq b
and
3\leq g
, we take the logarithm of (
3.1
) and, after performing simple calculations, we obtain

(3.2)

n\log{b}<\log{2}-\log{\left(b\pm 1\right)}+m\log{g}.

Let us divide the proof into two cases, depending on the value

{\log{2}-\log{\left(b\pm 1\right)}}
.

(i)

For
b=2
, let us observe

(3.3)

n\log{2}<\log{2}-\log{\left(2-1\right)}+m\log{g}.

Dividing (
3.3
) by
\log{2}
, we get
n<1+m\frac{\log{g}}{\log{2}}
.
Since
1<\log{3}\leq\log{g},1\leq m
and
\frac{1}{\log{2}}<1.5
, we get
n<2.5m\log{g}
.

(ii)

Let us examine the remaining cases.
Since
\log{2}\leq\log{\left(b+1\right)}
for every
b\geq 2
, and
\log{2}\leq\log{\left(b-1\right)}
for every
b\geq 3
, from (
3.2
), it follows that

n<m\frac{\log{g}}{\log{b}}
. For
b\geq 2
, since
\frac{1}{\log{b}}<1.5
we get
n<1.5m\log{g}<2.5m\log{g}.
For
b\geq 3
, since
\frac{1}{\log{b}}<0.92
we get
n<0.92m\log{g}<2.5m\log{g}.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

All solutions to the equations (
1.1

l\leq m<1.3\left(n+1.6\right)\frac{\log{b}}{\log{g}}+1.

Proof.

The statement obviously holds for

Let us assume that

From (
1.1

\left(b+1\right)b^{n}+1<\left(\left(b+1\right)b^{n}\right)^{1.3}

Taking the logarithm of (

∎

Let

Assume

and
g

, there would exist a prime

, a contradiction. But

, which does not hold in this case.

. Let us observe

\frac{b^{n}}{g^{l}}(b\pm 1)(g-1)=d_{1}+d_{2}g^{m-l}.

There exists

,

, and

If for any

\frac{b^{n}}{g^{l}}<g^{\frac{\log(g-2)}{\log 2}}.

If for each

Let us observe a prime

\beta(p_{i})n-\alpha(p_{i})l=k_{i}.

\displaystyle k_{j}

\displaystyle=\beta(p_{j})(n_{0}+\alpha(p_{i})\cdot t)-\alpha(p_{j})(l_{0}+\beta(p_{i})\cdot t)

.

. Hence, in both cases we have

. Observing (

In the second case, where
