Rewiring central metabolism in Komagataella phaffii for efficient mannose synthesis
Authors: Sijia Zhao, Yuheng Wang, Qian Li, Jiachen Xie, Xinxin Xu, Wei Zhang, Jie Zhang, Bo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMannose has wide-ranging applications but microbial fermentation remains underdeveloped compared to biotransformation for its production. The yeast Komagataella phaffii stands out as a premier synthetic biology platform, renowned for its safety profile and exceptional suitability for high-density fermentation. This established chassis organism is ideally positioned for large-scale mannose production through targeted rewiring of its mannose biosynthetic pathway via metabolic engineering.ResultsK. phaffii was metabolically engineered for efficient mannose production using a dual carbon source system: glycerol for biomass generation and glucose for mannose synthesis. To redirect carbon flux toward fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) accumulation at the glycolytic node, glycolytic flux was attenuated by knocking out the phosphofructokinase II (pfk2) gene and downregulating phosphofructokinase I (pfk1). Simultaneously, pentose phosphate pathway flux was reduced by downregulating glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (zwf1). To enhance mannose biosynthesis, conversion of F6P into mannose was promoted by suppressing phosphomannose isomerase (PAS_chr3_1115) and overexpressing the Escherichia coli-derived phosphatase gene yniC. Additionally, three genes involved in arabinitol and ribitol production (PAS_chr2–2_0019, PAS_chr4_0754, and PAS_chr4_0988) were deleted to suppress byproduct accumulation. The engineered strain achieved ~ 121.1 g/L mannose in high-cell-density, fed-batch fermentation, representing the highest reported titer via microbial fermentation to date.ConclusionsThis study achieved efficient mannose production in K. phaffii by remodeling central metabolism. It not only offers a new route for mannose biosynthesis but also establishes a model framework for engineering K. phaffii to produce other high-value bioactive compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-025-02886-8.Keywords: Mannose, Komagataella phaffii, Metabolic engineering, High-cell-density fermentation

Background

D-mannose, a natural monosaccharide renowned for its unique therapeutic bioactivity and physicochemical stability, has garnered significant attention due to its extensive applications in various fields including pharmaceuticals, food technology, cosmetic formulations, and advanced biomaterials [1–3]. The expanding use of this versatile compound has fueled a rapidly growing global demand.

D-mannose can be synthesized through chemical isomerization of glucose [4] or oxidation of mannitol [5], however there are environmental concerns with chemical synthesis due to hazardous chemical reagents. D-mannose can also be extracted from some mannose-rich plant biomass via hydrolytic enzymes [6], but this is not ideal for large-scale production due to cumbersome procedures, including but not limited to the preparation of hydrolytic enzymes and the processing of plant materials. D-mannose can also be enzymatically synthesized from D-fructose through catalytic conversion [7]. Enzymatic synthesis offers advantages such as high specificity, mild conditions, and fewer by-products compared to some chemical synthesis; however, its main drawbacks include high enzyme costs, enzyme sensitivity and instability, and challenges in achieving high productivity at scale. Recently, synthetic biology offers groundbreaking solutions to overcome the bottlenecks of traditional methods, leveraging rational design and automation to engineer microbial cell factories [8–10]. With advancements in metabolic engineering, engineered microbial cells with optimized metabolic pathways have been successfully harnessed for high-efficiency production of mannose [11]. In whole-cell catalyst, 25.2 g/L of mannose was achieved by an engineered E. coli strain with pfkA deletion and overexpression of pmi and yniC [12]; 60.0 g/L of D-mannose was produced from 400 g/L D-glucose by an engineered E. coli strain with co-expression of D-glucose isomerase and D-lyxose isomerase within 8 h [11]. Usually, the whole-cell biocatalyst requires designing more production procedures, such as bacterial cultivation, separation, and reaction system setup; industrial scaling remains the primary hurdle. Microbial fermentation for mannose production has several advantages over whole-cell catalysis, including its simplicity in operation, relatively lower costs, and higher efficiency in resource utilization, while it has seldom been reported that D-mannose can be mass-produced through microbial fermentation.

Komagataella phaffii, a highly efficient eukaryotic expression system, has become a popular platform for recombinant protein production due to its strong promoters, high secretory capacity, ease of high-density fermentation, and post-translational modification capabilities [13–16]. With advantages such as low cultivation costs, strong environmental adaptability, and mature industrial scale-up technologies, K. phaffii demonstrates tremendous potential in the field of biomanufacturing. In recent years, breakthroughs in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering have enabled researchers to systematically engineered K. phaffii using genome-editing tools [17], successfully constructing multiple synthetic biology chassis cells [18]. These engineered strains not only efficiently produce therapeutic proteins such as antibodies and vaccines but can also synthesize high-value-added products including terpenoids, polyketide drug precursors, and biofuels [19, 20]. More importantly, owing to its Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status [21], K. phaffii is an ideal host for the biosynthesis of food-grade metabolites requiring high safety standards.

In this study, a series of engineered strains for mannose biosynthesis were developed using K. phaffii as the chassis strain through systematic metabolic engineering strategies (Fig. 1). First, to reduce glucose consumption and simultaneously enhance fructose-6-phosphate (F6P) accumulation, expression of zwf1 in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and pfk1 in the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway was attenuated, and pfk2 was subsequently deleted. Second, to enhance the conversion of F6P to mannose, expression of PAS_chr3_1115 encoding phosphomannose isomerase and the phosphatase gene yniC derived from E. coli was downregulated and upregulated, respectively. Third, to reduce byproduct accumulation of ribitol and arabinitol, three alditol dehydrogenase genes (PAS_chr2–2_0019, PAS_chr4_0754, and PAS_chr4_0988) were deleted. The resultant strain produced 121.1 g/L mannose at a yield of 0.28 g/g glucose and a volumetric productivity of 1.44 g/L·h in high-density fermentation. The developed strain engineering strategies and corresponding research findings provided critical guidance for high-efficiency mannose production in K. phaffii and broadly applicable metabolic engineering references for diverse microbial species.

Results

Reduction in glucose catabolic efficiency of K. phaffii by Attenuation of Glycolysis and the Pentose phosphate pathway

In K. phaffii, glucose metabolism initiates with its phosphorylation into glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) through the catalytic action of hexokinase. G6P subsequently diverges into two distinct metabolic pathways: the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). Within the EMP pathway, G6P undergoes isomerization to F6P mediated by phosphoglucose isomerase. F6P is then sequentially phosphorylated: first to fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP) by phosphofructokinase 1 (Pfk1, encoded by PAS_chr2–1_0402) and phosphofructokinase 2 (Pfk2, encoded by PAS_chr2–1_0047). By contrast, the PPP initiates with the oxidative decarboxylation of G6P catalyzed by glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase Zwf1. PPP serves as a critical biosynthetic route in K. phaffii, generating essential intermediates such as ribulose-5-phosphate while producing NADPH to sustain cellular biosynthesis and redox balance. In addition, F6P can also be converted into mannose 6-phosphate under the action of phosphomannose isomerase PMI, which is then catalyzed by phosphatase to generate mannose. Increasing mannose yield requires lowering the metabolic flux of glucose via the PPP and EMP pathway.

To attenuate G6P metabolic flux towards PPP and EMP, two engineered strains M1 and M2 from the parental GS115Δku70, designated as M0, were constructed through combinatorial genetic modifications: M1 with pfk2 deletion and attenuated expression of pfk1 and M2 with pfk2 deletion alongside dual attenuation of pfk1 and zwf1. Shake-flask cultivations revealed carbon source-dependent growth phenotypes; both mutants exhibited parental strain-like growth kinetics with glycerol (2% w/v) during 0–34 h yet displayed marked growth retardation in glucose (2% w/v), requiring 72 h to achieve OD600 values comparable to M0 at 34 h (Fig. 2). This metabolic decoupling demonstrates that pfk2 deletion coupled with pfk1 suppression effectively reduced glycolytic flux, while additional zwf1 attenuation in M2 further compromised PPP-derived NADPH synthesis.

Impact of PAS_chr3_1115 overexpression on mannose yield

Analysis of KEGG, NCBI, and other databases revealed that the phosphomannose isomerase PMI encoded by PAS_chr3_1115 in K. phaffii catalyzes the interconversion between F6P and mannose-6-phosphate (M6P). However, the impact of PAS_chr3_1115 expression level on mannose production remains unclear, particularly how overexpressing this gene would shift the equilibrium between F6P and M6P. To address this, PAS_chr3_1115 was first overexpressed in strain M2, resulting in the engineered strain M3.

Shake-flask fermentation was conducted with M3 and the control strain M2. Both strains initially used glycerol as a carbon source for growth, followed by glucose supplementation at 24 h. To investigate the potential impact of glucose concentration on mannose production, two initial glucose levels (40 and 70 g/L) were tested in later fermentation stages. Notably, regardless of the initial glucose concentration, M3 consistently reached its maximum OD600 by 72 h, outperforming M2 in growth efficiency (Fig. 3A).

During the fermentation process, strain M3 exhibited higher glucose utilization. At 86 h, M3 consumed ~ 50 g/L of the initial 70 g/L glucose and completely depleted the glucose of the initial 40 g/L, respectively. By contrast, strain M2 consumed much less glucose to reach stationary phase (Fig. 3B). When the carbon source was switched from glycerol to glucose at 24 h, M2 continued to produce mannose, whereas the mannose produced by M3 was gradually consumed. Mannose production in M2 peaked at 2.2 g/L but was rapidly consumed after 72 h (Fig. 3C). Overexpression of PAS_chr3_1115 accelerated glucose utilization in the strain and alleviated growth delays caused by weakened glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways, but led to consumption of the target product mannose. It also suggested that PAS_chr3_1115 overexpression might conflict with experimental objectives, prompting subsequent attempts to attenuate PAS_chr3_1115. Ribitol and arabitol were produced as by-products in significant amounts, reaching 2.8 g/L and 4.5 g/L, respectively.

Enhancing mannose production via Attenuation of PAS_chr3_1115 and deletion of key genes involved in byproduct synthesis

Based on previous experimental results (data unpublished), knockout of the alditol dehydrogenase genes PAS_chr2–2_0019, PAS_chr4_0754, and PAS_chr4_0988 significantly reduced arabinitol and ribitol levels in the product. Therefore, these genes were knocked out and PAS_chr3_1115 expression was simultaneously attenuated in M2, resulting in M4. These strains were subjected to flask fermentation. M4 exhibited growth similar to M3, but its OD600 remained lower than that of M2 (Fig. 4A). After attenuation of PAS_chr3_1115, M4 achieved a peak mannose production of ~ 2.8 g/L, significantly higher than M2 (Fig. 4B), which indicated that attenuating PAS_chr3_1115 generated higher mannose concentrations compared to its overexpression. This might be attributed to a more pronounced weakening of glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway after PAS_chr3_1115 attenuation, leading to elevated F6P levels. This shift likely enhanced the conversion of F6P to M6P, thereby boosting mannose production. The arabinitol in M4 dropped below the detection limit (Fig. 4C), while ribitol continued to accumulate, reaching 2.7 g/L by the end of fermentation (Fig. 4D). This demonstrated that knockout of the three alditol dehydrogenase genes (PAS_chr2–2_0019, PAS_chr4_0754, and PAS_chr4_0988) effectively suppressed arabinitol synthesis but had limited impact on ribitol accumulation.

Screening of phosphatase genes

The final step in mannose production involves the dephosphorylation of M6P, and phosphatases regulate the dephosphorylation of key intermediates (e.g., M6P) in metabolic pathways, thereby influencing mannose production. Screening for phosphatases that specifically act on M6P while exhibiting low activity toward G6P could redirect carbon flux toward mannose synthesis and attenuate off-target byproducts.

Based on literature reports [22], several E. coli-derived genes encoding phosphatases against substrates such as G6P and M6P were selected. Among them, the phosphatases encoded by yniC, ybiV, yidA, and yigL showed high substrate specificity for M6P, prompting their selection for further investigation. We first cloned these four genes into overexpression plasmids and tried to electroporate them into M4. However, attempts to transform the plasmid carrying yigL into M4 failed, possibly due to excessive dephosphorylation activity causing cell lethality. The remaining plasmids were successfully transformed into M4, generating strains M5 (overexpressing yniC), M6 (overexpressing ybiV), and M7 (overexpressing yidA).

Shake-flask fermentation was performed using M4 as a control, alongside M5, M6, and M7. During the initial 24 h with glycerol as the carbon source, all strains exhibited comparable growth. After switching to glucose as the carbon source at 24 h, M4 displayed faster growth, reaching an OD600 of 27 by 72 h. M5 showed higher OD600 than M6 and M7, while the latter two strains exhibited no significant difference (Fig. 5A). Simultaneously, M5 achieved the highest mannose production at 17.0 g/L, significantly surpassing M6 (7.1 g/L), M7 (4.1 g/L), and M4 (2.1 g/L) (Fig. 5B). In addition, it was observed that arabinitol levels remained extremely low and were consumed in later stages (Fig. 5C), whereas ribitol accumulated continuously. By the end of fermentation, ribitol concentrations reached approximately 2.2 g/L in M4, M6, and M7, and 1.6 g/L in M5 (Fig. 5D). These results demonstrated that yniC overexpression in K. phaffii yielded the highest mannose production with minimal byproduct accumulation. Except for yniC, the strain with ybiV overexpression exhibited notable performance. Consequently, yniC and ybiV were selected for subsequent experiments to further optimize mannose biosynthesis.

Mannose production by overexpression of yniC and ybiV in the host chromosome

Given that plasmid instability could compromise strain phenotype retention, it was necessary to integrate the yniC or/and ybiV expression modules into the host chromosome. The expression cassettes of yniC or/and ybiV were integrated into the M4 genome, successfully constructing strains M8 (PGAP-yniC) and M9 (PGAP-yniC-PTEF-ybiV). Shake-flask fermentation revealed that M8 and M9 exhibited growth delays during glucose-fed growth (after 48 h) compared to M4 (Fig. 6A).

Mannose production in M8 reached a maximum of 10.7 g/L, while M9 (co-expressing yniC and ybiV) displayed reduced mannose accumulation (Fig. 6B). Although both YniC and YbiV are phosphatases, they exhibit divergent substrate preferences. The simultaneous overexpression of both enzymes probably led to the non-specific dephosphorylation of a broad range of intracellular metabolites, which disrupted cellular homeostasis and imposed a substantial metabolic burden, ultimately compromising overall cell vitality and diminishing the capacity for mannose synthesis. Byproduct analysis showed that arabinose alcohol (arabinitol) levels in M8 and M9 were approximately twice as high as in M4 at 168 h (Fig. 6C), while ribitol accumulated steadily, finally reaching 1.4 g/L and 2.2 g/L, respectively (Fig. 6D). The elevated arabinitol suggested that the phosphatases’ lack of substrate specificity may have reactivated pathways linked to the previously knocked-out genes (PAS_chr2–2_0019, PAS_chr4_0754, and PAS_chr4_0988), partially reversing their suppression effects.

High-density fermentation of M8 and mannose production

Strain M8 demonstrated robust mannose production performance in shake-flask fermentation; high-density fermentation was performed with M8 as follows. Glycerol was used as the carbon source for cell biomass until the wet cell weight reached approximately 160 g/L, after which glucose was added to enhance mannose production. During fermentation, glucose concentration was maintained between 40 and 60 g/L. Mannose accumulated continuously throughout high-density fermentation, finally reaching ~ 121.1 g/L at 0.28 g/g glucose yield (Fig. 7A). Ribitol as the primary byproduct maintained at 5.86 g/L (Fig. 7B). Notably, arabinitol generated during the process remained at a low level by the end of fermentation (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

Mannose holds significant value across diverse fields, driving strong demand for efficient synthesis methods [23]. Despite increasing market potential, scaling D-mannose production remains costly and inefficient. Presently, most commercial D-mannose relies on plant-based extraction, yet scalable production is constrained by scarce mannan-rich biomass sources [6]. Compared to plant extraction or chemical synthesis [1], biological production of mannose offers scalable, cost-effective, and eco-friendly advantages [6, 12]. A variety of biotechnological strategies were established for D-mannose production, which can be broadly classified into three categories: in vitro enzymatic catalysis, whole-cell catalytic synthesis, and microbial fermentation. Numerous reports on mannose production via in vitro enzymatic catalysis and whole-cell catalytic synthesis were documented, and the highest mannose yield or conversion rate was concisely summarized as follows. The conversion of D-fructose or D-glucose into D-mannose is enabled by some isomerases and epimerases, such as MIase, LIase, AGEase, MEase, and Cease [24]. Notably, in vitro enzymatic catalysis using an AGEase from Thermobifida halotolerans enabled the maximal production of 180 g/L D-mannose from 500 g/L D-fructose, achieving a conversion yield of 35.8% [25]. The use of low-cost and readily available D-glucose further enhances the economic attractiveness of D-mannose production. For instance, a titer of 122 g/L D-mannose from 500 g/L glucose was obtained with a yield of 24.4% [26]. The primary bottleneck of in vitro enzymatic catalysis lies in its high cost due to enzyme purification and cofactor regeneration, despite its capability for rapid substrate conversion. Subsequently, whole-cell catalysis emerged as a more promising approach for large-scale D-mannose production compared to traditional enzymatic methods. Using a recombinant B. subtilis strain expressing MIase from Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, a D-mannose titer of 130.1 g/L was achieved from D-fructose, corresponding to a conversion rate of 26.0% [27]. In another study, an engineered E. coli strain demonstrated the highest conversion efficiency of 63%, producing 25.2 g/L D-mannose from 40 g/L glucose [12]. For industrial mannose production, microbial fermentation offers a more viable route than whole-cell conversion, owing to its superior cost-effectiveness, simpler process, and greater scalability. However, microbial fermentation for D-mannose production from glucose has been less frequently reported. Palur et al. described an engineered E. coli strain via high-cell-density fermentation produced 20.75 g/L D-mannose along with 20.61 g/L D-sedoheptulose from 91.30 g/L D-glucose [28]. Obviously, the reported yield of D-mannose through microbial fermentation was significantly lower compared to those achieved via in vitro enzymatic catalysis and whole-cell bioconversion. The lower efficiency may arise from several inherent challenges in microbial fermentation, such as competitive carbon flux toward cell growth and by-product formation, complex metabolic regulation, and potential product reabsorption or degradation within the cells. Systematic metabolic engineering paves the way for efficient microbial D-mannose fermentation by overcoming critical hurdles in pathway regulation, enzyme activity, and metabolic bottlenecks. Furthermore, our previous research successfully established K. phaffii as a microbial cell factory for the high-efficiency production of myo-inositol, which demonstrated the strong potential of K. phaffii as a versatile chassis for the synthesis of high-value bio-chemicals [29]. Thereby, a streamlined approach for mannose production via the engineered K. phaffii fermentation was developed, which simplified the process of mannose production by replacing the traditional whole-cell two-stage biotransformation with a single-phase process.

Using economical carbon sources and minimal synthetic steps is critical in industrial-scale production. We developed the dual-carbon source fermentation system based on the following factors. Glucose was selected as the principal carbon substrate for mannose biosynthesis because of its direct metabolic connectivity with mannose precursor molecules. It should be noted that glucose was also used as a carbon source for cell growth, thereby reducing the efficiency of its conversion into mannose. Thus, remodeling of the glucose metabolic pathway was carried out. However, redirecting glucose flux toward mannose synthesis inherently suppresses cell growth by compromising native energy metabolism (Fig. 2), thereby extending fermentation cycles. To resolve this trade-off, glycerol served as the growth-phase carbon source, supporting rapid biomass accumulation without competing with mannose synthesis pathways (Fig. 2). Glucose was supplemented at a predefined biomass threshold to exclusively drive mannose production via engineered pathways. The separation of growth and production phases, maximizing both biomass yield (via glycerol) and mannose titer (via glucose-optimized catalysis) to minimize fermentation time and carbon waste.

Following the successful remodeling of the central metabolic pathway to redirect carbon flux, the majority of glucose was now efficiently channeled towards F6P. This shift, however, brought a new requirement: significantly enhancing the downstream conversion efficiency from F6P to the target product, mannose. This conversion process fundamentally comprised two critical enzymatic steps: (1) the isomerization of F6P to M6P, and (2) the subsequent dephosphorylation of M6P to yield free mannose. Consequently, precise regulation of the expression levels of the key enzymes catalyzing these steps becomes essential for maximizing mannose production. The bidirectional enzyme phosphomannose isomerase PMI, which catalyzes the reversible equilibrium between F6P and M6P, might be a major bottleneck in the pathway. Theoretically, overexpressing PMI should drive F6P towards M6P accumulation. Paradoxically, PMI overexpression resulted in a counterproductive decrease in mannose production. Conversely, strategic attenuation of PMI activity could beneficially shift the F6P/M6P equilibrium towards M6P, subsequently promoting mannose accumulation. This unexpected finding suggested that fine-tuning PMI expression to an optimal level, rather than simply maximizing it, should be a focus for future strain engineering. The dephosphorylation of M6P to free mannose represents a known rate-limiting step. To address this, various E. coli-derived phosphatases were screened and expressed in the K. phaffii host. Among the candidates, the phosphatase YniC [12] demonstrated exceptionally robust activity, enabling highly efficient mannose production. However, a significant challenge emerged: these phosphatases exhibit considerable substrate promiscuity. This lack of specificity risks undesirable off-target dephosphorylation reactions, potentially diverting flux away from the desired pathway or consuming other essential phosphorylated intermediates. Therefore, future optimization necessitates either engineering-improved variants of existing phosphatases (like YniC) with heightened specificity for M6P, or discovering novel phosphatases inherently possessing this critical trait, to minimize metabolic inefficiencies and further maximize mannose yield.

The formation of byproducts decreased the substrate-to-product conversion yield and simultaneously complicated the downstream purification process of the target compound. During the analysis of mannose production, significant amounts of ribitol and arabitol were detected as byproducts. Targeted deletion of three genes encoding enzymes involved in ribitol and arabinitol metabolism was highly effective for eliminating arabinitol as a byproduct, reducing its concentration from 4.5 g/L to a relatively low level. However, this intervention unexpectedly led to an accumulation of ribitol. Despite this increase in ribitol, the net effect was a significant reduction in the total byproducts. This shift indicated a successful redirection of carbon flux away from the arabinitol branch, although complete suppression of the parallel ribitol pathway may allow for further optimization. Despite the extensive application of K. phaffii as a premier microbial host for recombinant protein production, fundamental research was not intensive. For example, its genome was sequenced over a decade ago, however a substantial fraction of genes remained functionally unannotated. Current understanding of the ribitol synthesis pathway in K. phaffii is incomplete, especially regarding the key enzymatic genes. Reducing ribitol production will depend on identifying and manipulating these genes in the next study.

A major unresolved challenge was intracellular mannose catabolism by host metabolism, and effective export of intracellular mannose to the extracellular environment would prevent its accumulation in the cells, thereby reducing its catabolism. Mannose and glucose are both hexose sugars, and many enzymes, such as hexose transporters, can recognize both substrates. According to the literatures [30, 31], the major hexose transporter genes in K. phaffii include HXT1, HXT2, GTH1, and HGT1. To investigate their functions, strains with individual expression of one of these genes and knockout of all four genes were developed, respectively. When streaked on plates containing 0.1%, 1%, or 5% glucose or mannose, the strain with deletion of HXT1, HXT2, GTH1、HGT1 still exhibited growth, indicating that K. phaffii possesses additional transporter(s) beyond HXT1, HXT2, GTH1, and HGT1 capable of mediating both glucose and mannose uptake. To maximize cytoplasmic mannose export, heterologous expression of hexose transporters from diverse species (e.g., S. cerevisiae Hxt1, E. coli GalP) were tried, but no significant improve in mannose titer was observed. The efficient export of intracellular mannose remains a critical challenge and represents a major focus for future research.

Conclusion

Combined overexpression of native/heterologous genes and dynamic pathway regulation engineered K. phaffii (GRAS) into an efficient mannose producer from glucose. The resultant strain achieved a highest yield of mannose (~ 121.1 g/L) with a yield of 0.28 g/g glucose and a volumetric productivity of 1.44 g/L·h. The process, which utilized low-cost glucose as the carbon source, matched the highest in vitro enzymatic titer (122 g/L) and surpassed it in conversion efficiency (28% vs. 24.4%), demonstrating the microbial fermentation as a competitive mannose biomanufacturing strategy with enhanced scalability and productivity. Challenges remained in transporter specificity and enzymatic optimization for industrial scaling. The engineering strategy developed here could be readily adapted for producing other high-value biochemicals, particularly using glucose as the substrate.

Mutant screening

The strong promoters PGAP and PTEF were used for overexpression of genes such as yniC and ybiV, while the glycerol inducible promoter PGUT1 was used for downregulated expression of genes such as pfk1 and zwf1.

The pJQ derivative plasmids for gene editing including gene deletion, gene replacement, or gene overexpression were used as described previously [29]. The resultant plasmids were first linearized and then transformed into K. phaffii by electroporation according to the method in Thermo Fisher Scientific Pichia Expression Kit User Guide. The transformants were verified by colony PCR and subsequent DNA sequencing.

Batch fermentation

Shake flask fermentations using various engineered strains were conducted in 1 L flasks containing 200 mL medium, incubated at 30 °C with agitation at 220 rpm. The fermentation medium consisted of a modified inorganic salt solution (1.2 g/L KH₂PO₄, 0.5 g/L CaSO₄, 6.5 g/L MgSO₄, 5 g/L K₂SO₄, 18 g/L NH₄H₂PO₄, and 13.4 g/L YNB) supplemented with 20 g/L glycerol or glucose. When a dual-carbon-source fermentation was carried out, glycerol was first used as the carbon source for cell growth, and various concentrations of glucose were then added to the medium to promote mannose production. Biotin and histidine were added to the medium at inoculation.

High-cell-density fed-batch fermentations were performed in a 10-L bioreactor with a 7-L working volume. The initial glycerol concentration in the inorganic salt medium was 50 g/L; upon glycerol depletion, glucose was supplemented up to 50 g/L. Once residual glucose decreased below 40 g/L, a glucose solution (700 g/L) was fed to maintain a concentration of 40–60 g/L. Temperature and pH during the fermentation were maintained at 30 °C and 5.0, respectively. During the initial 24-hour glycerol consumption phase (cell growth), the dissolved oxygen (DO) was maintained at > 30%. Subsequently, in the glucose consumption phase (mannose production), the DO setpoint was adjusted to 30%, 15%, or 5% and controlled automatically via aeration rate and agitation speed.

Seed cultures of all K. phaffii strains were grown in BMGY medium to an OD₆₀₀ of 10–12. A 10% (v/v) inoculum from these seeds was used for fermentation, and the experiments in shake flask and bioreactor were performed in triplicate.

Analysis of products using high-performance liquid chromatography

After centrifuging 1 mL of fermentation broth at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the precipitate was discarded. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, and 200 µL of the filtrate was transferred to an insert vial for storage at 4 °C prior to analysis. The separation and quantification of glucose, mannose, and arabitol were performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a refractive index (RID) detector (Waters 2695). Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent Hi-Plex Ca column (7.7 × 300 mm, 8 μm) maintained at 80 °C. 10 µL of samples after appropriate dilution was injected, and ultrapure water was used as the mobile phase with a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The RID detector cell was stabilized at 40 °C.

Standard solutions of mannose, glucose, and ribitol were prepared through gradient dilution. Calibration curves were established by plotting peak areas against standard concentrations, demonstrating good linearity with R² values exceeding 0.99.
