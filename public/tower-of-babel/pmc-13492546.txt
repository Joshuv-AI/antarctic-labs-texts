Trust in large language model-based solutions in healthcare among people with and without diabetes: a cross-sectional survey from the Health in Central Denmark cohort
Authors: Jonas Rosborg Schaarup, Anders Aasted Isaksen, Kasper Norman, Lasse Bjerg, Adam Hulman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveLarge language models have gained significant public awareness since ChatGPT’s release in 2022. This study describes the perception of chatbots in healthcare among people with and without diabetes.Methods and analysisIn 2024, an online survey was sent to 136 229 people, aged 18–89 years in the Health in Central Denmark cohort, including equestions related to the perception of artificial intelligence (AI) and chatbots. Questions assessed trust in chatbots in various healthcare scenarios (lifestyle, diagnostic, contact with general practitioner (GP) and emergency contact) alongside participants’ level of experience with ChatGPT. In one item, participants were randomly presented with either a more severe (emergency) or less severe (GP contact) scenario. We used multinomial logistic regression to investigate the association of diabetes status and demographic characteristics with trust in chatbots in different scenarios.Results39 109 participants completed the questionnaire. The majority were aware of AI (94%), although fewer had heard of ChatGPT (76%), and only 21% had tried it. Most participants trusted chatbots with involvement of healthcare professionals (HCPs) (49%–55%), while few trusted without them (3%–6%). The degree of trust depended on the severity of the scenario, demonstrated by lower odds (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.66) of trusting the chatbot under the control of HCPs in emergency care compared with contact with the GP. Type 2 diabetes, but not type 1 diabetes, was associated with less trust in chatbots than people without diabetes. Moreover, age, sex, duration of education and experience with ChatGPT also had an impact on trust.ConclusionChatbots are seen as supportive tools among public users when controlled by HCPs but are met with more scepticism in more severe situations. Digital exclusion risks and demographic differences, such as age, sex and disease-specific conditions (eg, type 2 diabetes), need to be addressed to ensure equitable and meaningful implementation.

Introduction

Chatbots are software applications designed to simulate human-like conversations with users. In recent years, large language model (LLM)-based chatbots such as ChatGPT have gained increasing attention for their potential to revolutionise healthcare domains by handling large volumes of texts (eg, electronic health records) and providing access to vast knowledge via natural language interactions.1 Powerful tools like ChatGPT have become accessible to everyone with a computer or smartphone and well-known in the general public.1 At the same time, demographics are shifting in large parts of the Western countries where the populations are ageing, resulting in changes in chronic disease burden, as for example, more people are living with diabetes.2 Thus, efficient healthcare solutions are required to meet the growing needs of prevention and patient care.3

Our previous study showed that people with diabetes were less likely to accept the replacement of healthcare professionals (HCPs) with artificial intelligence (AI) solutions compared with people without diabetes.4 Still, the majority would have accepted AI solutions as long as healthcare providers maintain control, which highlights that human interactions and accountability are important in care with AI.4–6 In previous surveys on public perception of AI in healthcare, the use of chatbots in contact with health services was the most polarising scenario.4 5 Since the release of ChatGPT in 2022, chatbots have become an increasing part of public discourse and policy-making,7 being used widely in daily life by the public, and even by patients and doctors, for example, to search medical information.8 9 In 2023, there were 57 registered ongoing clinical trials using chatbots in healthcare.10

Given the current focus on LLM-based chatbots, there is a need to assess the perception and use of these technologies among patients, and their trust in its use in healthcare. People with diabetes tend to have more frequent contact with healthcare services than those without, making them a relevant group for examining attitudes towards chatbot solutions. Among them, people with type 1 diabetes often rely on digital tools in their daily care, raising questions about whether this familiarity with technology shapes their attitudes towards chatbot-based health solutions.

Therefore, we aim to describe the trust in chatbots in healthcare, and how trust differs in various healthcare scenarios. Moreover, we will investigate to which degree trust in chatbots depends on having diabetes along with other characteristics. We hypothesise that people with diabetes, as frequent healthcare users, are more sceptical of chatbots, exhibiting lower trust in them compared with those without diabetes. Moreover, we hypothesise that the degree of trust depends on the acuteness and the severity of the scenario.

Methods and analysis

Study population

All individuals aged 18–89 years with diabetes (type 1 or type 2), residing in the Central Denmark Region, and a matched sample (matched on age, sex and municipality) without diabetes were invited to participate in a survey in 2020, forming the Health in Central Denmark (HICD) cohort,11 with a subgroup followed up in 2022.4 Diabetes status (type 1, type 2 or without diabetes) was determined using the Danish registries based on a previously described open-sourced algorithm.12 In 2024, a new survey was conducted, inviting 136 229 participants, providing the basis of our current study.

Data collection

Participants received a link to an online survey via the Danish Digital Post service or by mail. In addition to our survey on views on AI, chatbot and data sharing, the questionnaire covered themes such as self-reported health, medication use, dental health and diet, among others. The cohort was linked to the Danish national registries through the civil personal registration number, a unique identifier assigned to all residents in Denmark.

We adapted our previous pilot-tested questionnaire on AI in general to focus exclusively on the perception of chatbots.4 The questionnaire included eight close-ended questions related to use of chatbots, the benefits and risks of general AI in healthcare and the use of ChatGPT.

Four hypothetical scenarios, where chatbots might assist or replace human clinical care in the future, were presented to all participants (table 1). All participants were asked about scenarios involving the use of chatbots to support lifestyle recommendations and early detection of disease. To determine whether trust depends on the acuteness of the scenario, two scenarios were randomised among participants. They were assigned to respond to the same scenarios but involving contact to different medical services: a general practitioner (GP) or the emergency service. The four possible answers in all scenarios were ‘Yes’; ‘Yes, but I would also seek information from HCP’ or ‘Yes, but only with control by HCP’ depending on the scenario; ‘No’; ‘I don’t know’. The full questionnaire is available online.13 The Danish word for ‘trust’ (‘at stole på’) refers to having confidence in something, believing it to be reliable, truthful or capable. These concepts are in line with a commonly used definition of trust relying on three pillars: ability, benevolence and integrity.14 However, in our study, we were interested in respondents’ perceived trust based on their own understanding of trust, which might differ culturally, and not to dissect whether any of the different subconstructs were prominent.15 We asked participants, “Would you trust this assessment?” to understand their willingness to rely on a chatbot and the extent to which they would accept uncertainty in the given scenario, demonstrated with or without involvement of an HCP. As the scenarios in the study are hypothetical, we emphasise that trust refers to the respondents’ perceived trust. It has to be noted that ours is not the only definition of trust.16 Age, sex and duration of education (<10 years, 10–15 years, >15 years) were extracted from the Danish registries.

Statistical analysis

Population characteristics were described by diabetes status. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were described using the mean and standard deviation (SD).

Distribution of responses to the scenarios was presented as percentages with waffle plots.

We used multinomial logistic regression to investigate the association between diabetes status and other characteristics (age, sex, duration of education) and trust in chatbot in the different scenarios. All determinants were included in the same model. In a separate model, we further included participants’ use and knowledge of ChatGPT as a separate factor. We used multinomial logistic regression to investigate whether the trust of chatbots depended on the acuteness of the contact (questions C and D in table 1), comparing the effect of randomisation to a GP versus emergency contacts on chatbot trust. The answer ‘No’ was used as the reference category for the outcome.

To assess potential selection bias among survey responders and its effect on OR estimates and distribution of study population characteristics in the presented analyses, we applied stabilised propensity score weights based on the distribution of sex, age, duration of education and diabetes status in both the background population and the invited population, capped at the 95th percentile.

Complete case analyses were conducted. The statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical computing environment (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; V.4.1) and we used the waffle, nnet and dplyr packages to conduct analyses and for illustrations.

Patient and public involvement

A user panel of people with diabetes and their relatives at Steno Diabetes Center Aarhus was first involved in 2022 when we pilot-tested our initial survey on perceptions of AI in healthcare. Later, we presented the findings at a workshop and at follow-up discussions with the user panel. During these discussions, a person with diabetes highlighted how their perception of AI had changed after trying ChatGPT. This feedback directly inspired the development of this study in 2024, focused specifically on perceptions of chatbots in healthcare. Thus, the user panel helped shaping the research question and hypothesis by emphasizing the importance of understanding how emerging chatbot technologies like ChatGPT are trusted in healthcare among people with and without diabetes. Their input also informed our decision to randomize the healthcare scenario (e.g., emergency services vs. general practitioner contact) to explore how trust varied depending on the context.

From the invited population, 39 109 participants (29% response rate) completed the AI-related questions in the HICD 2024 survey (figure 1). The mean (SD) age of the respondents was 63 years (SD=11), with 46% being women. 60% did not have diabetes, 4% had type 1 diabetes and 36% had type 2 diabetes. No systematic differences were observed between the randomisation arms of scenarios C and D (online supplemental Table S3). Awareness and use of ChatGPT and AI

Most responders (76%) had heard of ChatGPT, and 8166 individuals (21%) had tried or used the application. In contrast to participants with type 1 diabetes or without diabetes, larger proportions (30%) of those with type 2 diabetes had not heard about ChatGPT. Overall, 94% had heard about AI in general. The weighted distribution based on both the background population and the invited population did not materially differ from that of the responders (online supplemental tables S1 and S2).

Acceptance of AI and chatbots in healthcare scenarios

When asked about the potential benefits and risks of AI in healthcare, 46% (n=17 996) of participants were uncertain whether the advantages would outweigh the risks. Meanwhile, 27% (10 413) believed that the advantages did outweigh the risks, 9% (3572) responded that the risks were greater and 6% (2186) had not heard of AI. The weighted distribution based on both the background population and the invited population did not materially differ from that of the responders (online supplemental table S1 and S2).

Across all scenarios, except for the more severe emergency call scenario (scenario D), about half (49%–55%) would trust chatbots when they were controlled by HCPs (figure 2). For both scenarios A and B (lifestyle recommendations and diagnoses based on symptoms and medical history), 53-54% trusted chatbots with control by HCPs. A proportion of 22%–42% expressed that they did not trust the decisions made by the chatbot. Only 3%–6% of the responders trusted chatbots without the control of HCPs across the scenarios.

Women were less likely to trust chatbots with HCPs (ORs 0.68–0.95) compared to men. Except in the scenario of chatbot-assisted diagnosis OR 1.00 (95% CI 0.99 to 1.01), older age was associated with trusting chatbot in healthcare with control of HCPs (OR 1.10–1.12 per 5 years), but older individuals were also more likely to respond ‘don’t know’ (online supplemental figure S2). With increasing age, higher proportions of participants were more likely to be uncertain. After age 30 years, the trend of trusting chatbots with HCPs either remained unchanged or increased across scenarios until age 70+ years (online supplemental figure S1). When contrasting trust in chatbots with HCPs against chatbots without HCPs, older individuals were less likely to trust chatbots without HCPs (online supplemental figure S6). The duration of education and more experience with ChatGPT was gradually associated with trusting AI with control of HCPs compared with not trusting chatbots (figure 3). Being a woman, older age and shorter education and less experience with ChatGPT were associated with being more likely to report ‘don’t know’, compared with not trusting (online supplemental figure S2). Weighted analysis towards both the background population and the invited population did not materially change the trends of factors associated with trust across scenarios (online supplemental figures S3 and S4).

Discussion

In this large cross-sectional study, we investigated the trust in chatbots among people with and without diabetes, spanning an age range from 18 to 89 years. Almost all participants have heard about AI, but one in four has not heard of ChatGPT. Most were uncertain whether the benefits of AI in healthcare outweighed its risks. Our findings showed a predominant trust in chatbots for the healthcare system as long as HCPs remain part of the process, although some participants expressed uncertainty or lack of trust. Trust in chatbot-assisted services was dependent on the acuteness and severity of the different scenarios, with lower levels of trust observed in more acute health contexts, such as emergency care contacts. Across all scenarios, few participants trusted chatbots without the involvement of HCPs. While participants with type 2 diabetes had lower trust than those without diabetes, we did not find any evidence for a difference between people with type 1 diabetes and those without diabetes.

Earlier studies have investigated public and patient perceptions of chatbots in healthcare, primarily focusing on general attitudes and usability.4 5 17 18 However, only a few studies have examined perceptions following the introduction of ChatGPT,19–21 and none have explored its use across different healthcare contexts. Our study contributes to this emerging area by investigating perceptions of chatbots under various scenarios in the ChatGPT era, using a large cohort of over 39 000 individuals with and without diabetes from the Central Denmark Region. Survey responses were linked to national registries, enabling integration of clinical and socioeconomic data, including diabetes status and duration of education. However, the study is limited to survey data and does not include interviews that explore personal experiences and beliefs about chatbots in healthcare.

Our results suggest that public awareness of AI has increased over recent years, gaining broader public exposure compared with earlier studies.4 22 Almost half were uncertain about whether AI would benefit healthcare, while one in four believed the benefits would outweigh the risks. Compared with our earlier study and a study from the UK, these findings suggest greater public uncertainty regarding AI in healthcare.4 22 In the current study, ChatGPT remains unknown for a fourth of the population, with one out of five having tried it, a figure lower than a recent report from the UK.23 People who have used chatbots were more open to trust them, although most remain convinced that chatbot solutions could not stand alone and should be used with control, follow-up consultation or second opinion by HCPs. This was the common opinion from all participants regardless of diabetes status, except in the scenario of emergency service where the majority would not trust the chatbot. While this finding supports chatbots assisting clinicians, the benefit of this is uncertain. In a recent study showcasing how chatbot-based solutions can be used in clinical settings with control of HCPs, in diagnostic aid of patients, no evidence of improved diagnostic performance was found. Although there is a notion that chatbots, when operating independently, have occasionally outperformed humans in specific tasks,24 another study found that ChatGPT performed worse in a comprehensive evaluation when applied in complex primary care settings.25 Moreover, chatbots’ potential as a patient and clinician support tool (eg, medical note-taking, consultations) has been widely recognised, although some risks (eg, hallucinations and biases) represent concerns.26 27 Previous studies have highlighted the importance of providing patients with explanations for clinical decisions and ensuring accountability in the decision-making process.28 29 Thus, strategies for the integration of HCPs in chatbot-based solutions remain important.

Despite high trust in chatbots with control of HCPs, almost half of participants either did not trust or were unsure about trusting chatbots. The complexity and perceived importance of the tasks (eg, lifestyle advice vs emergency decision-making) seemed to influence public trust. Additionally, patient characteristics such as sex, age, education and familiarity with ChatGPT impacted acceptance. The trends among factors observed align with findings from a multinational study on perceptions of AI in broader contexts.30 AI in healthcare, while offering transformative potential, can exacerbate digital exclusion when individuals are unable to access or use AI-driven technologies due to barriers. Digital exclusion has been shown to depend on experience with digital technology and sociodemographic factors, emphasising the need to address barriers and facilitators in AI systems in healthcare.31 Ignoring user characteristics could lead to digital exclusion, which risks differential uptake of chatbot-based solutions and potentially leads to inequalities in access to these healthcare solutions.

In this study, we found that trust in chatbots increased with age when HCPs were involved. However, older adults also showed more ‘don’t know’ responses, reflecting higher uncertainty by age. Thus, older adults are open to using chatbots with HCP support, but some may still lack confidence or familiarity with the technology. Our earlier HICD 2022 study showed that older adults were less likely to accept AI without HCP involvement compared with AI with HCP support, and in the current study, we confirmed these findings. These uncertainties among age groups highlight the risk of digital exclusion, which may arise from limited digital literacy, access to technology or hesitation to engage with unfamiliar tools.32 Nonetheless, a study has shown that older adults are viewing digital health solutions as a useful tool,33 but want HCPs to be involved.

Having type 2 diabetes was associated with being less likely to trust chatbots compared with people without diabetes. The use of chatbots in healthcare shows potential to empower patients by providing individualised, easily accessible and timely support with communication and knowledge sharing.8 Employees at a large Danish diabetes centre performed only slightly better than random chance (a coin toss) in distinguishing between chatbot-generated and human-written responses to questions related to diabetes care.34 Chatbot responses have been found to demonstrate greater empathy compared with doctors’ replies on a social media forum,35 but patients have shown to be more distrustful of the non-human responses.36 Managing diabetes requires personalised attention to lifestyle, medication and the monitoring of symptoms related to diabetes complications, which constitutes living with a higher overall disease risk.37 Such conditions can lead to increased distress and insecurity37 demanding human support and guidance, and thus making deviation from usual care less likely. For people with type 1 diabetes, adoption of advanced technologies such as continuous glucose monitors and insulin pumps has become a standard part of disease management over the last decade.38 Nearly a third of people with type 2 diabetes reported not having heard of ChatGPT compared with a fifth in people with type 1 diabetes or without diabetes. In our previous study, we found individuals with type 1 diabetes to be less open to AI solutions than people without diabetes; however, an emergency contact chatbot scenario was inconclusive and the most controversial of the scenarios studied.4 In the current study, people with type 2 diabetes were older. Earlier evidence suggests that older age is generally associated with lower digital literacy39 and, consequently, higher levels of uncertainty and lower trust in technologies. However, adjusting for age and familiarity with ChatGPT, used as a proxy for digital literacy,40 did not explain the differences observed within the diabetes group. Therefore, people with type 1 diabetes appear as open to chatbot solutions as those without diabetes, requiring further investigation into their readiness and trust compared with individuals with type 2 diabetes.

Sex differences in healthcare utilisation remain a challenge, with men often lagging in seeking care. In Denmark, women make more use of primary care than men, while men have a higher use of secondary care.41 As men showed to be more likely to trust chatbot solutions, one hypothesis could be that chatbots might help initiate earlier healthcare contact. This notion is supported by evidence of higher digital literacy among men compared with women.42 On the other hand, studies have shown that women value their interaction and relationship with their healthcare provider more than men.43 With the potential of chatbot solutions, sex differences should be considered to ensure that chatbots complement and enhance healthcare solutions while not replacing essential aspects of care. Chatbots could serve as an entry point to the healthcare system, potentially encouraging more men to seek timely medical care.

Our previous study based on the 2022 questionnaire included a subpopulation of 8420 participants, who responded to questionnaires about scenarios involving AI in healthcare.4 Compared with our current findings, this group demonstrated a higher acceptance of chatbots without HCP involvement (10%) than participants in the current study (3%–6%). Chatbots remain the most controversial application of AI in healthcare compared with other applications, for example, diagnosis based on images and monitoring by wearable devices. Our findings indicate that public concern about the role of AI in the healthcare system has increased, alongside growing uncertainty about whether the benefits of AI outweigh its risks. It is important to note that the 2022 HICD investigation involved a smaller non-random subset of participants and presented scenarios where AI applications were described as being as good as or better than usual care.4 In contrast, the current study excluded such explicit assurances, reflecting the rapid acceleration of AI and aligning more closely with the realistic implementation of these scenarios. As such, the differences between the populations and scenarios make direct comparisons challenging.

The current study investigated perceived trust in hypothetical scenarios among healthcare users. The trust-related questions were designed to reflect participants’ willingness to rely on future chatbot solutions and the extent to which they would accept such solutions with or without the involvement of HCPs. Our results show that trust is influenced by the acuteness of the scenario. This effect did not depend on whether the chatbot was used alone or in combination with a HCP. Trust in digital care is complex and involves multiple domains, such as perceived risks, privacy concerns and the accuracy or quality of the chatbot intervention.15 Since trust in the current study is based on hypothetical scenarios, it limits participants’ intention to use chatbots for healthcare purposes rather than trust based on actual experience. The only aspect reflecting experience-based trust is among participants who have tried ChatGPT, although this use did not occur in a healthcare context. These participants showed higher levels of trust both with and without the involvement of an HCP. Further research is needed to explore domains of trust to identify specific user concerns, ideally by combining validated questionnaires15 with qualitative methods.

This study is based on a large population from Central Region Denmark, including people with and without diabetes. Such a sample size allowed us to conduct randomisation of questions to demonstrate impact of severity of scenarios on perception of chatbot-based solutions. Moreover, we have a broad representation of demographic groups by age, sex, education, across diabetes status. The response rate was 29%, therefore certain subgroups are less represented in the questionnaires, for example, people with shorter education who are usually under-represented. A lower representation of groups with shorter education would be expected to exclude individuals with lower digital literacy. However, the weighted distributions and estimates based on both the background and invited populations suggested no major differences compared with the responders. Chatbots are often being updated, affecting the user experience. Thus, we acknowledge our findings are snapshots of perception of chatbots. Moreover, we acknowledge that AI is frequently discussed in the media,34 highlighting both the opportunities and risks of the technology, which might have influenced participants’ responses. The questionnaire was adapted from a previously pilot-tested version used in HICD 2022,4 originally based on a general AI study.5 The current version was not pilot-tested or systematically validated, which may introduce measurement bias in assessing trust and limit the generalisability of the findings to populations with different understandings of trust or digital literacy.15 Our study represents a population of predominantly Nordic European descent with access to high-quality diabetes care, and therefore their opinion may not generalise to other populations.

User involvement with groups of diverse representation, that is, age, sex, disease-specific conditions (eg, diabetes), educational level, digital literacy and ethnic background is needed to understand how AI can be used in a structured and meaningful way in different healthcare solutions both with and without HCPs. Patients already use chatbots for self-diagnosis and health recommendations.8 Diverse views and experiences with chatbots across population groups necessitate tailored solutions to facilitate uptake and measures to prevent structural discrimination based on individual backgrounds. Recent evidence suggests that exposure to ChatGPT can foster more positive attitudes towards AI in healthcare,44 demonstrating the potential of educating population groups through hands-on experiences and instructional materials on how to communicate with a chatbot. Age, ethnicity and socioeconomic disparities are risk factors for unequal chatbot performance due to linguistic variation.45 46 As chatbots are not yet implemented for healthcare purposes in Denmark, there is an urgent need for medically specialised systems to prevent potential harm or concern arising from biases in currently available models. This includes tailoring chatbots to prevent disparities in the quality of health recommendations across individuals with different background characteristics. While randomised controlled trials remain vital to demonstrate safety and effectiveness of the use of chatbots compared with usual care,47 we emphasise the importance of addressing user concerns to meaningfully integrate chatbots into high-stakes healthcare settings. This could be addressed using qualitative methods.

In conclusion, trust in chatbots is highest when controlled by HCPs, highlighting their essential role in guiding chatbot adoption. Moreover, trust varies by disease status, for example, people with type 2 diabetes were less trusting of chatbot-based healthcare solutions. This suggests that chatbots should complement rather than replace essential aspects of care, particularly for managing chronic conditions. The degree of trust is also influenced by the severity and acuteness of the health scenario, with greater openness to chatbot-based solutions in less severe settings, such as lifestyle recommendations, highlighting easier opportunities for implementation. Experience with ChatGPT along demographic characteristics was linked to trust. User involvement from patient groups is needed to understand population-specific perceptions and tailor meaningful implementation of chatbot-based interventions in healthcare.
