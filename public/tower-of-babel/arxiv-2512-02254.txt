Deterministic construction of typical networks in network models
Narayan G. Sabhahit ⋆, Moritz Laber ⋆, Harrison Hartle, Jasper van der Kolk, Samuel V. Scarpino, Brennan Klein, Dmitri Krioukov

Abstract
It is often desirable to assess how well a given dataset is described by a given model. In network science, for instance, one often wants to say that a given real-world network appears to come from a particular network model. In statistical physics, the corresponding problem is about how typical a given state, representing real-world data, is in a particular statistical ensemble. One way to address this problem is to measure the distance between the data and the most typical state in the ensemble. Here, we identify the conditions that allow us to define this most typical state. These conditions hold in a wide class of grand canonical ensembles and their random mixtures. Our main contribution is a deterministic construction of a state that converges to this most typical state in the thermodynamic limit. This construction involves rounds of derandomization procedures, some of which deal with derandomizing point processes, an uncharted territory. We illustrate the construction on one particular network model, deterministic hyperbolic graphs, and its application to real-world networks, many of which we find are close to the most typical network in the model. While our main focus is on network models, our results are very general and apply to any grand canonical ensembles and their random mixtures satisfying certain niceness requirements.



I
Introduction

Modeling real-world networks is one of the core research activities in network science
Cimini et al. 2019
, where it is often tempting to say that a particular real-world network appears “to come from” a particular model
Watts and Strogatz 1998
;
Barabási and Albert 1999
;
Watanabe et al. 2013
. Such statements usually mean that the real-world network in question appears to be (close to) a
typical
network in the model under consideration. This is usually supported by generating many random networks in the model, and by comparing their averaged properties of interest to the corresponding properties of the real network.

However, it is often desirable to “suppress noise,” and to work not with a zoo of random networks, but with just one—the “most representative” or “most typical” one—constructed deterministically. In network science, representative graph constructions are needed to boost reliability of statistical inference, to suppress or control sample-to-sample fluctuations, and for many other purposes
Cimini et al. 2019
;
Anand and Bianconi 2010
;
Squartini and Garlaschelli 2011
;
Cimini et al. 2022
;
Villegas et al. 2023
. More generally, the importance of representative typical states, as opposed to averages over many random realizations, has been emphasized repeatedly in many areas of statistical physics ranging from quantum typicality and thermalization to the breakdown of self-averaging near critical points and in disordered media
Popescu et al. 2006
;
Goldstein et al. 2006
;
Reimann 2007
;
Rigol et al. 2008
;
Boes et al. 2018
;
Schröder et al. 2013
. In more data-driven domains such as machine learning, stochasticity is a key source of irreproducibility, so the push towards more deterministic, reproducible benchmarks has become a major priority
Henderson et al. 2018
;
Nat 2021
;
Piantadosi et al. 2019
.

The deterministic construction of typical networks in random network models—a task reminiscent of the construction of expanders
Hoory et al. 2006
and pseudorandom graphs
Thomason 1987
;
Chung et al. 1988
;
Krivelevich and Sudakov 2006
—requires a definition of typical networks. The definition of network typicality
Park and Newman 2004
;
Bianconi 2009
is problematic if the probability
\mathbb{P}(G)
of networks
G
in the model is unknown or intractable, as is typically the case with network models defined by some (complicated) network-construction rules. Typical values of network properties in such models can be computed, but typical networks
per se
cannot be defined because a network with typical values of some properties may have atypical values of some other unchecked properties
Orsini et al. 2015
. Nor does it make much sense to ask for the “most typical” network in models with high degeneracy of typical sets. In the canonical Erdős-Rényi
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
model
Erdős and Rényi 1960
, for instance, the standard definition of typical sets based on the asymptotic equipartition property
Cover and Thomas 2005
;
MacKay 2003
says that the most typical networks are the ones with
n
nodes and
m=\left\lfloor Np\right\rceil
links, where
N=\binom{n}{2}
and
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
is the nearest integer function. However, any of these many
{N\choose m}
networks is equally typical, leaving substantial ambiguity in the selection of a single deterministic representative.

Here, we develop a general definition of
typical networks
in a model, and discuss a class of network models—equivalent to grand canonical ensembles in statistical physics—where this definition makes sense and allows for the selection of a unique
most typical network
. We also develop methods to construct such networks deterministically in a computationally efficient fashion, and show how these methods work in application to a particular grand canonical network model and to real-world network data.

II
Network typicality

The simplest case where a definition of the most typical network is possible is when the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

the probability
\mathbb{P}(G)
of networks
G
in a model depends on
G
only via some network properties
\boldsymbol{f}
:
\mathbb{P}(G)=\tilde{\mathbb{P}}[\boldsymbol{f}(G)]
;

2.

the distribution
\mathbb{P}(v)
of the values
v
of these properties in the model,
\mathbb{P}(v)=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}(G)\delta(\boldsymbol{f}(G),v)
, is sharply peaked (self-averaging); and

3.

the very top of this peak is populated by just one network
G^{\star}
.

Such
G^{\star}
is then the most typical network in the model, Fig.
1
. Condition (2) ensures there is a typical set, condition (3) says there is the most typical element in this set, while condition (1) allows us to extend the notion of typicality from network properties to networks
per se
because it ensures that nothing other than these properties matters in the model.

\varepsilon(G)

Condition (3) is satisfied if properties
\boldsymbol{f}(G)

;

—class of models in which the conditions above are satisfied, and which is particularly attractive for statistical analysis thanks to its maximum-entropy properties. This is the key feature of
exponential random graph models
(ERGMs), of which grand canonical models are a special case (Appendix
A
).

In grand canonical models, links between nodes
i
and
j
form independently with the Fermi-Dirac probabilities

p_{ij}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{ij}-\mu)}+1}=\frac{1}{e^{\alpha+\beta\varepsilon_{ij}}+1},

(1)

where the inverse temperature
\beta
and chemical potential
\mu
are the model parameters,
\alpha=-\mu\beta
, and
\varepsilon_{ij}
are energies of particle states
\{i,j\}
—particles are links. The models in this class differ in definitions of link energy. The probability
\mathbb{P}(G)
of network
G
in any grand canonical model is
Park and Newman 2004

\mathbb{P}(G)=e^{-\alpha m(G)-\beta\varepsilon(G)}/\mathcal{Z},

(2)

so the sufficient statistics
\boldsymbol{f}(G)
are the number of particles (links)
m(G)
in network
G
, and
G
’s energy, the sum of its link energies
\varepsilon(G)=\sum_{i,j}\varepsilon_{ij}G_{ij}
, where
\{G_{ij}\}
is
G
’s adjacency matrix. The partition function
\mathcal{Z}
is the normalization coefficient. As in any grand canonical ensemble
Jaynes 1957
, the
\mathbb{P}(G)
in Eq. (
2
) maximizes Shannon entropy across all other models with the same expected values of the number of links
\bar{m}
and network energy
\bar{\varepsilon}
(Appendix
A
).

The distributions of both the number of particles and energy are known to be self-averaging in grand canonical ensembles
Kardar 2007
. Therefore, we define a
(\delta_{m},\delta_{\varepsilon})
-typical set of networks as

\mathcal{G}_{T}=\left\{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}:\frac{|m(G)-\bar{m}|}{m_{n}}<\delta_{m},\,\frac{|\varepsilon(G)-\bar{\varepsilon}|}{m_{n}}<\delta_{\varepsilon}\right\},

(3)

where
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is the set of all networks of size
n
, and
m_{n}
is the asymptotic scaling of
\bar{m}
with
n
(
\bar{m}\sim m_{n}
). The self-averaging properties of grand canonical ensembles ensure that in the thermodynamic limit
n\to\infty
, the bulk of the probability mass is concentrated on the typical set:
for any
\delta_{\varepsilon},\delta_{m}>0
,
\mathbb{P}(G\in\mathcal{G}_{T})\rightarrow 1
. And if energies
\varepsilon_{ij}
are all different real numbers, then all different networks
G
have different energies
\varepsilon(G)
, so the top of the peak is occupied by one network—the most typical one in Fig.
1
. The question is how to find it.

III
Constructing typical networks

III.1
Grand canonical models

Selecting the most typical value
m^{\star}
of the number of links in a grand canonical model is easy:
m^{\star}
is the closest integer to the expected number of links
\bar{m}=\sum_{i<j}p_{ij}
,
m^{\star}=\lfloor\bar{m}\rceil
. Yet if the network size
n
is large, then the brute-force search for the network
G^{\star}
minimizing the network energy difference
|\varepsilon(G)-\bar{\varepsilon}|
across the resulting
N\choose m^{\star}
possible networks is computationally prohibitive, akin to the
\mathsf{NP}
-complete subset sum problem
Garey and Johnson 1990
and knapsack problem
Salkin and De Kluyver 1975
.

To address this problem, we devise a deterministic link creation process that emulates random sampling of links
(i,j)
with probabilities
p_{ij}
. It is presented in Fig.
2
(a). The process simply overlays the matrix
\mathbf{p}
of connection probabilities
\{p_{ij}\}
over a regular lattice with
m^{\star}
vertices, thus building a unique network
G_{\mathbf{p}}
for any
\mathbf{p}
, not necessarily grand canonical (
1
). However, if
\mathbf{p}
is grand canonical, then we show in Section
IV
that the energy of constructed networks
\varepsilon(G_{\mathbf{p}})
converges to its expected value
\bar{\varepsilon}
, implying that the networks themselves converge to the most typical network,
G_{\mathbf{p}}\to G^{\star}
.

(a) Grand canonical derandomization.

\mathbf{p}=\{p_{ij}\}_{i<j}

G_{\mathbf{p}}

(i,j)

(b) Hypercanonical derandomization.
To derandomize the random sprinkling of
n

d_{i}=-(1/n)\ln(1-u_{i})
using the inverse CDF method

n

u_{i}=(i-1/2)/n
,
i\in[n]

\sigma

is set to
x_{i}=x_{i-1}+d_{\sigma(i)}
, where
x_{0}=0
. In higher dimensions

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
in

d_{i}
between consecutive coordinates in different dimensions:
x_{i}=x_{i-1}+d_{\sigma_{1}(i)}
,

. It is used then again—
\sigma_{3},\sigma_{4}

(x,y)
-coordinates of the same point:
(x_{i},y_{i})=(x_{\sigma_{3}(i)},y_{\sigma_{4}(i)})

\sigma_{s}
,
s\in[2d]
, are defined via the
multiplicative congruential generator
(MCG)
Knuth 1997

\sigma_{s}
is the permutation that sorts the pseudorandom MCG numbers
\left\{b_{k}\right\}_{k=n(s-1)+1}^{ns}
in the increasing order, where
b_{k+1}=ab_{k}\mod c
,
a=7^{5}
,
b_{0}=42
, and
c=2^{31}-1
.

To show this convergence, we need to be able to increase the network size
n
in a systematic fashion. The easiest way to do so is in the context of hidden variable network models
Caldarelli et al. 2002
;
Söderberg 2002
;
Boguñá and Pastor-Satorras 2003
, an extremely general and well-studied class of models encompassing as special cases many quintessential network models in network science (Appendix
B
). One complication is that in these models, link energies are not fixed, but random

\varepsilon_{ij}=\phi(x_{i},x_{j}),

(4)

where
\phi
is some fixed function of random hidden variables
x_{i}
of nodes
i
. The resulting models are thus not grand canonical, but probabilistic mixtures of those, which we call
hypercanonical models
since random model parameters are called hyperparameters in statistics
Evans and Rosenthal 2010
. But then, for our typical network construction to be deterministic, we have to derandomize hidden variables
x_{i}
first. We address this task next.

III.2
Hypercanonical models

Without much loss of generality, we can assume that hidden variables
x_{i}
are independent uniformly distributed random variables in the unit
d
-cube,
x_{i}\sim U\left([0,1]^{d}\right)
. (If they are not, we can map them to
U\left([0,1]^{d}\right)
via their inverse cumulative distribution function (CDF), adjusting the function
\phi
accordingly.) The question is how to derandomize
n
random points in the unit cube, known as the binomial point process, which converges to the Poisson point process (PPP) as
n\rightarrow\infty

Last and Penrose 2017
.

This question does not have a unique answer since it depends on what properties of the PPP we want to preserve in our derandomization, reminiscent of the choice of distinguisher in pseudorandomness
Thomason 1987
;
Chung et al. 1988
;
Krivelevich and Sudakov 2006
. Here, we choose to preserve the distances between nearest points in a PPP, since these distances are one of the most basic definitive properties of PPPs
Last and Penrose 2017
. In a
1
-dimensional PPP of rate
n
, the distances
d_{i}
between consecutive points
i
on the real line are independent exponentially distributed random variables with rate
n
. The inverse CDF of the exponential distribution of rate
n
is
F^{-1}(x)=-(1/n)\ln(1-x)
. Therefore, to derandomize the independent sampling of
d_{i}
’s from the exponential distribution, we simply set them to
d_{i}=F^{-1}(u_{i})
, where
u_{i}=(i-1/2)/n
,
i\in[n]
, is a regular lattice on
[0,1]
. The choice of the
1/2
shift in it is justified in Appendix
C
. We then use a pseudorandom function—several times in higher dimensions—as shown in Fig.
2
(b), to scramble all the unwanted correlations that are not present in a PPP. In Appendix
D
we show that this deterministic process reproduces the distances not only between nearest points but also between two random points in
1d
and
2d
PPPs.

As soon as hidden variables are derandomized, the link energies (
4
) become fixed, and so are the link existence probabilities (
1
). This means that for each network size
n
, we now have a unique network
G_{n,\phi}
, which we claim converges to the most typical network
G^{\star}
in the model,
G_{n,\phi}\to G^{\star}
. We show this next.

IV
Convergence to the most typical network

To demonstrate the aforementioned convergence, we select one particular hypercanonical model—random hyperbolic graphs
Krioukov et al. 2010
. We choose this model because its energy function is real-valued, so the most typical network is well-defined. Another motivation to consider this model is that it attracted significant research attention over past decades in as diverse disciplines as machine learning, neuroscience, or Internet routing
Boguñá et al. 2021
, thanks to its ability to capture a collection of definitive properties of many real-world networks
Boguñá et al. 2021
. Yet another reason to consider this model is that it encompasses several quintessential network models as its limiting regimes
Budel et al. 2024
.

In the model, the hidden variables
x_{i}
are the coordinates of points sprinkled randomly over a hyperbolic disc, while energies
\varepsilon_{ij}
are hyperbolic distances between those points. In addition to the grand canonical parameters
\alpha,\beta
controlling the expected number of links and energy—both scaling as
\mathcal{O}(n)
in the model (
\bar{m}\sim\bar{\varepsilon}\sim m_{n}=n
)—the model has one more parameter
\gamma
, the tail exponent of the power-law node degree distribution. Further details are in Appendix
E
.

Since the model is not grand canonical but hypercanonical, we cannot rely on the known facts
Kardar 2007
that sufficient statistics, the number of particles and energy, are self-averaging in any grand canonical ensemble. We have to show that the number of links and energy are self-averaging in our hypercanonical model as well, thus satisfying the second concentration requirement in Section
II
. We show this in Fig.
3

F
, where we derive the following scaling of the coefficient of variations
C_{V}(n)
for these two properties in the model:

\displaystyle C_{V}(n)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\min\left\{1/2,\,(\gamma-2)/(\gamma-1)\right\}}(\ln(n))^{\mathbbm{1}\{\gamma=3\}/2}\right),

(5)

where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function. This implies that
C_{V}(n)\to 0
for any
\gamma>2
, so the distributions of
m
and
\varepsilon
are sharply peaked. Since they are sharply peaked, the definitions of (most) typical networks in grand canonical models in Section
II
extend to this hypercanonical model as well.

One natural application of the developed framework, mentioned in the introduction, is the assessment of how typical a given real-world network is in a given model. For such assessments, one needs to compare two networks—a real-world one and the most typical one in the model. There are many ways to compare two networks, none of which is ultimately the best. One option is to evaluate one or many graph similarity metrics on the two networks. For a recent review of such metrics, we refer to
Hartle et al. 2020
;
McCabe et al. 2021
. Here, instead of evaluating any of such distances, we showcase graph comparisons more directly via measuring a collection of basic properties of two networks, shown in Fig.
4
. We observe that according to these fundamental properties, the Internet and the Cora citation networks are fairly close to the most typical DHG network.

In Appendix
H
, we present the results of such measurements applied to a total of seventeen networks from different domains. We find that in many social, technological, and biological networks, the degree distribution, clustering, distance distribution, betweenness centrality, and other properties are close to the ones of the most typical DHG network. In contrast, the distance distribution of transportation networks, such as street and urban public transport networks, that are confined to Euclidean space and therefore are large worlds, are far from the ones of the most typical DHG, which is a small world. Across domains, we observe that larger networks are more closely aligned with the most typical DHG network, but even small networks can sometimes have properties surprisingly similar to the ones of the most typical DHG.

Summarizing, the most typical deterministic hyperbolic graph, with its three parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, two of which (
\alpha,\beta
) are the standard grand canonical ones, captures well a range of fundamental network properties—some of which, like betweenness or edge multiplicity distributions, are quite non-trivial—in many real-world networks coming from different domains, albeit with notable exceptions mentioned above.

VI
Discussion

The last observation in the previous section highlights the following open question: what other properties do we get “for free” in the most typical state in a model as defined in Section
II
? For example, the most typical deterministic hyperbolic graph has the most typical values of the number of links (
\alpha
), energy (
\beta
), and degree distribution tail (
\gamma
), yet what other network properties in this graph have their typical values as well? If the graph was random, this question could be answered by a variety of methods including the large deviation theory. It is less clear how to approach this question given that the graph is deterministic.

Potential applications of the developed framework are definitely not limited to the motivating one discussed in the previous section. One class of such applications can be called
derandomized or deterministic statistical inference
. It would work as follows. Observe that for a given model, our framework establishes a function from the model parameter space to the space of graphs. Any combination of the parameter values corresponds to only one deterministic graph, the most typical one in the model with these parameter values. Therefore, the method is a powerful parameter inference and model selection tool. Indeed, for a given collection of network properties or graph similarity metrics, one can simply find the parameter values that minimize the corresponding graph similarity metric between a given (real-world) network and typical networks in the model. We, in fact, use this approach for parameter inference to recover a set of model parameters in Appendix
H
. The model selection step compares these similarity metrics between a given (real-world) network and most typical networks (with inferred parameters) in different models. The one that minimizes the distance describes the data best, according to this choice of graph distance. The conceptually closest approaches to this programme are the minimum description length principle
Grünwald 2007
and algorithmic statistics
Gacs et al. 2001
.

More importantly, the developed framework is very general, definitely not just limited to the realm of networks, as it applies to any grand canonical or hypercanonical ensembles satisfying the minor niceness conditions discussed in Section
II
. These conditions are typically satisfied in most paradigmatic systems. While grand canonical ensembles are very common in statistical physics, hypercanonical ones are less so, yet they still appear in many mainstream systems, such as spin glasses with frozen disorder
Edwards and Anderson 1975
.

As a final note, to derandomize hypercanonical ensembles, we had to derandomize the Poisson point process in Section
III.2
. Derandomization of point processes appears to be a poorly explored territory that would be interesting to investigate on more rigorous grounds. Potential applications may turn out to be as abundant and fruitful as with derandomization of other discrete random structures in information theory and machine learning
Hoory et al. 2006
;
Vadhan 2012
.

Appendix A
Exponential random graph models

A random graph model is defined as a probability distribution, denoted
\mathbb{P}(G)
, over the set
\mathcal{G}
of all possible labeled graphs
G
of size
n
. On many occasions, we are given a graph
G^{\star}
and tasked with coming up with a model that reproduces a set of graph properties of interest, denoted
\boldsymbol{f}(G^{\star})
, e.g., the number of edges, the degree sequence, or the number of triangles. In such a scenario, it is desirable to develop a model that reproduces the given constraints but is otherwise maximally random; otherwise, the model injects extraneous information. This view is known as the maximum entropy principle
Jaynes 1957
;
Jaynes 2003
, whereby among all distributions satisfying the constraints, the one selected as a model should maximize Shannon entropy,

S[\mathbb{P}]=-\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}(G)\log\mathbb{P}(G).

(6)

Such a model can then be used as a
null model
for
G^{\star}
, the properties of which, beyond those constrained, can be compared against those of
G^{\star}
. If major differences are observed, it indicates the presence of statistical mechanisms beyond those implied by the constraints; if not, the constraints chosen are considered sufficiently explanatory.

If the constraints are sharply enforced, then we have a
microcanonical ensemble
and the form of
\mathbb{P}(G)
is just a uniform measure over the set of all graphs that satisfy the constraint exactly, i.e., drawn uniformly from the set
\{G\in\mathcal{G}:\boldsymbol{f}(G)=\boldsymbol{f}(G^{\star})\}
. If the constraints are enforced to be satisfied only in expectation,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{f}}=\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{f}(G)\mathbb{P}(G)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}(G^{\star}),

(7)

then we have a
canonical ensemble
; note the requirement of
\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}(G)=1
is an additional constraint on the values of
\{\mathbb{P}(G)\}_{G\in\mathcal{G}}
. This constrained optimization problem is solved using the method of Lagrange multipliers, resulting in Gibbs/Boltzmann exponential family distribution
Park and Newman 2004
,

\mathbb{P}(G)=\dfrac{e^{-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(G)}}{\sum_{\tilde{G}\in\mathcal{G}}e^{-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}(\tilde{G})}},

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is a vector of Lagrange multipliers whose values must be set to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\lambda^{\star}}
such that the soft constraint
\boldsymbol{\bar{f}}=\boldsymbol{f}(G^{\star})
is realized.

Since the value of
\mathbb{P}(G)
depends on the graph
G
only through
\boldsymbol{f}(G)
, the properties
\boldsymbol{f}(G)
are called
sufficient statistics
of the model. Formally, Eq. (
8
) are the entropy maximizing distributions subjected to the constraints
\boldsymbol{f}(G^{\star})
called
exponential random graph models
(ERGMs)
Jaynes 1957
;
Park and Newman 2004
;
Holland and Leinhardt 1981
;
Frank and Strauss 1986
. ERGMs and the maximum entropy formalism in general have been successful in modeling patterns of activity in neuronal circuits
Meshulam and Bialek 2025
, testing various hypotheses regarding the tie-formation mechanism in social networks
Robins et al. 2007
;
Lusher et al. 2012
, and as null models in network science
Cimini et al. 2019
;
Squartini and Garlaschelli 2017
.

A particular class of ERGMs which is of interest to us here is the class of
grand canonical
models
Park and Newman 2004
;
Kardar 2007
;
Garlaschelli and Loffredo 2009
;
Garlaschelli and Loffredo 2006
, where two nodes are connected independently with probability

p_{ij}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{ij}-\mu)}+1}=\frac{1}{e^{\alpha+\beta\varepsilon_{ij}}+1},

(9)

where
\beta
(inverse temperature) and
\mu
(chemical potential) are the model parameters,
\alpha=-\mu\beta
, and
\varepsilon_{ij}
are link energies. Letting
G_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
denote the adjacency matrix elements of
G
, the graph probability mass function is given by,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G)

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}p_{ij}^{G_{ij}}(1-p_{ij})^{1-G_{ij}},

(10)

\displaystyle=\exp{\left(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{ij}\log\left(\dfrac{p_{ij}}{1-p_{ij}}\right)+\log(1-p_{ij})\right)},

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\exp{\left(-\alpha m(G)-\beta\varepsilon(G)\right)}}{\mathcal{Z}},

where
m(G)=\sum_{i<j}G_{ij}
is the number of edges in graph
G
,
\varepsilon(G)=\sum_{i<j}G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}
is its energy, and
\mathcal{Z}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}1/(1-p_{ij})
is the partition function. Comparing the above equation with Eq. (
8
), it is clear that the grand canonical model is an entropy-maximizing distribution subjected to the constraint on the expected values of the number of edges and energy. In other words, the grand canonical model is an ERGM in which the sufficient statistics are the number of edges and energy.

Appendix B
Hidden variable network models

Hidden variable network models (HVNMs) are a widely studied class of random network models characterizing how heterogeneously distributed node-variables (hidden variables (HVs)) can give rise to various patterns of network connectivity
Boguñá and Pastor-Satorras 2003
;
Söderberg 2002
;
Caldarelli et al. 2002
. Graphs sampled from HVNMs arise through two stages of randomness:

a)

each node
i\in[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
gets a random hidden variable
h_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
in some space
\mathcal{X}
sampled independently from some probability density
\upsilon(h)
;

b)

edges form pairwise-independently with probability
p_{ij}=g(h_{i},h_{j})
where
g:\mathcal{X}^{2}\to[0,1]
is the connection probability function.

Consequently, the hidden variable configuration
H=(h_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
has a joint probability density of the form
\rho(H)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\upsilon(h_{i})
. Without much loss of generality, one may set
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]^{d}
and
\upsilon=U\left([0,1]^{d}\right)
in the case of
d
-dimensional HVs, since we can often map this hidden variable configuration to the intended hidden variable space via the inverse transform sampling
Devroye 1986
;
Robert and Casella 1999
. In this setting, the probability of a network given
H
is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(G|H)

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}p_{ij}^{G_{ij}}(1-p_{ij})^{1-G_{ij}},

(11)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{-\sum_{i<j}\lambda_{ij}G_{ij}}}{\mathcal{Z}},

\displaystyle\text{where}~\lambda_{ij}

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\dfrac{1-p_{ij}}{p_{ij}}\right),~\mathcal{Z}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(\dfrac{1}{1-p_{ij}}\right).

Thus, any HVNM conditioned on the hidden-variable configuration
H
(i.e., an HVNM with a specified “frozen” configuration
H
) is an ERGM (Appendix
A
) with
\binom{n}{2}
pairwise constraints of the form

\bar{G}_{ij}=g(h_{i},h_{j}).

(12)

However, some choices of specific functional forms of the connection probability function
g(\cdot)
—including the grand canonical one in Eq. (
9
)—collapse the
\binom{n}{2}
individual edge indicator sufficient statistics into only a few aggregated sufficient statistics, like the number of edges and energy in the grand canonical case.

The probability of graph
G
in an HVNM is

\mathbb{P}(G)=\int_{H}\mathbb{P}(G|H)\rho(H)dH.

(13)

Therefore, HVNMs are probabilistic mixtures of ERGMs,
\mathbb{P}(G|H)
, with mixing distribution
\rho(H)
. We refer to such distributions as
hypercanonical ensembles
, so all HVNMs are hypercanonical.

By appropriately choosing the hidden-variable distribution
\rho(\cdot)
and the connection-probability function
g(\cdot)
, hidden-variable network models (HVNMs) can encompass a broad spectrum of paradigmatic network models, including the Erdős-Rényi random graph model
Erdős and Rényi 1960
;
Gilbert 1959
, (hypersoft) configuration model
van der Hoorn et al. 2018
, stochastic block model
Karrer and Newman 2011
, latent-space models
Penrose 2003
;
Penrose 2016
;
Krioukov et al. 2010
;
Serrano et al. 2008
;
Boguñá et al. 2021
, and many others.

Appendix C
Justification for the lattice shift

In this section, we motivate the choice of shifting the regular lattice,
u_{i}=(i-z)/n
, horizontally by
z=1/2
, in the deterministic sprinkling process (outlined in Sec.
III.2
). The coordinate of point
i
in the
1d
deterministic sprinkling process on the unit interval is given by,

x_{i}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{i}\ln\left(1-u_{\sigma(i)}\right),

(14)

where
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
is a pseudo-random permutation function. We want these deterministically sprinkled points to have as many of the statistical properties of the Poisson point process (PPP) as possible. To that end, along with matching the (nearest neighbor) distance distribution of the PPP (see Appendix
D
), we also match the scaling of the maximal coordinate in deterministic sprinkling,
x_{n}
, with the scaling of the mean of the extreme value distribution of
U([0,1])

David and Nagaraja 2003
by shifting the regular lattice,
u_{i}=(i-z)/n
horizontally by
z=1/2
.

To show this formally, let us consider
n
i.i.d. uniform random variables, i.e.,
\xi_{i}\sim U([0,1])
and let
\xi_{\max}=\max\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n}\right)
. The cumulative distribution function of the maximal value is,

\mathbb{P}(\xi_{\max}\leq\xi)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\xi_{i}\leq\xi)=\xi^{n}.

(15)

This then allows us to derive the expected maximal value as,

\overline{\xi_{\max}}=n\int_{0}^{1}\xi^{n}d\xi=\frac{n}{n+1}=1-\frac{1}{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(16)

In order for our deterministic process to give the same result, we must set
z
in
u_{i}=(i-z)/n
appropriately such that
x_{\max}=x_{n}=1-1/n
. From Eq. (
14
) it is easy to see that
x_{n}=-1/n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln(1-u_{i})
, where the pseudo random permutation function,
\sigma(i)
, is irrelevant. The question now is how to choose
z\in[0,1]
such that the correct scaling is achieved. For some general value of
z
,

\displaystyle x_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\left(1-\frac{i-z}{n}\right),

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\ln\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(i+z)\right)+\ln n,

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n}\ln\left(\frac{\Gamma(z+n)}{\Gamma(z)}\right)+\ln n.

(17)

The transcendental equation
x_{n}=\overline{\xi_{\max}}
cannot be solved analytically, and we must therefore turn to asymptotics. In the setting
n\gg 1
, we can use the following,

\ln\left(\Gamma(w)\right)=\frac{2w-1}{2}\ln(w)-w+\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{w}\right),

(18)

to obtain,

x_{n}=1+\frac{1-2z}{2}\frac{\ln n}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\ln\left(\frac{\Gamma(z)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(19)

The term of order
\mathcal{O}(\ln n/n)
is not present in
\overline{\xi_{\max}}
. Therefore, it must be removed by setting
z=1/2
, leading to

x_{n}=1-\frac{\ln 2}{2}\frac{1}{n}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right).

(20)

This is not exactly the same expression as Eq.
16
, but the constant pre-factor in the second term has no effect on the scaling behavior, so, for instance, the scaling of the largest hidden degree is
\kappa_{\max}\sim n^{1/(\gamma-1)}
in both the random and derandomized cases.

In this section, we show that the hypercanonical derandomization in Sec.
III.2
results in a deterministic sprinkling of
n
points on the unit interval and unit square matching the (nearest neighbor) distance distribution of the Poisson point process (PPP).

The nearest neighbor distance distribution in
1d
and
2d
PPP of intensity
n
is
Last and Penrose 2017

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(nn)}_{1\mathrm{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=2ne^{-2nx},

(21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{(nn)}_{2\mathrm{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=2\pi nxe^{-\pi nx^{2}}.

The PPP distance distribution, on the other hand, is the probability density function of the distance between two random points on the unit interval,
\mathbb{P}_{1\text{d}}(x)
, or unit square,
\mathbb{P}_{2\text{d}}(x)
. It is given by
Mathai 1999

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{1\text{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2(1-x),&0\leq x\leq 1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise};\end{cases}

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{2\text{d}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2x\bigl(\pi-4x+x^{2}\bigr),&0\leq x\leq 1,\\[6.0pt]
2x\!\Bigl[4\sqrt{x^{2}-1}-(x^{2}+2-\pi)-4\arctan\!\sqrt{x^{2}-1}\Bigr],&1<x\leq\sqrt{2},\\[6.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In Fig.

(a,b), we show a realization of the

(c,d) and Figs.

(e,f) confirm that the deterministic sprinkling has the same (nearest neighbor) distance distribution as the PPP.

n=10^{3}

.

III.2
with

), versus the empirical nearest neighbor distance distribution in the deterministic sprinkling process with

), versus the empirical distance distribution in the deterministic sprinkling with

on the unit interval and unit square, respectively.

Appendix E
Random hyperbolic graphs (RHGs)

An important random graph model belonging to the class of HVNMs (Appendix
B
) is the degree-corrected latent space model, also known as random hyperbolic graphs (RHG)
Krioukov et al. 2010
, which combines the properties of the hypersoft configuration model
van der Hoorn et al. 2018
and soft random geometric graphs
Penrose 2003
;
Penrose 2016
. Here, we describe the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
equivalent versions of this hypercanonical model
Krioukov et al. 2010
;
Serrano and Boguñá 2022
;
Boguñá et al. 2021
.

In the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
formulation, sampling a network first involves sprinkling nodes quasi-uniformly within a disk of radius
R_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
on the hyperbolic plane of constant curvature
K=-1
. That is, the hidden variables of nodes
i
are their polar coordinates
(r_{i},\theta_{i})
which are sampled independently as
\theta_{i}\sim U[(0,2\pi)]
and
r_{i}\sim\rho
, where the support of the radial coordinate distribution
\rho
is
[0,R_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}]
, and its PDF is

\rho(r)=\tilde{\alpha}\dfrac{\sinh(\tilde{\alpha}r)}{\cosh{(\tilde{\alpha}R_{\mathbb{H}^{2}})-1}}.

(23)

The parameter
\tilde{\alpha}\geq 1/2
controls the power law exponent of the resulting degree distribution in the model via
\gamma=2\tilde{\alpha}+1
, while the radius of the disk

R_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}=2\ln\left(\dfrac{2n}{\bar{k}\beta}\csc{\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)}\left(\dfrac{\tilde{\alpha}}{\tilde{\alpha}-1/2}\right)^{2}\right),

(24)

fixes the average degree to
\bar{k}>0
at
n\to\infty
. The parameter
\beta>1
, the inverse temperature, controls the average clustering in the model.

Upon the assignment of the coordinates, two nodes are connected with the grand canonical Fermi-Dirac connection probability,

p_{ij}=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta(\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}-R_{\mathbb{H}^{2}})/2}}.

(25)

The fermionic energies
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}
are the hyperbolic distances between nodes
i
and
j
given by the hyperbolic law of cosines,

\cosh{\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}}=\cosh{r_{i}}\cosh{r_{j}}-\sinh{r_{i}}\sinh{r_{j}}\cos{\Delta\theta_{ij}},

(26)

where
\Delta\theta_{ij}=\pi-|\pi-|\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}||
is the angle between the two points. This distance is approximately equal to
Krioukov et al. 2010
;
Serrano and Boguñá 2022

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}\approx r_{i}+r_{j}+2\ln\left(\dfrac{\Delta\theta_{ij}}{2}\right).

(27)

The
\mathbb{H}^{2}
model is a hypercanonical mixture of the grand canonical models (Appendix
A
) with random link energies
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}
defined in Eq. (
27
). Their distribution is the distance distribution between random points on the hyperbolic disk.

In the equivalent
\mathbb{S}^{1}
formulation of RHGs
Krioukov et al. 2010
, each node is endowed with two random hidden variables: similarity
(x_{i})
and popularity
(\kappa_{i})
. The similarity space is a circle of radius
R_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}=n/2\pi
and
x_{i}=R_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\theta_{i}
, where
\theta_{i}\sim U([0,2\pi])
(two nodes close on the circle are “similar”). The radius scales with
n
so as to maintain the node density on the circle to be one. The popularity hidden variables of nodes are their expected degrees
\kappa_{i}\sim\rho(\kappa|\bar{\kappa},\gamma)
, where

\rho(\kappa|\bar{\kappa},\gamma)=(\gamma-1)\kappa_{0}^{\gamma-1}\kappa^{-\gamma},

(28)

is the Pareto distribution with mean
\bar{\kappa}
, power-law exponent
\gamma
, and support
\kappa\in[\kappa_{0},\infty)
, where
\kappa_{0}=\bar{\kappa}(\gamma-2)/(\gamma-1)
.

Once the hidden variables are assigned, two nodes are connected with the grand canonical Fermi-Dirac connection probability,

\displaystyle p_{ij}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{1+\left(\dfrac{d_{ij}}{\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)^{\beta}}=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{\beta(\varepsilon_{ij}-\mu)}},\text{ where}

(29)

\displaystyle d_{ij}

\displaystyle=R_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\Delta\theta_{ij},\quad\mu=\log(\hat{\mu}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{ij}

\displaystyle=\log\left(\dfrac{d_{ij}}{\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)

are the link energies in the model. The inverse temperature
\beta>1
is the same as in the
\mathbb{H}^{2}
formulation, while the chemical potential

\hat{\mu}=\dfrac{\beta}{2\pi\bar{\kappa}}\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)

(30)

fixes the expected average degree in the model to
\bar{\kappa}>0
at
n\to\infty
. For completeness, we re-derive this observation in Appendix
F
.

The
\mathbb{H}^{2}
and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
formulations of RHGs are equivalent because they have the same distributions of hidden variables and connection probabilities. Indeed, the angular coordinates in both formulations come from the same uniform distribution, while the radial coordinate and expected degree distributions
\rho(r)
and
\rho(\kappa)
in Eqs. (
23
,
28
) can be obtained from each other via the following change of variables:

\kappa=\kappa_{0}e^{(R_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}-r)/2},

(31)

which maps the connection probability in Eq. (
29
) to the one in Eq. (
25
)
Krioukov et al. 2010
.

This change of variables leads to the following relationship between the link energies in the two formulations:

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{ij}=2\left[\varepsilon_{ij}+\ln{\left(\dfrac{n}{\tau}\right)}\right],

(32)

where
\tau=\pi\kappa_{0}^{2}\hat{\mu}^{2}
. Consequently, the graph energy and expected energy are related via

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}(G)

\displaystyle=2\left[\varepsilon(G)+\ln{\left(\dfrac{n}{\tau}\right)}m(G)\right],

(33)

\displaystyle\bar{\tilde{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=2\left[\bar{\varepsilon}+\ln{\left(\dfrac{n}{\tau}\right)}\bar{m}\right].

All the analytical and simulation results reported in this paper are obtained using the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
formulation of the RHG model since it is easier to work with. In particular, to simulate RHGs, we take their size
n
and parameters
\bar{\kappa}>0
,
\beta>1
, and
\gamma>2
as input, and proceed as described the second half of this section.

Appendix F
Self-averaging of RHGs’ sufficient statistics

In this section, we derive the closed-form expressions for the mean values of the number of links
m(G)=\sum_{i<j}G_{ij}
, and energy
\varepsilon(G)=\sum_{i<j}\varepsilon_{ij}G_{ij}
of the random hyperbolic graphs (Appendix
E
) in the thermodynamic limit
n\to\infty
. We also show that the distributions of these random variables are self-averaging—their coefficient of variation goes to zero in the thermodynamic limit for any value of the degree exponent
\gamma>2
.

F.1
Average number of links
(\bar{m})

The average number of links in the model is,

\displaystyle\bar{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i<j}G_{ij}\right],

(34)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}],

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}m(\kappa_{i},x_{i})\rho(\kappa_{i})dx_{i}d\kappa_{i},

\displaystyle\text{where}~m(\kappa_{i},x_{i})

\displaystyle=(n-1)\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}p_{ij}\dfrac{\rho(\kappa_{j})}{n}dx_{j}d\kappa_{j}.

m(\kappa_{i},x_{i})
is the expected number of links attached to node
``i"
with hidden variable values
(\kappa_{i},x_{i})
. Due to the model being rotationally invariant, we can set
x_{i}=0
without loss of generality, i.e.,
m(\kappa_{i},0)=m(\kappa_{i},x_{i})
, which in the limit
n\to\infty
is,

\displaystyle m(\kappa_{i},0)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{1+\left(\dfrac{x_{j}}{\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)^{\beta}}\rho(\kappa_{j})dx_{j}d\kappa_{j},

(35)

\displaystyle=2\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\bar{\kappa}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right).

Hence, the average number of links in the model is,

\displaystyle\bar{m}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n}{2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}m(\kappa_{i},0)\rho(\kappa_{i})d\kappa_{i},

(36)

\displaystyle=n\hat{\mu}\bar{\kappa}^{2}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right).

By requiring that
\bar{\kappa}=\bar{k}=2\bar{m}/n
in the last equation, we get the expression for the chemical potential in Eq. (
30
).

F.2
Average energy
(\bar{\varepsilon})

Similarly, the average energy of the model is,

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i<j}G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\right],

(37)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}],

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\varepsilon(\kappa_{i},x_{i})\rho(\kappa_{i})dx_{i}d\kappa_{i},

\displaystyle\text{where}~\varepsilon(x_{i},\kappa_{i})

\displaystyle=(n-1)\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}p_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\dfrac{\rho(\kappa_{j})}{n}dx_{j}d\kappa_{j}.

Once again, due to the model being rotationally invariant, we set
x_{i}=0
without loss of generality, i.e.,
\varepsilon(\kappa_{i},x_{i})=\varepsilon(\kappa_{i},0)
, which in the
n\to\infty
limit results in,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(\kappa_{i},0)

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\dfrac{\log\left(\dfrac{x_{j}}{\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)}{1+\left(\dfrac{x_{j}}{\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)^{\beta}}\rho(\kappa_{j})d\kappa_{j}dx_{j},

(38)

\displaystyle=2\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\bar{\kappa}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\dfrac{\pi}{\beta^{2}}\left(\beta\log(\hat{\mu})-\pi\cot\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right).

Finally,

\displaystyle\bar{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n}{2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\varepsilon(0,\kappa_{i})\rho(\kappa_{i})d\kappa_{i},

(39)

\displaystyle=n\hat{\mu}(\bar{\kappa})^{2}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\dfrac{\pi}{\beta^{2}}\left(\beta\log(\hat{\mu})-\pi\cot\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right),

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n}{2}\bar{\kappa}\left(\log\left(\dfrac{\beta\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{\beta}\right)}{2\pi\bar{\kappa}}\right)-\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\cot\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right),

where in the last step, we have used the expression for the chemical potential in Eq. (
30
).

F.3
Asymptotics of the coefficients of variation of

m
and
\varepsilon

To prove that the distribution of the number of links,
m(G)
and energy,
\varepsilon(G)
of the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
model is self-averaging, we need to show that their respective coefficients of variation
C^{(m)}_{V}(n)=\sqrt{\text{Var}(m)}/\mathbb{E}[m]
and
C^{(\varepsilon)}_{V}(n)=\sqrt{\text{Var}(\varepsilon)}/\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]
go to zero in the limit
n\to\infty
. In Sec.
F.1
and Sec.
F.2
we have already calculated the expected values of the number of links and energy, showing that
\bar{m}
and
\bar{\varepsilon}
both scale as
\mathcal{O}(n)
, for some fixed value of
\bar{\kappa}
. We next need to find the variance of the random variables
m(G)
and
\varepsilon(G)
.

The variance of the number of links is,

\displaystyle\text{Var}[m]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\text{Var}[G_{ij}]+2\sum_{(i,j)<(k,l)}\text{Cov}[G_{ij},G_{kl}].

(40)

The first term scales as,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\text{Var}[G_{ij}]

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}]^{2}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=C_{1}n\cdot(1+o(1)),

\displaystyle\text{where}~C_{1}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mu}\bar{\kappa}^{2}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right),

since

, are simply zero because
G_{ij}
and
G_{kl}
are independent random variables. To that end,

\displaystyle 2\sum_{(i,j)<(k,l)}\text{Cov}[G_{ij},G_{kl}]

\displaystyle=6\binom{n}{3}\text{Cov}[G_{ij},G_{ik}],

(42)

\displaystyle=n^{3}\text{Cov}[G_{ij},G_{ik}]\cdot\left(1+o(1)\right),

\displaystyle\text{where}~\text{Cov}[G_{ij},G_{ik}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}G_{ik}]-\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}]\mathbb{E}[G_{ik}].

Once we get the asymptotics of the term
\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}G_{ik}]
we are done, since
\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}]\mathbb{E}[G_{ik}]=(4C_{1}^{2}/n^{2})\cdot(1+o(1))
. Notice that from the tower rule we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}G_{ik}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa_{i},x_{i}}[\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}G_{ik}|\kappa_{i},x_{i}]],

(43)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa_{i},x_{i}}[\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}|\kappa_{i},x_{i}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[G_{ik}|\kappa_{i},x_{i}]],

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa_{i},x_{i}}[\Phi(\kappa_{i},x_{i})^{2}],

\displaystyle\text{where}~\Phi(\kappa_{i},x_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}p_{ij}\dfrac{\rho(\kappa_{j})}{n}dx_{j}d\kappa_{j}.

Once again using the fact that the model is rotationally invariant, we set
x_{i}=0
without loss of generality, i.e.,
\Phi(\kappa_{i},x_{i})=\Phi(\kappa_{i},0)
. For
n\gg 1
, we get,

\Phi(\kappa_{i},0)=\dfrac{2\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\bar{\kappa}}{n}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right).

(44)

Further,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}G_{ik}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa_{i},x_{i}}[\Phi(\kappa_{i},0)^{2}],

(45)

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\dfrac{2\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\bar{\kappa}}{n}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right)^{2}\rho(\kappa_{i})d\kappa_{i},

\displaystyle=C_{2}\dfrac{\bar{\kappa^{2}}}{n^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{where}~C_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{2\hat{\mu}\bar{\kappa}\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right)^{2}.

Combining these results and plugging them back into Eq. (
40
),

\text{Var}[m]=C_{1}\left(1-4C_{1}\right)n+C_{2}\bar{\kappa^{2}}n.

(46)

For any
\gamma>2
, the first moment of the Pareto distribution is
\bar{\kappa}=\mathcal{O}(1)
but the second moment scales as,

\displaystyle\bar{\kappa^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\kappa_{0}n^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}}}\kappa^{2}\rho(\kappa)d\kappa

(47)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\gamma-1}{3-\gamma}\,\kappa_{0}^{2}\,n^{\frac{3-\gamma}{\gamma-1}},&2<\gamma<3,\\[6.0pt]
\kappa_{0}^{2}\,\ln n,&\gamma=3,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-3}\,\kappa_{0}^{2},&\gamma>3.\end{cases}

Hence, depending on the value of
\gamma>2
, the scaling of the coefficients of variation of the distribution of the number of links,
C^{(m)}_{V}(n)=\sqrt{\text{Var}(m)}/\mathbb{E}[m]
, in the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
model is given by,

C_{V}^{(m)}(n)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\sqrt{C_{2}(\gamma-1)/(3-\gamma)}\,\kappa_{0}}{C_{1}}\;n^{-\frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma-1}}\cdot(1+o(1)),&2<\gamma<3,\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{\sqrt{C_{2}}\,\kappa_{0}}{C_{1}}\;\sqrt{\dfrac{\ln(n)}{n}}\cdot(1+o(1)),&\gamma=3,\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{\sqrt{C_{1}(1-4C_{1})+C_{2}\kappa_{0}^{2}(\gamma-1)/(\gamma-3)}}{C_{1}}\;\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot(1+o(1)),&\gamma>3.\end{cases}

(48)

The same procedure above is repeated verbatim to derive the asymptotics of the variance of energy,

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\text{Var}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}]+2\sum_{(i,j)<(k,l)}\text{Cov}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij},G_{kl}\varepsilon_{kl}].

(49)

Consider the first term,

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\text{Var}\left[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{2}\left(\mathbb{E}[\left(G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\right)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}]^{2}\right).

(50)

The expression for
\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}]
is calculated in Sec.
F.2
; on the other hand,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{E}[\left(G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\right)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},x_{i})\rho(\kappa_{i})dx_{i}d\kappa_{i},

(51)

\displaystyle\text{where}~\varepsilon^{\prime}(x_{i},\kappa_{i})

\displaystyle=(n-1)\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}p_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}^{2}\dfrac{\rho(\kappa_{j})}{n}dx_{j}d\kappa_{j}.

Once again, using the fact that the model is rotationally invariant, we set
x_{i}=0
without loss of generality, i.e.,
\varepsilon^{\prime}(x_{i},\kappa_{i})=\varepsilon^{\prime}(0,\kappa_{i})
, which in the
n\to\infty
limit results in,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}(0,\kappa_{i})

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\dfrac{\log^{2}\left(\dfrac{x_{j}}{\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)}{1+\left(\dfrac{x_{j}}{\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}}\right)^{\beta}}\rho(\kappa_{j})d\kappa_{j}dx_{j},

(52)

\displaystyle=2\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}\bar{\kappa}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\bigg[\left(\log(\hat{\mu})-\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\cot\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right)^{2}\bigg]=\dfrac{2\kappa_{i}C_{1}^{\prime}}{\bar{\kappa}}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{E}[\left(G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\right)^{2}]

\displaystyle=nC_{1}^{\prime}\cdot(1+o(1)),

(53)

which is the asymptotic scaling of the first term in Eq. (
49
). Following a similar argument as in the previous case, the asymptotics of the second term in Eq. (
49
) is,

\displaystyle 2\sum_{(i,j)<(k,l)}\text{Cov}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij},G_{kl}\varepsilon_{kl}]

\displaystyle=n^{3}\big(\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}G_{ik}\varepsilon_{ik}]

(54)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}]\mathbb{E}[G_{ik}\varepsilon_{ik}]\big),

\displaystyle\text{where},~\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}]\mathbb{E}[G_{ik}\varepsilon_{ik}]

\displaystyle=(C_{2}^{\prime}/n^{2})\cdot(1+o(1));

\displaystyle\left(\bar{\kappa}\log\left(\hat{\mu}\right)-\bar{\kappa}\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\cot\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=C_{2}^{\prime}.

Further,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}G_{ik}\varepsilon_{ik}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\kappa_{i},x_{i}}[\Phi^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},x_{i})^{2}],

(55)

\displaystyle\text{where}~\Phi^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},x_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{-n/2}^{n/2}\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}p_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\dfrac{\rho(\kappa_{j})}{n}dx_{j}d\kappa_{j}.

But
\Phi^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},x_{i})=\Phi^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},0)
, which in the setting
n\gg 1
is,

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},0)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{2\hat{\mu}\kappa_{i}}{n}\bar{\kappa}\csc\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\dfrac{\pi}{\beta^{2}}\left(\beta\log(\hat{\mu})-\pi\cot\left(\dfrac{\pi}{\beta}\right)\right),

(56)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\kappa_{i}\sqrt{C_{2}^{\prime}}}{n\bar{\kappa}},

leading to the result,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}G_{ik}\varepsilon_{ik}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa_{0}}^{\infty}\Phi^{\prime}(\kappa_{i},0)^{2}\rho(\kappa_{i})d\kappa_{i}

(57)

\displaystyle=C_{2}^{\prime}\dfrac{\bar{\kappa^{2}}}{\bar{\kappa}^{2}n^{2}}.

Plugging all these results into Eq. (
49
) we have,

\displaystyle\text{Var}[\varepsilon]

\displaystyle=n\left(C_{1}^{\prime}-C_{2}^{\prime}\right)+nC_{2}^{\prime}\dfrac{\bar{\kappa^{2}}}{\bar{\kappa}^{2}}

(58)

Hence, depending on the value of
\gamma>2
, the scaling of the coefficients of variation of the distribution of the energy,
C^{(\varepsilon)}_{V}(n)=\sqrt{\text{Var}(\varepsilon)}/\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]
, in the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
model is given by,

|C_{V}^{(\varepsilon)}(n)|=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2\kappa_{0}}{\bar{\kappa}}\sqrt{\dfrac{\gamma-1}{3-\gamma}}\;n^{-\frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma-1}}\cdot(1+o(1)),&2<\gamma<3,\\[12.0pt]
\dfrac{2\kappa_{0}}{\bar{\kappa}}\;\sqrt{\dfrac{\ln(n)}{n}}\cdot(1+o(1)),&\gamma=3,\\[12.0pt]
2\sqrt{\dfrac{\left(C_{1}^{\prime}-C_{2}^{\prime}\right)+C_{2}^{\prime}\frac{\kappa_{0}^{2}(\gamma-1)}{\bar{\kappa}^{2}(\gamma-3)}}{C_{2}^{\prime}}}\;\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot(1+o(1)),&\gamma>3.\end{cases}

(59)

We conclude that the coefficients of variation of both the distribution of the number of links and energy scale as

C_{V}(n)=\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\text{min}\left\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma-1}\right\}}(\ln(n))^{\frac{\mathbbm{1}\{\gamma=3\}}{2}}\right)

(60)

and hence are self-averaging distributions for any
\gamma>2
since
\lim_{n\to\infty}C_{V}(n)=0
.

Appendix G
Deterministic hyperbolic graphs (DHGs)

In this section, we outline the exact details involved in the deterministic construction of typical networks in the random hyperbolic graph (RHG) model (Appendix
E
), which we call
deterministic hyperbolic graphs
(DHGs).

The input parameters are the same as in the RHG model:
n
,
\bar{\kappa}
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
. The rest is different. First, hidden variables,
(\kappa_{i},x_{i})
, are assigned to each node deterministically via the two-dimensional hypercanonical derandomization in Sec.
III.2
. This derandomization returns deterministic coordinates
(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})
on the unit square, which we then use to obtain the RHG hidden variables. We set the similarity space coordinate to
x_{i}=n\tilde{x}_{i}
, while the expected degree
\kappa_{i}
is obtained via the inverse CDF of the Pareto distribution applied to
\tilde{y}_{i}
:

\kappa_{i}=\bar{\kappa}\frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma-1}(1-\tilde{y}_{i})^{\frac{1}{1-\gamma}}.

(61)

Once the hidden variables are determined, the value of the chemical potential
\hat{\mu}
in Eq. (
29
) is obtained by finding the root of the equation

\sum_{i<j}p_{ij}-\dfrac{n\bar{\kappa}}{2}=0

(62)

numerically using Newton’s method with analytically calculated derivative of the objective function and absolute tolerance of
\tau=10^{-8}
. This ensures that the DHG exactly matches the desired value of one sufficient statistic, the number of edges
m^{\star}=\lfloor n\bar{\kappa}/2\rceil
. Another option would be to use the analytical thermodynamic-limit expression for
\hat{\mu}
in Eq. (
30
), but the convergence of the number of edges
m
and energy
\varepsilon
of DHGs to the ensemble average of RHGs would be slower in this case. Another reason to avoid this option is that upon hypercanonical derandomization, we end up dealing with a unique single instance of the grand canonical ensemble, so we can use it to find the exact value of the chemical potential.

Finally, having this grand canonical ensemble in place, in which all the pairwise connection probabilities are now determined, we use the grand canonical derandomization procedure in Sec.
III.1
to deterministically place all the
m^{\star}
edges.

We make the software package implementing the described DHG construction procedure available at
Laber 2025a
.

Appendix H
Real-world networks

H.1
Parameter estimation

To compare real-world networks with the corresponding most typical networks in the deterministic hyperbolic graph (DHG) model, we first have to measure the value of the model parameters—the numbers of nodes
n
and links
m
, the degree distribution exponent
\gamma
, and the inverse temperature
\beta
—in the real-world networks. We do this as follows.

Determining the number of nodes and links is trivial. To measure the degree distribution exponent, we follow the prescription in
Voitalov et al. 2019
. We employ the
tail-estimation
software package
Voitalov 2025
, and use three different estimators of the extreme value exponent
\xi
, which is related to the degree exponent by
\gamma=1+1/\xi
. These estimators are the Kernel estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{K}}
, Hill estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{H}}
, and Moment estimator
\hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{M}}
. We use the value of
\gamma
determined with the Moments estimator to construct networks in the DHG model.

To determine the inverse temperature
\beta
for fixed values of
n
,
m
, and
\gamma
, we first compute the average clustering in a real-world network, excluding nodes of degrees
<2
:

\bar{c}=\frac{1}{|V_{k\geq 2}|}\sum_{i\in V_{k\geq 2}}c_{i},

(63)

where
V_{k\geq 2}=\{i\in V:k_{i}\geq 2\}
is the set of nodes with degree
k
at least two,
c_{i}=t_{i}/\binom{k_{i}}{2}
is the clustering coefficient of node
i
,
k_{i}
is its degree, and
t_{i}
is the number of triangles it is in. Since the DHG is a deterministic model it generates a single graph with a fixed value of average clustering
\bar{c}_{\mathrm{DHG}}(n,m,\gamma,\beta)
for any combination of its parameters. We then use Brent’s method
Brent 2013
, as implemented in
SciPy

Virtanen et al. 2020
(v1.16.1) to find the root
\beta^{\star}
of

f(\beta)=\bar{c}-\bar{c}_{\mathrm{DHG}}(n,\bar{k},\gamma,\beta)

(64)

in the interval
[1,10]
and treat it as an estimate
\hat{\beta}=\beta^{\star}
of the inverse temperature. Since clustering is an increasing function of
\beta
, if we do not find a root in this interval and the empirical clustering is larger than the clustering in the model at
\beta=10
, we set
\hat{\beta}=10
. If we do not find a root in the interval and the empirical clustering is lower than the clustering in the model for
\beta=1
, we set
\hat{\beta}=1
.

(k>1)

H.3
Network visualization

We also develop an algorithm inspired by
LaNet-vi

Alvarez-hamelin et al. 2005
;
Beiró et al. 2008
;
Alvarez-Hamelin et al. 2011
to visualize the largest connected component of all networks. Note, that all quantities in this section are computed after subsetting a given network to its largest connected component. The visualization algorithm assigns a radial
r_{i}
and angular
\varphi_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
coordinate to each node
i
as follows.

First, we determine the coreness
u_{i}
of node
i
in the
k
-core decomposition. We encode this value using the color
Van
Der Velden 2020
of a vertex. Nodes of the same value of coreness
u
form a shell
Q_{u}=\{i\in V:u_{i}=u\}
. We also compute the adjusted coreness
u^{\prime}_{i}
of node
i
as a convex combination of its own coreness
u_{i}
and the coreness of its neighbors,

u_{i}^{\prime}=\varepsilon u_{i}+(1-\varepsilon)\frac{1}{k_{i}}\sum_{j\in V}G_{ij}u_{j},

(65)

where
k_{i}
is the degree of node
i
,
G_{ij}
the adjacency matrix, and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
a parameter of the algorithm that governs the relative influence of a nodes’ coreness
u_{i}
and the average coreness of its neighbors on the adjusted coreness. We set
\varepsilon=0.85
for our visualization.

The radial coordinate
r_{i}
of node
i
is then

r_{i}=(r_{1}-r_{0})\frac{u^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}-u^{\prime}_{i}}{u^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}-u^{\prime}_{\mathrm{min}}}+r_{0},

(66)

where
r_{1}\geq r_{0}>0
are constants governing the maximum and minimum radial coordinate in the visualization, and
u^{\prime}_{\min}=\min\{u^{\prime}_{i}:i\in V\}
and
u^{\prime}_{\max}=\max\{u^{\prime}_{i}:i\in V\}
are smallest and largest values of adjusted coreness.

The angular coordinate
\varphi_{i}
of node
i
is determined by constructing the node-induced subgraphs
S_{u}=(Q_{u},(Q_{u}\times Q_{u})\cap E)
of a given shell
Q_{u}
. We denote the
N_{u}
connected components of
S_{u}
as
C_{u,j}
where
j\in\{1,2,\dots,N_{u}\}
enumerate them in arbitrary order. Each connected component
C_{u,j}
is then assigned an angular segment
\Phi_{u,j}
of arc length proportional to their relative size:

\Phi_{u,j}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\frac{2\pi}{N_{u}}\frac{|C_{u,i}|}{|Q_{u}|},\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{2\pi}{N_{u}}\frac{|C_{u,i}|}{|Q_{u}|}\right).

(67)

Finally, the angular coordinate
\varphi_{i}
of node
i
belonging to connected component
C_{u_{i},j_{i}}
is chosen uniformly at random in
\Phi_{u,j}
, i.e.,
\varphi_{i}\sim\mathrm{U}[\Phi_{u_{i},j_{i}}]
.

All nodes also have sizes
s_{i}
, which grows with the logarithm of their degree
k_{i}
:

s_{i}=(s_{1}-s_{0})+\frac{\log k_{i}-\log k_{\mathrm{min}}}{\log k_{\mathrm{max}}-\log k_{\mathrm{min}}}+s_{0}

(68)

where
s_{1}\geq s_{0}>0
are constants that govern the maximum and minimum node size respectively, and
k_{\mathrm{max}},k_{\mathrm{min}}
are the maximum and minimum degree.

We only show a subset of all edges. Depending on network size and density we randomly select a subset of edges. Our visualizations retain between
0.25\%
and
100\%
of edges.

We make the code implementing the described graph visualization procedure available at
Laber 2025b
.
