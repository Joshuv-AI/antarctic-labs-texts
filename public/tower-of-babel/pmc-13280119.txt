Death and ICU Admission Associated with Sars-Cov-2 Variants in Immunocompromised Hospitalised Individuals: A National Observational Study
Authors: Antônio José Cordeiro Mattos, Francisco Jose Nigro Mazon, Lucas Ferreira Theotonio dos Santos, Juares Ednaldo Romero Bianco, Frederico Monfardini, Gustavo Prado dos Santos, Lívia Dias de Sousa, Mariana Martins Sasse, Luiz Vicente Rizzo, Henrique Andrade R. Fonseca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIndividuals who are immunocompromised (IC) with COVID-19 have consistently been associated with worse clinical outcomes than individuals who are non-immunocompromised (non-IC). The aim of this study was to describe and compare in-hospital outcomes between individuals who are IC and non-IC hospitalised with COVID-19, with a particular focus on mortality across periods defined by the predominant circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants.MethodsThis is an observational, retrospective, descriptive study based on a secondary database from Brazil's Unified Health System. Patients hospitalised with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 were included. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the cohort; categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the association between immunocompromised status and two key outcomes: in-hospital mortality and ICU admission, stratified by dominant SARS-CoV-2 variant. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsBetween March 2020 and July 2023, 361,898 hospitalised COVID-19 individuals were identified, with 2.07% (n = 7484) classified as IC. Those individuals consistently exhibited higher odds of death and ICU admission across all SARS-CoV-2 variant periods. The odds of in-hospital mortality ranged from OR: 1.20 (95% CI 1.04–1.39) during the original (WT) variant to OR: 2.39 (95% CI 1.54–3.71) during the Omicron XBB subvariant period; however, the absolute number of cases and deaths for XBB was small, and this OR might have limited clinical significance. The second highest risk for mortality was observed during the Delta wave (OR: 2.16; 95% CI 1.94–2.41), with a much greater number of cases and clinical significance.ConclusionImmunocompromised status was independently associated with higher odds of adverse hospital outcomes, including ICU admission and in-hospital mortality, regardless of the circulating SARS-CoV-2 variant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01355-y.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by SARS-CoV-2, has resulted in unprecedented global morbidity and mortality. Between 2020 and 2022, more than 349 million cases were confirmed worldwide, with approximately 5.6 million deaths [1, 2]. Among affected populations, individuals who are immunocompromised (IC), including those receiving immunosuppressive therapies, patients with cancer, organ transplant recipients, and individuals with untreated HIV, have demonstrated substantially higher risks of disease progression, admission to intensive care units (ICU), and mortality [3, 4].

As SARS-CoV-2 evolved, the emergence of variants such as B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron altered the epidemiological dynamics of the pandemic [5, 6]. Each variant introduced differences in transmissibility, immune evasion, and virulence, thereby directly influencing hospitalisation and mortality rates [7–11]. For instance, the Delta variant was associated with increased disease severity and higher rates of admission to the ICU, whereas the Omicron variant, despite greater transmissibility, was associated with comparatively lower mortality, particularly among vaccinated individuals [7, 9, 11]. Vaccination campaigns played a crucial role in mitigating these outcomes by reducing severe disease and the burden on health care systems across diverse populations [12–16].

Brazil has played a pivotal role in the global COVID-19 response, both as a major epicentre of infection and as a leader in vaccine deployment and variant surveillance [17]. The country’s robust public health infrastructure enabled the early detection of emerging variants, thereby contributing to global mitigation strategies [24–26]. However, hospitalised patients in the country faced challenges that varied according to the circulating variant, with marked differences in rates of admission to the ICU and mortality across successive waves of the pandemic [27–30].

Overall, individuals who were IC experienced poorer clinical outcomes, with a > 30% higher risk of in-hospital mortality compared with individuals who were non-immunocompromised (non-IC), alongside higher rates of admission to the ICU and longer hospital and intensive care unit lengths of stay (LoS) [18]. Despite the global reduction in COVID-19 cases associated with vaccination and population-level immunity, concerns persist regarding the safety of individuals who are IC, whose vaccine responses may be suboptimal, and who remain at risk of adverse outcomes, including higher mortality and a greater need for intensive care [19].

The existing literature is constrained by small sample sizes and a predominant focus on mortality, leaving substantial gaps in knowledge regarding other in-hospital outcomes and the impact of different SARS-CoV-2 variants. Elucidating the dynamics of the pandemic in this population, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, is essential to inform more effective strategies for mitigating infectious disease threats, especially in the context of limited recent data. Given the heterogeneity of outcomes observed across variants, a clearer understanding of these differences is critical for future pandemic preparedness. Accordingly, this study aimed to estimate and compare the odds of in-hospital death associated with different SARS-CoV-2 variants among individuals who were immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised hospitalised in Brazil during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study Design and Data Curation

This observational, retrospective study was based on secondary data from Brazil’s Unified Health System (Sistema Único de Saúde [SUS]). Data were collected between January 2021 and December 2023 from three public databases within the DATASUS system: the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome database (SRAG), the Hospital Information System (SIH), and the DATASUS immunisation database [20]. All data had been previously de-identified, ensuring that no sensitive participant information could be traced. The data pertained exclusively to the hospitalisation episode related to COVID-19.

The study population represents a substantial sample of individuals diagnosed with COVID-19 and recorded within the Brazilian public health registry system. Immunocompromised status was defined according to prespecified clinical conditions detailed in the Supplementary Material (Supplement 1) and a previous publication [21], including primary immunodeficiency, secondary immunodeficiency, high-dose or long-term moderate-dose corticosteroid treatment, end-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis, organ transplants in the last 5 years, stem cell transplants, solid tumours in the last 5 years, haematological malignancies in the last 5 years, and HIV/AIDS. End-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis was included because advanced kidney disease is associated with uraemia-related acquired immune dysfunction, characterised by impaired innate and adaptive immunity, increased susceptibility to infection, and reduced responsiveness to vaccination. These disturbances are well described in chronic kidney disease, particularly in patients receiving dialysis [22–26].

Outcomes Definitions

In this analysis, three primary clinical outcomes were assessed: admission to intensive care units, in-hospital death, and a composite of these outcomes. These outcomes were analysed based on the variant classification, which was determined by the dominant circulating SARS-CoV-2 variant in Brazil during each study period, according to genomic surveillance data reported through GISAID [27] and not on individual-level sequencing; outcomes were then stratified by immunocompromised status and vaccination status. Participants who had received at least two doses of a COVID-19 vaccine before symptom onset were classified as immunised. Those who experienced any of the outcomes before completing the vaccination schedule, or who remained unvaccinated, were categorised as non-immunised. To ensure analytical robustness, individuals with missing data on vaccination status, immunocompromised status, or clinical outcomes were excluded from the final analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted, with categorical variables summarised as counts and percentages. Age is presented as the mean in years with the corresponding standard deviation (SD), whereas LoS is reported as the median with interquartile range and was further categorised into predefined hospitalisation intervals to facilitate interpretation.

Logistic regression models were used to examine the association between circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants and the likelihood of admission to the ICU or in-hospital death. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Data visualisation included bar charts and block plots to illustrate the primary outcomes.

We did not undertake multivariable regression analyses for ICU admission or mortality because, despite the large national database, the effective analytical sample was substantially reduced after restriction of hospitalised patients who were IC and stratification by variant-predominant periods. In several strata, the number of outcome events was insufficient relative to the number of candidate covariates from the linked national datasets, making adjusted models potentially unstable and susceptible to overfitting.

Among IC individuals, in-hospital mortality attributable to COVID-19 was estimated at 19%. According to national estimates, IC individuals comprise approximately 1% of the Brazilian population [28, 29]. The DATASUS database contains approximately 60,000–70,000 records of COVID-19-related hospitalisations in Brazil. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, precision estimates were calculated for different expected scenarios. Assuming a hospital mortality of 19% from COVID-19 among IC individuals, a sample size of 6000 was expected to yield a precision of 2%, with a corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) of 18–20%. P < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software.

Compliance with Ethical Standards

The datasets underwent quality validation by removing duplicate records and assessing data consistency and completeness, in accordance with the ethical principles established by the Brazilian National Health Council. As the study relied exclusively on publicly accessible, anonymized databases, written informed consent was not required under national legislation and institutional regulations, and ethics committee approval was waived. Data anonymization ensured the protection of sensitive information about the individuals in the dataset.

Results

Clinical Characteristics

Between March 2020 and July 2023, 361,898 patients hospitalised with COVID-19 were identified, of whom 2.07% (n = 7484) were IC. The mean age was 59 years, with no significant difference between the IC and non-IC groups; 58.7% of patients were aged 18–64 years, and 16.7% were aged 70–79 years. The mean LoS was 9 (SD 4–12) days, with similar distributions across groups. The most common presenting features were low oxygen saturation (70%) and dyspnoea (78%). Comorbidities were more frequent among IC patients than among non-IC patients, including chronic liver (4.2% vs 0.7%), neurological (6.1% vs 3.3%), pulmonary (8.2% vs 3.3%), kidney (12.7% vs 3.1%), and haematological diseases (5.0% vs 0.5%) (Table 1).

SARS-CoV-2 Variant Distribution for ICU and Mortality

The distribution of SARS-CoV-2 variants among hospitalised patients differed in both prevalence and temporal sequence between the IC and non-IC groups. The Gamma variant was the most frequent, accounting for 32.7% of IC cases and 45.0% of non-IC cases. Delta was the second most common variant, detected in 17.8% of IC individuals and 22.4% of non-IC individuals.

Notably, Omicron, including the original strain and the XBB sublineage, was more frequent among patients who were IC than among those who were non-IC (17.7% vs 7.8%), with XBB identified in 1.2% and 0.4% of cases, respectively. The B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33 lineages were identified in 21.4% of IC and 17.8% of non-IC cases, while the original wild-type variant accounted for 10.4% and 7.1% of hospitalisations, respectively (Fig. 1).Fig. 1SARS-CoV-2 variants were classified according to the predominant circulating variant by period. For the original wild-type (WT) variant, in-hospital mortality was 45.1% among individuals who were non-IC and 49.7% among patients who were IC. Admission to ICU occurred in 39.4% and 44.2% of cases, respectively, with the composite outcome of death or ICU admission observed in 57.7% of non-IC and 63.0% of IC patients. Among patients infected with the B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33 lineages, mortality was 33.6% in non-IC individuals and 49.6% in IC patients. ICU admission rates were 37.3% and 42.7%, with composite outcomes of 49.0% and 61.6%, respectively. The Gamma variant, the most prevalent (n = 161,897), was associated with mortality rates of 37.9% in non-IC and 54% for IC individuals. ICU admission occurred in 36.8% and 41.3% of cases, with composite outcomes of 50.4% and 62.7%, respectively. For the Delta variant, mortality was 30.6% among non-IC individuals and 48.7% the IC patients. ICU admission rates were 44.2% and 50.9%, with composite outcomes of 50.5% and 65%, respectively. Among Omicron cases, in-hospital death occurred in 36.3% of non-IC patients and 50.8% of IC patients. ICU admission rates were 34.4% and 35.6%, with composite outcomes of 50.0% and 59.6%, respectively. The Omicron XBB subvariant accounted for 0.37% of cases (n = 1344), limiting statistical power. Nevertheless, mortality was 27.1% in non-IC individuals and 47.1% in IC patients; ICU admission occurred in 27.6% and 34.5%, and composite outcomes were observed in 40.7% and 54.0% of cases, respectively (Table 2). COVID-19 Vaccination and ICU Admission and Mortality

Among all hospitalised individuals, 9.8% (n = 35,463) had received two or more doses of a COVID-19 vaccine before admission. Within this vaccinated cohort, 17.5% (n = 1310) were IC, nearly twice the proportion observed among vaccinated individuals who were non-IC (9.6%, n = 34,153). No vaccinated cases were recorded during periods dominated by the WT and B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33 variants; nevertheless, IC individuals consistently exhibited higher rates of ICU admission and mortality than individuals who were non-IC.

Among vaccinated individuals infected with the Gamma, Delta, Omicron, and Omicron XBB variants, IC status was consistently associated with poorer clinical outcomes, including higher mortality (e.g., 70.3% vs 55.5% for Gamma) and increased ICU admission (e.g., 50.0% vs 37.6% for Gamma). Although some between-group differences were modest (e.g., ICU admission during Omicron predominance: 37.5% in individuals who were IC vs 34.8% in non-IC), the direction of effect was uniform across variants. Despite the limited sample size, the Omicron XBB subgroup also showed worse outcomes among IC individuals. Collectively, these findings highlight the persistent vulnerability associated with IC status despite vaccination.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that, throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, hospitalised individuals who were IC in Brazil experienced up to two-fold higher odds of death across successive SARS-CoV-2 variants than individuals who were non-IC. Notably, this association persisted over time, irrespective of the predominant circulating variant. Brazil implemented an extensive national vaccination programme during this period, coinciding with the emergence and replacement of successive SARS-CoV-2 variants; together, these dynamics might have shaped the patterns of ICU admission and mortality observed in our study [30].

IC individuals face substantially higher odds of adverse outcomes when affected by viral respiratory infections such as COVID-19. This heightened vulnerability arises from multiple, interrelated factors. First, IC individuals frequently present with a higher burden of comorbidities, reflecting the coexistence of immunocompromising conditions alongside non-immunocompromising diseases, such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [32, 33]. This multimorbidity burden adversely affects the clinical response to systemic infections by impairing the host’s capacity to mount an effective immune response and, critically, by increasing susceptibility to complications, thereby posing additional challenges for inpatient management [34, 35].

Despite presenting with broadly similar symptoms at hospital admission, IC individuals consistently exhibited lower peripheral oxygen saturation than non-IC individuals, suggesting a greater number of underlying physiological compromise that might not be readily apparent during initial clinical assessment. This finding is clinically important, as a mild or non-specific symptom profile in IC individuals should not be interpreted as indicative of lower disease severity, given their heightened risk of rapid clinical deterioration [31]. This pattern is consistent with the phenomenon of silent hypoxaemia, whereby individuals with COVID-19 may develop clinically significant hypoxia in the absence of overt dyspnoea. Moreover, peripheral oxygen saturation is a more objective and reliable marker of disease severity than subjective symptoms such as fatigue or dyspnoea, which may be chronic, under-recognised, or underreported among IC individuals [31–34].

Stratified analyses by predominant SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating in Brazil showed that, irrespective of the variant examined, including the original strain (WT), B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33 lineages, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron, IC individuals consistently had increased odds of severe outcomes, including death, ICU admission, and the composite outcomes of death or ICU admission. Notably, the Gamma variant, which predominated in Brazil in early 2021, was associated with a marked increase in severe cases and substantial strain on the health-care system, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, including individuals who were IC [35].

In the present study, among non-IC individuals, the highest mortality proportion was observed during circulation of the WT SARS-CoV-2 strain (45.1%), followed by the Gamma variant (37.9%). However, in terms of absolute numbers, the Gamma variant accounted for the most deaths (n = 60,460), followed by Delta (n = 24,216). Among IC individuals, the highest mortality proportion was also observed during the Gamma variant period (54.0%), followed by the WT strain (49.7%) and the B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33 lineages (49.6%). Consistent with this pattern, the Gamma variant accounted for the largest absolute number of deaths in this group (n = 1322), followed by the B.1.1.28 and B.1.1.33 lineages (n = 792).

This increased risk for IC individuals might reflect immune escape mechanisms, including reduced vaccine effectiveness in this population, likely attributable to the attenuated immune responses [36]. Although COVID-19 vaccines have demonstrated high effectiveness in the general population, accumulating evidence indicates that IC individuals mount diminished humoral and cellular immune responses following vaccination, thereby limiting the degree of protection conferred [37, 38].

Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, lending further support to our results. In a large prospective cohort conducted under the ISARIC WHO Clinical Characterisation Protocol in the UK, 156,552 hospitalised individuals were enrolled, of whom 14% were classified as IC. Among these individuals, 29% (n = 6499) died during hospitalisation compared with 21% of immunocompetent individuals. IC status was independently associated with in-hospital mortality, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.44 (95% CI 1.39–1.50) [39]. By comparison, in our study, the magnitude of this association varied according to the predominant SARS-CoV-2 variant, with odds ratios ranging from 1.20 to 2.39. Corroborating these findings, an analysis from the OpenSAFELY platform examining factors associated with COVID-19-related mortality identified immunosuppressed status as a strong predictor of death. In that study, individuals who were IC had a hazard ratio of 2.21 (95% CI 1.68–2.90) for COVID-19-related mortality, with transplant recipients representing the highest risk subgroup (HR 3.53; 95% CI 2.77–4.49) [22].

Therefore, the reduced vaccine effectiveness observed among immunocompromised individuals is unlikely to reflect intrinsic limitations of the vaccines themselves, but rather the severity of underlying conditions, the burden of multiple comorbidities, and the effects of ongoing immunosuppressive therapies [40, 41]. These findings underscore the critical need for tailored therapeutic strategies for this vulnerable population, including optimised vaccination approaches, such as additional doses or modified booster regimens, early initiation of antiviral therapies (e.g., nirmatrelvir-ritonavir), targeted use of monoclonal antibodies when indicated, and vigilant clinical monitoring, even among individuals presenting with initially mild symptoms. Furthermore, the consistently poorer outcomes observed across SARS-CoV-2 variants highlight the importance of developing and implementing variant-agnostic yet population-specific therapeutic and monitoring protocols during hospitalisation for this high-risk group. Continued surveillance and variant-specific research remain essential to inform future clinical practice and public health policy aimed at protecting vulnerable populations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is based on a large, nationally consolidated dataset that was systematically updated throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, enabling robust population-level analyses. Nevertheless, several limitations, inherent to the use of secondary data, warrant consideration. First, potential data entry errors, missing or misclassified variables, and structural inconsistencies may have affected data quality. Second, analytical challenges, including selection bias, information bias, residual confounding, time-related biases, and possible underreporting of clinical events, cannot be fully excluded. Third, mortality estimates for the Omicron XBB subvariant should be interpreted with caution given the the small number of deaths observed, as reflected in the wide confidence intervals. Fourth, the absence of detailed information on specific immunocompromising conditions precluded stratified analyses by cause of immunosuppression, limiting interpretation given the heterogeneity of underlying conditions and their differential impact on COVID-19 outcomes. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting the overall effect of being immunocompromised rather than risks associated with specific subgroups. Fifth, we did not perform a multivariable adjustment. Although the underlying database was large, the number of events available for modelling decreased substantially after applying the analytic restrictions and variant-period stratification. In this context, multivariable regression with numerous candidate adjustment variables could have produced unstable estimates because of sparse data, overfitting, and possible separation. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as descriptive, unadjusted comparisons rather than causal estimates of variant-specific severity. Sixth, a more detailed analysis of vaccination effects across variant-predominant periods was not pursued because the proportion of fully vaccinated immunocompromised individuals before hospitalization was low, and further stratification in this selected hospitalized cohort could yield a sparse data bias, temporal confounding, and potentially misleading inferences.

Despite these limitations, our results provide valuable evidence from a large, nationally representative dataset, elucidating patterns of ICU admission and in-hospital mortality between immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals across successive SARS-CoV-2 variant periods.

Although variant-specific severity has been primarily studied in the general population, IC individuals may have experienced these changes differently because of their higher baseline risk of severe disease. Accordingly, examining whether successive variant-predominant periods were associated with clinically meaningful differences in severe outcomes in this group remains relevant in the post-pandemic era. These analyses moved beyond descriptive epidemiology to assess whether population-level reductions in disease severity were mirrored in immunocompromised populations, while informing risk stratification, vaccination policy, early treatment, and health system preparedness. In this context, our national cohort provides an opportunity to better characterize the burden of ICU admission and mortality across variant-predominant periods in immunocompromised patients, thereby contributing to more evidence-based protection strategies for populations likely to remain vulnerable in future outbreaks and epidemics.

Conclusion

Immunocompromised status was independently associated with high odds of adverse hospital outcomes, including ICU admission and in-hospital mortality, irrespective of the circulating SARS-CoV-2 variant. These findings highlight the need to prioritise immunocompromised individuals in public health and clinical strategies through targeted vaccination approaches, early diagnostic testing, and timely access to antiviral therapies.
