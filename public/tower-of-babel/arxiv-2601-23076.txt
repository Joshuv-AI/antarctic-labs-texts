Learning-Based Signal Recovery in Nonlinear Systems with Spectrally Separated Interference
Jayadev Joy and Sundeep Rangan

Abstract
Upper Mid-Band (FR3, 7–24 GHz) receivers for 6G must operate over wide bandwidths in dense spectral environments, making them particularly vulnerable to strong adjacent-band interference and front-end nonlinearities. While conventional linear receivers can suppress spectrally separated interferers under ideal hardware assumptions, receiver saturation and finite-resolution quantization cause nonlinear spectral leakage that severely degrades performance in practical wideband radios. We study the recovery of a desired signal from nonlinear receiver observations corrupted by a high-power out-of-band interferer. The receiver front-end is modeled as a smooth, memoryless nonlinearity followed by additive noise and optional quantization. To mitigate these nonlinear and quantization-induced distortions, we propose a learned multi-layer Vector Approximate Message Passing (LMLVAMP) algorithm that incorporates spectral priors with neural network based denoising. Simulation results demonstrate significant performance gains over conventional methods, particularly in high-interference regimes representative of FR3 coexistence scenarios.



I
Introduction

Modern wireless communication systems increasingly operate in spectrally dense environments, where coexistence with strong adjacent-band signals is inevitable
[
11
]
. This is particularly critical in emerging upper mid-band (FR3, 7–24 GHz) deployments that must support diverse services such as satellite and terrestrial cellular networks
[
9
,
22
]
. Conventional receivers rely on linear front-ends and frequency-selective filtering
[
15
]
. While such filtering can ideally remove disjoint-band interference, nonlinear hardware effects such as amplifier saturation and analog-to-digital converter (ADC) clipping
[
19
]
cause spectral leakage, which are common in cost and energy efficient FR3 hardware. A major challenge arises when a strong out-of-band (OOB) interferer drives the receiver into a nonlinear regime. Even if the interferer lies outside the desired band, nonlinear distortion introduces in-band leakage, making classical filtering ineffective.

I-A

Related Work

I-A
1
Classical Nonlinearity Modeling

Traditional mitigation uses polynomial modeling and inverse compensation. Volterra and memory polynomial models have been applied to Radio Frequency (RF) front-ends
[
10
]
, while digital pre-distortion (DPD) is standard at transmitters
[
4
]
and has been extended to receivers. These methods demand accurate models of hardware nonlinearity and memory, which are difficult to obtain in practice, and adaptive estimation of high-order models is computationally prohibitive.

I-A
2
Classical Interference Suppression

Strong OOB interferers can saturate Low Noise Amplifiers (LNAs), mixers, and ADCs. Analog filtering and automatic gain control (AGC) circuits help, but are insufficient in wideband or reconfigurable radios
[
21
]
. Self-interference cancellation works in full-duplex systems, but requires careful hardware design and only cancels predictable interference. Digital methods such as pulse blanking and spectral notching fail when nonlinear saturation causes in-band leakage. Linearization via Bussgang’s theorem
[
3
]
reduces nonlinear effects to distortion noise, ignoring signal-dependent artifacts.

I-A
3
Message Passing Algorithms

Approximate Message Passing (AMP)
[
5
]
and Generalized AMP (GAMP)
[
18
]
enable scalable Bayesian inference but diverge under structured sensing. Vector AMP (VAMP)
[
17
,
20
]
improves robustness with right-orthogonally invariant matrices, but requires accurate priors and models. Classical denoisers in these methods are fixed and struggle with nonlinearities such as saturation. Thus, while rigorous under structured priors, they are limited in handling practical hardware distortions.

I-A
4
Data-Driven Approaches

Deep unfolding combines iterative structures with trainable modules. Learned VAMP
[
1
]
, Learned denoising-based AMP
[
14
]
, and Orthogonal AMP-Net
[
8
]
have shown strong results in linear and low-resolution tasks. However, they are not designed for nonlinearities from front-end saturation or OOB interference, and often generalize poorly beyond training conditions.

I-B

Motivation for Our Approach

We address the recovery of signals corrupted by strong OOB interference, nonlinear saturation, and coarse quantization, impairments common in modern FR3 receiver front-ends. We propose a learned version of the multi-layer VAMP (ML-VAMP)
[
7
,
16
,
13
]
framework, integrating model-based inference with data-driven adaptability. The nonlinear model accounts for receiver noise, smooth saturation, and optional quantization, while the estimator leverages spectral priors and trains its denoisers end-to-end to handle nonlinear effects. Unlike fixed model-based methods, the approach does not require explicit distortion knowledge, and unlike black-box networks, it retains interpretability via iterative inference.

Simulations show that the proposed estimator outperforms linear baselines, particularly in saturation-dominated regimes, representative of upper mid-band coexistence scenarios. These results highlight the importance of jointly modeling nonlinearity, noise, and quantization in the design of robust and cost-efficient FR3 receivers for future 6G systems.

II-C

Soft Saturation Model

As a specific instance of (
5
), we consider a soft-threshold model
[
12
]
. The noise vector is given by:

w[i]=(w_{a}[i],w_{b}[i]),\;\;w_{j}[i]\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2}),\;\;j\in\{a,b\},

(6)

with independent complex Gaussian components. The nonlinear measurement, illustrated in Fig.
2
, is given by:

\phi(r,w)=f\!\left(\frac{|r+w_{a}|}{\sqrt{P_{\text{sat}}}}\right)(r+w_{a})+w_{b},

(7)

where the index
i
is omitted for notational simplicity. Here,
P_{\text{sat}}
is the saturation power threshold, and
f(.)
is defined as:

f(x)=\frac{\tanh(x)}{x}.

(8)

In practical systems with finite-resolution ADCs, the output is quantized:

y_{q}[i]=\mathcal{Q}(y[i]),\quad\phi_{q}(r[i],w[i])=\mathcal{Q}\big(\phi(r[i],w[i])\big),

(9)

where
\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)
represents a scalar uniform quantizer.

This memoryless nonlinearity models RF front-ends by compressing amplitude while preserving phase. While the proposed model is general, it is particularly relevant to FR3 receivers, where large instantaneous bandwidths and relaxed analog filtering lead to frequent exposure to high-power adjacent-band signals. The receiver is assumed to know
S_{0},S_{1},B_{0},B_{1},P_{\text{sat}}
(estimated via pilots or spectrum sensing), but not
\phi(\cdot)
. The task is to estimate the desired signal
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
from
\boldsymbol{y}
or its quantized version
\boldsymbol{y}_{q}
.

III
Learned ML-VAMP for Nonlinear Filtering

III-A

Bayesian ML-VAMP Algorithm

As introduced, the proposed algorithm is a “learned” version of ML-VAMP
[
7
,
16
,
13
]
. Before describing it, we briefly review ML-VAMP for nonlinear interference. The measurements can be written as:

\boldsymbol{y}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{w}),\quad\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{\sf H}}\boldsymbol{x},

(10)

where
\boldsymbol{x}
is the frequency-domain vector and
\boldsymbol{r}
its time-domain representation. The goal is to estimate
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{r}
from
\boldsymbol{y}
. In ML-VAMP terminology, the system (
10
) is a two-layer network: the first layer maps
\boldsymbol{x}\to\boldsymbol{r}
, and the second maps
\boldsymbol{r}\to\boldsymbol{y}
. The algorithm iteratively updates estimates
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(t)}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(t)}
for
t=0,1,\dots
as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=G_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t)},\gamma_{0}),\quad\alpha_{0}^{(t)}=\langle G_{0}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t)},\gamma_{0})\rangle,

(11a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{0}^{(t)}}\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{\sf H}}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(t)}-\alpha_{0}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t)}\right),

(11b)

\displaystyle\gamma^{(t)}_{1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}^{(t)}(1/\alpha_{0}^{(t)}-1),

(11c)

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=G_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)},\gamma_{1}^{(t)}),\quad\alpha_{1}^{(t)}=\langle G_{1}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)},\gamma_{1}^{(t)})\rangle,

(11d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{1}^{(t)}}\boldsymbol{V}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(t)}-\alpha_{1}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)}\right),

(11e)

\displaystyle\gamma^{(t+1)}_{0}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{(t)}(1/\alpha_{1}^{(t)}-1),

(11f)

initialized with:

\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\gamma^{(0)}_{0}=0.

(12)

The updates in (
11
) use two functions,
G_{0}(\cdot)
and
G_{1}(\cdot)
, called
denoisers
. For OOB interference rejection,
G_{0}(\cdot)
is the
spectral denoiser
, acting on the frequency-domain signal, and
G_{1}(\cdot)
is the
nonlinear denoiser
, acting in the time domain where the nonlinearity occurs. In (
11a
) and (
11d
),
\langle\boldsymbol{v}\rangle
denotes the empirical mean of
\boldsymbol{v}
, defined as:

\langle\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v[i].

(13)

Accordingly, the scalar terms
\alpha_{0}^{(t)}
and
\alpha_{1}^{(t)}
represent the average divergences of the denoisers:

\alpha_{0}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\partial G_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t)},\gamma_{0}^{(t)})}{\partial z_{0}^{(t)}[i]},\;\alpha_{1}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\partial G_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)},\gamma_{1}^{(t)})}{\partial z_{1}^{(t)}[i]}.

(14a)

The choice of denoisers depends on the desired estimation criterion. Here, we focus on the minimum mean square error (MMSE) denoisers, where the goal is to approximate the MMSE estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\mathbb{E}\,[\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{y}]
.

Spectral denoiser

For MMSE estimation, the optimal spectral denoiser
G_{0}(\cdot)
in (
11a
), is given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}=G_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0},\gamma_{0})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{z}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}+\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\!\left(0,\gamma_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}\right)\right],

(15)

where the expectation is w.r.t. the prior on
\boldsymbol{x}
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
is a noisy observation of
\boldsymbol{x}
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
its conditional mean. In the interference filtering problem,
\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{\ell}\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
with Gaussian components as in (
2
). Since the intervals
B_{\ell}
are disjoint (
3
), the conditional mean (
15
) reduces to:

\widehat{x}[i]=\begin{cases}\mu_{\ell}[i]+\dfrac{\gamma_{0}S_{\ell}}{1+\gamma_{0}S_{\ell}}\big(z_{0}[i]-\mu_{\ell}[i]\big),&i\in B_{\ell},\\
0,&i\notin B_{\ell},\end{cases}

(16)

for all
\ell
. The parameter
\alpha_{0}
in (
11a
) then has the closed form:

\alpha_{0}=\langle{G_{0}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{z}_{0},\gamma_{0})}\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\frac{|B_{\ell}|}{N}\frac{\gamma_{0}S_{\ell}}{1+\gamma_{0}S_{\ell}}.

(17)

For convenience, let us define:

\mu[i]=\begin{cases}\mu_{\ell}[i],&i\in B_{\ell},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad S[i]=\begin{cases}S_{\ell},&i\in B_{\ell},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(18)

With this notation, (
16
) can be expressed compactly as:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{\gamma_{0}\boldsymbol{S}}{1+\gamma_{0}\boldsymbol{S}}\odot\big(\boldsymbol{z}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\big),

(19)

where
\odot
denotes elementwise multiplication and the fraction is taken elementwise.

Nonlinear denoiser

For MMSE estimation, the nonlinear denoiser
G_{1}(\cdot)
in (
11d
) is given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}

\displaystyle=G_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\,\!\left[\,\boldsymbol{r}\,\big|\,\boldsymbol{y}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{w}),\;\boldsymbol{r}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\gamma_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{I})\right],

(20)

i.e., the conditional mean of
\boldsymbol{r}
given the observation
\boldsymbol{y}=\Phi(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{w})
and Gaussian prior
\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\gamma_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{I})
. Since
\Phi(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{w})
is memoryless (
5
), denoising is componentwise:

\widehat{r}[i]=g_{1}(z_{1}[i],\gamma_{1}),\quad i=0,\ldots,N-1,

(21)

where the scalar denoiser is:

g_{1}(z_{1},\gamma_{1})=\mathbb{E}\,\!\left[\,r\,\big|\,y=\phi(r,w),\;r\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(z_{1},\gamma_{1}^{-1})\right].

(22)

III-B

Challenges with ML-VAMP

The ML-VAMP algorithm has been analyzed extensively in the large-system limit
N\to\infty
with random unitary
\boldsymbol{V}
. In this regime, performance is exactly characterized by a
state evolution

[
7
]
, which describes the joint distributions of both frequency-domain variables
(x[i],\widehat{x}[i])
and time-domain variables
(r[i],\widehat{r}[i])
. In certain cases, ML-VAMP can even be shown to be Bayes optimal
[
13
]
. Practical implementation, however, faces two challenges:

•

Nonlinear denoiser complexity
: While
G_{0}(\cdot)
admits a closed form expression (
16
),
G_{1}(\cdot)
in (
22
) does not. Computing it requires multidimensional numerical integration. For soft saturation (
6
), the variables are
w=(w_{a},w_{b})
and
r
, all complex, leading to integration over six real dimensions, which is computationally prohibitive.

•

Stability
: While
[
7
,
13
]
provide guarantees for fixed iteration
t
as
N\to\infty
, VAMP-based algorithms are well known to suffer from instability.

III-C

Learned ML-VAMP Algorithm

We propose a learned ML-VAMP framework that retains the iterative VAMP structure while replacing the intractable analytical denoisers with compact trainable neural networks, similar to
[
2
]
but adapted to VAMP.

To rewrite (
11
) in a learnable form, consider the spectral denoiser output.
In place of (
11b
), we use:

\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)}=\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{\sf H}}(\beta_{0}^{(t)}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(t)}-\beta_{1}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t)}),

(23)

where
\beta_{0}^{(t)}
and
\beta_{1}^{(t)}
are obtained as averages,
(\beta_{0}^{(t)},\beta_{1}^{(t)})=\langle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}^{(t)}\rangle
, with
\rho_{0}^{(t)}[i]
given by a neural network:

\rho_{0}^{(t)}[i]=f_{0}(z_{0}^{(t)}[i],S[i],\mu[i],t,\theta),

(24)

where
\theta
are network parameters and dependence on
t
allows a different network per iteration. Defining
\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(t)}=\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{\sf H}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(t)}
, (
23
) becomes:

\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(t)}=\beta_{0}^{(t)}\widehat{\boldsymbol{r}}^{(t)}-\beta_{1}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{V}^{\text{\sf H}}\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t)}.

(25)

The update of
\gamma_{1}^{(t+1)}
follows from the Wiener gain:

\gamma_{1}^{(t+1)}=\gamma_{0}^{(t)}\cdot\langle{\frac{\gamma_{0}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{S}}{1+\gamma_{0}^{(t)}\boldsymbol{S}}}\rangle^{-1}.

(26)

For the nonlinear denoiser, the update of
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t+1)}
is:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{0}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{v}^{(t)},

(27)

\displaystyle(v^{(t)}[i],\rho^{(t)}_{1}[i])

\displaystyle=f_{1}(z_{1}^{(t)}[i],\gamma_{1}^{(t)},t,\theta),

(28)

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{\rho_{1}}^{(t)}\rangle,

(29)

where
f_{1}(\cdot)
is a neural network that outputs
v^{(t)}[i]
and
\rho^{(t)}_{1}[i]
.

After
T
iterations, the signal estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is obtained by bandpass filtering in the frequency domain:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{m}_{B_{0}}\odot\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(T-1)},

(30)

where
\boldsymbol{m}_{B_{0}}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
is a binary mask selecting the band
B_{0}
.
This retains components in
B_{0}
while suppressing others. With quantization, the same procedure applies, using

in place of

III-D

Model Complexity

The main advantage of the learned ML-VAMP algorithm (
1
) lies in its reduced model complexity. A general learned approach would take three
N
-dimensional inputs,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
,
\boldsymbol{S}
, and
\boldsymbol{y}
, and output an
N
-dimensional estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which can greatly increase the number of parameters; for instance, a fully connected layer requires
O(N^{2})
parameters.

In contrast, learned ML-VAMP requires only two learned functions,
f_{0}(\cdot)
and
f_{1}(\cdot)
. For each iteration
t
with parameters
\theta
,
f_{0}(\cdot)
maps 4 inputs to 2 outputs, while
f_{1}(\cdot)
maps 3 inputs to 2 outputs. Hence, the complexity does not scale with
N
.

III-E

Neural Network Structure

The learned ML-VAMP framework employs two compact neural networks,
f_{0}(\cdot)
and
f_{1}(\cdot)
, both of which follow a simple two-layer architecture consisting of a hidden fully connected layer with 64 sigmoid units, followed by a linear output layer. Despite their simplicity, these networks effectively capture the required nonlinear dependencies. Both networks operate component-wise, and for notational clarity, the component index
i
is omitted in the expressions that follow.

Nonlinear denoiser
f_{1}(\cdot)

At iteration
t
,
f_{1}
processes the inputs
(z_{1}^{(t)},\gamma_{1}^{(t)},y)
, normalized by the saturation level
P_{\text{sat}}
, yielding feature vectors:

\big[|z_{1}^{(t)}|/\sqrt{P_{\text{sat}}},\;(\gamma_{1}^{(t)}P_{\text{sat}})^{-1},\;|y|/\sqrt{P_{\text{sat}}}\big].

The outputs are
[v^{(t)},\rho_{1}^{(t)}]
, where:

v^{(t)}=z_{1}^{(t)}+\kappa_{0}(y-\kappa_{1}z_{1}),\quad\rho_{1}^{(t)}=\frac{\gamma_{1}^{(t)}}{\exp(\log x_{\text{var}})},

with
\kappa_{0}
,
\kappa_{1}
, and
\log x_{\text{var}}
learned from the network. The posterior precision
\gamma_{0}^{(t)}
is the componentwise mean of
\rho_{1}^{(t)}
.

Message updater
f_{0}(\cdot)

Similarly at each iteration
t
,
f_{0}
processes
(z_{0}^{(t)},\gamma_{0}^{(t)},S,\mu)
, again normalized by
P_{\text{sat}}
, with feature vectors:

\big[|z_{0}|/\sqrt{P_{\text{sat}}},\;(\gamma_{0}P_{\text{sat}})^{-1},\;S/P_{\text{sat}},\;|\mu|/\sqrt{P_{\text{sat}}}\big].

The network output
\rho_{0}^{(t)}
is then averaged across components to yield the message update coefficients
\beta_{0}^{(t)}
and
\beta_{1}^{(t)}
.

III-F

Training Objective and Loss Design

The model is trained end-to-end on simulated data, with supervision applied in the frequency domain over the desired band
B_{0}
. To guide the iterative reconstruction, an intermediate loss penalizes the frequency-domain error at each iteration:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{early}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}w_{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}[B_{0}]-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(t)}[B_{0}]\right\|^{2},

(31)

where
w_{t}=t/\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}i
are normalized linear weights that assigns greater weight to later iterations. A final loss supervises the last iteration:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{final}}=\left\|\boldsymbol{x}[B_{0}]-\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{(T)}[B_{0}]\right\|^{2}.

(32)

The total loss is a weighted combination of both terms:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\eta\mathcal{L}_{\text{final}}+(1-\eta)\mathcal{L}_{\text{early}},\quad\eta\in(0.5,1].

(33)

This loss formulation encourages consistent improvement across iterations while maintaining training stability and convergence. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with an exponentially decaying learning rate.

V
Conclusion

We proposed a Learned ML-VAMP framework for signal recovery in nonlinear RF receivers with strong OOB interference and quantization, impairments commonly encountered in FR3 receiver front-ends. By integrating spectral priors with a trainable denoiser, the method combines model-based interpretability with data-driven adaptability. Its iterative structure alternates between nonlinear denoising and spectral filtering. Simulations show consistent gains over linear baselines in achievable rate, particularly under severe interference. These results highlight the effectiveness of hybrid inference for robust receiver operation in FR3 deployments. Future work includes extensions to time-varying interference, memory effects, and real-time implementations.
