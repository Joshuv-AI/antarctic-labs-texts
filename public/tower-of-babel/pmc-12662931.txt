Clinical applications of artificial intelligence in the histopathology of lymphoma: diagnosis, treatment and prognosis
Authors: Mengyao Kang, Zibo Yang, Tian Yu, Dongyu Li, Zhiqiong Wang, Liting Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) is an important branch of computer science. With the rapid development of AI, the application in oncology has become increasingly widespread. As a hematologic malignancy characterized by remarkable heterogeneity, lymphoma has long posed significant challenges in both diagnosis and treatment, particularly with regard to its complex classification and difficulties in prognostic evaluation. Breakthroughs in AI technology have provided a new paradigm for precision treatment of tumors. AI can integrate and analyze HE pathology slides and PET/CT images to enhance diagnostic efficiency; meanwhile, in terms of treatment prognosis, AI can identify biomarkers to accurately classify lymphoma subtypes for therapeutic guidance, simultaneously quantify biomarkers to minimize the influence of subjective variability, and predict the patients’ prognosis based on the extracted features to assist in the precise treatment of lymphoma. This review aims to provide an overview of AI related to various fields of lymphoma, introducing the core technologies and principles of AI, including deep learning, decision trees, regression models, and so on. We also discuss the clinical applications of AI in lymphoma pathology slides and PET/CT images, systematically analyze the clinical applications of AI in diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis, as well as innovatively summarize the cutting-edge applications of AI in 3D pathology of lymphomas, and finally, we emphasize the development potentials and current challenges of AI in the field of lymphomas to promote precision lymphoma diagnosis and treatment by providing a theoretical foundation.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Lymphoma, Precision medicine, Histopathology, Biomarkers

Introduction

Globally, the number of incident cases of hematologic malignancies has increased since 1990, reaching 1.34 million in 2019, showing an upward trend. Among them, the incidence of Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma increased to 457,080 and 87,510 cases, and the number of deaths increased to 254,610 and 27,550 cases, respectively [1]. As highly heterogeneous malignant tumors, lymphomas differ significantly in their subtypes at the level of morphological, immunophenotypic, and genetic features, and are accompanied by corresponding clinical heterogeneity, transforming from an indolent status to an aggressive progression [2]. The current clinical diagnostic system for lymphoma is mainly based on multi-dimensional integration, with basic assessment of biopsy samples through histopathological analysis, combined with laboratory tests such as chromosomal abnormality screening and identification of genetic variants, and resolution of immunophenotypic or molecular features through specific marker detection, as well as the use of imaging results to complete the localization of foci and the determination of staging [3–5]. However, there are significant limitations in this system, such as subjective variability in histopathological interpretation, lack of timeliness in molecular testing, and limitations in overall diagnostic accuracy, leading to clinical risks such as delayed treatment timing, reduced efficacy, and biased prognosis assessment in some patients. Therefore, it is of great significance to improve the diagnosis and treatment system of lymphoma and to increase the accuracy and diagnostic efficiency to improve the survival rate of patients. In recent years, the continuous advancement of computer science and breakthrough developments in deep learning algorithms have jointly propelled the widespread application of artificial intelligence (AI) technology in the medical field, offering novel technical pathways to address complex diagnostic challenges. As a crucial branch of AI, machine learning (ML) employs self-learning algorithmic models capable of identifying complex feature patterns from vast medical datasets. These algorithms autonomously establish feature correlations through medical data analysis, with their predictive efficacy progressively improving through iterative processes, demonstrating significant advantages in disease diagnosis and prognostic prediction [6]. The development of ML models comprises three core stages. The first step consists of selecting and preparing a set of training data that will be used to feed the ML model to learn how to solve the problem for which it is designed. The second step is to select an algorithm to run on the training dataset. The type of algorithm (regression algorithms, decision tree, random forest, support-vector networks, artificial neural networks, etc.) depends on the type and volume of training data and the type of problem to be solved. Finally, iterative parameter optimization through comparative analysis of predicted versus actual outcomes, dynamically adjusting variable weights to enhance model accuracy [7]. The development process necessitates the coordinated use of three distinct datasets: training set, validation set, and test set [8]. These components form an integrated modeling workflow that is indispensable for robust model development. AI not only directly processes tumor imaging data but also integrates multi-modal imaging information (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET)) to achieve precise tumor classification through cross-modal data fusion that provides complementary features [9]. Advancements in tissue clearing techniques and optical imaging technologies have expanded histopathological image acquisition beyond traditional two-dimensional sections, progressively enabling three-dimensional (3D) stereoscopic imaging [10]. This technological innovation significantly enhances image dimension and information richness, allowing AI systems to perform multi-dimensional tumor analysis and deep feature extraction, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy while expanding research perspectives. Types of artificial intelligence algorithms applied in lymphoma

Deep learning

Deep learning (DL), a subset of machine learning, simulates how the human brain perceives and interprets multi-modal information to capture complex structures within large-scale datasets. The algorithms mentioned earlier for training models are also applicable to DL. Since the introduction of the MCP model by McCulloch and Pitts in 1943 and the breakthrough in deep belief nets by Hinton et al. in 2006, DL has become a cornerstone of cross-disciplinary research. Among its architectures, the convolutional neural network (CNN)-inspired by the visual system-features a three-tiered functional hierarchy: convolutional layers (feature extraction), pooling layers (spatial dimensionality reduction), and fully connected layers (classification output) [11]. For instance, CNN has been applied to lymphoma classification, cell segmentation, and prognosis prediction. Jaruenpunyasak, J. et al. developed an MRI-based CNN to distinguish glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) from primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) [12]. Li et al. developed a CNN-based model to diagnose DLBCL with near 100% accuracy. Convolutional layers captured morphological features such as centroblasts, diffuse growth patterns, and vascular distribution, while transfer learning enhanced robustness against staining variations across hospitals. Pooling and fully connected layers further optimized feature extraction and enabled reliable discrimination between DLBCL and non-DLBCL [13]. Xu, C. proposed the MB-FSMIL framework, which combines imaging histological features from PET/CT, to achieve the differential diagnosis of DLBCL and FL [14]. In clinical-imaging fusion analysis, Xia et al. built a DL model using elastic-net–selected clinical and radiological features, achieving superior diagnostic performance to both junior and senior radiologists in an external test set (AUC = 0.85) [15].

Decision trees

Decision trees are a widely used classical-machine-learning algorithm that recursively partition the training data; each internal node applies a feature-based test, each branch corresponds to an outcome of that test, and each leaf node stores the final class label or regression value. Their core principle lies in data segmentation via “if-then” rules, offering intuitive interpretability and clear decision pathways. Random forests, an ensemble learning method, enhance accuracy and robustness by aggregating predictions from multiple decision trees. While their “collective intelligence” approach outperforms single trees, random forests suffer from reduced transparency and interpretability due to their complex rule systems. Both methods can classify lymphoma subtypes based on clinical features [16]. For example, Bobée, V. et al. developed a multi-modal model integrating PCR, next-generation sequencing (NGS), and a random forest classifier to subtype 510 cases of B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma into seven categories, achieving subtype-specific accuracy rates of 80%–100% [17]. The initial classifications were all determined by a panel of pathology experts based on the 2016 World Health Organization standards. Through the random forest algorithm, 137 gene expression markers were integrated, optimizing the classification of 510 cases of B-cell lymphoma. It is particularly suitable for distinguishing morphologically similar subtypes (such as DLBCL subtypes, FL and GCB DLBCL), and assisting in identifying prognostic molecular features. In order to predict Primary Treatment Failure (PTF)-DLBCL from initial imaging evaluation, Santiago R et al. employed a Random Forest ML approach to analyze 1,218 handcrafted radiomic features, constructing a classifier for the predictive model, which showed that the model distinguished between refractory and non-refractory patients with a mean accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of 73%, 62% and 82%, respectively. The area under the AUC of the two test sets was 0.83 and 0.79, respectively, which are higher than the existing standard clinical criteria [18].

Regression models

Regression models, a key ML algorithm category, predict continuous target variables by establishing mathematical relationships between independent variables (features) and dependent variables (outcomes). Their goal is to identify optimal fitting functions that minimize errors between predicted and actual values. Common types include linear regression, logistic regression, and support vector regression. These models are valuable for prognostic prediction, treatment efficacy evaluation, and risk factor analysis. Kang et al. constructed a LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression model using clinicopathological variables such as depth of invasion, histologic grade, tumor budding, lympho-vascular invasion (LVI), and mismatch repair (MMR) status. The model detected lymph node metastasis (LNM) in training (29 cases, 13.1%) and validation (12 cases, 12.6%) cohorts, with nine variables selected for the LASSO model. Japanese criteria recommends an additional surgical resection if one or more of the following is applicable: (1) positive vertical resection margin; (2) depth of submucosa invasion ≥ 1,000 μm; (3) lymphovascular invasion positive; (4) poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, or mucinous carcinoma; or (5) grade 2/3 budding at the site of deepest invasion. It adopts a binary approach of “surgery if a single indicator is positive”, which has a significant deviation compared to the LASSO model that makes judgments by integrating nine variables. In the validation set, AUROC demonstrated better predictive accuracy of LASSO model compared to Japanese criteria (0.765 in the LASSO model vs. 0.518 in the Japanese criteria, p = 0.003) [19].

The role of artificial intelligence in lymphoma diagnosis

Application of artificial intelligence in histopathologic diagnosis of lymphoma

Conventional lymphoma classification relies on expert histopathological assessment, which is time-consuming and requires specialized expertise. Its complex cellular morphology and molecular features further challenge accurate diagnosis. The advent of digital Whole slide image (WSI), increased network speeds, and ease of storage solutions have made it easier for pathologists to apply, manage, and share digital slide images for clinical use. Combined with advances in ML, these developments have driven the integration of AI into digital pathology [20]– [21].

One of the definitions of Digital Pathology is the use of whole slide scanners to convert histological slides into high-resolution digital slide images, followed by machine learning techniques to detect new morphological features and improve the performance of histopathological interpretation [22]. The accuracy of models applied with ML techniques has been validated in canine lymphoma [23], mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma (MALT) [24], follicular lymphoma (FL) [25, 26], diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [27], and lung cancer [28, 29] tissues. The accuracy of AI for lymphoma typing is widely validated. To address the challenge of multi-subtype cross identification, recent studies have demonstrated the potential of AI to surpass traditional pathology in diagnostic accuracy. Achi, Hanadi El et al. developed a multitasking CNN algorithm using 2,560 HE/IHC (Hematoxylin-eosin; Immunohistochemistry) pathology slides for the four-classification diagnosis of benign lymph nodes, DLBCL, Burkitt lymphoma, and small lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, achieving 95% accuracy on an independent test set [22]. Similarly, Miyoshi, Hiroaki et al. trained a DL-based classification model on 388 HE/IHC slides (259 DLBCL, 89 FL, and 40 reactive lymphoid hyperplasia), achieving 97.0% accuracy in morphologic assessment, significantly higher than the 83.3% accuracy by pathologists [30]. Particularly noteworthy is their model’s ability to maintain high performance with a more diverse sample set, suggesting strong clinical applicability.

Application of artificial intelligence in cytogenetic and molecular pathological diagnosis of lymphoma

In the diagnosis of lymphoma, it is often difficult to achieve accurate diagnosis and typing of lymphoma based solely on imaging and histopathology, and therefore further cytogenetic and molecular pathology tests are still required in many cases. Fortunately, advances in structural, functional, and metabolic analyzing, such as flow cytometry have improved the detection of subtle dynamic changes in cellular structure, chemical composition, and genetic alterations. Flow cytometry, in particular, serves as a powerful diagnostic tool, enabling rapid evaluation of cellular antigen expression and providing critical immunophenotypic data for disease classification. In clinical practice, measurable residual disease evaluation by multiparameter flow cytometry or quantitative polymerase chain reaction methods is an established standard of care for assessing risk of relapse before or after hematopoietic cell transplantation for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [31]. In summary, with the help of advanced detection technologies, AI can not only detect chromosomal abnormalities in lymphoma cells, but also detect gene mutations, and quantitatively analyze cell-surface biomarkers or intracellular molecules, which can help in the diagnosis and typing of lymphoma.

AI shows unique advantages in multi-dimensional biomarker analysis for lymphoma diagnosis. By integrating protein, gene, and cellular biomarkers with flow chemistry technology, it enables highly accurate analytical results. Recent advances in multi-omics approaches have significantly advanced biomarker discovery for lymphoma classification and prognosis. In proteomic analysis,, Deeb, S.J. constructed a support vector machine (SVM)-based feature screening framework to analyze the protein expression profiles of patients with DLBCL in an attempt to extract protein-based biomarkers with the highest segregating power, which showed that the proteins PALD1, MME, TNFAIP8, and TBC1D4 may serve as potential biomarkers to differentiate between GCB and ABC lymphoma subtypes as biological factors [32]. At the level of genetic markers, the team of Péterffy, B. used the Ward2 algorithm for hierarchical clustering analysis and found that miR-181a could be a highly efficient marker for the diagnosis of central nervous system leukemia (CNSL) by integrating centrifugal smears of cells from patients with CNSL and flow cytometry (FCM) data [33]. In addition, focusing on epigenetic modification mechanisms, Zhu et al. developed a lactylation-based Riskscore model for accurate prognosis prediction of patients with DLBCL by assessing TIDE scores of lymphoma patients with different lactylation modifications [34]. Most comprehensively, Zaitsev, A.‘s team attempted to develop a biomarker for TME of lymphomas at the cellular level, developing a decision tree-based algorithm Kassandra, which used RNAseq data from more than 9,400 sorted samples containing a variety of cell populations to identify 18 TME subpopulations in the examined tissues and 41 cell populations originating from PBMCs, yielding a total of 51 unique cell types [35]. The heterogeneity of the lymphoma TME is manifested by cellular compositional diversity, spatial topological heterogeneity and dynamic molecular interaction networks, which may constitute potential biomarkers for specific subtypes.

AI can be combined with digital WSI to explore the lymphoma tumor microenvironment, assisting in understanding the process of different cell interactions and tumor cell spreading, and providing a new paradigm for resolving lymphoma. AI can be extended to model the topology of cellular interaction networks for different immune cell types, their shapes and localization, and their interactions, which may help have a better understanding of lymphoma. For example, CD30 + cells behave differently biologically in lymphoma and reactive lymph node proliferation, and researchers parsing lymphoma tumor microenvironments have found that CD30 + cells aggregate in tumor tissues, and that aggregation in Nodular sclerosis classic Hodgkin lymphoma (NS-cHL) is typically higher [36]. J Jachimowicz, R.D. et al. attempted to identify characteristic biomarkers in the tumor microenvironment of advanced classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) using WSI to quantify T-cells, B-cells, Hodgkin-Reed-Sternberg cells, and macrophages in lymphoma tissues, and found that low B-cell counts were associated with significantly reduced progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), while low T-cell, macrophage, and Hodgkin-Reed-Sternberg cell levels were not associated with progression or relapse risk in the study cohort, suggesting that low B-cell content may serve as an important factor influencing the prognosis of advanced classical Hodgkin lymphoma (cHL) [37]. These studies demonstrated that AI revealed the spatial behavioral patterns of CD30 + cells by parsing the cellular ecological network of the tumor microenvironment and identified B-cell depletion as a novel biomarker of poor prognosis in cHL, which provides a theoretical basis for precision therapeutic strategies targeting the microenvironment.

Application of artificial intelligence in radiologic diagnosis of lymphoma

AI in lymphoma diagnosis 18-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18 F-FDG PET/CT) has been established as an important imaging tool in the field of lymphoma diagnosis and treatment by its high sensitivity and specificity characteristics, and it plays an important role in early screening, pathological identification, prognostic evaluation, and development of treatment strategies [38]. This fusion imaging technique acquires information on tissue metabolic function through PET, combined with the anatomical structure localization provided by CT, to achieve accurate identification of FDG-affinity lesions. Research data have shown that lymphoma lesions exhibit significant FDG uptake enhancement compared to normal tissues (except metabolically active organs), and the standardized uptake value (SUV) of high-grade lymphomas is significantly higher than that of low-grade subtypes [39]. Therefore, based on the above features, 18 F-FDG PET/CT provides clinicians with a multi-dimensional quantitative analysis framework to support systematic diagnostic and therapeutic evaluation of lymphoma patients [40]. In recent years, AI technology has made breakthrough progress in the field of radiomics, and its application in lymphoma diagnosis has been extended to multiple lymphoma subtypes. Taking primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL) as an example, 18 F-FDG PET/CT scan comprehensively assess tumor volume and metabolism to achieve staged diagnosis of primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma [41, 42].

AI plays an important role in the whole process from raw data acquisition to data processing, feature extraction, analysis, and clinical decision making. In the data preprocessing stage, 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) can be used for diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKL) whole-body FDG-PET/CT lymphoma lesion detection, feature extraction [18, 43–46].Based on the preprocessing results, Leung et al. developed a deep, semi-supervised transfer learning (DeepSSTL) approach for fully automated whole-body tumor segmentation and prognosis on 18 F-FDG and PSMA PET/CT images using limited annotations [47]. However, the generalizability of this model across different PET tracers and scanner platforms requires further validation. Further, corresponding clinical decisions can be made based on the analysis results. Wang et al. proposed a pseudo spatio-temporal radiomic approach (PST-Radiomics) based on PET/CT, which focuses on intra-tumor metabolic heterogeneity (ITMH) information and utilizes conventional radiomic features extracted from CT volumes to help analyze lymphoma subtypes and improve the performance of lymphoma classification [48]. Notably, this method provides unprecedented insights into lymphoma subtyping. However, the biological correlates of these radiomic patterns need further elucidation.

AI can also be used to make predictions using radiomics such as PET/CT, ranging from initial staging prediction, treatment planning, and subsequent efficacy monitoring. Mouheb M. et al. found the strong relationships between nearby involved lymph node regions and between more distant regions [49]. Since patients with malignant lymphoma may have other persistent lesions after treatment, Ferjaoui, R. et al. proposed ML-based computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) applied to whole-body diffusion-weighted radiographic images to identify evolving lymphomas and residual masses by combining functional, anatomical, and morphological features of Regions of Interest (ROIs), which aids in diagnostic labeling of evolving lymphomas or residual masses in need of new treatment mass, facilitating subsequent treatment decisions [50].

Application of artificial intelligence in 3D pathologic diagnosis of lymphoma

The development of 3D pathology analysis has been driven by the inability of traditional 2D pathology slides to provide a complete spatial structural relationship of the samples, which has significant advantages in disease diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and monitoring of treatment efficacy. Specifically, the advantages of 3D pathology include 3D expansion of tissue sampling volume, compatibility with non-destructive imaging technologies to support downstream proteomics and genetic testing, and significant improvement in the throughput and efficiency of pathology analysis [51]. As a complement to traditional two-dimensional pathology, combined with advanced algorithms and tissue transparency techniques, light-sheet microscopy can more accurately realize 3D reconstruction and image analysis of pathological sections, providing more detailed pathological images and deeper pathological information [52–54]. Some of the more commonly used tissue transparency methods are CLARITY [55], uDISCO [56], FDISCO [57] and so on. The main difference between these methods is that the selection and proportion of organic solvent systems are different, and the applicable tissues are different, providing customized solutions for 3D pathology analysis [58].

Tumor evolution and progression involves many complex cellular and molecular interactions that are spatially and temporally regulated in TME. Light-sheet microscopy provides deeper pathological information through 3D reconstruction and image analysis of pathological sections of Hodgkin lymphoma, which aids in understanding the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment, among others [59]. For example, Chen et al. performed high-throughput, high-resolution volumetric imaging of human lymphoma tissues labeled with multiple immunofluorescence (mIF) using Bessel-beam light-sheet fluorescence microscope (B-LSFM) and uDISCO tissue-transparency methods to achieve 3D visualization of the lymphoma tumor microenvironment and high-content cellular analysis [60]. Unique patterns of phenotypic heterogeneity in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis were identified at single-cell resolution in whole formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsy samples and quantitative and immunophenotypic analyses of individual tumor cells were performed by semi-automated algorithms [61].

Combined with DL models, 3D pathology analysis shows promise for diagnosis, prediction, and treatment. Song et al. developed TriPath, a deep learning platform for 3D tissue analysis, which outperformed 2D slice-based methods by reducing sampling bias and capturing tissue heterogeneity, thereby improving prognostic accuracy and reliability [62]. From static diagnosis to dynamic therapy, Wang et al. used high-throughput Bessel oblique plane microscopy for 3D real-time imaging of CAR-T cell-mediated cytotoxicity, which was achieved by dynamic 3D tracking to analyze the anti-tumor cytotoxic function of CAR-T cells, helping to establish a quantitative criterion for the T-cell function of individual patients on T-cell-based immunotherapy [63].

The role of artificial intelligence in lymphoma treatment

AI-assisted integration of multi-omics biomarkers to predict targeted therapeutic response

The combination of image analysis and AI can help develop new methods for assessing multiple biomarkers in preclinical and exploratory studies, revealing complex interactions within the TME and aiding in improving cancer diagnosis and therapy. For example, Patel, S.S. et al. found that CTLA-4:CD86 interactions are a component of the immunologically privileged niche surrounding HRS cells, indicating that patients with cHL refractory to PD-1 blockade may benefit from CTLA-4 blockade [60]. Phillips, D. et al. found that lymphoma TME in which effector PD-1 + CD4 + T cells, tumor cells and immunosuppressive Tregs in the lymphoma TME and proposed a spatial biomarker, Spatial Score, which may be closely associated with the pembrolizumab response in primary cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) [64]. Using biopsies from 487 patients with cHL treated with a BEACOPP-based regimen, Jachimowicz, R.D. et al. quantified T-cells, B-cells, Hodgkin-Reed-Sternberg cells, and macrophages using WSI and suggested that a low B-cell count was associated with significantly PFS and OS [37]. These studies have demonstrated the translational potential of AI-driven multi-modal resolution of the TME in the precision diagnosis and treatment of lymphoma.

The invasive phenotypic changes and treatment sensitivity dynamic changes during the evolution of tumor heterogeneity have been one of the challenges in clinical treatment. This innovative approach not only enables discovery of novel biomarkers but also significantly refines quantification criteria for established markers, representing a paradigm shift in precision oncology. Based on Yolov4, Motmaen, I. et al. quantified collagen fiber subtypes in HL biopsy samples and found that the percentage of faintly stained fibers was significantly correlated with post-chemotherapy QPET scores, which not only validates the feasibility of using AI to find new prognostic biomarkers of lymphoma, but also suggests the role of stromal remodeling in changes in treatment sensitivity [65]. Analyzed from the perspective of technology translation, AI is developing from three dimensions: resolving molecular typing, exploring mechanism changes, and screening therapeutic targets. In the field of parsing molecular typing, Stokes, M.E. team developed SubLymE classifier based on 1208 cases of newly diagnosed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma(ndDLBCL), classified ndDLBCL patients into seven clusters (A1-A7), and proposed TCF4 as a potential therapeutic target for A7 biology and patients [66]. In terms of exploring the mechanisms of recurrence, Hopper, M.A. compared the characteristics of the TME in low-grade B-cell lymphomas (LGBCL) and aggressive DLBCL by using gene sequencing and AI techniques, and found that immune cells have a tumorigenic role in a subpopulation of LGBCL patients, and that NOTCH, and NFκB may be associated with the alterations in tumor recurrence [67]. AI can also optimize established biomarker quantification criteria and enable screening for therapeutic targets. For example, in advanced mycosis fungoides (MF; stages IIB to IV) and Sézary syndrome (SS), both CD30 and Ki-67 expression are associated with poorer survival in patients with cutaneous tumors (T3 disease) [68]; Yan, F et al. assessed PD-L1 expression in patients with DLBCL through an AI-based image analysis method, which enhances the objectivity and interpretability of the PD-L1 quantification, and screens patients who can be targeted for treatment [69].

The classification system of lymphomas is diverse due to differences in biological dimensions. With the iteration of molecular diagnostic techniques, researchers have been able to resolve the pathophysiological mechanisms of lymphomas at a multi-omics level, thereby identifying biological subtypes with common targeted therapeutic potentials, such as combining proteomics and transcriptomics to analyze lymphoma subtypes [70, 71].

AI-assisted integration of multiple data to predict prognostic index

In clinical management, approximately 30% of patients have primary resistance or acquired relapse to standard of care chemotherapy and significant treatment response heterogeneity across lymphoma subtypes, making the establishment of prognostic prediction models a key component in driving clinical decision-making and precision therapy development [72, 73]. The limitations of traditional prognostic assessment systems are mainly reflected in the restricted data dimensions, which usually incorporate only three to five readily available clinical covariates, while ignoring high-dimensional information such as pathological image features and genomic variant load. The use of ML, DL and other AI technology processing through feature extraction, model training and validation can extract key information from diverse data, using complex and a large number of variables, which can be used to construct accurate prognostic prediction models [74–76]. Notably, the construction of prognostic models needs to go beyond the traditional biomarker categories and incorporate multi-level factors, such as socio-demographic and clinical decision-making factors, in addition to molecular and histological subtypes [77, 78]. Also, the machine learning methods used to construct prognostic models vary in terms of model predictability, with ensemble learning generally performing better than individual models [79–81].

The paradigm of tumor classification based on molecular features has been pioneered since 2000 when Alizadeh, A. A. first proposed the identification of different types of DLBCL through gene expression profile [82]. To further enhance the accuracy of classification, Wenzl, K. et al. used a multi-omics integration strategy to identify features of DLBCL with a higher risk of early clinical failure, analyzing 444 tumor biopsies of newly diagnosed DLBCL by WES and RNA-seq, identifying features associated with a high risk of early clinical failure independently of the International Prognostic Index (IPI) and cell-of-origin (COO), and found that inclusion of the ARID1A mutations led to the identification of 45% of early clinical failure cases [83]. Meanwhile, Steen, C.B. et al. explored the molecular switches that mediate treatment responsiveness and resistance in DLBCL by resolving features of DLBCL TME, such as cellular states, ecosystems, and re-stratifying the survival of DLBCL after treatment with standard immunochemotherapy regimens [59].

Although the International Prognostic Index (IPI) remains a benchmark tool for the clinical prognostic assessment of DLBCL, its inability to identify the molecular basis of clinical heterogeneity and to identify specific therapeutic targets limits its translational value in precision treatment decisions [84, 85]. To address this limitation, Albitar, M.‘s team developed a four-subcomponent hierarchical system based on the treatment response profile of the R-CHOP regimen (Rituximab + Cyclophosphamide + Doxorubicin + Vincristine + Prednisone) to reduce overfitting during the selection of biomarkers for predicting the response of various types for use in predicting patients with DLBCL who do not respond to R-CHOP [86]. However, the model’s clinical relevance remains questionable due to the absence of validation in ethnically diverse cohorts, raising concerns about its generalizability beyond specialized tertiary centers. In recent years, the prognostic assessment of DLBCL has shifted from a clinical phenotype-driven approach to a multi-omics integration-driven approach, such as resolving the immune microenvironment, to enable the prediction of therapeutic response in DLBCL [87, 88]. Merdan, S used both supervised and unsupervised ML approaches to identify gene expression signatures associated with survival, and constructed a multivariate regression model to predict overall DLBCL patient survival. Combining changes in TME immune cell content with risk prediction, it was proposed that CD8 + T cells and initial CD4 + T cells are associated with a favorable prognosis [88]. Distinguishing from prognostic modelling by cellular or treatment response, Hu, F. et al. proposed that the degree of stemness might correlate with lymphoma development from the negative correlation between stemness indices and prognosis in a variety of cancers, including leukemia, esophageal cancer and medulloblastoma, indicating a correlation between the degree of stemness, immune and stromal scores, PFS, and survival [89].

Disease progression within 24 months (POD24) in patients with follicular lymphoma (FL) is an independent poor prognostic factor for overall survival (OS). Zha, J. A prediction model was constructed for the risk of POD24 after first-line treatment in patients with newly diagnosed FL1-3a by combining clinical and imaging parameters, and it was found that FLIPI-C could accurately predict the risk of POD24 in FL1-3a patients [90]. In contrast to the use of POD24 as a prognostic index, Li, C. applied RSF to predict the survival and risk of FL patients using structured electronic health record (EHR) data, constructing an AI model that outperforms traditional methods and achieves similar predictive performance to POD24 [91]. Discussion

AI can integrate multi-dimensional biomedical information and analyze it through ML algorithms to provide personalized diagnostic, therapeutic and drug development solutions for patients. With the booming development of high-throughput technologies, Omics techniques have been innovated and expanded, including genomics, epigenomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics [92]. Multi-omics analysis can overcome the limitations of single genomics and improve diagnostic accuracy, such as combining transcriptomics and single-cell genomics to analyze the TME [83, 93], and combining radiomics and clinical data to build AI learning models [15]. Meanwhile, multi-omics can help to classify lymphoma subtypes more accurately, and improve the efficiency of pathologists, clinicians, and patients by identifying biological subgroups that are homogeneous and potentially targetable, enabling broad and accurate treatment [70]. With the advancement of personalized medicine, the development of high-throughput drug screening is driven to progress towards precision oncology. Combining drug sensitivity studies with tumor genotyping can correlate response to treatment with genetic alterations (biomarkers), thus facilitating the search for new personalized therapies. ML algorithms can be used for monotherapy prediction, outputting individual patient-specific treatment advice [94, 95].

Although the rapid development of AI has driven its use in the diagnosis and treatment of lymphoma, but the limitations of AI remain unavoidable. The foremost consideration is the collection of raw data. The training of AI models often requires large and comprehensive data sets [96]. Yet, for certain cases, it is often challenging to collect sufficient data to support training, thereby hindering the construction of satisfactory AI models. Secondly, AI models such as DL rely on complex model structures with millions of parameters, and this complexity makes it difficult to understand the decisions the models make and how they give predictions. This “non-interpretability” makes many clinicians uncomfortable, even if it does improve diagnostic or predictive accuracy [97]. Lastly, the advent of AI models has blurred the boundaries of diagnostic subjects. Traditionally, it was usually the doctor who diagnosed and treated the patient and was primarily responsible for the patient’s prognosis. Now, not only doctors, but also nurses, physician assistants, as well as therapists and medical students can engage in diagnosis with the aid of AI. When a patient receives AI for diagnosis and treatment, it needs to be clear whether the legal responsibility for the outcome or decision is attributed to the physician, the AI model, or the engineer who developed the model [98].

There maybe some possible limitations in this study. Firstly, in terms of literature investigation and collection, we conducted literature searches solely on Scopus and PubMed without performing a comprehensive and extensive survey, which might lead to the omission of critical information. Secondly, regarding data sources, we focused exclusively on patients’ biological information and did not incorporate clinical information such as genetic data, protein profiles, or family medical history. Future studies will integrate a broader range of data. Thirdly, methodologically, while we investigated multiple AI models, we regrettably did not combine two or more models or conduct comparative analyses to identify the most suitable approach. Subsequent research will prioritize these aspects to enhance methodological rigor.

Also, establishing a standardized scoring criteria and integrating multiple methods may help to accelerate the development of feasibility of clinically useful AI tools [99]. For example, multimodal environment of medical diagnosis and treatment. Multimodal transformer models in health care can effectively process and interpret diverse data forms, such as text, images, and structured data, which helps to apply in the complex, multimodal environment of medical diagnosis and treatment [100]. Recently, graph representation learning, mainly through graph neural networks and graph transformer architectures, stands out for its capability to capture intricate relationships and structures within clinical datasets.Graph AI facilitates model transfer across clinical tasks, enabling models to generalize across patient populations without additional parameters or minimal to no re-training [101].
