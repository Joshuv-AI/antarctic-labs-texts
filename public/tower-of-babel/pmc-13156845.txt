Salivary Interleukin-6 and Tumor Necrosis Factor-α changes following total oral rehabilitation in children with mild intellectual disability: a prospective interventional study
Authors: Aishani Baksi, Kavita Rai, Manju Raman Nair, Swagata Saha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate changes in salivary inflammatory cytokines Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) in children with mild intellectual disability (ID) and early childhood caries (ECC) following total oral rehabilitation (TOR), and to explore their potential as non-invasive indicators of oral inflammatory status.MethodsA prospective interventional study was conducted on 23 children aged 3–6 years with mild ID and ECC, recruited from the NITTE Special Child Care Centre and affiliated schools. Unstimulated saliva samples were collected at baseline (pre-TOR), 1 month, and 3 months post-TOR. TOR included comprehensive dental treatment—oral prophylaxis, restorations, pulp therapy, extractions, sealants, and fluoride application—alongside individualised diet counselling and reinforcement of oral hygiene. Salivary IL-6 and TNF-α levels were quantified using a high-sensitivity enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Data were analysed using the Friedman test, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (adjusted α = 0.017).ResultsThe median baseline salivary IL-6 and TNF-α levels were 257.97 pg/mL and 173.0 pg/mL, respectively. Both cytokines demonstrated significant reductions at 1 month (IL-6:98.45 pg/mL; TNF-α: 77.12 pg/mL) and 3 months (IL-6:98.33 pg/mL; TNF-α: 72.0 pg/mL) post-TOR (Friedman test, p < 0.001). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed significant reductions in IL-6 and TNF-α from baseline to 1 month and baseline to 3 months (p < 0.001). The additional reduction in TNF-α from 1 to 3 months did not remain statistically significant after adjustment (p = 0.033).ConclusionTotal oral rehabilitation was associated with significant reductions in salivary IL-6 and TNF-α levels in children with mild intellectual disabilities, consistent with measurable biochemical improvements. These findings support the potential role of salivary cytokines as non-invasive indicators of oral inflammatory changes; however, further controlled studies incorporating clinical correlations and larger cohorts are required before definitive biomarker validation.Trial registrationThe trial was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry – India (CTRI) with registration No: CTRI/2024/07/071143 dated 2024-07-23.Keywords: Early Childhood Caries, Intellectual Disability, Salivary Biomarkers, IL-6, TNF-α, Total Oral Rehabilitation

Introduction

Early Childhood Caries (ECC) remains one of the most prevalent chronic diseases in preschool children globally, despite being largely preventable [1]. Its consequences extend beyond the oral cavity, affecting mastication, speech, nutrition, growth, sleep, and overall quality of life, particularly in cases requiring comprehensive restorative care [2]. Children with Intellectual Disability (ID) are disproportionately affected, owing to impaired manual dexterity, reliance on caregivers for oral hygiene, dietary patterns, and limited access to specialised dental services [3]. These factors often result in advanced disease necessitating Total Oral Rehabilitation (TOR), typically performed under general anaesthesia (GA), which restores oral health comprehensively in a single session [4].

The pathogenesis of ECC involves not only microbial colonisation but also a complex host inflammatory response [5]. Cariogenic biofilms and pulpal injury stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α from odontoblasts, pulp fibroblasts, and infiltrating immune cells [6]. IL-6 modulates both acute and chronic inflammation, whereas TNF-α orchestrates tissue breakdown and pulpal signalling [7]. Elevated salivary levels of these cytokines have been correlated with active disease, severity of carious lesions, and symptomatic pulpal involvement, suggesting their potential as non-invasive biomarkers for oral inflammation [8].

Saliva offers a practical medium for biomarker assessment in paediatric populations, particularly those with special healthcare needs, because of its non-invasive collection, suitability for repeated sampling, and capacity to reflect local oral pathology [9, 10]. While reductions in IL-6 and TNF-α have been documented following dental interventions in neurotypical children, data on children with mild ID are scarce [11–13]. This population may exhibit distinct oral hygiene patterns, immune responses, and disease trajectories that could influence cytokine dynamics post-intervention.

The present study aimed to quantify salivary IL-6 and TNF-α levels in children with mild ID before and after TOR, with follow-up at 1 and 3 months post-rehabilitation. We hypothesised that TOR would significantly reduce these cytokine levels, indicating the resolution of oral inflammation and restoration of pulpal health. Establishing such biochemical improvements would support the use of salivary cytokines as reliable, non-invasive biomarkers for monitoring oral health recovery and inflammation in children with special healthcare needs.

Methods

Study design

This longitudinal in vivo study was conducted to evaluate changes in salivary inflammatory cytokines, IL-6, and TNF-α, in children with mild ID following TOR for ECC. The study was performed at the NITTE Special Child Care Centre (NSPECC), Mangalore, and affiliated special schools, which provide access to children with mild ID requiring specialised dental care. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ref: ETHICS/ABSMIDS/435/2024), and written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians. In addition, assent was obtained from children, where cognitively appropriate, to ensure adherence to ethical standards in paediatric research. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry – India (CTRI) with registration No: CTRI/2024/07/071143 dated 2024-07-23.

Participants

Children aged 3–6 years with mild ID, defined according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria [14] and an Intelligence Quotient (IQ) between 55 and 69, who had severe ECC, were included in the study. Intellectual functioning was determined using Malin’s Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (MISIC), a standardised adaptation of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, validated for the Indian population [15]. IQ assessments were previously conducted by a licenced clinical psychologist, and documented scores were obtained from institutional medical records before study enrolment. Mild ID was chosen to ensure sufficient cooperation during clinical procedures while representing a population at high risk for oral disease. Exclusion criteria included children with moderate to profound ID, IQ < 55, systemic medical conditions, seizure disorders, sensory impairments affecting cooperation, or current use of medications that impact immune function or salivary secretion. Children whose parents were unwilling to provide consent were also excluded. These criteria were intended to minimise variability and ensure a homogeneous study population suitable for evaluating the impact of TOR on salivary cytokines. Efforts were made to reduce confounding from dietary and oral hygiene variations by providing standardised counselling and monitoring adherence throughout the follow-up.

Sample size determination

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), based on a previous study evaluating changes in salivary cytokines following dental interventions in paediatric populations by Menon et al. [11]. An effect size of 0.7, alpha error of 0.05, and statistical power of 90% were used for a two-tailed test, resulting in a minimum sample size of 23 participants. The calculation was based on the primary outcome measure, change in IL-6 levels from baseline to post-treatment.

Total oral rehabilitation

Comprehensive TOR was performed on all participants to restore oral health. The treatment protocol included oral prophylaxis, restoration of cavitated lesions using appropriate materials, pulp therapy (pulpotomy or pulpectomy as indicated), extraction of non-restorable teeth, placement of pit-and-fissure sealants, and topical fluoride application (Fig. 1). Behavioural guidance techniques, including Tell-Show-Do, distraction, and positive reinforcement, were employed to facilitate cooperation [16]. Parental presence was encouraged to reduce anxiety and ensure comfort. In case of uncooperative children, the treatment was performed under general anaesthesia after obtaining prior paediatric clearance and informed consent from parents. All procedures were performed in accordance with standard paediatric dental protocols to maintain uniformity across participants. Treatment procedures were standardised and documented to minimise variability and bias.

Dietary and oral hygiene counselling

Dietary and oral hygiene counselling was provided to reinforce the effectiveness of TOR and minimise the recurrence of ECC. Parents were instructed to maintain a six-day diet diary documenting the type, frequency, and consistency of foods consumed by the child [17, 18]. Based on the diary, individualised dietary advice was provided, emphasising the limitation of sugar-containing foods to mealtimes, promotion of fibrous fruits and vegetables, and tailoring menus according to the child’s preferences [18]. Oral hygiene instructions included assessment and correction of brushing techniques, education on the importance of twice-daily supervised brushing with fluoridated toothpaste, and guidance on flossing or other at-home preventive measures [19]. Reinforcement of these instructions occurred during follow-up visits at one and three months post-TOR, including thorough intraoral examinations. Adherence to dietary and oral hygiene recommendations was monitored to account for potential confounding factors in salivary cytokine levels.

Saliva collection and handling

Unstimulated whole saliva samples were collected at baseline (pre-TOR), one month post-TOR, and three months post-TOR. Collections were scheduled between 9:00 and 11:00 a.m. to minimise diurnal variation [20]. Participants were asked to refrain from food and drink for at least one hour prior to collection. The unstimulated salivary flow rate was standardised by collecting saliva over a fixed 5-minute period and calculating the flow rate as total volume divided by collection time, expressed in mL/min, in accordance with established protocols [21]. Cooperative children were seated in the Coachman position, allowing saliva to drip passively into sterile polypropylene tubes [11]. For uncooperative children, alternative methods including micro-pipetting or sterile cotton swabs were employed [22, 23]. Saliva samples (2 mL per participant) were immediately stored at 4 °C, transported to the laboratory, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to remove mucins and debris, and aliquoted for storage at − 80 °C until biochemical analysis [11]. All samples were labeled with the name and other relevant data for identification and processed by laboratory personnel blinded to the timepoint of collection to reduce detection bias. Any missing or insufficient samples were prospectively planned to be documented, and participants would have been retained in analyses using available data; however, no missing samples occurred in the present study. To minimise intra-individual variability, the same saliva collection method was consistently applied to each participant across all three time points. Passive drooling was preferred for cooperative children, whereas sterile cotton swabs or micro-pipetting were reserved for participants unable to comply due to behavioral limitations. All samples were subjected to identical processing, centrifugation, storage, and assay protocols.

Biochemical analysis of salivary cytokines

Salivary IL-6 and TNF-α levels were quantified using commercially available high-sensitivity enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (GENLISA™, Krishgen Biosystems, India) following the manufacturer ’s protocols. All samples were analysed in duplicate to ensure reproducibility. Briefly, wells pre-coated with monoclonal antibodies specific to each cytokine were incubated with saliva samples and standards to allow antigen–antibody binding. Biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies and avidin–HRP conjugates were subsequently applied, followed by the addition of a tetramethylbenzidine substrate to produce a colorimetric reaction [12]. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader within 15 min of stopping the reaction [12]. The detection ranges were 6.25–400 pg/mL for IL-6 and 7.8–500 pg/mL for TNF-α. Intra- and inter-assay variability were minimised by performing duplicate measurements and strictly adhering to kit protocols.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome measures were the salivary concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α at baseline, one month, and three months post-TOR. These time points were selected to capture acute and intermediate changes in inflammatory cytokine levels following the restoration of oral health and reinforcement of preventive practices.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and found to be non-normal. Therefore, non-parametric tests were applied. Changes in cytokine levels over time were evaluated using the Friedman test, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Bonferroni adjustment was applied for multiple pairwise comparisons (three comparisons), and the adjusted level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.017. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals were calculated to assess the magnitude of the observed changes. Unless otherwise specified, statistical significance was initially set at p < 0.05. No missing data points occurred; however, the analytical plan prespecified the retention of participants using available data if missing values had arisen. Potential confounding due to diet and oral hygiene adherence was accounted for by monitoring and reinforcing preventive measures throughout the follow-up period. Given that only seven participants underwent treatment under general anaesthesia, a subgroup analysis was not performed because of limited statistical power; however, the distribution of GA cases was documented to enhance transparency. The effect size (r) for Wilcoxon signed-rank tests was calculated using the formula r = Z/√N, where Z is the standardised test statistic and N represents the total number of observations. 95% confidence intervals for r were estimated using Fisher’s z-transformation to approximate the sampling distribution and improve interval accuracy.

Results

Participant characteristics and flow

A total of 25 children with mild ID were assessed for eligibility. Two were excluded (one due to moderate ID and one due to parental refusal), resulting in 23 participants who underwent baseline evaluation and TOR. All 23 participants completed follow-up visits at 1 month (T1) and 3 months (T2), and all saliva samples were successfully collected and analysed, resulting in no missing data. Laboratory personnel performing the cytokine assays were blinded to participant identity and time points to minimise measurement bias.

The study cohort comprised 10 males (43.5%) and 13 females (56.5%). The mean age of male participants was 5.6 ± 0.7 years, whereas that of females was 5.38 ± 0.65 years. There were no significant differences in age distribution between sexes (p = 0.42). Other potential confounders, including baseline dietary compliance, were evaluated and found not to differ significantly across participants (Table 1; Fig. 2).

Of the 23 participants, seven children (30.4%) underwent total oral rehabilitation under general anaesthesia (GA), while 16 children (69.6%) were treated using behavioural guidance techniques alone. Baseline salivary IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations did not demonstrate extreme outliers among the GA-treated participants. Salivary cytokine outcomes

IL-6 Levels

Baseline salivary IL-6 concentration (T0) was 253.2 ± 36.5 pg/mL (median 257.9 pg/mL). Following TOR, IL-6 levels decreased sharply to 97.4 ± 14.6 pg/mL (median 98.5 pg/mL) at 1 month (T1) and stabilised at 96.9 ± 14.7 pg/mL (median 98.3 pg/mL) at 3 months (T2). The Friedman test confirmed a statistically significant overall change in IL-6 levels over time (χ² = 20.8; p < 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, with statistical significance set at p < 0.017.

Post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed significant reductions from baseline to T1 (Z = 4.19; p < 0.001; effect size r = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.71–0.94) and baseline to T2 (Z = 4.19; p < 0.001; r = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.71–0.94). The change from T1 to T2 was not significant (Z = 0.52; p = 0.62; r = 0.11), suggesting early stabilisation of IL-6 levels (Table 2; Fig. 3).

TNF-α Levels

The baseline TNF-α concentration was 177.2 ± 46.3 pg/mL (median 173.0 pg/mL). TNF-α levels decreased to 81.98 ± 21.6 pg/mL (median 77.1 pg/mL) at T1 and further declined to 75.49 ± 20.7 pg/mL (median 72.0 pg/mL) at T2. The Friedman test indicated a significant overall change (χ² = 19.4; p < 0.001). Pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons revealed significant reductions at all intervals: baseline → T1 (Z = 4.19; p < 0.001; r = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.70–0.94), baseline → T2 (Z = 4.19; p < 0.001; r = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.71–0.94), and T1 → T2 (Z = 2.12; p = 0.033; r = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.05–0.70). The change from T1 to T2 (Z = 2.12; p = 0.033; r = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.05–0.70) did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni adjustment (adjusted α = 0.017) (Table 2; Fig. 4).

TOR resulted in a rapid and sustained reduction in IL-6 levels within the first month, indicating early resolution of acute pulpal and gingival inflammation. TNF-α levels demonstrated a progressive decline, suggesting ongoing modulation of chronic inflammation post-treatment. No adverse events or missing data were reported, and potential confounders, including sex, age, and baseline dietary compliance, did not significantly influence cytokine trajectories.

Discussion

ECC remains a major global health concern, affecting nearly half of preschool-aged children and contributing disproportionately to the disease burden in low- and middle-income countries [1]. The consequences extend beyond tooth structure destruction: pain, sleep disturbances, feeding problems, and social withdrawal collectively impair a child’s growth and psychosocial development [2, 24]. These challenges are magnified among children with ID, who experience higher caries prevalence and greater treatment needs due to neuromuscular incoordination, sensory defensiveness, communication barriers, and dependence on caregivers for oral hygiene [3, 25]. Consequently, delayed detection and progression to advanced disease often necessitate comprehensive rehabilitation under GA [4]. Therefore, reliable non-invasive biomarkers capable of reflecting disease activity and therapeutic response would be of considerable clinical value, as they allow safe, repeatable monitoring of oral inflammation without subjecting children to invasive procedures, thereby aiding treatment planning and follow-up assessment.

Saliva offers a practical diagnostic medium in paediatric populations, especially those with limited cooperation, because it is non-invasive, cost-effective, and reflects whole-mouth inflammatory dynamics [9, 10]. Cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α, readily diffuse into saliva, mirroring both local inflammatory states within the caries–pulp complex and systemic immune activity [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of salivary biomarkers in quantifying microbial burden, inflammatory activity, and treatment response in neurotypical children with ECC [11–13, 26, 27]. However, evidence in children with ID—who may exhibit compromised immune profiles, dietary dependencies, altered microbiota, or stress-related inflammatory modulation [28]—remains scarce. To minimise biological heterogeneity, the present study included only children with mild ID and without systemic comorbidities.

Baseline IL-6 and TNF-α concentrations in our cohort were substantially elevated compared to post-rehabilitation levels, consistent with active caries and pulp–dentin inflammation. These values correspond with previous reports, indicating that untreated ECC promotes epithelial and immune cell activation, driving increased cytokine release into saliva [11–13, 27, 29]. Importantly, following full-mouth treatment under GA, both cytokines demonstrated a statistically significant and clinically meaningful reduction, indicating a temporal association between comprehensive rehabilitation and decreased salivary inflammatory burden. IL-6 decreased sharply by one month and plateaued thereafter, a pattern consistent with its role as an acute-phase mediator that normalises rapidly following the elimination of infectious foci [30]. Similar trends were reported by Menon et al. [11], who observed significant IL-6 reduction after comprehensive caries treatment in healthy children. Likewise, Anumula et al. [27] recorded postoperative IL-6 normalisation following pulpal therapy and restorative procedures. However, in the absence of concurrent longitudinal clinical indices and correlation analysis, these findings should be interpreted as demonstrating biochemical improvement temporally associated with treatment rather than definitive validation of biomarker utility.

TNF-α also showed a substantial reduction at one month, followed by a continued decline at three months. This gradual trajectory is biologically plausible, given the role of TNF-α in sustaining chronic, macrophage-driven inflammation and its association with persistent low-grade immune activation [12, 26]. Once the microbial load decreases and oral hygiene improves after comprehensive rehabilitation, TNF-α expression is expected to diminish progressively rather than abruptly. Importantly, while several studies have investigated salivary TNF-α levels in children with active ECC [12, 31–33], no published work to date has evaluated its post-treatment behavior—particularly in children with special healthcare needs. Thus, the present study provides the first evidence demonstrating longitudinal TNF-α normalisation following full-mouth rehabilitation in children with mild ID, suggesting a potential treatment-related biochemical response while acknowledging the absence of a comparator group.

The large effect sizes observed across both cytokines underscore the robustness of the observed biochemical changes over time, with IL-6 demonstrating a large and sustained reduction from baseline to both follow-up points (r = 0.87), while TNF-α showed a similarly large early reduction, with the additional decline from 1 to 3 months not remaining statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons (r = 0.44; p = 0.033). High follow-up completion, successful sample collection in all participants, and blinded laboratory assessment strengthen the internal validity of the findings, particularly noteworthy given the behavioural challenges often encountered in children with ID.

Several mechanisms may explain the cytokine reductions observed. First, the removal of necrotic dentin, devitalised pulp tissue, and cariogenic biofilms decreases exposure to microbial byproducts that stimulate immune activation [34]. Second, relief from chronic oral pain may reduce physiologic stress and improve sleep and dietary behaviours, factors known to modulate systemic inflammation [35]. Third, caregivers may provide better hygiene support once the child is free of discomfort, consistent with reports by Yilmaz et al. [36] showing improved caregiver adherence after comprehensive dental rehabilitation. Although these pathways are plausible, they were not directly measured and should therefore be interpreted as hypotheses rather than confirmed mediators.

Despite the strengths of the study, several limitations must be acknowledged. The absence of a neurotypical control group limits the ability to contextualise cytokine ranges relative to typically developing children. The single-arm design precludes causal inference beyond temporal associations, and cytokines—being dynamic biomarkers—are influenced by circadian rhythm, stress, salivary flow rate, and other unmeasured variables. Additionally, the three-month follow-up period, although sufficient to capture early biochemical stabilisation, does not address long-term relapse or inflammatory rebound. Finally, GA itself may transiently modulate cytokine expression, although its effects are short-lived and unlikely to explain sustained changes at three months. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as demonstrating temporal associations rather than definitive causal effects of TOR on cytokine reduction.

A potential methodological consideration in the present study is that 30.4% of participants underwent total oral rehabilitation under general anaesthesia. General anaesthesia and perioperative stress responses have been reported to transiently influence circulating inflammatory mediators, including IL-6 and TNF-α [37]. However, existing evidence indicates that anesthetic-related cytokine alterations typically peak within hours and normalise within 48–72 h postoperatively [37, 38]. In the present study, salivary samples were collected at one and three months following rehabilitation, well beyond the window of acute anesthetic-related immune modulation. Therefore, it is biologically unlikely that GA-induced inflammatory effects account for the sustained and marked cytokine reductions observed. Furthermore, the magnitude and consistency of cytokine decline across the cohort, combined with the absence of extreme baseline deviations among GA-treated children, suggest that the observed biochemical changes primarily reflect the resolution of the oral inflammatory burden rather than anaesthetic exposure.

The use of two saliva collection methods may represent a potential source of measurement variability. Absorbent materials, such as cotton swabs, may theoretically adsorb protein molecules and influence absolute cytokine concentrations. However, because the same collection method was maintained consistently within each participant across all time points, longitudinal intra-individual comparisons remain valid. Nevertheless, future studies should consider uniform collection techniques or validated synthetic swab systems to further reduce potential assay variability.

Additionally, the absence of standardised clinical indices, such as dmft/dmfs, PUFA, plaque index, or gingival index scores, and the lack of correlation analysis between biochemical and clinical parameters limit the ability to directly associate cytokine reductions with quantified clinical improvement. Future studies integrating objective clinical scoring with salivary biomarker profiling are warranted to establish diagnostic and prognostic applications.

Nevertheless, this study provides important evidence that children with mild ID exhibit measurable biochemical changes following dental rehabilitation, comparable to those in neurotypical populations. This highlights their capacity for inflammatory normalisation once the disease burden is addressed and underscores the importance of early, comprehensive interventions. Furthermore, the findings support the potential utility of salivary IL-6 and TNF-α as objective, child-friendly biomarkers for monitoring oral inflammatory status, assessing treatment effectiveness, and potentially identifying early signs of disease recurrence in populations with limited symptom communication.

Future research should explore multicentre cohorts with larger and more diverse ID subgroups, include neurotypical comparison arms, evaluate longitudinal changes beyond one year, integrate cytokine profiling with microbiome sequencing to clarify host–microbe interactions, and investigate threshold values predictive of clinical relapse. Incorporating behavioural assessments and caregiver compliance metrics may further elucidate factors influencing biomarker trajectories.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that comprehensive dental rehabilitation produces significant and sustained reductions in salivary IL-6 and TNF-α levels in children with mild ID, reflecting measurable biochemical improvement temporally associated with treatment. These findings suggest the potential role of salivary cytokines as non-invasive indicators of oral inflammatory changes; however, further studies incorporating clinical correlation, larger cohorts, and long-term follow-up are required before definitive biomarker validation can be established.
