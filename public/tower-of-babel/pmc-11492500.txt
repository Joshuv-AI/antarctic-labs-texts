Assessment of intraocular lens tilt and decentration after femtosecond laser-assisted and conventional cataract surgery at 12 months and beyond
Authors: Bia Z Kim, Hayoung Lee, Yoo Young Jeon, Kyu Sang Eah, Nahyun Park, Ho Seok Chung, Jae Yong Kim, Hungwon Tchah, Hun Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare long-term intraocular lens (IOL) decentration and tilt following femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS) and conventional surgery using swept-source anterior segment ocular coherence tomography (SS-ASOCT).MethodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, all patients underwent FLACS or conventional cataract surgery. Those with a minimum of 12-months follow-up since surgery were included. Patients with surgical complications were excluded. Demographics, preoperative ocular measurements (axial length, anterior chamber depth), and postoperative measurements [corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), autorefraction, keratometry, IOL type] were collected. Postoperative IOL tilt and decentration were compared using SS-ASOCT between patients with FLACS and conventional cataract surgery. Subgroup analysis was conducted to analyze tilt and decentration by haptic type (single-piece vs. three-piece).ResultsA total of 188 eyes were included [110 (58.5%) in the FLACS group and 78 (41.5%) in the conventional group]. No differences were observed between the FLACS and conventional groups regarding preoperative parameters. FLACS resulted in less IOL decentration compared to conventional cataract surgery (170 μm vs. 240 μm, p = 0.002). No statistically significant differences were observed between the FLACS and conventional groups in the magnitude and axis of IOL tilt. Both groups had a trend of IOL tilt toward the inferotemporal aspects. Moreover, no difference regarding postoperative CDVA, spherical equivalent, and keratometric astigmatism was observed.ConclusionsFLACS resulted in significantly better long-term IOL centration than conventional surgery with manual capsulotomy. No significant difference in IOL tilt or postoperative CDVA was observed between FLACS and conventional cataract surgery.Keywords: Tilt, Decentration, Conventional cataract surgery, Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery, Swept-source anterior segment ocular coherence tomography

Introduction

With the constant advancement in surgical equipment and intraocular lens (IOL) designs, the goal of cataract surgery has shifted from a mere restoration of vision to excellent visual quality and spectacle independence. These finer outcomes rely heavily on the predictability and long-term stability of IOL positions. Specifically, significant IOL decentration and tilt are reported to cause unwanted astigmatism and higher-order aberrations [1–4].

Limited studies have compared IOL positioning after femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS) and conventional cataract surgery with manual capsulorhexis [1, 2]. Nonetheless, they reported better IOL positions following FLACs, likely attributed to the better control of capsulorhexis size, circularity, and position [1–5]. Previous studies comparing FLACS and conventional cataract surgery analyzed retro-illumination photographs or Scheimpflug imaging to measure the IOL position in relation to the pupillary center [1, 2]. More recently, a second-generation swept-source anterior segment ocular coherence tomography (SS-ASOCT) device provided a closer approximation of the visual axis to which the IOL should align by taking the corneal vertex as the reference [6, 7]. Moreover, its image processor can automatically calculate the IOL position and is unaffected by pupil shape, which was a limitation of previous devices [8].

In the present study, we sought to accurately compare the long-term IOL decentration and tilt between the FLACS and conventional cataract surgery groups using second-generation SS-ASOCT.

Methods

A retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted for patients who had undergone FLACS or conventional cataract surgery with manual capsulotomy. All surgeries were performed at a single center (Asan Medical Center, Seoul). Patients reviewed at least 12 months after surgery were included. Those with a surgical complication (posterior capsular rupture or zonular weakness) were excluded.

Surgical technique

A standardized surgical technique, except for the method of capsulorhexis, was performed for all patients. Each patient received pupillary dilation and topical anesthesia. The FLACS group had a 5.0 mm scanned capsule capsulotomy using a femtosecond laser (CATALYS Precision Laser System, Johnson & Johnson). A pupil-maximized capsulotomy was performed if the pupil was small or not uniform. In the conventional group, a continuous curvilinear capsulotomy of 5.0 mm was created by the surgeon using a cystotome and/or capsulorhexis forceps. Following cataract extraction using phacoemulsification, a single-piece or three-piece hydrophobic IOL implant was folded and injected into the capsular bag. Viscoelastic material was removed by irrigation/aspiration. All incisions were hydrated with a balanced salt solution. Postoperative topical medications included levofloxacin hydrate 1.5% (Cravit) four times daily, prednisolone acetate 1.0% (Pred Forte) four times daily, ketorolac tromethamine 0.45% (Acuvail) two times daily, and cyclosporin 0.1% (Ikervis) once daily.

Measurements

Preoperative data, including patient age, sex, operative eye, method of surgery, months from surgery, IOL type, axial length, and anterior chamber depth (IOL Master 700, Zeiss), were recorded. Postoperative data on corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), autorefraction, and keratometry (ARK-1, NIDEK) were collected. A standardized value and axis of IOL tilt and decentration relative to the corneal topographic center were measured using SS-ASOCT (CASIA2, Tomey) (Fig. 1). IOL decentration is defined as the distance of ​​the IOL center from the reference axis, and the IOL tilt is the angle between the IOL axis and the reference axis The SS-ASOCT measures and automatically calculates the IOL tilt and decentration from an image taking the corneal vertex at the reference. All images used in this calculation were reviewed by the clinician and manually adjusted when the surface of the IOL was misrecognized by auto-detection [9].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. The normal distribution assumption was checked using the Shapiro–Wilks test. Differences.

between the two study groups were tested using the t-test for independent samples. Post hoc analyses were performed using the t-test for independent samples to compare IOL tilt and decentration between single-piece and three-piece IOLs in each group (FLACS and conventional cataract surgery). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (v. 21.0, IBM Corp.).

Results

A total of 188 eyes of 188 patients were included [110 (58.5%) in the FLACS group and 78 (41.5%) in the conventional cataract surgery group]. No differences were found between the FLACS and conventional groups in terms of age, sex, months since surgery (range 12–90 months), axial length, and anterior chamber depth (Table 1). In the FLACS group, 101 (91.8%) eyes had undergone scanned-capsule capsulotomies, and 9 (8.2%) had undergone pupil-maximized capsulotomies. In the FLACS group, 92 (83.6%) eyes received a single-piece IOL, and 18 (16.4%) eyes received a three-piece IOL. In the conventional cataract surgery group, 58 (74.4%) eyes received a single-piece IOL, and 20 (25.6%) eyes received a three-piece IOL. Moreover, postoperative CDVA, spherical equivalent of refraction, and keratometric astigmatism showed no difference between the FLACS and conventional cataract surgery groups (Table 1).

A statistically significant difference in the magnitude of IOL decentration was observed between the FLACS and conventional cataract surgery groups, with the FLACS groups having a decentration of 170 μm compared to the manual group with a 240 μm decentration (p = 0.002) (Table 2). This difference was evident for both right eyes (180 μm vs. 230 μm, p = 0.013) and left eyes (160 μm vs. 240 μm, p = 0.008) for the FLACS and conventional cataract surgery groups, respectively. The axis of decentration showed no significant difference (145° for FLACS vs. 152° for conventional cataract surgery, p = 0.678). However, the FLACS group showed less scatter than the conventional cataract surgery group (Fig. 2).

A subgroup analysis of haptic types in the conventional cataract surgery group showed that three-piece IOLs had greater decentration than single-piece IOLs (310 ± 140 μm vs. 210 ± 150 μm, p = 0.027). This difference was not evident in the FLACS group (150 ± 80 μm vs. 180 ± 100 μm, p = 0.370). No statistically significant difference in IOL tilt was observed between single-piece and three-piece IOLs in the conventional cataract surgery group (4.2 ± 1.7° vs. 4.1 ± 1.8°, p = 0.801), nor in the FLACS group (4.6 ± 1.5° vs. 4.3 ± 1.3°, p = 0.502).

Discussion

In the current study, the FLACS group showed significantly better long-term IOL centration than the conventional cataract surgery group. Also, the FLACS group showed less scatter in the amount and axis of decentration. Kranitz et al. reported similar findings using Scheimpflug imaging that showed less decentration and tilt in the FLACS group than in the conventional cataract surgery group [2]. Previous studies have reported a significant correlation between reduced decentration and better IOL overlap [3, 8]. We postulate that the superior long-term IOL positioning and stability following FLACS is likely due to its known precision in capsulorhexis size, circularity, and position that allows for a better IOL overlap by the capsule [3–5, 10].

Decentration and tilt of the IOL can induce astigmatism and higher-order aberrations, resulting in unwanted symptoms [11–14]. Despite better IOL centration following FLACS in our study, visual acuity was not significantly different between surgical methods. This may be due to the small amount of decentration in both groups, which is consistent with previous studies reporting that decentration less than 0.4 mm is not clinically significant [14–16]. The tilt of in-the-bag IOLs has a lesser impact on visual acuity than decentration, but becomes clinically significant when greater than 5 degrees [14–18]. In our study, the IOL tilt was less than 5 degrees with both surgical methods. FLACS and conventional cataract surgery showed no difference in the magnitude and axis of IOL tilt, however, a trend of IOL tilt toward the inferotemporal direction was observed [8]. Around 5 degrees of inferotemporal tilt is also evident preoperatively with the crystalline lens, indicating that the fovea is slightly temporal to the pupillary axis [6, 19, 20]. This tilt of the natural aspheric lens is reported to have very little effect on the image quality since the corneal and internal coma cancel each other out [21, 22]. A comparable compensation is observed for a wide range of aspheric IOLs with mild postoperative tilt [23, 24].

Significant IOL decentration and tilt can cause defocusing, astigmatism, and wavefront aberrations; however, the effect varies with different IOL designs [14, 25]. For example, tilted or decentered aspheric IOLs produce more optical quality degradation than spherical IOLs [13]. Interestingly, in the current study, a single-piece IOL resulted in better centration than a three-piece IOL following a manual capsulotomy. Zhong et al. also reported better stability of single-piece IOLs than three-piece IOLs at 3 months after conventional cataract surgery [26]. In contrast, Sato et al. reported greater decentration (but not tilt) of single-piece IOLs at 24 h and 2 weeks after surgery that became insignificant by 4 weeks [19]. In our study, the difference in decentration between haptic types was not evident following FLACS. The amount of decentration with both haptic types is likely too small following FLACS for a significant difference to be detected [14, 15, 19]. Although not investigated in the current study, IOL displacement has a greater effect on visual function when using aberration-correcting IOLs compared to spherical and standard aspherical IOLs [13, 16, 17]. The IOL designs with a higher negative spherical aberration are more affected by misalignment [17]. Misalignment also becomes more significant with multifocal IOLs that rely more on centration than monofocal IOLs [16, 27]. However, the effects of decentration and tilt were inconsistent among studies of multifocal IOLs since their designs differ between diffractive, refractive, hybrid, and segmental; with different haptic designs adding to the variability [27–30]. Furthermore, the effect on vision must be considered at near, intermediate, and distance, with variable pupil sizes [27, 30, 31]. In a retrospective study, Xu et al. analyzed the effects on optical quality from the decentration of monofocal, bifocal, and extended depth-of-focus (EDOF) IOLs [32]. Higher-order aberrations, coma, point spread function, and glare perception were better with monofocal and EDOF IOLs than with bifocal IOLs when decentration was greater than 0.25 mm [32]. Breyer et al. also reported that EDOF IOLs perform better than bifocal IOLs when decentration is evident [33]. The evaluation of previous studies is limited as many have been simulated, and there is no universal reference point to measure the IOL displacement.

Previous studies have calculated IOL decentration and tilt using Purkinje images, retro-illumination photographs, Scheimpflug imaging, UBM, and ASOCT [6, 7, 14, 23, 34]. Rosales et al. reported that Purkinje and Scheimpflug images can provide estimates within an accuracy of 0.026 mm for IOL decentration and 0.6 degrees for IOL tilt [23]. UBM has also been used, but it requires contact and a supine assessment [34]. A second-generation SS-ASOCT, as used in the current study, is not affected by pupil diameter or shape, which can be a limitation of other modalities [6, 8]. It shows high inter- and intra-observer reproducibility under both non-mydriatic and mydriatic conditions [6, 19]. Moreover, the device can automatically calculate the relevant parameters without a separate image-processing device. Additionally, Zhang et al. suggested measuring displacement with reference to the corneal topographic axis as most relevant [35]. This method achieved stronger correlations between CDVA and IOL decentration than using the pupillary axis or line of sight as references [35]. Swept-source OCT biometers with greater scan depth may be able to provide more certainty that measurements are along the visual axis as they scan the whole eye [7].

There are many variables that may affect IOL positioning apart from surgical method and haptic type, which were not specifically assessed in this study. We used a standardized surgical technique since capsulorhexis size, capsule polishing, and corneal wound site have been implicated in IOL tilt and decentration [36, 37]. In addition, axial length and previous vitrectomy have been reported to be associated with IOL tilt and decentration, however, there was no significant difference in axial length between the FLACS and the conventional cataract surgery groups [37, 38]. Other factors, such as zonular weakness and posterior capsule opacity, have also been reported to affect IOL positioning [37].

In the present study, we adopted a femtosecond laser-assisted scanned capsule capsulotomy instead of a pupil-maximized capsulotomy when feasible. Pupil dilation is often not uniform, can result in incorrect centering of the capsulorhexis, and affects some methods of postoperative IOL measurements [10, 21, 39]. Therefore, a scanned-capsule capsulotomy using the femtosecond laser can allow better centration of the capsulotomy and subsequent IOL implant with good overlap [10]. The number of patients with pupil-maximized femtosecond laser capsulotomies was too small in our study for statistical comparison. Further studies comparing the effects of scanned-capsule and pupil-maximized femtosecond laser capsulotomies on IOL decentration and tilt would be valuable.

This study has limitations as a cross-sectional study. The evaluation did not account for potential confounders such as preoperative lens decentration and tilt. Due to the cross-sectional nature of the current study, we did not assess for higher-order aberrations, point spread function, modulation transfer function or subjective perceptions that may be of clinical significance in relation to postoperative tilt and decentration. A larger study that evaluates the effect of tilt and decentration of these on visual quality outcomes using the different types of IOLs would be useful. Furthermore, detailed postoperative assessments of higher-order aberrations, coma, and glare perception may be useful in elucidating the clinical relevance of reduced decentration following FLACS.

In summary, in the long term, FLACS showed significantly better IOL centration than conventional cataract surgery with manual capsulotomy. Overall, IOLs tended to tilt in the inferotemporal direction following both surgical methods. Although the current study did not find a significant difference in postoperative CDVA with the small improvements in IOL decentration using FLACS, future studies of newer sophisticated IOL designs may tease out the finer effects on visual quality, especially with the help of SS-ASOCT.
