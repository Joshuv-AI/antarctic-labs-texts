Age-associated loss of lymphatic vessels promotes cardiac inflammation
Authors: Julian U G Wagner, Hamza Gulshan, Ibrahim Sultan, Salli Antila, Laia-Canes Esteve, David Rodriguez Morales, Mariano Ruz Jurado, David John, Emmanouil G Solomonidis, Katja Schmitz, Josefine Panthel, Haris Kujundzic, Susanne Hille, Oliver J Müller, Christoph Kugler, Haider Sami, Manfred Ogris, Simone-Franziska Glaser, Wesley T Abplanalp, Tara Procida-Kowalski, Marek Bartkuhn, Evelyn Ullrich, Marion Muhly-Reinholz, Sarmad Ahmad Khan, Tarik Bozoglu, Christian Kupatt, Florian Buettner, Jochen Pöling, Thomas Braun, Kari Alitalo, Stefanie Dimmeler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, but the role of the cardiac lymphatic vasculature in this process remains poorly understood. Here we show that aging reduces cardiac lymphatic vessel density in humans and mice and induces structural remodeling characterized by tighter, zipper-like endothelial junctions. These changes are associated with immune cell infiltration, fibrinogen and amyloid accumulation, and myocardial edema. Selective reduction of cardiac lymphatics in young mice, through Flt4 (VEGFR3) depletion or soluble Flt4 overexpression, recapitulates key features of cardiac aging, including inflammation and impaired lymphatic integrity. Mechanistically, aging induces selective upregulation of nuclear interleukin-33 (IL-33) in lymphatic endothelial cells. Unlike extracellular IL-33, nuclear IL-33 promotes lymphatic endothelial cell death and junctional remodeling. We identify VEGFC as an age-sensitive regulator that declines with aging and suppresses IL-33. Cardiac Vegfc overexpression or Il33 silencing restores lymphatic density and tissue homeostasis in aged hearts, identifying a potential therapeutic target for age-related cardiac dysfunction.Subject terms: Cardiovascular biology, Ageing, Lymphatic vessels

Main

Aging profoundly impacts various physiological processes and drives structural and functional decline in cardiac tissue. These changes include reduced left ventricular diastolic function, myocardial fibrosis, cardiac amyloidosis and microvascular dysfunction. Collectively, they increase the risk of chronic diastolic heart failure in the elderly. Age-related cardiac dysfunction arises from prolonged exposure to genomic and epigenetic damage, oxidative stress, impaired autophagy and the accumulation of waste products and of inflammatory and senescent cells. In addition, age-related deterioration in the structural and functional integrity of the microcirculation imposes further stress on the heart. While the role of the blood vasculature in cardiac aging has been extensively studied1–4, the effects of aging on the cardiac lymphatic system remain poorly understood, warranting further investigation into its role in age-related cardiac dysfunction.

The cardiac lymphatic system plays a crucial role in maintaining heart health by removing excess fluid, proteins and metabolic waste products from the interstitial space of the myocardium5–7. This network comprises lymphatic capillaries, precollectors and larger collecting vessels, distributed throughout the epicardium, myocardium and endocardium, with the highest density observed in the epicardium5,8,9. By facilitating the removal of interstitial fluid, cardiac lymphatics prevent edema and maintain tissue fluid homeostasis10–12. Furthermore, they enable the transport of immune cells and antigens to lymph nodes, supporting immune surveillance and response, while clearing metabolic by-products and cellular debris from the heart tissue. Here, we demonstrated that aging leads to an early decline in lymphatic vessels in the heart, contributing to age-associated alterations in the cardiac tissue.

Results

Aging leads to cardiac lymphatic vessel decline

We determined the impact of aging on cardiac lymphatics in 18–20-month-old C57Bl/6 mice compared with 3-month-old young mice. Whole-mount staining of cardiac lymphatics with LYVE-1 revealed a substantial decline in lymphatic vasculature coverage in old mouse hearts (Fig. 1a). Consistently, cross-sectional LYVE-1 immunostainings showed a significant reduction in lymphatic vessels in both female and male aged mice (Fig. 1b,c and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b), in agreement with a very recent study13. As tissue-resident macrophages may express LYVE1 as well, we further included CD68 as a macrophage marker. To exclude LYVE1+ macrophages, we quantified CD68-negative LYVE1+ cells, which were less abundant in the aged heart section, thus confirming our findings (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Analysis of spatial transcriptomic data14 confirmed the reduction in lymphatic vessel density by aging in humans (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Lymphatic density declined predominantly in the subendocardial region of the left ventricle, whereas right ventricular lymphatics were preserved in old mice (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1e). Epicardial lymphatic vessels additionally showed an enlarged lumen (Fig. 1e), as also has been reported for mesenteric lymphatics in aged rats15. Since LYVE-1 is also expressed by immune cell subpopulations, which may have confounded our analysis, we additionally used podoplanin (PDPN) as a second lymphatic marker. We found that PDPN+ and LYVE-1+ vessels also declined in hearts of 20-month-old mice (Fig. 1f and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). Moreover, protein expression of the lymphatic vessel-enriched VEGF receptor 3 (VEGFR3; encoded by Flt4) was significantly declined in 20-month-old versus 3-month-old mice (Fig. 1g,h).

While lymphatic capillary endothelial cells (ECs) are interconnected via loose ‘button-like junctions’ to allow fluid and immune cell drainage, ECs of precollector and collector tubes are tightly interconnected via a tight ‘zipper-like junction’ to prevent vessel leakage16. Given these distinct junctional architectures, we additionally assessed junction morphology of the lymphatic vessels in the epicardial regions of the hearts (Fig. 1i). Analysis of CDH5+ junctions in LYVE-1+ vessels revealed that aging induces a relative increase in zipper-like structures, but the remaining lymphatics have a similar number of button-like structures (Fig. 1j,k and Extended Data Fig. 1f). Together, these data demonstrate a decline in lymphatic density associated with changes in the junctional architecture in the left ventricle of old mice.

Reduced lymphatic density is associated with age-related defects in the heart

To assess whether reduced lymphatic density in the aged heart may have functional consequences, we determined pathophysiological processes linked to lymphatic dysfunction, including inflammation, edema formation and waste product accumulation. Time course analysis revealed that the decline in lymphatic vessel density, which started at 16 months of age (Fig. 2a), is followed by an accumulation of CD68+ macrophages and CD90+ T cells (pan T cell population), which reached statistical significance at 18 and 22 months of age, respectively (Fig. 2b,c). Dendritic cells also increased at 16 months (Fig. 2d), and LY6C+ monocytes and LY6G+ neutrophils showed progressive accumulation over time (Fig. 2e,f). To assess whether the accumulation of macrophages might indeed be a consequence of declined lymphatic function, we additionally measured macrophages in the draining lymph nodes. Indeed, mediastinal lymph nodes of aged mice (16 and 22 months) exhibited significantly reduced numbers of CD68+ macrophages, consistent with impaired macrophage clearance during aging (Fig. 2g).

Aging was further associated with increased edema formation beginning at 18 months (Fig. 2h), as well as the accumulation of plasma proteins such as fibrinogen starting at 16 months of age (Fig. 2i). We additionally observed an increase in amyloid deposition as assessed by Congo red staining (Fig. 2j,k). All these changes coincided or were present before the onset of cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, which was observed at 18 months, and fibrosis, which became substantial evident at 20 months (Fig. 2l,m), as previously reported17. Echocardiography further confirmed left ventricular hypertrophy and age-related diastolic dysfunction as indicated by an elevated E/E′ ratio (the ratio of early diastolic mitral inflow velocity to early diastolic mitral annular velocity, used as a surrogate for left ventricular filling pressure) (Fig. 2n,o). Systolic function was not changed, as determined by ejection fraction (EF) (Fig. 2p).

Reducing lymphatic vessels induces cardiac inflammation and diastolic dysfunction

To assess whether a direct reduction in lymphatic vessels can induce age-related cardiac phenotypes, we conditionally deleted Flt4, which encodes the essential lymphatic receptor VEGFR3, in lymphatic ECs (LymphECs) of young mice (referred to as Vegfr3iΔLEC). Cre-negative littermates served as controls (referred to as Vegfr3fl/fl). At 1 month after deletion, we observed a significant decline of LYVE-1+ and PDPN+ lymphatic vessel density in the heart (Fig. 3a), which was associated with an increase of CD68+ macrophages (Fig. 3b) and fibrinogen (Fig. 3c). However, we did not observe increased fibrosis at this time point (Fig. 3d). Blood capillary density, perimeter and cardiomyocyte size were also not affected (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Functional assessments by magnet resonance imaging suggests diastolic dysfunction as shown by elevated end-diastolic mass and enlarged left atrial size, while systolic function was preserved (Extended Data Fig. 2b–e). Similar findings were observed when overexpressing the soluble form of Flt4 (sFlt4), which acts as a trap for the VEGFR3 ligands VEGFC and VEGFD and inhibits their signaling, with adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9-sFlt4) (Fig. 3e,f). AAV9-sFlt4-treated mice showed a similar decline in lymphatic vessel density (Fig. 3g), which was accompanied by increased macrophage abundance (Fig. 3h) and fibrinogen accumulation (Fig. 3i). Echocardiographic analysis further revealed a significant increase in diastolic dysfunction (Fig. 3j). Systolic heart function, heart weight and cardiomyocyte size, fibrosis, and blood vessel capillaries were, however, not altered (Fig. 3k–m and Extended Data Fig. 3). Together, these data demonstrate that lymphatic vessel reduction exacerbates accumulation of inflammatory cells, promotes fibrinogen accumulation and induces diastolic dysfunction.

Aging induces expression of nuclear interleukin-33

To determine what drives lymphatic decline in aging, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on isolated Cdh5+ ECs from young and aged mouse hearts. To enrich for lymphatic vessels, we integrated this dataset to previously published scRNA-seq data from young and old hearts18,19 (Fig. 4a). The endothelial clusters were subclustered, and the LymphEC cluster showed the characteristic expression of lymphatic markers Cdh5, Lyve1 and Prox1 (Fig. 4b). Overall, 49 genes were significantly upregulated, while 2 were downregulated in aged LymphECs (Fig. 4c and Extended Data Table 1). The downregulated genes include Cldn5, which is mainly known for its role in maintaining the vascular barrier (Fig. 4d). Among the top upregulated genes, we found complement factor H (CFH), which is a key regulator of the alternative pathway of the complement system, and the cytokine interleukin-33 (IL-33). Il33 expression was specifically enriched in aged LymphECs (Fig. 4d). Immunostaining confirmed the upregulation of IL-33 protein levels in LymphECs at 16 months (Fig. 4e), the time point at which lymphatic vessel density begins to decline during aging (Fig. 2a). IL-33 is a cytokine belonging to the IL-1 family, which regulates fibrosis20, inflammation21,22 and angiogenesis23. In addition, IL-33 has a dual function: it acts both as a classic extracellular cytokine and as a higher-weight nuclear factor. This higher-molecular-weight isoform, often referred to as nuclear IL-33 (nIL-33), was shown to interfere with NF-kB and act as chromatin regulator23. Interestingly, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of isolated cardiac ECs2 revealed elevated expression of the nuclear Il33 isoform, which contains the nuclear localization sequence encoded by exon 3 in samples from aged mouse hearts (Extended Data Fig. 4). Consistently, immunostainings predominantly showed an accumulation of nIL-33 in lymphatics of the aging heart (Fig. 4f). Western blots (WBs) confirmed the upregulation of the higher-molecular-weight nuclear isoform in the aging heart (Fig. 4g).

While the secreted form of IL-33 was shown to induce lymphangiogenesis23, the function of nIL-33 in lymphatics has not been assessed. Therefore, we overexpressed nIL-33 in human dermal LymphECs (HDLECs) in vitro (Fig. 4h,i). Overexpression of nIL-33 reduced proliferation and induced apoptosis of HDLECs (Fig. 4j,k), whereas silencing of Il33 tended to increase cell number (Fig. 4l,m) and reduced zipper formation (Fig. 4n). In line with this, RNA-seq of IL33-overexpressing HDLECs revealed the downregulation of mitosis-related genes such as CDKN1A after IL33 induction (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). Further hallmarks of cellular aging, including senescence, DNA damage, mitochondrial abundance and autophagy were not changed upon IL33 overexpression (Extended Data Fig. 5d–j). Silencing IL-33 in lymphatic vessels of aged mouse hearts by using AAV9 encoding for Il33-targeting short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) rescued lymphatic density and reduced macrophage abundance (Fig. 4o–q). Together, these data suggest that age-induced augmentation of nIL-33 may contribute to the decline and structural alterations of the lymphatic vasculature during aging.

Interferon gamma induces IL-33 expression

Next, we sought to identify the upstream mechanisms driving age-related IL33 expression in cardiac lymphatics. Given that cardiac aging is characterized by persistent low-grade inflammation, commonly termed ‘inflammaging’, we examined whether proinflammatory cytokines—tumor-necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)—could induce IL33 expression in HDLECs in vitro. Among the tested cytokines, only IFN-γ significantly upregulated IL33 in HDLECs after 24 h of treatment (Fig. 5a). Supporting a role for IFN-γ in driving IL-33 upregulation in vivo, Ifng gene expression was elevated in aged hearts and predominantly localized to T cells and natural killer (NK) cells (Fig. 5b,c).

To determine whether T cells are positioned in proximity to lymphatic vessels and may thus engage in crosstalk with lymphatic endothelium, we analyzed spatial transcriptomics datasets from young and old mouse hearts24. These data revealed a close spatial association between T cells and lymphatic vessels in aged hearts (Fig. 5d). Immunofluorescence analysis corroborated the age-related accumulation of T cells (Figs. 2c and 5e) and their close spatial alignment with lymphatics (Fig. 5e). Collectively, these observations are consistent with a model in which cardiac T cells increasingly produce IFN-γ with age, thereby potentially promoting IL-33 expression and contributing to lymphatic dysfunction. Notably, EC-specific deletion of IFN-γ receptor 1 increased lymphatic density in a non-aging heart failure model (Extended Data Fig. 6), supporting a broader role of IFN-γ signaling in limiting cardiac lymphatic growth.

To elucidate how IFN-γ increases the splicing of nIL-33, we further assessed the contribution of transcription factor AP-2 alpha (TFAP2A). TFAP2A acts downstream of STAT126, which was shown to indirectly regulate IL33 splicing, resulting in the inclusion of exon 3, which contains the nuclear localization signal27. Indeed, siRNA-mediated TFAP2A silencing decreased IL33 expression in IFN-γ-treated HDLECs (Extended Data Fig. 7c).

Collectively, these data indicate that IFN-γ activates the JAK/STAT1 pathway to promote IL-33 expression in LymphECs. STAT1 may further induce TFAP2A, thereby promoting the inclusion of exon 3 (nIL-33) in IL-33 mRNA (Extended Data Fig. 7d).

VEGFC reduces macrophage accumulation but does not preserve cardiac diastolic function

Finally, we aimed to modulate Il33 expression in lymphatic vessels of aged mice. To this end, we screened lymphangiogenic growth factors, including VEGFC28, reelin29, apelin30 and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)30, hypothesizing that some may not only promote lymphatic vessel growth but also suppress Il33 expression. HDLECs were treated with recombinant VEGFC, reelin, apelin or S1P, and proliferation was assessed by phospho-histone-3 (PH3) staining. Among the tested factors, only recombinant VEGFC and reelin significantly stimulated HDLEC proliferation (Fig. 5f).

Next, we investigated whether VEGFC or reelin could counteract IFN-γ-induced IL-33 expression. Notably, only recombinant VEGFC attenuated IFN-γ-driven IL33 expression (Fig. 5g). Moreover, VEGFC more effectively reduced apoptosis in HDLECs following lentiviral IL-33 overexpression compared with reelin (Fig. 5h). Interestingly, of all tested growth factors, only Vegfc expression was significantly decreased in cardiomyocytes and endothelial clusters and tended to be reduced in immune cells in old mouse hearts, while Apln and Reln expression were not reduced (Fig. 5i). RT–qPCR confirmed age-related Vegfc decline in total heart samples from young and aged mice (Fig. 5j). Together, these findings suggest that VEGFC deficiency may contribute to age-related lymphatic dysfunction and that restoring VEGFC signaling may mitigate IL-33-mediated impairment of cardiac lymphatics.

To test this hypothesis in vivo, we overexpressed the full-length Vegfc isoform in 18-month-old mice and assessed its effects on cardiac lymphatic structure and function (Fig. 5k). Indeed, overexpression of full-length Vegfc (AAV9-Vegfc FL) decreased IL-33 expression in aged cardiac lymphatics and increased lymphatic vessel density in the aging heart (Fig. 5l,m). Moreover, AAV9-Vegfc FL treatment reduced age-associated accumulation of macrophages in the heart (Fig. 5n) but did not alter blood capillary density or cardiomyocyte size (Extended Data Fig. 8). Age-related diastolic dysfunction was not attenuated either (Fig. 5o). These data suggest that VEGFC rescues the age-associated decline in lymphatic vessel density by repressing Il33 expression and rescues the accumulation of inflammatory cells.

Discussion

Together, our data suggest that aging leads to a marked reduction in cardiac lymphatic vessel density, accompanied by distinct morphological transformations of the vessels, including the emergence of zipper-like endothelial junctions. These structural changes are paralleled by immune cell infiltration, fibrinogen accumulation, amyloid deposition and myocardial edema—hallmarks of cardiac aging and tissue dysfunction (Extended Data Fig. 9). Experimental depletion of lymphatic vessels via Flt4 deletion or overexpression of a soluble Flt4 recapitulates these phenotypes, underscoring a causal relationship. Particularly, we showed that the transfer of immune cells to the lymph nodes was reduced, leading to increased macrophage accumulation in the cardiac tissue. After 2 months of overexpressing soluble Flt4, which reduces lymphatic density by about 50%, we observed development of diastolic dysfunction. However, we did not observe changes in fibrosis or cardiac hypertrophy—typical hallmarks of the aging heart. This indicates that a more prolonged period of lymphatic impairment may be required to drive inflammation-dependent cardiac remodeling. Indeed, while reductions in lymphatic vessel density occur as early as 16 months of age in our mouse models, fibrosis and hypertrophy typically appear later, at >18 months.

Notably, our data indicate that lymphatic rarefaction can contribute to diastolic dysfunction through mechanisms independent of fibrosis or hypertrophy. One plausible explanation is increased myocardial edema, which raises interstitial pressure and impairs cardiomyocyte relaxation. Of note, acute ligation of the right and left epicardial lymphatics in dogs has been shown to impair active contraction and relaxation without inducing fibrosis31; however, although chronic depletion of lymphatic vessels and lymphatic-derived factors does not appear to affect systolic function32,33, its impact on cardiomyocyte relaxation properties has not been explored. Our findings additionally demonstrated increased lipid accumulation in aged hearts, suggesting a potential contribution of lymphatic dysfunction in the promotion of cardiac amyloidosis, a disease with increasing prevalence over the last two decades34,35. However, the relevance in the heart is not well studied. Of note, one recent report suggested that although regions with amyloid deposits typically lack lymphatic capillaries, the overall density of lymphatic capillaries was not significantly reduced in this cohort of patients with cardiac amyloidosis36. Further studies are needed to elucidate the role of lymphatics in fibrosis and amyloidosis, particularly in the aging heart.

At a molecular level, we identify elevated nIL-33 expression in LymphECs of aged hearts. The full-length IL-33 isoform induced LymphEC death and junctional remodeling, thus mirroring the changes that we observed in aging hearts. IL-33 has complex context and cell-type-specific functions, with not only beneficial but also proinflammatory and profibrotic effects reported in the heart20–22. IL-33 protein expression is controlled by splicing, leading to isoforms, which differ in their cellular localization and biological activity. For example, the cytosolic, secreted IL-33 splice variant lacking exons 3 and 4 but not the nuclear form is associated with airway type 2 inflammation25. Our findings suggest that, specifically, the full-length, high-molecular-weight nuclear isoform of Il33 impairs lymphatic vessels. The upregulation of this isoform reduced lymphatic cell survival, which could indirectly affect the clearance of the tissue, thereby supporting inflammaging and accumulation of lipids and fluids. This may explain some of the conflicting results regarding the effect of Il33 depletion in mouse models. On the basis of our data, the nuclear Il33 is expected to indirectly enhance inflammation.

To investigate the upstream regulation of IL-33, we exposed HDLECs to a panel of age-associated cytokines. Among those tested, only IFN-γ increased IL-33 expression. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that IFN-γ induces both the cytokine and nuclear forms of IL-33 in epithelial cells25,37. Interferons have also been reported to inhibit lymphangiogenesis and promote apoptosis in LymphECs38. These findings align with our observation that IL-33 overexpression triggers HDLEC apoptosis and suppresses proliferation, suggesting that interferons may impair the cardiac lymphatic system through IL-33 induction. The molecular mechanisms underlying IFN-γ-induced IL-33 expression in LymphECs, however, remain unclear. In epithelial cells, IFN-γ activates IL-33 transcription via the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and JAK/STAT1 pathways25. In line with this, our data demonstrate that JAK/STAT1 and TFAP2A are involved in the control of lymphatics as well (Extended Data Fig. 9). Further defining of the mechanisms responsible for augmenting nuclear Il33 in LymphECs could reveal potential therapeutic targets to counteract age-related lymphatic dysfunction in the heart.

Collectively, our findings position cardiac lymphatic vessels as an early contributor to inflammation and potentially to functional decline in the aging heart. An important unanswered question is whether restoring lymphatic density can prevent or reverse age-associated cardiac pathologies. Recent studies suggest that enhanced lymphangiogenesis contributes to the cardioprotective effects of exercise13. Consistently, our data demonstrate that overexpression of Vegfc can partially restore lymphatic vessel density and attenuate cardiac inflammaging. Furthermore, selective inhibition of nIL-33 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for age-related cardiovascular diseases. However, given the chronic and progressive nature of cardiac aging, the potential long-term side effects of any therapeutic intervention must be carefully evaluated. Ultimately, strategies enabling timely, precise and cell-type-specific modulation of lymphatic function may offer the most effective and safest avenues for intervention.

Methods

Laboratory animals

Aged male and female C57Bl/6J wild-type mice were purchased from Janvier and from Charles River. Homozygosity of these inbred mice was controlled by Janvier and Charles River using exome sequencing. The mice were kept on a 12-h day–night cycle. All animal experiments were executed in accordance with the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and were approved by the local authorities by the state of Hesse (Regierungspräsidium Darmstadt, approval nos. FU/2096 and FU/2114) or by the authorities in Helsinki (Eläinkoelautakunta, external approval nos. ESAVI/22042/2019 and ESAVI/30720/2022 and internal approval number KEK22-019).

Adeno-associated viral vector serotype 9 delivery

To overexpress full-length VEGFC in aged mouse hearts, we administered adeno-associated viral vector serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors carrying the Vegfc coding sequence under the control of the CAG promoter. To overexpress soluble VEGFR3 (sFlt4) in young mice, we administered AAV9 vectors encoding soluble Flt4. AAV9 particles without payload served as a negative control. The viral particles were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a concentration of 2.2 × 1011 viral particles per animal.

We silenced Il33 expression in cardiac lymphatic vessels of aged mice using AAV9 vectors encoding a shRNA targeting Il33 under the control of the endoglin promoter. Viral particles were coated with a G2-PAMAM linked to an endothelial-targeted peptide (PAMAM-G2CNN), which were generated according to Bozoglu et al.39 and referred to as EC-AAV9-shIL33. Viral particles were diluted in Opti-MEM GlutaMAXTM (51985026, Gibco) and coated with PAMAM G2CNN (1.8 µg of PAMAM-G2CNN to coat 2 × 1012 viral particles) for 30 min at room temperature (RT), before injection. A total of 2 × 1012 EC-AAV9-shIL33 were intravenously injected into 18-month-old C57Bl/6J mice. AAV9 encoding for a control shRNA served as a negative control (EC-AAV9-shCtrl). The animal experiment was conducted as approved by the state of Hesse.

VEGFR3 deletion in LymphECs in vivo

To delete VEGFR3 in LymphECs, the Vegfr3-flox line (referred to as Vegfr3fl/fl) was cross-bred with the Prox1-CreERT2 line (referred to as Vegfr3iΔLEC), as previously described40. Tamoxifen was injected i.p. to adult mice on five consecutive days to induce Cre-recombinase expression. Before and 8 weeks after the first injection, heart function was determined using magnet resonance tomography.

Echocardiography

To assess heart function via echocardiography, mice were anesthetized with 2–2.5% of isoflurane and monitored using the Vevo 3100 echocardiography system with the Vevo LAB software (Fujifilm VisualSonics). Echocardiography was performed before and at 4 and 8 weeks after injection.

Magnet resonance tomography

Cardiac imaging was performed on a 7.0-T Bruker Pharmascan scanner (Bruker), equipped with a 760 mT m−1 gradient system, using a cryogenically cooled four-channel phased-array element 1H receiver-coil (CryoProbe) and a 72-mm RT volume resonator for transmission and the IntraGate self-gating tool. Measurements and analyses were carried out as previously described41.

Edema quantification

To quantify cardiac edemas, the hearts were stained for fibrinogen accumulations (see below) or water content was determined. To quantify cardiac water content, the mice were euthanized, and their hearts were collected without perfusion. Heart weight was determined (wet weight), followed by incubation for 48 h at 65 °C. Heart weight was determined again (dry weight), and the ratio of dry to wet weight was calculated.

Euthanasia

Mice were anesthetized with 2–3.5% of isoflurane and euthanized via cervical dislocation.

WB

Protein expression in mouse hearts was analyzed by immunoblotting. In brief, hearts were homogenized in 1 ml of RIPA buffer (89900, Thermo Scientific) containing ¼-inch ceramic spheres two times for 20 s each at 4 m s−1. After centrifugation (20,000g, 4 °C for 20 min), the supernatants were collected. For immunoblotting, 30 µg of protein lysate was combined with Laemmli buffer, treated for 5 min at 95 °C and separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Proteins that were in gel were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane that was blocked using Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) (1061.1, Carl Roth GmbH & Co. KG) containing 5% skimmed milk (sc-281695, Santa Cruz). After blocking, the membranes were incubated in primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed three times in TBST for 5 min each and incubated in blocking solution containing the Rb anti-IgG antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (1:5,000; NA934V, Cytiva) for 1 h at RT. After washing three times for 5 min each with TBST, the signal was visualized using the chemiluminescence HRP substrate for enhanced chemiluminescence (WBKLS0500, Merck-Milipore).

The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:1,000 for WB; 2118, CST), goat anti-VEGFR3 (1:1,000 for WB; AF743, RnD Systems) and rat anti-IL33 (1:1,000 for WB; MAB5010, RnD Systems).

Analysis of immunofluorescence images

The images were analyzed using Volocity 7 software by Quorum Technologies, and the quantified signals were normalized to the DAPI area.

To visualize button and zipper junctions, CDH5+ areas within LYVE1+ tubes were displayed as an 8-bit image using Fiji. To quantify button and zipper junctions, a Python-based image analysis pipeline was developed to quantify junctional morphologies in LymphECs using processed immunofluorescence images. The Z-stack images were converted into two-dimensional maximum intensity projections to retain fine junctional structures for the purposes of analysis. Binary masks were generated for each protein channel using empirically determined intensity thresholds, selected by visual inspection of multiple representative images to best separate signal from background. The cadherin signal was isolated through the implementation of a binary mask, which retained only pixels above the upper threshold level and below the lower threshold level. The elimination of blood vessel-associated signals was achieved by masking out the IB4 channel, thus excluding regions that were positive for IB4. The resulting image was then further restricted to lymphatic or mitotic regions by intersecting with either the Lyve1 or PH3 mask, depending on the experimental context.

Contiguous clusters of nonzero pixels were identified using an 8-connectivity approach42, where a pixel is considered connected to its horizontal, vertical and diagonal neighbors. Clusters smaller than three pixels were discarded as noise, resulting in the removal of approximately 60–80% of the background signal. The remaining clusters were classified into button (<4.72 μm), intermediate (4.72–7.85 μm) or zipper (>7.85 μm) junctions, using length thresholds adapted from previously published criteria43. These measurements enabled the quantification of specific junction types to assess junctional remodeling within lymphatic regions.

scRNA-seq and data analysis

scRNA libraries were generated using the Gem-X kit (1000686, 10X Genomics), using standard GEM construction, amplification, fragmentation and indexing procedures.

Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a S4 flowcell kit (PE100, depth of 50,000 reads/nucleus) with the following specifications: Read 1: 28 base pairs (bp), Read 2: 90 bp, Index 1: 10 bp and Index 2: 10 bp.

Data processing was performed using Scanpy (version 1.10.1)45 operated under Python 3.11.8. The FASTQ files were mapped to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using the CellRanger (version 7.0.0) software from 10X. The count matrices were processed individually and then combined into one AnnData object. Genes expressed at low levels (<3 cells) and cells with a low number of genes (<800) and high mitochondrial content (>7.5%) were excluded from the analysis. After quality control, gene expression values were normalized and logarithmized using a scaling factor of 10,000. The processed data were integrated with the ECs from a public dataset from young and old mice to increase the statistical power18,19. The integration was performed using BBKNN46, and the Leiden algorithm was used to cluster the data. Annotation was performed using a curated list of markers47,48 and the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings were computed. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and MAST (Model-based Analysis of Single-cell Transcriptomics) with Benjamini–Hochhberh correction for multiple testing.

Spatial transcriptomics

The processed spatial transcriptomics from the left and the right ventricle were obtained from Kanemaru et al.14 and used to estimate the abundance of LymphECs across the different time points for each region (left and right ventricle). The predicted proportions of LymphEC per spots were previously estimated with cell2location as described in the original work14.

Bulk RNA-seq

For genome-wide analysis of gene expression, RNA-seq libraries from isolated mRNA were generated and sequenced by the Institute for Lung Health-Genomics and Bioinformatics, at Justus Liebig University Giessen (Germany). A total amount of 400 ng of RNA per sample was used to enrich for polyadenylated mRNA followed by cDNA sequencing library preparation utilizing the Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After library quality control by capillary electrophoresis (4200 TapeStation, Agilent), cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform generating 50-bp paired-end reads.

Mapping was performed utilizing the STAR aligner with the reference genome GRCh38 (version 2024-A) for human gene annotations. The subsequently mapped reads were then used for differential gene regulation analysis on HDLECs with and without IL-33 overexpression.

Differentially expressed genes were calculated in R (version 4.5.1) utilizing the ‘DESeq’ function specified with the Wald test and default parameters implemented in the DESeq2 package49. P values were adjusted (Padj) for multiple testing using the procedure of Benjamini and Hochberg and only genes with Padj < 0.05 were considered as differentially expressed genes.

Pathway analysis was performed using the webtool EnrichR50–52 and the Gene Ontology database. The terms were calculated for positive and negative DEGs separately and sorted according to the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P value.

Cell culture

HDLECs were purchased from PromoCell (C-12216) and cultured on fibronectin in PromoCell MV2 medium (C-22221) supplemented with the provided supplement mix (C-39221). The cells were cultured for 72 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere.

HDLECs were treated with recombinant IFN-γ (30 ng ml−1; 285-IF-100, R&D Systems), TNF (10 ng ml−1; 300-01 A, Peprotech), IL-1β (100 ng ml−1; 201-LB-025, R&D Systems), G-CSF (100 ng ml−1; AF-300-23-50UG, Gibco), GM-CSF (50 ng ml−1; 300-03-20UG, Gibco), VEGFC (100 ng ml−1; 9199-VC-025/CF, R&D Systems), reelin (100 ng ml−1; 8546-MR-050, R&D Systems), apelin (100 ng ml−1; ab152927, Abcam) and S1P (100 ng ml−1; 73914-1MG, Merck) for 24 h or with recombinant VEGFC (100 ng ml−1), reelin (100 ng ml−1), apelin (100 ng ml−1) and S1P (100 ng ml−1) for 48 h.

To assess autophagy, HDLECs were cultured in fully supplemented medium or Opti-MEM (51985026, Gibco) to starve the cells. The cells were treated with 10 µM chloroquine for 2 h before fixation.

For JAK inhibition, HDLECs were cultured with 5 µM ruxolitinib (83405S, CST) for 24 h.

Lentiviral overexpression

Lentiviral particles were generated by transfecting HEK293T cells (CRL-11268, ATCC) with the packing plasmids pCMV∆R8.91 and pMD2.G, as well as the plasmids harboring the IL-33 sequence (VB241001-1197hwt, VectorBuilder) or a stuffer sequence (VB241001-1200pax, VectorBuilder).

IL-33-overexpressing HDLECs were treated with recombinant VEGFC (200 ng ml−1), reelin (200 ng ml−1) and S1P (200 ng ml−1) for 24 h.

siRNA-mediated gene silencing

To knockdown IL33 in HDLECs, SMART pool On-Target siRNA pools were used, which were purchased from HORIZON (L-015122-01-0005). To silence STAT1, the SMART pool On-Target siRNA pool L-003543-00-0005 was used. To silence TFAP2A, the SMART pool On-Target siRNA pool L-006348-02-0005 was used. A nontargeting pool served as the negative control (D-001810-10-05). siRNAs (5 nM) were transfected using Lipofectamin RNAiMAX (13778075, Invitrogen), which was diluted in Opti-MEM GlutaMAX (51985034, Gibco).

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as mean, and error bars indicate standard error of the mean (s.e.m.). The Shapiro–Wilk test was carried out for normality distribution analysis. An unpaired two-sided t-test and an ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc test were used for comparison of two groups and more than two groups, respectively. Not normally distributed data were assessed using a Mann–Whitney test (GraphPad Prism 10.4.2). For scRNA-seq analyses, differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and MAST with Benjamini–Hochhberh correction for multiple testing. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The investigators were blinded during experiments and outcome assessment.
