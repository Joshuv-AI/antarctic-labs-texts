Association between thyroid feedback quantile‐based index and the risk of in‐hospital amputation in patients with diabetic foot ulcer
Authors: Jiale Wang, Yujie Huang, Funeng Wang, Meiling Lao, Lijun Lan, Peiling Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo investigate the relationship between the thyroid feedback quantile‐based index (TFQI) and the risk of amputation during hospitalization in patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFU).MethodsThis single‐center retrospective study included 381 hospitalized patients with DFU and normal thyroid function. Logistic regression assessed continuous TFQI and quartiles. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and smoking; Model 3 additionally adjusted for diabetes duration, HbA1c, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, hypertension, CRP, and dyslipidemia. RCS assessed dose–response patterns; Firth regression was used for sensitivity analysis.ResultsFifty‐six patients underwent amputation. In Model 3, each 1‐SD increase in TFQI (equivalent to a 1‐unit increase in pre‐scaled TFQI) was associated with higher odds of amputation (OR 2.27, 95% CI 1.63–3.15; P < 0.001). Compared with Q1, ORs were 2.94 (0.98–8.83; P = 0.055), 5.60 (1.88–16.67; P = 0.002), and 14.79 (5.22–41.89; P < 0.001) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively. RCS showed an overall association (P = 0.002) and nonlinearity (P = 0.046) in Model 3. Firth estimates were broadly consistent with conventional regression.ConclusionAmong hospitalized DFU patients with normal thyroid function, higher TFQI levels are associated with an increased risk of amputation during hospitalization. Nevertheless, the clinical utility of TFQI requires further validation in prospective studies.Keywords: amputation, diabetic foot, thyroid function

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is one of the most serious chronic complications of diabetes, characterized by a high risk of infection, difficult healing, and a high recurrence rate. The onset and progression of DFU involve multiple interacting mechanisms, including diabetic peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, infection, abnormal mechanical pressure, and impaired tissue repair. In severe cases, DFU can progress to tissue necrosis and lower‐limb amputation, substantially impairing quality of life and increasing healthcare burden. Despite recent advances in glycemic management, anti‐infective therapy, wound repair, offloading treatment, and revascularization techniques, patients with DFU continue to face a considerable risk of amputation. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Therefore, identifying novel risk factors and biomarkers associated with amputation is important for early risk stratification, individualized treatment strategies, and improved clinical outcomes. 5 , 6

Thyroid hormones participate in the regulation of energy metabolism, glucose and lipid metabolism, cardiovascular function, and tissue repair processes. 7 , 8 Free thyroxine (FT4) reflects circulating thyroid hormone levels, whereas thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH) reflects pituitary feedback regulation in response to circulating thyroid hormone concentrations. 8 , 9 The hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis maintains thyroid homeostasis through the feedback relationship between FT4 and TSH. 8 , 9 Recent studies have shown that even when thyroid function parameters remain within the normal reference range, subtle variations in FT4 and TSH levels may still be associated with metabolic abnormalities, vascular function alterations, and diabetes‐related conditions. 9 , 10 , 11

In patients with DFU, chronic hyperglycemia, infection, local ischemia, and persistent inflammatory states may contribute to systemic metabolic and stress responses, which could potentially influence HPT axis regulation. 3 , 7 , 9 However, individual assessment of FT4 or TSH only reflects a single component of thyroid regulation and may not fully capture the coordination between peripheral thyroid hormone levels and central feedback responses. The thyroid feedback quantile‐based index (TFQI) is a composite indicator constructed based on the feedback relationship between FT4 and TSH. By integrating peripheral thyroid hormone levels with pituitary feedback responses, TFQI is used to evaluate overall feedback sensitivity of the HPT axis. 8 , 10 , 12 Compared with individual thyroid function parameters, TFQI provides a comprehensive assessment of thyroid hormone feedback regulation and offers a potential approach for evaluating subtle alterations in thyroid homeostasis among individuals with thyroid function within the normal range.

In recent years, TFQI and other thyroid hormone sensitivity indices have increasingly been applied in studies of diabetes and related complications. Previous studies have demonstrated that impaired thyroid hormone feedback sensitivity is associated with diabetes and metabolic syndrome, 10 and subsequent investigations have further reported associations between thyroid hormone sensitivity alterations and diabetic nephropathy, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, and microvascular complications. 13 , 14 , 15 However, current evidence has primarily focused on general diabetic populations and metabolic, cardiovascular, or microvascular outcomes. Whether TFQI is associated with amputation risk in patients with DFU, a population characterized by severe tissue injury, infection burden, and high amputation risk, remains unclear.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between TFQI and the risk of in‐hospital amputation among patients with DFU and further evaluate its dose–response relationship and consistency across different clinical subgroups, providing new evidence for early risk stratification and clinical management of patients with DFU.

METHODS

Study population

This study is a single‐center, retrospective, observational study. The study population consisted of patients with DFU who were hospitalized at our hospital between January 2024 and March 2026. General clinical data, diabetes‐related information, foot ulcer characteristics, laboratory test results, thyroid function indicators, and amputation outcomes during hospitalization were retrospectively collected from the hospital's electronic medical record system. This study was approved by the hospital's Ethics Committee (Grant No. KY[2026]207). As this is a retrospective study and all data were anonymized, informed consent was waived.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Met the diagnostic criteria for diabetes; (2) Clinically confirmed diagnosis of diabetic foot ulcer; (3) Thyroid function tests completed during hospitalization, including free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), and thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH); (4) Thyroid function within the normal range, that is, FT3, FT4, and TSH all within the reference range of our hospital's laboratory; (5) Complete data on amputation outcomes during hospitalization.

Exclusion criteria

(1) History of a confirmed diagnosis of thyroid disease, pituitary disease, or disorders related to the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis; (2) Recent use of medications that may affect thyroid function, such as glucocorticoids, amiodarone, antithyroid drugs, or thyroid hormone replacement therapy; (3) Concurrent severe liver failure, renal failure, malignant tumors, acute critical illness, or end‐stage disease; (4) Lower limb amputation due to trauma, tumors, or causes other than diabetic foot; (5) Missing key clinical or laboratory data.

Data sources and variable collection

All data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital's electronic medical record system. The collected variables included the following categories:

(1) Demographic and lifestyle data: Gender, age, body mass index (BMI), place of residence, marital status, smoking history, and alcohol consumption history.

(2) Diabetes‐related data and comorbidities: Duration of diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease, diabetic nephropathy, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, diabetic peripheral vascular disease, lower extremity arterial occlusion or stenosis, lower extremity venous thrombosis, and dyslipidemia, among others. Peripheral arterial disease was recorded as a binary clinical comorbidity based on the available medical records rather than as a quantitative measure of lower‐extremity perfusion severity.

(3) Foot ulcer‐related data: Wagner classification, ulcer area, ulcer depth, presence or absence of fistulas, duration of the ulcer, and whether amputation occurred during hospitalization.

(4) Vital signs and laboratory parameters: systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, hemoglobin, fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and C‐reactive protein (CRP), etc.

(5) Thyroid function parameters: FT3, FT4, and TSH. Thyroid function results were based on the patient's first test after admission.

TFQI calculation methods and grouping

FT4 and TSH were all measured using chemiluminescent immunoassay, and the results from the first thyroid function test performed after hospital admission were selected. The TFQI was constructed based on the negative feedback relationship between FT4 and TSH and was used to assess the pituitary gland's sensitivity to thyroid hormone feedback. Following the method proposed by Laclaustra et al., the cumulative distribution function (CDF) values for FT4 and TSH were first calculated separately in the study population. The TFQI was then calculated using the following formula: TFQI = cdfFT4 − [1 − cdfTSH]. 10 Because the CDF values were derived from the distribution of FT4 and TSH in the current cohort, the TFQI represents a relative measure of central thyroid hormone sensitivity within this study population rather than an absolute biological threshold. A higher TFQI value indicates relatively insufficient TSH suppression at relatively high FT4 levels, suggesting a relatively reduced sensitivity of central thyroid hormone feedback. To compare the clinical characteristics of patients with different TFQI levels, this study divided the study subjects into Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups based on empirical quartile cutoffs for TFQI, where Q1 represents the lowest TFQI group, and Q4 represents the highest TFQI group. These quartile categories were defined according to the TFQI distribution of the present cohort and should therefore be interpreted as relative categories within this study population. Since identical TFQI values may exist at the quartile cutoffs, sample sizes across groups were not required to be exactly equal. In the regression analysis, the TFQI was included in the model both as a standardized continuous variable and as a quartile‐categorized variable; the continuous variable analysis assessed the association between the TFQI and the risk of amputation during hospitalization for each 1‐standard‐deviation increase in the TFQI.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was whether patients underwent a diabetes‐related lower‐limb amputation during the current hospital stay. A diabetes‐related amputation was defined as a lower‐limb amputation performed during the current hospital stay due to diabetic foot ulcers and related causes such as infection, gangrene, osteomyelitis, ischemia, or tissue necrosis. In accordance with the standard definitions of diabetes‐related foot conditions established by the International Diabetic Foot Working Group, amputations were classified as minor or major amputations. 16 Minor amputation is defined as an amputation performed at or distal to the ankle joint, including toe amputation, metatarsophalangeal joint disarticulation, metatarsal amputation, tarsometatarsal joint disarticulation, midtarsal joint disarticulation, and ankle joint disarticulation; major amputation is defined as an amputation performed proximal to the ankle joint, including tibial and fibular amputations, knee disarticulation, and femoral amputations, among others. The outcome was analyzed at the patient level, and each patient was counted only once based on the first amputation occurring during the hospitalization. Bilateral or repeated amputations during the same hospitalization were counted as a single patient‐level outcome.

In this study, minor and major amputations were combined as a composite outcome and recorded as amputations occurring during hospitalization; patients who did not undergo any lower‐limb amputation surgery were recorded as having no amputation. Amputations performed due to trauma, malignant tumors, or other causes unrelated to diabetic foot were excluded from the outcome events.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0 and R software. Continuous variables were first tested for normality. Those following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and comparisons between groups were performed using one‐way analysis of variance; those not following a normal distribution were expressed as median, and comparisons between groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test. Patients with missing data for key variables were excluded. For variables included in the analysis, the proportion of missing values was low; therefore, a complete‐cases analysis was performed. Patients were divided into four groups—Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4—based on the quartiles of the TFQI, and differences in demographic characteristics, diabetes‐related indicators, foot ulcer characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, and amputation rates were compared across different TFQI levels. For ordinal variables or data grouped by quartiles, trend tests were further conducted. A binary logistic regression model was used to analyze the association between the TFQI and the risk of amputation during hospitalization. The TFQI was included in the model both as a continuous variable and as a quartile‐categorized variable. In the continuous variable analysis, the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each 1 SD increase in the TFQI. In the quartile analysis, the Q1 group was used as the reference, and the risk of amputation was calculated for the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups, followed by trend tests. To control for potential confounding factors, three logistic regression models were constructed. Given the limited number of amputation events relative to the number of model parameters, Firth's penalized logistic regression was additionally performed as a sensitivity analysis using the same exposure definitions and covariate sets as those in the primary logistic regression models. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to further examine the dose–response relationship between TFQI and the risk of in‐hospital amputation. The RCS model included the same adjustment variables as the multivariate logistic regression. Two‐sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. TFQI was entered continuously using the prescaled TFQIsd variable in the analytic dataset, calculated as (TFQI +0.09)/0.39; no additional scaling was applied. Thus, the OR represents a 1‐unit increase in TFQIsd (approximately a 1‐SD increase). VIF values were calculated using auxiliary linear regressions. Model discrimination was assessed using AUC with DeLong 95% CIs and Nagelkerke pseudo‐R2. Hosmer‐Lemeshow tests were used for applicable models; the test was not informative for the unadjusted quartile model because it generated only four distinct fitted probabilities.

Statistically significant differences were observed between the different TFQI quartile groups in terms of Wagner grade ≥3, amputation rates, fasting blood glucose, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, FT4, and TSH (P < 0.05). Association between TFQI and the risk of diabetic foot amputation

Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that higher TFQI was significantly associated with increased odds of in‐hospital amputation among patients with DFU. When TFQI was analyzed continuously, each 1‐SD increase (equivalent to a 1‐unit increase in TFQIsd) was associated with higher odds of in‐hospital amputation (Model 3 OR 2.27, 95% CI: 1.63–3.15, P < 0.001). In the quartile analysis, compared with Q1, the ORs were 2.94 (95% CI: 0.98–8.83, P = 0.055) for Q2, 5.60 (95% CI: 1.88–16.67, P = 0.002) for Q3, and 14.79 (95% CI: 5.22–41.89, P < 0.001) for Q4. The primary logistic regression results are presented in Table 2. In the sensitivity analysis using Firth's penalized logistic regression, the results were broadly consistent with the primary analysis. In the fully adjusted model, the Firth‐adjusted OR was 2.16 (95% CI: 1.59–3.01, P < 0.001) for the continuous TFQI variable and 12.26 (95% CI: 4.85–35.87, P < 0.001) for Q4 versus Q1 (Table S2).

Dose–response relationship between TFQI and diabetic foot amputation

RCS analysis showed a significant overall association between TFQI and the risk of in‐hospital amputation across all three models (Model 1: P for overall <0.001; Model 2: P for overall <0.001; and Model 3: P for overall = 0.002). However, the evidence for nonlinearity differed across the models. The nonlinear component was not statistically significant in Model 1 (P for nonlinearity = 0.090) or Model 2 (P for nonlinearity = 0.176), whereas it reached borderline statistical significance in the fully adjusted Model 3 (P for nonlinearity = 0.046). In Model 3, using TFQI = −0.079 as the reference value, the estimated risk of amputation changed relatively gradually at lower TFQI levels and showed an increasing trend as TFQI approached and exceeded 0. The 95% CI widened substantially at higher TFQI levels, indicating limited precision of the risk estimates in this range (Figure 1).

Subgroup analyses of the association between TFQI and diabetic foot amputation

Subgroup analyses adjusted for covariates showed that, in the overall population, a 1‐SD increase in the TFQI remained associated with an increased risk of amputation during hospitalization among patients with DFU (OR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.64–3.19, P < 0.001). Across subgroups defined by the presence or absence of fistulas, as well as by ulcer area, depth, and duration, the direction of the association between the TFQI and the risk of amputation was generally consistent. Interaction tests showed that fistula presence, ulcer area, ulcer depth, and duration of ulceration did not significantly modify the association between TFQI and the risk of amputation. P for the interaction in the duration of ulceration subgroup was close to statistical significance (P = 0.059), suggesting a potential trend toward differences in the strength of the association across subgroups with different durations of ulceration (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

The present study found that higher TFQI levels were associated with an increased risk of in‐hospital amputation among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated by DFU. This finding suggests that, even when conventional thyroid function indicators do not show obvious abnormalities, differences in the FT4‐TSH feedback relationship may be associated with amputation risk in patients with DFU, providing a new perspective for exploring the relationship between thyroid hormone status and adverse diabetic foot outcomes.

Patients with DFU may progress to lower‐extremity amputation due to persistent wound nonhealing, infection progression, and tissue damage. Amputation is not only accompanied by substantial impairment of limb function but is also closely associated with increased long‐term mortality risk, reflecting the severe disease burden of DFU. 16 , 17 Therefore, identifying potential biological indicators associated with adverse DFU outcomes may contribute to early recognition of high‐risk patients and optimization of clinical management. Thyroid hormones participate in multiple physiological processes, including energy metabolism, vascular regulation, inflammatory responses, and tissue repair, and alterations in thyroid hormone status have been associated with metabolic abnormalities and vascular dysfunction. 18 , 19 In the present study, after adjustment for demographic characteristics and DFU‐related clinical factors, TFQI remained associated with in‐hospital amputation risk, suggesting that the FT4‐TSH feedback relationship may provide supplementary information beyond conventional risk factors for assessing amputation risk in patients with DFU.

FT4 and TSH reflect different aspects of thyroid function regulation. FT4 primarily represents circulating thyroid hormone levels, whereas TSH reflects pituitary feedback responses to circulating thyroid hormone concentrations. Compared with evaluating FT4 or TSH individually, TFQI incorporates the relative relationship between FT4 and TSH and provides an integrated assessment of thyroid hormone feedback sensitivity. 20 Previous studies have suggested that altered thyroid hormone sensitivity is associated with metabolic disorders, insulin resistance, cardiovascular abnormalities, and diabetes‐related complications. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 In addition, abnormalities in thyroid hormone signaling may influence vascular homeostasis, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory regulation, which are closely related to diabetic complications. 25 However, previous studies have mainly focused on general diabetic populations or metabolic, cardiovascular, and microvascular outcomes. Evidence regarding the relationship between TFQI and amputation risk among patients with DFU remains limited. Therefore, this study extended the application of TFQI to patients with DFU and evaluated in‐hospital amputation as a clinical outcome, providing preliminary evidence regarding the potential relationship between thyroid hormone regulation patterns and severe diabetic foot outcomes.

Higher TFQI generally indicates relatively insufficient TSH suppression at a given FT4 level, suggesting reduced thyroid hormone feedback sensitivity. 20 Thyroid hormones are involved in glucose metabolism, lipid metabolism, endothelial function, inflammatory regulation, and tissue repair processes, which may influence the biological environment associated with wound healing and tissue survival. 18 , 26 Previous studies have reported associations between thyroid hormone sensitivity indices and metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular risk factors, obesity‐related phenotypes, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated diseases. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 These findings suggest that altered thyroid hormone sensitivity may reflect differences in systemic metabolic and vascular states. In patients with DFU, such biological alterations may be relevant because impaired metabolic regulation, vascular dysfunction, and chronic inflammatory states are important characteristics of patients with poor wound outcomes.

Beyond metabolic regulation, thyroid hormones may contribute to wound repair through effects on angiogenesis and fibroblast function. In vitro evidence indicates that T4 promotes endothelial cell migration and tube formation through integrin αvβ3‐associated signaling, with activation of the PKD/HDAC5 pathway and increased basic fibroblast growth factor expression. 32 An ex vivo human skin wound model also suggests that T4 promotes re‐epithelialization and angiogenesis, partly through fibroblast growth factor‐mediated signaling. 33 Regarding fibroblast function, experiments using primary human dermal fibroblasts showed that inhibition of thyroid hormone deiodination suppressed proliferation, whereas supplementation with T3 restored proliferation, suggesting a role for local thyroid hormone activation in supporting repair‐related cellular activity. 34 These findings provide biological plausibility for a relationship between thyroid hormone signaling and wound repair. However, TFQI reflects the FT4‐TSH feedback relationship and does not directly measure thyroid hormone action or sensitivity within wound tissue. As local angiogenesis and fibroblast function were not assessed in this study, these mechanisms remain possible biological explanations rather than established mediators of the association between TFQI and amputation.

Peripheral ischemia and microcirculatory dysfunction are important contributors to delayed wound healing and adverse limb outcomes in patients with DFU. Reduced tissue perfusion may lead to insufficient oxygen delivery, impaired local immune defense, and delayed tissue repair, thereby increasing the possibility of ulcer progression and amputation. 35 , 36 Previous studies have emphasized the importance of vascular status and revascularization strategies in improving outcomes among patients with diabetic foot disease. 37 Therefore, the association observed between TFQI and amputation risk may potentially reflect the relationship between thyroid hormone regulatory status and vascular, metabolic, or tissue repair‐related conditions. However, detailed measurements of the ankle‐brachial index, transcutaneous oxygen pressure, vascular imaging findings, severity of arterial stenosis, revascularization procedures, and standardized IDSA/IWGDF infection grades were not consistently available and therefore could not be incorporated into the multivariable models. Although peripheral arterial disease was included as a binary covariate, it could not fully reflect the severity of lower‐extremity ischemia. Therefore, residual confounding related to unmeasured vascular impairment and infection severity cannot be excluded.

The RCS analysis demonstrated significant overall associations between TFQI and amputation risk across the three models, whereas the evidence for nonlinearity differed according to covariate adjustment. Specifically, the nonlinear component was not statistically significant in Models 1 and 2 but reached statistical significance in the fully adjusted Model 3. As TFQI increased, the risk of amputation generally increased, although the magnitude of risk elevation varied across different TFQI ranges. Flexible modeling approaches such as RCS are useful for identifying potential nonlinear associations between continuous biomarkers and clinical outcomes without imposing arbitrary categorization. 38 The overall upward trend was broadly consistent with the results of the continuous variable and quartile analyses, although the precise shape of the association was not entirely consistent across the three models. Similar spline‐based approaches have been recommended for exploring dose–response relationships in epidemiological studies. 39 , 40 Nevertheless, the CI became wider at higher TFQI levels, indicating limited precision due to fewer observations and amputation events in this range. Therefore, the nonlinear pattern should be interpreted cautiously. The present findings do not support establishing a clinically meaningful TFQI cutoff value, and TFQI should be considered a potential supplementary marker rather than an independent basis for amputation‐related decision‐making.

Subgroup analyses showed that the association between TFQI and amputation risk remained directionally consistent across subgroups stratified by sinus tract presence, ulcer area, ulcer depth, and ulcer duration, with no statistically significant interactions observed. Interaction analyses in observational studies are generally considered exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when subgroup sample sizes and outcome events are limited. 41 The interaction P‐value for ulcer duration approached statistical significance (P for interaction = 0.059), suggesting a possible difference in association strength among patients with different ulcer durations; however, this finding was insufficient to establish ulcer duration as a definite effect modifier. According to recommendations for interpreting subgroup findings, differences between subgroup estimates should not be considered reliable without adequate statistical evidence of interaction. 42

This study has several limitations. First, its single‐center retrospective design precludes causal inference and may have introduced selection and information bias. Second, TFQI was calculated from a single measurement of FT4 and TSH obtained at admission, which may have been affected by acute illness and could not reflect changes in thyroid hormone feedback over time. Moreover, because TFQI was derived from the empirical distributions within this cohort, its values and quartile cutoffs are population‐specific and should not be interpreted as universal clinical thresholds. Third, detailed indicators of lower‐extremity perfusion and infection severity, including the ankle‐brachial index, transcutaneous oxygen pressure, vascular imaging, revascularization history, and IDSA/IWGDF infection grade, were unavailable. Residual confounding related to vascular impairment and infection severity therefore cannot be excluded. If more severe vascular impairment also clustered with higher TFQI and a greater risk of amputation, this residual confounding could have inflated the observed association. Finally, the limited number of amputation events may have reduced the stability of the multivariable, quartile, subgroup, and RCS analyses. In particular, amputation events were sparse across TFQI quartiles (6, 11, 17, and 22 in Q1–Q4, respectively), limiting the precision of quartile‐specific estimates; the wide confidence interval for Q4 versus Q1 (Firth OR 12.26, 95% CI 4.85–35.87) warrants cautious interpretation. The estimated nonlinear pattern should also be considered exploratory. Larger multicenter prospective studies incorporating repeated thyroid function measurements and comprehensive vascular and infection assessments are needed to validate these findings. Future observational studies should also follow established methodological recommendations to improve transparency and reproducibility. 43

In conclusion, higher TFQI levels were associated with an increased risk of in‐hospital amputation among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated by DFU. This association remained consistent across continuous variable analysis, quartile analysis, and RCS analysis after adjustment for major clinical confounders. TFQI may reflect differences in FT4‐TSH feedback regulation beyond conventional thyroid function indicators and provide new insights into the relationship between thyroid hormone status and adverse outcomes in patients with DFU. Given the observational design, sample size, and limited number of amputation events, these findings require further validation in larger multicenter prospective studies.

CONCLUSION

This study shows that among hospitalized patients with diabetic foot ulcers whose thyroid function falls within the normal reference range, higher TFQI levels are associated with an increased risk of amputation during hospitalization. These findings suggest that the TFQI, which is derived from the feedback relationship between FT4 and TSH, may reflect the state of feedback regulation in the HPT axis beyond that captured by traditional thyroid function markers, and may provide supplementary information for stratifying the risk of amputation in patients with DFU. It should be emphasized that this study was a single‐center, retrospective study and cannot establish a causal relationship between the TFQI and amputation. The TFQI is currently more suitable as a potential risk assessment indicator and should not be used alone for clinical judgment of amputation risk. Future multicenter, prospective studies are needed to further validate the stability and clinical utility of the TFQI in risk assessment for patients with diabetic foot ulcers.
