Validation of the arabic multidimensional facebook intensity scale and its relationships with life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity
Authors: Hilmi Jelleli, Nashwan Nashwan, Omar Hindawi, Mohamed Ben Aissa, Mahmoud Rebhi, Wiem Melki, Mouna Saidane, Noomen Guelmami, Tore Bonsaksen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]Despite Facebook’s widespread use in Arab countries, validated Arabic tools to assess the intensity of its use and related psychological and behavioral outcomes are lacking. This study aims to validate the Arabic version of the Multidimensional Facebook Intensity Scale (A-MFIS) and examine its associations with life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity in a large Tunisian adult sample.[Methods]A cross-sectional online survey was conducted with 1,068 adults. The survey included sociodemographic questions, the A-MFIS, the Arabic Satisfaction with Life Scale (ASWLS), the Arabic Scale of Happiness (ASH), and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). The sample was randomly split into an exploratory group and a confirmatory group. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were used to assess the factorial structure, internal consistency, and construct validity of the A-MFIS.[Results]Analyses supported a second-order model with four factors and 13 items, showing acceptable model fit. The A-MFIS demonstrated strong internal consistency and construct validity. Significant negative correlations were found between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity levels.[Conclusion]The A-MFIS is a reliable and valid tool for assessing Facebook use intensity in Arabic-speaking contexts. It can facilitate future research, clinical assessments, and interventions aimed at improving digital well-being. Future studies should explore its applicability in other Arab cultures and longitudinal settings. This validated instrument provides researchers and practitioners in Arabic-speaking populations with a culturally appropriate tool to assess Facebook usage intensity, enabling future research and interventions on digital well-being.

INTRODUCTION

The remarkable increase in Facebook’s popularity has led researchers to develop valid and reliable instruments to analyse Facebook use and to investigate the relationship of its usage with wellbeing, life satisfaction [1,2] and physical activity [3]. The studies yielded diverse and contradictories outcome in different countries. For instance previous studies have indicated that moderate Facebook use can improve users’ social well-being by strengthening their social capital [4,5] as well as have a positive impact on life satisfaction and happiness [6-9]. On the other hand, other studies support the idea that Facebook use can be harmful [10-13]. In this case, a recent longitudinal study of a German population aged 18 to 56 confirmed that reducing the time spent on Facebook led to positive lifestyle changes [14]. It has been shown that Facebook use can be detrimental to well-being [15,16] For example, previous research findings have also indicated that quitting Facebook, even in the short term, significantly increased levels of cognitive and emotional well-being [17,18]. Another recent study found that overuse of social media can reduce well-being by decreasing levels of beneficial activities such as physical activities [19]. There is evidence that reducing time spent on Facebook increases time spent on offline activities [20-22]. Additionally, a recent study among German university students demonstrated that reducing the intensity of Facebook use allows people to maintain a healthier lifestyle through physical activity [23]. According to a recent study, the direction of the relationship between Facebook use and life satisfaction is not universal. The authors demonstrated that in an Italian population, Facebook use was associated with higher life satisfaction, while in the United States it was associated with lower life satisfaction [24].

Recent research in Arab countries has increasingly examined the complex relationship between social media use and health behaviors, including physical activity. The beliefs of young Saudi women regarding social media and exercise, highlighting the influence of cultural norms on physical activity engagement, have been explored [25]. Determinants of sedentary behavior among Saudi adults, demonstrating that high social media engagement often correlates with lower physical activity levels, have been investigated [26]. Social media use in the UAE was found to influence dietary habits, indirectly affecting participation in physical activities [27].

Daily social media use in Saudi Arabia showed mixed impacts on health behaviors [28]. Awareness campaigns shaping perceptions about physical activity and social media in Arabian Gulf residents were highlighted [29]. Adolescents’ digital life quality in the UAE, showing that social media engagement can influence lifestyle habits, including physical activity, has been examined [30]. The prevalence of sedentary behavior among Saudi university students, emphasizing the impact of digital engagement on active lifestyles, has been studied [31]. Physical activity awareness and practice among healthcare professional students in Saudi Arabia were investigated [32]. Predictors of physical activity levels among residents of Jeddah, demonstrating the influence of social media on exercise patterns, have been analyzed [33]. An Arabic sedentary behavior questionnaire among adults aged 18–30 years was validated, highlighting the need for culturally adapted assessment tools [34].

Collectively, these studies emphasize the nuanced interplay between social media engagement and physical activity in Arab populations, underscoring the need for culturally sensitive, validated instruments to accurately assess social media usage intensity and its association with psychological well-being and health-related behaviors.

Several self-report scales have been used to assess Facebook usage. The first scales for measuring Facebook usage focused on habits such as daily or weekly time spent on Facebook, number of contacts on Facebook, number of Facebook groups joined, and many other Facebook activities such as reading and writing [35-37]. However, the number of hours spent passively on Facebook, the size of one’s network of friends, or belonging to several Facebook groups does not necessarily indicate involvement in Facebook [38]. In a previous study, in order to assess the integration of Facebook into people’s daily activities, a unidimensional scale measuring the intensity of Facebook use was developed based on attitudes towards this platform [39].

More recently, a multidimensional scale for evaluating Facebook use was developed [40]. This tool, which purports to assess the motivation for using Facebook, generated open questions spread over seven dimensions (social connection, shared identities, photographs, content, social investigation, navigation on social networks, and status updates). Subsequently, a Facebook use assessment questionnaire was developed, but it did not present a clear distinction between the factors.

It is essential to recognize the notable gap between the intensity of Facebook use and the underlying motivations for its use [41]. People may have multiple motivations for engaging on Facebook, while exhibiting relatively low intensity in its use. Conversely, individuals may use Facebook intensely even if they do not have a multitude of motivations driving their use [38,42]. In terms of pathology it is necessary to distinguish between Facebook intensity and Facebook addiction [11]. Facebook reflects problematic feelings, urges and behavior [43] whereas Facebook intensity is not necessarily problematic [38,44]. However, intensity of Facebook use and Facebook addiction have been shown to be positively related [11,43-45]. The authors revealed that an addiction to Facebook would be linked to intense use of Facebook but intense use of Facebook does not necessarily refer to an addiction [11,43].

In short, Facebook use intensity refers to the degree of engagement in the platform and is indicative of the depth of Facebook integration into daily routines. Investigating Facebook usage intensity provides a valuable complement to investigations into Facebook-related habits, motivations, and potential addictions, enriching our understanding of users’ interactions with the platform.

To our knowledge, the only available measure that takes into consideration the multidimensional nature of Facebook usage intensity would be the Facebook Multidimensional Intensity Scale (MFIS). MFIS is described as a second-order scale with four factors: perseverance, boredom, overuse, and self-expression. The MFIS has also shown good reliability in terms of internal consistency and temporal stability [38]. Tunisia, with about 8798600 Facebook accounts, occupied the 51th place in the world [46]. A study conducted on young Tunisians aged 12 to 20 years revealed that using Facebook is the most frequent online activity, amounting to 75.1% of all cyber activity among this population [47].

Given the impact of this platform on people’s lifestyles, it is crucial to determine whether heavy Facebook use is associated with measures of psychological well-being, such as life satisfaction and happiness as well as physical activity in the Tunisian population, in which Facebook is commonly used. Research on the intensity of Facebook use in the Arab and, in particular, the Tunisian population is necessary, as previous studies appear to be non-existing. This lack of research on this topic could be due to the lack of a valid measurement tool in Arabic. The development of such a tool would therefore allow researchers to assess the intensity of Facebook use among the Arab population. This would be of help to understand relationships between psychological factors, such as life satisfaction and happiness, and the intensity of use of Facebook. In addition, it would facilitate an understanding of the association between the intensity of use of this platform and physical activity in an Arab population. Thus, this research aimed to explore the psychometric quality of the Arabic version of the MFIS (A-MFIS), and to examine how Facebook usage intensity was associated with life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity in the Tunisian population.

From a theoretical standpoint, the psychological impact of social media platforms such as Facebook has been widely debated in the literature. Scholars have noted that, while social media offers opportunities for social connection, identity expression, and emotional support, it may also contribute to adverse psychological outcomes, such as loneliness, anxiety, and reduced life satisfaction. These outcomes often depend on the user’s motivations and patterns of engagement, particularly when individuals passively consume content rather than actively interact with others [48]. Social Comparison Theory [49] offers one theoretical explanation for these divergent effects. According to this theory, individuals evaluate themselves through comparisons with others. In the context of Facebook, passive use, such as scrolling through curated posts and images, may trigger upward social comparisons, which can evoke envy, self-doubt, or feelings of inadequacy, ultimately diminishing well-being [50,51].

By contrast, active Facebook engagement, such as commenting, messaging, or sharing personal updates, may reinforce social bonds, increase perceived support, and enhance psychological well-being. These contrasting effects underscore that the impact of Facebook is not solely a function of the duration or frequency of use but also depends on the style of engagement and the psychological motives driving such use [52]. A complementary perspective is offered by the Uses and Gratifications Theory (UGT), which provides a robust framework for understanding user behavior on social media platforms. UGT posits that individuals actively choose media channels to fulfill specific psychological or social needs, such as entertainment, self-expression, information-seeking, or social interaction [53]. This theory explains the diversity of Facebook usage outcomes. For example, users motivated by social connectedness may derive emotional support and a sense of belonging, while those primarily driven by self-presentation motives may experience more mixed or ambivalent emotional outcomes [54,55].

Moreover, UGT aligns closely with the structure of the Multidimensional Facebook Intensity Scale (MFIS), which not only accounts for usage patterns but also incorporates users’ motivational and emotional dimensions. Like UGT, the MFIS recognizes that behavior on Facebook is not uniform but shaped by a combination of intentions, gratifications sought, and personal involvement [56,57]. Hence, the integration of these psychological theories with multidimensional constructs such as MFIS enhances the depth, cultural relevance, and contextual specificity of Facebook-related research. This theoretical grounding is particularly pertinent for studying diverse populations, including the under-researched Tunisian context.

In addition to these perspectives, the Displacement Theory offers another relevant explanation. This theory posits that engagement in one activity inevitably displaces time that could otherwise be allocated to alternative, potentially beneficial activities. In the context of Facebook use, prolonged engagement on the platform may reduce the time available for physical activities, social interactions in offline settings, or other health-promoting behaviors. Similarly, the time-use framework emphasizes that individuals have a finite amount of time, and excessive use of social media can crowd out opportunities for exercise and active lifestyles. Recent regional studies confirm this displacement effect; for example, research among Saudi university students highlighted that increased time spent on social media was associated with lower levels of physical activity and higher sedentary behavior [31].

Likewise, a study conducted in the United Arab Emirates revealed that social media engagement influenced lifestyle habits, often at the expense of healthier practices such as sports participation [36]. Another study from Tunisia emphasized that adolescents’ frequent use of Facebook was correlated with reduced time dedicated to outdoor activities and physical exercise [37]. These theoretical perspectives, supported by regional evidence, provide a broader understanding of how Facebook usage intensity may influence well-being not only through psychological mechanisms, but also through behavioral displacement.

METHODS

Instruments

Sociodemographic Questionnaire

This tool was used to gather basic participant information, including gender, economic level, marital status, employment status, and academic qualifications.

Multidimensional Facebook intensity scale (MFIS)

The MFIS includes 13 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), encompassing four subdimensions: Persistence (α = 0.79): Reflects habitual use of Facebook. Boredom (α = 0.85): Use of Facebook as a coping mechanism to alleviate boredom. Overuse (α = 0.76): Indicates excessive usage even in the absence of available time. Self-expression (α = 0.74): Captures the extent to which users express themselves via Facebook. The scale demonstrated strong psychometric properties (CFI = 0.98; TLI = 0.96; RMSEA = 0.05) [38]. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine the interrelationships among the variables. Moderate and statistically significant correlations were observed, ranging from 0.27 to 0.50, indicating moderate interdependence among factors without multicollinearity concerns.

Arabic scale of happiness (ASH)

The ASH is designed to assess happiness within the framework of positive psychology [58]. It comprises 15 core items and five filler statements, each rated on a 5- point Likert scale. Total scores range from 15 to 75, with higher scores reflecting greater subjective happiness. Factor analysis (principal axis factoring with oblique rotation) identified two primary dimensions: General Happiness and Life Success. Item-total correlations ranged from 0.42 to 0.77, supporting strong internal consistency. The ASH showed high reliability and temporal stability, with Cronbach’s alpha values and test-retest reliability coefficients ranging between 0.82 and 0.94. Construct validity was supported through significant correlations with psychological constructs such as mental health, life satisfaction, optimism, love of life, and self-esteem.

Arabic satisfaction with life scale (SWLS)

The SWLS assesses global cognitive evaluations of life satisfaction and consists of five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), yielding scores from 5 to 35. Interpretation ranges from “extremely dissatisfied” (5–9) to “extremely satisfied” (30–35). The SWLS was translated into Arabic, and its psychometric properties were systematically evaluated. Results indicated satisfactory test-retest reliability and internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.83 [58]. Further psychometric analyses demonstrated the scale’s reliability, validity, factor structure, and cultural applicability across various populations. The Arabic version, in particular, showed adequate internal consistency (α = 0.79) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.83, n = 49, p < 0.01) [59].

International physical activity questionnaire – short form (IPAQ-S)

Physical activity levels were assessed using the Arabic-validated short form of the IPAQ [60]. This tool categorizes activity into light, moderate, and vigorous intensity levels based on self-reported frequency and duration during the previous two weeks. Energy expenditure was calculated in MET-minutes per week using standard MET values: 3.3 (light), 4.0 (moderate), and 8.0 (vigorous). These were multiplied by the duration (in minutes) and frequency (in days) of each activity, and summed to compute total physical activity scores.

Translation Procedure and Cultural Adaptation of the A-MFIS

The adaptation of the MFIS was conducted in accordance with the Checklist of Guidelines for Scale Translation and Adaptation recommended by the International Testing Commission. A rigorous, committee-based translation approach was employed, involving three bilingual experts fluent in both Arabic and English, with professional backgrounds in psychiatry and psychology. The translation process began with a forward translation of the original MFIS into Arabic. The draft was then back-translated into English by the expert committee to ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence. Discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were carefully reviewed and resolved on an item-by-item basis, ensuring semantic equivalence, cultural appropriateness, and preservation of the original meaning across all scale dimensions [61].

To further enhance content validity and cultural relevance, the pre-final Arabic version was reviewed by an expert panel comprising psychometrics specialists, Arabic language experts, and subject-matter professionals. Minor wording adjustments were made based on their recommendations to improve clarity and cultural appropriateness. Additionally, a pilot test with a small convenience sample of 30 Tunisian participants was conducted to assess comprehensibility and acceptability of the items. Cognitive interviews were performed with a subset of 10 participants to identify and rectify any potential ambiguities before the main study and minor wording adjustments were made accordingly. The final Arabic version of the MFIS (A-MFIS) was then administered to the main study sample. These procedures ensured a robust translation, cultural adaptation, and content validity of the scale for the Tunisian context.

Population and Sample Size Determination

The target population of this study comprised active Facebook users residing in Tunisia. According to recent statistics, the number of Facebook users in Tunisia is estimated at 8,798,600 [46]. The study specifically focused on users aged 18 to 55 years, representing the demographic group most engaged with the platform. A quantitative cross-sectional design was employed. Participants were recruited using an online survey disseminated via Google Forms between November 8, 2023, and January 11, 2024. Recruitment relied on university mailing lists and various social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, WhatsApp, and Instagram) to maximize accessibility and ensure participant diversity. A total of 1,200 individuals initiated the survey, of whom 1,068 completed it, yielding a completion rate of 89%. After data screening, 132 participants were excluded due to incomplete responses (n = 85), failing attention checks (n = 30), or duplicate entries (n = 17), resulting in a final analytic sample of N = 1,068 participants. Quality control measures, including screening for incomplete responses, attention checks, and duplicate submissions, were implemented to ensure the reliability and integrity of the collected data. Eligibility criteria required that participants (1) be residents of Tunisia and (2) report being active Facebook users.

Psychometric Procedure

The full dataset (N = 1,068) was randomly split into two equal subsamples for psychometric evaluation: the first subsample (n = 534) was used for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and the second subsample (n = 534) for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). This split-sample approach follows recommended best practices for scale validation and helps prevent overfitting. All factor analyses were conducted using software, e.g., SPSS, and reliability indices (Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, λ6) were reported. This approach allowed for independent validation of the scale’s factor structure and enhanced the generalizability of the findings.

Quality Control

Data were collected using an online survey administered via Google Forms. To ensure high data quality, several safeguards were implemented. First, duplicate submissions were prevented by allowing only one response per IP address and by checking for duplicate combinations of demographic fields (e.g., age + email). Second, attention-check items were included (e.g., “Select ‘Agree’ for this item”), and participants who failed these checks (n = 30) were excluded. Third, a minimum completion time threshold was applied, and responses completed in less than 120 seconds were flagged and reviewed for plausibility. Fourth, completion of all core scale items was required to prevent item-level missingness, and participants with more than 20% missing items on key measures were excluded (n = 85). Additionally, clearly inconsistent or outlier responses (including duplicates, n = 17) were removed during data screening. For remaining sporadic missing values, mean imputation was applied for scale items. After these procedures, the final analytic sample consisted of N = 1,068 participants, yielding a completion rate of 89%. All datasets were further checked for duplicates and implausible patterns prior to analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using several advanced statistical software packages, including IBM SPSS version 26, Lavaan within RStudio, and JASP 2020. The initial phase involved a thorough preliminary examination of the numerical data to ensure data integrity and to detect any anomalies or missing values, employing Mahalanobis distance for identifying multivariate outliers. A core part of the analysis was the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA), conducted using the Unweighted Least Squares (ULS) extraction method, paired with Direct Oblimin rotation and Kaiser Normalization. The suitability of the dataset for EFA was confirmed by a Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure above 0.80 and a significant Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (p < 0.05).

The internal consistency of each subscale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α), McDonald’s omega (ω), and Gutmann’s lambda-6 (λ6). The following thresholds were applied to evaluate reliability: Cronbach’s α values above 0.70 were considered acceptable, above 0.80 good, and values between 0.90 and 0.95 excellent. For McDonald’s ω and Gutmann’s λ6, values exceeding 0.70 were deemed satisfactoryn [62]. To confirm the factorial structure of the instrument for the entire sample, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was performed using the robust Diagonally Weighted Least Squares (DWLS) estimation method. Factor loadings were interpreted based on Comrey and Lee’s criteria: loadings greater than 0.71 as excellent, above 0.63 as very good, above 0.55 as acceptable, and below 0.45 as poor [63].

Model fit for the CFA was evaluated using multiple indices, including the Chi-square (χ²) statistic, the χ²/degrees of freedom (χ²/df) ratio, the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Acceptable model fit was indicated by CFI and TLI values exceeding 0.95 and an RMSEA below 0.08, in line with the recommendations of Hu and Bentler [64]. Finally, concurrent validity of the Arabic Multidimensional Facebook Intensity Scale (A-MFIS) was assessed through correlation analyses with related constructs measured by the Arabic Happiness Scale (AHS), Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ).

RESULTS

The exploratory factor analysis yielded four distinct factors, with Factor 1 demonstrating the most substantial explanatory power. It possessed the highest eigenvalue of 5.40, accounting for 41.50% of the total variance, indicating that this factor captured the largest share of information. The subsequent factors, Factor 2 (eigenvalue = 1.33), Factor 3 (eigenvalue = 1.26), and Factor 4 (eigenvalue = 1.17), explained 10.20%, 9.70%, and 9.00% of the variance, respectively. Together, the four factors cumulatively explained a substantial proportion of the total variance, reinforcing the underlying structure of the scale (see Figure 1). These results reflect the dimensionality of the scale and provide a foundation for examining associations between Facebook intensity and outcomes such as life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity.

Reliability

The internal consistency of the Arabic version of the MFIS (A-MFIS) was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and Guttman’s lambda squared (λ2) coefficients, both established indicators of reliability in psychometric assessments [65,66]. As shown in Table 2, all four subscales demonstrated high internal reliability. The Persistence factor yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.853 (95% CI[0.832, 0.872]); the Boredom factor yielded 0.853 (95% CI[0.830, 0.874]); the Self-Expression factor showed 0.852 (95% CI[0.829, 0.873]); and the Over-Use factor demonstrated the highest reliability with an alpha of 0.876 (95% CI[0.856, 0.893]). These alpha values exceed the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70, indicating strong internal consistency [67].

Similarly, Guttman’s λ2 coefficients revealed consistent patterns: Persistence (λ2 = 0.854, 95% CI[0.837, 0.872]), Boredom (λ2 = 0.853, 95% CI[0.835, 0.869]), Self-Expression (λ2 = 0.853, 95% CI[0.830, 0.873]), and Over-Use (λ2 = 0.876, 95% CI[0.860, 0.890]). Guttman’s lambda squared provides a more accurate lower-bound estimate of reliability than Cronbach’s alpha in certain conditions [68,66]. These high λ2 values indicate a substantial proportion of shared variance among items within each factor, demonstrating that the items within each subscale are consistently measuring related constructs.

These results collectively indicate strong internal consistency, suggesting that items within each factor reliably measure the same underlying construct. Moreover, the narrow confidence intervals enhance the stability and precision of these estimates, which is important for the scale’s use in research and clinical settings [69]. Item-total correlation analyses further revealed that none of the individual items, if removed, would substantially change the reliability coefficients, indicating coherence among items within each factor [70].

Figure 2 presents the results of the final confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the Arabic version of the MFIS (A-MFIS). The model fit indices indicate an excellent correspondence between the model and the observed data. Specifically, the Chi-square statistic was 64.52 with 64 degrees of freedom, yielding a Chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio close to 1, which suggests minimal discrepancy between the observed and model-implied covariance matrices [71].

Multiple incremental fit indices also demonstrated very good fit: Normed Fit Index (NFI) = 0.990, Relative Fit Index (RFI) = 0.988, Incremental Fit Index (IFI) = 1.00, Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) = 1.00, and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) = 1.00, all exceeding conventional cutoffs of 0.90, and particularly the CFI and TLI values of 1 indicate a near-perfect fit [64]. Residual-based indices supported model adequacy, with the Root Mean Square Residual (RMR) at 0.048 and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) at 0.070 (CI = 0–0.026) [72]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the CFA model fits the data well, suggesting that the A-MFIS items are appropriately organized according to the intended factor structure in this Arabic-speaking sample.

Concurrent Validity and Effect Sizes

Table 5 presents the Pearson correlation coefficients, significance levels (p-values), and 95% confidence intervals for the relationships among the Subjective Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), the Arabic Happiness Scale (AHS), the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), and the Arabic version of the Modified Facebook Intensity Scale (A-MFIS). Effect sizes were interpreted following Cohen’s [73] guidelines: r ≈ 0.10 = small, r ≈ 0.30 = moderate, r ≈ 0.50 = large. Moderate correlations were observed for life satisfaction and happiness, while physical activity showed a small correlation with Facebook intensity.

The analysis revealed a moderate negative correlation between SWLS and A-MFIS (r = -0.407, p < 0.001, 95% CI[-0.456, -0.356]), suggesting that higher intensity of Facebook use is associated with lower life satisfaction. Similarly, a moderate negative correlation was found between AHS and A-MFIS (r = -0.438, p < 0.001, 95% CI[-0.485, -0.388]), indicating that greater Facebook engagement corresponds to lower levels of reported happiness. In contrast, a small negative correlation was observed between IPAQ and A-MFIS (r = -0.222, p < 0.001, 95% CI[-0.278, -0.164]), implying that more intensive Facebook use is modestly linked to reduced physical activity.

These results indicate that higher scores on the A-MFIS are associated with lower levels of life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity in this sample. According to Cohen’s [73] guidelines for effect size interpretation, the correlations with life satisfaction (r = -0.407) and happiness (r = -0.438) represent moderate negative associations, whereas the correlation with physical activity (r = -0.222) represents a small negative association. It is important to emphasize that these are correlational relationships; no causal inferences can be drawn from these cross-sectional data. These patterns are consistent with prior studies reporting associations between intensive social media engagement and indicators of psychological well-being and health-related behaviors [74,75]. Collectively, these findings support the concurrent validity of the A-MFIS by demonstrating meaningful and interpretable associations with well-established measures of well-being and physical activity, in line with accepted psychometric standards [64,71,72].

DISCUSSION

This research aimed to validate the A-MFIS and study its association with life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity among the Tunisian general population. Following the results of the exploratory factor analysis, the 13 items from the original questionnaire [38] were retained. The confirmatory factor analysis supported the existence of a second-order four-factor structure. Regarding reliability, the coefficients indicate high levels of internal consistency reliability for all factors, with narrow confidence intervals suggesting reasonable confidence in the estimated values. The overuse factor showed the highest reliability, which suggests that this dimension of Facebook intensity is particularly robust and consistent in the Arabic-speaking context.

In addition, the correlation coefficients between the scale sum score, AHS, SWLS and IPAQ were negative and moderate. These results confirmed the concurrent validity of the A-MFIS. Our results align with those of the initial validation of the MFIS, in which the second-order factor explained almost 95% of the total covariance [38]. This consistency in a different cultural context demonstrates the applicability and generalizability of the MFIS. Additionally, the results of the present study revealed a robust factor structure, high reliability, and good fit of the second-order four-factor model. Therefore, the A-MFIS would be a valid instrument by which to assess the intensity of Facebook use in the Arabic-speaking context.

This study highlighted the potential of the MFIS for cross-cultural applications, including Arabic-speaking populations, in surveys of social media use and its impacts. The results of the present study contribute to the growing body of research on the potential psychological implications associated with the use of Facebook in different cultural context. such as dependency, as measured by the Bergen Facebook addiction scale (BFAS) [76], or frequency and emotional attachment, as measured by the Facebook Intensity Scale [39]. The Bergen Facebook addiction scale has also demonstrated strong psychometric qualities in various cultural contexts [76]. However, it constitutes only a tool in the identification of addictive behaviors with dimensions such as salience, tolerance, mood modification, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse [76].

In contrast, the results of the current study offer a more comprehensive view of engagement on Facebook that extends beyond the addictive tendencies measured by the Bergen Facebook addiction scale. Although the A-MFIS overuse factor and the Bergen Facebook addiction scale both assess excessive Facebook use, the difference lies in the extent of the problems. The Facebook overuse factor of the A-MFIS refers to less severe problems (example: using Facebook to the detriment of sleep), than the pathological aspects of Facebook use measured by BFAS. An earlier study developed a one-dimensional scale named the Facebook Intensity Scale [39]. This scale was created to assess the frequency of use of Facebook, emotional attachment to this platform, and the perceived importance of Facebook in life [39].

In contrast, the present study demonstrated the multidimensional measurement qualities of A-MFIS, allowing for the assessment of a broader spectrum of the Facebook intensity concept. The included dimensions enable researchers to delve deeper into the qualitative aspects of use of Facebook, such as motivations resulting from persistence and self-expression, as well as potential negative consequences associated with excessive use. Although A-MFIS assesses intensity of use rather than addiction to Facebook, it can still provide valuable information about patterns of engagement on Facebook and their potential associations with user well-being.

The relationship between Facebook intensity, life satisfaction, and happiness is a complex interaction that reflects the multifaceted nature of online interactions and their impact on individuals’ well-being [77]. The results of the present study revealed a moderate negative correlation between satisfaction with life and excessive Facebook use. This result is consistent with the literature which highlights that frequent and intense engagement on Facebook may lead to decreased life satisfaction [11,15-17]. For example, a randomized controlled study was conducted among 140 German Facebook users [11]. The results of that study demonstrated that reducing the time spent on Facebook by 20 minutes daily for two weeks reduced active and passive use of Facebook.

The authors also revealed a considerable increase in life satisfaction due to the reduction in Facebook usage [11]. The present study highlights the importance of conscious and moderate Facebook use to maintain higher levels of life satisfaction. Alternatively, considering the possibility of reversed causality, it may be that people who are discontent with life are more inclined to use Facebook and other social media with a higher level of intensity. In this perspective, Facebook intensity may be viewed as an indicator of life satisfaction, rather than the cause of reduced life satisfaction.

More research, in particular longitudinal research, is needed to better understand the interplay between these factors. Contrary to our results, previous research has shown that Facebook use is positively associated with life satisfaction [18,19,20]. The authors revealed that people use Facebook not only to meet their needs, but also to contribute to their life satisfaction. They also add that satisfaction with the Facebook connection through communication, common events and a comparison of one’s position in relation to other users can lead to better life satisfaction [19]. On the other hand, following the evaluation of the influence of the use of social networks on life satisfaction in a population of students, a recent study indicated that there is no direct influence of social media use on life satisfaction [21]. Additionally, the results of the present study revealed a significant negative correlation between the IPAQ and the A-MFIS. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that individuals who spend more time on Facebook tend to be less physically active.

Furthermore, the results of the present study revealed a negative correlation between the A-MFIS and the AHS scores. These results suggest that people who interact moderately or weakly with Facebook report higher levels of happiness on the AHS. The results of the present research corroborate those of a previous study conducted on 782 Korean women. That study showed that social networks such as blogs, Instagram, LinkedIn and Facebook had a negative impact on happiness [22]. Additionally, disagreeing with our results, previous research has claimed that Facebook use can promote happiness among users [14]. The authors revealed that Facebook users were happier compared to those who did not use this platform. On the other hand, recent research has shown that there is no association between Facebook intensity and happiness [78].

Consistent with our results, previous studies have demonstrated the direct effect of time spent on social media on the time spent on physical activity [79-81]. Additionally, a study of 782 adults aged 25 to 75 in the United States suggested that middle-aged and older adults who used social media tended to engage in less frequent physical activity [82]. On the other hand, our results contradict those of other research which has stated that moderate use of social media is not detrimental to the health and well-being of adolescents [83,84].

Some research has suggested that overuse of social media is not harmful, whereas problematic use of social media decreases adolescent health [85,86]. It is important here to distinguish between these two aspects of social media use. The overuse of social media refers to the high frequency of communication via social media such as instant messengers and social networking sites. In contrast, problematic social media use refers to pathological behaviors similar to those of addiction. Thus, overuse of social media is not necessarily problematic [87].

A recent study of Canadian youth found that the level of physical activity was correlated with the nature of social media use. The authors demonstrated that intense active use of social media was associated with higher daily physical activity. On the other hand, they concluded that inactive and problematic use of social media was significantly associated with lower physical activity.

This study’s validation of the A-MFIS and investigation into its associations with life satisfaction, happiness and physical activity in the general Arab population in Tunisia highlights the need for culturally appropriate assessments of the use of social media. The A-MFIS allows for a comprehensive understanding of the intensity of Facebook use, helping researchers explore the nuanced dimensions of online behavior. These results highlight the importance of promoting conscious and balanced use of this platform to improve well-being and maintain a healthy lifestyle. Practitioners can use this information to develop targeted interventions aimed at fostering positive online habits and encouraging offline activities. Overall, this research contributes to a deeper understanding of the possible psychological and activity-related implications of Facebook intensity in the Tunisian context.

IMPLICATIONS

Theoretical Implications

The present study contributes to the theoretical understanding of social media use in several ways. First, by validating the A-MFIS in an Arabic-speaking population, it extends the cross-cultural applicability of Facebook intensity measurement tools. Second, the study demonstrates how multidimensional constructs (persistence, boredom, overuse, self-expression) offer a richer framework for understanding online engagement than previous unidimensional or addiction-focused scales [38,39,76]. Third, the observed associations between Facebook intensity, life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity provide empirical evidence for theoretical models such as Social Comparison Theory and Uses & Gratifications Theory. Specifically, Social Comparison Theory suggests that intensive Facebook use fosters upward comparisons that may reduce well-being, while Uses and Gratifications Theory explains how fulfilling needs for self-expression and entertainment may come at the cost of reduced physical activity. The correlations observed in this study (Table 5) are consistent with these predictions: moderate negative associations were found between A-MFIS scores and both life satisfaction (r = –0.407) and happiness (r = –0.438), while a weaker negative correlation was observed with physical activity (r = –0.222). Finally, the robust factor loadings reported in Tables 3 and 4 provide strong support for the multidimensional conceptualization of Facebook Use. Specifically, Persistence, Boredom, Self-Expression, and Overuse function as distinct yet interrelated components of intensive engagement, confirming the adequacy of the hierarchical model and reinforcing the theoretical argument for a multidimensional approach.

Practical Implications

The findings have several practical implications. Health professionals and educators can use insights from the A-MFIS to develop interventions promoting mindful and balanced Facebook use. By identifying users at risk of overuse or low well-being, targeted strategies can be implemented to encourage positive online habits and engagement in physical activity. Policymakers and digital wellness initiatives can leverage these results to design culturally relevant programs in Tunisia and other Arabic-speaking regions, fostering healthier digital environments and enhancing public mental health outcomes.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE RESEARCH

Like any study, the present research has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the associations reported here describe correlational relationships only. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to examine how Facebook use, life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity may evolve over time and interact in Arab populations.

Second, participants were recruited via online convenience and snowball sampling through university mailing lists and social media platforms (Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram) across different regions of Tunisia, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Arabic-speaking population. Future studies should adopt more geographically diverse and representative sampling strategies to strengthen external validity and further validate the A-MFIS and examine causal pathways between social media use and health behaviors.

Third, the study relied on self-report measures, including the A-MFIS, the Arabic Happiness Scale (AHS), and the Arabic Satisfaction with Life Scale (ASWLS). Self-report data are susceptible to social desirability and recall biases, which may affect the accuracy of responses. Future research could integrate objective measures or mixed-method approaches to complement self-report data.

Fourth, although multiple data quality control procedures were applied (e.g., attention checks, minimum completion time thresholds, duplicate removal), some response biases may still persist. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the psychometric properties of the A-MFIS and its associations with life satisfaction, happiness, and physical activity in Tunisian adults. Addressing these limitations in future research will enhance the robustness, generalizability, and applicability of the findings.

CONCLUSION

The present research demonstrated that the A-MFIS is a valid and reliable tool for assessing key dimensions of Facebook use, including persistence, boredom, overuse, and self-expression. The study further revealed significant negative associations between Facebook intensity and both life satisfaction and happiness among Tunisian adults. These findings suggest that excessive engagement with Facebook may adversely impact psychological well-being and reduce involvement in health-promoting behaviors such as physical activity. Promoting mindful and moderate use of Facebook could therefore serve as an effective strategy to enhance individual well-being while encouraging more active lifestyles. In addition, these results provide empirical support for theoretical frameworks such as the Social Comparison Theory and the Uses & Gratifications Theory, highlighting the complex interplay between social media engagement, motivational factors, and psychological outcomes. Future research is warranted to examine these relationships longitudinally, explore causal mechanisms, and expand the investigation to more diverse Arab populations. Such work would further inform the design of culturally sensitive digital wellness interventions and public health strategies aimed at mitigating potential negative consequences of excessive social media use.
