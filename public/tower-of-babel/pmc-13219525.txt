Facies classification and channel detection for reservoir characterization using self-organizing maps in geologically complex Groningen gas field, the Netherlands
Authors: Sarah El-Attar, Abdel-Khalek El-Werr, Eman Mohamed Abdel-Rahman, Mohamed Nabil Sobhy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Facies classification and channel detection remain challenging in geologically complex reservoirs. Faulting, thin-bed effects, and wavelet interference strongly influence seismic responses in such settings. This study presents an integrated multi-attribute seismic workflow utilizing Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) to enhance facies classification and delineate gas-bearing channels within the Permian Rotliegend reservoir of the Groningen Field, the Netherlands. A 3D post-stack seismic volume and four wells were utilized. The workflow integrates seismic interpretation, colored inversion, and unsupervised machine learning. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to evaluate attribute variability, followed by sensitivity testing to refine the final attribute set used in SOM classification. The SOM workflow successfully delineates NW-SE to NNW-SSE gas sand channels, consistent with the known fluvial-aeolian depositional framework of the Rotliegend Formation. Validation against well-log-derived lithofacies shows good agreement. The results highlight that multi-attribute SOM analysis provides clearer channel geometry and improved facies discrimination compared to individual seismic attributes.

Introduction

Reservoir characterization is critical for hydrocarbon exploration and production. It is crucial for understanding subsurface heterogeneity, estimating hydrocarbon volumes, and planning field development. Key tasks include facies classification and channel detection, which control the distribution of porosity, permeability, and fluid flow within the reservoir.

Facies classification in the structurally complex and heterogeneous gas-bearing system of the Groningen Field, onshore northeastern Netherlands, is a major challenge. Conventional methods such as core analysis and well-log interpretation are often time-consuming and lack sufficient accuracy. Therefore, a lower-cost method providing similar or improved accuracy and resolution is desirable1.

Machine learning (ML) techniques, particularly Self-Organizing Maps (SOMs), have recently become effective tools for addressing these challenges. SOMs are unsupervised artificial neural networks. They map similar high-dimensional data into neighboring clusters, producing ordered feature maps. They are also known as topology-preserving maps as they preserve the topological relationships among clusters on the SOM grid. This approach provides a clear and effective way to visualize and analyze datasets2,3. SOM algorithms have been widely used in seismic facies analysis4–7.

Roden and Santogrossi8 demonstrated that seismic multi-attribute analysis using SOM provides a more accurate assessment of thin beds and DHI characteristics compared to conventional methods. This classification process utilizes natural patterns in multi-attribute seismic space that are not limited by the resolution of conventional amplitude data. This enables interpreters to produce higher-resolution interpretations of reservoirs and stratigraphy.

According to Smith9 SOM has detected high-quality natural clusters in attribute space, allowing resolution well below the conventional seismic thin-bed resolution. Chopra and Marfurt10 demonstrated that SOM is an effective unsupervised approach for integrating multiple seismic attributes to produce meaningful and interpretable seismic facies classifications.

El-Dabaa et al.11 demonstrated that a multi-attribute ML workflow can effectively enhance seismic facies analysis of deltaic channel systems. The workflow combines PCA for attribute selection and SOM for unsupervised facies identification. This approach provided a clearer delineation of lithofacies and channel features compared to traditional interpretation methods.

Seismic attributes are common inputs to ML algorithms to determine seismic facies10,12,13. However, selecting the optimal combination remains a critical step. One popular method to choose seismic attributes is to apply principal component analysis (PCA). It is an unsupervised ML technique applied to multi-dimensional datasets to generate a set of principal components ranked by data variability. These components analyze the ability of individual seismic attributes to differentiate features in the seismic data volume14. Although PCA may better represent the data mathematically, it may not help differentiate geologic features of interpretation interest14–16. Lubo-Robles and Marfurt16 found that small-scale geological features with low reflectivity are less clearly represented in the first principal components, but are better captured in subsequent components representing the remaining variability of the data.

The Groningen Field represents an ideal case study for such geological complexity. The field is characterized by faulted blocks, varying stratigraphic thicknesses, and subtle channel features, which contribute to highly heterogeneous facies distributions. This study evaluates the effectiveness of using multiple seismic attributes within an established workflow to characterize diverse fluvial-deltaic environments and addresses the challenge of selecting the optimal attribute combination for accurate facies classification.

Geological setting

Groningen gas field, one of Europe’s largest natural gas fields, covers an area of approximately 900 km2. It is located onshore in the northeastern Netherlands between Latitudes 53° 03′ 12″ N and 53° 33′ 06″ N and Longitudes 6° 24′ 34″ E and 7° 9′ 38″ E. The field lies within the Southern Permian Basin (SPB) gas province, an extensive sedimentary basin stretching from the UK in the west to Poland in the east. Geological structures

The Groningen gas field has a remarkable structural setting. The field is a large structural high that originated during the Late Kimmerian tectonic phase19. It is bounded by a network of predominantly normal extensional faults along its western, southern, and eastern margins. These faults are primarily the result of Mesozoic tectonic activity, particularly during the Late Jurassic to Early Cretaceous rifting phase17. The main fault trend is NNW-SSE with further E–W and N–S trends.

Stratigraphic framework

The Westphalian coal measures and the basal Namurian organic-rich shales are the field’s primary and secondary gas sources, respectively20–23. The charge history of these rocks is due to heating events at the end of the Carboniferous uplift and during the Late Jurassic-Early Cretaceous rifting21,23. The combination of these source rocks with the structural evolution of the SPB has contributed to the field’s substantial hydrocarbon potential. These hydrocarbons migrated into the overlying Upper Permian Slochteren Formation, which hosts the main reservoir units of the Upper Rotliegend.

The Upper Rotliegend is subdivided into the Slochteren and Silverpit Formations. In the north of the field, the sandy and proximal Slochteren Formation passes laterally into the distal and clay-rich evaporitic Silverpit Formation. In the south of the field, the Slochteren Formation forms a massive conglomeratic sandstone sequence that is difficult to subdivide. The Silverpit Formation is subdivided into the Ten Boer Claystone and Ameland Claystone Members. The Ten Boer Claystone Member acts as a widespread seal above the Slochteren Formation, while the Ameland Claystone Member separates the Lower and Upper Slochteren Members24. These clastics were deposited under predominantly arid conditions, characterizing a desert environment. Sand dunes and river-formed wadis are prominent features, reflecting the mixed fluvial and aeolian processes that shaped this sedimentary environment.

The Zechstein evaporites overlie the Upper Rotliegend and form the regional effective seal in the field. They consist of rock salt, anhydrite, and dolomite beds that date back to the late Permian19. The Zechstein salt is a critical geological feature, acting as a detachment layer that strongly influences the field’s structural setting. Materials and methodology

Based on open-source data from the Netherlands Groningen field, this study used a sophisticated 3D seismic volume alongside four selected wells. The analysis focused on a segment of the seismic survey, spanning in-line range 8414 to 9405, and cross-line range 8073 to 9088, extending to a depth of 4543 m. Seismic interpretation was conducted using Petrel (2022) software, developed by Schlumberger Service Company, to delineate key subsurface structural and stratigraphic features. The Groningen field geological model was developed by Nederlandse Aardolie Maatschappij, NAM (2020), and published via the European Plate Observing System-Netherlands EPOS-NL26.

Seismic interpretation

Obtaining good data quality below the Zechstein Formation is challenging, particularly beneath the salt domes and walls along the field’s eastern and western boundaries. Nevertheless, the general shape and fault directions were mapped. The strong impedance contrast between the basal Zechstein anhydrites and carbonates and the softer underlying Rotliegend creates a sharp reflector that can be traced throughout the Southern Permian Basin17.

Well logging analysis

Well-log interpretation identifies different facies units with lithology and fluid distribution based on log response changes27. Formation evaluation over the Slochteren Formation was conducted using a full suite of logs from three wells (PPS-Z1, SDB-1, and KPD-12). The log dataset includes caliper, density, gamma ray, sonic, neutron, and resistivity logs. Evaluation of the petrophysical characteristics demonstrated that the Rotliegend reservoir exhibits good quality sandstone with effective Porosity ranging from 16 to 18 percent, water saturation from 27 to 33 percent, and gas saturation from 67 to 73 percent.

Litho-saturation cross-plots are essential for well-log interpretation. They provide a clear illustration of the gross character of the petrophysical parameters in terms of lithology fractionation and fluid saturation. Seismic colored inversion

The fast-track-colored inversion (CI) technique is a post-stack inversion method, introduced by Lancaster and Whitcombe28. It transforms seismic data into layered relative acoustic impedance using a single convolutional operator. First, acoustic impedance logs are converted from the time domain to the frequency domain, producing an amplitude spectrum. Seismic data are similarly converted to the frequency domain to obtain their corresponding spectrum. Next, an operator is computed to represent the difference between the log spectrum and the seismic spectrum. This operator is obtained by subtracting the seismic spectrum from the modeled spectrum, and the resulting difference is converted back to the time domain. Finally, a trace-by-trace convolution of the stacked seismic data with this operator is performed to generate the colored inversion results. Quality control (QC) is conducted by comparing the residuals between the inverted acoustic impedance and the well-log-derived impedance.

Seismic colored inversion is more effective than traditional methods such as model-based and band-limited inversion28. Conventional methods are time-consuming, costly, and not routinely applied by the interpreter. In contrast, seismic colored inversion is fast, cost-effective, user-friendly, and robust.

To apply colored inversion in the Groningen field, four wells (PPS-Z1, SDB-1, KPD-12, and SWO-1) along with the cropped seismic volume were utilized. The analysis focused on a depth interval of 2400 to 3400 m. To validate the results, a blind-well test was conducted. Accordingly, the SWO-1 well was excluded from the inversion process.

Seismic attributes selection

Seismic attributes selection plays a critical role in facies classification and reservoir characterization29,30. Seismic attributes are measures of information extracted from seismic data 31. They are used to map features from basin to reservoir scale and for quantitative and qualitative seismic interpretation 32,33. An appropriate attribute subset might be built by retaining relevant attributes and discarding the irrelevant ones34. Relevant attributes are highly correlated with the output classes, while redundant attributes are highly correlated with each other. Barnes35 suggests that many seismic attributes are redundant, which causes confusion in seismic interpretation and poses problems in machine-learning classification35,36.

The geological setting of the Groningen Field guided the selection of the initial seismic attributes used in this study. The Upper Rotliegend reservoir is characterized by a complex network of normal faults and lateral facies transitions with the presence of thin claystone and evaporite seals. These features require a multi-dimensional attribute set capable of capturing variations in structure, lithology, porosity, and thin-bed response. Therefore, several attributes were extracted from the seismic data volume. Based on a meticulous filtering process, we retained only the attributes with meaningful interpretive value and discarded those with minimal contribution.

The variance attribute cube was first extracted from the seismic volume to capture spatial discontinuities and delineate the fault network, compartmentalizing the Groningen Field37.

Subsequently, ten attributes were selected: instantaneous phase, Hilbert transform, envelope, sweetness, relative impedance, instantaneous frequency, dominant frequency, and spectral decomposition (SD) at discrete frequencies of 20 Hz, 30 Hz, and 40 Hz (low, mid, and high, respectively).

Instantaneous phase significantly improves the identification of geologic features such as pinch-outs and reflector continuity38. Hilbert transform, which involves a 90-degree phase shift of the seismic real trace, helps delineate faults and lithological changes by highlighting discontinuities39.

The envelope attribute highlights gas bright spots, lithology, and thickness tuning40. The sweetness attribute helps identify “sweet spots” and estimate relative net-to-gross ratios41. It is a relative number of response amplitude divided by the square root of response frequency. Sweetness is highly sensitive to gas fluid effects and attenuation because it amplifies low-frequency, high-amplitude responses. Relative acoustic impedance is closely related to variations in rock properties. It shows band-limited impedance contrasts that often correlate with key geological features such as porosity. High contrast may indicate possible sequence boundaries, unconformity surfaces, and discontinuities42.

Instantaneous frequency can be used as a hydrocarbon indicator and a sand/shale ratio indicator. It is also used as a bed thickness indicator; thinner shale bedding is indicated by higher frequencies, while sand-rich bedding is indicated by lower frequencies43.

The dominant frequency attribute plays a crucial role in characterizing subsurface geological features. Variations in dominant frequency can indicate changes in lithology or reservoir fluid properties44. It also helps estimate vertical resolution, as higher dominant frequencies allow the detection of thinner beds. Frequency variations can reveal stratigraphic features that are not visible in amplitude alone and are particularly sensitive to channel architectures.

Spectral decomposition (SD) is particularly sensitive to channel architectures and sand thickness. It decomposes the seismic data with normal frequency bandwidth into a set of sections with discrete or very narrow bandwidth45. SD is widely used for layer thickness estimation, identifying channel architectures, identifying DHI characteristics, determining fluid types, and lithologies46.

Unsupervised machine learning workflow

Python was used to apply the unsupervised machine learning workflow, utilizing multiple libraries including NumPy https://numpy.org/, Matplotlib https://matplotlib.org/, Pandas https://pandas.pydata.org/, Segyio https://segyio.readthedocs.io/en/latest/segyio.html, Scikit-learn (sklearn) https://scikit-learn.org/stable/ and Xarray https://docs.xarray.dev/en/stable/. NumPy provides efficient numerical operations and array handling. Matplotlib enables data visualization through plots, charts, and graphs. Pandas facilitates data manipulation and analysis for tabular datasets. Segyio allows reading, writing, and handling seismic data in SEG-Y format. Scikit-learn (sklearn) implements machine learning algorithms such as classification and regression. Xarray is designed for working with labeled multi-dimensional arrays, making it suitable for scientific datasets. Together, these libraries facilitated efficient coding, data handling, and model training. The details of the workflow are presented in the following sections.

Principal component analyses (PCA)

PCA is a linear mathematical technique widely used to reduce data dimensionality and decrease data redundancy 47,48. It reduces a large set of seismic attributes to a smaller, more manageable subset while retaining most of the independent information15. The first principal component represents the largest proportion of variance in the dataset, while subsequent components capture smaller fractions of the remaining variability48. The contribution of each attribute to each principal component is measured through eigenvalue and eigenvector analysis49. Each eigenvector has an eigenvalue that indicates the degree of variance in the data50. This analysis helps reduce computation time and enhance the clarity of attribute correlations before ML applications 14,51. Nevertheless, higher variability in PCA may not necessarily be relevant to geological features14,15.

The ten selected attributes were subjected to PCA to identify those most sensitive to channel-related seismic responses at the depth of interest. Based on the contribution values of the attributes in the eigenvectors, those with a relative contribution greater than or equal to 80% of the maximum contribution were initially retained. Self-organizing map (SOM)

After selecting the seismic attributes using PCA, the dataset was prepared for unsupervised analysis. SOM is a neural network used for facies classification and pattern recognition in seismic datasets53. It reduces high-dimensional seismic data into a 2D topological map. In this map, neighboring neurons represent geologically related patterns54. Each neuron represents a group of data points with similar attribute behavior, and their organization preserves their relative similarity55. By adjusting the number of neurons in the 2D map, the interpreter can control the number of natural clusters5.

In this study, eight seismic attributes were analyzed using an 8 × 8 two-dimensional grid (64 neurons). The grid size was selected after initial tests, as it provided an optimal balance between model resolution and computational efficiency. Smaller grids (e.g., 4 × 4) failed to capture the full variability of the seismic attribute space, while larger grids (e.g., 12 × 12) produced over-segmented and geologically unrealistic facies clusters. Thus, the 8 × 8 grid was used as the optimal configuration for facies heterogeneity in the Groningen Field. The model was trained for 100 iterations, which was sufficient to achieve stable convergence. Fewer iterations increased quantization error, while additional iterations provided no significant improvement. A Gaussian neighborhood function was employed to ensure smooth topological ordering. The neighborhood radius was initially set to half of the grid dimension and gradually reduced through an exponential decay schedule. This ensures that early iterations perform global map organization, while later iterations refine the local structure of the clusters. A decaying learning rate was used to rapidly establish overall data topology and refine neuron weights toward convergence. This schedule starts with a relatively high value and gradually decreases over the 100 iterations. A fixed random state of 64 ensured reproducibility. Together, these hyperparameters: grid size, learning rate schedule, neighborhood function, and number of iterations were selected based on sensitivity tests to produce stable and geologically meaningful facies patterns.

Discussion

The Groningen Gas Field presents a challenging environment for seismic facies classification due to the combined effects of intense faulting, reservoir compartmentalization, and thin, laterally discontinuous gas-bearing sands. In such settings, seismic responses are influenced by tuning effects, wavelet interference, and fault-related block boundaries rather than true depositional facies variations.

The initial selection of the ten seismic attributes was guided by the geological complexity of the Rotliegend reservoir and the expected seismic response of channelized sand bodies. These attributes include instantaneous phase, Hilbert transform, envelope, sweetness, relative impedance, instantaneous frequency, dominant frequency, and spectral decomposition (SD) at discrete frequencies of 20 Hz, 30 Hz, and 40 Hz.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was subsequently applied to evaluate attribute variability and reduce redundancy. It selected eight attributes that represent the largest variance in the data. In this instance, instantaneous phase and Hilbert transform were discarded. Envelope and sweetness were highly ranked in the second eigenvector. However, their redundancy was further confirmed by the correlation matrix. When these attributes were included in the SOM analysis, the neurons became dominated by high-energy gas-related responses. The resulting SOM 2D color map is less interpretable Fig. 10.

At this stage, attributes are discussed in terms of their contribution to the SOM results rather than their distinct interpretational meaning. Envelope responds to energy-related artifacts rather than true depositional facies. It cannot discriminate whether high amplitudes arise from actual sand bodies or constructive wave interference. Sweetness is strongly influenced by gas effects and attenuation rather than by lithologic variations. Consequently, the SOM classification becomes dominated by gas-related responses rather than by channel geometry or facies architecture. In contrast, frequency-dominated attributes improved the imaging of sand channels. These attributes are sensitive to sand thickness, vertical resolution, gas presence, and channel architecture. Relative Impedance is more tied to rock property variations.

After excluding envelope and sweetness, the SOM analysis relied on frequency behavior, spectral signatures, and impedance contrasts. This resulted in cleaner channel geometries, improved continuity across fault blocks, and more effective discrimination of gas-bearing channel sand facies Fig. 11. This attribute combination produced SOM results that are more consistent with the known depositional framework of the Rotliegend reservoir. The final attribute set was determined through sensitivity testing within the SOM analysis. In this context, attributes were evaluated based on their ability to generate geologically consistent and interpretable facies patterns.

Because PCA evaluates attributes based on variance within eigenvectors, rather than quantitatively estimating the impact of different attribute combinations, it may suggest attributes that are not optimal for the objective. Therefore, the selection of input attributes for seismic facies analysis cannot rely on PCA alone and remains partly dependent on the interpreter’s geological understanding and experience.

However, the effectiveness of this iterative approach in refining attribute selection and SOM outcomes highlights the importance of integrating geological insight with statistical analysis, though it may still be limited by the interpreter’s ability to recognize subtle patterns and integrate multi-scale geological knowledge, potentially leading to non-unique solutions. Conclusions

This study demonstrates that a multi-attribute Self-Organizing Maps (SOM) workflow provides an effective approach for seismic facies classification and gas channel detection in the geologically complex Rotliegend reservoir. The integration of seismic interpretation, colored inversion, and multiple seismic attributes enables improved discrimination between reservoir and non-reservoir facies compared to single-attribute analysis. Validation against well-log-derived lithofacies confirms the reliability of the SOM results, with an overall classification accuracy of approximately 88% and an F1-score of 84%. The SOM-derived facies map delineates compartmentalized gas sand channels trending NW–SE to NNW-SSE, consistent with the known fluvial-aeolian depositional setting of the Upper Rotliegend Formation. The study further highlights that optimal attribute selection cannot rely on PCA alone. Geological knowledge and sensitivity testing are essential to achieve meaningful and interpretable facies classification.
