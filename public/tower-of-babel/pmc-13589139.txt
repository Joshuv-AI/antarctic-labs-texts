Prevalence, genomic characterization, and biofilm-forming capacity of extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Escherichia coli from faecal samples of broiler chickens in Jinan City, China
Authors: Yunchen Zhou, Rongkun Jing, Rong Xi, Ban Li, Zhiyong Wu, Dexian Zhang, Jichang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Escherichia coli in food animals threatens both veterinary and human medicine by compromising critically important antimicrobials. This study characterized the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance profiles, molecular epidemiology, and virulence attributes of ESBL-producing E. coli isolated from broiler farms in Jinan City, China. From 600 faecal samples, 537 E. coli isolates were recovered (89.5 % isolation rate), with 71 (13.2 %) identified as ESBL producers. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed markedly more severe resistance among ESBL-producing isolates, with complete ampicillin resistance (100 %) and high-level resistance to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (94.37 %), streptomycin (87.32 %), chloramphenicol (78.87 %), and tetracycline (70.42 %). Resistance to extended-spectrum cephalosporins cefuroxime and ceftriaxone reached 43.66 % and 36.62 %, respectively, while amoxicillin/clavulanic acid susceptibility declined to 61.97 %. Whole-genome sequencing identified blaCTX-M-55 (29.58 %) as the predominant ESBL genotype, followed by blaCTX-M-64 (23.94 %), blaCTX-M-15 (19.71 %), blaCTX-M-14 (12.68 %), and blaCTX-M-65 (9.86 %). The plasmid-mediated colistin resistance gene mcr-1 was detected in 25.35 % of ESBL-producing isolates, indicating a substantial reservoir of last-resort antibiotic resistance determinants, though physical linkage between mcr-1 and ESBL-encoding genes remains undetermined due to short-read sequencing limitations. Multilocus sequence typing revealed ST117 (18.3 %) as the predominant sequence type, associated mainly with serogroup O78 (25.4 %). Virulence gene profiling demonstrated high prevalence of the serum survival gene iss (85.9 %), commonly associated with avian pathogenic E. coli, though avian pathogenicity was not experimentally confirmed. Notably, 90.14 % of ESBL-producing isolates demonstrated biofilm-forming capacity, with 16.90 % classified as strong biofilm producers forming mature, mushroom-shaped microcolonies. These findings demonstrate that broiler chickens in the sampled Jinan farms constitute a significant reservoir of multidrug-resistant, biofilm-forming ESBL-producing E. coli harboring clinically relevant genotypes, including mcr-1. The predominance of ST117, reported in both poultry and human clinical settings, along with extensive co-resistance profiles, underscores the value of integrated surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship in poultry production, though direct zoonotic transmission evidence requires further comparative genomic and epidemiological investigation.Keywords: ESBL-producing E. coli, Broiler chicken, Antimicrobial resistance, blaCTX-M, Biofilm

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the most pressing global health challenges of the 21st century. Among resistant pathogens, Escherichia coli (E. coli) that produce extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) are of particular concern (Zinsstag et al., 2023). ESBLs are enzymes that hydrolyze and confer resistance to a wide range of β-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins and extended-spectrum cephalosporins (e.g., ceftriaxone, cefotaxime), which are critically important for treating severe human infections (Paterson and Bonomo, 2005). This resistance phenotype severely limits therapeutic options, often relegating treatment to last-resort antibiotics like carbapenems or colistin, leading to increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.

The food animal sector, especially the poultry industry, is increasingly recognized as a crucial reservoir for ESBL-producing E. coli (Kuan et al., 2024). The global demand for chicken has driven intensification of production, often accompanied by the extensive use of antimicrobials for therapeutic, metaphylactic, and growth promotion purposes (Guo et al., 2021). In many countries, third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins are used in hatcheries and farms, creating a potent selective environment that favors the emergence and persistence of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae (Nguyen et al., 2019).

Chickens can become colonized with ESBL-producing E. coli early in life and shed these bacteria in their feces, acting as a silent reservoir within the farm environment (Robé et al., 2021). The faecal shedding contaminates the farm environment and, subsequently, during the slaughtering process, faecal contamination of skin and carcasses can occur, allowing these bacteria to enter the food supply chain (Aydin et al., 2024). This establishes a critical pathway for human exposure. Transmission to humans can occur through direct handling of contaminated raw meat, cross-contamination in kitchens, or consumption of undercooked products (Seo et al., 2021). Notably, molecular epidemiological studies have revealed a high genetic similarity between ESBL-producing E. coli isolates from chicken, poultry products, and human clinical cases, providing strong evidence of zoonotic transmission and the role of the food chain in the spread of AMR (Langkabel et al., 2023; Lopez et al., 2025).

Despite growing awareness, the full extent of the problem remains undefined. Variations in prevalence exist across geographic regions, production systems (e.g., conventional vs. organic), and farming practices. Furthermore, the diversity of ESBL gene variants (such as blaCTX-M and blaSHV) and their mobile genetic elements (plasmids) complicates tracking and control efforts (Mandujano et al., 2023). Understanding the local epidemiology, risk factors along the production chain, and the specific genetic mechanisms driving the spread of these pathogens is essential for developing effective One-Health interventions.

In China, ESBL-producing E. coli in poultry has been documented in several regions, including Guangdong, Henan, and other areas of Shandong Province (Liu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021). However, comprehensive genomic surveillance data remain geographically uneven, and the resistance genotypes, clonal lineages, and virulence profiles of ESBL-producing E. coli can vary substantially across different production regions due to local differences in farming practices, antimicrobial use patterns, and bacterial population structures. The Jinan region, despite being the capital city of Shandong Province, one of China's largest poultry-producing provinces, has been underrepresented in genomic AMR surveillance studies. Furthermore, most existing genomic studies on poultry-derived ESBL-producing E. coli in China have not included phenotypic biofilm assessment, and the relationship between biofilm-forming capacity and bacterial genotypic characteristics in these isolates remains poorly characterized. Additionally, the co-carriage of mcr-1 and blaCTX-M genes in E. coli from poultry in the Jinan area has not been systematically investigated since the initial discovery of mcr-1 in China. Addressing these gaps is important for establishing region-specific baseline data that can inform local antimicrobial stewardship policies and for understanding the dissemination dynamics of clinically relevant resistance determinants in intensive poultry production systems.

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to characterize ESBL-producing E. coli isolated from faecal samples collected from broiler farms in the Jinan region through an integrated approach combining phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing, biofilm formation assessment, and whole-genome sequencing-based molecular characterization, including MLST, phylogroup typing, serogroup prediction, distribution of resistance and virulence genes, and phylogenetic analysis.

Materials and methods

Sampling and farm information

Chicken feces were sampled from 10 broiler farms between 2022 and 2024 according to the standards set by National Poultry Improvement Plan (USDA, 2012). The farms were selected based on their willingness to participate and were confirmed to be epidemiologically independent (i.e., no shared personal, equipment, or vehicles between farms). Detailed farm-level information is summarized in Table S1.

The participating farms varied in size, with flock capacities ranging from approximately 10,000 to 50,000 birds per production cycle. All farms operated under conventional intensive production systems typical of the region, with birds raised on litter flooring in enclosed housing. The sampling was conducted during the late finishing phase (approximately 5-6 weeks of age, prior to slaughter).

Information on antimicrobial usage (AMU) during the production cycle was obtained through farm records and interviews with farm managers. Where available, the classes of antimicrobials administered and the purpose of use (therapeutic, metaphylactic, or prophylactic) were documented. However, complete AMU records were not available for all farms, and antimicrobial use history is reported descriptively where data were available (Table S1).

Briefly, approximately 10 g of freshly voided droppings was collected using sterile spatulas from 20 locations within a farm, and ensuring minimal contact with litter or soil. Each sample was then placed into a sterile tube containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH=7.2, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to maintain viability. Samples were transported to the laboratory in a cooler and stored at 4°C until use. For each faecal sample, a single presumptive E. coli colony was randomly selected from the MacConkey agar plate for further identification and analysis.

All samples were collected specifically for this study. E. coli isolation, identification, and initial preservation were performed contemporaneously with sample collection between 2022 and 2024. The isolates were subsequently retrieved from frozen stocks for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, ESBL screening, and genomic analysis as described below.

Bacterial identification

Samples were individually inoculated into 200 mL Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB, Oxoid, Shanghai, China) with novobiocin (100 μg/mL, MeilunBio, Dalian, China) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Pre-enriched E. coli broth with novobiocin (MeilunBio) was streaked onto MacConkey agar (AOBOX, Beijing, China) plates and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. One pink colony per sample was presumptively identified as E. coli and then subjected to matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) using a Microflex LT instrument (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

E. coli isolates used in this study were recovered from the faecal samples collected as described above and stored at −80°C in glycerol stocks until further use. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the microdilution method in MHB (Oxoid) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2017). A loopful of each E. coli isolate preserved in glycerinated MHB (Oxoid) was streaked on Mueller Hinton agar (MHA, Oxoid, Shanghai, China) and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Three colonies were inoculated in MHB (Oxoid), and the culture was adjusted to a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland standard (105-106 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL) using sterile normal saline. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined in 96-well microtiter plates. Each plate included positive growth control wells (containing MHB and bacterial inoculum without antimicrobial agent) and negative (sterility) control wells (containing MHB only). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. All determinations were performed in triplicate on separate occasions, and E. coli ATCC 25922 was included as a reference strain. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of antimicrobial agent that completely inhibited visible bacterial growth, as assessed by unaided visual inspection. Antimicrobial agents were purchased from MeilunBio (Dalian, China), and listed in Table 1. Antimicrobial susceptibility was interpreted according to the clinical breakpoints established by CLSI (2017).

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined according to the consensus criteria as non-susceptibility (i.e., resistant or intermediate) to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories. The 10 antimicrobial agents tested in this study were grouped into the following nine categories based on the agent class definitions: (1) penicillins (ampicillin), (2) β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (amoxicillin/clavulanic acid), (3) second-generation cephalosporins (cefuroxime), (4) third-generation cephalosporins (ceftriaxone), (5) aminoglycosides (gentamicin, streptomycin), (6) fluoroquinolones (enrofloxacin), (7) folate pathway inhibitors (sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim), (8) phenicols (chloramphenicol), and (9) tetracyclines (tetracycline).

Screening for ESBL-producing E. coli isolates

All E. coli isolates were screened for the production of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL) using the disc diffusion method according to the CLSI Guideline (CLSI, 2017). Isolates showing inhibition zone diameters of ≤27 mm for cefotaxime and ≤22 mm for ceftazidime were considered suspected ESBL-producers.

Suspected isolates were further tested using a phenotypic confirmatory test. Briefly, bacterial suspensions adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard were inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA, AOBOX, Beijing, China) plates. Four antimicrobial discs were placed on each plate: ceftazidime (30 µg), ceftazidime-clavulanic acid (30/10 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), and cefotaxime-clavulanic acid (30/10 µg) (all discs from MeilunBio, Dalian, China). Plates were incubated at 37°C for 16-18 hours. Inhibition zones were measured, and an isolate was confirmed as an ESBL producer if the zone diameter for either ceftazidime-clavulanic acid or cefotaxime-clavulanic acid was ≥ 5 mm larger than the zone diameter for the corresponding agent tested alone, in accordance with CLSI interpretive criteria (CLSI, 2017). The ESBL prevalence was calculated using the total number of E. coli isolates as the denominator.

Genomic DNA sequencing and analysis

DNA of ESBL-producing E. coli isolates was extracted using a TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China) and a Qubit fluorometer (ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China). Only DNA samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 and a concentration ≥ 20 ng/μL were used for library preparation.

Sequencing was performed using an Illumina Nextera XT library with 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads (BGI, Shenzhen, China), yielding an average of 2,232,425 reads per isolate (63 × average coverage). Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic v0.39 with the following parameters: removal of adapter sequences, trimming of bases with a Phred quality score below Q20 from both ends, exclusion of reads with an average quality score below Q20 over a 4-bp sliding window, and discarding reads shorter than 50 bp after trimming. The quality of both raw and trimmed reads was assessed using FastQC v0.11.9.

Filtered reads were de novo assembled using SPAdes v3.15.5 (Bankevich et al., 2012) with the '–careful' flag to reduce the number of mismatches and indels. Assembly quality metrics were evaluated using QUAST v5.0.2. Assemblies were retained for downstream analysis only if they met the following quality thresholds: total assembly size between 4.5 and 5.8 Mb, N50 ≥ 30,000 bp, and number of contigs ≤ 400. Genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM v1.1.3, with completeness ≥ 95 % and contamination ≤ 5 % required for inclusion. The draft genomes of the 71 ESBL-producing isolates had an average total assembly size of 5.12 Mb (range: 4.68–5.67 Mb), an average N50 of 132,456 bp (range: 48,235–298,764 bp), and an average of 198 contigs per genome (range: 85–378).

Genome annotation was performed using Prokka v1.14.6. Multilocus sequence types (MLST), plasmid replicon types, serotypes, virulence genes and antimicrobial resistance genes were identified using MLST 2.0, PlasmidFinder 2.0, SerotyperFinder 2.0, VirulenceFinder 2.0 and ResFinder 3.0, respectively, available from the Center for Genomic Epidemiology database (http://genomicepidemiology.org/). All analyses were performed between March and May 2025 using the CGE web-based services with their respective default databases. The database versions used were as follows: MLST (E. coli scheme, updated 2024-12-17), PlasmidFinder (database version 2024-10-15), SerotyperFinder (database version 2024-08-01), VirulenceFinder (database version 2024-09-30), and ResFinder (database version 2024-12-02). The following thresholds were applied for gene identification: minimum 90 % identity and minimum 60 % coverage for antimicrobial resistance genes (ResFinder default); minimum 85 % identity and minimum 60 % coverage for virulence genes; and minimum 95 % identity for plasmid replicon identification (PlasmidFinder default). The MLST 2.0 tool was used with the E. coli Achtman scheme for sequence type assignment. SeroTyperFinder 2.0 was used for in silico serotyping with default parameters (minimum 85 % identity, minimum 60 % coverage). Plasmids were also analyzed using PLACNETw (https://castillo.dicom.unican.es/upload/) for plasmid reconstruction and visualization. Phylogroup assignment was performed using the ClermonTyping method (Clermont et al., 2013), which assigns E. coli isolates to phylogroups (A, B1, B2, C, D, E, and F) based on the in silico detection of four genetic markers (arpA, chuA, yjaA, and TspE4.C2). The analysis was conducted using the ClermonTyping web-based tool (version 2023; http://clermontyping.iame-research.center/) with default parameters.

Parsnp v2.0 was used to align the core genome of ESBL-producing E. coli isolates against the reference genome E. coli K-12 MG1655 (GenBank accession: NC_000913.3), to call single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and to generate a core-genome maximum likelihood tree with 1,000 bootstrap resamples (Treangen et al., 2014). The core-genome alignment identified a total of 2,000 SNP positions across the 71 isolates. Recombination filtering was not performed; therefore, the inferred phylogenetic relationships should be interpreted with caution, as recombination events may confound true evolutionary distances. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and annotated using iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de/).

Biofilm formation

Biofilm formation was measured using a microtiter plate assay as previously described (Stepanovic et al., 2000) with modifications. The assay was performed in three independent experiments, each conducted on separate days. In each independent experiment, three biological replicates were tested per isolate. Briefly, each ESBL-producing E. coli isolate was incubated in tryptic soy broth (TSB, AOBOX, Beijing, China) at 37°C for a duration of 24 h. Subsequently, aliquots were diluted to achieve a turbidity equivalent to 0.5 on the McFarland scale. From each resultant bacterial suspension, 200 μL was inoculated into three wells of a 96-well microtiter plate (Corning, Shanghai, China). In each plate, three wells containing sterile TSB served as negative controls. To monitor plate-to-plate variation, the reference strain E. coli ATCC 25922 was included as an internal control on each plate, and the negative control OD values were required to be ≤ 0.10 for the plate to be considered valid. Following an additional incubation period of 24 hours at 37°C, the bacterial suspensions were aspirated, and the wells were washed three times with a 0.85 % sodium chloride solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Adherent bacteria were fixed by adding 200 μL of methanol (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15 min; thereafter, the plates were emptied and allowed to air-dry. Subsequently, the wells were stained with 200 μL of 2 % crystal violet for 5 min, after which the dye was removed by rinsing with running water, and the plates were dried at ambient temperature. The crystal violet bound to adherent cells was resolubilized using 160 μL of 33 % glacial acetic acid, and the absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader (ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China).

The mean OD value from the three biological replicates of each independent experiment was calculated. The final OD value for each isolate (ODi) was obtained by averaging the mean OD values from the three independent experiments. The cut-off OD value (ODc) was defined as three standard deviations above the mean OD of the negative control wells, calculated separately for each plate: ODc= mean OD negative control + 3 × SD negative control. Plate-to plate variation was assessed by comparing the OD values of the ATCC 25922 reference strain across plates; the coefficient of variation (CV) was maintained below 10 % across all plates. Based on the classification scheme described as reported (Lee and Lee, 2026), isolates were categorized into four distinct groups: non-biofilm producers (ODi ≤ ODc); weak biofilm producers (ODc < ODi ≤ 2 × ODc); moderate biofilm producers (2 × ODc < ODi ≤ 4 × ODc); and strong biofilm producers (ODi > 4 × ODc).

Biofilm observation using confocal laser scanning microscopy

To perform confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), three strains were randomly chosen from each of the strong, moderate, and weak biofilm-forming categories. Biofilm formation was visualized with an Olympus FV1000-IX81 inverted confocal microscope fitted with a 60 × oil immersion objective (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), following a previously described protocol (Zhang et al., 2020). A bacterial suspension was prepared by inoculating 3 to 5 colonies into TSB broth (AoBox) and incubating at 37°C for 18 h. The culture was then diluted to 10⁵−10⁶ CFU/mL, and 2-mL volumes were transferred to 35-mm glass-bottom petri dishes (ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China). After another 18-h incubation, the broth was removed, the wells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4, Solarbio), and the adherent biofilms were stained with the LIVE/DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability kit (ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Imaging was performed on the same confocal system. The SYTO 9 signal was acquired using 488-nm excitation with emission collection at 520 nm, while propidium iodide was excited at 559 nm and its emission recorded at 619 nm.

Association between biofilm formation and genotypic characteristics

To investigate the relationship between biofilm-forming capacity and bacterial genotypic traits, the 71 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were categorized into four biofilm groups: non-biofilm producers (n = 7), weak (n = 43), moderate (n = 9), and strong (n = 12) biofilm producers. For statistical analysis, due to the limited number of isolates in certain categories, biofilm groups were further consolidated into three categories: non/weak (n = 50), moderate (n = 9), and strong (n = 12). Associations between biofilm category and categorical variables including sequence type (ST, analyzed as ST117 vs. ST1771 vs. ST345 vs. others), serogroup (O78 vs. O125 vs. O91 vs. others), phylogroup (A vs. B1 vs. B2 vs. D vs. F), ESBL genotype (blaCTX-M-55 vs. blaCTX-M-64 vs. blaCTX-M-15 vs. blaCTX-M-14 vs. blaCTX-M-65 vs. others), mcr-1 carriage (positive vs. negative), individual virulence genes (presence vs. absence for genes with prevalence ≥ 10 %), virulence gene score (total number of virulence genes per isolate), and farm origin (F1-F10) were assessed. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test (when expected frequencies were < 5). Continuous variables (virulence gene score) were compared across biofilm categories using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to correct for multiple comparisons where applicable, with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5 %. Adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0.

Statistical analysis

Differences in antimicrobial resistance rates between ESBL-producing and non-ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were compared using Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test, as appropriate. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to correct for multiple comparisons across the 10 antimicrobial agents tested, with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5 %. Adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (OR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to estimate the magnitude of association between ESBL production and resistance to each antimicrobial agent. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0.

Results

Isolation and identification of E. coli

Based on colony morphology on MacConkey agar and MALDI-TOF MS identification results, a total of 537 E. coli strains were isolated from 537 individual faecal samples from broiler farms in the Jinan region of Shandong Province, China. The isolation rate was 89.5 % (537/600). From the remaining 63 samples, no E. coli colonies were recovered. Of the 537 E. coli isolates, 71 (13.2 %) were phenotypically confirmed as ESBL producers, and the remaining 466 (86.8 %) were classified as non-ESBL-producing isolates.

Antimicrobial resistance in non-ESBL-producing E. coli isolates

Non-ESBL-producing isolates displayed high resistance to older veterinary antimicrobials, with the highest rates observed for sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (87.98 %), streptomycin (79.61 %), tetracycline (78.97 %), and chloramphenicol (69.53 %) (Table 2). In contrast, susceptibility to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (79.61 %), cefuroxime (89.48 %), and ceftriaxone (81.55 %) remained largely preserved. Ampicillin resistance was moderate (35.62 %), while enrofloxacin and gentamicin resistance reached 43.78 % and 35.41 %, respectively, with a notable intermediate proportion for gentamicin (14.16 %). MIC distributions revealed a bimodal pattern for ampicillin, with a low MIC50 (4 μg/mL) contrasting with an elevated MIC90 (128 μg/mL), indicating an emerging resistant subpopulation. Similarly, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid maintained low MIC50 values (0.5-1/0.5 μg/mL) but elevated MIC90 values (32-128 μg/mL), reflecting the presence of resistant clones. For SXT, STR, and TET, both MIC50 and MIC90 were positioned at the upper range of tested concentrations. Enrofloxacin MIC50 was slightly elevated (2 μg/mL), suggesting reduced fluoroquinolone susceptibility even among non-ESBL strains.

Analysis of the antimicrobial resistance profiles of non-ESBL-producing E. coli isolates revealed that co-resistance to four antimicrobial agents, SXT, STR, TET, and CHL, was the predominant pattern, detected in 6.44 % (30/466) of the isolates (Fig. 1). The five-drug resistance profile, SXT-STR-TET-CHL-ENR, was the second most frequent, identified in 25 isolates (5.36 %). Patterns involving resistance to seven antimicrobials were also observed, specifically SXT-STR-TET-CHL-ENR-AMP-GEN (2.36 %, 11/466), SXT-STR-TET-CHL-AMP-AMC—CXM (0.86 %, 4/466), and SXT-STR-TET-CHL-ENR-AMP-CTX (0.86 %, 4/466). Resistance to only two drugs occurred least frequently, with the SXT-STR and SXT-TET patterns each accounting for 0.86 % (4/466) and 0.64 % (3/466) of isolates, respectively.

Antimicrobial resistance in ESBL-producing E. coli isolates

Of the 537 E. coli isolates screened, 71 (13.2 %) were phenotypically confirmed as ESBL producers. ESBL-producing isolates exhibited significantly elevated resistance compared to non-ESBL counterparts (Table 3). Statistical comparisons using Fisher's exact test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction revealed significant differences for eight of the ten antimicrobials tested (adjusted P < 0.05; Table 4). Complete resistance to ampicillin (100 % vs. 35.62 % in non-ESBL isolates; OR, not calculable; P < 0.001) was observed, alongside high-level co-resistance to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (94.37 % vs. 87.98 %; OR = 2.19; 95 % CI, 0.74–6.47; adjusted P = 0.179), streptomycin (87.32 % vs. 79.61 %; OR = 1.79; 95 % CI, 0.82–3.92; adjusted P = 0.182), chloramphenicol (78.87 % vs. 69.53 %; OR = 1.63; 95 % CI, 0.88–3.04; adjusted P = 0.159), and tetracycline (70.42 % vs. 78.97 %; OR = 0.63; 95 % CI, 0.36–1.12; adjusted P = 0.159). Critically, resistance to the extended-spectrum cephalosporins was markedly and significantly elevated in ESBL producers: cefuroxime (43.66 % vs. 8.80 %; OR = 8.04; 95 % CI, 4.29–15.07; adjusted P < 0.001) and ceftriaxone (36.62 % vs. 14.81 %; OR = 3.33; 95 % CI, 1.84–6.02; adjusted P < 0.001), while amoxicillin/clavulanic acid susceptibility declined significantly (resistance: 26.76 % vs. 15.88 %; OR = 1.94; 95 % CI, 1.07–3.51; adjusted P = 0.041). Gentamicin and enrofloxacin resistance rates did not differ significantly between ESBL and non-ESBL groups (43.66 % vs. 35.41 %; adjusted P = 0.209; and 36.62 % vs. 43.78 %; adjusted P = 0.289, respectively).

Phylogenetic groups, MLST and serotypes in ESBL-producing E. coli

The predominant phylogroup was group A (32/71, 45.1 %), followed by D (22/71, 30.9 %), B1 (8/71, 11.3 %), and F (5/71, 7.0 %), while only 4 ESBL-producing isolates belonged to phylogroup B2 (Fig. 3). Phylogenetic analysis based on 2,000 core-genome SNPs revealed considerable genetic diversity among the 71 ESBL-producing isolates, with pairwise SNP distances ranging from 1,363 to 2,000 SNPs. Isolates belonging to the same ST, particularly ST117 (n = 13) and ST1771 (n = 9), generally formed well-supported monophyletic clusters (bootstrap values ≥ 95 %), consistent with the clonal expansion of these lineages. In contrast, ESBL genotypes were distributed across multiple branches of the tree. For example, isolates carrying blaCTX-M-55, the most prevalent ESBL gene, were found in association with at least 4 different STs and did not form a single monophyletic group, suggesting that the dissemination of this resistance determinant has been driven in part by horizontal gene transfer rather than solely by clonal expansion. Similarly, mcr-1-positive isolates were dispersed across multiple phylogenetic clusters, indicating that this gene has been acquired independently by genetically diverse E. coli lineages.

MLST analysis of 71 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates identified ST117 as the most frequent sequence type (ST) with 18.3 % (13/71), followed by ST1771 (12.7 %, 9/71). ST345 was also identified (7.0 %, 5/71) (Fig. 3). These isolates were associated with serogroups O78 (25.4 %, 18/71), O125 (11.3 %, 8/71), O91 (8.5 %, 6/71), O44 (5.6 %, 4/71), O124 (4.2 %, 3/71), and O1 (4.2 %, 3/71) (Fig. 3).

Detection of genes in E. coli isolates

Genomic characterization of the 71 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates revealed a diverse resistome encompassing multiple antimicrobial classes (Fig. 3). Aminoglycoside resistance was predominantly mediated by aph(6)-ld (59.15 %, 42/71), aph(3)-lb (57.75 %, 41/71), and aadA1 (42.25 %, 30/71), with aph(3)-la detected at a lower frequency (18.31 %, 13/71). Among β-lactamase genes, blaCTX-M variants dominated, with blaCTX-M-55 identified as the most prevalent ESBL gene (29.58 %, 21/71), followed by blaCTX-M-64 (23.94 %, 17/71), blaCTX-M-15 (19.71 %, 14/71), blaCTX-M-14 (12.68 %, 9/71), and blaCTX-M-65 (9.86 %, 7/71). The plasmid-mediated AmpC gene blaCMY-2 was detected in 4.23 % (3/71) of isolates. Sulfonamide resistance was primarily conferred by sul2 (69.0 %, 49/71), with sul1 present in 33.8 % (24/71), while trimethoprim resistance was attributed to dfrA17 (33.8 %, 24/71) and dfrA1 (16.9 %, 12/71). Tetracycline resistance was predominantly encoded by tet(A) (73.24 %, 52/71), with tet(B) detected in 21.13 % (15/71) of isolates. The phenicol exporter gene floR was highly prevalent (66.2 %, 47/71). Macrolide resistance determinants mph(A) and mph(E) were identified at moderate frequencies of 23.94 % (17/71) and 8.45 % (6/71), respectively. Quinolone resistance was associated with the plasmid-mediated gene qnrS1 (42.25 %, 30/71) and the efflux pump genes OqxA (28.17 %, 20/71) and OqxB (28.17 %, 20/71). Notably, the colistin resistance gene mcr-1 was detected in 25.35 % (18/71) of isolates, highlighting a concerning genetic reservoir of last-resort antimicrobial resistance. It should be noted that colistin susceptibility testing was not performed; therefore, the phenotypic expression of colistin resistance in these mcr-1-positive isolates remains unconfirmed.

Virulence gene distribution

The distribution of virulence genes among ESBL-producing E. coli isolates revealed a diverse array of virulence-associated functions, with certain genes detected at markedly higher frequencies (Fig. 4). The most prevalent gene was iss (serum survival), identified in 85.9 % (61/71) of isolates, followed by gad (enzymes) at 69.0 % (49/71). Among toxin-related genes, astA was the most common at 43.7 % (31/71), while sta1 was detected in only a single isolate (1.4 %). Siderophore receptor gene iroN was present in 33.8 % (24/71) of isolates, and the bacteriocin-associated genes cma and mchH were found in 32.4 % (23/71) and 28.2 % (20/71), respectively. Several adhesion-related genes were identified at lower frequencies. The iha was detected in 19.7 % (14/71) of isolates, while F17, fim41, and toxB were each present in 4.2 % (3/71). Overall, the ESBL-producing E. coli isolates examined predominantly carried genes associated with serum survival and enzymatic activity, while adhesion factors, T3SS effectors, and certain toxins were detected at comparatively lower frequencies. Biofilm formation

To provide a visual illustration of biofilm architecture, three representative isolates were randomly selected from each of the strong, moderate, and weak biofilm-forming categories and examined using CLSM (Fig. 6). It should be noted that these images are qualitative and illustrative in nature, as only a small subset of isolates was examined and no quantitative measurements of biofilm thickness, biovolume, or biomass were performed. Representative images of strong biofilm formers displayed well-defined, mushroom-shaped microcolonies, a characteristic architectural feature associated with mature biofilms. In contrast, images of weak biofilm formers showed biofilms with less structural complexity. These qualitative observations are consistent with the classification derived from the crystal violet microtiter plate assay, but should not be extrapolated as population-level quantitative evidence.

Biofilm observation

The biofilm architecture of ESBL-producing E. coli isolates was visualized using CLSM. The observations revealed substantial heterogeneity in biofilm formation among the isolates (Fig. 6). Strong biofilm formers developed thick biofilms with well-defined mushroom-shaped microcolonies, a classic architectural feature of robust biofilms. Conversely, weak biofilm formers produced thinner biofilms marked by less structural complexity and an increased abundance of dead cells.

Association between biofilm formation and genotypic characteristics

Association analyses between biofilm-forming capacity and the genotypic characteristics of the 71 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were performed, and no statistically significant associations were observed between biofilm category and ST, serogroup, phylogroup, ESBL genotype, mcr-1 carriage, or farm origin.

Discussion

The global dissemination of ESBL-producing E. coli through the food chain is a paradigmatic One Health crisis, undermining the efficacy of critically important antimicrobials in both veterinary and human medicine. This study provides a comprehensive, multi-layered characterization of ESBL-producing E. coli from broiler farms in Jinan City of China, a region of intensive poultry production. Our findings reveal a high burden of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains carrying globally significant ESBL genotypes, co-harboring last-resort resistance determinants such as mcr-1, and demonstrating robust biofilm-forming capacity. These results are compared herein with national and international data to contextualize their epidemiological significance and to inform urgently needed mitigation strategies.

The isolation rate of E. coli (89.5 %) from broiler faecal samples is consistent with its well-documented high faecal carriage rate, reflecting its near-ubiquitous presence in the intestinal tract, and is comparable to rates reported in other poultry-dense regions of China (Wu et al., 2021). However, the resistance profile of the non-ESBL-producing subset serves as a critical baseline, revealing the extensive selection pressure exerted by older antimicrobial classes. The high-level resistance to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (87.98 %), streptomycin (79.61 %), tetracycline (78.97 %), and chloramphenicol (69.53 %) mirrors surveillance data from poultry operations in low- and middle-income countries, where these agents have been historically used for decades (Van Boeckel et al., 2019). In contrast, our study found high susceptibility to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (79.61 %) and extended-spectrum cephalosporins (89.48 % for cefuroxime, 81.55 % for ceftriaxone) among non-ESBL-producers, a pattern starkly divergent from regions like South Asia, where even non-ESBL E. coli from poultry often showed elevated resistance to these drugs due to widespread AmpC production (Brower et al., 2017). This discrepancy may reflect regional differences in on-farm cephalosporin usage, suggesting that, in our study area, these drugs may have been used more selectively or not at all prior to the selective sweep by ESBL producers. The clear bimodal MIC distribution for ampicillin (MIC50 of 4 µg/mL versus MIC90 of 128 µg/mL) effectively illustrates a population poised on the cusp of a resistance epidemic, with a minority of resistant clones persisting and awaiting further selection.

The ESBL-producing subset presented a profoundly more severe resistance profile. The complete resistance to ampicillin (100 %) and the near-complete resistance to sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (94.37 %) are hallmarks of the successful co-selection of resistance determinants on mobile genetic elements. Crucially, the resistance rates for the extended-spectrum cephalosporins ceftriaxone (36.62 %) and cefuroxime (43.66 %) among ESBL-producers represent a dramatic increase from the 14.81 % and 8.8 % resistance observed in non-ESBL isolates, directly attributable to the hydrolytic activity of ESBL enzymes. This shift is further reflected in the MIC data, the MIC50 for ceftriaxone increased from ≤0.5 µg/mL in non-ESBL isolates to 1 µg/mL in ESBL-producers, with the MIC90 reaching 64 µg/mL. This phenotypic transition is clinically decisive, as it represents the loss of susceptibility to a WHO-defined Highest Priority Critically Important Antimicrobial for human medicine (WHO, 2019). When compared to surveillance data from European poultry, where ESBL prevalence and cephalosporin resistance are generally lower due to stricter antimicrobial stewardship (EFSA and ECDC, 2021), our findings reveal a far more compromised therapeutic landscape. This disparity is further exacerbated by the high-level co-resistance to fluoroquinolones (enrofloxacin, 36.62 %) and aminoglycosides (gentamicin, 43.66 %), a co-resistance profile that severely limits therapeutic options for both avian colibacillosis and potential zoonotic infections. The predominant MDR pattern of six antimicrobial agents (AMP-SXT-STR-CHL-TET-GEN) in 7.04 % of isolates is a phenotypic signature of high-risk clones that stably maintain multiple co-selected resistance determinants.

Farm-level analysis revealed that ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were detected on all 10 sampled farms, suggesting widespread dissemination across the study region. However, the proportion of ESBL-positive isolates varied considerably among farms, ranging from 8.9 % to 21.4 %. Although complete antimicrobial use records were not available for all farms, the farms with numerically higher ESBL proportions (F3, 21.4 %; F4, 16.4 %; F1, 14.8 %) were those where tetracyclines, sulfonamides, or chloramphenicol were reportedly administered during the production cycle. This observation is consistent with the well-established role of non-β-lactam antimicrobials in co-selecting ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae through genetic linkage on multi-resistance plasmids (Wang et al., 2017). Nevertheless, the small number of farms and the incomplete AMU data preclude formal statistical analysis, and these observations should be interpreted with caution.

Molecular dissection confirmed the overwhelming dominance of the blaCTX-M group, with blaCTX-M-55 (29.58 %) as the most prevalent variant. This is in close agreement with recent studies identifying blaCTX-M-55 as an emerging and predominant ESBL genotype in Chinese poultry, surpassing the historically dominant blaCTX-M-14 in certain intensive farming systems (Liu et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020). In contrast, in the United States and parts of Europe, blaCTX-M-1 and blaCTX-M-15 have been more frequently reported from poultry sources, highlighting distinct geographical genotypic epidemiology shaped by local clonal expansion and plasmid dissemination (Ewers et al., 2012). The concurrent high prevalence of blaCTX-M-15 (19.71 %), which is the globally dominant ESBL gene in human clinical E. coli, is particularly alarming from a zoonotic perspective, as it suggests a dynamic bidirectional gene flow between human and animal reservoirs (Bevan et al., 2017).

A defining finding of this study is the substantial genetic carriage of the plasmid-mediated colistin resistance gene mcr-1 in 25.35 % of ESBL-producing isolates. This prevalence is markedly higher than the 5-15 % reported in some European poultry surveys but is consistent with other reports from China following the initial discovery of mcr-1 in the country’s animal production sector (Liu et al., 2016; Shen et al., 2018). The co-existence of mcr-1 and blaCTX-M genes in isolates from this study raises a significant public health concern. However, as this study employed Illumina short-read sequencing, the physical linkage of these genes, for example, whether they reside on the same plasmid or distinct genetic elements, could not be definitively determined. The concurrent detection of these resistance determinants in the same isolates suggests the possibility of co-carriage on compatible mobile genetic elements, but confirmation of their co-transferability would require long-read sequencing and conjugation experiments. Therefore, the potential for co-selection and co-dissemination of these last-resort resistance genes should be interpreted with caution. The detection of floR (66.2 %), tet(A) (73.24 %), and sul2 (69.0 %) at high frequencies provides a clear genetic explanation for the extensive phenotypic co-resistance observed, confirming the role of these genes as passengers on multi-resistance plasmids that are stably maintained by the selective pressure from any one of these antimicrobial classes.

The population structure of the ESBL-producing isolates, dominated by phylogroups A (45.1 %) and D (30.9 %), is typical of animal-associated and environmental E. coli. The presence of phylogroup D in relatively high proportions is noteworthy, as this phylogroup has been reported to include strains associated with extraintestinal infections in humans (Johnson et al., 2001). However, the assignment of pathogenic potential based solely on phylogroup membership should be made with caution, as phylogroup D also contains commensal strains.

The identification of ST117 as the most frequent sequence type (18.3 %) is consistent with previous reports documenting this lineage in both poultry and human clinical settings, where it has been associated with urinary tract infections and sepsis (Manges et al., 2019; Skurnik et al., 2016). Nevertheless, without direct comparative genomic analysis with human clinical isolates from the same geographic region, the zoonotic origin of these ST117 strains cannot be definitively established. The dominance of ST117 in our poultry isolates contrasts with reports from human clinical settings in China where ST131 is the predominant MDR ExPEC lineage, highlighting the genetic diversity among extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli populations and the need for caution in extrapolating pathogenic potential across host species (Zhang et al., 2019).

The serogroup O78, detected in 25.4 % of our isolates, is a serogroup commonly reported among avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC). However, serogroup alone is not a definitive marker of APEC pathogenicity, which requires fulfillment of additional criteria, including specific virulence gene combinations and experimental infection models. The high prevalence of the serum survival gene iss (85.9 %), a gene frequently identified in APEC strains, is consistent with the presence of APEC-associated genetic elements in these isolates. However, it is important to note that iss alone does not confer APEC status, and comprehensive virulence scoring systems or experimental infection studies would be required to evaluate the actual pathogenic potential of these strains (Johnson et al., 2008). The lower frequency of classical human-associated adhesins and toxins further indicates that the virulence gene repertoire of these isolates does not fully align with that of typical human ExPEC strains, and any inferences regarding zoonotic pathogenic potential remain speculative without functional validation.

The core-genome phylogenetic analysis revealed that ESBL-producing E. coli isolates from the study region are genetically diverse and do not represent a single clonal outbreak. The observation that closely related isolates were found on different farms suggests the possibility of between-farm transmission, potentially mediated by shared vehicles, personnel, or environmental reservoirs. However, alternative explanations, such as independent acquisition of similar mobile genetic elements by related E. coli lineages, cannot be excluded without plasmid-level analysis. The dispersal of identical ESBL genotypes (e.g., blaCTX-M-55) across multiple STs further supports the important role of horizontal gene transfer in the dissemination of resistance in this setting. These findings highlight the need for integrated surveillance that captures both clonal and horizontal transmission pathways.

The finding that 90.14 % of ESBL-producing isolates were capable of biofilm formation, with 16.90 % classified as strong producers, carries profound implications for food safety and contradicts the assumption that commensal or weakly pathogenic strains are poor biofilm formers. In comparison to studies on E. coli from other food sources, such as beef or vegetables, the biofilm-forming prevalence in our poultry-derived ESBL-producers is notably higher (Wang et al., 2017). The CLSM visualization of mature, mushroom-shaped microcolonies in strong biofilm formers provides tangible evidence of their ability to form complex, three-dimensional structures. This phenotype is not merely a laboratory observation; it translates directly into enhanced persistence in the farm environment, resistance to disinfection protocols in slaughterhouses, and the potential for re-contamination of carcasses during processing, undermining Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) programs (Bridier et al., 2015). Although based on a limited number of representative images, the qualitative CLSM observations suggested a reduced structural complexity in weak biofilms, whereas strong biofilm formers exhibited well-developed mushroom-shaped microcolonies. These architectural features, while illustrative rather than quantitative, are consistent with the established understanding that mature biofilm architecture confers a protective niche for the bacterial community, potentially enhancing resistance to environmental stressors and antimicrobials (Bridier et al., 2015). However, quantitative studies measuring biofilm thickness, biovolume, and viable cell distribution across a larger number of isolates would be required to confirm these observations at the population level.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. Initially, the sampling was geographically confined to 10 farms in the Jinan region of Shandong Province. As the farms were not selected through a randomized or stratified sampling design representative of the province or country, the prevalence data and resistance patterns reported herein should not be extrapolated to Shandong Province or China as a whole. These findings reflect the situation on the specific farms studied and indicate the need for broader surveillance. Additionally, while Illumina short-read sequencing provides high-fidelity gene-level resolution, it cannot resolve the complete structure of plasmids carrying blaCTX-M and mcr-1. Long-read sequencing is required to determine the genetic linkage of these critical resistance genes, which is essential for understanding their co-mobilization potential. Moreover, as the samples were faecal specimens collected from the farm environment rather than cecal contents obtained at slaughter, the results primarily reflect faecal shedding and may not fully represent the extent of intestinal colonization. Finally, our study is purely observational and does not include a quantitative risk assessment to model the probability of human exposure through the food chain.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that broiler chickens from Jinan City of China harbor MDR ESBL-producing E. coli carrying clinically relevant resistance and virulence-associated genes. The isolates exhibited high rates of co-resistance to frontline veterinary antimicrobials and reduced susceptibility to extended-spectrum cephalosporins, associated with the predominant of blaCTX-M-55 and blaCTX-M-15. The genotypic detection of mcr-1 in 25.35 % of isolates and the predominance of ST117, a lineage previously reported in both poultry and human infections, are noteworthy findings that merit further investigation. However, the genetic context of these resistance genes could not be resolved due to the limitations of short-read sequencing, and the extent to which they are co-mobilizable requires further investigation using long-read sequencing approaches. The high prevalence of biofilm-forming capacity (90.14 %) among these isolates suggests that this phenotype may contribute to environmental persistence, although its impact on food safety was not directly assessed in this study. Collectively, these findings support the need for ongoing surveillance of AMR in poultry production and reinforce the value of a One Health approach to monitoring clinically relevant resistance determinants in food animals. Future studies incorporating human clinical isolates, retail meat samples, slaughterhouse environmental sampling, and long-read sequencing are warranted to assess the public health implications of these findings and to elucidate the transmission dynamics of ESBL-producing E. coli along the food chain.
