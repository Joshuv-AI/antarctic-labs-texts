Evaluation of students’ satisfaction with obe teaching modes in the manual therapy course by students’ evaluation of educational quality questionnaire
Authors: Yuchen Zhang, Jiayang Yu, Chenrong Xia, Kailin Zhai, Yuanhao Shen, Bin Xiao, Jing Zhang, Qingguang Zhu, Min Fang, Chongjie Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo design a teaching model of innovative theoretical and practical workshop for manual therapy (MT, also known as Tuina) course within Chinese higher medical education based on outcome-based education (OBE) concept, and to explore its implementation effect and application evaluation.MethodsSixty undergraduate students were randomly divided into three groups: lecture-based learning (LBL), blended teaching (LBL with online self-study), and OBE. The OBE group received student-centered modules combining theory and cognitive training. Effectiveness was assessed using the students’ evaluation of educational quality questionnaire (SEEQ) for dimensions like enthusiasm and interaction, as well as final course score.ResultsThe OBE group achieved significantly higher final score (87.15 ± 3.30, 95% CI: 85.61–88.70) than blended (84.70 ± 3.18, 95% CI: 83.21–86.19) and LBL groups (81.80 ± 4.60, 95% CI: 79.65–83.95). OBE also showed superior SEEQ ratings in group interaction, satisfaction, and breadth than the other teaching models, with lower learning burden compared to blended teaching.ConclusionsThe OBE-based MT course showed capacity in improving teaching satisfaction and effectiveness by integrating theory with practice, fostering cognitive engagement, and advancing innovative thoughts. OBE-trained students demonstrated relatively enhanced learning and thinking abilities, supporting their career development. The study highlighted the potential significance of OBE in cultivating the professional competence of traditional Chinese medicine students, thus may bridge traditional therapeutic wisdom with modern healthcare needs. However, large-scale and multi-center studies need to be conducted to further support the existing findings.

Introduction

Manual therapy (MT), also known as Tuina, is a cornerstone technique of traditional Chinese medicine with reliable clinical outcomes in areas such as pain management, mental health relief and gastrointestinal treatment [1]. The teaching and transmission of MT has endured for millennia. Through oral instruction and hands-on clinical experience, the traditional master-apprentice model enables knowledge transfer and clarification of complex concepts, serving as a vital channel for cultivating skillful professionals [2]. From the second half of the 20th century, MT has been integrated into China’s higher medical education system. However, due to regional disparities in educational resources and stagnation in teaching innovations, the instructional approach has evolved toward didactic, textbook-driven instructional methods [3]. This pedagogical stagnation has resulted in systemic deficits in cultivating clinical proficiency, evidence-based reasoning, critical thinking and clinical innovation competencies [4]. Amid accelerating medical technological advancements and escalating demands for clinical innovation, a growing schism exists between current MT education and evolving practice requirements. Establishing an innovative teaching model oriented towards clinical demands has become an urgent need for the reform of MT education.

The theoretical framework of outcome-based education (OBE) was initially proposed by Spady W [5] and later systematically introduced into the field of medical education by Harden RM [6]. OBE aims at preparing doctors for the increasingly complex healthcare and the changing expectations of patients, employers, and the public. Driven by learning outcomes and centered on students’ autonomous development, OBE emphasizes shaping medical students’ clinical decision-making abilities and interdisciplinary practice competencies. The main approaches include reversed curriculum design and continuous quality improvement mechanisms based on feedback [7]. It has now become one of the core paradigms in the reform of higher medical education. The principles of OBE include: teaching guided by clear outcomes; curriculum design based on expected results; expecting students to make continuous progress and accept learning challenges; and expanding students’ learning opportunities to meet higher standards [8]. Under the OBE strategy, the clear definition of learning outcomes and abilities becomes the focus of education. By specifying what students should know and be able to do at the end of their educational journey, students are empowered to guide and self-regulate their own learning processes.

Given the pressing need to reform MT pedagogy and the efficacy of OBE in advancing competency-based medical education, this study pioneers the integration of OBE principles into MT curriculum development. We hypothesize that redesigning the MT curriculum using the OBE strategy will help students deepen their understanding of the course content, cultivate innovative thinking, and thus improve the quality of teaching. The study implemented a multi-dimensional evaluation framework to assess the educational quality of the redesigned OBE-MT program while identifying key determinants of learner-perceived educational quality. The investigation specifically targets the optimization of teaching methods for enhancing professional competence. Ultimately, this work seeks to cultivate a new generation of clinical practitioners equipped with greater professional competence, thereby bridging the gap between traditional therapeutic wisdom and contemporary healthcare demands through pedagogical innovation.

Study population

In this study, 60 undergraduate students from Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (SHUTCM) taking the MT course during the 2023 to 2024 academic year were recruited as the study population. The multi-pronged recruitment strategy included posting notices on campus bulletin boards, social media posts, and on-site presentations in relevant courses.

Study design

This prospective, controlled, randomized study was conducted from September 2023 to July 2024. The generation of random number was performed by an independent researcher (Qingguang Zhu), who was uninvolved in teaching and data analysis, using SPSS software. The allocation sequence was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Students were asked to open the envelopes before the first session and assigned to three groups with 20 each. Based on various allocation, participants received one of the following teaching interventions including conventional lecture-based learning (LBL), a blended teaching (BT) approach combining LBL with online self-study, or OBE courses. The LBL group was taught with teacher-centered learning. Students solely receive information from the teacher and attempt to memorize the content instead of being encouraged to actively comprehend and apply the concepts on their own [9]. Based on the LBL model, the BT model involves teachers regularly assigning online self-study content for students to learn independently. The OBE courses were taught with student-centered learning [10], including Phase A modules that were designed to guide the students through the content of the textbook in the first one-third of the class time, and Phase B modules that were designed in the remaining time to teach students how to do logical deduction, evidence collection, and content critique. Both phases aim to teach students the ability to do autonomous learning and have tangible application of what they have learned into practice.

Courses tailored to different teaching methods have been set up for peer lecturers to ensure uniform teaching quality. For the LBL and BT groups, the teaching components of the curriculum included basic theories and practice of MT, the rights and responsibilities of patients and doctors.

Defining outcome and design curriculum stage: course introduction and group discussion

The OBE paradigm refers to the organization of the educational system being directed towards outcomes. Consequently, it is necessary to define the desired outcomes first before defining what content it should include.

The first stage is conducted during the initial class session and consist of two parts: course introduction and group discussion. The teacher begins by briefly providing an overview of the MT course content. Subsequently, students are grouped into teams of 5–6 members, conducting thinking, research, and discussion to determine the outcomes they most desire from the course. Finally, the teacher collects feedback to synthesize and refine, culminating in the formation of the course’s ultimate objectives and designing the curriculum.

Deliver instruction stage: theoretical teaching and integrated cognitive training

The instruction stage consists of two phases: theoretical teaching and integrated cognitive training. In the theoretical teaching session, the students are taught the foundational knowledge of MT while required to analyze the background knowledge, specific applications, and potential improvements with the medical cases provided, complemented by appropriate demonstrations of MT techniques. The teaching format, based on theoretical instruction, case analysis and practical application, aims at enhancing the students’ understanding and internalization of theoretical and conceptual knowledge, thereby laying the cognitive and analytical foundation for the subsequent integrated cognitive training.

In the integrated cognitive training session, the instruction is structured in a progressive approach—the introduction of scientific research methodologies including skills for experimental design and data analysis, the sharing of scientific literature, and discussion on scientific issues—which collectively fosters the students’ autonomous learning capabilities, research abilities, and critical thinking skills. Concurrently, participants will be required to develop personalized MT protocols for specific diseases and implement them clinically. Throughout this process, instructors and group members will collaboratively evaluate the protocols to optimize treatment strategies and refine operational procedures through evidence-based discussion.

This educational strategy is underpinned by the emphasis of theoretical knowledge. However, it is the application of this knowledge through an outcome-based lens that truly augments the learning experience. By engaging in critical analysis of scientific literature and actively participating in the discourse of clinical problem-solving, students are equipped with the autonomous ability to enhance their innovation capabilities and clinical proficiency.

Document results and determine advancement stage: assessment

The evaluation of the MT course comprises of both practical application (worth 60%) and academic writing (40%). In the practical application session, students are divided into groups of two. Each student is assigned a medical case randomly and is expected to articulate the patient’s etiology and treatment modalities within 10 min. Subsequently, they are to demonstrate the corresponding manipulation techniques on their partner, simulating the clinical treatment process. This session assesses the students’ ability to extract and analyze information, as well as their proficiency in applying theoretical knowledge, thereby laying a solid foundation for their future clinical practice. In the academic writing session, students are asked to select a topic pertinent to MT studies and compose a literature review approximately 2000 words in length. The teaching team, including one professor, one associate professor, and two lecturers, will grade and evaluate the papers in terms of innovative thinking, logic, content form, information retrieval use, etc., and give some advice for advancement. The same evaluation method will be applied to each experimental group. This comprehensive assessment approach ensures that students are not only well-versed in practical techniques but also in the critical analysis and academic discourse in the discipline.

Evaluation methods

To assess the quality of teaching, the Students’ Evaluation of Educational Quality (SEEQ) questionnaire is selected as the instrument for the survey [11]. SEEQ was developed by Marsh in 1987 to assess students’ satisfaction with teacher effectiveness [12]. Recognized as a highly consistent and comprehensive assessment tool, SEEQ may contribute to enhancing teaching quality and optimizing student learning experiences [13]. Developed on the foundation of empirical research, SEEQ enables the collection of student feedback across multiple dimensions, including learning value, individual rapport, instructor enthusiasm, examinations, course organization, breadth, group interaction, feedback on assignments, and overall satisfaction. SEEQ consists of 32 questions for course evaluation. All questions are scored using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Therefore, a high score indicates students’ appreciation of the course content and teaching quality. SEEQ was translated into Chinese in 1998, and a previous study [14] supported its applicability in Chinese higher medical education contexts. All students were required to complete the Chinese-translated survey within two weeks after the course ended and rate each item in the questionnaire.

Reliability assessment

Internal consistency of the SEEQ was assessed using cronbach’s α coefficient. A value exceeding 0.7 was considered suitable

Statistical methods

The SPSS 25.0 software was employed to establish a database for data entry and statistical analysis. For categorical data, frequency and composition ratio were used for statistical description, while for measurement data, the mean ± standard deviation was adopted. The analysis was based on the assumption of no group differences. One-way analysis of variance with least-significant-difference t-test was applied to measurement data conforming to the normal distribution, otherwise, Kruskal-Wallis tests were used. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study was designed and conducted following the guidelines for ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All the research progress was carried out by teaching staff from SHUTCM. Participants were fully informed of their anonymity, the right to withdraw from the study at any time, and the voluntary nature of participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the study.

Results

General information

A total of 60 participants were included in the study, among whom 18 were males and 42 were females. The average age was 21.37 ± 0.75 years, and all of them were undergraduate students. The study population was randomly assigned to three pre-designed groups for teaching activities. There was no statistically significant difference in the distribution of gender, age and grade among the groups. In the final course score, students in the LBL group (81.80 ± 4.60 points, 95% CI [confidence interval]: 79.65–83.95) achieved significantly lower grades compared to those in the BT group (84.70 ± 3.18 points, 95% CI: 83.21–86.19) (P = 0.017). Meanwhile, the performance of students in the OBE group (87.15 ± 3.30 points, 95% CI: 85.61–88.70) surpassed that of the BT group (P = 0.043). (Table 2)

Overall scoring and impression of the courses

The total scores of the LBL, BT, and OBE groups were 90.25 ± 12.59 points (95% CI: 84.36–96.14), 90.70 ± 11.31 points (95% CI: 85.40–96.00), and 113.85 ± 9.62 points (95% CI: 109.35–118.35.35.35), respectively. There was no statistically significant difference between the former two (P = 0.90), while the total score of the OBE group was higher than that of the other two teaching modes (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A).

Students’ overall impression of the course is similar to the distribution of the total score (Fig. 1B). The scores of the overall impression within LBL, BT, and OBE groups were 6.00 ± 1.41 points (95% CI: 5.34–6.66), 5.30 ± 1.08 points (95% CI: 4.79–5.81), and 7.9 ± 1.17 points (95% CI: 7.35–8.45), respectively. There was no statistically significant difference between the first two groups (P = 0.08), and the total score of the OBE group was significantly higher than that of the other two groups (P < 0.001).

Evaluation of course engagement

The parts of the SEEQ scale that assess teaching enthusiasm, group interaction and individual rapport can well reflect students’ evaluations of the course engagement.

The score of teaching enthusiasm under the OBE group (14.25 ± 1.92 points, 95% CI: 13.35–15.15) was significantly higher than that of the LBL (9.85 ± 1.69 points, 95% CI: 9.06–10.64) (P < 0.001) and BT model groups (9.45 ± 1.64 points, 95% CI: 8.68–10.22) (P < 0.001), while the score of the latter two were similar (P = 0.70) (Fig. 1C). This is one of the modules with the largest score gap in this study, demonstrating the significant role of OBE in stimulating teaching enthusiasm and improving students’ concentration.

In terms of group interaction, there was no statistically significant difference in the score between the LBL (10.40 ± 1.93 points, 95% CI: 9.50–11.30) and the BT model (9.35 ± 1.76 points, 95% CI: 8.53–10.17) groups (P = 0.08), while students in the OBE group gave higher evaluations to the course interaction (13.35 ± 1.81 points, 95% CI: 12.50–14.20) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1D). Notably, compared with the LBL strategy (10.25 ± 1.71 points, 95% CI: 9.45–11.05), both the BT model (13.25 ± 1.77 points, 95% CI: 12.42–14.08) (P < 0.001) and the OBE group (14.05 ± 2.54 points, 95% CI: 12.86–15.24) (P < 0.001) achieved higher score in individual rapport (Fig. 1E). This may imply that students’ self-learning may be a vital factor in enhancing their personal engagement in the MT course.

Evaluation of course breadth

Given the knowledge gap between the textbook content and the industry’s cutting-edge level, appropriately expanding the course content and developing critical thinking is crucial for bridging the gap between theory and practice. In the evaluation of course breadth, the score given by the OBE group students (17.20 ± 2.14 points, 95% CI: 16.20–18.20) were significantly better than those of the LBL (13.15 ± 2.13 points, 95% CI: 12.15–14.15) (P < 0.001) and BT groups (13.65 ± 2.41 points, 95% CI: 12.52–14.78) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1G), suggesting that the student-centered teaching method has a positive effect in enhancing thinking abilities and supplementing new knowledge.

Evaluation of the course completion assessment

Regarding after-class assignment, the BT mode group (7.15 ± 1.79 points, 95% CI: 6.31–7.99) (P < 0.001) and the OBE group (6.8 ± 1.36 points, 95% CI: 6.16–7.44) (P < 0.001) scored significantly higher than the LBL group (5.20 ± 1.11 points, 95% CI: 4.68–5.72) (Fig. 1G), demonstrating that autonomous learning enhances course pertinence and content comprehension. However, the students in the BT group reported greater after-class study time (2.80 ± 0.77 points, 95% CI: 2.44–3.16) than the LBL (1.25 ± 0.55 points, 95% CI: 0.99–1.51) (P < 0.001) and OBE groups (1.30 ± 0.47 points, 95% CI: 1.08–1.52) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1H), suggesting that adding online self-directed learning to LBL may increase learning burden, whereas a full shift to OBE improved course quality without added burden.

In the evaluation of examination, no statistically significant difference emerged (P = 0.06) among the LBL group (9.90 ± 2.05 points, 95% CI: 8.94–10.86), the BT group (8.8 ± 1.70 points, 95% CI: 8.00–9.60.00.60) and the OBE group (10.10 ± 1.68 points, 95% CI: 9.31–10.89) (Fig. 1I), indicating comparable recognition between the OBE evaluation system and traditional methods. Interestingly, incorporating self-directed learning seems to effectively enhance students’ confidence in their mastery of knowledge, with higher expected score in group adopting BT (3.90 ± 0.72 points, 95% CI: 3.56–4.24) (P = 0.039) and OBE (4.10 ± 0.85 points, 95% CI: 3.70–4.50) (P = 0.003) than the LBL group (3.35 ± 0.75 points, 95% CI: 3.00–3.70.00.70) (Fig. 1J).

Instrument reliability

For the 32-item Likert-scale based SEEQ, the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.91, demonstrating appropriate internal consistency of the primary instrument used in this study.

Discussion

The educational landscape has been evolving, with a growing emphasis on outcome-based education (OBE) as a means to enhance the quality of teaching and learning. However, as a part of the long-standing traditional Chinese medicine, manual therapy (MT) lacks attention regarding innovative teaching methods after being incorporated into higher medical education. Currently, MT courses reveal critical gaps in both preserving traditional knowledge while fostering innovation, and insufficient integration between theoretical instruction and practical application, despite their emphasis on integrating theory with practice [15]. This study aimed to fill this gap by improving the quality of MT course using OBE, analyzing its effectiveness compared to conventional teaching methods, and identifying key factors that influence quality.

In the present research, the reformed MT course guided by the OBE strategy yielded higher overall student evaluations than lecture-based learning (LBL) or LBL-integrated online self-learning models. Specifically, OBE teaching enhanced course enthusiasm, engagement, knowledge expansion, and mastery confidence while minimizing learning burden. These findings align with prior studies [16, 17], indicating that the OBE concept enhances student satisfaction while comprehensively improving pedagogical effectiveness and clinical competencies among medical students. This demonstrates that implementing OBE strategies in medical education has potential in integrating theoretical knowledge with practical application, stimulates cognitive engagement and motivation, thereby advancing innovative thinking and problem-solving capabilities.

Granting students autonomy in learning appears to be a key driver for enhancing overall competency among medical students. A prior study [18] has established that OBE optimizes training by fostering active learning, which enhances gaining competency in key skills—a finding that converges with the superior summative assessment outcomes observed in the OBE cohort of this study. In terms of MT, the biomechanical parameters of manipulations are critical factors influencing therapeutic efficacy [19]. Learners typically require extensive clinical practice to master fundamental techniques such as pushing, rolling, kneading, rubbing, and grasping until these movements become second nature [20]. However, purely lecture-based theoretical learning may be insufficient to address the challenges within practical treatment [21], highlighting the essential role of self-directed learning and training in manual therapy. Similar to the OBE group, the blended teaching (BT) approach integrated online resource-based self-learning into the LBL framework, resulting in higher evaluations for individual rapport and expected score. Given that higher student confidence indicates a high acquisition rate of key skills, it may be beneficial to shape students’ greater enthusiasm for learning [22]. The implementation of autonomous learning enables students to transform from passive recipients of knowledge into active participants. In LBL, the teacher’s explanation ability, teaching speed, and students’ concentration can all pose obstacles to knowledge understanding [23]. This might prevent students from truly grasping the significance of mechanical parameters such as force, angle, and frequency in actual therapy within the MT course. In contrast, autonomous learning allows students to independently regulate their learning pace and resource allocation, which is crucial for deepening knowledge mastery [24]. Moreover, the implementation of OBE strategies enhances the development of critical thinking in clinical trainees [25], which are important elements for the professional development of medical workers [26, 27]. Although not directly measured in this study, the improvement of thinking ability may contribute to long-term developmental potential.

Teaching efficiency and course burden are thought to be another key factors influencing teaching quality. Although the course scores of the BT group were higher than those of the LBL group, the additional online learning tasks led to a significant increase in their time investment. As the duration of study and the degree of effort are difficult to quantify in the evaluation system, this may lead to a low satisfaction of the assessment method in this group. Considering that students usually need to study multiple courses simultaneously, the continuous increase in the learning burden will gradually lead to a relative shortage of study time for each course. Therefore, a decline in learning efficiency may exists [28, 29]. In contrast, OBE sets clear goals at the early stage of the course, which is conducive to the overall planning of the teaching process. Additionally, the OBE group employs case-based teaching during practical instruction to enhance students’ ability to organize and apply knowledge. This approach facilitates the integrated learning of MT with theoretical frameworks such as meridian theory, anatomical structures, and biomechanics, which may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding [30]. Compared to rote memorization of course-by-course theoretical content, this holistic method significantly reduces the cognitive load associated with knowledge retention. Eventually, it achieves better teaching output with a lower course burden, which is instructive for teaching reform.

Building upon the insights from this study, we have recognized several key areas where enhancements could be made to further the effectiveness of OBE in MT courses. Firstly, participants generally recognized the practical application of MT as an essential component of this diagnostic and treatment technology, expressing expectations for enhanced skill acquisition throughout the course. These observations highlight the significance of integrating OBE strategies with the specific teaching context and course, consistent with the viewpoints of other studies [31]. Additionally, further research could explore additional factors influencing course quality, such as teaching materials, and assessment methods. Moreover, longitudinal studies could track students’ academic performance and career outcomes to assess the long-term impact of teaching methods.

Although certain achievements have been explored, the study still has several limitations. Firstly, the study primarily relied on self-reported data, which might be influenced by subjective factors, recall bias, and response style variations. Future investigations should incorporate objective measures such as behavioral observations and observer-rated scales, while employing longitudinal designs with repeated data collection to mitigate recall bias, thereby enhancing the validity of findings. Secondly, the study measured the short-term outcomes of the OBE group without tracking the long-term impacts on students’ learning and career development. Given the potential delayed emergence of higher-order thinking skills (e.g., critical thinking), short-term comparisons may be insufficient to fully elucidate the sustained effects of the OBE framework. Future research could extend the follow-up period, and assess the students’ performance and achievements in subsequent courses, clinical practice, or professional settings, to evaluate the sustainability of the OBE strategy. Moreover, the study used academic writing and practical application as the ultimate outcome-based criteria, which might be too utilitarian or narrow in scope. Future research could enrich the outcome criteria, and include other aspects of learning to capture the holistic development of students. Finally, given MT instruction’s reliance on instructor proficiency and cross-regional standardization challenges for manipulation techniques, the current study was only conducted at SHUTCM. Limited amount of student at a single traditional medical institution restricted sample size, potentially compromising representativeness, diversity, and statistical power of this study. However, emerging educational innovations (e.g., inter-university course sharing) and AI-assisted MT technique standardization offer promising avenues for multi-center trials to verify the present preliminary findings [32].

Conclusion

In conclusion, the OBE-based MT course for traditional Chinese medicine undergraduates may improve teaching satisfaction and effectiveness. It integrates theory with practice, fostering cognitive engagement and enthusiasm, and advancing teaching research. OBE-trained students showed relatively improved learning and thinking abilities, which support their future career development. Given the pursuit of innovative medical talents, pedagogical reform requires further comprehensive, large-scaled and multi-centered studies.
