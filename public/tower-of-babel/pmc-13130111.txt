Generation of Clean and Renewable Energy through Compatible Piezoelectric Devices Based on NbO3 Material
Authors: Adnan Zaman, Fajer Alkulify, Amal BaQais, Abdulrahman Alsolami, Salman Alfihed, Ugur Guneroglu, Liguan Li, Naif Alshamrani, Jing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar energy harvesters can experience intermittent output due to rainfall, cloud cover, and rapid temperature variations. In this work, we present an experimental demonstration of a hybrid energy harvester that integrates single-crystal lithium niobate (LiNbO3) piezoelectric/pyroelectric devices with a silicon solar cell to enable multisource energy capture under variable weather conditions. Interdigital transducers (IDTs) patterned on LiNbO3 convert raindrop impacts and temperature swings into electrical signals through the piezoelectric effect, while the silicon cell produces photovoltaic power. Under controlled testing, the hybrid configuration increased photovoltaic output by 22.6% compared with the standalone solar cell. It improved the raindrop-induced peak-to-peak voltage by 8.3% relative to the standalone piezoelectric component. The LiNbO3 device generated 0.8 Vpp from a single raindrop and 7.54 Vpp at approximately 70 °C through pyroelectric conversion. Device durability was supported by extended cycling with ≤1% drift in Vpp and by post-test microscopy, which confirmed intact electrodes and a clean LiNbO3–metal interface. Overall, these findings demonstrate a robust and straightforward pathway toward weather-resilient hybrid energy modules that mitigate solar intermittency by harvesting light, mechanical rain energy, and thermal fluctuations within a single integrated stack. Overall, the demonstrated hybrid device delivers stable multisource energy harvesting with high repeatability and strong structural integrity after extended raindrop and thermal cycling.

Introduction

The intensifying global demand for clean, sustainable energy sources necessitates exploring innovative technologies to effectively harness renewable resources. While traditional solar cells have played a crucial role in the transition toward green energy, their efficiency can be limited by suboptimal weather conditions such as cloudiness or rain. This challenge has spurred research into alternative energy harvesting technologies that can complement traditional photovoltaic systems.

One promising avenue of research is developing piezoelectric devices that convert mechanical energy, such as the kinetic energy of raindrops, into electrical power. Piezoelectric materials possess the unique ability to generate an electric charge in response to mechanical stress, a phenomenon known as the direct piezoelectric effect. , This effect arises from the material’s asymmetric crystal lattice, which becomes polarized under mechanical deformation, generating charge and voltage across the strained material. The value proposition of piezoelectric devices in energy harvesting stems from their ability to tap into various underutilized mechanical energy sources in the environment. These sources extend beyond raindrops to include ambient vibrations from structures and acoustic noise, presenting a vast potential for enhanced energy scavenging, particularly in situations where conventional renewable technologies might be less effective. ,,

The exploration of pyroelectric materials offers yet another dimension to renewable energy technologies. These materials can generate an electric charge in response to temperature changes. This property can be harnessed to directly convert thermal energy from ambient heat or sunlight into electrical energy. Unlike the piezoelectric effect, which requires mechanical stress and strain, pyroelectricity is induced by changes in the material’s polarization due to thermal fluctuations. , This characteristic makes pyroelectric materials particularly attractive for energy harvesting in environments with fluctuating temperatures or for integration with other renewable sources to form hybrid systems. , For example, combining pyroelectric materials with solar panels can enhance energy capture efficiency during periods of high solar heat, thereby compensating for lower efficiency at cooler temperatures. ,

The combined use of piezoelectric and pyroelectric materials in a unified energy-harvesting system represents a holistic approach to efficient renewable energy generation. This approach broadens the spectrum of exploitable environmental energy sources and stabilizes energy production across diverse and unpredictable climatic conditions. Researchers are developing hybrid systems incorporating piezoelectric and pyroelectric properties, aiming to create more reliable and efficient energy solutions capable of operating under various environmental stimuli. This development represents a substantial step toward the goal of sustainable, clean energy generation. −

This paper introduces a groundbreaking approach for energy generation that utilizes NbO3-based piezoelectric devices. These devices are designed to be compatible with solar cells and to withstand the mechanical forces exerted by raindrops, resulting in a hybrid system capable of operating efficiently across various environmental conditions. The selection of NbO3 is critical due to its robust piezoelectric properties, which enhance energy-conversion efficiency from solar and mechanical inputs.

Integrating NbO3-based piezoelectric devices with traditional solar technology presents a novel hybrid model that addresses the intermittent nature of solar power and unlocks the untapped potential of rain as a renewable energy source. By harnessing the combined capabilities of these systems, this research aims to pave the way for more resilient and versatile energy solutions, particularly beneficial and well-suited for regions with frequent rainfall and fluctuating sunlight availability. Influence of NbO3 Material

This study investigates the use of NbO3, a material known for its exceptional piezoelectric properties, which has the potential to further revolutionize energy harvesting technologies. Unlike traditional piezoelectric materials such as quartz or PZT (Lead Zirconate Titanate), NbO3 exhibits superior mechanical and electrical characteristics, ideally suited for converting environmental mechanical energy into electrical energy (Figure ). This advantage stems from the asymmetrical atomic arrangement in NbO3’s crystal lattice. This structure gives rise to a pronounced piezoelectric effect, efficiently converting mechanical strain into an electrical signal, as shown in Figure . This property is particularly advantageous for the energy harvesting strategy, which focuses on capturing the kinetic energy of raindrops.

Furthermore, research has demonstrated that lithium niobate not only shares these robust piezoelectric properties but also exhibits both pyroelectric and photovoltaic effects. This unique combination makes it particularly valuable for a hybrid energy system. Lithium niobate can efficiently convert temperature changes and sunlight into electrical energy, adding another dimension to the system’s energy harvesting capabilities.

Lithium niobate’s complementary pyroelectric and photovoltaic capabilities are crucial for its integration into durable, efficient hybrid solar systems. These materials demonstrate resilience and robustness under varied environmental conditions, including high temperatures and pressures, ensuring reliable performance across diverse environments. The properties of Nb3 and lithium niobate collectively contribute to enhanced energy conversion efficiency and long-term performance stability, a critical factor for the coveted sustainability of renewable energy applications.

Pb-based piezoelectrics typically exhibit higher charge coefficients than lithium niobate. Commercial PZT-5H is widely reported with d33 ≈ 593 pCN-1, as noted in a calibration study for PZT-5H thin films. While state-of-the-art PMN–PT single crystals aligned along [001] routinely achieve d33 ≳ 1500 pCN-1 with k33 near 0.9 and Curie temperatures on the order of 150 to 180 °C, depending on composition. By comparison, LiNbO3 (single crystal) has a lower d33 (typical measured values ∼ 7 to 10 pC N-1, cut-dependent) but offers high thermal robustness (Curie temperature ∼ 1210 °C) and a substantial pyroelectric coefficient (about 95 μCm −2 K −1 in congruent crystals), together with optical transparency and a lead-free composition attributes advantageous for impact-dominated, high-impedance harvesting and photovoltaic cointegration. ,

Methodology

The methodology herein encompasses three critical stages: material synthesis, device fabrication, and system integration. First, high-purity crystalline material is synthesized to maximize its piezoelectric properties for efficient mechanical-to-electrical energy conversion. Second, the design phase optimizes the LiNbO3 device microarchitecture, utilizing advanced microfabrication techniques to create micropatterns with maximized surface area for stimuli such as raindrops or thermal variations. These patterns ensure that even minimal mechanical disturbances are converted into measurable electrical outputs. The fabricated LiNbO3-devices are then integrated into a hybrid system with silicon-based solar cells, where wafer bonding securely attaches the LiNbO3-layer while preserving light-absorption and mechanical energy-harvesting functionalities. Meanwhile, the LiNbO3-silicon interface is designed to maintain thermal and electrical conductivity, thereby preventing efficiency losses. This methodology prioritizes optimal utilization of LiNbO3 and overall system efficiency, thereby creating a versatile and efficient hybrid energy system. Consequently, this approach represents a significant advancement in renewable energy, offering a robust model for future sustainable technology development.

In the piezoelectric energy harvesting domain, the selection of piezoelectric materials is paramount, as it profoundly influences the functionality and performance of harvester devices. A critical parameter guiding this selection is the piezoelectric coefficient. This coefficient quantifies the material’s efficacy in converting mechanical stress into electrical polarization and strain into an electric field under constant environmental conditions. The piezoelectric coefficients are d31, d32, and d33. Each form corresponds to specific directional movements of ions within the material’s crystal lattice (as exemplified in Figure ). For instance, d33 represents the strain applied in the x3 direction with electrodes positioned on the x1-x2 face. Conversely, d31 reflects strain along x3 with electrodes on the x2-x3 face, and d32 signifies strain in x3 when electrodes are placed on the x1-x3 face.

Background and Methods

Raindrops represent a potentially significant renewable energy source, and research efforts are underway to develop efficient methods for converting their kinetic energy into electrical power. Piezoelectric materials, which generate electric charges in response to mechanical stress, are a promising technology for this application. This work examines recent studies on the design, materials, and performance of piezoelectric raindrop energy harvesters. ,

Palomba et al. investigated a novel cantilever harvester design that utilizes a liquid surface to enhance energy conversion from raindrop impact. Their design incorporates a square spoon at the free end of the piezoelectric beam to hold water. Indoor and outdoor experiments demonstrated that the effect on a liquid surface generated about four times the voltage compared to a dry surface. Mathematical modeling further validated the effectiveness of the fluid layer in boosting energy transfer. These findings suggest that incorporating a liquid surface can significantly improve the performance of piezoelectric raindrop harvesters.

Wong et al. comprehensively reviewed piezoelectric materials used for harvesting raindrop energy. They highlighted the advantages of piezoelectric harvesters, including their simple structure, ease of fabrication, and ability to convert mechanical vibrations directly into electrical charge. Their review identified polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as the most effective material due to its flexibility, low cost, and nontoxic properties. They also compared the performance of bridge and cantilever harvester configurations, with bridge structures exhibiting significantly higher voltage generation upon raindrop impact. This underscores the importance of structural design in optimizing energy harvesting efficiency. Wong et al. conclude that while the technology holds promise, challenges remain regarding robustness in outdoor environments and the intermittent nature of rain.

Liew et al. investigated strategies for developing and testing a raindrop energy harvester using Lead Zirconate Titanate (PZT) piezoelectric transducers. Their study focused on improving the power-generation efficiency of low-voltage devices by harnessing the kinetic energy of raindrops. They experimented with various AC-to-DC harvesting circuits and found that a series-parallel configuration could generate an output voltage of 0.236 V and a current of 0.023 mA. Their research demonstrates the potential of PZT-based harvesters with optimized circuit configurations and highlights the technology’s applicability in regions with abundant rainfall. , Ilyas and Swingler investigated the energy conversion efficiency of piezoelectric materials for harvesting energy from raindrops. Their detailed experiments revealed an efficiency of approximately 0.12% and an average power output of 2.5 mW per unit. The voltage output exhibited a two-stage process during raindrop impacts, suggesting potential for improvement through optimized material properties and mechanical interactions. This study emphasizes the need for further research to enhance the effectiveness of raindrop-based energy harvesting systems.

Chua et al. comprehensively analyzed piezoelectric materials and designs for capturing raindrop energy. Their evaluation of different materials and structures identified bridge designs with PVDF generally producing higher voltages than cantilevers. However, the energy conversion efficiency of these harvesters remained low (typically under 1%), and power density was minimal. These limitations highlight the need for advancements in both materials and harvester design. The study suggests that future efforts should focus on optimizing interface circuits and exploring new piezoelectric materials or composites to improve the effectiveness and practicality of raindrop energy harvesters.

In summary, piezoelectric device technology offers a promising approach for harvesting raindrop energy. Recent research has explored various materials, designs, and circuit configurations to improve the efficiency and performance of these systems. While challenges remain regarding efficiency, power density, and environmental durability, ongoing research holds promise for developing practical, sustainable raindrop energy-harvesting solutions.

Scientific and Engineering Challenges

Integrating LiNbO3 with silicon presents a compelling avenue for novel device functionalities. However, significant scientific and engineering challenges arise due to material property disparities. The primary obstacles lie in the contrasting thermal expansion coefficients and lattice structures of LiNbO3 and silicon. These discrepancies can lead to interfacial stress, delamination, and device failure. Thus, advanced wafer-bonding techniques are pivotal to establishing high-quality, functional interfaces between these dissimilar materials and to overcoming these challenges. ,

Among the various wafer-bonding technologies, direct wafer bonding is the cornerstone of successful LiNbO3-silicon integration. This method meticulously brings the surfaces of LiNbO3 and silicon into atomic-level proximity, fostering robust interfacial interactions. However, achieving a reliable bond necessitates meticulous surface preparation. This involves minimizing particle contamination and surface roughness through techniques such as chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP) or wet etching. In some cases, plasma activation might be employed. This treatment enhances surface energy, promoting stronger adhesion at lower temperatures, thereby preserving the integrity of the constituent materials. −

The integration process often necessitates low-temperature bonding techniques. This approach mitigates the detrimental effects of high temperatures, which can exacerbate the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between LiNbO3 and silicon. Excessive thermal exposure can induce significant stress at the interface, potentially leading to defect formation that compromises overall device performance. Research has shown that employing intermediary layers, such as silicon dioxide (SiO2), facilitates molecular bonding between LiNbO3 and silicon. These interlayers enhance the bond’s mechanical strength and promote improved compatibility between the two materials.

The techniques address the immediate challenges of mechanical and thermal compatibility and pave the way for the functional integration of piezoelectric (LiNbO3) and semiconducting (silicon) components. The resulting LiNbO3-silicon heterostructures can leverage the unique properties of both materials, enabling the development of advanced devices with unprecedented functionality. This opens doors to potential applications in various fields, including optoelectronic devices and integrated circuits with enhanced performance.

Fabrication of Piezoelectric Device

The hybrid solar cell system’s piezoelectric component uses lithium niobate (LiNbO3) for its exceptional piezoelectric and pyroelectric properties. This material selection facilitates the sensitive detection and conversion of mechanical and thermal energies into electrical power. To ensure consistent and reliable performance of piezoelectric devices, high-quality single-crystal LiNbO3 wafers were used instead of deposited LiNbO3 nanoparticle films. The fabrication process involved meticulously applying interdigital transducers (IDTs) directly onto the LiNbO3 nanoparticle wafers. These IDTs capture mechanical stimuli such as raindrops and ambient thermal variations. The design of the IDTs incorporates micrometer-scale gaps, which have been strategically chosen to optimize the sensitivity and efficiency of the energy conversion process.

The fabrication sequence commences with a meticulous cleaning protocol for the LiNbO3 wafers. This initial step removes any surface contaminants that could hinder device performance. A multistage solvent wash, employing acetone followed by methanol, ensures a pristine wafer surface ready for subsequent processing.

Following the cleaning procedure, the wafers undergo photolithography to define the intricate electrode patterns required for the IDT electrodes. This technique involves the application of a bilayer resist stack: a 500 nm-thick layer of LOR-5b (lift-off resist) and a two μm-thick layer of AZ1512 photoresist. The photolithography process meticulously defines the lift-off profile for the IDT electrodes, ensuring their accurate placement.

A layer of chromium (Cr), approximately 40 nm thick, is subsequently sputtered onto the wafer surface to function as an adhesion layer. A 200 nm-thick platinum (Pt) layer is deposited following chromium deposition. Platinum was chosen due to its excellent electrical conductivity and durability, which are crucial electrode properties in a piezoelectric device.

The IDTs are designed with narrow gaps between electrodes to maximize the efficiency of piezoelectric and pyroelectric energy capture. This strategic design choice, featuring micrometer-scale gaps, optimizes the device’s sensitivity to subtle mechanical pressures and temperature gradients, ultimately enhancing overall energy conversion efficiency. The precise arrangement and spacing of the IDTs enable the device to effectively respond to the diverse and frequent mechanical stresses encountered in real-world applications, solidifying its role as a highly effective piezoelectric transducer element within the hybrid solar cell system (Figure ).

Hybrid System Integration

The successful integration of piezoelectric devices with silicon solar cells in the hybrid system hinges on meticulous engineering to ensure optimal performance across diverse energy-harvesting modalities. Lithium niobate (LiNbO3) piezoelectric elements, equipped with intricately patterned interdigital transducers (IDTs), are strategically positioned and bonded to the photovoltaic cells. The critical assembly could benefit from the utilization of transparent conductive adhesives (TCAs), such as Indium Tin Oxide (ITO), in future iterations. These adhesives offer a compelling proposition, as they can maintain high light transmittance to the underlying silicon cells while simultaneously establishing efficient electrical connectivity between the components. This approach presents a promising solution to a key challenge in hybrid system design: energy efficiency. By employing a TCA layer, unimpeded sunlight access to the photovoltaic cells is ensured, effectively mitigating potential efficiency losses that might otherwise arise from shadowing by the piezoelectric components. Additionally, TCAs enhance the system’s mechanical stability, fostering robust integration that can withstand environmental stresses without compromising the electrical properties of either the piezoelectric or photovoltaic layers.

Thermally conductive, electrically insulating epoxies such as graphene could be used to facilitate heat dissipation from the integrated solar cells. Additionally, potential transparent conductive adhesives (TCAs) could be employed to enhance this process. These materials are crucial for managing the thermal load arising from both solar irradiation and ambient temperature fluctuations. Effective heat dissipation prevents overheating of the solar cells, thereby preserving their photovoltaic efficiency. More importantly, it stabilizes the operating temperature range of lithium niobate (LiNbO3), which is essential for the proposed system due to its pyroelectric properties. LiNbO3 generates electrical energy in response to temperature variations; thus, maintaining optimal thermal gradients within the material enhances its energy-conversion capability. This strategy ensures that while solar cells operate efficiently, LiNbO3 can simultaneously leverage controlled thermal fluctuations to maximize pyroelectric output, therefore contributing to the overall robustness, efficiency, and reliability of the hybrid energy harvesting system.

The placement of the piezoelectric elements is strategically designed to capture mechanical energy from raindrops. The IDTs are optimized to directly convert the kinetic energy of raindrop impact into electrical power, serving as a complementary energy source to the solar energy harvested on sunny days. This feature is advantageous in regions with frequent rainfall, where conventional solar panels might exhibit diminished performance.

Overall, this hybrid system effectively harvests energy from three distinct sources: sunlight, ambient heat, and mechanical impact from raindrops. Integrating these technologies into a single system maximizes overall energy yield, ensuring consistent power generation across diverse environmental conditions. Through detailed consideration of material properties and strategic mechanical and thermal design choices, the hybrid system enhances energy production while ensuring its reliability and durability throughout its operational lifespan (Figure ).

Design and Perspective

Design plays an integral role in developing piezoelectric devices, facilitating the optimization of device geometry for maximized energy conversion efficiency. Microstructural design empowers researchers to conduct parametric studies, enabling the adjustment and refinement of design features such as microstructure, material properties, and electrode configurations. This approach enables a granular analysis of how different designs respond to applied mechanical stresses.

Optimization workflows enhance energy conversion efficiency by fine-tuning the device geometry, electrode spacing, and material composition. Microstructural design can identify configurations that maximize electrical output while maintaining the device’s structural integrity. This structural integrity is crucial for practical applications where reliability is paramount.

In their investigation, Guigon et al. explored the potential application of piezoelectric materials for energy harvesting from raindrops. They highlighted the limitations of conventional power sources in powering microsensor devices. Subsequently, the authors proposed a system that leverages a piezoelectric sheet to scavenge vibrational energy generated upon impact by a water droplet. A theoretical model was presented to optimize this energy harvesting system, relating drop speed, height, and diameter. This model was then compared against experimental data. The analysis revealed that optimal performance is achieved when the piezoelectric material is composed of bands with a width equal to two-thirds the maximum diameter of a raindrop, and importantly, the material should not be prestressed. The experimental validation findings demonstrated a close correspondence between the recovered electrical energy and the theoretical predictions.

Researchers can refine designs to achieve optimal performance across a broader range of operational scenarios by incorporating piezoelectric and pyroelectric interactions, particularly under variable thermal conditions. Experimental Setup and Testing

A comprehensive evaluation was conducted to assess the performance and viability of LiNbO3 devices/films as lead-free harvesting materials for energy harvesting applications, with particular emphasis on their integration into a multisource energy utilization system. The critical factor maximizing energy capture resided in the specific design of the electrodes as interdigital transducers (IDTs) with micrometer-scale gaps. This configuration minimized energy dissipation while enhancing the direct piezoelectric effect, which is essential for capturing energy from mechanical stimuli, such as raindrops.

The testing methodology involved subjecting the devices to simulated raindrop impacts (Figure ) as a form of mechanical stimulation to quantify the output voltage. The setup ensures rigorous data accuracy and reliability verification, capturing the real-time responses of piezoelectric and photovoltaic components to environmental stimuli. The narrow spacing of the IDTs facilitated efficient energy capture from the small, high-frequency vibrations generated by raindrop impacts. Precision instruments, such as oscilloscopes and multimeters, ensured the accuracy and reliability of the collected data. A dropper pipet was mounted at a fixed height of 0.30 m above the device to standardize droplet formation and free-fall conditions across all measurements. This configuration ensured repeatable droplet size and impact velocity, with velocity determined by the free-fall relation v ≈ √2gh for the set height; runs were conducted under identical setup parameters to maintain repeatability throughout the test series.

Conceptual illustration of the measurement setup that employs oscilloscopes and other precision instruments to monitor the electrical outputs from piezoelectric devices under test conditions.

Furthermore, environmental simulation tests were conducted to assess the devices’ pyroelectric properties. This involved exposing them to controlled temperature variations, a crucial step in determining the impact of thermal fluctuations on energy output across diverse climatic conditions. The testing regime revealed that the IDT design effectively maintained the structural integrity and functional responsiveness of the LiNbO3 layers under temperature variations. This characteristic prevented a possible decline in both piezoelectric and pyroelectric efficiency.

Durability testing further validated the devices’ ability to withstand prolonged exposure to the environment without significant degradation. This finding supports the suitability of LiNbO3 and the IDT design for long-term energy harvesting applications. Notably, the micrometer-scale gaps between the electrodes ensured that even minute raindrops could generate measurable electrical output, further demonstrating the system’s operational efficiency under various environmental conditions.

Results and Discussion

Extensive experimental analyses of the hybrid energy system reveal significant advancements in capturing and converting energy from raindrops, solar heat, and light. This investigation into LiNbO3-based piezoelectric devices, meticulously engineered with a novel microstructural design, ,,, reveals exceptional responsiveness to mechanical stimuli from raindrops. Each raindrop impact effectively generates a peak-to-peak voltage of 0.8 V. This significant advancement in raindrop-induced responses is attributed to the meticulous design of the devices’ microstructures, which optimizes surface area and geometrical alignment. This optimization enhances sensitivity to raindrop impacts, enabling efficient and rapid transduction into electrical energy. These findings convincingly demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach in harnessing the kinetic energy of raindrops for substantial electrical power output. The hybrid system demonstrates remarkable heat-based energy harvesting capabilities, achieving a peak-to-peak voltage of 7.54 V at temperatures around 70 °C from heat alone. The device reached 8.85 V at temperatures around 80 °C when subjected to raindrops and heat generated by using a hot plate, as shown in Figure . This performance is primarily due to the pyroelectric properties of LiNbO3. LiNbO3 has a unique ability to directly convert thermal changes into electrical energy, a phenomenon resulting from its spontaneous electric polarization, which can be reoriented by temperature fluctuations. This pyroelectric effect is crucial to this system’s energy conversion process, particularly under varying solar intensity and ambient temperatures.

Under the combined influence of heat and raindrops, the hybrid system achieves a peak-to-peak voltage of 8.85 V at temperatures around 80 °C.

A key factor contributing to the system’s thermal responsiveness is the high pyroelectric coefficient of LiNbO3. This coefficient quantifies the level of electric charge generated per unit temperature change. High pyroelectric coefficients are crucial for maximizing the thermal response of this hybrid system. The integration of lithium niobate (LiNbO3) with silicon solar cells in the hybrid system evidently improves photovoltaic performance. The incorporation of piezoelectric layers, specifically LiNbO3, a notable increase in the overall photovoltaic output of the system. This enhancement can be attributed to the multifaceted role of LiNbO3’s unique electro-optic properties. Beyond its direct energy-conversion capabilities via piezoelectricity and pyroelectricity, LiNbO3 modulates light absorption and conversion efficiency within the silicon solar cells. , In solar-only mode at luminous intensity 3.79 cd and illuminance 44.21 lx, the LiNbO3 transducer produced Vpp = 0.40 V (corresponding to 56.82 μW across Rload = 680 Ω; 0.20 Vpp under reduced excitation), whereas in the hybrid configuration the transducer output rose to 0.68 Vpp and the comounted commercial mini-PV delivered 3.00 V DC, giving a combined measured output of 3.68 V, i.e., a 22.6% improvement over the PV baseline. LiNbO3’s contribution to this hybrid configuration is multifold. First, the piezoelectric material generates an electrical charge under mechanical stress, which is induced by wind or vibrations. This proves particularly beneficial in low-light conditions where solar energy input is limited. Second, LiNbO3’s pyroelectric properties enable the direct conversion of thermal changes into electrical energy, thereby adding a valuable thermal dimension to the system’s energy-harvesting capabilities. ,

Furthermore, LiNbO3’s electro-optic properties enable it to serve as an effective light modulator in the hybrid system. By dynamically adjusting its refractive index in response to external voltages, LiNbO3 layers can alter light propagation through the silicon layers. This enables real-time optimization of light absorption based on the intensity and angle of incoming sunlight. This dynamic modulation capability maximizes light capture during peak sunlight hours and enhances low-light performance, resulting in a more effective system across a broader range of light conditions. − The synergistic interplay between the piezoelectric, pyroelectric, and photovoltaic properties of LiNbO3 and the photovoltaic properties of silicon leads to a comprehensive enhancement in energy-harvesting efficiency. This integration exemplifies a forward-thinking approach to solar technology, leveraging the multifunctionality of advanced materials to address the limitations of traditional solar systems.

Long-term stability was evaluated by 100 heating–cooling thermal cycles and 20 repeated raindrop-impact measurements under identical conditions. The tracked outputs (Vpp under raindrop impact and PV output under illumination) showed ≤ 1% drift, confirming repeatability. Post-test SEM inspection verified strong electrode adhesion with no delamination or corrosion at the electrode/LiNbO3 interface. Tilted-view SEM at 45° (Figure ) illustrates uniform sidewall profiles and edge definition. Dimensional metrology of the IDTs shows an electrode line width of 20 μm and a gap of 50 μm as-fabricated, remaining within ± 2% of nominal after the long-run test. The observed stability of IDT geometry and the intact LiNbO3–metal interface support the durability required for sustained piezoelectric and pyroelectric transduction. For crystallographic context, LiNbO3 (R3c) exhibits a trigonal structure, which is noncentrosymmetric and thus permits both piezoelectric and pyroelectric responses. The combination of this crystal symmetry with the measured morphological integrity is consistent with the functional performance reported in section . Lithium niobate (LiNbO3) provides a strong baseline for durability in hybrid harvesters owing to its chemical and mechanical stability and high Curie temperature (∼1210 °C), which supports retention of piezoelectric and pyroelectric properties across wide operating ranges. Strategies for Upscaling Hybrid Solar Cell System

Scalability primarily depends on the LiNbO3–Si bonding yield, encapsulation for outdoor exposure, and low-resistance interconnects. These are standard considerations for scale-up when transitioning from proof-of-concept stacks to deployable modules. The efficiency of chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and physical vapor deposition (PVD) has been continuously improved to optimize the deposition of thin films onto silicon substrates. This optimization is crucial for enhancing the combined photovoltaic and piezoelectric properties of hybrid solar cells.

Modern CVD techniques, particularly those tailored for low-temperature processes like atomic layer deposition (ALD), offer precise control over film thickness and material quality. ALD is particularly advantageous for achieving exceptional uniformity and conformability on intricate geometries, a critical factor in creating efficient, multifunctional layers within hybrid systems. Conversely, PVD has witnessed advancements in the control of key process parameters, such as deposition rate, temperature, and pressure. This precise control allows film properties to be tailored to specific functional requirements. Innovations in PVD, including ion plating and magnetron sputtering, improve crystal orientation control, adhesion, density, and uniformity of the deposited films, ultimately enhancing the performance of the semiconductor components within the hybrid system.

Beyond traditional methods, the Tilting-Mirror-Type Floating Zone (TMFZ) technique has emerged as a prominent method for growing high-quality lithium niobate crystals. The TMFZ method offers distinct advantages, facilitating the production of large, defect-free crystals essential for ensuring high performance of both piezoelectric and photovoltaic functionalities in hybrid systems. The method enables growth with controlled doping and minimized imperfections, which is crucial for consistent performance at scale.

By incorporating these advanced material deposition methods and crystal growth techniques, the scalability of hybrid solar cell systems can be significantly enhanced. This ensures that the upscaling process does not compromise the efficiency or functional integrity of the individual cells within the system.

Results Compilation and Analysis

This study addresses inherent challenges associated with traditional solar cells and large-scale piezoelectric devices. Solar cells typically exhibit a decline in efficiency at elevated temperatures. At the same time, piezoelectric devices often exhibit sluggish response to vibrations and are susceptible to damage from bending. To address these limitations, the present work focuses on developing microstructured piezoelectric devices utilizing lithium niobate (LiNbO3). These devices efficiently detect raindrop vibrations and exhibit exceptional resilience to sunlight and heat exposure.

This enhanced performance is primarily attributed to the inclusion of micrometer gaps within the electrodes of the piezoelectric devices. The exceptional properties of lithium niobate (LiNbO3) are pivotal to the system’s stability and robust performance across diverse environmental conditions. The hybrid system not only maximizes the photovoltaic output of the solar cell component but also efficiently converts mechanical and thermal energy into usable electrical outputs. This multimodal energy harvesting approach ensures uninterrupted energy production, mitigating the impact of fluctuating environmental conditions and maximizing overall system efficiency. Given these advantages, this novel solution is ideal for augmenting energy production in solar farms and for powering solar-powered vehicles. To facilitate independent replication, the manuscript specifies the materials, geometries, stimuli, and analysis procedures used: single-crystal LiNbO3 interdigital transducers with a Cr/Pt electrode stack (Cr ≈ 40 nm/Pt ≈ 200 nm), IDT line width of 20 μm and gap of 50 μm, mechanically mounted on a small commercial silicon solar cell without transparent conductive adhesive; raindrop excitation from a fixed height of 0.30 m using a controlled droplet source; thermal/pyroelectric sweeps across 50–150 °C; and low-illuminance optical tests defined by luminous intensity and illuminance (3.79 cd and 44.21 lx). Electrical loading was selected by a power-versus-load sweep (100 Ω–1 MΩ), with the operating resistor chosen at the peak of P = V2 rms /R; time-domain voltages were acquired using high-impedance instrumentation, and transducer power was computed from Vrms over a fixed analysis window. Photovoltaic measurements were performed in solar-only mode using a standard setup, extracting DC metrics (e.g., Voc, Isc, Pmax) separately from the AC transducer metrics (e.g., Vpp, P = V2/R) to avoid conflation, and morphology/stability checks by plan-view and cross-sectional SEM verified electrode integrity and a clean LiNbO3–metal interface after operation.

It is important to employ corrosion-resistant electrode metals for devices exposed to water; long-duration evaluations revealed no structural degradation, corrosion, or interfacial damage at the LiNbO3–metal interface, consistent with the careful selection of a Cr/Pt stack and the protective role of Pt. Cr/Pt electrodes were selected to mitigate corrosion during water exposure. After long-duration cycling, SEM inspection confirmed no corrosion, delamination, or interfacial damage at the LiNbO3–metal interface. To further reduce electrical losses and improve transduction efficiency, wire bonding to low-resistance buses is recommended in future prototypes. Limitations of the Study

This study demonstrates a laboratory-scale hybrid energy harvester under controlled, repeatable conditions, enabling clear benchmarking and performance quantification. A few practical limitations are noted. First, raindrop excitation was generated using a fixed drop height and controlled droplet generation to ensure in-lab experimental reproducibility; therefore, the results presented herein primarily represent controlled impact conditions rather than the full range of outdoor precipitation variability (e.g., broader droplet-size distributions and wind-driven impacts). Second, illumination and thermal profiles were applied using laboratory setups to isolate the photovoltaic and piezo/pyroelectric contributions; consequently, long-duration outdoor exposure tests (e.g., extended UV, humidity/rain cycling, and dust) were outside the scope of this study and are appropriate next steps for application-oriented validation. Third, the manuscript reports device-level performance, stability, and repeatability. At the same time, full system-level demonstrations (e.g., optimized rectification, power management, and storage integration under specific sensor duty cycles) are not emphasized and can be addressed in future prototypes. Finally, scaling the concept to larger-surface-area modules will primarily involve engineering of packaging and encapsulation, as well as interconnects, while preserving optical access and stable mechanical coupling, which is typical scale-up manufacturing from proof-of-concept prototype devices to deployable modules.

Conclusion

This study presents a compelling investigation into the potential of integrating NbO3-based piezoelectric devices with conventional solar technology to establish a hybrid energy-scavenging system capable of operating across diverse environmental conditions. Through a comprehensive approach that incorporates experimentation, the findings reveal that this system effectively addresses the inherent intermittency of solar power generation while also harnessing energy from mechanical stimuli such as raindrops and thermal fluctuations. The synergistic integration of piezoelectric and pyroelectric properties in lithium niobate (LiNbO3) with solar technology has evidently yielded significant enhancements in photovoltaic output (22.6%) and energy capture from raindrop impacts (8.3%). These advancements render the hybrid system well-suited for deployment in regions with variable weather patterns, offering a reliable, continuous energy source that surpasses traditional systems’ capabilities.

Furthermore, the study emphasizes the critical role of material selection and microstructural design in optimizing performance and efficiency within energy conversion systems. The robust piezoelectric properties of LiNbO3, coupled with the multifunctional capabilities inherent to lithium niobate, play a pivotal role in maximizing the operational effectiveness of the hybrid system across the entire spectrum of tested environmental conditions.

With regard to future advancements, the principles and methodologies established in this research have the potential to pave the way for the development of next-generation renewable energy systems. These systems would significantly improve overall energy efficiency and advance sustainability goals by providing a stable, reliable, and environmentally friendly energy solution. Further research and development efforts are necessary to facilitate the upscaling of this technology, enhance its commercial viability, and broaden its application across diverse climatic zones globally. By achieving these advancements, renewable energy can be further solidified as a cornerstone of global energy strategies.
