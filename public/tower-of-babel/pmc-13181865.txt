Alkaptonuria in two Colombian patients: identification of HGD variants including a novel finding
Authors: María Camila León – Sanabria, Ana María Zarante-Bahamón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlkaptonuria (AKU) is a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism caused by deficiency of homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase (HGD), its deficiency results in homogentisic acid (HGA) accumulation, which oxidizes to form melanin-like pigments that deposit in connective tissues, leading to ochronosis and progressive multisystem complications. We describe two unrelated Colombian female patients with AKU, emphasizing clinical variability and novel genetic findings.Case reportsCase 1: Female with onset of symptoms at age 16, including scleral pigmentation, glaucoma, retinal detachment, and joint pain. Arthroscopy revealed ochronotic pigmentation and cystic changes in the glenohumeral cartilage, pigmented deposits were observed in the ears and sclera during adulthood. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified two HGD variants: c.164_166del (p.Thr55del) and c.774+69C>T, both classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Case 2: Female with reddish urine noted perinatally and recurrent darkened urine episodes during infections. At age 8, she developed intermittent bilateral bone pain in knees and wrists, worsened by physical activity. No evident ochronosis was observed. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) revealed compound heterozygosity for HGD variants: c.808G>A (p.Gly270Arg), likely pathogenic, and c.774+69C>T (VUS).ConclusionsBoth patients shared the intronic HGD variant c.774 + 69 C > T in heterozygosity, a VUS located outside the canonical splice site. Its recurrence in two unrelated individuals with AKU-compatible features and an additional HGD variant suggests potential pathogenic relevance. This report highlights the value of molecular diagnosis for confirming AKU and genetic counseling, and describes a previously unreported HGD variant in a Latin American population, contributing to the understanding of the disorder’s genetic heterogeneity and the importance of genomic characterization in underrepresented groups.

Background

Alkaptonuria (AKU) (OMIM #203500) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by deficiency of the enzyme homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase (HGD), which converts homogentisic acid (HGA) into maleylacetoacetic acid in the tyrosine degradation pathway. Therefore, it is an inborn error of metabolism of the amino acids’ phenylalanine and tyrosine [1]. Tyrosine is required for functions such as the production of melanin, hormones, and various proteins; however, most of the unused tyrosine is ultimately converted into acetoacetate and malate [1, 2].

In AKU, the HGD enzyme cannot convert homogentisic acid into 4-maleylacetoacetate, causing blood levels of HGA to increase up to 100 times above normal. HGA is converted into benzoquinone acetic acid, which forms pigment-like polymers similar to melanin. These accumulate in collagen, and this deposition process is known as ochronosis [3]. Ochronotic pigment deposits bind to connective tissues in various organs, leading to destruction of joints, calcification or regurgitation of the aortic or mitral valves, aortic dilatation, kidney stones, prostatic stones and hypothyroidism [1, 3]. The main features of AKU are dark urine or urine that darkens upon exposure to oxygen, ochronosis, and arthritis affecting the spine and large joints. Ochronosis generally appears after age 30, and arthritis often begins in the third decade of life [3].

Although the disease has been documented in various populations, the specific genetic variants may differ significantly depending on ethnic and geographic background. This article presents two cases of unrelated Colombian female patients diagnosed with AKU. Molecular analyses revealed a previously undescribed variant in the literature, highlighting the importance of genetic characterization in underrepresented populations and its relevance to understanding the genetic heterogeneity of the disease.

Cases presentation

Case 1

A patient began experiencing symptoms at age 16, initially presenting scleral pigmentation. By age 63, she reported glaucoma, retinal detachment, and joint pain. One year later, she developed right upper limb joint pain, prompting arthroscopy evaluation of the shoulder, which revealed cystic changes in the left glenohumeral joint. The patient continued to experience persistent arthralgias; rheumatologic evaluation included lumbar spine MRI demonstrating intervertebral disc narrowing and calcifications, as well as hip MRI showing inflammatory changes at the ischiotibial muscle insertions. Due to progressive hip pain with extreme walking limitations and these imaging findings, the patient subsequently underwent hip replacement surgery. The femoral head obtained during this procedure demonstrated characteristic ochronotic pigmentation (Figure 1). Urinary HGA levels were markedly elevated at 1,511 mg/24 h (approximately 8,990 µmol/24 h; reference range 0–10 mg/24 h) raising strong suspicion of AKU. During adulthood, pigmented deposits were visible in the ears and sclerae. Slit-lamp examination revealed asymmetric, dark, ring-like scleral pigmentation (Figure 2), along with loss of the photomotor reflex in the right eye. The auricles displayed pigment deposition in the antihelix and conchal regions bilaterally. Genetic testing by whole-exome sequencing (WES) identified two variants in the HGD gene (NM_000187.4): c.164_166del (p.Thr55del) (rs1708093744) and c.774 + 69 C > T (rs1399212408), both classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS).Fig. 2Case 1: Bilateral scleral pigmentation due to ochronosis

Genomic DNA was extracted and WES was performed using the Twist Comprehensive Exome v1 capture kit. Sequencing achieved a mean target coverage exceeding 100x, with more than 94% of target bases covered at ≥ 20x. Raw sequencing data were processed using Varsome Clinical software; base call (BCL) files were converted to FASTQ format, and reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37 (hg19) using BWA. Variant calling was performed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK).

Case 2

A patient presented with reddish urine noted at birth, followed by recurrent episodes of darkened urine during infections. At age 8, she began experiencing intermittent bilateral bone pain in the knees and wrists, which worsened with physical activity. No scleral lesions were observed, although a hyperpigmented spot was noted in the nasal quadrant of the left eyeball. Auricular cartilage showed no visible pigment deposits. Initial biochemical testing revealed markedly elevated HGA levels at 2,539 mg/24 h (approximately 15,100 µmol/24 h; reference range 0–10 mg/24 h), raising strong suspicion for AKU. Initial trio exome sequencing did not yield a conclusive molecular diagnosis, likely due to the use of a clinical exome strategy with limited target regions and coverage. Subsequent trio whole-exome sequencing (WES) and individual whole-genome sequencing (WGS), performed using different platforms and bioinformatic pipelines, provided improved coverage of the HGD gene and enabled the identification of compound heterozygosity for HGD variants (NM_000187.4): c.808G > A (p.Gly270Arg; rs120074174), classified as likely pathogenic and paternally inherited, and c.774 + 69 C > T (rs1399212408), an intronic variant of uncertain significance (VUS).

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood. WGS, including the mitochondrial genome, was performed using the MGIEasy FS DNA Library Prep kit and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-G400 platform, achieving a mean depth of coverage of approximately 30×. Sequence data were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37 (hg19), and variant calling and annotation were performed using the GenomeOne software (DreamGenics), applying established quality filtering criteria.

In addition to the variants identified in the HGD gene, other genetic findings were detected however were considered incidental findings and not contributory to the alkaptonuria phenotype.

As shown in Table 1, both patients presented with markedly elevated urinary HGA levels and carried compound heterozygous variants in the HGD gene. In both cases, the intronic variant c.774 + 69 C > T in heterozygosity was identified and classified as a VUS. The fact that the same alteration was found in two unrelated patients—both with clinical manifestations consistent with AKU and a second variant in HGD—suggests a possible pathogenic contribution that has not yet been fully elucidated.

Discussion and conclusions

AKU is a rare metabolic disorder with an estimated global prevalence of 1 in 250,000 to 1,000,000 live births (LB) [2]. In Colombia, the exact prevalence is unknown, however, the first documented case in the country dates back to 1981 [4]. According to the National Health Institute (Instituto Nacional de Salud, INS) and the Colombian Public Health Surveillance System (SIVIGILA) between 2019 and epidemiological period XII of 2024, a total of 7 cases of AKU were reported. Specifically, 2 cases were documented in 2019, 2 in 2021, 2 in 2022, and 1 case in 2024, bringing the total to 7 reported cases. No specific information was found for the years 2020 and 2023 [5]. Including the two patients presented in this report, a total of nine AKU cases have been reported in Colombia to date, and genetic analysis has been performed in two of these patients, to the best of our knowledge.

Although ochronosis is classically considered a late-onset clinical manifestation of AKU, typically becoming evident after the third decade of life, accumulating evidence indicates that the pathological process begins much earlier [6]. Subclinical and microscopic features of ochronosis can already be detected during childhood, preceding overt clinical signs. Histological and functional studies have demonstrated early connective tissue involvement, and gait analysis has revealed deviation profiles in pediatric patients, suggesting that musculoskeletal function is affected at an early stage [6]. In this context, the intermittent bilateral knee and wrist pain reported in Case 2 may represent early, subclinical ochronotic involvement, consistent with an ongoing degenerative process preceding the classical adult phenotype.

In addition, early clinical manifestations have been reported, including ocular ochronosis in a 14-year-old patient [7], pigmentary changes in the axillary regions between 8 and 10 years of age, as well as ear cartilage and scleral pigmentation in patients aged 12 and 13 years [8]. These observations support the concept that ochronosis develops progressively from childhood, with clinical recognition delayed until adulthood.

Despite its long-standing recognition, AKU remains poorly understood by many healthcare professionals in Colombia [9]. This lack of awareness is further compounded by significant diagnostic challenges, including limited access to reliable urinary HGA quantification and restricted availability of advanced molecular techniques. Genetic analysis of the HGD gene is particularly hampered by its technical complexity, cost of testing, and limited availability of comprehensive sequencing technologies.

The biochemical diagnosis is based on detecting a significant amount of HGA in the urine (typically 1 to 8 g per day). The molecular diagnosis (necessary to provide genetic counseling for family members) relies on the identification of biallelic pathogenic variants in the HGD gene [1]. The analysis of the HGD gene presents significant challenges due to its extensive allelic heterogeneity and the presence of deep intronic or synonymous variants with uncertain clinical significance (VUS). These features often require the use of high-resolution genomic technologies, such as WES or WGS, particularly in regions where AKU is exceptionally rare and most HGD variants are novel or previously unreported [10, 11].

The HGD gene (3q13.33) is a single-copy gene composed of 14 exons (54 kb of genomic DNA, NG_011957.1) [12, 13]. The corresponding transcript (1,674 bp linear mRNA, NM_000187.4) codes for a protomer composed of 445 amino acids (NP_000178.2), and its expression has been confirmed mainly in the liver and kidney [13]. The most prevalent variants are missense substitutions (64.2%), followed by splicing (16.1%), frameshift (10.6%) and nonsense (4.6%), as well as larger genomic deletions (4.1%) and one reported extension variant [13] Missense variants are predicted to affect the activity of the enzyme by three molecular mechanisms: decrease of stability of individual protomers, disruption of protomer–protomer interactions or modification of residues in the active site region [11].

Reflecting this molecular diversity, a substantial number of HGD variants have been reported in public databases such as UniProt, ClinVar, LOVD, MitoMap, VarSome, and PubMed. According to VarSome [14], 297 variants have been classified as pathogenic, while 109 are considered VUS. This high number of VUS highlights the ongoing challenge in interpreting novel or rare HGD variants, particularly in underrepresented populations, and reinforces the need for functional studies and population-specific data to improve variant classification and diagnostic accuracy [10].

The complexity of HGD variant detection is further exemplified by international experiences, for example, the missense variant c.808G > A (p.Gly270Arg) identified in Case 2 has been previously reported as pathogenic in patients with AKU in individuals from the Dominican Republic [15], its identification in our patient further supports its clinical relevance and contributes additional geographic evidence to the existing literature. The c.164_166del (p.Thr55del) variant identified in Case 1 represents a novel finding. Although classified as a variant of uncertain significance according to ACMG criteria (PM2, PM4), its location within the HGD coding region and the patient’s highly suggestive biochemical and clinical phenotype support its potential contribution to disease.

On the other hand, deep intronic variants required advanced molecular analyses for pathogenicity confirmation, in a Taiwanese cohort [10], the deep intronic variant c.16–2063 A > C was identified as disease-causing only after functional studies demonstrated its effect on abnormal splicing. Crucially, this variant was detectable because the affected cryptic exon had been previously characterized and deliberately included in the capture design for targeted sequencing [10]. Without such prior knowledge and probe customization, the variant would likely have gone unnoticed. This case underscores the critical importance of considering non-coding regions and cryptic exons in the molecular diagnostic approach to AKU and other ultra-rare diseases [10, 16].

According to Hertzog et al. [16], WES often provides limited coverage beyond the first 100–200 base pairs of intronic regions. However, in cases where the inborn error of metabolism (IEM) presents with a highly specific biochemical signature, this can serve as a compelling rationale to investigate non-coding regions of the relevant gene when no pathogenic variants are found in the coding sequence. With the growing use of WGS in diagnostic workflows, the recognition and reannotation of non-coding variants is expected to increase. Patients with a characteristic clinical or biochemical phenotype but negative findings on trio WES may benefit significantly from WGS. Additionally, reanalysis of WGS data for pathogenic variants has been shown to improve diagnostic yield in such cases [16].

A limitation of this study is that copy number variant (CNV) analysis and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) were not performed. Therefore, larger deletions or duplications affecting the HGD gene cannot be completely excluded.

Although AKU is primarily diagnosed through clinical and biochemical findings, genetic testing remains a valuable tool in selected cases. It enables confirmation of the genotype, supports genetic counseling, and contributes to expanding the known mutational spectrum of HGD [1, 13, 16]. Both patients in this case report, shared the intronic HGD variant c.774 + 69 C > T in heterozygosity—a VUS located beyond the canonical splice site—which limits its detection and interpretation through WES.

Functional splicing assays were not performed for the c.774 + 69 C > T variant. However, in silico splicing analysis using Alamut Visual Plus predicted that this variant creates a cryptic donor splice site within intron 10. Multiple algorithms (SSF, MaxEnt, NNSplice, GeneSplicer) support splice site activation, and SPiP analysis estimated a 69.3% risk of splicing alteration, supporting a potential impact on pre-mRNA processing.

The recurrence of the c.774 + 69 C > T deep intronic variant in two unrelated individuals supports its potential relevance. However, the molecular context differs between the two cases: Case 1 harbors two variants classified as VUS in the HGD gene, whereas Case 2 carries a likely pathogenic missense variant in combination with the same intronic VUS. This finding highlights the diagnostic limitations of WES in detecting regulatory non-coding variants that may affect splicing and underscores the complexity of variant interpretation in AKU.

In contrast, WGS allows for more comprehensive analysis of such regions [16], as demonstrated in the second patient, in whom this VUS was confirmed through WGS. These finding highlights both the diagnostic value of WGS in complex cases and the importance of considering deep intronic variants in AKU.

According to gnomAD v4, the c.774 + 69 C > T variant is an extremely rare allele reported across different populations, supporting its rarity while underscoring the importance of documenting its clinical relevance in underrepresented populations such as Latin Americans. This reinforces the need for broader genomic characterization in underrepresented populations to improve variant classification, enhance diagnostic precision, and expand our understanding of the genetic heterogeneity of AKU. Also, the identification of the same deep intronic HGD variant in two unrelated Colombian patients raises the question of a possible shared ancestral origin. However, given the limited number of cases, lack of extended segregation data, the very low frequency in other populations, we consider the available evidence is insufficient to support a founder effect. At present, this observation requires further investigation in larger cohorts.

To date, none of the 2 individuals identified in this article are receiving treatment with nitisinone, primarily due to its limited availability and access in Colombia. However, recent landmark studies such as SONIA-2 [17] have demonstrated that treatment with 10 mg of nitisinone daily can reduce urinary HGA levels by over 99% and significantly slow the clinical progression of the disease, including ochronotic pigmentation and joint deterioration [17, 18]. These findings are highly relevant for clinical decision-making and the urgent need to improve access to therapy in low and middle-income countries. Further research is needed to determine whether normalization of urinary HGA is essential and to better understand the long-term safety of initiating therapy at an early age [18].

Early diagnosis is crucial in enabling timely intervention and improving long-term outcomes. These cases highlight the critical role of clinical suspicion and biochemical testing in the diagnostic approach to AKU. In both patients, the clinical presentation and markedly elevated urinary HGA levels were strongly suggestive of AKU, supporting the diagnosis even when initial genetic testing yielded non-conclusive or uncertain results. This underscores the importance of expanding access to molecular tools such as WGS, as well as of promoting the identification and interpretation of population-specific variants. Strengthening local genomic databases and expertise will be key to improving diagnostic accuracy and tailoring care for patients with rare disorders like AKU in underrepresented populations.
