Algebraic Properties of PAC Codes
Vlad-Florin Drăgoi, Mohammad Rowshan

Abstract
We analyze polarization-adjusted convolutional codes using the algebraic representation of polar and Reed-Muller codes. We define a large class of codes, called generalized polynomial polar codes which include PAC codes and Reverse PAC codes. We derive structural properties of generalized polynomial polar codes, such as duality, minimum distance. We also deduce some structural limits in terms of number of minimum weight codewords, and dimension of monomial sub-code.



I
Introduction

Polar codes
[
10
]
are members of a larger family of codes called decreasing monomial codes (DMC)
[
2
]
, which also includes Reed–Muller codes. The algebraic formalism in
[
3
]
revealed that dual of a DMC is still a DMC, the minimum distance of DMC is directly related to its maximum degree monomials, and the permutation group of DMCs contains the lower-triangular affine group (LTA). These properties have since been exploited in code construction
[
14
,
22
]
, cryptography
[
2
,
4
]
, closed-form and algorithmic weight enumeration
[
6
,
20
,
23
,
13
]
, and automorphism-ensemble decoding
[
16
]
. However, this algebraic framework presently covers only decreasing monomial codes; more general monomial or polynomial-based families lie outside its scope, despite their increasing relevance. The arrival of polarization-adjusted convolutional (PAC) codes
[
11
]
, reverse PAC codes
[
8
]
, and profile-shifted PAC codes
[
9
]
calls for a more refined understanding of the structural properties of these families.

PAC codes enhance the finite-length performance of polar codes by concatenating a one-to-one convolutional pre-transform with the polar transform, thereby preserving channel polarization while effectively reducing the number of low-weight codewords, compared to plain polar codes, and improving error-correction performance at short block lengths, under near-ML decoding (e.g., sequential or list decoding), close to theoretical limits. Yet, existing work on PAC and reverse PAC codes has mainly focused on performance and approximate distance spectra
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, or leveraging algebraic code properties to further enhance distance properties
[
1
,
7
,
15
]
, and does not provide a systematic algebraic description of their duals or permutation groups. In particular, PAC codes are not decreasing monomial codes, are not closed under LTA permutations, and their codewords are not evaluations of polynomials in the span of the plain-polar information set
\mathcal{A}
.

PAC codes exhibit several structural differences from plain polar codes: they are not decreasing monomial codes, their generator matrices are not isomorphic to the polar generator, they are not closed under LTA permutations, and their codewords are not evaluations of polynomials in the span of the polar information set
\mathcal{A}
. In this work we place PAC codes inside a broader class of
generalized polynomial polar codes
and reinterpret them as
upper polynomial polar codes
using the algebraic representation of polar and Reed–Muller codes. Within this framework we show that, except at very low or very high rates, upper polynomial polar codes contain large decreasing-monomial and monomial subcodes; we characterize duals by first analyzing duals of monomial codes and then proving that the dual of an upper polynomial polar code is a lower polynomial polar code; and we derive lower bounds on the number of minimum-weight codewords, from which we conclude that a PAC code preserves the minimum distance of the underlying plain polar code.

Permutation group

The set of permutations that globally leave the code invariant, forms the
automorphism group
of the code
\mathcal{C}.

A bijective affine transformation over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
is represented by a pair
(\mathbf{B},\bm{\varepsilon})
where
\mathbf{B}=(b_{i,j})\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times m}
is an invertible matrix lying in the general linear group
{\rm GL}(m,2)
and
\bm{\varepsilon}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
. The action of
(\mathbf{B},\bm{\varepsilon})
on a monomial
g=\prod_{i\in\operatorname{ind}(g)}x_{i}
(denoted by
(\mathbf{B},\bm{\varepsilon})\cdot g

x_{i}
of
g
by a variable

as

y_{i}=x_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}b_{i,j}x_{j}+\varepsilon_{i}
. This new variable
y_{i}
, is in fact a linear form (a polynomial in which all terms have a degree at most 1), where the maximum variable is
x_{i}.

In
[
3
]
it was proved that the lower triangular affine transformation denoted by
{\rm LTA}(m,2)
is a subgroup of the permutation group of any decreasing monomial code.
{\rm LTA}(m,2)
is a subgroup of the affine group, where
\mathbf{B}\in{\rm GL}(m,2)
is a lower triangular binary matrix with

can be expressed as the following mapping from

The set of polynomials resulting from the action of

and denoted by

{\rm LTA}(m,2)\cdot f=\{(\mathbf{B},\bm{\varepsilon})\cdot f\mid(\mathbf{B},\bm{\varepsilon})\in{\rm LTA}(m,2)\}.

Since

acts as a permutation on
\operatorname{ev}(f)
, all elements in
{\rm LTA}(m,2)\cdot f
have the same Hamming weight.

III
Generalized polynomial polar codes

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be a polar code with information set
\mathcal{A}
with length
N=2^{m}
. The encoding step of a pure polar code is defined by the vector-matrix multiplication
\bm{v}\bm{G}_{N}
where
\bm{v}
is the rate-profile dependent vector, meaning that
v_{i}=0,\forall i\in\mathcal{A}^{c}.
A PAC code applies a pre-transformation via a binary polynomial
\mathbf{p}(x)
, effectively changing the input vector
\bm{v}
to
\bm{u}=\bm{v}\star\mathbf{p}\bmod 2
, so that the codeword is
\bm{c}=\bm{u}\bm{G}_{N}=\bm{v}\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}
, where
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N\times N}
is the Toeplitz matrix of
\mathbf{p}
, denoting exactly the rate-1 convolution operation

\mathbf{P}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}p_{0}&p_{1}&\dots&p_{l}&0&\dots&0\\
0&p_{0}&\dots&p_{l-1}&p_{l}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&p_{0}\end{array}\right),

with
p_{0}=1.
Its inverse

\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}

implements the terminated deconvolution:

\bm{v}=\mathbf{H}\bm{u}.

Here we will consider a broader class of polynomial codes, that we call
Generalized polynomial polar codes
.

Let

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be a upper polynomial polar code. Then,
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}}([\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1])
is a decreasing monomial code with dimension
N-1-\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})
satisfying
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}}([\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1])\subset\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}).

Proof.

First let us denote the set of monomials corresponding to
\mathcal{A}
by
\mathcal{I}
and the set of monomials corresponding to
[\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1]
by
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{F}}.

The dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}}(\mathcal{A}_{c})
equals
N-1-\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})
since all monomials in the set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{F}}=\{g\mid\operatorname{ev}(g)=\bm{G}_{N}[i],i\in\mathcal{A}_{c}\}
are linearly independent. Let us prove that
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}}(\mathcal{A}_{c})
is a decreasing monomial code. By definition, the monomial basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}}(\mathcal{A}_{c})
consists of all monomials corresponding to consecutive integers in the set
\mathcal{A}_{c}.
Now, suppose there is a monomial
f\preceq g
with
f\not\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
g\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{F}}.
Notice that we can not have
f\preceq_{w}g
, since this would imply
\operatorname{ind}(f)\subset\operatorname{ind}(g)\Rightarrow 2^{m}-1-i_{f}<2^{m}-1-i_{g}\Rightarrow i_{f}>i_{g}
which contradicts
f\not\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{F}}
or equivalently
i_{f}\not\in[\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1].
If
f\preceq_{\mathbf{sh}}g
, by definition we have
2^{m}-1-i_{f}<2^{m}-1-i_{g}\Rightarrow i_{f}>i_{g}
which again contradicts
f\not\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{F}}.

∎

IV-A

Properties of duals of monomial codes

From
[
3
]
, we use the multiplicative complement of a monomial
g\in\mathcal{M}_{m}
defined by
\widecheck{g}\triangleq\frac{x_{0}\dots x_{m-1}}{g}.
Remark that it is well-defined since all monomials divide
x_{0}\dots x_{m-1}.
Note that while
g=\mathbf{x}^{\bin(2^{m}-1-i)}
for a given
i
, its complement is
(\widecheck{g})=\mathbf{x}^{\bin(i)}.
We can extend the definition to a monomial set
\widecheck{\mathcal{I}}=\{\widecheck{g}\mid g\in\mathcal{I}\}
and to polynomials as well,
\widecheck{Q}=\sum\limits_{f\in\operatorname{terms}(Q)}\widecheck{f}
for any
Q\in{\mathbf{R}}_{m}
. We have

\begin{array}[]{cccc}g&\rightarrow\widecheck{g}=\dfrac{x_{0}\dots x_{m-1}}{g}&\quad\mathcal{I}&\rightarrow\widecheck{\mathcal{I}}=\{\widecheck{g}\mid g\in\mathcal{I}\}\\
i&\rightarrow\widecheck{i}=2^{m}-1-i&\quad\mathcal{A}&\rightarrow\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}=\{\widecheck{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{A}\}\end{array}

We know from
[
3
]
that the dual of a decreasing monomial code is still a decreasing monomial code:

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{M}_{m}\setminus\widecheck{\mathcal{I}})
. We can rewrite the equality
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}(\widecheck{\mathcal{F}}).
Indeed,
\mathcal{M}_{m}\setminus\widecheck{I}=\widecheck{\mathcal{M}_{m}}\setminus\widecheck{\mathcal{I}}=\widecheck{\left(\mathcal{M}_{m}\setminus\mathcal{I}\right)}=\widecheck{\mathcal{F}}.
Or, equivalently

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\widecheck{\mathcal{A}^{c}}).

(7)

However, the dual of a monomial code (where
\mathcal{I}
is simply a monomial set) is in general a polynomial code
[
5
]
.

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}(\psi(\widecheck{\mathcal{F}})),

where
\psi(\mathcal{I})=\{\psi(f)\mid f\in\mathcal{I}\}
for any monomial set
\mathcal{I}
, and
\psi(x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{s}})=(1+x_{i_{1}})\cdot\dots\cdot(1+x_{i_{s}}).
The action of
\psi
can also be described in terms of permutations. Indeed, if we denote by
\pi
(or in matrix terms
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
) the permutation that swaps
i
with
2^{m}-1-i
for all indices
i\in[0,2^{m}-1]
, then, we have
[
5
]

\operatorname{ev}(\psi(g))^{\pi}=\operatorname{ev}(g)

(8)

The following properties hold.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset[0,N-1]
and
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be a monomial code. We have
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}=\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}^{t}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}}(\widecheck{\mathcal{A}^{c}}).

Proof.

The first property is straightforward from the definition of
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}.
For the second we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:dual-monomial-dragoi}}{=}\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})^{\bot}\overset{\eqref{eq:psi-permutation-dragoi}}{=}\mathcal{C}(\psi(\widecheck{\mathcal{F}}))=\mathcal{C}((\widecheck{\mathcal{F}}))^{\pi}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}}(\widecheck{\mathcal{A}^{c}}).

We have used the mapping
\mathcal{A}^{c}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{F};\mathcal{A}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{I}.

∎

Algebraic properties of
\bm{G}_{N}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
will be at hand.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
be the matrix permutation of
\psi.
Then

•

\bm{G}_{N}=\bm{G}_{N}^{-1}

•

\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}=(\bm{G}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi})^{-1}
;

•

(\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi})^{2}=\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}

•

\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}=\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}.

We can deduce from the previous lemma that
\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}=\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}.

Proof.

The first identity comes from the fact that

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\bm{0}\\
\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}&\bm{0}\\
\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{2}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\mathbf{A}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

for any matrix
\mathbf{A}
defined over a field of characteristic 2. Apply this property to
\bm{G}_{N}
and use the fact that
\bm{G}_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{Id}
to obtain the result.
The second identity follows immediately from the definition of
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
and
\bm{G}_{N}^{-1}=\bm{G}_{N}.

Regarding the third identity we have that

\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{2}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\mathbf{A}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

for any matrix
\mathbf{A}
defined over a field of characteristic 2. Since
\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
is of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}&\mathbf{A}\\
\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{A}\end{pmatrix}
with
\mathbf{A}^{2}=\mathbf{Id}
we obtain the wanted result.

The last identity follows directly from the third identity and the first one.
∎

Remark 2
.

Notice that row
i
of
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
is equal to
\operatorname{ev}(\psi(\mathbf{x}^{\bin(i)})).
Also, each column of
\bm{G}_{N}
is the evaluation of
\psi(\mathbf{x}^{\bin(i)}).

A direct consequence of Lemma
2
and Proposition
2
is the following.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{M}_{m}
be a monomial set. We have

\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{I})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}^{c}).

(9)

Proof.

First notice that
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\widecheck{\mathcal{A}})=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
, due to the fact
i
becomes
2^{m}-1-i
by
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
and
i
becomes
2^{m}-i-1
by
\widecheck{i}.
Hence, taking information set
\widecheck{\mathcal{A}}
is equivalent to taking information set
\mathcal{A}
on the permuted matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}.

Therefore, we have

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}}(\widecheck{\mathcal{A}^{c}})=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}}(\mathcal{A}^{c}).

Using the properties of
\bm{G}_{N}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}
from
2
we have

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}}(\mathcal{A}^{c})=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}^{c}),

which ends our proof.
∎

Our results reads as follows. Given a monomial code, specified by an information set
\mathcal{A}
, compute the frozen set
\mathcal{A}^{c}
which will be the information of the dual code. The dual code is a lower polynomial code since
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}
is a monomial basis and
\bm{G}_{N}
is a lower triangular matrix.

becomes
P_{g}=x_{0}x_{1}x_{4}x_{5}+x_{0}x_{4}x_{5}+x_{4}x_{5}.
Hence, when taking the complement we obtain
\widecheck{g}=x_{2}x_{3}
and
\widecheck{P_{g}}=x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}.
Even if we apply
\psi(\widecheck{P_{g}})
there is no guaranty that
\operatorname{ev}(\psi(\widecheck{P_{g}}))
is orthogonal of all vectors in the PAC code. For example,
\psi(\widecheck{P_{g}})
is not orthogonal on
x_{4}x_{5}\in\mathcal{I}.

The main reason for that is because when we multiply these two polynomials we obtain a new polynomial which has among its terms the monomial
x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\psi(\widecheck{P_{g}})\cdot x_{4}x_{5}

\displaystyle=\psi(x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})\cdot x_{4}x_{5}

\displaystyle=(x_{2}+1)(x_{3}+1)\cdot x_{4}x_{5}

\displaystyle+(x_{1}+1)(x_{2}+1)(x_{3}+1)\cdot x_{4}x_{5}

\displaystyle+(x_{0}+1)(x_{1}+1)(x_{2}+1)(x_{3}+1)\cdot x_{4}x_{5}.

After expanding the terms we do obtain that
x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}\in\operatorname{terms}(\psi(\widecheck{P_{g}})\cdot x_{4}x_{5})
, which implies that
\operatorname{ev}(\psi(\widecheck{P_{g}})),\operatorname{ev}(x_{4}x_{5})
are not orthogonal.

A crucial property on orthogonality can be expressed in terms of product of polynomials.

Lemma 3
.

Let
P_{1},P_{2}\in{\mathbf{R}}_{m}.
Then
\operatorname{ev}(P_{1})\cdot\operatorname{ev}(P_{2})=0
iff
x_{0}\dots x_{m-1}\not\in\operatorname{terms}(P_{1}\cdot P_{2}).

Proof.

By definition of the scalar product between two vectors
\operatorname{ev}(P_{1})
and
\operatorname{ev}(P_{2})
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{ev}(P_{1})\cdot\operatorname{ev}(P_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\left(\operatorname{ev}(P_{1})\odot\operatorname{ev}(P_{2})\right)[i]=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\operatorname{ev}(P_{1}\cdot P_{2})[i].

Hence, as long as
\operatorname{ev}(P_{1}\cdot P_{2})
is of even weight the scalar product equals zero, due to the fact that summation is over
\mathbb{F}_{2}.
Since the code
\mathcal{R}(m-1,m)
is the code of all possible vectors of even Hamming weight, and
x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}
is the only monomial in
\mathcal{R}(m,m)\setminus\mathcal{R}(m-1,m)
we deduce the wanted result.
∎

We can explain how to compute the dual of upper polynomial polar code and reveal its structure. Our idea builds on the use of the multiplicative complement and
\psi.
We will thus search monomials
f
that are indexed by the frozen set
\mathcal{A}^{c}
and determine conditions for orthogonality with the polynomials in the basis of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}).

The first property that we exhibit states that when searching for duality conditions for upper polynomial polar codes we only need to look backwards in terms of indices.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be an upper polynomial polar code. Let
\operatorname{ev}(f)=\bm{G}_{N}[i]
with
i\in[0,2^{m}-1].
We have

\forall j>i\quad\operatorname{ev}(\widecheck{f})\cdot\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j]=0.

Proof.

Let
P=\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j]
. By definition
P=\bm{G}_{N}[j]+\sum\limits_{l\in J\setminus\{j\}}\bm{G}_{N}[l]
where
J
is the set corresponding to row
j
of
\mathbf{P}
, i.e.,
J=\supp(\mathbf{P}[j]).
We thus have

\operatorname{ev}(P)\cdot\operatorname{ev}(\widecheck{f})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N}\operatorname{ev}((\bm{G}_{N}[j]+\sum\limits_{l\in J\setminus\{j\}}\bm{G}_{N}[l])\cdot\psi(\widecheck{f})).

In order to have a non zero scalar product, by Lemma
3
, the monomial
x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}
should belong to the terms of
(\bm{G}_{N}[j]+\sum\limits_{l\in J}\bm{G}_{N}[l])\cdot\psi(\widecheck{f}).
This implies that there are some indices
l\in J
for which
\widecheck{f}\cdot\bm{G}_{N}[l]=x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}
which is equivalent to
\widecheck{\bm{G}_{N}[i]}\cdot\bm{G}_{N}[l]=x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}.
For this equation to be satisfied we must either have
\bm{G}_{N}[i]=\bm{G}_{N}[l]
or
\bm{G}_{N}[i]|\bm{G}_{N}[l]
which contradicts
j>i.

∎

Hence, the only way of combining polynomials from a set
\mathcal{A}
with multiplicative complements of monomials from frozen set
\mathcal{A}^{c}
such that they do not define orthogonal vectors is by combining monomials backwards. That is why we will define a matrix which acts exactly in the opposite direction of
\mathbf{P}.
To do so, the following property will be at use.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be a upper polynomial polar code. Let
\operatorname{ev}(f)=\bm{G}_{N}[i]
for an integer
i\in[0,2^{m}-1].
Then for any
j<i
we have

g\in\operatorname{terms}(\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j])\text{ and }x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}=g\cdot\widecheck{f}\Rightarrow\widecheck{g}|\widecheck{f}.

Proof.

For
j<i
the terms in
\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j]
are monomials
g
with
\operatorname{ev}(g)=\bm{G}_{N}[l]
for some values
l<i.
Thus,

x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}=g\cdot\widecheck{f}\Rightarrow\widecheck{f}\text{ is a multiple of }\dfrac{x_{0}\cdot x_{m-1}}{g}=\widecheck{g}.

∎

We can now define a matrix
\mathbf{M}
that counts the number of monomials/terms in each polynomial
P
,
\operatorname{ev}(P)=\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j]
, which fall under the previous condition. More exactly for each monomial
f
,
\operatorname{ev}(f)=\bm{G}_{N}[i]
we will count how may
g\in\operatorname{terms}(P)
satisfy the condition
\widecheck{g}|\widecheck{f}.

M_{i,j}=\left|\{g\in\operatorname{terms}(P),\mid\widecheck{g}|\widecheck{f}\}\right|\pmod{2}

(10)

where
\operatorname{ev}(P)=\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j],\operatorname{ev}(f)=\bm{G}_{N}[i].

Given a row index
i
, recall that our main issue comes from row indices
j<i.
Hence, if we consider a monomial
\widecheck{f}
, where
\operatorname{ev}(f)=\bm{G}_{N}[i]
the vector in the code
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
raising problems with respect to orthogonality are exactly those coming from indices
j<i.
If there are an even number of monomials
g\in\operatorname{terms}(P)
,
P
being the polynomial corresponding to a codeword
\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}[j]
, they cancel out each other due to
2=0
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}.
The problem is when there are an odd number of such monomials. In this case we need to combine our initial monomial
f
with another one that manages to cancel out the
x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}
resulting from
g\cdot\widecheck{f}.

Dual of upper polynomial polar code

The dual of an upper polynomial polar code can now be revealed.

Theorem 1
.

We have

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{{P^{t}}^{-1}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}^{c}).

(12)

Proof.

To determine a polynomial that gives an orthogonal vector over the entire code
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
we first select indices
i\in\mathcal{A}^{c}
and create the canonical vector
\varepsilon_{i}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
having only one
1
in the position
i.
Then we compute a vector
\bm{v}
satisfying
\bm{v}\mathbf{M}=\varepsilon_{i}.
The vector
\bm{v}
encodes the backward linear combination required for monomial
\bm{x}^{\bin(i)}
in order to generate a vector which is orthogonal on all codewords of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{p}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}).
This can be done since
\mathbf{M}
is invertible, and thus we have
\bm{v}=\varepsilon_{i}\mathbf{M}^{-1}.
Also, except for the trivial case when the dimension of the code is
2^{m}
and the dual is thus degenerate, the dual always exists since its dimension equals
\mathcal{A}^{c}.
This is because all polynomials defining the dual code are linearly independent, i.e,
\{\varepsilon_{i}\mathbf{M}^{-1}|i\in\mathcal{A}^{c}\}.

To select the polynomials indexed by the frozen set we need to apply our transformation to the monomial basis
\widecheck{\bm{x}^{2^{m}-1-i}}=\bm{x}^{i}.
Hence, one polynomial from the dual code is simply
\varepsilon_{i}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}.

Use Proposition
3
, and Lemma
2
to obtain the final result.
∎

Recall now that when
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{Id}
we have by Lemma
6
that
\mathbf{M}=\bm{G}_{N}
, which implies
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}^{c}).

Corollary 2
.

We have

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{{\mathbf{P}^{t}}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}}(\mathcal{A}^{c}).

(13)

Remark 3
.

Since
\mathbf{Q}_{\pi}\bm{G}_{N}
is a monomial matrix, and since
{\mathbf{P}^{t}}^{-1}\bm{G}_{N}
is an lower triangular invertible matrix, the dual of an upper polynomial polar code is a lower polynomial polar code.

Let’s gather all the results on decreasing monomial sub-codes for polynomial polar and their duals into one final result.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be an upper polynomial polar code. Then we have

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1])\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})\\
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1]).

(14)

Moreover,

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-1-\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1])\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}\\
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-1-\max(\mathcal{A}^{c}),N-1]).

(15)

Proof.

We have
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1])\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}^{\bot}\Rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1])^{\bot}.
Since
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1])
is a decreasing monomial code we have
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1])^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([\min(\mathcal{A}),N-1]).

The same can be applied to
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1])\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})^{\bot}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1])^{\bot}=\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([N-1-\max(\mathcal{A}^{c}),N-1]).

∎

Mininum distance and minimum weight codewords

Using the algebraic structure of the code, we can also study the minimum distance and the number of minimum‑weight codewords of an upper polynomial polar code. From
[
12
,
21
,
24
]
, the minimum distance of a PAC code is at least that of the underlying plain polar code. The proof in
[
21
, Lemma 1]
holds for any upper polynomial polar code. Here, we make a stronger claim: the minimum distance is preserved.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
be an upper polynomial polar code, with
\mathcal{A}
a decreasing monomial set, and suppose
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
has minimum distance
2^{m-r}.
Then, the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
is also
2^{m-r}
and the number of minimum weight codewords is lower bounded by
2^{r}
for any code rate and length.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
has minimum distance
2^{m-r}
then
\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}\cdot x_{r-1})\in\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A})
due to the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}}(\mathcal{A})
is a decreasing monomial code. Indeed, if there is any monomial
f\in\mathcal{I}
of degree
r
, which evaluates to a minimum weight codeword, then
x_{0}\cdot x_{r-1}\preceq f.
Since the row-index corresponding to
x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}
is
2^{r}+\dots+2^{m-1}
, the decreasing monomial code
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([2^{m}-2^{r},2^{m}-1])
is a sub-code of
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}).
We do have that
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([2^{m}-2^{r},2^{m}-1])
is the code generated by all monomials which are smaller than or equal to
x_{0}\cdots x_{r-1}
with respect to
\preceq.

We thus have
[2^{m}-2^{r},2^{m}-1]\subset\mathcal{A}
which implies
\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})>2^{m}-2^{r}
and finally
[2^{m}-2^{r},2^{m}-1]\subset[\max(\mathcal{A}^{c})+1,N-1].
We can infer

\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([2^{m}-2^{r},N-1])\subset\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}(\mathcal{A}).

This means that the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{P}\bm{G}_{N}}
is lower than or equal to
2^{m-r}.
Using Lemma 1 from
[
21
]
we deduce the wanted equality.
Also the code
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([2^{m}-2^{r},N-1])
has
2^{r}
minimum weight codewords which ends our proof.

∎

.

Remark 5
.

For any code length it exist some dimensions
k\in[0,N-1]
for which no improvements in the number of minimum weight codewords can be achieved, by any upper polynomial polar code.

Such polar codes are either exactly those containing only one maximum degree monomial, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{r}=\{x_{0}\cdots x_{r-1}\}
or those containing the maximum degree monomials in the code
\mathcal{C}_{\bm{G}_{N}}([\max(A^{c}+1),N-1]).

Let’s provide a more detailed analysis of the results reported in Table
IV
from bottom to top. We exclude the trivial cases
r=m
and
r=0.

•

Max. degree monomials
r=1\Rightarrow x_{0}
belongs to the polar code. In this case
\w_{\min}=2^{6-1}=32
, and we have
5
code rates for this case, corresponding to
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0}\},\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1}\},\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\},\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\},\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}.

The first order Reed-Muller code is not here is because
x_{0}x_{1}
is more reliable than
x_{5}.

Row 62 in Table
IV
corresponds to
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0}\}
hence the plain polar code is equal to any upper triangular polynomial polar code. The same holds for row 63 which corresponds to
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1}\}.

Just after this we will no longer have equality since all these polar codes have
x_{0}x_{1}
frozen while
x_{2},x_{3}
or
x_{4}
are in
\mathcal{I}.
Hence,
x_{0}x_{1}
is a the gap where we notice the first jump.

•

Max. degree monomials
r=2\Rightarrow x_{0}x_{1}
belongs to the polar code. In this case
\w_{\min}=2^{6-2}=16
, and we have
12
code rates for this case. The first four codes provide equal minimum weight codewords for both plain polar and upper polynomial polar codes. The argument is identical with the case
r=1.
Indeed, the first four codes will have

–

x_{0}x_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\Rightarrow 2^{2}=4
minimum weight codewords. This is the case for two code rates, namely
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{0}x_{1}\}
and
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{0}x_{1}\}.

–

x_{0}x_{1},x_{0}x_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\Rightarrow 2^{2}+2^{2+1}=12
minimum weight codewords. Here we have
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{0}x_{1},x_{0}x_{2}\}.

–

x_{0}x_{1},x_{0}x_{2},x_{1}x_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\Rightarrow 2^{2}+2^{2+1}+2^{2+2}=28
minimum weight codewords. Here we have
\mathcal{I}=\{1,x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{0}x_{1},x_{0}x_{2},x_{1}x_{2}\}.

The gap here is provided by the monomial
x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}
which is not of degree
2
but it has a larger index compared to any other degree monomial
x_{i}x_{j}
where at least one of the variables is
x_{3}\preceq x_{i}.

•

Max. degree monomials
r=3\Rightarrow x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}
belongs to the polar code. In this case
\w_{\min}=2^{6-3}=8
, and we have
19
code rates for this case. The first six polar codes have the same number of minimum weight codewords with the upper polynomial polar codes.

–

x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{3}
gives
2^{3}=8
minimum weight codewords. there are 3 codes having only this monomial of degree 3. Up to the next monomial of degree 3
x_{0}x_{1}x_{3}
there are 2 monomials in between or equivalently two indices,
x_{0}x_{3},x_{1}x_{3}.

–

x_{0}x_{1}x_{3}\in\mathcal{I}_{3}
gives
2^{3+1}=16
plus
8
(from
x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}
) makes
24
minimum weight codewords. There are only two codes rates with this number of minimum weight codewords, since after
x_{0}x_{1}x_{3}
the upcoming row corresponds to
x_{2}x_{3}.

–

x_{0}x_{2}x_{3}
gives
2^{3+2}=32
minimum weight codewords, and together with the previous
24
, we obtain
56.
There is only one polar code with such minimum weight contribution. Here, we notice that we have two code rates for which the lower bound is
56
while the polar code is one equal to the bound and two strictly bigger. The reason why the second polar code we obtain
88
minimum weight codewords is because the monomial
x_{0}x_{1}x_{4}
was considered. This means that there is a frozen pattern between
x_{0}x_{2}x_{3}
and
x_{0}x_{1}x_{4}.
Now, why
x_{0}x_{1}x_{4}
was taken before
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}
? The reason is because we ordered channels with respect to beta-expansion and we have considered
\beta=2^{1/4}
, which makes
x_{0}x_{1}x_{4}
more reliable than
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}.

After this we notice that the gap arises again from
x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}.
