Three-Neighbour Bootstrap Percolation in Thin Three-Dimensional Grids
Will Dolphin, Peter J. Dukes

Abstract
We improve the status of the problem of determining minimum-sized percolating sets in a × b × c a\times b\times c grids under the 3 3 -neighbour process. Using several new constructions, we show that optimal percolating sets exist whenever min ⁡ ( a , b , c ) ≥ 7 \min(a,b,c)\geq 7 . As an important step toward this, we also show that all grids with min ⁡ ( a , b , c ) ≥ 4 \min(a,b,c)\geq 4 have a percolating set whose size exactly achieves the lower bound ( a ​ b + a ​ c + b ​ c ) / 3 (ab+ac+bc)/3 whenever this value is an integer.

Introduction

Let
G
be a graph and
r
a positive integer. The
r
-neighbour bootstrap percolation process
on
G
begins with an initial set
A_{0}\subseteq V(G)
of ‘infected’ vertices; at each time step, a new vertex becomes infected if it has at least
r
infected neighbours. That is, for
t\geq 1
, the set of vertices infected at time step
t
is

A_{t}:=A_{t-1}\cup\{v\in V(G):|N_{G}(v)\cap A_{t-1}|\geq r\}.

We say that
A_{0}

percolates
under the
r
-neighbour process on
G
if
\cup_{t=0}^{\infty}A_{t}=V(G)
. An early application of bootstrap percolation was in modelling the dynamics of ferromagnetism
[
2
]
.

For finite graphs, it is natural to ask for the smallest size of an initial set which percolates. This paper builds on some recent work on the case
r=3
for a special class of graphs.

For positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
, the
d
-
dimensional grid graph
with side lengths
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
has vertex set
[a_{1}]\times\dots\times[a_{d}]
, with
two vertices
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
and
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})
adjacent if and only if, for some
i\in[d]
we have
|u_{i}-v_{i}|=1
and
u_{j}=v_{j}
for all
j\neq i
. Alternatively, this grid is the Cartesian product of paths
P_{a_{1}}\Box\dots\Box P_{a_{d}}
.
A focal point of research on bootstrap percolation assumes
G
is a grid graph; see for instance

[
1
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
and the references therein. The first two of these references make significant progress on the extremal problem with
r=d=3
; in other words, they investigated the smallest size of a percolating set under the
3
-neighbour process on
3
-dimensional grids. In particular, this question was answered in
[
3
]
for
a\times b\times c
grids whenever
\min(a,b,c)\geq 11
. One of our main results, Theorem
4.3
, is to extend this to
\min(a,b,c)\geq 7
.

It turns out (see Lemma
2.1
) that an initial set
A_{0}
which percolates on the
a\times b\times c
grid must satisfy the lower bound
|A_{0}|\geq(ab+ac+bc)/3
. When equality holds, the right side is an integer and the

3
-neighbour process always infects each new vertex in
A_{t}
with exactly three neighbours in
A_{t-1}
. We say that a grid is
perfect
if it admits a percolating set
A_{0}
exactly meeting this condition.

For convenience, we abbreviate the grid graph
P_{a}\Box P_{b}\Box P_{c}
with the ordered triple
(a,b,c)
and say that it has
thickness
equal to
\min(a,b,c)
.
It was shown in
[
1
]
that grids
(1,2^{k}-1,2^{k}-1)
are perfect under the
3
-neighbour process for each positive integer
k
. Later, it was shown in
[
3
]
that these are the only perfect grids of thickness
1
. Of course, grids of thickness
1
are equivalent to
2
-dimensional grids, so the problem is fully settled for
d=2
.

Using various nicely structured and computer generated initial sets
A_{0}
, it was also shown in
[
3
]
that all grids
(a,b,c)
with
3\mid ab+ac+bc
and thickness at least
5
are perfect. Here, we reduce this minimum thickness to
4
. This is helpful for our above-mentioned result answering the overall extremal problem for thickness at least
7
.

In the next section, we give the lower bound on
|A_{0}|
for
r=d=3
. Proofs also appear in earlier work on the topic, but we sketch a proof here for completeness. We also review a useful recursive construction from
[
3
]
that assembles a minimum-sized percolating set in terms of those for four smaller grids. In Section
3
, we give several new constructions of perfect grids having thickness
2
or
4
. Some of these are infinite families and analyzed in more detail; illustrations showing the percolation process are also given in the appendix. In Sections
3
and
4
, we feed these into the recursive construction to prove our main results. We conclude in Section
5
with a summary of the status of the problem.

2
Preliminaries

The following lower bound on
3
-neighbour percolating sets in
3
-dimensional grids serves as our starting point and benchmark for our constructions to follow. A proof can be found in the references, but we provide a sketch here for completeness.

Lemma 2.1

(Surface area lower bound)
.

Suppose
A_{0}
is a subset of the grid
(a,b,c)
that percolates under the
3
-neighbour process. Then

|A_{0}|\geq\frac{ab+ac+bc}{3}.

(2.2)

Proof.

Let
G
denote the grid and consider a set
A\subseteq V(G)
. For
x\in A
, let
n(A,x):=|N_{G}(x)\cap A|
, and define
n(A):=\sum_{x\in A}n(A,x)
.

Now, suppose
A_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{t}=V(G)
is a sequence of steps in the
3
-neighbour process on
G
. The key observation is that the quantity
6|A_{i}|-n(A_{i})
is nonincreasing over time steps
i=0,1,\dots,t
. This is because each newly infected vertex
x\in A_{i+1}\setminus A_{i}
adds six but subtracts two for every neighbour of
x
in
A_{i}
.

Suppose
\min(a,b,c)\geq 2
. There are

(a-2)(b-2)(c-2)
vertices of degree
6
,
2(a-2)(b-2)+2(a-2)(c-2)+2(b-2)(c-2)
vertices of degree
5
,
4(a-2)+4(b-2)+4(c-2)
vertices of degree
4
, and
8
vertices of degree
3
. So, after simplification,

n(G)=6abc-2ab-2ac-2bc.

(2.3)

Suppose, on the other hand, that
\min(a,b,c)=1
. If
a=1
and
b,c\geq 2
, there are
(b-2)(c-2)
vertices of degree
4
,
2(b-2)+2(c-2)
vertices of degree
3
, and
4
vertices of degree
2
. This gives
n(G)=4bc-2b-2c
, agreeing with (
2.3
). The case
a=b=1
(
G=P_{c}
) is trivial, with
n(G)=2c
. This also matches (
2.3
).

It follows that

6|A_{0}|\geq 6|A_{0}|-n(A_{0})\geq 6|A_{t}|-n(A_{t})=2ab+2ac+2bc,

which implies the bound (
2.2
).
∎

A grid
(a,b,c)
is
perfect
if it has a percolating set
A_{0}
achieving the lower bound in (
2.2
); it is
optimal
if it has a percolating set
A_{0}
of size equal to the ceiling of the fraction on the right of (
2.2
).

It is easy to see that a grid
(a,b,c)
can be perfect only if either two of the integers
a,b,c
are divisible by three, or all three of
a,b,c
are in the same congruence class (mod
3
). Further details, including a more general lower bound for higher-dimensional grids, can be found in
[
3
]
.

Next is an important recursive construction used in earlier work, and also useful for our main results to follow.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
3
]
)
.

Let
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
be positive integers for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Suppose that each of the grids
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{1})
,
(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})
and
(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})
is perfect.

(a)

If
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
is perfect, then so is
(a_{1}+a_{2},b_{1}+b_{2},c_{1}+c_{2})
.

(b)

If
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
is optimal, then so is
(a_{1}+a_{2},b_{1}+b_{2},c_{1}+c_{2})
.

3
Perfect grids of thickness 2 and 4

Recall that the grid
(1,b,c)
is perfect only if
b=c=2^{k}-1
for some positive integer
k
. Therefore, it is of limited use to apply Lemma
2.4
(a) with grids of thickness one as ingredients. In more detail, if (say)
a_{1}=1
, then the construction would require
b_{1}=c_{1}
and
b_{2}=c_{2}
. This would result in perfect grids
(a,b,c)
only when
b=c
, and indeed only for a sparse set of such values.

The construction becomes more versatile using grids of thickness
2
as ingredients.
In
[
3
]
, it was shown that grids of the form
(2,b,c)
are perfect if
b,c\geq 3
have different parity and
3\mid 2(b+c)+bc
. This is summarized below for later reference.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

The grid
(2,b,c)
is perfect if
b,c\geq 3
,
b\equiv c\equiv 0
or
2\pmod{3}
, and
b\equiv c+3\pmod{6}
.

To supplement these constructions, the next lemma gives some perfect constructions for thickness 2 which do not have the parity restriction on
b,c
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The following grids are perfect:

(a)

(2,3,c)
and
(2,6,c)
for all
c\equiv 0\pmod{3}
,
c\geq 6
.

(b)

(2,5,c)
and
(2,8,c)
for all
c\equiv 2\pmod{3}
,
c\geq 5
.

Proof.

We focus on the cases not already covered by Lemma
3.1
. The cases
(2,3,c)
for
c\equiv 3\pmod{6}
were shown to be perfect in Proposition 5.4 of
[
3
]
. For the remaining cases,
the reader is referred to Appendix
A.2
, which gives constructions based on periodic initial sets.
∎

Although Lemma
3.2
suffices for our purposes, we remark that certain cases admit easy generalizations to two parameter families
(2,b,c)
with general values
b\equiv c\pmod{6}
.

Shortly, it will be shown that Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
combine via the recursive construction to settle most cases with thickness
4
. However, we need a few special constructions when one or both of the other sides is small. The next two lemmas cover these cases.

Lemma 3.3
.

The following grids are perfect:
(4,6,6)
,
(4,6,9)
, and
(4,9,9)
.

Proof.

For
(4,6,6)
, we can apply Lemma
2.4
with
(a_{1},a_{2})=(1,3)
and
(b_{1},b_{2})=(c_{1},c_{2})=(3,3)
.
For
(4,6,9)
and
(4,9,9)
, explicit constructions meeting the bound (
2.2
) were found by computer. These are given in the appendix as Constructions
A.1
and
A.2
, respectively.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

The following grids are perfect:

(a)

(4,4,c)
for all
c\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,
c\geq 4
.

(b)

(4,7,c)
for all
c\equiv 1\pmod{3}
,
c\geq 4
.

Proof.

We divide each case into slightly separate constructions according to the parity of
c
. Similar to the thickness 2 families, the reader is referred to Appendix
A.2
for constructions based on periodic initial sets.
∎

We are now ready for the first of our main results, which characterizes all perfect grids of the form
(4,b,c)
.

Theorem 3.5
.

The grid
(4,b,c)
is perfect for all
b\equiv c\equiv 0
or
1\pmod{3}
,
b,c\geq 4
.

Proof.

Suppose without loss of generality that
b\leq c
.
For
b\in\{4,7\}
, we make direct use of Lemma
3.4
. If both
b,c\in\{6,9\}
, we apply Lemma
3.3
.
Therefore, in what follows we may assume
c\geq 10
. We divide the remainder of the proof according to cases on the congruence classes of
b
and
c
(mod
6
). In each of these cases, we use Lemma
2.4
(a) with
a_{1}=a_{2}=2
, and choose
b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2}
such that
b_{1}+b_{2}=b
and
c_{1}+c_{2}=c
.

Case 1
:
b\equiv c\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.
First, if
b=6
and
c\geq 12
, we can take

(b_{1},c_{1})=(3,6)
. Perfect grids exist for
(2,3,6)
and
(2,3,c-6)
by Lemma
3.2
(a). Suppose next that
b\equiv c\pmod{2}
with
b\geq 9
and
c\geq 15
. We can again take
(b_{1},c_{1})=(3,6)
. The grids
(2,3,6)
,
(2,3,c-6)
,
(2,b-3,6)
are perfect by
Lemma
3.2
(a), and
(2,b-3,c-6)
is perfect by Lemma
3.1
. Suppose
b\not\equiv c\pmod{2}
with
b\geq 9
and
c\geq 12
. This time, we take
(b_{1},c_{1})=(6,6)
. The grids
(2,6,6)
,
(2,6,c-6)
and
(2,b-6,6)
are perfect by Lemma
3.2
and
(2,b-6,c-6)
is perfect by Lemma
3.1
. In each of these cases, the recursive construction Lemma
2.4
(a) with the indicated ingredients gives us that
(4,b,c)
is perfect.

Case 2
:
b\equiv c\equiv 1\pmod{3}
.
If
b=c=10
, we can apply the recursive construction with four copies of
(2,5,5)
, which is perfect by Lemma
3.2
. So assume
c\geq 13
in the remainder of the argument.
If
b\equiv c\pmod{2}
, use
(b_{1},c_{1})=(5,8)
. If
b\not\equiv c\pmod{2}
, use
(b_{1},c_{1})=(5,5)
. In either case,
b_{2},c_{2}
are each at least
5
and have different parity. Thus all required perfect grids exist by Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
(b). The recursive construction shows that
(4,b,c)
is perfect.
∎

4
Optimal grids of thickness 7 to 10

In this section, we use Theorem
3.5
to lower the known minimum side length of optimal
3
-dimensional grids under the
3
-neighbour process. First, we set up some helpful specific constructions, beginning with a series of small optimal grids found by computer search.

Lemma 4.1
.

The grid
(a,b,c)
is optimal whenever
a\in\{4,5\}
and
b,c\in\{2,\dots,7\}
.

Proof.

Perfect constructions for
\min(a,b,c)\in\{2,3\}
follow from various small cases found in
[
3
]
, and for
\min(a,b,c)\in\{4,5\}
from Theorem
3.5
. Otherwise, explicit optimal (non-perfect) constructions for the remaining cases are given in Appendix
A.3
.
∎

The next lemma summarizes various results in Section 5 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

The grid
(3,u,v)
is perfect whenever
u\in\{2,\dots,7\}
and
v\equiv 3\pmod{6}
, with the exception of
(u,v)=(2,3)
.

Proof.

These follow directly from the following four results in
[
3
]
:
Proposition 5.4 for
u=2
,
Proposition 5.9 for
u=4
,
Proposition 5.11 for
u=6
, and
Proposition 5.13 for
u\in\{3,5,7\}
.
∎

With these tools in place, we are ready for our main result on optimal 3-dimensional grids.

Theorem 4.3
.

The grid
(a,b,c)
is optimal whenever
\min(a,b,c)\geq 7
.

Proof.

Suppose without loss of generality that
7\leq a\leq b\leq c
.
Theorem 1.6 of
[
3
]
handles all cases with
a\geq 11
. The proof of this uses the recursive construction, stated here as our Lemma
2.4
(b), combining one optimal and three perfect grids of thickness at least
5
. With our Theorem
3.5
and Lemma
4.1
, we have access to optimal and perfect grids of thickness at least
4
. The same proof as in
[
3
]
now works for
a=10
. We give a minor modification of the argument for each of the cases
a\in\{7,8,9\}
.

Suppose
a=9
. If either
3\mid b
or
3\mid c
, then

(a,b,c)
is perfect by Theorem 6.3 of
[
3
]
. Otherwise, let
a_{1}=6
and choose
b_{1},c_{1}\in\{2,4,5,7\}
such that

b-b_{1}\equiv c-c_{1}\equiv 3\pmod{6}
. Put
a_{2}=3
,
b_{2}=b-b_{1}
, and
c_{2}=c-c_{1}
. The grids

(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})
and
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{1})

are perfect by Lemma
4.2
. The grid
(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})
is perfect, either by Lemma
4.2
if
\min(b_{2},c_{2})=3
or by Theorem 6.3 of
[
3
]
when
\min(b_{2},c_{2})\geq 9
. The grid
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})
is optimal, either by Lemma
4.1
if
\max(b_{1},c_{1})\leq 5
or in the case
(6,7,7)
via optimal
(3,4,4)
and perfect grids
(3,3,3)
and
(3,3,4)
. With these choices of
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
, Lemma
2.4
(b) implies that
(a,b,c)
is optimal.

Suppose
a=8
.
If
b\equiv c\equiv 0\pmod{3}
or
2\pmod{3}
, then
(8,b,c)
is perfect by Theorem 6.3 of
[
3
]
. Otherwise, let
a_{1}=5
and choose
b_{1},c_{1}\in\{2,\dots,7\}
such that
b-b_{1}\equiv c-c_{1}\equiv 3\pmod{6}
. Put
a_{2}=3
,
b_{2}=b-b_{1}
, and
c_{2}=c-c_{1}
.
Since
b_{2},c_{2}\equiv 3\pmod{6}
, we know that
(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})=(5,b_{2},c_{2})
is perfect, either by Theorem 6.3 of
[
3
]
if
b_{2},c_{2}\geq 9
, or by Lemma
4.2
if
b_{2}
or
c_{2}=3
. Suppose, for the moment, that neither
(b_{1},c_{2})
nor
(c_{1},b_{2})
equals
(2,3)
. Then each of
(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})=(3,b_{1},c_{2})
and
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{1})=(3,b_{2},c_{1})
is perfect by Lemma
4.2
.

The case
(b_{1},c_{2})=(2,3)
implies
c=c_{1}+c_{2}\leq 7+3=10
while
b=b_{1}+b_{2}\geq 2+9=11
, a contradiction to our assumption that
a\leq b\leq c
.
The case
(b_{2},c_{1})=(3,2)
implies
b=b_{1}+b_{2}\leq 7+3=10
, and hence
b\in\{8,9,10\}
. Since perfect grids of thickness 8 are already settled and
c\equiv c_{2}=2\pmod{3}
, we may assume
b\neq 8
.

For
b=9
or
10
, we use the minor modification
(b_{1},b_{2})=(3,6)
or
(4,6)
, respectively.
We again have that
(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})=(5,6,c_{2})
is perfect, this time using Lemma
4.1
if
c_{2}=3
. The grids
(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})=(3,b_{1},c_{2})
and
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{1})=(3,6,2)
are perfect by Lemma
4.2
.

The grid
(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1})=(5,b_{1},c_{1})
is optimal for each choice of
b_{1}
and
c_{1}
, by Lemma
4.1
. It follows by Lemma
2.4
(b) that
(a,b,c)
is optimal.

Finally, suppose
a=7
. The proof in this case is similar to that for
a=8
, except for the following differences. Perfect cases occur when
b\equiv c\equiv 0\pmod{3}
or
1\pmod{3}
. We use
(a_{1},a_{2})=(4,3)
. When
b_{2}\equiv c_{2}\equiv 3\pmod{6}
, the grid
(a_{1},b_{2},c_{2})
is perfect by Theorem
3.5
. Grids
(a_{2},b_{1},c_{2})
and
(a_{2},b_{2},c_{1})
are again perfect by Lemma
4.2
unless
(b_{2},c_{1})=(3,2)
. This only occurs for
b\leq 10
and
c\equiv 5\pmod{6}
.
The same patch taking
b_{2}=6
works except when
b=7
. In that case, we have
(a,b,c)=(7,7,c)
. Here, we can use
(a_{1},a_{2})=(b_{1},b_{2})=(4,3)
. When
c=11
, the choice
c_{1}=5
yields one optimal and three perfect ingredients by Lemma
4.1
. When
c\geq 17
, the recursive construction with
c_{1}=8
works assuming an optimal grid
(4,4,8)
and perfect grids
(3,3,8)
and
(3,4,c-8)
. The first of these is given as Construction
A.30
in the Appendix and the other two are shown to exist in Propositions 5.8 and 5.9, respectively, of
[
3
]
.
∎

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the
3
-neighbour bootstrap percolation process on
a\times b\times c
grids. We have extended the near-classification of perfect grids via several new constructions. In particular, it is now known that
(a,b,c)
is perfect whenever
\min(a,b,c)\geq 4
and
3\mid ab+ac+bc
. As a consequence, we have also shown that
(a,b,c)
is optimal whenever
\min(a,b,c)\geq 7
.

Perfect two-dimensional grids were fully classified in
[
3
]
. To complete the classification of perfect three-dimensional grids, all that remains is to find certain still outstanding two-parameter perfect constructions for grids of thickness 2 and 3. Following this, a full classification of optimal
3
-dimensional grids should be within reach. The non-optimal grids are almost surely limited to
(2,3,3)
,
(2,2,c)
for
c\geq 8
, and
(1,b,c)
for certain cases of
b
and
c
. In the first case, the minimum size of a percolating set is 8. In the second family of cases, a straightforward argument gives
\lceil\frac{3c+1}{2}\rceil
as the minimum size. Finally, according to a very recent seminar announcement, Neal Bushaw has completed the determination of minimum-sized percolating sets in two-dimensional grids.
