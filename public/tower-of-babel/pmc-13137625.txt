Post-obstructive pulmonary edema following near-hanging: a case report and systematic review of published cases
Authors: Shimels Getaneh Weldemedhn, Behaylu Tesfamaryam Hagos, Rediwan Gashaw Geto, Yitayal Lebeza Alemu, Elias Muche Kebede, Wegderes Bogale Gebresilassie, Melaku Tsediew Berhanu, Genetu Dagnaw Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-obstructive pulmonary edema (POPE) is a rare, life-threatening complication of strangulation. Management is based on low-grade evidence, and outcomes are poorly characterized.ObjectiveTo report a systematic review of POPE following near-hanging, illustrated by a case report that represents the first documented African case.MethodsWe combined a novel case report with a systematic review of published case reports and case series, conducted per a pre-registered protocol on the Open Science Framework (OSF). A clinical trial number was not applicable. A comprehensive search of major databases identified all relevant published cases. Data on demographics, management, and outcomes were extracted and synthesized descriptively.ResultsFifteen cases from 11 publications were included. Patients were predominantly young (median age 24). Symptoms overwhelmingly presented pre-hospital (93%). Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) on admission was low (median 6), and the overall mortality rate was 40% (6/15). Adjunctive therapies were reported to be administered more frequently in survivors than in non-survivors, including diuretics (55.6% vs. 16.7%) and corticosteroids (44.4% vs. 16.7%), representing a potential clinical signal.ConclusionPOPE following strangulation is associated with high mortality in young, otherwise healthy populations. This review identified a tentative association between adjunctive diuretic or corticosteroid therapy and improved survival. While these hypothesis-generating findings require cautious interpretation due to the low-level evidence, they represent a potential clinical signal necessitating further prospective evaluation and standardized reporting.Keywords: Acute upper airway obstruction, NPPE, Pulmonary edema, POPE, Strangulation, Near-hanging, Africa

Introduction

Post-obstructive pulmonary edema (POPE), or negative pressure pulmonary edema, is a rare but serious condition where fluid rapidly fills the lungs following an upper airway obstructive events [1, 2]. It is categorized into two types based on the mechanism: Type I occurs after a sudden acute obstruction, as observed with hanging or strangulation, where the high negative pressure generated in the chest pulls fluid from the capillaries, while Type II occurs after surgical or less commonly non-surgical relief of a chronic obstruction where the pathophysiology involves a reperfusion injury [1].

Due to the infeasibility of randomized trials for this rare event, the management of POPE following strangulation is not standardized and relies largely on anecdotal experience and supportive care. Consequently, systematic review of case reports currently represent the highest level of evidence available to synthesize knowledge for this clinical scenario. This review aggregates all published cases of POPE following strangulation. We focused on this global cohort because these patients, whether from suicidal or accidental near-hanging, typically possess healthy baseline cardiovascular function. This allowed us to isolate the clinical trajectory and mortality of POPE as a purely transient, mechanical process.

Notably, this is the first reported case from sub-Saharan Africa, with only one other case currently documented on the continent (Morocco) [3]. The apparent rarity of POPE in Africa likely reflects delayed presentations and the frequent misattribution of respiratory distress to aspiration pneumonia.

A high index of suspicion is critical. Recognizing that these patients are generally young and previously healthy justifies the prioritization of scarce resources: early intervention with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) or mechanical ventilation can be life-saving. In resource-limited settings, identifying POPE as a non-infectious, rapidly reversible condition, rather than treating it as aspiration pneumonia, enables better triage, avoids unnecessary antibiotics, and ensures an accurate prognosis of rapid clinical resolution.

Case presentation

A 47-year-old male was brought to the emergency department approximately an hour after a suicide attempt by hanging from a two-meter height, where he was discovered unconscious. During prehospital transport, the patient developed symptoms of acute respiratory distress, including tachypnea and a cough productive of pink, frothy sputum. On admission, vital signs were significant for tachycardia (HR 146/min), tachypnea (RR 40/min), and severe hypoxemia with an SpO2 of 74% on room air. His blood pressure was 120/70 mmHg. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 11 (E3V4M4) with midsized pupils that are reactive to light. Cervical examination evidenced ligature marks with bruising and abrasions. Pulmonary auscultation revealed bilateral coarse crackles.

Initial laboratory studies, including complete blood count, serum glucose, and renal function tests, were within normal limits. The Electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated sinus tachycardia but was otherwise unremarkable, with no evidence of ischemia or chamber enlargement. A chest X-ray (Fig. 1) demonstrated bilateral diffuse alveolar infiltrates consistent with acute pulmonary edema, notably with a normal cardiac silhouette.

Supine Anteroposterior (AP) chest X-ray demonstrating extensive bilateral airspace opacities

The patient was immediately initiated on mechanical ventilation with Assist-control ventilation (set as per ARDSnet protocol) to maintain SpO2 between 88 and 95%. Other management included intravenous furosemide (40 mg administered every 8 h), targeting a negative fluid balance, and a single dose of intravenous dexamethasone (8 mg) for possible laryngeal edema. His clinical course showed progressive improvement, and he was successfully weaned from ventilatory support on the third hospital day, with a follow-up chest radiograph confirming significant resolution of pulmonary infiltrates. After two days of continued monitoring on the medical ward with no clinical deterioration, he was discharged into the care of the psychiatric service for further evaluation and management.

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of POPE following hanging. Given the rarity of this condition and the lack of standardized management guidelines, we sought to contextualize our experience by systematically reviewing the entirety of the published literature on this topic. The following systematic review aims to aggregate demographic, clinical, and outcome data from all available cases to identify common themes and evaluate potential management strategies.

Methods

This study integrates a single-patient case report with a comprehensive systematic review. The case report was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical standards, while the systematic review adhered to the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) recommendations. To ensure transparency, the review protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) (10.17605/OSF.IO/KY387). prior to data extraction and analysis. While the project evolved from an initial case report into a systematic review, with the literature search initiated before formal OSF registration, a clinical trial registration number is not applicable given the study’s design.

A systematic search of the literature was conducted to identify all published case reports and case series of POPE following strangulation. The search was performed on PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar with no time limit. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms. The PubMed search query was: “Negative pressure pulmonary edema” OR “post-obstructive pulmonary edema” OR “POPE” AND “Hanging” OR “hanging” OR “strangulation”. The Scopus query was adapted accordingly. A manual snowballing search was also performed by reviewing the reference lists of retrieved articles.

Two independent reviewers screened records by title and abstract, followed by a full-text review (Fig. 2). Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. Studies were included if they were English-language case reports or series detailing patients who developed POPE directly after strangulation. Patients were required to have no significant pre-existing cardiopulmonary disease to minimize confounding.

Due to the inherent heterogeneity and limited level of detail within the identified case reports, while we only included peer reviewed articles, no formal quality appraisal tool was applied.

Data extraction was performed independently by two authors using a standardized form. Extracted data included publication details, patient demographics, clinical features such as time to POPE onset and GCS on admission, treatments provided including mechanical ventilation, diuretics, and steroids, and patient outcomes.

Data were synthesized descriptively. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges. The in-hospital mortality rate was calculated and compared against the pre-registered hypothesis of 25%. Formal inferential statistical testing and comparisons were not performed given the small sample size and heterogeneity of reporting. Finally, all findings regarding treatment provided and patient outcome are presented as descriptive and hypothesis-generating signals rather than evidence of causality.

Results

The review included 15 cases from 11 unique publications (Table 1). The cohort was 53.3% male (8/15) with a median age of 24 years (range: 10–52, IQR: 16–33). The vast majority (13/14, 92.9%) had symptom onset before hospital admission. GCS on admission, available for 9 patients, indicated severe neurological depression (median: 6, range: 3–8, IQR: 3.5-7). Mechanical ventilation was required in 66.7% (10/15) of cases. The overall mortality rate was 40% (6/15).

Our analysis revealed a notable disparity in the administration of supportive pharmacological interventions between survivors and non-survivors. Diuretic therapy was administered to 55.6% (5/9) of survivors compared to 16.7% (1/6) of non-survivors. Similarly, steroids were used in 44.4% (4/9) of survivors versus 16.7% (1/6) of non-survivors. While the retrospective, uncontrolled nature of this data cannot establish causality and may be influenced by confounding by indication (where less critically ill patients were more likely to receive additional therapies), the strength of this association is notable.

Discussion

POPE was first described in pediatrics by Capitano et al. (1973) [14, 15] and subsequently in adults by Petitjean et al. (1975) [16]. Despite its limited availability as an abstract, Petitjean’s work established a critical historical link between near-hanging and the development of POPE.

Post-obstructive pulmonary edema, a form of non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, is a recognized, though not universally common, complication following near-hanging or strangulation. It results from the interaction between elevated negative intrathoracic pressure following forceful inspiratory efforts against an obstructed airway, hence the name Negative Pressure Pulmonary Edema (NPPE), and secondary neurogenic or hypoxic effects [17].

The incidence of POPE following hanging varies widely in literature, ranging from a relatively low rate of less than 2% in one Australian study of 161 near-hanging victims [18] to as high as 27% in a small pediatric series of 11 patients [19], while other reports cite an overall pulmonary distress rate of approximately 11% [20]. Patients who develop POPE and related pulmonary distress, such as those with low oxygen saturation or high ventilator requirements, face a significantly worse prognosis; while aggressive management with supportive care and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) often leads to resolution in patients with isolated POPE, the presence of this complication is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality, often implicated in hospital deaths following survival of the initial hanging event [19, 20].

The recognized forms of POPE are dichotomized into Type I and Type II. Type I follows an acute upper airway obstruction, commonly postoperative laryngospasm, with other triggers including epiglottitis and croup [17, 21]. Critically relevant to the present manuscript’s focus are the mechanisms of strangulation and hanging. In contrast, Type II POPE manifests after the resolution of a chronic airway obstruction, exemplified by surgical interventions like tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy or the excision of an upper airway tumor [17].

The pathophysiology behind type I POPE includes the elevated intrathoracic negative pressure (as in Muller maneuver), hypoxia, and a hyperadrenergic state. The initiation event in POPE is the generation of significantly negative transpulmonary pressure during a forceful inspiration against a closed upper airway [17, 22, 23]. Thus, the excessive negative intrapleural pressure increases pulmonary blood flow due to increased venous return to the right side of the heart; however, the sudden influx of blood to the thorax causes a decrease in flow from the left side due to increased afterload, due to systemic vasoconstriction resulting from hypoxia and hyperadrenergic state. This combination causes increased pulmonary blood volume and elevated pulmonary venous pressures, resulting in an increase in hydrostatic pressures and edema formation [17, 24].

Patients who survive a hanging attempt (near-hanging) present with a spectrum of uncommon but serious complications affecting the brain, airway, lungs, and heart. While many of these injuries, such as cervical spine fractures, laryngeal trauma, or blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI), occur in less than 5% of patients [25], the development of acute respiratory distress and altered mentation presents particular diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. The pulmonary complications in this context can include type I POPE, neurogenic pulmonary edema (NPE), and aspiration pneumonitis [26], which may be difficult to distinguish from one another.

Although the initial emergency management following the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles is similar for all patients, outcomes are improved by subsequent, specific management of the underlying pathology. This is particularly true for POPE and NPE. A detailed clinical history is crucial, as the time course can offer a clue: POPE typically has a rapid onset, often developing within minutes of the upper airway obstruction. In contrast, NPE usually evolves sometime after a neurological insult, which in the context of hanging could range from hypoxic brain injury to BCVI or traumatic brain injury (TBI). The similarity in their presentations, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray and hypoxemia, often forces clinicians to rely on brain imaging (CT) to identify a neurological cause and differentiate NPE from other forms of hydrostatic edema [25–27].

Treatment strategies, as shown from our literature review, vary depending on the severity of the POPE symptoms and local expertise. Supportive measures include maintaining a patent airway and ensuring adequate oxygenation with supplemental oxygen and PEEP guided by pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas measurements, but severe cases necessitate intubation and mechanical ventilation with PEEP [24].

These overlapping clinical presentations, seen with the complications of near-hanging, combined with the need for advanced interventions, create a significant challenge in resource-constrained settings. Limitations may include a shortage of specialized personnel, lack of access to diagnostic imaging (such as CT or MRI), an inability to perform frequent arterial blood gas (ABG) monitoring, and a limited availability of life-saving mechanical ventilators capable of delivering protective lung strategies required for managing these various respiratory condition.

The observed association between steroid and diuretic administration and lower mortality aligns with the dual-mechanism pathophysiology of Type 1 POPE. Diuretics may rapidly alleviate the acute central fluid shifts and extreme pulmonary hydrostatic pressures generated by forceful inspiration against an occluded airway [23]. Concurrently, steroids may mitigate the non-cardiogenic capillary leak triggered by mechanical stress failure and subsequent hypoxia-reoxygenation injury upon relief of the obstruction. While limited by the observational nature of case report data, this mechanistic rationale generates a plausible clinical hypothesis: the adjunctive use of diuretics to reduce hydrostatic pressure, alongside steroids to blunt endothelial inflammation, might offer a survival benefit. This combination warrants formal investigation in larger-scale, prospective studies.

Limitations

The findings of this systematic review must be interpreted within the context of its inherent limitations, which are largely determined by the nature of the available evidence.

First, the analysis is based on a small number of cases (n = 15) derived from case reports and small case series. This small sample size, combined with the lack of a control group, precludes any causal inference and makes the findings hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The observed association between diuretic/steroid use and survival, while notable, may be subject to confounding by indication, where less severely ill patients might have been more likely to receive such adjunctive therapies.

Second, the review is susceptible to several forms of selection and reporting bias. Our search was restricted to English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant cases documented in other languages, potentially skewing the demographic or clinical picture. Furthermore, the exclusion of non-open access articles due to accessibility constraints means that some published cases may have been missed. There is also a risk of publication bias, as the existing literature is likely skewed toward reporting unusual or severe presentations, which may not reflect the full spectrum of POPE cases following strangulation.

Third, the quality and consistency of data extracted from the primary reports were variable. Critical data points, such as the GCS on admission, were missing for a substantial proportion of cases (6 out of 15). This incomplete reporting limits the ability to fully assess the relationship between the severity of the initial neurological insult and subsequent outcomes. Finally, due to the heterogeneous and descriptive nature of the source material, a formal quality appraisal tool was not applied, which may have allowed for the inclusion of reports with variable methodological rigor.

Conclusion

POPE is a potentially under-recognized and fatal complication of strangulation. This review highlights that while POPE is transient, its clinical recognition is vital in resource-limited settings. Given that these patients are typically young with healthy cardiovascular baselines, high clinical suspicion and the prioritization of positive end-expiratory pressure are essential for optimizing outcomes and managing scarce ventilator resources.

Our analysis identifies a significant association between survival and the adjunctive use of diuretics and steroids, providing a vital clinical hypothesis for managing these high-stakes cases. We recommend that clinicians consider these therapies alongside standard supportive care while rigorously documenting outcomes. Establishing an international registry is a necessary next step to validate these associations and develop formal, evidence-based management guidelines for this rapidly reversible condition.
