New-onset asymptomatic focal subacute thyroiditis: a potential pitfall for both 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG PET) and ultrasonography
Authors: Mehmet Emin Mavi, Seyfettin Ilgan
Published: 2026

Introduction

Subacute thyroiditis (SAT), also known as granulomatous or De Quervain thyroiditis, is a female-dominant, self-limiting inflammatory condition of the thyroid gland, mostly caused by viral etiology. Diagnosis is mainly based on clinical symptoms together with laboratory and imaging findings. Patients typically present with neck pain and systemic symptoms of thyrotoxicosis, elevated serum levels of inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) (1). Ultrasonography (US) is the first-line imaging modality for SAT. Main ultrasonographic findings of SAT are thyroid enlargement and focal or multiple ill-defined and/or irregularly shaped hypoechoic areas with absence of Doppler flow in one or both thyroid lobes (1,2). Although the typical clinical framework of SAT has been well-defined historically, an increasing number of patients with unusual and confounding clinical presentations have been documented recently. This change and heterogeneity in clinical characteristics have made the diagnosis of SAT even more challenging (1,3). Herein, we present a case of unusually and incidentally detected focal SAT and review and discuss several aspects of the challenging imaging features of SAT.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old male underwent an excisional biopsy for a suspicious skin lesion on the right supra-alar region of nose and was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computerized tomography (FDG PET-CT) and neck US were ordered for staging. A Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TIRADS) V nodule was reported in the left thyroid lobe on US exam performed before the FDG PET-CT study on same day. The following FDG PET-CT scan showed focal FDG uptake in the left thyroid lobe, corresponding to a hypodense nodule on the CT component (Figure 1A-1D). No other findings suggestive of metastatic disease were identified on both study.

Correlative US exam repeated in nuclear medicine department after PET imaging revealed an irregular, ill-defined, avascular hypoechoic area which does not create a typical nodular formation in the left thyroid lobe (Figure 1E-1G). The patient described no symptoms including neck pain or tenderness even with palpation during US examination. The patient had no personal or family history of thyroid malignancy. The patient also reported no recent upper respiratory tract infection, viral illness or systemic symptoms in the weeks preceding imaging. However, the findings were interpreted as suggestive of new onset focal SAT because it appeared as an ill-defined, non-nodular hypoechoic area lacking internal Doppler flow and suggesting parenchymal involvement rather than a well-circumscribed thyroid nodule. Therefore, short-term US follow-up was preferred over performing immediate fine needle aspiration (FNA) biopsy. Initial thyroid function test as well as inflammatory markers were within normal limits [thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH): 2.52 mIU/L, free thyroxine (fT4): 18.3 pmol/L, ESR: 5 mm/h, CRP: 0.82 mg/L]. During follow-up, thyrotoxicosis and elevated inflammatory markers (ESR: 52 mm/H, CRP: 34.4 mg/L) were detected. No specific viral serology tests were performed because the patient had no symptoms or history suggestive of recent viral infection.

The typical clinical findings of SAT emerged 3 weeks after the initial US examination. On the serial follow-up US examinations, the hypoechoic area enlarged gradually and filled the entire left lobe by the 8th week. Subsequently, similar hypoechoic areas appeared in the right lobe and spread to the whole gland by the 12th week. The ultrasonographic findings resolved completely by the 6th month (Figure 2A-2F). Discussion

Incidental thyroid gland uptake on FDG PET-CT is uncommon and can be diffuse or focal. Both diffuse and focal uptake patterns are mostly caused by benign conditions, though the malignancy rate is high in cases of focal uptake. Further clinical investigation with thyroid function tests and US, with or without cytological evaluation, is recommended in cases of thyroid FDG uptake (4-7). SAT appears mostly as diffuse thyroid uptake on FDG PET-CT (8-13), although uptake can also be focal (14), as in our case. Increased FDG uptake in SAT is thought to reflect increased glucose metabolism in activated inflammatory cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes, as well as increased metabolic activity associated with inflammatory tissue damage and repair processes (8,14,15).

Ultrasonographic findings of SAT are characterized by thyroid gland enlargement and focal or multiple ill-defined and/or patchy hypoechoic areas in one or both thyroid lobes (2). The hypoechoic areas may initially begin as focal lesion(s), then spread and fill the lobe in which they first appear, and at a later stage, similar findings begin to appear in the contralateral lobe (16,17). US findings of SAT mostly resolves completely in an interval of 9 weeks to 12 months, although some changes may persist (17-19). SAT cases with typical clinical presentation may not pose diagnostic difficulty (20). However, depending on the timing of the first US examination, asymptomatic focal SAT cases may mimic high risk nodules and trigger unnecessary FNA attempts which may further complicate the diagnostic chaos (3,20-23). When FNA results are suspicious for malignancy or inconclusive, which is not uncommon, patients may undergo unnecessary surgery (24-26). FNA is the standard diagnostic procedure for evaluating suspicious thyroid nodules. Current international risk-stratification system guidelines recommend FNA primarily for discrete thyroid nodules that meet specific sonographic risk criteria such as marked hypoechogenicity, irregular margins, microcalcifications, taller-than-wide shape, and evidence of extrathyroidal extension and relevant size thresholds (27-29). However, inflammatory thyroid diseases such as SAT may produce ill-defined hypoechoic regions that mimic nodular lesions and may receive high scores despite not representing true nodules (21). In such cases, the absence of a well-circumscribed nodular formation and the presence of poorly marginated patchy parenchymal involvement may suggest an inflammatory rather than neoplastic process. In the present case, the lesion initially reported as a suspicious nodule on US was re-evaluated and demonstrated features more consistent with focal inflammatory thyroid involvement, including ill-defined margins, lack of discrete nodular configuration, and absence of internal vascularity on Doppler imaging. Therefore, immediate FNA was deferred and short-term US follow-up was preferred. This approach allowed observation of the dynamic evolution of the lesion, which subsequently showed the typical migratory course of SAT. Based on our experience, in the early stages of the nodular form of SAT, regional hypoactive areas may be observed on thyroid scintigraphy that may be indistinguishable from hypoactive nodules. Therefore, thyroid scintigraphy was not performed, as it was not expected to provide sufficient additional diagnostic value to justify the extra radiation exposure to the patient.

A significant change in the characteristics of SAT has been reported recently. More and more patients, as in our case, present without neck pain and/or tenderness, which was previously regarded as a cardinal diagnostic criterion (3). The reason for the symptom-free presentation of SAT is not well understood (20). However, one study indicated that US changes may appear before the onset of clinical symptoms (18). This atypical presentation may pose a grater challenge in the diagnostic process especially when focal FDG uptake accompanies US findings, as in our case.

Proposed mechanisms of SAT following coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) infection include post-viral inflammatory and immune-mediated responses and direct viral injury via angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors, the receptor for cellular entry of COVID-19 virus, expressed in thyroid tissue (30). A recent systematic review demonstrated overall heterogeneity in clinical and laboratory findings of SAT as post-COVID-19 complication. In this extensive review, most patients still exhibited typical symptoms, particularly neck pain, indicating that completely asymptomatic presentations remain uncommon (31). COVID-19 related SAT appeared to present similarly to conventional SAT (32).

SAT is a challenging clinical entity, comprising several confounding features and pitfalls, especially when it presents atypically or is detected incidentally on imaging modalities such as PET-CT and/or US in the absence of characteristic symptoms. Although FNA remains the standard diagnostic tool for evaluating thyroid nodules according to current guidelines, its indication is primarily based on the presence of a discrete nodule with suspicious sonographic features and appropriate size criteria. Inflammatory thyroid diseases such as SAT may produce ill-defined, non-nodular hypoechoic areas that can be misinterpreted as high-risk nodules on initial examination. In the present case, US demonstrated features more consistent with inflammatory parenchymal involvement rather than a true nodular lesion. Based on these findings, short-term US follow-up was preferred over immediate biopsy, allowing observation of the lesion’s dynamic evolution, which subsequently confirmed the diagnosis of SAT. However, this approach should not be generalized. Clinical decision-making should be individualized and guided by established thyroid nodule management guidelines. The present case is therefore best interpreted as an example of a potential imaging pitfall, highlighting the importance of careful correlation of clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings to avoid both misdiagnosis and unnecessary invasive procedures.
