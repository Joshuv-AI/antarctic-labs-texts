One Small Step by the National Institutes of Health Can be a Giant Leap for Persons With Disability
Authors: Zuhair Niazi, Taimur Sher
Published: 2024

On December 3 each year, the United Nations (UN) reaffirms its global commitment to promote the rights and well-being of persons with disabilities (PWDs). Noting that PWDs are 15 times more likely to perceive their health as bad or very bad, in its 2024 Disability and Development report, the UN raises the alarm—this large group of humanity is being left behind in the march toward Sustainable Development Goals 2030.1 The United States and the rest of North America are no exception.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 1 in 4 (28.7%) Americans have some type of disability. Compared with persons without disabilities, Americans with disability have a disproportionately higher prevalence of obesity (30% higher), heart disease (3 times higher), and diabetes (2 times higher).2 Poor outcomes have been noted in PWDs and cancers. A large Canadian study reported that people with intellectual disabilities and cancer were 2.28 times more likely to die of breast cancer, 2.57 times more likely to die of colorectal cancer, and 1.38 times more likely to die of lung cancer compared with persons without intellectual disabilities.3 Analysis of SEER-Medicare data has painted a very similar picture for PWDs and cancer in the United States.

Researchers have proposed several reasons for the poorer health-related outcomes in PWDs. We intend to highlight a fundamental systemic barrier that, if addressed, can positively impact the health outcomes of this vulnerable group—the dearth of clinical evidence to guide the care of PWD. Take cancer as an example. There are no clear guidelines from highly credible professional society platforms such as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, the American Society of Clinical Oncology, the American Society of Hematology, or the European Society of Medical Oncology, among others, for approaching and managing cancer patients with disabilities, despite recognized poor outcomes in this segment of society. The state of affairs is very similar to other leading causes of mortality and morbidity, such as heart disease and stroke.

It is critical to note that this does not reflect the medical community’s lack of will or good intentions. Substantial resources have been channeled across the whole spectrum of professional societies to address the increasingly recognized source of health care disparities in the form of social determinants of health. In our opinion, the main reason for the current state of affairs is the lack of good-quality data to guide professional societies in organizing, evaluating, and appraising the evidence and developing guidelines for PWDs. For example, in our review of the primary articles for all published studies of antineoplastic therapies that led to their approval by the US Food and Drug Administration from 2016 to 2019, we did not find any reference to PWDs. In the era of big data and artificial intelligence, with the status quo, this predicament is expected to worsen. Suppose artificial intelligence–based health care solutions developers use data from populations without PWD cases or limited access to disability-related variables. Those tools, when implemented in the general population where 1 in 4 individuals experience disabilities, may not account for the unique barriers these patients face and their unique needs. In that case, the PWDs are at tremendous risk of losing out. When implemented, such tools, whether descriptive, predictive, or prescriptive, can exponentially worsen and perpetuate the disparities.

Awareness and recognition of a problem is the first, and perhaps the most important, step to its solution. On September 26, 2023, the watershed moment for the health of PWDs unfolded as the director of the National Institute on Minority Health and Health Disparities designated PWD as a population with health disparities for research supported by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).4

A key hurdle one can anticipate in advancing in this direction is the complexity surrounding the concept and construct of disability. We can draw parallels with the challenges faced by researchers, health care organizations, and medical journals in reporting social determinants of health in biomedical research—how to define, classify domains, standardize, collect, and analyze these variables. Perhaps, a reasonable starting point is to use the 6 questions of the US census that focus on disability. Fashioned on the work of the Washington Group on Disability Statistics and endorsed by the UN, these questions view disability through a biopsychosocial lens. This model assesses disability as the outcome of interactions between a person’s health and physical, cultural, and policy contexts.

The integrity and credibility of the NIH affords it high standing in and outside of biomedical enterprise. Policy directives and guidelines issued and enforced by the NIH profoundly impact the 3 shields of medicine—practice, research, and education. Take, for example, the impact of implementing the NIH Revitalization Act of 1993. To address the disparities, NIH mandated the reporting on sex and race in NIH-sponsored research. This directive consistently improved the participation of women and minorities in clinical research over time.5 It is reasonable to believe that enlarging the scope of this policy to include specific reporting on PWD in clinical trials using the 6 US census questions in the case report forms will address the problems of inclusion, participation, visibility, and acceptability of this ipso facto vulnerable population with health disparities.
