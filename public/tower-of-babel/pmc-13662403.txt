HURDLER: a cloning method to overcome length limits in designed repeat proteins
Authors: Wenzhao Dai, Matthias Schuster, Oliver Zerbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe number of internal repeat modules remains a major limiting factor in the construction and functional exploration of long repeat proteins. As a solution, we present High-Utility Recursive Directional Ligation for Extended Repeat proteins (HURDLER)—a modular cloning strategy combining codon optimization, seamless insertion of restriction sites, and modified recursive directional ligation. It presents a very robust technique to clone DNA for large repeat proteins, which may also contain non-repetitive parts, with high fidelity requiring a minimal number of individual cloning steps. Using HURDLER, we cloned designed armadillo repeat proteins with up to 85 internal modules, corresponding to >3700 residues. The method also generates intermediate variants with defined numbers of internal repeats, enabling systematic investigation of how the number of modules influences protein structure and function. HURDLER thereby provides a reliable solution to overcome restrictions in the overall number of internal modules used in the construction of very long repeat proteins. Such proteins are increasingly utilized as building blocks in macromolecular protein assemblies with defined structures.

Introduction

DNA vector construction is the first step in the production of recombinant proteins in a host cell. Appropriate DNA fragments, either reverse-translated from the amino acid sequence or directly from genomic DNA, need to be inserted into a vector that contains the crucial elements for expression in the desired host. Given the high effectiveness of solid-phase oligonucleotide synthesis, DNA nowadays is often produced within a few days by commercial suppliers. The high efficiency of synthetic DNA fragment production was instrumental for the revolutionary success of biochemistry and synthetic biology.

Vector construction normally involves two parts: synthesis of the DNA fragment and its insertion into a plasmid. To synthesize long DNA fragments in the absence of a template, shorter oligonucleotides with partially overlapping sequences are chemically synthesized. These overlaps enable correct annealing through Watson–Crick base pairing and guide the precise order of fragment assembly [1]. To insert linear DNA fragments into an expression plasmid, various insertion techniques were developed, such as restriction enzyme (RE) cloning, concatenation [2], Golden Gate cloning [3], Gibson assembly [4], and recursive directional ligation (RDL) [5–7]. RDL was developed to insert DNA fragments one at a time into a previously cut plasmid with exposed sticky ends [5]. In addition, the plasmid recombination RDL (PRe-RDL) variant allows doubling the length of the inserted fragment in each insertion cycle [6, 7]. A type IIS RE–based assembly strategy using BsaI and BpiI sites was employed for cloning designed armadillo repeat proteins (dArmRPs) with a defined number of internal modules [8].

In contrast to concatenation, RDL provides plasmids with a defined number of insertions. Compared to polymerase chain reaction assembly, RDL avoids the risk of misalignments and introducing point mutations. Compared to Golden Gate cloning, RDL has a higher success rate when assembling multiple long DNA fragments. Nevertheless, RDL has disadvantages when cloning repeat proteins: It is very laborious, especially when each DNA fragment encodes only a few repeat modules. The plasmid choice for RDL is limited to plasmids that have type IIS RE sites at the termini, and the created full-length fragment needs extra cloning steps to be transferred to another plasmid. Additionally, RDL requires an extra step to add inserts that encode for cap(s).

Very large repeat proteins present a particular challenge for cloning as the related DNA sequence, due to the highly repetitive protein sequence, tends to have repetitive recurrent subsequences. Especially repeats comprising >13 bp can complicate the annealing. Repeat proteins present a naturally occurring class of proteins with a repetitive amino acid sequence where each repeat is often referred to as a module. Some repeat proteins contain modified modules at the termini called caps. To allow for correct assembly from chemically accessible fragments, the DNA sequence must be desymmetrized initially as much as possible. An alternative approach is to only ligate double-stranded DNA with sticky ends in order to avoid annealing to internal sequences and thereby reduce constraints on the sequence.

Type II restriction endonucleases produce these sticky ends. Different subclasses (Type IIP, Type IIT, and Type IIS) are commonly used and differ primarily in their recognition patterns and cleavage site positions. Type IIP enzymes recognize palindromic DNA sequences and cleave within the recognition site. Type IIT enzymes have two different strand-specific cleavage positions, resulting in asymmetric cutting of the two DNA strands. Type IIS enzymes recognize a defined sequence but cleave outside the recognition motif [9].

In this paper, we introduce a modified cloning strategy based on RDL, termed High-Utility Recursive Directional Ligation for Extended Repeat proteins (HURDLER), to overcome the above limitations. HURDLER presents a modular cloning strategy that enables the efficient, seamless, and scalable construction of very large repeat protein assemblies. HURDLER has achieved these goals in three steps: (i) codon optimization using a genetic algorithm (GA) to allow for encoding more internal modules in a single commercially available DNA fragment, (ii) selecting and positioning RE sites that can be inserted inside the module sequence seamlessly and that are silenced by ligation, and (3) a modified RDL procedure that enables insertion of modules between the terminal caps. We present the synthesis of a dArmRP with >80 modules and a size larger than 400 kDa. We also demonstrate that HURDLER cloning can be used to create complicated/extended DNA sequences required for protein engineering.

Materials and methods

Genetic algorithm

Codon optimization was performed using a custom genetic-algorithm Python script. An initial population of 500 candidate sequences was evolved over 800 generations. At each generation, sequences were scored by a weighted penalty function penalizing internal subsequence repeats (8–13mers), high local GC content, potential hairpins, and unintended restriction sites (Supplementary Table S1). Parents were selected by tournament selection, and offspring were generated by uniform crossover and point mutation. The top‐scoring sequence at generation 800 was chosen for gene synthesis. Rare codons were removed from the codon table in this step.

Molecular biology

All synthesized DNA was ordered as gBlocks from Integrated DNA Technologies and was cloned into a pET28a plasmid. Chemically competent DH5α cells were used for plasmid construction. The proteins were expressed in NEB Stable Cells and were co-transformed with the pQE30 plasmid (Addgene plasmid #124138), expressing the T7 RNA polymerase [10].

To avoid recombination, all steps at 37°C were lowered to 30°C. Frozen competent cells (50 µl) were thawed on ice for 10 min. Five microliters of 100 ng/µl plasmid DNA was gently added to the cells, followed by an incubation for an additional 8 min on ice. The cells were then heat-shocked at 42°C for 1 min and immediately returned to ice for 2 min. Subsequently, 250 µl of Super Optimal Medium (SOC) medium was added, and the mixture was incubated at 30°C for 30 min while shaking at 200 rpm. Cells were then plated on LB agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotics. For cloning in DH5α, the plates contained kanamycin (50 µg/ml). For expression in NEB Stable cells, the LB plates contained kanamycin (50 µg/ml), ampicillin (100 µg/ml), streptomycin (50 µg/ml), and tetracycline (10 µg/ml). Plates were incubated overnight at 30°C.

A single colony was picked from the transformation plate and inoculated into 7 ml of LB medium in a 15 ml Falcon tube. The preculture grew at 30°C while shaking at 220 rpm overnight. The plasmid was purified with the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (QIAGEN). Final constructs and selected intermediates were sent for whole-plasmid sequencing. Sequencing was performed by Microsynth AG (Switzerland) using Oxford Nanopore Technology (ONT) with rapid barcoding chemistry and proprietary full plasmid analysis. Additional sequencing was performed by Plasmidsaurus using ONT and custom bioinformatics pipelines.

During the RDL, the initial linear DNA fragment was digested at sites IV and V, whereas the secondary fragment was digested at sites I and III, using the restriction-enzyme combinations shown in Fig. 3 and listed for the corresponding constructs in Supplementary Tables S4–S6. Incubation times and buffer conditions were selected according to the RE manufacturer’s instructions. The digested fragments were separated by agarose-gel electrophoresis, and bands of the expected sizes were excised and purified by gel extraction; the purified initial fragment was used for the first insertion, and the same prepared secondary-fragment stock was used throughout the subsequent cloning rounds. For the initial insertion, the acceptor plasmid was digested at sites IV and V, whereas confirmed intermediate plasmids were digested at sites I and II in subsequent extension rounds. After each plasmid digestion, the linearized plasmid band was isolated by agarose-gel electrophoresis and gel extraction, thereby removing the REs and the excised DNA segment before ligation. The purified plasmid of 4 ng/µl was ligated with T4 DNA ligase at 16°C overnight with the corresponding prepared insert with a molar ratio three times higher than the plasmid concentration. Then the ligation mixture was transformed into homemade chemically competent DH5α cells. Six colonies were selected for overnight culture and miniprep, and candidate plasmids were screened by diagnostic restriction digestion followed by agarose-gel electrophoresis. One plasmid showing the expected diagnostic pattern was used as the acceptor for the next round, and the digestion–purification–ligation–transformation–screening cycle was repeated until the target construct was obtained, which was confirmed by nanopore sequencing.

The secondary fragment (depicted in blue) is digested to display the sticky ends of site-I and site-III. (B) The digested initial fragment is ligated into the linearized plasmid. Expression and purification

All cultures were grown in LB medium for protein expression supplemented with kanamycin (50 µg/ml), ampicillin (100 µg/ml), streptomycin (50 µg/ml), and tetracycline (10 µg/ml). A single colony was picked from the transformation plate and inoculated into 7 ml of LB medium in a 15 ml Falcon tube. The preculture grew at 30°C with shaking at 220 rpm overnight. The following day, the entire 7 ml preculture was transferred into 330 ml of fresh LB medium in a 2 L baffled flask. The culture was incubated at 30°C while shaking at 80 rpm. Protein expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside when the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.6–0.8. After induction, the temperature was lowered to 20°C, and the culture was incubated overnight to allow protein expression.

Following expression, the culture was harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellet was transferred to a 50 ml Falcon tube and stored at −80°C until further purification.

Protein purification was performed using a modified protocol based on a previously published method [11]. The bacterial pellet was thawed on ice and resuspended in 15 ml Resuspension Buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 20 mM imidazole, 400 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.1 mg/ml DNase, 2 mg/ml lysozyme, 2 mM MgCl2, pH 8.0). Fifteen milliliters lysis buffer (80 mM Tris–HCl, 4% Triton X-100, 20 mM imidazole, 400 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, pH 8.0) was then added, and the Falcon tube was gently rotated at 4°C for 1 h. Next, 13 g of urea powder was added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 20 min at 4°C with gentle rotation.

The lysate was centrifugated at 18 000 × g for 45 min at 4°C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter and loaded onto a Ni-NTA affinity column pre-equilibrated with Wash Buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 20 mM imidazole, 400 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, pH 8.0). The resin was washed sequentially with 10 column volumes (CV) of wash buffer, 20 CV of high-salt buffer (80 mM Tris–HCl, 4% Triton X-100, 20 mM imidazole, 1000 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, pH 8.0), and another 5 CV of wash buffer. His-tagged proteins were eluted with 3 CV of elution buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 400 mM NaCl, 200 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). The eluted fractions were dialyzed overnight at 4°C against size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 20 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) using a 20 kDa cutoff membrane in the presence of TEV protease to cleave the His-tag. Following cleavage, the dialysate was subjected to reverse Ni-NTA purification to remove the TEV protease, cleaved tag, and uncleaved protein. After an additional 1 CV wash with SEC buffer, the pooled protein was concentrated with Amicon Ultra-15 with a 30 kDa cutoff. The protein concentration was determined by NanoDrop. The yield of repeat proteins was between 10 and 40 mg/l of culture.

Size-exclusion chromatography

SEC was performed on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) using an Agilent 1260 Infinity system. The flow rate was 0.5 ml/min. Elution profiles were monitored by measuring absorbance at 280 nm, corresponding to aromatic amino acid residues, and fluorescence at 488 nm to detect EGFP by its fluorescence. Raw chromatographic data were extracted using the open-source MATLAB package chromatography (https://github.com/chemplexity/chromatography), and further analyzed using in-house Python scripts.

Electron microscopy

To yield the sample for negative-stain electron microscopy (nsEM), the purified protein was diluted to 0.04 mg/ml in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES and 150 mM NaCl, adjusted to pH 8.0. A 5 µl droplet of the diluted sample was applied to carbon-coated 200-mesh copper grids, incubated for 60 s, and blotted. The grids were subsequently stained with 0.5% (w/v) uranyl acetate (pH 4.0). Screening was performed using a 120 kV Talos L120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Acquired images in TIFF format were converted to MRC format using EMAN2 [12] and further processed in CryoSPARC [13] for particle picking and 2D classification.

Results

Most designed repeat proteins consist of a number of internal modules and N/C- terminal caps. HURDLER assembles the full-length protein-encoding DNA fragment from two shorter DNA fragments. The initial fragment encodes the caps and at least one internal module, referred to as N(cap)-(M)i-C(cap). The secondary fragment encodes s number of internal modules, referred to as (M)s. Based on the number of internal modules in this sequence, the N(cap)-(M)i-C(cap) DNA sequence is designed. In the procedure, an initial fragment (M)i is incorporated into the plasmid, into which the secondary fragment (M)s is inserted many times.

In the first step, the DNA sequence corresponding to (M)s is designed such that it is synthetically accessible from a commercial source and that it encodes as many internal modules as possible. Based on the number of internal modules in this sequence, the N(cap)-(M)i-C(cap) DNA sequence is designed.

In the second step, suitable restriction sites are identified based on the amino acid sequence of the internal module and seamlessly inserted into the DNA sequence. The DNA sequence is then rearranged and can be commercially ordered as DNA fragments. Those fragments corresponding to N(cap)-(M)i-C(cap) and (M)s are referred to as initial and secondary fragments in the following.

In the third step, the initial fragment is cloned into the plasmid, after which the secondary fragment is inserted repetitively. An important feature of this step is that the restriction site next to the growing insert is silenced after insertion, and therefore, the same two REs can always be used to cut the plasmid.

Codon optimization

The first step of the in silico design (Fig. 1) is the codon optimization of the initial DNA fragments. To allow a reliable synthesis of longer DNA fragments that encode multiple internal (possibly sequence-identical) modules, the codon usage of the target DNA sequence is optimized by a GA.

HURDLER workflow to clone a large repeat protein of the type N(cap)-(M)n-C(cap). (M)n is the target module length. (M)s is the length of the modules that are added in each step. (M)i is the length of the initial module between the N(cap) and the C(cap). The initial and secondary fragments can be commercially ordered.

The GA fitness score is a weighted penalty function that enforces DNA synthesis or cloning requirements (Supplementary Table S1). The fitness score weights are parameterized based on the GA optimizer, which guides the process toward the highest commercial availability (Fig. 2A). With more GA iteration cycles, the fitness score decreases, and a higher fraction of sequences can be purchased (Fig. 2B).

Performance of codon optimization. (A) Workflow for parameterizing the fitness function used by the GA. For a listing of the final parameter, see Supplementary Table S1. (B) The fitness score is shown in dependence on the GA optimization cycles. (C) Commercial availability of the designed DNA sequence for cloning of long dArmRPs from a supplier for 25 independent DNA designs, shown for different numbers of internal repeats.

The higher the number of internal modules encoded by a single secondary DNA fragment, the fewer cloning steps are needed. To design the longest possible DNA fragment, sequences containing different numbers of modules are optimized individually and checked if they can be purchased from vendors (Fig. 2C).

With this codon optimization procedure, we demonstrate the successful synthesis of a dArmRP with 13 sequence-identical internal modules of 42 residues each from a single DNA fragment.

Recursive directional ligation

In HURDLER, a very long repeat protein is cloned by inserting the initial fragment (depicted in gray in Fig. 3A), which may contain N- and C-caps, once into the plasmid, followed by iterative insertion of the secondary fragment (depicted in blue) t times (see also Fig. 1).

To this end, the initial plasmid is linearized by site-IV and site-V REs (Fig. 3A). The initial fragment, encoding N(cap)-(M)i-C(cap), is digested so that site-IV and site-V sticky ends are exposed, while the secondary fragment is digested at site I and site III (Fig. 3A). The primary fragment is inserted into the linearized plasmid, introducing site I and site II (Fig. 3B). In the next step, the plasmid is cut again using site-I and site-II REs, and the secondary fragment is ligated into it (Fig. 3C). The following steps are repeated: digestion of the plasmid with RE-I and RE-II and ligation of the secondary fragment into the plasmid. This cycle is repeated until the desired number of internal repeats has been reached. As described in more detail in the next section, RE site II is silenced after insertion of the secondary fragment into the plasmid. However, because a new site II is introduced via the newly inserted secondary fragment, the plasmid is always cut at sites I and II by the same two REs. As a result, in each iteration, a small fragment between site I and site II is removed while a large fragment is inserted, resulting in rapid extension of the DNA insert.

Sequence verification is needed before proceeding to the next cloning cycle. Short-read sequencing techniques like Sanger sequencing are not suitable for such long repeat sequences. Long-read sequencing techniques, like Oxford Nanopore sequencing, are either time-consuming or expensive. In practice, failure of cloning mainly results from failed insertion or incomplete plasmid digestion. Therefore, confirmation of insertion length by electrophoresis is a fast alternative. Unfortunately, this strategy fails for very long inserts, as the migration distance scales logarithmically with insert length, and hence very long inserts produce minimal shifts in migration distance. When introducing another, so-far-unused, restriction site VI, the number of internal repeats can be easily identified on a gel by comparing the relative band intensity from the fragments to the linearized host plasmid (Fig. 3D).

Restriction enzyme site selection and insertion

To enable RDL, three different REs are selected, and their combined use allows repetitive insertion of parts of the secondary fragments into the plasmid (see Fig. 3A and B). The optimized sequence therefore needs to be modified to contain these RE sites before ordering the DNA fragment.

The secondary fragment sequences encode three RE sites (Fig. 4A): site I, site II and a type IIS RE site (site III), which produces a sticky end that matches site II. Optionally, another RE site can be added for quality control after each step during the recursive ligation (site VI). To insert the initial fragment into the plasmid, sites IV and V are used in order to digest both initial fragment and plasmid. Independent of the repeat protein sequences, the REs that are available for HURDLER should meet a series of requirements, including commercial availability, selectivity for a unique RE site, sticky-end overhang length, the potential to re-ligate after cleavage, and insensitivity to 6mA or 5mC methylation (Supplementary Fig. S1). The insensitivity against these methylations is needed because the plasmid of a long repeat protein contains multiple homologous regions, which increases the risk of recombination. Therefore, Escherichia coli strains carrying the recA1 mutation, such as DH5α or NEB Stable, are needed, for which DNA methylation remains active. Moreover, the RE site should not occur elsewhere when the MCS is removed from the plasmid, and hence different plasmids have different sets of suitable REs [9]. Selected REs with their compatible plasmids are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. The distance between site I and site II should not be too short, as some REs require flanking base pairs for efficient digestion.

Subsequently, a list of 9 bp fragments that translate to a given tripeptide and that contain a specified RE site. (B) Integration of the 9 bp fragment inside the DNA sequence based on the AA sequence, demonstrated here for the AA sequence “ANSWER.” The input AA sequence is reverse translated to the DNA sequence. The AA sequence is also split to tripeptides. If any of the tripeptides is in the list of a RE, that RE can be used and its 9 bp replace the 9 bp in the original sequence. (C) Procedure to generate RE site II. After cleavage with the corresponding RE, the sticky end is ligated with another fragment with the complementary sticky end, which, however, contains a 1 bp mutation in the uncut region. The mutation is chosen as to not affect the translation result. (D) Sequence reshuffle that generates the required final DNA. The RE site II is colored in purple, site I in blue. (E) Probability that 2 RE sites can be inserted seamlessly, site II can be silenced, and the two sticky ends are sufficiently orthogonal to prevent unintended ligations. The calculation is done with random sequences of different lengths, and for different host plasmids.

Conventionally, RE sites are located based on the DNA sequence after reverse translation. To evaluate whether a RE can be used for a given sequence, a lot of reverse translation is needed. Here, we present a fast and robust RE site screening procedure that avoids reverse translation. To this end, each RE site is added to a list of potential 9 bp DNA sequences, taking into account frame shifts and alternative nucleotides for padding. For type IIT RE, the reverse complementary site sequence is also considered. The 9 bp sequences are then translated into the related tripeptide sequences. Therefore, as long as one of the 3 amino acids coding for a given RE can be detected in the protein sequence, the corresponding RE can be inserted seamlessly [9].

An example for the EcoRI site, based on the 6 bp RE site “GAATTC,” where 256 potential 9 bp and 63 unique stop-codon-free three-amino acid sequences are obtained, is shown in Fig. 4A. This list includes “ANS,” so EcoRI can be inserted inside the peptide sequence “ANSWER” (Fig. 4B) [14].

To enable seamless insertion of RE site II, a 1 bp mutation is introduced in the uncut region in the RE recognition site, but such that the resulting 9 bp fragment still codes for the same amino acid. To introduce the point mutation, the secondary fragment is digested with a type-IIS RE that produces the complementary sticky end but is 1 bp different in the uncut region. Thereby, the sticky ends from the template DNA and the new insert will anneal, but afterwards the site is no longer recognized by the original RE (Fig. 4C).

When using the three REs, compatibility of the RE sites needs to be guaranteed. The two exposed sticky ends produced from sites I and II should be sufficiently orthogonal to prevent unintended ligations. Further, the two RE sites are preferred in close proximity but should be separated by at least 6 bp to cover the entire recognition sequences. The site-II RE and the site-III RE should produce the same type of overhang, which limits the type of site-II RE. The site-I and site-II REs that fulfill these requirements are summarized in Supplementary Figs S3 and S4.

Before ordering the fragments from vendors, the secondary fragment requires further shuffling. The purpose of the shuffling is to rearrange the sequence such that the restriction sites are located at the termini. To this end, the sequence is separated into two subsequences that contain the two restrictions sites at either their 3′ or 5′ termini. One subsequence contains the RE site II at one terminus, the other subsequence the RE site I at the other terminus. In addition, a 1 bp mutation is inserted in the uncut region of the subsequence that includes RE site II, with a type-IIS RE site appended that results in the same sticky end, ultimately transforming it into site III. The nearby RE site I is removed by changing the codon usage. Then the two subsequences are merged in reversed order. The resulting sequence presents the secondary fragment (Fig. 4D). With these modifications, the reshuffled sequence may be more complex and therefore needs to be checked if it can still be ordered as DNA fragment.

Unfortunately, it is not possible to seamlessly insert the three RE sites into every sequence. However, the longer the repeat module is, the higher are the chances that suitable RE sites can be inserted (Fig. 4E).

To check the performance for other naturally occurring repeat proteins or designed variants thereof, we assembled 25 913 nonredundant natural repeat modules and 182 designed modules from the RepeatsDB [15]. Out of these, 24 987 natural (96.43%) and 181 designed modules (99.45%) [16–30] allowed for the required RE insertion. Within a 3000-bp coding-fragment limit, live complexity screening using the website from the IDT supplier indicated that the required genes can be made for 95.8% of the natural and 86.2% of the designed repeat proteins. Across all accepted modules, the maximum verified construct of natural and designed sequences encoded an average of 6.2 and 5.2 repeats, respectively, with observed maxima of 17 and 10 copies. Most unsuccessful modules failed because no synonymous sequence satisfying the selected RE-pair constraints and local sequence-quality requirements were found. Note that these results reflected the specified optimization budget and IDT scoring policy rather than the physical limits from synthesis.

We also tested performance on low-complexity, short repetitive sequences. To this end, a 6-amino-acid fragment was generated, as that is the minimum number to insert 2 RE sites. For example, if the repeat of interest is “ILV,” then “ILVILV” is tested. In an exhaustive search, all possible sequences comprising the 20 amino acids were tested with HURDLER, comprising 400, 8000, 160 000, and 3 200 000 sequences for the 2-, 3-, 4-, or 5-amino-acid fragments, respectively. Amongst them, 0/202 (0%), 681/203 (8.5%), 19 864/204 (12.4%), and 552 390/205 (17.3%) sequences are compatible with HURDLER (Fig. 4E).

Application for cloning large armadillo repeat proteins

To evaluate the method, we report the cloning of two long dArmRPs. dArmRPs have been developed by the Plückthun lab as general binding scaffolds to recognize unfolded peptides [8, 30, 31]. dArmRPs consist of a number of concatenated modules, each of which comprises 42 residues that form 3 helices arranged in staircase fashion [30, 31]. They form very stable repeat proteins with a supercoil that bind peptides via sidechain and backbone contacts on a continuous surface formed by helix 3 of each module. dArmRPs with 7 sequence-identical repeats display femtomolar binding affinities against (KR)7 peptides [17, 32]. Here we are particularly interested in their properties as stiff structural elements that can be incorporated into supramolecular structures, and which can bind any cargo that is fused to a recognition peptide sequence. The system would thereby allow the attachment of cargo proteins via specific recognition tags at defined positions. We applied HURDLER to construct two distinct and technically challenging classes of long armadillo repeat proteins (Fig. 5A and B). Through codon optimization, we designed a DNA fragment encoding 13 internal repeat modules; for practical handling, a fragment containing 12 internal modules was used as the secondary fragment (Fig. 2C).The first application of HURDLER is the construction of very long repeat proteins composed of (nearly) identical internal modules. The parent DNA fragment encodes for NMC, containing N- and C-terminal caps N and C and one internal module M, while the secondary fragment encodes 12 internal M modules. Site-I, site-II, and site-III REs are SacI, BsrGI, and BfuAI, respectively. Two types of internal modules were used: M12 represents 12 consecutive repeats of the module M. M6Q6 consists of six repeats of module M followed by three repeats of module Q2, where Q2 is a modified variant of module M2. Sequential insertion of four M12 fragments yielded NM49C (n = 49, i = 1, s = 12, t = 4). In contrast, seven rounds of M6Q6 insertion produced N(M6Q6)7MC, containing 85 internal modules—in total >3500 residues (n = 85, i = 1, s = 12, t = 7) (Fig. 5A). The repeat numbers were chosen to sufficiently demonstrate the scalability and robustness of the HURDLER strategy rather than to exhaustively explore its upper limit.

The second application of HURDLER is the construction of a single-chain triangle-shaped protein assembly, in which repeat proteins are genetically fused to additional protein inserts within a single polypeptide chain. The final construct exceeds 2100 residues and is encoded by six distinct DNA fragments that were assembled in six steps. The first step is to replace the MCS with the N and C termini of the assembly, which include the coiled-coil segments, and a segment corresponding to the non-repetitive sequence of a “corner” of the triangle, steps 3 and 5 insert the other two corner segments. Note that both the N- and C- termini (the two coiled-coil sequences) are already included in the initial DNA template and the segments 2–6 are added in between by the subsequent RDL steps. Step 2, 4, and 6 insert the “edges” of the triangle, which consist of repetitive dArmRP sequences. The corresponding DNA was subcloned from the DNA sequence described in the previous paragraph. The assembly of multiple large fragments using Golden Gate cloning is impractical due to the low efficiency of simultaneously ligating multiple long inserts. By contrast, HURDLER enables stepwise insertion of individual fragments, where each step is analytically verified, effectively dividing the cloning process into manageable stages with substantial improvement in reliability (Fig. 5B).

Because HURDLER is a multi-step cloning process, rapid and low-cost quality control of intermediate constructs is important. RE digestion followed by agarose gel electrophoresis was therefore used to assess proper insertions (Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Figs S5–S12). In practice, the most common cloning failure arises from recovery of the parental plasmid rather than the desired inserted construct. For very long repeat proteins, verification was performed by digestion at site VI. Multiple site-VI positions are present in constructs containing repeated insertions, spaced at intervals corresponding to the length of the secondary fragment. As a result, digestion yields a characteristic pattern in which the number and relative intensities of excised fragments reflects the number of inserted repeats. By quantifying the signal intensity fraction of the released fragment, the number of inserted repeats can be determined, allowing for selection of correct constructs for subsequent cloning steps. For example, when HindIII was used as site-IV, the expected fragment intensity fraction is 0.44 for N(M6Q6)₅MA and 0.50 for N(M6Q6)₆MA, consistent with their respective repeat numbers (Fig. 5C).

Before protein expression and purification, the final plasmid product was confirmed by nanopore sequencing (see Supplementary information). SEC was used to check the sizes of various synthesized repeat proteins, where the retention time decreased with an increasing number of internal modules of the dArmRP (Fig. 5D). The size of the largest dArmRP is >400 kDa and, based on a molecular model, the protein is expected to be around 70 nm in length. However, only 40 out of the expected 70 nm could be visualized clearly in the nsEM image, after 2D averaging (Fig. 5E). Flexibility at the protein termini causes their density to be averaged out during the nsEM 2D averaging.

Discussion

Repeat proteins comprise ~14% of all proteins [34] and play important roles in many biological processes such as intercellular transport, signaling, cell–cell adhesion, or as anti-freeze proteins. Repeat proteins offer structural modularity and adaptability, and therefore have recently attracted much attention as promising scaffolds for engineering novel biological functions [35]. They can be used to design proteins with novel shapes [16, 22, 36–40], nanomaterials [26, 41, 42], specific peptide-binders or modules mediating protein–protein interactions, or as ligand sensors [43–45]. The modules are arranged with translational or helical symmetry, which facilitates the development of new methods in protein design [35, 46, 47].

To address the challenge of cloning very long repeat proteins with high fidelity, the here-described HURDLER approach combines two existing strategies: codon optimization to generate long single fragments, and RDL to insert the same fragment into a plasmid multiple times. Traditional codon optimization methods struggle to reduce internal subsequence repeats, and therefore we introduced a GA with a fitness function designed to penalize occurrence of such repeats. As standard RDL does not support insertion inside a pre-cloned fragment, we modified the method by using type-IIP or type-IIT RE sites integrated into the initial fragment instead of inserting type-IIS sites into the plasmid. Additionally, we introduced a strategy to embed restriction sites seamlessly within the protein sequence and silence them after ligation. The RE site that can be silenced by ligation is similar to the recut blocker in the one-step Expanded Golden Gate method [48]. In contrast to the latter, the silenced RE site is introduced from the inserted fragment, rather than into the plasmid MCS site, and only one RE site is silenced. For a short comparison of cloning methods see Supplementary Table S8.

Initial and secondary fragments are inserted into the host plasmid sequentially with HURDLER. The cloning workload in the lab largely depends on how many modules each secondary fragment carries—fewer steps are needed when more modules are inserted per step, making longer fragments more desirable. For such secondary fragments codon optimization is absolutely crucial. The codon usage space for a given protein sequence is vast, and different codon choices result in reverse-translated DNA fragments from which proteins are synthesized with different efficiency. Codon optimization typically considers reducing GC content or rare codons, and avoiding secondary structure formation such as hairpins. For highly repetitive sequences, however, the primary limitation is the presence of internal subsequence repeats.

Several tools exist for codon optimization. Commercial providers use proprietary algorithms, while academic groups like the Edinburgh Genome Foundry offer open-source tools like DNA Chisel [36]. Nevertheless, sequences encoding multiple repeat modules are often still rejected due to the presence of internal subsequence repeats, even when their occurrence was minimized during codon optimization. This fact emphasizes the need for placing a higher weight on reducing sequence symmetry to avoid repetitive elements. In HURDLER we use a GA for codon optimization. GA offers the flexibility to assign penalty scores to various factors, such as those affecting protein expression and ease of DNA synthesis, within a weighted fitness function. Reducing subsequence repeats can then be achieved by increasing their weight. The fitness function is transparent and easy to adjust, making it straightforward to adapt the method.

In HURDLER, the codon-optimized sequence is inserted into the plasmid as a secondary fragment, but unlike RDL or pre-RDL methods, the insertion occurs within the coding DNA sequence (CDS) rather than at its ends. Achieving this requires careful selection of REs and precise positioning of their recognition sites within the CDS.

In this work we used HURDLER to successfully clone long repetitive sequences from dArmRPs. Our tests on other naturally occurring repeat proteins from the RepeatsDB indicated, however, that a very large fraction (> 95%) of these can also be cloned, including both natural and designed sequences. This reflects the fact that a very large fraction of these contain sufficient sequence variability to allow for insertion of the RE sites. It was very different when testing cloning repetitive sequences comprised of only a few amino acids, corresponding to very short repeat modules, where for example only 12% of all possible sequences could be cloned for tetrapeptides. Sequences from such repeat proteins (e.g. for the C–terminal domain of Saccharomyces cerevisiae RNA polymerase II that contains many heptapeptide repeats) will therefore remain very challenging.

Due to constraints in RE selection and RE site compatibility, not all repetitive sequences can be cloned using this RDL-based method (Fig. 4E). However, for designed proteins the success rate is often acceptable. As modern sequence design methods such as ProteinMPNN generate far more sequences than can be tested experimentally, HURDLER can be integrated into the design pipeline as a post-design sequence filter. Only sequences passing HURDLER’s criteria proceed to experimental validation, ensuring that all selected designs are compatible with HURDLER-based cloning. We also like to remark that although the process for optimizing initial and secondary fragments is complicated it is entirely handled by a Python script, and the only task remaining for the user is to check whether the designed DNA sequences can be purchased from vendors.

Although HURDLER was experimentally validated here for protein-coding repeats, it can be used to clone repetitive DNA whenever compatible active or latent restriction sites are present. We screened 145 210 artificial exact tandem-array targets containing 2, 4, 8, 16, or 32 copies, derived from 29 042 public elements from CRISPRCasdb [49], Rfam [50], and Ribocentre-Aptamer [51]. Complete computational HURDLER routes were identified for 15 535 targets (10.70%). Because DNA cannot be synonymously recoded to introduce suitable restriction sites, this extension is sequence-dependent and not universally applicable.

Beyond cloning existing repeat proteins with many internal modules, HURDLER also enables new applications in protein engineering and design. It facilitates the fast and cost-effective construction of large proteins composed of multiple functional domains connected by repeat-protein scaffolds such as the triangular-shaped protein shown in Fig. 5B. Additionally, HURDLER offers the advantage that each plasmid generated during the iterative process encodes a complete, functional repeat protein, including both terminal caps and only differing in length. As a result, the final construct comes with a series of length variants. This is especially valuable when studying how protein length affects dynamics or function, where a panel of constructs with varying lengths is often needed.

To conclude we developed a new approach for cloning long repetitive DNA, called HURDLER, and demonstrate using dArmRP as a model protein, that this method overcomes the length limitations of previous approaches. Using HURDLER an ArmRP consisting of >80 sequence-identical internal repeats and a molecular weight of ~400 kDa was successfully cloned into a standard pET vector. The method optimizes the length and complexity of the fragments and thereby minimizes the number of individual ligation steps. When picking six clones per RDL round, at least one displayed the expected size, demonstrating functional efficiency in practice. Ultimately, a plasmid with the correct sequence was always obtained. The corresponding proteins could be expressed at 10–40 mg/l in an E. coli culture with LB media.
