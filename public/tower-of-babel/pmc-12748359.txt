Single-cell aggrephagy landscape delineates intercellular communication of tumor microenvironment associated with ovarian cancer progression and immunotherapy
Authors: Feng Fang, Jiemiao Chen, Xinran Li, Cao Zhou, Yilan Dai, Yuanyuan Chen, Xin Yang, Yong Xie, Fujie Qin, Huijiao Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAggrephagy plays a pivotal role in ovarian cancer (OC) progression by clearing misfolded proteins. However, the functional role of aggrephagy within the tumor microenvironment (TME) remains elusive. Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) was employed to analyze 43 aggrephagy-related genes (ARGs) on 65,820 single cells from scRNA-seq data of 12 samples (7 OC tissues and 5 normal tissues). The OC cohort from 9 public databases and TIDE-based immunotherapy scores were used to identify prognosis and immune response patterns of these TME cell clusters. Furthermore, the association between ARGs and CD8+ T cells was validated by the ID8 ovarian cancer mouse subcutaneous tumor model. Fibroblasts, T cells, macrophages and B cells identified from scRNA-seq of OC samples were subjected to biological process enrichment and classified based on ARGs expression patterns. Additionally, it revealed that ARGs may be significantly associated with clinical and biological characteristics of OC and pseudotime trajectories of major TME cell types. Bulk-seq analysis revealed that these aggrephagy-associated TME cell subsets were closely linked to the prognosis of OC patients and exhibited significant heterogeneity in predicting response to immunotherapy. Among them, CAF and CD8+ T cells showed particularly significant immune responsiveness. Notably, CellChat analysis revealed that aggrephagy-associated subtypes of TME cells engage in diverse and extensive interactions with tumor epithelial cells. Meanwhile, significant co-localization of TUBA1B with CD8+ T cells was observed in OC tissues from the mice model. This study is the first to delineate the role of aggrephagy regulators-mediated intercellular communication of the TME in regulating OC growth and antitumor immunomodulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04060-3.Keywords: Single-cell, Aggrephagy, Tumor microenvironment, Ovarian cancer, Prognosis, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) serves as the second lethal gynecologic malignancies worldwide, posing a persistent challenge to women’s health [1]. Despite decades of the intensive research endeavors, the absence of robust diagnostic biomarkers for early OC detection has hindered timely intervention. As a result, the majority of OC cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, culminating in unsatisfactory five-year survival rates primarily attributable to the limited efficacy of existing treatment [2]. Thus, unraveling the underlying etiological mechanisms driving OC pathogenesis is imperative to identify innovative therapeutic strategies and improve clinical outcomes.

Recently, enhanced proteostasis has been proposed to be a novel hallmark in cancer biology [3]. Balanced proteostasis promotes normal cells survival by maintaining the equilibrium among protein translation, folding, trafficking, subcellular localization, and degradation [4]. Cancer cell instigates excessive protein synthesis to cope with a series of metabolic stress triggered by rapid proliferation. Accompanied by the excessive protein production, misfolded proteins can accumulate into toxic aggregates when they are not efficiently degraded [5], thereby impeding tumor growth. Proteostasis in cancer is regulated by intricate proteostasis networks (PNs) such as the ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy-lysosomal pathways (ALPs) [6]. Wherein ALPs might play a central role in proteostasis maintenance. A study has revealed that autophagy was conducive to the proteostasis of multiple myeloma [7]. However, though sufficient studies had already elucidated the close association between OC tumorigenesis and inadequate autophagy [8, 9], the specific mechanisms on ALPs during the modulation of proteostasis in OC remain poorly understood, warranting further investigation.

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved lysosome-driven catabolic pathway shared by eukaryotes, serving as a cellular defense mechanism against proteotoxic stress, hypoxia and nutrient starvation [10]. In the context of autophagy degradation, substrates labeled with ubiquitin as an “eat me” signal are typically recognized by the selective autophagy receptors (SARs) and recruited to autophagosomes for degradation. The specialized form of selective autophagy targeting protein aggregates is termed aggrephagy [11]. So far, Atg19 (S. cerevisiae) and SEPA-1 (C. elegans) were validated as the specific SARs for aggrephagy. Moreover, other ubiquitin-binding receptors, P62 (SQSTM1), NBR1, TAX1BP1, Optineurin, and Tollip, were implicated in facilitating this autophagic degradation process. However, the presence of aggrephagy in OC, as well as its regulatory impact on cancer cell fate remains unexplored. More knowledge on aggrephagy might enable the design of strategies that interfere with this specific autophagic process and pave the way for innovative therapeutic strategies for OC.

Currently, increasing evidence has reported that metabolic crosstalk between cancer and stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) modulates their autophagy levels and cancer progression [12]. The stromal cells comprise various cell types including pericytes, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and other immune cells [13]. Recently, Xia et al. reported that naïve T cells are susceptible to apoptosis due to the selective depletion of autophagy element FAK family-interacting protein of 200 kDa (FIP200) in OC patients and mouse model [14]. Consistent findings demonstrated that FIP200 knockout led to Tim-4+ TAM depletion, concomitant with elevated T cell immunity and suppressed ID8 tumor growth in vivo [15]. Nowadays, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has advanced our understanding of intricate intercellular communication dynamics among heterogeneous cellular compartments within TME of OC [16]. Nevertheless, less research sheds light on elucidating the interactions among aggrephagy, stromal cells and OC cells in TME.

In this study, we integrated 65,820 single-cell transcriptomic profiles derived from 7 OC samples and 5 matched normal tissues and the ARGs in TME cells were analyzed in depth. Nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) clusters of 43 ARGs were characterized in terms of pseudotime trajectory, intercellular communication, ligand-receptor connectivity and immunotherapy. This multi-dimensional analysis revealed intricate dynamic crosstalk mechanisms governing ARG regulation, enabling a comprehensive characterization of tumor immunity and prognosis. Our data first drew attention to a potential molecular role fot aggrephagy involving in the communication within diverse TME, facilitating the therapeutic optimization and validation of biomarkers for diagnosis and progression of OC.

Methods

Study design and data collection

Single-cell mRNA sequence data of OC were collected from 12 samples, including 7 tumor tissues and 5 normal tissues, to analyze the landscape of 43 aggrephagy regulators. The collection of aggrephagy-related genes (ARGs) was acquired from the GSEA website (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/cards/REACTOME_ AGGREPHAGY). Through initial integration of the samples, we generated gene expression matrices for 65,820 scRNA-seq datasets. After rigorous quality control procedures (such as removing low-quality cells and doublets), the final number of cells used for analysis was 59,324. The completed data were downloaded from GSE184880 in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) [17]. Additionally, 9 public datasets of bulk mRNA sequence or microarray data for 1451 OC samples were accessed from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC, https://icgc.org) and GEO databases (Additional file: Table S1). All data generated or analyzed in the course of this study are freely available in previous publications or in the public databases.

Visualization of TME cell types and subtypes in OC

Based on the Seurat package in R software, the total cell and individual cell types of the scRNA-seq gene expression matrix were converted into Seurat objects. Next, using the FindVariableFeatures function of the Seurat package, the top 2000 genes were selected as the top variable features as the baseline for normalization of scRNA-seq data for each cell. In addition, the number of principal components (PCs) based on Seurat objects was calculated by performing the ScaleData and RunPCA functions. The top principal components were further summarized by t-SNE (t-distribution stochastic neighbor embedding) dimensionality reduction. Finally, Idents and DimPlot functions were used to annotate and visualize the major TME cell types or subtypes based on cell type annotated markers.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis of aggrephagy regulators in TME cells

The Monocle R package was used to analyze single-cell RNA data from all cell types in OC to investigate the relationship between cellular pseudotime trajectories and aggrephagy regulators [18]. Genes with mean expression ≥ 0.1 and dispersion_empirical ≥ 1* dispersion_fit were defined as highly variable genes. Dimensionality reduction is performed using the DDRTree method in the reduceDimension function. Afterward, the heatmap was visualized by the ‘plot_pseudotime_heatmap’ function, which shows the dynamic expression of aggrephagy regulators in the pseudotime trajectories of diverse TME cell types in OC.

Identification of aggrephagy regulators in TME cells using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) algorithm

Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) is a popular method for learning interpretable patterns in data. NMF algorithm (NMF R package, version 0.25) was used to observe the effect of aggrephagy regulators-mediated regulator expression on TME cell types [19, 20]. The 43 aggrephagy regulators (Additional file: Table S2) in all TME cells were subjected to dimensionality reduction analysis, and the respective cell subtypes of these cell types were characterized based on the scRNA expression matrix.

Cell-cell communication analysis for NMF ARGs subtypes in TME cells

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat, an R package that enables quantitative inference and analysis of intercellular communication networks from scRNA-seq data. The CellChat package contains databases of human and mouse ligand-receptor interactions, which helps to discover intercellular communication annotated as distinct cell clusters from scRNA-seq data and to establish cell-cell communication profiles in different tissues [21]. The major signal inputs and outputs between all TME cell clusters were evaluated by the CellChatDB.human function, and then, the strength or weakness of interaction network from the target cell cluster to the different cell clusters in all NMF cell clusters was displayed by the netVisual_circle function.

Identification of aggrephagy marker genes in TME cells

The characteristic genes of NMF clusters for each TME cell type in OC were screened by FindAllMarkers function. The parameter of logfc.threshold was set to 0.5 and genes with adjusted P-value < 0.05 were filtered out. The highest expressed genes in NMF clusters were displayed by Dotplot function. Based on the differentially expressed genes in the NMF cell clusters, the signature scores were calculated using the AddModuleScore function. The FeaturePlot function was used to display the distribution of characteristic genes of NMF cell clusters in the TME of OC.

SCENIC analysis for NMF ARGs subtypes

The differential cellular transcriptional state is associated with cellular heterogeneity, which is determined and maintained by transcription factor-dominated gene regulatory networks (GRNs). Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) is an algorithm for GRNs developed specifically for scRNA-seq data that identifies highly reliable GRNSs dominated by transcription factors [22]. Transcription start sites (TSS) and gene regulatory networks of scRNA-seq data in OC were detected using the gene-motif ranking (hg19-tss-centered-10 kb) of the RcisTarget database. Co-expression networks were calculated by Gene Network Inference with Ensemble of trees 3 (GENIE3). Subsequently, the regulated activity of each cell was scored using AUCell (version 1.16.0). The average regulatory activity of transcription factors for each cluster of cells was demonstrated by heatmap.

Functional analysis for NMF aggrephagy-related subtypes

The enrichKEGG function of the clusterProfiler R package [23] was used to detect the enrichment of those marker genes in NMF clusters of different TME cell types in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database. The scMetabolism package [24] quantifies the metabolic activity based on the single cell level using the VISION algorithm to score each cell and finally obtain the activity score of the cell in each metabolic pathway.

Survival analyses with aggrephagy-related signatures in public bulk RNA sequence datasets

The FindAllmarker function in the Seurat R package was used to generate aggrephagy-associated signature genes for the NMF cell clusters. Subsequently, the GSVA package was used to calculate these gene signature scores for the total 9 OC public datasets. Finally, Cox regression analysis and log-rank test were performed to explore the relationship between aggrephagy-associated NMF signatures and patient prognosis. The cut-off values for each NMF cell cluster were determined using the survminer R package for plotting Kaplan-Meier curves.

Prediction of immunotherapy response

We predicted the likelihood of individual immune escape using Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE, http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu) for 9 ovarian cancer datasets. Also, immunotherapeutic responses were assessed by real-world data from the IMvigor210 cohort (http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies) for subpopulations of cells associated with aggregated autophagy [25]. LogOR represents the natural logarithm of the odds ratio. A low logOR value indicates that high expression of the aggrephagy-related signatures is associated with decreased probability of immunotherapy response.

Construction of C57BL/6 mice model of ovarian cancer

The 5–6 weeks female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Centre and raised in SPF animal rooms. All animal experiments were performed under guidelines evaluated and approved by Dongguan People’s Hospital Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethical Committee. The ID8 subcutaneous tumor mouse model was established through injecting 5 × 107 ID8 cells (derived from C57BL/6 mouse ovarian surface epithelial cells) suspended in 100 µL PBS into the right dorsal side of mouse near the root of the thigh [26]. Experiments were performed on mice when the tumor volume approached 300–400 mm3.

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance of differences between variables conforming to a normal distribution in cell subgroups was calculated by Student’s t test, and differences in non-normally distributed variables were calculated by Wilcoxon rank sum test or Kruskal-Walli’s test. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate differences in categorical variables in cell subgroups. Correlations of different cellular characteristics or gene expression for each TME cell type in OC were estimated by Pearson correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses were performed via R software (version 4.1.3), and two-tailed P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Result

The landscape of aggrephagy regulators in TME cells in OC

As previously elaborated, we harnessed a comprehensive scRNA-seq transcriptomic profiles derived from 7 tumor tissues and 5 matched normal tissues to investigate the landscape of aggrephagy regulators (Fig. 1A). After rigorous quality control procedures, the final number of cells used for analysis was 59,324. Through downscaling and visualization techniques, scRNA-seq data from 12 samples containing a total of 59,324 TME cells were annotated into major cell types, including fibroblasts, myeloid cells, epithelial cells, T cells, B cells and plasma cells (Fig. 1B). Cell-cell communication analysis unveiled intricate interactions among these cell types (Fig. 1C). Notably, single-cell data analysis demonstrated differential expression patterns of aggrephagy regulators across the six OC cell types (Fig. 1D). The expression profiles of four aggrephagy regulators (TUBA1A, TUBA1B, VIM, RPS27A) were further characterized across these cell types (Fig. 1E). In summary, these findings establish a systematic atlas of aggrephagy regulator expression across heterogeneous cellular landscape of the TME in OC at single-cell resolution, revealing heterogeneous patterns and laying the groundwork for subsequent subtype analyses.

Fibroblast subtypes associated with aggrephagy regulators contributed to the TME of OC

Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that fibroblasts, T cells, and macrophages were distributed along a continuous biological process (Fig. 2A and Fig. S1). Notably, key aggrephagy regulators (e.g., TUBA1B, TUBA1A) significantly and continuously exhibited distinct dynamic expression along pseudotime, implying their potential involvement in regulating functional state transitions of these TME cells. Then, based on NMF analysis, we resolved OC-derived fibroblasts into ARGs fibroblast clusters denoted as: TUBA1B+CAF-C1, DYNC1H1+CAF-C2, TUBA1A+CAF-C3, Unclear-aggre-CAF-C4, and Non-aggre-CAF-C5 (Fig. S2). To systematically evaluate interaction strength between ARGs fibroblast subpopulations and epithelial cells, we employed CellChat for quantifying the number of ligand-receptor pairs and communication probability. The TUBA1B+CAF-C1, DYNC1H1+CAF-C2 and TUBA1A+CAF-C3 clusters showed significantly elevated interactions with epithelial cells (C1: 7 pairs; C2: 14 pairs; C3: 7 pairs) and higher communication probabilities than the Non-aggre-CAF-C5 cluster (6 pairs) (Fig. 2B). Quantitative assessment of the number, weights, and strength of ligand-receptor links between clusters and epithelial cells revealed that ARGs fibroblast clusters exhibited greater scores (Fig. 2D). Specifically, within the GAS signaling axis, the TUBA1A+CAF-C3 emerged as the dominant signaling source (Fig. 2F). These findings indicate that CAF clusters with specific aggrephagy regulator expression profiles engage in more active intercellular communication with tumor epithelial cells. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the TUBA1A+CAF-C3 cluster exerted functions associated with the IL-17 signaling pathway and protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 2G). The IL-17 pathway is well recognized for its roles in inflammation and cancer, whereas endoplasmic reticulum protein processing is closely associated with the unfolded protein response (UPR) and cellular stress. Additionally, Pan-CAF signatures [27] indicated that TUBA1A+CAF-C3 scores were strongly associated with inflammation-related CAFs (Pan-iCAF and pan-iCAFs-2) (Fig. 2C). Further analysis of the transcription factors (TFs)-mediated gene regulatory network revealed significant differences in the expression of 43 TFs across the five clusters. Notably, TFs such as EGR1, JUNB, JUN, ATF3, JUND, FOSB and FOS were significantly upregulated in the TUBA1A+CAF-C3 cluster (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, we compared the average expression of genes in common signaling pathways across the five aggrephagy regulators-mediated CAF clusters. The pathway heatmap demonstrated significant differences in the expression patterns of these genes between the aggrephagy-associated clusters and Non-aggre-CAF-C5 (Fig. 2G).

Aggrephagy-mediated T/B cell phenotypes reveal distinct immune responses

In this analysis, four major cell types were identified among 15,720 T cells, including CD4+ cells, CD8+ cells, NK cells, and conventional T cells (Fig. 3A). Aggrephagy-associated cell clusters, including six subtypes of CD8+ cells, four subtypes of CD4+ cells, and three subtypes of NK cells, were further identified via the NMF algorithm (Fig. 3B, F and H). Our findings demonstrated an association between aggrephagy and CD8+ T cells in terms of exhaustion and cytotoxic score (Fig. 3C). The regulatory analysis of TFs revealed significant differences in the expression of TFs within these aggrephagy-associated T cells (Fig. 3D, G and I). Additionally, to evaluate the overall effect of aggrephagy-associated T clusters on T cells, we observed notable differences in the expression profiles of immune genes related to co-stimulation and co-inhibition (Fig. 3E). This finding illustrates that aggrephagy-related CD8+ T_cell clusters exhibit distinct functional tendencies: clusters such as PARK7+CD8+T_cells-C1 show greater cytotoxicity alongside reduced expression of exhaustion markers, while others (e.g., TUBA4A+CD8+T_cells-C2) display elevated exhaustion signatures. This points to a potential link between aggrephagy regulator expression and functional heterogeneity in CD8+ T cells. NMF analysis of tumor-associated B cells identified four aggrephagy-associated B cell clusters (Fig. S3). In comparison to B cells, we observed a significantly higher proportion of Non-aggre-B_cells-C4 in plasma cells (Fig. 3J). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the TUBA1A+B_cells-C3 cell cluster is functionally associated with the IL-17 signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 3K). IL-17 is a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, typically produced by Th17 cells and other cell types in contexts of infection or autoimmunity. It plays a central role in recruiting neutrophils and modulating inflammatory responses. Within the TME, the IL-17 signaling pathway has been shown to exert complex effects—it can both enhance anti-tumor immunity and drive pro-tumor inflammation and angiogenesis. The activation of this pathway in CAFs and B cells suggests that these aggrephagy-related stromal and immune cells may influence tumor progression by actively contributing to the shaping of an inflammatory TME. Collectively, these findings highlight that aggrephagy regulators delineate functionally distinct subpopulations in T and B cells, with implications for immune activation, exhaustion, and pathway engagement in the TME.

Characteristics of aggrephagy regulators-mediated macrophages

Macrophages were annotated within myeloid cells and NMF analysis was conducted on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Consequently, we identified five major aggrephagy-Mac clusters (Fig. S4). There was a close ligand-receptor interaction between aggrephagy-Mac clusters and tumor epithelial cells (Fig. 4A). Specifically, the number of interactions between the TUBA1B+Mac-C1 cluster and tumor epithelial cells was significantly higher than that of the Non-aggre-Mac-C5 cluster. Meanwhile, the scMetabolism package was utilized to evaluate the metabolic activity of each aggrephagy-Mac cluster. The results revealed that the TUBA1B+Mac-C1 cluster exhibited significantly enhanced metabolic activation, whereas the metabolic pathway activation of Non-aggre-Mac-C5 was not significant (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, SCENIC analysis of macrophages revealed significant differences in the activation of potential TFs between the TUBA1B+Mac-C1 and Non-aggre-Mac-C5 clusters (Fig. 4C). The high metabolic activity in TUBA1B+Mac-C1 may be sustained through post-transcriptional mechanisms (e.g., metabolic enzyme activation via phosphorylation or allosteric regulation) or signaling pathway activation (e.g., mTOR, AMPK) that do not require continuous high TF input. Conversely, high TF activity in Non-aggre-Mac-C5 may support functions other than metabolism, such as proliferation, cytokine production, or stress responses. We speculate that this difference may be associated with the M1/M2 polarization status of macrophages. The high metabolic activity in TUBA1B+Mac-C1 appears to be more dependent on post-transcriptional or signaling mechanisms rather than TF-driven transcription, highlighting the complex interplay between aggrephagy-associated states and transcriptional regulation. The analysis of M1 and M2 macrophage markers revealed that the TUBA1B+Mac-C1 cluster had higher levels of M1 macrophages compared to the Non-aggre-Mac-C5, though the observed difference did not reach statistical significance. Moreover, there was no significant distinction in the levels of M2 macrophages between the two clusters (Fig. 4D).

TME patterns associated with aggrephagy regulators contribute to the prognosis of OC

Utilizing the DEGs associated with aggrephagy in TME cells, we applied GSVA to calculate scores for ARGs subgroups within the TCGA dataset. All scores were subjected to Cox regression analysis. The association between these ARGs subgroups and patient prognosis was further investigated via Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 5A–I). Our findings indicate that specific cell subgroups, namely PARK7+CD8+T_cells-C1, TUBA1B+CD8+T_cells-C3, TUBA1B+Mac-C1, and VCP+B_cell-C2, were associated with favorable patient prognosis. Conversely, the presence of TUBA1A+CAF-C3, TUBA4A+CD4+T_cells-C3, TUBB4B+NK_cells-C1, TUBA1C+Mac-C3, and TUBA1A+B_cell-C3 subgroups correlated with poorer patient prognosis. We extended this analysis to 1451 OC patients across nine datasets, revealing significantly different survival outcomes associated with the dominant aggrephagy genes in these cell subtypes, spanning CAF, macrophages, CD8+ T, CD4+ T, NK, and B cells (Fig. 5J). In summary, the expression signature of specific aggrephagy-related TME subtypes show consistent and significant associations with patient survival across multi-cohort analysis, reinforcing their potential clinical relevance. TME patterns associated with aggrephagy regulators contribute to immunotherapy in OC

We utilized the TIDE algorithm to predict the immunotherapy response of each aggrephagy-associated TME cell in OC patients from the TCGA dataset. The results revealed eight aggrephagy-associated TME cell subgroups that act as risk factors for immunotherapy (Fig. 6A). These aggrephagy-related subgroups associated with immunotherapy resistance were: TUBA1B+CAF-C1, DYNC1H1+CAF-C2, TUBA1A+CAF-C3, DYNC1I2+CD4+T_cells-C1, TUBA1A+CD4+T_cells-C2, TUBA1A+CD8+T_cells-C2, TUBB4B+NK_cells-C1, and TUBA1A+NK_cells-C2. Additionally, logistic regression analysis revealed that ARGs cell subtypes were similarly effective in predicting the response to immunotherapy across the nine OC datasets (Fig. 6B). In the IMvigor210 cohort of patients undergoing immunotherapy, aggrephagy-associated TME cell subpopulations, including TUBA1B+CAF-C1, DYNC1H1+CAF-C2, TUBA1A+CAF-C3, and TUBA1C+Mac-C3, were similarly identified as risk factors for immunotherapy (Fig. 6C). The survival analyses in Fig. 6D–G were performed using data from the IMvigor210 cohort. Some of these subgroups were associated with poor patient prognosis (Fig. 6D–F). Notably, the TUBA1B+CD8+T_cells-C3 and TUBA1B+Mac-C1 subgroups were identified as favorable factors for immunotherapy in bladder cancer (Fig. 6C), which were associated with a favorable patient prognosis (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, ID8 ovarian cancer mouse subcutaneous tumors were constructed for co-localization experiments (Fig. 7A). Significant co-localization of TUBA1B protein with CD8+ T cells was observed by immunofluorescence in ID8 ovarian cancer mouse subcutaneous tumor (Fig. 7B). The expression of aggrephagy regulators (TUBA1A, TUBA1B and TUBA1C) were also increased in OC tissues compared to normal ovarian tissues (Fig. 7C and Fig. S5, S6).

Discussion

The process of cellular autophagy involves the engulfment of damaged organelles or misfolded proteins, culminating in the formation of double-membrane structures that are trafficked to lysosomes for degradation [28, 29]. Autophagy plays a crucial role in various physiological and pathological processes and exhibits a bidirectional impact on tumorigenesis. Specifically, insufficient autophagy levels contribute to the initiation of early-stage cancers, whereas excessive autophagy activity support the survival of tumor cells under nutrient-deficient microenvironments [30]. The delicate balances between protein synthesis and degradation underpin the oncogenesis and progression of tumors. The generation of misfolded protein aggregates are often caused by mutations, inadequate translation, improper post-translational modifications, and oxidative stress [31]. The aggregation of misexpressed proteins is a characteristic feature of cancer. Aggrephagy, a subtype of autophagy, selectively degrades aggregated proteins, thereby mitigating aggregate formation and reducing cellular damage [32]. In this study, the selection of 43 genes was not restricted to classical selective autophagy receptors (SARs), instead, it was intended to construct a broader gene set with potential relevance to the aggrephagy process. First, tubulins (such as TUBA1B) are critical components of autophagosome transport and maturation, and their expression and stability directly impact the efficiency of autophagy flux such as aggrephagy. Second, proteasomal and ubiquitination-related genes (e.g., RPS27A, VCP) were included, as aggrephagy is funtionally intertwined with the ubiquitin-proteasome system. Thus, our analytical strategy was to treat these genes as an integrated “aggrephagy-related functional module,” rather than requiring each gene to be a direct mediator of substrate recognition as a SAR.

Normal epithelial cells can regulate cytoskeletal proteins to eliminate oncogenically transformed cells, thereby exerting potent antitumor activity. Conversely, cancerous epithelial cells are closely associated with tumor development [33]. In this study, we observed heterogeneous expression profiles of aggrephagy regulators across tumor epithelial cellsand diverse stromal components within the TME, including fibroblasts, T cells, macrophages, and B cells. To date, no studies have systematically interrogated the correlation between aggrephagy regulators and OC pathogenesis using single-cell analysis. Here, we pioneer a comprehensive exploration of aggrephagy regulatory landscapes in major cellular compartments within the ovarian cancer TME. Additionally, we delineate intercellular communication networks among ARGs cell subtypes and cancer cells at the single-cell level. This unprecedented perspective provides a preliminary insight into the regulatory role of aggrephagy across different cellular components of the TME, highlighting intricate communications between associated TME cells and tumor epithelial cells. In the cluster analysis of various cell types, it was found that the expression levels of TUBA1A, TUBA1B, DYNC1H1, and PARK7 are distinguished across clusters. Each protein shows a distinct function in the aggrephagy process, acting at different stages: PARK7 in prevention, TUBA1A/B in providing the infrastructure, and DYNC1H1 in active transport. The different cellular phenotypes arise precisely from this functional specialization. The heterogeneous expression of these regulators across cell subpopulations suggests that different cells within the TME adopt different strategies to manage proteotoxic stress: (1) high expression of PARK7 may prioritize prevention of protein aggregation; (2) high expression of TUBA1A/B and DYNC1H1 may be specialized for the clearance of existing aggregates through efficient transport and degradation. This functional heterogeneity enables different cell subtypes to adapt to varying levels and types of proteostatic stress within the TME.

CAFs represent a crucial component of the TME, with previous studies classifying them into five subtypes: pan-myCAFs, pan-dCAFs, pan-iCAFs, pan-iCAFs-2, and pan-pCAFs [27]. Despite their recognized significance, the potential role of aggrephagy regulators in CAFs has remained uncharted. Our investigation pioneers the elucidation of this gap, revealing that aggrephagy-CAFs engage in more extensive intercellular crosstalk with tumor epithelial cells compared to non-aggrephagy CAFs. Additionally, aggrephagy-CAFs displayed unique molecular features distinct from pan-CAFs. Notably, the TUBA1A+CAF subtype exhibited a strong association with inflammation-associated CAFs (Pan-iCAF and Pan-iCAFs-2). Pathway analysis further indicated that the TUBA1A+CAF cell cluster is significantly enriched for the IL-17 signaling cascades and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum. CAFs are recognized as regulatory factors that contribute to their pro-inflammatory and tumor-promoting functions [34, 35]. Therefore, we hypothesize that these CAF subtypes with specific ARGs expression patterns are associated with pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, contributing to shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Autophagy functions as a crucial regulatory axis in initiating and supporting both innate and adaptive immunity. T and B cells with autophagy deficiency exhibit impaired maturation and impaired activation patterns [36]. Aggrephagy, a subtype of autophagy, specifically targets protein aggregates. Activated T cells demonstrate enhanced autophagosome biogenesis and clearance [37]. CD4+ T cells, responsible for regulating immune responses through cytokine production, and CD8+ T cells, involved in killing pathogenic infections or malignant cells, exhibit distinct effector functions [38]. The heterogeneity of effector T cell subsets is intricately regulated by the convergence of autophagy and metabolism in a lineage-specific manner [36]. Our study identified significant differences in immune gene co-stimulatory and co-inhibitory programs across T cell clusters associated with aggrephagy regulators, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and NK cells. The findings indicate a complex interplay between aggrephagy regulators and T cell subsets, where each immunological lineage executes distinct effector functions. Leveraging NMF clustering, we observed that aggrephagy-associated TAMs clusters exhibited pervasive intercellular interactions with tumor epithelial cells. Notably, both CAFs and TAMs demonstrated significantly elevated communication with malignant epithelial cells, suggesting the involvement of aggrephagy regulatory networks in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Notably, CellChat analysis revealed that the TUBA1B+Mac-C1 cluster exhibited the most extensive communication with tumor epithelial cells, showing the highest number of predicted ligand-receptor interactions among all macrophage subpopulations (Fig. 4A). This robust communication pattern suggests a potentially important role for this aggrephagy-associated macrophage subtype in modulating tumor cell behavior. Prognostic analysis further revealed a positive correlation between its expression and survival outcomes in patients. The dynamic modulation of the microenvironment reshapes the genetic landscape of macrophages, leading to distinct adaptive metabolic reprogramming. Modulation of TAMs functional polarization can effectively impact tumor progression, displaying heterogeneity from anti-tumor to pro-tumor phenotypes [39, 40]. Our study demonstrated that aggrephagy-activated TAMs, particularly the TUBA1B+Mac subtype, is associated with the activation of metabolism-related pathways, including those related to glucose, fatty acids, and steroid hormones. Collectively, these findings underscore that aggrephagy regulators may be implicated in immune escape and tumor promotion through metabolic reprogramming of macrophages and T cells.

We performed a comprehensive analysis of the specific gene regulatory networks of TFs at the single-cell level. In general, CAFs, TAMs, and T cells exhibited distinct signature TF landscapes. Notably, TUBA1A+CAF subsets displayed characteristic TFs, including EGR1, JUNB, JUN, ATF3, JUND, FOSB, and FOS. Previous studies have implicated EGR1 and JUN in the regulation of autophagy in OC [41, 42]. Further investigation is warranted to determine whether these unique TFs function in the regulation of aggrephagy. Interestingly, in TAMs, despite the significant activation of metabolic activity in TUBA1B+Mac subtype, its associated TFs were significantly down-regulated. While M1 macrophages are characterized by heightened glycolytic metabolism and ROS production, and M2 macrophages with tissue repair support [43], our findings reveal the potential for a more nuanced understanding of metabolic heterogeneity and functional plasticity in immune regulation [44]. While classical immunometabolism links fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to M2 polarization, previous studies have highlighted context-dependent functions of lipid metabolism in macrophages. In an inflammatory microenvironment, some macrophages co-utilize glycolysis and FAO to sustain effector functions [45, 46]. Fatty acids can act as precursors for inflammatory lipid mediators (e.g., prostaglandins) or support membrane biosynthesis during rapid phagocytosis [47, 48], those processes aligned with M1-like phenotypes. Our data suggest TUBA1B+Mac-C1 may represent a metabolically adaptable pro-inflammatory state shaped by lipid-rich TME conditions. Although our study did not identify a significant association of ARGs clusters with M1 and M2 macrophages, the gene expression of M1 macrophages was significantly elevated in ARGs subsets compared to non-aggrephagy subsets. Furthermore, we identified ARGs subtypes within T cells with distinct TF profiles. In summary, aggrephagy appears to regulate diverse networks of TFs, reshaping and reprogramming TME cells. Additionally, based on the OC public bulk RNA-seq cohort, we comprehensively summarized the scores of these ARGs clusters in terms of survival prognosis and response to immunotherapy. Notably, significant inter-cluster differences were observed in patient survival outcomes and response to ICB treatment among different ARGs TME cells in OC patients, highlighting the critical role of TME cell aggrephagy regulators that warrant further functional experiments for investigation. Interestingly, Fig. 5D showed that TUBA1B+CD8+ T cell was positively associated with improved prognosis, while Fig. 6G showed that TUBA1B+CD8+ T cell may be beneficial for cancer immunotherapy. We observed significant co-localization of TUBA1B with CD8+ T cells in ovarian cancer tissues of mice, which suggested that TUBA1B may be a promising target for cancer therapy. The TUBA1C+Mac-C3 cluster showed a significant hazard ratio in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 5G) but failed to reach statistical significance in the multi-cohort meta-analysis (Fig. 5J). This discrepancy likely reflects cohort-specific biological or technical heterogeneity. While most associations remained consistent across cohorts, some clusters may exert context-dependent effects, and that the TUBA1C+Mac-C3 signature may be more relevant in specific molecular subtypes or patient populations. Although TUBA1A, TUBA1B, and TUBA1C have not been established as selective autophagy receptors, emerging evidence highlights their important indirect roles in aggrephagy and cancer biology. These α-tubulin isoforms act as fundamental components of microtubules that provide the structural framework for intracellular transport. Specifically, they facilitate the movement of autophagosomes and their fusion with lysosomes–essential steps in aggrephagy execution. Studies have demonstrated that tubulin composition affects microtubule stability and function, with TUBA1B particularly implicated in preserving microtubule networks required for efficient autophagy flux.

Our study has several compelling strengths. Firstly, it is grounded in the scRNA-seq technology to explore the intricate landscape of intercellular communication among aggrephagy regulators within the TME. The application of scRNA-seq provides a novel perspective for the characteristics of aggrephagy regulators across diverse TME cell populations, thereby mitigating tumor heterogeneity in OC. Secondly, we integrated OC cohorts with substantial sample sizes from multiple public databases to conduct survival prognosis and predict immunotherapeutic responses. This approach enhances the reliability and stability of our model, and the identified cell clusters exhibit refined biological characteristics that hold promise for guiding clinical precision therapy, particularly in the realm of targeted immunotherapy for OC. Nevertheless, our study has inherent limitations. Firstly, the sample size for scRNA-seq is limited, and further validation in a larger cohort of patients is warranted to bolster the robustness of our findings. Although efforts were made to predict the response of OC to immunotherapy, the absence of relevant datasets on OC immunotherapy precludes the validation of our results. Second, there is a lack of direct functional experiments on key aggrephagy regulatory factors, such as knocking down TUBA1B or other genes in specific TME cell subtypes, to validate causal relationships. And these cell clusters were computationally delineated based on expression similarity, and their functional characteristics (i.e., whether they truly exhibit higher aggrephagy activity) still await subsequent experimental verification. Moreover, the absence of our independent OC patient cohorts represents a significant constraint on the opportunities for further confirmation. Despite the current lack of direct functional experiments, we believe that the extensive correlative evidence provided by this study—including prognostic significance in independent large cohorts, predictive value for immunotherapy response, and the co-localization of TUBA1B with CD8+ T cells in mouse models—strongly underscores the functional importance of aggrephagy and lays a robust directional and foundational framework for subsequent in-depth mechanistic investigations. We remain confident that these preliminary yet systematic findings still retain significant instructive value for the field.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study sheds light on the role of aggrephagy regulators-mediated intercellular communication within the TME in the regulation of tumor growth and antitumor immunomodulation. These findings may pave the way for targeted interventions that modulate TME cell metabolism and immune cell function to enhance treatment efficacy of OC.
