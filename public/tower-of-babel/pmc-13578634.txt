Metagenomic and physicochemical insights into ultrasound-assisted fermentation: Reprogramming Komagataeibacter intermedius metabolic efficiency for astringency control
Authors: Wangxin Liu, Jiemei Shen, Qiaomei Chen, Zhibin Liu, Jinzhi Han, Li Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Kombucha, Ultrasound-assisted fermentation, Astringency mitigation, Metabolic reprogramming, Data-driven modeling

Introduction

Kombucha is a widely consumed symbiotic fermented tea beverage that has attracted considerable attention because of its high levels of tea polyphenols, organic acids, and bioactive enzymes, which contribute to its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [1]. Despite these functional benefits, prolonged fermentation is frequently accompanied by the development of pronounced astringency, greatly reducing consumer acceptability [2]. Astringency is not solely a taste perception but also a tactile sensation associated with aggregation and friction at the oral mucosal surface. At the molecular level, this sensation mainly arises from interactions between tea polyphenols, particularly catechin monomers and their polymers, and salivary proline-rich proteins and mucins through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, ultimately leading to the formation of insoluble aggregates [3]. Conventional approaches for astringency reduction mainly depend on physical adsorption, chemical clarification, or the degradation of polyphenols to suppress the perception of harshness. However, these strategies inevitably reduce the retention of key bioactive polyphenols, including catechins, thereby potentially weakening the antioxidant capacity and health-promoting effects of the beverage [4], [5]. Therefore, a major challenge in the biomanufacturing of high-quality fermented tea beverages is to regulate the aggregation behavior of polyphenols and salivary proteins during fermentation while preserving the structural integrity and biological activity of these functional polyphenols.

Ultrasound, as an emerging non-thermal physical field, induces cavitation that generates transient high temperatures, high pressures, and intense shear forces, thereby enhancing mass transfer and activating microbial stress responses [6]. In our previous study, an optimized low-power ultrasound-assisted fermentation process (16.68 min, 242.25 W, 30% duty cycle) successfully reshaped the terminal microbial community of kombucha and mitigated perceived astringency [7]. Concurrently, ultrasound has been shown to alter protein secondary conformations to alleviate precipitation in fermented systems [8], improve debittering enzyme kinetics [9], and dynamically remodel volatile metabolic pathways during kombucha fermentation [10]. However, existing studies have largely overlooked the time-series progression of non-volatile microbial metabolism, particularly the biosynthesis of functional polysaccharides. Consequently, how ultrasound-induced temporal metabolic shifts interact with tea polyphenols to govern their nanoscale colloidal assembly with salivary proteins remains poorly understood, impeding a mechanistic understanding of non-destructive astringency mitigation.

In complex colloidal systems, water-soluble polysaccharides (WSPs) have been demonstrated to function as a nanoscale exclusion network. Owing to their abundant hydroxyl groups and the steric hindrance of polymer chains, they can competitively bind proteins or encapsulate polyphenols within soluble complexes, thereby effectively suppressing the formation of macroscopic polyphenol–protein precipitates. In Kombucha fermentation, Komagataeibacter spp., with K. intermedius as a core dominant strain, represents a major contributor to extracellular polysaccharide biosynthesis. Previous studies have shown that members of this genus, such as K. xylinus, exhibit pronounced adaptive metabolic reprogramming under physical stresses including ultrasound and cold shock. Such stresses may redirect cellular resources from growth toward extracellular polysaccharide production. However, it remains unclear whether low-power ultrasound induces a similar metabolic shift in key Kombucha-associated microorganisms and how these strain-specific responses contribute to polysaccharide accumulation and subsequent protein–polyphenol interactions. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of low-power ultrasound on microbial metabolic activity during Kombucha fermentation and to clarify its relationship with polysaccharide production and protein–polyphenol interactions. We hypothesized that low-power ultrasound enhances the metabolic activity of Komagataeibacter, a major polysaccharide-producing genus, thereby promoting water-soluble polysaccharide (WSP) accumulation and altering the aggregation behavior of polyphenol–salivary protein complexes. We further proposed that the reduction in astringency is primarily associated with physicochemical changes in polyphenol–protein interactions rather than extensive degradation of tea polyphenols. To verify these hypotheses, temporal variations in WSPs, tea polyphenols, saliva–polyphenol interactions, and microbial community dynamics were monitored throughout fermentation. Subsequently, strain-specific metabolic efficiency analysis was combined with time modeling to infer the potential metabolic drivers of ultrasound-induced polysaccharide accumulation and astringency reduction.

Materials and methods

Materials

Green tea and a commercial SCOBY starter culture were sourced from Yifutang Tea Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) and Zhishi Weibao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaocheng, China), respectively. Standards for organic acids (acetic, citric, D-glucuronic), polyphenols (EC, EGC, ECG, EGCG, GA, FA), anthrone, and lysozyme were purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology (Shanghai, China). Other biochemicals, including L-lactic acid, lactoferrin, α-amylase, and bovine serum albumin (BSA), were supplied by Aladdin, Macklin, and Solarbio (China). DNS reagent was obtained from Scientific Phygene (China), and HPLC-grade solvents (acetonitrile and methanol) were provided by Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China). Unless otherwise specified, all other reagents were of analytical grade, and deionized water was used throughout the study.

Kombucha preparation and experimental design

Kombucha was prepared according to the previous method described with minor modifications [7]. Briefly, green tea (8.0 g, 2.67% w/v) and sucrose (50.0 g, 16.7% w/v) were dissolved in 300 mL deionized water in a 500 mL beaker, boiled for 10 min, cooled to room temperature, and filtered to remove solids. The cooled infusion was inoculated with 100 mL of SCOBY starter (25% v/v). Beakers were covered with sterile eight-layer gauze and incubated at room temperature for 7 days. Two experimental groups were established: control (CK, without ultrasound), and continuous ultrasound (US, applied daily from day 2 to day 7). To monitor fermentation dynamics, samples were collected on days 0, 3, 5, and 7 and subjected to subsequent analyses. Ultrasound treatments were performed using a 20 kHz processor (Model 20KLS-1200B, Wuxi YuanShengTe Intelligent Technology Co., Ltd., China) equipped with a 10 mm titanium probe. A 400 mL sample in a 500 mL beaker was treated at room temperature (25 ± 2℃) in intermittent mode with the probe submerged 1 cm. Following our previous protocol, ultrasound irradiation was performed at 240 W for 16.68 min with a 30% duty cycle [7]. At an electrical input power of 240 W, the corresponding sound intensity on the probe surface is 137.6 W/cm2. At sampling, broths were centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Samples were filtered through 0.22 µm membranes when required.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory profiling was conducted by a trained panel of 13 assessors (7 females and 6 males, aged 20–30 years) recruited from the sensory laboratory of Fuzhou University, adhering to institutional ethical guidelines. Panelist training was standardized according to ISO 8586:2012 over 4 sessions (2 h each) to align descriptor definitions and scale ratings. Scale anchors (0, 5, and 10 on a 10-point line scale; 0 = not perceptible, 10 = extremely strong) were calibrated using authentic chemical reference standards: tannic acid (0, 0.5, and 1.0 g/L for astringency), EGCG (0.8 g/L for dryness and roughness), sucrose (0, 40, and 80 g/L for sweetness), and citric acid (0, 1.0, and 2.0 g/L for acidity). Kombucha samples (30 mL) were served at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) in randomized, three-digit coded odorless plastic cups. Temporal attributes (onset and duration of astringency) were recorded in seconds. To prevent sensory adaptation and carry-over effects, panelists cleansed their palates with deionized water and unsalted crackers, with a mandatory 2-min inter-stimulus interval between samples. Panel reliability was statistically verified on the formal evaluation dataset: the internal consistency among assessors was confirmed with Cronbach’s α values of 0.884 for astringency intensity, 0.862 for roughness, 0.897 for dryness, and 0.854 for duration (composite α = 0.887), and a two-way ANOVA (Panelist × Sample) revealed significant sample discrimination (F = 5.24, p < 0.001) with no significant interaction effect (F = 1.21, p = 0.224 > 0.05), demonstrating high reproducibility and consensus across sensory evaluations.

Physicochemical analysis

Water-soluble polysaccharide, reducing sugars and titratable acidity

Samples were ethanol-precipitated overnight at 4 °C, centrifuged (3,000 rpm, 10 min), washed, and redissolved in distilled water. Water-soluble polysaccharide were quantified at 620 nm via the anthrone–sulfuric acid method using a glucose standard. Reducing sugars were determined at 540 nm using the DNS method. Titratable acidity was determined by titrating 10 mL of sample against 0.01 mol/L NaOH to pH 8.20, and the results were expressed as g/L of acetic acid.

Total phenolic content

Total phenolics were determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu assay [11]. Samples (200 µL) were mixed with 1.0 mL of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent for 5 min, followed by the addition of 800 µL of 7.5% Na2CO3. After a 60-min incubation in the dark, absorbance was recorded at 765 nm, and results were expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE).

Determination of organic acids

Organic acids (acetic, L-lactic, citric, and D-glucuronic) in 0.22 μm-filtered Kombucha samples were quantified via external calibration using a Thermo Fisher Vanquish Horizon HPLC system. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Acciaim OA column (5 μm, 4.0 × 250 mm) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase was an isocratic 100 mM Na2SO4 solution (pH adjusted to 2.65 with methanesulfonic acid), delivered at a flow rate of 0.600 mL/min. The injection volume was 20 μL, and UV detection was monitored at 210 nm with a total run time of 30 min.

Preparation of artificial saliva and saliva-Kombucha interaction system

Artificial saliva was prepared according to the previous method [12] with slight modifications. The artificial saliva protein solution consisted of lactoferrin (1.457 g/L), lysozyme (0.193 g/L), α-amylase (4.258 g/L), and bovine serum albumin (1.032 g/L). All components were dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) until completely dissolved. While this simplified formulation cannot capture the full proteomic complexity of human saliva in vivo, this protocol serves as a highly reproducible in vitro colloid alternative that eliminates individual biological differences, thus enabling reliable comparisons of saliva-polyphenol aggregation behavior under different treatment conditions. For the interaction assays, kombucha samples were mixed with artificial saliva at a volume ratio of 1:4 (v/v) and incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C for 20 min to facilitate saliva–polyphenol binding. Following incubation, the reacted mixtures were immediately utilized for turbidity measurement, particle size analysis, catechin determination, and precipitate structural characterization.

Turbidity and particle size analysis

Turbidity analysis

The turbidity of the saliva-Kombucha mixtures (prepared in Section 2.5) was evaluated in 96-well microplates by measuring the absorbance at 650 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Singapore).

Measurement of particle size

Particle size distribution of the saliva-Kombucha mixtures (3 mL) was determined in a 1 cm glass cuvette using an Omni multi-angle particle size analyzer (Brookhaven Instruments Corporation, USA). Measurements were recorded at room temperature with a 90° scattering angle and a 180-s acquisition time.

Catechin analysis by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Two types of 0.22-μm filtered samples were analyzed: (1) five-fold diluted Kombucha, and (2) supernatants collected from centrifuged saliva-Kombucha mixtures (5000 rpm, 10 min; Section 2.5). Chromatography was performed on an Agilent reversed-phase C18 column (4.6 × 200 mm, 5 μm) maintained at 35 °C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.2% aqueous acetic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min with an injection volume of 10 μL. Detection was monitored at 278 nm. The gradient elution program was: 0–20 min, 80% A; 20–21 min, linear transition to 60% A; and 21–25 min, 80% A for column re-equilibration. Target analytes, including epicatechin (EC), epigallocatechin (EGC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), gallic acid (GA), and ferulic acid (FA), were quantified using external standard calibration curves.

Characterization of saliva–polyphenol precipitates (FT-IR, Steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy, SAXS)

Following the saliva-Kombucha interaction, the mixtures were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min), and the collected precipitates were freeze-dried for structural analysis. For FT-IR spectroscopy, 2 mg of the dried precipitate was blended with KBr, pressed into a pellet, and scanned from 4000 to 400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 (32 scans) on a Thermo Scientific spectrometer (USA). Steady-state fluorescence emission spectra (380–700 nm) were acquired under an excitation wavelength of 370 nm using an FLS1000 spectrometer (Edinburgh Instruments, UK) equipped with a xenon lamp and a PMT-980 detector (step size: 1 nm, dwell time: 0.1 s, and slit widths: 1 nm). Finally, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) profiles were collected on a Xeuss 3.0 UHR system (Xenocs, France) to resolve the nanoscale microstructural features of the complexes.

Metagenomic sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the centrifuged Kombucha precipitate using a FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, Shanghai, China). DNA concentration and integrity were verified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Qualified DNA was sheared to ∼350 bp using a Covaris M220, and libraries were prepared with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq kit (Bioo Scientific, USA) prior to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp (v0.20.0) and assembled into contigs (≥300 bp) via MEGAHIT (v1.1.2). Open reading frames (≥100 bp) predicted by Prodigal (v2.6.3) were clustered with CD-HIT (90% identity and coverage) to establish a non-redundant gene catalog, with abundances quantified using SOAPaligner (v2.21). Taxonomic assignment and functional annotation were performed using DIAMOND (v2.0.13, e-value ≤ 1 × 10-5) against the NCBI NR and KEGG databases, respectively. Statistical differences in taxonomic and functional profiles were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Sensory reconstitution (CAI) and machine learning-based driver attribution

The raw experimental datasets were obtained from a Kombucha fermentation system sampled at three distinct time points (Day 3, Day 5, and Day 7) under control (CK) and ultrasound-treated (US) conditions, with three independent biological replicates per treatment group. The baseline raw dataset comprised: (i) trained sensory panel scores for Astringency Intensity, Roughness, and Dryness; (ii) physicochemical measurements including water-soluble polysaccharide content (mg/mL, determined via ethanol precipitation) and specific polyphenols (EGCG, determined via high-performance liquid chromatography); and (iii) metagenomic taxonomic relative abundances at the species level alongside functional-taxonomic contribution matrices. To reduce the dimensionality of oral tactile perception, a Comprehensive Astringency Index (CAI) was reconstructed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Scores for the three core astringency-related sensory traits from all biological replicates (N = 18) were standardized using Z-score normalization. The first principal component (PC1) was extracted, and its sign was aligned with Astringency Intensity (where CAI = − PC1 if negatively correlated) to ensure that higher CAI values represented greater perceived astringency.

To identify the key functional drivers responsible for this sensory evolution, an efficiency-driven Partial Least Squares Regression (PLS-R) model was constructed. Crucially, rather than utilizing raw taxonomic abundances, the model was trained to predict the CAI using fermentation time, treatment group, and the log10-transformed specific metabolic efficiencies (SMEs, mathematically defined in Section 2.11) of the dominant species (incorporating a 10-5 pseudo-count). The model’s predictive reliability was audited via Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) and validated by evaluating the Spearman correlation between the observed and predicted CAI values. Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores were extracted from the optimized PLS-R model to rank driver importance. Finally, taxa lacking polysaccharide-synthetic potential were filtered out, and the remaining keystone features were categorized and annotated based on the Spearman correlation (r) between their specific metabolic efficiency and the CAI.

Metagenomic functional profiling, specific metabolic efficiency, and causal cascade

Metagenomic functional annotation was performed against the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) database to profile the functional capacity of the non-redundant gene catalog. Target polysaccharide biosynthesis KEGG Orthology (KO) groups were identified by scanning the functional annotations for synthesis-related keywords (e.g., glycosyl, transferase, synthase, polysaccharide, and cellulose). Taxon-specific functional contributions for the dominant species were extracted from HumAnN3-derived stratification data, yielding population abundance (% of total biomass) and target functional efficacy (% of polysaccharide-synthetic KOs). The biological reproducibility of metagenomic replicates (n=3) was empirically confirmed by the tight intra-group clustering in NMDS analysis. Statistical analysis

All fermentation experiments and analytical measurements were conducted in at least independent biological triplicates. Conventional experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Basic statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.4.0, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, or independent Student’s t-tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. For the advanced multidimensional data mining, machine learning (PCA and PLS-R), and temporal causal analyses detailed in 2.10, 2.11, custom analytical scripts were executed in Python (version 3.12). Visual analysis was implemented using the SciPy, Pingouin, Matplotlib, and Seaborn libraries.

Results and discussion

Sensory evaluation and physical validation of astringency mitigation

At the late fermentation stage (Day 7), ultrasound-assisted Kombucha exhibited a pronounced suppression of the sensory hallmarks of astringency. Specifically, the sensory intensity of both astringency and dryness was significantly lower in the US-7 group than in the passive control (CK-7) (p < 0.05, Fig. 1A and 1C). The sensation of astringency is primarily governed by the loss of oral lubricity, which occurs when tea polyphenols (specifically esterified catechins like EGCG) cross-link and precipitate salivary proline-rich proteins (PRPs) and mucins through cooperative hydrophobic association and hydrogen bonding [13]. To quantitatively investigate this, an in vitro saliva precipitation assay (OD650nm) was performed. The US-treated Kombucha exhibited a significantly reduced salivary protein-binding and precipitation capacity at both Day 5 (p < 0.05, Fig. 1H) and Day 7 (p < 0.05, Fig. 1I). In human salivary and sensory trials, the depletion of high-molecular-weight salivary mucins has been shown to be closely associated with the breakdown of the oral boundary lubricating film, which can trigger mucosal dryness [13]. The lower OD650nm turbidity of the US-5 and US-7 groups suggests that the ultrasound-assisted fermentation effectively mitigated the cross-linking and subsequent precipitation of these critical lubricating proteins. Similar efficacy of ultrasound in remodeling macromolecular structures and suppressing secondary protein precipitation has also been documented in other fermented systems [8]. This reduction in protein precipitation aligns with the decreased sensory astringency scores evaluated by the trained panel, indicating a potential preservation of oral lubrication.

At the colloidal level, this biochemical reduction in saliva precipitation is physically mediated by the suppression of aggregate growth. Under normal conditions, unhindered tea polyphenol-salivary protein interactions lead to the cooperative growth of large, irregular aggregates. These large colloidal aggregates act as solid, abrasive particles between oral sliding contacts, disrupting thin-film boundary lubrication, spiking the friction coefficient, and stimulating mucosal mechanoreceptors to cause oral dryness and roughness [14]. Ultrasound-assisted fermentation, however, interrupts this macroscopic aggregation pathway. This physical intervention is directly confirmed by the aggregate size dynamics, where the average particle size of saliva-induced complexes exhibited a decreasing trend in the US-5 group (p = 0.06, Fig. 1E) and the particle size distribution (span) was narrowed on Day 7 (Fig. 1F). Limiting aggregate growth to a tighter, sub-micron colloidal scale effectively prevents the transition to boundary lubrication failure, thereby alleviating the macroscopic sensations of oral roughness and dryness. From a physicochemical perspective, this particle size limitation may be induced by the steric hindrance of ultrasonically enriched WSPs. As supported by subsequent SAXS and spectroscopic analyses, poly molecules incorporate into the primary polyphenol–protein nuclei, forming a bulky, hydrated steric shield around them. This macromolecular boundary expands the nanoscale radius of gyration (Rg) and provides electrostatic and steric repulsion, which physically blocks secondary hydrophobic aggregation and prevents the formation of large macroscopic precipitates. This particle size limitation is hypothesized to stem from the steric hindrance of endogenously produced soluble polysaccharides, which may form a protective shield around the developing saliva–polyphenol complexes to prevent further hydrophobic aggregation [15].

Temporal evolution of primary physicochemical properties

The dynamic changes in primary physicochemical parameters (reducing sugars, total acids, total phenols, and water-soluble polysaccharide) of Kombucha during the 7-day fermentation under control (CK) and ultrasound-treated (US) conditions are presented in Fig. 2. The temporal profile of reducing sugars (Fig. 2A) and water-soluble polysaccharide content (Fig. 2D) highlighted the profound impact of ultrasound on carbohydrate metabolism. In Fig. 2A, the US group maintained a slightly higher reducing sugar level at Day 5 (p = 0.0513), followed by a sharp, highly significant drop on Day 6 compared to the CK group (p < 0.01).This sudden depletion of hexoses in the late stage of ultrasound-assisted fermentation closely mirrors the experimental findings of Marina et al. (2020) [16]. In their study on ultrasound-intensified cider fermentation, they demonstrated via kinetic modeling that power ultrasound temporarily enhances yeast cell membrane permeability without causing cell lysis. This membrane modification significantly accelerates glucose and fructose uptake through hexose transporters, leading to a rapid, steep decline in reducing sugars during the active metabolic phase, which explains the sharp drop observed at US-6 in our study. Concurrently, the water-soluble polysaccharide content (Fig. 2D), representing the soluble fraction of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) secreted predominantly by acetic acid bacteria (AAB), exhibited a significant step-wise increase in the US group. On Day 3, the US-3 group reached 1.34 ± 0.15 mg/mL (p < 0.05, compared with CK-3), and further accumulated to 1.69 ± 0.20 mg/mL on Day 7 (p < 0.01, compared with CK-7). This is highly consistent with the findings of the empirical study on stress-induced fermentation pathways in Komagataeibacter xylinus [17]. The authors demonstrated that sonication stress fundamentally alters cell behavior, redirecting metabolic activity to promote exopolysaccharide (bacterial cellulose) synthesis instead of cell growth, resulting in a 1.3-fold increase in yield under a brief 60-second sonication. This physical stress modulates the expression of specific genes related to cellulose production, inducing a random accumulation of fibers and a significant increase in intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

(D) Water-soluble polysaccharide content. *, **, and *** indicate significant differences (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively); ns indicates no significant difference. “CK” indicates the traditional static fermentation group, and “US” indicates the ultrasound-assisted fermentation group.

Moreover, total acidity in the CK group peaked at Day 2 and then underwent a steady decline, dropping to 7.27 ± 0.32 mg/mL by Day 7 (Fig. 2B). In stark contrast, the US group maintained a consistently higher and stable acid level from Day 3 through Day 7, showing significant differences compared to the CK group at Day 3 (p < 0.01), Day 5 (p < 0.01), and Day 7 (p < 0.05). Similarly, Thatyane et al. reported that sonication for 15 min significantly promoted the conversion of sucrose in Kombucha into gluconic acid, propionic acid, and isobutyric acid [18]. Both the CK and US groups exhibited fluctuating but overall upward trends in total phenol content (TPC), eventually reaching similar maximum values (∼2.85–2.91 mg/mL) at Day 7 (Fig. 2C). Collectively, ultrasound-assisted fermentation reshapes the carbon and acid metabolic pathways in Kombucha by accelerating yeast hexose assimilation, stress-inducing a metabolic redirection in Komagataeibacter toward exopolysaccharide synthesis, and maintaining elevated organic acid levels, without compromising the accumulation of bioactive polyphenols.

Nanostructural and molecular conformational dynamics of saliva-Kombucha complexes

To elucidate the physical mechanism underlying the restriction of aggregate growth, small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) was employed to resolve the nanoscale spatial conformation of the saliva-Kombucha primary complexes (Fig. 3A). The radius of gyration (Rg) extracted from the distance distribution function p(r) provides critical insight into the structural extension of these complexes in solution. Notably, the complexes from the ultrasound-treated groups consistently exhibited larger Rg values than their passive counterparts, peaking at 608.67 nm for US-7 (compared to 593.20 nm for CK-7). Importantly, the SAXS-derived Rg and the particle sizes measured by DLS represent different structural characteristics: Rg reflects the spatial distribution and extension of the scattering complexes, whereas DLS provides the hydrodynamic size distribution of the particle population. Therefore, the larger Rg observed in the US group does not necessarily indicate a larger macroscopic aggregate size. While seemingly paradoxical to the smaller macroscopic aggregate sizes observed in Fig. 1E, this spatial expansion at the nanoscale may be consistent with steric stabilization in ternary colloidal systems [19]. In the CK system, strong hydrophobic interactions may favor the formation of relatively compact protein–polyphenol assemblies, resulting in a lower Rg. These compact assemblies may subsequently undergo further aggregation and flocculation, contributing to the larger macroscopic aggregates observed by DLS. Conversely, in the US system, the ultrasonically enriched water-soluble polysaccharides (WSPs) may participate in the assembly and increase the hydration and spatial extension of the protein–polyphenol complexes. This structural organization may reduce the tendency of the primary complexes to undergo further aggregation, thereby contributing to the smaller particle sizes observed by DLS. This ternary assembly therefore provides a plausible explanation for the simultaneous increase in Rg and decrease in macroscopic aggregate size observed after ultrasound treatment. However, the present SAXS and DLS results provide indirect evidence for this interpretation, and the proposed role of WSPs in mediating steric stabilization and restricting secondary aggregation cannot be conclusively established from these measurements alone. Further structural and molecular investigations are required to directly verify this proposed mechanism.

This steric shielding and the attenuation of hydrophobic cross-linking are further corroborated by intrinsic molecular spectroscopy. Fluorescence spectroscopy (Fig. 3B) captures the microenvironmental alterations of the salivary proteins' hydrophobic pockets. The altered quenching behavior observed in the US groups, especially the weaker spectral quenching in US-7 than in CK-7, suggests that the hydrophobic interactions between catechins and salivary proteins were reduced. As reported by Ma et al. (2026)[3], enhanced fluorescence quenching is associated with enhanced interaction between polyphenols and salivary proteins, accompanied by greater changes in protein conformation. Therefore, the reduced fluorescence quenching observed here suggests that WSPs may reduce astringency by competitively binding to weaken the interaction between catechins and salivary proteins. Furthermore, FTIR spectra (Fig. 3C) confirm the molecular remodeling of the interaction network. Compared to the severe aggregation-induced peak shifts observed in the CK groups, the US groups display an Amide I and II profile (1700–1500 cm−1) that more closely resembles the pure salivary proteins. This indicates that the native conformational states of the salivary proteins are better preserved, successfully mitigating the severe polyphenol-induced structural rearrangements, in line with previous spectroscopic evidence that ultrasound processing effectively modulates protein secondary structures and conformational transitions to reduce precipitation in food systems [8]. Crucially, the enhanced absorption features in the 1000–1100 cm−1 region (C–O–C and C–O stretching) and the broadened 3200–3400 cm−1 band (O–H stretching) in the US groups provide direct spectroscopic evidence for the integration of polysaccharides into the primary complexes. The extensive hydrogen-bonding network introduced by the water-soluble polysaccharides (WSPs) effectively disrupts the propagation of hydrophobic polyphenol–protein cross-links, rendering the resulting ternary assemblies highly hydrated and colloidally stable against macroscopic precipitation.

Dynamic evolution and preservation of monomeric catechins

A major challenge in the processing of astringent beverages is that conventional mitigation strategies indiscriminately strip or degrade bioactive phenolic compounds, thereby sacrificing the beverage’s core health benefits. To ascertain whether the observed astringency mitigation under ultrasound (US) was driven by macromolecular steric shielding (as elucidated in Section 3.3) rather than the undesired chemical degradation of catechins, the dynamic profiles of key monomeric phenolics were quantitatively monitored (Fig. 4).

Throughout the 7-day fermentation, the contents of gallic acid (GA), ferulic acid (FA), epicatechin (EC), and epicatechin gallate (ECG) exhibited stable trajectories with no significant variance between the CK and US groups (Fig. 4A, 4B, 4E, and 4F). The highly active epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG, Fig. 4D) displayed a gradual, spontaneous decline from its initial concentration in both groups, a typical biochemical phenomenon during Kombucha fermentation resulting from the progressive enzymatic hydrolysis of ester bonds and auto-oxidation of trihydroxyl groups [20]. Moreover, the ultrasonic field did not accelerate the degradation or cleavage of these core catechins compared to the passive control. This indicates that the applied low-power acoustic cavitation possesses a non-destructive nature toward the phenolic benzene rings, effectively preserving the structural integrity of the catechins, while facilitating taste and flavor modulation in fermented beverage systems [9].

The most prominent divergence occurred in the evolution of epigallocatechin (EGC, Fig. 4C). While EGC levels remained relatively parallel during the initial 5 days, the CK group experienced a sharp, significant depletion at Day 7. In stark contrast, the US group maintained a significantly higher EGC concentration at Day 7 (p＜0.05), indicating greater EGC retention under ultrasound treatment. EGC, as a highly reactive non-esterified catechin, is particularly susceptible to oxidative transformation and polymerization into quinones and other oxidation products during prolonged aerobic fermentation. The greater retention of EGC in the US group may therefore be associated with reduced oxidative depletion during the late stage of fermentation. As empirically demonstrated by Li et al. (2021) [21], integrating highly reactive tea polyphenols into the micro-domains of polysaccharide assemblies provides a robust physical barrier. Therefore, the ultrasound-induced increase in WSPs may have facilitated interactions between WSPs and EGC, resulting in partial encapsulation of EGC within polysaccharide-associated structures. This may have reduced its susceptibility to oxidation and contributed to the higher EGC content observed at the end of fermentation. Overall, these results suggest that the decrease in astringency was mainly associated with polysaccharide-mediated changes in the colloidal system rather than extensive degradation of tea polyphenols, allowing the major phenolic compounds to be largely preserved.

Dynamic profiling of organic acid metabolites

As a secondary indicator of microbial metabolic flux, the temporal evolution of key organic acids was quantified (Fig. 5). Overall, the ultrasound-treated (US) groups exhibited a significantly intensified acidification profile during the late fermentation stage. Specifically, lactic and acetic acids in the US-7 group reached 5.72 ± 0.46 mg/mL (p＜0.01) and 10.34 ± 1.79 mg/mL (p＜0.05), respectively, significantly outperforming the passive control (Fig. 5B and 5C). This sustained accumulation stems from the dual, synergistic effects of acoustic processing: physical acoustic micro-streaming and cavitation enhance mass transfer to prevent local oxygen limitation for aerobic bacteria [6], while the concurrent sublethal acoustic stress acts as a physical elicitor that stimulates intracellular metabolic pathways. While glucuronic acid maintained a relatively stable profile (Fig. 5A), citric acid displayed a dramatic surge at Day 7 in the US group (p＜0.001), Fig. 5D). This elevated accumulation of a key tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate directly corroborates stress-induced metabolic adaptation under acoustic stimuli [22]. Collectively, the intensified organic acid synthesis confirmed the stimulatory effect of ultrasound on the microbial consortium, which provided a robust acidic background that may further synergize with polysaccharide shielding to mask astringent perceptions.

Metagenomic succession and identification of ultrasound-responsive keystone taxa

To understand the microbiological basis for the enhanced polysaccharide synthesis and subsequent astringency mitigation, the temporal succession of the Kombucha microbiome was profiled using metagenomic sequencing (Fig. 6, Fig. S1, Fig. S2).

(E) Circos plot showing the relative abundance of dominant microbial taxa at the species level. (F) Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) identifying biomarker taxa with an LDA score > 2.0 across different fermentation stages and treatments. *, **, and *** indicate significant differences (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively). “CK” indicates the traditional static fermentation group, and “US” indicates the ultrasound-assisted fermentation group sampled on days 3, 5, and 7.

A common concern with ultrasound-assisted fermentation is the potential lethal effect of acoustic cavitation on the microbial consortium. However, the alpha diversity metrics, including the Chao richness index (Fig. 6B), Shannon index (Fig. 6C), and Simpson index (Fig. 6D), exhibited no significant decrease in the ultrasound-treated (US) groups compared to the static control (CK) groups. This stability suggests that the applied low-power ultrasonic field acted as a sub-lethal physical stimulus, preserving the ecological richness and probiotic viability of the fermentation system. This is consistent with previous findings that controlled acoustic fields can function as a sub-lethal processing aid, sustaining microbial viability while actively steering metabolic pathways during kombucha fermentation [10]. While the overall richness remained stable, beta diversity analysis indicated a spatial restructuring of the community architecture. The Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) plot (Fig. 6A, stress value = 0.016) displayed clear temporal successions for both conditions. The evolutionary trajectory of the US microbiome diverged from that of the CK group, particularly at Day 7. This separation implies that prolonged acoustic stress selectively guided the microbial community toward a distinct functional configuration.

To identify the specific taxa driving this divergence, community composition (Fig. 6E) and Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe, Fig. 6F) were evaluated. The Circos plot indicated that acetic acid bacteria, predominantly the Komagataeibacter genus, were the most abundant microorganisms throughout the fermentation process. Furthermore, the LEfSe analysis identified biomarker taxa enriched in the late stage of ultrasound fermentation. At Day 7, the US group (D7_US) was characterized by the significant enrichment of specific species, most notably Komagataeibacter intermedius, Komagataeibacter oboediens, and Komagataeibacter xylinus (LDA scores > 3.0). This targeted enrichment aligns with the observed polysaccharide accumulation and astringency mitigation. Species within the Komagataeibacter genus are well-documented producers of extracellular polysaccharides and bacterial cellulose in Kombucha ecosystems [23]. Importantly, these specific strains exhibit profound metabolic plasticity in response to external environmental interventions. An empirical study by Absharina et al. (2026) [24] demonstrated that altering the fermentation microenvironment through agro-derived nitrogen substitution dynamically redirected the metabolic flux of K. xylinus, significantly enhancing both the volumetric yield and the structural robustness of its exopolysaccharide network. Given this established capacity for metabolic adaptation, the significant enrichment of K. intermedius and related taxa in the D7_US group supports the hypothesis that the applied ultrasonic field functioned as a potent physical elicitor. This acoustic stimulus likely activated these responsive keystone strains to overproduce the steric-shielding polysaccharides proposed in earlier sections to physically restrict polyphenol-protein aggregation.

Temporal succession dynamics of dominant Komagataeibacter species

To detail the community shifts across fermentation stages, the relative abundances of the top eight Komagataeibacter species were tracked (Fig. 7). At Day 5, the ultrasound (US) group showed a distinct pattern: stress-tolerant taxa like K. saccharivorans (Fig. 7A) maintained higher relative abundances under ultrasound. Conversely, major exopolysaccharide (EPS) producers—such as K. oboediens (Fig. 7C), K. europaeus (Fig. 7D), K. intermedius (Fig. 7E), and K. xylinus (Fig. 7F)—were temporarily suppressed by the acoustic stress. By Day 7, these core EPS-producing species recovered in the US group, reaching relative abundances comparable to, but not significantly higher than, those in the static control (CK).

This abundance trend directly contrasts with the physicochemical results from Section 3.1, where the US treatment led to a substantial increase in soluble polysaccharides by Day 7. Since the physical accumulation of polysaccharides did not align with a proportional expansion of the EPS-producing bacterial population, the acoustic field likely acted at the metabolic level rather than the ecological level. In acetic acid bacteria, environmental stressors often trigger a shift in carbon flux from cellular replication toward the synthesis of protective extracellular matrices [25]. Therefore, the elevated polysaccharide yield in the US group likely resulted from an upregulated specific metabolic efficiency of these keystone strains, rather than a simple increase in their numbers. This decoupling highlights the need to quantify the per-cell metabolic efficiency of K. intermedius, which is explored in the subsequent phase-space and multivariate analyses to clarify its role in astringency mitigation.

Spatiotemporal succession of sensory phenotypes and functional decoupling of dominant species

Traditional Kombucha fermentation typically suffers from a progressive increase in perceived astringency over time, driven by the continuous extraction and accumulation of low-molecular-weight polyphenols. As illustrated by the phenotypic evolution trajectory (Fig. 8A), the control group (CK) exhibited a steady increase in the Comprehensive Astringency Index (CAI), climbing from 0.56 at Day 3 to 1.77 at Day 7. Conversely, the ultrasound-assisted group (US) underwent a radical phenotypic divergence, with the CAI dropping precipitously to −2.40 by Day 7. Although K. intermedius constituted a modest fraction of the total community biomass (6.3% of total relative abundance), it disproportionately monopolized the target functional niche for water-soluble polysaccharide biosynthesis, accounting for 20.7% of the total pathway transcripts (Fig. 8B). In contrast, the most abundant species, Komagataeibacter saccharivorans (15.4% abundance), only contributed 14.0% to the target polysaccharide synthesis pathway. This severe functional-taxonomic decoupling highlights the necessity of shifting the research focus from raw abundance to cell-specific metabolic contributions [26].

Multivariate PLS-R modeling, directed driver attribution, and multi-omics causal cascade

An efficiency-driven PLS-R model constructed using the specific metabolic efficiencies (SMEs) of dominant taxa predicted sensory astringency under cross-validation (R = 0.402, p = 0.098; Fig. 9A). While the predictive power reflects the inherent biological noise of multi-species fermentation, the model's primary utility lies in dimensionality reduction and feature prioritization. VIP profiling (Fig. 9B) identified the polysaccharide metabolism efficiency of Kozakia baliensis (VIP≈1.95, r = 0.54) and Novacetimonas pomaceti (VIP≈1.90, r = 0.37) as the primary astringency-enhancing features, suggesting their active pathways likely contribute to astringency development. Conversely, Komagataeibacter intermedius emerged as the leading astringency mitigator, with K. nataicola and K. oboediens functioning as auxiliary mitigators. The temporal dynamics of K. intermedius SME revealed its functional activation, showing an upward trend Day 3 under ultrasonic processing compared to the static control (p = 0.096, Fig. 9D). This rapid activation suggested that sublethal physical stress redirected carbon flux away from cell replication toward protective extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) synthesis [27], [28]. As conceptually illustrated in the synchronization dynamics (Fig. 9C), this metabolic trigger (red curve) peaked at Day 3, preceding intermediate polysaccharide accumulation at Day 5 (green curve) and culminating in final sensory mitigation at Day 7 (blue curve). This chronological delay explained why the immediate synchronous correlation between metabolic efficiency and polysaccharide content was slightly negative (r = -0.09, p = 0.077; Fig. 9E). This temporal phase shift was resolved by time-lagged cross-correlation pairing early-phase specific efficiency (T) with late-phase polysaccharide yield (T + 2), which showed a strong negative trend (r = -0.714, p = 0.11). These late-stage poly molecules form ternary complexes that attenuate salivary protein aggregation through steric hindrance [2], [7]. The global correlation matrix (Fig. 9E) integrated this regulatory cascade, illustrating a rate-yield trade-off between the physical abundance (Hero Biomass) of K. intermedius and its specific metabolic efficiency (r = -0.89, p = 0.031; p = 0.957 for biomass vs. CAI). Under ultrasonic stress, cellular resources appeared to be redistributed from binary fission toward exopolysaccharide biosynthesis [22]. Crucially, the resulting polysaccharide accumulation showed strong time-adjusted negative correlations with both Astringency Intensity and the Comprehensive Sensory Index. This indicated that physical de-astringency was not mainly attributable to biomass expansion, but rather by the elevated availability of specific polysaccharides synthesized by highly efficient K. intermedius cells, which physically disrupt polyphenol–protein cross-linking to preserve oral lubrication [29], [30].

(B) Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores of dominant taxonomic metabolic efficiencies, with red and blue bars representing positive (enhancing astringency) and negative (mitigating astringency) correlations with CAI, respectively. Conclusion

In summary, this study proposes an ultrasound-assisted fermentation strategy (240 W, 30% duty cycle) for the non-destructive mitigation of astringency in Kombucha. Unlike conventional fining processes, this method significantly alleviates oral dryness and roughness by Day 7 while maintaining the stable concentration and structural integrity of core catechins. Multi-scale characterizations indicate that this sensory improvement is primarily associated with a significant accumulation of water-soluble polysaccharides. These polysaccharides are proposed to act as a macromolecular steric shield that expands the nanoscale conformation of polyphenol-salivary protein complexes, thereby restricting their macroscopic precipitation. Furthermore, machine learning-based driver attribution and temporal causal modeling suggest that this process involves a distinct functional-taxonomic decoupling mechanism. Rather than inducing the population expansion of the keystone species K. intermedius, sublethal acoustic cavitation appears to upregulate its specific metabolic efficiency at the early fermentation stage, which likely contributes to the subsequent overproduction of shielding polysaccharides. While this work outlines a data-driven “microbiome-metabolite-structure-sensory” cascade, these relationships remain associative at the systems level, and the sampling resolution primarily captured macroscopic fermentation milestones. Future investigations should employ higher-resolution temporal sampling coupled with metatranscriptomics and targeted pure-culture models to mechanistically validate the intracellular genetic regulation of K. intermedius under acoustic stress. Ultimately, this process intensification approach provides a promising theoretical framework for optimizing the sensory and nutritional profiles of functional fermented foods.
