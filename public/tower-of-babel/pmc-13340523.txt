A novel JAK1 variant in chronic eosinophilic leukaemia with response to benralizumab
Authors: Isabel C. Vallecillo‐Viejo, Michael D. Claiborne, Charles F. Anderson, Samantha Shafer, Alan Saven, Bryce A. Seifert, Morgan Similuk, Magdalena Walkiewicz, Irina Maric, Pia R. Crespo, Lori Broderick, Andrew A. White, Amy D. Klion
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) is defined as hypereosinophilia (absolute eosinophil count [AEC] >1.5 × 109/L or marked tissue eosinophilia) on two examinations at least 1 month apart with eosinophil‐associated clinical manifestations. 1 Genetic forms of HES involve variants in genes regulating eosinophil proliferation and survival, including JAK2, STAT3 and STAT5B. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 More recently, JAK1 variants have been shown to drive the disease process, 6 consistent with the ability of both Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) to activate signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) through phosphorylation. Historically, treatment for HES was limited to corticosteroids, cytotoxic and immunosuppressive therapies. The approval of imatinib for HES in 2001 altered the outcomes for patients presenting with myeloid/lymphoid neoplasms with eosinophilia and tyrosine kinase gene fusions (MLN‐eo‐TK). However, it was not until the approval of mepolizumab in 2020 that a safer treatment option for other clinical subtypes of HES became available.

Despite these advances in HES therapy, patients with primary myeloid subtypes of HES, including imatinib‐insensitive MLN‐eo‐TK and chronic eosinophilic leukaemia (CEL), remain challenging to treat. Although JAK inhibitors have been shown to be effective in some cases of clonal eosinophilia associated with genetic abnormalities involving the JAK–STAT pathway, 7 , 8 toxicities associated with long‐term ruxolitinib use remain a concern. Here, we report the case of a patient found to have CEL secondary to a somatic JAK1 variant successfully treated with benralizumab, an afucosylated monoclonal antibody to interleukin‐5 (IL‐5) receptor α (IL‐5RA) that depletes eosinophils through antibody‐dependent cell cytotoxicity.

The patient was evaluated at the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and research samples were obtained on either an IRB‐approved research protocol designed to study eosinophilia (NCT00001406) or healthy controls (NCT00090662). Written informed consent was obtained. DNA from peripheral blood and skin was analysed by Genomic Testing Cooperative (GTC). In addition, DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leucocytes for whole genome sequencing (WGS) data at the Human Genome Sequencing Center Clinical Lab using established library preparation, sequencing methods and analysis. 9 Phospho‐STAT5 was assessed in whole blood or isolated eosinophils by flow cytometry, and phospho‐STAT1 was evaluated in HEK293T cells expressing wild‐type or mutant constructs (see Supporting Information: Methods).

The patient is a 28‐year‐old female found to have peripheral eosinophilia on routine bloodwork at 13 years of age, when she presented to the ED with concerns of difficulty breathing while swimming and was diagnosed with paradoxical vocal fold motion. Shortly thereafter, she started experiencing chronic urticaria and was treated with second‐generation antihistamines and topical steroids without improvement. After an unrevealing workup, including Strongyloidiasis serology, serum tryptase and vitamin B12 levels, bone marrow aspirate and biopsy with clonality studies and myeloid FISH (PDGFRA, PDGFRB, FGFR1, JAK2, BCR::ABL1) and endoscopy/colonoscopy, she was referred to allergy/immunology. She remained symptomatic with an AEC of approximately 9.0 × 109/L, prompting a diagnosis of HES. She was treated with three doses of mepolizumab (300 mg subcutaeously [SC] monthly) and a 5‐day prednisone burst without response. A GTC Hematology Profile Plus next‐generation sequencing (NGS) on peripheral blood detected a novel insertion/deletion variant in the pseudokinase domain of JAK1 (JAK1 NM c.1886_1892delinsC) with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 30.1% that was predicted to be pathogenic, as well as a known pathogenic variant in NBN (c.156_157del; VAF 38.14%). One month prior to evaluation at NIH, she developed an unprovoked deep venous thrombosis of the right subclavian vein. She was diagnosed with thoracic outlet syndrome, started on apixaban for anticoagulation and underwent an uneventful first rib resection.

Evaluation at the NIH revealed persistent peripheral hypereosinophilia with AEC 6.36 × 109/L. Flow cytometry and molecular pathology showed no evidence of an aberrant or clonal lymphocyte population. Serum vitamin B12 and tryptase levels were normal. Molecular testing for BCR::ABL1, JAK2 V617F and FIP1L1::PDGFRA was negative. Inflammatory markers (eryhtrocyte sedimentation rate [ESR], C‐reactive protein [CRP]) were unremarkable. Echocardiogram (EKG), pulmonary function testing [PFT] and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) were normal. computed tomography [CT] scan of chest, abdomen and pelvis demonstrated nonspecific subcentimetre pulmonary nodules but was otherwise without specific findings. A STAT phosphorylation assay on whole blood showed markedly increased STAT5 phosphorylation in eosinophils from the patient (ΔgMFI =420) compared to that in eosinophils from healthy donors (n = 10; ΔgMFI range: 0–100). This was completely abrogated by ruxolitinib treatment, consistent with a JAK1 gain‐of‐function variant (Figure 1).

Whole genome sequencing of peripheral blood confirmed the presence of the JAK1 variant (VAF 20%) and the heterozygous NBN variant (VAF 30.7%) in peripheral blood and identified a pathogenic variant in F5 (Table S1). To assess the origin of the JAK1 variant, NGS was performed on DNA extracted from a formalin‐fixed skin biopsy. The variant was detected with a VAF of 0.39%, well below that expected for a heterozygous germline variant. To determine whether this variant directly enhances JAK–STAT signalling, STAT1 phosphorylation was assessed following IFN‐α stimulation in HEK293T cells expressing wild‐type or mutant constructs. Following IFN‐α stimulation, cells expressing JAK1 p.Arg629Ile631delinsThr demonstrated significantly increased pSTAT1 MFI and pSTAT1/total STAT1 ratio compared with wild‐type (WT) controls, while total STAT1 levels remained unchanged across conditions (Figure S1). In the absence of stimulation, no increase in baseline STAT1 phosphorylation was observed, indicating that the variant does not confer constitutive activation but instead enhances cytokine‐induced signalling. Treatment with ruxolitinib reduced pSTAT1 levels and normalized the phosphorylation ratio, supporting JAK‐dependent, stimulation‐dependent hyperactivation associated with this variant.

Prompt intervention was recommended due to the association between pathogenic variants (including those involving JAK), HES and an increased risk of thrombotic events. 10 Although ruxolitinib was recommended, a trial of benralizumab 30 mg SC was initiated at the patient's request due to the favourable safety profile. Despite data suggesting a lack of efficacy in JAK2 mutation‐associated HES, 11 she experienced a profound reduction in her eosinophil and basophil counts within the first month of benralizumab treatment (Figure 2) as well as a reduction in the frequency and severity of chronic urticaria, which has been sustained for more than 10 months.

This is the first reported case of benralizumab‐responsive CEL secondary to JAK1. Benralizumab, an afucosylated monoclonal antibody to IL5RA that depletes eosinophils, basophils and their precursors through enhanced antibody‐dependent cell cytotoxicity, has shown safety and efficacy in reducing eosinophils and improving clinical manifestations in clinical trials in HES. 11 , 12 Although eosinophilia was unchanged in the two patients with JAK2 variants enrolled on the phase 2 trial, benralizumab induced NK‐mediated killing of the eosinophils from these patients in vitro, implicating factors other than the JAK2 variant (e.g. the cytokine milieu) in the lack of clinical efficacy in these patients. The current case suggests that this may be different in JAK1‐driven disease.

The growing use of biologics for HES, especially in women of childbearing age, underscores the need to assess their safety in pregnancy. The patient described expressed interest in starting a family. Although benralizumab has been used successfully during pregnancy for the treatment of hypereosinophilia in a single case report, 13 a recent pharmacovigilance study suggested the increased risk of delivery complications including preterm birth yet lower frequencies of spontaneous fetal death and spontaneous abortion with its use compared to other biologics, including mepolizumab, omalizumab and dupilumab. 14 Conversely, data on ruxolitinib in pregnancy are limited, though one case reported successful first‐trimester use for myelofibrosis. 15

This patient's initial negative workup led to the diagnosis of idiopathic HES. Her lack of response to mepolizumab and steroids, however, prompted genetic evaluation which revealed a JAK1 variant that otherwise would have been missed. Similar, though not identical, activating JAK1 variants have been reported previously. 6 , 8 As noted by Groh et al., these variants cluster in regulatory regions of JAK1 and lead to constitutive STAT5 activation, consistent with the signalling pattern observed in our patient. The present case is notable for a markedly earlier age of onset than previously described patients, highlighting the importance of genetic evaluation looking for variants in JAK–STAT signalling in patients with persistent, unexplained hypereosinophilia irrespective of age. Identification of JAK variants carries therapeutic implications, particularly given the availability of JAK inhibitors. In our patient, sustained disease control was achieved with benralizumab, suggesting that depletion of IL‐5Rα–expressing eosinophils via antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity may represent an effective alternative strategy even in the context of upstream JAK–STAT activation.
