Introduction

In many cases in combinatorial geometry, counting questions involving distances, slopes, collinearity, etc., can be reformulated as analogous counting questions involving grid points lying on certain algebraic varieties. A unified study of such problems began with a question of Elekes
[
1
]
about expansion of bivariate real polynomials
f(x,y)
.
Specifically, he asked: For a bivariate polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and given finite sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
, how small can be the image set

f(A,B)=\{f(a,b)\mid a\in A,b\in B\}.

Elekes conjectured that the image of
f
on an
n\times n
Cartesian product must be of cardinality superlinear in
n
, unless
f
has a very concrete
special form
. This was confirmed in 2000 by Elekes and Rónyai
[
2
]
, who proved the following dichotomy: Either
f
is one of the forms

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=h(p(x)+q(y))\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=h(p(x)q(y)),

(1)

for some univariate real polynomials
p,q,h
, or, otherwise, for every finite
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
, each of size
n
, we have

|f(A,B)|=\omega(n).

(2)

In
[
4
]
, Raz, Sharir and Solymosi introduced a new proof of the Elekes–Rónyai theorem, which also yields improved bounds on the expansion of
f
.
Roughly speaking, for a given
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and two finite sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
, they bounded the number of quadruples
((a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}))\in(A\times B)^{2}
satisfying
f(a,b)=f(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
by reducing it to a point-curve incidence problem in the plane. Concretely, incidences between the point set
A\times A
and the family of curves

\{\gamma_{b,b^{\prime}}\mid(b,b^{\prime})\in B\times B\},

where
\gamma_{b,b^{\prime}}
is given by the equation

f(x,b)=f(y,b^{\prime}).

If these curves are distinct, known incidence bounds can be applied to obtain the desired estimate. However, it may happen that many of the curves coincide, in which case the incidence bound breaks down. Raz, Sharir, and Solymosi showed that this “failure” occurs if and only if

f
has a special form, namely one of the forms described in (
1
).
In the non-special case, their analysis yields the lower bound
\Omega(n^{4/3})

Solymosi and Zahl
[
10
]
recently improved the expansion bound for
f
in the non-special case, establishing the lower bound
\Omega(n^{3/2})
. Their argument builds on the framework of
[
4
]
, but applies the incidence bound to carefully selected subsets of points and curves. In particular, they restrict to points
(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A
with
a
and
a^{\prime}
sufficiently close, and, similarly, to curves
\gamma_{b,b^{\prime}}
with parameters
(b,b^{\prime})\in B\times B
with
b
and
b^{\prime}
sufficiently close. This refinement, referred to as the
proximity method
,
overcomes a loss incurred in the earlier argument from an application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

A
3
-variate analogue of the Elekes–Rónyai theorem was studied in Raz, Sharir, and De Zeeuw
[
6
]
. The case of

k\geq 4
variables is studied by Raz and Shem Tov in
[
7
]
.

Theorem 1

(
[
6
,
7
]
).

Let
k\geq 3
and assume that
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
depends non-trivially on each of its varaiables. Then one of the following holds:

(i) For every finite
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}
, with
|A_{i}|=n
, for
i=1,\ldots,k
, one has

|f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})|=\Omega(n^{3/2}),

where the constant of proportionality depends only on
\deg(f)
and on
k
.

(ii)
f
is of one of the special forms

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=h(p_{1}(x_{1})+\cdots+p_{k}(x_{k}))

(3)

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=h(p_{1}(x_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{k}(x_{k}))

for some univariate real polynomials
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k},h
.

Similar to the bivariate case, the analysis for
k=3
also reduces to a point-curve incidence problem in the plane.
Specifically, for a trivariate polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y,z]
and finite sets
A,B,C\subset~\mathbb{R}
, define
D:=f(A,B,C)
and consider incidences between the point set
A\times D
and the family of curves

\{\gamma_{b,c}\mid(b,c)\in B\times C\},

where
\gamma_{b,c}
is given by the equation

w=f(x,b,c).

In some respects, the trivariate case is simpler than the bivariate one. Here, the structure of
f
is more directly reflected in properties of the variety

w=f(x,y,z)
, whereas in the bivariate case one must analyze the more intricate variety
f(x,y)=f(z,w)
. Moreover, in the trivariate setting the argument avoids
an application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, therefore eliminating the loss in the estimate that arises in the bivariate case.

As mentioned above, the proximity method of Solymosi and Zahl
[
10
]
improves the bivariate bound precisely by overcoming this loss from the Cauchy–Schwarz step. For this reason, it is not immediately clear how their method could extend to the trivariate case.

In this paper, we show how proximity can in fact be used to obtain a stronger expansion bound for trivariate polynomials. In particular, we establish an improved bound for a concrete family of trivariate polynomials. Namely, we prove the following main result.

Theorem 2
.

Let

f(x,y,z)=(x-y)^{2}+(\varphi(x)-z)^{2},

where
\varphi(x)
is a univariate real polynomial of degree at least
3
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
and any finite sets
A,B,C\subset\mathbb{R}
, each of size
n
, we have

|f(A,B,C)|=\Omega(n^{5/3-\varepsilon}),

where the constant of proportionality depends on
\deg\varphi
and on
\varepsilon
.

This improves upon the previous bound of
\Omega(n^{3/2})
,
which follows from Theorem
1
.
To the best of our knowledge, prior to this work no trivariate polynomial was known to have expansion exceeding
\Omega(n^{3/2})
.

We remark that our approach is more general
and extends to other trivariate polynomials.
A natural direction for future research is to determine the precise subfamily of polynomials
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}[x,y,z]
for which the method yields the improved lower bound
\Omega(n^{5/3-\varepsilon})
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Point-curve incidences in the plane

For a finite set of points
{\cal P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a finite set of planar curves
{\cal C}
, we let
I({\cal P},{\cal C})
denote the set of point-curve incidences; that is

I({\cal P},{\cal C})=\{(p,\gamma)\in{\cal P}\times{\cal C}\mid p\in\gamma\}.

The classical Szemerédi–Trotter theorem
[
11
]
asserts that, for the special case where
{\cal C}
is a set of lines, and putting
m:=|{\cal P}|
and
n:=|{\cal C}|
, one has

\left|I({\cal P},{\cal C})\right|=O\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right).

Since the Szemerédi–Trotter paper, numerous alternative proofs and related problems have been studied. For our result, we require an extension of Szemerédi–Trotter to point-curve incidence problems, established by Sharir and Zahl
[
9
]
, in which the curves are algebraic and form an

s
-dimensional family.
We now recall the relevant definitions from their work.

A bivariate polynomial
h\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
of degree at most
D
is a linear combination of the form

h(x,y)=\sum_{0\leq i+j\leq D}c_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}.

Note that the number of monomials
x^{i}y^{j}
such that
0\leq i+j\leq D
is
\binom{D+2}{2}
.
In this sense, every point
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
(other than the all-zero vector) can be associated with a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, given by the zeroset of the bivariate polynomial whose coefficients are the entries of
\vec{c}
.
If
\lambda\neq 0
, then
f
and
\lambda f
have the same zero-set.
Thus, the set
of algebraic curves that can be defined by a polynomial of degree at most
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can
be identified with the points in the projective space

{\bf P}\mathbb{R}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
.

Define an
s
-dimensional family of plane curves of degree at most
D
to be an algebraic variety
F\subset{\bf P}\mathbf{\mathbb{R}}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
such that
\dim(F)=s
. We will call the degree of the variety
F
the
complexity of the family
.

They then proved the following incidence bound:

Theorem 3

(
Sharir–Zahl
[
9
]
).

Let
\Gamma
be a set of
n
algebraic plane curves that belong to an
s
-dimensional family of curves of degree at most
D
of constant complexity at most
K
, no two of which
share a common irreducible component.
Let
P
be a set of
m
points in the plane. Then
for any
\varepsilon>0
, the number of incidences
|I(P,\Gamma)|
between the points of
P
and the curves
of
\Gamma
satisfies

|I(P,\Gamma)|=O_{\varepsilon}\left(m^{\frac{2s}{5s-4}}n^{\frac{5s-6}{5s-4}+\varepsilon}\right)+O\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right),

where the constant of proportionality depends on
s
,
K
,
D
, and in the first term also on
\varepsilon
.

Given a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a transformation
T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we say that
T

S

T(S)=S
.
We say that a transformation

The following lemma is proved by Pach and De Zeeuw

Lemma 4

(
Pach and De Zeeuw
[
3

).

An irreducible plane algebraic curve of degree
d
has at most
4d
symmetries, unless it is a line or a circle.

, one has

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j,k)\in T_{d}^{\prime}}\left|\binom{G_{d}\cap(A_{i}\times B_{j}\times C_{k})}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s-1}{2}\sum_{(i,j,k)\in T_{d}^{\prime}}|G_{d}\cap(A_{i}\times B_{j}\times C_{k})|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s-1}{4}|G_{d}|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s}{5}|G_{d}|,

where the last inequality assumes
s\geq 5
.
Thus we get

\displaystyle|Q|

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\in D}\sum_{(i,j,k)\in[t]^{3}}\left|\binom{G_{d}\cap(A_{i}\times B_{j}\times C_{k})}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{d\in D^{\prime}}\sum_{(i,j,k)\in T_{d}^{\prime}}\left|\binom{G_{d}\cap(A_{i}\times B_{j}\times C_{k})}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s}{5}\sum_{d\in D^{\prime}}|G_{d}|

\displaystyle\geq(9/50)sn^{3},

where the last inequality uses (
7
).
This completes the proof of Proposition
6
.
∎

Next we prove an upper bound on
|Q|
.

Proposition 7
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, we have

|Q|\leq O_{\varepsilon}\left((s^{2}n|D|)^{9/8+\varepsilon}\right)+4\deg(\varphi)n^{3}.

Proof.

We reduce the problem of upper bounding
Q
to a point-curve incidence problem in the plane.
For this, we associate with each
((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in(B\times C)^{2}
the planar curve (with coordinates
(x,x^{\prime})
) given by the equation

f(x,b,c)=f(x^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}).

Note that distinct choices of
((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))
might give rise to the same curve, or to distinct curves that share an irreducible component.
Let

\Gamma:=\{\text{$\gamma\subset\gamma_{b,c,b^{\prime},c^{\prime}}$ is irreducible }\mid\text{$((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in(B\times C)^{2}$, $b\sim b^{\prime}$, $c\sim c^{\prime}$}\}.

Consider the set of parameters associated with the curves, that is, the set

\hat{\Gamma}:=\{((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in(B\times C)^{2}\mid\;b\sim b^{\prime},\;c\sim c^{\prime}\}.

For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
we define

m(\gamma)=\{((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in\hat{\Gamma}\mid\gamma\subset\gamma_{b,c,b^{\prime},c^{\prime}}\}

We show that there exists a finite subset
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
such that
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}
we have
|m(\gamma)|\leq 4
.
Indeed, let
\gamma\in\Gamma
and suppose that
|m(\gamma)|\geq 5
.
So there are three pairs
p_{i}:=(b_{i},c_{i})
,
p_{i}^{\prime}:=(b_{i}^{\prime},c_{i}^{\prime})
, with
(p_{i},p_{i}^{\prime})\in m(\gamma)
for
i=1,2,3
, such that without loss of generality
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
are distinct.

Let
{\bf p}=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
and
{\bf p}^{\prime}=(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},p_{3}^{\prime})
and recall the definition of
\Sigma_{{\bf p},{\bf p}^{\prime}}
from Section
2.3
.
By definition of
m(\gamma)
, this means that for every
(x,x^{\prime})\in\gamma
one has

((x,\varphi(x)),(x^{\prime},\varphi(x^{\prime}))\in\Sigma_{{\bf p},{\bf p}^{\prime}}.

By Lemma
5
, either
{\bf p},{\bf p}^{\prime}
are congruent, or
\sigma_{{\bf p},{\bf p}^{\prime}}
is a conic section. In the latter case this implies that
\{(t,\varphi(t))\mid t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a conic section, which is a contradiction because
\deg(\varphi)\geq 3
and this curve is irreducible.
Thus necessarily
{\bf p}
and
{\bf p}^{\prime}
are congruent.
Thus, there exists an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
R
, such that

(x^{\prime},\varphi(x^{\prime}))=R(x,\varphi(x)),\text{ for every $(x,x^{\prime})\in\gamma$.}

Since
\gamma
is infinite, this implies that
R
maps
(t,\varphi(t))
to itself.
By Lemma
4
, we have that
R
is one of at most
4\deg(\varphi)
possible isometries, which in turn determines the corresponding curve
\gamma
up to at most
4\deg(\varphi)
possibilities. We conclude that

|\Gamma_{0}|\leq 4\deg(\varphi)

and

for every

\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}
we have
|m(\gamma)|\leq 4
.

(9)

Next, let

\hat{\Gamma}_{0}:=\{((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in\hat{\Gamma}\mid\text{there exists $\gamma\in\Gamma_{0}$ such that $\gamma\subset\gamma_{b,c,b^{\prime},c^{\prime}}$}\}.

We claim that

|\{((a,b,c),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in Q\mid((b,c),(b^{\prime},c^{\prime}))\in\hat{\Gamma}_{0}\}|\leq 4\deg(\varphi)n^{3}.

(10)

Indeed, by what has just been argued, there exists an isometry
R
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, one of at most
4\deg(\varphi)
possibilities, such that

(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})=R(b,c)
and
(a^{\prime},\varphi(a^{\prime}))=R(a,\varphi(a))
. In other words,
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
is determined by
(a,b,c)
, up to at most
4\deg(\varphi)
possibilities.
Thus (
10
) follows.

Finally, let

P=\{(a,a^{\prime})\in A\times A\mid a\sim a^{\prime}\},

and apply Theorem
3
to bound to
|I(P,\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0})|
.
Note that

|P|=\Theta\left(\binom{n/t}{2}t\right)=\Theta(n^{2}/t)=\Theta(sn^{1/2}|D|^{1/2})

and

\displaystyle|\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0}|\leq\deg(f)|\hat{\Gamma}|

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\binom{n/t}{2}\binom{n/t}{2}t^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\Theta(n^{4}/t^{2})

\displaystyle=\Theta(s^{2}n|D|).

We get

\displaystyle|Q|

\displaystyle\leq 4|I(P,\Gamma\setminus\Gamma_{0})|+4\deg(\varphi)n^{3},

\displaystyle=O_{\varepsilon}\left((sn^{1/2}|D|^{1/2})^{9/4+2\varepsilon}\right)+4\deg(\varphi)n^{3}.

This proves Proposition
7
.
∎

Finally, combining the inequalities from Proposition
6
and Proposition
7
, we conclude that

\displaystyle sn^{3}\leq|Q|

\displaystyle=O_{\varepsilon}\left((n|D|)^{9/8+\varepsilon}\right)+4\deg(\varphi)n^{3}.

Choosing
s>8\deg(\varphi)
and rearranging, we get

|D|=\Omega_{\varepsilon}(n^{5/3-\varepsilon^{\prime}}).

This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
\square
