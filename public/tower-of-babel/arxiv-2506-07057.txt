Uncovering the topology of an infinite-server queueing network from population data
Hritika Gupta, Michel Mandjes, Liron Ravner and Jiesen Wang

Abstract
. This paper studies statistical inference in a network of infinite-server queues, with the aim of estimating the underlying parameters (routing matrix, arrival rates, parameters pertaining to the service times) using observations of the network population vector at Poisson time points. We propose a method-of-moments estimator and establish its consistency. The method relies on deriving the covariance structure of different nodes at different sampling epochs. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the method yields accurate estimates, even in settings with a large number of parameters. Two model variants are considered: one that assumes a known parametric form for the service-time distributions, and a model-free version that does not require such assumptions. Keywords. 1.
Introduction

In operations research, when analyzing a stochastic system, it is typically assumed that the underlying model is known and expressed in terms of one or more stochastic processes, each characterized by a set of parameters. The goal is then to evaluate specific performance metrics, which inform system design; for example, being able to compute these metrics allows for optimizing an objective function subject to constraints. However, in practice, model parameters are not known a priori and must be inferred from data. The simplest scenario occurs when the stochastic processes are directly observed, making inference a conventional statistical task. In many cases, though, these processes are not observed directly (see
[
15
]
), complicating the inference process significantly.

A prototypical example of inference based on indirect observations, in the context of operations research, is as follows. Suppose one observes the workload of a storage system at specific points in time (for instance equidistantly, or at Poisson times). Given these workload observations, can the parameters of the system’s input process be estimated? This type of problems is often referred to as
inverse problems
: observing an endogenous
process, we wish to estimate parameters of the corresponding exogenous process.
Even if the observed workload process is assumed to be in stationarity, this class of inference problems is highly challenging due to the intricate dependence structure of the observed workload values. Concretely, only in very special cases it is possible to write down the likelihood pertaining to the observed data. A survey on parameter inference for queueing systems can be found in
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we consider parameter inference in a network of
n
infinite-server queues. In our setup customers arrive at the network’s nodes, undergo service, are routed according to a routing matrix, and eventually leave the network. Importantly, all nodes are of the infinite-server type
[
17
, §10.3]
, meaning that customers can be served in parallel, i.e., they do not experience any waiting time. We throughout assume that the service-time distributions are either known or follow a specified parametric form. The objective of this paper is to develop an algorithm for uncovering the network structure using observations of the queue length vector only — specifically, to estimate the model’s arrival rates and routing matrix. A secondary goal is to estimate the parameters pertaining to the service times. Finally, we aim to address the setting of noisy observations, in which each customer present at an observation time is detected with a certain (unknown) probability.

The main contribution of this work concerns a procedure that is capable of estimating all model parameters, including the ‘observation probabilities’. In our setup, the data available is the
n
-dimensional vector of queue lengths observed at Poisson times (with the sampling rate being known). Our approach is based on the
method of moments
. Under the mild assumption that we observe the network in stationarity, we utilize explicit expressions for the expected values of the queue lengths and the covariances between any pair of queue lengths at two subsequent observation times (i.e., the inter-observation times have an exponential distribution). This procedure results in
n^{2}+n
moment equations:
n
for the mean queue-lengths at each station and
n^{2}
for the cross-moments corresponding to all pairs of queues observed at two consecutive observations.

In case the observation probabilities and the service-time distributions are known, then
the number of moment equations matches the number of unknown parameters (namely: the
n^{2}
entries of the routing matrix and the
n
external arrival rates).
In case each of the service-time distributions is characterized by a single parameter, we have to identify
n
additional parameters. In this case, and in the case that the observation probabilities are not known, the above set of moment equations alone would be insufficient. However, this issue can be resolved by also including the covariances between any pair of queue-lengths observed at times
two
steps apart (i.e., in which the inter-observation times have an Erlang-2 distribution). We in addition present a ‘model-free approach’, i.e., an approach which has the advantage that one does not need to know the parametric class of the service-time distributions.

Our main result states that the resulting estimator is consistent: we establish that the estimator in probability converges to the true value of the parameter as the number of observations grows large. The proofs use tools presented in
[
16
]
; most notably, it is shown that the moments (as being used in the moment equations) are estimated in a consistent manner, and that this consistency is inherited by our parameter estimator. Remarkably, the method can also recover the
directed
structure of distinct networks that share the same stationary distribution. This is possible because, for each pair
(i_{1},i_{2})
of stations, it utilizes cross-moments between observation times, which are not necessarily symmetric in
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
.

We demonstrate the efficacy of our procedure through a series of experiments, which show that even for models with a substantial number of parameters, our method accurately estimates them. Specifically, the experiments highlight the ability of our estimator to recover the structure of the underlying routing matrix.

We continue with a brief overview of the relevant literature, focusing initially on estimation in the context of
single
infinite-server queues. A foundational contribution is by Brown
[
7
]
, who introduced a nonparametric estimator for the service-time distribution in the M/G/
\infty
queue, relying solely on observations of arrival and departure times (i.e., without having any information about the way these arrival and departure times are coupled). This approach results in a consistent estimator, with further extensions being discussed in
[
5
]
. Subsequently,
[
10
]
analyzed the estimation procedure from
[
7
]
, presenting exact non-asymptotic expressions for the mean squared error as a key result. Another relevant contribution is
[
4
]
, which explores both parametric and nonparametric estimation techniques for M/G/
\infty
systems, based on either the queue-length process or sequences of busy and idle periods; from a practical perspective, a challenge with the latter approach is that, in infinite-server queues with substantial load, busy periods can be disproportionately long, rendering the estimator inefficient. Finally,
[
9
]
investigates a similar estimation framework, assessing the maximal risk of the nonparametric estimator for the service-time distribution.

It is worth noting that there is a distinct body of literature focused on
single-server
queues. These systems are inherently more complex than infinite-server queues because customers
do
interfere with each other: unlike in infinite-server systems, waiting is a factor. Notable contributions in this area include
[
11
]
, which develops a non-parametric estimator for the service time distribution in the M/G/1 queue, and
[
13
]
, which provides an estimator for the Laplace exponent of the input process in a Lévy-driven storage system. In the context of a network of
finite-server
queues
[
15
]
suggest a Bayesian inference method for network in which only some of the jobs are observed. This is related to one of our model variants, with the important difference that in the framework of
[
15
]
the full path of a sample job is observed, whereas in our framework only periodic observations of the network population vector are available.

The literature on networks of infinite-server queues is relatively limited. Wichelhaus and Langrock
[
18
]
examine a class of feedforward networks (with a strong focus on systems of tandem type), where both the external arrival and departure processes are observed. Their objective is to infer the service-time distributions at the individual stations and the routing probabilities. This is achieved by employing cross-spectral techniques for multivariate point processes, building on the framework developed in
[
6
]
. Additionally, Schweer and Wichelhaus
[
14
]
extend existing methods based on covariance functions, with the key result being a functional central limit theorem for the proposed estimators.

There is a compelling connection between the methodology developed in this paper and the concept of
causality

[
12
]
. Causality refers to the relationship between causes and their effects, where a cause can potentially lead to the occurrence of an effect. This concept is fundamental across a wide range of scientific disciplines, including economics, physics, and sociology, where identifying causal relationships helps explain phenomena and predict future outcomes. Recognizing that causes must precede their effects temporally, time series data plays a crucial role in gaining insight into causality, particularly in determining the direction of causality. In the context of the technique presented in this paper, we are able to detect the model’s routing matrix, which can be interpreted as a reflection of an underlying causal structure.

Another area of research within network-related inverse problems is
network tomography
. The primary objective, motivated by applications in large-scale communication networks, is to infer a network’s internal characteristics using only end-point data. For a more detailed description of this field, we refer to
[
8
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
defines our model and states the paper’s objectives. Our approach is based on the method of moments; expressions for the required moments, in terms of the model parameters, are given in Section
3
. Section
4
presents our estimator and the corresponding consistency proof for the case that the routing matrix and the external arrival rates are estimated (for given service-time distributions and observation probabilities, that is).
Section
5
discusses an extension in which also service-time parameters are estimated; this section also includes the model-free approach, which does not require knowledge of a parametric class of the service-time distributions. In Section
6
we point out how one can also include the estimation of the observation probabilities. Numerical experiments are given in Section
7
.

2.
Model and objectives

In this paper we consider a network that consists of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
stations. Each of these stations is to be interpreted as an
infinite-server queue
, i.e., a service facility at which all customers present are served in parallel. Recall that infinite-server queues are not queues in the strict sense, in that customers do not interfere with each other. The network is characterized by three elements: the system’s external arrivals, the customers’ per-station service times, and the routing mechanism.

\circ

External arrivals.
The external arrival rate at station
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
is
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
. The corresponding arrival processes are assumed to be Poisson processes.

\circ

Service times.

The per-customer service time in station
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
is distributed as the non-negative, finite-mean random variable
G_{i}
that is characterized via its Laplace-Stieltjes transform
{\mathscr{G}}_{i}(\cdot)
; the corresponding cumulative distribution function is denoted by
G_{i}(\cdot)
.
In our setup all service times are assumed independent, and the per-station service times are in addition assumed to be identically distributed.

\circ

Routing mechanism.

We denote by
q_{ij}
the probability that a customer goes to station
j
after having been served at station
i
, where
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
; here, the case
j=0
corresponds to the customer leaving the network, so that
\sum_{j=0}^{n}q_{ij}=1
for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We denote by
Q
the
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is given by
q_{ij}
.

Remark 1
.

In the setting where we infer the vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and the matrix
Q
, covered by Section
4
, we allow self-transitions: for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we may have that
q_{ii}>0
. In the setting that we also infer the parameters corresponding to the service-time distributions, covered by Section
5
, we put
q_{ii}=0
to avoid issues concerning identifiability. To see the necessity of this choice, observe that if
q_{ii}
were positive and
G_{i}
would have an exponential distribution with parameter
\mu_{i}
, then the time the customer spends at station
i
is exponentially distributed with parameter
\mu_{i}(1-q_{ii})
, entailing that one can just estimate
\mu_{i}(1-q_{ii})
, and not
\mu_{i}
and
q_{ii}
individually.
\Diamond

In our setup we impose the following two conditions on the model parameters. (i) In the first place we want each queue
j
to be ‘non-trivial’, in that customers flow in at any station. This is guaranteed if

(1)

\lambda_{j}>0,\>\>\mbox{or there is a path $(i_{0},\ldots,i_{k})$ such that $\lambda_{i_{0}}q_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots q_{i_{k}j}>0$.}

(ii) In the second place we want each queue
j
to be ‘non-explosive’, in the sense that customers eventually flow out. This is guaranteed if

(2)

q_{j0}>0,\>\>\mbox{or there is a path $(i_{0},\ldots,i_{k})$ such that $q_{ji_{0}}\cdots q_{i_{k-1}i_{k}}q_{i_{k}0}>0$.}

Throughout this paper, we consider this network of infinite-server queues to be (strongly) stationary. This entails that the
effective arrival rate
vector
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}
follows as the unique non-negative solution of the
traffic equations
. Indeed, the effective arrival rate at station
i
satisfies

\lambda_{i}^{\rm eff}=\lambda_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{ji}\,\lambda_{j}^{\rm eff},

or, in evident vector/matrix notation,

(3)

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}=(I-Q^{\top})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}};

noting that
I-Q
is a weakly chained diagonally dominant matrix
(by virtue of the assumptions we imposed on the probabilities
q_{ij}
), it follows that there is a unique solution.

In this paper we denote by
{\boldsymbol{M}}(t)
the vector of queue lengths at the network’s stations at time
t\geqslant 0
; indeed, its
i
-th entry corresponds to the number of customers at station
i
at time
t
, with
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. It is a classical result that the stationary distribution of the network’s population size vector has Poisson marginals, where in addition the number of customers at the various stations are independent in stationarity. As a consequence of this fact, using that we start in stationarity, we have for any
t\geqslant 0
and
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}
that, for
loads

\varrho_{i}
,

\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{M}}(t)={\boldsymbol{m}})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{e^{-\varrho_{i}}\,(\varrho_{i})^{m_{i}}}{m_{i}!};

here the per-queue loads are given by, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(4)

\varrho_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{\rm eff}\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}].

The main goal of this paper is to infer the model parameters by observing the network population vector
{\boldsymbol{M}}(\cdot)
at the times of a Poisson process with known parameter
\beta
. The method that we propose is based on the method of moments, motivating why in the next section we analyze moments and cross-moments pertaining to
{\boldsymbol{M}}(\cdot)
at Poisson times.

3.
Moments pertaining to the network population

The main goal of this section is to derive expressions for various expectations that involve the network population vector. It is clear that, for any
t\geqslant 0
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(5)

{\mathbb{E}}[M_{i}(t)]=\varrho_{i},

by virtue of the multivariate Poisson stationary distribution in combination with the assumed stationarity. In this section, the object of interest is

\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(t)M_{i}(t+T_{\beta})]=\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})],

with
T_{\beta}
an exponentially distributed random variable with mean
\beta^{-1}
, sampled independently of everything else.

3.1.
Auxiliary results

We start our computation of
\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]
by analyzing
P_{ij}(\beta)
, defined as the probability that a customer who
just entered
station
i
resides at station
j
after an independently sampled time interval
T_{\beta}
.
By an elementary conditioning argument, distinguishing the scenario that ‘the exponential clock
T_{\beta}
rings’ after the end of the customer’s service time (at station
i
) and the scenario where these events happen in the opposite order, and in addition appealing to the memoryless property of the exponential distribution, we find

\displaystyle P_{jj}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{P}(G_{j}\in{\rm d}x)}\,\beta\,e^{-\beta t}\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{jk}\,P_{kj}(\beta)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}(G_{j}\in{\rm d}x)}\,\beta\,e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}G_{j}(t)\,\beta\,e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}t\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{jk}\,P_{kj}(\beta)+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-G_{j}(t))\,\beta\,e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\mathscr{G}_{j}(\beta)\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{jk}\,P_{kj}(\beta)+(1-\mathscr{G}_{j}(\beta)),

where in the last equation an elementary integration-by-parts argument has been used.
Similarly, for
i\not=j
we find

P_{ij}(\beta)=\mathscr{G}_{i}(\beta)\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{ik}\,P_{kj}(\beta).

We thus have found, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, a system of
n
equations in equally many unknowns.
These relations can be compactly summarized in convenient vector/matrix notation, as follows. Define by
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{j}(\beta)
the vector
(P_{1j}(\beta),\ldots,P_{nj}(\beta))^{\top}
.
Then, with
{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{v}}\}
the diagonal matrix with the (column) vector
{\boldsymbol{v}}
on the diagonal, and with
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}
denoting the
j
-th unit vector,

(6)

(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\,Q)\,{\boldsymbol{P}}_{j}(\beta)=\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big)\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j}.

Applying strict diagonal dominance, the matrix
(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\,Q)
is non-singular, so that uniqueness of the solution follows (for any positive
\beta
).
Note that in addition there is the option of the customer having left the network at the exponentially distributed time
T_{\beta}
. With
P_{i0}(\beta)
denoting the corresponding probability, we evidently have

P_{i0}(\beta)=1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}(\beta).

The following lemma summarizes our findings so far. We define by
{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
the
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
P_{ij}(\beta)
, with
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and by
{\boldsymbol{1}}
the
n
-dimensional all ones vector.
The above linear equations can be rewritten as, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and any
\beta>0
,

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{j}(\beta)=(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\,Q)^{-1}\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big)\,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{j},

which further simplifies to the following statement.

Lemma 1
.

For any
\beta>0
,

(7)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)=(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\,Q)^{-1}\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big).

In addition,

{\boldsymbol{P}}_{0}(\beta)={\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta){\boldsymbol{1}}.

In our computations so far, we have considered the situation that the customer
has just entered
station
i
. In stationarity, however, a customer present at station
i
has a
residual service time
, i.e., a service time that is sampled from the so-called
excess life distribution

[
3
, §V.1]
. Concretely, the excess life distribution pertaining to a customer residing at station
i
is characterized through the cumulative distribution function

G^{\rm(res)}_{i}(t):=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]}\int_{0}^{t}(1-G_{i}(s))\,{\rm d}s.

We proceed by calculating
P_{ij}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
, being the counterpart
of
P_{ij}(\beta)
but then starting with such a residual service time in station
i
(rather than a ‘fresh’ service time, that is). Using the same reasoning as above, we find, for
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle P_{ij}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{G}^{\rm(res)}_{i}(\beta)\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{ik}\,P_{kj}(\beta),

\displaystyle P_{jj}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{G}_{j}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{jk}\,P_{kj}(\beta)+(1-\mathscr{G}_{j}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)),

\displaystyle P_{i0}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}P^{\rm(res)}_{ij}(\beta),

where, using the above expression for the cumulative distribution function
G^{\rm(res)}_{i}(t)
, the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of the residual service time can be expressed in terms of its ‘fresh counterpart’ via

\mathscr{G}_{i}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)=\frac{1-\mathscr{G}_{i}(\beta)}{\beta\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]}.

The next lemma presents these findings in a compact manner, where
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}_{j}(\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
are defined as the ‘residual counterparts’ of
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{j}(\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
, respectively (i.e., in their definitions the probabilities
P_{ij}(\beta)
are to be replaced by
P^{\rm(res)}_{ij}(\beta)
).

Lemma 2
.

For any
\beta>0
,

{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)={\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}\,Q\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)+\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}\big),

with
{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
being given by Lemma
1
.
In addition,

{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}_{0}(\beta)={\boldsymbol{1}}-{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta){\boldsymbol{1}}.

3.2.
Computation of the cross-moments

The next step is to evaluate the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}(M_{i}(T_{\beta})\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)={\boldsymbol{m}})
, which will later allow us to set up an expression for the ‘mixed expectation’
\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]
.
The expression for this conditional expectation is given by, as before exploiting that the system is in stationarity at time
0
, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\mathbb{E}(M_{i}(T_{\beta})\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)={\boldsymbol{m}})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}\,P_{ji}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma_{ji}(\beta)

where
\Gamma_{ji}(\beta)
is the expected value of the number of customers that arrive at station
j
between time
0
and time
T_{\beta}
and that reside at station
i
at time
T_{\beta}
; here it has been used that customers move through the network without interfering with each other.

Now note that, in case the exponential clock
T_{\beta}
attains the value
t>0
, then the expected number of external arrivals at station
j
is
\lambda_{j}t
, each of them arriving at a uniformly distributed time in the interval
[0,t].
Hence, with
{\mathfrak{p}}_{ji}(t)
denoting the probability that a customer that arrives at time
0
at station
j
is at station
i
at time
t
,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ji}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta t}\lambda_{j}t\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{t}\,{\mathfrak{p}}_{ji}(t-s)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t=\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta t}\int_{0}^{t}{\mathfrak{p}}_{ji}(s)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{s}^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}t\right){\mathfrak{p}}_{ji}(s)\,{\rm d}s=\lambda_{j}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta s}\,{\mathfrak{p}}_{ji}(s)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}\,\frac{P_{ji}(\beta)}{\beta}.

This can be rewritten in more compact, self-evident notation:
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\beta)=\beta^{-1}{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\}\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta).
As an aside we mention that
\Gamma_{ji}(\beta)\to\varrho_{i}
as
\beta\downarrow 0
, or, equivalently,
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\beta)\to{\boldsymbol{1}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}
, as anticipated.

Our next goal is to identify, for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the ‘mixed expectation’
\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]
. By conditioning on
{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)={\boldsymbol{m}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}}\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)={\boldsymbol{m}}]\,{\mathbb{P}}({\boldsymbol{M}}(0)={\boldsymbol{m}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}}m_{j}\mathbb{E}[M_{i}(T_{\beta})\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)={\boldsymbol{m}}]\,\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{e^{-\varrho_{\ell}}\,(\varrho_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}}}{m_{\ell}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{n}}m_{j}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}m_{k}\,P_{ki}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{ki}(\beta)\right)\,\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{e^{-\varrho_{\ell}}\,(\varrho_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}}}{m_{\ell}!}.

We thus obtain, by distinguishing the cases
j=k
and
j\neq k
, and recalling that
\varrho^{2}+\varrho
is the second moment of a Poisson random variable with mean
\varrho
, the following result:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]

\displaystyle=(\varrho_{j}^{2}+\varrho_{j})P_{ji}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+\sum_{k=1,k\not=j}^{n}\varrho_{j}\varrho_{k}\,P_{ki}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+\varrho_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{ki}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\varrho_{j}\,P_{ji}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+\varrho_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varrho_{k}\,P_{ki}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+\varrho_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Gamma_{ki}(\beta).

By plugging in the relation
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\beta)=\beta^{-1}{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\}\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
that we derived above, in vector/matrix notation we find the following representation.

Proposition 1
.

For any
\beta>0
,

(8)

\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]=\>\big({\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}+{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\}\big)\,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+{\beta}^{-1}\,{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta),

with
{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
being given by Lemma
1
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
being given by Lemma
2
.

As a sanity check, we now verify that
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]\to{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}
as
\beta\downarrow 0
; this property should hold, since
T_{\beta}\to\infty
as
\beta\downarrow 0
, so that we should have that
{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})
become independent in the limit. To see that we indeed have that
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]\to{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}
, use that
P_{ij}(\beta)\to 0
,
P^{\rm(res)}_{ij}(\beta)\to 0
and
\Gamma_{ij}(\beta)\to\varrho_{j}.

We also remark that the result found in Proposition
1
is consistent with (known) expressions for the case of a single infinite-server queue; let the arrival rate be
\lambda
and
G
be the service time with Laplace-Stieltjes transform
{\mathscr{G}}(\cdot)
. Then
\varrho=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[G]
, so that

\mathbb{E}[{M}_{1}(0){M}_{1}(T_{\beta})]=(\varrho^{2}+\varrho)\,{\mathbb{P}}(G^{\rm(res)}\geqslant T_{\beta})+\varrho\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta t}\,\lambda t\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{t}{\mathbb{P}}(G\geqslant t-s)\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}t,

the first term taking care of the contribution to
M_{1}(T_{\beta})
due to customers already present at time
0
, and the second term of the contribution due to customers arriving between times
0
and
T_{\beta}
.
After some elementary calculations, it turns out that this can be rewritten as

(9)

(\varrho^{2}+\varrho)\,{\mathbb{P}}(G^{\rm(res)}\geqslant T_{\beta})+\beta^{-1}\,\varrho\lambda\,{\mathbb{P}}(G\geqslant T_{\beta}),

as desired. It can be further simplified by expressing the probabilities
{\mathbb{P}}(G^{\rm(res)}\geqslant T_{\beta})
and
{\mathbb{P}}(G\geqslant T_{\beta})
in terms of
{\mathscr{G}}(\beta).

3.3.
Erlang-distributed inter-observation times

So far we have considered cross-moments
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
pertaining to exponentially distributed inter-observation times. In other words, we have considered the network population, in stationarity, at the
k
-th and
(k+1)
-st Poisson observation times.
We conclude this section by pointing out how one can extend that procedure to Erlang-
2
inter-observation times, corresponding to the cross-moments at the
k
-th and
(k+2)
-nd Poisson observation times.

Let
E_{\beta,2}
be an Erlang-2 random variable with mean
2\,\beta^{-1}
, i.e., the sum of two independent exponentially distributed phases, each having mean
\beta^{-1}
. The key insight is the following relation, by which one can express
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]
in terms of
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
:

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\beta}\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta t}\,\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(t)^{\top}]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta t}\,\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(t)^{\top}]{\rm d}t-\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta te^{-\beta t}\,\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(t)^{\top}]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\beta^{-1}\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]-\beta^{-1}\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}],

where we have used that the probability density function of
E_{\beta,2}
reads
\beta^{2}t\,e^{-\beta t}
, for
t\geqslant 0
.
By rearranging this identity, we readily obtain

(10)

\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]=\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]-\beta\,\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\beta}\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}].

In our analysis, a crucial role is played by the matrices
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
and
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
, denoting the entry-wise derivatives of
{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
, respectively. These can be identified as follows. From (
6
) we find, using the standard differentiation rules for matrix products, that

(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}Q)\,\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)-{\rm diag}\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}Q\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)=-{\rm diag}\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\},

so that

(11)

\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)=(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}Q)^{-1}\,{\rm diag}\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\,(Q\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)-I).

From this relation and Lemma
2
we can identify
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
:

(12)

\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)={\rm diag}\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)\}\,Q\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)+{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)\}\,Q\,\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)-{\rm diag}\{\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)\}.

Applying (
10
) in combination with Proposition
8
, we thus end up with the following result.

Proposition 2
.

For any
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]=

\displaystyle\>\big({\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}+{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\}\big)\,\big({\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)-\beta\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)\big)+2\,{\beta}^{-1}\,{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)-{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta),

with
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
being given by (
11
) and
\dot{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
by (
12
).

Along the same lines, the counterpart of Proposition
2
for higher-order Erlang inter-observation times can be found. One more differentiation and similar manipulations, for instance, lead us to an expression for
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,3})^{\top}].

4.
Method of moments estimator and its consistency

As pointed out in the preceding sections, the parameters of our interest are the routing matrix
Q
, the arrival rates
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, and the parameters associated to the service-time distributions. In this section we assume that the service-time distributions are given, leaving us with the task of estimating the
n
arrival rates in the vector
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, the
n^{2}
entries of the routing matrix
{Q}
, yielding a total of
n^{2}+n
parameters. As mentioned in the introduction, Section
5
will address how inference of the service-time distributions can be incorporated, while Section
6
will discuss how to handle noisy observations, which requires estimating the

n
observation probabilities as well.

The main objective of this section is to propose and analyze an estimator for the parameters
Q
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
. The moment equations we will work with are those related to
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)]
and
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
, as given in Section
3
. These equations total
n^{2}+n
, matching the number of parameters to be estimated. Our primary result concerns the consistency of the estimator of
Q
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, under standard regularity assumptions.

4.1.
Estimator and assumptions

In this subsection we define our estimator, and state the assumptions under which we can prove consistency.
Throughout we let our parameter vector be represented through

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\equiv\left\{Q\in\Delta_{n},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in[0,\Lambda]^{n}\ \right\}.

Here
\Delta_{n}
consists of all
n\times n
matrices with non-negative entries in which each row sum is less than or equal to one. Furthermore,
\Lambda\in[0,\infty)
is to be interpreted as an upper bound on each of the external input rates. We let
\Theta
be the set of such parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and we denote the true parameter by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}\equiv(Q_{0},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0})\in\Theta
.

Let
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{m}
denote the sequence of inter-sampling times, with
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
denoting the number of observations. As we are working with Poisson observation times, these inter-sampling times are independent, exponentially distributed samples, say with mean
\beta^{-1}
; the sampling rate
\beta
is omitted from our notation for the sake of brevity. The sampling times are then given by the corresponding partial sums:
S_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\tau_{i}
, with
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. In the sequel, we denote the queue size at station
i
and observation time
k
by
M_{i,k}:=M_{i}(S_{k})
, and the vector recording the queue sizes at all
n
stations by
{\boldsymbol{M}}_{k}
.

The next step is to use the expressions found in Section
3
to set up the moment equations that we use in our estimator.
To this end, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
, we define two types of random objects:

\displaystyle\psi_{i,k}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=M_{i,k}-\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[M_{i}(0)]=M_{i,k}-\,\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]\ ,

for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, and

\displaystyle\psi_{i,j,k}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=M_{j,k}M_{i,k+1}-\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]\ ,

for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
. Observe that all these quantities have mean zero for
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
. The method-of-moments estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}
solves the following system of moment equations:

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m})=0,

\displaystyle\quad i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ ,

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m})=0,

\displaystyle\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ .

Throughout the following assumptions are in place.

Assumption 1
.

The routing probabilities are such that

(i)

For any station
j
, and for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\equiv(Q,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\in\Theta
,

\lambda_{j}>0,\>\>\mbox{or there is a path $(i_{0},\ldots,i_{k})$ such that $\lambda_{i_{0}}q_{i_{0},i_{1}}\cdots q_{i_{k}j}>0$.}

(ii)

There exists a positive constant
\epsilon
such that for any station
j=1,\ldots,n
, and for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\equiv(Q,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\in\Theta
,

q_{j0}>\epsilon,\>\>\mbox{or there is a path $(i_{0},\ldots,i_{k})$ such that $q_{ji_{0}}\cdots q_{i_{k-1}i_{k}}q_{i_{k}0}>\epsilon$.}

Assumption 2
.

For any
j=1,\ldots,n
,
\mathbb{E}[G_{j}]
is positive and finite.

We proceed by providing an intuitive explanation as to why these assumptions need to be imposed.
Assumption
1
specifies that all nodes have a non-zero rate of incoming work and that all jobs leave the system eventually. These conditions are similar to the ones imposed in (
1
)–(
2
), but now it should be in place for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
, where it is noted that in part (ii) we need the uniform lower bound
\epsilon
to make the consistency proof work. Assumption
2
ensures, together with Assumption
1
.(ii), pointwise almost sure convergence of the empirical moments (
13
)–(
14
), as appearing in the moment equations, to 0. The assumption that all mean service-time times are strictly positive excludes the degenerate case where all jobs in a certain node have zero service time and immediately depart; this is without any loss of generality, as such a node can be removed from the network.

4.2.
Identifiability

In this subsection we wish to identify the unique solution to the moment equations, recalling that we focus on the case that the service-time distributions are known. A statistical model is identifiable if
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}=\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\theta}}
if and only if
\theta=\tilde{\theta}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
is the joint distribution of the data corresponding to parameter
\theta
(see e.g.,
[
16
, Ch. 5]
).
This is not sufficient for consistency of method-of-moment estimators, but rather we need to verify that there is a one-to-one mapping between the moments and the parameters.
We next verify that this is indeed the case in our setting.

For brevity, denote by the vector

\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}
the stationary moments
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{M}(0)}]
and by the matrix
\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}
the stationary cross-moments of
{\mathbb{E}}[{\boldsymbol{M}(0)}{\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
. Firstly, (
4
) and (
8
) imply that any parameter pair
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},Q)
yields a unique pair
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})
. We now verify that the other direction holds as well.

Evidently, from (
4
) and (
5
), we find, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(15)

\lambda_{i}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]})=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{i}^{[0]}}{{\mathbb{E}}[G_{i}]}.

Now consider the moment equation, with the second equality being due to (
3
),

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}

\displaystyle=\big({\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}+{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\}\big)\,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+{\beta}^{-1}\,{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta),

\displaystyle=\big({\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}+{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\}\big)\,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)+{\beta}^{-1}\,{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\,({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}))^{\top}(I-Q){\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta).

In this equation we replace
\boldsymbol{\varrho}
by the moment vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}
, and likewise
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}
by
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]})
.
By Lemmas
1
and
2
we have expressions for the matrices
{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
and
{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
. Inserting these into the previous display, we readily arrive at

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}=\>

\displaystyle\Phi\,\Big({\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}Q\,\big((I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}(\beta)\}Q})^{-1}(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\})\big)+I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\Big)\,+

\displaystyle{\beta}^{-1}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}\,(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}))^{\top}(I-Q)\,\big((I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}(\beta)\}Q})^{-1}(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\})\big),

where

\Phi:=\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]})^{\top}+{\rm diag}\{\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}\}.

It remains to show how
Q
can be isolated from this equation. The idea is to post-multiply the entire equation by
(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\})^{-1}(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}(\beta)\}Q})
; for compactness we rewrite this matrix as

\Xi_{1}+\Xi_{2}\,Q,

where

\Xi_{1}\equiv\Xi_{1}(\beta):=(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\})^{-1},\>\>\>\>\Xi_{2}\equiv\Xi_{2}(\beta):=-(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\})^{-1}{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}.

After elementary manipulations, we thus obtain the equation

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}\,\big(\Xi_{1}

\displaystyle+\Xi_{2}\,Q\big)

\displaystyle=\>

\displaystyle\Phi\,\Big({\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}Q+\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big)\big(\Xi_{1}+\Xi_{2}\,Q\big)\Big)+{\beta}^{-1}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}\,({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}))^{\top}(I-Q).

Observe that this equation is linear in the matrix
Q
. More precisely,
with

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})

\displaystyle:=\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}\,\Xi_{2}-\Phi\Big({\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}+\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big)\Xi_{2}\Big)+{\beta}^{-1}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}\,({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}))^{\top},

\displaystyle\Omega_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})

\displaystyle:=-\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}\,\Xi_{1}+\Phi\,\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big)\Xi_{1}+{\beta}^{-1}\,\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}\,({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}))^{\top},

we have thus identified the mapping of moments to the pair
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},Q)
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]})
be given by (
15
). Then

\displaystyle Q(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})

\displaystyle=\Omega_{1}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})^{-1}\,\Omega_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]})

\displaystyle=(I-Q(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})^{\top})\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]}).

A direct corrollary of the proposition is that there is a one-to-one relation between the moments
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})
and the parameters
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}},Q)
.

Remark 2
.

Proposition 2 further has practical implications because the parameters estimators can be computed by replacing
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[0]},\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}^{[1]})
with the empirical counterparts. Note, however, that for finite samples this may yield estimators outside of the parameter space, e.g., negative arrival rates or non-stochastic routing matrices. Thus, some truncation or projection may be required. In Section
7
further details are provided for the implementation of the estimation procedure.
\Diamond

4.3.
Consistency

In this subsection we argue that the proposed estimator is, under the assumptions imposed, consistent.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Then, for any sampling rate
\beta>0
there exist functions
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[0]}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[1]}:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, as
m\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\>\to_{\rm as}\>\psi_{i}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\>\>i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ ,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\>\to_{\rm as}\>\psi_{i,j}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\>\>i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ .

Moreover, for
\ell=0,1
,
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[\ell]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})={\boldsymbol{0}}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

Proposition
3
entails that, under our assumptions, the model is
identifiable
in the sense that there are no distinct parameter vectors
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}\in\Theta
such that

\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})]=\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})],\>\>\mbox{and }\>\>\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1})]=\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2})],

where the expectation is taken with respect to the stationary distribution.

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
4
consists of three steps:

(i)

We employ a coupling argument to construct an (upper) bounding system. This majorizing process is the queue length process of a specific M/G/
\infty
queue, i.e., a queue with Poisson arrivals, i.i.d. service times, and infinitely many servers; the precise construction of the majorizing M/G/
\infty
queue follows later in Section
4.4
.
The majorizing process, in the sequel denoted by
(\tilde{M}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, is set up such that
M_{i,k}\leqslant\tilde{M}_{k}
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, with probability one; this holds for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
, where we note that the majorizing process depends on
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
as well. We further verify that under Assumptions
1
–
2
, this majorizing process has ergodic limits for the first moment and the lag-one cross-moment. Indeed, we argue that for any
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
and for finite numbers
\bar{M}
and
\bar{M}_{2}
,

(16)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\tilde{M}_{k}\>\to_{\rm as}\>\bar{M}\ ,\>\>\>\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\tilde{M}_{k}\tilde{M}_{k+1}\>\to_{\rm as}\>\bar{M}_{2}\ ,

as
m\to\infty
.

(ii)

By (
16
), in combination with the fact that
(\tilde{M}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
majorizes
({M}_{i,k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}M_{i,k}<\infty\ ,

almost surely. As we have assumed that the system is in stationarity, we have that
\mathbb{E}[M_{i,k}]=\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]
for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. As a consequence, Césaro’s lemma implies that, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\>\to_{\rm as}\>\lambda_{i,0}^{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]-\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]=:\psi_{i}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ ,

with
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}^{\mathrm{eff}}\equiv(\lambda_{1,0}^{\mathrm{eff}},\ldots,\lambda_{n,0}^{\mathrm{eff}})^{\top}
defined in the evident manner.
Following an identical argumentation, we can verify the limit, for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\>\to_{\rm as}\>\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}\big[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})\big]

\displaystyle=:\psi_{i,j}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})<\infty\ ,

again in evident notation.

(iii)

As a final step, Proposition
3
ensures that there is a unique solution
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
to the system
\{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{0}\ ,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{0}
}.

Hence, it is left to prove that (
16
) holds, which will be done in Subsection
4.4
.
∎

It has thus far been established that the estimation equations converge pointwise for every individual
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
. In order to establish consistency of the sequence of estimators we must strengthen this to
uniform
convergence on the entire parameter space
\Theta
. Specifically, we present sufficient conditions for the sequence of estimation equations to be a
Glivenko-Cantelli class
(see
[
16
, Ch. 19]
). Before doing so we must specify a norm for our parameter space, which has a slightly non-standard structure.

It is convenient to stack the elements of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
into a single
(n^{2}+n
)-dimensional vector. This vector is set up such that its first
n^{2}
entries are given by the transition probabilities in the routing matrix
Q
, and the other
n
entries by the external arrival rates
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.
We can now define a standard
L_{1}
-norm on
\Theta
:

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n^{2}+n}|\theta_{\ell}-\tilde{\theta}_{\ell}|\ .

In an evident manner, this distance can be separated into a sum of the distances for each ‘parameter group’ in the parameter space. Indeed,

(17)

|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|q_{ij}-\tilde{q}_{ij}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}|.

This means that we can verify continuity and convergence conditions for each type of parameter separately and conclude that these hold for the entire parameter space.
In our proofs, a key role is played by the following lemma, proven in Subsection
4.5
. Let

(18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\equiv\Big(\psi_{i,k}^{[0]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ ,\,i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\ ,\>\psi_{i,j,k}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\,i,j\in\{1\ldots,n\}\Big)\ .

Lemma 3
.

There exists a finite constant
\kappa
such that for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
\boldsymbol{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
,

(19)

|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\leqslant\kappa\,|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|\ .

We have arrived at the main result of this section: the consistency of our estimator as
m\to\infty.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
2
hold. Then, the estimator is consistent, i.e., for any sampling rate
\beta>0
,

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}\>\to_{\mathbb{P}}\>{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},\>\>\mbox{as}\>\>m\to\infty.

Proof.

Recall that the routing probabilities appearing in the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
should be component-wise nonnegative and such that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}q_{ij}\leqslant 1
for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, i.e., they belong to specific simplices. In addition, we assumed
\lambda_{i}\in[0,\Lambda]
for
\Lambda<\infty
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Hence, the parameter space
\Theta
is compact.

Observe from (
18
) that
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}+n}
is a vector-valued function. With the system being in stationarity at time zero, Proposition
4
entails that

(20)

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\>\to_{\rm as}\>\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\ ,\>\forall\theta\in\Theta\ .

The Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\cdot)
, stated in Lemma
3
, combined with the pointwise convergence in (
20
), thus yields that
\{m^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):\ \boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\ ,k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is a Glivenko-Cantelli class (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Thm. 3]
). Hence, as
m\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\right|\>\to_{\rm as}\>0\ .

As
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\boldsymbol{0}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
by Proposition
4
, we conclude that the estimator is consistent (see, e.g.,
[
16
, Thm. 5.9]
).
∎

4.4.
Upper bound system

The objective of this subsection is to verify the almost sure convergences in (
16
). In this proof we work with a coupling which features a majorizing process that is of the M/G/
\infty
type.

Let
M^{+}(t)={\boldsymbol{M}}(t)^{\top}{\boldsymbol{1}}
denote the total network population at time
t\geqslant 0
. The key observation is that
M^{+}(\cdot)
is an M/G/
\infty
process. In the first place, its arrival process is Poissonian with rate
\lambda:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}
.
Secondly, the clients’ service times are independent and identically distributed random variables, distributed as a generic random variable
H
, independent of the arrival process, that can be described as follows. Let
H_{i}
be the generic random variable that represents how long a job stays in the network, given it enters at node
i

H\equiv H({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
equals
H_{i}
with probability
\lambda_{i}/\lambda.
Here it has been used that the times that the customers spend in the network are independent, as a direct consequence of the underlying infinite-server mechanism.
We observe that, evidently,
M_{i}(t)\leqslant M^{+}(t)
almost surely, for any
t
and
i=1,\ldots,n.

Let
K_{i}
denote the number of nodes visited by an arbitrary customer entering the network at node
i=1,\ldots,n.
Then, with
g:=\max\{{\mathbb{E}}[G_{1}],\ldots,{\mathbb{E}}[G_{n}]\}
, we have that
{\mathbb{E}}[H_{i}]\leqslant g\,{\mathbb{E}}[K_{i}]
; here it has been used that, by Assumption
2
,

is finite. By Assumption
1
we conclude that
{\mathbb{E}}[K_{i}]
is finite; to this end observe that

can be interpreted as the hitting time of an absorbing state in a discrete-time Markov chain with finite state space. This in particular means that
{\mathbb{E}}[H]<\infty
; we even have
due to Assumption
1
.(ii) that there is a finite
h_{1}
such that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}{\mathbb{E}}[H(\boldsymbol{\theta})]<h_{1}.

By the ergodic theorem for stationary processes, recalling that
(M^{+}(S_{k}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is stationary, it follows that, as

,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}M^{+}(S_{k})\to_{\rm as}{\mathbb{E}}[M^{+}(S_{1})]=\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[H],

using that
M^{+}(S_{1})\sim{\rm Poiss}(\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[H])
, with
\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[H]<\infty

We thus conclude that the first claim in (

Regarding the second claim in (

(M^{+}(S_{k})M^{+}(S_{k+1}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}M^{+}(S_{k})M^{+}(S_{k+1})\to{\mathbb{E}}[M^{+}(S_{1})M^{+}(S_{2})],

which, in view of (

\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}M^{+}(S_{k})M^{+}(S_{k+1})\to_{\rm as}(\varrho_{+}^{2}+\varrho_{+})\,{\mathbb{P}}(H^{\rm(res)}\geqslant T_{\beta})+\beta^{-1}\,\varrho_{+}\lambda\,{\mathbb{P}}(H\geqslant T_{\beta})<\infty,

Lipschitz continuity of estimation equations

In this subsection we establish the Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\cdot)
, which was stated in Lemma
3
.

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
from (

).
Hence, for any
\boldsymbol{\theta},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
,

(21)

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\ .

— First contribution in (
21
).
We start by analyzing the first sum in the right-hand side in (
21
). As a first step, observe that

\displaystyle|\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|

\displaystyle=|\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]|\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}|\leqslant g\,|\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}|.

We proceed by analyzing
|\lambda_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}-\tilde{\lambda}_{i}^{\mathrm{eff}}|

As a consequence of Cramer’s rule, where
{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},i)
denotes the determinant of
I-Q^{\top}
but with the
i

I-Q^{\top}
being replaced by
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

{\lambda}^{\rm eff}_{i}=\frac{{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},i)}{{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top})}.

Let
{\mathscr{L}}
denote the set of Lipschitz-continuous functions (on
\Theta
), and
{\mathscr{L}}^{\circ}
the subset of
{\mathscr{L}}
that is in addition bounded (on
\Theta
).
Recall that (a) if
f,g\in{\mathscr{L}}
, then
f+g\in{\mathscr{L}}
, and (b) if
f,g\in{\mathscr{L}}^{\circ}
, then
fg\in{\mathscr{L}}^{\circ}
. Bearing in mind that a determinant is a sum of products of the matrix’ entries, we thus find that both
{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top})
and
{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},i)
are in
{\mathscr{L}}^{\circ}
, also recalling that all parameters (the entries of
Q
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, that is) are uniformly bounded on the compact parameter space.
By Assumption
1
, we know that there is some
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that
{\rm det}(I-Q)>\epsilon_{0}
for all
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
. As a consequence, the effective arrival rate
\lambda_{i}^{\rm eff}
is the product of the functions
1/{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top})
and
{\rm det}(I-Q^{\top}\,|\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},i)
that are both in
{\mathscr{L}}^{\circ}
.
We conclude that there is a finite constant
\kappa^{[0]}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\leqslant\kappa^{[0]}\,|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|.

— Second contribution in (
21
).
A similar procedure can be followed for the cross-moment equations.
Now the starting point is

\displaystyle|\psi_{i,j,k}^{[1]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle-\psi_{i,j,k}^{[1]}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta})}|=|\,\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]-\mathbb{E}_{{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})]\,|.

Using Proposition
1
, by applying the triangle inequality, we majorize it by

(22)

\displaystyle|\,{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}-{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}}^{\top}\,|_{i,j}+|\,{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\}\,{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)-{\rm diag}\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)\,|_{i,j}+\frac{1}{\beta}\cdot|\,{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)\,|_{i,j},

with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varrho}}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
having the obvious meaning (and, by Lemmas
1
and
2

\tilde{Q}
).
Following the same procedure as before,
1/{\rm det}(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\,Q)
is in
{\mathscr{L}}^{\circ}
for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta

is in

such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})|\leqslant\kappa^{[1]}\,|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}|,

5.
Unknown service-time distribution

Where so far we have assumed that the service-time distributions were known, in this section we point out how the analysis can be extended to the case in which they are not.

5.1.
Service-time distributions in parametric family

We start by discussing the setting in which the service-time distributions are in a given parametric family. Let for ease each service-time distribution be characterized by
d
parameters.

We let our parameter vector be represented through

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\equiv\left\{Q\in\Delta_{n},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in[0,\Lambda]^{n}\ ,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathscr{E}^{n}\right\}\ ,

where
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

is some parametric space; it means that have that
G_{j}\equiv G_{j}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j})
,
with
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}\in{\mathscr{E}}
, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. As before, we refer to the set of such parameters as
\Theta
, and we denote the true parameter by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}\equiv(Q_{0},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{0})\in\Theta
.

As discussed in Remark
2
, we now impose the condition
q_{ii}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, to avoid any identifiability issues.
Observe that the dimension of the parameter vector has now become
n^{2}+dn
:
n^{2}-n
entries in
Q
,
n
entries in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
dn
entries in
\boldsymbol{\eta}
. This means that unless
d=1
we need to add cross-moments pertaining to Erlang inter-observation times, where as long as
d\leqslant n+1
just Erlang-2 inter-observation times suffice (leading to a grand total of
2n^{2}+n
moment equations). In that case, define

\displaystyle\psi_{i,j,k}^{[2]}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=M_{j,k}M_{i,k+2}-\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(E_{\beta,2})]\ ,

for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The system of equations providing the moment estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}
should now also include, besides the
n^{2}+n
moment equations (
13
)–(
14
) that we already had, additional equations of the type

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m-2}\sum_{k=1}^{m-2}\psi^{[2]}_{i,j,k}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m})=0,

\displaystyle\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\};

out of these
n^{2}
equations the required
(d-1)n
equations can be picked. In case
d>n+1
, one has to add sufficiently many cross-moments pertaining to higher order Erlang inter-observation times.

To demonstrate how the parametric estimation of the service-time distributions works, we discuss the example in which
G_{i}
is distributed exponentially with mean
\mu_{i}^{-1}.
It means that
\varrho_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{\rm eff}/\mu_{i}
, and, defining by
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\beta)
the
n
-dimensional vector whose
i
-th entry is
\mu_{i}/(\mu_{i}+\beta)
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\left(I-{\rm diag}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}(\beta)}\right\}\,Q\right)^{-1}(I-{\rm diag}\{\boldsymbol{\mu}(\beta)\}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)

\displaystyle={\rm diag}\left\{{\boldsymbol{\mu}(\beta)}\right\}\,Q\,{\boldsymbol{P}}(\beta)+(I-{\rm diag}\{\boldsymbol{\mu}(\beta)\});

here it has been used that for exponentially distributed service times we have that
{\mathscr{G}}_{i}(\beta)={\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}_{i}(\beta).

By Proposition
1
, we can now write
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

One could attempt to establish a consistency proof in much the same way as before.
Suppose we impose the requirement that there are
M^{-}
and
M^{+}
such that, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

(23)

0<M^{-}\leqslant\mu_{i}\leqslant M^{+}<\infty.

Then it is straightforward to verify that under the assumption (
23
) the analogue of Lemma
3
(i.e., Lipschitz continuity) still holds with the new parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. However, deriving a counterpart to Proposition
3
may prove to be more challenging.

A similar approach can be followed as long as the
G_{i}
follow a specified distribution that is characterized through a single parameter. Notably, it is not needed that all
G_{i}
stem from the same parametric class; one could for instance have that some service times are exponentially distributed, while others are Erlang-2 distributed. In case some of the
G_{i}
have distributions with more than one parameter, one needs to add the required number of moment equations in the way discussed above.

5.2.
Model-free approach

Interestingly, in case one does not know the parametric form of the service-time distributions, one can still perform an inference procedure. In this case the vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
consists, besides
Q
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, also of

({\mathbb{E}}[G_{1}],\ldots,{\mathbb{E}}[G_{n}]),\>\>({\mathscr{G}}_{1}(\beta),\ldots,{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(\beta)),\>\>(\dot{\mathscr{G}}_{1}(\beta),\ldots,\dot{\mathscr{G}}_{n}(\beta)),

i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
has become a vector of dimension
n^{2}+3n
. Observe that, as a consequence, for
n\geqslant 2
our
2n^{2}+n
moment equations (deriving from the
n
stationary moments, the
n^{2}
cross-moments pertaining to exponential inter-observation times, and the
n^{2}
cross-moments pertaining to Erlang-2 inter-observations times) suffice.

It is directly seen that

\boldsymbol{\varrho}
,
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
, and
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]
can be expressed in terms of the entries of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Assuming that, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
{\mathbb{E}}[G_{i}]
,
{\mathscr{G}}_{i}(\beta)
, and
\dot{\mathscr{G}}_{i}(\beta)
are Lipschitz in
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
and
0<g^{-}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}[G_{i}]\leqslant g^{+}<\infty
, one again obtains consistency; the requirement
{\mathbb{E}}[G_{i}]\geqslant g^{-}>0
is needed to make sure that

\mathscr{G}_{i}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)=\frac{1-\mathscr{G}_{i}(\beta)}{\beta\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\dot{\mathscr{G}}_{i}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)=-\frac{1}{{\mathbb{E}}[G_{i}]}\frac{1-{\mathscr{G}}_{i}(\beta)+\beta\,\dot{\mathscr{G}}_{i}(\beta)}{\beta^{2}}

are Lipschitz for
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
.

Evidently, this approach is particularly useful in case one does not know the service-time distribution’s parametric form. The price to be paid is that one needs to estimate as many as three parameters per service time.

6.
Unknown observation probabilities

So far we have worked in the setting in which we have access to observations of the network population vector. In this section, however, our goal is to explore a more general setting where not all customers are necessarily observed, i.e., the setting with
censored observations
. In addition to estimating the parameters associated with our infinite-server queueing network, we also aim to estimate the parameters related to the underlying observation mechanism.
In this section, we present the moments associated with the
observed
network population. Having these at our disposal, the estimation procedure works essentially as before, but now with a parameter vector that also includes the
observation probabilities
.

Let
p_{j}
be the probability that a customer residing at station
j
is actually observed. We assume that each customer is observed independently, i.e., if there happen to be
m_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
customers at station
j
, the number of observed customers is binomially distributed with parameters
m_{j}
and
p_{j}
. In addition, we assume that this observation process happens ‘independently in time’ (i.e., at every new observation time, the ‘binomial selection’ of which customers are observed occurs independently from the selection at previous observation times) as well as ‘independently in space’ (i.e., at every station, the ‘binomial selection’ occurs independently from the selection at other stations).

By the tower property, at any
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathbb{E}[N_{i}(t)]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[N_{i}(t)\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}(t)]\big]=\mathbb{E}[p_{i}\,M_{i}(t)]=p_{i}\,\varrho_{i}=p_{i}\,\lambda_{i}^{\rm eff}\,\mathbb{E}[G_{i}].

Also, again by the tower property, at any
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{j}(t)N_{i}(t+T_{\beta})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[N_{j}(0)N_{i}(T_{\beta})]=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[N_{j}(0)N_{i}(T_{\beta})\,|\,{\boldsymbol{M}}(0),{\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})]\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[p_{j}p_{i}M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})\big]=p_{j}p_{i}\,\mathbb{E}\big[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})\big],

with
\mathbb{E}\big[M_{j}(0)M_{i}(T_{\beta})\big]
as given in Proposition
1
. We have shown the following statement.

Lemma 4
.

For any
\beta>0
,

\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{N}}(0){\boldsymbol{N}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]={\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\}\,\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]\,{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\},

with
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
being given by Proposition
1
.

Because the number of unknown parameters exceeds
n^{2}+n
(even in the case the service-time distributions are known), we need additional moment equations. We could again utilize the moment equations deriving from
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{N}}(0){\boldsymbol{N}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]
, where it is noted that this covariance matrix can be found from
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{N}}(0){\boldsymbol{N}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
in the same way as
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]
was found from
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
.

7.
Numerical experiments

In this section we assess the performance of our estimator through a series of experiments. Before doing so, we discuss the moment equations that are to be solved in Subsection
7.1
, and provide a specific algorithm for the case that
\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{1}
in Subsection
7.2
. The actual experiments are presented in Subsection
7.3
.

7.1.
Solving the moment equations

To evaluate our estimator, it is crucial to ensure that the number of equations matches the number of unknown parameters. In case the unknown parameters are
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
Q
, as in Section
4
, we can base our computations on the moment equations related to the ‘snapshots’
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)]
and lag-one covariances
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
. This leads to
n^{2}+n
equations that define the
n^{2}+n
parameters, where the estimator follows directly from Proposition
3
.

In case there are more parameters, we have to add sufficiently many moment equations related to lag-two covariances. For instance, in the case that
the service-time distributions
G_{i}
are characterized by a single parameter
\eta_{i}
and the observation probabilities
p_{i}
are unknown, there are
n^{2}+2n
unknown parameters: recalling that in this framework the diagonal elements of
Q
are assumed
0
, there are
n^{2}-n
parameters related to
Q
, and
n
parameters related to each of the vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
and
\boldsymbol{p}
. This requires us to use the
n
‘snapshots’
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{N}}(0)]
, the
n^{2}
lag-one covariances
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{N}}(0){\boldsymbol{N}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
and in addition
n
lag-two covariances
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{N}}(0){\boldsymbol{N}}(E_{\beta,2})^{\top}]
(where we picked the cross moments that appear on the diagonal of this matrix). The parameter estimates are obtained by equating these analytical expressions of the moments (which are functions of the unknown parameters) to their empirical counterparts.

Observe that simply solving the moment equations does not guarantee that the solution lies within the parameter space
\Theta
; in principle, it may lead to, e.g., negative arrival rates, negative probabilities, or row sums of the routing matrix exceeding 1. In our numerical experiments, we therefore adopted a pragmatic approach: minimizing the sum of squared differences between the left-hand and right-hand sides of the moment equations, with the optimization carried out over
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
.

7.2.
Efficient procedure if all customers are observed

Supposing that
{\boldsymbol{p}=\boldsymbol{1}}
and again considering the situation that each service-time distribution is characterized by a single parameter, we have
n^{2}+n
unknown parameters. The parameters (i.e., the entries of
Q
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
) can be estimated using the moment equations in Equation (
5
) and Proposition
1
, replacing
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0)]
and
\mathbb{E}[{\boldsymbol{M}}(0){\boldsymbol{M}}(T_{\beta})^{\top}]
with their empirical counterparts. Specifically, define the empirical first-moment vector, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}
by

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}_{i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\psi^{[0]}_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

and the empirical second-moment matrix
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[1]}
by, for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[1]}_{ij}=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\psi^{[1]}_{i,j,k}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Let
\boldsymbol{G}
denote the vector of expected service times, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{G}_{i}=\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]
, and define

\displaystyle t_{1}:

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}\,(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]})^{\top}+{\rm diag}\{\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}\},

\displaystyle t_{2}:

\displaystyle={\beta}^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}\,(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]})^{\top}\,\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{G}}\}^{-1}\,(I-Q),

\displaystyle t_{3}:

\displaystyle=(I-\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}Q)^{-1}\big(I-\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}}(\beta)\}\big)\,.

The system of equations to solve is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}={\rm diag}\{\boldsymbol{G}\}\,(I-Q^{\top})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[1]}=t_{1}\,\big(I-{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}\big)+\big(t_{1}\,{\rm diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}\,Q+t_{2}\big)\,t_{3}\,.

Here we should realize that
{\boldsymbol{G}}
,
{\mathscr{G}}(\beta)
and
{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}(\beta)
can all be expressed in terms of the
n
-dimensional unknown parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

Note that in the above moment equations we have used
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[0]}
in place of the explicit expression for
\boldsymbol{\varrho}
in the cross-moment equations, so as to simplify the computations.
Furthermore, observe that the cross-moment equations can be written solely in terms of
Q
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
; indeed, we can eliminate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
due to the relation

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\top}=\big({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\rm eff}\big)^{\top}\,(I-Q)={\boldsymbol{\varrho}}^{\top}\,\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{G}}\}^{-1}\,(I-Q)\,.

After having eliminated the
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, we have arrived at a system of
n^{2}
equations in the
n^{2}
unknowns (i.e., the parameters relating to
Q
and
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
). Hence we can solve for
Q
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
using the cross-moment equations, and then subsequently identify
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
from the single snapshot equations using the estimated
\widehat{Q}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
.
We finally remark that the right-hand side of the cross-moment equations is quite intricate and can be computationally demanding. Our numerical experiments indicate that the following alternative formulation, obtained by an elementary reordering, often leads to a significant reduction in computation time:

\big(t_{1}\,\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}Q+t_{2}\big)^{-1}\,\big(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{[1]}-t_{1}\big(I-\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{\mathscr{G}}^{\rm(res)}}(\beta)\}\big)\text{diag}\{{\boldsymbol{p}}\}\big)-t_{3}=0\,.

Remark 3
.

An important strength of our technique is its ability to distinguish between different network topologies, even when the underlying stochastic systems share the same stationary distribution. For example, consider the following two cases: (i) a circular topology with homogeneous arrival rates (
\lambda_{i}=\lambda
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
) and homogeneous service rates (
\mu_{i}=\mu
), with clockwise routing and uniform exit probabilities (say,
q
); and (ii) an otherwise identical system, but with counterclockwise routing. Although these two systems clearly have the same stationary distribution (which is
n

8.
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have studied a queueing network in which each node is modeled as an M/G/
\infty
queue. Based solely on noisy observations of the number of individuals at each node, we have developed a procedure to identify the service rates, arrival rates, and the routing matrix. Notably, we can infer not only the existence of links between nodes but also their direction — an essential feature for causality analysis.

Our work has practical relevance in multiple domains. For example, in social networks, understanding the routing matrix, and thus the underlying structure, can help identify influencers, communities, or potential spreaders of information (or misinformation). Such insights are valuable in applications such as marketing, healthcare, and political campaigns. Moreover, knowledge of the network structure facilitates the detection of bottlenecks and supports strategic decisions, such as whether to add or remove a route between specific nodes.

Future research directions include:

\circ

Single-server queue analysis
: Extend the analysis to networks where each station is a
single
-server queue (operating under, say a first-come, first-served discipline), rather than an infinite-server queue. An even more general setup could concern queues where the expected waiting time increases with the number of individuals already present. In particular, one could consider cases in which the arrival rate at a station can be modeled as a function of its current occupancy, reflecting customer reluctance to join when the queue is more congested. A simple example is a step-function arrival rate: for instance, let
\lambda_{i}
remain as before when
M_{i}>m_{0,i}
, for some
m_{0,i}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and set
\lambda_{i}=0
otherwise.

\circ

Endogenous routing dynamics
: Study scenarios where customers
choose
their next station after service completion, making the routing matrix endogenous rather than exogenous. This could involve modeling customer decisions based on real-time information on queue lengths or other system states.
