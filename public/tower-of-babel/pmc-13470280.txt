Hydrogen sulfide primes drought tolerance in plants by metabolic control of DNA methylation
Authors: David Montesinos-Pereira, Jing Zhang, Nazaret Navarro, Jingjing Zhuang, Hongping Zhao, Yanjie Xie, Cecilia Gotor, Luis C. Romero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHydrogen sulfide (H2S) is a gasotransmitter that contributes to plant stress responses. In this study, we investigated the mechanisms underlying H2S-induced priming in rice drought tolerance. Combining transcriptomic and epigenomic analyses, we show that H2S pretreatment establishes a coordinated regulatory program associated with enhanced stress resilience. H2S attenuated drought-induced transcriptional changes while promoting the expression of dehydrin genes, a hallmark of the primed state. Under non-stress conditions, H2S triggered sustained transcriptional reprogramming, including activation of stress-related transcription factors and repression of cell wall-associated genes. Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing revealed global DNA hypomethylation accompanied by increased CG methylation within gene bodies. Metabolite analyses showed that H2S reduces S-adenosylmethionine levels without affecting S-adenosylhomocysteine, thereby lowering cellular methylation capacity; restoring SAM-reversed DNA hypomethylation. These findings indicate that H2S primes drought tolerance through metabolic control of DNA methylation, linking sulfur signaling to epigenetic regulation.

Introduction

After considerable debate regarding the nature of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), it is currently well established as a signaling molecule that plays a key role in mammalian and plant physiology.1,2,3,4,5,6 H2S is endogenously produced by cells and regulates processes for adequate plant performance, including abscisic acid-dependent stomatal movement and autophagy, at a threshold concentration.7,8,9 H2S also regulates plant responses to a wide range of environmental conditions, particularly abiotic stresses, such as metal stress, drought, salinity, hypoxia, and heat. Therefore, it contributes to plant adaptability and viability, and its beneficial effects play crucial roles in various aspects of development.8,10,11,12

Various mechanisms of action of H2S have been proposed based on its chemical properties, although the most well-known of these mechanisms is a post-translational modification (PTM) known as persulfidation. This PTM modifies protein cysteine residues to form persulfides, thus altering protein function.1,13,14,15,16 Diverse high-throughput proteomic analyses have revealed that persulfidation is widely distributed and have identified an extensive catalog of persulfidated proteins in Arabidopsis and crops.13,17,18,19,20,21,22,23,24 The effects of persulfidation on specific proteins involved in different biological processes and the identification of specific cysteine residues as targets of persulfidation have been described.25,26 Recently, we demonstrated the reversibility of persulfidation by the action of the thioredoxin (TRX) system and highlighted the mitochondrial TRXo1 as a depersulfidase.27

There is currently growing interest in the role of H2S in mitigating the adverse effects of climate change stresses. Research across different plant species has conclusively highlighted H2S as essential for conferring stress resistance.26 Interestingly, recent studies have explored the effects of pretreating plants with H2S before experiencing stress conditions such as drought. In various plant species, H2S pretreatment has been shown to enable plants to respond more rapidly and efficiently to subsequent stress through the activation of a priming mechanism, and protein persulfidation was proposed to be involved.18,24,28

Although the role of H2S in enhancing tolerance to drought has been related to protein persulfidation, it is well known that plant drought responses are multilevel in nature. Plants induce a complex regulatory network to survive, which involves stress perception, triggering specific signal transduction, and inducing transcriptional, translational, and post-translational changes.29,30,31 In particular, studies on the mechanisms by which H2S promotes drought resistance at the transcriptional level are very rare. In wheat, the effects of H2S pretreatment on drought-responsive genes were analyzed by global RNA sequencing. Genes involved in processes such as transport, hormonal signal transduction, protein processes, and fatty acid and amino acid metabolism were revealed to be responsible for H2S-enhanced tolerance.32 Other related transcriptomic studies have been performed in Arabidopsis plants under drought stress, but wild type and a double mutant that is defective in cytosolic H2S production have been compared with highlight the signaling role of H2S in drought resistance.33

Most of the previous studies on the ability of H2S pretreatment to promote drought tolerance have focused on protein and metabolite levels. In this study, we investigated the effect of H2S pretreatment on rice drought responses at the transcriptional level to gain further insight into the mechanism by which H2S enhances tolerance. In addition, we further explored the effect of H2S pretreatment on rice plants without any imposed stress conditions to gain a comprehensive understanding of the mechanism underlying its role as a priming agent. RNA sequencing and whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) analyses have demonstrated that H2S triggers transcriptional changes and decreases DNA methylation levels to prepare plants for future challenges. Therefore, we unravel a multifaceted mechanism underlying the function of H2S as a priming chemical.

Results

Transcriptional analysis and sequencing data quality

Research has shown that pretreatment with H2S can induce protective mechanisms in various plants, increasing their tolerance to subsequent stresses, including drought stress. To study the effects of H2S on rice transcriptomic responses to drought stress, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis under four experimental treatments: watered rice plants (control sample, C), rice plants watered with sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS) solution, as a H2S donor (H2S treatment, S), rice plants subjected to drought stress (drought, D) and rice plants pretreated with NaHS and subjected to drought stress (S_D). The experimental design was the same as that previously described.24 Thus, 25-day-old plants grown in soil under physiological conditions were divided into two batches, with one batch irrigated with water and the other with 50 μM NaHS for 10 days, and the NaHS solution was replaced every two days. After this period, each batch was subsequently divided into two new batches and subjected to water irrigation or drought for another 15 additional days. At the end of the full treatment, four replicates of each condition were obtained and processed independently. The four sequenced libraries yielded 897.3 million clean reads, each with more than 91.4% reads perfectly mapped to the rice genome. The samples presented a Q30 Phred quality score of at least 83% (Table S1).

Correlation analysis of the biological replicates according to all the gene expression levels (FPKM) of each sample revealed high similarity between the control and H2S treatment samples (C and S) and between the drought-treated samples (D and S_D), with a squared Pearson correlation coefficient (R2) greater than 0.96 (Figure S1A). The same observation was extracted by principal-component analysis (PCA), where the intragroup replicas were clustered and samples between groups were dispersed, more so the drought samples (D and S_D) than in the C and S samples (Figure S1B). The expression level was quantified for a total of 20,874 genes, 17,539 of which were common across the four conditions (Figure S2).

Hierarchical cluster analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the groups revealed similar patterns between the two samples without stress (C and S), which were different from those of the two other drought sample groups (D and S_D). Four different subclusters were distinguished: two clusters that presented the opposite pattern of upregulated (cluster 1; 5,637 genes) and downregulated (cluster 3; 4,083 genes) DEGs between the drought and control samples and two other clusters with similar trends, which presented mainly upregulated genes in the H2S-treated plants (cluster 2; 439 genes) with respect to the control samples (cluster 4; 273 genes) (Figure 1). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs under drought stress

To ascertain the significantly enriched biological functions and pathways associated with the DEGs in each comparison, we analyzed the up- and downregulated genes using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) bioinformatic resources (false discovery rate [FDR] <0.05). We first analyzed the effects of drought stress in the D vs. C comparison, which revealed 3,902 upregulated genes and 3,580 downregulated genes. Functional characterization of the upregulated DEGs revealed enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes commonly associated with plant drought responses, with the GO response to water, which consists of five different dehydrins, emerging as the most enriched process (Table S2). Dehydrins belong to the LEA protein family and play essential roles in protecting plants from dehydration. They are highly hydrophilic proteins and function primarily as chaperones, although they have also been implicated in the regulation of stress-responsive genes and proposed to participate in transcriptional regulation during stress responses.34,35 Additional enriched terms included response to abscisic acid, encompassing several drought-responsive transcription factors, and abscisic acid-activated signaling pathway, containing multiple pathway components. Other terms that showed elevated fold enrichment were associated with different catabolic processes, such as glutamate catabolic process, fatty acid beta-oxidation, and lignin degradation. Furthermore, cell wall macromolecule and chitin catabolic processes, both consisting of the same 11 chitinases also included in the term polysaccharide catabolic process, were significantly enriched. Consistent with these findings, the enriched KEGG pathways were related to the above biological processes, such as fatty acid and amino acid metabolism. In contrast, the most enriched GO biological processes and KEGG pathways among the downregulated DEGs were associated with photosynthesis, carbon and nitrogen metabolism and photomorphogenesis (Tables S3). Therefore, the transcriptional changes observed in rice plants under drought conditions represent common adaptative responses aimed at mitigating the effects of water deficit and ensuring plant survival.29,30 Accordingly, we observed the induction of typical drought marker genes, such as dehydrins, genes involved in ABA signaling, drought-responsive transcription factors, and those associated with cell-wall modification. Energy reprogramming by activation of lipid and amino acid catabolism and the repression of photosynthesis and carbon and nitrogen metabolism was also observed.

Similarly, in the comparison between H2S-treated plants under control conditions and H2S-treated plants subjected to drought (S_D vs. S), the functional enrichment analysis of the up- and downregulated DEGs revealed the same terms as those identified in the previous D vs. S comparison (Tables S4; S5). These findings confirm that the drought stress was properly perceived by the plants within our experimental system and underscore the high degree of similarity between H2S-treated plants and untreated control plants.

Interestingly, the number of DEGs identified between H2S-pretreated and untreated plants under drought stress (S_D vs. D) was markedly reduced, with 1,167 upregulated and only 191 downregulated genes (Figure 2). Functional enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes revealed only seven enriched GO biological processes with an FDR <0.05, and among them, response to water and cold acclimation exhibited exceptionally high fold enrichments of 33- and 14-fold, respectively (Table S6). The genes associated with both terms belong to the dehydrin family, which has also been reported to function as cryoprotectants during cold stress.35 Other enriched biological processes included responses to different abiotic stresses and hormone-mediated signaling pathways, whereas no significantly enriched KEGG pathways were identified. Furthermore, analysis of the downregulated genes did not reveal any enriched biological processes or KEGG pathways at the applied significance threshold. These results demonstrate that only a limited number of transcriptional changes underline the enhanced drought tolerance observed in H2S-pre-treated rice plants, consistent with previous findings.24 Thus, at least at the transcriptional level, our data suggest that the induction of dehydrin family genes constitutes a key mechanism contributing to H2S-enhanced drought tolerance in rice.

Kinetics of the H2S-induced upregulation of the transcription factors

qPCR analysis was performed to validate the RNA sequencing results and further demonstrate that non-toxic H2S concentrations induce the upregulation of stress-related transcription factors (Figure 5). We examined the kinetics of the H2S effect on TF gene expression because the S samples used for the RNA-seq analysis were collected at the end of the full treatment,24 i.e., from 25-day-old plants irrigated with water and then treated with NaHS for 10 days, followed by an additional 10 days of watering. To this end, the same treatment was carried out in parallel with separate batches of plants grown under control conditions. Leaf samples (four replicates) from control (C) and H2S-treated plants (S) were collected at 5 days after the initiation of H2S treatment (D5), at the end of the H2S treatment (D0), at 4 days after completion of H2S treatment (D4) and at the end of the subsequent 10-day watering period (D10). At the midpoint of the H2S treatment, the transcript levels of all TFs were significantly higher in the control samples than in the H2S-treated plants. In contrast, upon completion of the treatment, this pattern was reversed. H2S-treated plants exhibited a pronounced increase in TF expression compared with the control samples, with each gene reaching its maximum expression at distinct time points (Figure 5). Consistent with the RNA-seq data, DREB1B showed the highest transcript abundance, followed by WRKY24, whereas ZFP36 and ERF20 displayed comparatively low expression levels. Notably, the upregulation of the TFs in H2S-treated plants was evident throughout the post-treatment period, with of DREB1B peaking at D4, whereas WRKY24, ZFP36, and ERF20 reached maximal induction at the end of the full treatment (D10). These results corroborate the RNA-seq evidence of H2S-dependent transcriptional regulation and suggest that such modulation may enhance plant resilience to subsequent stress.

Regulation of DNA methylation by H2S treatment

The alteration of the transcriptional profile observed by H2S treatment may be associated with modifications in DNA methylation. To analyze the DNA methylation pattern and gain knowledge about the regulation of cytosine methylation by H2S treatment, WGBS of leaves from plants grown under control conditions and treated with H2S (four biological replicates) was performed as indicated in the methods. In total, approximately 17–16 million and 16–13 million high-quality reads were obtained for the four replicates of the control plants and the H2S-treated plants, respectively. The percentage of clean data out of the raw data was approximately 90%, the quality score Q30 was greater than 91%, and the bisulfite conversion rate was greater than 99% for all the replicates (Table S8A). The filtered clean reads were aligned to the reference genome, with an average mapping rate of 76.45% of uniquely mapped reads. This resulted in an average site coverage of the genome higher than 5.4% and at least above 88% of the percentage of bases with a minimum coverage of one (Table S8B).

Analysis of the global DNA methylation ratios [methylated C/(mC + non-mC)] revealed an average methylation level of 2.89% in the control plants and 1.93% in the H2S-treated plants, indicating an overall reduction of approximately 33% in total cytosine methylation in response to H2S. This decrease was evident across all sequence contexts. In control plants, the average methylation levels were 7.97%, 4.79%, and 0.90% for CG, CHG, and CHH contexts, respectively, whereas H2S-treated plants exhibited averages of 5.38%, 3.24% and 0.57%, for the same contexts. Among them, the CHH showed the greatest relative change between control and treated plants (Figure 6; Table S9). Furthermore, the chromosomal distribution of methylation levels revealed more pronounced differences in the CG context between H2S-treated and control plants. Distinct regions exhibiting condition-specific methylation areas were also detected on chromosomes across different contexts (Figure S3A). Analysis of methylation patterns within functional genomic regions revealed only minor differences between the S and C samples; however, CG methylation in promoter regions and CpG islands was slightly higher in H2S-treated plants. Comprehensive DMR analysis further revealed extensive context-dependent hypo- and hypermethylation, particularly enriched in CHH and CG contexts, indicating that H2S profoundly reshapes DNA methylation landscapes at both genome-wide and gene-associated levels.

Metabolic regulation of H2S-induced hypomethylation

DNA methylation in the form of 5-methylcytosine requires the metabolite S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as the methyl group donor. SAM is synthesized from methionine and ATP by methionine adenosyltransferase, also known as AdoMet synthetase. Following the transfer of the methyl group, which is catalyzed by specific methyltransferases, SAM is converted into S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). SAH is subsequently hydrolyzed to produce homocysteine, which is then used by methionine synthase (MS) to regenerate methionine, thereby completing the SAM cycle36 (Figure 8A). Importantly, several enzymes that participate in the generation and turnover of methyl groups, including those involved in SAM biosynthesis, SAH turnover and folate-dependent remethylation, have been identified as targets of H2S-dependent persulfidation in distinct proteomic studies in both Arabidopsis and rice.13,17,18,19,24 These widespread PTM,25 suggested that H2S might influence the SAM cycle at multiple points, thereby altering the cellular methylation capacity. Because SAH is a strong competitive inhibitor of SAM-dependent methyltransferases, the SAM/SAH ratio, commonly referred to as the methylation index, is widely used as an indicator of the cellular capacity to sustain methylation reactions, particularly regarding DNA methylation levels.37 Accordingly, the total contents of both metabolites were quantified in leaves from plants grown under control conditions and those treated with H2S, and subsequently the methylation index was calculated for each condition (Figure 8B). While SAH levels remained unchanged in both conditions, H2S-treated plants exhibited a pronounced decrease in SAM content relative to control plants, leading to a significant reduction in the methylation index. This selective depletion of SAM, without concomitant accumulation of SAH strongly indicates that H2S primarily limits methyl-group availability rather than inhibiting SAH hydrolysis. These data indicate that the decrease in global DNA methylation observed in H2S-treated plants can be attributed to a substantial reduction in the cellular SAM pool.

To functionally test whether restricted SAM availability is the cause of H2S-induced hypomethylation, we analyzed the effect of exogenous SAM supplementation on the total level of 5-methylcytosine in protoplasts isolated from H2S-treated plants, using an ELISA-like microplate-based assay (Figure 8C). Treatment with the H2S produced a significant reduction of around 35% in total DNA methylation, a value close to the reduction of 33% detected by WGBS (Figure 6). Remarkably, incubation of H2S-treated protoplasts with SAM largely restored the 5-methylcytosine level to that of untreated protoplasts. Conversely, the decrease in global DNA methylation observed in control protoplasts upon SAM over-supplementation may reflect a metabolic feedback inhibition since an excess of SAM can lead to SAH accumulation or other catabolites that inhibit methyltransferase activity, effectively reducing the pool of functional methyl donors.38,39

In conclusion, H2S induces a marked depletion of the cellular SAM pool, and thereby limiting the availability of methyl groups required for DNA methylation. Consistent with this metabolic constraint, exogenous SAM restores 5-methylcytosine levels in H2S-treated protoplasts, demonstrating that H2S-induced hypomethylation is mediated through metabolic regulation of the SAM cycle.

Discussion

Most studies investigating the role of H2S as a signaling molecule have focused on its regulatory functions at the protein and metabolite levels, with particular emphasis on protein persulfidation. This PTM modulates the activity of key enzymes involved in stress responses, including those related to redox homeostasis, metabolism, and water transport.18,24 However, studies addressing its role in transcriptional regulation remain relatively limited. In this study, we extend these findings by investigating the effect of H2S on the transcriptional response of rice to drought, with the aim of assessing the impact of H2S pretreatment prior to stress application. To achieve this goal, we employed the same experimental approach previously used to analyze persulfidome responses.24

Drought stress triggered well-characterized transcriptional responses in plants,29,40 validating the robustness of our experimental system. When comparing drought-stressed plants with well-watered control plants, responses to water scarcity, ABA signaling, and various catabolic processes were among the most enriched biological processes involving the upregulated genes. Conversely, the downregulated genes were predominantly associated with photosynthesis and carbon and nitrogen metabolism. A similar pattern of enriched GO terms was observed when drought-stressed plants pretreated with H2S were compared with their respective unstressed and pretreated plants, indicating that the overall transcriptional behavior of H2S-treated plants was comparable with that of control plants. However, the comparison between H2S-pretreated drought-stressed plants with drought-stressed plants without H2S pretreatment, revealed a markedly reduced number of DEGs. Notably, response to water stress emerged as the most significantly enriched term, including multiple dehydrin-encoding genes. This finding highlights a critical role for H2S in modulating drought responses. Our findings support the importance of dehydrins in rice drought tolerance, consistent with their proposed conservation during rice domestication/evolution.41 By comparison, proteomic analysis has shown that H2S primarily influences water transport through the persulfidation-dependent regulation of aquaporins.24

Unexpectedly, a substantial number of DEGs were detected when comparing plants grown under control conditions with those treated with a non-toxic concentration of H2S. Because H2S was applied 10 days prior to sample collection, these results indicate that the transcriptional effects of H2S are stable and persist long over after treatment. The most significant and abundant group of genes induced by H2S treatment were TFs associated with stress responses. In particular, H2S-treated plants showed strong induction of specific TFs, mostly ethylene-responsive factors involved in dehydration response, which exert broad physiological effects due to stomatal closure triggered by multiple stress conditions.29,42 Among other induced TFs, the zinc finger protein ZFP36 has been demonstrated to play an important role in ABA-mediated stress resistance. Overexpression of ZFP36 in rice enhances antioxidant enzyme activity and increases tolerance to both water deficit and oxidative stress.43 Therefore, the H2S-induced increase in ZFP36 expression may contribute to the enhanced drought resistance previously reported.24 In addition, other transcription factors linked to plant immunity, including JA-responsive factors and others associated with MAPK signaling were also activated.44 Expression kinetics of selected TFs confirmed their H2S-induced upregulation, with transcript levels peaking after completion of the treatment. Together, these observations suggest that H2S triggers durable transcriptional reprogramming.

Our results therefore indicate that non-toxic concentrations of H2S progressively induce the transcriptional activation of stress-responsive TFs, even in the absence of stress conditions. This conclusion is supported by a recent transcriptomic study comparing the effects of H2S and Cys treatments in Arabidopsis seedlings, in which GO enrichment revealed that a substantial fraction of H2S-responsive DEGs encode DNA-binding proteins, including ERF factors.45 Similarly, a transcriptomic study of tobacco roots exposed to NaHS showed a broad regulatory effect on TF gene expression regulation, with ERFs predominantly upregulated.46 In banana fruit, NaHS treatment was also found to significantly induce the expression of several TFs, particularly different WRKY, NAC, and ERF genes, associated with the mitigation of chilling injury.47 Moreover, H2S-mediated activation of the ethylene response factor ERF.D3 has been shown to delay both leaf senescence and tomato fruit ripening, although these effects additionally require H2S-dependent protein persulfidation. Direct regulation of TF activity by H2S through persulfidation has been documented as well. For example, persulfidation of MYB10 suppresses anthocyanin accumulation and delays pear fruit ripening,48 and persulfidation of WRKY6 similarly delays tomato fruit ripening.24

Consistent with the transcriptional activation of the TFs, H2S treatment appeared to orchestrate a strategic metabolic shift, characterized by the repression of genes associated with primary cell wall biosynthesis and secondary metabolism including significant downregulation of genes involved in cellulose synthesis. Furthermore, numerous genes related to secondary metabolism were repressed. Cell wall remodeling, encompassing alterations in cell wall structure, composition, and organization, constitutes an important abiotic stress response.49,50 A reduction in cellulose synthesis typically results in increases in pectin and lignin deposition, thereby enhancing cell wall rigidity and stress resistance.51 These observations suggest that H2S primes structural adjustments even in the absence of stress.

Consequently, our findings demonstrate that treatment of rice plants with non-toxic concentrations of H2S triggers transcriptional reprogramming networks, establishing a primed physiological state, characterized by the time-dependent induction and repression of specific gene sets. Such changes likely precondition the plants for enhanced responsiveness to subsequent stress, as previously reported not only in rice but also in Arabidopsis and barley.18,24,28 Therefore, H2S pretreatment appears to establish a physiological state of heightened readiness that increases resilience to future stress episodes, a phenomenon referred as defense priming.52 Several sulfur species have been proposed as priming agents across different studies,53,54,55 although the underlying mechanism remains elusive. Based on our results, we propose that H2S-mediated transcriptional regulation constitutes a plausible mechanism, although the contributions of protein persulfidation cannot be excluded.

Beyond direct transcriptional regulation, our results suggest that H2S acts as a regulator of cellular methylation capacity. DNA and histone methylation are among the best-characterized epigenetic modifications regulating eukaryotic gene expression. A genome-wide analysis of DNA methylation confirmed that H2S-treated plants exhibited overall lower cytosine methylation across all sequence contexts. This reduction was reflected in the predominance of hypomethylation in the DMRs identified between H2S-treated and control plants. Although a larger proportion of DMRs occurred in promoter regions than within gene bodies, a notable enrichment of hypermethylated gene-body DMRs was specifically observed in the CG context. Gene-body methylation in the CG context is a characteristic feature of plant genomes, and accumulating evidence suggests that it contributes to the maintenance of stable gene expression. Moreover, gene-body methylation has been associated with increased plant fitness.56 Overall, these results indicate that H2S decreases global DNA methylation while simultaneously promoting gene-body methylation, a combination that may underlie the transcriptional changes observed in H2S-treated plants. Consequently, the H2S-induced DNA methylation alterations described here could contribute to the enhanced drought tolerance previously reported in rice.24

DNA methylation relay on the metabolite SAM as the universal methyl group donor, as well as on distinct modifying enzymes, such as methylases and demethylases.57 Notably, SAM is a metabolite tightly linked to sulfur assimilation.36 Moreover, multiple enzymes involved in the SAM cycle including SAM biosynthesis, SAH turnover, as well as methylases and demethylases, have been identified as targets of persulfidation in different proteomic studies.13,17,18,19,22,23,24 Consistent with these observations, our data allow us to refine the mechanistic interpretation of H2S-induced changes in DNA methylation. Although two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms can be envisaged, namely (1) a metabolic/post-translational regulation of the methionine/SAM cycle and (2) a direct epigenetic regulation through modification of the DNA methylation machinery, our results strongly support the former as the primary mechanism.

In particular, the marked decrease in SAM levels and in the SAM/SAH ratio, together with the restoration of DNA methylation upon SAM supplementation, indicate that H2S primarily reduces methyl-donor availability.37 We therefore propose that H2S regulates the activity of enzymes of the methionine/SAM cycle, most likely through persulfidation, leading to a decrease in cellular methylation capacity and consequent DNA hypomethylation, which is then reflected in transcriptional changes.

The functional validation through protoplast supplementation assays provides a definitive link between signaling and one-carbon metabolism. The fact that exogenous SAM supplementation largely restores 5-methylcytosine levels in H2S-treated plants confirms that the primary cause of the epigenetic reprogramming is indeed restricted methyl donor availability. This metabolic “rescue” strongly parallels the genetic evidence provided by Meng et al., where the DNA methylation defects of Arabidopsis mat4 mutants were reversed by SAM treatment.58 By establishing this parallel, we propose that H2S treatment effectively creates a transient, chemical phenocopy of a methionine adenosyltransferase deficiency, thereby inducing a controlled state of hypomethylation.

Numerous studies in plants have described stress priming, a phenomenon in which exposure to a primary stress enhances the plant’s ability to withstand a subsequent stress through modifications at multiple regulatory levels, including epigenetic mechanisms.59 Among these mechanisms, genome-wide DNA hypomethylation has been reported in different studies as a response to biotic stress.59,60 In line with this, our findings indicate that treatment with a non-toxic concentration of H2S, representing a non-stress condition, induces a primed state in plants, mediated by transcriptional reprogramming and alterations in DNA methylation patterns, particularly global DNA hypomethylation. Importantly, we demonstrate that H2S-dependent modulation of global DNA methylation is driven by changes in intracellular SAM levels, thereby linking H2S signaling to metabolic regulation of the SAM cycle (Figure 9). H2S signaling interferes with methionine (Met) metabolism, leading to depletion of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor in cellular methylation reactions. Reduced SAM levels (and accumulation of S-adenosylhomocysteine [SAH]) result in decreased DNA methylation, causing global hypomethylation. Limitation of the study

While this study provides insight into the role of H2S in transcriptional and epigenetic priming, some aspects merit further investigation. The analyses were performed in rice, and future studies will be useful to determine the extent to which this mechanism is conserved across plant species. In addition, although our results establish a clear link between H2S treatment, SAM depletion, and DNA methylation changes, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this regulation, including the potential involvement of protein persulfidation of enzymes in the SAM cycle, remain to be fully characterized. Moreover, the use of bulk tissue analyses may not capture cell-type-specific regulatory dynamics. Finally, while SAM complementation experiments support a functional connection between methyl donor availability and DNA methylation, complementary genetic approaches will help to further strengthen this relationship and refine the mechanistic framework.

Experimental model and study participant details

Plant growth and sulfide treatment protocol

Rice (Oryza sativa L., japonica, Jsendra cultivar, Spain)61 was used in this study. Rice growth and treatments were performed as previously described.24 Briefly, germinated seeds were sown in soil under a photoperiod of 12 h of white light (100 μmol m−2 s−1) at 28 °C and 12 h of darkness at 28 °C and grown for 25 days under a physiological water regime. The plants were subsequently divided into two batches, one water-irrigated and the other treated with 50 μM NaHS (as a H2S donor compound), for an additional 10 days. After this period, each batch was subsequently divided into two new batches and subjected to water irrigation or drought for another 15 days. At the end of the whole treatment, four different plant samples were obtained as follows: control (C, water-irrigated plants during all periods), H2S-treated (S, H2S-treated plants without any drought stress), drought (D, drought-treated plants with no H2S treatment), and H2S-pretreated plus drought (S_D, H2S-pretreated and drought-treated plants). Leaves from different plant samples were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Method details

RNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing, and differential expression analysis using DESeq2

Total RNA was extracted from the leaves of the four different plant samples described above using the Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini Kit. Four independent biological replicates of each sample were used for RNA-seq performed by Novogene Co. Ltd. Briefly, after previous analyses of RNA quantification, integrity and purity using an Agilent 5400 analyzer, mRNA was purified using poly-T-oligo-attached magnetic beads. For library preparation, the mRNA was fragmented, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by the second-strand cDNA synthesis. Subsequent end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, amplification and purification were performed. The library was checked using Qubit and real-time PCR for quantification and a bioanalyzer for size distribution detection. Clustering of the index-coded samples was performed, the library preparations were sequenced on an Illumina platform, and paired-end reads were generated.

The raw data (raw reads) in fastq format were first processed through fastp software.62 In this step, clean data (clean reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapters, ploy-N-sequences and low-quality reads from the raw data. Moreover, the Q20, Q30 and GC content of the clean data were calculated. All the downstream analyses were based on high-quality, clean data. The obtained clean reads were compared with the Oryza sativa japonica reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5 software (ensemblplants_oryza_sativa_japonica_ group_irgsp_1_0_gca_001433935_1).64 Feature Counts v1.5.0-p3 was used to count the number of reads mapped to each gene. The fragments per kilobase of transcript sequence per million base pairs (FPKM) of each gene were calculated based on the length of the gene and the number of reads mapped to that gene. Differential expression analysis of two conditions (four biological replicates per condition) was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0).65 The resulting p values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach to control for the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p value ≤0.05 and log2 (fold change) > 1 according to DESeq2 were considered differentially expressed. RNA-seq data have been deposited in the ArrayExpress database at EMBL-EBI (www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress) under the accession number E-MTAB-15224. The functional enrichment analysis and functional annotation of the differentially expressed genes were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) web server.66

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS): DNA extraction, bisulfite conversion, bismark alignment, methylation calling, DMR identification using DSS

DNA was extracted from the leaves of two plant samples—control (C, water-irrigated plants during all periods) and H2S-treated (S, H2S -treated plants without any drought stress)—using the Qiagen DNeasy Plant Mini Kit. Four independent biological replicates of each sample were used for WGBS performed by Novogene Co. Ltd. Briefly, fragmented DNA of 200–400 bp was bisulfite treated to generate single-stranded DNA in which unmethylated cytosine was converted into uracil and methylated cytosine remained unchanged. Double-stranded DNA was synthesized and subjected to PCR amplification, and the library was ready after size selection. The quantified libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina platform and paired-end reads were generated. Before analysis, the raw sequencing data were subjected to quality assessment using FastQC (fastqc_v.0.11.5) and processed using Trimmomatic (Trimmomatic-0.36) software.67 All the reads that passed all the filtering steps were counted as clean reads and used for subsequent analyses. Bismark software (version 0.16.3)68 was used to perform alignments of bisulfite-treated reads to the reference genome (ensemblplants_oryza_sativa_japonica_ group_irgsp_1_0_gca_001433935_1). The bisulfite conversion rate, which is the proportion of methylated clean reads to the reference genome, was calculated. The methylation information of cytosine sites was extracted based on the principle of cytosine–thymine conversion. The cytosine sites were classified into CG, CHG, and CHH contexts (where H stands for A, T, and C). A binomial distribution test was used to determine the methylation status and cytosine sites. The genome-wide methylation levels were analyzed using a 10,000 bp window size, and the sum of the methylated and unmethylated read counts in each window was calculated. The methylation level of each window or C site is defined as follows: ML = reads(mC)/reads(mC) + reads (C).

Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified using DSS Bioconductor package.63 According to the distribution of DMRs throughout the genome, we defined the genes related to DMRs as genes whose gene body region or promoter region overlapped with the DMR. WGBS data have been deposited in the ArrayExpress database at EMBL-EBI (www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress) under the accession number E-MTAB-15231.

qPCR validation: RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, primer design, amplification conditions

Quantitative real-time RT–PCR was used to analyze the expression of four selected transcription factors. Total RNA was extracted from Oryza sativa L. leaves using the Qiagen RNeasy Plant Mini Kit. The RNA was reverse transcribed using oligo (dT) and the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene-specific primers for each gene were designed using Vector NTI Advance 10 software (Table S11) to amplify single products, and their specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis. Primer efficiencies exhibited amplification efficiencies between 90% and 110%. Real-time PCR was performed using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), and the signals were detected using a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cycling profile consisted of 95 °C for 10 min followed by 45 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The reference genes OsUBQ1 and OsUBQ5 were selected based on their stable expression across the experimental conditions, as verified by minimal variation in Ct values among treatments. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The results are shown as the means ±SDs for at least four independent RNA samples.

S-adenosylhomocysteine and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM/SAH) quantification by HPLC-MS

Approximately 200 mg of frozen plant tissue was homogenized in 2-mL Eppendorf tubes twice for 2 min at maximum speed using a Retsch ball mill (MM400). Homogenized plant samples were extracted by incubating them with 1.5 mL of a precooled (−20 °C) methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and methanol (MeOH) (3:1, v/v) mixture. The extracts were incubated for 30 min in an orbital shaker at 4 °C and then sonicated for 15 min in an ice-cooled sonication bath. The samples were subsequently centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 × g at 4 °C. A portion of the supernatant, 0.5 mL, was transferred to a new 2 mL microcentrifuge, and 0.5 mL of a water:methanol mixture (3:1, v/v) was added. The samples were vortexed and centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 × g and 4 °C. A 400 μL aliquot from the lower phase was collected in a new tube and dried in a vacuum concentrator. The dried samples were resuspended in 50 μL of 0.1% HCl and analyzed by HPLC-MS.

HPLC separation was performed using the ExionLC UPLC system (Sciex, Framingham, MA) with a reversed-phase column (100 mm × 4.6 mm × 100 Å particle, Kinetex XB-C18). The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in H2O (Buffer A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Buffer B). A 5 μL sample was loaded per injection, and the gradient was applied at a flow rate of 600 μL min−1. Mass spectra were acquired using a QTRAP 6500+ triple quadrupole (Sciex, Framingham, MA) instrument equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in positive ionization mode with an ion spray voltage of 4500 V. The quantification of SAH and SAM was performed using external standards of both compounds.

Global DNA methylation quantification in protoplasts

Protoplasts from 1-month-old rice seedings, either untreated or treated NaHS, were isolated following the method previously described.69 Subsequently, protoplasts were incubated for 6 h in the presence or absence of 200 μM S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), 10 μM Vitamin B6,10 μM Vitamin B12, and10 μM folic acid. Total DNA was then extracted using the plant DNA isolation kit (Vazyme). Global DNA methylation levels were quantified using the MethylFlash Global DNA Methylation (5-mC) ELISA Easy Kit (Epigentek), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Four biological replicates were used for each sequencing condition. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package, with statistical significance assessed by applying the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and a log2 fold change >1 were considered differentially expressed. For qPCR and DNA methylation assays, statistical significance was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between groups were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 unless otherwise indicated.
