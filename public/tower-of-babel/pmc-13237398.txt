Morphological and molecular identification of Fusarium wilt resistance in tomato cultivars mediated by defense gene response
Authors: Ahmed Khan, Ghulam Rasool, Atta Ullah, Muhammad Waris, Kashif Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tomato is a major vegetable crop in Quetta, Balochistan, Pakistan, but its production is severely affected by Fusarium wilt caused by Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici. The region has a continental semi-arid Mediterranean climate, which exacerbates the impact of this disease. This study aimed to identify resistance in tomato cultivars against Fusarium wilt and also understand the underlying mechanisms of host defense gene response. Sources of resistance against F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici in diverse tomato genotypes were identified, and expression profiling of defense genes strengthened these findings. For a comprehensive understanding of the host-pathogen interaction, the pathogen was isolated using a standard tissue isolation method. Morphological characterization revealed oval-shaped conidia of the pathogen by microscopic examination, similar to F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici. Molecular identification via PCR amplification of the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) and Translation Elongation Factor (TEF1-α) regions supported these morphological observations and the amplified product sequence of Tom-BAC1-5 showed 99–100% similarity with the F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici species complex. Pathogenicity assays demonstrated that 3 cultivars (Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr05, and Tom/cr/pr04) were highly susceptible to FOL and two cultivars (Tom/cr/pr07 and Rio Grande) were moderately resistant. The 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine DAB staining of infected tissues visually showed that highly susceptible cultivars exhibited darker staining and significant vascular damage. In contrast, moderately resistant cultivars exhibited pale staining and reduced vascular damage. Gene expression profiling further showed that these two cultivars (Tom/cr/pr07 and Rio Grande) had significantly elevated transcript levels of PR1a, WRKY33, and PAL, suggesting activation of salicylic acid signaling and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways. Conversely, susceptible cultivars showed delayed or reduced expression of these defense-related genes, underscoring the importance of early transcriptional responses for effective resistance. Overall, this study highlights WRKY33 and PR1a as potential molecular markers for resistance screening and provides novel insights into the defense signaling mechanisms against F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici. These findings contribute to the sustainable management of Fusarium wilt and enlighten future tomato breeding programs to enhance disease resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46899-8.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), belongs to the Solanaceae family, it is one of the most extensively cultivated horticultural crops globally. It is highly valued for its versatility, being used both in its raw form for fresh consumption and in the production of wide range of processed food items, such as sauces, juices, and canned goods1. In the year 2024, the worldwide production of tomatoes attained an estimated volume of 186.8 million metric tons (MT), cultivated across an area of 4.9 million hectares Stein et al.2. In Pakistan, tomatoes are grown on 150,000 hectares all year round3. Among provinces in Pakistan, Balochistan province contributes 40.3% of Pakistan’s tomato production, yielding 0.31 million tons in 2023–2024 (Agricultural Statistics Balochistan4. Tomato is affected by various diseases; among them, Fusarium wilt is the most common soil-borne disease, significantly reducing tomato production and causing losses of up to 10–80%5. According to reports, the economic losses from Fusarium wilt vary across Asian countries, and it poses a serious threat to tomato production under ideal conditions6. Amongst the diseases, fungal diseases are most prevalent and economically destructive threats to vegetable production in the region. In Balochistan, Pakistan, Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici (FOL) causes significant yield losses in tomato and is one of the most damaging hemibiotrophic pathogens in the region, resulting in losses of up to 80%. This soilborne fungus invades the vascular system of tomato plants, causing wilting and eventual plant death through mechanisms such as xylem obstruction and mycotoxin production7.

The development of resistance to diseases has always been a challenge for plant breeders. However, resistance cannot always be incorporated through conventional breeding methods because the germplasm for the resistance (R) genes is limited. Plants have evolved an innate immune system to resist microbial invasion; however, some pathogens can overcome this resistance8. Under stress conditions, plants activate many defense-responsive genes involved in (R) gene resistance in order to cope with the invasion of pathogens9.

The recognition of effectors directly or indirectly by host plant resistance (R) protein leads to effectors triggered immunity (ETI)8,10. The idea of virulence factor in the pathogen and a corresponding avirulence gene in the plant was pioneered by H. H. Flor in 195511. According to Flor’s ‘gene-for-gene’ hypothesis, the interaction of an Avirulence (Avr) gene in the pathogen and a corresponding R gene in the plant results in an incompatible pathogen interaction leading to disease resistance. R gene-mediated resistance offers absolute resistance against invading pathogens by hypersensitive response (HR) leading to cell death of the infected plant area. This can completely inhibit the spread of pathogens into adjacent healthy plant tissues by cutting off nutrient supply to that area and allowing the cells to die. This is a strategy to prevent the infection from spreading to other plant parts, thereby increasing the chance that the rest of the plant will survive. Different approaches have been used to control FOL, including chemical, biological, host resistance, and cultural practices12. However, the most effective control strategy for FOL remains management of host resistance, which offers a promising, long-term solution to mitigate the impact of this pathogen on tomato production13,14).

Expression profiling is one of the most effective approaches for defining the expression level of a specific fungal infection in tomato plants15,16. Recent research using high-throughput gene expression methods has demonstrated the implication of early immunological response in tomatoes during FOL infection. Genes such as PR1a, WRKY33, and PAL show significant increases in resistant cultivars shortly after exposure, demonstrating their roles in systemic resistance and secondary metabolite production17. Similarly, genes associated with the ethylene and jasmonic acid pathways, such as ACS2 and LOX1, have raised expression in response to stress, indicating their role in hormone-mediated defense and Biocontrol-based techniques have also been promisingly demonstrated by Sun et al.18. Moreover, Hasna et al.19, reported that priming tomato plants with Trichoderma asperellum increased tolerance to FOL by growing expression of the same defense-responsive genes. Despite these findings, there is little comparative evidence on how these markers function across genotypes with known susceptibilities. It has been reported that WRKY33 and other genes plays a positive role in providing resistance to plants against the specified necrotrophic pathogens20. The modification of gene regulation for gene expression in a direct, antagonistic manner requires WRKY33, which may act as a gene-specific transcriptional activator21.

Seasonal disease infections that affect the tomato crop have been identified as the cause of the overall yield loss, which has been increasing over time. FOL-induced wilt is one of the most prevalent and destructive diseases affecting the tomato crop. Moreover, resistant tomato cultivars are not widely available. Pakistan’s Balochistan province is endowed with abundant arable land, and a significant percentage of disease fields are affecting the seasonal tomato production. Conventional management practices that rely on chemical fungicides to control wilt disease are challenging and often ineffective.

It is hypothesized that tomato cultivars exhibit differential resistance to FOL, which is associated with the early activation of key defense-responsive genes. Fusarium wilt caused by Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici remains a major constraint to tomato production, particularly under the agro-climatic conditions of Balochistan. Therefore, the present study was designed to screen Forty-five commercial tomato cultivars for resistance against FOL under field conditions, and to evaluate the expression patterns of selected defense-related genes in resistant and susceptible cultivars to elucidate the molecular basis of host resistance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, experimental site, and field screening

Certified seeds of Forty-five commercially grown varieties, along with one highly susceptible variety Pusa Ruby (used as check variety), were obtained from Plant Breeding and Genetic Laboratory. The names of varieties and their source of origins are given in (Supplementary Table S1). The varieties were screened in severely affected farmer fields in the Quetta district (30°22’24.2"N 66°57’34.1"E) during kharif season in consecutive years 2022 and 2023. The plant to plant spacing was 36 inches, and the row distance was kept 2 m. All recommended agronomic practices for tomato cultivation were carefully followed throughout the study. A randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications was used. A highly susceptible genotype Pusa Ruby was repeatedly planted after every 5 rows of test entries. The disease symptoms were carefully monitored at different growth stages. The final wilt incidence was calculated according to Marley and Hilocks’22 method. A disease index scale of 1 to 5 was used to assess the resistance levels such as highly resistant cultivar (0–10%), moderately resistant cultivar (11–30%), susceptible cultivar, susceptible cultivar (31–50%), and highly susceptible cultivar (> 50) as shown in (Supplementary Table S2) of the tested tomato cultivars in response to F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici attack. The percentage of wilting in tomato crop was calculated following the standard method.

Isolation and morphological identification of fungal pathogen

The isolation of fungal pathogens and morphological characterization were carried out at Plant Pathology laboratory Balochistan Agriculture College, Quetta (30°16’02.8"N 66°55’47.0"E) as explained by Amr et al.23. Symptomatic stems, roots and branches showing yellowing and wilting of selected tomato cultivars were detached and then cut into tiny pieces, each measuring between 2 and 2.5 cm in length, and were sterilized with 1% NaOCl2 for a minute, followed by three consecutive rinses with distilled water. Then, samples were aseptically moved into sterile petri plates that contained potato dextrose agar (PDA), supplemented with Streptomycin sulfate (500 g/L) to suppress bacterial growth, and then were incubated at 27 °C for 7 days. The obtained pure cultures were stored at 4 °C for further use.

Visual observations of colony growth, colony color, and mycelium character (texture and color) were used to examine the morphological features of the isolated fungi after 8 ± 1 days following mycelial growth pattern. Microscopic examinations of spore morphologies (chlamydospores, macroconidia, phialides and microconidia) and hyphal characteristics produced by the fungus were examined as prescribed by Ginting24.

Molecular identification

Genomic DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Under aseptic conditions, a mycelial disc of a pure culture of isolated Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici (FOL) viz., Tom-BAC1, Tom-BAC2, Tom-BAC3, Tom-BAC4 and Tom-BAC5 as given in (Supplementary Table: S3) were inoculated individually into a conical flask containing PDB (potato dextrose broth) media. The flask was then incubated for seven days at 27 ± 1 °C. Mycelial mat that was ten days old was collected, allowed to air dry, and then utilized to isolate genomic DNA using the Cetyl (hexadecyl) Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) method as explained by Stewart and Via25 with minor modification. The extracted DNA was kept for further molecular studies at −20 °C. PCR to amplify the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS1-4) region of fungi was carried out using general primers, ITS1 forward sequence labeled as ITS1-F: (5′ -TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3′), and ITS4 reverse sequence labeled as ITS4-R: (5′ -TCCTCCCGCTTATTG ATATGC-3′) (thermoscientific), along with translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF) primer was used as, EF1–EF2:F(5′-CATCGAGAAGTTCGAGAAGG-3′) and EF1–EF2R: (5′-TACTTGAAGGAACCCTTACC-3′) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Baltics UAB, Vilnius, Lithuania). PCR amplification was carried out in 20 µL reaction volume consisting of template DNA (30 ng/2µL), 1.5 µL primer (each of ITS-1 F and ITS-4R), 10 µL of DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (is a pre-mixed solution that includes all the required ingredients in a handy packaging, such as DreamTaq DNA polymerase, optimized DreamTaq Green buffer, MgCl2, and dNTPs, to make PCR setup easier.), and 5 µL of Nuclease-free distilled water. PCR was performed using the (T100 Thermal Cycler Bio-Rad) system. Using a thermal cycler, the amplification was performed in 35 cycles, starting with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, first extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and final extension at 72 °C for 7 min.

After PCR amplification, the resulting products are examined using agarose gel (1% w/w) electrophoresis to verify their size and existence. For the (ITS1-4) region, this is typically predicted to be between 550 and 600 base pairs and for the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF) primer, this is typically predicted to be between 650 and 750 base pairs and to verify that specific DNA sections were amplified, gels were stained with ethidium bromide (10 µg/mL) and examined under Gel imaging system (iBright Fl 1500 Thermo Fisher). Additionally, the amplified DNA products were sequenced in both directions at the Centre of Excellence in Molecular Biology (CEMB) Pvt. Ltd., Lahore, Pakistan, after being eluted from the gel using a Qiagen gel elution kit as shown in (Supplementary Table S3).

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of Fusarium spp. isolates

The (ITS1-4) gene sequences obtained in this study were compared to other fungal isolate sequences retrieved from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) database using nucleotide BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi, 21 March 2025) in GenBank. Low quality regions including gapped or ambiguous regions from both reverse and forward regions of the multi-sequences were removed using AliView (Software for aligning viewing and editing DNA sequences), and aligned in MAFFT v7 software program. For phylogenetic analysis, the Fusarium staphyleae strain NRRL 22,316 was chosen as an outgroup. A phylogenetic tree was produced using the 1Q-TREE26, PAUP27, and BEAST28 tools, respectively, with analyses combining maximum likelihood (ML), maximum parsimony (MP), and Bayesian posterior probabilities.

Sowing of screened tomato seedlings and pathogenicity

Selected tomato genotypes were sown, surface sterilization of seeds for five minutes with sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl2, 5.0%, v/v), after that the seeds were rinsed with double-distilled water and then sown in plastic cups (5 × 5 cm) in three replicates, containing autoclaved peat, perlite and sand (1:1:1, v/v/v) ratio. The seedling was cultivated under controlled conditions with a temperature of 28 ± 1 °C, relative humidity between 80% and 90%, and a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod in plant growth chamber (PHcbi MLR-352 H, Japan).

The purpose of the pathogenicity test was to assess the resistance/susceptibility of selected tomato genotypes against fast growing isolate of Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici (FOL), The Fusarium spp. isolate FOL was cultured on PDA plates for 7–14 days at 25–28 °C until full mycelial growth was obtained. The fungal discs (5 mm) were transferred from PDA plate and the mycelial growth of FOL, mycelia from fast growing FOL, were transferred into sterilized flasks with Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) and incubated for 7–14 days at 25 °C on a shaker at 120 rpm to produce abundant mycelium. The mycelial mats were filtered out and blended gently in sterile water and then mixed with autoclaved soil or sand (100 g mycelium per 1 kg sterile soil) to create a uniform mycelial inoculum. Loamy soil and sand (1:1) were mixed and then autoclaved to avoid any pathogens. Each 5 L pot was filled ¾ with sterilized soil. 150–200 g of the mycelial inoculum was mixed into the top 10 cm of soil in each pot. Healthy, sprouted, and uniform seed tubers of the five cultivars were used. The tubers were dipped in 1% NaOCl2 for 3–4 min, rinsed with sterile water, and then dried. One tuber of FOL per pot was inoculated at the depth of 5–8 cm per cultivar with 3 replications. About 28 °C temperature and 75–85% relative humidity was maintained. The pots were watered regularly to keep the moist conditions. After 7, 14, 21 and 28 days of planting the symptoms were observed, such as no symptoms, Moderate wilt and yellowing, Moderate chlorosis/wilt, Severe chlorosis and wilt, and complete wilting and the cultivars were classified using disease rating scales as shown (Supplementary Table: S2).

Diaminobenzidine staining

To validate our approach for the detection of hydrogen peroxide H₂O₂ in tomato leaves, DAB staining was performed according to the method of Daudi and O’brien29, the in-situ detection of (H₂O₂) is evaluated in mature fresh symptomatic Fusarium wilt leaves by staining with (DAB). Precisely, 100 ml of distilled water were used to dissolve roughly 0.1 g of fresh DAB. 0.1 N HCl was used to keep the pH at 3.8. Fresh leaves with the classic signs of Fusarium wilt were gathered and put in Petri dishes with the DAB solution. Following eight minutes of vacuuming, samples were incubated in the DAB solution for six hours at the ideal temperature of 25 °C. After removing the DAB solution, the tissues were boiled for 10 min in 95% ethanol to brown the chlorophyll. The bleaching solution (Glycerol: acetic acid: ethanol = 1: 1: 3) was incubated at 90 °C until the tissues became transparent. Brown participates produced in the tissues were observed indicating accumulation of H₂O₂. The leaves were photographed on a white paper.

Expression profiling of defense-responsive genes via qRT-PCR

Tissue sampling and RNA preparation

To investigate transcriptional responses following pathogen exposure, foliar samples were taken from five tomato genotypes Tom/cr/pr04, Tom/cr/pr05, Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr07, and Rio-Grande 48 h after inoculation (hpi) with Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici (FOL). Healthy leaves from mock-treated plants acted as negative controls. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for later use. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NanoDrop™ spectrophotometry (Thermo Scientific) verified RNA purity and concentration, while 1% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to test integrity. To reduce genomic DNA contamination, RNase-free DNase I was used. The RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to create cDNA from 1 µg RNA.

Gene selection and primer design

Five essential defense-associated genes were chosen based on earlier research on their role in host-pathogen interactions, as mentioned below:

SlPR1a – marker of systemic acquired resistance (SAR),SlWRKY33 – transcription factor involved in biotic signaling,SlPAL – key enzyme in phenylpropanoid pathway,SlLOX1 – jasmonate biosynthesis regulator,SlACS2 – ethylene biosynthesis mediator.

Gene-specific primers were created with Primer3Plus using GenBank CDS data. The tomato Actin gene was utilized as a reference.

Real-time PCR conditions, primer and gene metadata and data analysis

qRT-PCR reactions for gene expression analyses were performed using three independent biological replicates and three technical replicates per gene using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a StepOnePlus™ system. Each 20 µL reaction included 10 µL SYBR mix, 0.5 µM primers, 2 µL cDNA, and nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol included 3 min of initial denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 58 °C. The expression levels were determined by standard method as prescribed by Livak and Schmittgen30. Moreover, the details of all studied genes are listed in (Supplementary Table S4) and Mean fold changes were adjusted to Actin gene and displayed against the untreated controls15.

Statistical analysis

A scatter plot was created from the phenotypic data in the R Studio using ggplot2 and dplyr packages. The data in terms of pathogenicity screening was subjected to one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and means were compared using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference) at probability level of p ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

Resistance responses of tomato genotypes to F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici under field conditions

Forty-five tomato genotypes and one check variety Pusa Ruby were evaluated in the field condition against Fusarium wilt, to identify potential resistant sources. None of the genotypes exhibited complete resistance; all genotypes showed some degree of wilt symptoms. The mean wilt severity (%) of two consecutive years is given in (Supplementary Table S5). The scatter plot visually highlights the trend of increasing disease severity as the resistance of the genotypes decreases, offering a clear distinction between tolerant and susceptible genotypes (Fig. 1). The two genotypes Tom/cr/pr07 (wilt severity 16.05%) and Rio-Grande (wilt severity15.93%) were categorized as resistant, exhibiting moderate wilt and yellowing symptoms, which suggests partial resistance to Fusarium wilt. The Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr05, and Tom/cr/pr04 cultivars were categorized as susceptible cultivars. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported varying levels of resistance in tomato Šimkovicová et al.31. According to Chitwood-Brown et al.13, partial resistance is a common and practical approach to managing Fusarium wilt in tomato, offering a viable solution to the disease. While 34 genotypes showed moderate susceptibility with 26–50% wilt. 5 genotypes showed severe chlorosis and wilt symptoms, while 2 genotypes, including the check variety showed complete wilting. These findings align with previous studies reported by Srinivas et al.32, which highlight that the large-scale germplasm screening is effective for identifying resistance/susceptibility among the genotypes, providing valuable insights into utilizing host resistance. Overall, the screening results confirm that none of the genotypes exhibited complete resistance against the Fusarium wilt, however, the identification of moderately resistant genotypes provided a baseline for future wet laboratory experiments, which will further validate the resistance and susceptibility of selected cultivars.

Morphological identification of Fusarium spp. isolates

Infected samples (root, stem and leaves) with typical wilt symptoms of Fusarium spp. were collected and, under aseptic conditions, inoculated onto Petri plates with PDA material using sterile forceps and needles. Tom-BAC1-5 pure culture colonies of Fusarium spp. isolates were obtained. The morphology (Front and reverse) of Fusarium spp. on PDA media and microscopic observation are shown in (Fig. 2A-F). Joshi et al.33 stated that the isolation process is the first stage of any research study since it enables the morphological and molecular investigations that follow, which shed light on the biology, behavior, and role of Fusarium spp. as fungal diseases in tomato plants.

Macro- and micro-morphological characterization was carried out using standard diagnostic criteria commonly applied for the identification of Fusarium species. Representative isolates initially produced white to woolly colonies with abundant aerial mycelium when incubated at 26 ± 2 °C, which gradually turned pink with 17 to 30 days of incubation. Microscopic examination revealed septate, hyaline hyphae and asexual reproductive structures, including macroconidia and microconidia. Microconidia were abundant, single-celled, and oval to ellipsoidal in shape, whereas macroconidia were slightly curved, thin-walled, and typically possessed 3–5 septa. These macroscopic and microscopic features are widely recognized as key diagnostic traits for Fusarium wilt pathogens as prescribed by Ginting24.

Isolates recovered from five commercial tomato varieties (Tom/cr/pr07, Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr05, Tom/cr/pr04, and Rio-Grande) were morphologically identified as Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici. The colony appearance and conidial morphology observed in the present study were consistent with previously reported descriptions of F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici34, 35, supporting accurate pathogen identification.

This indicates that the five isolates were grouped together in the phylogenetic tree based on their genetic similarities, suggesting that they are closely related and share a recent common ancestor.

According to most reports, macro- and micro-morphological characteristics are often influenced by culture conditions and may lack stability for precise taxonomic resolution37. Therefore, to overcome the limitations associated with morphology-based identification, molecular approaches such as ITS and TEF1-α gene sequencing were employed, providing a more objective and reliable framework for species-level confirmation38,39. FOSC is a broad category of soil-borne fungal pathogens that are known to cause vascular wilt diseases in more than 100 plant species and can become opportunistic pathogens in subjects, especially those with weakened immune systems40. Moreover, it is difficult to distinguish species within the same clade since they cannot be distinguished based on their physical characteristics due to the wide range of hosts they affect, or their geographic distribution41–43. Many researchers worldwide have long employed ITS1-4 region amplification and sequencing, followed by sequence analysis, to detect the fungal diseases affecting various crop plants.

Pathogenicity test and disease severity in tomato cultivars

The purpose of this test was to assess the resistance/susceptibility of selected tomato genotypes to check the disease severity on tomato cultivars against fast-growing isolate of Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici (FOL). The Fusarium sp. isolate FOL was used to inoculate the tomato cultivars. The disease development in five tomato cultivars was studied through a pathogenicity assay following pathogen inoculation. Disease severity was recorded, ranging from 2% to 80% across cultivars during the first to fourth weeks of post-inoculation (Table 2). Among the inoculated cultivars, Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr05, and Tom/cr/pr04 were found to be highly susceptible cultivars, exhibiting the highest disease severities of 72.6%, 79% and 80.3%, respectively, during the fourth week of post-inoculation.

H2O2 accumulation is reduced in resistant cultivars

DAB results staining revealed different patterns of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation between F. oxysporium inoculated susceptible and resistant tomato cultivars. Inoculated resistant cultivars, “Tom/cr/pr07” and “Rio-Grande” showed pale staining and less damage across the veins of infected leaf areas, indicating maximum reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging activities around the infected leaf tissue (Fig. 6A-B). At contrary, leaves of the inoculated susceptible cultivars “Tom/cr/pr06”, “Tom/cr/pr05” and “Tom/cr/pr04” exhibited intense brown pigment with severe damage around the veins of infected leaf areas, which indicated the excess accumulation of ROS.

DAB staining is a useful method for observing the vascular dysfunction linked to Fusarium wilt in tomato plants, which is mainly caused by FOL. This staining technique accentuates the dark brown discoloration of vascular tissues, a sign of fungal invasion and the xylem vessels obstruction that follows because of tyloses, mycelial development, and other occlusive substances12. Rio-Grande and Tom/cr/pr07, two of the cultivars under investigation, displayed the highest reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging activity around the diseased leaf tissue, with pale staining and less damage across the veins of infected leaf sections. The reason for this could be because resistant cultivars possess I-1 and I-2 genes, which trigger the defensive mechanism by generating possible ROS and upregulating pathogenesis-related proteins to limit Fusarium oxysporium’s ability to colonize plant xylem vessels.

The leaves of the inoculated susceptible cultivars Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr05, and Tom/cr/pr04, on the other hand, showed a strong brown coloration and significant damage around the veins of the infected leaf sections as compared to the control. This suggested that ROS had accumulated excessively. This can be attributed to the absence of effective resistance genes in susceptible cultivars. These deposits (ROS) prolong the symptoms of wilting by blocking water flow and aiding in the waterproofing of vessel walls. The current study findings are aligned with Fujikawa et al.44.

Gene expression analysis of defense pathways in resistant and susceptible tomato genotypes

To investigate the host resistance mechanisms of selected tomato genotypes against Fusarium wilt, gene expression analysis was carried out based on the morphological findings. Key defense genes such as PR1a, WRKY33, PAL, LOX1 and ACS2, which play important role in plant defense, were selected to assess the transcriptional accumulation of these genes in resistant and susceptible genotypes. Specially, PR1a, WRKY33 and PAL genes are integral to the salicylic acid and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, both of which are crucial for plant defense responses.

Gene expression analysis indicated significant differences in the expression of these genes between resistant and susceptible genotypes. In resistant lines (Tom/cr/pr07 and Rio-Grande) had much higher levels of PR1a, WRKY33, and PAL, showing that the salicylic acid and phenylpropanoid pathways were strongly activated. In contrast, sensitive lines (Tom/cr/pr04, Tom/cr/pr05, and Tom/cr/pr06) showed relatively modest induction of these genes (Fig. 7), which suggests a weaker activation of defense responses.

The PR1a, WRKY33, and PAL signify important molecular components of tomato immune signaling against Fusarium oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici. PR1a is a well-established marker of salicylic acid (SA)-dependent defense and systemic acquired resistance (SAR), which improves resistance by priming distal tissues and supporting transcriptional activation of pathogenesis-related proteins, that further control pathogen spread. Similarly, WRKY33 functions as a vital transcriptional regulator of defense networks, directing early immune signaling and downstream expression of defense genes. Additionally, PAL is the entry-point enzyme of the phenylpropanoid pathway, donating to the accumulation of lignin and antimicrobial phenolics, that reinforce cell walls and restrict fungal penetration and vascular colonization. Collectively, the elevated expression of these genes in moderately resistant cultivars indicates activation of SA-mediated immunity (SAR) and reinforcement of structural defenses, demonstrating an effective immune response against Fusarium wilt.

The transcriptional patterns reported here indicate a substantial relationship between gene expression levels and resistance traits. Our qRT-PCR profiling of PR1a, WRKY33, PAL, LOX11, and ACS2 at 48 hpi with F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici revealed significant differences between resistant and susceptible cultivars. Resistant lines showed significant overexpression (~ 5–6 fold) of PR1a, WRKY33 and PAL, indicating SA-mediated systemic resistance, ROS-triggered responses, and phenylpropanoid-based cell wall strengthening. This is consistent with prior studies by Hariharan and Prasannath45 and Fujikawa et al.44. The modest expression of LOX1 and ACS2in these lines suggests interaction between the jasmonate and ethylene pathways. In contrast, susceptible genotypes-maintained a near-basal level of defense gene expression, which could be attributed to impaired or delayed signaling. This finding further supports the hypothesis that a lack of effective activation of key defense genes may contribute to susceptibility to Fusarium wilt, as reported by lai et al.46. In another findings, Gujjar et al.47, evaluated the expression pattern of drought-responsive genes such as WRKY4 and others in both drought tolerant and susceptible lines of Solanum habrochaites under the drought conditions using real time PCR to authenticate the gene expression and found that qRT-PCR results were almost similar to current findings of WRKY33 gene responses and the inconsistency observed in SolyWRKY4 gene expression with array result and qRT-PCR deduced experimental data is not clear. However, the findings are attributed to the hemi-biotrophic mode of FOL pathogenesis because the FOL begins its infection cycle as a biotroph but later shifts to the necrotrophic mode of life cycle48.

SA-mediated defense activation via PR1a in resistant tomato genotypes

PR1a levels increased approximately 6.5-fold in resistant cultivars (Fig. 7), indicating substantial activation of SA-mediated expression. PR1a is SA marker gene, often considered a key indicator of the SAR pathway triggered by salicylic acid, playing a vital role in preventing the spread of disease to distal parts of the plants. Additionally, SAR is involved in regulating HR related cell death, which contributes to limiting pathogen spread. Previous studies have also demonstrated that the constitutive expression of the PR1agene enhances plant resistance to various pathogens Gao et al.49. Previous investigations report similar PR1 development in tomato lines resistant to FOL, with early PR1 accumulation aligning with less vascular colonization and reduced disease progression50.

WRKY33 as a key transcriptional node in Fusarium wilt resistance

WRKY33 expression increased by ~ 5-fold in resistant genotypes, consistent with previous research showing that Soly WRKY33 is one of the few WRKYs sharply upregulated during FOL infection, particularly in root tissues where expression increases progressively from ~ 2.8-fold at 24 hpi to ~ 5.0-fold by 48 hpi50. The fine-tuning of gene parameter for gene expression in a straight and antagonistic way, it requires WRKY and PR1 that may act as transcriptional activators or repressors in a gene-specific manner21. Functionally, WRKY33 acts as a transcriptional regulator, interacting with MAP kinase cascades and binding W-box elements to control downstream defense genes such as PR1 homologs and ROS-associated genes.

PAL-driven structural reinforcement in Fusarium wilt resistance

PAL transcripts increased by ~ 5-fold in resistant genotypes, showing activation of phenylpropanoid metabolism and lignin biosynthesis, which strengthen cell wall architecture and prevent pathogen spread. This is consistent with transcriptome investigations in FOL-resistant tomato lines, which revealed activation of PAL and downstream secondary metabolism genes during early defense phases18.

Hormonal crosstalk in resistance: role of LOX1 and ACS2

Resistant cultivars had a moderate (~ 3-fold) increase in LOX1 and ACS2 (Fig. 7), indicating coordinated activation of the JA and ET pathways. These hormones work closely with SA, either synergistically or antagonistically, to fine-tune defense responses. A molecular review underlines that JA signalling finely balances resistance, as several Fusarium spp. pathovars exploit the JA route, making its regulation crucial in limiting susceptibility18,51. Enhanced ACS2 expression is similarly related to ET production and defense priming, as demonstrated in ethylene biosynthesis mutants that influence both ripening and stress responses in tomato52.

Integrative defense model in resistant tomato genotypes

In resistant lines, expression trends support a comprehensive defense model: early and strong WRKY33 activation primes SA-responsive genes (PR1a) and phenylpropanoid synthesis (PAL), while a medium activation of JA and ET pathways (via LOX1 and ACS2) refines hormonal signaling networks38,53. In contrast, susceptible genotypes showed insufficient or delayed activation across these pathways, indicating compromised immune coordination. These patterns are consistent with transcriptome investigations of resistant and susceptible interactions18.

Conclusion

Tomato is an important and valuable vegetable crop; it is severely affected by the F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici in Balochistan. In this study, 45 tomato genotypes, along with one check variety, were screened for resistance against Fusarium wilt to identify potential source of resistance. Five better performing genotypes were selected for isolation and identification of wilt causing pathogen, and results revealed that F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici was identified as causal agent in all commercial tomato cultivars grown in Balochistan. Pathogenicity assays indicated that cultivars Tom/cr/pr06, Tom/cr/pr05, and Tom/cr/pr04 were highly susceptible to Fusarium wilt. Whereas, Tom/cr/pr07 and Rio Grande exhibited partial resistance, similar findings were observed during DAB staining demonstration of the same cultivars. Gene expression profiling further supported the partial resistance in these cultivars, which indicated a coordinate defense response likely involving in key resistance genes. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the transcriptional architecture that underpins tomato resistance against F. oxysporium f. sp. lycopersici. Higher expression of PR1a, WRKY33, and PAL was observed in resistant genotypes (Tom/cr/pr07 and Rio Grande) indicates the activation of a coordinated SA-dependent and phenylpropanoid-mediated defense axis. Concurrent moderate induction of LOX1 and ACS2 suggests that the JA and ethylene signaling pathways may have a modulatory function. Taken together, these findings establish PR1a and WRKY33 as strong molecular indicators of resistance, supporting their use in marker-assisted selection for breeding FOL-resilient cultivars. Further research should focus on the validation of resistance across a broader range of germplasm and on conducting functional characterization of resistance alleles to accelerate resistance breeding in tomato cultivars.
