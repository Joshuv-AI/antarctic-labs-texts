Comparative histopathological and immunological analysis of LRV2-positive and LRV2-negative Leishmania tropica in a BALB/c mouse cutaneous leishmaniasis model
Authors: Mustafa Öztatlıcı, Hülya Öztatlıcı, Süha Kenan Arserim, İ Cüneyt Balcıoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeishmaniasis is a vector-borne disease endemic in more than 99 countries and manifests primarily as cutaneous, mucocutaneous, or visceral forms. This study aimed to assess the impact of Leishmania RNA virus 2 (LRV2) on immune responses and disease pathology in mice infected with Leishmania tropica. BALB/c mice were inoculated subcutaneously in the right hind footpad with L. tropica either lacking or carrying LRV2 (LRV2– and LRV2 + groups, respectively) and monitored for 24 weeks. Footpad and liver tissues were collected post-sacrifice, and parasite presence was determined using microscopy, culture, and molecular methods. Biomarkers of inflammation (IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β1), oxidative stress (iNOS and eNOS), and apoptosis (Caspase 3) were analyzed by immunohistochemistry and qPCR. Leukocyte infiltration was observed in footpad lesions of both LRV2– and LRV2 + groups; however, lesion sizes did not differ significantly between them. In footpad tissues, TNF-α, TGF-β1, eNOS, and Caspase 3 expression levels were significantly higher in both experimental groups compared to controls. In liver tissues, IL-6 and eNOS expression was significantly elevated in both experimental groups relative to controls, while TNF-α expression was notably higher in the LRV2 + group. Furthermore, both footpad and liver tissues from LRV2 + mice showed increased eNOS and iNOS expression compared to LRV2– mice. Overall, these findings indicate that the presence of LRV2 does not significantly influence inflammatory cytokine production or apoptosis in footpad and liver tissues, but it markedly enhances oxidative stress in both.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-025-08562-5.Keywords: Leishmania RNA virus 2 (LRV2), Leishmania tropica, Cytokines, Cutaneous Leishmaniasis, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Leishmaniasis is a parasitic disease caused by more than 20 species of Leishmania (L.). It manifests in three main clinical forms: cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL), visceral leishmaniasis (VL), and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis. CL is the most common form and is primarily caused by L. tropica, L. major, L. infantum, and L. donovani. While L. tropica is responsible for anthroponotic CL, L. major causes zoonotic CL (Desjeux 2004; Haddadan et al. 2023).

The life cycle of Leishmania involves both vertebrate hosts and sand fly vectors. During a blood meal, sand flies ingest amastigotes from an infected host. In the midgut of the vector, these transform into promastigotes, multiply, and subsequently develop into infective metacyclic promastigotes. Transmission to new vertebrate hosts occurs during later blood meals (Killick-Kendrick 1979; Meinecke et al. 1999).

From an epidemiological perspective, leishmaniasis is endemic in 99 countries, placing over 1 billion people at risk. Each year, an estimated 50,000–90,000 new VL cases and more than 1 million CL cases occur. The majority of CL cases are reported from the Americas, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and Central Asia (WHO 2024).

Pathogenesis is characterized by the ability of amastigotes to survive intracellularly within macrophages, triggering a complex inflammatory response. Disease severity varies with the infecting species and the host immune response, ranging from localized cutaneous lesions to severe systemic disease. For example, L. tropica infection has been associated with elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-10, and iNOS in humans (Rostamian and Niknam 2019). Moreover, the presence of Leishmania RNA viruses can modulate host immunity; endosymbiotic dsRNA viruses identified in L. aethiopica isolates have been shown to amplify pro-inflammatory cytokine responses (Zangger et al. 2014).

Diagnosis of leishmaniasis typically relies on microscopic detection of amastigotes in Giemsa-stained preparations, culture of promastigotes, or PCR and antigen detection methods. Treatment options comprise pentavalent antimonials, amphotericin B, miltefosine, paromomycin, and pentamidine. The choice of therapy depends on the infecting species, disease severity, and patient-specific factors such as pregnancy (Tarr et al. 1988; Mehregan et al. 1999; El-Hassan and Zijlstra 2001; Olivier et al. 2012; Matheoud et al. 2013; Theel and Pritt 2016; Davidson 2017; Özbilgin et al. 2020).

An interesting aspect of Leishmania biology is the existence of Leishmania RNA viruses (LRVs), which were first reported in 1988. These viruses belong to the family Totiviridae and are found in both Old and New World Leishmania species. LRVs may contribute to more severe disease manifestations by increasing the pathogenicity of host Leishmania species. However, studies in some regions show that LRV infection may not be directly related to the severity of the disease. A study conducted in Brazil found that the presence of LRV1 was not associated with mucosal involvement (Pereira et al. 2013). The co-evolution of these viruses with Leishmania hosts suggests an adaptive advantage for the parasite, potentially aiding its transmission and persistence (Tarr et al. 1988). LRV is divided into two species: LRV1, which infects New World parasites, and LRV2, which infects Old World parasites. There is less than 40% genetic similarity between LRV1 and LRV2. LRV2 was discovered in L. major in 1995 and differs from LRV1 in amino acid sequences (Çavuş et al. 2017). Studies show that viruses and Leishmania parasites have evolved together and have similar phylogenetic trees. It has also been reported that viral dsRNA stimulates the production of pro-inflammatory interferon-β, leading to chronic inflammation and increased metastatic potential of L. guyanensis (Rossi et al. 2017). These findings suggest that virus-carrying Leishmania isolates have a survival advantage in animal models (Grybchuk et al. 2018).

Due to the limited number of studies on the LRV2 virus, information regarding its impact on the pathogenicity of CL remains insufficient. In the Old World, L. tropica and L. major are the most common causative agents of cutaneous leishmaniasis (Harman 2015). Historically, L. tropica has been responsible for anthroponotic CL in Turkey (Yildiz Zeyrek et al. 2020). Therefore, this study aimed to assess the impact of Leishmania RNA virus 2 (LRV2) on immune responses and disease pathology in mice infected with Leishmania tropica. In the present study, two L. tropica strains were used; one lacking and one carrying LRV2. These isolates were obtained from patients with CL in Turkey and had been previously characterized.

By using both strains, we sought to establish whether the presence of LRV2 alters infectivity and host immune–histopathological responses when tested under identical conditions. In this context, we investigated the histopathology of lesions in the footpad and liver tissues of uninfected and infected (w/wo LRV2) mice. Additionally, we analyzed the mRNA/protein expression of interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) to assess the immune response; endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) for oxidative stress; Caspase 3 for apoptosis. This is the first study to examine the histopathological and immunological changes caused by L. tropica isolates w/wo LRV2 in an animal model of CL.

Materials and methods

Cultivation of Leishmania tropica isolates

In this study, a total of 10 Leishmania tropica isolates stored in the Parasite Bank of the Faculty of Medicine, Manisa Celal Bayar University, were initially screened for the presence of Leishmania RNA virus 2 (LRV2) using PCR, as previously described (Karabulut et al. 2023). Based on the screening results, one LRV2-positive isolate (MHOM/TR/2018/CBU61) and one LRV2-negative isolate (MHOM/TR/2017/CBU56) were selected for detailed in vivo analyses. The rationale for using two strains was to enable a direct comparison of their biological behavior in vivo and to determine whether LRV2 presence has any impact on infectivity and host tissue responses. These isolates were obtained from Turkey and have been previously characterized (Karabulut et al. 2023). The isolates were inoculated into Novy–MacNeal–Nicolle (NNN) medium and cultured at 25 °C. The media were monitored for growth by preparing slide smears and examining them under a light microscope on consecutive days. Promastigotes obtained from the NNN medium were transferred to RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% gentamicin (Gibco). Culture flasks were examined daily for growth using an inverted microscope, and promastigotes in the logarithmic growth phase were counted under a light microscope using a Thoma hemocytometer (Çavuş et al. 2017).

Detection of Leishmania RNA virus 2 in Leishmania tropica isolates

For the detection of LRV2 in L. tropica samples, after cultivation, the samples were placed in a sterile Falcon tube and centrifuged at 2500 × g for 5 min. The pellet was then resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the centrifugation process was repeated. Total RNA isolation was performed using the Total RNA Purification Kit (TR01, GeneMark, Taiwan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and concentration were assessed using the MaestroNano (MaestroGen, USA) instrument. The obtained RNA samples were immediately transcribed into cDNA using the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA™ (4,368,814, Invitrogen) kit.

For LRV2 detection, the following primers were used:

LRV2: F-TGACTGGCGTTTTGGACTTG and R-GCCACCTACACCCATGAACA

Reference gene (Kinetoplastid Membrane Protein-11, KMP-11): F-AGATGCAGGAACAGAACGCC and R-TGCTTGAAGTGCTCCGAGTG (Kurt et al. 2019; Karabulut et al. 2023).

Reference gene (Kinetoplastid Membrane Protein-11, KMP-11): F-AGATGCAGGAACAGAACGCC and R-TGCTTGAAGTGCTCCGAGTG (Kurt et al. 2019; Karabulut et al. 2023).

Conventional PCR was performed in a 25 µL final reaction volume, consisting of 1 µL of undiluted cDNA, 5 µL of 5X PCR Dye Master Mix II (GMBiolab), 1 µL of forward and reverse primer mix, and 17 µL of nuclease-free water. The PCR thermal cycling program was as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 30 s of denaturation at 95 °C, 45 s of annealing at 55 °C, and 3 min of extension at 72 °C, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplicons were visualized using SYBR® Safe DNA gel stain (Invitrogen, USA) in 2% agarose gel electrophoresis (Kurt et al. 2019).

Animals and study approval

This study was performed with the approval of the Animal Experiment Review Committee of Manisa Celal Bayar University, Faculty of Medicine (07/06/2022/77.637.435/228). Fifteen healthy BALB/c male mice were used as an experimental model of infection. The animals were obtained from the animal facility of the Manisa Celal Bayar University, Turkey. Mice aged 4–6 weeks were kept at 23 ± 2 °C with 55% ± 5% humidity, a 12-h dark and 12-h light cycle, and food and water were given ad libitum and maintained in individually ventilated cages. Ethical regulations were strictly followed according to national and institutional guidelines.

Establishment of cutaneous leishmaniasis infection model

In the experiment, 4–6-week-old male BALB/c mice weighing 25–30 g (n = 15) were used. The animals were randomly divided into three groups: control (n = 5), L. tropica LRV negative (LRV2 −, n = 5), and L. tropica LRV positive (LRV2 +, n = 5). Mice in the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups were subcutaneously inoculated in the right hind footpad with an L. tropica suspension prepared at a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ promastigotes/mL. The control group received an equal volume of PBS in the same region. The thickness of the footpads with CL lesions was measured weekly for 6 months using a digital caliper with an accuracy of 0.01 mm. All animals in each group were included for histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses. At the end of the experiment, all animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation following the administration of an overdose of anesthesia (75 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine). Footpad and liver tissues were immediately harvested, and each sample was divided into five equal parts using a sterile surgical blade. One portion was fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histopathology and immunohistochemistry. Another portion was washed twice with ice-cold sterile Ca2⁺- and Mg2⁺-free PBS, centrifuged, and the pellet was stored in liquid nitrogen for RNA isolation and relative gene expression analysis. A third portion was inoculated into NNN medium and incubated at 25 °C. A fourth portion was stored at – 20 °C for DNA isolation and qRT-PCR to detect the presence of the parasite. The final portion was used for touch preparation and stained with Giemsa.

Detection of Leishmania tropica in footpad and liver tissues

DNA isolation was performed on portions of footpad and liver tissues allocated for molecular methods using the High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit (11,796,828,001, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. To detect L. tropica in DNA-isolated tissue samples, the Leishmania spp. internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) probe qRT-PCR test was applied. The ribosomal ITS1 region, which separates the genes encoding the SSU rRNA and 5.8S rRNA of Leishmania parasites, was amplified using the following primers: Forward primer: 5′-CTGGATCATTTTCCGATG-3′, Reverse primer: 5′-GAAGCCAAGTCATCCATCGC-3′, along with the QuantiTect Probe PCR Kit Master mix and the specific probes listed below:

Probe 1: 5′-CCGTTTATACAAAAAATATACGGCGTTTCGGTTT-Fluo-3′

Probe 1: 5′-CCGTTTATACAAAAAATATACGGCGTTTCGGTTT-Fluo-3′

Probe 2: 5′-LCRed-640-GCGGGGTGGGTGCGTGTGTG-Pho-3′ (Toz et al. 2013; Özbilgin et al. 2023).

Probe 2: 5′-LCRed-640-GCGGGGTGGGTGCGTGTGTG-Pho-3′ (Toz et al. 2013; Özbilgin et al. 2023).

The prepared 20 µL reaction mixture contained 4.5 µL H2O (PCR-grade water), 1 µL forward primer, 1 µL reverse primer, 0.5 µL probe 1, 0.5 µL probe 2, 12.5 µL QuantiTect Probe PCR Kit Master mix, and 5 µL genomic DNA. The thermal profile used to identify intra-species differences (distinguishing L. tropica, L. infantum, and L. major) consisted of denaturation, amplification, melting curve analysis, and cooling steps. It was saved as a working protocol in the Rotor-Gene Q device’s program. Results were obtained through melting curve analysis (Toz et al. 2013).

Histopathological study

Infected footpad and liver tissues from all groups were fixed with formalin for 48 h. The tissues were dehydrated in ascending grades of alcohol, cleared with xylene (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), and infiltrated with molten paraffin (Surgipath, Cambridgeshire, UK). The 5-μm thick sections were obtained using a rotary microtome (RM 2135, Leica). Two sets of serial sections were prepared; the first set was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological examination, while the second set was used for immunohistochemical staining, as described below. H&E-stained sections were examined with a light microscope (DM750, Leica, Heerbrugg, Switzerland), and photomicrographs were taken with a camera (DP26-CU, Olympus, Germany).

Immunohistochemical assay

Paraffin-embedded footpad and liver sections were used for immunohistochemical staining. All sections were kept at 60 °C overnight and afterward dewaxed with xylene for 30 min. Then, after hydration with an ethyl alcohol series (100%, 96%, 80%, 70%, and 60%), they were rinsed three times with distilled water. A 2% trypsin (ScyTek Laboratories, Logan, Utah, USA) was applied for 15 min at 37 °C and sections were incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution for 15 min to inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, footpad and liver sections were incubated with anti-Caspase 3 (sc-56053), anti-eNOS (sc-376542), anti-iNOS (sc-7271), anti-IL-6 (orb6210), anti-TNF-α (sc-133192), and anti-TGF-β1 (sc-130348) primary antibodies in a 1/200 dilution, for 18 h at + 4 °C. After incubating with a secondary antibody (TP-125-HL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 15 min, sections were incubated with diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB, ACK125, ScyTek) for 5 min to detect the immunoreactivity. For counterstaining, Mayer’s hematoxylin was performed (HMM999, ScyTek). Finally, the sections were cleared in xylene and covered with a mounting medium (01730, Surgipath), and observed with a light microscope (Birinci et al. 2021; Eren et al. 2023). Quantifications of staining intensities were performed using the free version of Fiji (ImageJ ver. 2.0), an image processing software, as previously described (Algaidi et al. 2019). Briefly, photomicrographs of five different non-overlapping fields were taken at × 200 (microscopic field area of 695.65µm2) and × 400 (microscopic field area of 347.83 µm2) magnification for footpad and liver tissues, respectively. Each image was color-deconvoluted into three distinct colors (green, brown, and blue) using a DAB vector (the H-DAB function). The mean gray value of five non-overlapping regions of stained tissue was measured in order to calibrate the brown DAB image. The intensity numbers were measured and converted into the OD using the following equation: OD = log (max intensity/mean intensity), where the max intensity = 250 and the mean intensity = mean gray value of the photomicrograph.

RNA isolation and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

According to the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA from homogenized footpad and liver tissues was isolated using the Total RNA Purification Kit (TR01, GeneMark, Taiwan). RNA quality and concentration were determined using the MaestroNano (MaestroGen, USA) instrument. The obtained RNAs were immediately reverse transcribed into cDNA using the High Capacity RNA-to-cDNA™ (4,368,814, Invitrogen) kit. For each reaction, 1 μg of total RNA was added and incubated at 25 °C for 10 min, at 37 °C for 120 min, and at 85 °C for 5 min, and cDNA conversions were made from all RNA samples. Forward (F) and reverse (R) primer sets of target genes were purchased from Sentegen Biotech (Ankara, Turkey). The cDNAs were stored at – 80 °C until use. The obtained cDNAs were used as templates in PCR reactions at equal concentrations (10 ng), with a total volume of 20 μl, Power SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (4,367,659, Thermo Scientific), and 500 nM forward and reverse primer mix. qPCR was performed in triplicate for each target and sample using the Rotor-Gene Q Real-Time PCR System (Qiagen); a negative control (water) was added for each sample. The qPCR temperature program comprised the following steps: 10 min initial denaturation at 95 °C, 40 cycles of 15 s denaturation at 95 °C, and 1 min annealing/extension at 60 °C. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the reference gene, and the relative gene expression differences were calculated by the 2−∆∆Ct method (Özbilgin et al. 2021; Hasdemir et al. 2024). Statistical analysis

The immunohistochemical data were obtained from five representative non-overlapping fields that were captured as photomicrographs. qPCR was performed in triplicate for each target and sample (Algaidi et al. 2019; Hasdemir et al. 2024). Mean and standard deviation (SD) values of immunohistochemical optical density and qPCR were calculated. The data obtained from the experimental groups were statistically compared using PRISM v7 software (GraphPad, California, USA). A one-way ANOVA test, and the Tukey test as a post-hoc test were used for statistical analysis. Data are given as mean ± SD. P < 0.05 values were considered statistically significant.

Results

L. tropica culture and detection of LRV2

To evaluate the effects of LRV2 presence on the immune response and disease pathology in experimental animals infected with Leishmania tropica, the identified LRV2-positive L. tropica (MHOM/TR/2018/CBU61) and LRV2-negative L. tropica (MHOM/TR/2017/CBU56) isolates were used (Fig. 1A, B).

Infection with LRV2 − and LRV2 + L. tropica in the footpad resulted in similar non-ulcerative lesions in CL

We investigated the footpads of mice for cutaneous lesions after the injections of only PBS (control group), LRV2 − L. tropica (LRV2 − group), and LRV2 + L. tropica (LRV2 + group). After the third week of infection, mice in the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups developed cutaneous lesions, which were characterized by swelling on the infected footpad (Fig. 2A–C). The footpads of the mice were also measured for 24 weeks from the beginning of the experiment. No significant difference was observed in the footpad size of the mice in the control group. After 3 weeks of no symptoms, the infection developed into a nodule-like, non-ulcerated, and self-limited inflammatory lesion that continued to grow in size even at 24 weeks in LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups. The increases in footpad size in the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups were determined to be statistically significant when compared with the control group (p < 0.05). There was no significant footpad size difference between LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups of mice during the experimental period (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2D, Table S1).

After sacrifice, some pieces of the footpads and liver tissues of the animals were separated for culture incubation, touch preparation, and qRT-PCR to detect the presence of parasites. At the end of a 15-day incubation, no growth was observed in the footpad and liver tissue samples of the control group, and no growth was seen in the liver samples of the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups. However, L. tropica promastigotes grew in the footpad cultures of the LRV2 − (Fig. 3A) and LRV2 + groups (Fig. 3B). In the microscopic examination of touch preparations with Giemsa staining, L. tropica amastigotes were observed in the footpad samples of the LRV2 − (Fig. 3C) and LRV2 + groups (Fig. 3D). Amastigotes had visible nuclei and kinetoplasts, the extranuclear aggregates of minicircle DNA. L. tropica amastigotes were not found in liver touch preparations in all groups. qRT-PCR examination of footpad tissue samples in the LRV2 + and LRV2 − groups revealed the presence of L. tropica DNA (Fig. 3E). The control group samples did not contain any L. tropica DNA. Furthermore, no L. tropica DNA was detected in the qRT-PCR analysis of liver tissue samples from all groups.

LRV2 − and LRV2 + L. tropica infection increased the inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in the footpad

We investigated the important markers related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis by immunohistochemistry, a method to measure the protein expression, as well as qPCR, a method to determine the mRNA levels. According to our immunohistochemistry results, no significant change was observed in IL-6 cytokine expressions in the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups compared with the control group (p > 0.05). The mRNA levels of IL-6 were also correlated with protein expression (p > 0.05). Both mRNA and protein expressions of TNF-α and TGF-β1 significantly increased in the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups (p < 0.05). The results of eNOS, one of the oxidative stress markers, showed that the immunoreactivity of this enzyme statistically increased in infected LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups compared to the control (p < 0.05). The mRNA levels of eNOS also increased similarly to protein levels in both infected groups (p < 0.05). Moreover, the increase was significantly higher in the virus-containing LRV2 + group than the LRV2 − group (p < 0.05). The other oxidative stress marker, iNOS, had increased mRNA levels in LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups. Similar to eNOS mRNA results, the virus-containing LRV2 + group showed higher iNOS mRNA levels than the LRV2 − group, and these results were statistically significant (p < 0.05). In addition to mRNA, the immunostaining intensity of iNOS was increased in the LRV2 + group compared to the control and LRV2 − groups (p < 0.05), and there was no difference between control and LRV2 − groups (p > 0.05). We analyzed the Caspase 3 to detect apoptosis in the footpads. The Caspase 3 expression significantly increased in both LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups (p < 0.05). In the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups, mRNA levels showed a significant increase compared to the control group, parallel to the immunostaining results (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5, Table 2, and Table S2).

LRV2 − and LRV2 + L. tropica infection increased mostly oxidative stress, not apoptosis, in the liver

As a result of immunohistochemical staining of liver tissues of uninfected and infected mice, IL-6 expression showed statistically higher immunoreactivity in LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups than in the control group (p < 0.05). The mRNA levels of IL-6 were correlated with the immunostaining and were increased in both infected groups compared to the control (p < 0.05). TNF-α mRNA levels were significantly higher in LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups than in the control (p < 0.05), while the protein expressions increased in only the LRV2 + group (p < 0.05), compared to the control group. Although it was observed that TGF-β1 mRNA levels increased in LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups compared to the control group (p < 0.05), it was determined that this mRNA increase did not affect protein expression between the groups (p > 0.05). Both mRNA and protein levels of eNOS and iNOS were found to be significantly increased in the LRV2 + group (p < 0.05). Additionally, the mRNA levels of these oxidative stress markers were significantly higher in the LRV2 + than the LRV2 − group (p < 0.05). Immunoreactivity and mRNA results for Caspase 3 were similar in all groups, and there was no significant difference (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6, Table 3, and Table S3).

Discussion

The clinical form of CL is the most prevalent manifestation both in Turkey and worldwide. CL causes skin lesions, including papules, nodules, and ulcers, particularly on exposed areas of the body (Harman 2015). The most common species causing CL in Turkey is L. tropica, followed by L. infantum, L. Major, and L. donovani. Various factors that can influence Leishmania infection include the parasite species and load, the presence of LRV, the virulence factors of the isolate, and the host immune response (dos Santos Meira and Gedamu 2019). LRV is classified into two species. New World parasites are infected by LRV1, while Old World parasites are infected by LRV2. LRV is reported to affect Leishmania pathogenesis and the host immune system (Mirabedini et al. 2023). Some studies on LRV1 suggest that the virus has an effect on the severity of CL (Rahmanipour et al. 2023) and is particularly associated with the development of mucocutaneous leishmaniasis and treatment failure (Adaui et al. 2016; Bourreau et al. 2016). However, a few studies have reported that LRV1 plays no role in disease severity (Abtahi et al. 2020; Ginouvès et al. 2021). There is also limited information regarding the effect of LRV2 on the pathogenicity of CL due to the small number of studies conducted with the LRV2 virus (Rahmanipour et al. 2023).

Saura et al. stated that the elimination of LRV2 from L. major led to profound transcriptional changes and impaired growth (Saura et al. 2022). In the experimental animal model of CL in mice, the average lesion sizes in groups formed with LRV2 + and LRV2 − L. major isolates, were larger in the LRV2 + isolates than in the LRV2 − isolates (Saberi et al. 2022). On the contrary, in our CL mouse model study with LRV2 + and LRV2 − L. tropica isolates, no difference was found between the average lesion sizes in the LRV2 + and LRV2 − groups. After sacrifice, L. tropica amastigotes were detected in touch preparations of footpad tissues of the LRV2 + and LRV2 − groups, and L. tropica promastigotes were detected in footpad cultures. PCR analysis of footpad tissues confirmed the presence of L. tropica DNA in all infected groups. Furthermore, PCR analysis of L. tropica parasites isolated from the footpad lesions of BALB/c mice confirmed the presence of LRV2 in the LRV2(+) group, demonstrating that the virus persisted in vivo throughout the infection period. L. tropica primarily causes cutaneous leishmaniasis; however, it has rarely been reported to cause visceral leishmaniasis (Magill et al. 1993). Therefore, liver histopathology was included in the study to assess potential systemic dissemination and to investigate whether LRV2 influences hepatic immune responses. Hematoxylin-and eosin staining of footpad and liver sections from the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups, revealed that amastigotes were present in footpad, but not liver samples. As parasites were not detected in liver tissue preparations, in culture, or via PCR analysis, this suggests that the infection was limited to the footpad. Histopathological findings were consistent with the results of touch preparation, culture, and molecular analyses. These results suggest that the presence of LRV2 did not influence the clinical manifestations of the L. tropica isolate.

There is an urgent need for new treatments for CL because many of the drugs currently used to treat patients are expensive, toxic, and require multiple rounds of treatment. Moreover, treatments have high failure rates, possibly because they target only the parasite and do not attenuate the immunopathological responses that lead to disease in many forms of CL (Scott and Novais 2016). Proinflammatory cytokine production and type 1 T helper (Th1) cellular response are important components of immune protection against CL, but overproduction of proinflammatory cytokines has been associated with severe immunopathology (Maspi et al. 2016). Therefore, in addition to the development of new drugs targeting the parasite, detailed investigation of the histopathological and immunological changes observed in CL is also necessary. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine produced by a variety of cell types, including macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), and T cells, acting as both a pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokine (Scheller et al. 2011). IL-6 promotes hepatocyte activation, regulation of hematopoiesis, and plasma cell growth (Kling et al. 2011). Titus et al. reported that the production of both Th1 and Th2 cytokines decreased in L. major-infected IL-6 knockout BALB/c mice, but there were no significant differences between lesion size in IL-6-knockout and wild-type (WT) mice (Titus et al. 2001). In our study, it was determined that IL-6 cytokine expression did not change in the footpad tissues of all groups. On the other hand, IL-6 expression was observed to be significantly increased in the liver tissues of the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups compared to the control group. However, it was determined that there was no significant difference in IL-6 expression between the LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups in the footpad and liver tissues, thus the presence of LRV did not change the IL-6 expression in the lesion area.

TNF-α is a crucial pro-inflammatory cytokine in the inflammatory cascade, activating the Th1 immune response, and enhancing the activity of macrophages (Arens et al. 2018; Bosch-Nicolau et al. 2019). A positive correlation between lesion size and TNF-α levels has been reported in patients with CL (Scott and Novais 2016; Maspi et al. 2016). As expected, we found that TNF-α expression in footpad tissues was significantly increased in both LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups. A significant increase was observed only in the LRV2 + group in liver tissues. TNF-α is responsible for activating the leishmanicidal activity of macrophages and leads to the induction of iNOS (Allenbach et al. 2006; Viana et al. 2013). iNOS catalyzes the synthesis of nitric oxide (NO), a potent microbicidal agent that leads to the death of intracellular parasites (Viana et al. 2013; Kopelyanskiy et al. 2022). It has been reported that TNF-α and iNOS expressions are significantly increased in lesion biopsies of CL patients infected with L. tropica compared to skin biopsies of healthy individuals (Kumar et al. 2010). In this study, we found that iNOS expression was increased in the footpads of both infected groups, similar to TNF expression. The highest iNOS expression levels were detected in virus-containing LRV2 + groups in both footpad and liver tissues. The expression of eNOS, another oxidative stress marker, was found to be significantly higher in both LRV2 − and LRV2 + groups in footpad tissues. Moreover, the increase was significantly higher in the LRV2 + group than the LRV2 − group. In the liver tissues, a significant increase in eNOS expression was observed only in the LRV2 + group. These results suggest that the presence of the LRV2 virus had a higher impact on inflammation and oxidative stress in leishmaniasis pathology.

TGF-β1 is a pleiotropic growth factor that has important anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties. It is produced by different cells, including Tregs, monocytes, neutrophils, and DCs. The regulation of immune responses against foreign pathogens, preservation of homeostasis, and enhancement of immunological tolerance rely on TGF-β1 cytokine and Treg cells (Maspi et al. 2016). TGF-β1 expression has been reported as significantly increased in skin lesions of hamsters (Montoya et al. 2018) and skin, spleen, liver, and heart tissues of mice (Al-Muhsin Al-Khayat et al. 2021) in CL models formed by L. (V) braziliensis in hamsters and L. major in BALB/c mice. Similarly, increased TGF-β1 expression has been shown in human skin lesions infected with L. major (Hejazi et al. 2012) and L. tropica (Taslimi et al. 2020). In this study, the expression of TGF-β1 was significantly elevated in both experimental groups relative to controls. However, no difference in TGF-β1 expression between the infected groups was observed. These findings suggest that the presence of the LRV2 virus in L. tropica does not appear to alter the level of TGF-β1 expression in the lesion region. Increased mRNA levels of TGF-β1 were seen in liver tissues of infected mice, but this increase was limited and was not translated into protein. Studies have reported that increased TGF-β1 in plasma and biopsies of patients with diffuse CL contributes to parasite proliferation (dos Santos Meira and Gedamu 2019).

While some studies suggest that Leishmania parasites can suppress the death of the original host cell, thereby leaving the niche intact, other studies have suggested that Leishmania parasites induce cell death of the infected host cell, thereby increasing the possibility of cell-to-cell transmission. Baars et al. reported increased Caspase 3 expression and apoptosis in the skin lesions of mice infected with L. major (Baars et al. 2023). Another study stated that Caspase 3 expression increased during the spread of L. aethiopica among human macrophages, but no such increase was found in L. mexicana (Ranatunga et al. 2020). In our study, when we compared the footpad tissues of LRV2 − and LRV2 + mice with the control group, we found a slight increase in Caspase 3 expression. Nevertheless, it was observed that Caspase 3 expression did not change in liver tissues in any groups. These findings suggest that L. tropica increases apoptosis at the lesion site, but the presence of LRV2 has no effect on Caspase 3 expression.

Conclusion

Our study revealed the effects of LRV2 on inflammatory responses and cellular processes in different tissues. L. tropica infection caused non-ulcerative lesions and leukocyte infiltration on the footpad. The presence of LRV2 did not result in any major variations in inflammatory cytokine production and apoptosis in the footpad and liver compared to the absence of LRV2. However, the presence of LRV2 showed a significant increase in oxidative stress in both footpad and liver tissues. Although it has been reported that the presence of the virus may provide survival support to a few Leishmania parasites, this hypothesis has not been investigated in a significant number of Leishmania species. To our knowledge, our study is the first to demonstrate the effects of the LRV2 presence in L. tropica. These findings provided important information for understanding the complex effects of LRV2 on host immune responses and shed light on future research. Additionally, different Old World Leishmania species infected with LRV2 should be examined to evaluate the pathological and immunological effects of the viruses.

While our study provides initial insights into the effects of LRV2 on L. tropica pathogenesis, we recognize that genetic heterogeneity among isolates may influence both virulence and immune responses. Given the limited number of isolates available, broader investigations using larger and genetically diverse collections of L. tropica are required to fully delineate the role of LRV2 in pathogenesis. Our findings suggest that the presence of LRV2 in L. tropica may not markedly alter clinical disease severity, but could influence qualitative aspects of the inflammatory response, indicating a potential indirect role in disease progression. Future large-scale studies with genetically diverse isolates will be essential to clarify the immunomodulatory capacity of LRV2 and its contribution to host–parasite interactions.
