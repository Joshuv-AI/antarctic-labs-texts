Multivariate stochastic generation of meteorological data for building simulation through interdependent meteorological processes
Authors: Zhichao Jiao, Jihui Yuan, Craig Farnham, Kazuo Emura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, the uncertainty of weather conditions and the impact of future climate change on building energy assessment has received increasing attention. As an important part of these studies, several types of methods for generating stochastic meteorological data have also been developed. Since solar radiation drops to zero at night, unlike the continuous 24-hour data for elements such as temperature and humidity, this has posed challenges for previous research to fully account for the simultaneity among multiple elements. Therefore, this study proposes a framework for meteorological data generation: First, perform multivariate time series modeling of meteorological data of air temperature, solar radiation and absolute humidity at 12:00 of each day of a typical year based on the S-vine copula method and simulating daily series data at 12:00 for 365 days. Then, based on the probability of change of each element evaluated from the historical meteorological observation data, the daily series data at 12:00 were expanded to 24 h, after which the yearly stochastic weather data were obtained. The analysis of 30 years of stochastic data generated by this method, compared with the original data, reveals that air temperature and solar radiation closely match the original distribution characteristics, except for a minor deviation in the absolute humidity’s kurtosis. Furthermore, the comparison of thermal load distributions for office buildings shows that the original data curve falls within the range of the generated data. This suggests that the generated data includes more information about uncertainty but still keeps the original data’s characteristics.Keywords: Stochastic weather generator, Multivariate time series, Building energy simulation, S-vine copula, Statistical downscalingSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Humans spend about 80–90% of their time inside buildings and it is well recognized that more than 30% of global energy consumption comes from building energy processes1,2. Therefore, it is very important to estimate the indoor environmental comfort as well as the energy consumption of the building3. Dynamic building simulation is a commonly used method and a very important element of it is meteorological data that can be used as input data to represent the outside weather conditions4. The meteorological data usually consist of multi-year of actual observations5 or a Typical Meteorological Year (TMY)6,7. However, some underdeveloped regions have insufficient meteorological observation data, or some phenomena such as extreme weather and continuous high temperatures are statistically consistent with local climatic characteristics but not observed, which may affect the uncertainty of the building simulation8,9. Furthermore, with the increasing global warming, it is also a great challenge to create meteorological data to design and retrofit existing buildings to meet future needs based on possible future meteorological conditions10,11. Therefore, it is essential to generate stochastic meteorological data based on the characteristics of the local climate for different requirements of use12,13.

Due to the limitations of historical observation weather data for building simulation, researchers have begun to experiment with synthetic weather data that can simulate weather behavior14,15. The weather generators can be roughly categorized into parametric and non-parametric types. The parametric type is mainly based on the statistical characteristics and distribution functions of weather data, while the data-driven non-parametric type is based on the simulation and resampling method16. This study focuses on parametric weather generators due to their greater universality and the abundance of related research. One of the most commonly used parametric generators is the rainfall-based “Richardson-type”17, which models the number of wet or dry days as a Markov process and simulates the daily precipitation, and then generates maximum and minimum temperatures based on the rainfall, as well as related variables such as solar radiation. The model parameters are usually different for each month to reflect the seasonal changes in the magnitude of the variables and their cross-correlations. As the most commonly used method, there are numerous weather generators that are based on Richardson-type such as WGEN18, CLIGEN19, ClimGen20, CRU-WG21. However, Richardson-style weather generators are restricted by their inability to model long periods of consistently wet or dry weather, which means that extreme events are unlikely to be represented correctly22.

In addition to this, there are several other methods to generate weather data. For example, many studies have shown that changes in solar radiation cause changes in humidity and air temperature, and that temperature differences lead to the generation of air movement (wind). Therefore, some studies use solar radiation as the key parameter to generate values of other weather elements23. The approach first generates the monthly values of solar radiation and calculates the daily series of clear sky day clearness index based on the Markov transformation matrix to determine the clear conditions for each day, then the hourly values are obtained by modeling with time correlation, autoregression. Hourly air temperature values are generated based on the assumption that the magnitude of daytime temperature change is roughly proportional to daily global radiation, particularly under clear or partly cloudy conditions24. Another approach is to try to model time series of meteorological data. Nygard Ferguson et al.25 propose Markov and Auto-Regressive Moving Average (ARMA) processes to model hourly solar radiation and air temperature. Solar radiation is modeled based on two Markov chains of daily clarity index and hourly clarity index and temperature is modeled as a time series by ARMA. Rastogi and Andersen26 decomposed the temperature and humidity data into periodic and nonperiodic components. Time series modeling using SARMA then models the nonperiodic component and resamples the data to generate a new nonperiodic component. The generated non-periodic component is added to the original periodic component to produce the new stochastic data. The solar radiation data are based on correlation with temperature and actual observations are selected from historical data. In addition, several studies have been conducted to generate multi-site and multi-element weather data through methods such as high-dimensional auto-regression and empirical copula27,28. Although these studies consider correlations between elements to some extent, they do not specifically analyze the simultaneous occurrences between multiple elements in the hourly data.

According to the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report29, the average temperature of the Earth’s surface may increase by 2.6–4.8 degrees by the end of the 21st century compared to the end of the 20th century, which could lead to an increase in the frequency of extreme weather events and have a significant impact on the indoor environment of buildings as well as on energy consumption. To investigate the impact of future climate change, the most advanced tools, general circulation models (GCMs), are often utilized. It assesses changes in the global climate system under different scenarios of increasing greenhouse gas concentrations by modeling physical processes in the ocean, atmosphere, land surface, and cryosphere30. However, due to the low spatial (~ 100 km) and temporal (daily average) resolution of some models, it is difficult to apply directly to building energy simulations that require hourly data. In general, there are two main approaches to process GCM outputs: dynamic and statistical downscaling methods. Dynamic downscaling methods involve running a high-resolution regional climate model (RCM) within the boundary conditions provided by GCMs to achieve more detailed regional-scale predictions31. This approach allows researchers to gain a deeper understanding of climate changes, frequency, and intensity of extreme weather events that specific areas might experience under future climate conditions. However, this method requires significant computational resources and depends on the accuracy of the boundary conditions supplied by GCMs, and the generated data needs to be corrected for systematic errors before applied in the building simulation32.

Given the aforementioned limitations, more studies frequently utilize a statistical downscaling method known as “Morphing”33. This method performs a shifting (addition), stretching (multiplication) or a combination of shifting and stretching on the current weather file based on future monthly values simulated by GCMs. In simple terms, the Morphing method generates a new meteorological year by shifting and stretching the existing meteorological data for a particular year, tailored to the objectives of use34. While the Morphing method reflects average changes in weather conditions, it heavily depends on the characteristics of the current weather data. Due to the uniqueness of the data generated, changes in weather patterns cannot be modeled, and the occurrence of extreme events cannot be simulated in morphed files. Since adjustments are made to each weather element separately, this also impacts the inherent correlation between these elements. Additionally, several other statistical downscaling methods exist, including weather classification35 and weather generator methods36. Weather classification methods predict or estimate climate change or weather conditions at smaller scales by classifying large-scale meteorological data into different weather types. This approach is based on the idea that similar large-scale weather patterns tend to lead to similar small-scale weather phenomena, and therefore by identifying and classifying these patterns, climate change in a particular region can be predicted more accurately. The previously described weather generator can also be used for downscaling by generating the daily sequences of multiple variables such as daily precipitation, maximum and minimum temperatures, and humidity, based on the output of the GCM each month. However, some extreme weather events that exhibit persistence on this temporal frequency, such as heatwaves, will not be captured. Furthermore, statistical downscaling methods are heavily reliant on historical meteorological data and are susceptible to inheriting systematic biases from GCMs37. In general, despite recent advances in meteorological data generation as mentioned previously, considerable research efforts are necessary to develop weather generators that can precisely reflect the temporal and spatial coherence observed in meteorological data across multiple sites and variables, as well as the complex interdependencies among various weather parameters38.

Considering the critical importance of the simultaneity of the meteorological elements and the fact that they have not been adequately analyzed in previous studies, synthetic weather data may not be well suited for realistic analysis of building energy consumption and the indoor environment. Therefore, this study aims to explore the correlation of multiple meteorological elements and propose a model-based approach to generate hourly weather data containing three elements that have a large influence on building thermal loads: air temperature, solar radiation and absolute humidity. Due to nighttime conditions, solar radiation is zero for part of the day, with the duration varying by region and season. This variability makes it challenging to model these three weather elements in a multivariate time series using traditional methods. In previous studies, it was common to ignore the simultaneity between them, and in particular to model the solar radiation independently. For building environment and energy consumption, the outside weather conditions at midday are particularly important. Therefore, this study divides the data generation method into two steps: First, it models the weather data only for 12:00 as a multivariate time series and generates the daily series data at 12:00 for a year. Second, it expands the 1-hour data for each day into 24-hour data based on the transition probabilities of each meteorological element.

Methodology

Data generation

The specific procedure for synthetic weather generation is illustrated as a flowchart in Fig. 1 and can be summarized as follows:

Procedure of synthetic weather generation.

Daily series: (1) Decompose the data for the three meteorological elements at 12:00 into periodic and non-periodic components using the STL decomposition method39. (2) Model the non-periodic components as a multivariate time series based on the S-vine (stationary vine) copula model40. (3) Simulate 365 stochastic non-periodic component points for each element from the constructed S-vine model and combine them with the periodic element components to generate the daily series data at 12:00 for a year.

Data expansion: (1) Discretize the observational data over a 30-year period and evaluate the probability of change (transition probability) of each weather element from the current time point to the next. (2) Expand the simulated stochastic daily series data at 12:00 for each day to cover a 24-hour period based on the probability of change for the current state of each weather element. Then, weather data values for one year, assumed to be 365 days or 8760 h, can be obtained that take into account the simultaneous occurrence of multiple weather elements.

Daily series

In time series analysis, many standard analytical tools require the data to be stationary. However, meteorological data time series, like the ones in this study, are usually non-stationary. To implement time series stationarity, this study applied the STL (seasonal-trend decomposition procedure based on LOESS) method to decompose the time series into three main components: trend, seasonality, and residual, where trend and seasonality are predictable deterministic elements and residual are unpredictable non-deterministic elements but with stationarity.

The LOESS (locally estimated scattered smoothing) used in STL is a non-parametric statistical method based on fitting a weighted polynomial regression41. LOESS works by fitting simple models to localized subsets of the data to create a smooth curve through the points. For each point requiring a smoothed value, LOESS selects a subset of data points around it, determined by a neighborhood window. This window can be of fixed size or vary based on the density of points. Each point in the neighborhood is weighted based on its distance from the target point (the point where the smooth is being calculated). Typically, a tri-cubic weight function is used:

where wi is the weight and Δi is the normalized distance of the ith data point from the target point.

In addition, another “span” or bandwidth is also a smoothing parameter in the LOESS method, which dictates the fraction of data points considered for each local regression. Essentially, the span parameter controls the extent of smoothing in the fitted curve: a larger span includes more data points in each local regression, resulting in a smoother curve that minimizes minor variations, while a smaller span uses fewer data points, producing a curve that more closely follows the data’s finer details and local irregularities. On the other hand, a larger span includes more data points for each local fit, producing a smoother curve that highlights the overall trend of the data rather than the specifics of individual points. In this study, it was found by comparison that span equal to 0.5 is a suitable choice in order to generate better fitting meteorological data.

The residual component is calculated by subtracting the values of the seasonal and trend components from the time series. The stationarity of the residual component is then verified using the ADF (Augmented Dickey-Fuller) test42, a statistical technique for investigating time series data for unit roots (non-stationarity).

Mathematical preliminary

The model should consider two types of dependence: cross-sectional and serial dependency. Cross-sectional dependency refers to the relationship among these weather elements at a specific time, while serial dependency pertains to the interrelation of each element across different time intervals. In this study, a specific vine copula structure, the Stationary Vine (S-vine) copula, is utilized to analyze these two types of dependency relationships in multivariate time series models.

Copulas. The copula approach is a statistical function used in the field of probability theory and statistics to describe the relationship between two or more random variables43. The main idea is that a joint distribution function can be decomposed into dependencies between variables and their marginal distributions44. Assume that F is the joint distribution of a d-dimensional random variable X. It can be represented by the copula C and marginal distribution functions Fi, i = 1, …, d.

The copula C is also a d-dimensional distribution and the marginals are the standard uniform distributions. Thus, it can capture the dependence structure between variables and combine it with the marginal distributions to obtain a joint distribution function.

Theoretically, the conventional copula method can be used for multidimensional modeling. However, in practical applications, due to the large computational volume of high dimensionality and the different dependencies between elements, it is difficult to represent with a copula from the same family.

Vine Copula. The vine copula, an approach that can construct d-dimensional copulas using only bivariate copulas as building blocks, was proposed by Joe45 and developed by Bedford and Cooke46. Moreover, each level of the Vine Copula hierarchy consists of a set of bivariate copulas describing dependencies between pairs of variables. Depending on the hierarchy and link pairs of variables, there are different types of Vine Copulas, such as Regular Vine (R-Vine) and Canonical Vine (C-Vine). R-Vines offer more flexibility in construction, allowing any arrangement that adheres to specific connectivity and acyclic rules. In a C-Vine, each level has a central node, with other nodes arranged around this central node. Vine Copulas are often represented graphically, which aids in understanding and interpreting the complex dependencies among multiple weather elements47,48 in this study. As an example, Fig. 2 shows the possible three-dimensional C-vine structures for different central nodes, where nodes represent variables, and lines represent the bivariate copulas. In a C-vine Copula model, selecting a central node that has a strong correlation with other elements (variables) is generally desirable. This is because the central node plays a crucial role in connecting other variables within the model, and choosing a node with strong correlations can enhance the model’s interpretability and applicability. In this study, the selection of the appropriate c-vine structure was based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Stationary vine copula

Suppose Xt = (Xt,1, ., Xt,d), t = 1, ., T is a d-dimensional time series. The general idea involves analyzing the dependencies of all variables at every time point through the application of a comprehensive vine copula model. Such a model needs numerous marginal distributions and corresponding copulas, which can make the analysis exceedingly complex. In practical applications, it is commonly assumed that the time series is stationary due to its complexity and specificity. The stationary of multivariate time series also means that the marginal distributions of the variables and the corresponding pair-copulas are constant despite variations in time49. This feature of the copula model is referred to as translational invariance.

Data expansion

Based on the S-vine model constructed previously and generated by simulation for 365 points, the generated points are then added to the trend term obtained from the STL decomposition to get the weather data for :00 each day of the year. After generating weather data, the next step is to expand it from one hour per day to 24 h to obtain complete yearly hourly meteorological data.

For instance, if the temperature is known at one time and one wishes to generate the value for the next point in time, the characteristics of the temperature change between these two points need to be specified. To simplify this issue, it can be assumed that the probability distribution of the weather elements at the next moment depends only on the state at the current moment. That is to say, the variation of each meteorological element during the day follows a Markov process. Therefore, the changes in meteorological elements between two time points can be analyzed using the transition probabilities of the Markov chain model.

Since the observed values of meteorological elements can be considered continuous, it is necessary to discretize these values to correspond to the state transitions of the Markov chain. The size of the discretization intervals (DI) affects the precision of the transition probabilities, with too large an interval leading to lower precision, and too small an interval leading to too small a sample size when specifying transition probabilities. Therefore, this study replaces the probability of the next state with the probability of the change for the current state. This helps to increase the accuracy of the analysis and reduce the amount of calculations. Similarly, solar radiation and absolute humidity can be obtained in the same way to achieve complete 24-hour data. However, since the expanded weather data was derived based on the probability distribution, the values of these elements may become unreasonable or exhibit discontinuities when transitioning from 24:00 to 1:00. For instance, unreasonable values such as humidity below 0 or solar radiation exceeding the solar constant need to be adjusted to reasonable ranges. As for discontinuities, smoothing can be achieved by averaging the data from 23:00 to 2:00 h the following day. Typically, temperatures during this period do not represent the maximum or minimum values of the day, thus having negligible impact on subsequent heat load analyses.

Method evaluation and case studies

Evaluation of data expansion

The accuracy of the data expansion method is verified by extending the original TMY data from 12:00 each day to cover 24 h. Mean absolute error (MAE), R-squared, MAE of daily maximum, MAE of daily minimum and mean values are employed to assess differences between the original TMY and expanded data. The results and discretization interval of observation and evaluation of data expansion are shown in Table 1. The results show that the mean values of the two types of data are nearly identical. The MAE is approximately 5% of the maximum range of the corresponding observed values for each element, which indicates that the generated data are overall similar to the original data, but with some fluctuation. Furthermore, MAE of the daily maximum and minimum shows that there is little difference in the daily range of fluctuations between the expanded data and the original TMY data. And in terms of the deviation of the daily extremes, the deviation of the daily minimum temperature and the daily maximum solar radiation are slightly larger, accounting for roughly 5–7% of the entire range of observations. If the expanded data are considered as the predicted values of the model, the results of R-square show that the differences between the predicted and actual values of all three elements are small, which indicates a better model performance. Moreover, the relatively small R-squared values may be attributed to the potential large fluctuations in solar radiation throughout the day.

Case study

Take the Osaka 2010 version of the TMY data51 as an example. Observations of air temperature, absolute humidity, and solar radiation at 12:00 for each day were initially extracted. Subsequently, the data were decomposed into trend and residual components using the LOESS method. This step can be easily implemented in R with the span parameter set to 0.5 as described in Sect. 2.2. The results of the decomposition are shown in Figs. 6 and 7. The black and orange lines in Fig. 6 represent the observed values and trend components for each element, respectively. It can be seen that the trend components change smoothly with the fluctuation of the observed values, which can reflect the trend of each meteorological element in a year. On the other hand, the residual components of all three elements in Fig. 7 fluctuate up and down around 0, which aligns with their inherent properties. Furthermore, the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) and Phillips-Perron (PP) tests are employed to verify the stationarity of the residual components obtained from the decomposition. The ADF test addresses serial correlations with lagged differences, while the PP test adjusts for serial correlation and heteroskedasticity without specifying lag length. Employing both tests ensures a thorough and reliable assessment of stationarity. The null hypothesis (H0) test assumes that the time series is non-stationary, while the alternative hypothesis (H1) assumes that the time series is stationary. The p-values for each test element are shown in the subplots in Fig. 7. Given that all p-values from these two tests are < 0.01, the residual components obtained from the decomposition can be considered stationary.

Then, the simulated value of the generated residual components is added to the previous trend components to get the value of the weather element at 12:00 for each day of the year. This result basically satisfies the purpose and requirements of this study. However, due to the existence of some unreasonable values, such as negative humidity and solar radiation, it is necessary to adjust them to a reasonable range. For example, an absolute humidity value of less than 0.5 g/kg is converted to a random value between 0.5 and 1.0 g/kg, and a solar radiation of less than 0 W/m2 is converted to a random value between 0 and 50 w/m2.

Results and discussion

To analyze the model performance, 30 years of meteorological data were generated based on the obtained model. The density distributions of these three elements of summer and winter for the generated and original TMY database are shown in Fig. 14. Although it may be more accurate to use long historical records for validation, TMY data were selected here because they are representative of the characteristics of historical meteorological data and can be compared easily. In the case of the generated summer air temperature data, the probability density of high temperatures (over about 30 ℃) is slightly lower than in the original data, while in the warm range of the temperatures (about 23 ~ 28 ℃), it is higher than in the original data. In winter, the probability density of low temperatures (under about 3 ℃) of the generated data are higher than the original data, and the part with the highest density probability deviates from the original data by about 3 ℃ higher. For absolute humidity, there is a deviation in the kurtosis between the two data. This is due to the strong randomness of daily variations in absolute humidity, making it difficult to generate data with a particularly high probability density in any given interval. Although the annual means are very similar, the results of the difference in the distributional characteristics still indicate that the absolute humidity annual characteristics of the generated data are not the same as the original data. For solar radiation, the distributional characteristics of both datasets are very similar. Overall, except for the deviation of the kurtosis of the absolute humidity distribution, the distribution characteristics of the generated data and original data during the summer and winter seasons are essentially identical. This suggests that the developed model effectively captures the cross-sectional and serial dependencies in the multivariate time series.

Density distribution of the three elements of summer (top) and winter (bottom) for the generated and original TMY database.

As one of the main purposes of meteorological data generation, both types of data are used in this section for building performance simulation. The standard models of office buildings that are often used in Japan were selected here. The typical levels of the selected models are 605 m2 and the window-to-wall ratio is 0.3. Operational hours for the air conditioning systems are set from 08:00 to 18:00 on weekdays, including a one-hour warm-up period. Ventilation rates are maintained at 6 m³/m² per hour. To ensure a comprehensive analysis, only three representative building models were chosen. Detailed specifications and usage parameters of these models are discussed extensively in the supplementary materials54. Then, the building simulation tool NewHASP55, commonly used for thermal load calculation in Japan56, was implemented to calculate the thermal loads of the standard office building under the conditions of all meteorological database. The empirical cumulative distribution functions for the corresponding heat loads of summer and winter air conditioning operation are shown in Fig. 15, where positive values represent cooling loads, and negative values represent heating room loads.

CDF of the thermal load of summer (left) and winter (right) for the generated and original TMY database.

In summer, the CDF of the generated data for the portion of the heat load from − 50 W/m2 to 0 W/m2 is lower than that of the original data, while the opposite is observed for heat loads in the portion from 25 W/m2 to 50 W/m2. As the CDF is close to 0 and 1, the line of the original data is within the range of the lines of the generated data, which indicates that the characteristics of the distributions in the tails of the two data are similar. For winter, the distribution characteristics of the middle part of the thermal load corresponding to the two databases are different, while the distribution characteristics of the tails are not significantly different. The generated decades of data have more meteorological types and distributional characteristics compared to the original one-year data, especially providing more possibilities in the extreme high and low temperature scenarios that are essential for the simulation of building performance.

Conclusions

This study proposes a method to generate hourly meteorological data for building simulation, based on dependencies between multiple weather elements: air temperature, solar radiation, and absolute humidity. The method can be divided into two steps: (1) Modeling multivariate time series of these three elements at 12:00 for TMY using the S-vine method and then 365 three-element data were generated as the parameter for each day of the generation year at 12:00. (2) Expand the generated daily series data at 12:00 for each day to 24 h based on the probability of change matrix for each element. In preparation for modeling, the data was decomposed into periodic and non-periodic components using the STL method, with the latter being analyzed in the multivariate time series analysis. Additionally, discretizing the original data and quantifying the magnitude of change in each element are necessary to estimate each element’s probability change matrix.

By expanding the daily series data at 12:00 of the TMY and comparing them with the original data, the expanded data of air temperature and solar radiation fitted the TMY data very well under sunny conditions due to the stable daily trends. Under cloudy days (small daily solar radiation values), the air temperature and solar radiation data showed slight deviations from the original data, due to unstable weather trends. In addition, the expanded absolute humidity values deviate from the original data due to the unstable daily trend of absolute humidity throughout the year. Generally, the expansion method, based on the change probability matrix, effectively restores the data characteristics of the original TMY database.

The results of the multivariate time series analysis of the non-periodic components of the three meteorological elements using s-vine show that both the marginal distributions and the dependencies between the elements of the original data are well captured by the constructed joint distribution. A comparison of the simulated data, generated for 30 years, with the TMY reveals that the temperature and solar radiation in the generated data accurately reflect the distribution characteristics of the original data during the summer and winter seasons. The kurtosis of the density distribution curve for absolute humidity in the generated data is slightly lower than that in the TMY data. The cumulative distributions of heat loads for office buildings based on the two types of meteorological data show that the curve for the TMY is within the region of the generated data, which indicates that the heat loads of the generated data have been supplemented with more uncertainty-related information while maintaining the characteristics of the original data.

For future research, this method serves as a meteorological data generator that ensures correlation between multiple elements. It can be used to analyze the impact of weather inputs on building simulation uncertainty by synthesizing stochastic weather time series. Additionally, this research method can serve as a downscaling approach to generate more realistic stochastic future weather data, which is conducive to a more comprehensive analysis of the impacts of future climate change on energy consumption and other fields.
