The glutamatergic NTS-PAG/PVN pathway underlies myocardial injury and anxiety-like behavior induced by myocardial ischemia-reperfusion
Authors: Fan ZHANG, Zheng-jie LUO, Nai-xuan WEI, Jian-qing YU, Wen-jing SHAO, Wei LIU, Zhi-hao XU, Qi SHU, Xiang ZHOU, Jie WANG, Ling HU, Guo-ming SHEN, Rong-lin CAI, Qing YU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor patients with myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI), cardiac dysfunction accompanied by anxiety is an extremely common comorbidity. Although the association between these two conditions has received some attention, the underlying neural mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we functionally characterized two distinct glutamatergic (Glu) neural pathways in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) of male mice, a pathway from the NTS to the periaqueductal gray (PAG) that mediates anxiety-like behaviors in MIRI mice, and a pathway from the NTS to the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN) that influences cardiac dysfunction following MIRI. Chemogenetic inhibition of these two pathways simultaneously ameliorated both the anxiety-like behaviors and cardiac dysfunction in MIRI mice. Thus, our study reveals that the distinct circuits NTSGlu-PAG and NTSGlu-PVN separately encode anxiety-like emotions and cardiac dysfunction after myocardial ischemia-reperfusion, thereby providing insights at the neural circuit level for improving both cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like emotions following MIRI. Subject terms: Diseases, Neuroscience

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is regarded as the leading cause of morbidity and mortality, and its economic impact far exceeds that of other diseases. Studies have shown that anxiety is associated with an increased risk of CVD [1]. The European Society of Cardiology considers anxiety an independent risk factor for new-onset coronary heart disease, post-AMI cardiogenic death, and cardiac events in patients with heart disease [2]. Compared with the general population, CVD patients are more prone to anxiety, and at the same time, anxiety patients have a higher tendency to develop CVD [3–5]. Preclinical studies have reported behaviors such as depression and cognitive impairment in animal models of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion [6–8]. These findings suggest that myocardial ischemia-reperfusion can trigger negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression. However, the underlying mechanisms linking these two conditions remain largely unclear.

The nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) is a critical relay station in the brainstem for visceral sensory information transmission [9], receiving and preliminarily integrating signals from visceral afferents and cardiovascular inputs to maintain cardiovascular function [10]. Meanwhile, the vesicular glutamate transporter Vglut2 in the NTS is expressed in over 99% of vagal sensory neurons but is absent in vagal motor neurons [11, 12]. Although the brainstem/midbrain vagal pathways and emotion-related circuits governing cardiovascular responses have been well characterized [13, 14], the fundamental mechanisms linking cardiovascular regulation to emotional activity remain unclear. This highlights the ongoing challenge of unraveling the neural circuits that drive cardiovascular-brain interactions.

Under classical conditions, neural tracing methods have revealed the existence of neuroanatomical connections between the PVN and brain regions involved in cardiovascular regulation, including the intermediolateral nucleus (IML) of the spinal cord and the rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), among others [15–17]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that the PVN plays a critical role in cardiovascular regulation following myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) [18–20]. Specifically, after myocardial ischemic injury, enhanced glutamatergic input leads to hyperactivity of presympathetic PVN neurons and increased sympathetic overflow, thereby contributing to cardiac dysfunction [21, 22]. The periaqueductal gray (PAG) is generally regarded as a downstream site in neural networks that mediates diverse behaviors and serves as a key structure integrating aversive information with response outputs in defensive and emotional behaviors [23, 24]. Neurons in the PAG coordinate negative emotions [25] with autonomic, neuroendocrine, and immune systems while modulating corresponding cardiovascular changes [26, 27]. Due to its extensive connections with the prefrontal cortex and various brainstem nuclei [28], the PAG functions as a critical midbrain hub for sensory-motor integration [29, 30]. However, the distinct neural connections of the NTS with the PVN and PAG serve as key regulators of cardiovascular-emotional interplay. Therefore, elucidating the roles of the NTS-PAG and NTS-PVN circuits in coordinating post-MIRI cardiovascular homeostasis and emotional regulation is of significant importance.

In this study, we delineated the functional organization of the NTS-PAG and NTS-PVN neural circuits and investigated their mechanistic roles in the development of cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like behaviors following myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI). Using fiber photometry, we monitored activity dynamics within these two neural pathways post-MIRI. Through optogenetic and chemogenetic interventions targeting specific circuits, we demonstrated their distinct regulatory effects on either cardiac impairment or anxiety-like manifestations after MIRI. Our findings reveal that the NTS-PAG and NTS-PVN circuits coordinately regulate cardiovascular activity and emotional states, serving as the neural substrate for concurrent cardiovascular dysregulation and anxiety-like behavior post-MIRI. These results may open new therapeutic avenues for alleviating both MIRI-related cardiovascular and anxiety symptoms by targeting this neuromodulatory pathway.

Results

Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury enhances anxiety-like behavior

We first examined the effects of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury(MIRI) on anxiety-like behavior using a mouse model of Myocardial Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury, prepared by temporary occlusion of the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) for 30 min, and reperfusion was performed for 120 min (Fig. 1A).Compared with the sham group, the ECG recordings in the MIRI group demonstrated elevated ST segments and altered T waves (Fig. 1B). The ST segment of the ECG was significantly elevated during left anterior descending (LAD) artery ligation and exhibited partial recovery 2 h post-reperfusion (Fig. 1C).Additionally, the myocardial surface was observed to appear pale or cyanotic, which corroborated the successful establishment of the model according to predefined criteria. The modeling procedure was performed as described previously [31]. Open field test (OFT) and elevated plus maze (EPM) were employed to assess anxiety-like behavior in mice one week post-operation. MIRI mice exhibited fewer entries (Fig. 1D), shorter duration (Fig. 1E) and reduced distances traveled (Fig. 1F) in the central area of the OFT compared to Sham mice, suggesting heightened anxiety-like behavior (Fig. 1G, H). In the EPM test. Both the frequency of open arm entries and the duration of stay in the open arms were reduced in MIRI mice relative to Sham mice (Fig. 1I, J). The open arm movement distance was significantly lower in MIRI mice than in Sham mice (Fig. 1K). Based on these findings, the anxiety index of the mice was calculated, revealing a significantly higher anxiety index in the MIRI group compared to the Sham group (Fig. 1L), thus confirming enhanced anxiety in MIRI mice (Fig. 1M). Further supporting the presence of heightened anxiety-like behavior.

In addition, we investigated the relevant indicators of cardiac function in mice. Our findings revealed that the myocardial infarction area was significantly increased in the MIRI group compared to the sham group (Fig. 1N). Heart rate variability analysis demonstrated that the LF/HF ratio in the MIRI group was elevated relative to the sham group at 7days post-myocardial ischemia- reperfusion, with no significant difference observed prior to MIRI induction (Fig. 1O). The echocardiographic results showed that cardiac function in the MIRI group mice was decreased compared to the Sham group (S Fig. 1A-1E). Sympathetic nerve activity scores indicated increased sympathetic tone after MIRI (S Fig. 1F, G). Furthermore, the level of norepinephrine (NE) in the hearts of the MIRI group was markedly higher than in the sham group (Fig. 1P) (S Fig. 1N), a finding that was consistently confirmed. These results suggest an imbalance in autonomic nervous system regulation, with sympathetic dominance. MIRI mice exhibited increased levels of cardiac troponin T (cTnT) in the myocardium (S Fig. 1H) and serum (S Fig. 1I), as well as elevated CK-MB in the myocardium (S Fig. 1M). Histomorphological observation revealed myocardial tissue damage (S Fig. 1J) and myocardial fibrosis (S Figs. 1K, 1L) in MIRI mice. Based on the above findings, mice exhibited significant anxiety-like behaviors and developed cardiac dysfunction following MIRI.

MIRI induces the expression of the spiking of NTS excitatory neurons and anxiety

Interoception, pertaining to the sensations of internal bodily states, plays a vital role in various physiological and emotional processes. A considerable amount of research has verified that various interoceptive stimuli from visceral organs are primarily transmitted to the brain through relay areas such as the nucleus tractus solitarius(NTS). As a key central node for cardiovascular regulation, the NTS dynamically responds to the physiological status and stimuli of the heart. Hence, we investigated how the NTS brain region mediates the anxiety-like expression induced by MIRI. We employed in vivo electrophysiological recordings to study the neuronal firing patterns in the NTS brain region of MIRI-induced anxious mice (Fig. 2A). We discovered that neurons could be categorized into different units based on their spike characteristics: wide-spiking(WS) presumed excitatory neurons(EPs) and presumed inhibitory interneurons(F-INs) (Fig. 2B, C). In comparison with the Sham group mice (Fig. 2D), the firing frequency of EPs in the MIRI group mice was higher (Fig. 2E). Nevertheless, there was no significant difference in the firing frequency of F-INs between the two groups (Fig. 2F). The aforementioned results indicate that MIRI-induced anxiety in mice leads to an enhanced firing frequency of EPs in the NTS brain region, but does not affect the firing frequency changes of F-INs.

Elevated NTSglu activity underlies the impact of cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like emotional behavior induced by MIRI

To confirm that excitatory neurons in the NTS are activated in MIRI induced anxiety-like state, we dynamically monitored the activity of Vglut2-positive neurons in the NTS using optical fiber photometry during open field and elevated cross test in MIRI mice (Fig. 2G, H). We found that in vivo calcium imaging captured the activity of NTSGlu neurons during the OFT and EPM, with a significant enhancement in NTSGlu neuronal activity following MIRI (Fig. 2I, J). MIRI mice spent less time exploring in the central OFT (Fig. 2L, N), and the exploration time of MIRI mice in the EPM open arm was reduced, MIRI mice exhibited anxiety-like behaviors (S Figs. 2A, 2B). Meanwhile, the mean Ca2+ activity and the rate of change in Ca2+ fluorescence of NTSglu neurons in MIRI mice were significantly increased (Fig. 2K, M, O). These results suggest that MIRI activation of NTSglu neurons is associated with anxious behavior.

Therefore, we investigated whether inhibiting the activity of NTSglu neurons could alleviate the cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like behavior induced by MIRI by specific inhibition of NTSglu neurons in mice (Fig. 3A). The activity of NTSglu neurons was inhibited by CNO injection (Fig. 3B). At the behavioral level, inhibition of NTSglu neurons in MIRI mice reduced anxiety-like behavior, with significant increases in exploration time and central activity distance in the central OFT region (Fig. 3C-F). Similarly, inhibition of NTSglu neurons increased the duration and distance of the open arm in the EPM (Fig. 3G-J). Next, the effect of chemogenetic inhibition of NTSglu neurons on behavioral changes in vivo was examined. In terms of cardiac function, our findings demonstrate that compared with the MIRI group, the MIRI + CNO group exhibited a significant reduction in myocardial infarct size (Fig. 3K). Heart rate variability analysis revealed that the LF/HF ratio was decreased in the MIRI + CNO group at 30 min post-ischemia compared to the MIRI group, while no significant difference was observed prior to MIRI induction (Fig. 3L). Echocardiographic results showed that the cardiac dysfunction was reversed in the MIRI + CNO group compared to the MIRI group (S Fig. 3A-3E). Additionally, NE levels were reduced in the MIRI + CNO group (Fig. 3M). Notably, we found that chemogenetic inhibition of NTSGlu neurons in MIRI mice reduced c-Fos activity in PAG and PVN neurons (S Fig. 3F-H). These findings support a role for NTSglu neurons in cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like behavior in MIRI.

The PAG/PVN receives direct inputs from NTSglu neurons

To investigate the potential neural circuits, we examined the downstream brain regions of NTSGlu neurons. We injected the anterograde virus AAV-DIO-eGFP into the NTS brain region of Vglut2-cre mice (Fig. 4A). Three weeks after the virus injection, we observed EGFP+ neurons in 25 brain regions (S Fig. 4C). It is notable that we observed neural fiber projections in both the PAG and PVN (Fig. 4B). Subsequent screening identified 3 anxiety-related brain regions, including PAG, PB and CeA (S Figs. 4A, 4B). Next, we found that compared with the Sham group, in the MIRI group, only the expression of c-Fos in the PAG increased while inducing anxiety-like behavior (Fig. 4C, D), rather than in PB and CeA (S Fig. 5A-5D). The c-Fos immunohistochemistry experiment was performed immediately after the behavioral tests. Meanwhile, our previous research has reported that the PVN plays a crucial role in inducing cardiac dysfunction after MIRI [18–20]. In this study, we also observed a significant increase in c-Fos activity in the PVN of the MIRI group compared to the Sham group (Fig. 4E, F). Thus, we hypothesized that NTS-PAG and NTS-PVN neural circuits are involved in MIRI to induce anxiety-like emotions and cardiac dysfunction.

The NTSglu → PAG neural circuit is involved in anxiety-like behaviors induced in MIRI mice

Based on these findings, we focused on the NTSglu → PAG pathway in our subsequent experiments. We injected AAV-hSyn-Cre virus into the NTS and AAV-DIO-GCaMP6m calcium indicator virus into the PAG, then employed fiber photometry combined with behavioral assays to observe in vivo the involvement of the NTSglu → PAG circuit in anxiety-like behaviors induced by MIRI (Fig. 5A, B). We found that in vivo calcium imaging recorded the activity of NTSglu → PAG neurons during the OFT behavioral test. MIRI mice spent less time exploring in the central OFT (Fig. 5C), and the mean Ca2+ activity and the rate of change in Ca2+ fluorescence of NTSglu → PAG circuits in MIRI mice were significantly explored in the lateral zone during OFT exploration (Fig. 5D, E, F). The results showed that the exploration time of MIRI mice in the EPM open arm was reduced (Fig. 5G), and the mean Ca2+ activity and Ca2+ fluorescence change rate of NTSglu → PAG circuits in MIRI mice during the closed arm exploration were significantly higher than those in the open arm exploration (Fig. 5H, I, J). These results suggest that MIRI activation of NTSglu → PAG circuits is associated with anxious behavior.

In addition, to selectively monitor changes in PAG neuronal activity following optogenetic inhibition of the NTSglu → PAG circuit in MIRI mice. We injected the optogenetic viral vector AAV-DIO-eNpHR4.0-eGFP into the NTS of Vglut2-cre MIRI mice and implanted an optical fiber above the PAG (Fig. 5K, L). Optogenetic stimulation of eNpHR4.0-expressing NTSglu → PAG terminals was electrophysiologically confirmed to reduce firing rates in PAG neurons (Fig. 5M, N). These data demonstrate that NTSglu neurons send monosynaptic excitatory projections to PAG neurons. Additionally, we observed anxiety-like behaviors in MIRI mice were abolished following optogenetic inhibition of the NTSglu → PAG neural circuit. The open field test results demonstrated that optogenetic inhibition of the NTSglu → PAG circuit in MIRI mice significantly increased the number of entries into the central zone, greater distance traveled within the central zone (S Fig. 6A-6E), and reduced anxiety index (Fig. 5O, P). The elevated plus maze test demonstrated that yellow light-induced inhibition of the NTSglu → PAG circuit significantly increased both open arm entries, distance traveled (S Fig. 6F-6I), and reducing the anxiety index in MIRI mice (Fig. 5Q, R). It is noteworthy that optogenetic inhibition of the NTSglu → PAG circuit failed to reverse myocardial injury (Fig. 5S), cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 5T), or cardiac NE levels (Fig. 5U) in MIRI mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the NTSglu → PAG excitatory pathway serves as a critical mediator of anxiety-like behaviors following MIRI, where its dysregulation exacerbates such behaviors, while inhibition of this circuit effectively reduces anxiety-like manifestations in MIRI mice.

The NTSglu → PVN neural circuit is involved in cardiac dysfunction induced in induced in MIRI mice

Next, we investigated the NTSGlu → PVN pathway. We injected AAV-hSyn-Cre virus into the NTS region of mice and AAV-DIO-GCaMP6m calcium indicator virus into the PVN region. Subsequently, using fiber photometry recording technology, we observed in vivo the activity changes of the NTSGlu → PVN neural circuit before and after myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (Fig. 6A, B).The results showed that after myocardial ischemia-reperfusion, both the mean Ca2+activity and the Ca2+ fluorescence change rate of the NTSGlu → PVN neural circuit were significantly enhanced (Fig. 6C-E). Meanwhile, to exclude the influence of the surgical procedure, we compared the Sham group with the MIRI group (S Figs. 7A, 7B). The results demonstrated that after MIRI, both the mean Ca2+ activity and the Ca2+ fluorescence change rate of the NTSGlu → PVN neural circuit were significantly enhanced (S Fig. 7C-7E). These findings indicate that the NTSGlu → PVN pathway plays a critical role in regulating cardiac function in MIRI mice.

To selectively monitor changes in neuronal activity in the PVN following optogenetic inhibition of the NTSGlu → PVN circuit, we injected the optogenetic viral vector AAV-DIO-eNpHR4.0-eGFP into the NTS region of Vglut2-cre MIRI mice and implanted an optical fiber above the PVN (Fig. 6F, G).Electrophysiological verification confirmed that optogenetic stimulation of eNpHR4.0-expressing NTSGlu → PVN nerve terminals reduced the firing frequency of PVN neurons (Fig. 6H, I). Results from the open field test showed that compared to the MIRI+mCherry group (Fig. 6G), inhibiting the NTSGlu → PVN circuit in MIRI mice did not yield statistically significant differences in the number of entries into the central area, the distance traveled in the central area, the time spent in the central area (S Figure 8A-8E). Discussion

While previous clinical studies have detailed myocardial dysfunction and the anxiety phenomena in patients with myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [32, 33], the underlying neurofunctional pathways have remained elusive. This study identified two distinct neural circuits originating from NTSGlu neurons (the NTS-PAG and NTS-PVN pathways), which respectively mediate anxiety-like behaviors and cardiac dysfunction induced by MIRI. These findings demonstrate the important functional roles of glutamatergic innervation in the NTS-PAG/PVN circuits in both cardiovascular pathology and anxiety-like behaviors in MIRI mice.This phenomenon of “functional dissociation” was observed within the acute intervention model employed in this study and does not preclude potential interactions between the two pathways over longer time scales or under different conditions. In this study, we observed that cardiac function was impaired immediately after MIRI, and anxiety-like behavior was detected in mice one week later through behavioral tests. There is a complex temporal dynamic relationship between cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like behavior. Although independent neuromodulation experiments support the functional dissociation of the NTS → PAG and NTS → PVN pathways, our current data cannot definitively determine whether cardiac injury and emotional disorders occur simultaneously in the pathological process of MIRI or if there is a clear sequential cascade. However, in the clinical progression of MIRI, it is often the complex interaction between the heart and brain that plays a role, with multiple neural circuits exhibiting both independence and information integration.

There is a close relationship between cardiac function and emotional states, wherein the heart and brain interact bidirectionally through the brain-heart axis [34]. The NTS, a key nucleus encoding visceral sensation, has been extensively studied for its role in transmitting sensory signals from peripheral organs. However, its function in anxiety induction has long been overlooked [9]. Studies have reported significant neuronal activation in the periaqueductal gray (PAG) of mice exhibiting marked anxiety-like behaviors (e.g., avoidance of open areas), which is consistent with our findings [35, 36]. Our study delineates a neural circuit connecting the NTS and PAG, which functionally links the cardiac sensory afferent role of the NTS with the PAG—a brain region critically involved in emotional disorders. The NTS has been identified as a central hub for integrating visceral and cardiac sensory signals into the brain [37]. However, how brain regions respond to peripheral sensory signals transmitted from the NTS to trigger anxiety-related behaviors remains elusive.Using viral tracing techniques, we confirmed the structural connectivity of the NTS-PAG circuit. Specific inhibition of glutamatergic inputs from the NTS to the PAG alleviated anxiety-like behaviors induced by MIRI. These findings suggest that NTS glutamatergic neurons may serve as a bridge between visceral sensory signals and the PAG, thereby facilitating emotional responses. PVN neuronal activation following MIRI represents a physiologically coherent response, as the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus serves as a pivotal integration center for cardiovascular regulation and stress responses [38], with its neural activity being directly modulated by viscerosensory inputs from the NTS [39]. Through calcium imaging and electrophysiological recordings, we confirmed that hyperactivation of the NTSGlu → PVN circuit following MIRI significantly enhances the firing activity of PVN neurons. This aberrant activation may exacerbate cardiac impairment through two distinct pathways: by increasing sympathetic output leading to elevated catecholamine release, and by activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis to promote cortisol secretion. Notably, chemogenetic inhibition of the NTSGlu → PVN pathway not only attenuated MIRI-induced myocardial injury but also markedly improved cardiac dysfunction while reducing myocardial norepinephrine content. These findings demonstrate that abnormal PVN neuronal activation post-MIRI constitutes a crucial mechanism in cardiac functional deterioration, and targeting the NTSGlu → PVN pathway may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for improving clinical outcomes. In our study, we employed in vivo electrophysiology to observe neuronal firing patterns in the NTS of mice with MIRI-induced anxiety. The results demonstrated an enhanced firing frequency of excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) in the NTS of anxiety-model mice subjected to MIRI. This finding was further corroborated by fiber photometry calcium imaging, which confirmed the activation of excitatory neurons in the NTS under MIRI-induced anxiety-like states. Furthermore, chemogenetic inhibition of NTSGlu neuronal activity was found to alleviate both cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like behaviors induced by MIRI.

Accumulating evidence suggests that myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) is recognized as a systemic clinical syndrome, wherein cardiac insufficiency is interconnected with multi-organ dysfunction [40]. The interplay between cardiac injury and multiple central nervous system (CNS) functions underlies the clinical manifestation of emotional disorders, including cognitive, depressive, and anxiety-related symptoms, in patients [41]. Mechanistically, altered upstream signals from the heart following MIRI are key to the development of anxiety-like behaviors. This is primarily characterized by hemodynamic changes resulting from cardiac ischemic injury, leading to compromised cerebral perfusion [42]. Concurrently, ischemia-induced abnormalities in wall tension or pressure fluctuations can activate cardiac mechanoreceptors, such as Piezo1 (predominantly located in the atrial and ventricular walls) [43]. These signals are then transmitted via vagal afferent fibers, ultimately projecting to the NTS. Furthermore, ischemia instigates sterile inflammation, releasing cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β, which can enter the CNS through pathways such as extracellular vesicles, thereby modulating NTS activity [44, 45]. The NTS has been established as a hub for integrating visceral sensory signals en route to the brain. Although we found that the NTS sends neural projections to multiple brain regions, specific downstream targets distinctly mediate anxiety-like behaviors and cardiac dysfunction in mice. Prior studies have reported that NTS projections to the cortex are involved in visceral sensation (pancreatic)-induced anxiety-like behaviors [46], and that visceral signals from the gut activate NTS neurons via the vagus nerve, which in turn engage brain regions known to regulate feeding behavior, thereby contributing to anorexia [47]. Thus, the NTS represents a complex neuronal cluster that constitutes a critical interface between visceral afferent information and the modulation of widespread central circuits. When visceral injury occurs, brain regions governing respiration, autonomic function, feeding, and emotional behavior differentially respond to visceral feedback from the NTS, a divergence that may originate from differences in the encoding of visceral afferent signals. Indeed, research indicates that distinct viscera exhibit a significant spatial mapping relationship within the NTS via vagal afferents. Concurrently, the NTS contains spatially defined domains for specific sensory inputs, each composed of transcriptionally defined cells with both distinct and shared types [9], which may provide a plausible explanation for our findings. As our current data do not yet establish that this divergence is mediated by different NTS neuronal receptors, we cannot exclude the possibility that such receptor heterogeneity contributes to this process.

In summary, Our study has identified two distinct neural circuits (NTSGlu→PAG and NTSGlu→PVN) that respectively mediate cardiac dysfunction and anxiety-like behaviors following myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI). Clinical studies have reported that patients with heart failure frequently develop psychological dysfunctions such as anxiety [48], a phenomenon primarily attributed to autonomic dysregulation following heart failure [49, 50]. This clinical observation corroborates our finding that post-MIRI anxiety is not merely a consequence of stress or pain induced by the surgical procedure. Importantly, our study revealed that while cardiac dysfunction was evident immediately following MIRI, anxiety-like behaviors did not emerge until one week later, highlighting a complex temporal relationship between these two pathologies. Although our independent neuromodulation experiments suggest a functional dissociation between the NTS → PAG and NTS → PVN pathways, the present data do not permit a definitive conclusion as to whether cardiac injury and emotional disorders arise concurrently or in a clear temporal cascade during MIRI pathogenesis. However, the clinical progression of MIRI is characterized by intricate heart-brain interactions, where multiple neural circuits demonstrate both autonomy and information integration. It should be noted that this study only included male mice as subjects, and female mice were not investigated. However, previous studies have reported that females may be more susceptible to anxiety due to a higher abundance of CRF neurons in the BNST compared to males [51]. Additionally, visceral sensory nerve fiber conduction is stronger in female mice, potentially leading to a more pronounced perception of visceral dysfunction [52]. Based on these findings, we speculate that in the MIRI model, female mice may exhibit greater susceptibility to anxiety than their male counterparts. The neural mechanisms underlying cardiac dysfunction and the development of anxiety following MIRI in humans are likely more complex than those in mice. Our current study offers a potential therapeutic strategy for emotional dysfunction in patients post-MIRI. Given that psychiatric medications used to treat anxiety often have the side effect of disrupting cardiac function, it will be of significant clinical importance to determine whether selectively modulating glutamatergic transmission from NTS terminals in the PAG can alleviate anxiety without compromising cardiac function.

Experimental section/methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice,vglut2-cre mice(Cyagen Biosciences Inc.) aged 6 to 8 weeks and obtained from Hangzhou Resources Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Laboratory Animal Production License: SCXK (ZHE) 2019-0004), were utilized in this study. The animals were housed under controlled environmental conditions, including a constant ambient temperature of 23 °C ± 2 °C, ad libitum access to food and water, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle starting at 8:00 am daily. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number table. Behavioral experiments were performed during the light phase (9:00-17:00). All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with guidelines approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (AHUCM-mouse-2025080).

Viruses and stereotaxic surgery

Mice were administered isoflurane anesthesia (induction: 3.0%; maintenance: 0.5-1.0%) in an oxygen-enriched environment for respiratory anesthesia. Following induction, the mice were anesthetized and secured in a stereotaxic frame (Reward Life Sciences). To protect their eyes from exposure to bright light, erythromycin ointment was applied. After preparing the skin, the scalp was incised to expose the skull, which was kept clean by rinsing with normal saline. The virus solution was injected into the NTS (AP: -7.07 mm; ML: 0.3 mm; DV: -5.4 mm relative to bregma), PAG (AP: -4.76 mm; ML: 0.46 mm; DV: -2.45 mm relative to bregma) or PVN (AP: -0.85 mm; ML: 0.28 mm; DV: -4.75 mm relative to bregma). The injection was allowed to remain in place for 10 min before being slowly withdrawn to prevent reflux of the virus solution. Subsequently, the scalp incision was sutured. After virus injection, mice were allowed to recover from anesthesia under a warming lamp.

For the local optical fiber photometry operation, a ceramic implant (outer diameter 200 mm, NA 0.37, length 3.5 mm, thinktech Co., Nanjing, China) was implanted into the NTS with the tip of the optical fiber positioned 100 to 200 mm above the virus injection site. The ceramic inlay was then fixed to the skull using cranial nails and dental cement. Calcium signal recordings were performed at least 3 weeks after surgery. After the experiment, brains were collected, sectioned, and imaged to confirm the location of viral expression and the site of implantation. Animals showing significant leakage to the surrounding area were excluded from the analysis.

Surgery for the myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury model

The mice were subjected to a 12-hour fasting period prior to the operation. A myocardial ischemia model was established by temporarily ligating the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD). Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (induction: 3.0%; maintenance: 0.5-1.0%) for respiratory anesthesia, and connected to a small animal ventilator with the following settings: respiratory rate of 120 breaths per minute, respiratory ratio of 1:2, and tidal volume of 1 mL. Thoracotomy was performed through the third to fourth left intercostal space under assisted ventilation. The LAD was ligated with a 10-0 silk thread for 30 min, followed by release of the ligation to allow 120 min of reperfusion (MIRI). Dynamic changes in the electrocardiogram (ECG) were recorded throughout the procedure. Successful establishment of the model was confirmed by significant ST-segment elevation and T-wave elevation on the ECG, as well as pallor or cyanosis of the heart surface. conversely, mice that did not successfully meet these model criteria were excluded. In the sham operation group, an empty puncture was performed without LAD ligation, while all other treatments remained identical to those administered to the myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) group.

Behavioral tests

Behavioral tests were performed sequentially over a 2-day period, with the OFT followed by the EPM test. Mice were acclimated to the test chamber for at least 3 h on each test day. Experimental animal behavior was analyzed using the ANY-Maze animal behavior video tracking system (Global Biotech, USA). Each mouse was washed with 75% alcohol and water in turn before testing to eliminate the information of the previous mouse.

Open field test: a white box (40 cm wide; 40 cm long; Height 35 cm). The area was virtually divided into a central area (20×20 cm) and a peripheral area by ANY-Maze. At the beginning of the test, each animal was gently placed in the central area, while an adaptation time of 30 s was preset, after which the center of the animal body was tracked to record the activity of the mice in the open field for 300 s. The total distance moved by mice was recorded. Number of central area entries, length of stay, and distance.

EPM test: The device consisted of two closed arms (30 cm each), two open arms (30 cm each), and a central area (7×7 cm). It was elevated by more than 50 cm. Each animal was gently placed in the central area, and an adaptation time of 30 s was preset, after which the activity of the 300 s mouse in the cross elevated maze was recorded by tracking the center of the animal’s body. The total distance moved by mice was recorded. Number of open arm entries, length of stay, and distance.

In vivo electrophysiological recordings

The Plexon multichannel neural signal acquisition and analysis system (Plexon System) was used. For neuro-electrical signal acquisition, a custom-built with a microwire array electrode(MWAs) 15-mm in length and the electrode site spacing is 300 μm. MWAs are fabricated with metal wire coated polyimide, fixed by epoxy and further covered with polyethylene glycol, which can melt when meeting the water. MWAs was implanted into areas of NTS (AP: -7.07 mm; ML: 0.3 mm; DV: -5.4 mm relative to bregma), PAG (AP: -4.76 mm; ML: 0.46 mm; DV: -2.45 mm relative to bregma) or PVN (AP: -0.85 mm; ML: 0.28 mm; DV: -4.75 mm relative to bregma), recording electrodes were attached to a 32-channel digital headstage processor(DHP), and headstage was connected to a 16-channel preamplifier (gain 100× bandpass filter from 150 Hz to 9 kHz for unit activity and from 0.7 Hz to 170 Hz for field potentials, Plexon).

The neuronal signals were amplified and stored using a OmniPlex amplifier and OmniPlex data acquisition software (Plexon Inc). Spiking activity was digitized at 40 kHz and bandpass filtered from 250 Hz to 8 kHz. The offline SpikeSorter software (Plexon Inc, USA) was used for spike sorting to define the wave peak classification of a single unit, that performed with the T-Dis E-M algorithm built. The spike was detected when the lowest waveform reached the amplitude threshold, which was three standard deviations above the noise amplitude. Next, the waveform spikes were checked manually to confirm consistency. In the ISI histogram, all identifiable units showed a refractory period ( > 2 ms). Only units with sufficient separation were included in the data analysis (L ratio <0.2, isolation distance >15). To avoid analysis of the same neuron recorded on different channels, we computed cross-correlation histograms. The k-means-based unsupervised clustering approach was used to categorize the neurons into wide spine neurons (WS) and narrow spine neurons (NS). The three-dimensional (3D) space was defined based on the Area under peak(μV), Firing rate(HZ), and Trough to peak duration (μs) at the baseline of each neuron. Those with full width at half maximum (FWHM) and discharge rate were defined inhibitory interneurons (F-INS). Based on the baseline discharge frequency, the NS group was further subdivided into the hypothetical fast-spiking parvalbumin (FS-PV Ins) ( > 10 Hz) and into non-FS NS neurons. At the end of the experiment, the placement of the multi-channel electrode was examined in all mice, and data from animals with placements outside the target brain region were discarded.

Fiber photometry recording

The GCaMP signal in the NTS brain region was recorded by optical fiber photometry system (thinktech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China). The fluorescence signal was excited using a 470 nm laser, with a 405 nm laser as a reference. The commutator and the implanted fiber were connected via an optical fiber with an optical density of 230 μm and 0.37 nA. The laser power at the fiber tip was adjusted to 10 to 20 μW to minimize laser bleaching. Fluorescence signals were recorded continuously and normalized (ΔF/F mean signal), and for analysis of event frequency, data that exceeded the median of baseline, absolute deviation (MAD) and remained above baseline MAD2 were considered calcium signal events. A self-developed MatLab program was used to draw heat maps and average calcium trace curves. At the end of the experiment, the locations of virus injection and fiber optic placement were examined in all mice, and data from animals with placements outside the target brain region were discarded.

Chemogenetics and optogenetics

For in vivo chemogenetics experiments, animals expressing hM4D (Gi) were intraperitoneally (ip) injected with clozapine -Noxide (CNO) at a dose of 1 mg/kg.

Before the optogenetic stimulation, the animals underwent at least two habituation sessions, each lasting 20 min, during which the experimental animals acclimated to the connection of the fiber jumper. Optogenetic suppression was performed using an LED light source (yellow light 570 nm), which provided approximately 3-5 mW light at the end through the fiber. In addition, photoinhibition was delivered using a 5-s on and 5-s off mode, given a continuous administration of light. At the end of the experiment, the locations of virus injection and fiber optic placement were examined in all mice, and data from animals with placements outside the target brain region were discarded.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with 0.9% normal saline and 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2-7.4). The brains of the mice were removed and fixed in 4% PFA solution overnight, then transferred to sucrose solution and dehydrated according to a gradient of 15% and 30%. Mouse brains were sectioned coronal at a thickness of 30 mm using a freezing microtome. Brain slices were immersed in antifreeze (30% ethylene glycol, 20% glycerol and 50% PBS, pH 7.3-7.4, osmolal concentration 300-305 mOsmkg-1) and stored at -20 °C for immunofluorescence staining. Various antigens on the surface of brain slices were blocked to prevent non-specific binding by rinsing three times (10 min each) with PBS on a shaker and incubating with blocking buffer containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) + 0.3% triton X for 1 h at room temperature. These sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight (16-18 h) at 4 °C. Brain sections were washed three times (10 min each) with PBS and incubated with fluorescent dye-conjugated secondary antibodies in the dark at room temperature for 1.5 times, after which they were placed on a shaker and rinsed three times (10 min each). Brain slices were mounted on glass slides (4 to 6 brain slices per slide). After mounting the brain slices on slides, DAPI (DAPI 1:500) was added and the slices were washed again three times (5 min each). Next, a 70% glycerol sealing film was applied so that it could be observed or imaged after wiping with absolute ethanol. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-c-Fos (1:800, abcam). Secondary antibodies included donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500, abcam).

Staining with TTC

Mouse hearts were rapidly frozen in a -20 °C freezer for 20 min before removal. Five to six sections were cut in the coronal section at 2 mm intervals from the apex of the heart and placed in 2% TTC. The cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min at 37 °C in an incubator. The morphological changes of myocardial tissue were observed under a light microscope, and the infarct size of myocardial tissue was calculated. Ischemic risk zone (AAR), Infarct size (INF), Left ventricular area (LV).

Image acquisition

To examine eYFP, GFP, or mCherry expression and fiber location, animals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with 90% normal saline with 4% paraformaldehyde. Coronal brain sections (30μm) were made using a freezing microtome. Each section was imaged under a scanning machine (VS200) or a confocal microscope (Olympus).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Software Inc version 8.0 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. At least three biological replicates were set for each experimental condition, and three technical replicates were performed for each biological sample. For comparing data among different groups, we employed either a one-way ANOVA or an unpaired t-test as appropriate. The thresholds for statistical significance were set at P values of less than 0.05, 0.01, and 0.001. Furthermore, the results are presented as means plus or minus standard error of the mean (SEM). For detailed information on all statistical procedures used in this study, please refer to the footnotes below the figures.The modeling/surgery, data collection, and data analysis were performed by different experimenters, who were blinded to the group allocations.
