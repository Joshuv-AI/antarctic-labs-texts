Acute sacral osteomyelitis mimicking hip pathology in a child: A diagnostic challenge
Authors: Ayoub Boutahar, Hind Abou Eljaoud, Tayeb Bentayeb, Lamiae Chater
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pelvic osteomyelitis is rare in children, and sacral involvement is particularly unusual. Due to its deep anatomical location, clinical presentation is often nonspecific, frequently leading to diagnostic delays or misdiagnosis as hip arthritis or abdominal pathology. We report the case of a 10-year-old boy who presented with high-grade fever, right hip pain, and inability to bear weight. Initial hip ultrasonography was normal, but careful clinical examination revealed sacral tenderness, prompting pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). MRI demonstrated acute osteomyelitis of the right hemi-sacrum associated with a small paravertebral collection, diffuse iliopsoas muscle infiltration, and a minimal sacroiliac joint effusion, without spinal canal or neural foraminal involvement. Blood cultures remained negative. The patient was treated with 10 days of intravenous antibiotics followed by 3 weeks of oral therapy, resulting in complete clinical, biological, and radiological recovery. Follow-up MRI confirmed resolution of the marrow edema and disappearance of the paravertebral collection. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of sacral osteomyelitis presenting as hip pathology and emphasizes the pivotal role of MRI in establishing an early diagnosis, assessing disease extent, and guiding appropriate management.

Introduction

Acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AHO) is a relatively common infection in children, predominantly affecting the metaphyses of long bones due to their rich vascular supply [1,2]. However, localizations in flat bones, such as the scapula or the pelvic girdle, are significantly rarer [3,4].

Sacral osteomyelitis is one of the least frequent pelvic sites and is often associated with pyogenic sacroiliitis [5]. Because the pelvic bones are deeply situated, the classical signs of infection—such as localized redness, heat, or obvious swelling—are frequently absent [3,6].

This nonspecific presentation frequently leads to initial misdiagnosis as septic arthritis, transient synovitis, or even abdominal pathology, resulting in diagnostic delay and potential complications [4,5]. In this context, imaging—particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—plays a crucial role in establishing an early and accurate diagnosis.

The present case is noteworthy because sacral osteomyelitis initially presented as an apparent hip disorder, despite the absence of hip abnormalities on ultrasonography. MRI revealed not only sacral marrow involvement but also associated paravertebral collection, iliopsoas infiltration, and minimal sacroiliac joint effusion, while excluding spinal canal extension. This case highlights the diagnostic pitfalls of atypical pelvic osteomyelitis and underscores the essential role of MRI in establishing the diagnosis and assessing disease extent.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old boy with no significant past medical or surgical history was admitted to our institution for fever and right hip pain associated with an inability to bear weight for 2 days. There was no history of recent trauma, previous osteoarticular infection, or underlying chronic disease.

On admission, the patient was febrile with a temperature of 39.7°C. Clinical examination revealed complete functional impairment of the right lower limb and painful limitation of both active and passive movements of the right hip. Despite the predominance of hip symptoms, careful physical examination identified marked tenderness over the sacral region. No neurological deficit, sphincter dysfunction, or cutaneous abnormalities were noted.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated an inflammatory syndrome with a C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 100 mg/L, an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 30 mm/h at the first hour, and a white blood cell count of 9200/mm3. Blood cultures were obtained before initiation of antibiotic therapy but remained sterile. Hip ultrasonography showed no joint effusion and excluded septic arthritis. No psoas abscess or intra-abdominal collection was detected.

Given the discrepancy between clinical findings and negative ultrasound results, a pelvic MRI was performed (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The pelvic MRI demonstrated abnormal marrow signal involving the right hemi-sacrum, characterized by low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on STIR sequences, consistent with acute osteomyelitis. A poorly defined right paravertebral collection measuring 24 × 9 mm demonstrated peripheral contrast enhancement. Associated diffuse inflammatory infiltration of the right iliopsoas muscle was present. A small right sacroiliac joint effusion was also identified and appeared to communicate with the adjacent paravertebral collection.

No evidence of cortical destruction, epidural extension, spinal canal involvement, neural foraminal extension, or neurological compression was detected. The collection was considered too small and poorly defined to allow safe image-guided aspiration or drainage.

The hip joints appeared normal, with no joint effusion or imaging features suggestive of septic arthritis.

Empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy consisting of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid combined with gentamicin was initiated. The patient showed rapid clinical improvement, with defervescence occurring within the first days of treatment and progressive resolution of pain. CRP levels decreased significantly during hospitalization. The patient regained the ability to walk independently within 10 days. Intravenous antibiotic therapy was continued for 10 days and subsequently switched to oral antibiotics for an additional 3 weeks.

Clinical follow-up evaluations were performed at 3 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months. The patient remained afebrile and asymptomatic, with complete recovery of functional capacity and no evidence of recurrence. Follow-up MRI demonstrated complete resolution of the bone marrow edema within the right hemi-sacrum and disappearance of the previously described paravertebral collection, confirming a favorable response to antibiotic therapy.

Discussion

Pediatric acute hematogenous osteomyelitis (AO) is primarily a hematogenous infection. In long bones, bacteria settle in the nutrient artery loops and venous sinusoids of the metaphysis. In flat bones like the sacrum, the infection occurs in “metaphyseal equivalents” [1,3]. In the pelvis, the iliac bone is the most commonly affected site due to its rich blood supply, followed by the ischium and sacrum [3,7]. The infection can extend to adjacent subperiosteal areas and overlying soft tissues [1,4]. The deep anatomical position of the sacrum masks external inflammatory signs, which explains why tenderness on deep palpation is often the only localizing physical finding [3,5].

The incidence of pediatric acute osteomyelitis ranges from 1 to 13 per 100,000 children [1,8]. Pelvic AO typically occurs in children aged 7-14 years, which is slightly older than the average for long-bone osteomyelitis [3,4,6]. The most common presenting symptoms include the classic triad of fever, low back or buttock pain, and difficulty weight-bearing [5,9]. As seen in this case, pain is often referred to the hip, which can lead to unnecessary investigations of the joint.

CRP is the marker of choice because it is more sensitive and normalizes faster than the erythrocyte sedimentation rate with appropriate therapy [1,10]. Testing CRP at diagnosis has a reported sensitivity of 95% [1,11]. Blood cultures are vital and identify the pathogen in 50%-80% of pediatric cases [3,9,12].

Sacral osteomyelitis frequently represents a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific clinical presentation. Several differential diagnoses should be systematically considered. Hip pathology is often suspected initially, as referred pain to the hip is common, mimicking conditions such as septic arthritis or transient synovitis [13]. In this context, a normal hip ultrasound constitutes an important negative finding that should prompt further evaluation of the pelvic bones [5,14,15]. According to Mnif et al. [13], flat bone AO can progress through 3 stages: an initial stage mimicking arthritis, an intermediate stage with soft tissue extension, and a final stage involving deep soft tissue diffusion causing a spinal syndrome [16]. Abdominal conditions may also be considered, particularly when pain is localized to the iliac fossa, potentially leading to a misdiagnosis of appendicitis and necessitating abdominal imaging. Malignancies such as Ewing sarcoma or leukemia should be ruled out, as they may present with similar bone involvement and systemic manifestations. Finally, chronic inflammatory conditions, including chronic recurrent multifocal osteomyelitis, should be considered, especially in cases with a relapsing course or atypical localization. [5,17].

Initial plain radiographs are rarely helpful, as bone changes only become evident after 7-21 days [1,3]. Ultrasound is primarily useful for excluding hip joint effusions or abdominal processes like appendicitis [5,13]. MRI is the gold standard due to its superior sensitivity for detecting bone marrow edema and periosteal reactions within 24-48 hours of symptom onset [2,18,19]. In our patient, microbiological confirmation could not be obtained because blood cultures remained negative and the paravertebral collection was small, poorly defined, and not considered suitable for image-guided aspiration. Nevertheless, the diagnosis was strongly supported by the characteristic MRI findings, elevated inflammatory markers, and complete clinical, biological, and radiological response to antibiotic therapy.

Distinguishing sacral osteomyelitis from malignant bone tumors, particularly Ewing’s sarcoma, is crucial, as both conditions may present with similar imaging features, including bone marrow edema and soft tissue involvement on MRI [2,13]. From a clinical perspective, acute osteomyelitis typically presents with a rapid onset over a few days, often associated with high fever and markedly elevated inflammatory markers. In contrast, Ewing’s sarcoma generally follows a more insidious course over several weeks, with persistent pain that is often more pronounced at night. Radiologically, although both entities may demonstrate marrow signal abnormalities, Ewing’s sarcoma is more likely to present as an aggressive, infiltrative soft tissue mass. Conversely, osteomyelitis is more commonly associated with fluid collections, subperiosteal abscesses, and characteristic findings such as bone sequestrum, which appears as a low-signal intensity fragment on all MRI sequences [13,21]. Finally, the therapeutic response can be a key distinguishing factor. Rapid clinical and biological improvement following antibiotic therapy strongly supports an infectious etiology rather than a malignant process. In our patient, several elements strongly supported the diagnosis of osteomyelitis. The acute onset of symptoms associated with high fever and markedly elevated inflammatory markers was more consistent with an infectious process. Imaging findings on MRI did not demonstrate any aggressive soft tissue mass suggestive of malignancy. Moreover, the rapid clinical and biological improvement observed after initiation of antibiotic therapy further confirmed the infectious origin, making a malignant diagnosis highly unlikely.

Contrast-enhanced MRI plays also a crucial role in evaluating the extent of infection, particularly in assessing the spread from the sacral cortex to adjacent structures, including the spinal canal [5,18]. It also allows detection of soft tissue involvement, such as infiltration of adjacent muscle groups like the psoas, which strongly suggests an infectious etiology [18,22]. A severe and potentially life-threatening complication is the extension of infection into the spinal canal [7,11]. Clinically, this may manifest as reluctance to sit or stand, as well as reduced ability to bend forward, reflecting possible spinal involvement [1,23]. In rare cases, the presence of gas within the spinal canal, as observed in emphysematous osteomyelitis, indicates a particularly severe form of infection [7,24]. These complications are associated with a significantly increased mortality rate, reaching up to 30% within 60 days [25,26].

Staphylococcus aureus remains the most common causative organism of pediatric osteomyelitis across all age groups [27]. In younger children, particularly those under 4 years of age, Kingella kingae is also a frequently identified pathogen [20,26]. According to the French Pediatric Infectious Disease Group (GPIP) guidelines, empirical antibiotic therapy should primarily target methicillin-sensitive S aureus, using high-dose amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, cefuroxime, or cefazolin. The addition of gentamicin is recommended in severe or septicemic presentations [27,28]. Although our patient received 10 days of intravenous antibiotic therapy, recent evidence supports an early switch to oral treatment after 3-7 days, provided that the patient is afebrile and shows a significant decrease in inflammatory markers, particularly a reduction of at least 50% in CRP levels [10,17,29]. The total duration of antibiotic therapy for uncomplicated osteomyelitis is generally recommended to be at least 3-4 weeks [2,15,27].

Delayed diagnosis of sacral osteomyelitis may lead to serious complications, including the formation of sequestra (necrotic bone) and abscesses that may require surgical drainage. A rare but severe complication is the contiguous spread of infection into the spinal canal, which can result in ascending spinal infection or epidural abscess formation. These conditions are associated with a significant increase in morbidity and may adversely affect patient outcomes [30,31]. In our case, the early use of MRI despite inconclusive initial investigations allowed prompt diagnosis and initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy, resulting in a favorable clinical and biological outcome. This highlights the importance of early suspicion and timely imaging in preventing severe complications.

Conclusion

Sacral osteomyelitis is a rare but important diagnosis in children presenting with febrile limp. Its atypical presentation often leads to diagnostic delay and potential complications. Early recognition, careful clinical examination, and prompt use of MRI are essential for accurate diagnosis. Timely initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy ensures favorable outcomes and reduces the risk of severe complications.
