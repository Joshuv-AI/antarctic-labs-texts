Pandemic shadows in the operating room how COVID-19 altered the risk and timing of surgical site infections: a multivariable risk assessment and time-to-event analysis in a cohort of abdominal surgery patients
Authors: Fatimah Alshahrani, Ibraheem Altamimi, Ahmed Alhawamdeh, Sultan Alshehri, Mohamed Yaser Alquhidan, Saud Alhasani, Badr Alshehri, Mohammed Alshehri, Bassam Tarig Alkhuwaitir, Mohammed N. Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infections (SSIs) are among the most frequent and preventable complications following abdominal surgery, influenced by patient, procedural, and systemic factors. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic introduced new perioperative protocols—including universal masking, visitor restrictions, enhanced personal protective equipment (PPE) use, and modified operating-room workflows—that may have altered infection dynamics. This retrospective cohort study, conducted in a large tertiary center in Saudi Arabia, examined whether these changes affected SSI risk and timing using survival analysis.ObjectivesTo determine SSI incidence, identify independent risk factors, and assess how the COVID-19 pandemic influenced infection onset and outcomes among patients undergoing abdominal surgery.MethodsWe reviewed 809 adult abdominal-surgery cases between January 2019 and December 2022. Data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health-record system by two independent reviewers using a standardized form under institutional approval (IRB No. E-22–6926). Data confidentiality was strictly maintained. SSIs were defined according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors, while Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses evaluated infection-free survival and time-to-event outcomes.ResultsOf 809 patients, 47 (5.8%) developed SSIs, a rate consistent with international benchmarks. Independent predictors included diabetes mellitus (adjusted odds ratio [OR] 2.14; 95% CI 1.05–4.34), contaminated wounds (OR 3.67; 95% CI 1.58–8.54), and postoperative hyperglycemia ≥ 7 mmol/L (OR 2.95; 95% CI 1.32–6.57). During the pandemic, infections occurred later in the postoperative course, suggesting a delayed onset despite similar median times to infection. No multicollinearity was observed, and model diagnostics confirmed stability.ConclusionSSIs remain a significant postoperative concern. Diabetes, wound contamination, and hyperglycemia were consistent predictors. Pandemic-related procedural adaptations may have delayed infection onset, underscoring the need for extended postoperative surveillance and context-specific infection-control strategies during public-health emergencies.

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain among the most frequent and consequential healthcare-associated infections following abdominal surgery, increasing postoperative morbidity, mortality, and costs [1, 2]. The burden is particularly high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resource constraints and uneven adherence to evidence-based perioperative practices contribute to elevated rates [3]. Abdominal procedures—such as colectomies and exploratory laparotomies—are especially vulnerable because of longer operative times, exposure to complex gastrointestinal microbiota, and frequent contact with contaminated tissues [4].

During the past decade, multiple initiatives have aimed to reduce SSIs, including timely antimicrobial prophylaxis, optimized skin antisepsis, perioperative normothermia, glucose control, and the World Health Organization (WHO) Surgical Safety Checklist [5, 6]. Evidence from meta-analyses and registries has shown mixed results across settings, reflecting differences in patient case mix, baseline quality, and implementation fidelity [4]. These variations underscore the importance of local data from high-volume centers that manage diverse abdominal surgeries and can account for patient, procedure, and system-level factors.

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic introduced system-wide changes that plausibly altered perioperative infection dynamics. Hospitals adopted universal masking, enhanced personal protective equipment (PPE), restricted visitation, and modified patient flows and staffing patterns [7]. In operating rooms (ORs), environmental controls—such as positive-pressure ventilation, high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration, and increased air-change rates—were emphasized to mitigate aerosol transmission. Although primarily designed for respiratory-virus control, these measures may have influenced bacterial contamination by altering traffic patterns and workflow discipline [8].

Published studies during the pandemic have reported heterogeneous trends in SSI incidence across surgical specialties and regions. Some centers observed reductions attributed to stricter hygiene and access controls, whereas others reported stable or increased rates, potentially linked to case deferrals, a shift toward urgent or open procedures, staff redeployment, and interruptions in prophylaxis pathways [9, 10]. These inconsistencies suggest that pandemic-era effects on SSIs are context-dependent and warrant analyses that disentangle patient-, procedure-, and system-level contributors.

Beyond overall incidence, the timing of infections may have shifted during the pandemic because of altered discharge practices, telemedicine follow-up, and changes in inpatient exposure. Time-to-event methods are well suited to explore such changes, enabling comparison of infection-free survival while adjusting for covariates [11]. Established patient-level risk factors—such as diabetes mellitus, obesity, malnutrition, smoking, and perioperative hyperglycemia—remain important, as do operative variables including wound classification, surgical approach, duration, and urgency [2, 12]. Recent meta-analyses indicate that telemedicine-based postoperative wound monitoring can enhance early detection and reduce unnecessary readmissions [13]

Against this background, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of abdominal-surgery patients in a high-volume tertiary referral center. The objectives were to estimate SSI incidence, identify independent predictors after adjusting for patient and operative factors, and evaluate whether the COVID-19 period was associated with differences in infection timing using Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional-hazards analyses. By integrating clinical, procedural, and system-level variables, this study seeks to inform perioperative infection-prevention strategies in both routine and disrupted healthcare environments.

This study therefore aimed to assess the incidence, determinants, and timing of SSIs in abdominal surgery at a tertiary center in Saudi Arabia, comparing pre-pandemic and pandemic periods to inform sustainable infection-prevention strategies

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at King Saud University Medical City, a high-volume tertiary referral center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, which performs a wide range of elective and emergency abdominal surgeries. The objective was to evaluate the incidence, risk factors, and timing of surgical site infections (SSIs) in patients undergoing abdominal surgery, comparing outcomes before and during the COVID-19 pandemic. Ethical approval was obtained from the King Saud University Institutional Review Board (IRB No. E-22–6926). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study involved only retrospective record review. All procedures followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

A total of 809 adult patients who underwent abdominal surgery between January 2019 and December 2022 were included. Patients were identified from the hospital’s electronic surgical database using procedure codes for colectomy, hemicolectomy, sigmoidectomy, low anterior resection, and exploratory laparotomy. Based on the timing of surgery relative to the WHO pandemic declaration, patients were grouped into two cohorts: pre-pandemic (January 2019–February 2020) and pandemic (March 2020–December 2022).

Data collection and quality control

Data were extracted from the hospital electronic health-record system (BestCare) using a structured data-collection form to ensure uniformity. Two independent researchers entered and validated all records. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third senior reviewer acting as an arbitrator. The data were anonymized before analysis, and confidentiality was maintained in compliance with institutional and national research-ethics standards.

Antibiotic prophylaxis and skin preparation protocols

Per institutional and CDC-aligned guidelines, a first-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin 2 g IV) was administered within 60 minutes before incision; metronidazole (500 mg IV) was added for colorectal procedures. Antibiotics were discontinued within 24 hours postoperatively unless contamination warranted therapeutic continuation. Skin preparation was performed using povidone-iodine 10% solution for most procedures; chlorhexidine-alcohol (2%/70%) was reserved for selected clean-contaminated cases depending on surgeon preference. All patients were draped using sterile technique, and glove/instrument changes were routinely performed before wound closure.

Wound examination and postoperative care

Postoperative wound assessment was performed daily by the attending surgeon or a trained surgical nurse, following CDC criteria for SSI. Dressings were kept intact for the first 48 hours and replaced with sterile gauze thereafter, unless drainage or contamination occurred. Wound swabs were collected when infection was suspected, and culture results were reviewed by both the surgical and infection-control teams. Postoperative dressing type may also influence infection outcomes, as shown in Cochrane reviews comparing standard and advanced materials [14].

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was SSI occurrence, classified as superficial incisional, deep incisional, or organ/space infection according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definitions. Secondary outcomes included time to SSI, postoperative sepsis (per Sepsis-3 criteria), and total hospital stay. All SSI diagnoses were confirmed through clinical evaluation, wound culture, or imaging within 30 days postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviation (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) as appropriate, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Univariate comparisons used independent-samples t-tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Variables with p < 0.20 in univariate analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression models using backward elimination to identify independent SSI predictors. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional-hazards models assessed SSI-free survival and timing. Model adequacy was checked by variance-inflation factors (VIF < 5) and Schoenfeld residuals for proportionality. Significance was set at p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

All analyses followed the STROCSS 2021 and SAMPL reporting recommendations for surgical observational studies

Results

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics (N = 809)

The study included 809 patients with a mean age of 49.2 years (SD 19.9). Males accounted for 55.3% (n = 447), while females made up 44.7% (n = 362). Most patients were ASA class 2 (60.1%), followed by class 3 (22.2%) and class 1 (17.8%). The average BMI was 26.0 (SD 7.4). Regarding smoking, 90% were non-smokers. Diabetes was present in about a quarter of patients (24.9%). Preoperative glucose averaged 6.04 mmol/L, rising to 8.31 mmol/L postoperatively. Cardiovascular disease was found in 11.7% of patients.

Laparoscopic surgery was the most common approach (55.7%), followed by laparotomy (40.7%). Surgeries were nearly evenly split between pre-pandemic (54.1%) and pandemic (45.9%) periods. The majority of procedures were elective (85.7%). Chlorhexidine was used in only 1.4% of cases; betadine dominated at 98.3%. Nearly all patients (93.7%) received antibiotic prophylaxis, and gloves and masks were universally used. Most wounds were classified as grade 2 (97.2%). Average surgery duration was 231 minutes, and most patients (91.1%) had drains placed.

SSIs occurred in 5.8% of patients, with superficial infections making up half of these cases, followed by organ-space (32.5%) and deep SSIs (26.5%). The average hospital stay was notably long at 43.6 days, reflecting complexity or complications. Positive wound cultures were found in 61.9% of tested cases, and sepsis developed in 12.5%. Low anterior resection (26%) and hemicolectomy (17.5%) were the most common procedures. Comparison before and during the COVID-19 pandemic (N = 809)

Comparing pre-pandemic and pandemic periods revealed important shifts. Median BMI increased during the pandemic (26.0 vs. 24.65, p = 0.001), as did cardiovascular disease prevalence (17.0% vs. 7.0%, p < 0.001). The proportion of laparotomies rose significantly during COVID-19 (49.5% vs. 32.9%, p < 0.001), with a corresponding drop in laparoscopic surgeries. Emergent surgeries also increased during the pandemic (17.3% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.023).

SSI rates doubled during the pandemic, rising from 3.9% to 8.1% (p = 0.015), driven mainly by an increase in superficial SSIs (68.0% vs. 26.3%, p = 0.014). Antibiotic prophylaxis use declined significantly during the pandemic (88.9% vs. 97.7%, p < 0.001). Notably, exploratory laparotomies increased sharply during COVID-19 (25.8% vs. 8.6%, p < 0.001), suggesting a trend toward more invasive, urgent surgeries with higher risk profiles. Characteristics by presence of SSI (N = 809)

Patients who developed SSIs were more likely to have had surgery during the pandemic (8.1% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.015). All patients with SSIs underwent wound swabs (100% vs. 50%, p = 0.012), and they exhibited higher postoperative complication severity, as measured by the Clavien-Dindo score (96.3% with score 2 vs. 3.7% in non-SSI, p = 0.003). Preoperative glucose was also higher in the SSI group (median 5.41 vs. 4.64 mmol/L, p < 0.001). Although SSI rates varied by procedure type, colectomy and exploratory laparotomy had notably higher infection rates. Cox regression on time to SSI

Smoking significantly increased the hazard of developing SSI by over six-fold (HR 6.13, p = 0.047). Elevated postoperative glucose was also associated with increased risk (HR 0.74, p = 0.048), while antibiotic prophylaxis greatly reduced risk (HR 0.17, p = 0.025). Discussion

This study represents one of the first analyses from a large tertiary center in Saudi Arabia evaluating how the COVID-19 pandemic altered surgical site infection (SSI) dynamics using survival analysis. The 5.8% incidence aligns with international benchmarks, yet infection onset during the pandemic occurred later, reflecting shifts in perioperative care and infection-control practices.

Pandemic influence on SSI incidence and timing

Our SSI incidence is comparable to findings from the FALCON multicentre trial conducted in low- and middle-income settings [15], indicating that the COVID-19 era altered the epidemiology of hospital-acquired infections. Some investigators documented reduced SSI rates, attributed to heightened hygiene, masking, and visitor restrictions [10]. Others reported no significant change or even increases, possibly due to longer operative times, delays in antibiotic prophylaxis, or reductions in elective procedures [16, 17]. The observed delay in SSI onset in our cohort aligns with these mixed trends and suggests that intensified environmental hygiene and restricted patient movement may have slowed early wound contamination, although prolonged hospital stays and higher case complexity during the pandemic could have sustained overall infection risk.

Enhanced air-handling measures—including positive-pressure ventilation and high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration—implemented to reduce airborne viral transmission might also have influenced SSI timing by improving operating-room airflow quality [8]. Nevertheless, such environmental benefits may have been partially offset by changes in staff allocation and infection-control focus away from traditional SSI prevention bundles [2, 6].

Patient-related risk factors: diabetes and hyperglycemia

Diabetes mellitus and postoperative hyperglycemia emerged as independent predictors of SSI, consistent with prior evidence linking impaired glucose regulation to delayed wound healing and immune dysfunction [18]. Hyperglycemia may also reflect stress responses to surgical trauma rather than diabetes alone. Perioperative glucose control is therefore a key modifiable element within the CDC and WHO SSI-prevention bundles, which recommend maintaining serum glucose below 10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL) for at least 48 hours postoperatively [2; 6]. Implementing standardized perioperative glycemic-management protocols—coordinated by surgical, anesthetic, and endocrine teams—remains essential for both diabetic and non-diabetic patients.

Procedure-related factors: wound contamination and operative conditions

Wound classification retained a strong association with infection risk, reaffirming that contaminated or dirty wounds markedly increase SSI incidence. Adherence to aseptic technique, timely antibiotic prophylaxis, and controlled intraoperative traffic flow are therefore critical. The pandemic period saw more urgent laparotomies, which may have contributed to elevated contamination levels and the higher SSI rate observed in our study.

In line with WHO and CDC guidance, perioperative prophylaxis at our institution consisted primarily of cefazolin (2 g IV) within 60 minutes of incision, supplemented with metronidazole (500 mg IV) for colorectal cases. This combination covers common gram-positive and anaerobic flora in clean-contaminated surgeries. The use of prophylaxis beyond 24 hours is not recommended and was restricted in our analysis to therapeutic coverage for dirty or contaminated wounds, consistent with current guidelines [4].

Antiseptic practice and environmental considerations

The selection and application of skin antiseptic agents also influence SSI risk. Although povidone-iodine was the dominant agent in our cohort, growing evidence—including the WHOs most recent SSI guideline—supports the use of alcohol-based chlorhexidine gluconate over povidone-iodine for preoperative skin preparation [6]. Chlorhexidine–alcohol solutions offer rapid onset and residual activity, though availability and cost may limit adoption in some settings. Regardless of the agent, proper application technique and adequate drying time are critical determinants of antiseptic efficacy [19].

Environmental and workflow factors should not be overlooked. The COVID-19 response emphasized airflow optimization, PPE discipline, and surface disinfection [7]. These practices likely contributed to delayed infection onset by minimizing intraoperative contamination. However, sustained implementation of these environmental measures outside pandemic conditions could yield long-term SSI reduction benefits.

Antibiotic bowel preparation and perioperative bundles

Antibiotic bowel preparation remains a debated adjunct in colorectal surgery. Meta-analyses show that combining oral and mechanical bowel preparation significantly reduces SSI risk compared with mechanical preparation alone [20]. While not universally required, its selective use in high-risk colorectal cases may complement perioperative prophylaxis.

Comprehensive SSI-prevention bundles, as endorsed by the CDC and WHO, integrate multiple evidence-based measures: normothermia, oxygenation, glucose control, antimicrobial prophylaxis, skin antisepsis, and sterile technique [2, 6]. Routine sterile glove and instrument changes before wound closure, supported by the ChEETAh cluster-randomized trial [21], remain critical SSI-prevention measures. Meta-analytic evidence continues to support antibiotic prophylaxis in reducing infection risk across surgical categories [22]. Sustained compliance with these bundles, even amid healthcare disruptions, is critical to maintaining surgical safety and infection-control performance.

Future infection-control protocols could incorporate hybrid preventive frameworks that integrate environmental monitoring with data-driven SSI surveillance.

Clinical and policy implications

Our findings highlight the importance of adaptive infection-prevention systems capable of maintaining core SSI safeguards during crises. Institutions should routinely audit bundle adherence, monitor postoperative glycemic control, and ensure that antimicrobial prophylaxis follows standardized timing and spectrum. In parallel, environmental interventions introduced during the pandemic—such as airflow monitoring and restricted OR access—could be institutionalized as part of future quality-improvement programs.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, it employed a retrospective cohort design, which is inherently subject to selection and information bias. Although data were extracted from a validated electronic health-record system and reviewed by multiple investigators, certain variables—such as exact timing of antibiotic administration, intraoperative temperature control, or nutritional status—were not consistently documented.

Second, the analysis was conducted in a single tertiary-care center, which may limit the generalizability of results to other hospitals with different patient populations, resources, or infection-control infrastructure. Third, microbiological data were restricted to routine cultures, which might have underestimated polymicrobial or resistant infections.

Fourth, the study period encompassed both pre-pandemic and pandemic phases, during which systemic disruptions in hospital operations may have introduced unmeasured confounders. Despite these limitations, the use of standardized CDC definitions, rigorous data validation, and multivariable modeling enhances internal validity and strengthens confidence in the observed associations.

Future research should include multicenter prospective studies to confirm these findings, incorporate molecular pathogen profiling, and evaluate the long-term impact of pandemic-driven workflow changes on SSI outcomes. These steps will be essential for developing robust, context-specific infection-prevention strategies across diverse healthcare settings.

This analytical framework enabled a comprehensive assessment of both the determinants and timing of surgical site infections (SSIs). By integrating logistic regression with survival analysis, the study not only identified independent predictors but also evaluated how systemic changes during the COVID-19 pandemic influenced infection onset. The combination of rigorous data validation, standardized CDC outcome definitions, and robust multivariable modeling strengthens the reliability of the findings and provides a quantitative foundation for the interpretations presented in the subsequent sections.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a significant postoperative concern in abdominal surgery, even amidst enhanced infection-control measures during the COVID-19 era. Diabetes mellitus, contaminated wounds, and postoperative hyperglycemia were consistent independent predictors, underscoring the importance of perioperative metabolic optimization and strict adherence to antibiotic-prophylaxis protocols. The observed delay in SSI onset during the pandemic suggests that systemic adaptations—improved air-handling, restricted operating-room access, and intensified hygiene—may have modified infection dynamics without eliminating underlying risks.

These findings reinforce the value of sustained infection-prevention bundles that integrate evidence-based interventions from both the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the World Health Organization (WHO). Institutionalizing pandemic-era environmental and workflow adaptations could enhance surgical resilience, quality of care, and patient safety across variable clinical contexts.

Looking forward, multicenter prospective studies are warranted to determine whether these procedural and environmental modifications translate into durable reductions in SSI incidence. Such evidence will be essential to guide healthcare systems in building adaptable, evidence-based infection-control frameworks capable of withstanding future public-health crises.
