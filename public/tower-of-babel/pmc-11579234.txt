Efficacy of a Standardized Regimen of Therapeutic Plasma Exchange and IVIG for Treatment of Antibody‐Mediated Rejection in Lung Transplant Recipients
Authors: Amelework Wodajo, Ravi Sarode, Nicole De Simone, Vaidehi Kaza, Amena Usmani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibody‐mediated rejection (AMR) in lung transplantation has been associated with poor long‐term clinical course and is a risk factor for chronic lung allograft dysfunction and graft loss. Appropriate management of AMR is necessary to improve graft survival in lung transplant recipients. There is currently no standardized approach to the treatment of lung AMR, and practices vary by institution. We sought to examine the efficacy of a standardized protocol of plasma exchange (PLEX) and IVIG in decreasing donor‐specific antibodies (DSAs) and improving AMR in lung transplant recipients. A retrospective chart review was conducted on all lung transplant recipients who completed a course of PLEX per UT Southwestern AMR protocol between January 2012 and December 2019 for diagnosis of AMR. Data were collected on the patient clinical course, treatment regimen, pre‐PLEX DSA, post‐PLEX DSA, follow‐up (> 1‐month post‐PLEX) DSA, and pre‐and post‐PLEX biopsy, when available. Of 527 patients who underwent lung transplantation during the study period, 56 (11%) received an acute course of PLEX every other day per protocol for AMR of lung transplant. Forty (71%) of 56 patients had one episode of AMR requiring PLEX; 16 patients (29%) had repeat episodes of AMR within 6 weeks to 47 months of the first episode. Most patients showed improvement in AMR on biopsy (69%) and a decline in DSA (68%). Our data suggest that treatment with combined PLEX and IVIG protocol appears effective for treating lung AMR.Keywords: chronic lung allograft dysfunction, donor‐specific antibodies, lung transplant rejection, lung transplantation

Introduction

Lung transplantation has become the treatment choice for selected patients with advanced lung disease [1, 2]. However, long‐term survival outcomes remain discouraging when compared with other solid organ transplant, with median survival after transplantation of approximately 6–6.5 years [1, 3]. Antibody‐mediated rejection (AMR) occurs in the recipient when antibodies attack donor‐specific antigens and the body's B cells produce antibodies to HLA antigens expressed on the donor's lung tissue [1, 4]. Antibody‐mediated rejection may be a reversible cause of acute graft dysfunction; if not adequately treated, there is a high incidence of mortality and poor long‐term survival after AMR [5]. In patients with CLAD, lack of DSA clearance with AMR was shown to result in worse short‐term outcomes [6]. In lung transplant recipients, there remain no standard diagnostic criteria for AMR. However, a combination of clinical features, serology, histopathology, and immunological findings may suggest an AMR. Key features include the presence of donor‐specific antibodies (DSAs), characteristic lung histology on biopsy with or without evidence of complement fragment 4d (c4d), and clinical evidence of graft dysfunction [1, 6, 7, 8]. The incidence of AMR in patients who underwent lung transplantation is variable; recent studies that define AMR using the ISHLT consensus definition report a prevalence of between 4% and 27% for lung transplants [9, 10]. Because AMR has emerged as an essential risk factor for lung allograft dysfunction and mortality, the primary aims of therapeutic approaches for AMR are removing circulating DSA and reducing production of additional antibodies [1, 9, 11]. In the setting of AMR, lack of DSA clearance has been associated with poor survival, though isolated elevated DSA alone is not an independent risk factor for adverse outcomes [9]. Studies suggest that early preemptive treatment of DSA in lung transplant patients may reduce the subsequent risk of CLAD or death; however, the lack of clearance of DSA is not a risk factor for CLAD [12]. Studies support the need to understand the association between DSA and long‐term outcomes after lung transplantation and the potential role of preemptive antibody‐directed therapy for transplant recipients with developed DSA [7]. The lack of an accepted common standard for the treatment of AMR has been an obstacle to treat AMR, and specific treatment protocols remain insufficient [1, 5, 6, 13]. AMR can be reversible for some patients, but chronic rejection (BOS/CLAD) may lead to complications to better long‐term outcomes and the leading cause of death beyond the first year after transplantation [1, 7]. This is why finding an effective treatment protocol for lung transplant AMR is so critical for patients' long‐term survival and graft function.

Currently, there is no standardized protocol to treat AMR. Current AMR treatment options include corticosteroids, plasma exchange (PLEX), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), anti‐CD20 antibodies, proteasome inhibitors, and complement inhibitors [5, 6, 11, 13]. Studies in kidney transplant rejection have shown greater response to AMR treatment in patients receiving a combination of IVIG and PLEX ± rituximab, when compared to using either PLEX or IVIG alone [14, 15, 16]. Several retrospective studies with multimodality protocols that included IVIG/PLEX/rituximab showed clearance of DSAs and preserved lung function in a minority of lung transplants with suspected AMR surviving 6 months after therapy [5, 6, 11, 13].

Here, we report our experience using a standardized protocol of treating patients with lung AMR with an acute course of 5 PLEX with low‐dose IVIG given after each procedure, followed by a high dose of IVIG and/or rituximab.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a retrospective, single‐center, descriptive study of lung transplant recipients who received treatment for AMR. All adult lung transplant recipients who completed a course of PLEX for diagnosis of AMR between January 2012 and December 2019 were eligible for inclusion. For eligible patients, the following data were collected from the electronic medical record: demographics, indication for transplant, type of transplant, symptoms, treatment regimen, pre‐PLEX DSA, post‐PLEX DSA, follow‐up DSA (> 1‐month post‐PLEX), and pre‐and post‐PLEX biopsy, when available. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived. The study was approved through the UT Southwestern Institutional Review Board (IRB).

A decline in spirometry in the setting of elevated DSA is enough to trigger initiation of PLEX/IVIG if the patient is symptomatic or asymptomatic. Patients with ISHLT‐defined AMR that included DSA in the definition were part of the data collection and analysis. PLEX was performed using Terumo BCT's spectra Optia (Terumo BCT, Lakewood, Co, USA). Our criteria for performing PLEX were acute decline in PFT, with either biopsy evidence of rejection with or without an increase in the DSA, an increase in the DSA with or without biopsy evidence of rejection, or clinical symptoms of rejection in the setting of either increased DSA or biopsy evidence of rejection. Biopsy feature of AMR that triggered the initiation of PLEX in our patients include primarily c4d staining. Other biopsy features that are suggestive of AMR include capillary inflammation, acute lung injury ± diffuse alveolar damage, endothelialitis, and neutrophilic septal margination [17]. Our treatment protocol for the treatment of suspected AMR includes PLEX every other day, followed by IVIG 100 mg/kg infusions after each session. After the fifth PLEX session, loading dose of IVIG 500 mg/kg × 3 doses ± rituximab 375 mg/m2 is given. In patients with inadequate clinical response to PLEX + IVIG ± rituximab, antithymocyte globulin (ATG) 6–9 mg/kg total dose was given. If patients did not respond adequately to PLEX/IVIG/rituximab/ATG, they were also given loading dose of bortezomib. Inadequate response to therapy is defined by lack of clinical improvement or further clinical deterioration, lack of stability of pulmonary functions, and progressive changes on imaging.

Therapeutic PLEX Procedure

The acute course of PLEX was performed with a replacement fluid of 5% albumin, and if patients were deemed to be at high risk of bleeding based on their fibrinogen, liver function test, or procedure performed prior to PLEX (i.e., lung biopsy), plasma was added to their replacement therapy. The machines used for TPE were Spectra Optia (Terumo BCT, Lakewood, CO), which uses a continuous‐flow centrifugation‐based plasma separator technique. Each patient underwent 1–1.2 plasma volume exchanges per session.

Laboratory Tests

Complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) were checked prior to initiating PLEX. All hemostatic tests were performed prior to initiation of PLEX and as needed; these included prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen prior to the procedure. HLA antibody testing was performed using One Lambda LABScreen (One Lambda Inc., Canoga Park, CA) single‐antigen bead kit on Luminex platform (Luminex, Austin, TX). In our facility, DSA ≥ 4000 MFI was used as the threshold for initiation of treatment for DSA positivity in lung transplant recipients. Patients were noted to have a decline in post‐PLEX and/or follow‐up DSA if the immunodominant pre‐PLEX DSA decreased by > 10%, which accounts for interassay variability. The study follow‐up period ranged from 12 months up to 7 years following the first day of PLEX. We characterized patients' demographics using descriptive statistics.

Results

A total of 527 patients underwent lung transplantation between January 2012 to December 2019. Among 527 transplant recipients, 56 patients received an acute course of PLEX for treatment of AMR according to our protocol. The AMR occurred at a median of 594 days after transplantation (range 8–4 060 days); 10 recipients developed an AMR within 30 days of transplantation. Patients ranged from 16 to 78 years of age (mean, 61); with a slight preponderance of male patients (n = 31, 55%). Characteristics of these patients are given in Table 1. The most common indications for transplant in our patients include interstitial lung disease (n = 14; 25%), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (n = 11; 20%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (n = 9; 16%), pulmonary hypertension (n = 7; 13%), cystic fibrosis (n = 5; 9%), and sarcoidosis (n = 4, 7%), with smaller subset of other diagnoses (common variable immunodeficiency, pneumonia, chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, ARDS, and restrictive allograft syndrome). Forty‐five (80%) patients received bilateral lung transplants, nine (16%) received a single lung transplant, two (4%) patients received multiorgan transplant (one combined heart/lung and one heart/lung/kidney). Forty (71%) patients only had one episode of AMR requiring PLEX. However, 16 patients (29%) had repeat episodes of AMR (within 6 weeks to 47 months of the first episode), two received three acute courses of PLEX over 5–6 months and 14 completed two acute courses.

AMR treatment strategy and response to treatment.

Forty (71%) patients were symptomatic prior to initiation of PLEX. Presenting symptoms included shortness of breath, dyspnea on exertion, cough, fatigue, declining lung function, and decrease in home spirometry. Sixteen (29%) patients had no symptoms. Pre‐PLEX biopsy was available in 47 patients, of which 26 (55%) had histologic evidence of AMR, with positive C4d staining in 18 (38%). Of those 26 patients, 18 (69%) showed improvement in AMR on post‐PLEX biopsy. Five (19%) did not have post‐PLEX biopsy. Fifty‐five of 56 patients had pre‐PLEX DSA, with strength ranging from < 1000 MFI to > 20,000 MFI. Post‐PLEX DSA was available in 49 patients and was decreased in 36 (73%). DSA was decreased in 36 (68%) of the 53 patients with follow‐up DSA, and three (6%) patients had no detectable follow‐up DSA. Of the three patients with no detectable DSA at follow‐up, two were treated with PLEX, IVIG, and rituximab; the other patient received PLEX and IVIG only. Twelve patients did not show decline in post‐PLEX DSA, although three of these patients were found to have decline in follow‐up DSA. Table 3 depicts response to treatment (decrease in DSA, biopsy improvement, and improvement of symptoms) seen with the different treatment regimens. Of the patients that did not have decline in DSA, 83% had DSA > 5000 MFI (mean 10,351 MFI), and 92% had HLA‐DQ DSA. Five patients had increase in the strength of DSA.

All patients showed improvement in presenting symptoms. Overall, 27 of the 56 (48%) patients died during the study period. Fourteen (25%) of our lung transplant recipient patients with AMR died within 1 year following AMR treatment or diagnosis; 5 (36%) died of refractory BOS; 4 (29%) had AMR, 3 (21%) from hypoxemic respiratory failure, and 2 (14%) had multiorgan failure due to septicemia. One patient died during the AMR treatment hospital admission due to respiratory failure. One‐ and five‐year survival rates were similar between patients who met all three diagnostic criteria for AMR and patients who were treated for elevated DSAs and clinical lung dysfunction (Table 2). Survival rates between patients showing biopsy improvement were similar to those for patients that did not have improvement in biopsy evidence of AMR, though many of the patients that did not have an improvement in biopsy did show a decline in DSA. Survival rates among patients who showed improvement in DSA were slightly higher than for those who did not show improvement in DSA (Table 2).

Discussion

Our retrospective, single‐center analysis describes AMR management using a standardized protocol of PLEX and IVIG ± rituximab in 56 patients following lung transplantation. To our knowledge, our study is one of only a handful to describe a protocolized approach for treating AMR following lung transplantation and is the largest to date. Over the 7‐year study period, IVIG and rituximab were the most common treatments used in conjunction with PLEX. Other drugs used include bortezomib and thymoglobulin; these additional therapies were typically added only in patients that did not respond to initial treatment with PLEX, IVIG, and rituximab.

Almost all patients in our cohort had circulating DSAs at the time of initiating PLEX, and 26 patients had evidence of AMR on pre‐PLEX biopsy. We found that our protocol resulted in 18 of the 26 patients having improvement in AMR on post‐biopsy and a decrease in DSA for the majority of patients (68%), with clearance of all DSA observed in a small portion (6%). Similarly, the study by Neuhaus et al. used a multimodality treatment approach for 51 patients with 50 who received PLEX as part of their AMR treatment [5]. Forty‐three of their patients were positive for DSAs during AMR treatment. Of those, 43 patients 86% (37) had reduced DSAs following treatment. Of the 43 patients with positive DSA, 28 (76%) received a combination of IVIG, PLEX, and rituximab for AMR treatment. In addition, one patient received one concurrent dose of rATG. Their median number of IVIG doses per patient admission was 4 (1 g/kg). Rituximab (375 mg/m2) was given after the completion of PLEX and IVIG.

In contrast to our findings, Neuhaus et al. reported a better response rate, showing a slight reduction in DSA for 86% of their patient population [5]. A large proportion of our patients (79%) had multiple DSA, and the vast majority (84%) had DSA > 5000 MFI, which may account for the lower observed rate of DSA decline in our study. Zachary et al. looked at factors associated with persistence of DSA after treatment with PLEX and IVIG in kidney transplant recipients. They demonstrated higher rates of DSA persistence in patients with multiple DSA; this was more pronounced in those with HLA class II antibodies. They also found that higher DSA strength at initiation of treatment had a statistically significant impact on persistence of DSA after treatment [18]. Neuhaus and colleagues did not comment on strength, specificity, or the number of DSAs in their patient population, so it is possible that the higher response rate observed in their cohort is due to patients having DSA that were more amenable to removal via PLEX and IVIG. They did report that patients receiving IVIG ± PLEX alone had slightly lower DSA rate of patients with decrease in DSA (82%) compared to patients that received bortezomib (100%) [5]. We did not observe such a marked difference, as 63% of patients receiving IVIG + PLEX had decline in DSA, compared to 67% of patients that received bortezomib showing decline in follow‐up DSA. We did, however, observe that patients receiving rituximab as part of their treatment regimen did have slightly higher rate of DSA reduction (71%) compared to patients receiving IVIG and PLEX alone. This suggests that the optimum way to manage patients whose IgG synthesis rate is much greater, would be to arrest IgG synthesis via rituximab which destroys antibody‐producing memory B cells, or agents that directly affect plasma cells (bortezomib or daratumumab) if long lived plasma cells are driving aggressive IgG synthesis.

Although the majority of our patients (68%) had decline in DSA, five (9%) patients had an increase in antibody levels following treatment. It should be noted that the single‐antigen assay is only recognized as a qualitative test by the FDA. The lack of decline in some of these patient's DSA levels may be attributed to presence of prozone (or hook) effect, whereby excess antibody levels lead to falsely lower or sometimes even negative MFI values [19]. This can be overcome by pretreating patient serum (EDTA, DTT, heat, or C1 inhibitor) or by diluting patient serum [20]. All 5 patients to have increase in DSA levels post treatment had high‐level pre‐PLEX DSA (> 7000 MFI). It is possible that these patients may have had a decline in DSA that was not appreciated due to possible prozone effect due to bead saturation causing falsely low estimate of the pre‐treatment antibody levels. Tambur et al. postulated that serial titrations may actually be a better method for monitoring response to antibody removal therapies [21]. We did not perform serum treatment or dilutions in patients with higher MFI DSA levels to account for possible prozone effect, so we may have underestimated the percentage of patients that did have a decline in DSA with treatment. Another retrospective study by Daoud and Betensley identified 14 subjects with at least one potential marker of AMR and used a protocolized approach to treat seven of their subjects [13]. Their protocol included six sessions of PLEX followed by IVIG 500 mg/kg and rituximab 375 mg/m2 after the sixth PLEX session, followed by IVIG 500 mg/kg every 2 weeks for 3 months, then IVIG 500 mg/kg every 4 weeks for the next 3 months. If DSAs were still identified after 6 months post‐AMR, then IVIG 500 mg/kg was continued on a monthly basis until DSA became negative [13]. Of the seven patients that received IVIG and PLEX, four (57%) ultimately cleared all DSA [13]. It is difficult to draw direct comparisons between their study and ours, as they commented only on whether DSA became negative, and did not note decline in DSA. Although they reported a higher proportion of patients converting to negative DSA as compared to our study cohort, it should be noted that only two patients in their study became DSA‐negative after initial treatment course. Four patients required ongoing monthly IVIG infusions; of those, two remained DSA‐positive [13]. Similar to our study, the patients that had persistent DSA both had multiple DSA including HLA class II antibodies, which is consistent with prior observations that having > 1 DSA is associated with persistent DSA following AMR treatment with IVIG and PLEX [13, 18]. Witt et al. also identified 21 patients with acute AMR treated with a combination of IVIG, and rituximab ± PLEX, bortezomib, or eculizumab depending on patient presentation and severity [6]. A total of 8 patients received PLEX as part of their multimodality treatment for AMR. They found undetectable DSA for 9 (43%) patients after a mean of 83 ± 77 days, and 12 (57%) had persistent DSA [6]. Similar to our study, they found that patients with HLA‐DQ antibodies exhibited lower response rate to treatment, with only 5 of the 17 (29%) of patients in their study with DQ DSA clearing their antibodies compared to 100% of patients with non‐DQ antibodies clearing their DSA (p = 0.01) [6]. This also corresponds with our observation that the vast majority (92%) of patients that did not show a decline in DSA following AMR treatment had HLA‐DQ antibodies. These findings suggest that HLA‐DQ antibodies may be more difficult to remove than non‐DQ antibodies. Despite the higher rate of patients that cleared DSA reported by Witt et al., they had a smaller proportion of patients showing clinical improvement. Although we saw clinical improvement in all patients who presented with symptomatic AMR, they reported that 15 (71%) of their patients had improved clinically with resolution of breathlessness, hypoxemia, and pulmonary infiltrates [6]. The average dose of IVIG used at our institution was lower than the previous studies' protocol (100 vs. 500 mg/kg), so this may have also factored into the slightly lower rate of decline and/or clearance of DSA observed in our study; however, it did not seem to impact clinical response as all of our patients showed improvement in clinical symptoms. Similar to other studies, Vacha et al. also reported a higher rate of DSA clearance after implementing an aggressive multimodality protocol for treating suspected AMR that includes bortezomib, rituximab, PLEX, and methylprednisolone, followed by IVIG [11]. Of the 16 patients treated with this protocol, 11 (68.7%) were alive at 6 months and evaluated for clearance of DSA. Three (27.7%) cleared all identified DSAs within 6 months of therapy initiation. Despite using an aggressive multimodality protocol that resulted in complete clearance of DSA for 27% of their patients, their cohort had a high rate of allograft dysfunction and failure at 6 months. Even though our study cohort showed lower rate of DSA clearance compared to prior studies, our patient outcome was slightly better. The better response rate in our patient cohort may be because DSA present in blood does not always equate to antibodies binding to the allograft and causing rejection. Alloimmunization can result in fairly rapid development of AMR in a small subset of patients (5%–10%), and more commonly (50%–80%), this immunization may either be a biomarker or may play a role in immune signaling that can predispose patients to development of cellular rejection leading to CLAD (as most commonly manifested as BOS) that may more commonly present within the next year but may also present subsequently consistent with the annual incidence of BOS which approximates 8%–10% [22, 23, 24]. Twenty‐seven of the 56 (48%) lung transplant recipients in our cohort died during the 7‐year period (range, 26–1 501 days; average, 758 days; median, 391, and mean [SD] 463 days). Only one patient died during initial hospitalization for AMR due to respiratory failure. Fourteen (25%) of our lung transplant recipient patients with AMR died within 1 year following AMR treatment due to refractory BOS, respiratory failure, refractory AMR or septicemia. Similarly, other studies found that many patients experienced graft failure and death following AMR. However, compared to other studies, the mortality rate for our patients is slightly lower. By comparison, 7 (43.8%) patients in the Vacha study died within 12 months following treatment [11]. Witt et al. found that 15 of the 21 (71%) recipients died during the 8‐year study period, including 10 (47%) that died within 1 year of diagnosing AMR; 6 patients died of refractory AMR and 9 of CLAD [6]. Interestingly, they found that patients with DSAs and clinical allograft dysfunction without biopsy evidence of AMR had higher overall mortality than those patients that met all three criteria for AMR [6]. We did not notice a similar trend in our patient population, as 5‐year mortality was similar for both of those cohorts, 52% and 50%, respectively. Neuhaus et al. identified 21 (38%) patients who died within 1 year following treatment for AMR [5]. Interestingly, 10 of the 21 patients died during their admission for AMR treatment. By contrast, only one patient in our cohort died during initial admission for AMR. Of the remaining 11 patients in their study, 7 (64%) had graft failure and CLAD, 2 (18%) from hypoxemic respiratory failure, and 2 (18%) from septicemia [5]. If only the deaths related to AMR are taken into consideration, Neuhaus et al. reported a 28% 1‐year mortality [5]. We observed a much lower AMR‐related 1‐year mortality of 12.5% in our cohort. Only Daoud and Betensley reported similar or better mortality rates to ours. Five (36%) of the 14 patients in the study died during the study follow‐up period, and only 3 (21%) of those were within 12 months of AMR treatment [13]. Early identification and treatment of AMR in our patient population may have contributed to the slightly better mortality rate observed in our study.

Our study adds to the existing literature showing the efficacy of a protocolized multimodality treatment approach using PLEX and IVIG in the treatment of lung transplant rejection, and to our knowledge, is the largest study to date. However, our study has multiple limitations. Due to the retrospective nature of our study, which relied on data collection via manual chart review, it is possible that there is incomplete or missing data for some of our patients. Furthermore, the lack of uniform testing led to inconsistent follow‐up biopsy and DSA data for our study cohort. Another limitation of this study is that we did not routinely perform serum treatment or dilution to account for possible prozone effect on single‐antigen testing, so it is possible that we underestimated the percentage of patients that did have a decline in DSA with treatment.

Conclusions

Our data suggest that treatment with combined PLEX and IVIG protocol appears effective for the treatment of lung AMR, with the majority of patients showing improvement in AMR on biopsy (69%) and a decline in DSA (68%). High levels of DSA, particularly HLA‐DQ, and multiple DSA may be more difficult to remove with a course of 5 PLEX/IVIG. However, our patients showed clinical improvement even when DSA did not decrease. Further prospective studies are needed to evaluate whether a longer course of PLEX or use of additional immune‐modulating drugs would have any added benefit in patients with higher initial levels of DSA or multiple DSA. Further studies would likely benefit from serum treatment and/or serial titrations to more accurately assess DSA levels and response to therapy.
