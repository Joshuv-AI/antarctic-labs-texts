Asymmetric impact of microfinance on economic growth: Evidence from Bosnia and Herzegovina
Authors: Edib Smolo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This study explores the correlation between microfinance loans (MFL) and economic growth in Bosnia and Herzegovina (Bosnia). It utilizes the non-linear Autoregressive Distributed Lag (NARDL) method to examine cointegration and short-run dynamics by analyzing quarterly data spanning from 2010 to 2022. The findings underscore the link between MFL shocks and long-term economic growth. The study unveils the unique effects of both positive and negative MFL shocks on growth, suggesting a non-linear relationship between microfinance loans and economic growth in Bosnia. However, the study concludes that the impact of MFL on Bosnia's GDP is adverse. Short-term fluctuations in MFL show no substantial influence on Bosnian economic growth. The coefficient of the error correction model is both negative and significant indicating the stability of the long-term relationship. This implies a rapid correction, with 46.4 % of the previous quarter's imbalance rectified within the current quarter. While our results are based on a single country, they align with recent criticisms of microfinance practices. Furthermore, our study offers a novel approach as it represents the first examination of the asymmetric relationship between MFL and GDP in Bosnia, providing valuable policy recommendations.

Introduction

The term microfinance1 can be defined as “the provision of a broad range of financial services such as deposits, loans, payment services, money transfers, and insurance to poor and low-income households and their micro-enterprises” [1]. Additionally, microfinance serves as a means of generating employment opportunities while fostering financial inclusion and enhancing financial access for low-income households and micro-enterprises.

Even though microfinance has been practiced for centuries, its modern incarnation was pioneered by Muhammad Yunus in 1976 when he lent $27 to two village weavers. Their successful loan repayment improved their financial situation, earning Yunus the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006 and sparking a global movement.

Microfinance represents a noble concept. Borrowers’ earnings would increase as a result of capital provisions made to their businesses, alleviating poverty in the long run. Microfinance, however, is changing. Cull & Morduch [2] note that microfinance was previously considered entrepreneurial finance, but today it is widely considered household finance.

Initially, microfinance aimed to boost borrowers' earnings, reducing poverty over time. However, today, it's more commonly viewed as household finance rather than entrepreneurial finance [2]. Observers generally see microfinance as a useful financial service, but not a transformative intervention [3]. Some argue it has failed poor communities by focusing on profit over poverty alleviation [4]. Widespread coverage and effective regulation are still lacking, hindering its full potential [1].

Following the 1992–1995 aggression in Bosnia, microfinance emerged as a means to finance small entrepreneurs, including the poor, refugees, and returnees, aiding post-conflict recovery and development [5]. Before the 2009 global financial crisis, academic analyses celebrated Bosnia's microfinance model as a contributor to economic and social recovery, but these claims often relied on flawed methodologies. Some argue that microfinance hindered Bosnia's development and benefited only a small elite [6]. By September 2021, Bosnia had 26 microfinance institutions (MFIs) [7,8].2

In the realm of academic exploration regarding microfinance and its impact on economic growth, there exists a paucity of research. The precise influence of microfinance on economic prosperity remains elusive, partly due to data constraints. Existing studies, exemplified by the work of Cull and Morduch [2], often focus on micro-level effects, neglecting potential macroeconomic consequences. To address this gap, this study delves into the impact of microfinance lending on Bosnia's economic growth, utilizing data from the microfinance sector in the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBIH). Hence, the main objective of this study is to investigate asymmetric interaction between microfinance lending and economic growth in Bosnia.

The lack of prior empirical research on the microfinance-growth relationship serves as the primary driver for our current investigation. Through the application of the Auto-Regressive Distributive Lag (ARDL) technique, our study makes significant contributions to the existing literature. Firstly, this research represents the inaugural attempt to scrutinize the influence of microfinance loans on macroeconomic growth within Bosnia. While prior studies have predominantly focused on microfinance's effects on poverty reduction, job creation, and income augmentation, our work extends the examination into broader economic outcomes. Secondly, our study distinguishes itself by employing aggregate data from the microfinance sector in FBIH, in contrast to previous research that largely relied on household-level data. Thirdly, as detailed in forthcoming sections, the Bosnian microfinance model garners acclaim as an international success story, despite receiving substantial critique from industry observers. Hence, our study takes on the task of presenting a comprehensive analysis of this sector's real impact on the nation's economic growth. Finally, our research harnesses the non-linear ARDL (NARDL) technique, utilizing quarterly data spanning from 2010 to 2022, a departure from earlier surveys that primarily relied on descriptive analysis. Notably, this study pioneers an exploration into the non-linear aspects of the relationship between microfinance loans and economic growth, marking a novel contribution to the literature.

The remainder of the study comprises a concise exploration of the microfinance sector in Bosnia, a literature review, a section on data and methodology, and subsequent discussions and concluding remarks.

A snapshot of macroeconomic conditions and microfinance sector in Bosnia

Macroeconomic conditions of Bosnia

Bosnia, a relatively small and open economy, embarked on a transition process since 1995 when the Dayton Peace Agreement concluded the conflict. It comprises the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBIH), the Republika Srpska (RS), and the District Brčko, which functions as a de facto third entity. Furthermore, the FBIH is further subdivided into ten cantons.

With assistance from the international community, officials initiated the task of transforming the country from a centrally planned economy to a market-driven one. Despite facing challenges like the global financial crisis, the Eurozone crisis, and the recent COVID-19 pandemic, Bosnia managed to maintain reasonable macroeconomic stability.

However, the nation grapples with economic issues stemming from a lack of effective reforms, high unemployment rates, and sluggish economic growth. In 2020, the unemployment rate stood at 16.9 %, prompting a significant ‘brain drain’ as many individuals opt to migrate abroad, primarily to the European Union. Workers' remittances play a crucial role in sustaining numerous low-income families, with private consumption fueled by these remittances and low inflation supporting real disposable income [9].

Nonetheless, Bosnia faces inflationary pressure due to global trends, compounding the challenge of rising living costs, particularly since average salaries in the country are relatively modest. Furthermore, issues like lack of transparency, corruption, informal economies, inefficient institutions, and political instability contribute to the nation's struggles with low economic growth and foreign direct investment [[10], [11], [12]]. Transparency International ranks Bosnia as one of the most corrupt countries in Europe, placing it 111th out of 179 nations.3 The country also grapples with an unfavorable business environment and one of the least politically stable positions in Europe.4

This complex landscape can be attributed to Bosnia's decentralized government structure, where different levels of governance at the state, entity, and cantonal levels hold varying jurisdictions. Moreover, the presence of nationalist ideologies has hindered institutional and economic progress, impeding the implementation of effective policies. As a result, Bosnia exhibits characteristics of a weak state with inefficient, extractive institutions.

Microfinance in Bosnia: a historical overview

The aggression on Bosnia inflicted severe damage on its economy, ultimately catalyzing the emergence of the microfinance sector in the country. A microfinance project was initiated in Tuzla in 1996.5 By the late 2000s, Bosnia had risen to the second position in terms of microfinance ‘saturation,’ trailing only behind Bangladesh [[13], [14], [15]].

In order to facilitate Bosnia's recovery and economic development, the international community extended generous financial assistance. Rejecting government intervention, the international community advocated for the application of free-market principles, positioning Bosnia as a pilot project to champion the microfinance model. Thanks to substantial backing from the World Bank's Local Initiatives Project (LIP I), a network of MFIs structured as Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) was swiftly established.

The World Bank, in collaboration with various bilateral and multilateral donors, initiated the Local Initiatives Project (LIP I) in 1996 to contribute to Bosnia's reconstruction and economic revival. Their vision was centered on the belief that fostering self-employment through micro and small enterprises could generate fresh employment opportunities and prosperity, especially among the war-affected population and economically disadvantaged citizens of Bosnia and Herzegovina. The project successfully met its objectives, leading to the approval of LIP II by the World Bank in 2001, building on the accomplishments of LIP I.

In the initial stages, microcredit organizations were registered as non-deposit-taking, non-profit, non-governmental entities. Up until 2006, each entity (FBIH & RS) operated under its distinct set of laws and regulations governing their establishment and operations. However, by the mid-2000s, the microfinance model had been effectively integrated into the Bosnian economy, with a growing emphasis on its commercialization. Faced with several challenges as documented by Lyman [16], these institutions sought further progress. Consequently, legislative amendments were introduced and harmonized at the national level.

The new legislation introduced a framework for two types of Microfinance Institutions (MFIs). First, there were the Microcredit Foundations (MCF), designed as non-profit entities with a pronounced emphasis on their social role and contribution to society's welfare. Second, the legislation introduced Microcredit Companies (MCC), structured as for-profit entities [13,17].

Bosnian MFIs utilized a range of lending models, including individual loans, solidarity group loans, and membership-based village credit associations [18]. It was hailed as a global success story that other countries should follow [19,13,20,21]. Despite facing numerous challenges and experiencing a near collapse of the microfinance sector, the international development community maintained that these efforts contributed to poverty reduction, job creation, and economic growth. However, some critics argue that these successes are the result of flawed evaluation methods and inappropriate criteria [6,22,23].

As of September 30, 2021, Bosnia's microfinance sector consists of 13 MFIs operating within FBIH, including 10 MCFs (non-profit organizations) and 3 MCCs (profit organizations). Additionally, there are 13 MFIs operating in RS, with 2 MCFs and 11 MCCs. These MFIs collectively manage 689 organizational units. The microfinance sector in FBIH employs 1375 individuals, while RS employs 590 7,8.

Literature review

Several studies indicate positive impact of MFIs have been the subject of numerous studies, revealing their positive influence on local communities. These studies indicate that microfinance initiatives contribute to increased consumption, income, savings, and wages at the local level [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. However, it's worth noting that the physical distance between borrowers and MFIs may limit the effectiveness of microfinance [28].

An extensive study by Donou-Adonsou & Sylwester [29] spanning 85 developing countries illustrates that microfinance lending plays a crucial role in augmenting total factor productivity (TFP) and fostering economic growth. Furthermore, there is compelling evidence indicating that microfinance has a positive impact on economic growth in countries such as Nigeria [30] and Bangladesh [31].). It's important to note that in the case of Bangladesh, the relationship appears to be bi-directional in Bangladesh [32]. Ekpenyong & Nnamocha [33], utilizing descriptive analysis and graphical methods, to have concluded that microfinance loans exert a positive influence on Nigeria's GDP and various economic sectors.

Augsburg et al. [34] conducted a comprehensive investigation into the effects of microcredit in Bosnia, employing randomized controlled trials. Their findings revealed that self-employment, inventory, and labor supply among individuals aged 16 to 19 increased, while wage-based employment decreased across the entire samplewith no significant effect on profits The study also observed a decline in consumption and savings, although overall household income remained unaffected. A similar outcome was observed in Morocco, where Crépon et al. [35] reported increased self-employment and investment without a corresponding increase in income or consumption, mirroring the results from Bosnia.

Tarozzi, Desai, and Johnson [36] conducted an examination of the effects of microfinance access in rural Ethiopia, focusing on agriculture, animal husbandry, non-farm self-employment, labor supply, schooling, and indicators of women's empowerment. Despite a significant increase in borrowing, the study revealed that only a few socio-economic indicators exhibited noteworthy improvements. Conversely, Banerjee, Breza, Duflo, and Kinnan [37] found an increase in investment and profits in small businesses when studying the impact of microcredit access. However, average household consumption levels remained unchanged, and there was no significant impact on health, education, or women's empowerment.

In Mexico, Angelucci, Karlan, and Zinman [38] examined 37 outcomes related to microcredit, including microentrepreneurship, income, labor supply, expenditures, social status, and subjective well-being. The researchers found that microcredit access increases borrowing for investment in existing businesses and results in larger businesses, enhanced trust, increased female decision-making, and reduced depression and reliance on aid. In general, however, microcredit does not significantly impact these outcomes. Finally, Attanasio et al. [39] found the positive effects of a joint-liability microcredit program in rural Mongolia on female entrepreneurship and food consumption. However, this program did not significantly affect working hours, income, or poverty.

Microfinance has faced its share of criticism from scholars who have raised several concerns over the years. A key point of contention is the cost incurred by borrowers due to the high interest rates charged byMFIs [5,40]. Donou-Adonsou & Sylwester [29] estimate a median interest rate of approximately 27 % among MFIs, which can create challenges in serving the poorest individuals and potentially have adverse effects on economic growth (please see Table 2 for the average effective interest rates of MFIs in FBIH). While microfinance has gained global popularity based on the premise that it promotes sustainable economic and social development by providing access to the poor, the long-term consequences and opportunity costs have raised significant concerns. Surprisingly, this model has evolved into a substantial institutional and political obstacle to sustainable economic and social development and the reduction of poverty [42]. This is particularly evident in Latin America's economies, where microfinance has, in some cases, led to an adverse ‘anti-development’ trajectory [43].

The microfinance industry also experienced several ‘microfinance meltdowns.’ Bolivia experienced its first meltdown in 1999–2000, which was attributed to fierce competition rather than a failure of the microfinance model. Subsequently, meltdowns occurred in Morocco, Nicaragua, and Pakistan in 2008, characterized by significant losses, defaults, client withdrawals, and instances of bankruptcy or mergers due to over-indebtedness, defaults, and withdrawals. Bosnia's microfinance sector was near collapse in 2009, while Andhra Pradesh's collapsed in late 2010 [6,44].7

In essence, it is important to acknowledge that microfinance alone cannot guarantee continuous income growth or a reduction in poverty rates. Paradoxically, it can even harm some borrowers [45]. Finally, Bateman and Chang [42] offer several critical observations about microfinance: (i) it ignores the crucial role of scale economies; (ii) it ignores the ‘fallacy of composition’; (iii) it facilitates deindustrialization and infantilization of the local economy; (iv) it is delinked from the rest of the business sector; (v) it is geared toward a sub-prime-style oversupply of microfinance; and (vi) it disregards the importance of solidarity and local community ownership and control.

Studies on the Bosnian microfinance sector

In addition to the study mentioned by Augsburg et al. [34] briefly discussed above, several studies have addressed Bosnia's microfinance sector. Most of these studies employ descriptive methods or rely on longitudinal surveys and interviews with case studies.

For instance, Matul and Tsilikounas [46] and Dunn [47] reported that microcredit had a positive impact on clients, employment, investment, and wages of non-household employees. Berryman and Pytkowska [20] found that nearly all aspects of the sector demonstrated dynamism and success. L Other studies emphasized the role of microfinance in improving access to credit, alleviating financial constraints [21,48]; and contributing to poverty reduction [49]. Moreover, Smolo [13], based on his literature review and existing reports, concluded that microfinance in Bosnia enhances investment, productivity, and income, fortifies the formal economy, and supports displaced entrepreneurs.

However, it's important to note that the Bosnian microfinance model has faced criticism, as it appears to have deviated from its original goals of recovery and development [6,22,23]. In fact, Bateman et al. [6] go further, describing it as “Bosnia's own destructive sub-prime financial episode … that … has materially benefitted a tiny elite working within and around the microfinance sector whilst simultaneously destroying many of the most important pillars of the local economy and society” (p. 4).

According to Batemen and Sinković [23], the Bosnian microfinance model has faced multiple challenges and shortcomings, resulting in the following consequences: (i) job creation failure; (ii) an increase in individual and household over-indebtedness; (iii) destruction of solidarity and trust; (iv) gender disempowerment; & (v) deindustrialization, informalization, and primitivization. This study endeavors to offer conclusive evidence concerning the potential link between microfinance and economic growth. Despite previous studies, the empirical relationship between the two remains uncertain. Therefore, our objective is to carry out a comprehensive study using time-series data spanning from 2010 to 2022 within the Bosnian market, employing the ARDL model. This approach aims to illuminate the theoretical relationship between microfinance and economic growth and determine whether they coalesce in the long run. Additionally, we seek to identify which factor leads the other to provide policymakers with specific insights and recommendations.

Hence, this research constitutes a pioneering effort in the field, striving to establish a roobust empirical foundation for understanding the potential impacts of microfinance on economic growth in Bosnia.

Data and methodology

A theoretical framework

The theoretical underpinnings of the relationship between microfinance and economic growth are founded upon a plethora of distinct yet interconnected theories and frameworks. Initially, microfinance is perceived as a vehicle for augmenting accessibility to financial services for the unbanked or underbanked populace, a catalyst that can potentially invigorate economic expansion. This perspective underscores the pivotal role played by microfinance in reaching out to marginalized individuals, thus facilitating their active engagement in economic endeavors [50,51].

An alternate theoretical perspective perceives microfinance as a purveyor of essential financial instruments and services to individuals with modest incomes, thereby endowing them with the ability to initiate and sustain businesses. This empowerment consequently contributes to the advancement of economic development. Within this context, microfinance emerges as a propellant for entrepreneurial pursuits and income generation among those who have restricted access to conventional financial institutions [52].

Moreover, the microfinance-economic growth relationship is dissected through the prism of specific theoretical constructs. For instance, classical microfinance theory postulates a sequence of events: the provision of financial access leading to augmented investment, heightened productivity, and ultimately culminating in economic growth. This linear progression spotlights the transformative potential of microfinance in nurturing economic development [53].

Additionally, the resource-based view theory forms the basis for the notion that microfinance establishments, by facilitating access to financial resources, confer upon individuals a valuable economic asset akin to physical or human capital. When judiciously allocated to micro-entrepreneurs, this asset has the capacity to unlock their economic prowess, giving rise to income generation and localized economic expansion [54].

Yet another perspective accentuates the role of microfinance in fostering financial development, underscoring that its expansion can contribute to the broader growth of the financial system while simultaneously reducing economic disparities. This approach underscores how microfinance can play an instrumental role within the broader tapestry of financial sector development, with far-reaching positive repercussions for overall economic expansion [55].

Furthermore, Baumol's theory of entrepreneurship and the significance of institutional quality provide the theoretical scaffolding for comprehending the microfinance-economic growth nexus. Baumol's theory places emphasis on the role of entrepreneurship in propelling economic development, with microfinance acting as a catalyst for entrepreneurial activities among economically disadvantaged populations. Meanwhile, the importance accorded to institutional quality underscores the imperative of having supportive regulatory and governance frameworks for microfinance to thrive and actively contribute to economic expansion [56].

Lastly, the conviction that a well-developed financial system is an indispensable cornerstone for economic growth stands as a fundamental principle underpinning the microfinance-economic growth interrelation. This conviction is firmly rooted in the recognition that a functional financial system, encompassing microfinance, facilitates the allocation of capital, investment, and economic activity, ultimately nurturing growth [12,[57], [58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65], [66]].

According to Barr [67], microfinance contributes to financial development in four ways. First, microfinance contributes to poverty alleviation and financial development by deepening the financial system. Second, microfinance is a valuable tool for developing countries with poor governance and insufficient development strategies. Third, microfinance could enable all countries to reach financial market maturity. Lastly, microfinance could facilitate domestic financial reforms.

In short, the theoretical landscape pertaining to the connection between microfinance and economic growth is intricate and multifaceted, drawing upon a multitude of theories and frameworks. It underscores the transformative potential of microfinance in amplifying financial accessibility, igniting entrepreneurial spirit, and making substantial contributions to broader economic advancement, often within the context of conducive institutional and regulatory environments [68].

Methodology

Initially, the study relied on the ARDL model proposed by Pesaran and Shin [69] and Pesaran et al. [70] to investigate the short- and long-term nexus between microfinance loans and economic growth in Bosnia. In essence, an ARDL model represents a regression where a variable is regressed “over its own past plus the present and past values of a number of exogenous variables” [71]. The ARDL model assumes enough lags to capture the data generation process and starts with an unrestricted dynamic model before imposing restrictions. After this, the error correction model (ECM) - estimated via a simple linear transformation [72] – addresses “the issue of integrating short-run dynamics with long-run equilibrium without losing long-run information” [73].

This model remains applicable even in cases where there is a combination of I(0) and I(1) variables (such as in this study). It is robust to small sample studies [[74], [75], [76], [77], [78]], providing unbiased and efficient results. Importantly, it allows for simultaneous estimation of both short-run and long-run components while addressing issues like omitted variables and autocorrelation [79]. Finally, ARDL models can be estimated effectively even in scenarios involving endogeneity [80,81].

The ARDL model generally consists of two stages. In the initial step, we employ an unrestricted error correction model (VECM) to assess long-run relationships among variables. Each variable serves as a dependent variable to test the significance of lagged levels. In essence, this model calculates an F-statistic and compares it against two asymptotic critical values (bounds). The null hypothesis, indicating the absence of a long-run relationship, is rejected (accepted) if the F-statistic is above (below) an upper (lower) bound. However, when the F-statistic falls within the range of these bounds, the results are inconclusive. Generally, if one or more of these F-statistics are above the upper bound (i.e., significant), it implies the existence of a long-run relationship among the variables. The significance (insignificance) of the F-statistics further indicates whether the dependent variable is endogenous (exogenous or long-run forcing).

This study uses the following theoretical model specification that represents a slightly modified model of Sultan & Masih [32]:(1)GDP=∫(MFL+TO+I+GCF)where, GDP is the quarterly real GDP (in thousands BAM); MFL represents net microfinance loans (in thousands BAM) as a proxy of MFIs impact; TO, I, and GCF are control variables representing trade openness, inflation, and gross capital formation.

After confirming a long-run relationship, the method proceeds to selecting the optimum order of the variables using Akaike information criterion (AIC) or Schwarz information criterion (SBC) criteria and estimating the long-run coefficients. The final step involves estimating the adjustment coefficients for the error-correction term by estimating the error correction model (ECM). Empirical results discussion

As discussed briefly above, the ARDL model consists of two major stages and several steps. Below is a brief description of these steps.

Step 1 – unit root tests

The ARDL approach is applicable whether variables are stationary at level I(0) or first difference I(1). However, if any variable is stationary at the second difference I(2), it may lead to spurious estimates. Therefore, assessing the stationarity of time series is a crucial step to ensure that none of the variables exhibits an integration order greater than one, denoted as I(1). It is essential to note that the bounds test for cointegration is valid only when this stationarity condition is met. To determine stationarity, two established unit root tests, the Augmented Dickey Fuller (ADF) test [83] and the Phillips-Perron (PP) test [84] are employed. The ADF test addresses autocorrelation, while the PP test utilizes the Newey-West adjusted-variance method to tackle both autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity. Step 2 – BDS test for nonlinearity

In our research, we delve into the intricate world of nonlinearity within our time series data, utilizing the BDS (Brock, Dechert, and Scheinkman) test [86], a valuable tool pioneered by Brooks and Heravi [87]. This examination reveals robust evidence of nonlinearity among our selected variables, as the null hypothesis of linearity is consistently rejected. The presence of nonlinearity in our dataset signifies its pivotal role in shaping the dynamics we seek to explore. This recognition underscores the necessity of applying the NARDL model for precise estimation. Our study, much like those previously mentioned, underscores the critical role of non-linearity in time series data and its relevance for understanding economic phenomena, such as the impact of microfinance loans on economic growth in Bosnia.

Step 4 – cointegration results (F-bound test)

Following the selection of optimal lags based on the criteria outlined in the previous section, we examine the presence of cointegration among the variables. This assessment is conducted through OLS estimation, where we compare the computed F-statistic with the lower and upper critical values recommended by Pesaran et al. [70]. If the calculated F-statistic exceeds the upper critical value, it constitutes strong evidence of a long-term relationship among the variables. Conversely, if the F-statistic falls below the lower bound, it indicates an absence of a long-term relationship. When the F-statistic falls between the critical values, the results are inconclusive. In the context of our primary model, which explores the impact of MFL on GDP, the F-statistic is reported as 5.803 in Table 7. Our findings reveal intriguing insights into the relationship between microfinance loans (MFL) and economic growth in Bosnia. A positive MFL shock is associated with a 0.21 % reduction in long-term economic growth, while a negative shock corresponds to a 0.66 % increase in growth (Panel A of Table 8). Ultimately, our results suggest that MFL does not significantly contribute to Bosnia's GDP. These outcomes align with the studies conducted by Londo-Bedoya et al. [41] and Bateman [43] but contrast with the findings of Donou-Adonsou & Sylwester [29], Murad and Idewele [30] and Bin Amin and Jalal Uddin [31].

The pronounced significance of negative MFL shocks on GDP hints at a nonlinear relationship between microfinance loans and economic growth in Bosnia. This suggests that increased demand for these loans may burden borrowers due to high microfinance interest rates, potentially limiting disposable income for other activities. It may also signify underutilization of these loans for entrepreneurship and value creation. Conversely, reduced demand indicates improved economic conditions.

Regarding control variables, a 1 % increase in inflation and gross capital formation leads to a 0.005 % and 0.011 % GDP rise, respectively. Unlike Londo-Bedoya et al. [41] in Colombia, inflation's positive impact on Bosnian growth may stem from the currency board arrangement implemented by the Central Bank of Bosnia and Herzegovina (CBBH). This arrangement's stability affects inflation levels. Trade openness and the dummy variable show insignificant impacts, indicating that structural breaks have no substantial influence on overall economic growth in Bosnia, in line with the study of Donou-Adonsou & Sylwester [29] and Abrar et al. [88].

Short-run MFL changes exhibit no significant impact on Bosnian growth, though positive changes approach a 10 % significance level (see Panel B of Table 8). The error correction model coefficient is negative and significant at the 2 % level, demonstrating the long-term relationship's stability. It reveals a rapid adjustment, with 46.4 % of the previous quarter's disequilibrium corrected within the current quarter.

Step 6 - asymmetry and diagnostics test results

The NARDL model's uniqueness, as opposed to traditional ARDL, lies in its capability to capture asymmetric effects between MFL and GDP growth, confirmed by the significant p-values in the long-run Wald test (WaldLR(MC)). Conversely, the short-run asymmetry in the Wald test (WaldSR(MC)) is insignificant, aligning with Panel A and Panel B results.

Diagnostic tests in Panel C of Table 8 reinforce the NARDL model's adequacy. They confirm the absence of autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity, validate the relationship's specification, and establish that error terms exhibit a normal distribution, indicating a well-fitted model.

Apart from the above diagnostics tests, we are also using cumulative sum (CUSUM) and cumulative sum of squares (CUSUMSQ) to test the stability of the model during the study period. Concluding remarks

Amidst the prevailing consensus that microfinance possesses the potential to stimulate economic growth and alleviate poverty, recent research has cast doubt upon its substantial contribution to a nation's overall economic prosperity. Considering the findings of this study, microfinance loans in Bosnia and Herzegovina do not make a noteworthy contribution to economic growth, thereby challenging conventional wisdom regarding their role in economic growth. As emphasized by Cull and Morduch [2], microfinance needs fresh thinking.

Nonetheless, it is essential to acknowledge that various other variables hold significant sway over economic growth. These factors encompass inflation and the accumulation of gross capital, while the degree of trade openness is insignificant. Therefore, it becomes imperative for policymakers and practitioners to exercise prudence when advocating microfinance as a panacea for economic expansion and poverty alleviation. Instead, a broader spectrum of alternative measures should be considered, measures that may prove more effective in promoting sustainable economic growth and poverty reduction.

The conclusions drawn here are grounded in the findings of a single study, and it remains unclear whether these findings can be generalized to other contexts. To address this limitation, further research should be undertaken in diverse settings to ascertain the applicability of these conclusions on a broader scale. Another limitation is that we used the data from the microfinance sector of FBIH. Getting the data for the entire Bosnian microfinance sector would provide more reliable results. Finally, to validate these findings, we may explore other methods.
