Falsification-Driven Reinforcement Learning for Maritime Motion Planning
Marlon Müller, Florian Finkeldei, Hanna Krasowski, Murat Arcak, Matthias Althoff

Abstract
Compliance with maritime traffic rules is essential for the safe operation of autonomous vessels, yet training reinforcement learning (RL) agents to adhere to them is challenging. The behavior of RL agents is shaped by the training scenarios they encounter, but creating scenarios that capture the complexity of maritime navigation is non-trivial, and real-world data alone is insufficient. To address this, we propose a falsification-driven RL approach that generates adversarial training scenarios in which the vessel under test violates maritime traffic rules, which are expressed as signal temporal logic specifications. Our experiments on open-sea navigation with two vessels demonstrate that the proposed approach provides more relevant training scenarios and achieves more consistent rule compliance.



1
Introduction

Safe navigation at sea requires vessels to comply with maritime traffic rules.
These rules are essential for avoiding collisions, yet their situational and temporal dependencies are difficult to translate into control policies for autonomous vessels.
Therefore, training
reinforcement learning (RL)
controllers that achieve both rule compliance and task success is a central challenge for maritime autonomy.
The design of the training environment and the selection of training scenarios are crucial when
RL
agents must learn complex behaviors.
However, generating scenarios that reliably foster rule-compliant behavior is challenging.
Falsification methods aim to provoke specification-violating behaviors in a system under test and typically provide counterexamples that demonstrate where a specification has been violated.
While classical controllers are difficult to adapt directly based on such counterexamples, they can be incorporated into the
RL
training process to inform the agent about remaining failure modes and guide its decision-making toward improved specification compliance.
In this paper, we use counterexamples generated through falsification to improve the rule compliance of
RL
-controlled vessels.
Specifically, we develop an efficient counterexample generation algorithm based on the
covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES)
, which targets violations of maritime traffic rules expressed as
signal temporal logic (STL)
specifications by the current
RL
policy.
Our experiments demonstrate that integrating falsification into the training process leads to improved rule compliance. Our main contributions are:

1.

We propose an efficient and practical falsification-driven
RL
algorithm that generates counterexamples for complex temporal logic traffic rules and uses them as training scenarios;

2.

We extend the maritime traffic rule formalization with robustness measures that are efficiently evaluable;

3.

We evaluate our approach on a test set with 10,000 scenarios, demonstrating that falsification-driven
RL
improves traffic rule compliance.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows:
We review related literature in Sec.
2
and introduce preliminary concepts in Sec.
3
.
The falsification-driven
RL
approach is detailed in Sec.
4
, its application to maritime navigation is presented in Sec.
5
, and evaluated in Sec.
6
. We discuss the results in Sec.
7
and conclude in Sec.
8
.

Related Works

Our approach combines falsification with
RL
for maritime motion planning. Thus, we review related work on falsification and its combination with learning, advances in
RL
for maritime motion planning, and scenario generation for testing maritime systems.

2.1
Falsification Methods

The task of falsification is typically posed as an optimization problem, that finds inputs to the environment such that the system under test violates a specification
(
Corso et al. 2022
, Sec. 3.2)
.
Several numerical optimization strategies have been successfully applied for falsification
(
Corso et al. 2022
, Sec. 4)
, such as simulated annealing
(
Abbas et al. 2013
;
Yaghoubi and Fainekos 2019
)
, the cross-entropy method
(
Sankaranarayanan and Fainekos 2012
)
, evolutionary algorithms
(
Zou et al. 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Waga 2020
;
Sato et al. 2021
)
, Bayesian optimization
(
Deshmukh et al. 2017
;
Shahrooei et al. 2023
;
Ramezani et al. 2025
)
, and ant colony optimization
(
Annapureddy and Fainekos 2010
)
.
A benefit of these numerical optimization strategies is that they can falsify systems with unknown dynamics, so-called black-box systems. In some cases, black-box falsification techniques are preferable, even for white-box systems, e.g., if the system dynamic is too complex
(
Corso et al. 2022
, Sec. 2.2)
.

Besides numerical optimization, path planning algorithms can be used for falsification.
These approaches construct an input sequence from an initial state to a set of failure states
(
Corso et al. 2022
, Sec. 5)
, often using rapidly exploring random trees
(
Dreossi et al. 2015
;
Tuncali and Fainekos 2019
;
Koschi et al. 2019
)
.
Path planning algorithms tend to perform well in high-dimensional state spaces. However, their performance deteriorates if the set of failures is small compared to the state space
(
Kim et al. 2006
)
, and they require information about the current system state.

Lastly,
RL
-based falsifiers learn a policy that chooses the next system input based on its current state
(
Corso et al. 2022
, Sec. 6)
. Their reward function is designed such that it reinforces falsifying actions
(
Yamagata et al. 2021
)
. As
RL
-based falsification does not need to generate an input sequence for the whole time horizon, but only to learn a policy, it is well-suited for falsifying inputs over long time horizons. However, their training is tedious and must be conducted for each system individually.
In this paper, we use
CMA-ES
to create falsifying scenarios, as it scales well to complex specifications and higher-dimensional nonlinear system dynamics
(
Hansen et al. 2010
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Khandait et al. 2024
)
.

2.2
Falsification for Reinforcement Learning

The concept of improving the performance of
RL
controllers through adversaries is used in
(
Pinto et al. 2017
)
, which alternately trains an
RL
adversary alongside the
RL
protagonist. The reward function of the adversary incentivizes failures of the protagonist to obtain more robust policies.
The work in
(
Pan et al. 2019
)
extends
(
Pinto et al. 2017
)
to turn-based interactions and augments the action-value function with a risk term based on the variance of its outputs across different models.
Instead of training two competing
RL
agents, the work in
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
generates counterexamples using the cross-entropy method to improve the training of
RL
agents. Its application for a braking assistance and an adaptive cruise control system shows improved generalization and safety of the resulting
RL
controller.
A similar approach is
(
Gangopadhyay and Dasgupta 2021
)
, which uses Bayesian optimization as a different falsification strategy, applied to multi-agent
RL
tasks.
Similar to
(
Wang et al. 2020
)
, we add falsifying scenarios to the
RL
scenario set during training, while considering more complex
STL
specifications as falsification targets.

2.3
Reinforcement Learning for Maritime Navigation

RL
approaches for maritime navigation have been increasingly proposed with the success of model-free
RL
. The investigated applications range from maneuvering in harbors
(
Zweigel et al. 2019
)
to navigating in rivers
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Enevoldsen et al. 2023
)
to open-sea navigation
(
Johansen et al. 2016
;
Kufoalor et al. 2019
;
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
)
.
Among model-free
RL
algorithms,
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
has shown strong performance
(
Larsen et al. 2021
;
Cui et al. 2023
)
and is used in many
RL
approaches that inform the agent about maritime traffic rules through the reward
(
Zhao and Roh 2019
;
Rongcai et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
. The simulated traffic for these
RL
agents is either non-reactive
(
Meyer et al. 2020
;
Larsen et al. 2021
;
Chun et al. 2021
;
Rongcai et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Xie et al. 2024
)
, or based on other
RL
agents
(
Zhao and Roh 2019
;
Cui et al. 2023
;
Guan et al. 2024
)
.
In this paper, we train a
RL
agent using
PPO
within an environment that includes a rule-compliant, i.e., reactive traffic participant.

2.4
Scenario Generation for Maritime Navigation

Beyond learning controllers, several studies use
RL
to adversarially control surrounding ships and generate challenging scenarios to evaluate autonomous maritime decision-making systems
(
Vatle et al. 2024
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Laursen and Mengshoel 2025
)
. Additionally, generating challenging and representative scenarios to test maritime motion planners has been explored through approaches, such as manual specification of scenario sets
(
Sawada et al. 2024
)
, discriminative neural networks
(
Porres et al. 2020
)
, and formulating scenario synthesis as a constraint satisfaction problem
(
Frey et al. 2025
)
. AIS data have also been used to extract and characterize real-world traffic
(
Bakdi et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Xin et al. 2025
)
, generate synthetic scenarios from AIS-derived statistics
(
Zhu et al. 2022
)
, and bias scenarios toward high-risk encounters
(
Shi et al. 2023
)
.
Robustness-guided falsification for autonomous vessels using the cross-entropy method with Gaussian mixture distribution sampling is proposed in
(
He et al. 2022
)
.
As robustness score, the sum of distances towards a collision over time is minimized.
For the task of open-sea navigation, more nuanced robustness measures can be constructed from temporal logic formalizations of maritime traffic rules as proposed in
(
Krasowski and Althoff 2021
;
Torben et al. 2023
)
.
Torben et al. 2023
apply falsification using Gaussian processes on vessel controllers to test their traffic rule compliance. While
Torben et al. 2023
focus on testing existing controllers,
our work integrates falsification into the RL training process.
To quantitatively evaluate the rule-compliance of scenarios, we extend the formalization of
Krasowski and Althoff 2021
with robustness measures.

Preliminaries

To formulate our method, we first present the vessel dynamics and relevant notation. We then outline
STL
, the formal language used to specify falsification targets. Finally, we present the formalized maritime traffic rules.

3.2
Signal Temporal Logic

The syntax of an
STL
formula
\varphi
is
(
Bartocci et al. 2018
, Sec. 2.1)
:

\varphi\coloneq\mu\;|\;\neg\varphi\;|\;\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}\;|\;\varphi_{1}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi_{2}\,,

(3)

where
\mu\coloneq h_{\mu}(\eta_{k})>0
is an atomic predicate defined by the robustness measure function
h_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\neg
and
\vee
are the Boolean operators for negation and disjunction, and
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{I}}
is the until operator, where
\mathrm{I}
is of the form
[a,b]
or
[a,\infty)
, with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
a\leq b
. We denote that a state sequence
\boldsymbol{\eta}
satisfies
\varphi
at time step
k
as
(\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\models\varphi
and write
\boldsymbol{\eta}\models\varphi
when
k=0
.
The
STL
formula
(\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\models\varphi_{1}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi_{2}
holds if
\varphi_{1}
holds until the formula
\varphi_{2}
holds within
k\oplus\mathrm{I}
.
Additional constants and operators can be derived from (
3
), e.g., the true constant
\top\coloneq\varphi\vee\neg\varphi
, the eventually operator
\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi\coloneq\top\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi
, and the always operator
\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi\coloneq\neg\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{I}}\neg\varphi
.
The formula
(\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\models\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi
holds if there exists a time step in
k\oplus\mathrm{I}
where
\varphi
holds.
Conversely,
(\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\models\mathbf{G}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi
holds if
\varphi
is true at every time step within
k\oplus\mathrm{I}

(
Bartocci et al. 2018
, Sec. 2.1)
.

The robustness of complying with an
STL
formula quantifies the margin towards its violation. The robustness measure
\rho(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)
of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
with respect to an
STL
formula
\varphi
at time step
k
satisfies
\rho(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)>0\Leftrightarrow(\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\models\varphi
and is defined as
(
Donzé and Maler 2010
, Def. 1)
:

\displaystyle\rho(\mu,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)

\displaystyle\coloneq h_{\mu}(\eta_{k})\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\rho(\neg\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)

\displaystyle\coloneq-\rho(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\,,

\displaystyle\rho(\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2},\boldsymbol{\eta},k)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max\left(\rho(\varphi_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta},k),\rho(\varphi_{2},\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\rho(\varphi_{1}\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{I}}\varphi_{2},\boldsymbol{\eta},k)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max\limits_{i\in k\oplus\mathrm{I}}\bigl(\min\bigl(\rho(\varphi_{2},\boldsymbol{\eta},i),

\displaystyle\min\limits_{j\in[k,i)}\rho(\varphi_{1},\boldsymbol{\eta},j)\bigr)\bigr)\,.

This makes it possible to frame rule compliance testing as an optimization problem that minimizes the robustness
\rho(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)
. While we use
STL
in this paper, other temporal logics with quantitative semantics would also be applicable, e.g.,
metric temporal logic (MTL)

(
Fainekos and Pappas 2006
)
.

3.3
Traffic Rules

The
Convention on the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs)

(
International Maritime Organization 1972
)
specifies the traffic rules for open sea navigation and defines three types of encounters between two power-driven vessels that potentially collide in the near future: crossing, head-on, and overtaking. In these encounters, there are two types of vessels: give-way and stand-on vessels (see
Fig.

1
). The give-way vessel has to adjust its course detectably, while the stand-on vessel has to maintain course and velocity until the situation is resolved.
As specifications for the falsification, we adapt the formalization of maritime traffic rules from
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
. In particular, we focus on the three traffic rules where the autonomous vessel is the give-way vessel. These three rules are all instances of the specification
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}\coloneq

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{[0,\Delta tN]}\Bigl(\texttt{persistent\_$\langle$encounter$\rangle$}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k)\implies

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{[t_{\mathrm{p}},t_{\mathrm{p}}+t_{\mathrm{m}}]}\left(\texttt{maneuver\_$\langle$encounter$\rangle$}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},k)\right)\,\wedge

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{[t_{\mathrm{p}},t_{\mathrm{p}}+2t_{\mathrm{m}}]}\left(\neg\texttt{velocity\_obstacle}\left(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k\right)\right)\Bigr)\,,

where
\langle
encounter
\rangle
can take the values
crossing
,
head_on
, and
overtake
, and a persistent encounter
persistent_
\langle
encounter
\rangle
is present if
\langle
encounter
\rangle
is true for at least a duration of
t_{\mathrm{p}}
. For persistent encounters, the predicate
maneuver_
\langle
encounter
\rangle
evaluates whether the required course adjustment is satisfied.
The give-way maneuvers must be carried out within the time
t_{\mathrm{m}}
in order to resolve the collision risk within
2t_{\mathrm{m}}
. For detailed derivations of the maritime traffic rules, we refer the interested reader to
(
Krasowski and Althoff 2021
;
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
)
. We remark that the proposed approach can be applied to other rule sets as well.

For a given environment and safety specification, we aim to synthesize relevant training scenarios that define the initial conditions and the behavior of other dynamic entities. To this end, we use falsification to identify scenarios in which the current
RL
policy violates the specification and incorporate them into the training process, as shown in Fig.

Specifications with an implication structure, such as (
5
), can be satisfied in two ways. If the antecedent does not hold, the specification is vacuously satisfied. Otherwise, the consequent must hold, and the specification is nonvacuously satisfied.
We aim to enforce nonvacuous scenarios, i.e., persistent encounters, while driving the
RL
policy toward unsafe responses, i.e., failures to execute a rule-compliant maneuver; see (
5
).
However, the robustness of
STL
implications is defined as the maximum of the negated robustness of the antecedent and the robustness of the consequent. As a result, it does not provide separate information about the individual robustness values of each component.

Interface-aware STL (IA-STL)

(
Ferrère et al. 2019
)
addresses this issue by evaluating
the robustness of the antecedent and consequent separately: Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{O}
be the sets of atomic predicates on the left-hand and right-hand sides of the implications, respectively. The robustness measure in
IA-STL
semantics, denoted as
\rho_{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathcal{O}}(\mu,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)
, is analogous to (
4
), except for atomic predicates
\mu

(
Ferrère et al. 2019
, Sec. 3)
:

\rho_{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathcal{O}}(\mu,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\coloneq\begin{cases}\rho(\mu,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\,,&\text{if }\mu\in\mathcal{I}\wedge\mu\not\in\mathcal{O}\,,\\
\infty\cdot\operatorname{sgn}\left(\rho(\mu,\boldsymbol{\eta},k)\right)\,,&\text{if }\mu\not\in\mathcal{I}\wedge\mu\in\mathcal{O}\,,\\
0\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(6)

If the antecedent does not hold, the input vacuity
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\coloneq\rho_{\mathcal{I}}^{\emptyset}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta})
is positive and quantifies how far a scenario is from satisfying the antecedent. The output robustness
\rho_{\mathrm{out}}\coloneq\rho_{\mathcal{O}}^{\mathcal{P}\setminus\mathcal{O}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\eta})
is
\pm\infty
in the vacuous case. Otherwise, it is finite and measures the robustness of the consequent.

To generate scenarios that reveal specification violations, we propose solving the optimization problem in (
7
). We define a binary variable
\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{vacuous}}
that indicates the vacuous case, i.e.,
\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{vacuous}}\coloneq 1
if
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}>0
, and
\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{vacuous}}\coloneq 0
otherwise. In the vacuous case, the input robustness is minimized to achieve nonvacuity. Otherwise, the output robustness is minimized to falsify the specification.
To prioritize nonvacuous scenarios, we use the Big-M method and add a large number
M
to the objective in the vacuous case.
The input sequence of the adversary
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{A}}
is the decision variable, while the inputs of the ego system are computed based on the current policy
\pi^{\mathrm{E}}
.
Moreover, the adversary must comply with its specifications
\varphi
, resulting in:

\displaystyle\underset{\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{A}}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{vacuous}}(\rho_{\mathrm{in}}+M)+(1-\mathds{1}_{\mathrm{vacuous}})\rho_{\mathrm{out}}\vphantom{medint\int_{(k-1)\Delta t}^{k\Delta t}}\,,

Falsification-driven
RL
is relevant in maritime navigation, as the unstructured nature of open-sea environments makes it challenging to identify traffic situations in which a learning policy behaves unsafely. We focus on two-vessel scenarios, where an ego vessel
\mathrm{E}
should comply with
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
, in an environment that includes one adversarial vessel
\mathrm{A}
. With more adversarial vessels, it is easy to construct situations where the ego vessel faces conflicting constraints and must violate at least one traffic rule, e.g., acting as a stand-on vessel in an overtaking encounter while also needing to give way in a crossing encounter.

When the predicates
crossing
or
overtake
hold,
\mathrm{A}
is the stand-on vessel and must maintain its course and velocity, i.e., we set
a_{k}^{\mathrm{A}}=0
and
\alpha_{k}^{\mathrm{A}}
such that
\omega^{\mathrm{A}}\rightarrow 0
.
In a persistent
head_on
encounter,
\mathrm{A}
is a give-way vessel. Thus, we set
\alpha_{k}^{\mathrm{A}}
so that the vessel turns approximately
\delta
to starboard within
t_{\mathrm{m}}
, and then maintains the course for
t_{\mathrm{m}}
.
Subsequently, we detail the
RL
observation and action spaces, as well as the termination condition, and define the robustness measures for the atomic predicates of the traffic rules.

5.1
Reinforcement Learning

Agent interface
The
RL
action space is defined by the control inputs
u^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
. We define a nine-dimensional observation
o_{k}^{\mathrm{E}}
, which includes information about the ego state, the adversary vessel
\mathrm{A}
, and a goal centered at
p^{\mathrm{G}}
. The ego observations are the velocity
v^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
, the orientation
\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
, and the angular velocity
\omega^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
. The adversary observations consist of the Euclidean distance to the adversary, the relative orientation toward it, and the change in distance since the previous time step
\|p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|-\|p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k-1}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k-1}\|
. The goal observations are the distance to the goal center and the relative orientation toward the goal. The observation also includes the time steps until episode truncation. Each vessel has a circular protected zone of radius
d_{\mathrm{zone}}
that must not intersect with the zones of other vessels. An episode terminates when the goal is reached, i.e.,
\|p^{\mathrm{G}}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|\leq d_{\mathrm{goal}}
, or when protected zones intersect, i.e.,
\|p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|\leq 2d_{\mathrm{zone}}
.

Reward function
The reward function consists of three dense and three sparse components. The dense components reward progress toward the goal, penalize deviations from the nominal velocity range
[v_{\mathrm{low}},v_{\mathrm{high}}]
, and discourage angular velocity to encourage smooth trajectories. Specifically, the component
r_{\mathrm{\Delta goal}}
is proportional to the change in Euclidean distance from the goal center:

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{\Delta goal}}\coloneqq c_{\mathrm{\Delta goal}}(\|p^{\mathrm{G}}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k-1}\|-\|p^{\mathrm{G}}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|)\,,

(8)

where
c_{\mathrm{\Delta goal}}
is a scaling coefficient.
Since vessels navigating open waters typically operate within a nominal speed range, the component
r_{\mathrm{v}}
applies a penalty, weighted by the coefficient
c_{\mathrm{v}}
, when the linear velocity falls outside the range
[v_{\mathrm{low}},v_{\mathrm{high}}]
:

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{v}}\coloneqq\begin{cases}c_{\mathrm{v}}(v^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}-v_{\mathrm{high}})\,,&\text{if }v^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}>v_{\mathrm{high}}\,,\\
c_{\mathrm{v}}(v_{\mathrm{low}}-v^{\mathrm{E}}_{k})\,,&\text{if }v^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}<v_{\mathrm{low}}\,,\\
0\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\\
\end{cases}

(9)

To promote a straight course, the component
r_{\mathrm{\omega}}
penalizes the magnitude of the angular velocity, scaled by
c_{\mathrm{\omega}}
, i.e.,
r_{\mathrm{\omega}}\coloneqq c_{\mathrm{\omega}}|\omega^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}|
.

The sparse components account for
COLREGs
compliance, incentivize reaching the goal, and penalize collisions.
Note that evaluating the satisfaction of
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
requires the complete joint state sequence
\boldsymbol{\eta}
. Providing a reward only at the final time step
N
significantly increases the challenge of learning rule compliance due to the delayed feedback. We therefore define
\tilde{\mathds{1}}_{\mathrm{vacuous}}
as the vacuous indicator and
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}}
as the output robustness of
(
5
)
without the outer
\mathbf{G}_{[0,\Delta tN]}
operator. This allows
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}}
to be repeatedly evaluated with a delay of
t_{\mathrm{p}}+2t_{\mathrm{m}}
as derived in
Maler et al. 2007
.
If
\mathrm{E}
vacuously complies with
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
, the reward component
r_{\mathrm{colregs}}
returns zero.
Otherwise, i.e., in a persistent encounter,
r_{\mathrm{colregs}}
returns
c_{\mathrm{colregs}}
with a positive or negative sign, depending on whether
\mathrm{E}
complies with or violates the specification:

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{colregs}}\coloneqq c_{\mathrm{colregs}}(1-\tilde{\mathds{1}}_{\mathrm{vacuous}})\operatorname{sgn}(\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{out}})\,.

(10)

The component
r_{\mathrm{goal}}
returns
c_{\mathrm{goal}}
when the ego vessel reaches the goal. The final component
r_{\mathrm{zone}}
assigns the penalty
c_{\mathrm{zone}}
when the protected zones intersect.
The complete reward function is the sum of all components:

\displaystyle r\coloneqq

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{\Delta goal}}+r_{\mathrm{v}}+r_{\mathrm{\omega}}+r_{\mathrm{colregs}}+r_{\mathrm{zone}}+r_{\mathrm{goal}}\,.

(11)

5.2
Robustness Measures

The robustness measure functions
h_{\mu}
are defined for six atomic predicates, based on which the metric temporal logic traffic rule formalization in
(
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
)
can be transferred to STL.
To address that differently scaled atomic predicates can cause masking effects in the robustness calculations and reduce falsification performance
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
, the robustness values are rescaled to the time domain, e.g., by dividing distances by the maximum velocity.
Intuitively, the time domain scaling approximates the time required for vessels to violate (or satisfy) the predicate under worst-case conditions and, in doing so, provides a structured way to scale the robustness values. We categorize the atomic predicates into position, orientation, and velocity predicates, and then describe their connection to the formalization in
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
.

Figure 3
:
Illustration of the robustness measures (a)
h_{\texttt{p}}\coloneq h_{\texttt{position\_halfplane}}
and (b)
h_{\texttt{o}}\coloneq h_{\texttt{orientation\_halfplane}}
with
\beta,\gamma\coloneq\pi/4
.

Position predicate

The
position_halfplane
predicate reasons about the relative positioning of vessels. It determines if
p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
lies in the half-plane to the left of the line that passes through
p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
and is oriented at
\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\beta
, with
\beta\in[-\pi,\pi)
(see
Fig.

3
a). The robustness is defined as the signed distance from
p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
to the line, divided by
v_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\displaystyle h_{\texttt{position\_halfplane}}(x^{\mathrm{E}}_{k},x^{\mathrm{A}}_{k},\beta)\coloneq

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v_{\mathrm{max}}}\begin{bmatrix}-\sin(\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\beta)\\
\cos(\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\beta)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}(p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k})\,.

Orientation predicates

To reason about the relative orientations, the predicate
orientation_halfplane
is used. For
\gamma\in[-\pi,\pi)
, it determines if the vessel
\mathrm{A}
is oriented to the left or right of
\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\gamma
(see
Fig.

3
b).
The robustness is defined as the minimal signed angular difference between
\theta^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
and either
\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\gamma
or
\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\gamma+\pi
, divided by
\omega_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\displaystyle h_{\texttt{orientation\_halfplane}}(x^{\mathrm{E}}_{k},x^{\mathrm{A}}_{k},\gamma)\coloneqq

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega_{\mathrm{max}}}\arcsin\left(\sin(\theta^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-(\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\gamma))\right)\,.

Note that
\arcsin
and
\sin
in (
13
) do not cancel each other, as
\theta^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-(\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\gamma)
is not limited to
\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
.
The give-way maneuvers are evaluated using the atomic predicate
change_course
to determine if the orientation change exceeds
\delta
. This predicate depends on the reference orientation
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}
, which is set to the orientation of the vessel performing the maneuver when a persistent encounter is detected. The robustness is defined as the signed angular difference between
\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}+\delta
and the current orientation of the vessel, divided by
\alpha_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\displaystyle h_{\texttt{change\_course}}(x^{\mathrm{E}}_{k},\delta)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{\mathrm{max}}}(\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}^{\mathrm{E}}+\delta-\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k})\,.

(14)

Velocity predicates

To detect a potential violation of the required separation between vessels,
we define two tangent lines from
p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
to a circle of radius
2d_{\mathrm{zone}}
centered at
p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
to construct a velocity obstacle
(
Fiorini and Shiller 1998
)
. A velocity obstacle is the set of relative velocities that cause the protected zones to intersect if the vessels were to maintain their velocities and orientations from the time of evaluation onward. For details on its construction, we refer to
Krasowski and Althoff 2021
. Intuitively, we define the robustness via the required change in velocity to reach the boundary of the velocity obstacle.
Let
\epsilon_{k}
be the angle between the vector to the other vessel and the counterclockwise tangent line at time step
k
:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{k}\coloneq\arcsin\left(\frac{2d_{\mathrm{zone}}}{\lVert p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\rVert}\right)\,.

(15)

The robustness of the predicate
velocity_halfplane
is the signed perpendicular distance (in velocity space) from
v^{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{A}}
to the tangent line, divided by
a_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\displaystyle h_{\texttt{velocity\_halfplane}}(x^{\mathrm{E}}_{k},x^{\mathrm{A}}_{k},\epsilon_{k})\coloneqq

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{\mathrm{max}}\|p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|}\left(\operatorname{R}\left(\epsilon_{k}+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)(p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k})\right)^{\top}v^{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{A}}\,.

For determining if the violation of the zone can happen within a bounded time horizon
t_{\mathrm{h}}
, we define the robustness of the predicate
time_horizon
, which is the difference between
\|v^{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{A}}\|
and the minimum velocity required to travel the distance
\|p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|
, divided by
a_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\displaystyle h_{\texttt{time\_horizon}}(x^{\mathrm{E}}_{k},x^{\mathrm{A}}_{k})\coloneqq\frac{1}{a_{\mathrm{max}}}\left(\|v^{\mathrm{E},\mathrm{A}}\|-\frac{\|p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}-p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}\|}{t_{\mathrm{h}}}\right)\,.

(17)

The atomic predicate
drives_faster
determines whether the velocity of the ego vessel is greater than that of the other vessel in an overtaking encounter. The robustness measure is the difference in their velocities, divided by
a_{\mathrm{max}}
:

\displaystyle h_{\texttt{drives\_faster}}(x^{\mathrm{E}}_{k},x^{\mathrm{A}}_{k})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a_{\mathrm{max}}}(v^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}-v^{\mathrm{A}}_{k})\,.

(18)

5.3
Formalized Traffic Rules

The atomic predicates are used to build the STL traffic rules
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
, following the formalization in
(
Krasowski and Althoff 2021
;
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
)
.
We consider three give-way traffic rules. They share the same high-level structure as defined in (
5
) and mainly differ in parameter values.
For all give-way rules, an encounter predicate must persist for
t_{\mathrm{p}}
:

\displaystyle\texttt{persistent\_$\langle$encounter$\rangle$}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k)\iff

(19)

\displaystyle\neg\texttt{$\langle$encounter$\rangle$}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k)\,\wedge

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{[\Delta t,t_{\mathrm{p}}]}\left(\texttt{$\langle$encounter$\rangle$}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k)\right)\,,

The encounter predicates are
\{\texttt{crossing},\texttt{overtake},\texttt{head\_on}\}
, which are all a conjunction of a relative position, a relative orientation, and a collision possibility predicate. We exemplify the three encounter predicates with the crossing encounter predicate:

\displaystyle\texttt{crossing}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\beta},\overline{\beta},\underline{\gamma},\overline{\gamma})\iff

(20)

\displaystyle\texttt{in\_position}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\beta},\overline{\beta})\,\wedge

\displaystyle\texttt{in\_orientation}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\gamma},\overline{\gamma})\,\wedge

\displaystyle\texttt{velocity\_obstacle}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k)\,,

where
\underline{\beta},\overline{\beta}\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
\underline{\gamma},\overline{\gamma}\in[-\pi,\pi)
are angular bounds such that
\overline{\beta}>\underline{\beta}
,
\overline{\gamma}>\underline{\gamma}
, and
\overline{\beta}-\underline{\beta}\leq\pi
,
\overline{\gamma}-\underline{\gamma}\leq\pi
.
The
in_position
predicate detects whether
p^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
lies in the angular sector
[\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\underline{\beta},\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\overline{\beta}]
, relative to
p^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}
:

\displaystyle\texttt{in\_position}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\beta},\overline{\beta})\iff

(21)

\displaystyle\texttt{position\_halfplane}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\beta})\,\wedge

\displaystyle\neg\texttt{position\_halfplane}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\overline{\beta})\,.

Similarly, the
in_orientation
predicate detects whether
\theta^{\mathrm{A}}_{k}
lies in the angular sector
[\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\underline{\gamma},\theta^{\mathrm{E}}_{k}+\overline{\gamma}]
:

\displaystyle\texttt{in\_orientation}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\gamma},\overline{\gamma})\iff

(22)

\displaystyle\texttt{orientation\_halfplane}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\underline{\gamma})\,\wedge

\displaystyle\neg\texttt{orientation\_halfplane}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\overline{\gamma})\,,

with
position_halfplane
and
orientation_halfplane
being defined in (
12
) and (
13
).
The final predicate in (
20
) is
velocity_obstacle
, which evaluates whether the protected zones of the vessels intersect in the time horizon
t_{\mathrm{h}}
given the vessels keep their course and speed. It is formally defined as:

\displaystyle\texttt{velocity\_obstacle}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\epsilon_{k})\iff

(23)

\displaystyle\texttt{velocity\_halfplane}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,-\epsilon_{k})\,\wedge

\displaystyle\neg\texttt{velocity\_halfplane}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k,\epsilon_{k})\,\wedge

\displaystyle\texttt{time\_horizon}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{A}},k)\,,

where
\epsilon_{k}
,
velocity_halfplane
and
time_horizon
are defined in (
15
), (
16
) and (
17
) respectively.
The predicates
overtake
and
head_on
, as well as the values of
\beta
and
\gamma
, are specified in
Krasowski and Althoff 2021
.

Having specified the encounter predicates, the remaining component in (
5
) to construct
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
is the maneuver predicate, which encodes the required course adjustment for the give-way vessel:

\displaystyle\texttt{maneuver\_$\{$crossing,head\_on$\}$}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},k,\delta)\iff

(24)

\displaystyle\texttt{change\_course}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},k,-\delta)\,,

where
change_course
is defined in (
14
) and specifies the required course adjustment of
\delta
.
For overtaking, the vessel must turn in either direction:

\displaystyle\texttt{maneuver\_overtake}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},k,\delta)\iff

(25)

\displaystyle\texttt{change\_course}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},k,-\delta)\,\vee

\displaystyle\neg\texttt{change\_course}(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{E}},k,\delta)\,.

6
Numerical Evaluation

Next, we evaluate the falsification-driven
RL
framework using the
RL
formulation from Sec.
5.1
and the robustness measures of
\varphi_{\texttt{colregs}}
from Sec.
5.2
.
We first describe the experimental setup, including the software packages, the parametrization, and the evaluation approach, and then report the results.

6.1
Experimental Setup

The robustness is monitored using the
Real-Time Analog Monitoring Tool (RTAMT)

(
Yamaguchi et al. 2024
)
, which supports online monitoring of future-bounded
STL
and
IA-STL
.
The
CMA-ES
library is provided by
Nomura and Shibata 2024
, which is based on
(
Hansen 2016
)
.
The
RL
policy is trained using
PPO
, based on the implementation provided by
Stable Baselines3 (SB3)

(
Raffin et al. 2021
)
.

Table

2
summarizes the parameters used in our numerical experiments. For each scenario sample, the goal and initial state of the ego vessel are uniformly sampled from the distributions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{E}}_{0}
. The initial state of vessel
\mathrm{A}
is uniformly sampled from
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{C}}_{0}
,
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{H}}_{0}
, or
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{O}}_{0}
, which are designed to provide suitable initial conditions to induce crossing, head-on, and overtaking encounters, respectively.

CMA-ES
optimizes the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^{2}C)
, where
m\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
is the mean,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a scaling factor, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{2N\times 2N}
is the covariance matrix. In each generation,
\lambda
candidate solutions are sampled from this distribution and ranked using the objective of (
7
).
The policy and value function share a multi-layer perceptron with two hidden layers of 64 neurons each, using ReLU activations and otherwise default hyperparameters.
Since critical encounter scenarios are rare in maritime data, we generate 10,000 random scenarios to train a baseline policy, and another 10,000 to test trained policies on previously unseen scenarios. The initial states of both vessels are initialized the same way as in falsification-driven training. To produce more realistic state sequences than straight-line motion, the input sequence for the adversary is sampled as
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{A}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0_{2N},0.05^{2}I_{2N}\right)
. For the baseline, all generated scenarios are randomly sampled and added to the scenario pool at the start of training.
We evaluate the approach using five random seeds and train each
RL
policy for
T
steps.

7
Discussion

Our experiments indicate improved specification compliance compared to
RL
without falsification for all three traffic rules.
Besides an increased mean traffic rule compliance, the standard deviation of the rule compliance decreased. To ensure the safety of our approach, it could be combined with provably safe
RL
techniques, e.g., discrete action masking
(
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
)
.
However, provably safe
RL
for discrete action spaces can suffer from a reduced performance as they do not fully leverage the physical continuous action space.
Yet, the improved rule compliance of our approach would reduce the number of required safety interventions by provably safe
RL
, which should lead to a runtime-efficient and safe controller.

While traffic rule compliance is improved using falsification-driven
RL
, its performance in real-world experiments still needs to be investigated.
To better understand the impact of our work on real-world autonomous vessels, one could extend the proposed approach with more adversaries and traffic rules.
While the computational effort of the falsification increases with the number of adversaries, adding more vehicles likely reduces the ratio of vacuous scenarios, as they interfere more often. However, with multiple adversaries, there might be situations without rule-compliant solutions for the
RL
agents
(
Krasowski and Althoff 2024
)
.
To achieve real-world autonomy of vessels, adding further traffic rules that account for vessels beyond power-driven vessels, e.g., sailing yachts, is required.

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we introduce a novel falsification-driven
RL
approach.
By integrating efficient, sampling-based falsification techniques into the
RL
training process, our approach leverages counterexamples to iteratively refine policy behavior and promote compliance with complex
STL
specifications.
This paper is the first to apply falsification-driven
RL
to maritime motion planning.
Our experiments show that incorporating falsification improves the training process by generating more relevant scenarios, which in turn leads to more consistent rule compliance.
These findings highlight the potential of combining formal methods with learning-based control to enhance the safety of autonomous systems.
Future work will focus on scaling the approach to multi-agent scenarios and validating it in real-world maritime environments to bridge the gap between simulation and deployment.
