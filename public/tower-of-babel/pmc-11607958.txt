Clinical effect of medial meniscus posterior root repair combined with centralization technique in the treatment of medial meniscus posterior root tears
Authors: Yongwei Zhou, Qining Yang, Jiayu Kang, Jiajing Xu, Mingjian Chen, Congcong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the clinical effect of medial meniscus posterior root repair combined with centralization technique in treating medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMPRTs).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 87 patients with MMPRTs treated in the Department of Joint Surgery of Jinhua Central Hospital from January 2020 to May 2022. Thirty-four patients were males and 53 were females, with an overall average age of 56.4 ± 5.9 years. Patients were divided into two groups based on different surgical methods: the conventional group (those who underwent simple medial meniscus root repair, n = 42) and the modified group (those who underwent medial meniscus root repair combined with centralization technique, n = 45). Clinical effects were evaluated before and after 2 years of surgery using the Lysholm and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) scores. The inner meniscus displacement distance and the inner meniscus displacement ratio (medial meniscus extrusion (MME)) were compared before and after 2 years of surgery using magnetic resonance imaging. The degree of osteoarthritis (OA) and hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle were compared before and after 2 years of surgery using X-ray films. OA degree was evaluated using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system. Intergroup and intragroup comparisons were conducted.ResultsBaseline demographic data were comparable between the modified and conventional repair groups, with no statistically significant difference. All patients were followed up for 26.8 ± 2.4 months postoperatively. After 2 years of follow-up, both groups exhibited significant improvement in Lysholm and KOOS scores compared with preoperative values (P = 0.049, P = 0.003 ), with the modified group showing significantly higher scores than the conventional repair group (P = 0.0001), and differences were statistically significant. In the conventional group, the lateral displacement of the medial meniscus reduced from 4.5 ± 0.8 mm preoperatively to 4.0 ± 0.5 mm at 2 years postoperatively (P = 0.528), and the MME ratio improved from 41.2 ± 4.5% preoperatively to 39.3 ± 3.9% postoperatively (P = 0.661). In the modified group, the lateral displacement of the medial meniscus reduced from 4.6 ± 1 mm preoperatively to 2.6 ± 0.7 mm at 2 years postoperatively (P = 0.0019), and the MME ratio improved from 42.0 ± 5.2% preoperatively to 23.5 ± 3.6% postoperatively (P < 0.0001). After 2 years of follow-up, the modified group exhibited a higher proportion of KL grades 0 and I, whereas the conventional group had a higher proportion of KL grades I and II, with statistically significant differences between the two groups (P = 0.019). No significant difference in the change in HKA between the two groups (P = 0.321, P = 0.401).ConclusionBoth medial meniscus root repair and its combination with the centralization technique under arthroscopy can significantly enhance knee joint function and alleviate symptoms in patients with MMPRTs. Nevertheless, the combined technique can significantly reduce meniscus protrusion and delay the advancement of OA.Level of evidenceLevel III, retrospective observational study.Keywords: Medial meniscus, Extrution, Root Repair, Centralization, Osteoarthritis

Introduction

Medial meniscus posterior root tears (MMPRTs) are a specific type of meniscal tear that occurs within 1 cm of the bony attachment area of the posterior root of the medial meniscus, characterized by avulsion or radial tears [1, 2]. It has been reported that MMPRTs account for approximately 15–21% of all meniscal tears, affecting nearly 100,000 patients annually [2]. After the medial meniscus root tear, the circular fibers of the meniscus are disrupted, leading to medial meniscus extrusion (MME) under the axial stress of the knee joint, ultimately resulting in a reduction in the tibiofemoral joint contact area, increased stress peaks, and acceleration of cartilage degeneration and osteoarthritis (OA) formation and progression of [3, 4]. MME refers to the distance between the outer edge of the medial meniscus and the most lateral edge of the medial tibial plateau, with an extrusion of ≥ 3 mm generally considered pathological [5] (Fig. 1). An increasing number of knee biomechanics studies and systematic reviews have confirmed that MME is an independent risk factor for the occurrence and progression of knee OA [6–8]. Therefore, repairing the root tear of the medial meniscus to reduce MME is crucial for delaying the progression of OA. It is noteworthy that repairing the MMPRT alone may not correct MME and completely halt the progression of OA [9]. The present study sought to reduce MME by combining MMPRT repair with centralization techniques, to improve patients’ clinical symptoms and slow down OA progression. We hypothesize that arthroscopic repair of the medial meniscus root combined with centralization techniques can reduce the distance of MME and improve clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study subjects

The data of patients with MMPRTs undergoing arthroscopic repair of the medial meniscus root and arthroscopic repair combined with centralization techniques at the Department of Joint Surgery of Jinhua Central Hospital from January 2020 to May 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images showing posterior root tears of the medial meniscus (Fig. 2); (2) MMPRT-caused MME exceeding 3 mm; (3) no history of ipsilateral knee surgery; (4) no joint contracture; and (5) a minimum follow-up period of 2 years. Exclusion criteria: (1) Grade 3 or 4 OA according to the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) classification system; (2) Hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle of ≥ 0° indicating varus or valgus of the knee; (3) lateral meniscus extrusion. Finally, 87 patients were included in the analysis.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinhua Central Hospital [Ethics No.: 2020 (Ethical Review No.) 172]. Signed informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

General Information

Of the 87 patients enrolled, 40 were males and 47 were females, with an overall average age of 63.1 years (range 55–72 years). All patients were diagnosed with Type 2 MMPRTs based on preoperative MRI and arthroscopic findings (according to the Laprade classification [10], meniscus root tears are classified into five types based on the tear’s morphology: Type 1: partial root tear, Type 2: complete radial tear within 9 mm of the attachment site, Type 3: complete root tear with a bucket-handle tear of the meniscus, Type 4: complete root tear with a complex oblique or longitudinal tear, and Type 5: avulsion fracture at the tibial plateau attachment site) (Fig. 1). Patients were divided into two groups based on the surgical approach: the conventional group (those who underwent isolated repair of the medial meniscus root, n = 42) and the modified group (those who underwent repair of the medial meniscus root combined with centralization techniques, n = 45). Both groups were assessed for clinical outcomes using Lysholm and Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) scores preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively. The MME distance and MME ratio were compared preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively using MRI imaging. The degree of OA and hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle were compared preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively using plain X-ray films. The KL grading system was used to evaluate the degree of OA, and intragroup and intergroup comparisons were conducted.

Surgical methods

Conventional group: After general anesthesia, patients were placed in a supine position with a tourniquet applied to the thigh. With the knee flexed at 90°, a routine anteromedial portal (AMP) and anterolateral portal (ALP) were established. A standard 4 mm, 30° arthroscope was used. The knee joint’s suprapatellar pouch, medial and lateral intercondylar grooves, anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments, medial and lateral menisci, and the status of the articular cartilage were inspected using a probe. A probe was introduced through the AMP for patients with avulsion injuries of the posterior root of the medial meniscus to assess the severity of MMPRT and identify the tear type. In cases where the medial compartment was tight and with poor exposure, a standard outside-in approach with the “pie-crusting technique” was employed to release the medial collateral ligament. With the knee in an externally rotated and extended position, an 18-gauge needle was percutaneously inserted horizontally through the tibial plateau from outside to inside for pie-crusting release. After confirming that the medial compartment was open, adhesions between the medial meniscus and surrounding scar tissue were released to mobilize the meniscus. The synovium and inflammatory tissue at the posterior root of the medial meniscus were debrided, and the posterior root and surrounding tissues were freshened to induce capillary bleeding, promoting healing. If necessary, a small amount of cartilage near the anatomical attachment area was removed using a shaver. Under surveillance from the ALP, a suture hook was introduced into the knee joint through the AMP, and 0 polydioxanone suture (PDS) (ETHICON thread, Johnson & Johnson, USA) was used to perform two sutures. The sutures were then passed through a high-strength suture (Arthrex, USA) for later use. The sutures were kept at a safe distance from the edge of the tear to prevent suture cutting and loss of fixation. An anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) localizer was positioned at the anatomical attachment area of the posterior root of the medial meniscus, and a 2.0 mm K-wire was drilled through the medial tibial tubercle along the guide. A tibial tunnel was then created using a 4.5 mm drill bit. A tailed guide needle was used to pull the high-strength suture through the tibial tunnel into the joint cavity. With the knee in extension, meniscal root sutures were passed through the two central holes of the Endobutton button plate (Arthrex, USA) and suspended and fixed outside the tibial tunnel. The affected limb was dressed with a bandage and elastic bandage postoperatively, and a straight-position splint was fixed.

Modified group: The surgical procedure for repairing the posterior root of the medial meniscus was similar to that of the conventional group. After the high-strength suture was pulled through the tibial tunnel into the joint cavity using a tailed guide needle, the tail of the suture was temporarily clamped with a small vascular clamp. A high posteromedial portal was established, and one 3.0 mm anchor (Arthrex, USA) was placed on the outer edge of the medial tibial plateau through the high posteromedial portal. A suture hook (Suture Lasso, Arthrex, USA) was inserted through the anchor entrance, passed between the medial meniscus (MM) and the joint capsule, and then two different anchor sutures were pulled out, which were then tied and fixed. After the anchor sutures were fixed, with appropriate tension and the knee in extension, the meniscal root sutures were passed through the two central holes of the Endobutton button plate (Arthrex, USA) and suspended and fixed outside the tibial tunnel. The affected limb was dressed with a bandage and elastic bandage postoperatively, and a straight-position splint was fixed (arthroscopic operations are shown in Fig. 3A and F).

Postoperative management and efficacy evaluation

The rehabilitation protocol was the same for both groups. Immediately after surgery, isometric contraction of the quadriceps was initiated, transitioning to straight-leg raise exercises. A variable knee brace was worn, with movement within a 30° range during week 1, progressive range of motion training from 30° to 90° during weeks 2–4, and a knee flexion range of 120° by weeks 4–6. Non-weight bearing was maintained for 3 weeks, followed by partial weight bearing with crutch assistance after 3 weeks. Full weight bearing and practice of normal gait and proprioception were initiated at 6 weeks. Jogging was allowed after 3 months, with increased strength and endurance training. Exercise intensity, flexibility training, and return to non-contact sports were gradually increased after 6 months.

All patients had regular outpatient follow-ups. Clinical outcomes for both groups were assessed using Lysholm and KOOS scores preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively. The MME distance and MME ratio were compared preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively using MRI imaging (Fig. 4). The degree of OA and hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle were compared preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively using plain X-ray film. The KL grading system was used to evaluate the degree of OA, and intragroup and intergroup comparisons were performed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 21.0 software. Quantitative data were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as means ± standard deviations. Intragroup comparisons were carried out using paired sample t-tests, and intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent sample t-tests. Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages), and intergroup comparisons were performed using chi-square tests. A two-tailed test was used with an alpha level (α) set at 0.05.

Results

No statistically significant differences in the preoperative general information were found between the modified and conventional groups (Table 1). All patients were followed up for 26.8 ± 2.4 months postoperatively. The two groups had significantly improved Lysholm scores at 2 years postoperatively compared with preoperative scores (P < 0.05), with the modified group exhibiting significantly higher Lysholm scores than the conventional repair group (P < 0.05), and the difference was statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study has demonstrated that both arthroscopic medial meniscus root repair and its combination with centralization techniques can significantly improve knee joint function and alleviate symptoms in patients with MMPRTs. However, the combined technique can significantly reduce meniscal extrusion and delay OA progression.

It has been reported that the incidence of MMPRTs in patients undergoing medial meniscus repair or meniscectomy ranges from 10.1 to 21.4% [11]. Due to the squatting posture or high-flexion lifestyle common among Asians, the incidence of MMPRTs can be as high as 27.8% [12]. MMPRT is one of the causes of medial meniscal extrusion. When the meniscus extrudes, the “ring effect” disappears, accelerating the degeneration of the joint, making precise repair of MMPRT crucial [13]. Cadaveric studies have shown that the posterior root of the medial meniscus has a rich synovial and capillary network, and the area also has stretchable radial collagen fiber structures, providing a foundation for suture repair and healing post-surgery [14].

Studies have shown that medial meniscus root repair can improve the subjective clinical symptoms of most patients. A previous meta-analysis reported a significant improvement in subjective clinical scores after a mean follow-up of 30.3 months in patients who underwent meniscus repair [15]. A different meta-analysis also confirmed that repairing MMPRTs significantly slowed down OA progression compared with partial meniscectomy, total meniscectomy, and non-surgical treatment, reducing the likelihood of later knee replacement and serving as a cost-saving intervention [16]. A prospective study found that the extrusion distance measured by MRI increased from an average of 3.6 mm preoperatively to 5.0 mm 2 years postoperatively, indicating that isolated medial meniscus root repair does not significantly reduce meniscal extrusion [17]. Consistent with previous findings, the present clinical study found that isolated meniscus root repair did not significantly improve medial meniscal extrusion.

Currently, the duration of non-surgical treatment before surgical intervention for patients with MMPRTs remains controversial. Previous literature suggests early surgical treatment. Furumatsu et al. reported significant MME progression in 35 patients, with absolute MME increasing to 4.2 mm and 5.8 mm in the subacute (30–90 days) and chronic (120–365 days) stages, respectively, and relative MME to 49.2% and 60.3%, respectively [18]. Bernard et al. found that the rate of knee replacement in MMPRT patients not undergoing surgical repair was 27% at an average of 74 months and that in patients undergoing meniscus root repair was 0%, with significantly less progression of arthritis in the repair group [19]. Moon et al. divided patients undergoing meniscus root repair into two groups based on the change in the MME ratio compared with preoperative measurements, and compared intrinsic variables to determine the factors affecting the progression of postoperative meniscal extrusion, with the optimal time for surgical intervention being 13 weeks after symptom onset [20]. Therefore, early surgical intervention is recommended for patients with MMPRTs to prevent further wear of the knee cartilage.

Previous meta-analyses have reported that about 5.6% of patients with medial meniscus root repair progressed from KL grade 0 to grades 1–3 in an average follow-up of 27.7 months, with no patient progressing from grades 1–3 to grade 4; no significant improvement was found in meniscal extrusion after medial meniscus posterior root repair compared with preoperative measurements [9]. A recent systematic review reported that 49% of patients had at least grade 1 in the KL classification in 11 studies with an average follow-up of 4 years and 23% of patients had at least one grade progression of cartilage degeneration in 4 studies with an average follow-up of 31.6 months [21]. Although isolated medial meniscus root repair can slow down OA progression, it does not stop the progression in mid-term follow-ups. Krych et al. followed 25 patients for an average of 2 years and found significant improvements in resting visual analog scale (VAS), activity VAS, KOOS scores, and International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores. MRI measurements showed that the MME distance decreased from an average of 3.2 mm preoperatively to 1.6 mm postoperatively [22]. This is consistent with the results of the current clinical study. Overall, a combination of medial meniscus root repair with centralization techniques can improve medial meniscal extrusion, thereby slowing down the progression of OA.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. (1) This was a retrospective study, typically associated with low-quality evidence. (2) The sample size was relatively small, which may lead to statistical error. (3) The follow-up duration after surgery was relatively short, necessitating further follow-up to confirm long-term functional outcomes. Therefore, studies with higher quality of evidence and involving multivariate analysis are required to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings have demonstrated that both arthroscopic medial meniscus root repair and its combination with centralization techniques can significantly improve knee joint function and alleviate symptoms in patients with MMPRTs. However, the combined technique can significantly reduce meniscal extrusion and delay the progression of OA. This study provides a basis for future studies with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up periods, and involving multiple factors.
