The impact of nutritional and socioeconomic status on academic performance of primary urban school children in northern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tigist Enyew Gedamu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrban school children are challenged from varying degrees of poverty, access to resources and health services ultimately impacting their academic performance. Educational success is complex highlighting that several factors may contribute to its outcome. Among these, undernutrition and socioeconomic status significantly impact academic performance. The interplay between these determinants and educational outcomes is particularly significant in developing countries such as northern Ethiopia. Children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds often experience nutritional deficiencies that can adversely impact cognitive development leading for poor educational achievement. Thus, this research can fill existing gaps regarding the interaction between undernutrition and socioeconomic factors that enable to understand how they interact to affect children’s educational outcomes. In addition, the study will provide practical implications for policymakers and educators in Ethiopia as it enables them for developing more effective educational policies that address unique challenges faced by urban school children in northern Ethiopia. Therefore, our study aimed to assess predictors of academic performance among primary school students to address the multifaceted issues and develop effective interventions.MethodThis institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in three of seven primary public schools. Simple random sampling technique were employed to select the schools and proportional allocation was done to each school to draw a total of 399 study participants. An interviewer administered questionnaire were used to gather socioeconomic data from the children’s legal guardians. Academic performance was evaluated using the average scores from all subjects over two semesters obtained from school rosters. Nutritional status was assessed via anthropometric measurements with Height-for-Age (HAZ), Weight-for- Age (WAZ) and Body Mass Index-for-Age (BAZ) calculated according to the WHO 2007 standard reference guidelines. WHO Anthro version 3.2.2 was used for the analysis and interpretation of nutritional status. Data were entered, cleaned and validated using Epi Data version 3.1 and subsequently analyzed using SPSS version 24. A bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were done to determine candidate variables and significantly associated factors respectively. Variables with p-value of ≤ 0.05 with 95% confidence interval in the multivariate model were considered as factors associated with the outcome variable.ResultThe mean academic performance of study participants was 71.65 (SD = 12.63). The prevalence of stunting, wasting, underweight and overweight was 23.8%, 34.0%, 24.0% and 4.3% respectively. In the adjusted analysis, age of child (AOR = 2.26, 95% CL: 1.39–3.67), head of household (AOR = 2.79, 95% CL: 1.55–5.04), absenteeism (AOR = 1.73, 95%CL: 1.07–2.79), preschool attendance (AOR = 1.47, 95% CL: 0.93–2.31) and monthly income (AOR = 1.67, 95% CL: 1.02–2.74) were significantly associated with educational achievement.ConclusionUndernutrition particularly stunting remains a significant public health issue that might negatively affect academic performance. While undernutrition has showed association in the bivariate logistic regression, it does not adversely affect the children’s academic performance. Conversely, age of child, head of household, monthly income, school attendance and preschool enrollment significantly impacted educational outcomes suggesting that interventions should focus on these factors in collaboration with stakeholders, teachers, parents and the community. Yet this finding highlights the potential aims for intervention at primary public schools, further research is needed to comprehensively understand which factors reduced urban primary public school children’s academic performance.Keywords: Academic performance, Nutritional status, Primary school, Children

Introduction

Global trends revealed that undernutrition majorly affects low-income countries like Ethiopia [1]. Particularly stunting and wasting are the most serious global health problems that affect children’s academic performance [2]. Undernourished children will have poor intelligence, learning and cognitive performance and dverse reasons attribute for 58 million primary school students being out of school [3]. Developing countries faced delayed cognitive ability and poor educational attainment [4]. Different barriers attributed for delayed cognitive development and lower educational achievement of learner’s [4–6]. Stunted children under 2 years will have deficits in cognition and school achievement at their adolescence highlighting that brain development has specific critical periods during different developmental stages that depend on specific nutrients [5, 7, 8]. Children in sub-Saharan Africa experienced impaired mental development, repeated grades, dropped out of school and lower educational achievement [9]. Despite the national target for school dropout and repetition rate (< 1%) most of primary school children achieved poor [10]. Self-concept dimensions such as confidence, lack of anxiety and expectations of success are reported as the most important predictors of academic performance in children attending primary school [11].

Undernutrition have immediate and long term effects which range from immediate school dropout to delayed cognition resulted in poor educational achievement [5]. Various nutritional barriers in developed countries showed significant association with lowered educational achievement [4]. Almost 30% underweight, 23% overweight and 32% obese children exhibit poor academic performance [12, 13]. Children having school breakfast, regular lunch and dinner scored well while those get meals seldom achieved low [5]. Additionally 37% and 17% undernourished boys and girls scored poor to average respectively [14]. Study in Russia indicate conscious self-regulation has a substantial impact on children’s academic performance with enhanced academic motivation, school engagement and positive attitude towards learning [15]. A finding in Africa explains undernutrition in the form of stunting has long-term impact on children’s academic performance [8]. The burden of undernutrition on school-age children is well stated in previous studies of Ethiopia [7] Studies revealed children attending preschool perform better in their educational performance when compared to their counterparts [16]. Various socio-demographic factors like absenteeism, attending preschool, sex, travelling time and parental socioeconomic status showed significant relation with primary school children’s academic achievement [10, 17, 18]. Studies in low-income countries showed undernutrition and micronutrient deficiency has an impact on children’s academic performance [19].

Studies revealed academic achievement is influenced by various nutritional and socioeconomic factors [10, 20, 21].Studies particularly in low-income countries such as Ethiopia indicate that the link between undernutrition and academic performance of children is remained underexplored [2, 22].Thus, this study has examined the dual impact of nutritional and socioeconomic status on urban primary school children’s educational achievement. This will help to advance our understanding of educational disparities contributing for a meaningful knowledge that promote academic success in underprivileged urban children. Further the finding might contribute valuable insights for researchers, policymakers and educators to design strategies that enhance educational outcomes.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

An institution based cross-sectional study design conducted from 30, March to 30, April 2022 in Debre Tabor Town, Amhara region, North Central Ethiopia. The town is located 665kms Northwest Ethiopia of Addis Ababa and 97 kms of the zone capital city Bahir-Dar. The Town has 96,973 total population and 6,935 primary school children [23].The town has two preparatory schools, one from grade 9–12 and the other from grade 11–12, two high school from grade 9–10, fourteen elementary schools: seven public and seven private and nine kindergarten schools.

Source population

All children who enrolled at all public primary schools in the study area.

Study population

All children who enrolled in the selected public primary schools and full fill the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All primary school children in the selected public primary schools aged 7 to 14 years and have at least two semester total subject average during the study period. Children who were unable to communicate and have critical health problems that might affect their school attendance, ability to concentrate and learn during the study period were excluded.

Sample size determination and procedure

Sample size calculated using single population proportion formula by considering 95% confidence level, 0.05 margin of error and 62.25 mean academic achievement [24]. After considering 10% non-response rate, 399 students recruited as final sample size. Simple random sampling technique were used to select two third (2/3) of the seven primary schools that yield three primary schools for the study. Proportional allocation were done to each school based on the number of primary school children enrolled. Therefore, 138 students from Fitawurari-Gebreye, 150 students from Gafat and 111 students from Dagmawi Tewoderos public primary schools were included. Finally those proportionally allocated study participants were selected using systematic random sampling technique to pool the final sample size as (KGPS= 1261̸ 150 = 9, KFGPS= 1155̸ 138 = 9 and KDTPS= 928̸111 = 9). After selecting the first sample by lottery method, study participants were selected in every ninth interval) using their grade roster.

Data collection instrument and procedure

The tool adapted from previous studies to assess the children’s and their parental sociodemographic and economic status such us age of child, sex of child, preschool attendance, breakfast habit, absenteeism, time taking to school, work load at home, marital status, education status, occupation, family size, monthly income etc [5, 12, 13]. WHO 2007 standard reference used to assess nutritional status as stunted, wasted, underweight, overweight and obese. Academic performance measured using two consecutive semester overall subject average score taken from the school roster.

The variable “family support” indicates parental support or guidance during academic homework activities, study time and learning resources provision. While the variable “work load at home” indicates any domestic responsibilities such as care giving, cooking and other household task management. Both of the variables were quantified as “yes’ or “no” in order to show whether the student has parental support and any work load at home or not.

Nutritional status assessed using weight scale and height scale. Student’s body weight measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using PRESTIGE, SALTER WEIGH-TRONY British patent digital weight scale. Average weight taken from two independent data collector weight measurement. Height measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using meter scale. Child stood on flat surface with bare feet flat and against the wall. Two independent data collectors measured height and average score taken. Nutritional status interpreted using WHO standard reference guideline 2007. Therefore, weight for age Z score (WAZ) ≤-2SD, height for age z score (HAZ) <-2SD and body mass index for age (BAZ) <-2SD interpreted as wasted, stunted and underweight respectively. Body mass index for age (BAZ) > + 1SD and BAZ > + 2SD interpreted as overweight and obese respectively. The criteria for severe wasting, stunting and underweight were defined as z-scores below − 3 SD, moderate as z-scores between − 2 and − 3 SD while overall wasting, stunting and underweight explained as z-scores below − 2 SD (it incorporates both severe and moderate under nutritional status). Normal nutritional status was defined as z-scores between − 2 and + 2 SD whereas overweight was defined as z-scores above + 2 SD based on WHO Child Growth Standard ()2007. Data quality maintained through checking weight scale reading at zero before measurement, child stood bare feet flat and against the wall and interpreting using standard guideline. Six health extension workers and three BSc nurses were recruited to collect and supervise data collection process.

Variables

Academic performance is the outcome variable measured as good and poor based on the mean academic value. Explanatory variables include child nutritional status (WAZ, HAZ and BAZ), child and parental socio-demographic and economic characteristics (age, sex, religion, occupation, educational and marital status of parent’s, parental support for the students, monthly income of parents, head of house, breakfast habit, absenteeism, time taking to school, work load to the child, family size and attending preschool).Breakfast habit was included because breakfast consumption has been shown strong influence on children’s attention span, cognitive function and academic performance while data did not collected on other meals such us lunch, dinner and snacks as previous studies did not show their significant impact on academic performance.

Data processing and analysis

Data entered, cleaned and checked with Epi Data version 3.1 and analyzed using SPSS version 24 respectively. Nutritional status was analyzed and interpreted using WHO Anthro version 3.2.2 software as weight-for-age (WAZ), height-for-age (HAZ) and body-mass-index-for-age (BAZ). A bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were done to determine candidate variables at p-value of < 20% and significantly associated factors at p-value of < 0.05respectively. In the final model, variables with p-value of < 0.05 at 95% confidence interval were considered as factors associated with the outcome variable. Finally the result is presented in texts and tables.

Result

Most of study participants 209 (52%) were male with mean age of 12 (± 1.9 SD). Majority students 292 (73%) were in the age group of 11–14 years and 246 (62%) from second cycle (grade 5–8). Almost 383 (96%) participants were Orthodox and 91 (73%) from married families. More than half of participants 218 (55%) attend preschool and majority 386 (96.7%) have regular breakfast habit. Study participants scored 71.65 (± 12.63 SD) mean academic performance.

Being father of head of house (AOR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.55–5.04) almost increased the academic achievement by three fold. Variables like breakfast habit, grade, family support and height for age (HAZ) anthropometric nutritional status does not statically predicted academic performance in the final model. (See Table 3)

Discussion

Our study aimed to determine anthropometric nutritional status and socio-demographic predictors of academic performance among public urban primary school children. The result revealed a 56.14% of any form of an overall malnutrition. Study participants scored 71.65 (+ 12.63 SD) mean academic performance. Age of child, head of household, absenteeism, attending pre-school and monthly income predicted academic performance.

Overall anthropometric malnutrition were 56.14% in this study which is higher than previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa including Ethiopia [25, 26]. On the contrary, it is less than other previous study. These variability might be attributed to socioeconomic status, cultural influences, accessibility to nutritional resources and maternal education that crucially play to the children’s educational outcome. This study adds to the existing literature that how socioeconomic status interact with nutritional status to impact academic outcomes. In addition, focusing specifically to urban primary school children adds valuable insights as much of prior literatures focus on rural or both rural and urban settings in order to identify the impact of nutritional and socioeconomic status on academic performance. Further the finding has practical implications for policymakers and educators. This can guide future educational policies and public health initiatives aiming to improve academic performance.

In our study nearly 24% primary school children were stunted which is higher than previous studies in the world including Ethiopia [2, 6, 12, 27]. On the contrary this finding is less than other global studies [20, 28, 29]. The increased incidence of stunting might be attributed to early life undernutrition that occurred during the critical infant stage the period where particular nutritional vulnerability happened. This highlights that inadequate nutritional support at this stage can have long lasting effects on child development, health and academic outcomes [7, 8].

In our study 34% of study participants were wasted indicating a higher prevalence than previous Sub-Saharan African studies [30] while it is lesser than other African research finding [31]. The variation in acute malnutrition can be attributed to several interrelated factors, including health status differences, nutritional quality, meals size and the food intake frequency. In addition acute health conditions like diarrhea significantly contribute to the prevalence of wasting that diminish appetite and compromise the immune system further exacerbating nutritional deficiencies among affected children.

Our study indicate that 24% of primary school children were underweight highlighting a similar finding with previous studies [12]. On the contrary, this finding were higher than previous global research findings [12] and lesser than other studies done worldwide [20, 28, 29].These discrepancies might be attributed to socioeconomic differences, cultural food beliefs, meal frequencies and genetics. In addition our study highly that 4.3% primary school children were over weighted indicating that it is less than other previous studies particularly the developed ones [32–34]. These variability might be attributed to socioeconomic status as food insecurity is the issue of developing countries including Ethiopia highlighting its effect on meal size, frequency and composition later leading to poor academic performance [4].

The mean academic performance in our finding is 71.65 ± 12.63 SD indicating better achievement when compared with several previous studies in sub-Saharan African countries including Ethiopia [12, 19, 35]. These discrepancies in academic performance may be attributed to variations in individual intellectual self-concept, assessment methods, teaching strategies and availability of learning resources [11, 36–38].

Our study indicate that male students demonstrate better academic performance (72.02 ± 11.57) when compared to their female (71.24 ± 13.73) counterparts highlighting a consistent observations with previous studies that 2.4 times male students perform better [10, 21, 35]. Conversely, studies in various regions of the globe have reported that female student perform better than male students in educational achievement [12, 13, 18]. The observed disparities between genders may be attributed to the differing social status of females in various communities, the extent to which their academic efforts are recognized, the limited presence of role models for females and the high percentage of students lacking access to preschool education.

Our study indicate that student’s academic performance varied significantly across different age groups. Students in the age group of 7-10years perform better (76.61 ± 11.81SD) than those in the age group of 11-14years (69.84 ± 12.45 SD) in educational achievement which is in line with previous studies [39]. This may attribute to that younger students are often more skillful in routine learning and foundational skill acquisition. This highlights the period characterized by heightened receptivity to new information making it an optimal time for acquiring fundamental academic skills. Conversely, their counterparts in the age group of 11-14years may face increased cognitive demand as they transit into more complex subjects requiring critical thinking and problem-solving skills attributing to potential challenges resulting in lower academic performance. In addition, students in the age group of 11-14years may encounter larger class size, a more rigorous curriculum, greater destructions related to adolescence, peer influences and the desire for social acceptance may overshadow academic priorities that may affect their concentration and full engagement impacting their overall educational achievement. Therefore, understanding these factors is crucial for educators and policy makers to develop targeted strategies that support students learning across different stages of development particularly aiming to mitigate academic performance disparities and foster academic success for all students.

Being head of household of father increased student’s educational achievement almost by three fold which highlighted that fathers may frequently adopt a more directive and goal-oriented approach to instill a sense of discipline, encourage resilience and promote a strong work ethic in students when compared to mothers who more provide emotional support. Fathers may participate in reading, assisting homework or exploring educational content together attributing to enhanced critical thinking and problem-solving skills of the students.

Our study indicate that students who attend pre-school perform better than their counterparts which highlight that early childhood education equips children with essential cognitive, social and emotional skills attributing to language development, problem solving abilities and interpersonal skills that help them often prepared to enter the formal education system [16, 40]. By far, it serves to enhance the student’s capacity for academic achievement throughout their schooling.

Our study highlighted that absenteeism during primary education poses a significant barrier to academic success which attribute for disruption of continuing of learning and leads to gaps in knowledge and understanding [2]. The cumulative effect of absenteeism may attribute to lower academic performance, decreased motivation and increased grade repetition and dropout of school [27]. Therefore, ensuring regular attendance is crucial to maximize educational achievement.

Lastly, our study indicate that the influence of monthly income on educational achievement is profound which is consistent with previous studies [12, 18, 20, 35, 41]. Students from high income families may have greater access to educational resources such as tutoring, extracurricular activities and advanced learning materials [20]. Conversely, their counterparts may face challenges that impede educational achievement including insufficient access to technology, inadequate nutrition, and unstable home environment attributing for considerable economic effect of student’s educational opportunities and achievements [42].

In our study neither acute nor chronic under-nutrition predicted educational achievement of the students which is consistent with previous global studies [28, 43] while contradict with other studies [2]. Though nutritional status is widely acknowledged as critical factor influencing cognitive development and educational achievement it may not directly predict academic achievement highlighting that several factors like intrinsic motivation, access to educational resources and quality of teaching-learning strategies contribute to multifaceted educational outcomes [11, 15]. The reason undernutrition not being significantly associated with academic performance might be related with confounders such as the quality of the school environment including access to adequately trained teachers and educational materials that significantly affect student’s educational achievement. In addition, nutritional assessment in this study relies only on anthropometric indices but did not include clinical and biochemical nutritional parameters such as micronutrient deficiencies (Vitamin A, D & iron deficiencies. This might need further investigation to investigate the relationship between undernutrition and academic performance.

A significant association between better educational achievement and over-weight & obesity were observed in previous studies [35, 43–46] which is contradicting our research finding that neither of them predicting academic performance. Other previous studies indicate that there were significant relation between lower academic performance and severe stunting & underweight [2, 12, 19, 39, 41, 47, 48] that is contradicting our result that neither of them predict academic achievement. These discrepancies may attribute to multifaceted nature of academic performance such as socioeconomic status, parental involvement, access to educational resources and individual motivation that shadow the impact of nutritional status [11, 15, 42]. In addition, psychological and environmental factors including stress, mental health issue and adverse living conditions may significantly impede student’s ability to focus and perform better academically irrespective of their nutritional status [11, 15, 25]. Therefore, a thorough understanding of these factors is vital for developing effective interventions aimed at improving both nutritional status and educational outcomes among students.

It is important to acknowledge the strengths and limitations of the study. The study primarily focuses on a specific population within a distinct geographic location that is urban school children which is crucial to understand conditions and factors that might affect educational achievement. In addition, the study emphasizes on both nutritional and socioeconomic status which is vital to investigate multiple factors that enable holistic view of the challenges faced by children in their academic performance. This enables policymakers to develop interventional strategies that address not only academic support but also the foundational issues nutrition and economic stability. Despite these strengths, the study does not incorporate children from private schools and rural areas that might significantly compromise its representativeness and diminishes its richness and depth. Another significant constraint is cross-sectional study design while useful for identifying associations, it does not allow for causal inferences as it cannot definitively determine whether poor nutritional and socioeconomic status precedes and directly contribute to poor academic performance. In addition, nutritional status assessed using anthropometric indices while neglecting micronutrient deficiencies that might hinder the real association of nutritional status with academic performance. Another limitation of this study is it did not consider other meals such as lunch, dinner and snacks that might potentially influence children’s academic performance as breakfast consumption did. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of these dynamics will require further research employing diverse methodologies to explore causal relationships and capture a broader range of influences on educational outcomes.

Conclusions and recommendations

In this study the prevalence threshold for stunting (24%) and wasting (34%) was very high but low for overweight (4.3%) [31] indicating the highest burden of undernutrition among primary school children. Thus, strengthening nutritional intervention programs particularly in school-age children should be prioritized. Our study indicate that the mean score of academic achievement among primary school children is 71.65 ± 12.63 SD which vary when contrasted with different global findings highlighting that it is a result of several factors. Key determinants identified and attributed to this variation in this study include student’s age, being head of household, monthly income, pre-school attendance and absenteeism. Notably, neither of undernutrition significantly predicted academic achievement despite prior studies showing that dietary diversity and undernutrition significantly predicted academic performance. Therefore, policies aimed at improving school attendance, supporting low-income households and expanding early childhood education may have the greatest impact on improving academic outcomes in the study population. This study contributes to add a comprehensive understanding of how nutritional and socioeconomic status interact to impact academic performance. In general, the study serves as a comparative guide by emphasizing on the need for a more integrated understanding of several factors influencing academic performance in low-income settings such as Ethiopia.
