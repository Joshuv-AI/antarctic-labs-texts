Passivity, torque stability, and fracture resistance in screw-retained and dual-retained metal-ceramic implant-supported restorations: an in vitro study, part II
Authors: Safoura Ghodsi, Sareh Habibzadeh, Marzieh Alikhasi, Seyed Ali Mosaddad, Hosein Mohebbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the passivity, screw torque stability, and fracture resistance between dual- and screw-retained implant restorations, before and after thermomechanical loading.MethodsTwenty metal-ceramic restorations were divided into two groups (n = 10 each). Group 1 (G1) featured dual-retained restorations on prefabricated titanium abutments, while Group 2 (G2) had screw-retained restorations on UCLA chromium-cobalt cast-to abutments. Passivity, adjustment ease, and torque loss were compared, along with fracture resistance and mode, post-fabrication and after thermomechanical loading (500 thermal and 500,000 mechanical loading cycles). Independent t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare passivity characteristics and fracture resistance, while torque losses were analyzed using a generalized estimating equations (GEE) model with LSD post-hoc test (α = 0.05).ResultsG1 required significantly less adjustment time and frequency (P < .001) and exhibited a lower torque angle (higher passivity) (P = .001) compared to G2. In G1, the screw torque loss (%) measured 24.02 ± 3.01 before and 36.51 ± 4.28 after loading compared to the initial torque and 16.17 ± 3.67 after loading compared to the detorque value. In G2, these values were 24.76 ± 3.45, 39.9 ± 2.55, and 20.1 ± 3.96, respectively. While the rate of torque loss did not differ significantly before loading, it was significantly higher in G2 after loading (P < .05). Fracture resistance was significantly higher in G1 (P < .05). Both groups exhibited a mixed type of failure mode.ConclusionsDual-retained metal-ceramic restorations outperformed screw-retained counterparts, demonstrating improved passivity, lower screw torque loss percentages, and higher fracture resistance.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Dental implants, Dental prosthesis, Dental restoration failure, Fracture strength, Implant supported dental prosthesis, Single tooth dental implant

Introduction

In dentistry, implant therapy is widely acclaimed for its remarkable success rates, establishing itself as a cornerstone method for addressing edentulism across various clinical scenarios [1]. Clinicians face a range of treatment options for fixed implant restorations, including cement-retained, screw-retained, and dual-retained modalities, each tailored to specific clinical needs [2, 3]. The selection among these options remains a subject of ongoing debate, with consensus yet to be reached [4, 5].

Cement-retained restorations are praised for their ability to achieve passivity, enhanced aesthetics, and simplified occlusal control [4, 6–9]. The screw-retained approach offers advantages in retrievability, eliminating the concerns of cement excess, and suitability for limited restorative space [4, 6]. Screw-retained restorations could be fabricated using low profile abutments or, more routinely, by prefabricated universal casting long abutments (UCLA) [10], in overcast (cast-to) or castable mode. The overcast UCLA abutment features a plastic sleeve and a prefabricated machined metallic connection made from gold alloy or base metal alloys; the elevated cost of gold drives the rising preference for base metal connections. However, this type of connection is prone to possible deterioration during the casting and repeated firing cycles throughout the fabrication stages, adversely affecting the mechanical properties [11, 12].

Another variant of implant restoration, known variously as screw-retrievable cement-retained, dual-retained, cement-screw-retained, or combination prosthesis [13–16], entails definitively cementing the crown with an integrated occlusal access onto the abutment either extraorally or intraorally, followed by meticulous removal of excess cement. Afterward, the crown-abutment assembly is affixed to the implant using an abutment screw [15], and, the access hole is sealed with composite resin [13, 17]. The introduction of a screw access hole in cement-retained restorations was pioneered under the term “combination implant crown” by McGlumphy in 1992 [18]. Subsequent studies by Rajan and Gunaseelan (2004) [13] and Uludag et al. (2006, 2008) [14, 17] delved into the dual-retained restoration design for fabricating multi-unit restorations on custom cast abutments. This restoration type was designed to combine the advantages of cement- and screw-retained restorations and despite shorter history, dual-retained restoration claims enhancements in biological and mechanical properties. It offers easier retrievability, eliminates concerns regarding excess cement, improves retention in limited interarch spaces, and allows for better forming of the restoration’s emergence profile compared to cement-retained restorations [13]. Additionally, it provides improved passivity, reduced mechincal complications, simplified laboratory workflow, and the possibility to use regular abutments, compared to screw-retained restorations [13]. It has also been hypothesized that the intermediary cement layer serves as a stress-dispersing, minimizing the effect of the occlusal access hole on the porcelain fracture rate [19, 20]. Unlike screw-retained restorations fabricated with UCLA abutments, the abutments employed in these restorations do not undergo the thermal cycle associated with casting and porcelain firing, potentially preserving their fit accuracy and overall quality [21]. However, drawbacks such as compromised retention and porcelain weakening due to the access opening have been cited for these restorations, yet studies have yielded conflicting results [19, 22–24]. Nissan et al. evaluated 274 dual-retained restorations over 12 years, concluding that this prosthetic design enhances long-term survival rates and reduces maintenance costs without escalating the risk of porcelain fracture or screw loosening [25].

Existing studies have primarily examined the effect of the screw access hole on the strength of implant-supported restorations, yielding contradictory results [19, 22–24, 26–32]. While most investigations have employed titanium-based abutments to fabricate all-ceramic dual-retained restorations, it is important to highlight that this prosthetic design is not limited to all-ceramic materials; dual-retained restorations can also be produced in a metal-ceramic configuration, which offers high long-term success rates and well-documented mechanical reliability [30]. Moreover, these restorations can be fabricated using standard prefabricated abutments, expanding their clinical applicability.

Despite the increasing number of studies comparing screw- and cement-retained designs [33, 34], limited evidence is available on the mechanical performance of dual-retained metal-ceramic restorations fabricated on prefabricated abutments. This gap is clinically relevant, as both retention types continue to be used in daily practice, and their long-term success depends on factors such as passivity, torque stability, and fracture resistance. Consequently, the present study was conducted as a continuation of Part I [35] to comprehensively evaluate the mechanical behavior of these two retention designs under identical experimental conditions, thereby isolating the influence of prosthetic configuration on performance. The objective was to compare the passivity, ease of adjustment, abutment screw torque stability, fracture resistance, and failure pattern of dual-retained versus screw-retained metal-ceramic restorations before and after thermomechanical loading. The null hypothesis stated that no significant differences would be observed between the two groups.

Methods

The first part of the study [35] detailed the design and preparation of the study models, the fabrication of the restorations in both groups and initial examinations covering aspects such as connection alterations and rotational freedom. This study involved assessing other parameters, including the adjustment simplicity, passive fit, amount of torque loss before and after thermomechanical loading, fracture resistance, failure mode, and possible changes in the abutment screw. The necessary sample size for this study to compare pre- and post-intervention changes in screw-retained and dual-retained groups was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) following the repeated measures ANOVA model. Assuming an effect size of 0.8, a correlation coefficient of 0.8 between pre- and post-intervention measures, a Type I error rate of 5%, and a power of 90%, the estimated sample size was 18 specimens. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size was set at 20 samples (n = 10 per group).

A custom cobalt-chromium (Co-Cr) master block with mounted acrylic premolar and molar teeth was used, incorporating two implant sites prepared in removable implant blocks. Two groups of metal–ceramic restorations were fabricated (n = 10 each): dual-retained (cement- and screw-retained) using prefabricated cementable abutments, and screw-retained using UCLA abutments. In both groups, frameworks were CAD-designed from scanned abutments, incorporating a lingual collar, screw access, and 40 μm cement space. Patterns were 3D printed in resin, cast in Co-Cr alloy, and veneered with porcelain to standardized thicknesses (1 mm axial, 1.5 mm cuspal) using a silicone index for consistency. All laboratory steps were performed by a single blinded technician. The detailed description of the fabrication procedures has been reported in Part I of this study.

After completing the fabrication stage, the ease of adjustment of the two types of restorations was assessed within the master block; the fixture blocks were firmly secured in the center of their corresponding master block, using screws from the bottom surface. Subsequently, each specimen from the screw-retained group was positioned on its fixture, and necessary adjustments were made to the proximal contact areas to ensure complete seating. In the final torque, the proximal contacts were checked with dental floss to ensure the appropriate intensity. In G1, the abutments were initially torqued to 30 Ncm, followed by adjustment of the corresponding crowns until complete seating was achieved. The duration required to achieve appropriate proximal contact and the frequency of adjustments were recorded for each restoration. The G1 crowns were cemented to the abutments using a dual-cure self-adhesive resin cement (TheraCem, BISCO, Illinois, USA) [26]. A micro brush was utilized to eliminate any excess cement. The crowns were then clamped for 10 min with force equivalent to finger pressure, after which any remaining excess cement was removed [27]. The crown-abutment sets were re-evaluated to measure passivity (torque angle) in both groups. For this purpose, crown-abutment sets were first mounted onto the corresponding fixtures. The abutment screw for each specimen was manually torqued to 15 Ncm, and then, the extent of travel by the torque meter to reach 30 Ncm was recorded (Fig. 1).

The customized device designed to measure the torque angle representing the degree of passivity

New abutment screws were used for both groups to perform further evaluations. This replacement was necessary due to the frequent opening and closing of the screws during fabrication and prior assessments, which could impact torque measurements. Subsequently, crown-abutment sets in both groups were torqued to 30 Ncm on the master model using a digital torque meter, repeated after a 10-minute interval [36]. The torque required to open the screw (detorquing) was measured three hours after the second torque application as the pre-thermomechanical loading torque loss indicator [37]. After applying another 30 Ncm torque and reapplying it after a 10-minute interval, the screw holes were sealed with Teflon tape and composite resin (Solafil, Trentdent, UK). Then, the samples underwent thermocycling (Vafae Industrial Factory, Qom, Iran), consisting of 500 cycles lasting 30 s each between 5 and 55 degrees Celsius, with a 20-second interval [29]. Following thermocycling, mechanical loading was administered using a cyclic force of 500 N for 500,000 cycles, equivalent to approximately two years of clinical function, to simulate fatigue stress conditions representative of repetitive masticatory loading [38]. This mechanical loading was targeted at the center of the slightly flattened palatal cusp of the specimens, utilizing a chewing simulator (CS-4 SD Mechatronik, Feldkirchen, Germany). The torque needed to open the abutment screws was then measured to assess torque loss subsequent to thermomechanical loading. The torquing and detorquing processes were gauged using a digital torque meter (TQ-8800, Lutron Electronic Enterprise, Taipei, Taiwan), and the percentage of torque loss in the abutment screws before and after thermomechanical loading was determined through calculations based on the following three equations.

Subsequently, the abutment screws were retightened with a torque of 30 Ncm. The screw access channel was refilled with Teflon tape and composite resin, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fracture resistance was assessed using a Universal Testing Machine (Z050, ZwickRoell, Ulm, Germany) at a rate of 0.5 mm/min until reaching the fracture point [29]. This force was applied using a 4 mm diameter ball along the longitudinal axis of the implant to the center of the occlusal surface and in contact with the buccal and lingual cusp slopes [29]. Fracture resistance was determined based on the evident decline in the force application diagram.

The restoration’s failure mode was examined and documented under an optical stereomicroscope (30×) (SMZ25, Nikon, USA) [30]. These patterns were categorized into cohesive, adhesive, or a combination of both types (mixed), with cohesive fractures occurring within the ceramic material and adhesive fractures at the junction between the ceramic and metal components. Any changes in the screw threads, along with any deviations in the threads concerning the screw’s vertical axis, were assessed (30×). All measurements were conducted by a trained clinician (S.G.) with more than 10 years of clinical and investigative experience. The study parameters were summarized using mean ± standard deviation. Independent t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were employed to compare the passivity characteristics and fracture resistance between groups. To assess torque losses between and within groups, a generalized estimating equations (GEE) model was applied, followed by a least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc test. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (IBM Corp. 2016, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.), with p-values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The fracture resistance outcomes indicated a significant difference, with the dual-retained group demonstrating significantly higher fracture (P =.01). In both groups, all restorations demonstrated a mixed-type fracture pattern, involving fractures within the ceramic (cohesive) and at the interface between the ceramic and the metallic framework (adhesive). The fractures were primarily vertical, originating from the occlusal surface and propagating cervically along the cusp inclines in alignment with the loading vector. No horizontal fractures were observed. The detailed distribution of fracture locations is summarized in Table 3. In the dual-retained group, fractures occurred in six samples involving both the mesiopalatal and distopalatal cusps, in two samples affecting only the mesiopalatal cusp, and in two samples involving only the mesiobuccal cusp. Conversely, in the screw-retained group, fractures were observed in nine samples affecting both the mesiopalatal and distopalatal cusps, and in one sample affecting only the mesiopalatal cusp (Fig. 5). Additionally, examination of the abutment screws in all samples showed no signs of deformation, with the symmetry and shape of the screw threads remaining intact and sound.

Discussion

This study investigated the passivity, torque stability, and fracture resistance of screw-retained and dual-retained metal-ceramic implant-supported restorations, with results showing significant differences favoring dual-retained restorations in all parameters except for pre-thermomechanical loading torque loss, leading to a partial rejection of the null hypothesis.

Passivity, defined as the absence of resistance, is crucial in prosthetics, where it denotes the lack of mismatch between prosthetic components [39]. In implant-supported crowns, applying torque force to the abutment screw creates a retaining force, known as preload, through the compression of the screw threads against the implant threads [40]. Any misalignment between these threads can cause screw deformation, reducing the retaining forces and increasing stress on the abutment screw, thus elevating the risk of screw loosening and mechanical failure [41]. Passivity was evaluated in this study using two methods: first, by assessing the ease of adjustments, which is considered as an indicator of restoration quality, fabrication accuracy, and passivity in both implant- and tooth-based restorations [42–44]; and second, by employing the screw resistance test, originally introduced by Jemt [45]. Modified versions of this test have been used in various studies to evaluate the passivity of single- and multi-unit restorations [40, 46].

The results showed that dual-retained restorations required fewer adjustments and displayed a higher degree of passivity, indicating better initial fit and ease of achieving passivity. The adjustments required for restoration settling primarily involved minor modifications of the proximal contact areas to allow full seating of the restorations without resistance. These adjustments ensured optimal fit and passivity by eliminating premature contact interferences between adjacent crowns, thereby facilitating the accurate positioning of each restoration on its corresponding abutment. The average time required to adjust proximal contacts was significantly lower in dual-retained restorations (250 s–4 min and 10 s) compared to screw-retained restorations (655 s–10 min and 55 s). Furthermore, the dual-retained group needed fewer adjustments (7.5 times) than the screw-retained group (13.2 times). In screw-retained restorations, the integration of the abutment with the restoration means that any lack of passivity in the connection or interference from proximal contacts can hinder settlement, making adjustments more time-consuming and complex compared to dual-retained restorations [39]. Conversely, in dual-retained restorations, the abutment can be screwed separately on the implant, simplifying the adjustment process. Moreover, the intermediary cement layer in dual-retained restorations compensates for any minor misalignments, enhancing the overall passivity [47].

Furthermore, the torque angle, or the degree of rotation of the torque meter handle to reach from 15 to 30 Ncm, was significantly higher in the screw-retained group. The improved passivity seen in dual-retained restorations could be attributed to the fabrication process, which avoids repeated casting and firing cycles. These cycles can cause slight distortions in the abutment-restoration assembly [48], leading to poorer passive fit. As a result, in screw-retained restorations, the screw had to rotate more to sufficiently compress the contact surfaces between the screw and the implant, achieving the necessary preload. This lack of passivity could further reduce the initial torque of the abutment screw in the screw-retained group during the settling effect process. No similar studies were found comparing passivity between different types of implant restorations.

One of the most common problems that may occur for implant-supported restorations is abutment screw loosening [49, 50]. The occurrence of this event is reported to be 3.5% in the first year after implant loading and 7.1–8.5% after five years of follow-up [51]. Factors such as the type of implant-abutment connection, the shape and material of the abutment, the design and material of the abutment screw, the type of restoration, parafunctional habits, the width of the occlusal table, off-center forces, and the amount of initial abutment screw torque play a role in creating this problem [34, 52]. The accuracy of component fabrication has been recognized as a factor that can affect the preload and stability of the prosthetic connection [53]. Loosening of the abutment screw can lead to fracture of the abutment screw and even the implant itself. On the other hand, the micro-gap resulting from it can cause microleakage at the implant-abutment interface and, consequently, biological problems such as soft tissue infection and marginal bone resorption [51, 52]. Torque loss measurements in the present study indicated no significant difference in abutment screw torque loss between the groups before thermomechanical loading. However, after thermomechanical loading, a significant difference in torque loss was observed. To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have directly compared torque loss between dual-retained and screw-retained restorations; most existing studies focused on cemented versus screw-retained restorations [11, 37, 54]. Comparisons were drawn with these studies, suggesting that in the dual-retained group, factors like the access hole to the abutment screw and permanent cement’s role in force transfer during thermomechanical loading might have been overlooked. Kano et al.‘s study found a significantly higher torque loss after casting in screw-retained restorations (18.4%) compared to cemented ones (7.7%) [11]. Similarly, Kim and Shin’s study reported higher torque loss in screw-retained restorations, though the differences were not significant, with initial torque loss at 39.61% for cemented and 44.56% for screw-retained restorations, increasing slightly after thermomechanical loading [37]. Similar studies have also reported varying ranges of torque loss [54, 55]. The initial torque loss is primarily due to the settling effect, where contact surfaces of the abutment, screw, and implant gradually smooth out under torque, reducing preload force. Factors such as surface roughness, stiffness, and preload force magnitude influence this effect [56]. Consistently, studies report higher torque loss in screw-retained restorations compared to cemented or dual-retained ones, mainly due to the settling effect, although in this study, no significant difference was observed before thermomechanical loading. Additionally, casting procedures have been reported to reduce detorque values, even in pre-machined cast abutments. This effect may result from the surface roughness and irregularities produced during casting, which contribute to increased embedment relaxation and a greater loss of preload [11, 55]. Kim and Shin’s findings [37], similar to those of this study, showed that torque loss from thermomechanical loading was less impactful than initial torque loss due to settling. In this study, the mean initial and post-loading torque loss were 24.02% and 16.18% for the dual- and 24.76% and 20.01% for the screw-retained group. The greater compatibility in the implant-abutment connection area, as in the dual-retained group, appears to be one of the most effective factors in reducing abutment screw torque loss and delaying screw loosening [48, 57]. The intermediary cement layer in dual-retained restorations also plays a key role in maintaining torque stability. Screw-retained restorations, on the other hand, do not benefit from this cushioning effect, which can lead to increased micro-movements at the screw-abutment interface, especially under repeated loading conditions [11].

The fracture resistance is one of the vital functions of restorative materials for long-term success. The factors, including the elastic modulus, the luting materials, the restoration design, the surface characteristics as well as the existing residual stress of the components, are all involved in the fracture tolerance [19, 58]. The possible weakening of the occlusal surface due to the lost integrity by screw hole and some reduction of the sub-porcelain frame support may increase the risk of fracture [27–30]. Despite this, some researchers have demonstrated the opposite effect [19, 22, 26, 59], since it is believed that filling the access channel with composite resin can stabilize the ceramic layer [60]. This study demonstrated that the fracture resistance was higher in dual-retained restorations, with mixed fracture modes observed in both groups. The higher resistance to fracture in dual-retained restorations is likely due to the supportive function of the intermediary cement layer, especially around the screw access holes [31, 48]. The absence of thermal stresses during fabrication further preserves the strength of dual-retained restorations, enhancing their resistance to fracture. Various studies have compared the failure resistance of cemented and screw-retained restorations [19, 27, 28, 31, 59], but research on dual-retained restorations is limited [26, 29]. Malpartida-Carrillo et al. [26] reported similar results using a comparable procedure, with the key difference being the absence of thermomechanical loading before testing failure resistance. This likely explains the overall higher mean failure resistance observed in their study (2508 N for dual and 2125 N for screw-retained) compared to the present study (1953 N and 1357 N, respectively). In contrast to the current study, Al-Omari et al. found no significant difference in failure resistance between dual- and screw-retained restorations, possibly due to the use of zinc phosphate cement and not filling the abutment screw access hole [29]. In this study, the mean failure resistance of screw-retained restorations exceeded the maximum human bite force in the molar region (880 N) [61]. Thus, despite the differences with dual-retained restorations, the strength of screw-retained restorations appears to be within the clinically acceptable range.

Generally, it has been reported that veneer ceramic fractures are more often cohesive and occur more frequently in implant-supported crowns than in tooth-supported ones [40]. Ceramic fractures do not necessarily mean restoration failure, they nonetheless represent a costly and time-consuming repair process, constituting an unwelcome clinical problem [30]. Compressive forces and fatigue-inducing forces in implant restorations can damage not only the ceramic but also underlying structures such as screws, connections, and fixtures [62]. According to the results of this study, in both groups, all restorations exhibited mixed-type failure, consistent with Malpartida-Carrillo et al.’s findings [26]. In the present study, most failures occurred in the mesiopalatal cusp—six samples in the dual-retained group and nine in the screw-retained group—likely due to the application of cyclic force to this area during thermomechanical loading. The type of restoration failure appears to be more influenced by adherence to design and fabrication principles during laboratory procedures than by the nature of the restorations themselves. Notably, all failures in both groups were repairable. Furthermore, the abutment screws in both groups showed no significant axial changes or thread damage. This can be attributed to the vertical direction of the force and the anti-rotation features present in both restoration types. Additionally, this could be supported by the fact that, according to the manufacturer of the system used in this study, the connector screws are made from Grade 5 Titanium alloy (Ti-6Al-4 V), composed of 90% titanium, 6% aluminum, and 4% vanadium. The addition of aluminum and vanadium enhances the screws’ flexural and bending capacity, reducing the risk of fracture [63].

The limitations of this study include its in vitro nature, which does not fully replicate the intraoral environment. Factors such as patient-specific occlusal forces, parafunctional habits, and biological conditions could alter the clinical performance of the restorations. Additionally, while the sample size was adequate for the parameters studied, expanding future research to include a larger and more diverse sample population would help to validate these findings. The study focused solely on metal-ceramic restorations, and similar research on other material types would provide a broader understanding of the mechanical behaviors of these restorative designs. Future studies should explore long-term clinical outcomes, particularly under varied functional loads and patient conditions, to further elucidate the mechanical advantages of dual-retained restorations. Furthermore, the restorations in this study were fabricated using conventional casting procedures to ensure methodological standardization between groups and to focus exclusively on the effect of the retention design. Although digital manufacturing systems are progressively replacing the analogue workflow, the mechanical principles evaluated are independent of the fabrication method and remain clinically relevant. Nonetheless, differences in fit precision and microstructural uniformity achieved through digital techniques could influence the absolute values of these parameters [64–66], warranting further studies using digitally fabricated restorations. Additionally, the present findings may also support the transition toward digital fabrication techniques to overcome some drawbacks of conventional approaches, such as the repeated furnace exposures that abutments undergo during layering and esthetic modifications. These thermal cycles can induce minor distortions or changes in material properties. However, it should be noted that in certain digitally fabricated restorations—particularly those requiring partial veneering, deep staining, or other esthetic enhancements to harmonize with adjacent teeth—similar firing procedures may still be necessary, and thus this limitation may persist irrespective of the manufacturing workflow.
