Reemergence of Streptococcus pyogenes Infections in a Large Italian Hospital: A déjà vu from past Years
Authors: Flavio De Maio, Marilena La Sorda, Barbara Fiori, Delia Mercedes Bianco, Giulia Santarelli, Roberto Rosato, Tiziana D’Inzeo, Brunella Posteraro, Maurizio Sanguinetti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAt the end of 2022 and in the following months, an increase in the incidence of Streptococcus pyogenes infections was observed in many European countries that was simultaneously accompanying to enhance of invasive infections (iGAS). We have showed a risen trend of S. pyogenes infections among preschoolers after the pandemic event. A thorough epidemiological investigation of both paediatric and adult samples positive for S. pyogenes indicate a more complex scenario leading to need of important improvement in surveillance programs.Keywords: Streptococcus pyogenes, iGAS, Epidemiology

Correspondence

The increase in the prevalence of Streptococcus pyogenes (GAS) infections in Italy reflects the trends observed in other European countries and worldwide [1–3]. Unfortunately, data available from current surveillance programmes fail to elucidate this epidemiological pattern. It is widely held that the “immune debt”, (linked to non-pharmaceutical interventions used during the COVID-19 pandemic) the change in the circulation of respiratory pathogens and the emergence of some S. pyogenes emm types may explain this trend [4–6]. Due to the lack of a national surveillance system for GAS infection, we have poor and contrasting evidence, especially in the pre pandemic period, and only a universal deep whole genome sequencing approach may fully elucidate the current scenario [7].

To contribute to the understanding of GAS epidemiology, we have retrospectively investigated GAS infections from 2016 to 2023 in our tertiary centre (Fondazione Policlinico Universitario Agostino Gemelli - IRCCS, Rome). Positive specimens for S. pyogenes were stratified by age (infants-toddlers, aged 0–2 years; preschoolers, aged 3–5 years; middle childhood people, MC 1: aged 6–8 and MC 2: 9–11 years; young teens, aged 12–15; teenagers, aged 16–18; adults, aged > 18–64 and elders aged > 64) and type of infection. Invasive GAS (iGAS) cases were defined by isolation of GAS by culture or molecular methods from a normally sterile site, according to UKHSA guidelines. Other GAS infections were furtherly categorised into respiratory (rGAS) and other GAS (oGAS) infections. A total of 789 positive samples were collected corresponding to 731 patients from 2016 to 2023. Positive distribution did not significantly change in these years, except for 2016 and the pandemic period which accounted for the lowest numbers of positive samples (total positives were: 96 in 2016, 165 in 2017, 139 in 2018, 121 in 2019, 51 in 2020, 22 in 2021, 31 in 2022 and 164 in 2023). GAS infection seasonality, that was previously observed in paediatric cohorts both by ours and international groups was confirmed [1, 8, 9] (Fig. 1a). In 2023, GAS infections were mostly concentrated in the spring, while the number of positive remained comparable with pre-pandemic periods. At least in our geographical region, GAS infections had marked seasonal pattern that cyclically repeated itself. Interestingly it seems like the trend observed in 2023 may also be detected in the 2016–2017.

In 2023, rGAS infection appeared significantly associated to preschoolers and MC 1 accounted for about 20% of the total positives (Fig. 1b). These data highlight how GAS infections maintained comparable patterns in the studied years, except for the period from 2020 to 2022, when direct and indirect effects of the COVID-19 pandemic may have had a significant impact. Interestingly, there was no increase in the relative incidence of iGAS in the last years (2016: 14/96, 14.6%; 2017: 14/165, 8.5%; 2018: 20/139, 14.4%; 2019: 9/121, 7.4%; 2020: 9/51 17.6%, 2021: 5/22, 22.7%; 2022: 5/31, 16.1%; 2023: 21/164, 12.8%). During the pandemic, the absolute number of iGAS cases was significantly lower and invasive disease appeared to mostly affect individuals over 64, in fact, while in other years only 5% of cases affected the elders, the percentage doubled in this period 2020: 13.7%, 2021: 13.6% and 2022: 9.7%) (Fig. 1b). Interestingly, differently from other countries we did not notice an increase in iGAS cases in 2023.

Repeated positive samples were also investigated. Interestingly, many more repeated samples were carried out in the period 2016–2019, compared with following years (Fig. 1c). Most repeated samples in our data are from the same anatomical niche. Unfortunately, we are unable to distinguish whether repeated exams correspond to post-treatment follow up and consequently are indicating antibiotic failure, or if, when spaced in time, they are indicating strain persistence or a new infection. However, many repeated samples are performed in a narrow time range suggesting the need of more accurate guidelines. In this context, the absence of a concrete and standardized S. pyogenes surveillance program in Italy prevents the identification of genotypically diverse S. pyogenes isolates and the association of risk factors linked to the infected individuals [10].

Thus, it is challenging to compare our data to European ones, even considering epidemiological data from centres with a similar catchment area as some countries list iGAS as a notifiable disease. There is an impelling need for globally standardized surveillance programs to prevent the spread of S. pyogenes, and other human pathogens. Whole genome sequencing and genome comparison analysis of both paediatric and adult rGAS and iGAS strains may be an initial hint to acquire information on community circulating strains and to rapidly detect more virulent strains. These data together with an accurate systematic analysis may provide important insights about GAS of the last decade and of the foreseeable future.
