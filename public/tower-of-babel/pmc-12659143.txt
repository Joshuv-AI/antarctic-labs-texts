Key components of developing midwifery preceptors: a pathway to strengthen midwifery education and care in low resource settings
Authors: Brittney J. van de Water, Julie Mann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Strengthening midwifery education and the quality of midwifery care is one of the most impactful interventions to address maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality. In this perspective, we describe nine key components that make for an effective preceptor program that should be kept to ensure high fidelity and to support quality outcomes. A preceptor is an experienced healthcare provider who mentors students through structured experiences in the clinical setting to gain experience and apply their knowledge and skills in a supervised setting. For each of the nine key components, we delve into examples from a novel yearlong low-dose, high-frequency cohort preceptor program we developed to educate practicing midwives to become competent and confident preceptors of midwifery students in Sierra Leone. These nine key components include time, low cost, low-dose high frequency format, quality over quantity in cohorts, having a clinical component, partnership buy-in, ensuring an enabling learning environment and facilitators who can model ideal preceptor behavior, the ability to obtain accreditation for long-term sustainability, and leadership. Using these key components in other midwifery-led interventions may also prove beneficial. Finally, building in sustainability and having continuous measurement built into a program are also essential and should be considered when developing a new model of care or intervention. Ensuring midwives are equipped with quality education and prepared to provide quality care is essential to improving maternal health services for vulnerable populations globally.

The critical need for developing the role of a midwife preceptor

Midwives can deliver approximately 90% of essential Sexual Reproductive Maternal Newborn Adolescent Health (SRMNAH) interventions across the life course as members of an integrated healthcare system [1]. Yet there is a shortage of nearly 1 million midwives globally, with sub-Saharan Africa experiencing the greatest shortage, and the highest burden of maternal and neonatal mortality [2, 3]. Multiple estimates show that if universal coverage of midwifery-delivered care were achieved, 65 to 80% of maternal and neonatal deaths and stillbirths could be averted [4–6]. Educating midwives to international standards is one of the most impactful and cost-effective investments [7].

Investment in midwifery education and training is a significant challenge. According to the International Confederation of Midwives (ICM) Global Standards for Midwifery Education, 60% of a midwifery student’s education should occur in the clinical setting; yet, there is a lack of access to the clinical setting and a lack of clinical teachers for students [1, 8]. The World Health Organization (WHO) published Strengthening the Quality of Midwifery Education to Achieve Universal Health Care 2030 and identified that midwifery schools and students lacked critical access to clinical settings to support competency-based education [9]. The State of the World’s Midwifery Report (SOWM) 2021 found similar challenges in midwifery education outlining a lack of investment in educators, limited skills and knowledge in contemporary teaching and learning, and inadequate “hands-on” experience for students as key barriers to midwifery education [1].

Creating a strong cohort of midwifery preceptors in the clinical setting is one way to address this challenge in midwifery education and eventually, in maternal and neonatal mortality. A preceptor is an “experienced, actively practicing healthcare provider who provides structured opportunities for students to gain experience in the clinical setting” [10]. Their goal is to help students achieve competence and confidence in applying new knowledge and skills in a supervised setting, in part by providing continual feedback and using techniques such as active listening, questioning, positive feedback, and problem-solving to help create a positive learning climate [10]. Competent and confident midwives are essential to reducing maternal mortality. Midwifery-led models of care lead to fewer interventions and more satisfied care with at least comparable adverse outcomes for women and infants compared to other models of care [11].

Clinical teaching is very different from classroom teaching and requires a unique skill set that is both strong in clinical knowledge and bedside teaching to ensure competency. Preceptor programs have shown effective ways to promote critical thinking and advance learning of clinical concepts as well as improving patient outcomes [12–15].

Traditionally in midwifery, clinically practicing midwives have been expected to be preceptors. However, not all midwives desire to teach nor are they equipped with the skills to teach. Additionally, midwives are expected to teach students in addition to carrying a full clinical load of patients. When a midwife in the clinical setting has a student, they are often not compensated for their teaching work, and they are not given a reduced clinical workload in order to have time to teach at the bedside [16]. Currently, the ICM recommends that at least 60% of a midwifery curriculum is composed of clinical hours, while 40% is classroom-based. This necessitates a large number of clinically based midwives who are competent and confident to create an enabling learning environment to teach students when they arrive in the clinical setting [17, 18]. We hypothesize that a well-developed role and preceptor program, implemented in an enabling learning environment, will result in improved (1) preceptor, (2) student, and (3) patient outcomes over time.

A novel model for improving quality of midwifery education and care in low resource settings

In response to the critical need to have competent and confident midwifery preceptors to teach the next generation of midwives, we developed a novel low-dose, high-frequency training model for use in sub-Saharan Africa where there is a large burden of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, and a vast shortage of healthcare providers at the bedside [19, 20]. We are two North Americans—JM is a certified nurse-midwife with a Master’s in Public Health with 20 years practicing as a midwife and has healthcare leadership experience in both high- and low-resourced settings. BvdW is a pediatric nurse practitioner and nurse scientist with 15 years of clinical experience primarily in the US and 10 years of research experience primarily in sub-Saharan Africa. Both authors work part-time for the global health non-governmental organization (NGO), Seed Global Health, which invests in health workers and health systems in Malawi, Uganda, Sierra Leone, and Zambia. We co-developed this program for Seed Global Health with significant input and support from Seed country teams, educators, and partners, in its development, implementation, measurement, and adaptation.

Ample evidence has shown that repetitive educational interventions that use multiple teaching methods, accompanied by mentorship and supportive supervision result in greater clinical performance, knowledge and skill retention, and improved health outcomes compared to traditional once-off trainings [15, 21–25]. This novel midwifery precepting program, the Seed Midwifery Preceptor Program, is a year-long, four-step program to improve partnership building and program development (Step 1), provide an evidence-based course to expand competency and confidence in precepting students (Step 2), select preceptors to become train-the-trainers (ToTs) or facilitators (Step 3) and, secure accreditation for the program and support long-term sustainability (Step 4). Seed Global Health is an international nongovernmental organization that partners with ministries of health, health professional schools, hospitals, and clinics to educate and train doctors, nurses, and midwives to deliver high-quality health services and save lives [26]. Since 2019, Seed has worked in Sierra Leone, at the invitation of the Office of the Vice President, and partnered to strengthen quality services for primary care and maternal and child health, starting with midwifery education and practice [26]. Nine key components to sustain program fidelity

There is a limited but important body of literature and efforts aimed at bridging the critical gap between theory and practice in midwifery education and reducing maternal and newborn mortality. One notable program is the U.S. President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief, Nurse Education Partnership Initiative (NEPI), that launched clinical preceptor programs in Zambia and Malawi in 2013 [29]. In Zambia, they created a 1-year diploma preceptor program and in Malawi they developed a 6-week certificate preceptor program. Although laudable in their efforts to address this need, there is little published evidence of the effectiveness and sustainability of these NEPI programs and there is even less knowledge about how to develop and sustain such a program. One integrative review describes challenges and solutions in sub-Saharan Africa in nursing education—many of which apply and overlap with midwifery—including reform to increase the capacity of educators and mentors in order to improve the quality, quantity and relevance of nursing education, among other solutions [30].

In the first year of implementing the Seed Midwifery Preceptor Program, many lessons were learned that we feel are critical to successfully implementing and sustaining a clinical preceptor program [19]. Below, we outline nine key components and strategies that have made this program unique and highlight aspects that have been critical to its early success. As we have been approached to replicate this program, expand to other sites, and scale up, we detail some aspects of fidelity that could impact outcomes across settings, years, and scope as the program expands. Below, we provide an overview and rationale for each of the nine components, followed by a specific example within our program to exemplify how we integrated that component, detailing how it strengthened and created synergy between components to create an effective program in Sierra Leone, a low-resource context with few midwives and high maternal and neonatal mortality.

Time—the biggest investment

It is natural to desire a quick fix to every problem; however, rarely is the quick fix effective or sustainable. Therefore, the biggest investment one must make to implement a program is time. In a 2007 article by Ericsson et al., they describe that outstanding performance is the product of years of deliberate practice and coaching, not of innate talent or skill [31]. To become an expert, it takes hours, months, and years of intensive study, devoted instructors, and support. Time is important both for the development of a program and for the delivery of a program. Ensuring materials are accurate, appropriate, and ready to be distributed and taught helps the program run smoothly. Finally, giving learners time between sessions can enhance the learning process and aid in long-term memory and retention of knowledge [32].

As outlined in Fig. 1, this Seed Midwifery Preceptor Program, implemented in Sierra Leone at two sites, is 1 year and divided into four steps. Improving partnerships (Step 1) and Program Sustainability (Step 4) are the most time intensive only in the first year of implementation at a site as they require developing relationships with clinical and academic sites and establishing buy-in from all stakeholders. Steps 1 and 4 are ongoing throughout the first year but once the program is established, then the core of the program truly just consists, year after year, of Step 2, the 6-month preceptor course, and then Step 3, the 4-week training of trainers. Of note, we developed the program to be a 5-year program that cycles through five cohorts. Sustainability was built into the program (described in detail below in Number 7) with the goal of graduating a cohort of preceptors five years in a row.

Many interested stakeholders who are looking to replicate this program have asked if they can shorten the preceptor program. We strongly recommend against shortening this program, and most programs where complex concepts—like midwifery skills—are taught take time for students to learn, practice, and master with supportive mentorship. First, the effectiveness of our program in increasing clinical and teaching competency has been evaluated using the detailed Step 2 core curriculum and this takes 6 months to implement. Also, the pedagogical approach of low-dose, high frequency education and the incorporation of a supervised and mentored clinical component to the course were critical to participant success [20]. Secondly, the other steps of the program cannot be ignored as they are the foundation for building successful implementation (Step 1) and sustainability of the program (Steps 3 and 4).

Within budget and/or cost-effective program

Cost is a major factor when considering the initiation of any program. Ensuring the program stays within budget is critical so funding does not run out mid-program. Programs that take place within the institution are often cheaper than programs with high hotel fees for conference space rental, extensive travel costs to bring in facilitators and partners to one central location, and per diems for participants while traveling away from home. Integrating the program into the participants’ places of work to ensure a seamless transition of skills from classroom to bedside is also a benefit. An incremental cost and cost-effectiveness of a low-dose, high-frequency program in Ghana and Uganda have shown low-dose, high-frequency programs can be cost efficient and cost-effective [33, 34].

Given our program was woven into existing partnerships, added costs were relatively minimal. However, had the partnership not already been in existence, costs would have been substantially higher. The main additional costs included: the preceptor program launch, opening 2-day retreat, midpoint retreat, and graduation. Participants were provided transportation and meals for each of these special and cohort-building events. For those who participated in qualitative interviews, they were provided a nominal stipend for their internet data usage. Other expenses included the purchase of a scanner/printer, local transportation reimbursements for participants and publication fees (optional, non-program related). In the first year, only existing Seed educators facilitated the program, and their salaries were already covered by Seed Global Health. Additionally, salaries were already covered for country and global Seed staff as well. In year two and in future years, ToTs will be paid minimal stipends for their implementation of Step 2 of the program. Their expected time commitment is approximately 10 h per week during the intensive 24 weeks of program skill building and clinical mentorship.

This innovative model intentionally did not pay or provide stipends for participants, the individuals receiving the training, who are becoming the preceptors. This is counter to most training programs in sub-Saharan Africa; however, in an effort to take away the idea that people should be paid for their time to learn a new skill or partake in a program—and frankly, because this was a pilot and we did not have funding for it in the beginning nor did we think it would be sustainable to provide stipends—we did not pay participants. This has shown itself to be a double-edged sword. First, in qualitative interviews with participants, most wish they had been paid. Yet, we believe not paying participants supported the type of participants who applied. It was clear from the application process that there would be no financial incentive; therefore, only individuals who were intrinsically motivated applied [35]. Lack of financial incentive also has provided more flexibility and sustainability to the program, with it being more likely to be maintained or scaled by government. A potential extrinsic motivation was that we did inform participants about the long-term vision of creating a cohort of clinical preceptors (Step 4). This vision incorporates accreditation nationally creating a cadre of clinical preceptors, recognized and reimbursed by the government. This promise for potential professional growth and recognition was also discussed in interviews and likely was motivating.

Low-dose, high-frequency, multi-method education at clinical sites

Traditionally, interventions to increase knowledge and improve clinical care have focused on one-time sessions lasting anywhere from a few days to a week. These interventions are often lacking in any follow-up or supportive mentorship. They also require removal of healthcare workers from clinical care for extended periods of time. In a systematic review of interventions to improve health care provider performance in low- and middle-income countries, this traditional approach has shown low effectiveness [36].

Targeted, repetitive interventions using multiple techniques accompanied by mentorship or supportive supervision at clinical sites have been shown to result in greater clinical performance, knowledge and skills retention, and health outcomes than single, isolated interventions [15, 21–23, 37–39]. Such targeted and repetitive interventions are often called low-dose, high-frequency interventions. Low-dose, high-frequency education also aligns with competency-based learning in healthcare. In competency-based education, objectives are centered around the learner, learning is context-driven, dynamic, and progressively builds over time [40]. This is the same approach that low-dose, high-frequency interventions use in that it is embedded in the learner’s environment, their clinical sites. It is interactive, drawing on the learner’s needs, and is progressive extending over a long period of time.

This Seed Preceptor Program employs weekly, hour-long learning sessions accompanied by ongoing clinical mentorship and evaluation in the clinical environment. We believe it is a critical component in achieving competent clinicians and preceptors. Although this preceptor program is defined as “a year-long program,” the intensive Step 2 (6-month course) that the preceptor participants are involved in is less than the full year. These 6 months are comprised primarily of weekly in-person 1-to-2-h sessions (some didactic, some hands-on skills lab, some case-based) for 24 weeks, and followed by 4–6 weeks of intensive near-daily mentorship in the preceptor’s own clinical setting. During this time, there is observation, skills checks, and opportunity for 1:1 interaction between the program facilitator and preceptor and focus on ICM essential competencies for midwifery practice [41]. There is a need for significant commitment from participants; however, in initial feedback and interviews, preceptors, facilitators, and stakeholders overwhelmingly reported that the program was worth the time and effort [20]. As a few participants noted, midwifery precepting is a complex skill to learn; therefore, it requires dedication to become a competent and confident midwifery preceptor and cannot be learned in a simple 3-day training away at a conference center.

Ensure strong, evidenced-based clinical competency of preceptors

Quality of care is a significant factor when it comes to improving the maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality in low- and middle-income countries [42]. The State of the World’s Midwifery Report cited limited numbers of qualified clinical educators as a barrier to improving the sexual, reproductive, maternal, newborn healthcare workforce [1]. Insufficient in-service training has been shown to be a significant barrier to the provision of quality emergency obstetric and newborn care (EmONC) [43–45]. Therefore, midwifery providers need to build competency in the management and recognition of pregnancy complications. Any efforts aimed at increasing the midwifery workforce by educating more midwives must ensure that the healthcare professionals training the next generation of midwives have strong, evidence-based clinical competency.

The needs assessment performed by Seed Global Health prior to the development of this program partnership confirmed knowledge and skills gaps in clinical staff [46]. Therefore, a heavy emphasis on clinical competency of preceptors is built into this program, aligned with global midwifery recommendations. In fact, 50% of Step 2 of the preceptor course is aimed at re-enforcing the clinical knowledge, skills, and attitudes of the preceptors so that they can become competent and confident clinical experts capable of passing down their expertise to students.

Strong program facilitators continuously modeling an enabling learning environment

A strong facilitator is critical to the success of any program. An effective facilitator leads a cohort with strong clinical skills, robust teaching skills, and excellent listening and organizational skills. A facilitator must possess high emotional intelligence and be adaptable to the needs of the cohort. They must be able to anticipate the potential hiccups or unforeseen disruptions to the program, especially in a low-resource setting (i.e., general elections, holidays, strikes, unreliable electricity) [47]. Finally—and not to be overlooked—the facilitator must have good technology skills and be savvy with PowerPoint lectures, utilize Excel for tracking participant skill attainment, be responsive on email and group messaging systems, be able to upload and download documents, and manage and share folders on shared drives with a wider team for good communication and organization of the program. Facilitation goes beyond clinical and teaching skills and truly encompasses ushering through a cohort of midwives to become preceptors, educators, or skilled clinicians. Behavior modeling of how best to facilitate group discussions, to give an interactive lecture, and provide constructive feedback in a thoughtful way can mean the difference between a participant hearing they “did well” versus critically evaluating which aspect of the skill they did well and thinking through how they could evaluate that same skill in their future students [48].

Having an objective and rigorous evaluation process for choosing facilitators is important. In our program, Seed educators go through an extensive application and interview process. Seed educators fill out an application, answer essay questions, and have two interviews with a clinical director in their area of expertise, followed by an interview with a country director and partner organization to ensure a good fit between the educators’ skill set and the desired role they will complete. For ToTs in this program, there is also an application and interview process to maintain rigorous standards. Finalists are chosen by an objective rubric and a committee.

Quality over quantity of preceptors in this cohort model

Investing in quality and quantity is imperative to address the global maternal health crisis [1, 7]. A small, intimate learning environment of cohorts of 10 individuals was critical to program success. The preceptor program was adapted for use in pediatric critical care in Malawi, and the cohort was twice as large. As such, 20 preceptors were still a small enough cohort to have effective and significantly improved outcomes [25]. However, our model recommends having a 5:1 preceptor to facilitator ratio to keep the demands on the facilitator manageable, and the overall cohort size intimate, aligned with many cohort-based education principles including community, accountability, interaction, and impact [49]. This cohort model has proven successful in nursing and healthcare leadership globally and is rising in popularity due to its success and participant feedback [50–53]. Sustaining the quality of program components is critical in order to eventually lead to a change in both quality and quantity of competent and confident midwives to deliver lifesaving care to mothers and newborns.

Over the course of 5 years, this Seed Midwifery Preceptor Program plans to educate at least 220 preceptors, 38 ToTs, with over 8800 students receiving instruction from preceptors who went through this program. Although each cohort is kept small to prioritize quality of preceptors—through a rigorous and objective application process, a small and intimate learning environment, and building collaborative peer education through low-dose, high-frequency course meetings with facilitators—eventually, this will have an impact on the quantity of midwives trained. This aligns with the WHO’s policy to invest in quantity and quality in midwifery through (1) health workforce planning, management, and regulation and in the work environment, (2) midwife-led improvements to SRMNAH service delivery, (3) high-quality education and training of midwives, and (4) midwifery leadership and governance (7).

Sustainability through embedding the program in existing systems

Sustainability of any intervention is always a challenge especially in the context of strengthening capacity in global health [54–56]. Key barriers to sustainability in this setting are reliance on outside implementing partners (like NGOs) rather than local capacity implementation, limited funding/resources, lack of built-in mechanisms to address leadership, and failure to integrate interventions to local context [54, 56]. Another significant barrier is failure to tailor technical assistance to enable local stakeholders to adapt and lead the intervention [54].

Although Seed Global Health, an outside implementing partner, has developed the midwifery preceptor program and is funding initial implementation, we intentionally embedded steps into the program to address many of these sustainability challenges. Step 1 of the program, building partnerships, is aimed at bringing together the midwifery schools, clinical sites, and Seed to establish communication and collaboration to jointly adapt and co-lead the program together. Step 3 of the program, ToTs, trains local midwives and midwifery faculty at academic institutions to then implement and lead the program each year. Over a 5-year period, the program is designed to be fully run by ToTs and midwifery faculty without any assistance from the Seed Midwifery Educators.

Finally, Step 4 of the program, sustainability through accreditation and recognition of the program and preceptors, is perhaps most critical. This step involves continuing engagement with the midwifery schools, the Ministry of Health and the Nursing and Midwifery Council to embed the program into existing systems to become financially solvent and an integrated part of the national midwifery education system. The objectives in this step are to achieve accreditation of the program as a diploma program in midwifery schools. Once accreditation is achieved, midwifery faculty who have become ToTs begin to run the program, as mentioned above. Then, the next objective is to appeal to the Nursing and Midwifery Council to recognize the advanced training and diplomas of these midwifery clinical preceptors and elevate them as a separate cadre in the professional ladder with associated title and compensation. Once all of this is achieved, then the midwifery schools, which now implement and lead the program, can start charging a small fee for individuals to achieve this diploma, creating financial viability of the program.

Importance of partnership buy-in and communication

New programs benefit greatly from existing partnerships. The WHO African Region has a significant shortage of health workers and the highest burden of disease globally [57]. Therefore, clinicians and faculty often struggle to provide respectful and competent care to patients, educate students, and perform required administrative duties. Developing materials and having bandwidth to create and adapt tools, data tracking systems, and implementing a novel program—that is likely not required for school accreditation—often fall to the wayside. However, when partnered with an NGO with resources—time, human capacity, and technical expertise—programs become more feasible to develop, implement, evaluate, and maintain. Similarly, if in a silo, NGOs struggle to find eligible participants to enroll and may lack the credibility or network with schools and hospitals with whom to integrate. Therefore, existing mutual respect and partnership—collaboration—are integral for program success.

For this Midwifery Preceptor Program, Seed started conversations early about the potential of a novel project and ensuring it is aligned with partner and government priorities. The results of the needs assessment Seed and the government of Sierra Leone conducted in 2021 set the stage for developing this preceptor program [46]. Finding organic yet evidence-based and context-appropriate solutions and ensuring transparent and continuous communication are critical to success.

Additionally, close communication and partnership buy-in have aided in addressing challenges as the program is being implemented. For example, Seed and our partners have been able to advocate with the Ministry of Health for developing a new cadre of midwife preceptors to be recognized and compensated after participants and stakeholders voiced this need. After receiving buy-in from hospitals and developing closed-loop communication channels, participants have been provided more protected time from their institutions (with hospital administrators and ward managers) to partake in this program.

Leadership

Leadership is critical for educational and training programs, both in their development and implementation. Leaders are often defined by their actions, not always by their position of authority [58]. Yet, gaining support from individuals with authority can facilitate the success of a program. Providing midwives with skills in leadership development such as communication and collaboration, continued professional development, resource utilization, ethics in healthcare, leadership styles, along with mentorship can support the development of the next generation of midwifery leaders [58, 59].

In this preceptor program, there are many elements of leadership. Garnering partnership buy-in from stakeholders including ministers from the Ministry of Health; leadership from program developers in stewarding this program from vision to actualization; the leadership from facilitators and implementors in the day-to-day modeling of competent midwifery care; and in the small details ensuring continuous monitoring and evaluation. Finally, gaining accreditation from the nurses’ and midwives’ council in Sierra Leone has been a tremendous endeavor, and this is a testament to both leadership and partnership.

Conclusions

There are myriad ways in which to improve midwifery clinical education in low-resource settings. This innovative low-dose, high-frequency cohort precepting program to increase clinical competence and precepting confidence is one model. Ensuring nine key components are retained will ensure higher fidelity to this effective program. These key components include time, cost-effective, a low-dose high-frequency format, prioritizing quality over quantity of preceptors in cohorts, incorporating an evidenced-based, clinical skill-building component, ensuring partnership buy-in, supporting facilitators who model exceptional preceptor behavior, and developing a pathway for program accreditation and long-term sustainability, and leadership. In our program, we built in these nine core components and maintained high fidelity to them with well-defined measures—defined in our program manual—in the first 2 years of program implementation. Thus far, this program has shown positive results. Similar tools, metrics, and materials could be adapted to other areas of midwifery education following these general principles to teach the next generation of clinicians with high quality of skills to improve outcomes for underserved maternal and newborn populations.
