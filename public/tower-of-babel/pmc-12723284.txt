MRI-DTI contributes to evaluating diffuse neural injury following repetitive mild traumatic brain injury
Authors: Meimei Li, Yan Wang, Zihan Zhang, Yuanyuan Gao, Fanglian Chen, Ping Lei, Xintong Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryRepetitive mild traumatic brain injury (rmTBI) is an important risk factor for neurodegeneration, with early blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption playing a key role. Clinical outcome heterogeneity may stem from region-specific disparities in white matter integrity and metabolic homeostasis linked to BBB permeability. Using a controlled cortical impact mouse model, we observed marked BBB disruption (Evans blue leakage, downregulation of ZO-1/claudin-5), accompanied by microglial activation and a pro-inflammatory cytokine shift (elevated IL-6 and reduced IL-4/IL-10). Magnetic resonance imaging-diffusion tensor imaging (MRI-DTI) using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) and voxel-based analysis (VBA) analysis revealed diffuse white matter injury in regions like the olfactory bulb and hypothalamus, while 18F-FDG PET-CT detected hypometabolism in the thalamus and striatum. These findings position BBB disruption as an early event potentially driving diffuse neural injury, and the combination of TBSS and VBA for MRI-DTI analysis offers a robust quantitative framework for assessing acute rmTBI pathology.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) representing a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide,1 with an annual global burden of 69 million cases and an economic impact of $400 billion.2,3 TBI is clinically classified as mild, moderate, or severe categories, with mild traumatic brain injuries (mTBIs) comprising approximately 80%–90% of all cases.4 The pathophysiological mechanisms after repetitive mild traumatic brain injury (rmTBI) are multifactorial, involving structural and functional alterations in the brain.5 Importantly, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, neuroinflammation, axonal injury, and metabolic dysregulation contribute to the immediate and delayed consequences of rmTBI.6,7,8,9

The BBB is essential for maintaining brain homeostasis by restricting the entry of harmful substances into the central nervous system. It is formed by endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes, with tight junction (TJ) and adhesion junction (AJ) proteins linking endothelial cells.10 TJ proteins are multiprotein complexes comprising claudins, occludins, and transmembrane proteins that are anchored to zona occludens (ZO) proteins.11,12 ZO proteins act as scaffolds to bind junction molecules (e.g., claudin and occludin) to intracellular actin (a structural cytoskeletal protein). BBB dysregulation is associated with decreased expression of TJ proteins.13,14,15 The neuroinflammatory response is tightly regulated by the tissues and typically occurs during the acute phase of brain injury, including processes such as pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine release, regulation of neurotrophic factors, cell migration, and phagocytosis.16,17 Neuroinflammation, characterized by cytokine release, cell migration, and phagocytosis, manifests during the acute phase of TBI and may persist chronically following rmTBI, predominantly mediated by activated microglia.18 Microglia are involved in synaptic pruning, immune response coordination, and debris removal, promoting brain repair.19

Diffuse white matter injury, caused by shear and stretch forces during rmTBI, is commonly observed in brain-injured patients and affects brain signaling and cognitive function.20,21,22,23 Studies have shown that TBI-induced axonal damage often manifests as anisotropic changes in the diffusion of water molecules, which further affects neurotransmission and information processing between brain regions.24,25 Magnetic resonance imaging-diffusion tensor imaging (MRI-DTI) enables the detection of microscopic changes in white matter integrity, offering insights closely associated with neurological function.26,27,28 Abnormal brain metabolism is a core manifestation of neurological dysfunction after rmTBI. Glucose, the primary energy source for brain cells, shows reduced metabolism in brain regions post-rmTBI, contributing to neuronal dysfunction and apoptosis. Especially in the acute phase, the impaired energy metabolism in the brain may be one of the important mechanisms of neuronal apoptosis and loss of function. 2-Deoxy-2-[Fluorine-18]fluoro-D-glucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG PET-CT), as an effective means to assess brain metabolism, enables the evaluation of glucose utilization across different brain regions and provides critical insights into the metabolic underpinnings of neurological dysfunction following rmTBI.29,30

In this study, we evaluated the pathological changes during the acute phase after rmTBI using dual analysis of MRI-DTI, along with 18F-FDG PET-CT and molecular biological analysis. Our goal is to shed light on the pathologic process during the acute phase of TBI and to provide a theoretical basis for future early diagnosis and patient management.

Results

BBB disruption following acute phase of rmTBI

The temporal phases of TBI are commonly classified as follows: the acute phase (typically within 24–72 h post-injury), the subacute phase (spanning several days to weeks), and the chronic phase (extending from weeks to months or longer).3,31,32 To assess BBB damage in acute-phase following rmTBI, we employed Evans blue (EB) staining to quantitatively analyze BBB permeability. EB is a commonly used dye to evaluate BBB integrity, as it leaks into brain tissue through disrupted blood vessels and can be quantitatively measured after staining.33 Three days after rmTBI, brain tissue exhibited a marked increase in EB leakage compared to the control group, highlighting a clear increase in BBB permeability (Figures 1A and 1B). To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying BBB damage, we analyzed the expression of ZO-1 and claudin5 proteins. Both ZO-1 and claudin5 proteins are essential for maintaining BBB integrity and their downregulation is often associated with the loss of BBB function.34 Western blot analysis revealed that, 3 days post-rmTBI, the expression of both ZO1 and claudin5 proteins obviously decreased in the rmTBI group, indicating degradation of these critical proteins (Figures 1C–1E). These findings further support the disruption of BBB structure and function. Microglial activation and neuroinflammation after acute phase of rmTBI

Microglial cells are typically in a resting state but become activated following neural injury, migrating to the injury site and releasing various inflammatory cytokines that play roles in both neural repair and inflammation regulation.35 To precisely characterize microglial activation and phenotypic polarization following rmTBI, we performed immunofluorescence staining on brain sections. Quantitative analysis revealed a robust increase in Iba1+ microglia in the cortical region of rmTBI mice compared to control (Figures 2A and 2B). Further immunophenotyping at day 3 post-rmTBI demonstrated a pronounced shift toward pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, as evidenced by markedly elevated expression of CD86+microglia compared to anti-inflammatory CD206+ subsets (Figures 2C and 2D). The release of inflammatory cytokines plays a pivotal role in the neuroinflammatory response post-rmTBI. To investigate the relationship between microglial activation and inflammation, we quantified the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL)-6 and the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-1016. IL-6 is a common pro-inflammatory cytokine elevated after rmTBI and its increased levels correlate with the degree of neural injury and exacerbation of neuroinflammation. IL-4 and IL-10, typical anti-inflammatory cytokines, show reduced levels, suggesting potential dysregulation of the inflammatory response. The ELISA findings showed a notable rise in IL-6 concentrations, along with a marked reduction in IL-4 and IL-10 levels 3 days after the rmTBI (Figures 2E–2G). Elevated IL-6 levels indicate a robust inflammatory response post-rmTBI, potentially exacerbating neural injury, whereas decreased IL-4 and IL-10 levels suggest inadequate anti-inflammatory responses, which may further compromise rmTBI-induced neural damage. These changes are observed concurrently with microglial activation, reflecting the imbalance in neuroinflammatory processes following rmTBI.

White matter damage induced by acute phase of rmTBI

White matter damage following rmTBI is a critical feature of neurological damage, often associated with the onset of functional deficits.36,37 To quantitatively assess acute-phase white matter pathology, we employed MRI-DTI techniques utilizing both voxel-based analysis (VBA) and tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS).38,39,40 These approaches comprehensively evaluated the distribution and severity of microstructural disruption.

VBA identified widespread injury in the rmTBI group, such as the olfactory bulb, zona incerta, and caudoputamen (Figure 3A). Quantitative MRI-DTI revealed substantial microstructural disruptions across these regions, characterized by: decreased fractional anisotropy (FA) in the left olfactory bulb (Figure 3B); elevated mean diffusivity (MD) in the left zona incerta, right olfactory bulb and left caudoputamen (Figures 3C–3E); increased radial diffusivity (RD) in the right olfactory bulb and left caudoputamen (Figures 3F and 3G); and axial diffusivity(AD) elevations selectively in the left olfactory areas (Figure 3H). In summary, MRI-DTI metrics across multiple brain regions in rmTBI mice indicate widespread microstructural disruption in neural fibers, particularly within sensory (visual, olfactory, and auditory) and motor pathways. The consistent reduction in FA, coupled with elevated MD, RD, and AD, reflects profound damage and loss of integrity in these critical white matter networks.

Glucose metabolism alterations in the acute phase of rmTBI

Glucose metabolism is a critical indicator of cerebral energy metabolism and alterations in glucose metabolism after rmTBI often reflect the extent of neuronal damage and functional impairments.41 To assess changes in glucose metabolism following rmTBI, we utilized 18F-FDG PET-CT, a widely applied neuroimaging technique capable of precisely quantifying the regional glucose metabolic activity within the brain.

At 3 days post-rmTBI, 18F-FDG PET-CT scans demonstrated pronounced reductions in glucose metabolism across multiple brain regions in the rmTBI group, particularly in areas exhibiting severe white matter injury, including the cerebral cortex, corpus callosum, hippocampus, and thalamus (Figures 5A and 5B). This suggests that rmTBI induces substantial metabolic alterations in the brain. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that glucose metabolism was significantly decreased in multiple brain regions in the rmTBI group compared to controls (Figure 5C). Discussion

Despite the diverse clinical manifestations and pathological mechanisms of rmTBI, the pathophysiological response during the acute phase is essential for understanding its long-term outcomes. This study investigates the changes in various pathological processes in the brain during the acute phase following rmTBI, focusing on BBB damage, microglial activation, white matter injury, and alterations in glucose metabolism and explores the inter-relationships among these processes. Through imaging and biomarker assessments, we found that during the acute phase of rmTBI, these pathological changes are not isolated but are closely interconnected, reflecting the complex neurobiological responses triggered by the injury. Specifically, we construct a comprehensive model encompassing neural injury, inflammation, and metabolic disruptions, including BBB damage, microglial activation, white matter injury, and glucose metabolism alterations. Using imaging techniques such as MRI-DTI and 18F-FDG PET-CT, along with immunological analysis, we elucidate the broad and complex nature of nerve damage in the acute phase of rmTBI, particularly highlighting the close association between white matter injury and metabolic imbalance.

BBB disruption is one of the earliest events in nerve damage following rmTBI,31,33,34 which facilitates the influx of peripheral immune cells and inflammatory mediators into the CNS. This breach, confirmed by EB extravasation and downregulation of TJ proteins, creates a pro-inflammatory milieu that potently activates microglia. Microglia plays a pivotal role during the acute phase of rmTBI, as they not only release inflammatory cytokines but also potentially exert direct damage on adjacent neurons.16,17,19,35 Previous studies suggest that prolonged inflammation after rmTBI may promote BBB damage, creating a feedback loop that results in ongoing nerve damage.42 The BBB damage and inflammation foster a vicious cycle, compromising recovery mechanisms and exacerbating neuronal dysfunction.43

Our neuroimaging approach, integrating both VBA and TBSS analyses of MRI-DTI data, provides a comprehensive assessment of white matter injury following rmTBI. While each method has inherent strengths and limitations, their combination offers a more robust and convergent characterization of microstructural damage than either technique alone. MRI-DTI analysis reveals abnormal quantitative DTI measures in white matter regions, such as marked decreases in FA and increases in MD, RD, and AD, indicating the loss of white matter integrity.44,45,46 Notably, our VBA identified considerable alterations in regions including the bilateral olfactory bulbs, left olfactory cortex, left intermediate belt, and left caudoputamen. Conversely, TBSS, optimized for assessing core white matter tracts, detected injury in the right optic radiation, right anterior olfactory nucleus, right lateral lemniscus, left piriform area, and left septofimbrial nucleus. The partial spatial overlap and methodological complementarity of these results underscore the value of multimodal integration. VBA’s voxel-wise sensitivity may capture more diffuse or localized gray/white matter interface changes, while TBSS improves alignment and comparison of major skeletal pathways.47,48 This integrative strategy mitigates the risk of overlooking injury patterns dependent on a single analytical framework. The convergence of identified regions into functional networks yields novel pathophysiological insights. Injury within the olfactory system (e.g., olfactory bulbs, cortex, anterior olfactory nucleus, and piriform area) may explain frequently reported olfactory deficits in neurotrauma conditions.49 These structures are critical for sensory processing and have extensive connections to limbic regions, potentially linking sensory disruption to emotional and memory impairments. Similarly, damage to the caudoputamen (a key structure for motor control and habitual learning) and the cerebral peduncle (a major motor pathway) could underlie motor disturbances. The involvement of the septofimbrial nucleus, an integrative hub connecting hippocampus and diencephalon, might disrupt limbic-diencephalic communication, potentially contributing to alterations in hippocampal rhythms and emotional processing. Furthermore, injuries to the optic radiation and lateral lemniscus suggest potential visual and auditory processing deficits that extend beyond the primary olfactomotor implications, indicating a broader sensorimintegration disruption.

Alterations in glucose metabolism following rmTBI are crucial indicators of neuronal dysfunction. Previous research has demonstrated that metabolic dysregulation after rmTBI is strongly linked to functional decline, especially during the acute phase when metabolic reductions in specific brain regions may exacerbate nerve damage and hinder recovery.41 Our study further supports this notion. Integrating MRI-DTI and 18F-FDG PET-CT results, we hypothesize that white matter injury caused by rmTBI not only disrupts neuronal fibers but also impairs signal transmission between brain regions, compounding the injury by disrupting glucose metabolism.50,51,52 The dual changes in white matter injury and glucose metabolism may synergistically contribute to the neurofunctional decline observed in the acute phase of rmTBI.53 Moreover, the relationship between inflammation and metabolic dysfunction post-rmTBI warrants further investigation. Studies have shown that pro-inflammatory cytokines can suppress energy metabolism in brain regions through various mechanisms, accelerating neurodegenerative processes. This metabolic-inflammation feedback loop may offer novel insights into therapeutic strategies following rmTBI, particularly regarding early interventions targeting inflammation and metabolic recovery.54

In conclusion, our study underscores the relationships among BBB disruption, microglial activation, white matter injury and alterations in glucose metabolism in the early stage following rmTBI. White matter injury may act as the critical pathological change responsible for the development of diffuse brain injury. Dual analysis of MRI-DTI (TBSS and VBA) allows for a comprehensive quantitative assessment of white matter injury, which is crucial for the early and accurate evaluation of acute neuropathology in rmTBI.

Limitations of the study

Despite providing a comprehensive analysis of the mechanisms underlying neural damage during the acute phase of rmTBI, this study has several limitations. First, the study sample was relatively small and focused primarily on acute-phase changes. Future research should include long-term follow-up to assess chronic pathological changes following rmTBI. Second, while various experimental approaches were employed, some key mechanisms, such as the specific regulatory processes of BBB repair and metabolic recovery, remain poorly understood and should be further explored through more refined molecular experiments in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

Wild-type C57Bl/6J mice were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All experiments used 6-8 weeks old male mice weighing 22-24g. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly allocated to either the rmTBI or control group. These mice were housed in a controlled environment that adhered to standard pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Throughout the study, the mice were maintained at thermoregulated environment with a 12-hour alternating photoperiod, receiving continuous access to nutrition and hydration. There are up to four mice in each cage. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (approval number IRB2024-DWFL-072, February 26, 2024).

RmTBI mouse model

To induce anesthesia, mice were exposed to 5% isoflurane (vol/vol) in oxygen at a flow rate of 1 L/min and maintained under deep anesthesia with 1.5% isoflurane delivered via a precision vaporizer. Then, the mice were positioned prone within a custom-designed acrylic mold. The fur was first shaved and a concave metal impact pad was secured to the scalp. The mice then underwent a mild controlled cortical impact (CCI) procedure using a 6.3 model electronic control cortical impactor (American Instrument Company, Richmond, Virginia, USA). Post-procedure, the mice were transferred to an observation cage for monitoring until full recovery of consciousness. The procedure was repeated four times at 48-hour intervals between each session. The parameters for CCI were set as follows: impact velocity at 5 m/s, a dwell time of 100 ms and an impact depth of 2.5 mm.55,56,57,58,59,60,61 Mice that developed subcutaneous hematomas following modeling, as well as those exhibiting severely impaired motor function, were excluded from the study. Consequently, four mice were not included in the subsequent experimental procedures.

Method details

Assessment of BBB integrity using evans blue

To evaluate BBB permeability at day 3 post-rmTBI, mice received an intravenous injection of 2% Evans Blue (EB; Sigma-Aldrich, #E2129) dissolved in sterile saline. Following a 2-hour circulation period, animals were transcardially perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to thoroughly clear intravascular dye. Brains were then carefully excised, weighed, and homogenized in formamide (5 mL per gram of tissue) followed by incubation at 70°C for 24 hours. A standard curve was generated using serially diluted EB in formamide, with the following concentration gradient: 26.5, 13.25, 6.625, 3.3125, 1.65625, 0.828125, 0.4140625, 0.20703125, and 0.103515625 μg/mL. After centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was collected, and absorbance was measured at 630 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). EB concentration was interpolated from the standard curve and normalized to tissue weight (μg EB per gram of brain tissue), calculated as follows: EB content = EB concentration × formamide volume (mL) / brain weight (g).62

Western blotting

Following transcranial perfusion with ice-cold PBS, whole brain tissues were rapidly extracted and weighed. Tissue samples were homogenized in a freshly prepared lysis buffer mixture (RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors at a 9:1:1 ratio) using a volume of 9 ml per gram of tissue. After homogenization, samples were lysed for 30 minutes with gentle agitation at 4°C and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to pellet insoluble debris. The supernatant was carefully collected, mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, and denatured at 100°C for 10 min. Total protein extracts were separated via 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblotting. Primary antibodies used included ZO-1 (1:1000; 21773-1-AP; Proteintech), Claudin 5(1:1000; ab15106; Abcam) and GAPDH (1:1000; 2118S; CST). Protein bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

To assess neuroinflammatory responses following rmTBI, we evaluated microglial activation via immunofluorescence staining. Brain sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature, followed by three 5-minute washes in PBS. Permeabilization was carried out using 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 minutes, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody (Iba1:1:500; ab5076, Abcam; CD86:1:300; 553689, BD; CD206:1:300; ab64693, Abcam) diluted in blocking solution. After three 10-minute PBS washes, species-appropriate Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature under light protection. Following three 10-minute PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium (ab104139, Abcam). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and quantification was performed with ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Brain inflammatory cytokine levels were quantified using whole-brain samples that were harvested following transcardial perfusion and subsequently stored at -80°C. ELISA kits for Interleukin (IL) -4 (E-MSEL-M0008; Elabscience), IL-6 (E-MSEL-M0001; Elabscience) and IL-10 (RK00016; Abclonal) were utilized in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

MRI-DTI acquisition

The integrity of white matter tracts was evaluated using a 9.4-T Bruker BioSpec 94/30 USR MRI scanner (Bruker BioSpin GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany). T2-weighted coronal images of the entire brain were obtained using a rapid spin-echo sequence and a surface coil for mouse brain imaging. DTI data were derived from T2-weighted images. The mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane, with heart rate and respiratory rhythm monitor during scanning. MRI scan parameters: Echo Time (TE) = 1800 ms, Repetition Time (TR) = 36.38 ms, Slice Thickness = 0.15 mm, RARE factor = 12, Field of View (FOV) = 15 mm × 15 mm, 50 slices.

MRI-DTI preprocessing and analysis

Raw MRI-DTI data were preprocessing for spatial statistical analysis employing Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS) and Voxel-Based Analysis (VBA). Preprocessing included conversion to NIFTI format (dcm2nii), motion correction, eddy current correction, gradient direction adjustment, brain tissue masking and tensor fitting. DTI metrics, including fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD) and radial diffusivity (RD), were computed using FSL (FMRIB Software Library v6.0). In TBSS analysis, DTI indices were registered to standard space using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANT), followed by extraction of the white matter skeleton and subsequent group-level statistical analysis. In VBA, the registered data were smoothed with an 8mm kernel and statistical analysis was conducted using SPM12. One-way ANOVA with permutation testing was performed using OpenGL Mathematics tools.

18F-FDG PET-CT imaging and analysis

On day 3 post-rmTBI, glucose metabolism was evaluated using 18F-FDG PET-CT imaging. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1.5% concentration, 1.5 L/min flow rate) and intravenously injected with 3.7-4.07 MBq of 18F-FDG. After a 30-minute uptake period, imaging was conducted using a small animal PET-CT system (Novel Imaging, Beijing). Imaging parameters were: Scout scan with a 2×2 detector array, 50 kV and 0.1 mA tube settings; CT scan with 2×2 detector array, tube voltage 50 kV, current 0.1 mA, FDK reconstruction algorithm, matrix size 512×512×512, slice 0.18 mm; PET scan with 6 ns coincidence time window, energy window 350-650 keV, OSEM reconstruction, 600-second acquisition per bed position, matrix size 140×140×120, FOV = 70 mm.

PET-CT images were processed using the NMSoft-Animal Image Processing WorkStation (NMSoft-AIWS) for quality control and calibration. Data analysis was conducted using PMOD software (PMOD Technologies LLC, version 4.0). Spatial registration was conducted using a standardized mouse brain MRI-T2 template, followed by fusion of PET and CT images. Brain regions were segmented based on a multi-atlas template and 18F-FDG uptake was quantified. Standardized Uptake Values (SUV) were calculated using the formula: SUV = [Activity concentration in the region of interest (kBq/mL)] / [Injected dose (MBq) / Body weight (kg)].

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Data were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and presented as mean ± SD. Differences between groups were assessed using unpaired t-test or two way-ANOVA tests with least significant difference (LSD) post hoc correction. Statistical significance was determined by a P-value of less than 0.05 (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).
