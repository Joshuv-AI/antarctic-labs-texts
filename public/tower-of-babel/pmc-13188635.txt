Autonomy-supportive teaching, positive academic emotions, and agentic engagement in EFL contexts
Authors: Lingling Gan, Shenhai Zhu, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgentic engagement, defined as students’ proactive and constructive contributions to shaping their own learning processes, has been increasingly recognized as an important yet underexplored dimension of engagement. Drawing on an integrated motivational–affective framework informed by Self-Determination Theory and Control-Value Theory, this study examined how perceived autonomy-supportive teaching relates to agentic engagement among Chinese university students in English as a foreign language learning context, with positive academic emotions as a potential mediating mechanism. A convenience sample of 566 Chinese non-English majors (458 females, 108 males) was recruited from twelve College English classes at a public university in southwestern China. Participants were first- and second-year students with an average of over ten years of English learning experience, representing mixed proficiency levels typical of compulsory EFL instruction. Cross-sectional survey data were collected and participants reported their perceptions of autonomy-supportive teaching, positive academic emotions (enjoyment, hope, and pride), and agentic engagement. Structural equation modeling was employed to test the hypothesized direct and indirect associations among the study variables. The results indicated that perceived autonomy-supportive teaching was positively associated with agentic engagement. This association was both direct and indirect, operating through positive academic emotions. Enjoyment, hope, and pride jointly mediated a substantial proportion of the total effect, suggesting that positive emotions function as proximal psychological processes linking supportive instructional practices to students’ proactive engagement. The findings demonstrate that autonomy-supportive teaching fosters agentic engagement not only through motivational pathways but also by cultivating emotionally supportive learning environments. By highlighting the mediating role of positive academic emotions, this study advances an integrated motivational–affective account of engagement and provides contextually grounded evidence from Chinese higher education. The results underscore the importance of attending to students’ emotional experiences when promoting learner agency in instructional settings.Keywords: Autonomy-supportive teaching, Agentic engagement, Positive academic emotions, Self-determination theory, Control-value theory, EFL context

Introduction

Academic engagement has received sustained attention across educational domains and has gained increasing momentum in second language acquisition (SLA) research over the past two decades [1]. Among its multiple dimensions, agentic engagement highlights learners’ proactive contributions to shaping both the content and conditions of their learning, thereby playing a critical role in high-quality educational experiences [2, 3]. This form of engagement is particularly relevant in English as a Foreign Language (EFL) education, where successful learning requires sustained efforts, interaction, and willingness to communicate [4–6]. Yet, in many EFL contexts, especially those characterized by examination pressure and teacher-centered instruction, students often assume passive learning roles, which may constrain their engagement and academic development [7, 8]. In this regard, cultivating agentic engagement among EFL learners becomes critical, enabling students to proactively seek learning opportunities, negotiate L2 task demands, and contribute to EFL classroom decision-making processes. Such proactive involvement not only alleviates L2 anxiety arising from foreign language learning but also enhances students’ sense of autonomy, thereby fostering their autonomous motivation and self-directed learning capabilities that are indispensable for sustainable L2 acquisition beyond the classroom [3, 9, 10].

Autonomy-supportive teaching (AST), rooted in Self-Determination Theory (SDT; [11]), has been widely recognized as a key instructional approach for fostering students’ motivation and engagement. A substantial body of research across educational settings indicates that AST promotes autonomous motivation, self-regulated learning, and adaptive engagement behaviors [12–15]. In EFL contexts, emerging evidence suggests that perceived AST is positively associated with proactive learning behaviors through motivational pathways such as psychological need satisfaction and mastery goals [16, 17]. However, the specific mechanisms through which AST fosters agentic engagement in EFL classrooms remain insufficiently understood.

Recent advances in positive psychology in second language acquisition (SLA) have drawn attention to the role of emotions in shaping learners’ engagement and persistence [18]. Complementing SDT, Control-Value Theory (CVT; [19]) highlights achievement emotions as robust predictors of student engagement. Research has documented emotional mediation between teacher support and learner engagement in EFL learning [20–23]. Positive emotions such as enjoyment and pride function as critical mediators that translate environmental affordances into sustained behavioral investment, as they broaden learners’ momentary thought-action repertoires and build enduring personal resources conducive to adaptive engagement [19, 24]. Importantly, the significance of emotions extends beyond learners to encompass language teachers as well. Emerging research indicates that teachers’ emotional experiences and regulation strategies are crucial in shaping their well-being and instructional functioning [25], and are influenced by broader psychological factors such as future-oriented beliefs [26]. These perspectives position emotions as a core component of language education systems operating at both the learner and teacher levels. The mediating role of positive emotions is also pronounced in second language contexts where learners frequently face communicative apprehension, test anxiety, and performance pressure [27, 28]. However, little is known about whether positive academic emotions function as a key mechanism linking autonomy-supportive teaching to agentic engagement, particularly in Chinese EFL contexts, where sociocultural norms and students’ prior learning experiences may make agentic classroom contribution psychologically demanding [29, 30].

To address these gaps, the present study integrates SDT and CVT to examine the relationship between perceived autonomy-supportive teaching and agentic engagement, with positive academic emotions tested as a mediating mechanism. In doing so, the study also responds to recent calls to examine the future trajectories of language education, which emphasize the importance of understanding how psychological processes shape learners’ capacity to actively navigate and construct their learning experiences [31]. By demonstrating how autonomy-supportive teaching fosters agentic engagement through positive academic emotions, the present study offers motivational–affective insights for developing future-oriented, learner-centered language education that empowers learners to actively shape their own language learning.

Literature review

Integrating SDT and CVT: a motivational–affective framework

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Control-Value Theory (CVT) provide complementary perspectives for understanding the processes underlying student engagement in academic settings. SDT emphasizes the role of social-contextual factors in fostering high-quality motivation through the satisfaction of students’ basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness [11, 32, 33]. When teachers provide meaningful choices, offer explanatory rationales, and acknowledge students’ perspectives, students are more likely to internalize learning goals and regulate their learning in a self-endorsed manner [15]. Within this framework, agentic engagement refers to a proactive form of classroom involvement in which students actively contribute to their learning conditions by expressing preferences, asking questions, and seeking help [3, 34].

At the same time, CVT highlights the role of achievement emotions in shaping engagement. According to CVT, students’ cognitive appraisals of control over learning outcomes and the value they attach to academic tasks influence the types of emotions they experience during learning [19, 35]. High control and value appraisals tend to generate activating positive emotions such as enjoyment and hope, whereas low control or value may evoke anxiety or boredom that undermine sustained engagement. These achievement emotionsinfluence students’ effort, persistence, and use of learning strategies, thereby shaping multiple dimensions of engagement [36, 37].

Although SDT and CVT are often applied independently, integrating the two frameworks offers a more comprehensive account of engagement processes in classroom settings [37–39]. From an integrated perspective, autonomy-supportive teaching can be viewed as a social-contextual antecedent that fosters students’ psychological need satisfaction while simultaneously strengthening their control–value appraisals. These motivational and cognitive experiences may, in turn, give rise to activating positive emotions that energize students’ engagement [11, 19]. Engagement thus emerges as a dynamic outcome shaped by the interplay between instructional practices, students’ motivational experiences, and their affective responses [36, 40].

This integrated perspective is particularly relevant in second language (L2) learning contexts, where emotional experiences and sustained motivational investment play a crucial role in language development [41, 42]. Empirical studies provide partial support for different components of this framework. For example, longitudinal research has shown that autonomy-supportive teaching practices predict students’ engagement over time in L2 classrooms [43]. Similarly, research indicates that control–value appraisals influence achievement emotions and subsequent learning outcomes in language learning contexts [44].

Despite these advances, empirical research directly integrating SDT and CVT in L2 education remains limited. The sequential pathway linking autonomy-supportive teaching to agentic engagement, through students’ psychological need satisfaction (SDT), strengthened control-value appraisals (CVT), and the generation of positive achievement emotions, has rarely been examined in L2 research. This gap is notable in light of recent reviews indicating that Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET)—a central SDT mini-theory explaining how autonomy-supportive environments shape intrinsic motivation through perceived autonomy and competence—remains underexplored in L2 research [45]. This limited application of CET constrains current understanding of the psychological mechanisms through which autonomy support is translated into students’ classroom behaviours. Addressing this limitation calls for more systematic research that adopts an integrated SDT–CVT perspective to jointly account for the motivational and affective processes underlying agentic engagement in L2 classrooms.

Autonomy-supportive teaching in EFL contexts

Within Self-Determination Theory, autonomy-supportive teaching (AST) refers to instructional practices that support students’ volition and psychological need satisfaction in the learning process [15]. Across educational settings, extensive research has shown that AST promotes students’ autonomous motivation, engagement, self-regulation, and well-being [14, 46, 47].

In L2 education, a growing body of research indicates that perceived autonomy support is positively associated with adaptive learning outcomes, including self-determined motivation and engagement, through motivational pathways such as psychological need satisfaction and achievement goals (e.g., [16, 17, 43, 48]). Beyond these motivational effects, additional evidence also suggests that AST may foster more proactive forms of student engagement by supporting students’ sense of influence and participation in classroom processes, highlighting its potential relevance for agentic engagement [49]. However, in EFL contexts, empirical studies directly linking AST to agentic engagement remains scarce. Much of the existing EFL studies is embedded within broader teacher-support frameworks, making it difficult to isolate the unique contribution of autonomy support (e.g., Zhang and Hu, [50], Zhang, [51]; Zhou et al., 2022). Although limited studies have explained the association between AST and agentic engagement via achievement goals or learning empowerment [17, 52], agentic engagement as a distinct outcome is underexplored.

In addition, the mechanisms through which AST translates into agentic engagement are not yet well theorized and empirically examined, particularly with respect to emotional processes. This limitation is important in affect-laden EFL contexts, where students’ engagement decisions are shaped not only by motivational factors but also by emotionally charged experiences associated with language learning. Recent studies have shown that negative emotional orientations, including anxiety and demotivation, can inhibit L2 learners’ willingness to communicate and take initiative in classroom interaction [53–55], underscoring the importance of affective processes in shaping proactive engagement.

More importantly, the effectiveness of autonomy-supportive practices may vary across instructional contexts and subject domains [56], suggesting that findings from other disciplines may not directly generalize to university EFL classrooms. Given that the distinctive instructional and sociocultural conditions may heighten the emotional costs of agentic participation, there is a need for more context-sensitive research to elucidate how autonomy-supportive teaching facilitates agentic engagement in Chinese EFL contexts. Particular attention should be paid to positive academic emotions as a proximal affective mechanism linking autonomy support to agentic engagement.

Agentic engagement in EFL learning

Agentic engagement represents a distinct dimension of student engagement, referring to learners’ constructive and proactive contributions to the instructional process [3]. Unlike behavioral, emotional, or cognitive engagement, which primarily capture students’ responses to instructional input, agentic engagement emphasizes learners’ initiative and intentional influence on their learning conditions [39, 57]. Grounded in Self-Determination Theory, agentic engagement reflects the satisfaction of students’ basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence [11] and operates bidirectionally as both an outcome of autonomy-supportive environments and a mechanism through which learners shape more supportive learning contexts over time [34].

In L2 settings, this construct has received increasing attention as researchers recognize that effective L2 learning requires learners not only to sustain motivation but also to actively shape their learning experiences [10, 58, 59]. From a dynamic perspective, engagement in language learning has been conceptualized as an emergent and situated phenomenon arising from interactions between learners and their learning environments [1]. This perspective highlights language learners as active participants who contribute to classroom interaction rather than merely responding to instructional input.

Despite its theoretical significance, agentic engagement remains relatively underrepresented in empirical L2 research. A systematic review by Hiver et al., [1] indicates that engagement research in language education has predominantly focused on behavioral, emotional, and cognitive dimensions, while the agentic dimension has received comparatively limited attention. Existing EFL literature has largely examined agentic engagement in relation to motivational and individual-difference variables, such as psychological need satisfaction, achievement goals, growth language mindset, and L2 grit [17, 60, 61]. While this body of work has established several correlates of agentic engagement, the mechanisms through which classroom experiences translate students’ motivation into proactive engagement behaviors have yet to be fully understood. Prior research from general education points to a potential indirect pathway through which autonomy-supportive practices influence agentic engagement via students’ affective processes (e.g., Bordbar, [62], Bordbar, [63]. However, whether and how such processes translate into agentic engagement in EFL education remains largely unexplored.

Although agentic engagement has been widely recognized as a key component of effective language learning, current EFL research has provided limited understanding of how students’ motivational resources translate into proactive engagement behaviors in classroom interaction. Addressing this gap requires theoretical models that integrate affective processes with motivational explanations to clarify the mechanisms through which learners convert motivation into agentic classroom participation.

Positive academic emotions as mediators

Evidence from higher education research indicates that positive academic emotions function as key mediating mechanisms linking autonomy-supportive teaching to students’ agentic engagement ([62], [63]). Rather than being mere by-products of motivational processes, these emotions help transmit the effects of autonomy support by fostering students’ willingness to contribute proactively to instruction [34]. In L2 research, accumulating evidence underscores the importance of affective processes in shaping student engagement. However, most existing studies have examined positive academic emotions as mediators linking general forms of teacher support to overall or multidimensional engagement (e.g., Liu and Zhou, [20], Sadoughi, [21], Zhao, [23], rather than distinguishing autonomy support and agentic engagement as theoretically specific constructs. As a result, the role of positive academic emotions as a mediating mechanism through which autonomy-supportive teaching is translated into agentic forms of participation in L2 classrooms remains understudied.

This gap is salient given the demanding nature of agentic engagement in L2 learning environments. Actively expressing ideas, initiating questions, or influencing instruction often entails interpersonal risk, especially in contexts characterised by communication anxiety and fear of negative evaluation [27, 28]. Under such conditions, students’ affective experiences may play a decisive mediating role in shaping whether they are willing to act agentically. This issue is further intensified in Chinese EFL classrooms, where sociocultural norms may discourage student-initiated participation [64, 65]. In these settings, positive academic emotions may facilitate agentic engagement by reducing perceived interpersonal risk and enhancing students’ readiness to participate proactively [19, 34]. Examining this affective pathway therefore offers a more nuanced account of how autonomy-supportive teaching is translated into students’ active classroom involvement.

The present study

Despite growing interest in integrating SDT and CVT to explain academic engagement, several interrelated limitations remain in the existing literature. First, no study to date has simultaneously examined whether perceived AST predicts the full triad of positive academic emotions (i.e., enjoyment, hope, and pride) and whether these emotions, in turn, facilitate agentic engagement in EFL contexts. Second, although Bordbar ([62], [63]) provided compelling evidence in general education that positive academic emotions transmit the effects of AST on agentic engagement, the generalizability of this affective pathway to EFL learning contexts remains untested. Third, prior studies from Chinese tertiary EFL settings suggest potential limitations of exclusively motivational explanations. For instance, Jiang and Zhang, [17] reported a negative association between perceived AST and mastery-approach goals among Chinese EFL undergraduates, indicating that achievement goal orientations alone may not adequately capture the processes through which AST influences students’ agentic engagement. This warrants the need to consider alternative mechanisms (e.g., affective processes) that may translate AST into agentic behaviors.

The above mentioned gaps point to the need for an integrated model that foregrounds positive academic emotions as key mediators linking perceived AST to agentic engagement in Chinese tertiary EFL classrooms. Building on the integrated SDT–CVT framework proposed above, the present study aims to test an affective mediation model in which enjoyment, hope, and pride function as parallel mediators of the relationship between perceived AST and agentic engagement. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

It hypothesizes that PAST not only exerts direct effects on AE but also operates indirectly by eliciting PAE, proposing an affective pathway linking autonomy-supportive teaching and agentic engagement in EFL contexts. Methods

Sampling and participants

A convenience sampling strategy was employed in the present study. Participants were recruited from twelve College English classes taught by six instructors at a comprehensive public university in southwestern China. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, who were fully informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. To protect confidentiality and ensure anonymity, all identifying information was removed. Students provided informed consent before completing a battery of self-report questionnaires (see Measures) administered via Wenjuanxing, a widely used professional online survey platform in China.

All questionnaires were administered in Chinese and had undergone a rigorous back-translation procedure to ensure linguistic equivalence. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated. To prevent missing data, the online survey system required participants to respond to all items before submission. As a result, no missing values were identified. Following the removal of responses that exhibited random or inattentive answering patterns, a final sample of 566 valid participants was retained (N = 566; 458 females, 81%; 108 males, 19%). All participants were Chinese, non-English majors, and were enrolled in compulsory English as a Foreign Language (EFL) courses at the time of data collection. On average, they had studied English for more than ten years. The sample consisted of 411 first-year students (72.6%) and 155 s-year students (27.4%).

Measures

All instruments were administered in English and adapted slightly to reflect the EFL classroom context (e.g., specifying “English class/teacher”). Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Where necessary, minor wording adjustments were made to ensure contextual relevance without altering the original constructs.

Demographic information

Participants reported demographic information including gender (male = 0, female = 1), academic year, and major.

Perceived autonomy-supportive teaching (PAST)

Perceived autonomy-supportive teaching was measured using the 6-item short form of the Learning Climate Questionnaire (LCQ; [66]). Sample items include “My English teacher conveys confidence in my ability to do well in English.” Previous research has demonstrated good reliability of this scale in Chinese EFL contexts (e.g., Chiang, [67]. In the present study, the scale showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92).

Positive academic emotions (PAE)

Positive academic emotions were assessed using the 12-item Achievement Emotions Questionnaire–Short (AEQ-S; [68]), focusing on three positive emotions: enjoyment, hope, and pride. Sample items include “I enjoy the challenges that English learning brings.” and “I feel confident when learning English.” Prior studies have supported the reliability of the AEQ-S in Chinese EFL samples (e.g., Chen et al., [69]. In the present study, internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.95). Confirmatory factor analysis supported the three-factor structure of PAE, χ² = 238.52, df = 47, χ²/df = 5.07, RMSEA = 0.07, CFI = 0.97, TLI = 0.96, SRMR = 0.03. Standardized factor loadings were satisfactory across all indicators.

Agentic engagement (AE)

Agentic engagement was measured using the 5-item Agentic Engagement Scale (AES; [34]). A sample item is “I ask questions in English class.” Previous studies have demonstrated its applicability in Chinese EFL contexts (e.g., Zhao and Yang, [23]. In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94).

Data analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 26.0. Preliminary analyses, including descriptive statistics and zero-order correlations among the study variables, were performed in SPSS 27.0. The hypothesized mediation model was subsequently tested in AMOS 26.0 using structural equation modeling (SEM). Maximum likelihood estimation was adopted, as it has been shown to perform robustly and accurately even when data exhibit non-normal distributions [70].

Prior to testing the structural model, the measurement model comprising the latent variables perceived autonomy-supportive teaching (PAST), positive academic emotions (PAE), and agentic engagement (AE) was evaluated. After establishing acceptable measurement model fit, the hypothesized mediation model was examined, controlling for relevant demographic variables. The model specified direct paths from PAST to the outcome variables and indirect paths via the mediator PAE (see Fig. 2). Bias-corrected bootstrap analyses with 566 resamples and 95% confidence intervals were conducted to assess the significance and magnitude of both direct and indirect effects [71].

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, with acceptable fit indicated by a χ²/df ratio below 5.0, comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (TLI/NNFI) values of 0.90 or higher, and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) values below 0.08 [72, 73]. Effect sizes were interpreted based on standardized path coefficients (β), following the guidelines proposed by Hair and Alamer [74]: values of 0–0.10 were considered weak, 0.11–0.30 modest, 0.31–0.50 moderate, and above 0.50 strong.

To assess the effect sizes of these relationships, Cohen’s f² values were calculated. Discussion

The present study yielded several key findings regarding the motivational and affective processes underlying agentic engagement in EFL classrooms. First, perceived autonomy-supportive teaching (AST) was positively associated with both positive academic emotions and agentic engagement, supporting its role as a critical contextual resource for fostering students’ proactive classroom participation. Second, positive academic emotions emerged as a robust predictor of agentic engagement, exerting a substantial effect (β = 0.65, p <.001). Third, positive academic emotions served as central mediators in the relationship between AST and agentic engagement. The indirect effect accounted for a substantial proportion of the total effect (66.18%), while the direct effect remained significant, indicating partial mediation. These findings highlight the pivotal role of positive academic emotions in translating autonomy-supportive teaching into students’ active and agentic involvement in EFL classroom interaction.

Autonomy-supportive teaching as a direct antecedent of agentic engagement

Consistent with SDT, the present findings demonstrate that students’ perceptions of autonomy-supportive teaching are directly associated with higher levels of agentic engagement. This result aligns with a growing body of research showing that autonomy-supportive instructional practices foster students’ initiative, voice, and proactive involvement across diverse educational contexts, including both general education and language learning settings [16, 17, 43, 49, 52]. Notably, our study substantiates that autonomy-supportive teaching practices constitute a critical antecedent to the cultivation of agentic engagement in L2 educational settings. In Chinese tertiary EFL contexts, students often face dual vulnerabilities: the inherent anxiety and frequent risk-taking of communicating in a foreign language [41, 76], as well as the cultural expectation of respecting teacher authority [77]. Under such conditions, autonomy-supportive teaching transforms potentially threatening learning situations into opportunities for proactive engagement by highlighting learner agency [78, 79]. When EFL teachers provide clear rationales for L2 tasks and encourage students’ contributions during communicative activities, they legitimize proactive behaviors that may otherwise be constrained by hierarchical classroom norms [43]. By mitigating these potential risks of agentic action, autonomy- supportive teaching operates as a buffering mechanism that enables students to engage proactively despite situational vulnerabilities [80].

Our findings thus offer preliminary evidence supporting the potential cross-cultural applicability of SDT, indicating that autonomy-supportive practices may promote agentic forms of engagement even within sociocultural contexts that traditionally emphasize authority and conformity [81]. More importantly, the present study extends this claim to Chinese EFL higher education settings, where large class sizes, standardized curricula, and examination-oriented cultures might be expected to constrain student agency [82]. The emergence of agentic engagement as a significant outcome suggests that need-supportive instruction can flourish even within structurally demanding educational environments, provided teachers actively nurture students’ sense of volition and self-endorsed participation.

Autonomy-supportive teaching and positive academic amotions

Beyond its direct association with agentic engagement, autonomy-supportive teaching was found to foster students’ positive academic emotions. This finding aligns with recent L2 research suggesting that autonomy support promote positive emotional experiences by enhancing students’ self efficacy and psychological need satisfaction [83, 84], thereby highlighting the role of positive emotions as a key mechanism through which autonomy-supportive teachers influence students’ engagement-related outcomes. The robust association between AST and positive academic emotions identified in the present study is consequential in EFL classrooms, where evaluative pressure and communicative vulnerability often threaten students’ emotional well-being [85]. Grounded in SDT, when teachers provide meaningful rationales, acknowledge negative feelings, and offer optimal challenges, students are more likely to experience enhanced autonomy, competence, and relatedness—conditions that generate enjoyment, pride, and comfort while reducing anxiety [11, 15, 83]. This need-satisfaction pathway may alleviate the emotional threats inherent in L2 development. These emotional benefits indicate that AST addresses not only students’ behavioral engagement but also their emotional requirements for L2 acquisition.

Meanwhile, our findings also point to cognitive reappraisal as an additional mechanism. Autonomy-supportive practices foster psychological safety, prompting students to reappraise anxiety-provoking situations. This process shifts their focus from threat appraisals to challenge appraisals, a cognitive shift that generates excitement and eagerness [86]. In In EFL classrooms, where learners are frequently required to perform publicly and are exposed to potential errors [27, 28], this reappraisal function becomes particularly critical. By framing L2 mistakes as learning opportunities rather than signs of incompetence, autonomy-supportive teachers mitigate students’ shame and embarrassment while actively promoting pride and hope in L2 learning [83]. Our findings suggest that AST shapes the cognitive appraisal processes (e.g., perceived control and value) that give rise to positive academic emotions, thereby extending Control-Value Theory by positioning classroom context as an antecedent of students’ emotional experiences.

More importantly, the strong link between autonomy-supportive teaching and students’ positive academic emotions carries significance for EFL education, where the emotional demands of L2 acquisition are often compounded by the developmental needs of students navigating complex linguistic and identity-related transitions [87]. By proposing that autonomy-supportive teaching simultaneously addresses students’ basic psychological needs and reshapes their cognitive appraisal patterns, this study positions teacher autonomy support as an indispensable resource for fostering the emotional conditions conducive to L2 development.

Positive academic emotions as proximal antecedents of agentic engagement

Among the relationships examined, the association between positive academic emotions and agentic engagement was the strongest, highlighting the central role of affect in enabling students’ proactive engagement. This finding is consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that positive academic emotions function as proximal determinants of agentic engagement, mediating the influence of autonomy-supportive practices through students’ self-system processes and internal appraisals. In particular, autonomy support has been shown to shape students’ self-perceptions of competence, autonomy, and relatedness, which in turn foster positive emotions such as enjoyment and hope, ultimately energizing agentic forms of classroom participation [62, 63]. From CVT and the broaden-and-build perspectives, positive emotions are understood as psychological resources that expand individuals’ momentary action repertoires and facilitate exploratory, self-initiated behavior [24, 36, 88]. In EFL classrooms, where communicative risk-taking and active participation are essential for L2 acquisition [89], this energizing function is consequential. While enjoyment can momentarily reduce the anxiety associated with public speaking and initiative answers, pride and hope arising from successful completion of L2 tasks builds sense of competence and anticipation that motivates further attempts to contribute [19, 88]. These emotions together create a self-reinforcing cycle that sustains agentic engagement.

Agentic engagement is inherently affectively demanding, as it requires students to make themselves visible, risk negative evaluation, and potentially challenge established classroom norms [57, 90]. These emotional challenges are intensified in EFL classrooms, where students are confronted with the dual vulnerabilities of linguistic incompetence and public performance. Speaking in a foreign language exposes EFL students to uncertainty, error, and potential embarrassment that may increase the perceived costs of agentic behavior [91]. From this perspective, positive academic emotions may serve as proximal psychological antecedents that lower these perceived affective costs and increase students’ readiness to act agentically. Whereas autonomy-supportive teaching provides a more distal contextual affordance, positive emotions may operate at the experiential level, directly shaping students’ moment-to-moment willingness to take initiatives in classroom interaction. These agentic behaviors are pivotal for negotiating meaning and co-constructing learning opportunities in the L2 classroom [34, 37, 92].

Our findings therefore position positive academic emotions as more immediate antecedents of agentic engagement in L2 learning. This pattern highlights the importance of incorporating affective processes into models of student agency and proactive engagement, especially in culturally and institutionally constrained EFL classrooms where the emotional labor of L2 participation is most pronounced.

Integrating SDT and CVT: the mediating role of positive academic emotions

A significant contribution of the present study lies in demonstrating that positive academic emotions mediate a substantial proportion of the association between autonomy-supportive teaching and agentic engagement. Conceptually, the findings support a sequential pathway in which autonomy-supportive teaching shapes students’ need-relevant experiences and control-value appraisals, which in turn give rise to activating emotions that enable agentic engagement [11, 19]. Although empirical research directly examining this full pathway in EFL contexts remains scarce, the present findings are consistent with converging evidence from adjacent lines of research. Prior studies have shown that autonomy support facilitates positive academic emotions by shaping students’ control-value appraisals (e.g., Wang and Hu, [93], while a growing body of research has identified positive emotions such as enjoyment and hope as key predictors of student engagement in L2 learning contexts (e.g., Derakhshan and Yin, [94], Hu, [95]. The present study extends this emerging line of inquiry by integrating these relationships within the specific context of EFL classrooms.

From a contextual perspective, these findings highlight the importance of affective processes in EFL settings characterized by examination pressure and collectivist cultural norms. High-stakes tests such as the College English Test (CET-4/6) often induce performance pressure and test anxiety, encouraging a focus on scores rather than communicative competence [96]. At the same time, collectivist values may heighten concerns about losing face and social evaluation, discouraging risk-taking in L2 production [97, 98]. Within this context, the present findings suggest that positive academic emotions serve as a critical pathway through which autonomy-supportive teaching translates into agentic engagement. In other words, even under substantial contextual constraints, autonomy-supportive practices may foster emotionally supportive learning experiences that enable students to participate more proactively.

At the same time, the results indicate that autonomy-supportive teaching may promote agentic engagement through mechanisms beyond affective experiences. The significant direct effect observed in the model suggests the presence of additional mediational processes operating alongside emotional pathways. For instance, autonomy-supportive practices may enhance students’ perceptions of the instrumental value of L2 learning, such as its relevance for high-stakes examinations, thereby promoting engagement through cognitive appraisals (cf. Pekrun, [19], Vo, [99]. Alternatively, in cultural contexts characterized by teacher authority and relational harmony [97], students may respond to autonomy support in terms of interpersonal expectations, engaging more actively out of a sense of responsibility or relational obligation. These possibilities point to the need for future research to examine multiple, coexisting mechanisms linking autonomy-supportive teaching and agentic engagement in EFL classrooms.

By foregrounding affective mechanisms, this study contributes to a more integrated understanding of student engagement and underscores that agentic engagement is not solely a motivational disposition, but also an emotionally enabled form of self-initiated action shaped by instructional and cultural contexts.

Conclusion and pedagogical implications

The present study contributes to the EFL engagement literature by demonstrating that positive academic emotions function as a proximal mechanism through which autonomy-supportive teaching fosters students’ agentic engagement. Extending prior motivation-centered accounts, the findings highlight the critical role of affective readiness in enabling students to take initiative, particularly in examination-oriented and authority-sensitive EFL contexts. By integrating affective mediation into the autonomy–agency pathway, this study offers a more fine-grained and contextually grounded explanation of how instructional practices translate into proactive classroom involvement.

Our findings carry important pedagogical implications. First, fostering agentic engagement requires not only promoting behavioral participation but also cultivating emotionally supportive learning environments in which students feel secure and confident to express their ideas [100]. Autonomy-supportive practices, such as acknowledging students’ perspectives, providing meaningful rationales, and framing challenges constructively, can help generate positive academic emotions that facilitate students’ willingness to act agentically. For example, portfolio-based self-assessment may foster pride by enabling students to recognize their progress, while shared leadership roles in collaborative tasks can cultivate hope by positioning students as capable agents who actively address challenges. Second, the results point to the necessity of incorporating affect-sensitive approaches into EFL pedagogy and teacher education. Rather than viewing student agency as culturally constrained, EFL teachers may adopt autonomy-supportive practices as viable strategies for aligning instructional demands with learners’ psychological needs, even in examination-oriented contexts. Accordingly, EFL teacher education programs should place greater emphasis on developing teachers’ capacity to support students’ emotional experiences alongside their learning processes, thereby promoting more sustainable and learner-centered EFL instruction.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional, self-report design limits causal inference among autonomy-supportive teaching, positive academic emotions, and agentic engagement. Second, the study focused only on positive activating emotions, without considering negative or deactivating emotions (e.g., anxiety, boredom) that may also shape engagement in EFL contexts. Third, the sample was drawn from Chinese tertiary EFL classrooms, which may limit generalizability to other cultural or educational settings.

Future research could address these limitations by employing longitudinal or experimental designs, incorporating a broader range of emotional experiences, and conducting cross-cultural comparisons to examine the boundary conditions of the proposed relationships. Beyond these considerations, future studies may extend the present findings by examining more fine-grained emotional experiences, including the distinct roles of enjoyment, hope, and pride, as well as the interplay between activating and deactivating emotions in shaping student engagement. In addition, given the identified affective mediation pathway, future research could explore potential boundary conditions of this process, including individual differences and classroom contextual factors, as well as how autonomy-supportive practices and students’ emotional experiences co-evolve over time.
