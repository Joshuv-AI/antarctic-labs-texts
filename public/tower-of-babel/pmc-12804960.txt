Continuous piezoionicity-driven electromechanical coupling in mechanically robust conductive eutectogels
Authors: Chenggong Xu, Wenpeng Wang, Yange Feng, Zhengde Wang, Ao Xie, Qi Wei, Changhe Du, Haoyu Deng, Zhiguang Guo, Daoai Wang, Weimin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSomatosensory network-sensitive ionic currents, responding to tactile biosignals, serve as behavioral modulation for electromechanical transduction. Building on these biological principles, here we show mechanically robust, conductive piezoionic eutectogels with hierarchical structure enabling continuous pressure-driven ion migration, effectively transducing pressure into ionic streaming. This electromechanical coupling originates from pressure-sensitive differences in cationic and anionic mobility, producing net streaming potentials. The piezoionic eutectogels, reaching sensitive, continuous response of ~200 s to innocuous force, governed by the engagement of both pronounced mechanosensitivity under applied pressure and ion inactivation kinetics upon releasing. The piezoionic mechanism is attributed to hierarchically orientated ion-steaming and charge compensation mechanism, dynamically regulating ion transmission. This ion streaming-driven piezovoltage depends on pressure magnitude, pressure duration and mechanical environments, achieving continuous peak piezovoltage up to 40 mV at 3.0 MPa. This work provides synergistic molecular–structural design towards piezoionic dynamics and mechanical performances, offering insights into next-generation piezoionics.Subject terms: Gels and hydrogels, Sensors and biosensors, Chemical engineering

Introduction

In living organisms, the synergistic interactions between mechanosensory systems and mechanical stimuli relying on ion-selective protein channels can convert and transmit pressure signals into electrophysiological signals with excitatory and inhibitory features, such as soft cutaneous mechanoreceptors1–3. Intrinsically, the rich set of mechanoreceptors, mechanosensitive ion channels, found in human skin accurately transduce tactile sensations, i.e., mechanical stimulus, into electrophysiological signals that propagate to the central nervous system via mechanotransduction machinery to achieve biological perceptions and interactive responses (Fig. 1A)4–6. Inspired by the sensory behavior of soft mechanoreceptors residing within skin, iontronics is rapidly expanding owing to promising applications in biocompatible sensors, e-skin and biomedicine, and energy-conversion, harvesting and storage7,8. As suggested by the emergence of piezo-materials, capabilities for perceiving and responding to external stress stimuli have attracted considerable attention owing to their ability to generate iontronic/electrical signals or ionic/electronic currents. Piezoelectric materials can convert mechanical forces into electrical signals, whereas piezoionic materials tend to be based on the movement of ions rather than electrons in response to pressure gradients. Consequently, piezoionic materials, like electric organs, are regarded as a very good match for biosensing and biointerfaces.

Piezoionics, as fast-emerging iontronics, relies on pressure-induced electrogenesis via ion currents to integrate electromechanical coupling, providing an alternative for taking advantage of ubiquitous mechanical energy in behaviors and movements9. When subjected to mechanical force, piezoionic matrices produce ionic currents through one of two phenomena: either the concerted flow of ions of a single charge, or the selective transport of one ionic species over its counterions due to differential mobility within the polymer network, thereby establishing net charge imbalance10,11. Particularly, piezoionic hydrogel functions through converting the applied force into ion streams, enabling an important role in flexible bioelectronics and biosensors, such as a silent-speech recognition system transforming laryngeal mechanical vibrations into silent speech, and a neuromodulation interface eliciting hindlimb movement via tactile-induced current pulses, owing to exceptional biocompatibility, mechanical flexibility (Fig. 1B)11,12. Despite their promising potential, soft piezoionics face significant challenges, including limited mechanosensitivity and short-term pressure-induced piezovoltages under prolonged or strong pressure simulation. This phenomenon stems from an inherent compromise between mechanical robustness and piezoionic performance: soft materials readily establish a pronounced pressure gradient under stress, whereas stiff materials resist forming a significant pressure gradient.

Mechanically weak piezoionics fail to render the contribution to continuous ionic current production in response to mechanical forces, along with mechanical failure under persistent loading. Whereas the mechanosensitivity and ionic transmission may be sluggish in mechanically strong piezoionic gels owing to the weak pressure gradient caused by stress concentration. Therefore, a persistent challenge lies in balancing the structural requirements and molecular designs in soft piezoionics for integrating prominent mechanosensitivity, ionic transmission, and mechanical robustness to produce continuous, stabilized pressure-driven ion flux. Advances in strategies for establishing or designing the synergistic methodology from molecular and structural engineering perspectives have contributed to an explosion of personalized polymer gel13–15. Despite the supportive contributions of mechanical performances in realistic application scenarios, its synergism with functionality remains largely unknown owing to the incompatibility or mismatch between accessible performances and functionalities. To this end, we envisioned that the synergistic designs in molecular and structural engineering are an ideal orthogonal manufacturing technology to make mechanosensitive, mechanically strong piezoionic gels.

Herein, we have succeeded in developing a mechanically robust piezoionic eutectogels (PIEgels) for continuous piezoionicity-driven ionic currents via mechanically activated cation channels, employing Na+ as pressure-driven ion flux to generate piezovoltage. Structurally, PIEgels present hierarchically interpenetrating networks engineered via mechanical training, accelerating ionic conduction via directional transport of ion steaming and improving ionic transmission via lower spatial barriers in hierarchical networks. Molecularly, conductive polymer poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) doped with poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT:PSS) is regarded as a charge-compensated receptor of NaCl to improve mechanosensitivity, contributing to diffusion and inactivation kinetics of Na+ transmission. When applying pressure, the Cl−-to-hole binding in PEDOT skeletons substantially promotes the diffusion kinetics of Na+, resulting in considerable mechanosensitivity; whereas the Na+-to-(−SO3–) binding in PSS chains facilitates inactivation kinetics, leading to the continuous piezovoltage when removing the pressure. Benefiting from efficient synergistic effect in molecular and structural engineering, i.e., hierarchical structure and charge compensation mechanism, the resulting PIEgels combine enhanced mechanical robustness and piezoionic effects (peak piezovoltage of 40 mV at 3.0 MPa), and serve as continuous pressure-driven ion migration to generate piezoionic output of ~200 s.

Results

Molecular and structural design of mechanically robust conductive PIEgels

A key surprising feature of the transmembrane transport of ions in cutaneous mechanoreceptors is the conversion of mechanical stimulus acting on the skin into electrophysiological signals propagating to central nervous system, providing inspiration for electromechanical coupling in soft piezoionics (Fig. 1A, B). Following this transduction mechanism, we fabricated a mechanically robust, conductive PIEgels that produces continuous electric power via ion streaming relied on mechanical force. We utilized conductive polymer PEDOT:PSS-supported conductivity to construct mixed ionic–electronic conductors, i.e., PIEgels, which may provide an accessible pathway for facilitating pressure-driven ion migrations. The mechanically robust PIEgels are fabricated via mechanical training, which incorporates deep eutectic solvent (DES) as substituted solvent within semicrystalline PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel to conduct mechanical training for building hierarchical structure.

Particularly, the PVA were dissolved in PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution to fabricate PVA/PEDOT:PSS precursor. We firstly used the freezing-thawing and subsequent salting-out treatment to synthesize isotropic single-network PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel (SNH) cross-linked via hydrogen bonding. Then, we used DES, composed of glycerol (Gly) and choline chloride (ChCl) in a molar ratio of 2:1, to carry out solvent displacement of PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel for transforming into a single-network eutectogel (SNE). Because of abundant and strong hydrogen bonding nanocrystalline domains, PVA/PEDOT:PSS eutectogel embodies mechanical hysteresis, and allows mechanical training to be completed with only single pre-stretching. The SNE adopts reorientation of PVA nanofibrils to form a hierarchical network driven by external uniaxial force during the mechanical training. To further lock the aligned nanofibrils in the perpendicular to the stretching direction, we introduced a chemically cross-linked second network in SNE, i.e., P(AAm-co-AAc), into mechanically trained eutectogel to synthesize a double-network eutectogels (DNE) using UV-initiated radical polymerization, which features hierarchically interpenetrating network (Fig. 1C). More importantly, the pressure-driven ion migrations in hierarchically interpenetrated PIEgels, no longer like piezoionic hydrogels, but are orientated the transmission pathway. That is, the ion currents migrate between hierarchical nanofibrils and does not spread out in space, thus addressing the trade-off between mechanical robustness and piezoionic effect (Fig. 1D).

To our surprise, by synergistically deploying solvent substitution treatment and subsequent mechanical training process, weak and soft PVA hydrogel with 20 wt% PEDOT:PSS, treated with sodium chloride (20PP–Na), with Young’s modulus of 0.64 ± 0.04 MPa, fracture strength of 1.49 ± 0.04 MPa, and work of rupture of 3.45 ± 0.33 MJ m–3, is facilely transformed into SNE (20PP–SNE) via solvent substitution, with Young’s modulus of 15.61 ± 0.62 MPa, fracture strength of 8.16 ± 0.99 MPa and work of rupture of 29.56 ± 1.00 MJ m–3, and is significantly reinforced into strong DNE (20PP–DNE) via mechanical training and covalent network, with Young’s modulus of 21.72 ± 1.50 MPa, fracture strength of 23.53 ± 0.78 MPa and work of rupture of 42.39 ± 1.60 MJ m–3 (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1). From the perspective of conductivity, we demonstrated that PIEgels leverage the ion-electron coupling effect between PEDOT:PSS and ions (i.e., Na+ and Cl−) to promote ion exchange and facilitate electrical conductivity, resulting in exceptional electrical conductivity of 0.164 ± 0.0276 S cm–1 in comparison with the eutectogels without NaCl or without PEDOT:PSS. The primary assignment of PEDOT:PSS is not to provide electronic conductivity, but to facilitate ionic conductivity (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 2)16,17.

By integrating conductive polymer and progressive manufacturing procedures, the resultant PIEgels exhibit satisfactory mechanical properties and conductivity, contributing to the orientated, continuous pressure-driven ion steaming for producing piezovoltage output, with peak piezovoltage of averaged 40 mV at 3.0 MPa. (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast to previously reported piezoionic hydrogels, the resultant PIEgels achieve a harmonious integration of mechanical robustness and piezoionic performance, thereby resolving the inherent conflict between these two properties (Fig. 1H, I and Supplementary Table 1). The significant mechanical properties coexist with instantaneous piezoionicities within piezoionic gels in hopes of achieving neuromodulation devices, biohybrid machines, and energy harvesters10,18,19.

Mechanical properties and strengthening mechanism of PIEgels

We first investigated the in-depth mechanistic insight of considerably reinforced mechanical performance into the progressive manufacturing procedures, i.e., salting out, solvent substituting, mechanical training and chemical network, with the goal of network structure–mechanical performance correlation using wide-angle X-ray scattering (WAXS), small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. We evaluated the mechanical properties of various gels with different contents of PEDOT:PSS treated by salting out, i.e., 0%, 10% and 20%, with the nomenclature 0PP, 10PP and 20PP, respectively.

Notably, pure freeze-thawed PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel fails to carry out tensile experiment attributed to poor mechanical performances. Obviously, 20PP–Na exhibits a 2.4-fold-higher modulus, 1.4-fold-higher strength and 2.0-fold-higher work of fracture in comparison to 0PP–Na, which indicate that the moduli, strengths and work of fracture increased with the extension of PEDOT:PSS (Fig. 2A, B). This is attributed to chemical bonding in the −SO3− group of PEDOT:PSS and the –OH of PVA, which is consistent with network structure (Supplementary Fig. 4) and FTIR analysis (Supplementary Note 1)20. Structure-wise, the strong diffraction peak at 2θ = 19.5° in 1D WAXS profiles, corresponding to the typical reflection plane of (101¯) in semicrystalline PVA, indicates the higher PVA crystallization (Supplementary Fig. 6)21,22. Compared to the porous structure of 20PP, the SEM image indicates that the salting-out 20PP–Na demonstrates the anisotropic structure without only pore walls. This structural transformation originates from the strongly induced aggregation and crystallization of PVA during the salting-out process, mediated by phase separation (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8)23.

Similar trend is observed in the SNE without mechanical training, that is, the moduli, strengths and work of fracture of SNE gradually increased with the extension of PEDOT:PSS. Particularly, 20PP–SNE merely demonstrates a 1.2-fold-higher modulus, 1.0-fold-higher strength and 1.2-fold-higher work of fracture in comparison to 0PP–SNE (Fig. 2D, E). Diffraction peaks at 22.5°, belonged to (200) plane of PVA, appear in WAXS intensity plots after the DES substitution, indicating the enhanced crystallinity, and resulting in the formation of nanocrystalline domains (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 8). While potent, this processing fails to exhibit strong sensitivity to the PEDOT:PSS contents in comparison to salting-out hydrogels, which result from that DES primarily interacts with PVA to form nanocrystals, rather than PEDOT:PSS during solvent displacement24,25. That is, DES substitution-induced the nanocrystalline domains become the dominant dissipative energy, resulting in the reinforced mechanical properties in comparison to salting-out hydrogels.

Subsequently, mechanical training builds a hierarchical structure, which is verified by hierarchical alignment of nanofibrous observed in SEM images and SAXS. (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. 7). Additionally, mechanical training substantially reinforces mechanical performances of SNE, especially in terms of fracture strength, whereas results in the decrease in stretchability (Supplementary Fig. 9). To advance this disadvantage, we further introduced a chemically cross-linked second network via immersed in DES precursor containing monomer, i.e., P(AAm-co-AAc), to form DNE for enhancing mechanical properties. In particular, 20PP–DNE exhibits a 1.3-fold-higher modulus, 1.8-fold-higher strength and 4.4-fold-higher work of fracture in comparison to 0PP–DNE (Fig. 3G, H). Meanwhile, WAXS demonstrates that compared to SNE, DNE presents enhanced crystallinity, indicating that DES precursor solution further induces the formation of nanocrystalline domains. This immersion process presumably facilitated the enhanced bonding between PVA and PSS, thereby contributing to the significant mechanical improvement. Structurally, DNE has a hierarchically interpenetrated double-network, resulting in no obvious nanofibrous orientation (Fig. 2I and Supplementary Fig. 10). Compared with SNE, the enhanced mechanical property of DNE reveals the advantages of the hierarchically interpenetrated networks.

We further analyzed the average size of crystallites by fitting SAXS profiles, which affords a radius of gyration (Rg) of ~7.0 nm for 20PP–DNE, that is, nanocrystalline dimension (D) of ~7.0 nm (Fig. 2J, K). According to the SAXS profiles plotted with Lorentz correction, the interdomain spacing (L) is obviously reduced in progressive manufacturing procedures, revealed from the shift of qmax to the high-q region (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Fig. 2K)26,27. Taken together, these observations allow us to confirm the nanocrystalline morphology: salting out and solvent substituting significantly facilitate the nucleation and refinement of nanocrystalline domains, and mechanical training constructs long-range ordered nanocrystalline domains as constructing the hierarchical structures. Collectively, combined with the described above in mechanical performances and structural evolution, these experimental findings allow us to draw conclusions: structural transitions occur at multiple length scales including hierarchically interpenetrated networks, hierarchical nanofibrils and nanocrystalline domains, and intermolecular interactions resulting in energy dissipation multimechanisms from molecular to mesoscopic scales, and contributing to the improved mechanical performance of DNE, in terms of strength, stretchability and toughness (Fig. 3L and Supplementary Note 1).

Ionic transport resistances and ion-electron coupling of PIEgels

Having elucidated the mechanical properties behind the structure reconfiguration, we proceeded to investigate how DES as solvents and PEDOT:PSS as conductive polymer affect the electrochemical performances of PIEgels. We engineered PIEgels via incorporating electrically conductive and ionically conductive components into a gel matrix to achieve piezovoltage generation and efficient transmission under the pressure stimulus. Therefore, we investigated ionic transport resistances and ion-electron coupling using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements.

We fabricated a series of coin batteries assembled by PIEgels electrolyte (Size: 12 mm diameter × 2 mm thickness) and Zn anode/cathode as blocking electrode for electrochemical measurements (Fig. 3A). In ion-electron mixed PIEgels, electronic and ionic charge carriers do not display the same polarization phenomena, neither the same transport kinetics. EIS allows identifying the contributions of those two charge carriers to the ionic conductivity using established equivalent circuit analysis, involving electronic conduction pathway and ionic conduction pathway (Fig. 3B). EIS plots show two semicircles: one depressed semicircle at lower frequency and a segment of a greater semicircle within the higher frequency region, which indicates two kinds of conduction, respectively, electronic and ionic28,29. The impedance of PIEgels is measured over a frequency range of 10–1–10–6 Hz and the EIS analysis is represented in the form of Nyquist plots and Bode plots. In the Randles circuit model, the electronic and ionic resistance of PIEgels (Re and Ri) shows high correlations with PEDOT:PSS. In contrast to 0PP–DNE, the Re and Ri of 10PP–DNE increase, whereas they substantially decrease for 20PP–DNE, suggesting the ion-electron coupling. Furthermore, Nyquist plots and Bode plots of 20PP–DNE exhibit the characteristic electrochemical behavior of ionic conductor, with notable lower resistance and higher σi indicating exceptional ionic transmission (Fig. 3C–H and Supplementary Fig. 13).

These equivalent circuit outcomes reaffirm that ionic conduction is the predominant factor in the PIEgels, reflecting in the fact that the conductivity of PIEgels is dominated by ionic conductivity. As the PEDOT:PSS content increases, the Re and Ri of the eutectogel exhibits a markedly coupling than that of the hydrogel, while Re and Ri initially increased and subsequently decreased. Conversely, upon transformation of the eutectogel into a hydrogel via solvent exchange, Re and Ri plummets (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). These findings highlight the predominant influence of the PEDOT:PSS and DES on Re and Ri. While the intrinsic ionic conductivity of DES may contribute marginally, the observed dramatic fluctuations of Re and Ri is more likely attributable to the ion-electron coupling effect. Specifically, ion-exchange between PEDOT:PSS and Na+ or Cl−. The PEDOT facilitates Cl− transport through hole-polaron interactions, while the −SO3– on PSS chains electrostatically interact with Na+, synergistically enabling ion exchange that significantly delivers an order-of-magnitude-improved electrical performance (Fig. 3J). This distinction arises because Na+ originates solely from NaCl pairs, while Cl− derives from both NaCl pairs and the ChCl component of the DES. (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. 16). The strongly negative ΔGIE (−317.7 kJ mol−1) signifies highly favorable ion exchange kinetics, likely enabled by Cl− and Na+ acting as charge carriers within the PIEgels16,17.

Additionally, we also investigated the electrochemical stability based on CV curves, scan rate of 100 mV s−1 (Fig. 3K, Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Compared with other eutectogels and hydrogels, 20PP–DNE exhibits negligible changes in repeated CV curves, suggesting superior electrochemical stability against charging and discharging 200 cycles, and the stable ion-electron coupling formed via the ion-exchange reaction. Furthermore, 20PP–DNE has a higher charge storage capacity in comparison to 10PP–DNE and 0 PP–DNE, suggesting highly advantageous in flexible electronic devices and applications30,31.

Piezoionic performances and piezoionic coefficients of PIEgels

Piezovoltage is identified as the pressure-induced ion streaming potentials, probably resulting from a preferential displacement of the cation (Na+) over anion (Cl–) between hierarchical nanofibrils and resulting in a positive voltage output for PIEgels. We designed an indentation experiment to apply mechanical force stimulations in which the applied pressure and generated piezovoltage were measured. Applying force is analogous to pressing into skin using a finger or other object. As shown in Fig. 4A, a single PIEgels unit consists of two separated electrodes, attached to the force sensor on the premise of electrostatic shielding, and applied mechanical forces via the linear motors. Importantly, the pressure region covers the piezoelectric to avoid stress concentration and uniform piezoionic ion diffusion in the whole bulk. Interestingly, PEDOT:PSS has an effect on differential ion flows, with a positive or negative correlation via ion exchange, which has been confirmed by conductivity and electrochemical measurements. Therefore, we primarily attributed the piezoionic behavior to the electron-conducting-assisted ion transmission, integrated the hierarchical network, probably resulting in a rapid hierarchical migration of Na+ (Fig. 4B). Indeed, piezovoltage originates from the diffusivity difference of Na+ ions and Cl– ions under pressure. The greater the diffusivity difference of cation and anion, the higher the mechanosensitivity and piezovoltage10,11.

We observed the piezovoltage output by continuously applying step force stimulations. Particularly, the mechanosensitivity (S) during applying pressure and inactivation time (T) during removing pressure of 20PP–DNE, respectively, shows 1457 mV s–1 and 2.569 s, corresponding to piezoionic coefficient of 9.7 nV Pa–1 and piezovoltage (△V) of 30 mV (Fig. 4C, D and Supplementary Fig. 19A). The S and T of 10PP–DNE, respectively, is 9.8 mV s–1 and 0.841 s, corresponding to piezoionic coefficient of 1.9 nV Pa–1 and △V of 5.8 mV (Fig. 4E, F and Supplementary Fig. 19B). Whereas the S and T of 0PP–DNE respectively is 900 mV s–1 and 0.485 s, corresponding to piezoionic coefficient of 2.6 nV Pa–1 and △V of 7.9 mV (Fig. 4G, H and Supplementary Fig. 19C). These experimental findings confirms that 20 wt% PEDOT:PSS substantially promotes the piezoionic ion diffusion kinetics in applying pressure, accompanied by substantial ion inactivation kinetics after removing pressure, whereas 10 wt% hinders ion diffusion kinetics, in comparison to PIEgels without PEDOT:PSS. This correlation further confirms that PEDOT:PSS is capable of altering ionic conduction via ion-exchange reaction, which is dependent on the concentration (Supplementary Fig. 20). Furthermore, the variation trend of piezoionic performance with the PEDOT:PSS concentration is consistent with the electrochemical properties.

To further manifest piezoionic coefficients in response to pressure of PIEgels, we further measured the piezovoltage output performances under different pressures. In comparison to the covered force stimulus for piezoionic ion diffusion in the whole bulk, the localized pressure to manifesting piezoionic coefficients (Fig. 4I). The piezoionic coefficient (α) is the slope of the pressure (△P) and piezovoltage (△V) curves. Under a small pressure input of 0–1 MPa, the magnitude of piezovoltage is almost zero, indicating that no voltage is generated. A positive close-to-linear correlation between △P and △V under pressure input 1–5 MPa, with the higher degree of pressure was accompanied by a rapid increase in the piezovoltage output, from 0 to 47 mV, and piezoionic coefficient of 10.9 nV Pa–1. As the pressure continues to increase, the piezovoltage increases slowly with a low piezoionic coefficient of 1.44 nV Pa–1 (Fig. 4J, K, Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22). These findings have important implications for our understanding of the trade-off between mechanical robustness and piezoionic effect—that is, the hierarchical network not only supports the mechanical properties, but also orients the ion transport pathways–that is, along hierarchical nanofibrous rather than in space, reducing the energy barrier for ion transmission.

Structural and molecular determinant of piezoionic mechanism

Given the concept of synergistic design, the piezoionic mechanism of pressure-induced piezovoltage in PIEgels is derived from structural advantages and molecular coupling. The mechanosensitivity (S) during pressure application reflects the piezoionic activation kinetic, while the inactivation time (T) upon pressure release characterizes their inactivation kinetics.

Structurally, the hierarchically nanofibrous network constructed achieves the confinement of pressure-driven ion flux, producing the uniform orientation of ion migrations. Compared with isotropic three-dimensional networks, this structure potentially reduces the spatial energy barrier for ion migration, thereby facilitating ion transmission. Compared to non-hierarchical PIEgels, those with hierarchical structures exhibit a 3.6-fold increase in ∆V, a 3.5-fold enhancement in piezoionic coefficient, and a 2.2-fold improvement in S. However, they require a 22-fold longer T. These findings indicate that the hierarchical architecture facilitates greater ion migration, producing the optimized inactivation kinetics. Molecularly, to elucidate the role of DES on piezoionic performance, we immersed PIEgels in both NaCl solution and water, transforming them into hydrogels. Notably, this transition leads to a substantial deterioration in piezoionic properties, with a more pronounced reduction observed in water. These findings unequivocally demonstrate the dominant role of DES in maintaining piezoionic activation and inactivation kinetics, consistent with its function in electrochemical properties. Furthermore, these observations may suggest a beneficial role of Na+ in piezoionic performance (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 23). Therefore, we conducted comparative experiments that revealed significantly inferior piezoionic performances in eutectogels containing either NaCl or PEDOT:PSS alone, compared to the integrated PIEgels. Notably, NaCl-free and PEDOT:PSS-free eutectogels exhibit dramatically shortened the T, with the 49-fold and 5.3-fold reduction compared to PIEgels, respectively. These compelling evidences establish the pivotal role of ion-electron coupling in governing piezoionic activation and inactivation kinetics, contributing to exceptional, continuous piezoionic output (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 24).

The ion diffusion mechanism in electrical doping has been clearly revealed that the electronic gating occurs via Na+/Cl– inserted or removed from PEDOT:PSS-binding site, in which Na+ and Cl– are the charge-compensating species of PEDOT:PSS32,33.To further elucidate the ion-electron coupling effect, we performed DFT calculations that revealed strong bonding interactions between PEDOT and Cl− (‒142.7 kJ mol‒1), as well as between PSS and Na+ (‒309.1 kJ mol‒1). In contrast, the bonding energies of PEDOT‒Na and PSS‒Cl are only as low as ‒36.4 kJ mol‒1 and ‒37.9 kJ mol‒1, respectively. These computational results demonstrate the existence of a robust charge compensation via the ion-exchange reaction between NaCl and PEDOT:PSS. Furthermore, the PSS‒PVA interaction exhibits a binding energy (‒135.5 kJ mol‒1) second only to the PEDOT‒PSS bonding (‒173.6 kJ mol‒1), and approximately twice that of PVA‒PEDOT (‒63.9 kJ mol‒1). This pronounced energy differential establishes PSS as a preferential interfacial intermediary, bridging PVA chains and PEDOT backbones to form pressure-responsive hierarchal networks (Figs. 3J and 5D–F, Supplementary Figs. 26–28). Further explorations of structure transitions using Raman spectroscopy provide solid evidence for investigating the conformational transitions of PEDOT from a benzenoid structure (disordered coil conformation) to a quinoid structure (ordered linear conformation) before and after applying pressure. Additionally, XPS analysis reveals a significant increase in the binding energy of Na+ and Cl− ions under applied pressure, demonstrating the electron transport chain propagation via ion-exchange reactions34–36. Collectively, these findings indicate that the ion-electron coupling effect enables dynamic charge compensation via ion exchange between PEDOT:PSS and Na+/Cl−, while simultaneously boosting the pressure-driven ion activation and inactivation kinetics.

Comprehensively, these observations allow us to draw a clear demonstration of the molecular origins of piezoionicity in PIEgels, namely, hierarchical network orientation and charge compensation mechanism (Fig. 5K). On the one hand, hierarchical architecture facilitates greater ion migration by lowering the spatial energy barriers of ion transport. On the other hand, the charge compensation mechanism dynamically modulates ionic diffusion kinetics by leveraging pressure. The charge compensation mechanism operates through an ion-exchange reaction. Additionally, although the ion-exchange reaction is thermodynamically spontaneous, sufficient concentrations of both ions and electrons are required to ensure favorable reaction kinetics. Mechanistically, Cl–, as the main charge-compensating species of the holes on the PEDOT backbone, extends the electronic transmission chain and further accelerates Na+-ion transmission in response to an applied pressure. Additionally, nanoconfined Cl– ions struggle to migrate, providing a significant enhancement in the diffusivity difference between Na+ ions and Cl– ions, and contributing to high-performance piezovoltage output. Whereas removing pressure, Na+ binding sites on the −SO3– moieties on the PSS chains are activating to retard Na+-ion reverse migration, thereby facilitating ion inactivation kinetics and further guaranteeing the continuous pressure-driven piezovoltage output.

Piezoionic diffusion kinetics via molecular dynamics simulation

The piezoionic effect arises from pressure-driven ionic mobility difference of cations and anions. Previous reports have also shown that the mobile ions are intrinsically subject to a hydrodynamic drag force and the counterions' mobility, leading to a gradual quenching of the piezoionicity even if the action time of force is prolonged. At this point, the mobile ions are either not migrating or are balanced by counterion migration10,11. Therefore, to investigate the underlying mechanism of continuous piezoionicity, we employed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to gain mechanistic insights into piezoionic diffusion kinetics of PIEgels (Fig. 6A, B). The simulation systems in our work contain 10 PVA chains, 10 PEDOT:PSS chains, 30 NaCl molecules and sufficient DES molecules (light cyan region) (Supplementary Fig. 36). Furthermore, informed by the piezoionic mechanism—which relies on the collaboration between hierarchical structure and charge compensation—we computationally designed and realized a simulation system featuring such a hierarchical architecture. Another critical contribution is charge compensation arising from ion-exchange reactions; therefore, we performed MD simulations with varying numbers of NaCl to probe reaction kinetics. As the number of NaCl molecules increased from 30 to 60, Na+ and Cl− ions migrate simultaneously, yet with temporally alternating mobility, resulting in a partial offset of their opposing contributions. These simulation results indicate a kinetically unfavorable ion-exchange reaction due to the increased ion number, thereby disrupting the charge compensation mechanism (Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38).

As indicated by the square and oval regions in Fig. 6A, the applied pressure facilitates rapid interlayer migration of Na+ ions (purple) during the initial 0–2.5 ns timespan, whereas their motion slows markedly upon pressure release within 2.5–5 ns. This transport phenomenon attests to the highly asymmetric ion diffusion kinetics within PIEgels, underlying the continuous piezoionicity (Supplementary Note 2). During compression, the MSD of both Na+ and Cl− ions was markedly higher than that observed during pressure release, clearly demonstrating accelerated ion migration under applied mechanical stress (Fig. 6C). To further elucidate the characteristics of piezionic diffusion kinetics, we quantified the diffusion coefficient through mean-squared-displacement (MSD) calculations. Specifically, we investigated the piezoionic kinetics by monitoring the time-dependent diffusion coefficients. During the initial 0–1 ns period, the diffusion coefficients of both Na+ and Cl− ions remain near-zero, indicating that the applied pressure is insufficient to drive ion differential migration. The absence of ∆D between the cationic and anionic diffusion coefficients precludes the piezovoltage generation. Subsequently, as the applied pressure is progressively increased over a timescale of 1–2 ns, the acceleration of ion transmission is accompanied by a rapid increase in diffusion coefficients, contributing to a progressively enlarging ∆D, with 0.38 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 at 2 ns (i.e., 1.60 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 for Na+ ions and 1.22 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 for Cl− ions). During the final 2–2.5 ns period, ion differential migration decelerates despite further increases in pressure, accompanied by a concomitant reduction in ∆D with 0.18 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 at 2.5 ns (i.e., 1.84 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 for Na+ ions and 1.66 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 for Cl− ions). In summary, the piezoionic dynamics observed in our MD simulations are in agreement with the experimentally measured piezovoltage and piezoionic coefficients across varying pressures, as shown in Fig. 4J. Following the release of pressure at 2.5–5 ns, ion migration decelerated markedly, accompanied by a substantial decrease in ionic diffusion coefficients. Interestingly, however, the diffusion coefficient of Cl− ions (i.e., 0.19 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 at 4.0 ns) exceeded that of Na+ (i.e., 0.15 × 10–12 cm–2 s–1 at 4.0 ns), indicating preferential confinement of Na+ ions after releasing the pressure, a finding consistent with both experimental spectroscopies and DFT calculations (Fig. 6D, E).

Collectively, MD simulation further corroborates piezoionic characteristics and the underlying synergistic mechanism for PIEgels, demonstrating the pronounced asymmetry in the diffusion kinetics for decoupling the continuous pressure-driven ion migration. Such behavior—where the electromechanical coupling is activated only under sufficient pressure stimulation—stems from the synergistic effects of well-organized ion migration within a hierarchical structure and efficient charge compensation between ion and electron. Finally, we leveraged PIEgels to construct a prototype device that generates piezovoltage under mechanical stimulation, highlighting amenability as a modular component for self-powered devices and monitoring movements (Fig. 6F and Supplementary Note 3).

Discussion

It is counterintuitive that mechanically robust polymer gels allow for generating efficient piezovoltage output owing to negligible pressure gradients. Piezoionicity is usually constructed in soft hydrogels leveraging pressure-driven ion steaming. However, when considered further, we interrogated whether this applies to mechanically strong gels. The ion transmission randomly occurs in the interspace of a three-dimensional network for piezoionic hydrogels, in which ion migration, failing to be restrained, has to overcome the space barriers. To our surprise, the piezovoltage output was not limited to a completely disordered polymer network. Conversely, we confirmed that piezoionicity can also occur in an ordered hierarchical structure, providing an approach for designing mechanically strong piezoionic materials. In this study, we have investigated mechanically robust PIEgels with hierarchical nanofibrous structures for achieving a mechanosensitive, continuous response to innocuous pressure, in which the seamless compatibility of mechanical and piezoionic properties is engineered via synergistic design in molecular and structural engineering.

Structurally, we have constructed a hierarchically interpenetrated nanofibrous network using mechanical training, enabling the energy dissipation mechanisms and pressure-driven ion steaming. Molecularly, we have employed PEDOT:PSS as a conductive polymer, offering a charge-compensated receptor with Na+ and Cl–. We proposed the mechanistic picture of piezoionic response in PIEgels, where Cl– ion, as the main charge-compensating species of the holes on the PEDOT backbone under applied pressure, struggles to extend electron transmission chains for accelerating Na+ migrations, thereby amplifying the diffusivity difference between Na+ and Cl–. Whereas releasing pressure, Na+ binding sites on the −SO3– moieties on the PSS chains are activated to hinder Na+-ion reverse movement, enhancing ion inactivation kinetics. This structural advantage and charge compensation mechanism contribute asymmetric ion transport kinetics, providing an important implication for regulating the pressure-driven ion diffusion and inactivation kinetics.

Additionally, we have highlighted that the piezovoltage output of PIEgels exhibits a time-dependent response, achieving continuous pressure-induced ion steaming, thus addressing the critical need for high-performance piezoionic device construction and boosting next-generation ion-steaming power sources, and potentially allowing complex mechanical surroundings for self-powering motion detecting and energy harvesting.

Methods

Preparation of DES

Glycerol (Gly, J&K Scientific Ltd, 99%) as a hydrogen bond donor and choline chloride (ChCl, J&K Scientific Ltd, 98%) as a hydrogen bond acceptor were mixed in a predetermined molar ratio of 2:1, and followed by stirring for 1 h at 60 °C and sonicating for 30 min (Branson M1800) until achieving homogeneity and transparency.

Preparation of DES-based covalently precursor solution

The predetermined monomer containing acrylamide (AAm, J&K Scientific Ltd, 99%) and acrylic acid (AAc, J&K Scientific Ltd, 98%), covalent cross-linker N, N’-methylenebisacrylamide (MBAA, J&K Scientific Ltd, 98%) and photo-initiator 2-Hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)−2-methylpropiophenone (Irgacure 2959, Sigma Aldrich, 98%) were mixed with above-described DES by stirring for 3 h at 60 °C, followed by bubble removal with an ultrasonic bath (Branson M1800) for 30 min. The obtained solution was the as-prepared DES-based covalent precursor solutions.

Fabrication of physically single-network PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel

Firstly, PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution (Clevios PH1000, Heraeus) was filtered by a PTFE syringe-driven filter (0.45 μm, Jet Bio-Filtration Co., Ltd.) to make 10 wt% and 20 wt% aqueous solutions under vigorous stirring. Then, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, Sigma Aldrich, 99 + % hydrolyzed) powder was dissolved in PEDOT:PSS aqueous solution under vigorous stirring with an oil bath at 90 °C for 2 h to make a 10 wt% solution. After cooling to room temperature, the obtained solution was poured into a glass mold with a thickness of 2 mm, and was frozen first in a refrigerator at −20 °C for 24 h to synthesis prefabricated PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel. After that, the resultant sample is thawed at 40 °C for 3 h. The thawed sample is refrozen in a refrigerator at −20 °C for 24 h and thawed again at 40 °C for 3 h to repeat six times freeze-thawing process. Finally, the resultant freeze-thawed sample was immersed in was soaked in 2 M NaCl solution for 24 h to obtain PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel.

Fabrication of mechanically trained DNE

The PVA/PEDOT:PSS hydrogel prepared above was immersed in preprepared DES at 25 °C at the scheduled time of 6 h to conduct solvent substitution, and formed a single-network PVA/PEDOT:PSS eutectogel. To fabricate the prestreched PVA/PEDOT:PSS eutectogel, the PVA/PEDOT:PSS eutectogel was stretched to controlled strain of 150% and maintained for 10 min. After that, the prestreched PVA/PEDOT:PSS eutectogel was further immersed preprepared covalently precursor DES solutions containing AAm and AAc of 4 M as monomer, 0.01 mol% MBAA, and 0.01 mol% I-2959 at 30 °C for 24 h. The covalent network of eutectogel was formed by UV light-initiated radical polymerization with a wavelength of 365 nm, and completed within 10 min, to obtain mechanically trained DNE.

Fabrication of mechanically trained double-network hydrogel

The mechanically trained DNE prepared above were immersed preprepared 2 M NaCl solution and pure water at 30 °C for 24 h to conduct solvent exchange, to obtain a mechanically trained double-network hydrogel.

Characterization of eutectogels

The morphologies and elemental mapping of various gels were characterized on a SEM (FESEM, QUANTA FEG 650, FEI, USA), equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray analysis system. XPS (K-Alpha X, Thermal Scientific, USA) was used to determine the element composition of the various gels. The chemical components of gels were investigated by FTIR spectra using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, V70, Brooker, Germany). The chemical structure of PEDOT:PSS was analyzed by Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba, Japan).

Temperature-variable IR measurement

For sample preparation, a small quantity of gel was placed between two ZnSe tablets and compressed into an ultrathin film for transmission IR measurement on a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer. The tablets were sealed with Parafilm and subjected to a temperature gradient from 20 to 200 °C at intervals of 10 °C for the acquisition of temperature-variable IR spectra.

Two-dimensional correlation spectroscopy

The temperature-variable transmission FTIR spectra of various polymer gels, acquired from 20 to 200 °C, were used for performing 2D correlation analysis. 2D correlation analysis was carried out using the software, 2D Shige ver. 1.3 (©Shigeaki Morita, Kwansei Gakuin University, Japan, 2004–2005) and further plotted into contour maps by Origin Pro 2021. In the contour maps, red colors are defined as positive intensities, while blue colors as negative ones.

Mechanical properties characterization

The mechanical properties of the polymer gels were mainly evaluated via standard stress-strain experiments by using a testing machine (Shimadzu AGS-X-5000 N). The whole sample is machined into a cylindrical shape for tensile tests. Both ends of the dumbbell-shaped sample were connected to the clamps, with the lower clamp fixed. The testing rate was set at 50 mm min−1. Young’s modulus (E [MPa]) was determined from the initial slope of the stress-strain curves. Fracture strengths (Ef [MPa]) were appointed to represent the stresses at which the ultimate tensile failure occurs in the uniaxial tensile test. Toughness (Γ [MJ m−3]), a mechanical parameter that characterizes the work required to fracture the sample per unit, was obtained by integrating the area below the stress-strain curve until fracture. Each mechanical test was repeated with at least three individual samples.

All electrochemical measurements were carried out on a Potentiostat/Galvanostat/Zero resistance ammeter (Interface 1000E, Gamry) at room temperature (25 °C). MD simulation

The PVA chains and PEDOT:PSS chains were constructed using Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) software, consisting of 30 and 6 repeating units, respectively. The simulation system containing 10 PVA chains and 10 PEDOT:PSS chains was established using VMD software37, along with 30 NaCl molecules. The DES solvent contents were maintained consistent with the experimental conditions. The size of the simulation box is 16.68 nm (X direction) × 5.98 nm (Y direction) × 9.00 nm (Z direction). All MD simulations were carried out using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) software package38, employing the OPLS-AA force field applicable to choline chloride-based DESs. And the OPLS-AA force field parameters were adopted from Professor Orlando Acevedo at the University of Miami39. Prior to the formal simulation, the simulation system underwent a two-stage equilibration process: Firstly, the 1 ns relaxation under the NPT ensemble at 298.15 K; Subsequently, the 2 ns relaxation under the NVT ensemble at the same temperature, to achieve a stable and physically reasonable configuration. In a typical MD simulation procedure, two graphene plates, as a pressure applicator, each 5.5 nm in length, were placed at the upper and lower boundaries along the Z direction of the simulation box. A constant velocity of 1 nm ns−1 was applied to the graphene plates along the Z direction to simulate the compression of the PIEgels, which lasted for 2.5 ns. Afterward, the external pressure was removed, and the simulation system was allowed to evolve freely for an additional 2.5 ns. The integration time step was set to 1 fs throughout the simulation. Of note, periodic boundary conditions (PCBs) were applied in the X and Y directions, while the Z direction employed non-PCBs.
