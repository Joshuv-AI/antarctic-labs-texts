Real‐World Comparison of Dual Checkpoint Blockade and Immunotherapy Plus TKIs in Metastatic RCC
Authors: Kojiro Ohba, Masaharu Oki, Tsuyoshi Matsuda, Haruka Miyata, Hiroshi Kikuchi, Toru Inoue, Toshitaka Shin, Yuya Sekine, Shintaro Narita, Sei Naito, Norihiko Tsuchiya, Hirohito Naito, Mikio Sugimoto, Kazuyuki Numakura, Shin Kobayashi, Kosuke Ueda, Tsukasa Igawa, Kensuke Mitsunari, Tomohiro Matsuo, Ryoichi Imamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo compare differences in clinical outcomes between dual immune checkpoint blockade and immunotherapy plus tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) as first‐line treatment for metastatic renal cell carcinoma in routine clinical practice.Patients and MethodsRetrospective multicenter cohort study including 311 patients with International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium (IMDC) intermediate or poor‐risk metastatic RCC who received first‐line immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)‐based combination therapy between 2018 and 2023. Patients were treated with either ICI + ICI or ICI + TKI. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to reduce baseline imbalances. Progression‐free survival (PFS), second progression‐free survival (PFS2), overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR), and duration of response (DOR) were analyzed using weighted survival models.ResultsAfter adjustment, ICI + TKI was associated with a trend toward longer PFS compared with ICI + ICI (median 28.7 vs. 16.6 months, HR 0.69, p = 0.050). No significant differences were observed in PFS2 (median 46.6 vs. 35.5 months, HR 0.84, p = 0.436) or OS (median 57.4 vs. 57.0 months, HR 1.01, p = 0.965). ORR (56.5% vs. 52.2%, p = 0.473) and DOR (median 54.6 vs. 44.9 months, HR 0.83, p = 0.502) were comparable. Subgroup analysis suggested more noticeable PFS benefit with ICI + TKI in intermediate‐risk patients and those without central nervous system or bone metastases, whereas OS was similar across all subgroups. The incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events was comparable between groups.ConclusionIn this real‐world cohort, TKI‐containing combinations were associated with improved initial disease control, whereas no statistically significant difference in long‐term survival was observed between treatment strategies. Given the retrospective design and potential for residual confounding despite IPTW adjustment, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Treatment selection should therefore be individualized according to therapeutic priorities, such as the need for initial tumor control versus the expectation of sustained immune‐mediated benefit.

Introduction

The therapeutic landscape of metastatic renal cell carcinoma has evolved substantially with the widespread adoption of immune‐based combination strategies [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. In contemporary clinical practice, physicians commonly choose between dual immune checkpoint blockade and immunotherapy combined with targeted therapy. However, given these regimens were independently validated against sunitinib in separate randomized trials, direct comparative evidence between them remains lacking [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. This limitation is particularly relevant in real‐world settings, where patient characteristics and treatment decisions may differ from those represented in clinical trial populations [6, 7, 8].

Dual immune checkpoint inhibition is often considered capable of inducing durable responses in a subset of patients, whereas combinations incorporating tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) tend to achieve higher response rates and earlier tumor shrinkage [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Despite these differing clinical profiles, the absence of head‐to‐head comparisons makes it difficult to determine which strategy offers superior overall benefit in routine practice [13, 14].

Notably, the available evidence is derived from Phase III clinical trials that enrolled highly selected patient populations under strict eligibility criteria [6, 7]. In routine clinical practice, however, physicians frequently treat patients who would be underrepresented or excluded from clinical trials, such as those with poor performance status or non‐clear cell RCC (nccRCC) [8]. Although treatment continuation, discontinuation, and adverse event (AE) management are governed by predefined protocols in clinical trials, treatment decisions in real‐world practice are individualized and may differ substantially from trial settings. As a result, clinical outcomes observed in daily practice may not fully reflect those reported in randomized clinical trials (RCTs) [6].

In addition, there are currently no established criteria to inform optimal selection between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI for individual patients in real‐world settings. Given the absence of direct comparative trials, this uncertainty frequently complicates treatment decision‐making in routine clinical practice [15]. Therefore, evaluating these regimens solely on RCT data is insufficient; a comparative assessment in a real‐world context is essential to complement existing evidence.

Therefore, there remains a critical unmet need for direct comparative evidence to guide treatment selection between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI in routine clinical practice. To address this gap, we conducted a large multicenter real‐world study using IPTW‐adjusted analyses to directly compare the effectiveness of these two strategies.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This multicenter study, conducted as part of the Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Multi‐Institutional Project for Advanced Renal Cancer Therapy (IMPACT‐8), a collaborative study group of eight Japanese institutions, which focuses on real‐world outcomes of immune checkpoint inhibitor‐based therapies (approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions), was approved by our institutional ethics committee (Hokkaido University Hospital; approval number: SEI‐022‐0346) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its contemporary amendments. The institutional ethics committee waived any requirement for informed consent because of the retrospective nature of this observational review. All clinical and laboratory data were obtained from the medical records of patients with mRCC and deidentified.

Data from 397 patients treated with ICI‐based combination therapy as first‐line treatment at eight Japanese university hospitals between August 2018 and March 2023 were included in this study. To compare clinical outcomes between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI, we excluded patients with IMDC favorable, patients without evaluable follow‐up data because they were transferred to another hospital within 3 months after treatment initiation, and patients with missing data. Patients were included in the analysis if outcome data were available, regardless of early disease progression or death after treatment initiation. Finally, data from 311 patients were included in this analysis: 203 had been treated with ICI + ICI and 108 with ICI + TKI.

Outcome Evaluation

The treatment efficacy for tumors was evaluated by CT locally, which was performed at standard intervals of 6–12 weeks, depending on the patient's clinical condition. The treatment was continued until disease progression or an unacceptable AE occurred, and the decisions regarding discontinuation or changing the treatment regimen were made after consultation between the physician and the patient. The survival data were closed as of July 2025, and patients lost to follow‐up were censored at their last medical contact.

The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the duration from initiation of first‐line treatment to death from any cause. Other endpoints included progression‐free survival (PFS), second progression‐free survival (PFS2), objective response rate (ORR), and duration of response (DOR). PFS was defined as the duration from initiation of first‐line treatment to progression or death, and PFS2 was defined as the duration from initiation of first‐line treatment to progression of second‐line treatment or death. Patients who did not receive second‐line treatment were censored at the date of last follow‐up if they were alive without initiation of second‐line treatment. We reviewed the best overall response and classified it as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progression of disease (PD) according to the RECIST criteria (version 1.1) [16]. The ORR was defined as the proportion of patients who achieved a CR or PR. DOR was defined as the duration from the date of confirmation of objective response to disease progression or death, whichever occurred first. The classification and grading of AEs were determined using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests, whereas categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher exact tests. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model that included age, sex, IMDC risk classification, previous nephrectomy, clear cell histology, and metastatic sites (liver and central nervous system). The distribution of propensity score was examined before and after weighting (Figure S1). Using this score, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to adjust for baseline differences between treatment groups. IPTW weights were not stabilized and truncated. The median IPTW weights were 1.63 (interquartile range (IQR), 1.34–2.17), and only one patient had a weight > 10 (maximum weight of 11.1). Covariable balance before and after IPTW was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. In addition, the distribution of propensity scores by treatment group is shown in Figure S2, demonstrating adequate overlap between the two treatment groups. Survival data, including OS, PFS, PFS2, and DOR, were estimated using a Kaplan–Meier method. To evaluate trends in treatment efficacy, prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to IMDC risk category, prior nephrectomy status, histological subtype (clear cell vs. non‐clear cell), presence of central nervous system (CNS) metastasis, presence of bone metastasis, and baseline C‐reactive protein (CRP) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels. Subgroup analysis was performed using COX regression analysis to assess heterogeneity of treatment effect across subgroups. The risk estimates of survival analysis were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 19 (SAS Institute).

Results

Patient Background

The background of the 311 patients is shown in Table 1. Their mean age ± SD at the time of initiating treatment was 67.8 ± 9.9 years, and 230 (74.0%) were men. Overall, 43.1% had a prior nephrectomy, and 83.0% had ccRCC. At the time of initiating treatment, there were 195 patients in the IMDC intermediate‐risk group and 116 in the IMDC poor‐risk group. Metastasis involved the lung in 175 (56.3%) cases, lymph nodes in 118 (37.9%), bone in 93 (29.9%), and liver in 31 (10.0%). Patients in the ICI + ICI group were more likely to be male, younger, and more likely to be classified in the IMDC poor‐risk group than those in the ICI + TKI group. In this way, several baseline characteristics differed significantly between the two groups before IPTW adjustment. After IPTW adjustment, all covariables were well balanced, as indicated by SMD < 0.1 in the pseudo‐population created by the weighted sample size (Table 1 and Figure S1).

Treatment Outcomes

The median follow‐up duration in this study (time from the starting date of an individual patient's treatment to the date of clinical cut‐off) was 55.0 months (IQR 42.1–68.3 months). The follow‐up duration was 60.7 (48.2–71.7) months in the ICI + ICI group, and 43.4 (35.3–57.3) months in the ICI + TKI group. The regimen in the ICI + TKI group was pembrolizumab plus axitinib in 29 patients, avelumab plus axitinib in 22 patients, nivolumab plus cabozantinib in 20 patients, and pembrolizumab plus lenvatinib in 37 patients. Baseline characteristics of each regimen are shown in Table S1. Disease progressed in 177 patients. In the ICI + ICI group, 132 cases experienced disease progression, with 105 (79.5%) proceeding to subsequent therapy; in the ICI + TKI group, 45 cases experienced progression, and 35 (77.8%) transitioned to subsequent therapy (Table S2), and 116 patients died from any cause.

The PFS, PFS2, OS and DOR were analyzed before adjusting IPTW (Figure S3). We performed the analysis using IPTW, adjusting for patient background. Adjusted by IPTW, the median PFS tended to be longer in the ICI + TKI group than in the ICI + ICI group (median 28.7 vs. 16.6 months, HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.47–1.00, p = 0.050; Figure 1A). The median PFS2 showed no significant difference between the ICI + TKI and ICI + ICI groups (median 46.6 vs. 35.5 months, HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.55–1.29, p = 0.436; Figure 1B). The median OS was comparable between the ICI + TKI and ICI + ICI groups (median 57.4 vs. 57.0 months, HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.65–1.58, p = 0.965) (Figure 1C).

Kaplan–Meier curves of PFS (A), PFS2 (B), OS (C), and DOR (D) stratified by treatment after inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW).

There was no significant difference in ORR between the ICI + TKI and ICI + ICI groups (56.5% vs. 52.2%, p = 0.473; Figure 2). The median DOR was similar between these two groups (54.6 vs. 44.9 months, HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.47–1.44, p = 0.502; Figure 1D).

Subgroup Analysis

Next, we performed subgroup analyses to confirm the homogeneity of treatment effects across groups. According to COX regression analysis, the association between ICI + TKI treatment and longer PFS appeared to be more pronounced in the IMDC intermediate‐risk group and in patients without CNS or bone metastases (Figure 3A). However, because of the limited number of patients, these analyses are exploratory in nature and should be interpreted with caution. On the other hand, the PFS benefit in the ICI + TKI group was consistently favorable regardless of nephrectomy status, histological findings, or baseline laboratory parameters. Whereas for OS, both treatments showed comparable outcomes across all subgroup comparisons (Figure 3B).

Safety Profile

Table 2 shows the frequency of treatment‐related AEs (TrAEs) in the two treatment groups. Any TrAEs occurred in 154 (75.9%) patients in the ICI + ICI group and 94 (87.0%) patients in the ICI + TKI group (p = 0.020). There were no differences in severe TrAE (grade ≥ 3) between the two groups (49.8% vs. 50.1%, p = 0.844). Some severe TrAEs occurred at > 5% with adrenal dysfunction and liver dysfunction occurring in both groups. Other severe TrAEs, including skin rash and pituitary dysfunction, were more prevalent in the ICI + ICI group, whereas hypertension, proteinuria, and dysphonia were more prevalent in the ICI + TKI group. There was only one treatment‐related death due to hepatitis associated with ICI + ICI.

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study, we evaluated the comparative effectiveness of ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI combination therapies in patients with advanced RCC in a real‐world setting using IPTW‐adjusted data. ICI + TKI tended to produce a longer PFS than ICI + ICI, whereas the difference was smaller for PFS2; we found no significant difference in OS, ORR, or DOR between the treatment groups. These findings suggest that ICI + TKI may provide improved initial disease control, whereas both regimens may ultimately result in comparable long‐term survival outcomes. These results provide clinically relevant information for treatment selection in the absence of direct comparative evidence.

Our finding of a trend toward longer PFS with ICI + TKI is consistent with those of prior Phase III trials and may reflect more effective initial disease control with VEGF‐targeted therapy [2, 3, 4, 5, 11, 12]. In contrast, ICI + ICI may induce more durable responses in a subset of patients, which may contribute to comparable long‐term survival outcomes [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. This difference in response kinetics may explain why ICI + TKI achieved a trend toward superior PFS in our cohort, whereas OS remained comparable between the two treatment groups. In addition, there were no differences in ORR and DOR between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI. Regarding this point, given the larger number of patients with PD in the ICI + ICI group, the difference in disease control rate (CR + PR + SD) may have been reflected in the PFS. Furthermore, OS, DOR, and PFS2 results should be interpreted with caution due to the marked difference in follow‐up duration between treatment groups, which may have resulted in relatively immature data, and could partially explain the absence of an OS difference despite the observed trend toward improved PFS. Longer follow‐up is warranted to fully evaluate potential differences in long‐term survival outcomes. Treatment decisions should therefore consider both initial response dynamics and the potential for long‐term disease stabilization [23].

Although PFS differed between groups, OS and PFS2 were comparable. These findings are consistent with those of Phase III clinical trials, in which the final OS was also comparable. This may reflect the impact of subsequent therapies and durable immune responses [9, 10, 11, 12].

Our findings are consistent with previous real‐world and indirect comparative analyses evaluating ICI‐based combination therapies, which have tended to show better PFS with ICI + TKI but comparable OS between ICI‐based regimens [24]. Additionally, meta‐analyses and systematic reviews have highlighted the lack of definitive head‐to‐head randomized comparisons and emphasized the importance of real‐world evidence in informing treatment selection [13, 14]. Together, these findings support the clinical relevance of our results and suggest complementary roles for ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI in the management of advanced RCC. Notably, our study directly compares these regimens within the same real‐world cohort using IPTW adjustment, which may strengthen the validity of the comparative findings.

Subgroup analyses suggested that the PFS benefit of ICI + TKI was broadly consistent across subgroups, but appeared less pronounced in patients with IMDC poor‐risk as well as in those with CNS or bone metastases, which may reflect reduced treatment benefit in more aggressive disease settings. IMDC risk classification, originally developed to reflect TKI efficacy, may be particularly relevant to outcomes with ICI + TKI. In addition, CNS or bone metastases are associated with poor prognosis and aggressive disease [25], often accompanied by adverse prognostic features such as elevated CRP and LDH, which may partly explain these observations. However, these findings should be interpreted with extreme caution given the limited statistical power and the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses. Because propensity score estimation and IPTW were performed separately within each subgroup, formal tests for interaction were not performed. Therefore, these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in future prospective studies.

Across histological subgroups (clear cell and non‐clear cell), ICI + TKI showed no differential PFS benefit, and the two treatments yielded comparable OS outcomes. These findings are clinically relevant, as such populations are frequently encountered in routine clinical practice but underrepresented in clinical trials [6, 8]. The finding across histological subtypes represents an important insight derived from real‐world data. Furthermore, although randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing ICI + ICI with TKIs in patients with non‐clear cell RCC have reported the superiority of ICI + ICI regimens [26], there have been no direct comparisons between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI. Further prospective studies are needed.

Regarding safety, although any grade TrAEs were more frequent in the ICI + TKI group, the incidence of grade 3 or higher TrAEs was comparable between the two regimens. These findings are consistent with previous reports and reflect the distinct toxicity profiles associated with immune checkpoint blockade and VEGF inhibition [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Toxicity differences likely reflect the distinct biological targets of each regimen. Immune‐based combinations are characterized by inflammatory AEs involving various organ systems, whereas VEGF‐targeted therapies more commonly produce vascular and endothelial‐related toxicities [27, 28, 29, 30]. Despite the higher overall incidence of TrAEs observed with ICI + TKI, the rate of grade 3 or higher TrAEs was comparable between the groups [1, 2, 4]. This suggests that the toxic burden of ICI + TKI therapy involves both quantitative factors from the TKI and qualitative factors from the ICI. In particular, we previously observed that severe TrAEs could be reduced by optimizing the dosage and administration method of TKIs [31]. Importantly, both treatment strategies were manageable in routine clinical practice, supporting their feasibility in real‐world settings.

From a clinical perspective, these findings may help guide treatment selection between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI combinations. Patients requiring initial disease control, particularly those with IMDC intermediate‐risk disease and without CNS or bone metastases, may derive greater benefit from ICI + TKI regimens. In contrast, given the comparable long‐term survival outcomes observed, ICI + ICI may remain a reasonable option in selected patients where durable immune‐mediated responses are prioritized. These considerations highlight the importance of individualized treatment strategies based on disease characteristics and therapeutic goals. However, these observations should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating given the retrospective design. In the EU CARE‐1 trial, a randomized controlled trial comparing ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI for RCC, is currently underway, and the results are awaited.

A strength of this study is its multicenter design and inclusion of patients treated in routine clinical practice. In addition, this study provides a direct comparative evaluation of ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI using IPTW‐adjusted analyses, which may better approximate real‐world clinical decision‐making in the absence of head‐to‐head randomized trials. Clinical trials often enroll highly selected patient populations under strict eligibility criteria, limiting their generalizability to broader patient populations [6, 7]. By contrast, real‐world studies capture a more heterogeneous population, including patients with diverse clinical characteristics and histological subtypes, who would be excluded from clinical trials [8, 13]. Therefore, our findings complement existing clinical trial data and provide clinically relevant comparative evidence to support treatment decision‐making in real‐world practice, where direct randomized comparisons between ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI remain unavailable. These strengths enhance the clinical applicability of our findings in guiding treatment selection in routine practice.

This study has several limitations. First, because of its retrospective design, there is a risk of bias in patient characteristics, treatment regimens, and outcome assessment. Radiological response was assessed locally without central review. Although imaging was generally performed every 6–12 weeks according to routine clinical practice, imaging intervals were not systematically collected; therefore, minor differences in assessment timing between treatment groups cannot be excluded. To mitigate potential bias from baseline imbalances, we performed IPTW adjustment using propensity scores in the entire cohort. However, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded despite this adjustment. Although ECOG performance status was not included in the primary IPTW model because it is incorporated into the IMDC risk classification, it was evaluated in a prespecified sensitivity analysis by additionally including ECOG performance status as a covariate (Table S3). The resulting treatment effect estimates remained materially unchanged, supporting the robustness of the primary findings. Sarcomatoid differentiation was not included in the IPTW model because it was uncommon and well balanced between treatment groups; exploratory subgroup analyses suggested consistent treatment effects regardless of sarcomatoid status. Therefore, the omission of this variable is unlikely to have materially affected overall conclusions. However, retrospective design may still introduce selection bias related to factors such as metastatic burden and treating physician preference, as well as residual confounding factors that could not be fully controlled despite IPTW adjustment. In addition, treatment year was not included in the IPTW model. Because ICI + ICI and ICI + TKI became available during different time periods, calendar‐time bias cannot be excluded. Similarly, TrAEs were retrospectively collected from medical records, and lower‐grade toxicities may have been underreported. Furthermore, detailed information regarding treatment exposure, discontinuations, dose reductions, and standardized TrAE collection procedures were not available because treatment decisions and documentation were left to the discretion of the treating physicians at each participating institution. Second, heterogeneity among ICI + TKI regimens may have affected treatment outcomes. As shown in Figure S4, although the treatment efficacy trends for each regimen are similar reflecting the characteristics of ICI + TKI, this represents a limitation of the present study. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis, excluding patients treated with avelumab plus axitinib because the recommendation level for this regimen has changed in recent guidelines. The overall findings remained unchanged, with PFS favoring ICI + TKI and no significant observed in OS (Table S4). Finally, this study was conducted exclusively in Japanese institutions, and the generalizability of these findings to other populations warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, this multicenter real‐world study demonstrated that ICI + TKI provided a trend toward superior PFS compared with ICI + ICI, whereas no statistically significant difference in OS was observed. Given the retrospective study design, potential residual confounding despite IPTW adjustment, and differences in follow‐up duration between treatment groups, these findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be regarded as evidence of therapeutic equivalence. Rather, they support individualized treatment selection based on clinical priorities, such as the need for initial disease control versus the potential for durable long‐term response. These findings provide real‐world comparative data that may help inform treatment decision‐making in advanced RCC.
