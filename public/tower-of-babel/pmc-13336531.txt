Outcomes of GnRH Agonist Therapy in Boys with Central Precocious Puberty: A Systematic Review of Final Height and Long-Term Health Effects
Authors: Elham Hashemi, Najmeh Tavousi, Mahin Hashemipour, Neda Mostofizadeh, Noushin Rostampour, Niloufar Amini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Central precocious puberty (CPP) in boys can result in accelerated growth, early epiphyseal fusion, and reduced adult height. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists (GnRHa) are commonly used to delay pubertal progression and preserve growth potential. However, data on their long-term effects on final height, bone health, and body mass index (BMI) remain limited. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the outcomes of GnRH agonist therapy in boys with CPP, focusing on final height and reported side effects.Methods:A systematic literature search was conducted across five databases for studies published from 2000 to 2023. Studies included boys with idiopathic or non-idiopathic CPP treated with GnRH agonists, reporting outcomes related to final height, BMI, or bone health. Data were synthesized qualitatively.Results:GnRH agonist therapy significantly improved final height outcomes, particularly in idiopathic CPP cases, with boys often achieving heights near or exceeding their genetic target. Non-idiopathic CPP cases showed more variable results. Bone health assessments were limited, and long-term impacts on bone mineral density (BMD) remain unclear. Transient increases in BMI were observed during therapy, with no substantial long-term risk of obesity.Conclusions:In general, no serious side effects were reported. GnRH agonist therapy is effective in improving final height in boys with CPP, especially in idiopathic cases. However, more research is needed to understand its long-term effects on bone health and metabolic outcomes.

Introduction

Central precocious puberty (CPP) is characterized by the premature activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, leading to the early onset of secondary sexual characteristics before the age of 9 years in boys and 8 years in girls.[1] This early activation results in accelerated skeletal maturation and early epiphyseal fusion, which can significantly reduce final adult height if left untreated.[2] The causes of CPP can be idiopathic or related to organic pathologies such as central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities, hypothalamic hamartomas, and brain tumors.[34]

The primary goal of treating CPP is to delay pubertal progression and preserve growth potential. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists are commonly used to achieve this by suppressing the secretion of gonadotropins from the pituitary gland, thereby reducing the production of sex hormones.[5] These medications work by desensitizing GnRH receptors in the pituitary gland, leading to a temporary and reversible suppression of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH).[6]

Since their development in the 1980s, GnRH agonists have been widely studied for their effectiveness in improving final height outcomes in children with CPP.[2] The majority of studies have focused on girls with idiopathic CPP, showing that early treatment can significantly improve final height compared to untreated controls.[78] However, there is limited data on the use of GnRH agonists in boys with CPP.

The optimal timing and duration of GnRH agonist therapy remain subjects of debate. While some studies suggest that early treatment leads to better height outcomes, others emphasize the importance of factors such as initial bone age, baseline height SDS, and predicted adult height at treatment initiation.[28]

In boys with non-idiopathic CPP, such as those with CNS abnormalities or hypothalamic hamartomas, the outcomes are more variable.[9] These boys often deal with additional challenges related to their underlying conditions.

Although GnRH agonist therapy is generally considered safe, there have been concerns about potential side effects, including weight gain, changes in body composition, and metabolic alterations.[10] Some studies have reported increases in body mass index (BMI) during treatment, especially in girls,[10] but the long-term impact on BMI and obesity risk in boys remains unclear. Bone density impairment is another concern about the use of these drugs.[11]

Given the limited evidence on the effectiveness of GnRH agonist therapy in boys with CPP, this systematic review aims to address this gap by analyzing the available literature on final height outcomes, the impact of therapy on BMI, and potential side effects. The primary objective is to assess whether GnRH agonist therapy improves final adult height in boys with CPP and to identify factors that influence treatment outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[12] The primary objective was to evaluate the effect of GnRH agonist therapy on final adult height in boys with CPP. Final height is considered reached when annual growth is less than 0.5 cm, while near-final height is defined as a bone age exceeding 12 years in girls and 14 years in boys, indicating that at least 90% of their adult height has been attained.[13]

Study selection

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles to identify relevant studies. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were then reviewed against the inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool.[14] Each study was evaluated for potential biases related to patient selection, outcome measurement, and reporting.

Data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of the included studies, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, we conducted a qualitative synthesis of the data, focusing on final height outcomes, correlations between variables and final height, and the impact of treatment on BMI and side effects. We reported the results narratively, highlighting key findings from each study and identifying trends across the included articles. Final height SDS was calculated in studies where it was not reported using the mean final height reported.

Patient characteristics

The age at treatment initiation ranged from 3.9 to 12.1 years, while bone age at the start of treatment varied from 6.8 to 15 years. Final height SDS ranged from − 2.4 to + 0.4 SDS.

Drugs used

The studies used various types of GnRH agonists, often employing more than one drug within the same study. Below is a breakdown of the drugs used and the studies that reported them:

triptorelin in nine studies,[515161718192223] leuprolide acetate in six studies,[132122232628] deslorelin in two studies,[1328] nafarelin in one study,[13] and unspecified depot GnRH analogs in three studies.[202527]

Idiopathic vs. non-idiopathic cases

The following studies included only idiopathic cases: Shim et al.[22] (20 boys), Cho et al.[25] (18 boys), Galluzzi et al.[15] (11 boys), Lazar et al.[20] (11 boys), Carel et al.[5] (8 boys).

Several studies included non-idiopathic cases with causes such as hypothalamic hamartoma, neurofibromatosis, and brain tumors: Tsai et al.[23] (6 boys (4 with septo-optic dysplasia, 2 with SW congenital adrenal hyperplasia)), Ramos et al.[10] (7 boys (all with hypothalamic hamartomas)), Borges et al.[27] (5 boys (hypothalamic hamartoma, hydrocephalus, 3rd ventricle cyst)), Tanaka et al.[21] (13 boys (non-idiopathic cases unspecified)), Mul et al.[18] (26 boys (12 with organic causes such as neurofibromatosis and brain tumors)), Nahime Brito et al.[26] (9 boys (8 with hypothalamic hamartomas, pilocytic glioma, and exogenous androgen exposure)), Oerter et al.[28] (18 boys (15 with hypothalamic hamartomas and CNS abnormalities)), Rizzo et al.[17] (12 boys (3 with neurofibromatosis, craniopharyngioma, bacterial meningitis)), Bertelloni et al.[16] (9 boys (5 with hamartoma, neurofibromatosis, brain tumor, congenital adrenal hyperplasia)), and Paul et al.[13] (6 boys (causes not specified)).

Growth hormone evaluation in non-idiopathic cases

The studies did not consistently report evaluations of GH levels in boys with non-idiopathic CPP. However, some studies did assess related parameters. Galluzzi et al.[15] evaluated GH and insulin-like growt factor I (IGF-I) levels before, during, and after treatment, and found no significant alterations in GH peak levels or IGF-I concentrations during therapy. Rizzo et al.[17] mentioned that no patients received GH supplementation during GnRH agonist (GnRHa) treatment.

The study by Galluzzi et al.[15] clearly evaluated GH axis health through GH stimulation tests using clonidine. They assessed GH peaks and IGF-I levels in children both before and during GnRH analogue therapy, with results showing no significant decrease in GH peak levels or IGF-I concentrations during treatment. The study measured GH responses to clonidine stimulation and found that the GH axis remained intact during therapy. The IGF-I levels were monitored to assess whether treatment impacted growth factor production, and no adverse effects on GH secretion were noted.

Overall, the studies suggest that the final height improvements were mainly attributed to the suppression of pubertal progression regardless of GH pathways.

Side effects of GnRH agonist therapy

The studies reported relatively few side effects of GnRH agonist therapy. No other major side effects were reported. No systemic or local side effects related to the treatment were observed in boys. Mul et al.[18] reported some complaints of weight gain during therapy but did not observe any serious side effects.

Obesity and BMI changes in boys after therapy

Some studies assessed BMI and obesity as potential side effects of GnRH agonist therapy. Ramos et al.[10] observed that two boys were overweight or obese at the start of treatment, but all male patients had normal BMI and body fat percentages at adulthood. The study concluded that male patients did not experience long-term adverse effects on BMI from GnRH therapy. Oostdijk et al.[19] found that BMI SD scores increased slightly during therapy but returned to normal levels after discontinuation. The study emphasized that BMI changes during treatment were not significant enough to suggest an increased risk of obesity in boys.

Borges et al.[27] reported that some patients experienced temporary weight gain during therapy but noted that long-term BMI outcomes remained stable.

Studies[10] highlighted that male patients treated with GnRH agonists were less likely to experience long-term increases in BMI and body fat percentage, suggesting a sex-specific pattern in how GnRH therapy affects weight and metabolism.

The study by Bertelloni et al.[16] assessed bone mineral density (BMD) in boys treated with GnRH agonists. The study measured both area BMD and volumetric BMD by using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) in nine boys with CPP. The results showed that BMD values were within the normal range and comparable to healthy adolescents with normal puberty onset (Area BMD SDS: 0.2 ± 1.0, Volumetric BMD SDS: −0.1 ± 0.9).

**Correlations Reported**

Several studies reported correlations between treatment outcomes and various factors. Shim et al.[22] showed positive correlations between final height and target height. Cho et al.[25] reported significant correlations between final height and predicted adult height (P < 0.05). Tanaka et al.[21] found positive correlations between adult height and initial height SDS for chronological age, bone age at the start of treatment, and mean growth velocity during treatment. Rizzo et al.[17] indicated a negative correlation between peak LH/FSH ratio and final height. Oerter et al.[28] noted negative correlations between final height and treatment delay, as well as age at puberty onset, and positive correlations with treatment duration and initial height SDS. Paul et al.[13] reported a positive correlation between current predicted height and initial height SDS for bone age, and a negative correlation between final height and both chronological and bone age at therapy initiation. Bertelloni et al.[16] demonstrated that growth after treatment correlated with bone age at treatment cessation.

This review highlights the variability in treatment approaches and outcomes in boys with CPP. The use of GnRH agonists generally resulted in final heights comparable to target heights, particularly in idiopathic cases. However, boys with non-idiopathic CPP had lower final heights, as shown in the studies by Mul et al.[18] and Oerter et al.[28]

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to evaluate the impact of GnRH agonist therapy on final adult height in boys with CPP. The findings provide valuable insights into the variability of treatment outcomes based on patient characteristics, the type of GnRH agonist used, and whether the CPP was idiopathic or non-idiopathic. The studies included in this review highlight the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention to optimize growth outcomes in boys with CPP.

This limitation reflects the rarity of CPP in boys and the limited availability of high-quality studies on this population. A comprehensive and systematic search was performed, and no further eligible studies were found, despite rigorous screening. Therefore, the sample sizes remain small (Shim et al., Lazar et al.), which may affect the generalizability of the findings to broader CPP populations.[22]

Effectiveness of GnRH agonist therapy in idiopathic CPP

The studies in this review consistently reported that boys with idiopathic CPP achieved final heights close to their genetic potential after GnRH agonist therapy. For instance, Shim et al.[22] observed that boys treated with triptorelin or leuprolide acetate showed a significant improvement in final height compared to their predicted adult height at the start of treatment. The mean final height in their study was 173.4 cm, compared to an initial predicted height of 170.1 cm.

Similarly, Lazar et al.[20] reported that the final height achieved by boys treated with triptorelin was within the target range for their genetic potential. The study also emphasized the importance of treatment duration and bone age at the start of therapy as critical factors influencing height outcomes. In addition, Carel et al.[5] found that early initiation of therapy was associated with better height outcomes, reinforcing the need for prompt intervention in boys with idiopathic CPP.

Potential biases, including selection bias, were systematically assessed using the ROBINS-I tool. Most included studies attempted to reduce bias through clear inclusion criteria and consecutive patient enrollment. However, the absence of standardized treatment protocols remains a limitation, potentially contributing to outcome variability across studies.

Most studies included in this systematic review reported that GnRH agonist therapy was effective in improving final height in boys with CPP. For idiopathic cases, the therapy consistently helped patients achieve a final height within their genetic target range or higher than their predicted adult height at treatment initiation. Galluzzi et al.[15] reported a significant improvement in final height, with boys achieving heights close to or slightly above their genetic target heights. Ramos et al.[10] observed that all boys with hypothalamic hamartomas who received GnRH analogs reached normal final height within their target range. Importantly, the study highlighted that treatment was more effective when initiated at younger ages and maintained for a longer duration. Mul et al.[18] found that boys treated with GnRH agonists achieved a mean height SDS gain of 1.09, indicating that final height was significantly improved compared to their predicted height before treatment.

Boys with neurogenic CPP, including those with hypothalamic hamartomas or CNS tumors, exhibit more variable and often poorer final height outcomes than idiopathic CPP.[1028] This is attributed to hypothalamic-pituitary axis impairment and potential GH deficiencies caused by CNS lesions. Comorbidities such as neurofibromatosis may further limit growth.[19] Moreover, delayed diagnosis and treatment initiation in neurogenic cases contribute to poorer outcomes.[28] Nonetheless, GnRH agonist therapy remains beneficial, with some patients achieving normal or near-normal final heights.[1023] Adjunct therapies such as GH supplementation may be considered to optimize growth in selected cases

However, in boys with non-idiopathic (neurogenic) CPP, such as those with brain tumors or hypothalamic hamartomas, the height outcomes were more variable. For instance, Oerter et al.[28] noted that boys with neurogenic causes achieved lower final heights compared to idiopathic cases, although early treatment initiation still resulted in improvements.

Challenges in treating non-idiopathic CPP

In contrast to idiopathic cases, boys with non-idiopathic CPP, such as those with hypothalamic hamartomas, neurofibromatosis, or brain tumors, exhibited more variable height outcomes. Ramos et al.[10] studied boys with CPP due to hypothalamic hamartomas and found that their final height outcomes were less favorable compared to those with idiopathic CPP. However, the study highlighted that GnRH agonist therapy still helped these boys achieve a final height within the normal range, suggesting that the treatment is beneficial even in non-idiopathic cases.

Oerter et al.[28] also reported that boys with organic causes of CPP, such as hypothalamic hamartomas or CNS abnormalities, achieved lower final heights compared to idiopathic cases. However, the study emphasized that early intervention could mitigate the negative impact of these underlying conditions on growth.[28]

Tsai et al.[23] focused on boys with congenital adrenal hyperplasia and CPP and found that adding GnRH agonist therapy to conventional treatment significantly improved final height outcomes. The study demonstrated that GnRH agonist therapy can be an effective adjunct treatment in boys with non-idiopathic CPP, particularly when initiated early.[23]

In the study by Oostdijk et al.,[19] there was no improvement in adult height in boys compared to girls. The authors suggested that this could be due to underlying conditions such as neurofibromatosis that impair growth, potentially causing complications such as optic nerve gliomas, leading to GH deficiency. However, GH levels were not evaluated in these patients. The dataset shows that boys with organic causes of CPP, such as neurofibromatosis and arachnoid cyst, had their final height negatively affected, which explains the lack of improvement in adult height in this group.

Types of GnRH agonists used

Various GnRH agonists, including triptorelin, leuprolide acetate, deslorelin, and nafarelin, suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis by continuous receptor stimulation, leading to receptor desensitization and decreased gonadotropin secretion (Paul et al.[13] and Tanaka et al.[21]). Pharmacokinetic differences influence dosing schedules, with slow-release triptorelin allowing less frequent administration (monthly or quarterly), potentially improving adherence compared to daily injections required by some formulations of leuprolide or nafarelin (Oostdijk et al.[19]). All studied agonists demonstrated comparable efficacy in halting pubertal progression and preserving growth potential, with no significant difference in final height outcomes (Paul et al.[13] and Tanaka et al.[21]). Safety profiles were similarly favorable, with minimal adverse effects reported (Mul et al.[18] and Bertelloni et al.[16]). Despite pharmacological differences, current evidence supports equivalent effectiveness among these agents.

The studies included in this review utilized various GnRH agonists, including triptorelin, leuprolide acetate, deslorelin, and nafarelin. The choice of GnRH agonist did not appear to significantly affect final height outcomes, as all drugs were effective in suppressing pubertal progression and improving growth potential.

Paul et al.[13] compared the effectiveness of multiple GnRH agonists, including deslorelin, leuprolide, and nafarelin, and found that all three drugs were equally effective in improving final height outcomes in boys with CPP. Similarly, Tanaka et al.[21] reported that leuprolide acetate was effective in increasing final height and preserving gonadal function after treatment discontinuation.

However, some studies noted differences in patient compliance based on the administration method and frequency of the GnRH agonist. For example, Oostdijk et al.[19] highlighted the benefits of slow-release triptorelin, which required less frequent administration and resulted in better patient adherence to therapy.

Correlations Between Growth Parameters and Final Height

Several studies reported correlations between initial growth parameters and final height outcomes. Tanaka et al.[21] found a positive correlation between initial height SDS and final height, suggesting that boys who were taller at the start of treatment were more likely to achieve better height outcomes. Similarly, Rizzo et al.[17] reported that the initial peak LH/FSH ratio negatively correlated with final height, indicating that higher initial hormonal levels were associated with lower height outcomes.

In contrast, Oerter et al.[28] observed a negative correlation between the age at treatment initiation and final height, emphasizing the importance of early intervention. The study found that boys who started therapy at a younger age achieved significantly better final heights compared to those who started treatment later.[28]

The positive correlation between initial height SDS and final height outcomes suggests that earlier diagnosis and intervention, especially in boys with taller stature at baseline, may improve height outcomes.[21] This finding highlights the importance of timely treatment initiation and suggests that boys presenting with lower initial height SDS might require closer follow-up and consideration of adjunctive therapies to optimize growth.

Comparison with historical controls

Some studies included in this review compared the final height outcomes of boys treated with GnRH agonists to historical controls who did not receive treatment. For instance, Paul et al.[13] found that boys treated with GnRH agonists achieved significantly better final heights compared to untreated controls with similar initial characteristics. The study concluded that GnRH agonist therapy is essential in improving height outcomes in boys with CPP.[13]

Obesity and BMI outcomes

Several studies addressed the potential impact of GnRH agonist therapy on BMI and obesity risk in boys. While some studies reported temporary weight gain during treatment, the overall consensus was that boys did not experience significant long-term increases in BMI or obesity risk.

Ramos et al.[10] observed that male patients did not show an increased risk of obesity or abnormal BMI at adulthood after receiving GnRHa therapy. Oostdijk et al.[19] found a slight increase in BMI SD scores during therapy, but these returned to normal levels after treatment discontinuation. Importantly, these studies indicate that GnRHa therapy does not predispose boys to long-term obesity or metabolic issues.

However, it is noteworthy that some studies identified a sex-specific pattern in BMI outcomes. Female patients treated with GnRH agonists were found to be more likely to experience long-term increases in BMI and body fat percentage compared to their male counterparts.[1027] This suggests that while boys are less likely to experience adverse metabolic effects, girls may require closer monitoring for weight-related side effects.

Reported side effects of GnRH agonist therapy

Although short-term side effects of GnRH agonists were minimal in reviewed studies, long-term safety data are scarce. Therefore, prolonged follow-up is essential to evaluate metabolic health, bone density, and reproductive function in treated patients.[101627] This ongoing monitoring is critical to fully understand the sustainability and safety of GnRH agonist therapy.

GnRH agonist therapy was generally well tolerated, with minimal reported side effects. The most commonly reported adverse effects were temporary and resolved without discontinuing treatment.

There were no systemic side effects reported in boys.

Mul et al.[18] noted that some patients experienced complaints of weight gain during therapy, although this did not result in significant long-term consequences. These findings suggest that GnRH agonist therapy is safe for long-term use in boys with CPP, with only minor and transient side effects observed in the reviewed studies.

Bertelloni et al.[16] concluded that long-term GnRH agonist therapy does not impair BMD in boys during late adolescence. The authors suggested that bone health remains normal following therapy, and serum markers of bone turnover were also within the normal range.

Clinical implications and recommendations

The findings of this review have important clinical implications. First, they underscore the effectiveness of GnRH agonist therapy in preserving growth potential and achieving final heights close to the genetic target height, particularly in boys with idiopathic CPP. Second, they highlight the challenges associated with treating non-idiopathic CPP and the need for individualized treatment plans based on the underlying cause.

Moreover, this review emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and timely initiation of therapy to optimize height outcomes. Delayed intervention can negatively impact final height, as shown in several studies included in this review.[1728]

Future research should focus on optimizing treatment protocols for boys with non-idiopathic CPP and exploring the long-term effects of GnRH agonist therapy on metabolic and reproductive outcomes. Additionally, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to better understand the impact of different GnRH agonists and the role of adjunctive therapies, such as GH, in improving height outcomes. Future research should focus on multicenter trials with larger sample sizes and standardized treatment protocols to improve result reliability. Investigating adjunctive therapies, including GH supplementation, and longitudinal studies assessing long-term metabolic and reproductive outcomes are also recommended.

Conclusions

In conclusion, GnRH agonist therapy was generally effective in improving final height outcomes in boys with CPP, especially in idiopathic cases. While GH evaluation was not consistently reported in non-idiopathic cases, the therapy’s effectiveness in improving height appears to stem primarily from suppressing puberty rather than directly affecting GH pathways.

Reported side effects were minimal, with some transient weight gain and isolated cases of other minor effects. Importantly, BMI outcomes in boys remained stable after treatment, suggesting that GnRH agonists do not contribute to long-term obesity in male patients. However, studies pointed out a sex-specific pattern, with female patients more likely to experience adverse effects on BMI and body composition. More research is needed to understand its long-term effects on bone health and metabolic outcomes.
