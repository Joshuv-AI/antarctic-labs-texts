Predicting completion and early dropout in routine-care internet-delivered cognitive behaviour therapy: A temporal validation study
Authors: Ram P. Sapkota, Blake F. Dear, Nickolai Titov, Heather D. Hadjistavropoulos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn routine-care Internet-delivered cognitive behaviour therapy (ICBT), engagement may be better understood as a staged process (e.g., early discontinuation vs full completion), yet many studies use a single dropout definition and seldom test whether prediction models remain useful as services evolve over time.ObjectiveTo identify baseline predictors of completion and early dropout in routine-care ICBT and evaluate temporal transportability from earlier to later service years.MethodsWe analyzed de-identified routine-care data (N = 9057) from a transdiagnostic ICBT program delivered from 2013 to 2024. Outcomes were completion (all five lessons vs fewer) and early dropout (0–1 lesson vs two or more). Multivariable logistic regression models used baseline demographic, clinical, and contextual intake variables. Models were developed in TRAIN (2013–2021) and applied without refitting to TEST (2022–2024). TEST performance was summarized using Brier score, area under the curve (AUC), and calibration.ResultsDemographic factors, including age, education, ethnicity, and relationship status, were more consistently associated with engagement than baseline symptom severity. Completion predictions showed modest discrimination and modest overprediction in TEST, whereas early-dropout predictions showed weaker discrimination, slight underprediction, and limited separation of higher- and lower-risk clients.ConclusionsModels based on screening variables showed higher discrimination for completion than early dropout. Engagement prediction in routine-care ICBT should be temporally evaluated and monitored for calibration drift. Future research should examine whether early indicators during treatment, such as initial logins, lesson access, and early therapist contact, improve prediction of early dropout.

Introduction

Internet-delivered cognitive behaviour therapy (ICBT) is an evidence-based intervention modality for common mental disorders such as depression and anxiety. Large syntheses continue to support its efficacy across adult mental disorders (Cuijpers et al., 2025; Hedman-Lagerlöf et al., 2023), including in routine-care contexts (Etzelmueller et al., 2020). However, as in face-to-face therapy (Fernandez et al., 2015; Kullgard et al., 2022; Swift and Greenberg, 2012) engagement remains a primary implementation constraint in digital care, with many users not completing the full program, and a subset disengaging early (Bisby et al., 2022; Cross et al., 2022). In routine care, non-completion does not necessarily indicate treatment failure, as some clients stop because they feel sufficiently improved, the program is a poor fit for their needs or preferences, or because life events and competing demands make it difficult to continue. From a service perspective, the aim is not necessarily completion for every client. At the same time, engagement is not only a patient-level phenomenon; it can also reflect modifiable features of intervention and service design, including how digital programs are structured, supported, and integrated into routine workflows (McCall et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2025).

Digital interventions commonly show substantial loss of patients at multiple points, ranging from non-initiation to early discontinuation and partial completion. This pattern is often discussed in terms of Eysenbach's (2005) “law of attrition,” which highlights that drop-off is expected in digital programs and can occur at different stages; the concept describes the general pattern of attrition rather than implying any specific attrition rate for a given service. Yet attrition is not a single construct, and collapsing engagement into one “dropout” indicator can obscure distinct phenomena with different determinants and service implications. A recent meta-analysis comparing internet-based and face-to-face cognitive behaviour therapy distinguishes treatment non-initiation, dropout after starting, and broader attrition, underscoring the need to operationalize engagement as a staged process rather than a single endpoint (Linardon et al., 2025). Conceptual work on adherence in eHealth similarly emphasizes that “use” becomes “adherence” only relative to intended use, which varies by program design and by the clinical or service objective (Sieverink et al., 2017). Consistent with this, engagement appears heterogeneous and may cluster into distinct usage trajectories rather than a single completer vs dropout pathway (Chien et al., 2020), and qualitative work suggests discontinuation can reflect practical barriers, shifting motivation, or early experiences with the program and support rather than symptom severity alone (Lawler et al., 2021; Peynenburg et al., 2022). This literature suggests that disengagement is heterogeneous: when clients stop may matter as much as whether they stop, and the reasons for stopping may not be well captured by symptom severity alone.

Predictor research in ICBT has likewise been limited by inconsistent outcome definitions and analytic approaches that may not capture the full complexity of engagement. In routine care and pragmatic delivery settings, engagement may be shaped by patient characteristics (e.g., age and sociodemographic factors), clinical presentation, and service features (e.g., onboarding, support intensity, platform design) and external circumstances outside the service that can affect a person's ability to continue. Emerging work suggests that predictors may differ depending on how engagement is operationalized. For example, a secondary analysis of a web-based CBT program for primary-care patients with depression or anxiety symptoms examined predictors of program use and dropout, highlighting the need to distinguish different engagement endpoints rather than treating attrition as a single outcome (Rotondi et al., 2024). Related work in blended and digitally supported CBT similarly demonstrates that non-initiation and dropout can be predicted by different combinations of sociodemographic and clinical features (Wu et al., 2022). More recently, Zantvoort et al. (2024) used demographic, symptom, and online activity data collected during the first four weeks of routine-care ICBT to predict subsequent dropout, illustrating the potential value of in-treatment information in addition to baseline characteristics.

Recent personalization frameworks for ICBT also distinguish treatment-entry stratification from responsiveness during treatment, emphasizing that engagement and clinical outcomes are related but distinct targets for personalization (Aafjes-van Doorn and Christensen, 2026). In routine care, adherence modelling in guided ICBT suggests that engagement prediction can inform implementation planning, for example by helping services plan targeted outreach and support intensity (Österman et al., 2024). At the intervention level, some evidence suggests that program features intended to support engagement (e.g., persuasive design elements) may be associated with differences in outcomes in unguided ICBT, indicating that engagement is not solely a patient attribute but can also reflect modifiable aspects of intervention and service delivery (McCall et al., 2021; Peynenburg et al., 2022). Across studies, however, predictor patterns are not fully consistent and appear to vary depending on how engagement is defined and, on the population, and service context.

Within therapist-assisted ICBT, Edmonds et al. (2018) showed that baseline patient characteristics help explain who benefits and who drops out, but engagement was largely treated as a single dropout construct within a narrower service window. Building on this foundation, the present study conceptualizes engagement as staged (early dropout vs full completion) and evaluates temporal transportability by testing whether models developed in earlier service years retain performance when applied, without refitting, to later service years. This emphasis is important because prediction models can degrade when patient mix, workflows, or measurement practices shift over time. Evaluating performance under realistic, earlier-years → later-years deployment, conditions is now recognized as good practice in applied prediction (Bennemann et al., 2022; Collins et al., 2024).

A further gap limits the usefulness of the engagement literature for service planning. Many predictor studies are developed and interpreted within a single time period, without explicitly examining whether findings from earlier years still apply in later years of service delivery. This matters in digital programs that evolve over time, including changes in assessment scheduling, recruitment procedures, therapist workflows, and client mix. When these conditions change, conclusions based on earlier years may not hold in later years, so later-year evaluation is essential for practical service planning (Bennemann et al., 2022; Collins et al., 2024). The present study addresses these gaps using a large routine-care sample from a provincial ICBT service spanning more than a decade of delivery.

Engagement was operationalized using two binary outcomes derived from lesson-completion records: treatment completion (completion of all five core lessons) and early dropout (discontinuation after 0–1 lesson versus engagement through two or more lessons), consistent with staged conceptualizations of engagement and attrition (Eysenbach, 2005; Linardon et al., 2025; Sieverink et al., 2017). To examine whether findings based on earlier service years remained applicable to later service years, we also evaluated the temporal transportability of the engagement prediction models (Collins et al., 2024).

We focused on two clinically meaningful engagement endpoints: treatment completion and early dropout. By distinguishing these engagement stages and examining whether patterns observed in earlier years also apply in later years, this study aimed to clarify which screening factors are most consistently linked to completion and early dropout in routine-care ICBT and to inform service planning and practical engagement supports. This focus is directly relevant to decisions about the timing and intensity of early contact and whether additional support is offered universally or targeted to clients at higher risk of early disengagement. Targeted contact can improve adherence in some guided digital interventions (Pihlaja et al., 2020). However, disengagement may also reflect factors outside the service and will not always be preventable, so understanding reasons for stopping and identifying actionable early indicators remain important directions for future work. Because engagement patterns and service delivery features can vary across settings, the findings may not generalize directly to other service settings. However, they provide a practical example of how engagement patterns and their correlates can shift across implementation eras in a long-running routine-care service.

Methods

Design and data source

The Online Therapy Unit (OTU) provides provincially funded, therapist-assisted, transdiagnostic ICBT as routine clinical care in Saskatchewan (Hadjistavropoulos and Peynenburg, 2025). OTU maintains an ongoing program evaluation dataset compiled from standard screening and baseline questionnaires, as well as treatment-platform records. As part of routine service delivery, clients are invited to provide informed consent for research use of their data. All procedures have been approved by the University of Regina Research Ethics Board under a series of protocols that support continuous routine-care data collection as program operations and research aims have evolved over time. This study is a secondary analysis of fully de-identified routine-care data pooled across program delivery years; because the analyses used de-identified archival data collected under existing approvals, additional ethics approval was not sought. Data from this program have also supported prior publications (e.g., Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2022; Roemmel et al., 2025) but the current study addresses a different research objective focused on engagement prediction.

Data were drawn from routine delivery of the transdiagnostic Wellbeing Course delivered by OTU between November 2013 and December 2024. The analytic file included participants with observed engagement outcomes and complete data on the predictors used in the primary model specification. Consistent with staged conceptualizations of engagement and attrition (Eysenbach, 2005; Linardon et al., 2025; Sieverink et al., 2017), engagement outcomes were defined as (1) treatment completion, operationalized as completing five lessons versus fewer than five lessons, and (2) early dropout, operationalized as completing 0–1 lesson versus completing two or more lessons. A major program-era change occurred in assessment scheduling: from 2013 to 2019, symptom measures were administered in a lesson-anchored manner, whereas from 2020 onward they were administered weekly irrespective of lesson completion. This assessment-administration shift was used to contextualize engagement patterns over time.

To evaluate whether engagement prediction models developed in earlier service years remained accurate when applied to later service years, we used a prespecified time-based validation design. Models were estimated using screening data from 2013 to 2021 (TRAIN) and then applied unchanged to screening data from 2022 to 2024 (TEST). The three-year TEST window was selected in advance because early dropout was uncommon; pooling three years increased the number of early-dropout events and reduced sensitivity to chance variation or any single year that may have been atypical due to broader system or service changes, including the COVID-19 period. We did not assume that 2022, 2023, and 2024 were equivalent years; year-specific engagement rates are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1 to contextualize the evaluation window. This split also aligns with prior OTU prediction work that analyzed data through 2021 (e.g., Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2022; Roemmel et al., 2025), leaving 2022 to 2024 as a distinct post-2021 evaluation period. In the 2022–2024 TEST period, we evaluated discrimination, overall accuracy, and calibration (agreement between predicted and observed outcome rates), and pooled scoring summaries are also reported to summarize overall performance, although temporal TEST results provide the main evidence of transportability (Collins et al., 2024). Further details of the temporal validation procedures and performance indices are provided in the Data analysis section and Supplementary Methods.

Participants

Participants were adults who applied for the ICBT Wellbeing Course through the OTU. Referral sources included clinicians and other health system pathways as well as self-referral (e.g., online information, media, workplace/community sources). Applicants completed an online screening questionnaire assessing demographic and clinical characteristics, followed by a structured telephone interview with a clinician to confirm eligibility, clarify presenting problems, and assess risk. Individuals deemed ineligible were provided with information and referrals to alternative services. Eligible participants provided consent and were enrolled into the treatment platform prior to initiating the course. Routine-care eligibility criteria required applicants to be adults (≥18 years), reside in Saskatchewan, have sufficient internet access and ability to use the platform, and present with symptoms of depression and/or anxiety. Exclusion criteria generally included acute/high suicide risk or recent attempt, unmanaged psychosis or mania, and other clinical or contextual factors indicating that a different level or type of care was needed (e.g., severe substance-related impairment, significant cognitive impairment, or acute situational instability) or that would likely interfere with participating in an online course (e.g., inability to access the internet reliably or to engage with written materials).

Intervention

Participants received the transdiagnostic Wellbeing Course, a structured five-lesson ICBT program targeting depression and anxiety symptoms over approximately eight weeks (see Hadjistavropoulos et al., 2014; Titov et al., 2015). Lessons were delivered sequentially and included psychoeducation, skills practice, and homework assignments. Participants could also access additional self-help resources on the platform (e.g., sleep, worry, problem solving, motivation) and received therapist guidance primarily via asynchronous secure messaging with telephone support as needed, depending on the service model operating at the time.

Measures

Depression and anxiety symptom severity were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire–9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder–7 (GAD-7; Spitzer et al., 2006). Binary indicators of clinically elevated symptoms were created using standard thresholds (PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher; GAD-7 score of 10 or higher) and were used as baseline clinical predictors in the engagement models.

Demographic and contextual screening variables were obtained from routine intake questionnaires completed before treatment. These included age, gender, education, ethnicity, geographic location, relationship status, employment status, and current use of other mental health treatment. Engagement outcomes were derived from treatment-platform records of lesson completion.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). The analytic goal was to identify baseline predictors of engagement in routine-care ICBT using multivariable logistic regression. All predictors were derived from routine screening/intake (baseline) assessments completed before treatment started. Statistical tests were two-sided with α = 0.05.

Two binary engagement outcomes were examined: treatment completion (completed all five lessons vs fewer than five lessons; 1 = completer, 0 = non-completer) and early dropout (0–1 lesson vs ≥2 lessons; 1 = early dropout, 0 = engaged). Descriptive statistics were computed for analytic samples and stratified by engagement outcome where relevant. To provide context for temporal transportability, we also compared screening characteristics between the development period (TRAIN: 2013–2021) and temporal validation period (TEST: 2022–2024; Supplementary Table S2). This descriptive comparison was intended to characterize potential case-mix differences across service periods, rather than to test whether the periods were equivalent. Continuous variables are summarized using means and standard deviations, and categorical variables using frequencies and percentages.

Primary prediction models used complete-case data for the prespecified screening predictors. The final primary model sample included 9042 participants, including 6495 participants in TRAIN and 2547 participants in TEST. To describe engagement patterns over time, completion and early dropout rates were also summarized by screening year (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Predictor sets and model specifications

Primary models used screening predictors available across the full study window (2013–2024): age in years; gender (women, men, other); education (high school or less, post-high school certificate/diploma, university degree); ethnicity (White, Indigenous, other); geographic location (small rural, small-to-medium city, large city); relationship status (single/never married/dating, married/common law, separated/divorced/widowed); employment status (paid work, homemaker/household, retired, student, unfit for work due to health, no paid work for other reasons); current mental health treatment (yes/no); clinically elevated depression (PHQ-9 ≥ 10); and clinically elevated anxiety (GAD-7 ≥ 10).

Separate multivariable logistic regression models were estimated for each engagement outcome: treatment completion and early dropout. All predictors were entered simultaneously. Categorical predictors were modeled using indicator coding with prespecified reference categories: women for gender, high school education or less for education, White for ethnicity, small rural location for geographic location, single/never married/dating for relationship status, paid work for employment status, no current mental health treatment, and non-clinical baseline status for PHQ-9 and GAD-7. As a secondary analysis, we also fitted a multinomial logistic regression using three mutually exclusive engagement categories: early dropout (0–1 lesson), partial engagement (2–4 lessons), and completion (5 lessons), with partial engagement as the reference category and the same baseline predictors as the primary models.

Temporal validation and calibration

Temporal transportability was evaluated using the prespecified time-based split described in the Design section. For each outcome, logistic regression coefficients estimated in TRAIN were applied unchanged to participants in TEST to generate predicted probabilities. Performance was summarized using the Brier score for overall prediction error, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for discrimination, and calibration indices. Calibration was summarized using logistic recalibration, in which the observed outcome was regressed on the model linear predictor to estimate a calibration intercept and slope; ideal values are 0 for the intercept and 1 for the slope (Van Calster et al., 2019). Pooled models were also refitted in the full primary model sample to summarize adjusted associations and overall model performance across the full analytic period. As a sensitivity analysis addressing the change in assessment scheduling, we restricted analyses to the weekly-assessment era and fitted the same multivariable logistic regression models separately for participants screened in 2020–2021 and 2022–2024. Adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were compared across periods for both treatment completion and early dropout. Additional details of scoring, performance metrics, calibration-by-bin summaries, and decision-rule evaluation are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Missing data

Missingness in this routine-care dataset reflected both routine nonresponse and changes in the routine-care assessment measures over time, with some baseline measures introduced later or not administered in all service years. Measures affected included lifetime mental-health diagnosis, treatment-waitlist status, and lifetime psychotropic-medication history. Primary prediction models were restricted to screening variables available across 2013–2024; consequently, complete-case loss for the primary models was minimal. Of 9057 participants with observed engagement outcomes, 9042 had complete data on all prespecified primary predictors and were included in the primary analyses.

Of the 9057 participants, 571 (6.3%) met the early-dropout definition (0–1 lesson), 2619 (28.9%) completed 2–4 lessons, and 5867 (64.8%) completed all five lessons. Compared with participants who completed at least two lessons, early dropouts were younger and showed small differences in several demographic and baseline clinical characteristics; most effect sizes were small, with the largest difference observed for age (d = −0.35; Supplementary Table S1).

To contextualize the temporal validation, screening characteristics were compared between the development period (TRAIN: 2013–2021) and temporal validation period (TEST: 2022–2024) in the primary model sample (Supplementary Table S2). The TEST period included participants who were slightly older on average and showed small differences in gender, ethnicity, geographic location, and employment status. Education, relationship status, current mental health treatment, and baseline symptom severity indicators showed trivial differences across periods. Overall, these comparisons suggest modest case-mix differences across service periods, primarily in demographic and geographic characteristics rather than baseline symptom severity.

Engagement outcomes also varied across screening years (Supplementary Fig. S1). Completion rates were highest in the earliest years, ranging from 75.0% in 2013 to 79.3% in 2014, then declined during 2015–2017, fluctuated around 71% during 2018–2019, decreased in 2020–2021, partially rebounded in 2022–2023, and declined again in 2024. Early dropout remained low overall but was generally higher in later years, ranging from 4.3% to 7.1% during 2013–2016 and from 5.9% to 8.9% during 2020–2024. Completion was higher in the lesson-anchored assessment era (2013–2019) than in the weekly assessment era (2020+; 70.9% vs 60.0%; Pearson χ2(1) = 116.99, p < .001), while early dropout was lower in the lesson-anchored era than in the weekly era (5.1% vs 7.2%; Pearson χ2(1) = 16.78, p < .001). Temporal validation and model performance

Temporal validation using models developed in TRAIN (2013–2021) and applied without refitting to TEST (2022–2024), along with pooled model performance, is summarized in Table 3. For treatment completion, the TRAIN-derived score showed modest discrimination in TEST (AUC = 0.643, 95% CI [0.622, 0.665]) with a Brier score of 0.223. Mean predicted completion probability in TEST was 0.669, whereas observed completion was 0.624, indicating modest overprediction. Logistic recalibration produced a calibration intercept of −0.250 and a calibration slope of 1.060. The calibration slope was close to 1.0, while the AUC was similar in TRAIN (0.624) and TEST (0.643), indicating that discrimination was maintained over time.

For early dropout, the TRAIN-derived score showed weaker discrimination in TEST (AUC = 0.601, 95% CI [0.556, 0.645]) with a Brier score of 0.064. Because early dropout occurred in only 6.9% of the TEST sample, even a non-informative model assigning the same 6.9% risk to every participant would have a Brier score of approximately 0.064. The observed Brier score of 0.064 therefore indicates little improvement in overall probability accuracy from the baseline predictors. Mean predicted early-dropout probability in TEST was 0.059, whereas observed early dropout was 0.069, indicating slight underprediction. Logistic recalibration showed a calibration intercept of −0.917 and a calibration slope of 0.595, suggesting that the model tended to assign probabilities that were too high for some clients and too low for others in the later service period. Pooled models showed modest discrimination for both completion (AUC = 0.630, 95% CI [0.618, 0.642]) and early dropout (AUC = 0.632, 95% CI [0.608, 0.655]). Calibration-by-bin summaries and calibration plots for the temporal validation models are provided in Supplementary Tables S3–S4 and Supplementary Fig. S2A–B.

Discussion

This study examined screening predictors of engagement in a large routine-care ICBT service using multivariable logistic regression and a prespecified earlier-years to later-years temporal validation. Engagement varied across service years, with completion generally lower and early dropout generally higher in later years than in the earliest years of service delivery. Prediction performance was modest overall. In TEST, the completion model showed modest ability to distinguish completers from non-completers and, on average, predicted a somewhat higher completion rate than was observed. The early-dropout model showed weaker ability to distinguish early dropouts from other participants and, on average, predicted a slightly lower early-dropout rate than was observed. In models fitted to the full analytic sample while accounting for all baseline predictors, engagement was more consistently associated with demographic factors than with symptom severity at screening. Completion was more likely among older clients, those with a university degree, White participants, and those who were not separated/divorced/widowed. Early dropout was more likely among younger clients, those without a university degree, Indigenous participants, and participants in the other ethnicity category.

Engagement as a staged process

A central implication is that “attrition” is not a single phenomenon, and this distinction is important in routine-care ICBT because early discontinuation and later non-completion may have different predictors and service implications. Our staged engagement outcomes are consistent with broader CBT evidence showing that attrition occurs at multiple points, including before treatment starts and during treatment, and that dropout varies by delivery format and clinical context (Fernandez et al., 2015). Internet-delivered interventions, including ICBT, often lose users at multiple points (non-initiation, early discontinuation, and later non-completion), and treating engagement as a single “dropout” endpoint can blur distinct processes with different determinants and service implications (Christensen and Mackinnon, 2006; Eysenbach, 2005; Linardon et al., 2025; Sieverink et al., 2017). Recent engagement scholarship also emphasizes that engagement is multidimensional (behavioral, cognitive, affective) and dynamic over time rather than a one-time outcome (Nahum-Shani et al., 2022). Although the present study operationalized engagement using behavioral indicators (lesson completion and early dropout), the staged endpoints yielded meaningfully different predictability and partially different predictor patterns. The secondary three-category analysis showed a similar pattern: baseline characteristics were more informative for distinguishing completion from partial engagement than for distinguishing early dropout from partial engagement. Early disengagement may reflect not only short-term barriers and early experiences that are difficult to capture at screening, but also external factors such as life events, competing demands, or stopping after sufficient improvement (Lawler et al., 2021). Thus, distinguishing engagement stages was informative for showing that completion and early dropout were not equally predictable. Baseline variables modestly predicted completion but did not reliably identify individuals at risk of early dropout.

The results align with evidence from both meta-analytic work on online anxiety interventions and national digital mental health services showing substantial variability in uptake and completion (Bisby et al., 2022; Cross et al., 2022). In the present study, predictors differed across completion and early dropout, and baseline models were modest for completion but weaker and less well calibrated for early dropout when applied to later service years.

These findings reinforce the value of examining engagement stages separately, as in prior work distinguishing treatment initiation from subsequent continuation or dropout (Rotondi et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2022). From a service planning perspective, these findings suggest that screening-based risk stratification may have limited usefulness for identifying early dropout on its own, and that engagement support approaches may need to incorporate early in-treatment indicators rather than relying only on screening variables.

The pooled models suggest that engagement in routine-care ICBT was more consistently associated with demographic characteristics than with baseline symptom-severity indicators. Older age and university education were associated with higher odds of completion and lower odds of early dropout, whereas Indigenous participants and participants in the other ethnicity category had lower odds of completion and higher odds of early dropout than White participants. Participants who were separated/divorced/widowed also had lower odds of completion, although relationship status was not independently associated with early dropout. Clinically elevated depressive symptoms at baseline were associated with lower odds of completion, but not with early dropout, and clinically elevated anxiety symptoms were not independently associated with either outcome. These findings suggest that baseline symptom severity alone is unlikely to provide a sufficient basis for identifying clients at risk of disengagement, particularly early dropout.

Two additional considerations may help explain why symptom severity at screening was a weaker predictor of engagement than demographic characteristics. First, engagement often follows different usage patterns over time, such as intermittent start-stop participation versus continuous participation, so a single “non-completer” group can include people who stop for different reasons; screening predictors may not distinguish these pathways well (Chien et al., 2020). Second, qualitative studies of ICBT discontinuation commonly point to practical barriers, such as time constraints and competing demands, shifts in motivation, and early experiences with the program or support as reasons for stopping, factors that are not well captured by symptom severity at screening (Koelen et al., 2025; Lawler et al., 2021). Similar reasons are also reported in face-to-face psychotherapy dropout research (Fernandez et al., 2015; Kullgard et al., 2022; Swift and Greenberg, 2012).

Temporal transportability in long-running routine-care programs

An important contribution of this study is the explicit focus on temporal transportability, that is, whether models developed in earlier service years retain useful performance when applied, without refitting, to later service years. Because routine-care digital services evolve over time, prediction performance should be evaluated in later service years and with attention to calibration as well as discrimination (Bennemann et al., 2022; Collins et al., 2024). Consistent with expected changes over time, transportability differed by outcome. The completion model retained modest discrimination in TEST, while its average predicted completion rate was somewhat higher than the rate actually observed, suggesting that the main change was a lower overall completion rate in TEST rather than poorer discrimination. The early-dropout model showed weaker discrimination, slightly underestimated the overall early-dropout rate, and had a lower calibration slope, indicating that the spread of predicted risks was less well calibrated in later service years. The early-dropout Brier score was also essentially the same as that of a simple model based only on the overall early-dropout rate, indicating that the baseline predictors added little to overall probability accuracy. This pattern shows why temporal validation is necessary for service-related prediction in long-running routine-care programs. Different aspects of model performance may change over time; in this study, discrimination was relatively stable for completion, whereas early-dropout prediction was less stable in the later service period. Engagement patterns in the OTU service shifted substantially over time, with completion decreasing and early dropout increasing in later years. These changes in outcome rates likely contributed to the miscalibration observed when earlier-year models were applied to 2022–2024, because models trained in earlier years implicitly expected different baseline completion and dropout rates than those observed later. The TRAIN/TEST screening comparison also suggests that temporal validation occurred in the context of modest participant case-mix differences: the later evaluation period included slightly older participants and small shifts in gender, ethnicity, geographic location, and employment status, while education, relationship status, current mental health treatment, and baseline PHQ-9 and GAD-7 severity showed trivial differences. These differences may help contextualize calibration drift, although they should not be interpreted as causal explanations for changes in model performance.

In this service, a major operational change occurred in assessment administration, with lesson-anchored symptom monitoring during 2013–2019 and weekly monitoring from 2020 onward. However, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the weekly-assessment era showed that many adjusted predictor-outcome associations were broadly similar between 2020–2021 and 2022–2024, although some predictor-specific differences remained. Routine-care services may also evolve in other ways over time, including enrollment and onboarding procedures, staffing and workflow, platform features, messaging practices, and client referral pathways. More broadly, the rapid growth of virtual care, including physician-based mental health services delivered by phone or video in Canada and Saskatchewan during and after the COVID-19 period, may also have influenced demand, referral pathways, and the mix of clients entering ICBT in later years (Canadian Institute for Health Information, 2022). Together, these implementation-era and system-level shifts provide plausible context for the change in the overall completion rate and the weaker early-dropout prediction observed in 2022–2024. These trends are best viewed as indicators of a changing service context rather than evidence of any single driver. COVID-19 era system changes and routine program evolution may both have contributed. Declining completion should also be interpreted cautiously because some non-completion may reflect stopping after meaningful early improvement.

Implications

The findings support distinguishing stages of engagement but provide limited support for using baseline variables to identify individuals at risk of early dropout. This distinction is consistent with recent personalization frameworks that differentiate baseline risk assessment from responsiveness during treatment (Aafjes-van Doorn and Christensen, 2026). Future research should examine whether engagement support after the early phase is more useful when informed by early clinical response and the client's felt need to continue. Unlike in randomized trials, where completion is often treated as the primary adherence target, routine-care services do not necessarily aim for full completion for every client; some people stop early because they improve quickly or feel they have received enough benefit.

In this service, demographic factors (e.g., age, education, ethnicity, and relationship status) were more consistently linked to engagement than symptom severity. Given the limited predictive value of baseline variables for early dropout, these characteristics should not be used alone to determine who receives closer early monitoring. This caution is especially important for ethnicity. Differences in engagement by ethnicity may reflect structural, contextual, or service-related barriers rather than intrinsic individual risk, and using ethnicity alone to allocate additional monitoring or support could reinforce inequities. The ‘Other ethnicity’ category was also heterogeneous, and the Indigenous subgroup was comparatively small, so these estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Early in-treatment signals, such as lesson progress, time between log-ins, therapist contact, and emerging barriers to engagement, may be more informative and warrant evaluation in future research. Future studies could evaluate whether brief targeted telephone check-ins improve engagement for clients showing early signs of disengagement. Evidence from guided digital care suggests that additional contact can improve adherence in some settings (Pihlaja et al., 2020), although the value of targeting such support to clients at risk of early dropout remains to be established.

After the initial engagement period (e.g., after Dose 2), future research should examine whether support informed by early clinical response and engagement patterns improves retention. Because prior research links early engagement indicators to later discontinuation risk (Wu et al., 2022), services may benefit from monitoring engagement and clinical change, although the value of using these indicators to guide additional support requires further evaluation. Future studies should examine whether early symptom response and early engagement indicators improve prediction of subsequent completion and disengagement beyond baseline characteristics. For the clients who improve early, future research should examine how symptom change can inform decisions about whether additional lessons are still needed. For clients who are not improving or are struggling to engage, future research could examine whether stepped support addressing barriers, treatment fit, pacing, reminders, or support intensity improves engagement, given broader evidence linking greater engagement with better outcomes in digital interventions (Gan et al., 2021). Finally, engagement should be interpreted in context: more use is not automatically better, and services benefit from defining what “intended engagement” means for their program and evaluating engagement alongside outcomes (Smith et al., 2025).

Limitations

This study used observational routine-care data, so associations should not be interpreted causally. Unmeasured factors, such as digital literacy, competing demands, preferences, therapist factors, and early treatment experiences, likely influence engagement (Koelen et al., 2025; Lawler et al., 2021). The primary models were also restricted to baseline screening variables available across the primary analytic period. This approach supported temporal validation across service years, but it meant that potentially important early in-treatment indicators, such as initial logins, lesson access, early therapist contact, and first-week program experiences, were not included. This limitation is particularly relevant for early dropout, which may depend more strongly on what happens immediately after enrollment than on screening characteristics alone. Early dropout was also relatively uncommon, occurring in approximately 6–7% of participants, which limited the number of events available for model estimation and temporal validation and increased uncertainty around performance estimates. The service also evolved over time through both operational changes and intentional refinements, including the shift in assessment administration and other adjustments in guidance and course delivery procedures, such as contact schedules, review of homework records, extensions, booster resources, and related workflow updates. Because these changes can alter engagement rates and the relationships between predictors and outcomes, ongoing monitoring and recalibration would be necessary if engagement prediction models were implemented in routine service delivery. Finally, lesson-based outcomes, including completion and early dropout, are clinically meaningful but imperfect proxies: some non-completers may stop after experiencing sufficient benefit, and some completers may persist despite limited gains. More broadly, engagement research cautions against equating “more clicks” or “more modules” with meaningful engagement without considering intended use and clinical context (Nahum-Shani et al., 2022; Sieverink et al., 2017). Despite these limitations, the large routine-care sample and prespecified temporal validation provide an implementation-relevant picture of engagement patterns and their screening correlates in a long-running ICBT service.

Conclusions

Engagement in routine-care ICBT was more consistently related to demographic characteristics, including age, education, ethnicity, and relationship status, than to symptom severity at screening. Early dropout was especially difficult to predict from screening variables alone. Temporal validation showed that discrimination for completion was maintained in later service years, whereas discrimination for early dropout was weaker. Completion was modestly overpredicted in TEST, whereas early dropout showed slight underprediction and weaker separation of higher- and lower-risk clients, underscoring the importance of evaluating prediction models in later service periods and monitoring calibration over time. Practically, baseline variables had limited usefulness for identifying individuals at risk of early dropout. Future research should test whether targeted supports informed by early indicators, such as lesson progress, symptom change, or therapist contact, improve retention and equity in routine care.
