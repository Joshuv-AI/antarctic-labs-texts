Research on cerebral palsy football: a scoping review
Authors: Rui Bao, Xinpu Wang, Yuchen Shi, Miguel Ángel Gómez Ruano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study conducts the first scoping review in cerebral palsy (CP) football, a field that has received limited and uneven research attention. While the existing literature has primarily focused on aspects like match load and performance analysis, critical areas such as training interventions and health outcomes have not been consolidated or comprehensively reviewed. This review therefore aims to summarize available evidence, identify key research needs, and provide a comprehensive overview of existing knowledge.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Four selected databases (Web of Science, SPORTDiscus, PubMed and Scopus) were searched on 07/01/2025. Any original research written in FIFA languages published in peer-reviewed journals at any level, any age, and on any subject was eligible for inclusion in this study. All included studies were scoped for authors, journals, titles and abstracts. Population assessed number of participants, age groups, sex groups, level of play and the themes of publication were extracted. Data from the included studies were extracted and then examined to identify key research themes and gaps.ResultsA total of 299 records were found, but only 51 met the selection criteria and therefore scoped. The results highlighted the recent interest in the scientific literature. Match load and strength and conditioning topics constituted two top frequently explored areas. Notably, no studies focusing on the theme of medicine or psychology were identified. Regarding the participant population, the majority of studies were focused on professional players, most of whom were male players. The majority of research were conducted by Spanish scholars, showing the need for research in diverse socio-cultural contexts.ConclusionIn recent years, a continuous growth in academic attention has been observed in the field of CP football, reflecting broader trends in para-sport where increased participation, institutional investment, and professionalization, particularly within para-sport, have stimulated expanded research on the performance and physiological characteristics of athletes with disabilities. International competitions such as the IFCPF World Cup further elevate the visibility of the sport and underscore the practical need for evidence-based approaches to training, classification, and performance optimization. This study contributes to identifying the research gaps for further studies in this multifaceted, diverse and evolving area of CP football. More specifically, the findings of this study suggested that future research should address the gaps in the area of medicine and psychology, focus on female players and non-professional players, and conduct investigations within diverse sociocultural contexts, while also providing guidance for practices, such as the optimization of CP football training programs and the establishment of a comprehensive support system for footballers with CP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01436-4.Keywords: Athletic performance, Disabled, Motor disorders, Sports, Soccer

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) football, also named 7-a-side football, is an adaptation of football for players with CP or acquired brain injury. International CP football competition began in 1978 at an event organized by the Cerebral Palsy International Sports and Recreation Association (CP-ISRA) in Edinburgh, Scotland. In 1984, CP football was included as an official Paralympic sport, and the first World Championships were held in 1986 [1]. In recent years, CP football has become one of the most popular disability sports in the world as many countries and regions have started to develop this sport [1].

CP football is a modified version of regular football designed for athletes with CP, played on a smaller pitch that is 70 m long by 50 m wide, with goals measuring 6 m x 2 m [2]. It is worth noting footballers with CP are characterized by unique features. Specifically, they are characterized by non-progressive neuromotor impairments, primarily spastic hypertonia, which results in compromised motor control, postural stability, and gait [1, 2]. They compete in a sport demanding high intermittent physical capacity for sprinting and change of direction, while executing technical skills under constraints of neuromuscular fatigue. Their participation is defined by a classification system that ensures fairness by stratifying players according to impairment severity, alongside rule modifications such as a reduced field, seven-a-side format, and no offside rule, which adapt the game to emphasize their technical-tactical proficiency [1]. In addition, considering the relative physical limitations of footballers with CP, the match time is only 30 min each for the first and second halves [2].

To ensure competitive equity, the inclusion of athletes with eligible impairments and their subsequent classification into sport classes are fundamental. According to the IFCPF Classification Rules and Regulations [1], to be eligible for CP football, athletes must have a neurological impairment that causes a permanent and verifiable activity limitation, and must be affected by at least one of the following impairment types: hypertonia, ataxia, or athetosis. Following evaluation, athletes are assigned a sport class based on the degree to which this impairment impacts football-specific activities. A lower sport class number (e.g., FT1) indicates a more significant activity limitation. Consequently, regulations stipulate that a team must field at least one FT1 player and no more than one FT3 player at any time to maintain fairness [1, 3].

Although CP football has received ever-increasing attention in recent years, and the volume of related research has expanded considerably [2–8]. However, the body of research specific to CP football is limited in both scope and volume, particularly when compared to the extensive literature available for traditional football, futsal, and beach football [9–11]. CP football is often considered one of the para-sports with a more developed evidence base for classification research [1, 6]. However, research in other areas remains markedly underdeveloped [2, 4, 5, 7]. The scarcity of research on CP football has resulted in inadequate public awareness of the sport’s unique adaptations and athlete requirements. As such, this review aims to provide football practitioners and fans with a comprehensive understanding of CP football and the current state of its research.

This study aims to conduct a scoping review of the existing literature on CP football. According to our best investigations, this study is the first scoping review of CP football. We attempted to search all peer reviewed studies published in FIFA languages (English, Spanish, French and German) on any topic related to CP football in order to investigate the existing quantity of studies on this sport. It is important to further understand the amount of existing literature within this complex, heterogeneous and growing field of research. This could help us identify topics in current research and open up debates about the research gap, as well as being crucial in guiding the direction of future research [10, 11]. Any under-investigated topics within CP football constitute key focal points of our study, as it is precisely these overlooked aspects that our research aims to address, thereby filling the corresponding gaps in the literature. In addition, it is crucial to be concerned about the development of athletes with disabilities. Therefore, a scoping review of CP football is warranted and of high research value, helping CP football practitioners and stakeholders to better understand the sport and the potential development trends.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The protocol of this scoping review was developed by combining the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist, proposed by Tricco et al. [12], and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology provided by Peters et al. [13]. The protocol for this study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (10.17605/OSF.IO/6CXWK). In addition, the statement of consent was not needed for the present research.

Search strategy

The search aimed to comprehensively identify all literature types relevant to CP football, with no restrictions on study design. Four relevant databases were selected for the search: Web of Science, SPORTDiscus, PubMed and Scopus. According to our investigation, these four databases are among the most comprehensive databases worldwide and were therefore selected. The search was constructed using CP football and synonyms as search terms. Similar to other scoping review studies, the Booleans were added to optimize the search [10, 11]. Hence, the following keywords were employed: cerebral AND palsy AND football OR soccer OR CP football. Additionally, the search was performed on 7th January 2025.

Study selection

All records are imported into Rayyan (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar), a free online review management software [14]. Duplicates will be noted automatically in this review management software, the reviewer will conduct a cross check and delete the duplicates. Two reviewers performed independent analyses of titles and abstracts based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1), and calculated the initial Kappa coefficient to quantify baseline consistency (K = 0.65, indicating moderate consistency). After independent screening, discrepant records (where the first reviewer and the second reviewer’s judgments conflicted) were compiled into a ‘Disagreement Log’ and sent to the third reviewer (7 years of experience, blinded to the first reviewer/the second reviewer’s judgments) for independent re-screening with rationale. If the third reviewer’s judgment aligned with either the first reviewer or the second reviewer, that judgment was finalized; otherwise, a meeting with all three reviewers was held to reach consensus [11, 15]. After resolving all discrepancies, a final Kappa coefficient was recalculated based on the finalized judgments (K = 0.82, indicating substantial agreement), confirming that the resolution process effectively improved inter-reviewer consistency. For the abstract where the publication language was not clearly specified, full-text checks were conducted. For articles extracted from the four databases with no available abstract, reviewers conducted additional searches via Loughborough College Library, Technical University of Madrid Library, and East China Normal University Library. If full texts remained unavailable after these searches, the articles were excluded; otherwise, eligibility was determined based on full-text content. Additionally, in order to make the selection process is more robust, all selected journals were re-confirmed by reviewers that the peer-review process was in place. Ultimately, the full text of selected citations were retrieved and uploaded to Mendeley (Elsevier, Amsterdam, Netherlands), a free reference manager [16].

Critical appraisal of included studies

The critical appraisal of included studies was not performed in this study. The core objective of this scoping review is to map the breadth and landscape of CP football research, identifying themes, methodologies, and gaps, rather than synthesizing evidence to support definitive conclusions or recommendations. Given the heterogeneity of included studies, applying a unified critical appraisal tool would be methodologically inappropriate and irrelevant to the review’s purpose. This approach aligns with PRISMA-ScR guidelines, which note that critical appraisal may be omitted in scoping reviews when it does not serve the review’s aims.

Data extraction

The following information from the included studies were imported into an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft, Albuquerque, New Mexico): the author, journal, title, abstract, year and country. Two authors (RB & XPW) independently extracted the population assessed, number of subjects, age group involved, level of play involved, sex group group involved and conducted an inductive style thematic analysis to determine the publication themes based on the information provided in the title and abstract. Further, extraction and analysis strategies will be continuously modified and refined throughout the process, if required. In addition, it is worth mentioning that the theme titles are inspired by common terminology taken from the Fédération Internationale de Football Association (FIFA), Union of European Football Associations (UEFA), International Federation of Cerebral Palsy Football (IFCPF) and International Paralympic Committee (IPC). Meanwhile, according to our investigation, previous researchers have made similar thematic categorisations for women’s football [11] and women’s futsal [10]. Therefore, based on the previous studies and theme titles inspired by common terminology taken from FIFA, UEFA, IFCPF and IPC, the categorisation of this study was developed and presented in the following table (Table 2).

Data charting

All data was imported into a single Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) for charting. Using different types of graphs to show the data. For instance, using line graphs to describe the continuous data (i.e., the year of publication), and bar graphs to show the categorical data (e.g., journals, populations, themes, etc.). Results and discussion

After screening the titles, abstracts and full texts of the remaining 71 articles, 18 articles were found not to meet the inclusion criteria. In addition, one article was not available in full text, even after re-examination by three university libraries (Loughborough College, University of Edinburgh and Technical University of Madrid libraries). Consequently, only 51 articles were included in this study. The flow chart of study inclusion process is presented in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the study inclusion process

Publication tendencies

The first study regarding CP football was published in 2015. This reveals that people have just started focusing on this sport in the last decade. Since 2017, the number of publications about CP football has shown an upward trend at last, even though studies regarding this subject are also only in single digits. Until 2022, the number of publications exceeds double digits for the first time and marks the peak in the number of publications (n = 12) related to the topic to date (Fig. 2). There is an possible explanation for the increasing trend towards CP football related publications. In recent years, the increasing number of CP football competitions organized by the IFCPF and different national football associations can be an indication of the growing importance attached to this sport. With the massive media exposure and television coverage, this stands as clear evidence of the increase in CP football related publications. The publication tendencies are shown in Fig. 2.

Number of studies on CP football published over the years

Compared to the vast body of research on regular football, scholarly work on CP football (n = 51) is substantially limited. This disparity is reflected in its publication in a more limited selection of journals. According to our survey, studies on CP football have been published in 29 different peer-reviewed journals (Fig. 3). The number of studies published in the International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance was the highest with six studies. A close second is a journal from Spain known as Retos, with five studies. While highlighting the important role that these two peer-reviewed journals have played in disseminating research related to CP football, it also demonstrates the need for more journals to publish relevant studies in the future for the development of this sport. Journals with the most published studies on CP football are presented in Fig. 3.

Geographical distribution of authors

From a geographical perspective, the authors come from 11 different countries across three continents (Fig. 4). The European countries are Spain, United Kingdom, Italy, Netherlands, Switzerland, Portugal and Ukraine. The American countries are the United States, Chile and Brazil. The Oceania country is Australia. It is worth mentioning that authors from Spain participated in 41 studies, covering 82% of the total. Authors from Chile and Brazil followed, involving eight and five studies, respectively. Overall, the potential geographical bias does exist in scientific production on CP football, mainly dominated by Spanish scholars. As the research samples employed by Spanish researchers may primarily consist of domestic players, samples from other countries were relatively scarce. Therefore, more studies from other countries are needed in the future.

Geographic distribution of the authors of the studies on CP football

Sample characteristics

The majority of the studies focused on players (n = 50), with 47 of the studies focused on adult male footballers with CP (aged ≥ 16 years) and the other studies focused on adult female (n = 2, aged ≥ 16 years) and children (n = 1, aged < 16 years), respectively (Fig. 5). It is noteworthy that there is a paucity of research on female footballers with CP (n = 2). This is also evidenced by studies on other sports, where the number of studies on female athletes with disabilities is insufficient [17–19]. However, if more research was focused on women footballers with CP, it would greatly contribute to the discussion on how to coach female footballers with CP. Additionally, only one research was performed on referees. It is worth noting that research on the different roles within CP football will contribute to a better understanding of the internal and external factors that affect the sport’s motor performance, as well as the operational patterns of the CP football industry.

Schematic design of the populations addressed in the studies

Within the publications with levels stated, 50 studies (98%) contained data from professional CP football and only one study (2%) from recreational CP football (Fig. 5). There is an possible explanation for the reason why most publications have focused on professional rather than recreational. Compared to professional CP football, recreational CP football generally operates at a lower competitive level due to factors such as less structured training, variability in player experience, and limited access to resources. This perceived lower level of competition often results in reduced visibility and scholarly interest. The populations addressed in the studies are presented in Fig. 5.

The theme of publications

Research on CP football covers 11 different themes (Fig. 6). The largest number of publications were undertaken on Match Load (n = 10), which was the first major research theme. Studies on Strength and Conditioning followed behind (n = 8). The Performance Analysis (match play) and Performance Analysis (physical performance) themes tied for third place with six publications each. In general, although the body of literature on CP football is still developing, the distribution of research themes is notably diverse (Fig. 6).

Match load

A total of 10 studies focused on match load, with the population consisting of professional footballers with CP. Match load refers to the total physical and mental demands an athlete encounters during a competitive match [20, 21]. Six researches assessed the external match load of footballers with CP [5, 20–22, 24, 25]. In addition, the relationship of coordination tests with match physical load of footballers with CP from different sport classes was also investigated [23]. Other studies investigated neuromuscular factors, exploring how changes in neuromuscular performance relate to match loads in footballers with CP [26, 27]. Notably, Henríquez et al. conducted an interesting study which investigated the differences in physical responses of footballers with CP due to different match loads at different altitudes (sea level and moderate altitude) [28].

Theoretically, research on match load might help practitioners better understand the match load characteristics of CP football, providing theoretical basis for load management for this population. Further, from a practical perspective, numerous studies on match load could help coaches to better manage, process and optimize the training plan, which could enhance the players’ performance, as well as reduce the risk of injury.

Strength and conditioning

The studies in this area (n = 8) were conducted with professional footballers with CP. One study investigated player’s anaerobic fitness using the Wingate Anaerobic Test and vertical jumping tests. The result showed low anaerobic capacity and vertical jumping of footballers with CP compared to the general population and national level footballers with CP. This highlights the need for specific S&C training programs to improve lower limb explosive strength and anaerobic capacity in footballers with CP [8]. Other studies evaluated the vertical and horizontal jumping capacities in footballer with CP to reveal differences in motor performance in jumping among players with different levels of impairment [4, 7]. In addition, Henriquez et al. assessed the repeated sprint and change of direction abilities of footballers with CP by using shuttle run and the Illinois Agility Test, contributing to the discussion of the relationship between repeated sprint ability and change of direction ability of footballers with CP in different sport classes [29].

In order to investigate the Sprint Strength-Velocity profile and its validity and reliability in footballers with CP, Peña-González et al. identified strengths and weaknesses in the physical performance of footballers with CP by employing the vertical jumping, change of direction, 30-m sprint, dribbling and intermittent endurance tests to help S&C coaches optimize the players’ training strategies to improve their performance [30]. Additionally, a research was conducted to evaluate the effects of a self-training program on the physical fitness of footballers with CP during the COVID-19 lockdown. In this study, 15 players from the Spanish national team performed a 12-week self-training program using the free countermovement jump, the modified agility T-test, a dribbling test and 5, 10 and 20-m sprint. The results showed that this self-training program could help footballer with CP maintain their physical fitness during the non-competitive period. Furthermore, training four times a week including general (non-football specific) strength and endurance exercises also can help footballers with CP to maintain football specific physical fitness, which might be essential during the non-competitive period [3].

Another aspect analyzed by the studies is how to rapidly enhance the players’ physical performance through scientific S&C training program and exercise monitoring prior to major competitions [31, 32], which might contribute to the optimization and enhancement of preparation-period training programs for head coaches and S&C coaches.

Research on S&C is generally comprehensive and has made significant contributions both theoretically and practically, contributing to the reconstruction of people’s cognitive framework regarding physical training for disabled populations, as well as helping S&C coaches gain a deeper understanding of the specific abilities lacking in footballers with CP compared to able-bodied footballers during matches, which enables coaches to design more targeted S&C training programs that work for footballers with CP.

Performance analysis (physical performance)

In this area, 5 studies were conducted with professional footballers with CP and one study was performed on international CP football referees. The player-specific studies all focused on discussing the physical performance analysis of footballers with CP [33, 35, 36], showing that the physical performance of footballers with CP was much lower than footballers without disabilities [34]. In addition, Peña-González et al. indicated that no systematic variations in physical performance between footballers with CP according to their age [37]. Finally, a study has evaluated the physical performance and physical responses of international CP football referees during officiating, finding that the physical performance demands and physical responses of officiating top-level CP football games are much higher than officiating lower-level regular football games [38]. Overall, footballers with CP were found to have weaker physical performance than able-bodied footballers. This is reasonable, however, following research could focus more on how to optimize the physical performance of footballers with CP through training and competition.

Performance analysis (match play)

Studies focusing on professional matches have consistently observed an average of approximately 5 to 7 goals per game, a figure substantially higher than that found in able-bodied football [40–44]. The majority of goals were scored from open play [42, 44], and most of the goals were scored from inside the penalty area [40, 42, 44]. Additionally, in contrast to able-bodied football [64], the distribution of goals scored was not varied by halves or by 15-min duration [41, 42]. According to the investigation, in most matches, the team who scores first goal is associated with a higher probability of winning the match [41, 42]. Furthermore, other studies have explored goal scoring patterns, highlighting the most effective types of attack in CP football matches [39, 43]. Generally, in CP football, the goals scored characteristics, pattern and distribution are quite different from regular football. Due to the limited sample size available for match play, it is unclear whether these characteristics and trends will be seen in most CP football matches, and therefore further research is needed.

Classification of footballers with CP

The studies (n = 5) primarily focus on discussing the classification of professional footballers with CP through multidisciplinary scientific research, as classification is a defining feature of para-sport [45–49]. Notably, classification of footballers is a pioneering theme, which is not found in futsal, beach football or regular football. This theme was created due to the particular characteristics of CP football, which requires the classification of players. A mathematical model which called Data Envelopment Analysis was employed to make classification decision-making for players with different severity of coordination impairments, finding that the model could be used to detect and discriminate the players’ performance and magnitude of impairments [45]. In addition, Sarabia et al. investigated the appropriateness of using decision trees as a classification tool in CP football. The results demonstrate that Data Envelopment Analysis can offer reference descriptors and values to assist classifiers in decision-making and player classification within CP football [46]. In addition, several studies have utilized sport-specific classification systems to evaluate player performance and support classification processes [47, 48]. Furthermore, the relationship between classification and sex differences has also been examined [49]. Various training methods and models have been applied to classification, demonstrating the efforts of practitioners and researchers. However, current classification methods still have room for improvement, requiring additional research to be conducted in the future.

Anthropometry/Morphology

A total of four studies focused on the body, targeting professional footballers with CP. Most studies investigated the body composition and anthropometrical features of players [50, 51, 53]. Further, Peña-González et al. explored the differences in anthropometrical data and physical performance between national team selected footballers with CP and nonselected footballers with CP, providing evidence to support the selection process for footballers with CP [52]. Due to variations in samples, the findings of these studies do not reveal many commonalities. However, it is certain that conducting more research on this theme in the future will enable doctors and coaches to achieve a better knowledge of the body composition and anthropometric features of footballers with CP, thereby contributing to their performance and rehabilitation.

Physiology

The studies in the area (n = 4) were conducted with professional players. There is a research has been dedicated to analyzing physiological responses in the CP football game, investigating the neural and non-neural factors that influence sports performance [55]. Recently, Álvarez-Hernández et al. explored the specific physiological responses (heart rate and time spent at different zones of maximum heart rate) of footballers with CP during the game play [56]. In addition, the cardiovascular and locomotive demands of match play were also discussed [2]. Another aspect that has been the theme of research is the analysis of physiological responses of footballers with CP in two small-sided games and a simulated game [54]. The results of these studies all involved physiological responses such as heart rate and cardiovascular function. However, the potential mechanisms underlying the relationship between various indicators and athletic performance, as well as differences in physiological responses under different competition environments, have not been fully explored.

Biomechanics

The studies (n = 4) were performed on professional footballers with CP. The focus of the researches was on biomechanical variables that impact performance in jumping, balancing and heading [57–60]. The results of our investigation show consistent impairment in jumping and balance performance among footballers with CP across all severity levels of impairment. Hence, future research is needed to investigate how to optimize the balancing and jumping performance of footballers with CP.

Performance analysis (skills)

The studies (n = 2) were conducted on professional footballers with CP. Both studies have focused its attention on the dribbling skills [6, 61], suggesting that impairments could greatly affect players’ dribbling performance. However, to date, generalizability of the findings is lacking due to the limited availability of such studies.

Other emerging themes (medication and supplement & sociology)

Only one study focused on the aspect of medication and supplement, with the population consisting of professional footballers with CP. This study investigated the use of medication and supplement in the Disability Football World Championships, finding that the medication and supplement are overused in CP football. In particular, 70% footballers with CP reported using at least one substance per tournament, with 57.9% footballers with CP using at least one prescribed medication. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are among the most popular prescription medications [62]. In addition, only one study focused on sociology as well, however with the population of recreational minor players. A qualitative method was used to explore the perspectives of clinicians and parents on the participation of minor with CP in community-based football, highlighting the importance of the minor’s support system in facilitating the participation of minor with CP in community-based physical activity, especially the CP football [63].

What are the literature gaps need to be investigated in further research?

One of the main contributions of this study, in addition to describing the present landscape of research on CP football, is to identify the research gaps in relation to the investigated populations and themes that need to be investigated in further studies. With regard to the population, the authors highlight the need for research into CP football coaches, referees and managers, which have been overlooked up to now. While players, as the main protagonists on the pitch, rightly receive the most research attention, a comprehensive understanding of the sport demands insight into all its key actors. Referees, coaches, and team managers are integral to the game’s ecosystem, influencing outcomes, athlete performance, and overall dynamics. Therefore, research on these populations is highly relevant as it can lead to improved training protocols for officials, enhanced coaching strategies, and more effective team management, ultimately benefiting the entire sport. In addition, the authors also need to emphasize the lack of studies on women’s CP football, as there is only two existing study focused on women’s CP football. This lack of investigation limits our understanding of the specific physiological, biomechanical, and performance characteristics of female footballers with CP. Such limited evidence may hinder the development of targeted strategies to promote participation among women with CP. Meanwhile, focusing on women’s CP football also contributes to the promotion of gender equality. Only if women’s CP football is investigated as thoroughly as men’s, will the sport be expected to develop more inclusively and equitably. In addition, recreational footballers with CP represent a significant segment of the CP football community, underscoring the need for more research focused on this population. However, conducting such studies presents practical challenges, as data from professional CP football competitions are more readily accessible due to the public availability of most match statistics and performance indicators. This ease of data collection has led to a predominance of studies at the professional level. Consequently, to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of CP football as a whole, future research should deliberately prioritize and invest in studies involving recreational participants. Without research on these key populations, the development of CP football will be constrained, as well as its unique social and rehabilitative value might be overlooked.

Concerning the investigated themes, the authors found that the areas of match load and S&C dominated the research, supporting the development of match strategies and S&C training programs. However, according to our best investigation, it is surprising that no research has been conducted on the theme of medicine and psychology so far, which becomes urgent research gaps to be investigated. Exploring the aspects relating to medicine (e.g., injury epidemiology and risk factors) and psychology is of utmost importance in improving the sports performance of footballers with CP. Additionally, no study was found on coaching, marketing, history, business, law, finance and equipment, which also shows the need to focus on these fields.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review followed the guidelines of PRISMA-SR [12]. This approach was carried out based on our interest to scope the academic literature from the formal source of research (i.e., peer-reviewed journals). According to our investigation, 50 studies were published in English, with only one study published in Spanish. Although French and German as official FIFA languages were used as search languages for this study, no publications were found. Notably, such language-related bias may lead to the omission of research on other languages, resulting in findings biased toward English-speaking countries and neglecting unique research findings from other countries and regions, thereby restricting the generalizability of research findings. Based on this consideration, we searched other languages and found that some publications were written in Portuguese. However, since the authors are not proficient in Portuguese, publications written in Portuguese were excluded. In addition, it needs to be acknowledged that research not available from the selected databases will definitely not be included in the present study. Further, the methodological quality of the research revived demonstrates both strengths and limitations. Most of studies employed appropriate designs and provided clear descriptions of their data collection and analysis procedure. While both qualitative and quantitative research designs are applied, there is only one research primarily relied on qualitative data to present how environmental factors impact on the participation of CP football among young children [63]. This provides opportunities for qualitative research methodology to be applied in future research in CP football. Lastly, the authors manually classified the research into 10 different themes. However, it is worth mentioning that, although these themes were screened by two independent reviewers, it might still be impacted by a certain degree of bias. Last, future reviews in this field could adopt other classification frameworks (e.g., para-sport athlete classification framework) to facilitate more direct comparisons across studies.

Conclusion

This review was the first study to conduct a comprehensive peer-reviewed literature on all aspects of CP football. A total of 51 studies was included from 29 different peer-reviewed journals, highlighting the difference in the focus on CP football across different themes. The first study related to CP football was published in 2015. CP football is still at an early stage of development, however, in general, the number of studies on CP football shows an upward trend over the years. Additionally, the authors found that the publishing landscape is quite homogenous, mainly led by Spanish researchers, primarily focused on professional level and was developed in the area of match load and S&C. Further, given the unique regulation of CP football, more studies should be undertaken on how to better classify footballers with CP according to their impairments in order to facilitate the better development of this sport. Meanwhile, it is worth noting that medicine and psychology aspects of the study are highly needed. These findings provide instructive practical implications for practitioners. For coaches, existing research focused on match load and S&C could provide scientific guidance for optimizing training plans for footballers with CP, thereby enhancing their performance more effectively. Regarding policymakers, the homogenization of the publication landscape and the lack of classification-related research highlight the need to strengthen international cooperation and cross-regional research support, for instance, funding research teams in CP football-underdeveloped countries to conduct localized research. In terms of players, the gaps in medicine and psychology research also emphasize the need to strengthen support in these areas in the future, which could provide players with more precise medical care and mental support, contributing to their development. This scoping review contributes to the development of scientific research on CP football. Specifically, this study, by reviewing the existing literature, identifies the research gaps which could be investigated in further research, expanding the themes of investigation, populations and geographic locations. Overall, this study could contribute to guiding the focus of future research in this multifaceted, diverse and evolving area of CP football.
