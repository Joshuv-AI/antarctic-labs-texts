Reinterpreting the sunward electron deficit: Implications for solar wind acceleration and core population formation
Z. Nemeth

Abstract
Aims. This paper re-evaluates the relationship between the observed sunward electron cutoff energy and the depth of the Sun’s global electrostatic potential. It investigates whether taking into account the effects of local traps formed by magnetic fluctuations provides an alternative explanation for the observed electron deficit. Methods. The fluctuations of the highly variable interplanetary magnetic field form a series of shallow magnetic mirror traps that move at approximately the speed of the solar wind. The study investigates the dynamics of electrons as they move outward against an attractive solar electrostatic potential and interact with these traps. By following the motion of the electrons using first-principles calculations, we assess the effect of the traps on the velocity distribution of the particles. Electrons that escape the local trap continue to lose energy as they move outward until they are eventually captured by subsequent traps, preventing them from returning to the observer as sunward-moving particles. We derive a mathematical expression for the cutoff velocity, defined as the limit beyond which particles can no longer overtake the outer endpoint of a local trap. Results. It turns out that the observed cutoff energy characterizes only the local potential drop within a trap, rather than the total depth of the Sun’s potential well. The true potential well can be significantly deeper, scaled by the ratio of the radial distance from the Sun to the trap size. Furthermore, electrons captured by these moving traps contribute to the formation of the solar wind core population. Conclusions. The Sun’s electrostatic potential is a more significant factor in solar wind acceleration than previously interpreted from cutoff data. The interaction between electrostatic deceleration and moving magnetic traps provides a new framework for understanding the origin and behavior of the solar wind core electrons.



1
Introduction

Recent space missions, especially the unprecedented observations of the
Parker Solar Probe
(
Fox et al. 2016
)
, have enabled us to gain previously
unattainable knowledge about the nascent solar wind. Based on
theoretical considerations, it has long been assumed that the Sun has an
electrostatic field (formed because the Sun's
gravitational field alone cannot retain the more mobile electrons),
which plays a significant role in accelerating solar wind ions
(
Lemaire & Scherer 1971
)
. Previously, it was not possible to experimentally
investigate this field, but recently, some clues pointing to its
existence have been discovered in the anisotropies of particle
distributions observed near the Sun. Several articles
(
Berčič et al. 2021
;
Halekas et al. 2020
;
Halekas et al. 2021
;
Halekas et al. 2022
;
Raouafi et al. 2023
)
report that
some of the sunward moving electrons are missing above a certain energy
level. Since the simplest explanation for the existence of a population
moving toward the Sun is that an attractive potential turned back the
out-moving particles, the observed energy-dependent cutoff may indicate
that above this energy, electrons are able to escape the attractive
potential, i.e., the cutoff energy may characterize the depth of the
potential relative to the zero potential defined in spatial infinity.
Based on the above assumptions, calculating the depth of the potential
from the cutoff energy leads to the conclusion that the electric
potential of the Sun is significantly lower than what would be necessary
to accelerate the solar wind ions to the observed velocities. This
conclusion (if true) relegates the electric potential to the role of a
minor factor in solar wind acceleration and reopens the burning question
of what process might dominate ion acceleration in the collisionless
plasma of the solar wind.

It is tempting to suggest that we should revert to the fluid model of
solar wind acceleration pioneered by Eugene Parker
(
Parker 1958
)
,
especially since
Nemeth 2025
demonstrated that, under certain
conditions, magnetized plasmas can behave as fluids even in the absence
of particle-particle collisions. However, simple energy considerations
indicate that fluid effects alone cannot fully account for the
acceleration of the solar wind. The pressure gradient can only serve to
concentrate all the energy distributed among the different degrees of
freedom of the particles into a single translational degree of freedom.
(This is the same principle, which also underlies the theoretical limit
of outflow velocity that can be achieved using a de Laval nozzle.)
Simply put, if all particles entering the acceleration region exit on
the other side while no additional energy is introduced above the
thermal energy already present, then acceleration only means the
redistribution of energy between degrees of freedom – hence the upper
bound described above. The introduction of an electric potential,
particularly one that shifts from attractive to repulsive as an ion
moves away from the Sun, addresses this issue
(
Lemaire 2010
;
Maksimovic et al. 1997
;
Zouganelis et al. 2004
)
. Not all particles that enter the
process, and contribute energy, will escape the potential well of the
Sun. The portion that escapes takes energy away from the remaining
plasma, which consequently cools down. This aspect of the acceleration
is necessary to allow the observed high speed of the solar wind flow,
and this is what is called into question by the new observations.

To resolve this problem, we can proceed in one of two ways: either we
try to find a new solar wind acceleration process with appropriate
characteristics to replace the electric potential, or we can re-examine
the logic that relates the observed cutoff energy of sunward-moving
electrons to the depth of the potential well. In this paper, we pursue
the second option.

2
Methods and Results

What we can say with certainty based on the observations is that, above
a certain energy level, something prevents the outward-moving electrons
from returning to the measurement location. As we saw earlier, one
possibility is that these electrons completely leave the potential well
and disappear into infinity. The question is whether there is another
possibility.

In magnetized plasma, scattering on magnetic fluctuations plays a
decisive role in shaping the motion of particles along field lines.
These interactions determine not only the parallel pressure
(
Chew et al. 1956
)
, but also the fundamental nature of plasma behavior
(
Nemeth 2025
)
.
We can thus envision the entire field line system as a complex network
of shallow magnetic traps bounded by magnetic fluctuations. If the
energy of a particle is sufficiently small, it can be reflected by a
strong magnetic fluctuation or can even bounce back and forth in a
magnetic bottle formed by two significant field variations.

Add to this the effect of electrons continuously losing energy as they
move outward in an attractive potential together with the fact that the
magnetic traps also move away from the Sun on average as they travel
with the solar wind, and another possibility for explaining the observed
cut-off energy begins to emerge.

When an electron moves away from the Sun along a field line, it moves
against the electric field and thus slows down as it loses kinetic
energy. Similarly, if it approaches the Sun, it gains kinetic energy
from the electric potential. The endpoint of a magnetic trap closest to
the Sun has a lower potential than the endpoint furthest from the Sun.
Let us number the magnetic traps in ascending order from the Sun and
assume that the space probe performs its measurements in trap number
N
0
. Let us assume that a given outmoving electron has
enough energy to escape from this trap, and even from the next one,
etc., but that by the time it reaches trap N
0
+n, it has
lost enough energy in the electric potential to turn back. At the
turning point, the electron's velocity parallel to the
field line is zero, while its perpendicular velocity retains a finite
value.

If the magnetic traps were at rest, the electron returning to the
trap's inner endpoint would regain exactly as much
energy as it lost on its way from there, allowing it to pass through the
trap's ”bottleneck” on its way back as well. However,
since the traps move with the solar wind, the inner end of the trap
moves significantly further out during the time the electron needs to
move outwards, turn around, and start its sunward motion. Consequently,
the magnitude of the particle's parallel velocity is
significantly smaller when it encounters the inner end of the trap for
the second time than it was at the time of its first encounter.

It is easy to see that if the electron's speed is less
than twice the speed of the solar wind, the inner edge of the trap will
catch up with the particle before its parallel velocity drops to zero.
From this point on, the force arising due to the divergence of the
trap's magnetic field lines pushes the particle
outwards, i.e., the solar wind carries it away through the effect of the
trap. If the electron's speed is equal or only slightly
greater than twice the solar wind speed when it enters the trap, it will
still be trapped, as the ratio of parallel and perpendicular speeds will
be small enough for the reflected particle to avoid the loss cone. At
such or even higher speeds, however, there is a high probability that
the particle will pass through to the next trap, farther away, where it
will lose further energy and eventually slow down sufficiently to get
caught.

In other words, if an electron can escape from the trap denoted by
number N
0
, we will never see it again, because as its
energy decreases on its way out and, sooner or later, one of the traps
will catch and capture it. So, the electrons that we see moving inward
at the measurement site have been reflected within the first trap
(N
0
). It means that the cut-off energy observed in the
distribution does not characterize the full depth of the solar
electrostatic potential, but only its properties in the local
environment of the measurement site. As we will see, the cut-off depends
on the radial gradient of the potential together with the average size
of the trap, a.k.a, the distance between significant magnetic field
fluctuations.

3
Discussion

The question may be raised how the magnetic field can increase the
kinetic energy of the particle to compensate for the energy loss against
the electrostatic potential, but this question is misleading. In fact,
the static magnetic field and the solar electrostatic potential exist in
different frames of reference. In coordinates fixed to the Sun, the
magnetic field moves together with the solar wind plasma. In a frame
fixed to the plasma, we would measure continuously diminishing
electrostatic field. It is very enlightening to treat the problem in the
Sun-fixed frame. Suppose that the average of our divergent field line
bundle points in the radial direction. The perpendicular component of
the magnetic field is zero in the middle of the bundle, and it is
proportional to the perpendicular coordinates as we move away from its
middle (see Fig.
1
). If we compute the convective (motional) electric
field
uxB
, we find that the electric field lines circle around
the magnetic field line bundle and this electric field accelerates the
particles in the perpendicular direction. The perpendicular velocity is
then turned into the parallel direction by the mirror effect in the
usual way, and thus the parallel kinetic energy is replenished.

In summary, the fast electrons coming from the vicinity of the Sun can
traverse many consecutive magnetic traps moving outward, as long as
their parallel speed is high enough. However, the solar electric
potential acts to diminish their parallel energy, until finally the
speed of the electron decreases to close to solar wind speed. At that
time the magnetic traps embedded in the fluctuating magnetic field of
the solar wind are able to catch and carry away these particles. It is
worth noting that this process creates a cool electron population mowing
together with the solar wind, probably contributing to the core of the
solar wind electron distribution. A consequence of this process is that
any electron which escapes the magnetic trap situated around the
measurement site will not be seen as a reflected electron going towards
the Sun. Thus, the observed cut-off energy is not a characteristic of
the whole potential, only of the potential drop in the local trap.
Depending on the exact r dependence of the potential, the depth of the
potential well can be computed from the potential drop, and we find that
the depth is (up to a factor on the order of unity)

\frac{r_{0}}{\lambda}
times larger than the measured potential drop,
where
r_{0}
is the distance from the Sun and
\lambda
is the
characteristic radial size of the magnetic traps at that locality.

Another important aspect of the above results concerns the core
population of the electron component of the solar wind. It is often
assumed that this component consist of the electrons trapped between the
outer electrostatic and the inner magnetic mirror reflection points
(
Boldyrev et al. 2020
;
Lie-Svendsen & Leer 2000
;
Marsch 2006
;
Meyer-Vernet & Issautier 1998
, see e.g.:)
, but these reflection points are
considered to be global in the sense that the magnetic mirror arises
from the global divergence of the magnetic field lines (not the
fluctuations) and the electrostatic reflection point is determined by
the global properties of the electrostatic field without any influence
from magnetic fluctuations.
Boldyrev et al. 2020
explains the
observation that the core electron population moves at a speed that is
close to the solar wind speed with a slow collisional energization of
this trapped population. (When an electron gains parallel velocity in a
collision, the associated inner and outer reflection points shift, but
the shift of the outer reflection point is larger and thus the electrons
on average move away from the Sun). Here we have shown another process
which may contribute to the formation of the core population. As the
decelerated electrons are caught in the traps formed by magnetic
fluctuations, they contribute to a low-energy electron population. This
population inevitably moves together with the traps. Since the magnetic
traps move at a speed close to that of the solar wind, this population
moves together with the ion component as expected from the electron
core. Since the properties of magnetic traps are very likely to change
from flux-tube to flux-tube, this mechanism can also help to explain the
significant changes of core electron properties observed on flux-tube
boundaries
(
Borovsky et al. 2021
)
.

4
Conclusions

We have shown that the cut-off energy observed by the Parker Solar Probe
in the sunward moving portion of the electron distributions close to the
Sun is not directly related to the depth of the electrostatic potential
well at the measurement site, instead it characterizes the local
potential drop inside the magnetic trap surrounding the space probe.
Particles, which escape the local trap while moving away from the Sun
will never be seen again by the space probe, because they will be caught
by some subsequent magnetic trap after sufficient deceleration by the
electrostatic potential. Thus, the sunward moving electrons seen by PSP
are those already reflected inside the local trap, and thus their energy
cannot exceed the potential drop inside the local trap. The ratio of the
depth of the potential well to the potential drop in the trap equals to
(up to a factor on the order of unity) the ratio of the radial distance
of the measurement site from the Sun to the radial extent of the trap.
This means that the real depth of the potential well can be much larger
than that computed from the observed cutoff, consequently the
contribution of the electric potential to solar wind acceleration can be
more significant than prior interpretations of the cutoff would suggest.
The decelerated low-energy electron population caught in the traps will
move together with the traps, and thus with the solar wind. This trapped
population probably contributes to the core electron component of the
solar wind.
