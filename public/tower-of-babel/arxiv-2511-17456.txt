Fermat near misses and the integral Hilbert property
Jessica Alessandrì, Daniel Loughran

Abstract
. We consider the Diophantine equation x 4 + y 4 − w 2 = n x^{4}+y^{4}-w^{2}=n for n ∈ ℤ n\in{\mathbb{Z}} , which is related to near misses for the quartic case of Fermat’s Last Theorem. For certain n n we show that the set of solutions is infinite, or more generally not thin . Our approach is via the geometry of del Pezzo surfaces of degree 2 2 , and we prove a more general result on non-thinness of integral points on double conic bundle surfaces.

Primary 14G05; Secondary 11D25, 14J20, 14J26

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Fermat near misses

Which integers
n
are a sum of three integer cubes

x^{3}+y^{3}+z^{3}=n\;?

The case
n=0
is a special case of Fermat’s Last Theorem; for more general
n
one can view a solution as a “Fermat near miss”. A conjecture of Heath–Brown
[
14
]
predicts that the equation has a solution whenever
n\not\equiv 4,5\bmod 9
. Moreover, if a solution exists, then he conjectured that there are infinitely many solutions. Despite much study of this problem, including recent important computational work finding large solutions for various integers
n

[
3
,
2
]
, this conjecture is wide open in general. Infinitude is only known when
n
or
2n
is a cube.

We consider analogues for more general families of equations:

F(x,y,z)=n

where
F
is weighted homogeneous in
x,y,z
with weights
p,q,r
, respectively. To have a similar structure to sums of three cubes we require that
p+q+r=\deg F
.
We consider the next simplest possible weights
(p,q,r)=(1,1,2)
, giving an equation of weighted degree
4
. We also consider diagonal equations, being indefinite to allow the possibility for infinitely many solutions. This leads us to consider

(1.1)

x^{4}+y^{4}-w^{2}=n,\quad n\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

One can also view solutions to this equation as a kind of “Fermat near miss”, as the equation
x^{4}+y^{4}=w^{2}
plays a key role in the quartic case of Fermat’s Last Theorem.

These equations (
1.1
) have been recently been studied in
[
10
]
, where it was shown, rather surprisingly, that there are infinitely many solutions for
n=8
(the case
n=-1
appears on Mathematics Stack Exchange and MathOverflow
[
18
,
22
]
as a challenge problem, currently open). The solutions for
n=8
are constructed via a complicated recursive sequence. We put this argument into a geometric context, which allows us to prove existence of infinitely many solutions for a wide list of examples.
More than this, we are able to show that the integral points are
not thin
(see Definition
2.3
); this implies for example that they are Zariski dense.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and consider the affine surface

U_{n}:\quad x^{4}+y^{4}-w^{2}=n.

Then
U_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
is not thin provided there exists
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
(x,y,w)\in U_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
such that
one of the following holds.

(1)

n=m^{4}
or
n=-4m^{4}
.

(2)

n=m^{2}
is a square and
st(s+t)(s-t)>0
, where

s=x^{2}-m,t=w-y^{2}
.

(3)

n=2m^{2}
is twice a square and
st\neq 0
where
s=xy-m
,
t=x^{2}-y^{2}-w
.

(4)

n=-2m^{2}
is minus twice a square and
4s^{4}-12s^{2}t^{2}+t^{4}>0
and
st\neq 0
, where
s=xy-m
,
t=x^{2}+y^{2}-w
.

Any solution for some
n
, yields a solution for
nq^{4}
for any
q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
; so without loss of generality one can assume that
n
is fourth power free. Theorem
1.1
gives indirect evidence for Heath–Brown’s conjecture that any integer with at least one representation as a sum of three cubes has infinitely many. Theorem
1.1
applies to some non-rational surfaces (see Remark
5.3
), but the only case of sums of three cubes for which Zariski density is known is the Fermat cubic surface, which is rational.

We do not know in general whether the equations (
1.1
) have a solution, and there is no reason to expect this to be easier than sums of three cubes (some simple families do admit obvious solutions, e.g. if
-n
is a square). Hence a natural approach is computational. Our theorem says that provided one is able to find a “good” solution, then there are very many solutions. Good in the context of Theorem
1.1
means a solution satisfying some real conditions; these real conditions describe unbounded domains, hence we expect very many such solutions if they exist.

Remark 1.2
.

There are some “trivial” integer solutions for the cases
n=\pm 2m^{2}
. Let
xy=m
. Then we can rewrite the equation as
x^{4}\mp 2x^{2}y^{2}+y^{2}=w^{2}
, which rearranges to
(x^{2}\mp y^{2})^{2}=w^{2}
. Thus we obtain a solution by taking
w=x^{2}\mp y^{2}
. These solutions are not “good” since they clearly satisfy
s=0
in the notation of Theorem
1.1
. However, in the case
n=2m^{2}
, Theorem
1.1
applies as soon as one can find one non-trivial integer solution.

1.2.
Conic bundles

We prove Theorem
1.1
using geometric techniques. The natural compactifications of the surfaces
U_{n}
lie in weighted projective space
{\mathbb{P}}(1,1,1,2)
, and define
del Pezzo surfaces of degree
2
. The arithmetic of del Pezzo surfaces is a rich very and ancient topic, with lower degree surfaces representing a greater challenge than higher degree surfaces. Our key observation is that the surfaces in Theorem
1.1
admit a
conic bundle structure
; this is not at all obvious from the equation and finding these structures and writing them down explicitly requires some detailed geometric arguments.

A
conic
on a del Pezzo surface
X
is a connected reduced curve
C\subset X
of arithmetic genus
0
such that
-K_{X}\cdot C=2
. A single conic moves in a pencil and gives rise to a conic bundle structure
\pi:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
(see
[
17
, Lem. 2.8]
). Moreover if the degree of
X
equals
1,2,4
, then there is actually a second associated conic bundle, which we call the
dual
of
\pi
(see Lemma
4.1
). For the surfaces in Theorem
1.1
, the dual is obtained by applying the Geiser involution
w\mapstochar\rightarrow-w
. Given this, Theorem
1.1
is then a special case of the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
X
be a del Pezzo surface over
{\mathbb{Q}}
of degree
d\in\{1,2,4\}
.
Let
D\subset X
be a smooth anticanonical divisor and
\mathcal{X}
a projective model for
X
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathcal{D}
the closure of
D
in
\mathcal{X}
.

Assume that
X
admits a conic bundle structure
\pi_{1}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
with dual
\pi_{2}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.
Assume that
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})
admits an integral point which lies on a smooth fibre
C
of
\pi_{1}
which meets
D
in a real quadratic point such that
\pi_{2}|_{D}:D\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is unramified along
C\cap D
. Then
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathbb{Z}})
is not thin.

By definition
-K_{X}\cap C
is a finite scheme of degree
2
; our hypotheses in Theorem
1.3
require this to be irreducible and have a real point. This is fairly natural to impose, otherwise the conic has only finitely integral points (see Lemma
2.5
). More philosophically, real conditions are frequently required in the study of integral points on varieties. Our condition implies that
D({\mathbb{R}})\neq\emptyset
; this condition is necessary for the result to hold. For example, for the equation

x^{4}+y^{4}+w^{2}=n

there are clearly only finitely many integral solutions. In this case the relevant boundary divisor
x^{4}+y^{4}+w^{2}=0
has no real point. The technical unramified condition is fairly mild as it holds for all but finitely many conics (see Lemma
3.5
).

We have an analogous result for cubic surfaces. Recall that any conic bundle on a smooth cubic surface arises via taking the residual intersections of the family of planes through a fixed line.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
X
be a smooth cubic surface over
{\mathbb{Q}}

and
D\subset X
be a smooth hyperplane section. Let
\mathcal{X}
be a projective model for
X
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
\mathcal{D}
the closure of
D
in
\mathcal{X}
.

Assume that
X
admits two lines and let
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
denote the corresponding conic bundles.
Assume that
\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D}
admits an integral point which lies on a smooth fibre
C
of
\pi_{1}
which meets
D
in a real quadratic point such that
\pi_{2}|_{D}:D\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is unramified along
C\cap D
. Then
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathbb{Z}})
is Zariski dense.

If moreover the lines are coplanar, then
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathbb{Z}})
is not thin.

This result allows us for instance to recover non-thinness of integral points for sums of three cubes when
n
is a cube, a result originally due to Coccia
[
5
, Thm. 1.15]
(see Example
4.6
). Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
are all special cases of the following, which allows more general surfaces, applies over number fields, and to
S
-integral points. It is the main result of our article.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
k
be a number field and
X
be a smooth projective surface over
k
which
admits two distinct conic bundle structures
\pi_{i}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
for
i=1,2
. Let
D\subset X
be a smooth irreducible divisor. Let
S
be a finite set of places containing the archimedean places
and
\mathcal{X}
a projective model for
X
over
{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
and
\mathcal{D}
the closure of
D
in
\mathcal{X}
. Assume that the following conditions hold.

(1)

X\setminus(D\cup E)
is simply connected, where
E
is the union of curves that are constant for both conic bundles.

(2)

For all
P\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(k)
we have
\pi_{1}^{-1}(P)\cdot D=\pi_{2}^{-1}(P)\cdot D=2
.

(3)

\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D}
admits an
{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
-integral point which lies on a smooth conic
C:=\pi_{1}^{-1}(P_{0})
with
P_{0}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(k)
such that

(a)

C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is infinite.

(b)

If
k={\mathbb{Q}}
or an imaginary quadratic extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}
and
|S|=1
,
then the set
\pi_{2}(C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}))\cap\pi_{2}(D(k))\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{1}(k)
is finite.

(c)

The map
\pi_{2}|_{D}:D\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
is unramified along
C\cap D
.

Then
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is not thin.

In the statement,
C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
denotes the collection of integral points on
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the closure of
C
in
\mathcal{X}
.
Let us discuss the assumptions in Theorem
1.5
. Condition (
1
) is fundamental to the method as it guarantees that a finite cover of
X
ramifies along a divisor which is horizontal with respect to one of the conic bundles. The condition
\pi_{1}^{-1}(P)\cdot D=2
in (
2
) allows there to be conics in the family with infinitely many integral points. Condition (
3a
) gives some integral points to work with so that we can generate more. The finiteness condition in (
3b
) is fairly mild and holds for all but finitely many conics or if
D
has positive genus (see Lemma
3.4
). The condition (
3c
) also holds for all but finitely many conics (see Lemma
3.5
). For example, the assumption in Theorem
1.4
that the lines are coplanar is required to assure that Condition (
1
) holds (see Lemma
4.4
and Question
4.5
).

Theorem
1.1
naturally suggests the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and assume that the equation

x^{4}+y^{4}-w^{2}=n

has an integer solution. Then there are infinitely many solutions.

An interesting challenge case is when
-n
is a square; here there are obvious solutions, but infinitude is not known in any case aside from when
n=-4m^{4}
. Outside this case, there is no conic bundle structure by Proposition
5.1
.

Remark 1.7
.

The solutions to the case
n=8
of (
1.1
) in
[
10
]
arise via a complicated recursive relation which is formally similar to the more classical recursive relation for solutions to the Pell equation: an affine conic. It was this observation which led us to search for a conic bundle on the surface, which ultimately led to the classification of conic bundle structures given in Proposition
5.1
. Thus the solutions found in
[
10
]
all come from integer points on finitely many conics. Our new innovation is to find infinitely many conics with integer solutions, in fact so many conics to obtain the integral Hilbert property.

Similarly, on Mathematics Stack Exchange
[
18
]
is presented the relation

(17p^{2}-12pq-13q^{2})^{4}+(17p^{2}+12pq-13q^{2})^{4}=(289p^{4}+14p^{2}q^{2}-239q^{4})^{2}+(17p^{2}-q^{2})^{4}

for
p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is apparently due to E. Fauquembergue from the late 1800’s.
Taking
17p^{2}-q^{2}=1
one finds a Pell equation parametrising infinitely many solutions in the case
n=1
. Proposition
5.2
shows that this Pell equation is in fact part of an infinite family of solutions coming from a conic bundle structure. It does not seem to have been previously known that the integral points are Zariski dense on this surface for
n=1
; this is a very special case of our results.

1.3.
Method of proof and related literature

Theorem
1.5
is proved via the
double fibration method
. This method was first introduced by Corvaja and Zannier
[
7
]
to show that rational points on the Fermat quartic surface are not thin. This method was subsequently generalised by Demeio
[
8
,
9
]
(elliptic surfaces) and Streeter
[
21
]
(conic bundles).

The closest work to ours is Coccia
[
5
,
6
]
. Coccia proves in
[
6
, Thm. 1.7]
a result on the integral Hilbert property for double conic bundle surfaces, with possibly singular boundary divisor. He uses this to obtain the integral Hilbert property
[
6
, Thm. 1.6]
for del Pezzo surfaces after a possible finite field extension and after enlarging the set of places. Some differences with our Theorem
1.5
are that Coccia assumes in
[
6
, Thm. 1.7 (H2)]
that there is a non-thin set of fibres which have a Zariski dense set of integral points, whereas our main applications only require finding one “good” integral point.
Moreover the assumptions in
[
6
, Thm. 1.7]
imply that the fibres of the two conic fibrations have intersection number
2
, so this does not cover the case of del Pezzo surfaces of degree
2
as in (
1.1
), where the intersection number is
4
.

Our main innovation is to make this method work with weaker assumptions, and with particular care constructing affine conics over
{\mathbb{Z}}
with infinitely many integral points, since this is more difficult over
{\mathbb{Q}}
as there is only one place at infinity (see Lemma
2.5
). We also require a result on strong approximation for integral points on conics (Proposition
2.7
), which we combine with a result on lifting integral points to covers (Lemma
3.4
) and topological arguments in Lemma
3.6
to generate conics in the family with infinitely many integral points. We keep running our process to show that there are non-thin many conics with infinitely many integral points, from where our proof follows a similar strategy to that of Coccia.

In private communication, Coccia has informed us of forthcoming work where he applies the double fibration method to prove the integral Hilbert property for some affine cubic surfaces over
{\mathbb{Z}}
with possibly singular boundary divisor, including important classical surfaces such as Markoff type cubic surfaces. His approach builds upon a sketch of a proof, given in
[
6
, §6.4]
, regarding how to apply his method to a specific affine cubic surface over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Notation

For a variety
X
over a field
k
, say that
X
is
simply connected
if the étale fundamental group of the base change of
X
to the algebraic closure is trivial.

Let
k
be a number field and by
v
a place of
k
.
For
S
a finite set of places of
k
containing the archimedean ones, we denote by

{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}=\{\alpha\in k:v(\alpha)\geqslant 0\text{ for all }v\notin S\}

the ring of
S
-integers of
k
.
For a non-archimedean
v
, we denote by
{\mathcal{O}}_{v}=\{\alpha_{v}\in k_{v}:v(\alpha_{v})\geqslant 0\}
the associated valuation ring.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we provide some background notions that we will need, in particular on del Pezzo surfaces and conic bundles.

2.1.
Integral Hilbert property

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be an integral variety over a field
k
. A
thin map
is a morphism

Y\rightarrow X
from an integral variety which admits no rational section. A
thin subset

of
X(k)
is a subset contained in the images of the rational points of
the union of finitely many thin maps.

Let
k
now be a number field.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
X
be an integral variety over
k
and
D
a divisor on
X
. An
integral model
over
{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
for
(X,D)
is a pair
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{D})
of flat schemes over
{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
together with a choice of isomorphism between
X
and the generic fibre of
\mathcal{X}
, such that
\mathcal{D}
is the closure of
D
in
\mathcal{X}
.

An
S
-integral point of
\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D}
is a section
s_{P}
of
\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathrm{Spec}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
avoiding
\mathcal{D}
, i.e. for each
\mathfrak{p}\in\mathrm{Spec}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
we have that
s_{P}(\mathfrak{p})\notin\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{p}}
.
We denote the set of these points by
(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
. If
D
and the model is clear from the context, we also denote by
X^{\circ}=X\setminus D
and
X^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})=(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
.

For a closed subset
C\subset X
, we obtain a natural model
\mathcal{C}
for
C
by taking the closure of
C
in
\mathcal{X}
. We typically denote by
C^{\circ}:=C\setminus D
, and
C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}):=(\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite type integral scheme over
{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
. We say that
\mathcal{X}
satisfies the
S
-integral Hilbert property
if
\mathcal{X}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is not thin.

2.2.
Integral points on curves

First we recall the famous Siegel’s Theorem on finiteness of integral points for curves of positive genus
[
20
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
C
be an affine irreducible curve of genus
>0
over
k
. Then for any integral model of
C
, the
S
-integral points of
C
are finite.

We now take the following set up: let
C
be a smooth projective curve of genus
0
over
k
and
D
a reduced divisor on
C
of degree at most
2
; we often informally refer to
D
as the “points at infinity”. Let
S
be a finite set of places of
k
containing the archimedean ones and
\mathcal{C}
a normal projective model for
C
over
{\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
, with
\mathcal{D}
the closure of
D
in
\mathcal{C}
. We write
C^{\circ}=C\setminus D
and
C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})=(\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{D})({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})\neq\emptyset
, then
C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is infinite if and only if
there is a place
v\in S
for which
D(k_{v})\neq\emptyset
, and in the case that
\#D(k)=2
we have
|S|\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

This is a geometric reformulation of a well-known criterion, see e.g.
[
1
, Thm. 1.1]
or
[
13
, Cor. 5.4]
. One sees this
by noting that if
D
is irreducible, then
D(k_{v})\neq\emptyset
if and only if
v
is completely split in the field extension
k\subset k(D)
.
∎

Another result we require shows that only finitely many integral points lift to certain covers
[
6
, Lem. 5.3]
, as a consequence of Siegel’s Theorem.

Lemma 2.6
.

Assume that
\deg D=2
. Let
V
be a smooth projective irreducible curve and
f:V\rightarrow C
be a finite map. Assume that one of the following holds:

(1)

the map
f
is not totally ramified on
D
; or

(2)

the map
f
has at least one branch point outside the support of
D
.

Then
f(V(k))\cap C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is finite.

It is well-known that any non-simply connected variety fails strong approximation; this is the case for any affine conic with two geometric points at infinity. Nonetheless we shall require the following special case of strong approximation, which says that integral points can be arbitrarily close to the points at infinity.

Proposition 2.7
.

Assume that
C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is infinite. Let
v\in S
be such that
D(k_{v})\neq\emptyset
. Let
W\subset C(k_{v})
be an open neighbourhood of
D(k_{v})
. Then

C^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})\cap W
is infinite.

Proof.

We start with the perspective from
[
13
, § 5.1, 5.2]
. By
loc. cit.
there is an action of a
1
-dimensional linear algebraic group
G
on
C^{\circ}
which realises
C^{\circ}
as a
G
-torsor, and this torsor action extends to a group scheme model
\mathcal{G}
of
G
. On choosing an integral point on
C^{\circ}
, it suffices to prove the analogous result for
\mathcal{G}
, on identifying
C^{\circ}=G
, since the action of
\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
on the fixed integral point allows us to view
\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})\subset\mathcal{C}^{\circ}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
. Moreover as explained in
loc. cit.
, changing the model
\mathcal{G}
potentially changes
\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
only up to a finite index subgroup. This will not affect our proof method, hence we may choose the model
\mathcal{G}
. It is also easy to see under our assumptions that there is an element
g\in\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
of infinite order (this follows from similar arguments to below).

If
v
is archimedean then
the subgroup generated by
g
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}
and it
embeds as a discrete subgroup of
G(k_{v})
. However
C(k_{v})
is compact, thus
C(k_{v})\setminus W
is also compact. It follows that
{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus W
is finite, thus
{\mathbb{Z}}\cap W
is infinite, as required.

If
v
is non-archimedean we need to be more careful as the subgroup generated by a point need not be discrete (consider
{\mathbb{Z}}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
. Assume first that
\deg D=1
; here we may take
\mathcal{G}=\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}
. Then
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{a}}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})={\mathcal{O}}_{k,S}
is dense in
k_{v}
, so the result is clear.

Now assume that
\deg D=2
, so that
G
is a
1
-dimensional algebraic torus.
We use some properties of algebraic tori over local fields, which we now recall. Let
T
be an algebraic torus over
k
and
v
a non-archimedean place of
k
which is unramified in the splitting field of
T
. Let
T({\mathcal{O}}_{v})
be the maximal compact subgroup of
T(k_{v})
. Denote by
\widehat{T}=\Hom(T,\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}})
the character lattice of
T
. This is a Galois module which is finitely generated and free as an abelian group. Then the
degree map

\deg_{v}:T(k_{v})\rightarrow\Hom(\widehat{T}^{\Gamma_{v}},{\mathbb{Z}}),\quad t_{v}\mapstochar\rightarrow\left(\,\chi_{v}\mapstochar\rightarrow v(\chi_{v}(t_{v}))\,\right)

is well-defined and continuous where
\Gamma_{v}:=\Gal(\bar{k}_{v}/k_{v})
. By
[

it induces a short exact sequence

(2.1)

0\rightarrow T({\mathcal{O}}_{v})\rightarrow T(k_{v})\rightarrow\Hom(\widehat{T}^{\Gamma_{v}},{\mathbb{Z}})\rightarrow 0.

We use this as follows. Our torus

is either
\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
or the norm one torus
\mathrm{R}_{k(D)/k}^{1}\mathbb{G}_{\mathrm{m}}
, corresponding to a trivial or non-trivial Galois action on the character lattice, respectively. Moreover, in the latter case, as

, the place

is completely split in

, so that the action of
\Gamma_{v}
on the character lattice is trivial. In both cases, a theorem of Shyr

19
]
(a version of Dirichlet’s theorem for algebraic tori) implies that
\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})/\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S\setminus\{v\}})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}
. As
\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{v})
is a finite index subgroup of the maximal compact subgroup
G({\mathcal{O}}_{v})
, we deduce there is an element
g\in\mathcal{G}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
such that
g\notin G({\mathcal{O}}_{v})
. In which case by (

) the degree
\deg_{v}(g)
is non-trivial. It easily follows that

, and then the same argument as in the archimedean case applies to complete the proof.
∎

Definition 2.8

Of particular interest to us are del Pezzo surfaces of degree

2.4.
Conic bundles

Definition 2.9
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective surface

over a field

is a dominant morphism
\pi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
such that the fibres are isomorphic to plane conics.

The following facts are well-known and follow from the adjunction formula.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\pi:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be a conic bundle surface with fibre class

. Then

(1)

-K_{X}\cdot C=2
.

(2)

The singular fibers over

to two lines meeting in a single point.

To prove the existence of a conic bundle structure, we will use the following.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let

be a del Pezzo surface over a field

of characteristic

with a rational point. Assume that there exists a Galois invariant collection
\mathfrak{L}\subset X_{\bar{k}}

Proof.

This is a special case of
[

We sketch a proof, since it also furnishes an effective method to construct a conic bundle structure explicitly. Moreover the Stein factorisation step at the end of

Assume that

∎

Double conic bundle surfaces

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem

3.1.
Fundamental groups

We begin with some results on fundamental groups of complements of divisors and blow-ups.
Let
k
be a field.

In the following
\pi_{1}
denotes the étale fundamental group, which should not be confused with a conic bundle morphism (this conflict will occur nowhere else).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth proper surface over
k
and
D\subset X
an effective divisor. Let
E\subset X
be a collection of pairwise disjoint
(-1)
-curves and
f:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
the blow down of
E
. Then
\pi_{1}(X\setminus(D\cup E))\cong\pi_{1}(X^{\prime}\setminus f(D))
.

Proof.

The map
f
induces an isomorphism
X\setminus(D\cup E)\cong X^{\prime}\setminus f(D\cup E)
. Thus it suffices to show that
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime}\setminus f(D))=\pi_{1}(X^{\prime}\setminus f(D\cup E))
.
Let
L\subset E
be an irreducible component. If
L\cap D\neq\emptyset
then
f(D\cup L)=f(D)
so there is nothing to prove. If
L\cap D=\emptyset
, then
f(D\cup L)=f(D)\sqcup f(L)
is a disjoint union. The result then follows from the fact that removing a point from a smooth surface does not change the fundamental group, by Zariski purity of the branch locus.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
be a smooth proper surface over
k
which admits two conic bundle
structures
\pi_{i}:X\rightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
with fibre classes
C_{1},C_{2}
, respectively, such that
C_{1}\cdot C_{2}=1
. Let
E
be the divisor of curves which are constant with respect to both fibrations and
D\subset X
an integral divisor with
C_{1}\cdot D=C_{2}\cdot D=2
. Then
X\setminus(D\cup E)
is not simply connected.

Proof.

We may assume that
k
is algebraically closed. Blowing down fibre components of
\pi_{1}
, we obtain a map
f:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
to a smooth projective surface which admits a conic bundle with smooth fibres, i.e. a ruling. We now carefully analyse the possibilities for
X^{\prime}
.

Recall that the ruled surfaces are exactly the Hirzebruch surfaces
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
for
n\geqslant 0
. Denote by
F
the fibre class of the ruling and
E
the zero section. These classes generate the effective cone of
{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
, and we have the intersection numbers

E^{2}=-n,\quad F^{2}=0,\quad E\cdot F=1.

In our setting we are given integral curves
C,D\subset{\mathbb{F}}_{n}
such that

F\cdot C=1,\quad F\cdot D=C\cdot D=2.

We analyse the possibilities for these curves. Write
C=e_{1}E+f_{1}F
and
D=e_{2}E+f_{2}F
for
e_{i},f_{i}\geqslant 0
integers. From
F\cdot C=1
and
F\cdot D=2
we obtain
e_{1}=1
and
e_{2}=2
. From
C\cdot D=2
a short calculation yields
2f_{1}+f_{2}=2n+2
. As
D
is integral we must have
f_{2}>0
, since
2E
is not integral. Moreover, clearly
f_{2}
is even. It follows that
f_{1}\leqslant n
. However for
n\geqslant 1
, the linear system
|C|=|E+f_{1}F|
contains no integral elements, provided that
0\leqslant f_{1}<n
. Indeed we have
E\cdot(E+f_{1}F)=-n+f<0
, thus
E
is a fixed component of the linear system. We deduce that
f_{1}=n
, hence
C=E+nF
and
D=2E+2F
. But then
E\cdot D=2-2n
, so that
E
is a fixed component of
|D|
providing

, which contradicts that
D
is integral.

Returning to our original proof, we conclude that

, i.e. that
X^{\prime}={\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
with the conic bundles inducing the two rulings, or

is blow-up of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
in a single point with one conic bundle corresponding to the ruling and the other coming from pulling-back a pencil of lines in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
not passing through the blown-up point.
Applying Lemma
3.1
we find that the fundamental group of

is the same as

. However in both cases

has class

. Then the complement is not simply connected, since the Picard group has non-trivial
2

∎

C^{\circ}_{1,\pi_{1}(x)}({\mathcal{O}}_{k,S})
is infinite by Lemma
3.6
. Since
P\notin T_{2}
, then only a finite number of points of

are lifted to rational points of
\pi_{1}
-unramified covers.
On the other hand,
\pi_{1}(x)\notin T_{1}
, so
all
points of

are lifted to
\pi_{1}
-unramified covers. This gives a contradiction and hence finishes the proof. ∎

Example 3.10
.

Not all conic bundles with Zariski dense set of integral points satisfy the conclusion of Lemma
3.6
. Consider the affine surface

over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. This is isomorphic to

, hence

is not thin. It is equipped with various conic bundles, e.g. the fibration

. But each fibre of

Proof.

We first explain how condition (1b) in Corollary

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

We use the relation

From this
C\cdot C^{\prime}=8/d\geqslant 2
and

. So assume that

and

are singular conics in the family which share a common component. Then

However using

∎

Then that (1b) implies (1a) follows from the fact that

It remains to prove Corollary

.
∎

4.2.

1.3

The given conic has infinitely many integral points by Lemma

. The result now follows from Corollary
4.2
. ∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Any line on a smooth cubic surface gives rise to a conic bundle structure by taking the residual intersections with the family of planes containing the line. We first study the geometry of the double conic bundles which arise this way, in particular the difference between the lines being skew or not.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X
be a smooth cubic surface over a field
k
which contains two distinct lines
L_{1},L_{2}\subset X
. Consider the associated conic bundles
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
with divisor classes
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
. Let
E
be the divisor given by the common irreducible components of both fibrations and
D
a smooth hyperplane section.

(1)

If
L_{1}\cdot L_{2}=0
, then
C_{1}\cdot C_{2}=1
. Moreover
E
consists of
5
pairwise skew lines and
X\setminus(D\cup E)
is not simply connected.

(2)

If
L_{1}\cdot L_{2}=1
, then
C_{1}\cdot C_{2}=2
. Moreover
E
consists of a single line and
X\setminus(D\cup E)
is simply connected.

Proof.

The stated intersection numbers follow from the formulae

(L_{1}+C_{1})(L_{2}+C_{2})=3,\quad L_{1}\cdot(L_{2}+C_{2})=L_{2}\cdot(L_{1}+C_{1})=1.

Assume that (1) holds. It is well-known that two skew lines meet exactly 5 others; this can be seen from the fact that there are 10 lines which meet
L_{1}
, corresponding to the
5
singular fibres of the conic bundle, and
L_{2}
gives a section of the conic bundle, thus meets exactly 5 of these lines. From this description it is clear that they are pairwise skew. The complement is not simply connected by Lemma
3.2

Now assume that we are in case (2). The divisor
E
is given by the lines which lie in a common plane with both
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
. As
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are coplanar there is a unique such line. By Lemma
3.1
it suffices to note that the complement of a smooth hyperplane section for a quartic del Pezzo surface is simply connected
[
12
, Lem. 3.3.6]
.
∎

From Lemma
4.4
, it is now clear that in the setting of Theorem
1.4
, if the lines are coplanar then Corollary
4.2
implies that the set of integral points is not thin.

It remains to show Zariski density if the lines are skew. The argument is a minor variant of the proof of Lemma
3.6
. We are given a conic with infinitely many integral points. We consider conics from the second fibration passing through these integral points. As
D
has genus
1
, the finiteness property from Lemma
3.4
holds for all such conics (this does not use (
3.1
)). A topological argument as in the proof of Lemma
3.6
shows that there are infinitely many such conics with infinitely many integral points. Repeating this process with the fibrations swapped gives the result. ∎

The above Zariski density argument applies to more general double conic bundle surfaces, but all new interesting applications appear to be to cubic surfaces (for del Pezzo surfaces of degree
1,2,4
one can use Theorem
1.3
).

5.1.

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem

Proof.

We begin by excluding the cases without a conic bundle structure.

one

The Magma code from
[

Now assume that
[{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta,\sqrt[4]{n}):{\mathbb{Q}}]<16

becomes a square in
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)

\ker({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}/{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times 2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)^{\times}/{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)^{\times 2})=\{\pm 1,\pm 2\}

First consider the case where

is a square. We subdivide into the cases depending on whether

becomes a square or a fourth power in

Since
\ker({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}/{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times 4}\rightarrow{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)^{\times}/{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta)^{\times 4})=\{\pm 1,\pm 4\}

becomes a fourth power in

if and only if

or
-4n
is a fourth power. However if

is fourth power, or
-n

Therefore it remains to show that a conic bundle exists in the cases in the statement of the proposition. By Lemma

Remark 5.3
.

One can show using the formulae for the Picard rank of a conic bundle

11

that if one of

By Theorem

, which gives explicit descriptions for the conic bundle structures for the surfaces in Theorem

with dual conic bundle

obtained via the Geiser involution. For
