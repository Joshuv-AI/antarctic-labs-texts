Cognitive dispersion in established adulthood: etiology and implications for cognitive aging
Authors: Anqing Zheng, Elizabeth Muñoz, Robin P Corley, Daniel E Gustavson, Naomi P Friedman, Robert Plomin, Sally J Wadsworth, Chandra A Reynolds, Jennifer R Turner, PhD
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesCognitive dispersion, reflecting intraindividual variability across tasks, may signal dementia risk in older adults, but its origins and relevance before midlife are unclear. We examined dispersion as a potential marker of cognitive reserve in established adulthood and distinguished it from general ability using heritability analyses.Research Design and Methods1,245 primarily non-Hispanic White twins and siblings were assessed at 33.3 years (SD = 5.0; 53% female) using 2 cognitive batteries. Bayesian mixed effects location scale models estimated mean performance and dispersion across cognitive tasks, with tasks standardized against current sample-based norms (age 33) and adolescent norms (age 16). The mean of task scores standardized to current norms reflects current ability, while the mean of the same scores standardized to adolescent norms reflects growth in ability. The respective dispersion estimates represent task-specific deviations from general ability and task-specific gains.ResultsAcross 4 models fitted by battery and standardization, greater cognitive dispersion was associated with higher general ability (r = 0.23–0.56) and developmental gains (r = 0.62–0.93). Verbal tasks showed lower dispersion relative to performance tasks (β = 0.83–0.87). Dispersion was strongly heritable overall (43%–84%), and moderately heritable independent of general ability (36%–37%) and growth (2%–16%). Dispersion did not predict education or occupational outcomes beyond general ability and growth.Discussion and ImplicationsBefore midlife, cognitive dispersion is distinct from general ability and moderately heritable. Dispersion is associated with higher cognitive ability and growth, but not directly with educational or occupational attainment, suggesting dispersion may relate to cognitive reserve through other pathways.Keywords: Intraindividual variability, Cognitive specialization, Occupational prestige, Cognitive reserve

Variability in cognitive performance across tasks, dispersion, predicts impaired late-life cognitive functioning, but relatively little is known about its etiology and relevance to cognitive reserve in established adulthood, a salient and intervenable period adjacent to midlife (∼30–45 years). We observed that dispersion measures are heritable and are distinguishable from general ability. However, dispersion in established adulthood is not independently associated with educational or occupational attainment, suggesting its salience to later life functioning may act through different pathways.

Background and objectives

Dispersion,1 a form of intraindividual variability, refers to fluctuations in a person’s cognitive performance across different types of cognitive tasks. Cognitive dispersion represents signal and not merely random noise, as it uniquely predicts later life outcomes beyond overall performance (e.g., Bangen et al., 2019; Holtzer et al., 2008; Watermeyer et al., 2021). Dispersion may reflect variation in cognitive reserve, offering a distinct and potentially early indicator of long-term cognitive functioning. However, research on dispersion has largely focused on middle and late adulthood, with little understanding of its significance or etiology prior to midlife. Established adulthood (ages 30–45), a life stage situated between emerging adulthood and midlife (Mehta & Arnett, 2023; Mehta et al., 2020), is gaining attention for its relevance to cognitive health (Yang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), but dispersion during this life stage has received little empirical attention. Dispersion is conceptually distinct from inconsistency, which captures variability across repeated trials of the same cognitive task (Hilborn et al., 2009). Although inconsistency has shown modest genetic influence (Finkel & McGue, 2007; Finkel & Pedersen, 2014), the etiology of cognitive dispersion remains unknown. By examining dispersion during established adulthood, our study addresses a critical gap in the literature, evaluating its role as a correlate of cognitive reserve and distinguishing it from general ability to identify unique genetic and environmental contributions.

Developmental period is crucial to contextualizing dispersion. Theory suggests it follows a U-shaped pattern, decreasing from childhood through adolescence, plateauing by emerging adulthood, and then increasing into later life (Hultsch & MacDonald, 2004). Empirically, between-person differences in dispersion increase nonlinearly in cross-sectional analyses across early to late adulthood, mirroring age-related increases in variance of overall cognitive performance (see LaPlume et al., 2022), consistent with theoretical predictions (Hultsch & MacDonald, 2004). Individual differences in dispersion and in average cognitive performance both accelerate after age 60 and 65, respectively (LaPlume et al., 2021). Higher dispersion in older adults (70+) is linked to poorer memory and greater dementia risk, even when accounting for overall performance (Bangen et al., 2019; Holtzer et al., 2008; Watermeyer et al., 2021). However, cognitively healthy older adults show a wide range of cognitive dispersion, indicating that intraindividual variability is common and not necessarily a sign of dysfunction (Schretlen et al., 2003). Indeed, increases in dispersion from age 56 to age 65, indexed by episodic memory and executive functioning tasks, may predict less decline in overall performance (Klepacz et al., 2024). Moreover, dispersion at ages 56 and 65, and 9-year increases in dispersion, are positively associated with higher education levels. Yet, education–dispersion associations vary in direction across largely late-life cross-sectional samples (Yoneda et al., 2022), suggesting that the interpretation of dispersion requires a developmental lens. Studies have yet to examine the etiology of dispersion or the associations of dispersion with reserve-related traits such as education and occupational complexity prior to midlife, before age-related declines become more pronounced.

Earlier life cognitive abilities are associated with educational and occupational attainments (Kremen et al., 2019; Lovden et al., 2020), which in turn may build cognitive reserve and resilience against age-related decline (Kremen et al., 2022). Moreover, attaining more education bolsters late-life specific cognitive abilities rather than influencing general cognitive ability, after controlling for childhood IQ (Ritchie et al., 2015), consistent with a specialization effect. We posit that reserve and resilience may be supported by cognitive specialization, which becomes prominent during established adulthood (Mehta & Arnett, 2023; Mehta et al., 2020), a period when individuals hone expertise in chosen careers. Stimulating jobs are associated with broad cognitive and memory benefits (Marquie et al., 2010; Smart et al., 2014; Stebbins et al., 2022; Stine-Morrow & Manavbasi, 2022), whereas cognitive training, whether through work, hobbies like chess, or brain-training programs, tends to improve only the specific skills practiced, rather than transferring broadly (Smart et al., 2014; Stine-Morrow & Manavbasi, 2022). Such selective transfer findings imply that educational and occupational pursuits contribute to domain-specific expertise, likely mediated by gene and environment interplay such as niche-picking (active gene-environment correlation, rGE) (Plomin et al., 1977). That is, individuals may actively self-select into educational tracks and occupations that align with their cognitive strengths, amplifying initial genetic differences in domain-specific abilities over time (c.f., Tucker-Drob & Harden, 2012). Twin and genome-wide association studies support this pathway, showing that educational and occupational attainments are substantially heritable and share genetic variance with cognitive abilities (Cesarini & Visscher, 2017; Okbay et al., 2022). Put differently, dispersion, which reflects differentiated cognitive profiles, may itself be genetically influenced, emanating from self-selection processes that shape life outcomes.

Little is known about dispersion’s etiology, particularly during established adulthood. At the neural level, cognitive dispersion is associated with neural network dysfunction across mid and late adulthood (Bangen et al., 2019; Costa et al., 2019; Halliday et al., 2019; Holmqvist et al., 2023; Mulet-Pons et al., 2023). It is also associated with reduced white matter connectivity in both youth and older adults (Halliday et al., 2019; Tamnes et al., 2012), as well as a thinner prefrontal cortex in youth (Boen et al., 2021). At the genetic level, twin studies have evaluated cognitive inconsistency in mid- and late-life twins (Finkel & McGue, 2007; Finkel & Pedersen, 2014) but not dispersion. Since inconsistency is moderately correlated with dispersion (Costa et al., 2019), the moderate heritability estimates are relevant to what we may observe (∼10%–21%; Finkel & McGue, 2007; Finkel & Pedersen, 2014). Notably, genetic correlations were reported between decision time inconsistency and memory, and between movement time inconsistency and spatial and processing speed abilities, even after controlling for average performance (Finkel & Pedersen, 2014). Based on these results, we expect that individual differences in dispersion will show moderate heritability that is distinct from general ability.

Despite growing interest in intraindividual variability, little is known about dispersion during established adulthood, a life stage that has received less attention in cognitive aging research compared to the well-studied middle-aged and older adult periods. Existing research has not fully characterized (a) whether dispersion at this age reflects meaningful individual differences, (b) how dispersion relates to overall ability or developmental gains, (c) the relative genetic and environmental contributions to dispersion, or (d) whether dispersion is associated with education and occupational prestige beyond mean ability. To address these gaps, we leverage data from the Colorado Adoption Twin Study of Lifespan behavioral development and cognitive aging (CATSLife1), at mean age 33 years (28–49), and: (a) estimate overall mean performance and dispersion across two cognitive batteries applying mixed-effects location scale models (MELSM) (Hedeker et al., 2008); (b) compare CATSLife1 dispersion estimates calculated using current adult norms versus age-16 norms to differentiate contemporary variability from variability in developmental gains, i.e., specialization; (c) compare dispersion in verbal and nonverbal domains; (d) leverage a twin and sibling design to investigate the genetic and environmental contributions to both mean performance and dispersion estimates; (e) evaluated the concurrent validity of dispersion estimates with educational and occupational attainments. We hypothesized that (a) dispersion estimates would be positively associated with overall ability level and developmental gains; (b) dispersion estimates would demonstrate moderate heritable contributions independent of those contributing to overall ability level and gains; and (c) individuals who demonstrated higher dispersion would report higher educational attainment and occupational prestige.

Method

Participants

This cross-sectional study used data from the first assessment of the Colorado Adoption/Twin Study of Lifespan behavioral development and cognitive aging (CATSLife1; Wadsworth et al., 2019). CATSLife1 data collection occurred between 2016 and 2020, and recruited participants from two parent longitudinal studies with similar measures: the Colorado Longitudinal Twin Study (LTS; Corley et al., 2019), which included same-sex twin pairs, and the Colorado Adoption Project (CAP; see Wadsworth et al., 2019), which included adopted and biological sibling pairs. We included data from participants who participated in cognitive testing and provided information on the covariates (age, sex, and educational attainment). Among 1,327 CATSLife1 participants, 70 did not participate in cognitive testing, and 12 did not provide educational attainment information and were excluded from analyses. The analysis sample thus consisted of N = 1,245 individuals (MAge = 33.30, SDage = 5.00, range 28.05–49.33 years; 52.85% female sex) who self-reported as primarily non-Hispanic White (7.95% non-White, 5.70% Hispanic). See Supplementary Table 1 (Supplementary Material Section 1) for all descriptives by parent study, and Supplementary Table 6 for twin-specific descriptives.

Measures

Cognitive abilities

Two batteries of cognitive functioning are included: nine tasks from a specific cognitive abilities battery (SCA) adapted from the Hawaii Family Study of Cognition (DeFries et al., 1981) and 10 of 11 subscales from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale Third Edition (WAIS-III; Wechsler, 1997). The nine SCA tasks were Vocabulary; Things Categories Test (verbal); Card Rotations, Paper Form Board (spatial); Subtraction and Multiplication Test, Colorado Perceptual Speed (perceptual speed); Pedigrees and Things Categories (reasoning); Names and Faces, and Picture Memory (episodic memory). The ten subscales from the WAIS-III were Vocabulary, Similarities, Arithmetic, Digit Span, Information, Comprehension (verbal subscales); Picture Arrangement, Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, and Digit Symbol-Coding (performance subscales). Picture Completion was excluded due to a non-normal distribution.

We report results using two different standardization approaches for the cognitive measures. The first approach standardized each subtest score relative to the current sample’s mean and standard deviation for each project (CAP vs LTS) study, which provides a relative performance in established adulthood. The second approach standardized scores relative to the pooled mean and standard deviation (relative to project) of the subtest when participants were age 16 (denoted “Z16” in Supplementary Table 1), allowing us to interpret variations in current cognitive performance in reference to the prior distribution of scores from age 16 as indexing specialization. We then model the intraindividual dispersion as well as average performance using these two types of standardized scores (c.f. Figure 1).

We used WAIS-III scaled scores to facilitate harmonization with norms, which at age 16 for the CAP sample was the WAIS-R, while for the LTS samples, the WAIS-III was used. The Picture Completion scale score was excluded from analyses due to its non-normal distribution.

Sociodemographic variables

Our analyses controlled for age, sex, and project. Sex was effects coded (−.5 = male, .5 = female). Age was measured at the time when cognitive measures were administered and then centered within each project (CAP vs LTS) study. Project was dummy coded (CAP = 0, LTS = 1).

Analytic plan

Cognitive dispersion can be measured in several ways (e.g., individual standard deviations (iSD) or coefficients of variation; Kiselica et al., 2024; Klepacz et al., 2024), but the MELSM offers two key advantages. First, MELSM jointly estimates location (mean) and scale (dispersion) parameters, allowing their covariance to be modeled simultaneously, and reducing sampling noise through a latent dispersion parameter (Hedeker et al., 2008) Second, it mitigates concerns raised in work on Spearman’s Law of Diminishing Returns (SLODR; Murray et al., 2013) where non-normality of residuals may spuriously affect ability–dispersion associations. We implemented MELSM using the Holtzer et al. (2008) conceptualization of dispersion, z-transforming cognitive test scores, calculating deviations from individual means, and modeling the resulting dispersion. As noted, two z-transformations were applied to CATSLife1 scores, using current sample norms and using age-16 norms (see Figure 1).

The analytic plan consisted of two steps: (1) fitting MELSM; (Bürkner, 2017; Hedeker et al., 2008) to estimate individual differences in average performance and dispersion; and (2) employing biometric models to estimate the heritability of cognitive dispersion (σi) and average performance (µi) extracted from an MELSM. Details of the MELSM models, exploratory validity analysis, and biometric models are described in detail in Supplementary Material Section 2, with an overview in the next section.

Modeling overall performance and within-person dispersion

We employed Bayesian MELSM models (Bürkner, 2017; Hedeker et al., 2008) for two cognitive batteries: WAIS-III and SCA. These models allowed for individual variability in across-task performance (dispersion), captured as random effects for the sigma parameter (σi), and average cognitive performance modeled as random effects for individual intercepts (µi). In line with the MELSM approach, we used an exponential function in the scale sub-model to ensure that sigma values were non-negative, resulting in coefficients on a log scale. We included age, sex, and project as covariates. To account for sibling similarity, random effects for family were included in the means and scale parts of the model.

Individual estimates of the intercept (µi) and dispersion (sigma parameter, σi) were extracted from an age and sex covariate-adjusted model for subsequent analyses. For example, we compared extracted values to traditional person-specific descriptive statistics, i.e., individual means and standard deviations (iSD) commonly used in dispersion studies. Next, we conducted exploratory concurrent validity analysis using Bayesian regression to investigate whether individual differences in dispersion were associated with educational and occupational attainment traits. Last, we used these extracted values for biometric analyses.

All phenotypic analyses were conducted in R 4.3.1 (R Development Core Team, 2022) using the brms package (Bürkner, 2017). For all coefficients, we reported point estimates and 95% credible intervals (CIs). To evaluate whether the observed dispersion effect was meaningful, we performed model comparisons using Leave-One-Out Information Criteria (LOO-IC), which measures out-of-sample prediction accuracy. Following established conventions (Hollenbach & Montgomery, 2020; Johnson et al., 2022), models with a difference in expected log-predictive density (ELPD) greater than four and more than twice the magnitude of their standard error were considered better fitting. Higher ELPD values indicated better out-of-sample predictive accuracy.

Biometric analyses of heritability

We estimated sibling and twin correlations and descriptive statistics for extracted estimates of µi and exponentiated dispersion σi, estimates, grouped by sibling type. We employed an ACDE biometric model (e.g., Matteson et al., 2013) that leveraged data from monozygotic (MZ) twins (100% genetic similarity of segregating genes), dizygotic (DZ) twins, and non-twin biological siblings (50% genetic similarity of segregating genes), and adoptive siblings (0% genetic similarity of segregating genes). This model partitions the phenotypic variance into four components: additive genetic effects (A), common environmental factors (C), dominance genetic effects (D), and unique environmental influences (E). By incorporating these distinct sibling groups, the model captures both C and D variance components, offering a comprehensive estimate of heritability. To ensure robustness, we compared the ACDE model with a more parsimonious AE model.

Biometric analyses were conducted using Mplus 8.6 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017) via the MplusAutomation package in R (Hallquist & Wiley, 2018). Model fit was evaluated using the Satorra-Bentler scaled chi-square test for comparison between saturated models and nested models, and the model with the best fit was reported (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010). Between models with same number of parameters, we compared Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion values, with lower values indexing better model fit.

Given that the latent dispersion parameter follows a log-normal distribution, we transformed the fixed intercept of dispersion back to the scale of the outcome using exponentiation. The transformed effects were reported as Dispersion (exp). The random effects presented are standard deviations of the corresponding random intercepts on the log scale. We further describe dispersion using exponentiated fixed and random dispersion parameters.2 For the WAIS-III battery standardized to current norms, the model estimated a median population-level dispersion, α0(exp), at .795. Between-person variation exists around this median, arising from both individual-specific ν0ij, and shared family factors ν0j, with exponentiated standard deviations of these effects estimated at 1.025 and 1.113, respectively. In a case where an individual is +1 SD above the mean for both individual and family averages, the combined effects are: 0.795*1.025*1.113 = 0.907, a 14% higher dispersion than the population average. Age did not predict general ability or dispersion, while project effects suggested that the LTS sample, on average 9 years younger, showed smaller differential gains (specializations). Females showed slightly smaller dispersion using current norms compared to males, but comparable differential gains (specializations).

When standardized against age 16, the random effect for intercept indicates variability in growth, whereas the dispersion parameter indicates differential task-specific growth (specialization). Hence, individuals with higher intercepts tend to show more dispersion in current performance and estimated growth in cognitive skills from age 16. For example, for the WAIS-III analysis, the β0 of 0.859 suggests that, on average, individuals gain over two-thirds of a standard deviation in performance across all subtests at CATSLife1 compared to age 16 norms; the non-zero random effects, ν0ij ν0j, indicate some individuals show higher variability than others.

We examined the correlation between individuals’ dispersion and their overall performance, estimable for the individual random effect (within-family). Adjusting for covariates, when standardized against the current sample, dispersion was positively correlated with general ability (see Table 1, ρzCURR = .228 to .562). Even stronger associations were found between dispersion and general performance when standardized against the age 16 sample (ρz16 = .620 to .927).

To test for domain-specific patterns, we examined whether dispersion differed between verbal and non-verbal tasks. After adjusting for covariates, a consistent pattern emerged for the WAIS-III battery: participants exhibited significantly lower dispersion across verbal tasks compared to non-verbal tasks. This finding held regardless of whether scores were standardized against current norms (α4, zCurr=0.873[0.849, 0.898]) or adolescent norms (z16=0.822[0.799, 0.844]). A similar pattern was observed for the SCA battery. When standardized against current performance, verbal tasks were again associated with lower dispersion than non-verbal tasks (zCurr=0.905 [0.878, 0.933]). However, this pattern reversed when standardizing against age 16, in which verbal tasks were associated with higher dispersion (z16=1.118 [1.084, 1.152]). Comparison of the MELSM estimates to person-specific descriptives

We evaluated correlations between MELSM model-derived parameters (intercept and dispersion) and person-specific descriptive statistics, i.e., individual means and standard deviations (iSD) commonly used in dispersion studies (see Supplementary Table 3A and B). MELSM-extracted intercepts and person-specific means were nearly perfectly correlated (r= .988 to .999), whereas MELSM-derived dispersion parameters and iSD’s were less strongly correlated (r=.615 to .856). This divergence is expected as MELSM jointly estimates location (intercept) and scale (dispersion), and the latent dispersion parameter captures systematic differences in intraindividual variability while reducing sampling noise that affects raw individual iSDs; i.e., one aberrant test score is unlikely to pull the dispersion random effect away from the population estimate compared to typically calculated individual iSDs.

Exploratory concurrent validity analysis

Exploratory analyses evaluated whether dispersion in cognitive test scores was associated with educational and occupational outcomes (i.e., educational attainment, work-related experience, work-related education, and self-reported occupational prestige) above and beyond average cognitive performance (intercept) (see Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Across all analyses and predictor-outcome combinations, we found no evidence that adding dispersion meaningfully improved model predictive accuracy beyond the intercepts reflecting average performance (LOO-IC expected log predictive density, ELPD, differences <4 for all comparisons). Additionally, when both dispersion and intercept estimates were included in the same model, the patterns were consistent with statistical suppression, likely due to multicollinearity between the mean and dispersion terms. See supplemental materials and Supplementary Table 5 for full details.

Genetic and environmental influences on dispersion

The distribution of extracted dispersion values, used in ICC and biometrical analyses, is shown in Supplementary Figure 1 along with intraclass correlations (ICCs) for dispersion and intercepts across sibling types (Supplementary Table 6). MZ twins showed higher ICCs than DZ twins and biological siblings, with effect sizes more than doubled. This suggests both additive (A) and non-additive genetic variance (D) are salient. Adoptive siblings, who share a common rearing environment but lack genetic relatedness, were negligibly correlated, further emphasizing the predominance of genetic contributions to cognitive dispersion. Equality of means was supported for biological siblings, DZ, and MZ siblings, but adoptive siblings showed lower average dispersion and general ability; hence, we estimated their means freely from the other sibling groups. Equality of variances in dispersion and general ability was supported; hence those were equated.

To decompose the variance in dispersion, a full univariate model including additive genetic (A), dominance genetic (D), shared environmental (C), and nonshared environmental (E) factors was compared to a more parsimonious AE model (see Supplementary Table 7). For all four cognitive dispersion measures, dropping C and D did not result in a significant loss of model fit (ΔχSB2(2) = 0.475–2.416, ps > .29). Consequently, the AE model was retained with additive genetic factors accounting for 43%–84% of the variance in dispersion, and 16% to 57% attributed to nonshared environmental influences and measurement error.

Given the observed moderate-to-strong correlations between cognitive dispersion and mean performance, we fitted bivariate AE Cholesky latent models to decompose this association (see Figure 2). This allowed us to identify genetic and environmental influences that are unique to dispersion from those that are shared with mean performance. The results revealed that additive genetic influences unique to dispersion still accounted for sizeable variance, ranging from 16% to 37% across three of the four measures (see Table 2, Figure 2). However, for dispersion on the SCA battery standardized to age 16, unique genetic factors explained only 2% of the variance. Analysis of the covariance between cognitive dispersion and intercepts showed that shared genetic pathways were a main driver of phenotypic correlations, accounting for 23%–57% of the association for most measures. Again, the SCA-Age16 measure was an exception, with shared genetics explaining 83% of its correlation with the intercept.

Discussion

Our study provides the first examination of cognitive dispersion etiology in established adulthood. Overall, dispersion was positively correlated with general ability. Verbal tasks showed lower dispersion than performance tasks. Dispersion was moderately to strongly heritable, with genetic influences partly independent of general ability. Dispersion estimates were not associated with educational or occupational outcomes beyond general ability.

Our primary finding was that dispersion estimates were positively correlated with overall ability level and gains, suggesting that individuals who demonstrate specific cognitive strengths and differential gains among cognitive tests tend to demonstrate higher ability and overall improvement. Specifically, higher dispersion indicates a more differentiated cognitive profile, whereas individuals with low dispersion perform uniformly across tasks. Our finding that higher dispersion is associated with higher overall ability or gains suggests that individuals who develop pronounced strengths in specific cognitive domains tend to achieve higher general cognitive ability. Thus, specialization has a disproportionately large impact on overall cognitive gain across all tasks, whereby excelling in specific cognitive tasks (e.g., verbal reasoning, spatial processing) contributes more to overall improvement than weaker gains detract from it.

Our observed positive association between dispersion scores and overall performance contrasts with studies in older adults, where higher dispersion typically predicts lower cognitive performance and dementia risk (Bangen et al., 2019; De Vito et al., 2024; Holtzer et al., 2008). This discrepancy could reflect a life-stage-dependent meaning of cognitive dispersion. In established adulthood, individuals are actively building expertise and specializing in domains relevant to their careers and interests (Mehta & Arnett, 2023; Mehta et al., 2020); dispersion in this context may reflect adaptive cognitive differentiation whereby higher-ability individuals have a greater opportunity to develop pronounced strengths. In late life, dispersion may instead signal early neurodegeneration selectively affecting vulnerable abilities (e.g., processing speed, executive function) while sparing others (e.g., vocabulary), thus serving as a marker of decline rather than specialization (Costa et al., 2019). Notably, recent evidence suggests that increases in dispersion from age 56–65 may predict less overall decline (Klepacz et al., 2024), highlighting the complexity of dispersion’s developmental trajectory and the need for longitudinal studies spanning early to late adulthood.

Participants exhibited lower dispersion in WAIS-III verbal tasks compared to performance tasks, highlighting the influence of task characteristics on cognitive variability. This relative stability in verbal performance suggests that such tasks—often reliant on vocabulary and language exposure—are shaped by crystallized intelligence (gc), which draws on accumulated knowledge and tends to be more consistent across individuals. In contrast, performance tasks, which engage fluid intelligence (gf), involve novel problem-solving and are more sensitive to individual differences in cognitive strategies, processing speed, and problem-solving approaches. The greater dispersion observed in performance tasks may reflect the diverse cognitive processes they require, leading to more specialized skill development within this domain. This pattern is consistent with differential stability in rank-ordered performance across time in ability domains, where the stability is highest for general cognitive ability and strong but significantly lower for fluid abilities, processing speed, working memory, and auditory and visual processing (Breit et al., 2024). Moreover, comprehension (including verbal abilities) and quantitative abilities are as stable as general cognitive ability (Breit et al., 2024). In the SCA battery, which includes two verbal tasks alongside seven non-verbal tasks spanning episodic memory, spatial ability, perceptual speed, and general reasoning, dispersion patterns further illustrate this distinction. When standardized to current adult norms, verbal tasks show lower dispersion, suggesting more uniform performance. However, when standardized to age-16 norms, this pattern reverses unexpectedly. Given the limited number of verbal tasks in the SCA battery, we take caution not to interpret these findings.

To our knowledge, ours is the first behavioral genetic study of cognitive dispersion, and we observed the presence of substantial genetic influences for dispersion, from 43% to 84%, although, as expected, these heritabilities were reduced to 2% – 37% after adjustment for overall cognitive ability. Thus, our findings suggest systematic influences on divergence in cognitive performance due to developing and maintaining skills within specific cognitive domains. The finding that nonshared environmental factors account for the remaining portion of the variance in cognitive dispersion highlights the role of individual experiences and environmental influences that are unique to each person, even within twin pairs, as well as measurement error. We note, however, that compared to two-stage procedures, measurement error is relatively minimized using the MELSM approach as it captures systematic differences in intraindividual variability, avoiding outsized influence due to an outlying score on a particular test. Our findings of heritability suggest that including measured genetic influences is a needed next step. Studies of dispersion in older adults tend to adjust for APOE as a covariate but omit reporting associations, albeit in one study, APOE was not associated with dispersion (Watermeyer et al., 2020). More work would be fruitful in younger samples and with other polygenic scores.

Of relevance to cognitive reserve, we investigated whether individual differences in dispersion were associated with educational and occupational attainments, given that variability in cognitive functioning may reflect adaptive or maladaptive processes (c.f., Klepacz et al., 2024). Education and occupational prestige are associated with and sometimes used as proxies of cognitive reserve (Kremen et al., 2022). They may instead reflect mechanisms by which cognitive reserve and maintenance are realized (Kremen et al., 2022). To the extent that dispersion serves as a metric of specialization, it may reflect cognitive reserve, i.e., cognitive resources that individuals have built up at a given age (c.f., Kremen et al., 2022). Our results, however, do not support that intraindividual dispersion adds unique predictive value for educational or occupational attainment beyond what is accounted for by cognitive ability levels. Thus, our results support well-known phenotypic associations of education and occupational prestige with individual differences in overall general cognitive ability (Kremen et al., 2022). Nonetheless, the association between greater dispersion and higher overall ability, including overall gains in performance since adolescence, suggests that cognitive specialization may contribute to the enhancement and preservation of cognitive functioning through mechanisms that are distinct from formal educational and occupational pathways. We speculate that distinct pathways could include the strengthening of cognitive strategies (e.g., problem solving, memory) that flow from specific expertise.

Limitations and future directions

The strengths of our study include evaluating cognitive dispersion scores in established adulthood and their associations with attained education and occupation. Moreover, for the first time, we evaluated the etiologies of dispersion measures in contrast to variation in overall performance. While these are important advances, we acknowledge the limitations of our study, including the limited ethnic and racial demographics of the CATSLife sample, although the sample is reflective of state demographics at enrollment into the sibling and twin studies (Corley et al., 2019; Wadsworth et al., 2019).

With respect to related concepts of Spearman’s Law of Diminishing Returns (SLODR) and the possibility of spurious findings of ability-dispersion associations with non-normality of specific test scores (Murray et al., 2013), we took care to remove one non-normal WAIS subtest and used an approach that considered systematic variability of performance versus the mean. However, more work could be done to evaluate assumptions, including multivariate normality.

We do caution that the choice of norms may alter the distribution of dispersion estimates. We note that age was not significantly associated with dispersion within the CATSLife subsamples, although sample cohort differences were apparent across the LTS and CAP subsamples, which are about 9 years apart in birth years; careful consideration of age and time is needed in future work. Although longitudinal analysis of dispersion measures is beyond the scope of the current paper, future work will benefit from longitudinal examinations of dispersion at the conclusion of the currently ongoing CATSLife2 assessment, as the samples transition into midlife. Indeed, longitudinal work across the lifespan is needed to examine the theorized U-shape pattern of dispersion (Hultsch & MacDonald, 2004), its association with overall performance, and to establish construct validity across ages.

Conclusion

We took a fresh approach in evaluating the phenomena and meaning of intraindividual variability (IIV) in the understudied established adulthood period, focusing on multiple measures of cognitive dispersion. Our biometrical results suggest there is a salient systematic signal in dispersion measures, with moderate genetic influences contributing to differential cognitive strengths and skill development that are distinct from general ability level. These results underscore the importance of considering both genetic and environmental factors in understanding the development and manifestation of cognitive dispersion and its association with various life outcomes. With respect to cognitive reserve, higher dispersion is associated with higher general ability and relative gains in skills from age 16, suggesting that cognitive specialization may be associated with growth and preservation of ability. However, reserve pathways associated uniquely with dispersion are likely distinct from educational and occupational attainment. Other studies of established adulthood are needed to replicate our findings as well as longitudinal studies evaluating the meanings of dispersion and other IIV metrics across time.
