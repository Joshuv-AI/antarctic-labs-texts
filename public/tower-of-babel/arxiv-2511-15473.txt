A Critical Drift-Diffusion Equation: Intermittent Behavior via Geometric Brownian Motion on SL ​ ( n ) \textbf{SL}(n)
Peter S. Morfe, Felix Otto, Christian Wagner

Abstract
. This paper concerns the so-called diffusion in the curl of the 2d Gaussian free field, and its generalization to higher dimensions n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 , building on the scale-by-scale homogenization approach developed recently by Chatzigeorgiou, Morfe, Otto, and Wang [ 13 ] . It begins by reformulating the approximation scheme of that work in terms of SDEs in the length scale L L . This exposes an unexpected connection with a certain geometric Brownian motion on the special linear group SL ​ ( n ) \textbf{SL}(n) . The analysis of this process sheds light on the original problem, particularly as it pertains to intermittent behavior exhibited by the (averaged) Lagrangian coordinate.

Let us now interpret Theorem

As was already present in

Since a realization of the GFF

has closed stream lines, the process (

if it were not for diffusion. (Incidentally, this
collection of loops is an object of active research
in view of its emergent conformal invariance.)
Diffusion allows the process

to move from one level set to the next.
As
X
explores larger and larger neighborhoods of its starting point,
it occasionally hits loops of large diameter, which introduces a ballistic element.
In the absence of an infra-red (IR) cut-off, there are loops of larger and larger diameter,
which is at the origin of superdiffusivity.

Our Theorem
3
describes an intermittent behavior of the process:
Two particles that start at close points and whose motion is driven by the same Brownian
motion start exploring nearby level lines of the GFF. In rare cases (i.e., with high probability, a small volume fraction of possible starting points in a large ball), the two particles lie in the basin attraction of two long level lines, which rapidly diverge, resulting in the particles being ripped apart with a burst-like character. This manifests in the intermittent behavior of the increment of the expected position
u
, which has abnormally long, non-Gaussian tails due to these rare events. Since trajectories only diverge on very large time scales (large enough that the diffusivity is large compared to the distance between particles), this behavior is in contrast (but not in contradiction) with the quenched invariance
principle for
X
reported in
[
1
]
. Theorem
3

seems to capture more of
the fine structure of the level sets of the GFF.

Our results suggest that the intermittency exhibited by the drift-diffusion process is more a manifestation of geometry than topology. On the one hand, at the level of topology, the stream lines of

are closed if and only if

suggests that intermittency plays less and less of a role as the dimension

becomes less and less curved.

The geometric Brownian motion

provides a simplified description of the gradient
\nabla u
of the Lagrangian coordinate, in which the spatial noise is replaced by a temporal one. A conceptually similar reduction occurs in another scaling-critical system, namely, the 2d stochastic heat equation. Specifically, in the weak coupling regime, Dunlap and Gu

\mathbb{E}[XX^{*}]=\text{id}
.

Proof.

Since
X
is trace-free, (i) implies that
\mathbb{E}X
is trace-free and isotropic, hence it vanishes. It thus only remains to characterize the covariance of
X
. Since
X
takes values in endomorphisms of cotangent space, its covariance is a bilinear form on the dual space, namely, the endomorphisms of tangent space. Let
\bullet
be this covariance tensor, that is, for any two endomorphisms of tangent space
G,\tilde{G}
, we have

\mathbb{E}(G.X)(\tilde{G}.X)=G\bullet\tilde{G}.

Assumption (i) implies that
\bullet
is rotationally invariant:

(79)

OGO^{-1}\bullet O\tilde{G}O^{-1}=G\bullet\tilde{G}\quad\text{for any}\,\,O\in\textbf{O}(n),\,\,G,\tilde{G}\in{\rm End}({\rm tangent~space}).

Thus, we can appeal to the representation theory of
\mathbf{O}(n)
, as in Appendix
A
.

Since
X
takes values in
\mathfrak{sl}(n)
, the identity
id
is in the kernel of
\bullet
, that is,
G\bullet\text{id}=0
for any
G
. Therefore, Lemma
16
(i) in Appendix
A
, (
79
) implies that there are symmetric bilinear forms
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
and
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
, respectively, such that

(80)

G\bullet\tilde{G}=G_{\text{sym}}\bullet_{\text{sym}}\tilde{G}_{\text{sym}}+G_{\text{skew}}\bullet_{\text{skew}}\tilde{G}_{\text{skew}}\quad\text{for any}\,\,G,\tilde{G}\in\text{End}(\text{tangent ~ space}),

where
G_{\text{sym}}=\frac{1}{2}(G+G^{*})-n^{-1}({\rm tr}\,G)\text{id}
and
G_{\text{skew}}=\frac{1}{2}(G-G^{*})
.

The factorization (
80
) implies that
X
can be decomposed in the form
X=X_{\text{sym}}+X_{\text{skew}}
, where
(X_{\text{sym}},X_{\text{skew}})
is a pair of independent Gaussian endomorphisms,
X_{\text{sym}}
taking values in the subspace
\text{Sym}_{0}
of trace-free, symmetric endomorphisms with covariance
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
and
X_{\text{skew}}
taking values in the subspace
Skew
of skew-symmetric endomorphisms with covariance
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
. In particular, because of independence, assumptions (ii) and (iii) become

0=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}_{\text{sym}}]+\mathbb{E}[X^{2}_{\text{skew}}],\quad\text{id}=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}_{\text{sym}}]-\mathbb{E}[X^{2}_{\text{skew}}],

and, therefore,

(81)

\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{sym}}^{2}]=-\mathbb{E}[X_{\text{skew}}^{2}]=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{id}.

Finally, note that
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
and
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
are both invariant under the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
by conjugation. That is, (
79
) still holds if we replace
\bullet
by
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
and
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
. According to representation theory for the group
\textbf{O}(n)
, see Lemma
16
(ii) & (iii) in Appendix
A
, the symmetric bilinear forms on
\text{Sym}_{0}
(respectively,
Skew
) invariant under this action form a one-dimensional linear space. Thus, equation (
81
), being a nontrivial linear equation in
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
, uniquely determines
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
. By the same logic, (
81
) also completely determines
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
.

Concerning existence, fix positive-definite,
\mathbf{O}(n)
-invariant bilinear forms
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
and
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
, defined respectively on the spaces of trace-free symmetric and skew-symmetric endomorphisms (e.g., the restriction of the Frobenius inner product
(G,G^{\prime})\mapsto{\rm tr}\,G^{*}G^{\prime}
to the respective spaces). By
\mathbf{O}(n)
-invariance, if
X_{\text{sym}}
and
X_{\text{skew}}
are centered Gaussian random variables with covariances
\bullet_{\text{sym}}
and
\bullet_{\text{skew}}
, respectively, then
\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{\text{sym}}=c_{\text{sym}}\text{id}
and
-\mathbb{E}X^{2}_{\text{skew}}=c_{\text{skew}}\text{id}
for some constants
c_{\text{sym}},c_{\text{skew}}>0
. Up to multiplication by constants, there is no loss of generality assuming that
c_{\text{sym}}=c_{\text{skew}}=\frac{1}{2}
. The desired random variable
X
can then be taken to be the sum
X=X_{\text{sym}}+X_{\text{skew}}
of independent realizations of
X_{\text{sym}}
and
X_{\text{skew}}
, or, equivalently, by defining
X
to be the centered Gaussian with covariance determined by (
80
). ∎

Next, we use Lemma
11
and the construction in Lemma 7 to determine the Brownian motion
B
driving the SDE
dF=F\circ\!dB
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Since (ii) implies that
F
is a martingale, which implies that
\mathbb{E}[FF^{*}]_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}F_{\tau}F_{\tau}^{*}
, we can reformulate conditions (i)-(iii) in the following equivalent way:

(i)

OFO^{-1}\overset{\text{law}}{=}F
for any
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
.

(ii)

F\circ\!dB=FdB
.

(iii)

\frac{d}{d\tau}\mathbb{E}[FF^{*}]_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}F_{\tau}F_{\tau}^{*}
.

By Lemma 7, which itself is a consequence of Lemma 11, there is a unique Brownian motion
B
on
\mathfrak{sl}(n)
such that

(i’)

OBO^{-1}\overset{\text{law}}{=}B
for each
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
,

(ii’)

\mathbb{E}[B_{\tau}^{2}]=0
,

(iii’)

\mathbb{E}[B_{\tau}B_{\tau}^{*}]=\tau\,\text{id}
.

We will prove that each of the properties (i)–(iii) above are equivalent to the corresponding property of
B
in (i’)–(iii’).

We start with the equivalence of (i) and (i’). Given an
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
, observe that

(82)

\displaystyle d(OFO^{-1})

\displaystyle=OdFO^{-1}=O(F\circ\!dB)O^{-1}=(OFO^{-1})\circ\!d(OBO^{-1}).

Thus, if
OBO^{-1}=_{\text{law}}B
, then
OFO^{-1}=_{\text{law}}F
, and hence (i’) implies (i).

Next, we prove the converse. To this end, we apply Itô’s product rule to the bilinear map
F\mapsto F\otimes F
: To identify the quadratic variation, let us note that for endomorphisms
F
,
B
, we have
(F\otimes F)(B\otimes B)=FB\otimes FB
. Since the martingale part of
dF
equals
F_{\tau}dB
, we use this identity in conjunction with the Riemann sum approximation of
[F\otimes F]
to find

(83)

\displaystyle d[F\otimes F]=(F_{\tau}\otimes F_{\tau})d[B\otimes B],

which, since
F_{\tau=0}\otimes F_{\tau=0}
is equal to the identity and, by the properties of Brownian motion,

(84)

d[B\otimes B]=\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}\otimes B_{\tau=1}d\tau,

yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\Big|_{\tau=0}\mathbb{E}F_{\tau}\otimes F_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}\otimes B_{\tau=1}.

On the other hand, in view of (
82
), we can apply the same argument to
OFO^{-1}
. Since
d[OFO^{-1}\otimes OFO^{-1}]=(OF_{\tau}O^{-1}\otimes OF_{\tau}O^{-1})d[OBO^{-1}\otimes OBO^{-1}]
, this yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\Big|_{\tau=0}\mathbb{E}OF_{\tau}O^{-1}\otimes OF_{\tau}O^{-1}=\mathbb{E}OB_{\tau=1}O^{-1}\otimes OB_{\tau=1}O^{-1}.

If
OFO^{-1}=_{\text{law}}F
, then the last two displays are equal; thus showing that the covariance of
B_{\tau=1}
is invariant under the action of conjugation by
O
. By (
84
) and the independent increments property of Brownian motion, it follows that the full covariance of
B
is invariant under conjugation by
O
. Therefore, (iii) implies (iii’).

Next, we prove the equivalence of (ii) and (ii’). Regarding (ii), from the asymptotic midpoint formula for the Stratonovich integral, we have
F_{\tau}\circ\!dB=F_{\tau}dB+\frac{1}{2}d[FB]
. The martingale part of
dF
is then certainly
F_{\tau}dB
, hence
d[FB]=F_{\tau}d[BB]
by a noncommutative extension of standard manipulations. We therefore arrive at

F_{\tau}\circ\!dB=F_{\tau}dB+\frac{1}{2}F_{\tau}d[BB].

At the same time, (
84
) implies
d[BB]=\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}^{2}d\tau
and thus,
F_{\tau}\circ\!dB=F_{\tau}dB
if and only if
\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}^{2}=0
. Therefore, by another application of (
84
), (ii) and (ii’) are equivalent.

Next, we turn to property (iii).
Applying the contraction
F\otimes\tilde{F}\mapsto F\tilde{F}^{*}
for endomorphisms
F
and
\tilde{F}
to (
83
),
we find

d[FF^{*}]=F_{\tau}d[BB^{*}]F_{\tau}^{*}
,
which yields, by (
84
),

(85)

d[FF^{*}]=F_{\tau}\left(\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}B^{*}_{\tau=1}\right)F_{\tau}^{*}d\tau.

Hence (iii’) implies (iii).

Conversely, if (iii) holds, then we invoke (
85
) to find

\displaystyle\text{id}=\mathbb{E}F_{\tau=0}F_{\tau=0}^{*}=\frac{d}{d\tau}\Big|_{\tau=0}\mathbb{E}[FF^{*}]_{\tau}=\mathbb{E}F_{\tau=0}\left(\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}B_{\tau=1}^{*}\right)F_{\tau=0}^{*}=\mathbb{E}B_{\tau=1}B^{*}_{\tau=1},

which gives (iii’).
∎

Remark 2
.

From the properties of

established in the proof of Lemma

-invariant in law and determined by the normalization

2.3.
The Symmetric Part in
n=2

We close this section with a characterization of the symmetric part of the covariance of
B
in dimension
n=2
, which is an essential ingredient in our analysis of intermittency in the next section.

Lemma 12
.

If
B
is the Brownian motion of Lemma
1
and
n=2
, then, for any symmetric endomorphism
G\in\text{End}(\text{tangent ~ space})
,

\mathbb{E}(G.B_{\tau})^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\tau\big(({\rm tr}\,G)^{2}-4\det\,G\big).

Proof.

Since
B
is a Brownian motion, we only need to check the case
\tau=1
. First, we recall that the covariance of the symmetric part of
B
is invariant under the action of
\textbf{O}(2)
. Therefore, since the space of symmetric
\textbf{O}(2)
-invariant bilinear forms on the trace-free symmetric endomorphisms
\text{Sym}_{0}
is one-dimensional (Lemma
16
(ii) in Appendix
A
), there is a constant
\lambda>0
such that, for any
G_{0},\tilde{G}_{0}\in\text{Sym}_{0}
,

(86)

\mathbb{E}(G_{0}.B_{\tau=1})(\tilde{G}_{0}.B_{\tau=1})=\lambda{\rm tr}\,G_{0}\tilde{G}_{0}.

If instead
G,\tilde{G}\in\text{Sym}
(possibly with nonzero trace), then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(G.B_{\tau=1})(\tilde{G}.B_{\tau=1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(G_{\text{sym}}.B_{\tau=1})(G_{\text{sym}}.B_{\tau=1})=\lambda{\rm tr}\,G_{\text{sym}}\tilde{G}_{\text{sym}},

where as earlier we write
G_{\text{sym}}=G-\frac{1}{2}({\rm tr}\,G)\text{id}
.

Next, we compute
\lambda
using the identity (
81
). Namely, if
B_{\text{sym}}
is the symmetric part of
B
, then taking the trace of (
81
) yields

\mathbb{E}[|B_{\text{sym},\tau=1}|^{2}]=1.

On the other hand, after summing (
86
) over an orthonormal basis of
\text{Sym}_{0}
, we find

\mathbb{E}[|B_{\text{sym},\tau=1}|^{2}]=2\lambda,\quad\text{hence}\,\,\lambda=\frac{1}{2}.

Next, we can define a symmetric bilinear form
\tilde{\bullet}
on
Sym
by the rule

G\tilde{\bullet}\tilde{G}=({\rm tr}\,G)({\rm tr}\,\tilde{G})-2\det(e^{1}\otimes Ge_{1}+e^{2}\otimes\tilde{G}e_{2})-2\det(e^{1}\otimes\tilde{G}e_{1}+e^{2}\otimes Ge_{2}).

Note that the associated quadratic form is
G\tilde{\bullet}G=(\text{tr}\,G)^{2}-4\det\,G
. Further,
\tilde{\bullet}
is invariant under the action of
\textbf{O}(2)
on
Sym
. Thus, there is a symmetric linear map
\mathbf{T}
on
Sym
such that
O(\mathbf{T}G)O^{-1}=\mathbf{T}(OGO^{-1})
and
G\tilde{\bullet}G^{\prime}={\rm tr}\,G^{\prime}(\mathbf{T}G)^{*}
for each
G,G^{\prime}\in\text{Sym}
and
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
. Applying Lemma
17
to
\mathbf{T}
, we deduce that there is a
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
and a
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that

G\tilde{\bullet}\tilde{G}=\kappa{\rm tr}\,G\,{\rm tr}\,\tilde{G}+\mu{\rm tr}\,G_{\text{sym}}\tilde{G}_{\text{sym}}\quad\text{for any}\,\,G,\tilde{G}\in\text{Sym}.

Substituting
G=\tilde{G}=\text{id}
yields
\kappa=0
, and the choice
G=\tilde{G}=e^{1}\otimes e_{1}-e^{2}\otimes e_{2}
yields
\mu=2
. Therefore,

\frac{1}{4}G\tilde{\bullet}G=\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\,G_{\text{sym}}G_{\text{sym}}=\mathbb{E}(G.B_{\tau=1})^{2}.

∎

3.
Intermittent behavior of the Bessel-type process
R
in
n=2

The remainder of our study of the geometric Brownian motion
F
focuses on intermittency. Here we exploit structure specific to dimension
n=2
. In particular, we will deduce information about
F
by studying the process
R
given by
R_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}|F_{\tau}|^{2}
, where we recall
|\cdot|
is the Frobenius norm.

The analysis begins with the observation that
R
satisfies an SDE in its own right.

Lemma 13
.

If
R
is the process
R_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}|F_{\tau}|^{2}
, then
\mathbb{P}\{R_{\tau}>1\,\,\text{for each}\,\,\tau>0\}=1
and there is a one-dimensional Brownian motion
w
adapted to
B
such that

(87)

\displaystyle dR=\frac{1}{2}R_{\tau}d\tau+\sqrt{R^{2}_{\tau}-1}\circ\!dw.

Sketch of proof.

To motivate the appearance of the SDE (
87
), notice that since
dF^{*}=dB^{*}F^{*}
and
{\rm tr}\,F^{*}FdB^{*}={\rm tr}\,dBF^{*}F={\rm tr}\,F^{*}FdB
, an application of Itô’s lemma to the product
FF^{*}
yields

(88)

dR={\rm tr}\,F_{\tau}^{*}F_{\tau}dB+\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\,F_{\tau}d[BB^{*}]F_{\tau}^{*}={\rm tr}\,F_{\tau}^{*}F_{\tau}dB+Rd\tau.

At the same time, applying Lemma
12
with
G_{\tau}=F_{\tau}F_{\tau}^{*}
, writing
B^{\prime}
for an independent copy of
B
with law
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
, and using the fact that
G
is adapted to
B
, we see that the martingale
M
with
dM={\rm tr}\,F_{\tau}^{*}F_{\tau}dB=G_{\tau}^{\dagger}.dB
has quadratic variation

(89)

\displaystyle d[MM]=\mathbb{E}^{\prime}(G_{\tau}^{\dagger}.B^{\prime}_{\tau=1})^{2}d\tau=\frac{1}{4}(({\rm tr}\,G_{\tau})^{2}-4\det G_{\tau})d\tau=(R_{\tau}^{2}-1)d\tau.

This shows that
R
solves the following SDE in law:

(90)

dR=R_{\tau}d\tau+\sqrt{R_{\tau}^{2}-1}dw.

Since the solution of this SDE has
d[\sqrt{R^{2}-1}\,w]=\frac{R_{\tau}}{\sqrt{R_{\tau}^{2}-1}}d[Rw]=R_{\tau}d\tau
, the Stratonovich formulation of the equation is precisely (
87
).

To fully justify that
R
solves the SDE above, there is just one technicality, namely, the possibility that
R_{\tau}=1
. Indeed, since
F
takes values in
\textbf{SL}(2)
,
R\geq 1
holds globally, and
R_{\tau}=1
if and only if
F_{\tau}\in\textbf{SO}(2)
. Yet
\textbf{SO}(2)
is a compact, codimension-two submanifold of
\textbf{SL}(2)
, thus of vanishing capacity, hence the diffusion
F
cannot hit it. In Appendix
D
, we give a more elementary, but less transparent, proof based on the Feller test, along with the argument for the existence of the Brownian motion
w
in Lemma
13
. ∎

It turns out that the SDE solved by
R
can be reformulated in terms of the largest eigenvalue
S
of
F^{*}F
. From the definition of
R
and the fact that
\det F=1
, the two processes are related by the equation

(91)

R_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}(S_{\tau}+S_{\tau}^{-1}).

This equation has the explicit solution
S=\exp\text{arccosh}\,R
, from which we deduce that

(92)

dS=\frac{1}{2}S_{\tau}\frac{S_{\tau}^{2}+1}{S_{\tau}^{2}-1}d\tau+S_{\tau}\circ\!dw.

Here there is no ambiguity because
R
(hence also
S
) never hits one.

The SDE (
92
) is reminiscent of one-dimensional geometric Brownian motion. Specifically, the process
Q
defined by

(93)

Q_{\tau}=e^{\frac{1}{2}\tau+w_{\tau}}

solves the SDE

(94)

\displaystyle dQ=\frac{1}{2}Q_{\tau}d\tau+Q_{\tau}\circ\!dw.

We will see that the process
R
is qualitatively similar to
Q
. For instance, since
\frac{S^{2}+1}{S^{2}-1}>1
and
R\geq 2^{-1}S
by definition, the comparison principle for Stratonovich SDEs implies that

(95)

\mathbb{P}\{2R_{\tau}\geq S_{\tau}\geq Q_{\tau}\,\,\text{for each}\,\,\tau\geq 0\}=1.

Next, we show that a matching upper bound holds at the level of moments.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Applying Itô’s formula to the SDE (
90
), we find, for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle d\mathbb{E}R^{p}=\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)\mathbb{E}R^{p}_{\tau}d\tau-\frac{1}{2}p(p-1)\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}^{p-2}d\tau\leq\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}^{p}d\tau.

By the comparison principle, the inequality
\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}^{p}\leq e^{\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)\tau}
follows.

At the same time, from the formula (
93
) and the classical formula for the moment generating function of a Gaussian, we know that
\mathbb{E}Q_{\tau}^{p}=e^{\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)\tau}
. Thus, by (
95
), we have

\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}^{p}\geq 2^{-p}e^{\frac{1}{2}p(p+1)\tau}.

∎

The lower bound (
95
) can also be used to compute the top Lyapunov exponent of
F
, as explained in the next remark.

Remark 3
.

In the limit
\tau\to\infty
, we have

(96)

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\ln R_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{almost surely.}

In particular, the top Lyapunov exponent of
F
equals
\frac{1}{4}
.

Proof of (
96
).

First, notice that (
95
) implies that
S_{\tau}\to\infty
as
\tau\to\infty
almost surely. From this, we can use the SDEs satisfied by
\ln S
and
\ln Q
(readily computed from (
92
) and (
94
), respectively) to deduce that

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}(\ln S_{\tau}-\ln Q_{\tau})=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}\big(\frac{S^{2}_{\tau^{\prime}}+1}{S_{\tau^{\prime}}^{2}-1}-1\big)=0.

Thus, since
R_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}(S_{\tau}+S_{\tau}^{-1})
, we conclude

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\ln R_{\tau}\overset{\eqref{E: S equation}}{=}\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\ln S_{\tau}=\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{1}{\tau}\ln Q_{\tau}\overset{\eqref{E: Q formula}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{almost surely.}

∎

We conclude this section with the proof of Lemma
4
.

Proof of Lemma
4
.

We claim that it suffices to observe that

(97)

\mathbb{E}Q_{\tau}I(Q_{\tau}\geq\mathbb{E}^{\frac{3}{2}}Q_{\tau})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}Q_{\tau}.

Indeed, since
R_{\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}Q_{\tau}
, once we have established (
97
), we can use the identity
\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}=e^{\tau}=\mathbb{E}Q_{\tau}
(which follows directly from (
90
)) to find the desired estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}I(R_{\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}^{\frac{3}{2}}R_{\tau})\geq\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}R_{\tau}.

It only remains to prove (
97
). Since
Q_{\tau}
is given by (
93
) and
\mathbb{E}Q_{\tau}=e^{\tau}
, this identity is equivalent to

e^{-\frac{\tau}{2}}\mathbb{E}e^{w_{\tau}}I(w_{\tau}\geq\tau)=\frac{1}{2}.

Since
w_{\tau}
is a Gaussian with variance
\tau
, this in turn is equivalent to the elementary identity
\int_{\tau}^{\infty}dwe^{-\frac{\tau}{2}+w-\frac{w^{2}}{2\tau}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dw^{\prime}e^{-\frac{w^{\prime 2}}{2\tau}}
.
∎

Representation theory

Throughout this work, we need some classical statements from representation theory of
\textbf{SO}(n)
and
\textbf{O}(n)
that we summarize in this appendix. We start with the following lemma classifying invariant two-tensors.

Lemma 15
.

(i) We have

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\big\{\text{symmetric}~\textbf{SO}(n)\text{-invariant}~\text{bilinear}~\text{forms}~\text{on}~{\rm tangent~space}\big\}=1;

more precisely, this space is spanned by the tensor
(\dot{x},\dot{y})\mapsto\dot{x}\cdot\dot{y}
. In particular, every
\textbf{SO}(n)
-invariant symmetric bilinear form is also
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant.

(ii) We have

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\big\{\textbf{O}(n)\text{-invariant}~\text{endomorphisms}~\text{of}~{\rm cotangent~space}\big\}=1,

and consequently the space is spanned by the identity
{\rm id}
.

For the reader’s convenience we provide a proof of Lemma
15
further below. Of importance for us is the following corollary of point (i).

Corollary 1
.

We have

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\,\big\{\mathcal{F}c:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\rightarrow{\rm tangent~space}\otimes_{\rm sym}{\rm tangent~space}~{\rm satisfying}~\eqref{ao02c}~\&~\eqref{ao24repeat}\}=1.

More precisely, the space is spanned by the function
k\mapsto\delta^{ij}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}-k^{*}\otimes k^{*}
.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

Recall that we can identify the tensor space
{\rm tangent~space}\otimes_{\rm sym}{\rm tangent~space}
with the symmetric bilinear forms on
{\rm cotangent~space}
. In this vein, given
k\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, (
4
) states that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}c(k)~{\rm is~an~isotropic~symmetric~bilinear~form~on~the~orthogonal~complement~of}~k.

Thus by Lemma
15
(i)
32
32

32

technically speaking applied to forms on a subspace of
{\rm cotangent~space}
the tensor
\mathcal{F}c(k)
is a multiple of the Euclidean inner product on the orthogonal complement of
k
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}c(k).\xi\otimes\xi^{\prime}={\rm constant}(k)\,\xi\cdot\xi^{\prime}\quad{\rm provided}~\xi\cdot k=\xi^{\prime}\cdot k=0.

By (

\mathcal{F}c(k)
vanishes whenever
\xi
or
\xi^{\prime}
are parallel to

(\xi-(\xi\cdot k)k)\cdot(\xi^{\prime}-(\xi^{\prime}\cdot k)k)=\xi\cdot\xi^{\prime}-(\xi\cdot k)(\xi^{\prime}\cdot k)

\displaystyle(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mapsto\mathcal{F}c(k).\xi\otimes\xi^{\prime}\quad{\rm is~a~multiple}~{\rm constant}(k)~{\rm of}\quad(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mapsto\xi\cdot\xi^{\prime}-(\xi\cdot k)(\xi^{\prime}\cdot k).

Since the latter form already shares the invariance (

Next, we provide a proof of Lemma
15
. Note that in point (ii) we do not assume symmetry; thus it does not immediately follow from point (i).

Proof of Lemma
15
.

The first part is a standard consequence of Schur’s lemma, which we recall for the readers convenience: Given a bilinear form
f
on tangent space, there is a symmetric endomorphism
T
such that
f(\dot{x},\dot{y})=\dot{x}\cdot T\dot{y}
. By the spectral theorem, we can (orthogonally) diagonalize
T
. By the
\textbf{SO}(n)
-invariance we know that all the eigenspaces are
\textbf{SO}(n)
-invariant. Since any two unit vectors may be transformed into each other by an element of
\textbf{SO}(n)
, we learn that the eigenspaces of
T
are either trivial or equal to the full space. Therefore,
T
has only one eigenvalue and is thus a multiple of the identity, which proves
f
is a multiple of the Euclidean inner product.

For the second item, let
T
be an
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant endomorphism. For some vector
e
we consider the reflection sending
e
to
-e
and fixing orthogonal complement of
e
. By the
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariance of
T
we conclude
v\cdot Te=0
for any
v
orthogonal to
e
. Thus
Te
is parallel to
e
, i.e.
e
is an eigenvector. Since
e
was arbitrary, we learn that any vector is an eigenvector for
T
. Since any two unit vectors are mapped into each other by a orthogonal transformation,
T
has exactly one eigenvalue and is thus a multiple of the identity.
∎

The next lemma deals with the classification of
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant symmetric bilinear forms on
{\rm End}({\rm tangent~space})
. By
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant, we mean a form
\bullet
such that

\displaystyle(OEO^{-1})\bullet(OE^{\prime}O^{-1})=E\bullet E^{\prime}\quad\text{for each}\quad E\in\text{End}({\rm tangent~space}),\,\,O\in\textbf{O}(n).

Lemma 16
.

(i) Every
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant symmetric bilinear form on
{\rm End}({\rm tangent~space})
factors
{\rm End}({\rm tangent~space})
orthogonally into the space of isotropic, trace-free and symmetric, and skew-symmetric endomorphisms.

(ii) We have

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\big\{\text{symmetric}~\textbf{O}(n)\text{-invariant}~\text{bilinear}~\text{forms}~\text{on}~\text{symmetric}~\text{trace-free}~\text{endomorphisms}\big\}=1.

(iii) We have

\displaystyle{\rm dim}\big\{\text{symmetric}~\textbf{O}(n)\text{-invariant}~\text{bilinear}~\text{forms}~\text{on}~\text{skew-symmetric}~\text{endomorphisms}\big\}=1.

A.1.
Self-contained argument for Lemma
16

We refer to
[
21
, Theorem 5.3.3]
for a proof of Lemma
16
in the case of complex vector spaces. Since we are not aware of a reference covering the real case, we provide a self-contained argument in this subsection.

The argument involves elementary representation theory. As throughout the paper, we use the fact that the group
\textbf{O}(n)
acts on
\text{End}({\rm tangent~space})
via conjugation
E\mapsto OEO^{-1}
. Recall that an
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant linear subspace
V
of
\text{End}({\rm tangent~space})
is called
irreducible
if its only
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant subspaces are the zero subspace
\{0\}
and
V
itself. As stated in the next proposition, in this setting, the irreducible subspaces are known.

Proposition 2
.

The
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant subspaces
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
,
\text{Sym}_{0}
, and
Skew
of
\text{End}({\rm tangent~space})
are irreducible.

We will show that items (i)-(iii) of Lemma
16
are all consequences of irreducibility. In fact, (ii) and (iii) are equivalent to the irreducibility of
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
and
\text{Sym}_{0}
, respectively. We start with the proof of (i) next.

Proof of Lemma
16
(i).

Give a symmetric bilinear form
\bullet
on the endomorphisms of tangent space, there is a symmetric linear map
\mathbf{T}
such that
E\bullet E^{\prime}={\rm tr}\,E^{\prime}(\mathbf{T}E)
for any pair of endomorphisms
E,E^{\prime}
. Assume that
\bullet
is
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant. It then follows that
\mathbf{T}
commutes with the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
, that is,
O(\mathbf{T}E)O^{-1}=\mathbf{T}(OEO^{-1})
for any
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
and endomorphism
E
. To see that
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
,
\text{Sym}_{0}
, and
Skew
are orthogonal under
\bullet
, it only remains to show that if
V,W\in\{\text{span}\{\text{id}\},\text{Sym}_{0},\text{Skew}\}
and
\pi_{V}
and
\pi_{W}
denote the orthogonal projections onto
V
and
W
, respectively, then
\pi_{W}\circ\mathbf{T}\circ\pi_{V}=0
whenever
V\neq W
.

To see this, note that
\pi_{W}\circ\mathbf{T}\circ\pi_{V}
commutes with the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
since
\mathbf{T}
,
\pi_{W}
, and
\pi_{V}
all do. Thus, the kernel and range of
\pi_{W}\circ\mathbf{T}\circ\pi_{V}
are
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant subspaces of
V
and
W
, respectively. Therefore, by irreducibility (Proposition
2
), either
\pi_{W}\circ\mathbf{T}\circ\pi_{V}=0
or
\pi_{W}\circ\mathbf{T}\circ\pi_{V}
is an isomorphism between
V
and
W
. However, this second option is impossible: If
n>2
, then the dimensions of
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
,
\text{Sym}_{0}
, and
Skew
all differ so they cannot be isomorphic. If
n=2
, then this argument fails since
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
and
Skew
are both one-dimensional. Nonetheless, an isomorphism between them cannot commute with the
\mathbf{O}(n)
action. This is because while every
O\in\mathbf{O}(n)
acts as the identity transformation on
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
, this is not true of the action of
\mathbf{O}(n)
on
Skew
(e.g., by Lemma
15
(ii)). ∎

In the proof of Proposition
2
, we proceed by treating the symmetric and skew-symmetric subspaces separately.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\text{Sym}=\text{span}\{\text{id}\}\oplus\text{Sym}_{0}
denote the space of symmetric endomorphisms of tangent space. Let
\pi_{\text{span}\{\text{id}\}}
and
\pi_{\text{Sym}_{0}}
denote the orthogonal projections of
Sym
onto
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
and
\text{Sym}_{0}
, respectively. If
\mathbf{T}
is a symmetric linear map on
Sym
that commutes with the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
, then there are constants
\lambda_{\text{id}},\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathbf{T}=\lambda_{\text{id}}\pi_{\text{span}\{\text{id}\}}+\lambda_{0}\pi_{\text{Sym}_{0}}.

Proof.

By the spectral theorem and linearity, it suffices to prove that there are constants
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbf{T}\dot{x}^{*}\otimes\dot{x}=\lambda\text{id}+\mu\dot{x}^{*}\otimes\dot{x}^{*}
for any unit vector
\dot{x}
in tangent space. Since
\mathbf{T}
commutes with the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
, it suffices to consider the case when
\dot{x}=e_{1}
.

Again, by
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariance,
\mathbf{T}e^{1}\otimes e_{1}
is invariant under conjugation by elements of
\textbf{O}(n)
that fix
e_{1}
itself. That is, if
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
and
Oe_{1}=e_{1}
, then
O(\mathbf{T}e^{1}\otimes e_{1})O^{-1}=\mathbf{T}e^{1}\otimes e_{1}
. From this, we deduce that any eigenspace of
\mathbf{T}e^{1}\otimes e_{1}
is invariant under the action of such an
O
, from which it follows that eigenspaces belong to the set
\{\{0\},\text{span}\{e_{1}\},\{e_{1}\}^{\perp},{\rm tangent~space}\}
. Since the eigenspaces of
\mathbf{T}e^{1}\otimes e_{1}
span
{\rm tangent~space}
, we conclude that
\mathbf{T}e^{1}\otimes e_{1}=\lambda\text{id}+\mu e^{1}\otimes e_{1}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. ∎

Due to the equivalence between symmetric bilinear forms and self-adjoint endomorphisms, the irreducibility of
\text{Sym}_{0}
is equivalent to statement (ii) in Lemma
16
, as shown next.

Proof of Lemma
16
(ii).

If
\bullet
is a symmetric bilinear form on
\text{Sym}_{0}
, then there is a symmetric
\mathbf{T}\in\text{End}(\text{Sym}_{0})
such that
E\bullet E^{\prime}={\rm tr}\,E^{*}(\mathbf{T}E^{\prime})
for any
E,E^{\prime}\in\text{Sym}_{0}
. If
\bullet
is
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant, then
\mathbf{T}
commutes with the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
. Extending
\mathbf{T}
to the entire space of symmetric endomorphisms by setting
\mathbf{T}\text{id}=0
, we invoke Lemma
17
to deduce that
\mathbf{T}
is equal to some constant
\lambda
times the orthogonal projection onto
\text{Sym}_{0}
. This implies that
E\bullet E^{\prime}=\lambda{\rm tr}\,E^{*}E^{\prime}
. ∎

It only remains to treat
Skew
. Here it is convenient to work with bilinear forms
\bullet
rather than maps
\mathbf{T}
.

Lemma 18
.

If
\bullet
is an
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant symmetric bilinear form on
Skew
, then there is a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
E\bullet E^{\prime}=\lambda{\rm tr}\,E^{*}E^{\prime}
.

In particular, this lemma directly implies Lemma
16
(iii).

Proof.

Let
\bullet
be an
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant symmetric bilinear form on
Skew
. Given tangent vectors
\dot{x},\dot{y}
, denote by
\dot{x}\otimes_{\text{skew}}\dot{y}
the antisymmetric tensor product

\dot{x}\otimes_{\text{skew}}\dot{y}=\dot{x}^{*}\otimes\dot{y}-\dot{y}^{*}\otimes\dot{x}.

Since the endomorphisms
e_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{j}
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
form a basis of
Skew
, we only need to prove that there is a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(e_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{j})\bullet(e_{m}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{k})=\lambda\delta_{im}\delta_{jk}\quad\text{for any}\,\,i<j,m<k.

This is straightforward: We use the
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariance of
\bullet
together with the fact that any permutation of
\sigma
of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
gives rise to an element
O_{\sigma}\in\textbf{O}(n)
with
O_{\sigma}e_{\ell}=e_{\sigma(\ell)}
for all
\ell
. Since any pair
i<j
can be mapped to another pair
m<k
by some permutation
\sigma
, the diagonal entry
(e_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{j})\bullet(e_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{j})
does not depend on
\{i,j\}
. Concerning the off-diagonal entries, if
\{i,j\}\neq\{m,k\}
, then, assuming without loss of generality that
m\notin\{i,j\}
, we fix the
O\in\textbf{O}(n)
such that
Oe_{m}=-e_{m}
and
Oe_{\ell}=e_{\ell}
for
\ell\neq m

(e_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{j})\bullet(e_{m}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{k})=(Oe_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}Oe_{j})\bullet(Oe_{m}\otimes_{\text{skew}}Oe_{k})=-(e_{i}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{j})\bullet(e_{m}\otimes_{\text{skew}}e_{k}).

This proves that the off-diagonal entries all vanish.∎

Proof of Proposition
2
.

The irreducibility of
\text{Sym}_{0}
follows from Lemma
17
: If
V\subseteq\text{Sym}_{0}
is a linear subspace invariant under the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
, then the orthogonal projection
\pi_{V}
of
Sym
onto
V
commutes with the action of
\textbf{O}(n)
. Thus, Lemma
17
implies that
\pi_{V}=\pi_{\text{Sym}_{0}}
or
\pi_{V}=0
, that is, either
V=\text{Sym}_{0}
or
V=\{0\}
.

The irreducibility of
Skew
follows from an analogous argument: Given an
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant subspace
V\subseteq\text{Skew}
, consider the orthogonal projection
\pi_{V}
of
Skew
onto
V
. If we define the symmetric bilinear form
\bullet
by
E\bullet E^{\prime}={\rm tr}\,(\pi_{V}E)^{*}(\pi_{V}E^{\prime})
, then
\bullet
is
\textbf{O}(n)
-invariant since

is. Therefore, Lemma
18
implies
E\bullet E^{\prime}=\lambda{\rm tr}\,E^{*}E^{\prime}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, which can only hold if
V=\{0\}
or
V=\text{Skew}
.

Finally, we observe that
\text{span}\{\text{id}\}
is irreducible since it is one-dimensional. ∎

\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
of tangent space,

\displaystyle a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}e_{i}\otimes e_{i}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(\mu_{i},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=i})e_{i}\otimes e_{i},

where by an abuse of notation we use the same symbol
f
to denote the equivariant
vector field
f=f(a)
and its representation as a function
f=f(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{n})
which,
as the above notation is meant to suggest,
is invariant under permuting the last
n-1
entries.
Then (
123
) reduces
to an evolution of the unordered set
\{\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\}
, which is of the form

(129)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\tau}=f(\mu_{i},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=i})-\mu_{i}.

The strict monotonicity (
128
) translates into

(130)

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{\prime}\leq\mu_{i}\;\mbox{for all}\;i\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\mu_{i}^{\prime}\not=\mu_{i}\;\mbox{for some}\;i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(\mu_{i}^{\prime},\{\mu_{j}^{\prime}\}_{j\not=i})>f(\mu_{i},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=i}).

We start by establishing that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{i}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}>\\
<\end{array}\right\}1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{i}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}<\\
>\end{array}\right\}0.

Indeed, for notational simplicity let us replace
(j,i)
with
j\neq i
by
(i,1)
with
i\neq 1
, treat

\mu_{1}
as the numéraire, and consider the ratio
\sigma_{i}:=\frac{\mu_{i}}{\mu_{1}}
:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{d\sigma_{i}}{d\tau}=\mu_{1}\frac{d\mu_{i}}{d\tau}-\mu_{i}\frac{d\mu_{1}}{d\tau}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{blaao06})}}{{=}}\mu_{1}f(\mu_{i},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=i})-\mu_{i}f(\mu_{1},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=1}).

By homogeneity, cf. (
122
), we obtain

(135)

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{d\sigma_{i}}{d\tau}=f(\sigma_{i},\{\sigma_{j}\}_{j\not=1,i}\cup\{1\})-f(\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}},\{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{i}}\}_{j\not=1,i}\cup\{1\}).

It follows from (
130
) that

\sigma_{i}\mapsto f(\sigma_{i},\{\sigma_{j}\}_{j\not=1,i}\cup\{1\})
is strictly monotone decreasing,
and that
\sigma_{i}\mapsto f(\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}},\{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{i}}\}_{j\not=1,i}\cup\{1\})

is strictly monotone increasing. Hence the r. h. s. of (
135
) is strictly
monotone decreasing; it obviously vanishes for
\sigma_{i}=1
. This establishes (
B
).

We now establish the qualitative

(136)

\displaystyle\mu:=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\quad\mbox{remains in a compact subset of}\;(0,\infty)^{n}.

Let
\theta\in(0,1)
be such that the initial data are contained in
the set
\{\mu|\mu_{j}\geq\theta\mu_{\ell}\;\mbox{for all}\;j,\ell\}
.
It follows from (
B
) that this set
is forward invariant under the dynamical system (
129
) for any fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
:

(137)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}\geq\theta\mu_{\ell}\quad\mbox{for all}\;j,\ell.

Let us now consider

\displaystyle\mu_{*}=\min_{j=1,\ldots,n}\mu_{j}\quad\mbox{so that by (\ref{blaao10})}\quad\mu_{j}\leq\frac{1}{\theta}\mu_{*}.

Hence it follows from (
137
) in conjunction with the non-strict form of
monotonicity (
130
)

\displaystyle f(\mu_{i},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=i})\geq f(\frac{\mu_{*}}{\theta},\{\frac{\mu_{*}}{\theta}\}).

By the homogeneity (
122
) and the normalization (
124
) this implies

\displaystyle f(\mu_{i},\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\not=i})\geq\frac{\theta}{\mu_{*}},

so that by (
129
)

(138)

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{*}}{d\tau}\geq\frac{\theta}{\mu_{*}}-\mu_{*}.

Likewise,

(139)

\displaystyle\mu^{*}:=\max_{j=1,\ldots,n}\mu_{j}\quad\mbox{satisfies}\quad\frac{d\mu^{*}}{d\tau}\leq\frac{1}{\theta\mu^{*}}-\mu^{*}.

Both differential inequalities combine to (
136
).

We now are in a position to establish (
126
) in the form of

(140)

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\uparrow\infty}\mu_{i}=1.

Define
\theta^{*}
by

\displaystyle\theta^{*}(\tau):=\max\{\theta~|~\mu_{j}(\tau)\geq\theta\mu_{\ell}(\tau)\;\mbox{for all}\;j,\ell\}.

We claim that
\theta^{*}(\tau)\to 1
as
\tau\to\infty
. To see this, for any indices
i,j
such that
\mu_{j}(\tau=0)<\mu_{i}(\tau=0)
and any
\theta\in(0,1)
, it is convenient to define
T_{ji}(\theta)
by

T_{ji}(\theta)=\sup\{\tau>0\,\mid\,\mu_{j}(\tau)\leq\theta\mu_{i}(\tau)\}.

Since
\mu
solves the ODE (
129

g_{ji}
in
(0,\infty)^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{i}}=g_{ji}(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}),

see also (
135
).
Thus, by compactness (see (
136
)) and continuity, the merely qualitative positivity in (
B

such that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{i}}\geq c_{ji}(\theta)\quad\text{in}\,\,(0,T_{ji}(\theta)).

From this, it follows that, for any given
\theta\in(0,1)

T_{ji}(\theta)<\infty
holds, hence

\displaystyle\limsup_{\tau\to\infty}\theta^{*}\geq\theta.

This proves
\theta^{*}(\tau)\to 1
as claimed.

By the same argument as for (
138
) & (
139
) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{*}}{d\tau}\geq\frac{\theta^{*}}{\mu_{*}}-\mu_{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{d\mu^{*}}{d\tau}\leq\frac{1}{\theta^{*}\mu^{*}}-\mu^{*}

Rewriting the above equations in the form
\frac{d}{d\tau}e^{2\tau}\mu_{*}^{2}\geq 2\theta^{*}e^{2\tau}
and
\frac{d}{d\tau}e^{2\tau}\mu^{*2}\leq\frac{2}{\theta^{*}}e^{2\tau}
, we can integrate from an arbitrarily large time
\tau_{0}
to time

to deduce that
\liminf_{\tau\uparrow\infty}\mu_{*}\geq 1\geq\limsup_{\tau\uparrow\infty}\mu^{*}
,
which amounts to (

Appendix C
Computing Integrals

Recall from Lemma
7
& (
44
) that the three variables
\tau
,
\tilde{\lambda}
and
L
are related by
\tau=\ln\tilde{\lambda}
,
\tilde{\lambda}^{2}=1+\varepsilon^{2}\ln L
. For our analysis, it is important to know the asymptotics of the following integrals:

(141)

\displaystyle e^{p\tau_{*}}\int_{0}^{\tau_{*}}d\tau\,\frac{L^{2}(\tau)}{e^{p\tau}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\frac{L_{*}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}}\Big)^{2}\quad{\rm for~any}~0<\varepsilon\leq 1,~p>0,

(142)

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{*}}^{\infty}d\tau\,\frac{\tilde{\lambda}_{L(\tau)}^{2}}{L^{p}(\tau)}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{L_{*}^{p}}\quad{\rm for~any}~p>0,

(143)

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{*}}^{\infty}d\tau\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p}_{L(\tau)}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{p}}\quad{\rm for~any}~p>0,

or equivalently due to the relation
d\tau=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}

(144)

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{p}\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\frac{L_{*}}{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}}\Big)^{2}\quad{\rm for~any}~0<\varepsilon\leq 1,p>0,

(145)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}^{\infty}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{1}{L^{p}(\tilde{\lambda})}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{L_{*}^{p}}\quad{\rm for~any}~p>0,

(146)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}^{\infty}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{p}\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{p}}\quad{\rm for~any}~p>0.

Integral (
146
) (and thus (
143
)) follows from
\frac{2}{p}\frac{d}{d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p}}=-\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}
. In the other integrals it is crucial to capture the constant of order
\varepsilon^{2}
. In (
145
) (and hence (
142
)) this follows from the relation
L^{-p}(\tilde{\lambda})=e^{-\frac{p}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\tilde{\lambda}^{2}-1)}
, which implies
-\frac{d}{d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2p}L^{-p}(\tilde{\lambda})=L^{-p}(\tilde{\lambda})
. We use the same relation on (
144
) to integrate by parts: Since the integrand is positive, we have

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda}_{*})}{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{p+2}}+(\frac{p}{2}+1)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}.

For
\varepsilon^{2}\leq(\frac{p}{2}+1)^{-1}
we can absorb the latter integral to the l. h. s., which implies the claim. In the opposite regime
(\frac{p}{2}+1)^{-1}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\leq 1
we argue as follows: We split the latter integral at
\tilde{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{p}{2}+1
. On the interval
\frac{p}{2}+1\leq\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}
we can use the additional factor
\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\leq(\frac{p}{2}+1)^{-1}
to bound the latter integral by
\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}

\varepsilon
, the function
\tilde{\lambda}\mapsto\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}
may be estimated solely in terms of

\displaystyle(\frac{p}{2}+1)\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\lambda}^{2}}\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{2}}d\tilde{\lambda}^{2}\,\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda})}{\tilde{\lambda}^{p+2}}\lesssim_{p}1\lesssim_{p}\frac{L^{2}(\tilde{\lambda}_{*})}{\tilde{\lambda}_{*}^{p+2}},

where all the constants are uniform in
(\frac{p}{2}+1)^{-1}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\leq 1
but depend on
p
.

Appendix D
Proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
13
.

We only need to prove the first statement, namely, that
R_{\tau}>1
for all
\tau>0
with probability one. Indeed, once this is established, the identity (
89
) implies that we can define a Brownian motion
w
via
dw=(R_{\tau}^{2}-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\rm tr}\,F_{\tau}^{*}F_{\tau}dB
, and then
dR=R_{\tau}d\tau+\sqrt{R^{2}_{\tau}-1}dw
follows. As noted already in the discussion in Section
3
, the Stratonovich form of this SDE is precisely (
87
).

It remains to show that
R_{\tau}>1
for all
\tau>0
.
To this end, we claim that there is no loss of generality in assuming that the initial condition
R_{0}>1
. Otherwise, if
R_{0}=1
, then
R
immediately takes excursions away from one. Indeed, given an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, on the event that
R_{\tau}=1
for each
\tau\in[0,\epsilon]
, we know that the quadratic variation
[RR]\equiv 0
in
[0,\epsilon]
, hence (
88
) implies
1=R_{\epsilon}=1+\int_{0}^{\epsilon}d\tau R_{\tau}=1+\epsilon
, which is absurd. This proves that
R
instantaneously ventures away from one. Therefore, up to suitably stopping
F
and invoking the strong Markov property, we can assume that
R_{0}>1
.

Finally, fix an initial condition
R_{0}>1
. Following Feller (see
[
26
, Section 5.5.C]
), we work with the so-called scale function associated with the SDE
dR=R_{\tau}d\tau+\sqrt{R^{2}_{\tau}-1}dw
. To motivate the scale function, observe that if
u
is harmonic in the sense that it is annihilated by the generator, that is,
ru^{\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{2}(r^{2}-1)u^{\prime\prime}(r)=0
, in some interval
(a,b)\subseteq(1,\infty)
containing
R_{0}
, and if
T_{a,b}
denotes the first time that
R
hits the boundary
\{a,b\}
, then, by (
88
) and (
89
), the process
u(R_{\tau\wedge T_{a,b}})
is a local martingale and a standard argument shows that

(147)

\mathbb{P}\{R_{T_{a,b}}=a\}=\frac{u(b)-u(R_{0})}{u(b)-u(a)}.

At the same time, we can easily derive an explicit representation for
u^{\prime}
:

\frac{d}{dr}\{\ln u^{\prime}\}=\frac{2r}{r^{2}-1},\quad\text{hence}\quad u^{\prime}=\frac{\text{const}}{r^{2}-1}.

Integration of this last expression motivates the introduction of the scale function
u(r)=\ln(\frac{r-1}{r+1})
, a particular solution of the ODE.
Since
u(1^{+})=-\infty
and
u(+\infty)<+\infty
, we easily read off from (
147
) that
R
will escape to infinity without ever hitting one.
∎
