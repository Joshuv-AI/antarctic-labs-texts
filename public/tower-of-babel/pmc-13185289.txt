The elephant (bird) in the room: unknown mechanisms and unresolved impacts of low DNA yields in avian microbiome research
Authors: Kevin D Kohl, Rindy Anderson, Catherine Andreadis, Marcella D Baiz, Kasun H Bodawatta, Felipe Campos-Cerda, Jaime A Chaves, Anne B Clark, Maria S Costantini, Gabrielle L Davidson, Charli S Davies, Maurine W Dietz, Melanie Florkowski, Sarah M Hird, Jennifer L Houtz, William H Karasov, Sarah A Knutie, Haw Chuan Lim, Nicholas A Mason, Melissah Rowe, Elizabeth N Rudzki, Priscilla A San Juan, Heather R Skeen, Morgan C Slevin, Shane Somers, Annie G West, Sarah F Worsley, Elin Videvall, Brian K Trevelline
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo holistically understand the biology of animals, we must unravel the complexities and specificities of host-microbe interactions across animal taxa. Birds represent enigmatic and scientifically compelling hosts in which to understand these interactions. Here, we present a brief summary of a series of conversations among avian microbiome researchers regarding methodological challenges facing the avian microbiome field, where most research to date has focused on bacterial communities of the gut. Collectively, we acknowledged a commonly shared but underreported issue facing the avian microbiome field: that of difficulty in obtaining high-quality and high-yield microbial DNA from avian fecal samples. We discuss some of the potential reasons underlying low DNA yields, such as inhibitory compounds and rapid DNA degradation, and provide recommendations for how researchers in the avian microbiome field might cope with these methodological challenges. Collective and dedicated efforts to address these challenges will be required for a robust understanding of host-microbe interactions in avian systems.Keywords: Birds, DNA extraction, Host-microbe interactions

Introduction

Our understanding of ecology and evolution has rapidly expanded through acknowledging that hosts are inhabited by diverse microbial communities, or ‘microbiomes’: communities of bacteria, fungi, archaea, protozoans, viruses, and other microorganisms that may influence host biology [1]. However, research in this area has remained heavily biased toward model systems and mammalian hosts [2, 3]. Avian hosts and their associated microbiomes have been of particular interest due to their taxonomic and ecological diversity and fascinating life histories employed by these species, such as external embryonic development and parental care, and long-distance migration events. Moreover, the avian gastrointestinal tract has distinct morphological and physiological adaptation towards nutrition and flight which may involve distinct interactions with microbial communities at this body site [4, 5]. Birds, as a diverse and enigmatic taxonomic group, offer unique opportunities to enhance our understanding of host-microbe interactions [6–9]. Understanding how the microbiome impacts host fitness and health is imperative for the advancement of management strategies for endangered and threatened species [10, 11].

However, a number of methodological factors, in particular low DNA yields from avian microbiome samples, continue to impede our understanding of microbial interactions with avian hosts and may limit the reproducibility and comparability of results across studies. For years, this methodological complexity has represented, in every sense of the phrase, the unacknowledged elephant in the room. Or, more precisely, given our subject matter, it may represent the elephant bird in the room, in reference to the extinct Elephant Bird (Aepyornis maximus). It is not to say that methodological limitations of low DNA yields have not been addressed in the avian microbiome field; we describe our advances and progress in understanding below. However, we feel the field would benefit from more transparent reporting of this issue, methodological details and strategic approaches to address them, and open discussion of the implications that low DNA yields might have on our understanding of avian-microbe interactions.

Here, we report a summary of an effort to address the “elephant bird” in the room, through a roundtable workshop, organized by K.D. Kohl, E. Videvall, and B.K. Trevelline, and held on August 11, 2021, during a virtual meeting of the North American Ornithological Conference. During this roundtable, a group was convened as a means to enhance progress by explicitly identifying and discussing the methodological challenges facing the avian microbiome field. This process was accomplished using a series of charrettes: a meeting method used to harness the talents and energies of multiple interested parties [12]. We used structured small group discussions with assigned roles (e.g., discussion leader, scribe) to gather thoughts on (i) conceptual challenges and (ii) methodological challenges facing the avian microbiome field. Collated notes from these discussions were organized and summarized for themes by K.D. Kohl. A secondary meeting with interested parties was held virtually on October 28th, 2021, which involved more collective group brainstorming with additional thoughts again collated by K.D. Kohl. Following, updated findings and publications were shared among the author group to ensure updated information and citations list. Collectively, the group recognized that the avian microbiome field faces a complexity of issues that preclude a thorough understanding of host-microbe interactions in this animal group (Fig. 1).

A number of these challenges have been identified in publications elsewhere [6–8]. For example, it was unclear what diversity metrics of the microbiome are biologically meaningful, an issue that plagues most microbiome studies [13]. Additionally, it was largely recognized that much of our functional understanding of avian immunology and host-microbe interactions comes from a restricted number of species, largely domestic poultry [14], where there has been significant progress made in understanding the implications of methodological variation on biological understanding [15, 16]. However, poultry species represent only a fraction of the diversity of avian life, which encompasses over 11,000 extant species. Thus, there remains great interest in how aspects of avian life history interact with microbial ecology, and to what extent the avian microbiome plays a functional role in avian physiology, ecology, and evolution, especially in wild bird species. Consequently, our conversations in 2021 often converged on the need for research towards understanding the functional role of microbiomes in contributing to life history traits across the avian species [6]. However, another area of importance that was highlighted in our 2021 discussions, and in follow-up virtual discussions and emails were methodological challenges that require attention and are the focus of this article. While these methodological hurdles have been addressed in poultry systems [15, 16], they remain a challenge the majority of wild avian taxa.

We also note that most of our discussion revolves around the gut microbiome of wild birds, as this is the most common community studied in host-microbe interactions [2]. Communities associated with other body sites, such as the skin [17], eyes [18], feathers [19], respiratory tract [20], reproductive tract [21], eggs [22], uropygial gland [23], and external sites, such as the eggshell [24] or nest [25] warrant further study from both conceptual and methodological standpoints. Additionally, while the term ‘microbiome’ refers to a diverse community of microorganisms, most avian microbiome research to date has focused on associated bacterial communities. The research field would benefit by better understanding the diversity of viruses [26, 27], fungi [28–30], protozoa [31], and archaea [32, 33] across avian taxa to accumulate a collective understanding of these interactions across hosts. Therefore, future research efforts should expand to additional body sites and microbial taxa to better understand which interactions are most important for the diverse ecological contexts and evolutionary trajectories occupied by birds.

Interestingly, many of the methodological challenges our group identified were widely familiar across avian microbiome researchers but not presented explicitly in all peer-reviewed papers. Further, a number of established avian microbiome researchers shared stories of offering support, guidance, or validation to those working with avian samples for the first time and newly facing the methodological challenges we describe below (especially those regarding DNA extraction from fecal samples). But how many other researchers have abandoned their newly developing avian microbiome projects due to these methodological difficulties? Here, we aim to increase transparency and accessibility to the field for newcomers and junior scientists.

Methodological challenges shared with other microbiome subfields

Many methodological challenges faced by the avian microbiome field are not necessarily unique, as the technical approaches used to characterize the microbiome have limitations and caveats. For example, the widely-used methodology of 16S rRNA gene sequencing to characterize bacterial communities provides taxonomic insights, though results can vary depending on the primer set used [35]. Further, this technique does not discriminate between live and dead bacteria, transient bacteria from resident bacteria, and does not provide information regarding microbial density or absolute abundance [36]. Additionally, the various statistical methods used for testing differential abundances of taxa can offer contrasting results, making interpretation more challenging [37]. Metagenomic sequencing may be used to inventory the functional capabilities of a microbial community [38, 39]. However, only a small subset of these sequence reads can be reliably assigned to function [36]. To enhance our understanding of microbial functions, a call has been made for the return of classic microbiological approaches, such as culturing and characterization of microbial isolates [40]. However, host-associated bacteria from the gut and other body sites often have complex nutritional and environmental requirements for cultivation, sometimes requiring the design of new growth media [41]. Collectively, the avian microbiome field shares these methodological challenges with other microbiome subfields. However, avian microbiome research also faces distinct challenges, such as complexities of experimental design, and in particular, issues of low DNA yields from avian feces [42]. These challenges may require specific attention to understand the potential assumptions and caveats associated with low biomass samples [43].

Sample types and access

All microbiome studies include critical decisions on the process and timing of sample collection, and these decisions can have large implications for downstream results and conclusions. Studies involving avian hosts may present unique challenges regarding sample collection and storage. For example, many avian species are protected by layers of federal, state, and local laws to protect populations, such as the Birds Directive in the European Union, NOM-059 in Mexico, or the international Migratory Bird Treaty Act [44, 45]. Due to limitations in lethal collection, researchers often collect opportunistic or non-lethal samples such as feces [46–48] or cloacal swabs [49, 50]. Bacterial community inventories from feces or cloacal swabs do not capture the entire microbiome [51–53], but are largely reliable proxies of the hindgut microbiome [51, 54–56]. Additionally, gut microbiome inventories can be acquired from carcasses of recently deceased birds, such as specimens collected from avian collision events [57, 58]. Experimental work shows that in birds, microbiomes of intestinal sections converge during the first 24 h and remain thereafter relatively stable for up to 72 h postmortem [59], which is comparable to results of the abdominal cavity microbiome in small mammals [60]. Finally, there may be opportunity to investigate the microbiome from museum specimens stored in fluid along with gut contents [49, 61], though efforts should be made to understand the impacts of contamination and DNA degradation in these samples [62].

Other research opportunities may be presented by following methodologies developed in the human and primate microbiome field, where invasive sample collection is similarly difficult due to access and protections. In fact, it is often through fecal samples that we have greatly expanded our understanding of host-microbe interactions in humans [63]. Novel experimental approaches in mammals have been developed to assay the transcriptomic profiles of host tissues through sequencing the cells present in fecal material [64], allowing inventories of host epithelial gene expression from non-invasive samples. Additionally, ingestible devices developed for sampling small intestinal microbial communities of humans [65, 66] could be used to collect internal samples from larger avian species. Finally, cultured cell lines and organoid systems allow for ex vivo approaches to understand host-microbe interactions [67, 68], and could be applied towards a diversity of avian systems [69, 70]. Such approaches will propel our understanding of avian host-microbe interactions even in the face of difficulty acquiring samples.

Research settings and taxonomic diversity

Valuable and integrative information will come from interrogating the dynamics of avian microbiomes under both laboratory and field settings [71]. Studies have used oral antibiotics, commercial probiotics, or gut-derived microbial strains isolated from the host species, to manipulate the gut microbiome of developing nestlings in the wild [72–75]. The use of antibiotics can be difficult to biologically interpret, given that these compounds have complex and off-target effects beyond the microbiota [76]. Other approaches have been taken to disentangle the effects of host genetics and environment on the microbiome; these studies include: experimental cross-fostering of nestlings in the field [77], comparing the microbiome of brood parasites to their host siblings [78–81], and comparing the microbiome of migrating birds across multiple time points and locations [82, 83]. Finally, researchers have experimentally manipulated other factors, such as temperature [84], diet [85], food availability [86], parasitism [84, 87], parental efforts [88, 89], plumage coloration [90], and conservation management strategies [91], and then investigated downstream effects on microbiome composition. Research under natural settings should come with careful experimental design heavily based on life histories that guide sample collection and variable selection, to facilitate downstream statistical analysis and interpretation.

The development of more avian laboratory models will also advance our understanding of avian-microbe interactions would be the development of more experimental laboratory models. The use of model species, such as mice and rats, has greatly expanded our understanding of host-microbe interactions with applicability to wild mammal systems [92–95]. The development of sterile (i.e. germ-free) hosts in these systems offers even greater power to disentangle microbial contributions to host phenotypes [96]. In birds, most captive or experimental work related to the microbiome has been conducted in commercial and domesticated poultry [6], including the development and use of germ-free individuals that can be inoculated with specific microbial taxa to understand host-microbe interactions while controlling for confounding variables (e.g., environment and host genotype; [97, 98]). This work relates directly to human food productivity while also providing insights into physiological relationships between microbes and avian hosts in economically and culturally important species [99]. However, just as mice and rats are not perfect proxies for all mammals, a few avian species do not represent all birds [1]. To expand the diversity of hosts, studies have used captive songbirds [100–103], ostrich farms [104, 105], and conservation centers [106–109] to explore microbial dynamics in a diversity of captive avian hosts, which also allow for easier collection of repeated samples. Pending the development of additional germ-free avian species beyond domesticated poultry, researchers looking to investigate the avian microbiome in non-model species may pursue methods such as cross-fostering [100], administration of probiotics [75, 110, 111], antibiotics [23, 101], or microbiome transplants [112].

Given the large diversity (approximately 6,500 species and ~ 60% of all birds [113]) and imperiled status of many passerine songbird species [114], it would be valuable to establish a captive model within this order for understanding host-microbe interactions. Promising options may include Great Tits (Parus major; [115], House Sparrows (Passer domesticus; [101], or the widely used lab model of Zebra Finches (Taeniopygia guttata; [116]. Understanding host-microbe interactions in other imperiled groups, such as parrots and marine birds, may also be particularly important. Overall, merging experimental model systems with microbiome techniques across a diversity of host taxa will enhance our understanding of the dynamics and functions of the avian microbiome [1, 117], allowing for the advancement of management strategies for endangered and threatened birds [10, 11].

DNA extractions and yields

The most widespread and seemingly underreported issue associated with avian microbiome samples is the difficulty to obtain high-quality and high-concentration DNA from extractions [118–122], an issue that was widely reported amongst attendees to this roundtable. For example, DNA extracts from avian fecal samples often exhibit low-to-absent DNA concentrations and may even show absence of visible DNA bands on gels after PCR-amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene. A recent study comparing the utility of several commercial DNA extraction methods for use on fecal samples from Barn Swallows (Hirundo rustica) found wide ranges in recovered DNA concentrations from ~ 2.5 ng / µL for one kit, to roughly ~ 1300 ng / µL for another kit [123]. There may also be host taxonomic effects, as a study using the same extraction methods recovered different quantities of DNA across songbirds, pigeons, and flamingos [119]. The resulting sequenced communities are robust and tractable across species, such that differences in microbial community structure across avian taxonomy seem robust to extraction differences [119]. However, we still lack an understanding of how extraction yields might influence our understanding of microbial variation across individuals of a species or within individuals across time. Thus, a systematic analysis of the characteristics of a sample (host taxonomy, sample types, storage methods) and methods of extraction (commercial kits, incubation times, etc.) would inform what steps might offer the best route to increase DNA extraction yields.

However, from our roundtable discussions, numerous avian microbiome researchers reported that many published datasets include DNA extracts that were low-yield (< 20 ng/µL) or of questionable purity values (260/230 ratios < 1.5), yet services from sequencing facilities produced microbiome inventories that resemble gut communities and show significant differences according to experimental or ecological metadata. That being said, not all samples produce sufficient sequence return, and success here does not seem to be predictable based on DNA concentrations [122]. Further, approaches such as functional metagenomics are often impeded due to reliance on greater DNA concentrations for shotgun sequencing [39].

The specific reason(s) underlying the low DNA yields from avian samples remains unclear. Birds maintain smaller guts compared to non-flying mammals as a means to lessen the energetic costs of flight [4], which may translate to a lower total abundance of gut microbes. However, approaches such as flow cytometry and culturing methods demonstrate the presence of bacteria in the avian cloaca and excreta [106, 124–126], suggesting that DNA should be recoverable. It is often suggested that the high uric acid concentrations present in avian feces may inhibit DNA extraction and act as a PCR inhibitor [118, 127, 128], though this idea lacks empirical evidence. While birds indeed produce uric acid physiologically, it seems this compound is modified prior to excretion, as analysis of avian feces found ammonium urate, not uric acid [129]. One study attempted to remove urea by placing coffee filters in songbird holding bags, so that liquid urate would absorb off fecal samples into the filter prior to collection [130], though the impact of these methodological adjustments on DNA yield has not been systematically evaluated.

Another explanation for low DNA yields is a potentially high activity of DNA-degrading enzymes present in avian feces, which may act to degrade nucleotides. A study in Capercallie (Tetrao urogallus) found high activities of DNase in the feces of these birds, and found that DNase activities in the feces were correlated with DNA quality of the sample [131]. This study also suggested that these DNase enzymes were free in solution, as filtered samples retained higher DNA quality [131]. Another study investigating the use of avian feces for molecular prey inventories found that DNA yields from fecal samples varied depending on exposure to sunlight and rain, indicative of DNA degradation [132]. In conclusion, the authors suggest rapid collection and preservation of samples to prevent degradation [131, 132]. However, even fecal samples collected from captive birds and frozen immediately can result in low DNA extraction yields [118].

Therefore, the low DNA yields from avian fecal samples may be caused by the degradation of a significant portion of microbial cells or DNA upon defecation. The technical implications of this could be quite high, if degradation processes result in biased community structures in feces compared to those residing in the hindgut, which is observed in both birds and mammals [51, 54, 133, 134]. However, the particularly low DNA yields from avian feces can preclude metagenomic approaches which rely on greater DNA concentrations [39]. Further, microbial DNA is ubiquitous, including in commercial DNA extraction kits, causing the potential for contaminant DNA to be amplified and incorrectly identified as members of the environmental microbiome of interest [135, 136]. This risk is relatively low for samples that often contain high densities of microbial DNA, such as mammalian feces, since the contaminant DNA is in such low abundance compared to the DNA that is genuinely endogenous to the sample. However, for samples such as swabs, blood, submucosa, or avian feces, there may be large potential for contaminant DNA to impact results and conclusions [135, 136].

Thus, it may be especially important for the field to quantify the microbial biomass of avian microbiome samples, their susceptibility to contamination, and the technical or biological mechanisms for low DNA yields. There are various approaches to measuring or controlling for microbial densities or absolute abundances, including culturing for counts of viable bacteria [102], flow cytometry [106], quantitative PCR of DNA extracts [137], spike-in dilutions [138, 139], or use of spike-in controls [140]. Gathering information regarding microbial densities will better contextualize avian microbiome research to date and comparisons across avian taxa and may inspire new questions related to host-microbe interactions. Host reliance on microbes may lie on a spectrum, from deep, evolutionary interactions (e.g., ruminants and the foregut fermenting hoatzin; [141]), to loose associations (e.g., carnivorous mammals or some caterpillars; [142]). The smaller gut size and low microbial densities have been proposed as weight-saving adaptations towards flight, which may lead to more passive host-microbe interactions, resulting in the relatively stochastic community assemblies typically seen in birds and bats [143]. But to date, some of these conclusions could be dependent on methodological limitations. Better knowledge regarding the validity and limitations of our methods will yield greater clarity in our understanding of the avian microbiome and the nature of these host-microbe interactions.

Recommendations

General recommendations for the study of avian-microbe interactions have been published elsewhere [42]. Here, we focus on methodological practices that can overcome the challenges associated with low biomass samples [43]. For example, contamination can be reduced through aseptic techniques, and identified through the sequencing of negative controls – both experimental (such as empty collection bags [144]), and technical negative controls, such as sequencing DNA from blank extractions [43]. Additionally, mock communities of known identities can be included in extractions and sequenced as positive controls [145]. The generation and widespread use of mock communities of known avian symbionts would increase reproducibility, as has been proposed for understanding host-microbe interactions in poultry [15]. Computational approaches have been developed to remove sequences identified as contaminants from communities of interest [146, 147], though some of these could also represent symbionts [50]. Thus, for transparency and repeatability, it is advisable to include various types of controls in future studies of the avian microbiota and report the identity and removal of any potential contaminants.

Relatedly, it may be important to understand how storage methods may differentially impact avian samples compared to high biomass samples, such as samples of mammalian feces. Several studies have compared how different storage methods (freezing, RNAlater, etc.) may introduce slight shifts in the recovered microbiome communities of mammalian samples [148, 149]. Fortunately though, individual signatures or “fingerprints” of the mammalian gut microbiome are well-maintained and stronger than the changes induced by storage methods [150]. Similarly, microbial signatures of different avian species are well retained across different DNA storage and extraction methods [119]. But, given the low DNA extraction yields and potentially low biomass of avian samples, it is prudent to understand whether individual variation is also maintained across storage and extraction methods. Currently, a general recommendation is consistency in collection methods across samples within a study [151, 152]. Simultaneously, it is important to highlight that most of our current knowledge on avian microbiomes relies on a constantly growing but still modest representation of all the avian lineages and life histories.

Finally, we propose enhanced communication as a means to overcome these methodological challenges facing the avian microbiome field. For example, regardless of the steps taken to collect and prepare samples, we urge greater transparency in reporting methods and relevant methodological results (such as mass of extracted samples, DNA yields, average number of sequences retrieved, and rarefaction depths) to facilitate future meta-analyses and for readers to be able to contextualize results. For example, some studies incorporate data on time between sample collection and freezing, and or until extraction [153–155]. Calls for authors to increase reporting of experimental approaches and methods as a means to assess the potential contributions of contamination are consistent with recommended best practices for low-biomass samples [43]. Additionally, greater networking and communication among members of the avian microbiome field will reduce duplication of efforts and expedite progress. The stories, frustrations, and methodological challenges shared at the virtual roundtable which resulted in this publication highlighted the need for researchers to share methods and troubleshoot results. There have been calls for open science and interactive virtual networks in the animal microbiome field, especially for the benefit of early-career researchers [156, 157]. Addressing these methodological challenges head-on and in a collaborative fashion will be required to gain an understanding of these dynamic microbial systems.
