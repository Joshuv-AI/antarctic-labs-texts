Study on the influence of steel reinforcement on the bonding performance and crack width of UHPC-NSC interface
Authors: Haonian Li, Xiaokun Ren, Bo Yang, Nan Jiang, Fuhai Li, Huiqi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) exhibits favorable bonding performance with normal-strength concrete (NSC) and provides a potential solution for improving the durability of wet-joint interfaces. In this study, uniaxial tensile tests were conducted to investigate the influence of steel fiber content on the bonding performance of UHPC–NSC specimens. Subsequently, the mechanism by which rebar restrains interface crack development in UHPC–NSC specimens was examined through uniaxial tensile tests and four-point bending tests. The results show that the failure characteristics and stress–strain responses of unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens were essentially independent of steel fiber content. With increasing steel fiber content, the uniaxial tensile strength of UHPC specimens increased approximately linearly, whereas that of unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens first increased and then decreased, reaching a maximum at a steel fiber content of 2.0%. The bonding performance of the UHPC–NSC interface with 2.0% steel fiber content was superior to that of the NSC–NSC interface. For reinforced UHPC–NSC specimens with 2.0% steel fiber content, two failure modes were observed: rebar debonding failure and UHPC matrix failure. Increasing the rebar diameter and anchorage length, as well as replacing smooth round rebar with ribbed rebar, improved the uniaxial tensile strength of UHPC–NSC specimens and reduced the interface crack width growth rate.

Introduction

Precast concrete bridge components, such as bridge decks and T-shaped girders, are widely used in medium- and small-span bridges, which account for approximately 80% of bridge structures, owing to their advantages of rapid construction, relatively low cost, and reduced environmental impact1–3. Precast components are commonly connected by cast-in-place wet joints to form an integral structural system. This connection method has relatively low requirements for dimensional accuracy and offers good construction adaptability. However, field observations have shown that wet joints are often the weak regions in precast bridge structures and are prone to cracking, interfacial debonding, water leakage, and local fracture4–7. Once damage occurs at the wet joint before the adjacent precast components are fully mobilized, the load-transfer capacity and structural integrity between components are weakened, thereby affecting the durability and service safety of the bridge8. Therefore, improving the interfacial bonding performance and crack-control capacity of wet joints is essential for enhancing the long-term service performance of precast concrete bridges.

The relatively weak bonding performance of conventional normal-strength concrete wet joints is mainly associated with the characteristics of the new-to-old concrete interface. During casting, the existing concrete substrate may absorb water from the newly cast concrete, resulting in the formation of a more porous interfacial transition zone, which adversely affects interfacial bonding performance9,10. In addition, coarse aggregates in normal concrete may hinder sufficient filling of the interfacial region by cement paste, thereby reducing interfacial compactness and mechanical interlocking11.

Various methods have been adopted in engineering practice to improve the bonding performance of wet joints, including transverse steel-plate bonding, bridge deck overlay strengthening, chemical grouting, external transverse prestressing, and the use of high-performance cement-based materials12,13. Among these approaches, ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC) is considered a promising wet-joint material because of its low water-to-binder ratio, high flowability, dense microstructure, excellent mechanical properties, and superior durability14,15. Compared with normal-strength concrete, UHPC can more effectively fill the interfacial region and improve the compactness of the UHPC–NSC interface, thereby enhancing the bonding performance between the wet joint and the existing concrete substrate. In addition, the high strength and durability of UHPC can extend the service life of wet-joint structures and reduce maintenance demand. Recent studies on cementless and alkali-activated UHPC have provided potential routes for reducing cement consumption and carbon emissions, and data-driven strength prediction methods may further support the mixture optimization of low-carbon UHPC systems16,17.

Steel fibers are a key component governing the tensile performance, flexural behavior, and post-cracking response of UHPC. Previous studies have shown that the shape, aspect ratio, orientation distribution, and volume fraction of steel fibers significantly affect the crack-bridging capacity, tensile performance, flexural toughness, and energy dissipation capacity of ultra-high-performance fiber-reinforced cementitious materials18–21. Yoo et al.21 investigated the effects of fiber shape, aspect ratio, and volume fraction on the flexural behavior of UHP-FRCC and reported that fiber geometry and volume fraction markedly influence post-cracking load-bearing capacity and flexural performance. Recent studies on fiber pullout behavior and fiber–matrix interfacial bonding mechanisms in UHPC further indicate that steel fibers not only improve the strength of the UHPC matrix but also play an important role in crack control, energy dissipation, and post-cracking load transfer22.

In reinforced UHPC members, steel fiber content and rebar configuration jointly affect cracking behavior and load-bearing performance. Feng et al.23 investigated the flexural and cracking behavior of reinforced UHPC beams with different reinforcement ratios and steel fiber contents, and showed that both steel fiber content and reinforcement ratio significantly influence flexural capacity, crack spacing, and crack-width development. These findings indicate that the role of steel fibers should be considered not only at the material level but also in relation to reinforcement-assisted crack control and load transfer in UHPC-based joint systems.

However, compared with the effect of steel fibers on the mechanical properties of the UHPC matrix, their influence on the bonding performance of the UHPC–NSC interface remains insufficiently understood. In particular, whether increasing steel fiber content can continuously improve the bonding performance of the UHPC–NSC interface is still unclear24. On the one hand, an appropriate steel fiber content may reduce the risk of shrinkage-induced cracking in UHPC and improve the crack resistance of the UHPC-side matrix. On the other hand, excessive steel fiber content may reduce the flowability of the UHPC mixture, disturb interfacial compactness, and induce local defects near the interface, thereby impairing the effective bonding between UHPC and the NSC substrate. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate the relationship between steel fiber content and the interfacial tensile performance of UHPC–NSC specimens, and to determine a reasonable steel fiber content suitable for interfacial bonding.

In addition to steel fibers, rebar configuration parameters are also important factors affecting the mechanical performance of UHPC wet joints. In precast bridge wet joints, straight rebar lap connections anchored in UHPC can reduce on-site welding, shorten anchorage length, decrease wet-joint width, and improve construction efficiency25. Existing studies have mainly focused on the bond performance between rebar and UHPC or the overall mechanical behavior of reinforced UHPC members15,23,26–28. However, systematic studies on the effects of rebar diameter, rebar type, and anchorage length on the interfacial tensile performance, failure mode, and interface crack-width development of UHPC–NSC specimens remain limited. Furthermore, the distinctions among UHPC–NSC interfacial failure, rebar debonding failure, and UHPC matrix failure, as well as their corresponding load-transfer mechanisms, require further clarification.

Based on the above research gaps, this study investigates the bonding performance and crack-propagation behavior of UHPC–NSC interfaces under different influencing factors. Uniaxial tensile tests were conducted to examine the effect of steel fiber content on the bonding performance between UHPC and NSC and to determine the optimum steel fiber content. Splitting tensile tests and four-point bending tests were then performed to compare the bonding performance between UHPC and NSC at the optimum steel fiber content. Finally, uniaxial tensile tests on reinforced UHPC–NSC specimens were carried out to investigate the effects of rebar diameter, rebar type, and anchorage length on the bonding performance between UHPC and NSC. Combined with crack-monitoring measurements, the restraining effect and mechanism of rebar on interface crack development were further analyzed.

Experimental design

In this study, based on 18 UHPC specimens and 18 unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens, the influence of steel fiber content on the bonding performance of UHPC-NSC interface was investigated through uniaxial tensile tests, and the optimal steel fiber content for the bonding performance of UHPC-NSC specimens was determined. Furthermore, splitting tensile tests and four-point bending tests were conducted to verify whether UHPC, as a wet joint material, exhibits superior bonding performance compared to NSC. Based on 54 UHPC-NSC specimens with reinforcement, the influence of rebar diameter, rebar type, and rebar anchorage length on the bonding performance of UHPC-NSC specimens was investigated through uniaxial tensile tests. Additionally, by combining concrete crack observation instruments, the development of interface cracks in UHPC-NSC specimens was observed and analyzed to reveal the mechanism of the rebar effect on the crack resistance performance of the UHPC-NSC interface.

The NSC specimens used in this experiment were prepared with a strength grade of C65, a water-to-binder ratio of 0.29, and a 28-day compressive strength test value of 75.1 MPa. This strength value is higher than that of many conventional precast bridge deck substrates or ordinary structural concretes. Therefore, the NSC used in this study should be regarded as a high-strength concrete substrate rather than a typical low- or medium-strength bridge deck substrate. The purpose of using this substrate strength was to reduce the potential interference caused by premature failure or local crushing of the NSC portion, thereby allowing the effects of the UHPC–NSC interface, steel fiber content, and rebar parameters to be examined more clearly. Microscopic morphology characterization

To further examine the local microstructural characteristics associated with interfacial failure, SEM observations were conducted on the fractured regions near the UHPC–NSC interface. Unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens with steel fiber contents of 1.5%, 2.0%, and 2.5% were selected for observation, corresponding to the increasing, peak, and decreasing stages of the interfacial tensile strength response with increasing steel fiber content. ImageJ was used to process the SEM images and quantify the apparent pore/void area fraction of the selected regions through threshold-based image segmentation31.

Results and discussion

Uniaxial tensile tests of UHPC and unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens

Failure modes

The failure mode of the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimen is shown in Fig. 7a. Prior to reaching the ultimate load, the specimen did not produce any noticeable sound. As the load continued to increase, the specimen suddenly produced a “bang” sound, and the UHPC and NSC portions rapidly separated from the interface, indicating that the specimen had reached its ultimate load. The failure mode exhibited clear characteristics of brittle failure. Furthermore, no cracks were observed in either the UHPC or NSC portion after failure, indicating that regardless of the variation in steel fiber content within the UHPC matrix, the weakest point of the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimen remained at the interface. By comparing the interface after failure of the UHPC-NSC specimen with a steel fiber content of 2.0% and 0% (shown in Fig. 8), it can be observed that the UHPC interface of the specimen without steel fibers was relatively smooth, with only a few aggregates from the NSC being pulled off and adhering to the UHPC interface due to bonding forces. In contrast, the interface of the UHPC-NSC specimen with steel fibers contained more NSC aggregates and cementitious slurry, showing a significant intermingling between UHPC and NSC. It should be noted that although some steel fibers remain on the NSC interface in Fig. 8a, the steel fibers did not penetrate into the NSC matrix, indicating that the steel fibers at the UHPC–NSC interface did not exhibit a direct bridging effect.

Figure 7b shows the failure mode of the UHPC specimen. Without the addition of steel fibers, the failure mode of the UHPC specimen is consistent with the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimen. They both exhibited immediate cracking and fracture, with no observable UHPC spalling around the fracture surface. With the addition of steel fibers, the behavior of the UHPC specimen prior to reaching the peak load was similar to the specimen without steel fibers. Upon reaching the peak load, a muffled sound was heard and a prominent primary crack was observed on the surface of the specimen, but the specimen remained intact at this point. Subsequently, the load began to decrease slowly, and the primary crack widened continuously, accompanied by the “snapping” sound of steel fibers being pulled out. Even after the load decreased and stabilized, the specimen remained unbroken, demonstrating typical ductile failure characteristics. Examination of the primary crack after failure revealed that numerous steel fibers were pulled out at the crack, indicating that the steel fibers restrict the development of the primary crack through the “bridging effect.” This is the main reason for the transformation of UHPC from brittle failure to ductile failure32.

Uniaxial tensile strength

Figure 9a shows the variation in tensile strength of UHPC specimens with different steel fiber contents. The tensile strength of the UHPC specimens increased approximately linearly with increasing steel fiber content. Compared with the UHPC specimen without steel fibers, the specimens with steel fiber contents of 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5%, 2.0%, and 2.5% exhibited increases in tensile strength of 9.80%, 17.64%, 25.19%, 32.13%, and 37.25%, respectively. The standard deviations of the specimens in each group ranged from 0.15 to 0.27 MPa, indicating relatively low scatter in the tensile strength results of the UHPC specimens and a stable increasing trend in tensile strength with increasing steel fiber content. This improvement can be mainly attributed to the ability of steel fibers to bridge microcracks within the UHPC matrix and dissipate energy through slip, debonding, and pullout processes, thereby delaying the formation and propagation of dominant cracks22. As the steel fiber content increased, the fiber network within the matrix became denser, and more fibers participated in crack bridging and energy dissipation. Consequently, the tensile strength of the UHPC specimens increased continuously.

Figure 9b presents the variation in tensile strength of the unreinforced UHPC–NSC composite specimens with different steel fiber contents. Unlike the intact UHPC specimens, the tensile strength of the unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens first increased and then decreased with increasing steel fiber content. When the steel fiber content increased from 0% to 2.0%, the average tensile strength increased from 2.44 MPa to 2.90 MPa, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 18.9%. When the steel fiber content further increased to 2.5%, the average tensile strength decreased to 2.82 MPa, which was 2.76% lower than that of the UN-2.0 specimen. The standard deviations of the different groups ranged from 0.07 to 0.09 MPa, indicating relatively low scatter in the interfacial tensile strength results. To evaluate the statistical reliability of this non-monotonic trend, Welch’s t-test was performed for adjacent steel fiber content groups of the unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens. Welch’s t-test is suitable for comparing the means of independent samples without assuming equal variances, and is therefore appropriate for the present small-sample dataset in which variance homogeneity could not be reliably verified33,34. The results showed that, except for the difference between the UN-0 and UN-0.5 groups, the differences between the other adjacent fiber-content groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Therefore, under the present experimental conditions, 2.0% can be regarded as the preferable steel fiber content corresponding to the interfacial tensile performance of the unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens.

The strengthening mechanism of steel fibers in unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens differs from that in intact UHPC specimens. For intact UHPC specimens, steel fibers mainly improve tensile performance through crack bridging within the UHPC matrix. However, as indicated by the interfacial failure observations in Fig. 8, the unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens primarily failed along the UHPC–NSC interface, and no effective direct fiber bridging across the interface was formed. Owing to the difference in casting time between UHPC and NSC, differential shrinkage may generate additional tensile stress near the interface, which is one of the main factors responsible for the deterioration of interfacial bond performance35. The incorporation of an appropriate amount of steel fibers can enhance the crack resistance of the UHPC-side matrix and restrain UHPC shrinkage deformation, thereby reducing the differential shrinkage between UHPC and NSC and the resulting interfacial stress concentration. Therefore, the increase in the interfacial tensile strength of unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens is more likely attributed to the indirect improvement of the UHPC-side matrix properties and interfacial deformation compatibility, rather than to direct fiber bridging across the UHPC–NSC interface22,36. However, when the steel fiber content exceeded 2.0%, the average tensile strength slightly decreased, which may be related to the reduced flowability and less uniform fiber dispersion of the UHPC mixture at high steel fiber contents, which could increase the probability of local defects or voids near the interface and weaken the effective UHPC–NSC bond22,37.

Microscopic morphology

Figure 10 shows the 2000× SEM images of fractured regions near the UHPC–NSC interface for specimens with steel fiber contents of 1.5%, 2.0%, and 2.5%. To further compare the local microstructural characteristics, the apparent pore/void area fraction was evaluated from the SEM images using ImageJ31, and the results are summarized in Fig. 11.

The specimen with 1.5% steel fibers exhibited a relatively compact microstructure, although several visible pores and local defects were still observed. When the steel fiber content increased to 2.0%, the fractured region appeared denser, and the apparent pore/void area fraction decreased from 4.48% to 3.05%, indicating improved local compactness near the fractured region. This observation is consistent with the highest average interfacial tensile strength obtained for the UN-2.0 specimens.

When the steel fiber content further increased to 2.5%, the apparent pore/void area fraction increased to 9.10%, and more visible voids and discontinuous regions were observed. This trend suggests that excessive steel fiber content may reduce the local compactness of the fractured region and weaken the effective bond between UHPC and NSC, thereby contributing to the decrease in tensile strength of the UN-2.5 specimens.

Tensile strength ratio

Figure 12 shows the variation of the tensile strength ratio between the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens and the UHPC specimens with different steel fiber contents. It can be observed that at the same steel fiber content, the tensile strength of the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens was significantly lower than that of the UHPC specimens. The maximum tensile strength ratio was only 0.368, indicating a sharp decrease in tensile strength due to the presence of the bond interface. Furthermore, it can be noted that the tensile strength ratio decreased with an increase in steel fiber content, and the improvement in tensile strength of the UHPC specimens due to steel fibers is more significant compared to the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens.This is mainly because steel fibers lack a direct fiber-bridging effect across the UHPC-NSC interface to enhance bond strength. Furthermore, their addition may cause localized interfacial damage, thereby limiting the net gain in bonding performance38. As a result, the increase in tensile strength of the specimens is relatively small, leading to a decrease in the tensile strength ratio.

Stress-strain curve

The stress-strain curve of the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens is shown in Fig. 13a. It can be observed that before reaching the peak stress, the stress-strain relationship of the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens exhibited an approximate linear growth trend, indicating that the tensile stiffness of the specimens remained relatively constant. As the peak stress is approached, the stress-strain relationship transformed into a curved ascending state, and the tangent slope of curve decreases continuously, indicating the gradual initiation and rapid propagation of microcracks within the UHPC-NSC specimens. After reaching the peak stress, the strain remained nearly constant while the stress experiences a sudden drop, eventually leading to failure at the bond interface. This is mainly because the tensile strength of the unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens relies on the bond strength between UHPC and NSC. Thus, when cracks appear at the interface, the bond strength rapidly deteriorates, causing a rapid decrease in stress and ultimately leading to specimen failure.

The stress-strain curve of the intact UHPC specimens is depicted in Fig. 13b. It can be observed that for the unreinforced UHPC specimens without steel fibers, the stress-strain behavior is similar to that of the UHPC-NSC specimens. After the initial crack, the stress decreases rapidly with increasing strain, exhibiting strain-softening characteristics, indicating that the specimens do not show plastic deformation capacity. For the UHPC specimens with steel fiber contents of 0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5%, the stress does not exhibit a sharp drop after reaching the peak stress. Instead, it gradually decreases with increasing strain, and the decreasing rate becomes slower with higher steel fiber contents. Therefore, when the steel fiber content is relatively low, the steel fibers bridging on both sides of the main crack can continue to bear the load with increasing strain, providing the UHPC specimens with partial plastic deformation capacity39. However, the UHPC specimens still exhibit stress-softening behavior. For the UHPC specimens with a steel fiber content of 2.0%, the stress shows a slight drop after the initial crack, followed by a slow rise with increasing strain. In contrast, the stress of the UHPC specimens with a steel fiber content of 2.5% does not exhibit a decreasing trend after crack initiation but continues to gradually increase. After reaching the peak stress, the stress of both types of specimens remains relatively constant with increasing strain for a certain period, demonstrating typical low-strain hardening behavior.

Therefore, under the present experimental conditions, the 2.0% steel fiber content corresponded to the highest average interfacial tensile strength of the unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens and was selected for subsequent bonding tests.

Splitting tensile test and four-point bending test of UHPC-NSC specimens

Figure 14 presents the splitting tensile strength and four-point bending strength of the NSC–NSC and UHPC–NSC bonded specimens, as well as the intact NSC and UHPC specimens, at different curing ages. The error bars represent ± 1 standard deviation. Although a certain degree of scatter was observed in the bonded specimens, the main strength development trends remained consistent at different curing ages. Therefore, the following discussion is mainly based on the mean strength trends, together with the error bars, failure characteristics, and interfacial mechanical mechanisms.

As shown in Fig. 14a, at curing ages of 3, 7, and 28 days, the splitting tensile strength of the UHPC–NSC specimens was 12.6%, 18.9%, and 18.4% higher than that of the NSC–NSC specimens, respectively. Similarly, the four-point bending strength increased by 24.1%, 20.9%, and 15.9%, respectively. These results indicate that, at the same curing age, the UHPC–NSC bonded specimens exhibited superior interfacial bonding performance under both splitting tensile and flexural loading compared with the NSC–NSC specimens.

This improvement can be attributed to the low water-to-binder ratio of UHPC and the absence of coarse aggregates, which enhance the compactness of the UHPC–NSC interfacial transition zone and consequently improve the bond strength relative to the NSC–NSC interface40,41. In addition, the UHPC–NSC interface is expected to contain a higher amount of C–S–H gel than the NSC–NSC interface42. The increased formation of C–S–H gel may reduce the effective thickness of the weak interfacial region and improve the pore structure near the interface, thereby further contributing to the enhancement of interfacial bond strength.

However, at the same curing age, both the splitting tensile strength and four-point bending strength of the UHPC–NSC specimens remained lower than those of the intact NSC specimens. The splitting tensile strength of the UHPC–NSC specimens reached only 67%, 74%, and 76% of that of the intact NSC specimens at 3, 7, and 28 days, respectively, while the corresponding four-point bending strength reached only 60%, 58%, and 56%. These results indicate that UHPC improves the bond performance of the wet joint, but the UHPC–NSC interface remains the strength-controlling region. Therefore, the advantage of UHPC should be interpreted as an improvement in interfacial load transfer rather than a complete elimination of interface weakness.

From the perspective of curing-age development, the specimens exhibited the highest strength growth rate within the first 3 days of curing, and most of the strength development was completed within 7 days. This indicates that both matrix strength and interfacial bond performance developed rapidly at early ages. The relatively high early-age strength of the UHPC–NSC specimens suggests that UHPC, when used as a wet-joint material, can shorten the strength development period of the joint region, which is beneficial for rapid construction and early service of precast bridge wet joints.

Uniaxial tensile tests of reinforced UHPC-NSC specimens

Failure modes

Figure 15 shows two types of failure modes (rebar debonding failure and UHPC matrix failure) observed in the reinforced UHPC-NSC specimens in uniaxial tensile tests. H8D2, H8D4, H10D2, H10D3, C10D2, C10D4, C10D6, H12D2, and H14D2 specimens experienced rebar debonding failure in UHPC-NSC, as shown in Fig. 15a. Taking the H10D2 specimen as an example, during the loading process, no noticeable sound was heard until the tensile load abruptly stopped increasing or decreased slightly in a short period. At this point, microcracks were observed on the UHPC-NSC interface, but the specimen did not immediately fail. This is mainly because the bond strength between the rebar and UHPC is still able to carry the load. As the load further increases, the microcracks gradually extended and formed visible macrocracks along the UHPC-NSC interface. When the load exceeded the ultimate bond stress between the rebar and UHPC, relative slippage and a gradual decay of bond stress occurred, resulting in a significant increase in the widening rate of macrocracks. When the load gradually decreased and stabilized, the rebar completely disengaged from the UHPC, and the UHPC-NSC specimen lost all its load-bearing capacity. The main crack after failure was located at the bond interface, and no cracks were observed in other parts of the specimen. This indicates that the rebar configurations used in these specimens did not effectively shift the governing failure region from the UHPC–NSC interface to the UHPC matrix.

The remaining reinforced UHPC–NSC specimens exhibited UHPC matrix failure, as shown in Fig. 15b–d. Compared to the specimens with rebar debonding failure, the specimens with UHPC matrix failure exhibited slower propagation of microcracks to macrocracks at the bond interface (discussed in subsequent chapters). When the specimens were loaded to the peak load, a distinct crack appeared on the UHPC matrix and rapidly developed into the main crack. The crack width at the interface abruptly decreased, indicating a shift in the load-bearing region from the bond interface to the UHPC matrix. Subsequently, the load began to slowly decrease, and the main crack emitted a continuous “snapping” sound as steel fibers are pulled out, ultimately leading to specimen failure. Comparing the locations of the main crack in UHPC-NSC specimens with different rebar configurations, it is observed that the main crack precisely occurred at the anchor end of the rebar within the UHPC matrix. This indicated that the weakest region of the specimen shifted from the UHPC-NSC bond interface to the interface at the anchor end of the rebar. At this point, the governing failure region had shifted from the UHPC–NSC interface to the UHPC matrix near the rebar anchorage end.

Uniaxial tensile strength

For reinforced UHPC–NSC specimens, the reported tensile strength represents the nominal tensile capacity of the whole reinforced bonded specimen rather than the pure UHPC–NSC interfacial bond strength, because the peak load may be governed by either rebar–UHPC debonding or UHPC matrix cracking after interfacial cracking. As shown in Fig. 16, it can be observed that because the rebar–UHPC anchorage continued to transfer load after cracking of the UHPC–NSC interface, the uniaxial tensile strength of specimens with rebar debonding failure was significantly higher than that of unreinforced UHPC-NSC specimens with the same steel fiber content. The overall improvement ranged from approximately 13.8% to 98.6%. The uniaxial tensile strength of specimens with UHPC matrix failure was generally higher than that of specimens with rebar debonding failure under the same conditions (same steel type and diameter). On average, the uniaxial tensile strength increased by 55.8%. It should be noted that the uniaxial tensile strength of specimens with UHPC matrix failure is not equivalent to the U-2.0 specimen with the same steel fiber content. Compared to the uniaxial tensile strength of 8.76 MPa for the U-2.0 specimen, the uniaxial tensile strength of specimens with UHPC matrix failure decreased to varying degrees depending on rebar diameter and anchorage length, with the maximum decrease reaching 30.7%.

Fig. 16Uniaxial tensile strength of reinforced UHPC-NSC specimens: (a) specimens with rebar debonding failure; (b) specimens with UHPC matrix failure.

Uniaxial tensile strength of reinforced UHPC-NSC specimens: (a) specimens with rebar debonding failure; (b) specimens with UHPC matrix failure.

Figure 17 illustrates the crack initiation and development mechanism at the ribbed rebar–UHPC interface under tensile loading. It can be observed that after the cracking of the UHPC-NSC interface, the bond strength between the rebar and the UHPC interface primarily comes from the mechanical interlocking force between the rebar ribs and the UHPC, which is represented by the oblique compressive force in Fig. 17. The UHPC located between the ribs is subjected to axial component force due to the oblique compression force. Due to the extremely high mechanical performance of UHPC, the damage caused by the axial component force to the UHPC can be considered negligible. The UHPC surrounding the ribs experiences radial component force due to the oblique compression force. When the tensile strength of UHPC is lower than the radial component force, microcracks will develop internally in the UHPC and eventually propagate into macroscopic cracks. Therefore, the circumferential tensile stress generated by the rebar ribs causes premature microcracking in the UHPC. Moreover, compared to the unreinforced area of the UHPC matrix, the presence of the rebar reduces the fiber content at the end interface of reinforcement anchorage, and thus weakens the crack resistance. Consequently, the uniaxial tensile strength of specimens with UHPC matrix failure decreased compared with the U-2.0 specimen. Additionally, different rebar anchorage lengths and diameters have varying effects on the circumferential tensile stress within the UHPC. This leads to inconsistent tensile loads causing cracks in the internal UHPC of the specimens, which is the main reason for the variation in uniaxial tensile strength among specimens with different rebar configurations. Furthermore, the main crack in specimens with UHPC matrix failure can only occur at the end of the reinforcement anchorage. This is because if the main crack were to form within the UHPC matrix anchorage region, the required tensile load would need to consider the reinforcing effect of the rebar in addition to the failure load of the end interface of reinforcement anchorage.

Influence of rebar parameters on uniaxial tensile strength

Figure 18a shows that for specimens with rebar debonding failure at the same anchorage length, the uniaxial tensile strength gradually increases with increasing rebar diameter. The H8D2 specimen had the smallest rebar diameter, with an average tensile strength of 3.66 MPa. When the rebar diameter was increased to 10, 12, and 14 mm, the corresponding increase in uniaxial tensile strength is 10.7%, 18.6%, and 30.9%, respectively. Increasing the rebar diameter significantly increases the contact area between the UHPC and the rebar, thereby improving the rebar–UHPC bond and increasing an increase in the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimen. For specimens with UHPC matrix failure at the same anchorage length, the variation trend of the uniaxial tensile strength with rebar diameter is similar to that of specimens with rebar debonding failure, showing an increasing trend with increasing rebar diameter. This may be attributed to with the increase in rebar diameter, the relative rib height of the rebar decreases, and the relative rib spacing of the rebar increases, reducing the oblique compressive force between the rebar and UHPC43. This results in a reduction in circumferential tensile stress in UHPC under the same load, delaying the formation of microcracks within the UHPC and leading to an increase in the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimen.

Figure 18b reflects the influence of rebar type on the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimens. It can be seen that when the anchorage lengths were 20, 40, and 60 mm, compared to the UN-C10 specimens, the UN-H10 specimen exhibited an increase in uniaxial tensile strength of 22.7%, 23.8%, and 13.8%, respectively. As for the UN-H10D4 and UN-H10D6 specimens, since their failure mode is UHPC matrix failure, the measured tensile capacity was more than 23.8% and 15.3% higher, respectively, than that of the smooth round rebar specimens under the same conditions. Therefore, compared with smooth round rebar, ribbed rebar had a more pronounced effect on improving the uniaxial tensile strength at the interface of the specimen. This is because the bond strength between ribbed rebar and UHPC further enhances the mechanical interlocking force44,45, and the friction resistance is relatively higher than that of smooth rebar46, resulting in a stronger anchorage effect at the interface of the specimen and a higher uniaxial tensile strength. It is worth noting that the uniaxial tensile strength of UN-C10D6 is increased by 8.5% compared to the UN-H10D4 specimen, but its final failure mode is still rebar debonding. This is mainly due to the fact that the interaction force between the smooth rebar and UHPC during tension only occurs in the radial direction, causing no circumferential microcracks to form in the UHPC matrix45, resulting in no change in the failure mode of the specimen.

Figure 18c reflects the influence of anchorage length on the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimens. It can be seen that for the UN-H10 specimens, compared to the specimens with an anchorage length of 20 mm, the specimen with an anchorage length of 30 mm exhibits an increase in uniaxial tensile strength of 42.2%. This means that when the failure mode of the specimen is rebar debonding, the uniaxial tensile strength increases with an increase in anchorage length. The analysis suggests that under the same rebar diameter, increasing the anchorage length enhances the bonding area between the rebar and UHPC, allowing for sufficient bond between them47, thereby increasing the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimen. As the anchorage length increased to 40 mm, the failure mode of the specimen transitions from rebar debonding to UHPC matrix failure, and the uniaxial tensile strength increases, exceeding 6.70 MPa. Subsequently, the failure mode of the specimen remained unchanged with increasing anchorage length, while the uniaxial tensile strength further increased, eventually reaching 8.27 MPa. The mechanism by which the anchorage length affects the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimen is similar to that of the rebar diameter. By increasing the bonding area, the circumferential tensile stress between the rebar and UHPC under the same load is reduced, requiring a greater tensile load for the formation of microcracks, thereby increasing the uniaxial tensile strength of the specimen.

The load increased linearly with displacement until the first crack appeared at the UHPC-NSC interface, reaching the initial cracking load of the specimen. The synergy stage ended at this point.Anchorage enhancement stage: After the occurrence of interface cracks, the load-displacement curves of specimens with an anchorage length of ≤ 40 mm showed a slight decrease, followed by a continuous increase until the peak load was reached. However, for specimens with an anchorage length > 40 mm, the load-displacement curves showed little or no decrease, with only a slight decrease in slope. For specimens with UHPC matrix failure, although the fiber bridging effect contributes to some residual load-bearing capacity in the specimens, it is significantly lower than that in rebar debonding specimens, resulting in a faster descent rate and a steeper curve.

Therefore, increasing the rebar diameter not only enhances the bonding performance between the rebar and UHPC, reducing the relative bond slip, but also reduces the deformation of the rebar itself, further limiting the expansion of the interface cracks.

Compared to ribbed rebar, the interface crack width growth rate of specimens with smooth round rebar is nearly twice as high. This is mainly due to the lack of mechanical interlocking force, which significantly reduces the resistance to bond slip between the smooth round rebar and UHPC. In fact, there are cases where the interface crack width growth rate of the C10D6 specimen is still 31.5% higher than that of the H10D4 specimen, indicating that the rebar type had a greater influence on the interface crack width growth rate compared to the rebar diameter.

Conclusions

This study investigated the tensile bond performance and crack-control behavior of UHPC–NSC wet-joint interfaces with different steel fiber contents, rebar diameters, rebar types, and anchorage lengths. Based on the experimental results and mechanistic analysis, the following conclusions can be drawn:The unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens failed in a brittle manner along the UHPC–NSC interface, indicating that the interface remained the strength-controlling region. Steel fibers enhanced intact UHPC mainly through matrix crack bridging, whereas no effective direct fiber bridging across the UHPC–NSC interface was observed in the unreinforced bonded specimens. The interfacial tensile strength first increased and then decreased with increasing steel fiber content, reaching the highest average value at 2.0%, where it increased from 2.44 MPa to 2.90 MPa, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 18.9%. SEM observations and ImageJ-based analysis further showed that the apparent pore/void area fraction was lowest at 2.0% and increased at 2.5%, suggesting that excessive steel fiber content may reduce local compactness near the fractured region and weaken the effective UHPC–NSC bond. Therefore, 2.0% steel fiber content can be regarded as the preferable dosage for UHPC–NSC wet joints under the present experimental conditions.Under the high-strength NSC substrate condition adopted in this study, UHPC improved the interfacial load-transfer capacity of wet-joint bonded specimens. Compared with NSC–NSC specimens, UHPC–NSC specimens exhibited 12.6%–18.9% higher splitting tensile strength and 15.9%–24.1% higher four-point bending strength at different curing ages. However, the strengths of UHPC–NSC specimens remained considerably lower than those of the corresponding intact NSC specimens, reaching only 67%–76% of the splitting tensile strength and 56%–60% of the four-point bending strength of intact NSC. These results indicate that UHPC enhances the bond performance of wet joints, but does not fully eliminate the inherent weakness of the new-to-old concrete interface.Rebar reinforcement significantly altered the post-cracking load-transfer path and the governing failure region of UHPC–NSC specimens. Compared with unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens, reinforced specimens exhibiting rebar debonding failure showed an increase in tensile capacity of approximately 13.8%–98.6%. When the anchorage effect was further enhanced and the failure mode shifted to UHPC matrix failure, the average tensile strength increased by 55.8% compared with specimens exhibiting rebar debonding failure. This demonstrates that sufficient rebar anchorage can shift the governing failure region from the UHPC–NSC interface to the UHPC matrix near the rebar anchorage end, thereby improving the overall tensile capacity of the bonded system.Rebar anchorage length and rebar type were the key parameters controlling tensile capacity and interface crack growth in reinforced UHPC–NSC wet joints. For the UN-H10 series, increasing the anchorage length from 20 mm to 60 mm increased the tensile strength from 4.05 MPa to 8.27 MPa, corresponding to an increase of approximately 104.2%, while reducing the interface crack width growth rate by up to 82.5%. Under the same rebar diameter and anchorage length, ribbed rebar increased the tensile strength by 13.8%–23.8% and reduced the interface crack width growth rate by 46.1%–52.9% compared with smooth round rebar. Therefore, the design of UHPC–NSC wet joints should not rely solely on the high strength of UHPC; sufficient anchorage length and ribbed rebar are also required to improve bond-slip resistance and crack-control performance.

The unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens failed in a brittle manner along the UHPC–NSC interface, indicating that the interface remained the strength-controlling region. Steel fibers enhanced intact UHPC mainly through matrix crack bridging, whereas no effective direct fiber bridging across the UHPC–NSC interface was observed in the unreinforced bonded specimens. The interfacial tensile strength first increased and then decreased with increasing steel fiber content, reaching the highest average value at 2.0%, where it increased from 2.44 MPa to 2.90 MPa, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 18.9%. SEM observations and ImageJ-based analysis further showed that the apparent pore/void area fraction was lowest at 2.0% and increased at 2.5%, suggesting that excessive steel fiber content may reduce local compactness near the fractured region and weaken the effective UHPC–NSC bond. Therefore, 2.0% steel fiber content can be regarded as the preferable dosage for UHPC–NSC wet joints under the present experimental conditions.

The unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens failed in a brittle manner along the UHPC–NSC interface, indicating that the interface remained the strength-controlling region. Steel fibers enhanced intact UHPC mainly through matrix crack bridging, whereas no effective direct fiber bridging across the UHPC–NSC interface was observed in the unreinforced bonded specimens. The interfacial tensile strength first increased and then decreased with increasing steel fiber content, reaching the highest average value at 2.0%, where it increased from 2.44 MPa to 2.90 MPa, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 18.9%. Under the high-strength NSC substrate condition adopted in this study, UHPC improved the interfacial load-transfer capacity of wet-joint bonded specimens. Compared with NSC–NSC specimens, UHPC–NSC specimens exhibited 12.6%–18.9% higher splitting tensile strength and 15.9%–24.1% higher four-point bending strength at different curing ages. However, the strengths of UHPC–NSC specimens remained considerably lower than those of the corresponding intact NSC specimens, reaching only 67%–76% of the splitting tensile strength and 56%–60% of the four-point bending strength of intact NSC. These results indicate that UHPC enhances the bond performance of wet joints, but does not fully eliminate the inherent weakness of the new-to-old concrete interface.

Rebar reinforcement significantly altered the post-cracking load-transfer path and the governing failure region of UHPC–NSC specimens. Compared with unreinforced UHPC–NSC specimens, reinforced specimens exhibiting rebar debonding failure showed an increase in tensile capacity of approximately 13.8%–98.6%. When the anchorage effect was further enhanced and the failure mode shifted to UHPC matrix failure, the average tensile strength increased by 55.8% compared with specimens exhibiting rebar debonding failure. This demonstrates that sufficient rebar anchorage can shift the governing failure region from the UHPC–NSC interface to the UHPC matrix near the rebar anchorage end, thereby improving the overall tensile capacity of the bonded system.

Rebar anchorage length and rebar type were the key parameters controlling tensile capacity and interface crack growth in reinforced UHPC–NSC wet joints. For the UN-H10 series, increasing the anchorage length from 20 mm to 60 mm increased the tensile strength from 4.05 MPa to 8.27 MPa, corresponding to an increase of approximately 104.2%, while reducing the interface crack width growth rate by up to 82.5%. Under the same rebar diameter and anchorage length, ribbed rebar increased the tensile strength by 13.8%–23.8% and reduced the interface crack width growth rate by 46.1%–52.9% compared with smooth round rebar. Therefore, the design of UHPC–NSC wet joints should not rely solely on the high strength of UHPC; sufficient anchorage length and ribbed rebar are also required to improve bond-slip resistance and crack-control performance.

It should be noted that only one high-strength NSC substrate was considered in this study, and the number of replicate specimens was limited. Therefore, the absolute bond strength values and failure thresholds should not be directly generalized to lower-strength substrates without further verification. Future studies should examine C30–C50 concretes with larger replicate datasets to evaluate the influence of substrate properties on UHPC–NSC interfacial bond performance.
